Development and Validation of RP-HPLC Method for trans-Resveratrol in HPβCD-Loaded Stealth Liposomes: Stability and Release Studies
Authors: Adilah Marwa, Anton Bahtiar, Ahmad Fuad Shamsuddin, Kartika Sari, Mulkan Syarif, Witta Kartika Restu, Mahdi Jufri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
trans-Resveratrol (trans-RES) and cis-resveratrol are isomers that are difficult to separate due to their structural similarities. This study developed a method to enhance the solubility and stability of trans-RES, focusing on preventing its isomerization to the inactive cis form. A reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) method was optimized for quantifying trans-RES complexed with hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD) in stealth liposomes. The technique used an isocratic mobile phase of water:acetonitrile (65:35 v/v) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, with UV detection at 306 nm. Validation showed excellent linearity (R 2 = 0.9997), precision (RSD < 2%), accuracy (98–102% recovery), and sensitivity (LOD 0.96 μg/mL, LOQ 2.91 μg/mL), with high resolution (R s > 16.5) between trans- and cis-isomers and robustness against minor parameter variations. The dual-delivery system combined HPβCD inclusion complexes with stealth liposomes. Liposomal formulations F1–F5 showed sustained trans-RES release over 24 h, with F5 complexed in HPβCD demonstrating the sustained release profile (49.8% at 12 h). Release kinetics followed the Korsmeyer–Peppas model (R 2 > 0.90), indicating a non-Fickian mechanism. Furthermore, the formulation protected trans-RES from UV-induced isomerization, confirming enhanced stability.

Introduction

Resveratrol (3,4’,5-trans-trihydroxy-stilbene; C14H12O3) is a phytoalexin polyphenol compound that has been shown to have anti-inflammatory, , antioxidant, and anticancer activities. However, its therapeutic use is limited due to extremely low oral bioavailability (<1%) caused by rapid metabolism in the liver and intestines, as well as absorption inhibition by P-glycoprotein. Additionally, resveratrol has low solubility (Log P = 3.1) and is easily degraded by light, oxygen, and heat, leading to isomerization into the cis form, which lacks bioactivity. , This stability limitation poses a critical challenge in developing resveratrol nutraceutical products, as stability is the most important quality requirement for pharmaceutical products. −

As a primary quality parameter, stability facilitates the selection of better formulation components and packaging. Stability also determines optimal storage conditions and expiration dates, ensuring the product’s identity, efficacy, potency, safety, and purity until use. − In stability studies, quantification methods must be specific, capable of accurately measuring the target substance without interference from other sample components or degradation products, by trans-RES testing recommendations in the International Conference on Harmonisation ICH Q1A­(R2) guidelines. To meet these requirements, liquid chromatography is widely regarded as the preferred analytical technique in method development due to its ability to separate all compounds in a single analysis and facilitate the identification of impurities and degradation products. ,

High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC), and Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) or Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) have been reported for the estimation of trans-RES in various matrices. − LC-MS/MS is known for its high sensitivity and selectivity, particularly in biological or complex matrices. However, these techniques are often limited by high-cost equipment and the requirement for technical expertise. , In particular, a typical GC-MS analysis for resveratrol is a time-consuming process due to necessary derivatization steps and a slow oven temperature gradient required to achieve separation. As demonstrated by Gonthier et al. such a GC-MS method results in retention times of over 11 min for each resveratrol isomer, contributing to a lengthy total analysis time per sample. In contrast, UV-based methods, such as those developed by Sonule et al. for nano formulations, offer a more cost-effective and accessible approach. Nevertheless, these methods lack sufficient specificity, especially in distinguishing trans-RES from its cis-RES and other excipients commonly found in formulation matrices. Meanwhile, HPLC-based methods have primarily focused on method development without addressing full method validation in complex pharmaceutical formulations. − As demonstrated by Rigon et al., the trans- and cis-resveratrol isomers were successfully separated and detected at a specific wavelength of 306 nm using RP-HPLC-PDA with a three-phase gradient system 75:0:25 (v/v) water/methanol/acetonitrile. This method achieved complete chromatographic separation within 10 min using a Phenomenex Luna C18(2) column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). However, it was relatively solvent-intensive, consuming up to 14.4 mL per injection, which reduces its suitability for routine analysis. Similarly, Mota-Lugo et al. employed RP-HPLC-DAD using Halo C18 columns (3.0 × 75 mm, 2.7 μm, USA) with a 70:30 (v/v) ammonium formate/acetonitrile mobile phase, but the narrow retention time difference (0.8–1.3 min) between the isomers increased the risk of coelution under declining column performance. , These limitations highlight the need for a simple and efficient isocratic RP-HPLC method.

This study developed a dual delivery system involving the formation of inclusion complexes resveratrol with HPβCD (RHCD) to enhance solubility and physicochemical stability. The encapsulation of RHCD into PEG-coated stealth liposomes prolongs systemic half-life while avoiding the reticuloendothelial system (RES). To support the development of this formulation, an efficient and straightforward RP-HPLC DAD (306 nm) method was designed to quantify trans-RES in the system. Additionally, the effects of lipid composition and RHCD on physical stability and drug release profiles were comprehensively evaluated, including analysis of release mechanisms using mathematical kinetic models. Thus, the developed formulation and analytical method are expected to serve as a foundation for broader applications of resveratrol and similar bioactive compound delivery.

Methods Validation Results

Serial quantitative dilutions of the resveratrol stock solution were used to establish linearity at seven concentration levels, ranging from 0.5 to 50 μg/mL. Chromatographic responses were recorded, and the concentrations of the standard solutions were plotted against the average peak area (Figure ), where the X-axis represents concentration. The Y-axis shows the peak area response. The analysis method, using a mobile phase mixture of water:ACN (65:35, v/v) with a flow rate of 1 mL/min and a column temperature of 30 °C, showed excellent linearity with a regression equation of y = 116.43x + 311.86 and an R 2 of 0.9997 in the range of 0.5–50 μg/mL. This R 2 value exceeds the ICH Q2­(R1) requirement of at least 0.999 for pharmaceutical analysis. , All calibration points show deviations <2%. Thus, this method meets all linearity requirements for quantitative analysis.

The robustness of the method was evaluated through deliberate variations in detection wavelength (±5 nm), flow rate (±0.2 mL/min), and column temperature (±5 °C). Statistical analysis (ANOVA with Tukey’s test, α = 0.05) revealed significant differences in retention times (Rt) and peak area between specific parameter levels (denoted by superscript letters in Table ). However, these variations exhibited no practical impact on analytical performance. Wavelength alterations (301–311 nm) induced statistically significant but analytically negligible Rt shifts (Δ ≤ 0.02 min), while maintaining exceptional precision (RSD ≤ 0.152%). Additionally, changes in wavelength significantly influenced peak area, with RSD values for peak area ranging from 0.412% at 301 nm to 1.632% at 311 nm. The observed peak area increase at 306 nm further supports the hypothesis that trans-RES exhibits a maximum absorption at this wavelength. According to the Beer–Lambert law, the absorbance is directly proportional to the concentration of the analyte and its molar absorptivity at the specific wavelength. , Therefore, the higher peak area observed at 306 nm compared to other wavelengths (301 and 311 nm) directly correlates with the enhanced absorption of trans-RES at this wavelength. − This confirms the specificity and effectiveness of 306 nm as the optimal detection wavelength for trans-RES in this method, validating its accurate quantification in the analyzed samples.

Flow rate adjustments (0.8–1.2 mL/min) produced expected inverse Rt relationships (4.62–6.98 min), with all levels not differing significantly; crucially, precision remained robust (RSD ≤ 0.539%). Similarly, elevated column temperatures (25 to 35 °C) significantly reduced Rt (6.14–5.62 min) due to enhanced analyte mobility, yet precision stayed well-controlled (RSD ≤ 1.403%). Peak area was also affected by flow rate and column temperature adjustments, but RSD values for peak area remained within acceptable limits (RSD ≤ 1.224%). No significant degradation in peak symmetry was observed across all experimental conditions. This demonstrates that statistically significant Rt changes reflect method sensitivity to operational parameters but do not compromise validity. The stability of peak area further supports the method’s robustness. These results unequivocally demonstrate compliance with ICH validation requirements, confirming that the method is reliable and precise under the tested conditions.

The developed analytical method demonstrated excellent performance, with a Limit of Detection (LOD) of 0.96 μg/mL and a Limit of Quantification (LOQ) of 2.91 μg/mL, calculated based on a residual standard deviation (Sy/x) of 33.916, and a linear regression coefficient with a slope of 116.425 and an R 2 of 0.9997. Notably, the method is highly specific for complex matrices as evidenced by the baseline resolution of trans- and cis-RES (R s > 16.5). The method also serves as a stability-indicating assay, capable of detecting cis-isomer formation under UV exposure. Furthermore, it uses a simple isocratic elution system that minimizes analysis time and solvent use 7.5 mL/run, making it cost-effective and well-suited for routine quality control. These features collectively demonstrate that the method is not only sensitive enough for formulation and degradation studies but also practical, efficient, and robust for widespread application. Compared to a previous study by Rigon et al. that utilized HPLC-PDA with a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.29 μg/mL and a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 4.29 μg/mL, this method demonstrated higher sensitivity. In addition, the method offers a wide linearity range 0.5–50 μg/mL, with good precision, indicated by low Sy/x values.

Characterization and Release of Resveratrol from Stealth Liposomes

The combination of lipid composition and the presence of RHCD inclusion complexes affects the particle size, PdI, and zeta potential of liposomes. Formula F1, with the highest EPC composition, produces the smallest particles, reflecting a high content of unsaturated fatty acids, which increases EPC flexibility and facilitates the formation of vesicles with smaller particle sizes. Conversely, an increase in HSPC, a phospholipid with a high content of saturated fatty acids, causes an increase in particle size. PdI values below 0.4 in all formulas indicate a homogeneous system, with F3 (EPC:HSPC 1:1) showing the best kinetic stability. The presence of mPEG2000-DSPE helps maintain steric stability and prevent aggregation. High negative zeta potential in all formulations ensures colloidal stability through electrostatic repulsion, although a decrease in zeta potential in formulations with high HSPC indicates interaction with RHCD, which remains within stable limits (>±30 mV). , The trans-RES content in Table remains relatively consistent across all formulations, with slight variations. Formula F1, containing the highest EPC content, shows the highest resveratrol content, suggesting that unsaturated fatty acids favor its encapsulation. Formulas with higher HSPC content, such as F5, F6, and F7, show a minor decrease in trans-RES content, likely due to stronger interactions between HSPC and resveratrol, which could affect its solubility or encapsulation.

Based on the HPLC analysis results in Figure , all stealth liposome formulations F1–F5 showed a sustained release profile over 24 h with significant variations in release rates. Formula F6, which contains the RHCD inclusion complex, achieved the highest release rate, reaching 99.8% at the 12th hour, highlighting the crucial role of HPβCD in enhancing the solubility and diffusion of resveratrol through the formation of more hydrophilic inclusion complexes. Conversely, F7 without RHCD showed the lowest release, 27.3% at 24 h. Other formulas, F1–F5 showed release patterns consistent with lipid composition effects. EPC-based formulas tended to release the drug faster than HSPC-based ones because the higher membrane fluidity of EPC facilitates drug diffusion, while HSPC forms a rigid bilayer that slows release.

Release profile of trans-RES from stealth liposome after 24 h of analysis.

The decrease in resveratrol levels at 24 h was particularly pronounced in F6, which dropped from 99.8% to 87.3%. This phenomenon is attributed to the instability of resveratrol in PBS at pH 7.4, which triggers drug degradation. , Resveratrol is known to be unstable under neutral/basic conditions because its hydroxyphenol group is susceptible to oxidation and isomerization. A study by Zupančič et al., 2015, showed that in PBS pH 7.4, resveratrol can lose over 30% of its stability within 24 h due to oxidative reactions that convert it into inactive compounds. Environmental factors such as light exposure and temperature during in vitro testing also contribute to resveratrol instability, particularly because this compound is sensitive to photooxidation in aqueous solutions. Therefore, the decrease in release percentage at 24 h more accurately reflects resveratrol degradation in the PBS medium.

Notably, F5 showed the most favorable sustained release profile, balancing both release rate and duration. This formulation combined HPβCD inclusion complex and free resveratrol, resulting in a prolonged release over 24 h. The dual presence of solubilized resveratrol HPβCD complexed and uncomplexed drug entrapped within the liposomal bilayer enables a biphasic release pattern, an initial diffusion-driven phase followed by a slower sustained phase governed by lipid bilayer permeability. This result highlights the synergistic effect of HPβCD in enhancing solubility, while stealth liposomes control and stabilize drug release.

Based on the release kinetics analysis results in Table , the Korsmeyer–Peppas model yielded an R 2 value greater than 0.90 for all formulas, indicating that a combination of diffusion and matrix erosion influences the release mechanism of resveratrol. This is consistent with the theory that the release mechanism from liposome-based delivery systems follows a non-Fickian model, where not only diffusion but also physical changes in the liposome matrix influence the release rate. The diffusion exponent (n) in the range of 0.45–0.89 indicates that drug release from liposomes follows a non-Fickian or anomalous mechanism involving two main processes, drug diffusion through the liposome matrix and relaxation of the lipid vesicle structure. This relaxation process is crucial in liposome systems because stiffer lipid structures (HSPC) adapt more slowly, while flexible lipids (EPC) tend to release drugs more rapidly.

Stability of trans-RES under UV Exposure

The standard trans-RES chromatogram (Figure B) shows cis isomer peaks after 1 and 24 h, indicating the occurrence of photochemical isomerization. This phenomenon is caused by the photosensitive nature of trans-RES, where exposure to light (especially UV) induces conversion to the cis isomer through rotation of the alkene double bond. In stark contrast, no cis-isomer peak is observed in the RHCD inclusion complex (Figure C), as cyclodextrin’s hydrophobic cavity shields the phenolic ring and alkene bond from light, preventing isomerization. , Similarly, the liposome formulations (Figure D-F) did not show a cis peak, as the phospholipid matrix acts as a barrier against light and oxygen. The dual protection in RHCD stealth liposomes results in the highest stability, as evidenced by the absence of the cis isomer.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Resveratrol (≥99% purity) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), while trans-RES and HPβCD were obtained from Xi’an Natural Field Bio-Tech, China. Hydrogenated soy phosphatidylcholine (HSPC), methoxy-polyethylene glycol–distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine (mPEG-DSPE), and sodium oleate were supplied by Lipoid GmbH (Ludwigshafen, Germany). Cholesterol (Chol) and oleic acid (OA) were from Xian Natura Chemicals (China). All solvents used for analysis, including methanol, acetonitrile, and dichloromethane, were of HPLC grade (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Other chemicals and reagents used were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Methods

Chromatographic Conditions

The absorbance spectrum was recorded between 280 and 400 nm to determine the trans-RES’s maximum absorbance wavelength (λmax) using a Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–vis spectrophotometer. As shown in Figure , the λmax was found at 306 nm and selected for quantification. This wavelength corresponds to the strongest π → π* transition typical of conjugated aromatic compounds like resveratrol, ensuring optimal sensitivity and linearity as described by the Beer–Lambert law. , Peak areas were analyzed using reversed-phase HPLC with an Agilent Diode Array Detector G1315B and an Agilent Capillary Pump G1376A for 11 min. A 250 mm × 4.6 mm ID, 5 μm ZORBAX Eclipse Plus RP-C18 column (Santa Clara, California, USA) was used, and the analysis was performed at 30 ± 1 °C.

UV–vis absorption spectrum of trans-RES, showing the maximum absorbance at 306 nm.

Gradient elution was performed, and the mobile phase consisted of water/acetonitrile (v/v) ratio 60:40, 65:35, and 70:30. This mobile phase composition was optimized and validated based on system suitability test parameters, which demonstrated acceptable retention time, peak symmetry, resolution, and theoretical plate number to separate trans- and cis-resveratrol effectively. The flow rate was kept constant at 1.0 mL/min.

Method Validation

The proposed method was validated by the US Pharmacopeia (USP) guidelines and the International Council for Harmonization (ICH). , The analytical parameters chosen for validation included accuracy, precision, linearity, specificity, limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), and robustness.

Specificity

Specificity refers to the method’s ability to distinguish between trans-RES and other structures present in the sample, such as lipids, cholesterol, and HP-B-CD. Specificity was evaluated by analyzing the filtrate from the resveratrol stealth liposome excipient and comparing the resulting chromatogram with the chromatogram obtained from a 50.0 μg/mL trans-RES standard sample. ,

Linearity

Linearity is the linear relationship between concentration and test measurement. Standard stock solutions were prepared by dissolving 10 mg of Resveratrol standard in methanol to obtain a final concentration of 1000 μg/mL. A calibration curve was created by diluting the standard stock with the mobile phase to obtain a concentration range of 0.5–50 μg/mL. All solutions were filtered using a 0.45 μm PTFE membrane before HPLC injection. Linearity was determined through linear regression analysis.

Accuracy

The method’s accuracy was tested using the spiked recovery method with previously known matrix samples. Resveratrol standards were added to the sample solution at three concentrations, namely 20, 25, and 30 μg/mL. The spiked samples were then analyzed, and the recovery percentage was calculated using the following formula: %RecoveryofSpiked=Concentrationspiked−ConcentrationunspikedConcentrationadded×100%

Precision

Precision testing was conducted by measuring resveratrol concentrations at three levels, namely 20, 25, and 30 μg/mL, repeatedly three times in 1 day (intraday) and on three different days (interday). The measurement results were calculated as the mean and standard deviation, and the precision value was expressed as relative standard deviation (RSD).

Sensitivity

The limit of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were determined based on the signal-to-noise ratio. The LOD is the lowest concentration, producing a peak height three times greater than the baseline noise. The LOQ is the lowest concentration that produces a peak height at least ten times the baseline noise. The formulas for calculating LOD and LOQ are as follows: LOD=3.3×σS LOD=10×σS

SD is the standard deviation of the intersection point Y, and S is the slope of the calibration curve.

Robustness

Robustness is defined as the ability of a method to produce consistent results despite minor changes in the experimental conditions of chromatography, demonstrating the method’s reliability during regular use. Varying measurements tested robustness on three parameters, namely temperature, flow rate, and wavelength. The temperature parameters varied were 25, 30, and 35 °C, the flow rate was changed to 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 mL/min, and the wavelength was measured at 301, 306, and 311 nm.

Preparation of Stealth Liposome RHCD

The trans-RES stealth liposome formulation was prepared using a thin-layer hydration technique, modified from the method of Yuwanda et al. A resveratrol concentration of 8 mg/mL was formulated in a 10 mL vial and stored at 2–8 °C until further analysis.

Stealth liposomes containing trans-RES and the RHCD combination were prepared according to the formula in Table . Before liposome preparation, the RHCD inclusion complex was obtained by codissolving trans-RES and HPβCD in water under a homogenizer at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, followed by 5 min of ultrasonication at 55% amplitude. The mixture was then spray-dried to yield a solid inclusion complex. The lipid components (HSPC, mPEG2000-DSPE, cholesterol, and oleic acid) were dissolved in dichloromethane and mixed with trans-RES in ethanol. The mixture was evaporated using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C to form a thin lipid layer, which was then stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Afterward, the lipid film was hydrated using a phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4) containing the RHCD powder at 55 °C. Hydration was performed with glass beads at 100 rpm for 30 min. The resulting multilamellar vesicles were downsized using probe sonication (35% amplitude, 5 min). Samples were then characterized for their average hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index, and zeta potential using dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK), with three replicates prepared in ultrapure water.

Resveratrol Release Test

The release of resveratrol from stealth liposomes was tested using cellulose dialysis (MWCO 12–14 kDa) in phosphate buffer pH 7.4 (100 mL) at 37 °C with stirring at 100 rpm. Sampling was performed at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h, with 1 mL taken and the same amount of buffer replaced. HPLC analyzed resveratrol concentration, and release data were evaluated using zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas kinetic models to determine the release mechanism.

Measurement of trans-RES Stability under UV Exposure

Methanol, trans-RES powder, RHCD inclusion powder, and stealth liposomes were exposed for 1 and 24 h to 365 nm UVA light in a Benchtop 2UV Transilluminator (LMS-20, UVP Ltd., Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). Resveratrol chromatograms were analyzed using a validated HPLC method.

Statistical Analysis

For parametric data, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s test were used for statistical analysis with the help of SPSS software version 26. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated an effective RP-HPLC method for the quantitative analysis of trans-RES in stealth liposome formulations containing HPβCD inclusion complexes. The analytical method developed using an aqueous acetonitrile mobile phase (65:35 v/v) demonstrated high performance with high resolution (R s > 2.0), optimal column efficiency (NTP > 10,000), and consistent retention times. Method validation confirmed linearity with R 2 = 0.9997 over the concentration range of 0.5–50 μg/mL, high accuracy (recovery 98–102%), satisfactory precision (RSD < 2%), and adequate sensitivity (LOD 0.96 μg/mL and LOQ 2.91 μg/mL). The stealth liposome formulation combined with HPβCD inclusion complexes has been shown to enhance the physicochemical stability and solubility of trans-RES significantly. This delivery system exhibits a sustained release profile of up to 24 h. Kinetic release analysis indicates a non-Fickian mechanism involving a combination of diffusion and matrix erosion. The primary advantage of this formulation is its ability to protect trans-RES from isomerization caused by UV exposure.
