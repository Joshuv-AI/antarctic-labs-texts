Cosmological viability of anisotropic inflation in Thurston spacetimes
Devika J.S, Tanay Gupta, Sukanta Panda

Abstract
Abstract : The introduction of anisotropy into large scale geometry of the universe has led to the development of Thurston spacetimes possessing an inherent anisotropic curvature in them. Studies related to these anisotropic spacetimes have revealed how they could explain the evolutionary dynamics and light propagation in the universe. Here, we consider these spacetimes under the inflationary epoch to examine whether we could address the possibility of anisotropic inflation in the universe. Researches have proposed inflationary models where a vector field coupled to the inflaton was found violating the cosmic no-hair theorem for the anisotropic Bianchi type I spacetime through the existence of a stable anisotropically inflationary fixed point. Lately, this study was extended to axisymmetric spacetimes of Bianchi type II, III and the Kantowski-Sachs metric and it was inferred that the entire family of spacetimes was attracted to the anisotropic Bianchi I fixed point. By fabricating Thurston spacetimes as inflationary models with its shear sourced by a vector field, we perform dynamical stability and phase space analyses to confer the feasibility of anisotropic inflation. The results obtained for the considered set of Thurston geometries showed the presence and convergence to a unique, stable inflationary fixed point which was found to be similar to those obtained in Bianchi spacetimes, thereby indicating the cosmological viability of inflation with anisotropic hair.

Introduction

Understanding the evolution of the universe and describing its underlying geometry have been the foundational aspects of modern cosmology. Examining the shape, which pertains to the large scale geometry of the universe, and the curvature, explained through General Relativity which predicts the flat, open or closed curvature of the universe, contingent upon the mass and energy density in the space, we develop our insights into the structure and geometry of the spacetime. The development of cosmological models have been traced over time from the early geocentric and heliocentric views, followed by the revolutions initiated by the Einstein’s theory of gravity and the concept of expanding universe discovered by Edwin Hubble that motivates the dynamical modeling of the universe, further to the formulation of the Big Bang, the inflationary and the Standard

The rapid expansion of the universe captured by the period of cosmic inflation, explains how it dilutes the irregularities and asymmetries from the early universe, thereby smoothening it onto a homogeneous, isotropic background at large scales. Here, we perform a similar study for the inflationary modeling and dynamical stability analysis with the Thurston spacetimes to check whether it illustrates the existence of any stable anisotropically inflationary fixed point, validating anisotropic inflation.

The inflationary model

We refer to the inflationary model considered for the axisymmetric spacetimes from

Following the same formalism as in

(10)

6
Dynamical stability analysis

The objective of this section is to investigate the phase-space structure and stability by applying the dynamical systems approach for the anisotropic inflationary models proposed through Thurston spacetimes. Understanding the field equations associated with each of the spacetimes and formulating them as autonomous system of differential equations, parametrized by the appropriate dynamical variables help to describe the phase space of the system. Further, we find the fixed points of the system and classify them based on the theory of linear stability
[
24
]
to look forward for the existence of stable and unique anisotropic fixed points which would infer the cosmological feasibility of anisotropic inflation in Thurston spacetimes.

The fixed points (also called
critical points
) are those points where the system remains in a steady state (or equilibrium) indefinitely. The critical points for each system is found by solving its corresponding set of differential equations by setting them to zero. The obtained points are then studied to find the stability of the system in a linear regime close to these points.

The linear stability theory approximates the system linearly around the fixed points,
\frac{d\delta X^{i}}{d\alpha}=J\delta X^{i}
, and the eigenvalues of J, the
Jacobian matrix
(or
stability matrix
) evaluated at the critical points conveys the stability at that point. If all the eigenvalues of
J
at a given critical point have positive real parts, then trajectories are repelled from that critical point and it is an
unstable
point (or a
repeller
). If all eigenvalues have negative real parts, the point would attract all the nearby trajectories and it is
stable
(
attractor
). Lastly, if at least two eigenvalues have real parts with opposite signs, the corresponding critical point is known as a
saddle point
, attracting trajectories in certain directions while repelling them in others.

Results & Analysis

The inflationary modeling of the Thurston spacetimes resulted in the analysis of the existence and dynamics of an anisotropic inflationary epoch in the universe. Formulation of dynamic phase space systems for the Thurston spacetimes from their field equations and subsequent stability evaluations depicted how each of these geometries adhere to the stable existence of an anisotropic inflation. The phase flow analysis from figs.
1(a)
and
1(b)
in section
6.4
leaves a clear indication on these remarks which will also be interpreted from the following section.

From our observations in section
7
corresponding to our results shown in figs.
2
-
3
, we analyze a few important aspects. Foremost, the plots are
potential-dependent
: a power law potential, being a specific case of exponential potential, allows one to differentiate among geometries; while the more general exponential one hard-codes them. Further, in the power law potential, only the Thurston geometry having a positive cylindrical curvature (
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
) has a sharp
kink
in the beginning of phase space plot which is purely a characteristic of its inherent spatial curvature, since all the rest of the geometries carrying a negative curvature behave roughly the same. Talking of this, one can even distinguish among those with negative spatial curvatures by observing the
field lag
between the two epochs of cosmic inflation. Specifically, in Cartesian open geometries (Nil & Solv), the transition is almost instantaneous while in cylindrical open geometry (
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}
), there is a noticeable lag of about
\phi\simeq
1 units, suggesting that not only the spatical curvature but also the
inherent geometry
of the spacetime itself plays a vital role in determining the physics of early universe.

It is interesting to see that this latter case of an open cylindrical geometry (
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}
) is the only one with a smooth anisotropy transition with e-folding numbers among all the anisotropic Thurston geometries. Indeed a stable sttractor exists in all our geometries, but the origin is
sudden
and
spontaneous
in all the other geometries except
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}

c
), the late the anisotropy starts to rise with the e-fold number. This directly verifies the presence of anisotropy-causing axisymmmetric vector field.

The exponential potential, being a much more rapidly varying function of
\phi
as compared to power law potential, does not directly allows one to distinguish among geometries. It is a characteristic of this potential that a
stable
isotropic inflationary phase does
not
exist;
\phi
steadily increases (through what in power law potential was a
stable
isotropic epoch) and settles at steady anisotropic one, from where it continues to its stable attractor point.

The exponential potential also smoothes-out anisotropies to a greater extent as compared to power law, as the stable attractor point has about 10 times smaller anisotropy as compared to power law one. We also see that for this potential also, the weaker the coupling strength, the lower the initial anisotropy is, thus re-verifying the presence of our axisymmetric vector field. Further, the exponential potential has also been observed to force the anisotropies to decay
strongly linearly
upto about 8 e-folds, from where they traverse towards their stable attractor point.

All such observations clearly point to a residual
anisotropic stable attractor point
post anisotropic inflationary era. Thus. cosmic no hair theorem is clearly
violated
for the case of
Thurston spacetimes
for both power law and exponential potentials.

Conclusion

The paper focusses around the inflationary epoch of the universe through modeling and analyzing its underlying geometry defined under the framework of Thurston spacetimes. After developing inflationary models from the considered set of Thurston geometries, we formulated them as dynamical systems to be analyzed in the phase space. We employed the linear stability approach to show the existence of a stable anisotropic fixed point unique to all the considered set of Thurston spacetimes given by the point A.
The phase space trajectories for each of the considered Thurston spacetimes with arbitrary initial conditions converge towards the common fixed point A, describing that the universe will get close to this attractor after a few e-folds of inflation. Therefore, the stable and anisotropically inflationary fixed point imbibe a major fraction of e-folds of the inflationary epoch of the universe.

Our analysis has become more specific and general through understanding the existence of
two
slow roll phases, corresponding to
isotropic
which was then followed by
anisotropic
epochs. The evolution of this anisotropic phase have been traced throughout sufficient number of e-foldings of inflation. Hence, we address the existence of anisotropic inflation in the universe and present a
counter example
to the theoretical cosmic no-hair theorem.

Coupling function & anisotropic condition

From
[
26
]
, the condition for the occurrence of anisotropic inflation reads

M^{2}_{\text{(}Pl)}\frac{f^{\prime}(\phi)}{f(\phi)}\frac{V^{\prime}(\phi)}{V(\phi)}>2

(127)

Then considering one Thurston geometry at a time, assuming slow-roll and ignoring the backreaction of the field in the field equations, we have from the reduced Einstein & scalar field equations,

3\dot{\alpha}^{2}\simeq\frac{V(\phi)}{M^{2}_{\text{Pl}}}

(128)

and

3\dot{\alpha\phi}\simeq-V^{\prime}(\phi)

(129)

so that, upon dividing we have

\frac{d\alpha}{d\phi}=-\frac{1}{M^{2}_{\text{Pl}}}\frac{V(\phi)}{V^{\prime}(\phi)}

(130)

Integrating, we get

\boxed{\alpha(\phi)=-\frac{1}{M^{2}_{\text{(}Pl)}}\int\frac{V(\phi)}{V^{\prime}(\phi)}d\phi+C_{0}}

(131)

where
C_{0}
is an arbitrary constant of integration. For various geometries we then have

\begin{split}f(\phi)&=e^{-n\alpha}\\
&=C_{1}e^{\frac{n}{M^{2}_{\text{Pl}}}\int\frac{V(\phi)}{V^{\prime}(\phi)}d\phi}\end{split}

(132)

where
C_{1}=e^{C_{0}}
.

Considering chaotic inflation with the potential

V(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\text{m}^{2}\phi^{2}

(133)

where m (
\approx 10^{-5}
) denotes the mass of inflation field, we solve for
f(\phi
) in equation
132
to obtain

\begin{split}f(\phi)&=C_{1}e^{\frac{n}{M^{2}_{(Pl)}}\int\frac{\phi}{2}d\phi}\\
&=C_{1}e^{\frac{n}{M^{2}_{(Pl)}}\frac{\phi^{2}}{4}}\\
&=\boxed{C_{1}e^{n\phi^{2}/4M^{2}_{(Pl)}}}\end{split}

(134)

Thus substituting values of V(
\phi
) and f(
\phi
) from eqs.
133
and eq.
134
respectively into eq.
127
, we obtain for our various anisotropic geometries,

\boxed{n>2}
