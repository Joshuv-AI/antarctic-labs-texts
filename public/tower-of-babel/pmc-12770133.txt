Molecular signatures under hexavalent chromium, particulate matter 2.5, and cigarette smoking exposures
Authors: Yun-Xia Xie, Guang-Qi Li, Xiao-Nan Wang, Wen-Wen Ran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to air environment pollutants is one of the leading causes of lung cancer. To date, no relevant studies have compared the molecular signatures under exposure to lung cancer-related public air pollution, occupational exposure and personal pollutants, including hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)], particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) and cigarette smoking. In this study, we conducted the Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify the key modules under pollutant exposure, and used Mfuzz analysis to discover dose- or time-dependent gene sets. Pathway enrichment analysis and differentially expressed gene analysis were utilized to identify pollutant-induced signature genes. The results showed that the PI3K–AKT pathway is the most significantly upregulated pathway in Cr(VI) exposure, with significant upregulation observed in the genes CCND1, GDNF, IL7R, ITGA4, KIT, and PRKAA2. We found that PM2.5 activated the growth factor receptor binding pathway, significantly upregulating the expression of the proteins IL1A, TGFA and PDGFA on this pathway with increasing treatment time and dosage.Furthermore, cigarette smoking exposure activated MAPK cascade pathway and growth factor activity and the key genes ACTA2, FGF2, ROR2, FGF21, INHBE and MDK levels were elevated with increasing exposure duration. Our study delineates the molecular landscapes under three environmental pollutants and reveals the molecular mechanisms underlying the induction of malignant cell transformation by different environmental pollutants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04207-2.Keywords: Hexavalent chromium, Particulate matter 2.5, Cigarette smoking, Molecular characteristics

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cancer-related cause of mortality worldwide in man and women, among which environmental factors are well-documented the major risk factors, such as tobacco consumption, air pollutants, harmful metal exposure in industries [17, 24, 33]. According to previous studies, we classified the risk factors of environment exposure into three categories: occupational exposure, public air pollution and personal lifestyle habits. As we all know, lung cancer caused by occupational exposure is a category that should not be underestimated. A report estimating the number of cancers attributable to occupational exposure in France showed that lung cancer accounted for 75% of deaths due to occupational exposure [4]. Another study from the UK also pointed out that the proportion of lung cancer cases caused by occupational factors was 14.5% [30]. Hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] was identified as one of the occupational carcinogens [1, 26]. Due to its irreplaceable commercial value, Cr(VI) has been widely used in industries, such as leather manufacturing, stainless steel production, fuel production, welding and so on [32]. Current Cr(VI) exposure limits under EU Directive 2004/37/EC are 10 µg/m3 as a general limit and 25 µg/m3 for the welding industry [8]. The exposure limit of Cr(VI) recommended by ACGIH (American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists, 2018) is 0.002 mg/m3. A meta-analysis from 14 articles evidenced that pooled concentration of Cr(VI) was estimated 0.037 ± 0.002 mg/m3 which was higher than the recommended exposure limit [25]. More seriously, Cr(VI) dust can be emitted into the air, posing an enormous hidden threat to public health in human beings. To data, though the changes of epigenetic regulation and inflammatory responses caused by Cr(VI) exposure were considered as one of the reasons to trigger lung cancer, there are no molecule markers applied in clinical practice [5, 7, 27]. And, the systematic and in-depth analysis of molecular mechanism has rarely been conducted in the past.

Air pollution has always been a threat to public health. All over the world, 80% of the population lives in areas with poor air quality, and millions of residents have endured health problems due to widespread exposure to particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) [28]. It has been reported that exposure to environments with a diameter of 2.5 μm or less can cause up to 7 million deaths a year [13]. Previous studies evidenced that the molecule mechanism of PM2.5 exposure-inducing lung diseases associated with inflammatory response [39], but deeper regulatory pathways still need to be explored. In the lifestyle habits, cigarette smoking is an explicit trigger of lung cancer. Approximately one third of adults are known to cigarette smoking, and the number of female smokers is also increasing every year [16]. About 85% of lung cancers were caused by smoking, with an additional proportion caused by passive smoking in non-smokers [38]. Several studies found the causes of lung cancer induced by smoking, including NF-κB pathway activation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, oxidative stress and so on [18, 34]. In recent years, the rapid development of omics technology has provided new strategies for revealing the patterns of disease occurrence and development. Therefore, the integration and analysis of omics data will help elucidate the comprehensive molecular characteristics of cigarette smoking exposure.

In this study, we systematically analyzed gene expression profiles under three types of environmental pollutants to delineate their molecular landscapes and further compared the characteristic molecular signatures induced by pollutants. This work is helpful to optimize precise treatment for lung cancer patients exposed to air pollutants.

Materials and methods

Publicly available gene expression profiles

We selected BEAS-2B cell line (an immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line) as research model, and searched datasets using the keywords ‘chromium’, ‘particulate matter 2.5’, ‘cigarette smoking’, ‘gene expression profile’, ‘BEAS-2B cell line’ in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/) and ultimately downloaded the gene expression profiles of BEAS-2B cells with Cr(VI) (GSE24025), PM2.5 (GSE155616, GSE158954) and cigarette smoking (GSE14383, GSE14385) treatment. In GSE24025 dataset, BEAS-2B cells were exposed to 0.5 µM potassium chromate (K2CrO4) for 1 month and then Cr(VI)-treated cells were assessed for anchorage-independent growth and tumorigenesis in vivo. In GSE155616, BEAS-2B cells were treated with a single exposure of high (30 µg/cm2) or low (1 µg/cm2) dose of PM2.5 for 24 hours, or repeated exposed to low dose (1 µg/cm2) PM2.5 every day for 7 days. In GSE158954, BEAS-2B cells were exposed to PM2.5 from biomass combustion for 5 weeks, and the viability of cells were evaluated by MTT assay. In GSE14383, BEAS-2B cells were treated with low doses (20 µg/mL) cigarette smoking condensate or DMSO (negative control) for 4 months, and assessed for the ability of cell anchorage-independent growth. In GSE14385, BEAS-2B cells were treated twice weekly with 20 µg/mL CSC for 1 month, equivalent to 0.002 cigarettes/mL, and cells treated with DMSO served as control.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) is an effective method for screening key genes related to phenotype. To find key modules significantly correlated with Cr(VI) exposure, WGCNA was performed in R (version 4.3.1, WGCNA package) [20]. Through calculating the standard deviation of each gene, the top 25% of genes were selected for further analyzing. The optimal β was selected to satisfy the scale-free distribution by the “pickSoftThreshold” function in the WGCNA package (soft threshold β = 9). The correlation between different modules with the phenotypes was calculated and the most significant modules were selected to obtain key genes. P-value < 0.05 and the absolute value of the correlation coefficient (r) greater than 0.5 were considered statistically significant.

Identification of genes with similar time or dose expression patterns

GSE155616 dataset includes different dose treatment groups (0, 1, 30 µg/cm2) and different time treatment groups (control, 24 hours, 7 days). GSE14383 dataset includes different time treatment groups (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 months). The discrete clusters of features with similar expression changes for gene expression profiling data were identified on fuzzy c-means algorithm performed in R (Version 4.3.1, Mfuzz package, http://mfuzz.sysbiolab.eu) [19]. Vegan package was used to identify optimal number of clusters.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

The gene expression profiles from array (GSE24025, GSE158954 and GSE14385) were analyzed by using limma package in R (version 4.3.1) [29]. Limma provides linear models and empirical Bayesian methods for analyzing differentially expressed genes. The threshold was adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| >[mean(|log2FC|) + 2sd(|log2FC|)]. The differential expressed genes were shown by volcano plot (ggplot2 R package) and heatmap (pheatmap R package).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, Gene ontology (GO) analysis and REACTOME pathway enrichment were performed to determine the biological function of differentially expressed genes by Cluster Profiler package (R version 4.3.1). P-value was adjusted by Benjamin Hochberg method and the threshold was adjusted P-value < 0.05. Other parameters were default.

The single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)

The gene expression profile from GSE24025 was performed ssGSEA to analyze the enrichment of PI3K–AKT–MTOR signaling pathway from Molecular Signature Databse (MSigDB) in each sample by using GSVA R package (v1.34.0) [14]. The threshold was P-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. Other parameters were default.

Statistical analysis

All data processing, analyzing and plotting were conducted in R studio software (R version 4.3.1). The analysis of Protein-protein interaction network (PPI) was completed in Cytoscape software (version 3.7.1, installed module: ClueGo version 2.5.9 + Cluepedia version 1.5.9) and the parameters were all set using default settings. Continuous variables were compared between two groups by the Student’s t-test. Log rank test was used for overall survival and disease-free survival. The overlap between the different datasets was done by using VennDiagram R package. The Sankey diagram was drawn by ggsankey R package. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The construction of gene co-expression network based on gene expression profile under Cr(VI) exposure

To explore the molecular landscapes under Cr(VI) exposure and identify gene modules related to Cr (VI), the gene expression profile of Cr(VI)-transformed BEAS-2B cells from GSE24025 was analyzed using WGCNA. First, we calculated the gene correlation matrix. According to the scale independence (Fig. 1A) and the mean connectivity (Fig. 1B), 9 was identified as the soft threshold β (R2 = 0.9) for constructing the scale-free network. Subsequently, based on the similarity of gene expression, all genes were divided into 27 modules by the hierarchical clustering method (Fig. 1C). Ultimately, the topological overlap matrix (TOM) transformed from the adjacency matrix displayed the correlation between genes, in which the correlation between genes within the module was significantly higher, especially in the blue module (Fig. 1D). Besides, the correlations between modules were analyzed and depicted by eigengene adjacency heatmap (Fig. 1E).

B The mean connectivity of the network for different soft threshold powers. C The gene hierarchical clustering tree base on the constructed scale-free network. D The heatmap of the topological overlap matrix of all genes. E The eigengene adjacency heatmap displayed the correlation between modules

Functional enrichment analysis of critical modules related to Cr(VI) exposure

The correlation analysis between 27 modules and phenotypes (control and Cr-T groups) revealed that MEblue (1531 genes) and MEyellow (221 genes) modules had a significantly negative correlation with Cr(VI) treatment, whereas MEturquoise (1616 genes) and MEpink (169 genes) modules were positively correlated with Cr(VI) treatment (Fig. 2A). The associations between module memberships in MEblue (Fig. 2B), MEyellow (Fig. 2D), MEturquoise (Fig. 2F) and MEpink (Fig. 2H) with the gene significance for Cr(VI) treatment were depicted. Moreover, we found that the genes in MEblue module were associated with pathways such as the regulation of TP53 activity, metabolism of RNA, cellular responses to stress and so on (Fig. 2C). The genes in MEyellow module were involved in pathways like p75 NTR receptor-mediated signalling, post-translational protein phosphorylation and pexophagy (Fig. 2E). The genes in MEturquoise module were significantly enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle, metabolism of RNA and proteins, infectious diseases, and more (Fig. 2G). In MEpink module, genes were related to cell cycle (mitotic), activation of gene expression by SREBF and DNA damage bypass (Fig. 2I). Overall, these pathways enriched from critical modules are related to the ability of cell proliferation.

The molecular characteristics under Cr(VI) exposure

Based on the gene expression profile from GSE24025, we performed the differentially expressed gene analysis and identified 270 genes that were significantly differentially expressed in both Cr-T small and large colonies (Fig. 3A, Supplement 1), including 88 up-regulated genes and 182 down-regulated genes (Fig. 3B). Further overlapped differentially expressed genes and module genes with statistical significance from WGCNA, we obtained 241 key genes (Fig. 3C). KEGG enrichment pathway analysis revealed that these genes were enriched in crucial pathways such as the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules, tight junction, and axon guidance (Fig. 3D). Notably, some up-regulated genes were associated with the non-small cell lung cancer, indicating their potential role as triggering factors for lung cancer. Importantly, the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway, a tumor-related signaling pathway, was most dramatically activated, involving the upregulated genes such as CCND1, GDNF, IL7R, ITGA4, KIT and PRKAA2 (Fig. 3E). Moreover, single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) also demonstrated that Cr(VI) exposure triggered PI3K–AKT–MTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 3E).

Identification of genes with similar time and dose expression patterns under PM2.5 exposure

We conducted Mfuzz analysis using the GSE155616 dataset, which comprises gene expression profiles of BEAS-2B cells treated with PM2.5 at different doses and time points, to identify the genes with similar dose expression patterns. According to the K-means clustering algorithm, 8 was considered as the optimal number of subgroups (Fig. 4A). Various dynamic patterns were observed at different doses as shown in Fig. 4B, indicating that PM2.5 caused complex molecular changes. Subsequently, genes from different time treatment groups were classified into 7 dynamic patterns (Fig. 4C, D). With increasing treatment dose, the expression levels of genes in Cluster 1 and Cluster 6 showed a whole downtrend. As the PM2.5 treatment time increased, the expression levels of genes in Cluster 1 and Cluster 7 decreased. By further overlapping these 4 clusters, we identified 1739 downregulated genes affected by both treatment dose and time (Fig. 4E). To investigate the biological function of these overlapped genes, we performed KEGG enrichment pathway analysis and found that these genes were significantly related to the cell cycle, metabolism process, Rap1 signaling pathway and so on (Fig. 4F). Similarly, as the PM2.5 treatment dose and time increased, 3456 genes with an up-regulated expression trend were found (Fig. 4G, dose: Cluster 2 and Cluster 3, time: Cluster 2 and Cluster 4). The results of KEGG enrichment pathway analysis showed that tumor-related pathways were activated, including NF-kappa B signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Notch signaling pathway and p53 signaling pathway (Fig. 4H).

The molecular characteristics under PM2.5 exposure

To identify the key genes induced by PM2.5 exposure, we further performed the analysis of differentially expressed gene on gene expression profiles from GSE158954 (PM2.5 treatment for 5 weeks), and found 462 up-regulated genes and 396 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A, B, Supplement 1). Through overlapping the down-regulated genes from GSE158954, genes in Cluster 1/6 from the dose group and genes in Cluster 1/7 from the time group, a total of 57 down-regulated genes were found (Fig. 5C, Supplement 1). These genes were related to extracellular matrix, defense response to substance, enzyme activity and so on (Fig. 5D). Likewise, 77 highly expressed genes were identified according to up-regulated genes from GSE158954 and genes with time- and dose-dependent up-regulation (Fig. 5E). These genes are involved in processes such as the cellular response to environmental stimuli, the hypoxia response, and kinase activity, and are particularly implicated in cancer-related pathways—specifically, growth factor receptor binding. This includes the upregulated genes IL1A, TGFA, and PDGFA (Fig. 5F).

Identification of genes with similar time expression patterns under cigarette smoking exposure

Based on the gene expression profiles of cigarette smoking exposure samples with different treatment time (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 months) from GSE14383, we conducted Mfuzz analysis to identify genes with similar time expression patterns. According to the K-means clustering algorithm, 10 was identified as the optimal number of clusters (Fig. 6A). Multiple dynamic patterns were observed as shown in Fig. 6B, among which Cluster 3 showed significant downtrend with treatment time, whereas the gene expression in Cluster 7 was up-regulated in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6B, Supplement 1). The results of GO (BP, CC, MF) enrichment pathway analysis revealed that the genes in Cluster 3 were involved in the response to oxidative stress, positive regulation of cell adhesion, protein binding and so on (Fig. 6C). The upregulated genes in Cluster 7 were enriched in the Wnt signaling pathway (tumor-related pathway), the regulation of cell cycle phase transition, ubiquitin-protein transferase activity and so on (Fig. 6D), which these pathways have been identified to be strongly associated with the development of lung cancer.

The molecular characteristics under cigarette smoking exposure

To identified the key genes under cigarette smoking exposure, we further performed differentially expressed gene analysis on gene expression profile from GSE14385 (treated with 5 µg/ml of cigarette smoking for 1 month). 455 down-regulated genes and 234 up-regulated genes in cigarette smoking group were found (Fig. 7A, B). Integrated results from different analytical methods revealed that 73 downregulated genes were enriched (Fig. 7C). These genes are involved in multiple signaling pathways, such as the Wnt signaling pathway, regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, and cell-cell adhesion (Fig. 7D). 36 genes were overlapped based on genes in Cluster 7 and up-regulated genes from GSE14385 (Fig. 7E). These genes were related to the pathway of positive regulation of MAPK cascade and growth factor activity, including upregulated genes ACTA2, FGF2, ROR2, FGF21, INHBE and MDK (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate the gene expression data of Cr(VI), PM2.5 and cigarette smoking exposure from three different perspectives: occupational exposure, public environment, and personal lifestyle habits. Our study is based on gene expression profiles to systematically analyze the gene expression changes exposed to environmental pollutants, attempting to reveal and compare the molecular characteristics, and further underlying key genes related to lung cancer under different exposure conditions. Accumulating epidemiological evidence supports the link between Cr(VI) exposure and the occurrence of lung cancer. It has reported that lung cancer risk was significantly elevated among workers in the chromate-producing industry in the United States [10]. The malignant transformation of cells induced by Cr(VI) was regarded as the beginning of tumor occurrence or precancerous stage, with cells acquiring the ability to proliferate rapidly, grow independently of an anchorage and induce subcutaneous tumors in nude mice. Recent studies had focused on the molecular mechanism of Cr(VI) inducing malignant transformation of bronchial epithelial cells, such as Max Costa et al. found that Cr(VI) may target histone methyltransferases and demethylases, which caused the silencing of tumor suppressor gene MLH1 [35]. It has reported that the expression of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) was induced in a Cr(VI) dose-dependent manner [11], and CDK14, POLA1, lnc-NCS1-2:1 and lnc-FOXK1-4:1 were key drivers for chromium-induced cytotoxicity [40]. Consistently, our results also found that NOX4 and CDK14 were significantly high expression, and MLH1 expression was down-regulated (Supplement 1). Recently, Changmao Long et al. established a whole-body dynamic exposure mouse model for hexavalent chromium aerosol and then performed multi-omics analysis on mice lung tissues, including miRNA sequencing, mRNA sequencing, and metabolomic, finally identifying that differentially expressed genes were involved in oxidative phosphorylation, glycophospholipid metabolism, and inflammatory signaling pathways [22]. Our comprehensive analysis of gene expression and cellular signaling pathways revealed activation of the PI3K–AKT pathway, which is associated with the recruitment of inflammatory factors and tumor proliferation, suggesting its role in Cr(VI)-induced malignant transformation. Additionally, FoxO signaling pathway related to antioxidation and stress response was upregulated. In our study, we found that CCND1, GDNF, IL7R, ITGA4, KIT and PRKAA2 genes in PI3K–AKT pathway were significantly induced by Cr(VI). This aligns with previous studies reporting abnormal CCND1 expression in lung carcinogenesis [2, 3].

In PM2.5 exposure analysis, we used the Mfuzz method to screen for key genes affected by treatment dose and time, further narrowing candidates using differential gene expression analysis. With prolonged PM2.5 exposure, we found that the molecular function of growth factor receptor binding was activated and the expression level of TGFA was significantly increased. It has reported that PM2.5 exposure induced lung injury and fibrosis by TGF-β signaling [12]. However, the study of the association between TGFA and PM2.5 has not been found so far. Previous study has identified that TGFA promoted primary and acquired resistance to ALK/c-Met kinase inhibitors in non-small-cell lung cancer [37]. Additionally, PM2.5 exposure enhanced the response to hypoxia, consistent with the prior finding that it induces hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) expression [31]. Recently, Chen et al. reported that PM2.5 increased glycolysis metabolism by paralleled ribosome sequencing and RNA sequencing [6]. Although it is known that HIF-1α induces glycolysis reprogramming, pathway analysis in our study did not reveal any abnormalities in the glucose metabolism pathway, suggesting that changes in glycolysis may not be the main feature in the initial stage of PM2.5 exposure. Under cigarette smoking exposure, we observed activation of the MAPK cascade, a well-studied mechanism in lung cancer associated with angiogenesis, progression, and gefitinib resistance [9, 21, 41]. In the study of blood proteomics assisted early diagnosis of lung cancer in smokers, researchers found that a total of 36 growth factors, tumor necrosis factor receptors, chemokines, and cytokines could predict the occurrence of lung cancer [23], among which growth differentiation factor GDF-15 upregulated time-dependently in our study (Supplement 1).

Comparing key genes from three pollutants, we found that the most significant change in Cr(VI) malignantly transformed cells was the PI3K–AKT pathway, suggesting that tumor-promoting signal was activated in the cells, possibly leading to tumorigenesis. The differential genes under PM2.5 exposure show that the cumulative PM2.5 caused DNA damage, impaired cellular enzyme activity, and activated transforming growth factor alpha, which related to cell carcinogenesis. The most significant pathway caused by cigarette smoking exposure was MAPK cascade pathway and we also found that growth factor activity is activated. These analysis results suggest that there are differences in the dysregulated molecular regulatory networks caused by different pollutants, and that a single therapeutic target may not be suitable for lung diseases caused by environmental pollutants. In clinical practice, exposure history assessment serves as one of the bases for selecting treatment modalities. For patients with exposure to chromium, PM2.5, or tobacco, priority should be given to testing for their specific molecular targets to guide clinical therapy.

In addition, we found that the same molecular signatures in cells treated with three pollutants for 1 month, and a total of 8 genes were overlapped, among which the expressions of NPR3, IGFBP7 and ID1 all were down-regulated under three pollutants exposure (Supplement 2). NPR3 was related to oxidative stress response and it has been reported that NPR3 genes decreased cell sensitivity to H2O2 stress [36]. Therefore, exposure to three environmental pollutants leads to an increase in the sensitivity of cells to oxidative stress. ID1 as an inhibitor of DNA binding is involved in anti-inflammatory response. Recent studies have shown that phosgene can induce acute lung injury by depressing the ID1-dependent anti-inflammatory response [15], which suggested that ID1 is a common effector of exposure to multiple environmental carcinogens. Besides, IGFBP7 can stimulate prostacyclin production and cell adhesion. Interestingly, we found that JAG1, an activator of the NOTCH pathway, is involved in tissue fibrosis and is significantly overexpressed under PM2.5 exposure (Supplement 2). In our study, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the data solely originate from the sequencing of cells from small sample sizes, and the analysis results lack experimental validation and validation from patient samples. Based on multi-center data, the analysis results contain inevitable batch bias. Secondly, we have not explored whether cross-talk between the microenvironment and cells is a driving factor in the malignant transformation of cells in this study. However, it provides new directions for future research. As more single-cell sequencing data is generated, the relationship between the malignant transformation of cells and the microenvironment or immune cells will be revealed. In addition, the molecular changes induced by the cumulative effects of three environmental pollutants have not been fully explored. Therefore, we are currently attempting to construct a mouse model of spontaneous tumor formation induced by environmental exposure. Our goal is to use this model to determine the key links between environmental exposure and lung cancer occurrence, by depicting the pathology of lung tissue at different exposure time points.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study characterizes the molecular landscapes induced by three environmental pollutants and uncovers the mechanisms underlying pollutant-induced malignant cell transformation.
