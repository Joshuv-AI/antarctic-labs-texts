Impact of postoperative hyperamylasemia and post-pancreatectomy acute pancreatitis following pancreaticoduodenectomy in a single-center cohort
Authors: Ativitch Asavachaisuvikom, Pongsatorn Tangtawee, Narongsak Rungsakulkij, Wikran Suragul, Somkit Mingphruedhi, Paramin Muangkaew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical significance of postoperative hyperamylasemia (POH) and post-pancreatectomy acute pancreatitis (PPAP) following pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) is understudied. Hence, we aimed to determine the incidence, risk factors, and short-term clinical impact of post-PD POH and PPAP.MethodsA retrospective, single-center, observational study was conducted with consecutive adult patients who underwent surgery between January 2012 and September 2025. POH and PPAP were defined and graded using the 2022 International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) consensus criteria.ResultsAmong the 244 participants, 25.4% developed POH and 31.1% developed PPAP. Those with PPAP demonstrated significantly higher rates of clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula (CR-POPF), postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo classification ≥3), and longer hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stays compared to those without PPAP. There was no difference in the 90-day mortality rate. Perioperative factors were significantly associated with PPAP in the univariate analysis, including male sex [odds ratio (OR) =1.83; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.05–3.17; P=0.03] and the presence of preoperative biliary drainage (OR =1.84; 95% CI: 1.01–3.35; P=0.047). Two intraoperative characteristics were identified as significant risk factors for PPAP: soft pancreatic texture (OR =2.58; 95% CI: 1.39–4.81; P=0.003) and a narrower pancreatic duct (OR =0.77; 95% CI: 0.66–0.99; P=0.002). CR-POPF was associated with an increased incidence of PPAP (OR =11.13; 95% CI: 5.61–22.07; P=0.001). In the multivariate analysis, male sex (OR =2.49; 95% CI: 1.13–5.51; P=0.02) and CR-POPF (OR =17.55; 95% CI: 7.13–43.19; P<0.001) were identified as risk factors for PPAP.ConclusionsPPAP is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. It has potential as an indicator of surgical morbidity and of prolonged hospital and ICU stays.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Post-pancreatectomy acute pancreatitis (PPAP) is a significant and serious clinical condition that is associated with an increased risk of adverse pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) outcomes, including significantly higher rates of clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula, postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo classification ≥3), and longer hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stays.

What is known and what is new?

• Following the year 2022, the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery established formal definitions for postoperative hyperamylasemia (POH) and PPAP. Nevertheless, the available data remain limited, and this condition continues to be underrecognized.

• This study aims to demonstrate the impact of POH and PPAP in the context of PD.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• High clinical burden: PPAP is a primary driver of major postoperative morbidity, re-interventions, and extended hospital/ICU stays, whereas POH remains clinically non-significant.

• Routine amylase surveillance: serum amylase monitoring may serve as a supportive tool for evaluating disease progression over time; however, definitive diagnosis ultimately relies on a comprehensive assessment incorporating patient symptoms and radiographic evaluation.

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) is the standard surgical treatment for various malignant and benign conditions that occur in the periampullary region and pancreatic head. While advancements in surgical techniques have reduced mortality rates to below 5% in high-volume centers, postoperative morbidity rates remain high, ranging from 30% to 50% (1-4). A significant concern is the development of a postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) (5), which can lead to serious complications, such as intra-abdominal abscesses and multi-organ failure (6,7).

Early postoperative inflammation of the pancreatic remnant is another topic of interest, particularly its role in the development of serious complications. However, limited studies have been conducted in this area, and a standardized definition for such inflammation was lacking until recently. In addition, the serum amylase concentration is often transiently elevated after PD, a condition termed postoperative hyperamylasemia (POH), although its progression to a clinically significant inflammatory state has been inconsistently reported (8,9). To address these issues, in 2022, the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) introduced a consensus definition for post-pancreatectomy acute pancreatitis (PPAP), distinguishing it from POH (10,11).

Current clinical evidence suggests that patients who present with a pancreas that is soft in texture and a small main pancreatic duct (diameter ≤3 mm) have a substantially higher risk of developing POH and PPAP (4). Despite the identification of these anatomical risk factors, early clinical prediction of POH and PPAP is challenging. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to determine the incidence, risk factors, and clinical impact of POH and PPAP within a cohort of patients who underwent PD. Our findings on the correlations between early biochemical markers and significant morbidities can be used to enhance risk stratification and optimize postoperative management protocols to reduce the severity of post-PD complications. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-0374/rc) (12).

Methods

Patient population

The data analyzed in this study were collected from consecutive patients who underwent PD at Ramathibodi Hospital between January 2012 and September 2025.

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study. The primary outcomes were the incidences of POH and PPAP. The secondary outcomes were the surgical outcomes of POH and PPAP as well as the risk factors for PPAP.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (No. MURA2025/872). Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study.

Definitions of clinical parameters

The ISGPS specifies that three criteria must be met for a diagnosis of PPAP: (I) a sustained serum amylase greater than the institutional upper limit of normal that lasts for >48 h after surgery; (II) the presence of clinically relevant features; and (III) radiological findings consistent with PPAP. Serum amylase was measured routinely on postoperative days 0, 1, and 3 in all patients (normal level was 30–110 U/L). In this study, we classified the patients into the following three groups: the none group (normal serum amylase level during the first 48 h postoperatively), the POH group (an elevated serum amylase level persisting for over 48 h following surgery, accompanied by the absence of pancreatitis) and the PPAP group (elevated serum amylase level that lasted >48 h postoperatively, plus clinically relevant features and radiological evidence of PPAP) (10,13). For PPAP grading, grade B is defined as a mild or moderate complication that requires acute medical treatment. This includes the need for antibiotics, steroids, nutritional supplement support, or procedures such as interventional radiology, endoscopic-guided drainage, or angiography. Grade C is defined as a severe, life-threatening complication that results in persistent organ failure lasting at least 48 h, which may lead to admission to intensive care, surgical intervention, or death (10).

Preoperative data were collected on the following clinical characteristics: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification, preoperative biliary drainage, serum albumin, and serum amylase. Intraoperative data were collected on the following parameters: main pancreatic duct size, pancreatic gland texture, estimated blood loss, blood transfusion, surgical procedure performed, simultaneous vascular resection, and operative time. The occurrence of postoperative complications was recorded, including the development of a POPF, postpancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH), and delayed gastric emptying (DGE), according to ISGPS criteria (5,13,14). Other postoperative complications were noted using the Clavien-Dindo classification (15).

Surgical procedures and postoperative management

Both pylorus-preserving PD (PPPD) and the classic Whipple procedure were performed, followed by reconstruction. The modified Blumgart technique was used primarily to perform the pancreatojejunostomy (16). The decision to use a pancreatic duct stent was made based on the surgeon’s preference. Drainage tubes were placed in all cases. Postoperative abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) imaging was performed when there were concerns about abdominal complications due to the patient’s clinical deterioration or to assess the patency of the portal vein when portal vein reconstruction had been performed. All operation in this study was open surgery.

Statistical analysis

All statistical data were analyzed using STATA V.18 for Windows software. Continuous variables were compared between two groups using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For categorical variables, the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using a binary logistic regression model. All clinically relevant variables were included in the multivariate analysis. When P<0.05, the result was considered statistically significant. Missing data were not imputed. Participants with missing values in any of the evaluated variables were excluded from both univariate and multivariate analyses.

Results

The type of operation (Whipple procedure vs. PPPD) and operative time did not differ significantly across the groups. However, the PPAP group exhibited substantially more challenging surgical anatomy; specifically, 70.8% of patients in the PPAP group had a soft pancreas, compared with 40.9% in the none group (P=0.005). Additionally, the pancreatic duct diameter was significantly smaller in the PPAP group (3.4±1.8 mm) compared to the none (4.8±2.7 mm) and POH (4.2±2.3 mm) groups (P=0.001). Vascular resection was performed less frequently in the POH group (6.5%) than in the other groups (P=0.048).

Surgical outcomes and complications

The incidence of POH was 25.4% (62/244), while the incidence of PPAP was 31.1% (76/244). In a cohort of patients diagnosed with PPAP, 65 individuals were classified as grade B, while 11 were classified as grade C. Among the grade C cohort, one patient succumbed to severe intra-abdominal hemorrhage within 90 days following the diagnosis of PPAP and POPF. Moreover, nine patients necessitated surgical intervention, and one patient developed organ failure subsequent to the onset of PPAP. A significant difference was observed in the median amylase levels during the first 48 h between those with PPAP (335 U/L) and those without PPAP (123 U/L, P<0.001). However, the median amylase levels recorded during the first 48 h did not differ between the patients with POH and those with PPAP, as illustrated in Figure 2.

The incidence of clinically relevant POPF (CR-POPF) was significantly higher in the PPAP group (53.9%) compared to the none (8.5%) and POH (11.3%) groups (P<0.001). Of the 41 patients with PPAP, 31 have POPF grade B and 10 have POPF grade C. Among the patients with POPF grade B, 16 patients received intervention management, while 15 patients were managed with prolonged drainage alone. Additionally, a higher rate of postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ 3a) was observed in the PPAP group compared to the none and POH groups. As a result, the median length of stay (LOS) reported for the patients in the PPAP group was significantly longer than that of the patients in the other groups (33 vs. 15 days, P<0.001). The patients in the PPAP group also experienced longer stays in the intensive care unit (ICU; median: 5 days, P<0.001). Three patients in the PPAP grade B group had prolonged stays in the ICU for reasons unrelated to PPAP. Among these, one patient had a history of ventricular tachycardia (VT) and required electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring, while the other two developed minor pulmonary embolisms after surgery and needed oxygen monitoring. However, there were no significant differences among the three groups in terms of the 90-day mortality rate or the incidence of PPH or chyle leakage. The postoperative data are presented in Table 2.

Identification of risk factors for PPAP

As shown in Table 3, several perioperative factors were significantly associated with PPAP in the univariate analysis. Notably, two preoperative characteristics were identified as significant risk factors for PPAP: male sex [odds ratio (OR) =1.83; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.05–3.17; P=0.03] and the presence of preoperative biliary drainage (OR =1.84; 95% CI: 1.01–3.35; P=0.047). Two intraoperative characteristics were identified as significant risk factors for PPAP: soft pancreatic texture (OR =2.58; 95% CI: 1.39–4.81; P=0.003) and A narrower pancreatic duct (OR =0.77; 95% CI: 0.66–0.99; P=0.002). CR-POPF was associated with an increased incidence of PPAP (OR =11.13; 95% CI: 5.61–22.07; P=0.001). Other baseline demographic characteristics, including age and BMI, did not correlate significantly with PPAP.

Discussion

Despite improvements in surgical techniques and perioperative care, postoperative complications remain a major determinant of the outcomes of PD (17). More specifically, POPF, DGE, and POH are clinically significant complications that substantially affect postoperative recovery (18-21). In addition, PPAP has recently been recognized as a distinct and clinically relevant post-PD condition, and it is increasingly being linked to adverse short-term outcomes (10). Previous studies have reported the incidences of POH and PPAP to be between 7.4% and 53% (8,22-24). In our study, the incidences of POH and PPAP were 25.4% and 31.1%, respectively. The broad range of reported incidences may have arisen from differences in surgical techniques, variations in patient populations, and inconsistencies in data-collection timing and in the methods used to measure biochemical markers. All these factors can influence the reported outcomes and their interpretation. Holmberg et al. (4) demonstrated the clinical impact of POH and PPAP. While POH was not found to be clinically significant, PPAP had a notable effect on postoperative complications, resulting in longer hospital and ICU stays. Our study significantly extends these observations by providing a more granular evaluation of PPAP-related clinical trajectories. We demonstrate that the elevated LOS and ICU utilization observed in these patients are fundamentally driven by an increased burden of complex postoperative morbidity and a heightened demand for invasive re-interventions, thereby establishing a clearer link between pancreatic inflammation and adverse surgical outcomes. These factors ultimately extend the recovery period and require intensive monitoring.

Several factors reportedly influence PPAP development, including a narrow main pancreatic duct (22,24). Consistent with other studies, we found that a narrow pancreatic duct was an independent risk factor for PPAP. The width of the pancreatic duct is thought to affect pancreatic drainage, and patients with a narrower duct may be more susceptible to pancreatic parenchymal injury from excessive manipulation and impaired drainage, leading to inflammation of the remnant pancreas. Moreover, previous studies have shown that a soft pancreas is a risk factor for PPAP (22,25). Laaninen et al. (26,27) demonstrated that in a soft pancreas, the cut surface had a high proportion of acinar cells (>40%), which is a risk factor for developing postoperative complications. In our study, the proportion of patients with a soft pancreas was highest in the PPAP group; however, it was not deemed an independent risk factor for PPAP in multivariate analysis, possibly due to the small sample size. In addition, previous studies have shown male sex was an independent risk factor for PPAP (25). The impact of male sex on PPAP remains unclear. However, anatomically, male patients exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of visceral adiposity and pancreatic steatosis (fatty pancreas) compared to females (28). The infiltration of adipocytes into the pancreatic stroma alters the tissue architecture, making the parenchyma soft and fragile. During procedures such as pancreatic transection and anastomotic reconstruction, this soft pancreatic tissue is particularly vulnerable to mechanical shear stress, ischemia, and parenchymal injury.

The relationship between PPAP and POPF has not been well understood. While numerous studies have established that there is a significant association between these two conditions, others have shown that they may also occur independently of one another (24,25,29). This correlation is likely indicative of multifactorial mechanisms in which the enzymes implicated in PPAP contribute to inflammation of the pancreatic remnant, potentially leading to pancreatitis. Conversely, PPAP may cause cellular damage to the pancreatic parenchyma, prolonging healing time of the pancreatic anastomosis and increasing the risk of POPF. In our study, the incidence of POPF was notably higher in the PPAP group compared to the non-PPAP group (53.9% vs. 9.5%). It is important to note that PPAP and POPF may share some risk factors.

This study has several limitations. This retrospective, single-center study evaluated patients who underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) between 2017 and 2024, with an average annual volume of 20 to 30 cases. It is important to note that some medical records prior to 2017 have been irretrievably lost due to the hospital’s record-keeping system. Second, Postoperative CT scans were performed based on consistent clinical indications—such as suspected intra-abdominal infection or hemorrhage—and patient preference, ensuring that diagnostic practices remained uniform throughout the study period, which may have led to an underestimation of certain complications. Third, intraoperative variables that could influence the development of PPAP and POPF—such as the degree of pancreatic manipulation, the microvascular perfusion of the pancreatic remnant, and the specific anastomotic techniques used—were not documented in detail. These factors may play a significant role in the underlying pathophysiology and cannot be comprehensively analyzed. Nevertheless, this study has several notable strengths. First, we thoroughly evaluated the incidence and clinical effects of POH and PPAP, as well as the risk factors for PPAP, in a well-defined cohort of patients who underwent PD. We also used the ISGPS’s internationally accepted definitions. This standardized approach enhances the reliability and comparability of our findings with those reported in the literature.

Conclusions

PPAP is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. It has potential as an indicator of surgical morbidity and of prolonged hospital and ICU stays.
