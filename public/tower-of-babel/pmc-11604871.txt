Equity and Outcome Events in Hidradenitis Suppurativa: Exploring Effect Modifiers Associated with Diagnostic Delay in the Real World
Authors: Nicole Murray, Isabel Truman, Gary Milligan, Himanshu Modi, Nicholas Adlard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) experience significantly delayed diagnoses of 7–10 years from symptom onset on average, but the reasons for this remain largely unknown. This study investigated drivers of diagnostic delay from the perspective of healthcare system equity.MethodsA literature review was performed to identify published factors associated with delayed HS diagnosis to inform data analysis. Clinical and demographic data from the Adelphi HS Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a real-world cross-sectional survey of dermatologists and their consulting patients in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the UK and the USA in 2020/2021, were used to model factors influencing delay to diagnosis from onset of symptoms and first consultation.ResultsFactors influencing delay to HS diagnosis in the literature with the most available evidence were misdiagnosis, delay in specialist referral and patient embarrassment.Data analysis revealed that increasing age was associated with reduced diagnostic delay after symptom onset. Patients with HS who were White or in Germany were also more likely to receive a faster diagnosis. Smokers, patients with concomitant conditions, or a family history of HS were slower to be diagnosed.When time to diagnosis following first consultation was assessed, increasing age was associated with quicker diagnosis. Moreover, patients with a family history of HS were diagnosed quicker, whereas those with high body mass index, more concomitant conditions, in employment, managed by multiple physicians or European were more delayed.ConclusionOn the basis of a thorough analysis of real-world data, multiple factors that potentially influenced the timely diagnosis of HS have been identified. For the first time, this study quantifies the relative impact of these modifiers, providing valuable insights into areas that require attention for faster diagnoses and improved disease outcomes.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-024-01291-0.Keywords: Delayed diagnosis, Equity, Hidradenitis suppurativa, Real world

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Patients suffering from hidradenitis suppurativa frequently face years of delay prior to receiving a correct diagnosis, the reasons for which are poorly characterised.In this study, we used data from a large multinational survey of patients with hidradenitis suppurativa and their treating physicians to investigate factors affecting diagnostic delay.What was learned from the study?A wide range of factors significantly affected the time to diagnosis from both symptom onset and first consultation following symptom onset, including patient ethnicity, family history, smoking status, BMI, concomitant conditions, geographical location, and employment status.These identified factors contributing to diagnostic delay in hidradenitis suppurativa highlight target areas for physician and patient education to achieve faster diagnoses and better disease outcomes.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, inflammatory skin disease characterised by deep-seated lesions progressing to scarring and suppuration as a result of tunnelling [1]. HS is difficult to manage and is associated with disability and a high morbidity burden, as well as a significant unmet clinical and socioeconomic need [2–4], including a substantial psychological burden and increased rates of suicide [5].

Estimates of the prevalence of HS vary significantly [6], indicating possible issues in diagnosis. Recent meta-analyses have estimated overall prevalence to be 0.3–0.4% [6, 7], but estimates vary from as low as 0.03% in juvenile US populations to 6.4% of Black dermatology patients in the USA [6, 7]. Prevalence is higher in women [8] and Black populations [9]. Diagnosis of HS is based solely on clinical presentation and medical history, with no specific serological markers available, and correct diagnosis of HS is therefore reliant on the ability of the treating physician to accurately recognise symptomology [10, 11]. An added complication is the large degree of heterogeneity in the presentation of HS [12].

Evidence suggests that patients with HS often delay seeking care and have poor engagement with healthcare services [13, 14]. Physician attitude towards HS has been cited by patients as an important factor in engagement [15]. Significant delays between symptom onset and diagnosis are common; an average time of 7–10 years has been reported [16, 17], with some patients waiting in excess of 20 years for a diagnosis [18]. This compares to an average of 1.6 years for patients with psoriasis [16].

Delayed HS diagnosis contributes to worse outcomes, with longer delays associated with intensifying disease severity and disease progression, as well as a greater range of comorbidities, possibly due to delays in receiving anti-inflammatory treatment [19]. Patient-reported barriers of biologic use include fear of side effects, the high cost or lack of insurance coverage and frequency of administration [20]. Increased numbers of consultations and incorrect treatments due to delayed HS diagnoses also place a greater burden on healthcare systems.

Although the reasons for this diagnostic delay in HS are not completely understood, possible causes include lack of disease awareness in primary care physicians, misdiagnoses, and underreporting by patients due to embarrassment [16, 19, 21–23]. In a survey, 32% of French primary care physicians reported difficulty in HS diagnosis due to non-specific early signs [22]. Qualitative research in HS patient groups has identified similar themes, with lack of knowledge amongst primary care physicians and difficulty accessing specialist care being frequently cited by patients [13, 24]. These findings only represent some of the factors potentially influencing time to diagnosis.

To date, no comprehensive modelling has been conducted on the impact of addressing specific areas of healthcare inequality in the diagnosis and treatment of HS. Real-world data on health inequity in HS are required for accurate modelling and could provide important evidence on the wide-reaching implications of health inequity in this disease. However, these data are not currently available.

In this study, we combine a targeted literature review and analysis of real-world clinical data to investigate and quantify the contributions of different factors affecting diagnostic delay in HS from the perspective of improving healthcare system equity.

Methods

Targeted Literature Review

A targeted literature search was performed in Embase, MEDLINE, MEDLINE In-process and Cochrane to find publications discussing factors directly associated with delayed HS diagnosis. The targeted literature search strategy is presented in Table S1. The following exclusion criteria were used to eliminate studies from the targeted literature search: patient population not of interest, outcomes not of interest/not reported, study design not of interest (e.g. case report, case series, editorials, letters etc.), duplicate, and study topic not of interest. From the remaining records, disease-related, demographic, patient-related and health system-related factors were identified. Evidence quality analysis was based on publication type, sample size, study setting, number of publications supporting evidence, statistical significance, and the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist [25].

Patient Data

The data source used was the Adelphi HS Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a real-world, cross-sectional survey of dermatologists and their consulting patients with HS in five European countries (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the UK, collectively referred to as Europe [EUR]), and the USA, conducted between November 2020 and April 2021. The methodology has been previously described [26, 27], validated [28], and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [29].

Physicians (general dermatologists) actively involved in making management and treatment decisions for two or more patients with HS per month were included in the survey. Recruitment was by local fieldwork agencies and eligibility was confirmed on the basis of the criteria described above. Physicians were compensated for participating in the DSP according to fair market rates consistent with the time involved.

Physicians returned demographic and clinical information, including time of first consultation and time of diagnosis, on five to seven consecutively consulting patients with HS. Patients over 10 years of age with a physician-confirmed diagnosis of HS and not enrolled in a clinical trial were eligible for inclusion.

Each patient aged over 12 years for whom the physician returned data was invited to complete a patient questionnaire including detailed questions on demographics and disease history, including age of symptom onset.

Statistical Analysis and Modelling

Time-to-event analysis was conducted on the patient-reported onset of first symptom to physician-reported time of first consultation for HS symptoms with any healthcare professional, diagnosis, and initiation of targeted therapy, and time from physician-reported time of first consultation to diagnosis for HS symptoms with any healthcare professional, and initiation of targeted therapy. Targeted therapy (TT) was defined as any biologic or the small molecules apremilast, tofacitinib and baricitinib.

Parametric survival modelling was conducted for each time period using potential effect modifiers identified from previous literature: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity (White or not), smoking status, employment status, co-management with other physicians, number of concomitant conditions, family history of HS and patient country (all at time of data collection).

For analyses regarding time from first consultation, modelling was also done on a per-country basis. The same patients and covariates were used for all models to ensure that all models are compatible for use. Per-country analysis was not possible for analyses starting from onset of symptoms, because of an insufficient number of patients for which these data were available, as completion of the patient survey was voluntary.

For all models, potential distributions investigated included exponential, Weibull, Gompertz, lognormal, log-logistic, and generalized gamma. The best-fit model in each case was determined through assessment of Bayesian and Akaike information criteria. No multiple comparison correction was done, and a p value of below 0.05 was considered significant. Only significant variables are reported in the Results section and are highlighted with a red box in all figures.

Missing data were not imputed; therefore, the base of patients for analysis could vary from variable to variable and is reported separately for each analysis. Statistical analysis was done using Stata/MP (version 17.0; StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics

The survey was performed in compliance with the European Pharmaceutical Market Research Association (EphMRA) [30] and in full accordance with the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) 1996 [31] and the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. Ethical approval was granted by the Western Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board (WCG-IRB, approval number AG8836). Using a checkbox, patients provided informed consent to take part in the survey. Data were collected in such a way that patients and physicians could not be identified directly.

Results

Targeted Literature Review

A total of 394 publications of interest were identified from the literature search (Fig. S1), from which 26 key publications (as defined in Supplementary methods) were selected (Table S2). A range of factors contributing to HS diagnostic delay were identified, but misdiagnosis, patient embarrassment and delay in specialist referral had the most available supporting evidence (Fig. 1).

Patient Demographics

Data were collected from 312 physicians (EUR, n = 231 [74.0%]; USA, n = 81 [26.0%]), who returned data on 1787 patients with HS (EUR, n = 1305 [73.0%]; USA, n = 482 [27.0%]). Geographical details of physician and patient samples are in Table S3.

Patient demographic, clinical and treatment details are described in Table 1. In the overall HS population, the mean ± standard deviation (SD) age was 34.4 ± 12.2 years, mean BMI was 27.8 ± 5.3 kg/m2, 57.6% were female, 77.7% were White and 35.0% smoked. Patients took 38.4 ± 72.24 months to receive a diagnosis from symptom onset.

The most common physician-reported comorbidities were acne (24.2%), obesity (20.5%), anxiety (13.4%), depression (12.0%) and dyslipidaemia (8.4%). During the course of their treatment, the majority of patients had received antibiotics (66.2%) or topicals (58.5%), but only 28.4% had ever received biologics. Of those patients whose HS was considered by physicians to warrant use of biologics, 18.9% were not prescribed one. Patients were most frequently co-managed with a primary care provider (26.9% of patients) or a second dermatologist (8.9% of patients).

Time-to-Event Analysis

Survival analysis conducted using the time of patient-reported onset of first symptoms included a maximum 291 patients for whom symptom onset age was available and all relevant variables were known. Of these patients, 60 had received TT prior to data collection. No patients from the UK returned questionnaires; therefore, the UK was excluded from any analysis starting at symptom onset.

Data from first consultation was available for 894 patients. Of these, 247 (27.6%) received a diagnosis at first consultation, leaving 647 patients to be included in the analyses exploring factors associated with diagnostic delay. The full dataset of 894 was used to explore disease modifiers affecting the likelihood of receiving TT following first consultation. Of these, 413 had gone on to receive TT.

Factors Affecting Time Between Symptom Onset and Diagnosis

In order to investigate factors that affect the time between symptom onset and diagnosis, factors were modelled using a generalized gamma approach. Patients who were older, White and from Germany were diagnosed significantly faster from symptom onset. Smokers, patients with concomitant conditions, patients with a family history of HS, and patients from Spain were significantly slower to be diagnosed (Fig. 2).

Factors Affecting Time Between Symptom Onset and First Consultation

Time to diagnosis may also be affected by delayed consultations. Therefore, we modelled the time between symptom onset and first consultation using a log-logistic model. Patients who were White were significantly more likely to consult quicker from symptom onset. Patients from France and Spain were more likely to face a delay to consultation (Fig. 3).

Factors Affecting Time Between First Consultation and Diagnosis

A proportion of patients were diagnosed on the day of their first consultation (27.6%). A comparison by logistic regression of those patients who received a diagnosis at first consultation with those who did not (Fig. S2) showed that patients who had more than one concomitant condition, were co-managed, or from France, Germany, Italy, or Spain were significantly more likely not to be immediately diagnosed.

For those patients who did not receive a diagnosis on the day of their first consultation, we analysed factors affecting time to diagnosis using a log-logistic approach. Being older and having a family history of HS were significantly associated with a faster diagnosis. However, those who were overweight, employed, co-managed, had one or more concomitant condition or were from Europe significantly faced delayed diagnosis (Fig. 4).

However, different factors were associated with diagnostic delay in different countries (Fig. S3). There was a significant association between increasing age and speed of diagnosis in France, Italy, and the USA. White patients were diagnosed quicker in Germany, but slower in Spain. Employed patients were diagnosed slower in France and Italy, whereas being co-managed with another physician was associated with a slower diagnosis in the USA, but with less delay in Germany.

Factors Affecting Being Prescribed Targeted Therapy (TT)

Lastly, there may be a delay in being prescribed targeted therapy. We investigated the factors affecting the time to being prescribed TT using a generalized gamma model. This showed patients who were co-managed with another physician were prescribed TT significantly quicker after onset of symptoms. Patients from France were slower to be prescribed TT (Fig. S4).

Some patients may never be prescribed TT. We investigated factors affecting the likelihood of being prescribed TT from first consultation using a Weibull model, showing that older patients, those co-managed with another physician, or those with a family history of HS had a significantly higher likelihood of being prescribed TT. Patients who were employed or from Europe were less likely to receive TT (Fig. S5). When individual countries were analysed (Fig. S6), older patients were still significantly more likely to receive TT in France, Germany, Spain, and the USA. Employed patients or those with more concomitant conditions were more likely to receive TT in the UK, but less likely to in France. In Germany, those who were co-managed were significantly more likely to receive TT.

Discussion

Patients with hidradenitis suppurativa commonly face significant delays in receiving a HS diagnosis. Here, we have combined a targeted literature review with analysis of real-world clinical data to investigate the reasons for delays at each stage of the diagnostic process.

Analysis of the existing literature revealed a number of factors previously associated with diagnostic delay in HS. Those with the most available evidence included misdiagnosis, patient embarrassment and delay in specialist referral. However, the amount of supporting evidence does not necessarily correlate with the relative contribution of each factor to diagnostic delay. Our analysis of the Adelphi Real World HS DSP dataset allowed us to, for the first time, determine the exact contribution of each factor.

Our analysis highlighted that ethnicity is a key driver of delay in time to first consultation, with White patients up to four times less likely to experience such a delay, and also less likely to experience delayed diagnosis from symptom onset. This may be due to additional barriers for non-White patients to engage with healthcare services, including language barriers, cultural differences and lack of racial diversity amongst healthcare professionals [32]. A recent survey suggested Black patients were more engaged with their treatment than White patients [33], further suggesting physician-related factors may be partially responsible for delays based on ethnicity.

These data add to a growing body of evidence on a disparity in disease trajectories based on ethnicity. Although significant unmet need also remains amongst White patients, evidence suggests that in the USA, Black patients with HS were more likely to have more severe disease [34] and require HS-related hospitalisations [34, 35], illustrating the need for patient equity in achieving a timely diagnosis and preventing disease exacerbations.

Our findings reveal that patient lifestyle factors can impact time to HS diagnosis. Patients with a higher BMI experience delayed diagnosis, despite the fact that high BMI is a known risk factor for HS from an early age onwards [36], and is associated with more severe disease [37] and poorer response to treatment [38]. In addition, smokers also experience a significant delay to diagnosis, despite high levels of smoking being associated with HS [39], and incidence of HS in smokers is double that of non-smokers [40]. It is unclear why these known risk factors were associated with a delay in diagnosis, but this may represent a lack of knowledge in treating physicians surrounding risks for HS.

Our analysis revealed that patients with a family history of HS were also more likely to experience a delay in time to diagnosis but were diagnosed faster once seen, and were more likely to be prescribed targeted therapy (any biologic or the small molecules apremilast, tofacitinib and baricitinib) from first consultation. Delays in diagnosis for those with a family history of HS have been reported before, and it has been suggested that a degree of disease normalisation in those already familiar with HS may cause a higher threshold to seeking medical help [16]. This may result in patients with a family history not seeking medical attention because of a perception of their disease being less serious than it is. Additionally, it may be the case that patients from families who have experience with suboptimal outcomes from existing treatments see less urgency and need to seek treatment, because of low expectations of treatment efficacy.

Although employed patients did not experience any delays in consulting for their symptoms, there was a significant delay for this subgroup to receive a diagnosis, and they were less likely to receive TT. This contrasts with a finding in psoriasis, where those employed in professional or managerial roles were more likely to be prescribed biologics [41]. Given that a successful diagnosis of HS generally involves a number of consultations with different physicians, often located at specialist centres, reduced likelihood of TT prescription may be due to difficulties finding time to attend these consultations whilst being employed.

Use of TT is not routine for all patients with HS, and some may never be prescribed TT during their disease course. In our study, 19% of patients in our study were considered to have a condition that warrants a biologic, yet they were not prescribed biologic treatment. This may reflect the fact that HS is a relatively new indication for biological therapies, and the available data, though largely positive, are limited [42, 43]. There may also be healthcare system-specific limitations preventing physicians from prescribing TT to their patients, such as reimbursement policies or local regulatory restrictions. Patients who were not co-managed were more likely to face a delay to TT initiation, suggesting that a more multidisciplinary approach to care may drive more optimal prescribing. Younger patients were less likely to receive TT as dermatologists were possibly exhausting other treatment options before prescribing a TT.

Lack of prescribing of more optimal therapies may also lead to low treatment adherence, with patients less likely to adhere to treatment they view as less efficacious or as having high levels of side effects [44]. This further highlights the need for patients to be receive the most optimal treatment as soon as possible, to improve disease outcomes. Treatments may also be increasingly targeted in the future by taking emerging data on genetic risk factors for HS into account, which may point to particular inflammatory target pathways [45, 46], further providing optimised treatment for individual patients.

Our analysis has found differential impacts of various effect modifiers between the countries included in this study. Regional differences in HS prevalence have been noted [7]; these differential impacts likely arise from differences in medical education, treatment guidelines and reimbursement policies. Although there is overlap between published treatment guidelines from regions, significant differences remain [47], which likely affect the prescription of targeted therapies.

Despite there being significant evidence for sex disparity in the prevalence [48], presentation [49, 50] and severity of HS [51–53], no effect of sex was found in any of our analyses. This may indicate these differences are not sufficiently large to have a significant impact on diagnostic timings.

Delay in diagnosis has substantial consequences, both personally for the patient and economically for the wider healthcare system. Even when correctly diagnosed, patients with HS still suffer from a high disease burden [54], pain and mental health issues[55], impacted quality of life [56], and all-cause mortality [57] and have an increased likelihood of suffering from depression and committing suicide [5]. A delayed diagnosis, therefore, only adds to this burden.

Limitations

Participating patients may not reflect the general HS population since the DSP only includes patients who are consulting with their physician, meaning patients who consult more frequently have a higher likelihood of being included. The DSP is based on a pseudo-random sample of physicians or patients. While minimal inclusion criteria governed the selection of participating physicians, participation was influenced by their willingness to complete the survey. Screening and recruitment of physicians reflected geographically diverse samples subject to meeting DSP inclusion criteria.

Patient eligibility was based on the judgement of the respondent physician and not on a formalised diagnostic checklist; however, it is representative of the physician’s real-world classification of their patients. To minimize selection bias, physicians were asked to provide data for a consecutive series of eligible patients.

Recall bias, a common limitation of surveys, might also have affected responses of both physicians and patients. However, physicians did have the ability to refer to the patients’ records when returning data, thus minimising the possibility of recall bias.

Data collection took place before the approval of anti-interleukin-17 therapeutics for HS and therefore any changes in patient treatments following their introduction were not captured in this study.

Conclusion

The analyses presented here provide, for the first time, a comprehensive quantitative investigation of the factors associated with a diagnostic delay in HS in a multinational, real-world patient population. However, to further understand the implications of addressing these inequalities in the diagnosis and treatment of HS, the clinical and financial benefits of awareness and educational campaigns could be modelled. Improving healthcare equity for all patients with HS will provide an opportunity to speed up diagnosis, improve clinical outcomes and reduce healthcare expenditure.
