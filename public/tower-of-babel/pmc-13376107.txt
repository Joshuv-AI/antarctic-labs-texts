A comprehensive review of iatrogenic rectovaginal fistula after rectal cancer surgery
Authors: Andrea Martina Guida, Luigi Eduardo Conte, Antonella Paone, Amedeo Antonelli, Alessio Lucarini, Andrea Divizia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIatrogenic rectovaginal fistula (RVF) following total mesorectal excision for rectal cancer is an uncommon but profoundly disabling complication, imposing major physical, functional, sexual, and psychosocial burdens. Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, often arising in an inflamed, irradiated, or fibrotic pelvis, and compounded by coexisting anastomotic defects. This review is based on a narrative synthesis of the available literature; its aim is to provide an updated overview of current knowledge and clinical practice in the management of RVF, a highly specialised and technically demanding field with limited high-quality evidence.

Introduction

Rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is an abnormal, epithelialized communication between the rectum and the vagina that results in the involuntary passage of gas or faecal material through the vagina. Although not life-threatening, this condition markedly compromises quality of life, leading to recurrent vaginitis, malodorous vaginal discharge, mucous leakage, and perineal irritation [1–3].

Iatrogenic RVFs following low anterior resection (LAR) for rectal cancer represent a particularly challenging subset, accounting for approximately 5% of all fistula cases [4]. Unlike cryptogenic or obstetric fistulas, these lesions develop in a field of inflamed, often irradiated, and fibrotic tissues, where spontaneous closure is almost impossible and conservative management frequently fails, requiring multimodal and often repeated surgical interventions [5, 6]. Therefore, the burden of disease extends beyond the fistula. Women affected by postoperative RVF may endure prolonged therapeutic pathways, including multiple operations, temporary or prolonged faecal diversion, and in refractory cases, abdominoperineal resection with permanent stoma formation [4–6]. Each of these alternatives has its own functional and psychosocial consequences. Repeated pelvic surgery causes chronic pain and sexual impairment [7], while stoma formation has a well-documented impact on body image, social confidence, and overall wellbeing [8]. As modern rectal cancer management increasingly emphasises survivorship and quality of life, these sequelae represent a major clinical challenge.

Surgical repair is the mainstay of treatment. However, no standardized guidelines are currently available, and the choice of procedure largely depends on local experience and surgeon preference. Local repairs, such as simple advancement flaps, plugs placement and seton drainage, are rarely effective with success rates ranging from 5% to 12% due to the poor vascularization and dense fibrosis of the affected area [6]. Consequently, more invasive reconstructive options—such as interposition of well-vascularized muscle flaps, including gracilis or bulbocavernosus (Martius) flaps—are often required, with stoma creation increasing overall success rate from 6% to 32% comparing patients without and with faecal diversion [5, 6].

This review provides a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic workup, and surgical management of iatrogenic RVF following LAR for rectal cancer, outlining the specific clinical challenges of this condition and proposing a practical therapeutic algorithm to support clinical decision-making.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify studies published up to October 1, 2025, using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to rectovaginal fistula and rectal cancer surgery, including: (“rectovaginal fistula” OR “vaginal fistula” OR “anastomotic vaginal fistula”) AND (“anterior resection” OR “low anterior resection” OR “rectal resection” OR “surgery for rectal cancer”) AND (“rectal cancer” OR “rectal carcinoma” OR “rectal neoplasm”) AND (“iatrogenic” OR “postoperative” OR “complication” OR “surgical”). Reference lists of all relevant publications were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

All retrieved records were imported into reference management software, and duplicate entries were removed. The search yielded 283 records, which underwent a two-stage screening process after deduplication. Title and abstract screening were independently performed by three reviewers. The inclusion criteria comprised cohort studies, case series, case reports, and clinical reviews addressing management of women who developed rectovaginal fistula following rectal cancer resection, including low anterior resection, ultra-low anterior resection, coloanal anastomosis, or total mesorectal excision. Papers dealing exclusively with obstetric fistulas, Crohn’s disease–related fistulas, primary gynaecologic fistulas, or fistulas arising after non-oncologic colorectal surgery were excluded. Only articles published in English were considered. Detailed information on study selection and reasons for exclusion is provided in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

Disagreements at any stage of the selection process were resolved by discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a fourth senior reviewer made the final decision.

Overall, 32 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis.

Given the heterogeneity of the included studies and the predominance of low-level evidence (case reports and small case series), no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis was performed. Data were extracted qualitatively and synthesised narratively, taking into account study design, sample size, and clinical context.

Epidemiology and risk factors

Reported rates of RVF after rectal cancer surgery range from 0.9% to 9%, with higher incidence reported in series including patients with larger fistulas and without a diverting stoma [9–11]. Indeed, small fistulas and fistulas in patients with a diverting stoma may remain asymptomatic and are often detected only at the time of stoma reversal or remain unrecognised if reversal is not performed.

The timing of iatrogenic RVF diagnosis is highly variable. Most cases are diagnosed within 1–3 weeks after surgery and present with pelvic sepsis, fever, or the passage of flatus or stool through the vagina [1, 10–15]. Early RVFs frequently result from inadvertent incorporation of the posterior vaginal wall during double stapling, becoming apparent once faecal flow reaches the anastomosis [16, 17]. However, several studies describe median detection times of approximately 20 days, with some fistulas occurring even months after surgery. These delayed-onset RVFs typically arise in the context of chronic pelvic sepsis, persistent sinus tracts, or progressive anastomotic stenosis, which can lead to localised pressure and tissue erosion [13, 18, 19].

Despite improvements in colorectal surgical techniques, RVF incidence has not significantly decreased, underscoring its multifactorial aetiology. Among the most relevant there are patient-related risk factors, including poor preoperative nutritional status, low haemoglobin levels, and previous gynaecological surgery—which may alter pelvic anatomy and compromise tissue integrity [9, 19] and tumour-related factors, such as advanced T stage, low tumour location, and preoperative chemoradiotherapy (CRT) [10, 13].

Patient-related risk factors

Patient-related factors influence tissue healing, vascular integrity, and overall susceptibility to RVF formation. Low preoperative haemoglobin has been identified as an independent predictor of RVF, likely reflecting impaired oxygen delivery and compromised anastomotic repair [9]. Nutritional status is similarly relevant: both low serum albumin and reduced prognostic nutritional index (PNI) have been associated with increased RVF risk, as nutritional deficiency contributes to microvascular hypoxia and diminished tissue stability [19]. The role of BMI remains unclear; while several studies found no significant association, Matthiessen et al. observed that women who developed RVFs had more frequently lower BMI, with 76% having a BMI < 25 compared with 54% in the non-fistulising group, whereas only 24% had a BMI ≥ 25 compared with 46% of controls (p = 0.07), suggesting that reduced pelvic soft-tissue buffering may contribute to fistula formation [10].

Age and general comorbidity burden do not appear to independently increase RVF risk. Multiple retrospective cohorts have shown no significant association with age, ASA class, diabetes, hypertension, or other chronic diseases [9, 13, 19]. However, frailty-related microvascular impairment may exert an indirect effect, suggesting that physiological rather than chronological age may be more relevant [13].

In contrast, previous gynaecological surgery—particularly hysterectomy or bilateral oophorectomy—has consistently been linked to higher RVF rates [9]. Altered pelvic anatomy, adhesions, and reduced vaginal vascularity may predispose to inadvertent vaginal wall incorporation during stapling or exacerbate ischaemic injury to the rectovaginal septum [13, 19].

Among all patient-related variables, prior pelvic radiotherapy represents the most consistently reported risk factor. Radiotherapy-induced vascular injury, submucosal fibrosis, and impaired tissue oxygenation significantly compromising anastomotic healing and increasing the likelihood of fistulisation [9, 13, 19, 20].

Tumour-related characteristics

Intrinsic tumour characteristics are key determinants of RVF risk. Among these, tumour distance from the anal verge is the most consistent predictor: lower rectal tumours place the anastomosis in close proximity to the posterior vaginal wall, increasing the chance of mechanical injury or inadvertent tissue incorporation during stapling. Large cohort studies confirm that each additional centimetre of tumour–anal distance reduces RVF risk, while lesions within 5 cm of the anal verge carry a markedly higher incidence; up to 65% of RVFs occur in this subgroup [9, 10, 13]. These observations reflect the limited soft-tissue separation and restricted visualisation in the female pelvis, which may compromise anastomotic precision.

Tumour stage further modulates risk. Locally advanced lesions (T3 cancers), especially those involving the anterior rectal wall, require more extensive dissection near the vagina and may involve the rectovaginal septum, increasing the likelihood of operative injury or devascularisation [10, 19].

Neoadjuvant CRT is another major contributor. Although beneficial for oncological control, CRT induces submucosal fibrosis, microvascular damage, and impaired collagen remodelling, collectively reducing tissue perfusion and healing capacity [21–23]. Numerous clinical series identify preoperative CRT as one of the strongest predictors of RVF, with irradiated patients demonstrating higher rates of delayed tissue breakdown, chronic pelvic sepsis, and anastomotic failure [9, 11, 13, 19]. RVF is often described as a particularly severe form of anastomotic leak that disproportionately affects irradiated pelvises [1, 10, 15]. Additional data show impaired healing and increased reoperation rates in these patients, frequently necessitating delayed coloanal anastomosis with transanal pull-through or alternative reconstructive strategies [4, 24, 25].

Technical factors

Technical and intraoperative factors play a critical role in RVF pathogenesis, mainly through their impact on anastomotic integrity. Indicators of operative difficulty—such as prolonged operative time and significant intraoperative bleeding—have been associated with higher postoperative morbidity, including RVF [13]. Extensive lymph node dissection, particularly lateral pelvic lymphadenectomy, may further increase susceptibility by exacerbating pelvic tissues devascularisation [19].

The type of surgical approach (open vs. laparoscopic) does not appear to independently influence RVF risk. Fistulas occur more frequently following low and ultra–low anterior resections due to the proximity of the anastomosis to the posterior vaginal wall [9]. Among technical variables, the double stapling technique (DST) is most consistently implicated. RVFs related to DST generally stem from stapler misalignment or inadvertent inclusion of the posterior vaginal wall, leading to direct communication or ischaemic injury [11, 15–17]. In particular, Nakagoe et al. reported a 2.9% RVF incidence in women undergoing DST anastomosis, with all fistulas occurring adjacent to the staple line and attributed to DST-specific trauma [26]. Kosugi et al. similarly observed that most RVFs arose in the context of stapled low anastomoses [14].

Although DST has made low anastomoses safer and more reproducible, its use in very distal lesions, suboptimal stapler positioning, or incomplete doughnut formation may increase the risk of rectovaginal injury in female patients.

The protective role of diverting stomas remains debated. Several studies reported no significant reduction in RVF rates with routine diversion [10, 13], whereas others advocate its use in high-risk situations—such as very low anastomoses, poor nutritional status, or preoperative radiotherapy—to limit septic contamination in the event of microleakage [11, 19].

Postoperative complication: anastomotic leak and pelvic sepsis

Postoperative events—particularly anastomotic leakage and pelvic sepsis—constitute the most consistently reported pathway leading to RVF after low anterior resection. Evidence from multiple retrospective cohorts indicates that RVF typically develops secondary to inflammatory complications: leakage of intestinal contents into the presacral space promotes microbial contamination, phlegmon, and abscess formation, which may drain spontaneously and evolve into a persistent fistulous tract [10, 13, 19].

Several authors regard RVF as a distinct subset of anastomotic leaks. Matthiessen et al. observed symptomatic leaks in 13.3% of patients, with 5.1% subsequently developing RVF, highlighting that fistulisation often represents a predictable consequence of specific leak patterns in the female pelvis [1, 15]. Consistently, Zheng et al. identified postoperative leak as one of the strongest independent predictors of RVF, reinforcing the importance of secure anastomotic construction [13].

Lamazza et al. reported that all RVFs following low anterior resection occurred in the presence of a pelvic abscess on CT imaging, underscoring the central role of localised infection in fistula formation. Chronic pelvic sepsis disrupts granulation tissue, fosters necrosis, and promotes persistent sinus tracts capable of eroding through the rectovaginal septum [27].

Additional postoperative contributors include excessive tension on a low anastomosis, devascularisation during pelvic dissection, or tissue compromised by prior radiotherapy. Postoperative haemorrhage or haematoma formation may exacerbate local hypoxia by increasing tissue pressure and impairing microcirculatory flow [11, 13, 19].

Clinical presentation

RVF after LAR results in a broad spectrum of symptoms that negatively affect physical, functional, emotional, and social well-being. The hallmark manifestations are the passage of flatus or faecal material through the vagina—reported universally across postoperative series and typically appearing early, particularly in the context of anastomotic leakage or inadvertent vaginal incorporation during stapled anastomosis [5, 28]. These symptoms are frequently accompanied by malodorous discharge, mucous leakage, recurrent vaginitis, and perineal maceration, contributing to significant daily discomfort and challenges with local hygiene [13, 28]. Symptom severity often fluctuates with stool consistency, with looser stools exacerbating leakage and odour.

Beyond these cardinal features, RVF commonly coexists with bowel dysfunction, especially after low colorectal or coloanal anastomoses. Many women experience urgency, clustering, incomplete evacuation, and frequent small-volume defaecations characteristic of low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), which further aggravate vaginal leakage and interfere with routine activities. Data from coloanal anastomosis cohorts consistently report high rates of moderate-to-severe LARS, with up to 61% of patients exhibiting major dysfunction and marked reductions in gastrointestinal quality-of-life in the early postoperative period [13, 25].

The functional burden is confirmed in larger series. In the multicentre GRECCAR study of delayed coloanal anastomosis (DCAA) for complex RVF, 42% of patients experienced major LARS or ultimately required permanent stoma formation, with a median LARS score of 25 and only 58% achieving no or minor LARS [4]. Comparable findings were observed in a single-centre DCAA cohort from Saint-Antoine Hospital, where mean LARS scores approached the major range and 61% of patients had severe dysfunction despite anatomically successful reconstruction [25].

Sexual function is also frequently impaired. A systematic review of gracilis muscle interposition showed a 64% fistula healing rate and improvements in continence and global quality of life, but sexual function outcomes remained heterogeneous and often suboptimal [11].

These findings—with population-based studies indicating major LARS rates of 50–60% after standard low anterior resection [24, 25]— highlight that even after technically successful RVF repair, a substantial proportion of women continue to experience urgency, clustering, incontinence, and fragmented sexual and social functioning that profoundly restrict daily life and overall well-being.

Quality of life

The impact of RVF on quality of life (QoL) is profound, multidimensional, and frequently disproportionate to the anatomical size of the defect. Across case series, qualitative reports, and retrospective cohorts, women consistently describe embarrassment, fear of social exposure, and a sense of diminished bodily integrity, all of which markedly restrict daily functioning and participation in normal activities [25, 26]. Foul-smelling discharge and unpredictable leakage generate persistent anxiety and often lead to avoidance of travel, social gatherings, and routine work commitments, while necessitating demanding hygiene measures.

Sexual dysfunction represents one of the most debilitating consequences of RVF. Dyspareunia, fear of vaginal soiling, avoidance of intimacy, and distress related to altered body image are recurrent themes in the literature, with several studies reporting complete cessation of sexual activity before fistula repair, even in women with preserved continence [29, 30]. Shame, reduced self-esteem, and concern over partner reactions contribute to chronic emotional stress.

The burden extends across social, emotional, and occupational domains. Many patients restrict professional activities or adopt socially isolating behaviours to avoid potential embarrassment. The psychological toll is compounded by the prolonged and often unpredictable trajectory to definitive closure, with repeated failed repairs, long periods of diversion, and treatment delays further worsening QoL. Economic and logistical challenges are also substantial; one retrospective analysis showed that women with RVF had longer hospital stays and higher treatment costs than matched postoperative controls, reflecting the complexity of management and the frequency of reintervention [30]. The presence of a diverting stoma—although often necessary—adds an additional layer of psychological strain.

Successful fistula repair is associated with marked improvement across most QoL domains. Case series describe resolution of discharge, restoration of sexual activity, and resumption of normal social functioning after approaches such as transvaginal repair, perineal flap interposition, or combined abdominal–perineal techniques, particularly once pelvic sepsis has been controlled [30]. Nevertheless, the emotional impact may persist, with some women reporting continued fear of recurrence or anxiety during sexual activity.

Overall, RVF represents a condition in which physical symptoms, sexual dysfunction, emotional distress, and bowel-related impairment converge to produce a severe, multidimensional reduction in quality of life that extends well beyond the anatomical lesion itself.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis of RVF relies on the structured integration of patient-reported symptoms, pelvic examination, and targeted imaging. Typical complaints—including vaginal passage of stool or gas, malodorous discharge, and recurrent vaginitis—are usually sufficient to raise clinical suspicion and prompt focused evaluation [5, 28–30]. Pelvic examination, with digital rectal and bimanual palpation, may reveal tenderness, scarring, or direct visualisation of a low fistulous opening. Several reports also describe identification of retained staple fragments or inadvertent vaginal capture during the double-stapling technique, providing both diagnostic confirmation and insight into aetiology [5, 28].

Endoscopic assessment is central to the diagnostic work-up. Flexible sigmoidoscopy allows visualisation of the internal opening, appraisal of anastomotic integrity, and detection of stenosis or inflammatory cavities, while vaginoscopy offers complementary inspection of the vaginal orifice. In small, early, or minimally symptomatic fistulas, dye testing—typically methylene blue instilled via the rectum or vagina—may aid detection when clinical or endoscopic findings are inconclusive [11, 13].

Cross-sectional imaging is essential in complex or recurrent RVFs. Pelvic MRI provides high-resolution delineation of fistulous tracts, associated abscesses, fibrosis, and radiation-induced changes, and is particularly valuable for high lesions or where pelvic anatomy is distorted by prior surgery or congenital anomalies [30]. MRI can also identify multiple tracts or arborising extensions. When there is uncertainty regarding anastomotic integrity, contrast enema is recommended to detect occult leakage or stenosis, both of which strongly influence management decisions [11].

Exploration under anaesthesia (EUA) remains useful in diagnostically challenging cases. Dye testing may be better tolerated under anaesthesia, and EUA permits direct assessment of fistula anatomy, rectovaginal septum thickness, and the degree of inflammation or fibrosis, thereby informing operative planning [13, 26].

Overall, a structured diagnostic algorithm—combining symptoms, thorough pelvic examination, endoscopy, and advanced imaging—enables accurate characterisation of fistula anatomy, identification of contributing factors, and optimal stratification for definitive management.

Management

The management of iatrogenic RVF following LAR for rectal cancer remains challenging and requires an individualized approach. Treatment selection primarily depends on fistula size, anatomical location (low, mid, or high), and surrounding tissue conditions, particularly the presence of pelvic sepsis, prior radiotherapy, fibrosis, or impaired vascularization. Patient comorbidities, oncologic status, and functional expectations should also be considered. The main goals of treatment are sepsis control, restoration of anatomical integrity, and improvement of quality of life.

Although clinical practice guidelines provide a broad spectrum of potential interventions for RVF in general [20], evidence specific to RVF after rectal cancer surgery remains limited and largely based on small retrospective series. Numerous alternative therapeutic strategies have been described, still their overall effectiveness remains limited, with reported success rates consistently low, common recurrences, and many patients requiring multiple interventions, resulting in a considerable and persistent burden on quality of life [5, 6].

Most centres currently adopt a pragmatic step-up strategy, progressing from less invasive to more complex procedures. However, since outcomes are strongly influenced by fistula anatomy and tissue viability, treatment should be tailored rather than strictly sequential.

In general, small low fistulas in well-vascularised, non-irradiated tissue may be amenable to local repair, whereas large, high, recurrent, or irradiated fistulas—especially when associated with sepsis or anastomotic failure—frequently require tissue interposition or abdominal reconstruction.

Conservative treatment

Conservative treatment represents the least invasive strategy for RVF management and typically includes sitz baths, antibiotic therapy, and stool-bulking fibre supplementation. Although conservative management may be effective for benign or obstetric RVF occurring in healthy, non-irradiated tissue, with reported success rates ranging from 52% to 66% [31, 32], its role in iatrogenic RVF remains limited. Following rectal cancer surgery, the local environment is often characterized by chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and microvascular compromission due to anastomotic leakage or radiotherapy [5]. These factors markedly reduce spontaneous healing potential.

Only few case reports and case series analysed the effect of conservative treatment on iatrogenic RVF. Emoto et al. reported the non-operative management of a particularly favourable case with a small in diameter (10.2 mm) and minimally symptomatic RVF. The approach combined oral fasting, total parenteral nutrition, magnesium oxide, and antiflatulents to reduce mechanical stress on the fistula tract. Intravenous factor XIII (1440 IU/day for 5 days) was added in response to its postoperative depletion and its known function in stabilizing fibrin clots and supporting tissue repair. Although the short-term outcome was positive, the follow-up was limited to four months, which restricts conclusions regarding long-term durability [12].

Lohsirivat et al. emphasized that conservative therapy is appropriate only for a narrow subset of patients—those with small, minimally symptomatic fistulas and no history of pelvic irradiation. They also suggested adjunctive strategies such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy and the administration of adipose-derived or mesenchymal stem cells, which have shown benefit in obstetrical and Crohn’s-related RVF, respectively, by enhancing tissue oxygenation or modulating local inflammation [11]. However, their effectiveness in iatrogenic RVF following rectal cancer surgery remains untested and may be limited by the fibrotic, irradiated tissue environment typical of oncologic patients.

More recently Hiraki et al. reported a successful case of combined use of oral estriol, polyglycolic acid sheet and fibrin glue for the treatment of a symptomatic but small RVF (3 mm in diameter). Estriol is known to increase vaginal compliance, blood flow, and lubrication while reducing vaginal pH [33]. Although its isolated effect has never been formally evaluated, the favourable physiological changes it induces suggest that its oral administration may be considered in those carefully selected patients with favourable fistula anatomy and undergoing conservative management.

Overall, conservative management should be reserved for highly selected patients with very small, minimally symptomatic fistulas, absence of pelvic irradiation, and no evidence of ongoing sepsis. In symptomatic patients—particularly those with passage of stool or gas through the vagina—expectant management rarely leads to durable closure and may delay definitive treatment while allowing tract epithelialisation. Adjunctive measures such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy, stem cell therapy, or hormonal treatment have been described, but evidence in postoperative RVF is extremely limited and largely extrapolated from other disease settings.

Faecal diversion

Faecal diversion plays an important supportive role in the management of iatrogenic RVF. By reducing faecal contamination, intraluminal pressure, and mechanical stress, a diverting stoma may facilitate resolution of inflammation and sepsis and improve tissue conditions prior to repair.

Spontaneous closure with diversion alone is uncommon but may occur. In highly selected cases—typically small, minimally symptomatic fistulas—faecal diversion alone may result in spontaneous closure in up to 25%−100% of cases [11], even in the absence of adjunctive local procedures. Historical data from an ASCRS survey reported a 35% success rate with diversion alone, probably attributable to the lack of neoadjuvant radiotherapy prior to surgery [15].

In contrast, two additional reports described failure of diversion alone—whether via transverse colostomy or ileostomy—necessitating subsequent local or abdominal procedures to achieve definitive fistula closure [18, 34, 35].

The true efficacy of diversion as monotherapy remains uncertain, as most published experiences combine diversion with additional interventions. In these settings, diversion is typically used as an adjunct to local or reconstructive procedures or as a preliminary step before definitive repair, with the aim of improving local conditions for healing. This role is supported by a recent retrospective study including 25 postoperative RVFs, in which the presence of a diverting stoma emerged as an independent predictor of successful local treatment in multivariate analysis [6].

These heterogeneous and conflicting findings make it challenging to determine the true role of faecal diversion in the management of iatrogenic RVF. The available evidence suggests that the clinical rationale for diversion is strongest when it is used as an adjunct to local procedures, potentially enhancing the success rates of less invasive interventions.

Endoscopic and local treatment

Endoscopic techniques—including stents, sealants, clips, and vacuum therapy—have been reported as minimally invasive options, but the available evidence consists almost exclusively of isolated case reports or very small series [9, 29, 33, 36]. These approaches may be considered experimental and should be reserved for carefully selected patients with small defects and favourable tissue conditions or when conventional surgery is contraindicated.

Sealant materials and clipping techniques rely on adequate tissue viability and minimal inflammation, conditions that are frequently absent in irradiated or chronically infected postoperative fields [33, 37]. Similarly, stenting may reduce local contamination but does not address underlying tissue damage [9]. Overall, current evidence does not support endoscopic therapies as standard treatment for iatrogenic RVF after rectal cancer surgery.

Perineal procedures

Perineal approaches—including transanal, transvaginal, and transperineal repairs—are most suitable for low or mid-level fistulas in well-vascularised tissue.

Direct closure or advancement flap techniques remain widely used in selected cases, regardless of fecal diversion [15], with rectal advancement flaps being one of the most widely adopted perineal technique even in case of refractory fistulas [33, 35]. Transvaginal repair may also be effective in selected patients, regardless of diversion or the use of biologic reinforcement materials [2, 26, 38], although outcomes are variable; for example, Ohta et al. reported failure of a diverted transvaginal repair requiring subsequent laparoscopic management [36].

However, their success is strongly dependent on tissue quality. In devascularised, fibrotic, or irradiated tissues, direct repair through transanal or transvaginal routes has a high risk of failure. Under these conditions, reinforcement with well-vascularised tissue seems to be preferable.

In complex or recurrent RVFs, interposition of well-vascularised tissue—such as Martius, levator ani, gracilis, or gluteal muscle flaps, or biologic meshes—can enhance local vascularity and reduce recurrence. Evidence for gluteal flaps is limited to case reports without long-term follow-up or detailed anatomical characterisation [14, 39], and puborectal sling interposition has been described only once in a J-pouch–vaginal fistula, with uncertain applicability to standard LAR with Knight–Griffen anastomosis [3]. Martius and Falandry flaps have shown a 72.7% success rate in a small series of ten patients with a mean follow-up of 12.6 ± 10 months [28]. Graciloplasty is widely used but yields inconsistent results; a European retrospective study of recurrent RVFs of mixed aetiology identified postoperative perineal infection and lack of antibiotic prophylaxis as risk factors for failure, with unsuccessful outcomes in approximately 40% of patients irrespective of cause [40].

Abdominal procedures

Abdominal surgery is reserved for cases in which less invasive methods are unsuitable or have failed, or when associated anastomotic complications are present. Standard techniques include excision of the fistula tract and omental interposition to enhance healing. However, only one transabdominal omental flap for an iatrogenic RVF after LAR has been described so far. In that case, laparoscopic fistula resection with vaginal and rectal repair and diverting colostomy achieved durable closure at 12-month follow-up [34]. Other reports suggest that laparoscopic repair alone may be sufficient, with successful outcomes even without omental interposition [36].

Wang et al. noted that the transabdominal route may be challenging in the presence of dense adhesions, whereas a transperineal approach may be difficult in irradiated or inflamed tissues. They therefore proposed a combined transabdominal and transanal technique involving a full-thickness rectal incision above the dentate line, placement of a purse-string suture, dissection between the mesorectum and pelvic sidewall, and perineal dissection between the rectum and vagina. This synchronous exposure facilitated fistula repair, rectal resection, and reconstruction with a new colorectal anastomosis, achieving successful closure in two patients [18].

In complex cases, particularly when the primary anastomosis is compromised, redo colorectal or coloanal anastomosis remains the most definitive restorative option, especially when performed in specialised centres. A French series of 28 patients reported an 86% success rate for delayed coloanal anastomosis after a mean follow-up of 23 ± 23 months, with outcomes improving to 91% among those with a diverting stoma [28]. These findings were corroborated by the larger GRECCAR cohort, although that series included fistulas of mixed aetiology (67% iatrogenic RVF after LAR) [4].

Proposed therapeutic algorithm

Based on the available evidence and on our experience, we propose a practical anatomy- and tissue-based stepwise algorithm to support clinical decision-making (Fig. 2). This algorithm should be interpreted as a pragmatic, expert-informed framework rather than an evidence-based or guideline-endorsed pathway. Management of iatrogenic RVF after LAR should primarily be guided by fistula size, anatomical level, and tissue condition, particularly the presence of pelvic sepsis, prior irradiation, fibrosis, and anastomotic integrity. Initial evaluation should focus on controlling infection and optimising local tissue conditions, often with faecal diversion when inflammation or sepsis is present. Conservative management may be considered only in highly selected patients with very small, minimally symptomatic fistulas in well-vascularised, non-irradiated tissue.

Proposed algorithm for the treatment of RVF after LAR for rectal cancer. The algorithm is based on limited and heterogeneous evidence and on the authors’ clinical experience

For low or mid-level fistulas with preserved tissue viability, perineal approaches such as transanal or transvaginal repair could represent appropriate first-line surgical options. In the presence of compromised tissue quality, recurrent disease, or larger defects, repair should be reinforced with well-vascularised tissue interposition. Endoscopic therapies may be considered only in exceptional cases – when surgery is contraindicated, given the limited supporting evidence.

High, complex, or recurrent fistulas, particularly those associated with anastomotic failure, extensive fibrosis, or persistent sepsis, generally require abdominal reconstruction. In these settings, redo colorectal or coloanal anastomosis—often performed as a delayed procedure after sepsis control—represents the most definitive restorative strategy in suitable candidates. Permanent diversion remains the final option when reconstructive approaches are not feasible or have failed.

Conclusions

Iatrogenic RVF after TME for rectal cancer, although uncommon, remains a profoundly life-altering complication with major physical, sexual, and psychosocial consequences. Its multifactorial aetiology and heterogeneous presentations complicate diagnosis and delay targeted management. Therapeutic strategies range from minimally invasive endoscopic techniques to complex perineal, transvaginal, abdominal, or combined procedures. However, no standardized management pathway currently exists, and failure rates remain considerable. In selected cases, redo anastomosis offers the best restorative option, while a permanent stoma may be unavoidable in refractory disease. Multidisciplinary care is essential to optimise outcomes and preserve long-term quality of life in this vulnerable patient population.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the available evidence on this topic is limited and consists predominantly of case reports and small retrospective case series. Sample sizes were generally small, and most studies were descriptive in nature. In addition, substantial heterogeneity existed across studies regarding fistula aetiology, anatomical characteristics, previous treatments, and therapeutic approaches, limiting direct comparability of findings. This marked clinical and methodological variability precluded quantitative synthesis and made meta-analysis unfeasible. Therefore, the conclusions of this review should be interpreted with caution. Further well-designed studies involving larger patient cohorts are needed to provide stronger evidence and better guide clinical decision-making.
