Sinonasal Mucosal Melanoma: A Comprehensive Review and Clinical Experience
Authors: Dongyoung Kim, Seung-No Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sinonasal malignant melanoma is an extremely rare and aggressive malignancy. Diagnosis is often delayed because of nonspecific symptoms and complex histopathology. The primary treatment is surgical resection, with adjuvant radiotherapy frequently employed, although its survival benefit remains controversial. Systemic therapies, including immunotherapy and targeted therapy, show limited efficacy compared with cutaneous melanoma, partly due to distinct genetic profiles. The authors present the experience of a single-referral center with 10 patients diagnosed with sinonasal malignant melanoma. Despite treatment, which primarily involved surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy (n=7), outcomes were poor, with frequent recurrence and metastasis. Only one patient who achieved clear surgical margins and received adjuvant radiotherapy remained disease-free for more than five years. Based on these data, achieving clear surgical margins and establishing an early diagnosis are critical prognostic factors in the treatment of sinonasal mucosal melanoma. Management of this condition remains challenging, and no standardized treatment protocol has been established. Continued investigation and research are imperative to develop definitive therapeutic approaches.

INTRODUCTION

Melanoma is a malignant neoplasm that originates from melanocytes. These melanocytes develop from precursor cells known as melanoblasts, which are derived from the neural crest [1]. Melanoblasts migrate to various locations throughout the body, including the epidermis, hair follicles, and mucosal surfaces, where they subsequently differentiate into melanocytes. Under certain conditions, these melanocytes may undergo malignant transformation, leading to the development of melanoma [2]. Although most malignant melanomas present as cutaneous melanomas [3], melanocytes are also found in various mucosal membranes where they can undergo malignant transformation. Rhinologists may encounter not only cutaneous melanomas of the facial region but also mucosal melanomas arising within the sinonasal cavity. Malignant melanomas originating in the sinonasal cavity are often difficult to detect because of the region’s complex anatomy and the absence of disease-specific clinical characteristics. Patients frequently present with nonspecific nasal symptoms, resulting in substantial diagnostic delays. Consequently, these lesions are typically identified only after significant local invasion has occurred, complicating treatment strategies.

Furthermore, even when a tissue biopsy is performed, the confined space of the nasal cavity often limits complete surgical excision of specimens, making it difficult to obtain clear surgical margins. In addition, the nonspecific histopathological characteristics of these lesions frequently lead to prolonged pathological assessment and carry a risk of misdiagnosis, occasionally resulting in treatment delays [4].

For sinonasal malignant melanoma (SNMM) without distant metastasis, the standard treatment approach generally involves surgical resection followed by radiation therapy. However, because of the rarity of this disease, establishing a unified treatment protocol remains difficult. Even experienced rhinology specialists rarely encounter this tumor, making it challenging to accumulate sufficient clinical experience.

In this review, we aim to examine current best practices and the state of scientific research regarding SNMM, a specific variant of malignant melanoma. In addition, the authors present a 10-year experience with primary SNMM cases treated at a single referral hospital.

INCIDENCE AND PROGNOSIS

Melanomas arising from cutaneous tissues are considerably more common than those originating in the sinonasal cavity and represent the fifth most common malignancy in the United States [5]. Unfortunately, since 1975, the incidence of melanoma has increased by approximately 320%, with an annual growth rate of 2%–3%; however, the mortality rate has decreased by 1%–2% annually [6]. According to the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program, the 5-year relative survival rate has reached 94%, making melanoma one of the malignancies with the highest overall survival rates [7].

In contrast, mucosal melanomas represent an extremely rare variant, accounting for only 0.8%–3.7% of all melanoma cases [8-10]. While SNMM comprises approximately 80% of all head and neck mucosal melanomas, it remains exceptionally uncommon, with an incidence of merely 0.2–1 case per million population [11].

Despite its rarity, the highly aggressive nature of SNMM has consistently drawn attention from rhinologists. The 5-year survival rate for SNMM ranges from 24.2% to 33%, which is comparable to the 32% survival rate of metastatic cutaneous melanoma and significantly lower than the 71% survival rate observed in regional metastatic cutaneous melanoma [12-15].

Compared to Caucasians, people of color generally have a lower prevalence of cutaneous melanoma because their skin contains more dispersed melanin and melanosomes, which absorb and deflect ultraviolet (UV) radiation [16]. Indeed, studies conducted in Japanese populations have demonstrated that the prevalence of cutaneous melanoma is more than ten times lower than that observed among Caucasian populations [17-19].

Therefore, unlike Caucasian populations, where cutaneous melanoma represents the predominant form of malignant melanoma, people of color exhibit a distinct distribution pattern. According to a single-institution retrospective study from Duke University, mucosal melanoma accounted for only 1.1% of 10,393 melanoma cases, although this proportion may reflect the predominantly Caucasian study population (87%) [20]. In contrast, a Chinese study reported that mucosal melanoma comprised 24% of all melanomas [21], while a Japanese study found a rate of 9.5% [19]. These findings suggest that mucosal melanoma constitutes a higher proportion of total melanoma cases among Asian populations, likely reflecting the relatively lower incidence of cutaneous melanoma in these populations.

Among head and neck melanomas, approximately two-thirds occur in the nasal cavity and one-third in the oral cavity [22]. A prospective study by Lian et al. [23] found that 23% of mucosal melanomas develop in the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, making this region the second most common site after the lower gastrointestinal tract. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and treatment modalities, the survival rate for SNMM remains poor. A systematic review published by Rojas-Lechuga et al. [24] in 2024 reported a 5-year overall survival rate of only 34.8%, underscoring the highly lethal nature of this disease [24].

Primary SNMM more frequently originates in the nasal cavity than in the paranasal sinuses [25]. Cases arising from the frontal or sphenoid sinuses are rarely reported, and sphenoid sinus involvement has been described only in sporadic case reports [26]. Among the paranasal sinuses, the maxillary sinus is the most commonly involved site, followed by the ethmoid sinus [27]. Melanomas arising in deeper anatomical regions such as the ethmoid, frontal, and sphenoid sinuses pose significant diagnostic challenges due to their limited accessibility and proximity to critical structures such as the skull base and orbit, leading to delayed recognition. Consequently, when SNMM occurs in these sites, it is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with extension into adjacent critical structures. In such cases, surgical resection becomes technically demanding, and identifying the precise site of origin may be less clinically relevant for treatment planning. Therefore, when advanced cases are excluded from epidemiological studies, the nasal cavity and maxillary sinuses appear as the most frequently reported primary sites, reflecting a potential selection bias toward surgically accessible, earlier-detected lesions. For this reason, most case series do not classify primary sites according to specific anatomical subsites within the sinonasal cavity, and some studies even report the maxillary sinus as the most common origin, necessitating careful interpretation of the literature [28,29].

In contrast, the anterior nasal cavity, including the septum, offers better visibility during examination than the paranasal sinuses. This difference in accessibility may help explain why SNMM generally has a poorer prognosis than oral cavity mucosal melanomas [30].

IMMUNOHISTOCHEMICAL MARKERS

Primary SNMM shares its immunophenotype with cutaneous melanoma, with common markers including S100 protein, SOX10, HMB-45, MART-1, melan-A, MiTF, tyrosinase, and vimentin [31]. However, no single marker, including strongly positive S100 protein or SOX10, is sufficiently sensitive or specific to establish a diagnosis of melanoma independently [32].

Canaz et al. [33] conducted a study on neuroendocrine differentiation in mucosal melanomas of the head and neck region, examining the positivity rates of synaptophysin, chromogranin, CD56, and INSM-1. Their findings showed that 72.2% of cases demonstrated positivity for at least one of these markers, providing valuable diagnostic reference data.

Preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma (PRAME) has drawn considerable attention as a melanoma-specific marker since its first report in 1997 [34]. Multiple studies have demonstrated high PRAME detection rates in mucosal melanoma and SNMM, contrasting with its weak or absent expression in benign nevi [35]. Consequently, PRAME has been proposed as a useful differential diagnostic marker for malignant melanoma. According to Scheurleer et al. [36], PRAME expression was identified not only in SNMM but also in surrounding melanocytes that had not yet undergone malignant transformation. This observation suggests the concept of “field-melanomization,” analogous to field-cancerization in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [37].

However, a retrospective study by Cazzato et al. [38] highlighted diagnostic limitations of PRAME, as it can also be expressed in certain benign lesions despite its presence in various malignant melanoma subtypes. The authors particularly emphasized potential inaccuracies arising from the lack of consistent criteria for interpreting PRAME expression intensity and distribution.

Nevertheless, Blount et al. [39] underscored PRAME’s diagnostic value in distinguishing malignant from benign conditions in diagnostically challenging subtypes such as SNMM. Moreover, Toyama et al. [40] suggested that PRAME expression may correlate with poor prognosis, leading to growing interest in PRAME-targeted immunotherapy as a potential new treatment strategy.

Unlike cutaneous melanomas, which are associated with UV radiation exposure, risk factors for SNMM remain poorly defined [41]. These etiologic differences are reflected in their molecular characteristics. Compared with cutaneous melanoma, mucosal melanoma exhibits a distinct genetic profile. Although mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) pathway mutations are common in both, the mutational spectrum differs substantially. Cutaneous melanoma predominantly harbors the canonical V600E mutation, whereas mucosal melanoma more frequently shows noncanonical BRAF mutations such as G469A, resulting in weaker MAPK activation [42]. KIT mutations are more common in mucosal than in cutaneous melanoma, offering a potentially targetable therapeutic avenue for this aggressive subtype [43-45].

STAGING SYSTEM AND PROGNOSIS

For patients with suspected SNMM, a comprehensive evaluation is necessary to differentiate between primary and metastatic melanoma by identifying the primary site. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography and brain magnetic resonance imaging are useful for assessing distant metastasis and local invasion. Owing to its rarity and the limited number of cases, there is no staging system developed with robust statistical validation. However, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification for head and neck mucosal melanoma is currently the most widely used and recognized system.

Ballantyne’s staging system [46], established in the 1970s, classifies disease into three stages: stage I for localized disease, stage II for regional lymph node metastasis, and stage III for distant metastasis. However, this system inadequately reflects mucosal melanoma’s distinctive characteristics, such as high local recurrence rates and extremely poor prognosis, highlighting the need for a more nuanced classification. In response, Prasad et al. [47] proposed the Ballantyne/Prasad system in 2004, subdividing stage I into three categories based on tumor invasion depth. Despite this refinement, the system failed to gain wide acceptance due to inconsistent prognostic accuracy [48,49].

A pivotal change occurred with the AJCC 6th edition [50], which introduced TNM classification for carcinomas of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, although a specific SNMM staging system did not yet exist. From the AJCC 7th edition [51] in 2009 onward, mucosal melanoma was separated as an independent section, a framework maintained in the 8th edition. SNMM is included as a subcategory of mucosal melanoma, sharing its staging approach with other head and neck mucosal melanomas occurring in the oral cavity, larynx, and upper respiratory tract.

Reflecting the aggressive nature of mucosal melanoma, the AJCC system omits T1 and T2 categories; even localized disease confirmed histologically is classified as T3, thus beginning staging at stage III. T4 disease is further divided into T4a and T4b. T4b indicates invasion of the brain, dura, skull base, lower cranial nerves, carotid artery, prevertebral space, mediastinal structures, or masticator space, whereas T4a denotes invasion limited to deep soft tissues, cartilage, bone, or skin [52].

For N staging, the AJCC employs a dichotomous classification: N0 and N+. Unlike cutaneous melanoma, where nodal involvement carries strong prognostic significance, SNMM shows no clear survival differences based on nodal metastasis size or extent [53]. Accordingly, such distinctions are excluded from the current classification system [54].

The prognostic relevance of lymph node metastasis in SNMM remains uncertain. The reported incidence of nodal involvement at the time of diagnosis ranges from 3.5% to 20%, most commonly affecting levels I and II [55,56]. Some studies associate nodal metastasis at diagnosis with poorer prognosis, whereas others report no significant survival difference, making this an area of ongoing debate [53,55]. Consequently, the efficacy of elective neck dissection is considered limited. Therefore, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend performing neck dissection selectively, only in cases with confirmed lymph node metastasis [57]. Although some studies have shown benefits in reducing regional relapse rates, these findings have not translated into improved 5-year survival outcomes. Given the limited available evidence, treatment strategies for clinically node-negative (cN0) patients should be approached with careful consideration.

In SNMM, Breslow thickness—a key parameter in cutaneous melanoma staging—is not utilized. This criterion, which requires precise measurement of tumor depth, is difficult to apply consistently because biopsy or excision specimens are often fragmented due to the complex anatomy of the sinonasal region [52]. While depth of invasion is an established prognostic factor in squamous cell carcinoma of the oral cavity, and several studies have explored its prognostic relevance in oral cavity mucosal melanoma as an extension of this concept [58-60], no comparable studies have assessed its prognostic value in SNMM using post-surgical specimens. As a result, its clinical significance remains uncertain, leading to its exclusion from current mucosal melanoma staging systems [61].

Distant metastasis is a critical determinant of prognosis in SNMM. A 2018 retrospective study conducted at MD Anderson Cancer Center identified distant metastasis as the most common cause of treatment failure, occurring in 35% of patients. The study also demonstrated that distant metastasis and paranasal sinus origin were independent predictors of poor survival outcomes [62]. Subsequent investigations have consistently confirmed the profound impact of distant metastasis on prognosis in this patient population [24,63-66].

CURRENT TREATMENT STRATEGIES AND CLINICAL OUTCOMES

The general treatment principle for SNMM is primary surgical resection aimed at achieving negative margins. However, securing negative margins is often extremely challenging because of the complex sinonasal anatomy, the tumor’s multifocal and lentiginous growth patterns, and its irregular morphology [67,68]. In many cases, tumors are diagnosed only after significant invasion into surrounding soft tissues and organs, further complicating resection and contributing to poor clinical outcomes. Historically, open surgical approaches such as maxillectomy via lateral rhinotomy, Weber–Fergusson, or midfacial degloving incisions were commonly performed. However, with advances in endoscopic surgical techniques, endoscopic resection is increasingly being adopted.

Comparisons between endoscopic and open resections for sinonasal malignancies have been reported in multiple studies, with many indicating that endoscopic resection offers comparable or superior survival and local control outcomes relative to open resection [69-73]. Numerous studies focusing specifically on SNMM have also demonstrated favorable outcomes following endoscopic resection [74-85]. Endoscopic surgery generally provides additional advantages, including shorter hospitalization and reduced postoperative morbidity, suggesting that open resection should not be considered mandatory in all cases.

The role of adjuvant therapy after surgery remains controversial, including both postoperative radiotherapy and systemic treatment. The NCCN guidelines strongly recommend considering postoperative radiotherapy to the primary site in T3 cases without lymph node metastasis [57]. However, published results are inconsistent. Some studies report minimal benefit in local control specifically for SNMM [86], while others indicate improved local recurrence control in head and neck mucosal melanoma, including the sinonasal subtype. Despite these findings, overall outcomes remain poor, with 5-year local control rates below 55% [87-90]. Moreover, meta-analyses have shown inconclusive evidence regarding postoperative radiotherapy’s effects on distant metastasis or survival benefit [91]. Nevertheless, because several studies have demonstrated improvements in overall survival for SNMM, treatment planning should integrate these findings [85,92].

Data on proton beam radiation therapy for SNMM remain limited despite growing clinical interest. Takayesu et al. [93] reported excellent one-year overall survival and local control outcomes following proton therapy. Given the close proximity of SNMM to critical structures, proton therapy—which minimizes radiation damage to surrounding tissues compared with conventional photon therapy—may become an important alternative treatment if its efficacy is confirmed through large-scale clinical trials [94-96].

Definitive or palliative radiotherapy may be considered for patients with advanced or inoperable disease, yielding meaningful outcomes in local control and overall survival. Some patients have even achieved complete remission, demonstrating its potential as a viable treatment option. However, given the poor long-term prognosis due to distant metastasis, this approach should be reserved for carefully selected cases [97-99].

Regarding systemic therapy, the NCCN guidelines recommend adjuvant treatment for patients with advanced T stage or node-positive disease, specifically nivolumab immunotherapy and combination cisplatin/temozolomide chemotherapy [57].

Traditional chemotherapeutic agents such as dacarbazine (DTIC) have demonstrated limited efficacy in SNMM. Although DTIC remained the only FDA-approved systemic agent for melanoma for many years, response rates ranged only from 12.1% to 17.6% [100-103]. Consequently, it is now considered a last-line option, largely supplanted by immunotherapy. A 2013 randomized trial conducted in China demonstrated significant improvements in both recurrence-free and overall survival with cisplatin/temozolomide combination therapy, leading to its inclusion in the NCCN and American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines, where it remains a recommended regimen today [103,104].

Following the approval of ipilimumab, an anti–cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) antibody, in 2011 and its demonstrated efficacy in patients with advanced melanoma, subsequent approvals of anti–programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) monoclonal antibodies such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab have opened new horizons in melanoma treatment, particularly through research into combination therapies [105]. These agents represent immunotherapeutic approaches that target CTLA-4 and PD-1 receptors on T cells, thereby harnessing the body’s immune system to eliminate tumor cells. Immunotherapy has since become a cornerstone of treatment for advanced cutaneous melanoma [106-108]. The advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has further revolutionized melanoma management by enabling the identification of targetable genetic mutations. Molecular profiling now serves as a guide for selecting both targeted and immunotherapeutic treatments [109]. However, studies evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy specifically in SNMM remain limited. Although systematic reviews consistently report significant survival benefits associated with these agents, notable concerns persist. The therapeutic effect appears to vary depending on metastatic status, and non-responsive patient cohorts demonstrate no appreciable benefit. These findings underscore the critical need for identifying predictive biomarkers capable of forecasting immunotherapy responsiveness in this distinct melanoma subtype [110-114].

Notably, research by Thierauf et al. [115] demonstrated that programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression levels are lower in mucosal melanoma compared with cutaneous melanoma, suggesting that immunotherapy efficacy may differ according to melanoma subtype. This differential expression pattern may account for the variable treatment responses observed across melanoma classifications.

While targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy may be appropriate for melanomas harboring specific genetic alterations, mucosal melanoma exhibits distinct molecular characteristics. BRAF mutations, commonly seen in cutaneous melanoma, are detected in only 5.6%–8% of mucosal melanoma cases, and V600E mutations are particularly rare. Instead, mucosal melanoma demonstrates a higher prevalence of NRAS mutations, emphasizing the genetic divergence between mucosal and cutaneous melanoma subtypes [116,117].

Consequently, Amit et al. [116] proposed the potential utility of MEK inhibitors for SNMM patients with NRAS mutations, a strategy also supported by studies from Lund [118] and Riobello et al. [119]. Additional targeted therapeutic options, including KIT inhibitors [120-122], have been suggested, with some studies indicating enhanced efficacy when used in combination with immunotherapy [117,119]. This emerging field represents a promising frontier, necessitating further large-scale studies to establish the definitive clinical benefits of these combined therapeutic approaches.

CLINICAL CASE SERIES FROM A SINGLE INSTITUTION

In addition to the preceding literature review, we report our 10-year experience at Seoul National University Hospital in managing malignant mucosal melanoma, summarizing clinical patterns and treatment outcomes. Only patients who presented to the otolaryngology department and were diagnosed with SNMM were included. Those with insufficient follow-up (<1 year) or who received primary treatment at other institutions were excluded. In total, 10 patients with malignant mucosal melanoma were analyzed. The mean age at diagnosis was 62.9 years, comprising 4 males and 6 females. Most patients presented with stage T3N0M0 disease (n=6), followed by T4aN0M0 (n=3) and T3N0M1 (n=1).

Surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy was the most common treatment modality (n=7), followed by surgery alone (n=1), surgery with radiotherapy and pembrolizumab (n=1), and concurrent chemoradiotherapy (n=1). The mean follow-up duration was 29.45 months. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 1.

Patient 1 initially underwent surgical resection alone but developed local recurrence, requiring additional surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy. Although not shown in the table, this patient experienced subsequent recurrences at 27.7 months after salvage treatment—managed with further resection and pembrolizumab—and again at 7.1 months, necessitating another surgical intervention for local recurrence. The primary lesion was located at the inferior turbinate, and complete excision was initially believed to have been achieved. Given the favorable anatomical site and negative margins, adjuvant radiotherapy was deferred in favor of close surveillance. However, local recurrence developed, necessitating salvage surgery and postoperative radiotherapy. Despite multimodal treatment, repeated recurrences occurred, underscoring the aggressive behavior of SNMM even in anatomically favorable cases.

Patient 2 developed distant pulmonary metastases and was referred to hospice care for palliation. Patient 8 initially presented with regional neck metastases, but pulmonary metastases were discovered during surgical planning, resulting in hospice referral. Patient 9 received sequential treatments including pembrolizumab, the CVD regimen (cisplatin, vinblastine, dacarbazine), and the TC regimen (paclitaxel, carboplatin) for progressive disease before being transferred to hospice care, where death was presumed. Patient 10 developed distant bone metastases and received further treatment at a local medical center, where detailed salvage regimens were unavailable. Patient 5 received pembrolizumab in addition to radiotherapy upon patient request, but later developed regional neck and pulmonary metastases and is currently receiving palliative care. Among all cases, only Patient 7, who achieved clear surgical margins and received adjuvant radiotherapy, remained disease-free for more than five years. Conversely, Patient 2 experienced poor outcomes despite clear surgical margins and appropriate radiotherapy due to advanced disease stage at presentation. These findings emphasize that early diagnosis and complete surgical resection with negative margins are key prognostic factors; however, the anatomical complexity and nonspecific symptoms of SNMM often hinder early detection.

This case series raises a critical question: is adjuvant radiotherapy essential for all SNMM patients who achieve complete resection? Several patients in our cohort experienced recurrence despite undergoing both radiotherapy and immunotherapy following salvage surgery. Considering the significant morbidity and quality-of-life impairments associated with radiotherapy in the head and neck region, clinicians should carefully evaluate whether adjuvant therapy should be routinely administered, particularly in cases involving anatomically accessible primary sites amenable to reliable endoscopic surveillance. Because the survival and local control benefits of adjuvant therapy after surgical resection remain inconclusive, an individualized approach incorporating close monitoring may be appropriate.

CONCLUSION

SNMM is a rare but highly aggressive malignancy characterized by delayed diagnosis due to nonspecific symptoms and a generally poor prognosis. The cornerstone of management remains surgical resection, often combined with adjuvant radiotherapy, though achieving negative margins is difficult and the survival advantage of radiotherapy remains debated. While recent advances in systemic therapies such as immunotherapy and targeted treatments offer potential benefits, their efficacy in SNMM remains less certain than in cutaneous melanoma due to distinct biological differences. These challenges underscore the urgent need for continued research to develop more effective, evidence-based strategies for improving outcomes in this challenging disease.
