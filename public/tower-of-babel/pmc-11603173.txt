Metabolomics and transcriptomics profiling of three Paris species with varied polyphyllin compositions
Authors: Ping Xu, Jia Chen, Qi Mi, Wenchun Li, You Lu, Wenxiu Luo, Mengwen Yu, Xuan Zhang, Zhenhua Tu, Honggao Xu, Guowei Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Most of the species in Paris have important medicinal values in China. Polyphyllins are the key components in Paris, with varying levels and compositions among different species. This study investigated polyphyllin compositions of 206 Paris samples from 16 different producing areas. 9 Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis, 3 Paris fargesii, and 3 Paris forrestii were selected based on polyphyllin levels and composition for metabolome and transcriptome analysis. A total of 1,243 metabolites were identified among these samples, along with 92.11 Gb of full-length transcriptome data. This study provides potential candidate genes involved in polyphyllins synthesis in Paris, aiming to facilitate the biosynthesis of different polyphyllins.

Background & Summary

Species within the genus Paris (Melanthiaceae) have been utilized for their significant medicinal properties in China for over 2,000 years1. They are cultivated across more than 220,000 acres in Yunnan province, China, with an output value exceeding 6.2 billion RMB. The primary active ingredients in these species are polyphyllins, with polyphyllin I, II and VII identified as key components in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). These components, along with other polyphyllins have demonstrated important anti-tumor activities2,3. Specifically, polyphyllin I demonstrates notable anti-tumor effects against osteosarcoma4 and vasculogenic mimicry5; polyphyllin II shows anti-gastric tumor activity6; polyphyllin III has demonstrated efficacy against breast cancer7 and hepatocellular carcinoma8; polyphyllin VI can induce apoptosis in human osteosarcoma cells9; and polyphyllin VII can inhibit the growth of human cervical cancer cells10. Beyond their anti-tumor properties, polyphyllins also display other activities; for example, polyphyllin H has shown efficacy in treating inflammatory skin conditions11, and polyphyllin I and gracillin may be effective against monogenean parasites in aquaculture12. Due to their diverse pharmacological activities, polyphyllins have been utilized as raw materials in almost 100 Chinese patent medicines13.

The biosynthetic pathway of polyphyllins is intricate and involves a multitude of genes14. Putative genes involved in polyphyllins biosynthesis include 2,3-oxidized squalene cyclases (OSCs), sterol side chain reductase (SSR), C-4 sterol methyl oxidase (SMO), cyclopropylsterol isomerase (CPI), sterol C-14 demethylase (CYP51G), sterol C-5(6) desaturase 2 (C5-SD), sterol 8,7 isomerase (8,7 SI), 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase (7-DR), sterol C-14 reductase (C14-R), cytochrome P450s (CYPs), and UDP-glycosyltransferases (UGTs)15,16. OSCs play a crucial role as the key enzyme in catalyzing the formation of cycloartenol, with one PpCAS and one PpOSC transcript identified in P. polyphylla15,17. Extensive research has been conducted on the synthesis of cholesterol in Paris16. The transformation of cholesterol into diosgenin is catalyzed by CYPs, with four CYP genes reported in Paris15. The conversion of diosgenin into various polyphyllins is mediated by UGTs, with six UGT genes identified in Paris15,18–20. It is suspected that additional transcripts of OSC, CYP and UGT may also participate in the synthesis of polyphyllins in due to the large genome of Paris. Further investigation is required to fully elucidate the roles of these genes in the biosynthesis of polyphyllins.

The composition of different polyphyllins varies among different Paris species. Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis, a primitive medicinal plant mentioned in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition), contains high levels of polyphyllin I and II. Paris fargesii, a species included in the Sichuan Province Standard for Tibetan Medicine, is particularly rich in polyphyllin VI and H21. Paris forrestii, a commonly used folk medicine in Yunnan and Tibet1, is abundant in polyphyllin I and III. These Paris plants require at least 7 years of cultivation to meet medical standards22, and the biosynthesis of polyphyllins in Paris has garnered significant attention19. These three Paris species are valuable resources for studying genes related to polyphyllin biosynthesis. The integration of transcriptomics and metabolomics is a valuable approach for investigating specific metabolic pathways and their regulatory genes23,24. We hypothesized that the comparing the transcriptomic and metabolomic characteristics of Paris with varying polyphyllin compositions could offer insights in to the molecular mechanisms underlying the synthesis of specific polyphyllins. In this study, 206 samples of Paris were collected, and 9 Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis (PPY), 3 Paris fargesii (PFa), and 3 Paris forrestii (PFo) significant differences in polyphyllin contents were screened for metabolomics and transcriptomics analysis (Table 1). A total of 15 samples were used, including, resulting 211.53 Gb of clean data and 1,243 metabolites. These data represent a comprehensive, open and integrated data set of Paris samples, which should be helpful to understand the synthesis process of polyphyllins. Methods

Plant materials

A total of 206 fresh 7-year-old Paris rhizomes were collected from 16 different producing areas. This collection comprised 188 Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis, 5 Paris fargesii, and 13 Paris forrestii (Fig. 1, Table 2). After removing of fibrous roots, the rhizomes were immediately rinsed with running water and sliced. Standards and chemical reagents

Eight types of polyphyllins standards (polyphyllin I, polyphyllin II, polyphyllin III, polyphyllin V, polyphyllin VI, polyphyllin VII, gracillin, and polyphyllin H) were obtained from Chengdu Alfa Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Methanol and acetonitrile were purchased from Yunnan Luodebao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and all chemical reagents utilized were of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade.

Determination of polyphyllins

Polyphyllin levels were analyzed using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) as described by Zhao et al. with minor modifications25. Initially, 0.5 g of rhizomes powder was ultrasonically extracted with 5 mL methanol for 60 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22 μm pore size) and then analyzed by HPLC using an Agilent 1200 system equipped with an Agilent SB-C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm). By comparing the retention time and maximum UV absorbance of 8 polyphyllin standards with our samples, we accurately determined the levels of polyphyllin components based on UV absorption at 203 nm. The HPLC conditions included a mobile phase of water-acetonitrile (60:40), a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min, a sample volume of 10 μL, and a column temperature of 25 °C.

Preparation of samples for metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses

Variations in polyphyllin levels were analyzed in the rhizomes of 188 P. polyphylla var. yunnanensis, 5 P. fargesii, and 13 P. forrestii collected from 16 different producing areas (Fig. 1 and Table 2). Significant differences were observed in the levels of polyphyllins among the samples, with the total key components ranged from 0.6 to 20.19 mg/g and total saponins ranged from 1.84 to 33.05 mg/g (Table 1). Based on polyphyllin compositions and levels, three P. polyphylla (low, medium, and high polyphyllins) were selected and labeled as PP1, PP2 (5), PP3 (2-2), respectively. Among the analyzed polyphyllins, polyphyllin I, II, and III are diosgenin saponins, while polyphyllin VI, VII, and H belong to pennogenin saponins. P. polyphylla and P. forrestii are primarily composed of diosgenin saponins, whereas P. fargesii is predominantly composed of pennogenin saponins. The content of polyphyllin II in PP3 is 9.18 mg/g, which is significantly higher than that in P. forrestii and P. fargesii. The content of polyphyllin III in P. forrestii (16) is significantly higher at 8.89 mg/g than in P. polyphylla and P. fargesii. P. fargesii (12-2) exhibits significantly higher levels of polyphyllin VI, VII, and H compared to the other two species, with polyphyllin H reaching 21.06 mg/g (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Polyphyllin VI is recognized for its hemostatic properties26, while polyphyllin III shows anti-proliferative effects on certain cancers7. Variation in specific polyphyllin levels in P. fargesii and P. forrestii suggest their potential as alternative sources for specific saponin materials, optimizing the utilization of Paris resources. Metabolomic analyses and data processing

Samples were freeze-dried and ground to a powder. Subsequently, 50 mg of the powder was extracted with 1.2 mL of a 70% methanolic aqueous solution pre-cooled to −20 °C. The extraction process involved vortexing for 30 s every 30 min, repeated 6 times. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore size microporous membrane and then analyzed using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system equipped with an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (pure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The analysis utilized a gradient program starting with 95% A and 5% B. A linear gradient to 5% A and 95% B was applied over 9 minutes, and then the composition was kept for 1 minute. Subsequently, the composition was adjusted to 95% A and 5.0% B, within 1.1 minutes and maintained for 2.9 minutes. The flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min, the column oven temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 4 μL. The effluent was connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS. The ESI source temperature was 500 °C, ion spray voltage was + 5500 V or −4500 V, and ion source gas 1, gas2, and curtain gas were set at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively. QQQ scans were acquired using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium.

Plant transcriptomic sequencing and data processing

A total amount of 1 µg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® UltraTM RNALibrary Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. In order to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 250~300 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Then 3 µL USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was used with size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min followed by 5 min at 95 °C before PCR. Then PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers and Index (X) Primer. Atlast, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and library quality wasassessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumia) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Data analysis

Unsupervised Principal Component Analysis was performed using uses stats 3.5.1 function package of R version 3.5.1 for analysis. The results of the Hierarchical Cluster Analysis were obtained using the ComplexHeatmap 2.12.0 package in R version 4.2.0. One-way ANOVA analysis was conducted using SPSS 13.0, and significance was determined using Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD).

Data Records

The raw metabolomics data for rhizome tissues of different Paris plants can be accessed on the MetaboLights database under the accession numbers MTBLS1068227. The raw transcriptome data were submitted to the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC) Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) with full-length transcriptome data (Pacbio sequencing) accession number CRA01641328, and NGS Transcriptome data (Illumina sequencing) accession number CRA01635929.

Technical Validation

Technical validation of the two datasets was performed using established best practices specific for each data type. For widely targeted metabonomics, the preparation of a Quality Control (QC) sample through the blending of individual sample extracts serves as a pivotal tool for gauging the consistency of samples subjected to identical processing protocols. Throughout the analytical workflow, a QC sample is judiciously interspersed after every tenth sample, acting as a sentinel for monitoring the reproducibility of the analytical process. By overlapping and displaying the total ion current diagram (TIC diagram) of different QC samples, we can judge the repeatability of metabolite extraction and detection, that is, technical repetition. The high stability of the instrument provides an important guarantee for the repeatability and reliability of data. The metabolome variances among different Paris plants were evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA). The first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, explained 22.11% and 16.69% of the overall variance, respectively (Fig. 3a). Samples of P. polyphylla varieties (PP1, PP2, and PP3) clustered together, while samples from different Paris species (P. polyphylla, P. fargesii, and P. forrestii) showed clear separation, indicating substantial metabolic differences.
