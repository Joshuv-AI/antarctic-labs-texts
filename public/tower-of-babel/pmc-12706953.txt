Hierarchical therapeutic potential in the mesenchymal stem cell landscape
Authors: Jocelynn R Pearl, Annette Marleau, Dariel O Pacheco, Vijay Mahant, J Christopher Mizer, Patricia Juarez, Ali Asadi, Francesco M Marincola, Diego Guerena, Edward Clay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are heterogeneous and versatile cells comprising distinct subpopulations with varying regenerative potential. MSCs have been widely explored in clinical research owing to their regenerative, tissue trophic, immune modulatory, and anti-inflammatory properties. Tissue-sourced autologous and allogeneic MSCs including those isolated from bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord have been applied to clinical studies of a breadth of indications, providing ample evidence supporting the safety of MSC-based therapies. In some instances, clinical studies of MSCs have yielded variability in cell quality, potency, and therapeutic impact. Significantly, the Food and Drug Administration’s approval of the first MSC therapy, the allogeneic bone marrow MSC product Remestemcel-L-rknd (Ryoncil®), has fueled optimism for the clinical prospects of other investigational MSC products. The functional heterogeneity of MSCs has been demonstrated by studies of MSC subpopulations, often selected by surface marker expression or functional characteristics, that vary in clonogenicity, immunomodulatory potency, trophic factor production, and differentiation potential. While MSCs possess the ability to differentiate into mesodermal lineage cells, the potential for transdifferentiation of MSCs has been a matter of extensive scientific debate. There has been a strong impetus to identify MSC subpopulations that may possess enhanced self-renewal capabilities, higher clonogenicity, and greater plasticity. Certain MSC subpopulations, defined by specific surface markers or functional traits, have been reported to exhibit or enhanced therapeutic attributes compared to heterogeneous MSCs. Specific MSC subpopulations that have been examined include STRO-1 + cells, STRO-3-selected cells, CD146 + cells, CD271 + cells, aldehyde-dehydrogenase-expressing cells, side population (SP) cells, and stage-specific embryonic antigen-3 (SSEA-3) + cells. Due to the lack rigorous characterization of certain MSC subpopulations, their precise biological identities and the potential therapeutic advantages over heterogeneous MSCs remains to be fully defined. Here we review the preclinical and clinical advancements in the understanding of the therapeutic properties of MSCs and their subpopulations, assessing the evidence supporting their use in regenerative medicine.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cell, Regenerative medicine, Cell therapy, Multipotent, Immune modulation

Milestones in the therapeutic advancement of mesenchymal stem cells

The history of mesenchymal stem cell use in clinical trials reflects the evolving understanding of their therapeutic potential and biological complexity. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were first identified in the 1960s by Alexander Friedenstein, a Russian scientist, who observed unique cells in bone marrow that could differentiate into various cell types while maintaining the ability to self-renew [1]. Friedenstein’s experiments demonstrated that these cells, which he initially referred to as colony-forming unit-fibroblasts, could form bone, cartilage, and fat when cultured in vitro. He noted their spindle-shaped morphology and ability to adhere to plastic surfaces, distinguishing them from hematopoietic stem cells [2]. This discovery laid the foundation for MSC research, though the term “mesenchymal stem cell” wasn’t coined until the 1990s by Dr. Arnold Caplan [3], who described multipotent progenitor cells capable of multilineage differentiation into various mesodermal lineages. Caplan’s early work demonstrated that MSCs could be isolated from adult bone marrow and expanded ex vivo, laying the foundation for their therapeutic applications. Caplan’s research also highlighted the immunomodulatory and trophic properties of MSCs upon homing to sites of injury or disease [4], which led him to propose renaming MSCs as “medicinal signaling cells” [5].

Clinical trials using mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) date back to the 1990s and early 2000s and have included studies of MSCs for treatment of cardiovascular diseases, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), liver disease, Crohn’s disease, bone/cartilage disease, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, diabetes, and other indications [6]. Pioneering studies conducted by Dr. Darwin Prockop investigated the efficacy of allogeneic bone marrow-derived MSCs for treating osteogenesis imperfecta, a genetic disorder characterized by bone deformities, frequent fractures, and joint laxity. The data provided histologic evidence of improved bone density and reduced incidence of fractures in MSC-treated children [7]. Prockop and colleagues deduced that secretion of cytokines and other paracrine factors by MSCs underlies the therapeutic benefits of these cells [8]. Extensive investigations were conducted by numerous investigators to decipher the mechanisms of action underlying the reparative effects of MSCs. Specifically, studies focused on assessing whether the ability of MSCs to alter the tissue microenvironment occurs primarily via secretion of soluble factors or whether these cells exhibit plasticity for differentiation into different cell types in vivo. Dr. Donald Phinney and colleagues, who contributed significantly to early studies of MSC isolation and characterization [9–11], identified the existence of subpopulations of MSCs based on transcriptome analysis [12, 13] and intra-population and donor-to-donor functional heterogeneity of MSCs [14]. Phinney’s work supported a mechanistic link between the stem/progenitor functions of MSCs and their paracrine angiogenic, anti-inflammatory and immune modulatory actions through the transcription factor Twist1, which was shown to regulate cell growth, survival, multi-potency, and the secretome [15].

A major clinical breakthrough occurred in 2004 when LeBlanc and colleagues published a case study demonstrating successful treatment of severe, steroid-refractory GVHD in a patient using haploidentical mesenchymal stem cells [16]. This study highlighted the immunosuppressive activity of MSCs. In a subsequent Phase II study of patients with severe, steroid-refractory GVHD, MSC infusion induced complete responses in more than half of treated subjects with a favorable safety profile and improved survival [17]. These studies contributed to an accumulating body of evidence demonstrating the safety and feasibility of MSC therapy.

Key clinical trials also included studies of Prochymal® (Remestemcel-L), an allogeneic, culture-expanded MSC type from Osiris Therapeutics, for treating acute GVHD in pediatric patients unresponsive to steroids [18, 19]. The mechanism of action of MSCs involved immune modulation through downregulation of T cell activity, anti-inflammatory cytokine production, and paracrine support for tissue repair [20]. This MSC product was investigated in clinical studies for several other indications including acute myocardial infarction [21], Crohn’s disease [22], type 1 diabetes [23], and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [24, 25]. The Phase I/II POSEIDON (Percutaneous Stem Cell Injection Delivery Effects on Neomyogenesis) trial was a landmark study directly comparing transendocardial delivery of autologous or allogeneic bone marrow–derived MSCs in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy and moderate-to-severe left ventricular dysfunction [26]. The trial results showed that both autologous and allogeneic MSCs were safe, non-immunogenic, and exhibited similar efficacy in improving patient functional capacity. In the subsequent POSEIDON-DCM trial, a Phase I/II randomized study done in patients with non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, superior efficacy of allogeneic MSCs compared to autologous MSCs was demonstrated [27, 28]. These studies solidified the therapeutic prospects that exist for scalable “off-the-shelf” MSC products for a breadth of other indications.

Standardized criteria for defining MSCs were established in 2006 by the International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT), requiring the following: (a) plastic adherence; (b) positive surface marker expression for CD73, CD90, and CD105; (c) negative surface marker expression for markers of hematopoietic lineages including CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR; and (d) trilineage differentiation potential into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondroblasts in vitro [29]. The last decades have seen exponential growth in MSC research and applications, driven by their versatility and safety profile. Advances in understanding MSC heterogeneity revealed that their efficacy varies by tissue source, donor, and culture conditions, prompting efforts to optimize their therapeutic use. A significant achievement in MSC therapeutics came with the 2024 FDA approval of Mesoblast’s Ryoncil (remestemcel-L-rknd), an allogeneic bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cell therapy, for use in pediatric steroid-refractory acute GVHD [30]. The approval was based on the Phase III MSB-GVHD001 trial that demonstrated an overall response rate of 70% (95% CI: 56.4–82.0%) at day 28 and a complete response rate of 30% (95% CI: 18.0-43.6%). Approval of this therapy fills a treatment gap in the population of pediatric patients who have received allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplants, a group with high mortality rates and limited treatment options.

Table 1 summarizes representative clinical trials using autologous or allogeneic MSCs derived from various tissue sources for different indications, highlighting positive signals in conditions such as GVHD, autism, degenerative disc disease, osteoarthritis, and fracture repair. Despite these advancements, the field of MSC therapeutics continues to face challenges such as modest therapeutic effects, poorly defined dose-response relationships, and variable study outcomes. The tissue source of MSCs, administered dose, delivery route, characteristics of the subject population or subgroups, and other trial design elements, can substantially influence clinical responses. The mechanisms by which MSCs exert their effects are also expected to vary by disease state, ranging from trophic factor secretion, immune modulation, anti-inflammatory effects, and other effects that may be less studied. While many clinical studies to date have employed heterogeneous MSCs, investigations have also examined whether specific MSC subpopulations within the broader pool may be better suited for certain clinical indications. Further research is required to establish whether these distinct MSC subsets possess functional specializations that could translate into enhanced therapeutic efficacy in clinical settings.

Therapeutic versatility of mesenchymal stem cells

MSCs are broadly defined by a set of core characteristics that underpin their therapeutic potential. First, MSCs possess remarkable immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties by direct activity and by stimulation of T regulatory cells, which endow these cells with therapeutic potential for a plethora of chronic diseases [42, 43]. MSCs secrete bioactive molecules such as cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles that modulate immune responses [44]. Inflammatory cues in the tissue microenvironment can strongly influence the behavior of MSCs. In particular, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) activate signaling pathways including NF-κB and JAK/STAT, which reprogram MSCs and alter the spectrum of bioactive molecules produced by these cells. This response includes the upregulation of immune suppressive and immunomodulatory factors by MSCs including indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) [45–47]. The sensitivity of MSCs to external signals underscores the critical role of the microenvironment in shaping their immunomodulatory and regenerative functions, as well as the composition of the secretome.

MSCs can differentiate into key mesodermal lineages such as adipocytes, osteocytes, and chondrocytes. This inherent mesodermal versatility underpins their broad utility in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. For example, MSC-based therapies have shown promise in osteoarthritis, where intra-articular injections of MSCs promote cartilage regeneration and reduce pain, with phase II trials reporting improved joint function in patients [48]. Paracrine signaling by MSCs further enhances tissue repair by stimulating endogenous progenitor cells and angiogenesis, broadening their therapeutic scope beyond direct differentiation.

Additionally, MSCs are relatively easy to isolate and expand in vitro, addressing practical challenges in clinical translation. Unlike embryonic stem cells, MSCs can be harvested from easily accessible sources of donor material, such as adipose tissue or umbilical cord, with minimal ethical concerns. MSCs maintain their phenotype and functionality for multiple passages during expansion on tissue culture plastics, although the number of passages MSCs can undergo before functional decline and replicative senescence is finite and depends on factors such as the tissue source and culture conditions [49, 50]. Expanded allogeneic MSCs provide a scalable platform for off-the-shelf therapies, whereby master cell banks derived from a single donor can be expanded to produce doses for broad patient use, enabling rapid deployment in clinical settings.

Finally, MSCs have a favorable safety profile that is supported by clinical studies. Unlike pluripotent stem cells, MSCs exhibit low tumorigenic potential, owing to their restricted differentiation capacity and genomic stability in culture, which together minimize the risk of uncontrolled growth or malignant transformation. The relatively hypoimmunogenic properties of MSCs, characterized by low MHC class I expression and little or no MHC class II under resting conditions, limits recognition by host immune cells [51–54]. In addition, a range of immune regulatory molecules expressed by MSCs can suppress immune responses. While the immunogenicity of MSCs can increase under inflammatory conditions, these properties collectively contribute to a reduced likelihood of immune rejection, supporting their use as allogeneic therapies.

Functional stratification within mesenchymal stem cell compartments

MSCs represent a heterogeneous population, consisting of subgroups with distinct phenotypic and functional characteristics. Individual cells within an MSC culture can differ in surface marker and transcription factor expression, proliferative capacity, secretory activity, and differentiation potential, reflecting inherent biological diversity. This heterogeneity suggests that MSC cultures are composed of a mosaic of cells with varying regenerative and reparative abilities, which may impact their therapeutic effectiveness.

MSCs exhibit variable differentiation capacities that can be influenced by factors such as isolation methods, culture conditions, and donor source. While bulk or unselected MSCs are broadly recognized as multipotent and capable of differentiating into mesodermal lineages, in particular osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes, some studies have reported that specific MSC subpopulations may acquire features of other cell types derived from all three germ layers. These cell types include retinal progenitor cells [55], cardiomyocyte-like cells [56, 57], and hepatocyte-like cells [58, 59] that reportedly differentiate under defined inductive conditions. Such observations are suggestive of developmental plasticity in MSCs, although the extent to which these findings represent true transdifferentiation versus phenotypic adaptation remains an area of investigation. An important consideration is how closely the in vitro differentiation of an MSC subpopulation reflects its capacity to perform the functions of a target cell in vivo, especially given that MSCs may not express the full complement of structural and functional genes of the desired cell type. Within the heterogeneous MSC pool, there are subpopulations of cells having increased mesodermal differentiation potential, enhanced proliferative capacities, and stronger immune modulatory or immune suppressive abilities. The characterization of MSC subpopulations with distinct functional properties could, in the future, aid in identifying cells that may be best suited for tissue replacement strategies. For example, certain MSC populations may be particularly useful for tissue replacement roles, while other more differentiated cells within the MSC pool may be leveraged for supporting structural or trophic roles in tissue repair. The isolation of specific MSC subpopulations may also eliminate cells having the potential to interfere with the potency or effectiveness of a therapeutically administered cell dose.

Defining the functional landscape of mesenchymal stem cell subpopulations

The search for the most therapeutically versatile MSC population has reflected an ongoing effort to identify cells with superior regenerative or repair capacity, targeted homing, immune modulation, and safety, with the goal of maximizing clinical efficacy across a broad range of diseases and tissue repair contexts.

Surface-expressed or intracellular molecules have been identified as useful markers for sub fractionating MSCs with differing tendencies toward specific biological activities. Specific MSC subpopulations that have been examined include STRO-1 + cells, STRO-3-selected cells, CD146 + cells, CD271 + cells, aldehyde-dehydrogenase-expressing cells, side population (SP) cells, and stage-specific embryonic antigen-3 (SSEA-3) + cells. Table 2 outlines the reported characteristics of MSC subpopulations, such as their production of trophic factors and their clonogenicity (i.e., proliferative capacity measured by colony-forming assays), as compared to heterogeneous MSCs. For example, aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH+)-expressing MSCs may represent a more primitive subset among heterogeneous MSCs, having an enhanced ability to give rise to mesoderm lineage cells. Additionally, there has been an interest in determining whether certain MSC subpopulations exhibit enhanced plasticity, referring to the flexibility to give rise to multiple cell phenotypes or to undergo transdifferentiation in the presence of specific stimuli. MSCs have been characterized in terms of their “stemness”, which relates to a collection of properties including the ability to self-renew and to differentiate into multiple specialized cell types. It is generally assumed that MSC subpopulations maintain the core functions of heterogeneous MSCs; however, these properties have not been systematically evaluated across all these subpopulations. In some cases, specific MSC subpopulations have been shown to exhibit enhanced or enriched characteristics relative to the broader MSC population, as summarized in Table 2, suggesting potential functional heterogeneity within the MSC pool.

The therapeutic application of a well-defined MSC subpopulation has been viewed as a desirable strategy for providing enhanced or concentrated regenerative, repair, or trophic effects. Accordingly, this section reviews the functional heterogeneity of MSC subpopulations with respect to attributes such as colony formation, immune suppressive activity, and differentiation, as well as what is currently known about their interrelatedness in terms of phenotype and function. While attention is on MSC subpopulations and their functional potential, it should be noted that procedural variables including MSC isolation techniques, culture conditions, and other cell manipulations can intentionally or inadvertently modify the characteristics of MSCs.

STRO-1 + STRO-3-selected STRO-1 + mesenchymal precursor cells

The term “mesenchymal precursor cells” (MPCs) was coined to describe MSCs considered to have high tissue reparative capabilities, which are commonly selected using STRO-3 monoclonal antibody that recognizes a specific isoform of tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase on human bone marrow stromal precursor cells [60]. The STRO-3 monoclonal antibody isolates a subset of STRO-1bright cells that comprise multipotent colony-forming unit fibroblasts in the bone marrow and in other tissues and excludes CD34 + hematopoietic stem cells [60]. Among primary human bone marrow mononuclear cells fractionated into STRO-1 + and STRO-1-negative populations, the STRO-1 + fraction was demonstrated as being enriched for clonogenic progenitor cells [61].

This subset of MSCs exhibits high clonogenic capacity, robust proliferative potential, and multipotent differentiation ability [60, 62]. In terms of differentiation, STRO-1⁺ MSCs readily differentiate into the classical mesenchymal lineages consisting of osteoblasts, chondrocytes and adipocytes [63, 104]. In one study, the STRO-1 + cell fraction from human bone marrow was shown to form a mineralized matrix in colony-forming unit-fibroblast (CFU-F) cultures that were switched to osteogenic inductive conditions [104]. These cells also secrete a plethora of paracrine factors that elicit proangiogenic and immunomodulatory effects on target cells [62, 64]. Studies have shown that conditioned medium from STRO-1bright cells more effectively promotes cardiac cell proliferation, endothelial tube formation, and cell migration as compared to that from STRO-1dim cells [62]. This subset of MSCs exhibits high functional efficacy in tissue repair models such as cardiovascular trophic effects [62]. Intramyocardial injection of STRO-1 + MSCs induced vasculogenesis and cardiac recovery in an athymic rat model of acute myocardial infarction [65]. In a sheep model, allogeneic STRO-3-selected mesenchymal precursor cells were isolated from bone marrow and administered to the sheep at the time of myocardial infarction to explore the therapeutic effects of these cells [66]. The mesenchymal precursor cells attenuated infarct expansion and reduced left ventricular dilation and dysfunction. This effect was associated with left ventricular cellular and extracellular remodeling that was evident by altered dynamics of collagen turnover post-infarction.

Heterogeneity exists in cell populations sorted based on STRO-1 expression and its expression on MSCs is not stable during culture [105, 106]. Given the limitations of using STRO-1 as a marker for selective isolation of MSC from tissues, STRO-3-based selection, representing a subset of STRO-1 + cells, has been considered the desirable strategy for providing a consistent, stable progenitor population for therapeutic products. STRO-3-selected allogeneic MPCs immunoselected from adult bone marrow (Rexlemestrocel-L developed by Mesoblast Limited) were the subject of the Phase III DREAM-HF trial for the treatment of subjects with chronic heart failure. While this clinical trial failed to meet its primary endpoint of reducing recurrent hospitalizations or urgent heart failure events, there were notable reductions in nonfatal myocardial infarctions and stroke, modest improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction, and subgroup effects in patients with systemic inflammation marked by high C-reactive protein levels [107, 108]. This study provided pivotal proof-of-concept signals for the therapeutic applicability of MSCs, particularly in inflammatory settings [109].

CD146 + mesenchymal stem cells

CD146 (or melanoma-associated cell adhesion molecule; MCAM) is a membrane glycoprotein expressed on a subpopulation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) found in many tissues including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and dental pulp. CD146 + MSCs are perivascular in origin, residing in the vicinity of capillaries and microvessels [67].

CD146 + MSCs exhibit multilineage differentiation potential, having the ability to differentiate into adipocytes, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and myocyte-like cells when cultured under appropriate conditions [67]. For example, CD146 + CD34 − CD45 − CD56 − cells isolated from placental villi were shown to be highly myogenic, generating dystrophin-positive myofibrils in culture, and promoted angiogenesis after transplantation in an animal model [68]. Another study evaluating fetal and adult human CD146 + MSCs isolated from human myocardium, which were termed “perivascular mesenchymal precursor cells”, revealed their expression of typical MSC markers as well as neuron/glia antigen 2 (NG2), platelet-derived growth factor receptors, alpha smooth muscle actin, and smooth muscle myosin heavy chain [69]. These cells were demonstrated to possess angiogenic and cardiomyogenic in vitro and upon intramyocardial transplantation in vivo.

A notable study identified CD146 + MSCs in human bone marrow as a population comprising stromal progenitors and skeletal progenitors, which give rise to hematopoiesis-supporting stroma and bone, respectively, thereby establishing the hematopoietic microenvironment in vivo [70]. CD146 + cells were reported as being integral to angiogenic events through production of factors including angiopoietin-1 for establishing the bone marrow sinusoids prior to hematopoiesis. CD146 + cells functioned as self-renewing, clonogenic progenitors that established heterotopic ossicles, including bone, sinusoids, adipocytes, and CD146 + adventitial reticular cells upon transplantation. Cultured CD146 + MSCs also reportedly support hematopoietic stem cells through cell to cell contact and activation of Notch signaling [71]. The regenerative and supportive capabilities of CD146 + cells make them promising therapeutic candidates for musculoskeletal and cardiovascular repair.

CD271 + mesenchymal stem cells

CD271 (p75 neurotrophin receptor or low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor) is expressed by MSCs isolated from various tissues and is considered a marker of primitive MSCs with higher colony forming ability and greater trilineage differentiation potential than unselected MSCs [72–74]. Studies have demonstrated superiority of CD271 + MSCs for chondrogenesis as compared to plastic-adherent heterogeneous MSCs, suggesting their particular suitability for cartilage repair applications [75, 76]. CD271 + MSCs were found to be less angiogenic in vitro and in vivo in terms of their production of angiogenic factors [76]. Additionally, CD271 + MSCs have improved immune regulatory functions as compared to unselected MSCs [77]. This subpopulation also promoted enhanced engraftment of CD133 + hematopoietic stem cells when co-transplanted into immune deficient mice as compared to unselected MSCs [77]. Based on the immunosuppressive and properties of CD271 + MSCs as well as their engraftment-promoting properties, CD271 + cells are potentially valuable for improving delayed T cell recovery and graft-versus-host disease following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

CD271 + MSCs exhibit phenotypic and functional overlap with other subpopulations such as MSCs expressing CD106 (Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule-1) that have also been studied separately, although the degree of overlap has been incompletely characterized. CD271 + CD106 + MSCs also exhibit higher clonogenic capacity, self-renewal, and trilineage differentiation as compared to CD271 + CD106- MSCs and total CD271 + MSCs, indicating the existence of subpopulations within the CD271 + pool [78]. As is the case for MSCs selected for CD271 expression, potent immune regulatory activity has been identified in the CD106-selected subpopulation [110]. While CD271 is a suitable marker for isolating MSC subpopulations from bone marrow or adipose tissue, this molecule is minimally expressed or absent in certain other tissues such as umbilical cord [111].

Side population of mesenchymal stem cells

The side population (SP) refers to a minor subset of MSCs found in multiple tissues, distinguished by their capacity to actively efflux fluorescent DNA-binding dyes such as Hoechst 33,342, a feature that is commonly associated with primitive cells [112]. When analyzed by flow cytometry, SP cells form a distinct low fluorescence “side” cluster, separated from the main population of MSCs that retain the dye. This dye efflux property of SP cells is mediated primarily by expression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters associated with the ABCB1, ABCC1, and ABCG2 genes. While characterizing SP cells in umbilical cord blood, Storms et al. reported heterogeneity within this population, marked by the presence of CD34 + and CD34- populations, corresponding to hematopoietic and mesenchymal cell populations, respectively [113]. Similarly, Andersen et al. identified cells that were CD45 + and CD45- within the SP cell pool from freshly isolated adipose stem cells from the stromal vascular fraction [114]. These findings indicate that isolating SP MSCs solely based on dye efflux is insufficient for effectively distinguishing them from hematopoietic cells.

SP cells of mesenchymal origin have been the subject of numerous preclinical assessments using various tissue sources. In one study, SP cells were isolated from the mesenchymal layer of human amniotic membrane based by staining the nuclei with Hoechst 33,342 dye and sorting the population having low Hoechst fluorescence [79]. When these cells were expanded in culture and subjected to the appropriate differentiation conditions, multilineage differentiation to neuroectodermal, osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic cells was induced [79]. For generating neuroectodermal cells, SP cells were first cultured in basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) for 10–12 h as a preinduction step. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in a neurogenic induction medium consisting of butylated hydroxyanisole, forskolin, dimethyl sulfoxide, heparin, valproic acid, bFGF, and platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB). Differentiation of the SP cells was detected based on the neural cell markers Musashi-1, neuron-specific class III beta-tubulin (Tuj-1), neurofilament medium chain (NF-M) and microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2).

SP cells isolated from human dental pulp, exhibiting MSC characteristics, showed enhanced colony forming capacity compared to heterogeneous MSCs and were capable of differentiating into cells with features of odontoblasts, osteoblasts, adipocytes, neurons, and endothelial cells under the appropriate culture conditions [80]. Compared to heterogeneous MSCs, SP cells expressed higher levels of markers related to stemness, including the Oct4 gene, which is associated with pluripotency, as well as the BMI1 and Stat3 genes that are associated with self-renewal, proliferation, and survival. Similarly, an analysis of canine SP cells from dental pulp, bone marrow, and adipose tissue revealed that cells from all three tissues exhibited high clonogenicity and expressed CD29, CD44, CD73, and CD90. SP cells from all three tissues expressed the stem cell-related markers Sox2, telomerase reverse transcriptase (Tert), BMI1, and Oct4, as well as C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (Stat3) [81].

The functions of mesenchymal SP cells have been studied in lung tissue, where these cells influence the development of both the epithelium and vasculature. In a murine study, mesenchymal SP cells from adult lung included CD45-/CD31 + cells that did not express vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), which may serve as progenitors of endothelial and smooth muscle, as well as CD45-/CD31+/VEGFR2 + cells, thought to represent more committed endothelial progenitors [82]. There is also evidence that mesenchymal SP cells are involved in wound healing and fibrosis-mediated remodeling of tissue. Ramos et al. studied the wound-healing potential of SP cells isolated from adipose tissue by intradermally injecting them into NOD/SCID mice that had undergone full-thickness skin incisions [83]. They observed complete wound closure and regeneration of the dermis and epidermis layers within 7 days, with minimal scarring, compared to control mice treated with a phosphate buffered saline vehicle. In a study of bleomycin-mediated lung injury in mice, ABCG2-positive SP cells in the lungs differentiated into myofibroblasts that contributed to detrimental, fibrotic tissue remodeling [84]. This study demonstrated that ABCG2-positive MSCs are influenced to differentiate into myofibroblasts by a TGF-β1-enriched microenvironment. These findings illustrate that the microenvironment can steer MSCs toward functions that are either beneficial for tissue stability or deleterious.

Aldehyde dehydrogenase-positive mesenchymal stem cells

Another marker that has been leveraged to characterize a subpopulation of mesenchymal stem cells is the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) which metabolizes various toxic aldehydes by oxidizing them into non-toxic carboxylic acids, protecting stem cells from oxidative stress and aldehyde-induced damage [85]. ALDH-expressing MSCs represent a metabolically distinct subpopulation that are identified based on high activity of the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase, typically detected using the ALDEFLUOR assay. This assay measures the conversion of a fluorescent substrate (BODIPY-aminoacetaldehyde) into a fluorescent product retained in cells with high ALDH activity [86]. ALDH-high MSCs demonstrate high regenerative capacity across multiple tissue types, which is attributable to their enhanced stemness and differentiation potential. Bone marrow-derived MSCs sorted for high ALDH activity (ALDH-high) exhibited greater multilineage differentiation into osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic cell lineages compared to ALDH-low MSCs. ALDH-high MSCs also showed increased proliferation and colony-forming unit (CFU) efficiency, possibly indicative of a more primitive phenotype [94]. Similarly, ALDH-high MSCs from adipose tissue displayed upregulated genes associated with stemness (e.g., Sox2, Nanog) and multilineage differentiation, outperforming ALDH-low MSCs in tissue repair assays [87].

In studies of vascular regeneration, ALDH-high MSCs from bone marrow were shown to enhance limb revascularization in ischemic models. When transplanted into mice with hindlimb ischemia, ALDH-high MSCs significantly improved blood flow recovery and capillary density compared to ALDH-low or unsorted MSCs, due to their secretion of pro-angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and their differentiation into endothelial-like cells [88]. Another study demonstrated that umbilical cord blood-derived ALDH-high MSCs promoted myocardial repair in a rat model of myocardial infarction, with greater reduction in infarct size and improved cardiac function compared to control MSCs, highlighting their superior regenerative potency [89].

A major therapeutic advantage of ALDH-high MSCs is believed to be their heightened resistance to oxidative stress, a critical factor in hostile microenvironments like ischemic or inflamed tissues. ALDH enzymes detoxify reactive aldehydes (e.g., 4-hydroxynonenal) generated during oxidative stress, protecting cells from apoptosis and DNA damage [90]. A study of decidua basalis MSCs with high ALDH activity demonstrated that these cells were more resistant to hydrogen peroxide-induced toxicity than chorionic villous MSCs with lower ALDH expression [91].

ALDH-high MSCs are also reported to possess enhanced immunomodulatory properties relative to ALDH-low MSCs, an important attribute for therapeutic applications targeting inflammatory and autoimmune conditions. In a study of foreskin-derived MSCs, ALDH-high subsets expressed higher levels of immunomodulatory genes (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and suppressed T-cell proliferation more effectively than ALDH-low MSCs in co-culture assays [95]. These findings are linked to the role of ALDH in retinoic acid synthesis, which modulates immune responses via regulatory T-cell induction.

Angiogenesis is crucial for tissue repair, and ALDH-high MSCs excel in promoting vascularization compared to control MSCs. In a study of human bone marrow-derived MSCs, ALDH-high MSCs co-cultured with human microvascular endothelial cells induced greater tube formation and expressed higher levels of pro-angiogenic factors (i.e., VEGF, platelet-derived growth factor-alpha, and angiogenin) and produced molecules with chemoattractant functions (e.g., TGF-β, C-X-C motif chemokine ligands 1 and 2) [93]. In vivo, ALDH-high MSCs enhanced neovascularization in a Matrigel plug assay, with significantly more functional blood vessels than those formed by ALDH-low MSCs [93]. When ALDH-high MSCs purified from human bone marrow were administered to NOD/SCID mice rendered diabetic by streptozotocin treatment, the cells increased islet cell proliferation and vascularization with a concomitant improvement in insulin secretion by the islets [92]. These findings underscore the potential utility of ALDH-high MSCs for therapies aimed at promoting vascular growth and regeneration.

Multilineage-differentiating stress-enduring (Muse) cells

Muse cells were first described by Dr. Mari Dezawa and her research group in 2010 as a unique population of stem cells found among MSCs that self-renew, form clusters in suspension culture, express pluripotency markers, and differentiate into all three germ layers in vitro and in vivo [96]. Muse cells were identified by their expression of the stem cell marker stage-specific embryonic antigen-3 (SSEA-3) as well as the mesenchymal markers CD90 and CD105 [97–99]. Muse cells were also found to express the other markers that are associated with pluripotency, namely Nanog, Oct3/4, PAR-4, and Sox2, and alkaline phosphatase [97]. In the initial report characterizing this cell type, Muse cells were isolated from adult human skin fibroblasts or bone marrow [97], although they were later isolated from other sites including adipose tissue [99]. In adherent cultures, Muse cells were reportedly indistinguishable from other MSCs, however in suspension cultures, they formed clusters (referred to as Muse-cell-derived cell clusters or “M-clusters”), exhibited self-renewal, and expressed markers that are typically associated with pluripotent stem cells [97].

Muse cells have been described as unique among MSCs in their ability to differentiate into cell types of all three germ layers. In one published study, adipose-derived Muse cells reportedly grew as spheres in suspension, expressed pluripotent markers and differentiated into mesenchymal (82%), endodermal (75%), and ectodermal (78%) lineages under induction conditions [115]. Reported methods for culturing Muse cells involved allowing the cells to form “M-clusters” in single cell suspensions, which were then be transferred onto gelatin-coated dishes and induced to differentiate into cells that expressed α-fetoprotein (endodermal), cytokeratin 7 (endodermal), α-smooth muscle actin (mesodermal), desmin (mesodermal), and neurofilament-M (ectodermal) [97]. When Muse cells were transplanted into immunodeficient mice, the transplanted cells reportedly engrafted into damaged skin, muscle, or liver and differentiated into cytokeratin 14-, dystrophin-, or albumin-positive cells in the respective tissues [97]. In contrast to embryonic stem cells, Muse cells have been characterized as having lower proliferation rates, whereby proliferation ceases in suspension cultures when the cell clusters reach a certain size [97]. Studies demonstrated that Muse cells do not form teratomas when transplanted into immune deficient mice in vivo [97–99]. Muse cells were also demonstrated to have low to negligible telomerase activity [99], which contrasts with the high telomerase activity observed in embryonic stem cells. Muse cells harvested from rabbits and studied for reparative functions in infarcted myocardial tissue in vivo were found to express HLA-A, -B, and -C and lacked HLA-DR, but displayed HLA-G, a molecule that is typically found in immune-privileged organs such as the placenta during pregnancy [100]. Muse cells reportedly also differ from other MSC subpopulations in terms of their high stress tolerance, having the ability to survive oxidative and genotoxic stress due to enhanced DNA damage repair pathways, including non-homologous end joining, and reactive oxygen scavenging [102, 103].

Methods for isolating and culturing Muse cells from different tissue sources have been described in the literature [116]. Muse cells have been isolated from various mesenchymal tissues, constituting a very small fraction of total MSCs: ~1–3% of tissue-derived MSCs and 0.03% of bone marrow mononuclear cells [97]. Several methods for isolating Muse cells that leverage their stress tolerance and SSEA-3 expression have been described that utilize fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS), or stress-based enrichment methods. For example, bone marrow aspirates were processed to isolate MSCs or mononuclear cells, and Muse cells were subsequently enriched as SSEA-3+/CD105 + cells via FACS or magnetic sorting techniques [98, 117]. Another published method involves long-term trypsin incubation under stress conditions to select stress-tolerant Muse cells [116]. Muse cells isolated using these methods reportedly form embryoid body-like clusters in methylcellulose suspension cultures [117]. In other published methods, lipoaspirates were digested with collagenase, MSCs were cultured, and Muse cells were isolated as SSEA-3+/CD90+/CD105 + cells using FACS or stress-based methods involving hypoxia, serum deprivation, and low temperatures [99, 115].

The research group that originally described Muse cells published data from several preclinical studies suggesting the therapeutic uniqueness of Muse cells. In models of middle cerebral artery occlusion and intracerebral hemorrhage, Muse cells that were injected intravenously or intranasally 2–7 days post-injury engrafted in peri-infarct cortex, with 62–65% of the cells differentiating into neurons and 12–26% into oligodendrocytes, which was associated with improvements in motor function in treated animals [118, 119]. In a study of perinatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, Muse cells or non-Muse MSCs were injected intravenously into rats 72 h post-hypoxia [120]. The study reported that xenogeneic Muse cells expressing neuronal/glial markers engrafted in the per-infarct area of the brain without the need for immunosuppression, which was associated with improved motor/cognitive functions up to 5 months. Reports of the effects of Muse cells showed dampened production of glutamate metabolites that typically cause neuronal toxicity, reduced microglial activation in the brain, and reduced levels of pro-inflammatory TNF-α and inducible nitric oxide synthase. In a model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a fatal neurodegenerative disease marked by progressive motor neuron loss, the potential of Muse cells to support motor neuron survival and reduce neurogenic myofiber atrophy in the spinal cord was evaluated. In G93A-transgenic ALS mice, human Muse cells were labeled and injected intravenously, and homing to the spinal cord was tracked [121]. Multiple Muse cell doses reportedly improved clinical scores in the rotarod test, hanging-wire test, and muscle strength of the lower limbs. Muse cells homed to the lumbar spinal cord, lung, and bone. In the lumbar spinal cord, Muse cells reportedly expressed the astroglial marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and assumed a glia-like morphology. In another study involving a rabbit model of acute myocardial infarction, ischemia was induced by occluding the coronary artery, followed by reperfusion, and administration of autologous, allogeneic, or xenogeneic (human) Muse cells into the ear vein 24 h later [100]. Labeled Muse cells were reported to home to the postinfarct heart; approximately 14% of the injected Muse cells were detected at this site. This study reported that engrafted Muse cells expressed cardiac markers, atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), cardiac troponin-I, sarcomeric α-actinin, and connexin-43, without fusing with host cardiomyocytes. In yet another study involving an aortic aneurysm model, Muse cells were reported to reduce aneurysm size by 45–62% compared to controls, with approximately 60% of the Muse cells differentiating into vascular smooth muscle cells and 20% into endothelial cells [122].

At present, the understanding of Muse cells remains incomplete, particularly with respect to the contributions of various possible mechanisms of action such as anti-inflammatory effects, immune modulatory behavior, and trophic factor production, to their putative therapeutic effects. Further examination of the Muse cell secretome may provide valuable insights into these functions. In a study comparing conditioned media from bone-marrow-derived MSCs, adipocyte-derived MSCs, and Muse cells, analysis by ELISA and mass spectrometry identified pathways enriched in Muse cells that are associated with stemness, survival under stress conditions, and immunomodulation [103]. Notably, pathways that were identified as potentially contributing to these Muse cell characteristics involve proteins belonging to Protein kinase A signaling, farnesoid X receptor (FXR)/retinoid X receptor (RXR) activation, and liver X receptor (LXR)/RXR activation.

Clinical translation of muse cells

Several small-scale clinical studies of Muse cells including Phase 1/2 and Phase 2 studies have been published by the group led by Dr. Mari Dezawa who originally described this cell population, and their colleagues. These studies and outcomes for safety and efficacy endpoints are summarized in Table 3. These studies explored the impact of CL2020, a clinical-grade, frozen allogeneic Muse cell product (manufactured by Life Science Institute, Tokyo, Japan). The cell doses reported in clinical trials were ~ 1.5 × 106 and 1.5 × 107 CL2020 cells/dose using single or multiple intravenous infusions. Most clinical studies of Muse cells published to date have been performed in small cohorts of 3–10 subjects, apart from a larger 35 patient study. Across the published studies thus far, treatment with CL2020 was reported to be safe without serious adverse events related to the product. There were no reports of treatment-related tumor formation reported and no requirement for immunosuppression in CL2020-treated subjects.

In terms of clinical efficacy of Muse cells, the trials performed to date have provided preliminary indications of potential therapeutic benefit, however, robust and conclusive evidence for any specific clinical application is lacking. Large carefully controlled and well-powered trials with longer follow-up will be needed to establish safety and efficacy. Given the broad spectrum of conditions potentially treatable with MSC-based therapies, it remains unclear which patient populations would derive the greatest benefit from Muse cell administration. The mechanisms by which Muse cells engraft, differentiate, and integrate into damaged tissues, and how long they persist, remain incompletely understood, but elucidating these processes will be crucial for guiding safe and effective clinical applications. Once these fundamental questions are addressed, it will also be essential to define variables including the optimal cell dose, timing of administration, and most suitable routes of administration on an indication-specific basis.

Additionally, while numerous preclinical and early-stage clinical studies of Muse cells have been reported, these findings have not been widely replicated by independent groups. Critical aspects such as their isolation, culture conditions, and mechanisms of action have largely remained confined to the original investigative group and their collaborators. The reproducibility of Muse cell isolation, culture methods, and scalability in independent laboratories remains to be demonstrated before their distinct biological and therapeutic identity can be substantiated.

An area requiring effort involves clarifying the mechanisms of action of Muse cells across different disease contexts. Muse cells are capable of migration to sites of injury, secretion of paracrine factors, modulation of immune responses, anti-inflammatory effects, and lineage differentiation [96]. However, the relative contributions of these mechanisms likely differ between indications, therefore, systematic translational studies are needed to establish indication-specific mechanisms of action for the therapeutic activity of Muse cells. The potential of Muse cells to outperform other MSC subpopulations or heterogeneous MSCs remains unproven and hinges on their precise mechanisms of action in specific therapeutic context. There is also a need for development of robust, quantitative potency assays that capture biologically relevant mechanisms of action that are linked to the intended clinical effect of the therapeutic cells. Advancing these priorities will provide a strategic framework for furthering the clinical translation of Muse cell-based therapies.

Conclusions

The wealth of preclinical data pertaining to MSCs and the ongoing improvements in the understanding of their mechanisms of action has led to advancements their therapeutic application. However, there remains a need for clinical validation and translation of much of the information that has been gained. Moreover, improvements in cell culture techniques and ex vivo cell manipulation offer an opportunity to optimize the intrinsic abilities of these MSC subpopulations by customizing growth conditions, preconditioning protocols, or genetic and epigenetic modifications to their specific biological traits. Such approaches should account for the degree of cellular primitiveness or stemness inherent to the MSC population, particularly when differentiation-driven tissue repair is desired. By refining the MSC culture and manipulation methods, regenerative medicine could shift toward precision-engineered MSC-based therapies with maximized therapeutic efficacy.

There has been interest in identifying MSC subpopulations with specialized or enhanced regenerative or repair functions. Subsets identified by expression of surface markers such as STRO-1, CD146, CD271, ALDH, and SSEA-3, as well as SP cells and STRO-3-selected MSCs, have shown potentially superior therapeutic properties compared to heterogeneous MSCs, but their precise biological identities and definitive advantages remain incompletely characterized. Substantial nonclinical and clinical investigation is still needed to determine whether any MSC subpopulation can be advanced to provide meaningful advantages over others.
