Carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli infections in Japanese patients with cancer
Authors: Naoya Itoh, Takanori Kawabata, Nana Akazawa, Kayoko Hayakawa, Masahiro Suzuki, Aki Sakurai, Kohei Uemura, Yasufumi Matsumura, Ryota Hase, Hideaki Kato, Takehiro Hashimoto, Takashi Matono, David van Duin, Norio Ohmagari, Yohei Doi, Sho Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbapenem-resistant bacteria represent a significant challenge for patients with cancer; however, the characteristics and prognoses of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (CRGNB) infections in Japanese patients with cancer remain unclear. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the features and outcomes of CRGNB infections in this population. This multicenter prospective observational cohort study prospectively enrolled 167 patients with CRGNB infections, with or without cancer from April 2019 to March 2022. The 30-day mortality rate was numerically higher, although not significantly (18.2% vs. 14.0%, p = 0.45), in the cancer group than in the non-cancer group. The average length of hospital stay was similar (44.6 days vs. 51.0 days, p = 0.55). Similarly, the incidence of the composite outcome—defined as the 30-day mortality or events associated with worsening clinical course—was also not significantly different (56.1% vs. 43.6%, p = 0.12). Propensity score analysis using inverse probability weighting showed no significant difference in the 30-day mortality and average length of hospital stay (p = 0.25 and 0.66). However, the incidence of the composite outcome was significantly higher in the cancer group (odds ratio, 2.36; p = 0.02). Patients with cancer and CRGNB infection experienced worse composite outcomes than those without cancer, highlighting the need for preventive measures for CRGNB infections in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54591-0.

Introduction

Infectious diseases caused by drug-resistant organisms have become a significant public health concern worldwide, with carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria posing a particular challenge. In 2024, the World Health Organization designated carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii as the highest priority target organisms for immediate antimicrobial research and development1. Carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa has also been designated as a priority pathogen immediately following these organisms1. Stenotrophomonas maltophilia is commonly identified in clinical settings as a carbapenem-resistant pathogen2. These carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (CRGNB) frequently exhibit multidrug resistance, resulting in infections with limited treatment options. The mortality rate associated with CRGNB infections is higher than that associated with infections caused by carbapenem-susceptible organisms3,4.

Patients undergoing treatment for cancer and those who have undergone hematopoietic stem cell transplantation frequently develop bacterial infections, requiring multiple rounds of antimicrobial therapy throughout the progression and treatment of their underlying disease5. In addition to high healthcare utilization, patients with cancer have unique and profound immunologic vulnerabilities—including chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, mucosal barrier injury associated with mucositis, and treatment-related impairment of cellular and humoral immunity—that fundamentally increase their susceptibility to severe infections5. Excessive use of antimicrobial agents exerts substantial selective pressure not only on a patient’s normal flora but also on the surrounding environment, potentially contributing to the emergence of resistant organisms. Carbapenems are commonly prescribed to treat infections in patients with malignancies. Patients with cancer are at high risk of developing CRGNB infections due to longer hospital stays and frequent exposure to broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy6–8. Furthermore, CRGNB infections in immunocompromised patient populations, such as patients with cancer, have been associated with high 30-day all-cause mortality rates ranging from 52% to 67%7,9. Thus, CRGNB infections are a serious threat to patients with cancer.

The epidemiology of drug-resistant organisms varies significantly worldwide10, and to the best of our knowledge, no study has reported on the specific characteristics and prognoses of CRGNB infections in patients with cancer in Japan. Carbapenems are frequently prescribed as empirical therapy to patients with cancer in Japan5, which may further drive selective pressure and influence patient outcomes. Moreover, unlike in Europe and the United States, where CRE are more prevalent, Japanese data are distinguished by a lower overall prevalence of CRE but a predominance of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE)-harboring imipenemase (IMP)-type carbapenemases11. Therefore, this multicenter study conducted at tertiary hospitals in Japan aimed to elucidate the characteristics and prognosis of CRGNB infection in patients with cancer.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We prospectively enrolled hospitalized patients, either with or without cancer, from whom CRGNB was cultured from any clinical specimen from April 1, 2019, to March 31, 2022, in the MultiDrug Resistant organisms clinical research network (MDRnet), consisting of eight tertiary care facilities in Japan. Participant selection was nonrandomized. All CRGNB cases reported at the participating hospitals were screened, and all unique hospitalized patients with confirmed CRGNB infections were included. Isolates were centrally retested using the disk diffusion method and whole-genome sequencing. Only cases with confirmed carbapenem-resistant or carbapenemase gene-positive isolates were included. Cases of colonization (non-infection) and those involving outpatients were excluded from the study cohort. All eligible hospitalized patients were prospectively and consecutively enrolled, and no patients meeting the inclusion criteria were excluded, ensuring complete case capture. A total of 167 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Determination of infection and colonization

Infection and colonization were determined by the site investigators at each participating institution using predefined definitions (see Supplementary Appendix S1). Infection was defined as CRGNB isolated from the bloodstream or a usually sterile site. Predefined diagnostic definitions were applied when the CRGNB was identified in respiratory (such as sputum), urine, and wound specimens. For CRGNB detected at any other site, infection was defined by the presence of systemic inflammation (i.e., white blood cell count > 10,000 or < 4,000 cells/µl and/or body temperature > 37.5 °C or < 35.5 °C) on the date of specimen collection. In the absence of systemic inflammation, the patient was considered to be colonized with CRGNB.

Carbapenem-resistant bacteria

The CRGNB examined in this study included any gram-negative bacilli, including Enterobacterales, P. aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and Burkholderia cepacia. CRGNB infection was defined as an infection caused by these organisms. Considering the frequent use of carbapenems in patients with cancer5, we included both organisms with acquired resistance and those with intrinsic resistance, as each represents a significant clinical challenge. CRGNBs were identified at the participating hospitals through routine clinical care, and carbapenem non-susceptibility was centrally confirmed by the disk diffusion method. CRE was defined as isolates resistant to meropenem (zone diameter ≤ 22 mm), while carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA) and A. baumannii (CRAB) were defined as isolates resistant to either meropenem or imipenem, with breakpoints of ≤ 18 mm for CRPA and ≤ 17 mm (meropenem) or ≤ 21 mm (imipenem) for CRAB, according to the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines12,13. If carbapenem-non-susceptible gram-negative rods or carbapenemase-producing gram-negative rods (including carbapenem-susceptible carbapenemase-producing) of the same species were detected more than once in the same patient during a single admission, only the first episode was included. Species identification was performed using standard microbiological procedures in the clinical microbiology laboratories at the participating hospitals, utilizing MicroScan (Beckman Coulter, Sacramento, CA), MBT Compass (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany), Vitek 2 (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA), or Vitek MS (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA) systems, supplemented with additional testing as appropriate. Antimicrobial susceptibility was centrally determined using customized 96-well microbroth dilution panels (Eiken Chemical Co., Tokyo, Japan), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and the results were interpreted based on the CLSI criteria (CLSI M100, 31 st edition), with the exception of Aeromonas spp., for which the CLSI M45, 3rd edition, was used13. Whole genome sequencing was conducted as previously reported14.

Data collection

Information was collected and registered in an electronic data capture system on the collection date (± 2 days) of the culture specimen from which CRGNBs were identified, up to 90 days after the end of antimicrobial treatment or discharge. The following information was collected: age; sex; dates of admission, discharge, and culture collection; comorbidities (myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, collagen disease, ulcerative disease, liver disease, diabetes, hemiplegia, renal disease, solid tumor, leukemia, lymphoma, and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome); residence (home, transfer from another hospital, long-term care facility/nursing home); hospitalization within 30 days before admission; readmission within 90 days after discharge; detection site of CRGNB; diagnosis of infection; endoscopy and surgery within 30 days of culture collection; antimicrobial use within 14 days of culture collection; immunosuppressive status (including steroid use, chemotherapy, biologic agents, immunosuppressive agents, and transplant status; see Definitions for details); Pitt bacteremia score (severity score)9; clinical course after culture collection; ICU stay, intubation, and dialysis from the culture collection to the last day of antimicrobial therapy; death within 30 days of the culture collection; and history of antimicrobial allergy, maintenance dialysis prior to admission, overseas travel within 90 days of admission, and ICU admission and intravenous therapy within 30 days of culture collection.

Definitions

Solid organ tumors were defined as tumors without metastases treated within 5 years. Leukemia was defined as acute leukemia, chronic leukemia, and erythrocyte hyperplasia, encompassing newly diagnosed, relapsed, and refractory phases. Lymphoma was defined as newly diagnosed, relapsed, and refractory cases of macroglobulinemia and myeloma. Steroid use was defined as a history of oral or intravenous steroid administration within 30 days before the index culture, regardless of the dosage. Chemotherapy was defined as any chemotherapy received within 3 months of the index culture. Biologic agents included the following administered within 3 months of the index culture: tumor necrosis factor-alpha inhibitors, selective T-cell co-stimulation modulators, anti-interleukin-6 receptor antibodies, and anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies. Immunosuppressive agents included the following administered within 3 months of the index culture: Janus kinase inhibitors, antimetabolites, calcineurin inhibitors, alkylating agents, and methotrexate within 3 months of detecting the organism.

Primary outcome measures

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. The secondary outcomes were as follows: (1) composite outcome including death within 30 days of culture collection, worsening of clinical course after culture collection, defined as newly worsening clinical course within 7 days (± 1 day) after culture collection or 48 h before discharge, ICU stay and intubation occurring between the date of culture collection and the end of antimicrobial therapy, new dialysis occurring between the culture collection and the end of antimicrobial therapy, and all-cause readmission within 90 days of discharge; and (2) length of hospital stay after isolation of CRGNBs, excluding cases of death.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test was used for the analysis of categorical variables. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables, with two-sided p values < 0.05 regarded as statistically significant. For the primary and secondary outcomes, a simple linear regression model was applied to the continuous outcome and a logistic regression model was applied to the binary outcome (unadjusted univariate analysis). To adjust for confounding effects due to patient background imbalance between the cancer and non-cancer groups, the propensity scores were subjected to inverse probability weighting (IPW). Propensity scores, defined as the probability of a patient being in the cancer group conditional on observed covariates, were calculated using a logistic regression model that included the following variables: age, sex, length of hospital stay, comorbidities, dialysis history prior to admission, history of antimicrobial allergy, hospitalization within 30 days of admission, residence, travel history within 30 days of admission, readmission within 90 days of discharge, site of detection of CRGNB, diagnosis of infection, history of ICU admission within 30 days of culture collection, history of intravenous therapy within 30 days of culture collection, endoscopy within 30 days of culture collection, surgery within 30 days of culture collection, antimicrobial use within 14 days of culture collection, immunosuppressive status, causative organisms, and Pitt bacteremia score. The inverse of the estimated probabilities was used for weighted (adjusted) regression analysis. Participants with extremely high propensity scores (outside the range of 0.1 to 0.915–17 were trimmed to ensure stable weighting. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.0.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 167 patients (cancer group, n = 66 [39.5%]; non-cancer group, n = 101 [60.5%]) were included in this study. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the study patients in each group. In the cancer group, 35 (53.0%) patients had solid tumors without metastasis, 20 (30.3%) had solid tumors with metastasis, 9 (13.6%) had leukemia, and 6 (9.1%) had lymphoma (three patients had solid tumors without metastasis plus leukemia, and one patient had a solid tumor with metastasis plus leukemia). The distribution of comorbidities differed between the two groups in terms of myocardial infarction (cancer group, n = 0 [0.0%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 9 [8.9%]; p = 0.012), congestive heart failure (cancer group, n = 2 [3.0%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 16 [15.8%]; p = 0.01), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (cancer group, n = 5 [7.6%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 1 [1.0%]; p = 0.036). The incidence of history of hospitalization within 30 days was significantly higher in the cancer group than in the non-cancer group (cancer group, n = 32 [48.5%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 23 [22.8%]; p < 0.01). Chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation were more common in the cancer group (chemotherapy; cancer group, n = 21 [31.8%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 0 [0.0%]; p < 0.001, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation; cancer group, n = 5 [7.6%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 1 [1.0%]; p < 0.001), while the use of immunosuppressive agents was more common in the non-cancer group (cancer group, n = 3 [4.5%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 16 [15.8%]; p = 0.026). Respiratory infections were the most common infections (cancer group, 20/66 [30.3%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 32/101 [31.7%]; p = 0.866), followed by bloodstream infections (cancer group, 19/66 [28.8%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 25/101 [24.8%]; p = 0.593), but no significant differences were observed. Pitt bacteremia scores were significantly higher in the non-cancer group than in the cancer group (cancer group, mean score of 1.47 vs. non-cancer group, mean score of 2.08; p = 0.03).

Comorbidities

Myocardial infarction

In terms of causative CRGNBs, P. aeruginosa was the most frequently isolated species in both the groups, although no significant difference was observed (cancer group, 36/66 [54.5%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 59/101 [58.4%]; p = 0.87). S. maltophilia was significantly more frequent in the cancer group than in the non-cancer group (cancer group, 17/66 [25.8%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 8/101 [7.9%]; p = 0.003). For the carbapenemase gene in Enterobacterales and P. aeruginosa, 43 cases were tested in the cancer group and 80 cases in the non-cancer group. The rate of carbapenemase gene identification differed significantly (cancer group, n = 1/43 [2.3%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 15/80 [18.9%] = 0.003) between the two groups: blaIMP−1 and its variants were the most commonly identified carbapenemase genes in both groups (cancer group, 1/1 [100%] vs. non-cancer group, n = 13/15 [86.7%]).

The 30-day mortality rates in the cancer and non-cancer groups were 18.2% (12/66) and 14.0% (14/101), respectively (p = 0.45) (Table 2). The incidence rates of the composite outcome in the cancer and non-cancer groups were 56.1% (37/66) and 43.6% (44/101), respectively (p = 0.12). Furthermore, the average length of hospital stay was not significantly different between the groups (cancer group, 44.6 days vs. non-cancer group, 51.0 days; p = 0.55). Propensity score analysis using IPW showed no significant difference in 30-day mortality and length of stay (p = 0.25 and 0.66); however, the incidence rates of the composite outcome were significantly higher in the cancer group than in the non-cancer group (odds ratio, 2.36; p = 0.02). In an additional analysis excluding patients with non-metastatic solid tumors, no significant difference in 30-day mortality was observed before or after IPW (p = 0.25; and 0.65, respectively). For the composite outcome, a significant association was observed before IPW (p = 0.02), but this was not significant after IPW (p = 0.12).

Discussion

Despite the increasing number of cancer patients in a rapidly aging society, data on the characteristics and outcomes of CRGNB infections in patients with cancer in Japan are limited. The results of our multicenter cohort study suggest that Japanese patients with cancer and concurrent CRGNB infections may experience a higher frequency of adverse events, particularly in terms of the composite outcome, than those without cancer, indicating the threat posed by CRGNB infections in this vulnerable population.

Mortality rates associated with nosocomial CRGNB infections are higher than those associated with carbapenem-susceptible organisms4,18, with reported rates ranging from 15.8% to 79%19–25. Patients with cancer, particularly those with hematologic malignancies, are at an even higher risk of infection-related mortality7,9,18,26,27. In our study, although mortality tended to be higher in patients with cancer than in those without cancer, the difference did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited statistical power related to the relatively small sample size. In addition, most patients with cancer in our cohort had solid tumors (83.3%), and the inclusion of patients with non-metastatic solid tumors may have attenuated the mortality difference between groups, as these patients often have a more favorable prognosis than those with advanced or hematologic malignancies. In a subgroup analysis excluding patients with non-metastatic solid tumors, no significant difference in 30-day mortality was observed, and the association with the composite outcome was attenuated after IPW, possibly reflecting reduced statistical power due to the smaller sample size. Nevertheless, the incidence of the composite outcome was significantly higher in the cancer group than in the non-cancer group, suggesting that CRGNB infections represent a substantial clinical threat in this population.

In our study, P. aeruginosa and S. maltophilia were the most commonly detected CRGNBs, while infections caused by Enterobacterales were less common. CPE are receiving increasing attention; however, there is also growing awareness of the impact of carbapenem resistance in non-fermentative gram-negative organisms such as A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and S. maltophilia2. Although data are limited in some regions, the overall burden of disease due to CRGNBs is similar in most regions, such as the Asia-Pacific, the Indian sub-continent, Europe, North America, and Latin America, with non-fermentative gram-negative organisms, such as P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, being the most prevalent ones and CRE being relatively less common2,28. In a CRGNB infection study by Cai et al. in the U.S., A. baumannii (22%) and P. aeruginosa (60.3%) infections were more common than Klebsiella pneumoniae and E. coli (17.7%) infections4. The Japan Nosocomial Infections Surveillance 2021 report, which includes data from 2,200 facilities, states that the imipenem and meropenem non-susceptibility rates, as per CLSI 2012 breakpoints, are 0.1% and 0.1% for E. coli, 0.2% and 0.4% for K. pneumoniae, 0.9% and 0.8% for Enterobacter cloacae, 0.7% and 0.9% for K. aerogenes, 15.8% and 10.3% for P. aeruginosa, and 0.3% each for Acinetobacter spp29. In our study, P. aeruginosa was the most commonly isolated CRGNB, consistent with previously reported Japanese epidemiology data. Furthermore, infections caused by A. baumannii and Enterobacterales were infrequent, highlighting a unique epidemiology of CRGNB infections in Japan that differs from that in many countries. CRE has been associated with higher mortality rates than CRPA30,31, indicating that the distribution of causative organisms in our study may have contributed to the observed mortality rate.

In addition, only 10% of all CRGNB infections were due to CPE in our cohort. CPE infections are difficult to treat because of their resistance to most β-lactams and multiple antimicrobial agents31,32,33. The mortality rate for CPE infections is 20–40%34–36. K. pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC)-producing bacteria are the most common carbapenemase-producing bacteria in Europe and North America. In Asia, however, the predominant carbapenemase types vary by country: KPC-producing Enterobacterales are widespread in China and South Korea, whereas New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase-producing organisms are reported in several South Asian countries37. In contrast, IMP-type CPE is commonly found in Japan11. In our cohort, all CPE carried blaIMP. In Japan, IMP-type carbapenemase-producing bacteria are often susceptible to piperacillin-tazobactam, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, amikacin, and sulfamethoxazole trimethoprim, likely preserving treatment options for patients who develop CPE infections38,39. As our cohort had fewer cases of CPE infection that included infections by IMP-type Enterobacterales, the mortality rate in our study was lower than that in previous CRGNB infection studies4,18,40, irrespective of the presence of cancer.

Patients with cancer often experience recurrent infections due to cancer treatment and progression, resulting in substantial use of antibiotics5,41,42. These patients are at a high risk of developing CRGNB infection owing to long hospital stays and frequent exposure to broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy6–8. Our study revealed that CRGNB infections have worse clinical outcomes in patients with cancer than in non-cancer patients. Appropriate antimicrobial use in this population is, therefore, particularly important in reducing the risk of CRGNB infections. In light of these findings, active implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs specifically tailored to oncology patients is essential. Such programs should include optimization of empirical carbapenem use, timely de-escalation based on microbiological results, and reinforcement of infection prevention practices to minimize the emergence and transmission of CRGNB in this vulnerable population43.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size of patients with cancer was small. Given the size of our cohort, subgroup analyses for specific patient populations (e.g., allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) or types of organisms was not possible. Second, residual confounding factors were not completely eliminated. Third, this study was based on a predefined protocol for data collection; however, many clinical aspects of patients with cancer cannot be quantified or measured, and the risk of residual confounding factors cannot be eliminated. We tried to mitigate this risk to the extent possible by using the IPW method. Specifically, we incorporated available variables reflecting disease severity and immunosuppressive status into the weighting model. However, these variables can only partially serve as proxy markers, and the influence of unmeasured prognostic factors, such as cancer stage, neutrophil count, and prior carbapenem exposure, cannot be excluded. Finally, outcome definition bias, surveillance bias, and treatment heterogeneity across centers may have influenced the observed associations despite propensity score adjustment.

In conclusion, the 30-day mortality rate was similar between patients with and without cancer in Japan; however, a significant difference was noted in the composite outcome. The study findings highlight the need for urgent targeted measures to prevent CRGNB infections in this population, reduce infection-related morbidity, and improve the overall prognosis for patients with cancer, especially in light of the clinically important finding of worse composite outcomes in this group.
