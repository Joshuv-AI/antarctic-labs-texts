A multiple occupancy cell fluid model with competing attraction and repulsion interactions
R. V. Romanik, O. A. Dobush, M. P. Kozlovskii, I. V. Pylyuk, M. A. Shpot

Abstract
An analytically solvable cell fluid model with unrestricted cell occupancy, infinite‑range Curie–Weiss–type attraction and short‑range intra‑cell repulsion is studied within the grand‑canonical ensemble. Building on an exact single‑integral representation of the grand partition function, we apply Laplace’s method to obtain asymptotically exact expressions for the pressure, density and equation of state. The model exhibits a hierarchy of first‑order transitions, each terminating at a critical point. We determine the coordinates of the first five such points. Recasting the formalism in dimensionless variables highlights the explicit temperature dependence of all thermodynamic functions. This enables us to derive a closed‑form expression for the entropy. The results reveal pronounced entropy minima around integer cell occupancies and reproduce density‑anomaly isotherm crossings analogous to those in core‑softened models.



keywords
Entropy, Cell fluid model, Curie-Weiss interaction

1
Introduction

Lattice models have played an important role in statistical physics for a long time,
offering simplified yet physically meaningful representations of many-particle
systems. Despite the spatial discretisation they impose,
lattice models can qualitatively reproduce the essential thermodynamic
properties of fluids and offer convenience for numerical simulations
Panagiotopoulos 2000
. The most extensively studied lattice gas model is the one isomorphic to the Ising model, in which each lattice site may be either occupied by a single particle or left empty
Lee and Yang 1952
. In contrast, models that allow more than one particle per site have been studied less extensively, even though they can show equally or even more interesting physical behavior. A notable example is the double-occupancy lattice gas, isomorphic to the Blume-Capel model, recently analyzed in
Liu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
. More generally, models allowing arbitrary multiple occupancy have been explored in
Likos et al. 1998
;
Finken et al. 2004
;
Frydel and Levin 2018
.
The models of this kind possess richer phase behavior compared to the single-occupancy lattice gas. Similar features arise in quantum many-body physics, where lattice-boson models
are inherently multiple-occupancy systems
Fisher et al. 1989
;
Szabados et al. 2012
;
Krasnov and Stasyuk 2019
.

As emphasized by Stanley
(
Stanley 1971
, Appendix A)
, it is often more
physically transparent to speak of the occupancy of cells by the centers of particles, rather than restrict the atoms’ positions to a discrete set of sites in a lattice. This perspective underpins the usage of the ‘cell’ terminology, rather than the ‘lattice’ one
Barker 1954
;
Dunne et al. 2006
;
Rebenko 2013
.
Within this framework, one can vary both the maximal occupancy and the range of inter-particle interactions. Extending the interaction to infinite range drives the system to mean-field behavior, and often allows for an exact solution
Baxter 1982
;
Biskup et al. 2006

A cell model that combines unrestricted cell occupancy with an infinite-range Curie-Weiss-type attraction was introduced in
Kozitsky and Kozlovskii 2020
;
Kozitsky et al. 2020
and
Kozitsky et al. 2018
.
The main features of the model are as follows:
(i) each cell may contain an arbitrary number of particles;
(ii) a global attractive interaction is imposed between all particle pairs in the system, regardless of their positions;
(iii) a local repulsive interaction acts pairwise between particles within the same cell;
(iv) the thermodynamic stability of the system is ensured by assuming that the repulsion dominates over the attraction, preventing particle collapse or unlimited clustering.
In the thermodynamic limit, the model’s grand partition function can be calculated asymptotically exactly
Kozitsky et al. 2020
, revealing a hierarchy of critical points analogous to those of the generalized Curie-Weiss magnet
Eisele and Ellis 1988
.

The goal of this study is twofold. First, we revisit the known results for the cell model with Curie-Weiss interaction and rewrite them in the notation standard to the theory of simple liquids
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, thereby making the temperature dependence of
thermodynamic quantities explicit. Second, we derive a closed-form expression for the entropy, a quantity left unexplored in previous treatments, and analyze its behavior near the multiple critical points.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we formulate the model, establish the notation to be used throughout the paper, and present the expression for the grand partition function. In Section
3
, the explicit expressions are derived for the pressure and the average number of particles as functions of temperature and chemical potential, as well as for the equation of state.
Section
4
focuses on the details of calculating the critical points’ coordinates.
In Section
7
, we proceed with the calculation of the entropy.
Finally, summary and conclusions are present in Section
8
.

2
Model

We begin by introducing the model and notation, and then summarize the main known results.
In this section, we closely follow the argument of
Kozitsky et al. 2020
. Subsequently, we extend its framework by introducing dimensionless physical quantities as standard in the theory of fluids
Hansen and McDonald 2013
.

General setting.

The statistical mechanics model defined below is studied within the formalism of the grand canonical ensemble (see e.g.
(
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, Sec. 2.4)
). An open system of point particles is considered in volume
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
in three space dimensions. The total volume
V
is partitioned into
N_{v}
non-overlapping congruent cubic cells
\Delta_{l}
,
l\in\{1,...,N_{v}\}
, each of volume
v
, such that
V
is the union of all
\Delta_{l}
’s:

V=\bigcup_{l=1}^{N_{v}}\Delta_{l},\qquad\Delta_{l}\cap\Delta_{l^{\prime}}=\emptyset,\text{ if }l\neq l^{\prime},\qquad\mbox{and}\quad V=vN_{v}.

(1)

Interaction.
Consider a configuration
\gamma_{N}=\{\mathbf{r}_{1},...,\mathbf{r}_{N}\}
of particles contained in the volume
V
,
where
\mathbf{r}_{i}
is the position vector of the
i
-th particle and
\absolutevalue{\gamma_N}\equiv N
is the number of particles in this configuration.
In fact, for any given number
N
of particles, there are infinitely many associated configurations
\gamma_{N}
each corresponding to a specific random distribution of these particles over
V
and, thus, over cells
\Delta_{l}
.
The total potential energy of interaction between particles in a given configuration
\gamma_{N}
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{N_{v}}(\gamma_{N})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j}\in\gamma_{N}}\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})=\frac{1}{2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}}\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j}),

(2)

where
\Phi_{N_{v}}=\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})
is given by the two-point interaction in the form

\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})=-\frac{J_{1}}{N_{v}}+J_{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{v}}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r}_{i})\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r}_{j}).

(3)

The first term in
\Phi_{N_{v}}
describes a global Curie-Weiss (mean-field-like) attraction for any pair of particles in the system.
The strength of this attraction is controlled by an energy parameter
J_{1}>0
. The second term in
\Phi_{N_{v}}
describes a local repulsion between two particles contained within the same cell
\Delta_{l}
and is characterized by the parameter
J_{2}>0.

Here,
\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r})
is the indicator function of the cell
\Delta_{l}
,

\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,\quad\mathbf{r}\in\Delta_{l},\\
0,\quad\mathbf{r}\notin\Delta_{l}.\end{array}\right.

(4)

Therefore, two cases are possible for
\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})
, depending on whether two particles belong to the same cell or to different ones. Explicitly, one has

\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-J_{1}/N_{v}+J_{2},&\text{when particles are in the same cell,}\\
-J_{1}/N_{v},&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(5)

The sum over all particle pairs in the total energy
W_{N_{v}}(\gamma_{N})
brings about a competition between the global attractive energy of particles and their repulsive energy at “short” distances. This competition leads to a certain kind of frustration in the system, which is responsible for the non-trivial phase behavior discussed in
Kozlovskii and Dobush 2022
.

Note that the self-interaction terms
\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{i})
are included in
W_{N_{v}}
from (
2
). This does not affect the physics of the model. Given that
\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{i}\in\gamma_{N}}1=\absolutevalue{\gamma_N}=N
, we combine the definitions (
3
) and (
2
), which yields

W_{N_{v}}(\gamma_{N})=-\frac{J_{1}}{2N_{v}}N^{2}+\frac{J_{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j}\in\gamma_{N}}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{v}}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r}_{i})\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r}_{j})\,.

(6)

Let us notice the different character of summations over positions
\mathbf{r}_{i}
and
\mathbf{r}_{j}

in two terms present in (
6
).
The first term in (
6
) is the result of the double summation
\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j}\in\gamma_{N}}
applied to a constant, which evaluates to
N^{2}
and does not depend on any specific distribution of points
\mathbf{r}_{i}
within the configuration
\gamma_{N}
.
The situation is different with the second term. Here, the result of summation over
\bm{r}_{i}
and
\mathbf{r}_{j}
heavily depends on the distribution of particles with these coordinates over the cells constituting the volume
V
.
According to the rule (
5
), the number of appearances of
J_{2}
directly depends on the number of particles and, consequently, on their pairs, occupying each cell
\Delta_{l}
in the volume
V
.

Stability.
For thermodynamic stability of the system with two-point interactions

\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
, the rigorous inequality
Kozitsky et al. 2020
;
Ruelle 1970

\int_{V}\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}){\rm d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}>0

(7)

must hold for any
\mathbf{r}\in V
.
Taking into account (
3
) and (
5
), we
see that there is one contribution to this integral from a cell containing the coordinate
\mathbf{r}
, and
(N_{v}-1)
contributions from other cells:

\displaystyle\int_{V}\Phi_{N_{v}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}){\rm d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{J_{1}}{N_{v}}+J_{2}\right)v-(N_{v}-1)\,\frac{J_{1}}{N_{v}}\,v=(J_{2}-J_{1})v.

Thus, the requirement (
7
) implies that the energies of repulsion and attraction interactions must obey the inequality

J_{2}>J_{1}.

It is useful to introduce their ratio via

a\equiv J_{2}/J_{1}>1.

(8)

The quantity
a
can be considered as one of two adjustable parameters of the studied model, another one being
v^{*}
introduced below (
20
).

The grand partition function
(GPF) is given by the following sum over all possible numbers
N
of particles in volume
V
(see e.g.
(
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, (2.4.6))
):

\Xi=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{N}}{N!}Z_{N}.

(9)

Here
\zeta
is the activity

\zeta=\frac{\exp(\beta\mu)}{\Lambda^{3}},

(10)

\beta=1/(k_{\rm B}T)
is the inverse temperature,
k_{\rm B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the (absolute) temperature, and
\mu
is the chemical potential.
The value
\Lambda=(2\pi\beta\hbar^{2}/m)^{1/2}
is the de Broglie thermal wavelength,

\hbar
is the Planck constant, and
m
is the mass of a particle.
The presence of
\Lambda
in (
9
) arises from (a) the choice of the normalization factor in the definition of the partition function, ensuring consistency between classical and quantum statistical mechanics, and (b) the integration over momenta (see, e.g.,
(
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, Section 2.3)
).

The configuration integral
Z_{N}
is defined as

Z_{N}=\int\exp(-\beta W_{N_v}(\gamma_N))({\rm d}\mathbf{r})^{N},

(11)

with
({\rm d}\mathbf{r})^{N}\equiv{\rm d}{\mathbf{r}_{1}}\dotsc{\rm d}{\mathbf{r}_{N}}
.
In
Z_{N}
, the integration goes over all configurations
\gamma_{N}
of
N
particles inside the volume
V
.

The mean values of physical quantities are defined as statistical averages over the grand canonical ensemble via

\langle\ldots\rangle=\frac{1}{\Xi}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}(\ldots)\frac{\zeta^{N}}{N!}Z_{N}.

(12)

Within the framework of the grand canonical ensemble, the observable average number of particles in the system is given by

\langle N\rangle=\beta^{-1}\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu}\,=\frac{1}{\Xi}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}N\frac{\zeta^{N}}{N!}Z_{N}\,.

(13)

This is an important quantity for further consideration.

Taking into account the expression (
6
) for the total energy
W_{N_{v}}(\gamma_{N})

we write the GPF of our model in the form

\Xi=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda^{-3N}}{N!}\int\exp\left[\beta\mu N+\frac{\beta J_{1}}{2N_{v}}N^{2}-\frac{\beta J_{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j}\in\gamma}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{v}}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r}_{i})\mathbb{I}_{\Delta_{l}}(\mathbf{r}_{j})\right]({\rm d}\mathbf{r})^{N}.

(14)

In addition to introducing the cell fluid model with competing Curie-Weiss attraction and intra-cell repulsion interactions,
one of the main achievements of
Kozitsky et al. 2020
was the representation of the GPF appearing in (
14
) in the form of a single integral. The corresponding result can be found in

(
Kozitsky et al. 2020
, (2.13)–(2.15))
. We slightly modify its derivation by taking into account the presence of the de Broglie thermal wavelength
\Lambda
in the definition of the GPF (see (
9
) and (
10
)). Moreover, we express the results in terms of the
reduced dimensionless thermodynamic quantities widely accepted in the theory of simple liquids (see e.g.
Hansen and McDonald 2013
).

We start the following section by introducing these reduced quantities. Next, we provide the explicit expressions determining the integral representation for the GPF (
14
) of the cell model.

3
Thermodynamics

Natural units for energy and length in the model under consideration are
J_{1}
, the Curie-Weiss attraction’s strength, and
c=v^{1/3}
, the length of the cell’s edge, respectively. It is a standard practice to express thermodynamic results in terms of dimensionless quantities normalized by natural units of this kind. The advantage of using such quantities is that their numerical values are typically of order of unity, which simplifies analysis. This also opens a direct possibility for comparisons with results available in the theory of liquids.

3.1
Reduced dimensionless quantities

For any dimensional quantity
Q
, it is convenient to introduce its dimensionless, or “reduced”, counterpart
Q^{*}
. Thus, we define the following reduced quantities:

\displaystyle T^{*}:=\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{J_{1}}

– the reduced temperature;

\displaystyle\beta^{*}:=\beta J_{1}=\frac{1}{T^{*}}

– the reduced inverse temperature;

\displaystyle\mu^{*}:=\frac{\mu}{J_{1}}

– the reduced chemical potential;

\displaystyle P^{*}:=\frac{Pv}{J_{1}}

– the reduced pressure;

\displaystyle\rho^{*}:=\frac{\langle N\rangle}{V}v

– the reduced density;

\displaystyle S^{*}:=\frac{S}{k_{\rm B}\langle N\rangle}

– the reduced entropy.

(15)

Taking into account (
1
) and (
13
), we see that the reduced density
\rho^{*}
actually represents the average particle number per cell:
\rho^{*}=\langle N\rangle/N_{v}
.

Working within the grand canonical ensemble, we naturally express all physical quantities in terms of the reduced temperature
T^{*}
and chemical potential
\mu^{*}

Laplace’s method

3.4
Pressure

The pressure

\frac{PV}{k_{\rm B}TN_{v}}=E(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})\,.

The reduced dimensionless pressure
P^{*}=P^{*}(T^{*},\mu^{*})
can therefore be expressed as

P^{*}=T^{*}E(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})

(37)

in terms of the thermodynamic variables defined in (
3.1
).
Note that the maximum position
\bar{y}_{\rm max}
in (
37
) is itself a function of
T^{*}
and
\mu^{*}
:
\bar{y}_{\rm max}=\bar{y}_{\rm max}(T^{*},\mu^{*})
.
Since an analytical expression for
\bar{y}_{\rm max}(T^{*},\mu^{*})
cannot be derived, we determine this function numerically from equation (
25
) and present the results graphically in Figures
2
,
2
as well as in the following sections.

The relation (
37
) actually represents the equation of state for the system under consideration, expressed in terms of thermodynamic variables
P^{*}
,
T^{*}
, and
\mu^{*}
. It encompasses the fact that the relevant thermodynamic behavior in the thermodynamic limit is determined by the stable global maximum of the function
E(T^{*},\mu^{*};y)
from the integral representation (
16
).

3.5
Average number of particles

The average particle number
\langle N\rangle
is given by the fundamental thermodynamic relation

\langle N\rangle=-\left(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V},

(38)

where
\Omega=-k_{\rm B}T\ln\Xi
is the grand potential.
Hence, using the definition (
13
) for
\langle N\rangle
along with the formula (
34
) for pressure, we obtain

\frac{\langle N\rangle}{V}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial\mu}\,,

(39)

where the derivative with respect to the chemical potential
\mu
is taken at constant temperature
T
.

In terms of the reduced quantities defined in (
3.1
), equation (
39
) becomes

\rho^{*}=\left(\frac{\partial P^{*}}{\partial\mu^{*}}\,\right)_{T^{*}}.

(40)

To derive the reduced density
\rho^{*}=\rho^{*}(T^{*},\mu^{*})
for our cell model, we differentiate (
37
) using the chain rule:

\rho^{*}=T^{*}\left[\frac{\partial E(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})}{\partial\mu^{*}}+\frac{\partial E(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})}{\partial\bar{y}_{\rm max}}\,\frac{\partial\bar{y}_{\rm max}}{\partial\mu^{*}}\right].

(41)

The second term in (
41
) vanishes due to the extremum condition (
3.3
), which determines
\bar{y}_{\rm max}
itself. Consequently, using the definitions (
17
) and (
18
), we obtain

\rho^{*}=\frac{K_{1}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})}{K_{0}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})}.

(42)

Since the maximum position
\bar{y}_{\rm max}
satisfies the extremum equation (
25
), the formula (
42
) simplifies to the remarkably simple form

\rho^{*}=T^{*}\bar{y}_{\rm max}(T^{*},\mu^{*}).

(43)

This result provides an interesting direct relationship between the reduced density and the maximum position
\bar{y}_{\rm max}
.

In the literature on simple liquid theory
Hansen and McDonald 2013
,
\rho^{*}
represents the reduced particle number density. Within our cell model framework, it has an additional interpretation as the average number of particles per cell, or the mean cell occupancy (cf. p.
3.1
and
(
Kozitsky et al. 2020
, (2.26))
).

3.6
Equation of state

We have found the pressure
P^{*}
and the particle density
\rho^{*}
, both as functions of thermodynamic variables
T^{*}
and
\mu^{*}
, in equations (
37
) and
(
43
), respectively.
To obtain the equation of state in the conventional form
P^{*}=P^{*}(T^{*},\rho^{*})
, we need to eliminate the chemical potential
\mu^{*}
between these two relations.

Formally, we can solve the equation (
43
) for
\mu^{*}
to obtain
\mu^{*}=\mu^{*}(T^{*},\rho^{*})
, and then substitute it into (
37
). This procedure yields the pressure as a function of temperature and density (cf.
(
Kozitsky et al. 2020
, (2.28))
),

P^{*}=T^{*}E\left[T^{*},\mu^{*}(T^{*},\rho^{*});\frac{\rho^{*}}{T^{*}}\right],

(44)

the equation of state in the widely used
P
-
\rho
-
T
representation.

However, the inversion of equation (
43
) to solve for
\mu^{*}(T^{*},\rho^{*})
analytically is not feasible, and we must resort to numerical methods. Despite this limitation, the formal representation (
44
) of equation of state proves valuable for theoretical analysis, particularly in determining critical point coordinates, as discussed in Section
5
.

The most natural way to investigate this question in many-body interacting systems is in terms of pressure and density. We will proceed with such an investigation.

5
Equation of state and critical point coordinates

General thermodynamic theory of phase transitions provides us with a recipe for finding the coordinates of a critical point from an equation of state in the form
P=P(T,\rho)
,
see e.g.
(
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, p. 184)
.
Since the critical point is a horizontal inflection point of the critical isotherm
at the top of the phase coexistence curve
(
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, Fig. 5.12)
, we have two conditions:

\left(\frac{\partial P^{*}}{\partial\rho^{*}}\right)_{T^{*}}=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P^{*}}{\partial\rho^{*2}}\right)_{T^{*}}=0

(45)

to be satisfied at the critical point
(T^{*}_{c},\rho^{*}_{c})
. The physical content of the first condition in (
45
) is that the isothermal compressibility diverges at the
critical point.

In Appendix
10
, we establish the connection between the standard
critical point conditions (
45
) and the corresponding equations specific for the system we consider throughout the paper. These special equations are

E_{2}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})=0
and
E_{3}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})=0
, where
the functions
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
are the derivatives of the basic function

E(T^{*},\mu^{*};y)
, see (
17
), (
29
), evaluated at the stable-maximum position
y=\bar{y}_{\rm max}
.

Along with equation (
3.3
) determining the maximum’s position

\bar{y}_{\rm max}
, we have three equations in terms of functions
E_{j}
and variables
T^{*}
,
\mu^{*}
, and
\bar{y}_{\rm max}
:

\begin{cases}E_{1}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})=0,\\
E_{2}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})=0,\\
E_{3}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})=0.\end{cases}

(46)

This system of three equations in three unknowns can be solved numerically to determine the critical point coordinates
T^{*}_{c}
,
\mu^{*}_{c}
, and
\bar{y}_{\rm max,c}
.

In order to ensure that the coordinate
\bar{y}_{\rm max,c}
indeed corresponds to a global maximum of
E(T^{*},\mu^{*};y)
, we still have to perform a negativity check (similar to that in (
3.3
))

E_{4}(T^{*}_{c},\mu^{*}_{c};\bar{y}_{\rm max,c})<0

(47)

distinguishing it from other possible stationary points.

A note on the application of the Laplace’s method at the critical point is in place here.
Since
E_{2}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})=0
at the critical point, the usual Laplace approximation formula (
33
) is not applicable in this case.
However, the inequality (
47
) allows us to use a modified version of the Laplace’s method with the result
(
Bender and Orszag 1999
, 6.4.19d)

\Xi=\sqrt{\frac{N_{v}T^{*}}{2\pi}}\frac{2\Gamma(1/4)(4!)^{1/4}}{4[-N_{v}E_{4}(T^{*}_{c},\mu^{*}_{c})]^{1/4}}\exp[N_{v}E(T^{*}_{c},\mu^{*}_{c};\bar{y}_{\rm max,c})],

(48)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Euler Gamma function.
In the thermodynamic limit
N_{v}\to\infty
, the formula (
48
) leads to the same expression for pressure as given in (
37
).

7
Entropy

By thermodynamic formulas for the grand canonical ensemble, we have for the entropy

S=-\left(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial T}\right)_{V,\mu}=V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}.

(49)

At this point, there are two possibilities to normalize entropy. One possibility is to normalize it by the average number of particles

S^{*}=\frac{S}{k_{\rm B}\langle N\rangle},

(50)

and call this quantity the entropy per particle. Another possibility is to normalize it by the number of cells

S^{*}_{v}=\frac{S}{k_{\rm B}N_{v}},

(51)

and call this quantity the entropy per cell. The usage of the entropy per particle,
S^{*}
, is more common in the theory of many-particle systems
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, while the entropy per cell,
S^{*}_{v}
, may be useful in cell (or lattice) models. We will also call
S^{*}
the
reduced entropy
.

Entropy per particle
reads

\displaystyle S^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho^{*}}\left(\frac{\partial P^{*}}{\partial T^{*}}\right)_{{\mu^{*}}}=\frac{(\partial P^{*}/\partial T^{*})_{{\mu^{*}}}}{(\partial P^{*}/\partial\mu^{*})_{T^{*}}}.

(52)

By (
37
)

S^{*}(T^{*},\mu^{*})=\frac{1}{\rho^{*}(T^{*},\mu^{*})}\left[E(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})+T^{*}\left(\frac{\partial E(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})}{\partial T^{*}}\right)_{\mu^{*}}\right].

(53)

The result of calculation is

S^{*}(T^{*},\mu^{*})=\left(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\mu^{*}}{T^{*}}\right)-\frac{1}{T^{*}}\frac{K_{1}}{K_{0}}+\frac{K_{0}\ln K_{0}}{K_{1}}+\frac{a}{2T^{*}}\frac{K_{2}}{K_{1}},

(54)

where for the special functions we imply
K_{i}=K_{i}(T^{*},\mu^{*};\bar{y}_{\rm max})
.
The first contribution to the entropy corresponds to the entropy of non-interacting molecules in a lattice, or an ideal lattice gas contribution, see
(
Hill 1956
, (47.4))
, if we account for the ideal-gas chemical potential
\mu_{\rm id}=k_{\rm B}T\ln(N\Lambda^3/V)
.

The entropy per cell
S^{*}_{v}
is related to the entropy per particle

8
Conclusion

In this study, a many-particle interacting system is studied based on a cell model with unrestricted cell occupancy. The interaction between particles is defined through two contributions, namely, a global attractive one between all particle pairs, and a short-range repulsive one for particles within the same cell. It is shown that such model posesses multiple critical points.
For low temperatures, the system exhibits a sequence of phase transitions of the first order between multiple phases, while for high temperatures there exists only a single phase.

The statistical-mechanical theory is derived within the framework of the grand canonical ensemble, and asymptotically exact solution (
33
) is obtained. The previously obtained results are revisited with explicit account for the de Briglie wavelength
\Lambda
in the definition of the grand partition function, and, as a consequence, for the explicit temperature dependence of computed physical quantities, such as pressure, density, and entropy.
The values of these, and a few other, physical quantities are calculated at first five critical points and summarized in Table
1
.

The entropy of the considered model has been calculated for the first time. Explicit expressions are obtained for both the entropy per particle (
54
) and entropy per cell (
57
). The dependence of the entropy per particle on the chemical potential, as well as that on the density is presented graphically in Fig.
8
and Fig.
8
, respectively. Qualitative similarity in behavior of the entropy is noticed between the considered model and the three-dimensional Hubbard model applied to cold atoms subject to an optical lattice
De Leo et al. 2011
. The obtained entropy results are of interest in general, and require a dedicated research.

Another non-trivial result is that the isotherms in the pressure-density plane intersect, see Fig.
6
. In this regard, we want to bring to our attention studies
Vilaseca and Franzese 2010
and
Netz et al. 2006
, where such density anomaly was also found. A model with continuous-shouldered-well potential was studied in
Vilaseca and Franzese 2010
, and that with a ramplike discretized potential in
Netz et al. 2006
, both papers discussing a possibility of more that one critical point in such systems.
To study of our model in this context is also in our plans.
