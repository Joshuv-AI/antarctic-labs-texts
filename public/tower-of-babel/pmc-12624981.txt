Relationship between the degree of degenerative rotator cuff tear and the thickness of coracoacromial ligament on inferior surface of acromion
Authors: Sheng Li, Liang A
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the coracoacromial ligament thickness measured by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on the inferior surface of the acromion, and to find the correlation between coracoacromial ligament thickness and rotator cuff tear size.MethodsThe coracoacromial ligament thickness of the inferior surface of the rotator cuff was assessed in 127 patients with rotator cuff tears using 3.0 MR system. The patients were grouped according to the size of the rotator cuff tear and whether the rotator cuff was degenerated or not.ResultsThe mean coracoacoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion was 3.58 ± 1.02 mm. The association between rotator cuff tear severity and coracoacromial ligament thickness was statistically significant (β=-1.531, P < 0.001), but not in the trauma cohort (β=-0.059, P = 0.731).Conclusion(1) MRI is a reliable tool to assess the thickness of the coracoacromial ligament on the inferior surface of the acromion; (2) The increased degree of degenerative rotator cuff tear is associated with the decreased thickness of the coracoacromial ligament on the inferior surface of the acromion; (3) The change of the degree of traumatic rotator cuff tear is not associated with the thickness of the coracoacromial ligament on the inferior surface of the acromion. These findings provide further insight into the mechanism of injury in rotator cuff tears.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09305-4.Keywords: Degenerative rotator cuff tear, Coracoacromial ligament, Ligament thickness, Inferior acromial surface, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Rotator cuff tears are a common condition contributing to shoulder pain and dysfunction, and their pathogenesis is closely related to degenerative and traumatic factors. The coracoacromial ligament (CAL), as an important component of the subacromial space, morphological changes may play a key role in the progression of rotator cuff tears [1]. Previous studies have shown that mechanical impingement and ligament degeneration of the inferior surface of the acromion may correlate with the degree of rotator cuff injury [2], but a specific relationship between coracoacromial ligament thickness and the degree of rotator cuff tear has not been clearly established. The purpose of this study is to quantitatively assess the coracoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion by 3.0T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and to explore the correlation between coracoacromial ligament thickness and the degree of degenerative and traumatic rotator cuff tears, so as to further reveal the pathological mechanism of rotator cuff tears and provide theoretical basis for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

Study object

Radiographic data of 127 patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff tear surgery at our institution between January 2024 and December 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Exclude: Previous shoulder surgery, shoulder dislocation, calcific tendonitis, moderate to severe osteoarthritis of the shoulder. Patients who had received prior conservative treatments (e.g., corticosteroid injections, physical therapy) within 3 months before MRI were also excluded to minimize potential confounding effects on CAL thickness. The surgery was performed by a surgical chief physician (Liang A). There were 60 male patients and 67 females; mean age: 57.75 ± 10.63 years. The degree of rotator cuff tears is classified as common, massive (defined as rotator cuff tears greater than 5 cm or injury involving complete rupture of more than two tendons); and degenerative, traumatic according to the rotator cuff tear mechanism. Degenerative tears: chronic symptoms (> 3 months) without acute trauma, often with tendinopathy on MRI. Traumatic tears: acute onset following a specific injury event. Tear size was measured intraoperatively and confirmed by MRI. The degree of rotator cuff tear, coracoacromial ligament thickness, and factors associated with subacromial impingement syndrome (age, sex, dominant side, acromiohumeral distance (AHD), and acromion tilt (AT)) were statistically compared between the groups. Subgroup analysis was performed to explore whether the relationship between CAL thickness and tear size differed between degenerative and traumatic tears, given their distinct pathomechanisms.

Imaging assessments

Shoulder scans were performed using a 3.0T MRI system (device model) with standard sequences including coronal, sagittal, and axial T1/T2 weighted images. Measurement location: at the midpoint of the CAL attachment on the inferior acromial surface. The thickest portion of the CAL was identified on T2-weighted sagittal images and measured using PACS tools. The maximum thickness of the coracoacromial ligament on the inferior surface of the acromion was measured in a double-blinded fashion by two senior radiologists and the degree of tears in the rotator cuff tendons was assessed.

Covariate

This study refers to previous research [3], and there were no significant differences in patient characteristics and any factors related to subacromial impingement (age, gender, dominant shoulder, AHD, AT) in the rotator cuff tear group.

Description of statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (quartiles) and categorical variables as frequency (%). For the comparison of tear group characteristics, a t-test was used for continuous variables if normally distributed, otherwise a nonparametric test was used. Categorical variables were used χ2 Tested. Linear regression was used to analyze the association between tear severity and coracoacromial ligament thickness. Take the thickness of CAL on the lower surface of the acromion as the dependent variable and the tear size of the rotator cuff as the independent variable. Include age, gender, dominant shoulder, AHD, AT, and tear cause as covariates in the multiple linear regression model. The correlation between the influencing factors in this study is relatively low (with a maximum of 0.471), and the tolerance is greater than 0.1. The variance inflation factors are all less than 10, indicating that there is no multicollinearity among the influencing factors (see attachment for results). SPSS 26.0 was used for data analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Assessing Coracoacromial Ligament Thickness with MRI.

In this study, changes in AHD and AT were assessed by X-ray in patients with rotator cuff tears (degeneration group, trauma group); CAL and rotator cuff tear were assessed by magnetic resonance imaging in patients with rotator cuff tears (degeneration group, trauma group) as shown in Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4. Studies assessing shoulder MRI in torn patients have shown that the average coracoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion is 3.58 ± 1.02 mm (see Table 1), and that greater degrees of rotator cuff tear are associated with less coracoacromial ligament thickness [3] (see Table 2), suggesting that MRI is a reliable tool for assessing coracoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion [4]. MRI has the advantages of high resolution and multi-plane MRI, it can clearly show the morphology and structure of coracoacromial ligament, accurately measure its thickness, and provide reliable data basis for further research.

Relationship between rotator cuff tear and coracoacromial ligament thickness in patients with degenerative rotator cuff tear.

Increased levels of degenerative rotator cuff tears have been found to be associated with decreased coracoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion (see Table 3). This may be related to the pathophysiology of degenerative rotator cuff tears. This result supports the hypothesis that “secondary ligament degeneration”: Prolonged subacromial impingement and mechanical friction may lead to ligament fibrosis or atrophy, accelerating rotator cuff degeneration. With increasing age and long-term use of the shoulder, the rotator cuff tissue gradually undergo degenerative changes, such as fiber rupture, fatty infiltration, etc., leading to rotator cuff stability decreased. At the same time, coracoacromial ligament as a stable structure of shoulder, under the action of long-term bearing abnormal stress, degenerative changes may also occur, manifested as decreased ligament thickness. This change may further affect the mechanical balance of the shoulder and aggravate the degree of rotator cuff tear.

Relationship between rotator cuff tear and coracoacromial ligament thickness in traumatic injuries.

Unlike degenerative rotator cuff tears, changes in the degree of traumatic rotator cuff tears do not correlate with coracoacromial ligament thickness at the inferior surface of the acromion (see Table 3). Traumatic rotator cuff tears are usually caused by acute trauma, such as landing on the hand, impingement on the shoulder, etc. during a fall. In this case, coracoacromial ligament injury may not be the primary cause, and the degree of rotator cuff tear depends more on the severity and mode of action of the injury.

The findings of this study further elucidate the mechanism of injury in rotator cuff tears. The development of rotator cuff tears is a progressive process that is closely associated with degenerative changes and mechanical imbalance in the shoulder. Decreased coracoacromial ligament thickness may be an important factor in the development and progression of degenerative rotator cuff tears. The acromioacromial ligament is triangular and connects between the lateral margin of the coracoid process and the anterior margin of the acromion apex. The anterior part is thicker, the middle part is very thin, and the membrane shape. This ligament forms the coracoacromial arch and has the function of preventing medial-superior dislocation of the humeral head. Biomechanical data suggest that the coracoacromial ligament acts as a dynamic delivery scaffold between the coracoid process and acromion rather than a static constraint on the upward migration of the humerus, which plays an important role in shoulder biomechanics. When the biomechanical stability of the shoulder joint is compromised, coracoacromial ligament degeneration can be induced, which can aggravate the shoulder joint pathology. Degeneration of the coracoacromial ligament has been shown to be more severe in anterosuperior extensive rotator cuff tears [5]. Radiographic parameters did not correlate with Bonar scores for CAL degeneration, indicating that CAL degeneration was secondary to anterosuperior rotator cuff tears. This phenomenon implies that instability of the humeral head after rotator cuff tear, rather than the acromial bone structure, results in chronic wear of the CAL. Degeneration of the rotator ligament is also considered a source of pain in coracoid impingement syndromes and is a factor in external impingement of the rotator cuff tear on the side of the bursa.

The suturing procedure involves performing acromioplasty to address factors that affect impingement, such as the results of this study showing that acromiohumeral distance (AHD) decreases as the degree of rotator cuff tear increases (see Table 1), but controversy remains as to when acromioplasty is performed and how CALs are managed in acromioplasty because of the increased risk of anterior and superior displacement of the shoulder after acromioplasty. Some clinical studies also suggest that sacrifice of the coracoacromial ligament may lead to shoulder instability, which questions the benefit of acromioplasty [6]. Subacromioclavicular decompression has been shown to increase the risk of shoulder stiffness following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, as extensive synovial tissue, coracoacromial ligament resection, and capsular injury alter the normal biomechanics of the shoulder, leading to excessive scar formation and adhesions; this indicates that surgeons do not recommend subacromial decompression unless necessary [7]. Patients were further randomized into two groups according to different grades of CAL degeneration, and those with a CAL grade III showed better improvement in shoulder function after acromioplasty or CAL release, probably because anterolateral acromioplasty helps to reduce gliding resistance of the subscapular tendon [8]. However, for patients with grades II and I (mild degeneration group), there was no significant difference between the acromioplasty and non-acromioplasty groups. Therefore, the degree of CAL degeneration should be considered as a key factor in determining whether to perform acromioplasty. How is the degree of subacromial CAL release defined? When performing acromioplasty, the coracoacromial ligament (CAL) should be spared as much as possible to avoid anterosuperior instability, and if the coracoacromial ligament is thickened, careful debridement rather than complete resection should be performed [9]. Anatomical study of a fully released CAL pattern [10]: starting at the lateral edge of the acromion insertion, CAL fibers were removed in a region of about 16 mm medio-lateral dimension and 15 mm antero-posterior direction [11]. Removal of two-thirds of the coracoacromial ligaments during subacromial debridement eliminates the risk factors for rotator cuff tears due to degeneration of the coracoacromial ligament, while the remaining coracoacromial ligaments maintain the original function of inhibiting upward movement of the humeral head, the study states. Even in the modified Latarjet procedure [12], investigators consider reconstruction of the coracoacromial ligament with a pectoralis minor myofascial flap, sufficient to illustrate the importance of preserving the coracoacromial ligament. The most recent investigators reported [13] that the degree of degeneration of the coracoacromial ligament did not affect the rate of supraspinatus tendon re-tear even in the absence of acromioplasty, recommending a more cautious approach to CAL release and acromioplasty.

Traumatic rotator cuff tears are mainly caused by acute trauma and have little relationship with coracoacromial ligament thickness. Understanding these injury mechanisms will help clinicians to better understand the development of rotator cuff tears and provide the basis for individualized treatment planning.

This study has several limitations. First, intraoperative arthroscopic changes in coracoacromial ligament thickness were compared with radiographic changes. Second, measurement of ligament thickness may be influenced by MRI resolution and patient positioning, and future needs to be combined with intraoperative measurements or ultrasound dynamic assessment to improve accuracy. In addition, no healthy control group was included in this study and subsequent studies allowed further comparison of ligament thickness differences between normal and patients. Finally, the manuscript lacks comprehensive information on the factors influencing CAL based on demographic characteristics, as it does not include patient height, weight, and the time from preoperative MRI to surgery. In future research, I will collect relevant data for evaluation.

Conclusion

MRI is a reliable tool for assessing coracoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion; increased degree of degenerative rotator cuff tears is associated with decreased thickness of the coracoacromial ligament on the inferior surface of the acromion; changes in the degree of traumatic rotator cuff tears are not associated with coracoacromial ligament thickness on the inferior surface of the acromion. These findings provide further insight into the mechanism of injury in rotator cuff tears.
