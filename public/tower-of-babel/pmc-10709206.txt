A step towards food security: The effect of carbon emission and the moderating influence of human capital. Evidence from Anglophone countries
Authors: Edem Segbefia, Baozhen Dai, Philip Adotey, Agyemang Kwasi Sampene
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the influence of carbon emission, population growth, economic growth and human capital on food security (FOS) among five selected African nations (Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Zimbabwe, and Tanzania) by analyzing panel data from 1990 to 2021. Moreover, the study evaluated the moderation effect of human capital on the connection between carbon emission and food security. The study's initial findings confirm that the countries selected were interdependent, and the models' parameters were stationary and co-integrated. The empirical analysis from the cross-sectionally augmented autoregressive distributed lag (CS-ARDL) indicated that (1) carbon emission and population growth have an inverse connection with FOS. (2) In contrast, the results revealed that human capital and economic growth improve FOS. (3) The study outcome proved that human capital moderates the interplay between carbon emission and food security. This result means that the association between carbon emission and food security can be enhanced through the iterative role of human capital. (4) The causality analysis revealed a unidirectional connection between economic growth, population growth, human capital and food security. (5) A bidirectional causality exists between carbon emission and food security. The current analysis enriches literature studies on FOS and environment nexus by providing new insight from the five selected African countries. The study proposes an investment into human capital by African countries because it can complement and solidify the interaction between carbon emissions and food security.Keywords: Carbon emission, Food security, Human capital, Population growth, Economic growth

Introduction

In recent decades, developing nations' economic stability and food security have been hampered by carbon emissions (CEM) and ecological pollution [1]. As indicated by Ref. [2], one of the most prevalent and immediate concerns of this era is the issue of food security (FOS). The COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in an economic recession in various countries and has also worsened the hunger situation in the world. It has been estimated that by 2030, there may be over 800 million undernourished people around the globe [2]. Moreover, FOS is recognized as one of the most prevalent topics and a growing concern for stakeholders, government and scholars [3]. asserted that almost 8.9 % of the world's population was estimated to have FOS challenges, resulting in hunger. Moreover [2], indicated that the number of undernourished individuals globally is projected to rise between 83 and 132 million, and by 2030 it could reach about 840 million people. Dwelling it on Africa [4], recounted that, on average, more than 20 % of the population on this continent are confronted with FOS issues.

A recent report by the [5] revealed that for almost three years consecutively, African nations have been experiencing severe food and nutrition insecurity issues, with more than 23 million people needing food relief to survive. In addition, it is anticipated that nearly 33.4 million individuals will experience a worse situation, such as hunger and malnutrition, owing to the FOS challenges African economies face. This situation has worsened due to unexpected problems such as the Ukraine and Russia wars, political unrest and ecological destruction experienced by the African countries [3,6]. Hence, to mitigate such issues, the World Bank has responded to the FOS crises and made significant progress towards achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (thus SDGs 2, 4, and 12) and further realizing the Paris Agreement on CEM [5]. The world bank provided support programs to help improve FOS by providing climate-smart agricultural support projects to some Africa nations. These projects result in increased food production and enhanced food system resilience to climate alteration. Therefore, this study evaluates the effect of CEM on FOS in the selected African nations. Despite the efforts by international bodies such as the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Food Programme (WFP), the issue of FOS in African countries continues to be prolonged. This calls for scientific research to evaluate the possible cause of food insecurity and provide strategies that stakeholders and policymakers can implement to provide a long-lasting solution to this menace. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the effect of CEM in the form of CO2 hurts people's health, ecology, energy, urbanization, economic growth and agricultural productivity [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. All these challenges tragically threaten FOS and food supply systems [12].

Human capital is crucial in ensuring food security at both individual and societal levels. At the individual level, human capital, which encompasses education, skills, knowledge, and health, empowers individuals to make informed choices about nutrition and agricultural practices [13]. Well-educated and skilled individuals are better equipped to adopt sustainable farming techniques, manage resources efficiently, and adapt to changing environmental conditions, all essential for food production and distribution [14]. Furthermore [15], noted that a healthy and educated workforce is more productive, contributing to increased agricultural output and economic growth, which are fundamental drivers of food security at the national and global levels. Additionally, at the societal level, investments in education and healthcare systems can enhance a nation's capacity to address food security challenges, such as food distribution, policy formulation, and response to food crises [16]. Human capital is a linchpin in the complex web of factors influencing food security, shaping our ability to produce, access, and sustainably manage food resources in an ever-changing world.

The trade-off between economic growth (ECG) and FOS is the most challenging issue for emerging economies such as African countries. As a result, it is critical to consider effective mechanisms that can help these nations achieve higher FOS and sustainable development. Thus, a crucial question that needs to be addressed is how African Countries might improve ECG to enhance the level of FOS on the continent. To address this question, the present study highlights the long-run effect of ECG on FOS with a panel data set from 1990 to 2021. The study is distinctive in that it merges three FOS parameters (food availability, food accessibility and food utilization) into a single research model to extend prior studies [2,17,18]. In addition, the study evaluates the impact of population and economic growth on food security. Finally, the moderation role of human capital is examined.

Moreover, the globe's population growth (POG) is increasing by nearly 1.1 % yearly, and the projections from the United Nations revealed that the world's population will reach almost 9.7 billion by 2050 [19]. In addition [19], further indicated that more than half of the global population increases are anticipated in Africa. With these statistics, a vast demand for food is expected to increase in the coming years, especially in Africa [20].enunciated that matching the rapid rise in population and the higher demand for food in socially and environmentally sustainable ways is among the global challenges [21]. reported that nearly 20 % of the African populace is undernourished. Thus, the issue of food availability remains a crucial concern on the continent, given the upsurge in POG and poverty levels. Recent estimates by the [21,22] indicated that food insecurity rose from 210.7 to 277 million between 2014 and 2018, affecting nearly 21.5 % of the population in Africa. An empirical research conducted by Ref. [23] found an inverse connection between POG and food production in Nigeria. The authors argued that emerging nations with higher population growth will likely face challenges such as FOS and lower agricultural production growth rates. Therefore, it is imperative to explore the effect of POG on FOS in Africa. Accordingly, this study examines the impact of POG on FOS among the five selected Africa countries.

FOS is a critical issue affecting humanity's sustainability [24]—the recent drought and heatwave on the African continent evidences CEM. Moreover, CEM has substantially influenced agricultural produce, extreme FOS issues, and poverty [2,17]. The thematic themes of FOS are based on food availability, which involves the possibility of a group of people receiving food via import or domestic production of sufficient quantities. In comparison, food accessibility denotes households accessing a cluster of resources (entitlements) to attain healthy foods. In addition, food utilization refers to the possibility of individuals' families maximizing the efficient usage of the food and water obtained, sanitation, and health care that meet their physiological needs [18,25]. The research by Refs. [1,[25], [26], [27]] highlighted the effect of CEM on FOS; however, there is a gap in empirical studies that can verify this connection. As a result, to close this literature gap, the current analysis contributes to knowledge by employing an econometric approach to evaluate the impact of CEM on FOS. In addition [28], there is a need to explore factors such as sustainable economic development and human capital development trends on the connection between FOS and CEM. Their research further revealed a literature gap on how to strike a balance between mitigating carbon emissions and ensuring food security. Therefore, in response to this call, the current analysis provides a framework that explores the iteration effect of HCI in improving the connection between CEM and FOS in Africa.

The concept of human capital (HCI) has begun to gain momentum in environmental studies. For instance Ref. [29], revealed that most African countries are experiencing higher levels of ecological footprint and CEM issues due to a higher rate of unawareness of environmental matters. It is reasonable to presume that these countries have not focused more on the role of HCI in dissipating CEM. Under such circumstances, a rise in the HCI level in these economies could be forecasted to help create awareness among farmers for ensuring FOS and the effective exploitation of natural resources. Interestingly, the studies by Ref. [30] found that the HCI levels improve the African continent's ecological stability. In the same vein, several studies have provided empirical outcomes that evaluated the impact of HCI on FOS [[31], [32], [33]]. However, these studies have failed to provide the moderation role of HCI in this association. Hence, this study empirically evaluates how HCI can influence the connection between CEM-FOS.

Against this backdrop, the focal point of this analysis is to evaluate the impact of carbon emission, human capital, population growth and economic development on FOS. In addition, the moderation influence of HCI on the connection between CEM-FOS is empirically tested. This study, therefore, addresses the following questions:

Does carbon emission, population growth and economic development influence FOS among the selected African countries?

Does human capital moderate the association between FOS and CEM among the selected African countries?

What is the nexus between these variables regarding causality, and what steps should decision-makers take to improve FOS and avert carbon emission in the selected African countries?

The novelty of the current analysis is that it presents a new stream of empirical research that considers the moderating role of HCI on the connection between CEM and FOS. Thus, the study investigates how African nations can improve FOS through investment in HCI. The study's findings highlight that these countries can develop and implement strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of extreme weather events, such as droughts and floods, on agricultural productivity through HCI. Furthermore, HCI can contribute to informed policy formulation and advocate for climate-resilient food systems at the national and regional levels. In essence, human capital in Africa is a pivotal bridge between the pressing issues of carbon emissions and food security, offering the knowledge and skills necessary to navigate the complexities of sustainability and climate resilience in the agricultural sector. In addition, another major strength of this study is that it employs the latest econometric estimation approach that considers the concerns of cross-sectional dependency (CSD) that might affect the robustness of the study data. Theoretically and practically, this study provides the following significant contribution: First, the research enriches studies on FOS and carbon emission by incorporating the role of human capital in strengthening this association. Thus, this study contends that a well-educated and skilled workforce can drive innovation and the adoption of sustainable agricultural practices that minimize carbon emissions associated with food production. These individuals can implement technologies like precision farming, crop rotation, and sustainable land management, reducing the environmental footprint and enhancing crop yields and food production efficiency.

In addition, it is essential to note that prior studies have explored the moderation effect of HCI on food security. For instance Ref. [34], investigated the influence of HCI on food insecurity on the status of rural households in Nigeria. Their research findings indicated HCI measured with education, age, age-squared, body mass index, and education have an inverse connection with food insecurity. In addition [35], concluded that as HCI improves, FOS also enhances. Therefore, this research is distinctive and fills gaps in the literature because the current analysis recognizes that the interplay between environmental factors and human resources is crucial in understanding the dynamics of food security. By focusing on Anglophone countries, the study comprehensively analyses regions sharing a common language. It facilitates cross-country comparisons and the potential for policy recommendations tailored to this group of nations. Moreover, the novelty of this research lies in its potential to bridge the gap between environmental sustainability and socio-economic development. By examining how human capital can moderate the effects of carbon emissions on food security, it highlights the role of education and skill enhancement as drivers of economic growth and as instruments to mitigate the adverse consequences of climate change on agriculture. This interdisciplinary approach brings together insights from environmental science, economics, and education policy, offering a holistic understanding of the complexities of ensuring food security in the face of global environmental challenges. Ultimately, this research has the potential to inform policy decisions and interventions that promote sustainable agriculture and enhance human capital development to safeguard the food security of Anglophone nations and beyond.

Moreover, they can facilitate the transition towards cleaner energy sources and develop climate-resilient crops, simultaneously addressing carbon emissions and food security concerns. Second, the study further improves the existing stock of ideas and knowledge by analyzing the effects of economic growth, population growth and carbon emission, for which there are scanty studies on the five African countries. Previous research has paid chiefly attention to Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa (BRICS) economies [36,37]; Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria and Turkey (MINT) [[38], [39], [40]], European Union (EU) [41,42]. Hence, this research provides new insight into how POG, ECG, HCI and CEM affect FOS. Third, the research offers practical insight and policy measures to improve FOS among these five African countries. Fourth, the susceptibility of African countries to CEM requires timely implementation of approaches to curb this menace. Global efforts to mitigate climate change and its impacts have yielded little benefit in Africa [43]. Natural disasters, conflicts, deterioration of the environment, a lack of infrastructure, finance, markets, and technology, as well as the effects of climate change, are currently impeding food productivity across the continent [44]. Although the continent commands almost 60 % of the globe's uncultivated arable land, Africa contributes about 10 % to global food production [44]. noted that the severity of the low agricultural productivity in Africa is exacerbated by rapid POG coupled with CEM and reduced workforce in commercial farming. Hence, this situation requires a sustainable solution to help improve FOS on the continent. This research, therefore, provides stringent policies and strategies that can help improve FOS.

Literature review

This study highlights some empirical findings that have analyzed the association among the study parameters. The association among all the three indicators of CEM, POG, ECG and the moderation effect of HCI are discussed.

Carbon emission and food security nexus

CEM fluctuation influences agricultural practices, especially crop cultivation and farm animal production [45]. Moreover [46], argues that annual rainfall and variation exacerbate food shortages by reducing per capita food production and maximizing the number of malnourished people. CEM has decreased the world's food supply by causing an international cereal disparity [47]. Thus, a comprehensive evaluation of crop cultivation in Africa and Asia revealed that food production could fall by 8 % by 2050 due to CEM factors, which could eventually limit FOS [48,49]. Furthermore, the rise of new pathogens in cereal crops, like wheat explosions, due to CEM poses a risk to agriculturalists and endangers their food production, possibly affecting FOS [50,51]. Hence, the study anticipates that CEM will positively affect FOS [52]. describes food accessibility as the resources and priorities that allow individuals to efficiently convert their food cravings into wants. CEM influences adequate entitlements such as legal and political backing, social and economic arrangements, and the like, affecting inland food production and agricultural imports [53]. observed that domestic food supplies in several nations primarily depend on international food markets, but extreme CEM factors influence food products at the local and international levels. Extant studies discovered that rising temperatures and rainfall negatively affect stipulated entitlement that promotes net cereal income. Reduced food production exacerbates the resources for poverty and malnourished households to access nutritious food in rural and city areas [25,54]. Additionally, extreme CEM events can disrupt the distribution of foodstuffs, culminating in price hikes and a decline in poor people's survival, particularly in low-income nations [55]. As a result, assigning a large portion of earnings to food supplies will adversely influence people's buying power. This situation will further result in global deprivation and food insecurity, which high food prices would exacerbate, weather extremes, variation, and relatively small accessibility to food [26,55]. Hence, this study posits that CEM threatens FOS.

Population growth and food security nexus

Increased urbanization and the transformation of agricultural land into urban areas are frequent consequences of POG. As confirmed by Ref. [56], unsustainable farming methods can also harm the soil and lower agricultural output. Food security depends on striking a balance between the requirement for agricultural land and urban expansion. According to Ref. [25], the effects of climate change, which can cause unpredictable rainfall patterns, droughts, and floods, are hazardous for Africa. FOS problems can be made worse by several environmental issues that disrupt agricultural output and food supply chains. It may be difficult for African nations with greater income inequality to guarantee that all the populace can access a sufficient and nourishing meal [57]. Food insecurity can be exacerbated by poverty and inequality. Regarding the studies of [58], adequate food storage and transportation are essential for ensuring food security. POG increases may burden the infrastructure already in place, making it difficult to supply food efficiently to all locations, particularly distant ones [59]. Several social, economic, and environmental factors in Africa influence POG and food security. A comprehensive strategy incorporating sustainable farming methods, infrastructure development, poverty reduction, and climate resilience techniques is needed [60]. The economies of Africa are intertwined, and trading in agricultural products is essential for guaranteeing food security [61]. Trade agreements and regional collaboration can help reduce food price volatility and increase food access. As postulated by Ref. [62] adaptive methods are needed to increase resilience to the effects of POG growth on FOS This entails developing resilient crop varieties, putting money into irrigation systems, and taking precautions against disasters.

Economic growth and food security nexus

As illustrated by Ref. [63], the creation of more reliable and effective food delivery systems is frequently a result of ECG. This can provide a more consistent food supply and help stabilize food costs. Unchecked ECG may raise costs and restrict access to food, but it can also result in market concentration and monopolistic behaviour [64]. ECG can cause rural-to-urban migration as people move to cities for better job prospects. The availability of agricultural labour may be disrupted by this migration, thereby hurting food production. Urban areas can simultaneously see an increase in food demand [58]. Increasing investments in market, storage, and transportation infrastructure are frequently associated with economic growth. Regarding the investigation of [65], a more effective infrastructure can lower food losses in storage and during transportation, ensuring that more food reaches customers and promoting FOS. If native production cannot meet demand, nations may become more reliant on food imports as they develop [59]. Due to this, countries may become more susceptible to changes in global prices and supplies, which could impact food security. As stated by Ref. [66], ECG and FOS can be mediated by government programs and social safety nets. To safeguard vulnerable populations from food insecurity, governments should implement policies that aid smallholder farmers, encourage sustainable agricultural methods, and create safety net programs [67]. Environmental problems can worsen, and economic expansion can increase natural resource demand. Climate change can interfere with food supply systems and production [68]. Sustainable ECG strategies should consider environmental preservation and adaptation to reduce these hazards. A balanced approach that takes into account income distribution, agricultural productivity, market dynamics, infrastructural development, and social well-being is necessary to achieve sustained economic growth that improves food security.

Moderation effect of human capital

These studies have documented HCI's role in improving ecological sustainability [69,70]. In addition [71], established a direct and significant connection between HCI and environmental quality enhancement. Their conclusion is supported by the fact that HCI has the potential to reduce reliance on fossil fuels and thus improve ecological quality while having no adverse effect on economic development and food production [72]. Moreover, HCI offers economic growth with a creative and unique workforce, which ultimately helps the economy boost its FOS and environmental benefits [73]. Furthermore [69], revealed that the extent of HCI a person has could also significantly affect their choices for agricultural products, renewable energy, compliance with CEM policies, and ensuring environmental sustainability. This outcome, notwithstanding, contradicts the findings of [72,73], who discovered that HCI causes carbon emissions in developed economies in which education programmes do not incorporate environmental protection content. Hence, this analysis expands these studies by empirically testing how HCI can shape the interaction between FOS and CEM [[74], [75]]. Thus, the study examines the moderation influence of HCI on the association between CEM-FOS among the selected African countries.

Data and method

Study area

The current analysis is based on annual panel data from five Anglophone economies among the selected African countries from 1990 to 2020. These five Anglophone countries, which include Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Zimbabwe, and Tanzania, were considered for this study because of the serious effect of CEM on their level of agricultural production in recent times. As indicated by Ref. [25], these Anglophone economies have experienced the impact of CEM on crop yield, precision farming, weather patterns, increased temperature, disease and pest invasion, water security and poverty. The repercussions of the unfavourable CEM have had a more significant impact on rural agricultural production and natural resources in these economies [3,25,76].

Theoretical framework

The dependent variable in this analysis is food security. To measure FOS, the study used a yearly food production index. Theoretically, prior studies have asserted that FOS is not just about food production and supply; it is also connected with other factors, including food quality, economic growth, purchasing power, population growth and CEM [1,27]. Hence, describing and understanding the nature of these indicators is essential; if overlooked, it will imply FOS issues. These studies applied these parameters and proxy to measure FOS [2,17,77]. The annual CO2 emission of each country was utilized as the proxy for CEM in this study. The research by [78] highlighted that CO2 emissions are mainly accountable for almost 75 % of global environmental deterioration and damage. Furthermore, CO2 emission has been resorted to as a measure for CEM by several erstwhile studies [9,79,80]. Hence, this study anticipates an inverse connection between CEM and FOS. This research measured economic growth with the gross domestic product (GDP) metrics captured by the values of a nation's goods and services produced within a specific period. Thus, growth in these metrics and figures over time indicaterevealed, the concept of HCI is acqs economic expansion. It is crucial to consider how the ECG of these nations can translate to higher FOS and standard of living. Prior studies have suggested that improved ECG positively influence FOS [21,81]. Accordingly, this research predicts a positive association between ECG and FOS. The study also examines the effect of POG on FOS. POP accounts for the total number of citizens living in a nation at a specific period. Moreover, the interplay between POG and FOS is crucial; while a growing population can strain resources, well-planned and managed demographic changes can lead to increased market potential. A balanced approach involves monitoring these metrics individually and analyzing their interdependencies to develop policies that promote sustainable population growth and FOS, ensuring that the needs of current and future generations are met. Hence, based on prior studies [19,21,81], this study incorporated the effect of POG in the theoretical model. HCI is considered one of the production system's most essential and fundamental concepts [30]. As [82] revealed, the concept of HCI is acquired through education, pro-environmental behaviour and environmental awareness of individuals that can enable them to promote ecological sustainability. Thus, HCI can aid citizens in engaging in green behaviour, adopting ecologically friendly tools, and using energy efficiency practices at the workplace and in the household [31,83]. Hence, this study argues that HCI moderates the interaction between CEM-FOS. The theoretical model of this study is depicted in Fig. 1.

Long-term estimation approach

The present analysis applied the CS-ARDL approach, a novel estimation technique to predict panel data's short- and long-term estimates [94]. Prior studies have employed this estimation approach and have concluded that it is a reliable and robust method for assessing panel data [70,95]. The CS-ARDL produce accurate outcome because it helps overcome CSD and heterogeneity issues among the candidate series of the research [96]. One of its primary advantages is its flexibility in accommodating short-term and long-term relationships in economic and financial data. CS-ARDL allows researchers to capture dynamic interactions between variables, making it particularly useful for forecasting and policy analysis. Furthermore, it addresses issues of endogeneity and non-stationarity, common challenges in empirical research, by applying a differencing approach and providing robust coefficient estimates [97,98]. Additionally, CS-ARDL is versatile in handling various data types, making it applicable in various fields, from economics to finance and environmental studies. Its ability to provide robust, interpretable results while accommodating the complexities of real-world data makes the CS-ARDL method a valuable asset in empirical research and decision-making processes [99]. The CS-ARDL (Model 1) findings highlight that an upsurge in CEM significantly causes a decline in FOS among the selected African countries. More specifically, a 1 % rise in CEM caused a decrease in FOS by 0.725 % in the long-case scenario and 0.697 % in the short-run. The inference from this outcome is that the five African Countries' FOS level is inversely affected by temperature alterations and extreme weather conditions. Hence, the level of food production, crop production and cereal yield is gradually falling due to the rise in CEM. This outcome supports [2] that the CEM inversely impacts FOS. Similarly [18], expressed their concerns that peasant farmers in Ghana should expect to experience higher levels of food insecurity because of the escalation in global warming. In addition [17], concluded that FOS in the Eastern and Northern African economies are negatively affected by CEM. Moreover, burning fossil fuels such as coal and oil and manufacturing and commercial operations causes air pollution and water [[104], [105]]. Such combustions increase weather conditions during the planting season, directly impacting FOS [105,106]. The troposphere ozone is hazardous to crop output among the various air pollutants. The rapid increase in CO2 emissions is expected to have a negative impact on crop yield and food quality in the following decades, posing a significant threat to FOS [19,107]. Furthermore, other findings have revealed that the CEM inversely impacts FOS [25,54,55].

In addition, the study investigated the influence of POG on FOS. The empirical findings from the research discovered that POG has an adverse effect on FOS among the selected Africa nations. More specifically, the results enunciated that a 1 % rise in POG will lead to a decline in food security by 0.358 % in the long-term and 0.697 in the long-term (Model 1). The inference from this outcome is that as the population increases in these countries, it goes a long way to affect the decrease in food security. The possible explanation for this outcome is that as these economies experience a rapidly increasing population, the demand for food also escalates. This puts immense pressure on agricultural resources and infrastructure, often leading to over-exploitation of arable land, soil degradation, and decreased agricultural productivity. With limited access to modern farming techniques and technologies, erratic climate patterns and inadequate infrastructure, these African economies face challenges in meeting the nutritional needs of their growing population. Moreover [108], suggested the unequal distribution of wealth and resources exacerbates food insecurity as marginalized communities struggle to access food and participate in the agricultural sector. Addressing these complex issues, including improving agricultural practices, investing in infrastructure, and promoting sustainable resource management, becomes imperative to mitigate the adverse impact of population growth on food security in Africa. The findings from this study align with [19,21,81].

Additionally, the empirical findings evidenced that economic growth positively affects FOS. Thus, a 1 % rise in ECG tends to improve FOS by 0.615 % in the long run and 0.550 % in the short-term (Model 1). This discovery may be driven by the fact that when a nation's economy expands, it often increases its citizens' income levels. This, in turn, gives people greater purchasing power, allowing them to afford nutritious food and essential agricultural inputs like seeds, fertilizers, and modern farming equipment. ECG can also stimulate investment in the agricultural sector, leading to improved farming practices, increased productivity, and more efficient food distribution systems. Additionally, it can facilitate investments in infrastructure, such as roads, storage facilities, and irrigation systems, which are essential for reducing post-harvest losses and ensuring food reaches consumers promptly. Overall, the empirical outcome has demonstrated that economic growth can enhance food access and strengthen communities' resilience, thereby positively impacting food security in Africa. This study's outcome aligns with these prior studies [21,81].

The analysis further evaluated the effect of HCI on FOS; the empirical findings indicated that HCI enhances FOS. Thus, a 1 % rise in HCI will improve FOS in the five selected African countries by 0.284 % in the long-run and 0.411 in the short-run (Model 1). Therefore, this finding supports prior studies that indicated HCI in the form of education and environmental awareness of the citizens could enhance FOS [70,109,110]. However, the findings coincide with [111], whose analysis proposes that a low level of HCI, including an unskilled labour force and inadequate educational level, can cause a decline in FOS. The study further analyzed the moderation effect of HCI on the connection between CEM and FOS. The outcome of the iterative impact of HCI has been presented in Table 6 (Model 2). The outcome from the CS-ARDL model revealed that HCI significantly and positively moderates the interplay between CEM and FOS by 0.594 % in the long-run and 0.780 % in the short run. In other words, HCI can strengthen the association between FOS and CEM. Hence, the improvement in HCI can offset the impact of CEM on FOS in the selected African countries. This conclusion is based on the claim that HCI can reduce dependence on fossil fuels and encourage sustainable energy sources in agricultural techniques, which can lower CEM. HCI improves the knowledge and ecological awareness of farmers in the labour force, enabling them to use the resource efficiently to increase their crop yields without causing any damage to the environment [30,[112], [113], [114].

The outcome of the short-run elasticity from the CS-ARDL approach indicated similar findings to those outlined by the long-term estimates. The research further explored the model's error correction term (EMT). The statistical value of the EMT 0.886 % exhibits that modifications made in previous decades can be reversed in later periods in the selected African countries. Hence, this finding implies that a low speed of alteration to the steady state results from slight changes to the EMT value. This demonstrates that in the study model, when the explanatory parameter FOS, the correction rate towards stability is relatively rapid at 88.6 %. It is possible to eliminate imbalance and fast return to normal. Moreover, the R2 values of 0.93 % and adjusted R2 of 0.94 % also indicate that the study model is accurate. Furthermore, Table 7 illustrates the robustness test via two models (CC-ME and AMG) to confirm the outcome of the CS-ARDL model. Conclusions and policy implications

Conclusions

The current study has devised an econometric framework for evaluating the influence of CEM, ECG, POG and HCI on FOS by employing panel data from 1990 to 2021. The study further analyzed the iteration effect of HCI on the connection between CEM and FOS. A preliminary examination, which consists of the CSD test, stationarity test, and cointegration analysis, was examined before estimating the short- and long-term estimators. The study's initial findings confirm that the countries selected were interdependent, and the models' parameters were stationary and co-integrated. The empirical analysis from the CS-ARDL indicated that: (1) The outcome of the study revealed that CEM and POG cause a decline in FOS. This implies that CEM and POG cause more damage to the level of FOS in the selected five African countries. (2) Additionally, HCI has a beneficial effect on FOS in the selected African countries. (3) The study outcome proved that HCI moderates the interplay between CEM and FOS. This result means that the association between CEM and FOS can be enhanced through the iterative role of HCI. (5) Lastly, unidirectional causality flows from EGC, POG, and HCI to FOS and a bidirectional between CEM and FOS.

Policy implications

In light of the findings of this investigation, the following proposed policy implication is made:

First, the research findings proved that CEM is a significant cause of a decline in FOS; therefore, this study suggests the following measures. Investing in agricultural mechanization is one of the essential mechanisms through which the impact of CEM on FOS can be mitigated in the five selected African countries. Most of these countries' local farmers still rely on outdate equipment, fossil fuel and manual power for farming activities. Hence, this study proposed that governments in these countries can make an effort to provide incentives and loan facilities to farmers to purchase agricultural mechanization equipment that can help them produce higher food production yields and simultaneously reduce the effect of CEM on FOS. In addition, the study recommends tree-planting initiatives in these economies. Tree planting is suggested as a step towards neutralizing CEM effects on the environment. and FOS. Hence, these economies must promote actions such as replanting, resisting deforestation and improving afforestation projects. Thus, restoring the forest areas and landscapes is a better response to mitigating CEM. The promotion of research and development is also vital. Therefore, the government should task higher institutions and provide them with financial assistance to investigate how CEM affects food production and yield reduction. Another key policy recommendation is an investment in irrigational projects. State of the art regarding irrigational systems in most African countries is outdated and in poor condition. In juxtaposition to advanced countries, agricultural production in the selected African countries mostly depends on the natural ecosystem. Therefore, strategic decisions should be taken by policymakers and the government to extend financial support to local farmers to purchase modern irrigation system equipment. This equipment can enhance the water management approach to help combat rainfall variability and off shortages in selected African countries.

Secondly, the study outcome captured that ECG contributes to a high FOS in the selected African countries. Several recommendations can be considered to harness the positive impact of economic growth on food security in Africa. (i) Governments and international organizations should prioritize investments in agricultural research and development, extension services, and technology adoption to boost productivity and resilience in the agricultural sector. (ii) Policies that promote inclusive economic growth, such as poverty reduction programs, access to credit for small-scale farmers, and support for rural infrastructure development, should be implemented to ensure that the benefits of economic growth are shared across society. (iii) Trade and market access should be improved to enable African countries to participate in global food markets, enhancing the potential for export earnings and import diversification. (iv) Efforts to mitigate the impact of climate change on agriculture, such as promoting sustainable farming practices and investing in climate-resilient crops, are essential to safeguard food security gains achieved through economic growth. By pursuing these strategies, African nations can capitalize on economic growth as a catalyst for improved food security and overall well-being for their populations.

Third, several recommendations are crucial to address the challenges posed by population growth's inverse impact on food security in Africa. First, there must be a concerted effort to invest in modern and sustainable agricultural practices, including improved irrigation systems, crop diversification, and resilient crop varieties. (i) Stakeholders should focus on education and family planning programs to promote responsible family size and empower women with the knowledge and access to reproductive health services, which can help slow population growth rates. (iii) Plans and programs should be developed to reduce post-harvest losses and improve food distribution systems, ensuring that the food produced reaches those in need efficiently. Additionally, fostering economic opportunities beyond agriculture, such as job creation in other sectors, can help alleviate the pressure on land and resources caused by population growth. (iv) International cooperation and support for food security initiatives in Africa must provide technical expertise and resources to help countries address these interconnected challenges effectively.

Lastly, the research proved that HCI is critical in mitigating the effect of CEM on FOS. The study, therefore, proposes that authorities should embark on environmental education initiatives from the early stages of the educational sector in these countries since HCI has a beneficial impact on both FOS and reduction in CEM. In addition, policymakers and stakeholders should encourage higher institutions to introduce the concept and ideology of green farming because acquiring knowledge on environmental awareness can be instrumental in promoting ecological stability. Due to this, plans and strategies designed to increase environmental sustainability should be enacted, and proposals should be launched to inform the general population about the climate via higher education institutions and non-governmental agencies. Moreover, farmers in these countries should be trained to use environmentally friendly equipment, cleaner energy sources and smart technology to improve their farm produce and, simultaneously, improve environmental sustainability.

Limitations and future research

There are some limitations of this analysis that future research can address. First, the study data was limited to only five African countries member countries for which future studies can expand to all the other nations in this economic bloc. Second, future analysis can evaluate the effect of the ecological footprint on FOS since some studies have indicated that carbon emission denotes a small portion of the complex issues with environmental pollution that rising nations must deal with. In addition, this study did not analyze the analysis among the cross-sections of the individual countries examined. Furthermore, theoretical concepts such as the Environment Kuznets Curve (EKC) and other environmental theories are missing in the study. Therefore, future studies might concentrate on these constraints and setbacks to add a new empirical and theoretical contribution to the environment and FOS literature.
