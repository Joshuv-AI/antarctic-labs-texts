Robotic knee arthroplasty: systems, alignment strategies, and future directions
Authors: Christos Koutserimpas, Luca Andriollo, Pietro Gregori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic knee arthroplasty has shifted attention from whether a surgical plan can be executed accurately to which plan should be selected for each patient. This Collection examines robotic platforms, alignment strategies, safety, outcomes, and the evidence needed to translate technical precision into meaningful clinical benefit.

Robotic assistance has moved rapidly from a niche technology to an increasingly prominent part of knee arthroplasty. Its central promise is compelling: patient-specific planning, reproducible bone preparation, intraoperative assessment of limb alignment and soft-tissue balance, and more controlled execution of the surgical plan [1]. The key question is no longer whether robotic systems improve geometric accuracy, but whether this increased precision translates into clinically meaningful benefit, which patients derive the greatest advantage, and how it ultimately influences function, satisfaction, safety, and implant longevity [1].

The term “robotic knee arthroplasty” encompasses a wide range of technologies. Systems may be image-based or imageless, and active, semi-active, or passive. They differ in preoperative planning, landmark registration, cutting mechanisms, haptic constraints, implant compatibility, and intraoperative workflow. These are not merely technical distinctions [2]. They affect the learning curve, operating-room efficiency, sources of error, registration verification, and the types of complications that may occur. Evidence obtained with one platform should therefore not automatically be extrapolated to another [2].

Most contemporary systems are semi-active. The surgeon remains in direct control of the cutting instrument, while bone preparation is constrained within a planned three-dimensional boundary [2]. This collaborative model can reduce unintended deviation from the plan, but it does not eliminate the need for surgical judgement. Registration errors, movement of tracking arrays, pin-related fractures or infections, soft-tissue injury, technical problems, and workflow-related issues may still occur [1, 2]. Precision is only valuable when the underlying anatomical model is reliable, the plan is appropriate, and the surgeon is prepared to reassess the workflow or convert to conventional instrumentation when necessary.

Robotic technology has also changed the discussion surrounding alignment. Mechanical alignment remains the most established strategy, but robotic platforms allow surgeons to execute kinematic, restricted kinematic, inverse kinematic and functional approaches with a level of reproducibility that may be difficult to achieve with conventional instrumentation [3, 4]. As a result, attention has increasingly shifted from a universal neutral target toward phenotype-based reconstruction. The Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification, Functional Knee Phenotypes, as well as the Dynamic Alignment of the Knee (DyAK) classification, may provide useful frameworks for describing constitutional alignment and joint-line obliquity, and for evaluating whether surgical strategies preserve or modify the native knee phenotype [5–7].

Perhaps the greatest challenge in the current literature is separating the effect of robotic technology from that of alignment philosophy. Robotic systems can reproduce virtually any alignment strategy with high precision, but this does not imply that one alignment concept is superior to another [3]. Because many comparative studies evaluate both variables simultaneously, it remains difficult to determine whether reported differences reflect the robotic platform, the chosen alignment strategy, or an interaction between the two [2]. Furthermore, improvements in early recovery or patient-reported outcomes may become less apparent with longer follow-up, while robust evidence on implant survivorship remains limited for newer robotic systems and personalized alignment strategies. Future studies should therefore compare different alignment philosophies using the same robotic platform, implant, perioperative pathway, and outcome measures, with adequate follow-up and transparent reporting of both planned and achieved component positions.

The economic and organizational context is equally important. Robotic adoption requires capital investment, maintenance, training, operating-room integration, and sufficient procedural volume [1]. These costs must be considered alongside potential reductions in alignment outliers, instrumentation, length of stay, complications, or revision burden. Cost-effectiveness is unlikely to be uniform across healthcare systems. High-volume centers may realize benefits that are not reproducible in lower-volume settings, particularly during early implementation. Learning-curve studies should therefore assess more than operative time [2]. Registration quality, intraoperative plan modifications, abandoned robotic workflows, complications, and the point at which the entire surgical team achieves proficiency are also relevant.

This Collection, “Robotic knee arthroplasty: systems, alignment strategies, and future directions,” brings together these technological, surgical, and clinical perspectives. It welcomes clinical, cadaveric, biomechanical, computational, registry-based, and review studies on robotic knee arthroplasty. Particular areas of interest include comparisons between image-based and imageless systems; active, semi-active, and passive technologies; planning and registration accuracy; implementation and learning curves; alignment strategies and surgical boundaries; soft-tissue balancing; system-specific complications and pitfalls; functional outcomes, patient satisfaction, and survivorship; and economic or organizational analyses. The contributions assembled in this Collection highlight the breadth of questions currently shaping robotic knee arthroplasty, including robot-assisted unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, individualized coronal alignment planning, and postoperative restoration of knee phenotypes [8–10].

The next phase of robotic knee arthroplasty should be defined less by technological enthusiasm and more by methodological rigor. Standardized terminology, platform-specific reporting, meaningful comparator groups, longer follow-up, and outcomes that matter to patients are essential. Robotics makes it possible to execute increasingly personalized plans with remarkable consistency. The enduring challenge is to identify which plans are biomechanically sound, clinically beneficial, economically justified, and reproducible beyond expert centers. We hope that this Collection will contribute to that transition; from technical precision to evidence-based personalization.
