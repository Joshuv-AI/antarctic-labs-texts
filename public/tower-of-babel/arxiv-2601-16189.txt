Robust Bell Nonlocality from Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill States
Xiaotian Yang, Santiago Zamora, Rafael Chaves, Ulrik L. Andersen, Jonatan Bohr Brask, A. de Oliveira Junior

Abstract
Bell tests based on homodyne detection are strongly constrained in continuous-variable systems. Can Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) encoding turn homodyne detection into a practical tool for revealing Bell nonlocality? We consider a physically motivated model in which each party performs homodyne detection and digitizes the continuous outcome via a fixed periodic binning, corresponding to logical Pauli measurements. Within this framework, we derive a bipartite no-go: CHSH cannot be violated for Bell-pair states. Moving beyond two parties, we show that finitely squeezed GKP-encoded GHZ and W states nevertheless exhibit strong multipartite nonlocality, violating multipartite Bell inequalities with homodyne-only readout. We quantify the required squeezing thresholds and robustness to loss, providing a route toward homodyne-based Bell tests in continuous-variable systems.

Can the GKP encoding unlock robust Bell violations with homodyning? A first step toward bridging non-locality and the GKP code was taken via a device-independent quantum cryptography protocol
Marshall and Weedbrook 2014
. Alice generates an entangled GKP state and shares one half with Bob to establish a secret key based on a CHSH violation. The drawback of this approach is that the violation requires a non-Gaussian T-gate, whose experimental implementation is challenging. This leads us to ask: given a GKP encoding, can we violate a Bell inequality using only Gaussian operations? Realistic GKP states are approximate, with finite squeezing and sensitivity to loss that can blur their grid structure. Whether this residual, noise-degraded non-Gaussianity is sufficient to overcome the homodyne no-go and yield an observable Bell violation has been unclear. In this work, we show that GKP states enable sizeable Bell violations using only homodyne detection, even in the presence of finite squeezing and noise (see Fig.
1
for a summary).

Big picture–
We characterise Bell nonlocality under a strict measurement constraint: each party performs homodyne detection of a quadrature, and the continuous outcome is converted into a binary result by a fixed binning rule. While the shared state may be non

In quantum theory, Bell violations arise from entanglement probed by incompatible local measurements. We now introduce the two main ingredients of our continuous-variable implementation: the shared entangled resource and the local measurement settings. The resource is provided by embedding a logical qubit into a single bosonic mode using GKP grid states, while the measurement choices are implemented using homodyne detection followed by a fixed binary post-processing (binning) of the continuous outcome.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}=\Bigl\{\boldsymbol{m}\equiv\quantity(k,\tfrac{k(k+1)}{2})\pmod{2}\Bigr\}

Multipartite Bell-test & GKP encoding–
We consider an
N
-party Bell experiment with
N\geq 3
, where each party holds a single bosonic mode encoding one logical qubit. In each run, party
j
receives an input
x_{j}\in\{0,1\}
selecting one of two homodyne angles
\theta_{x_{j}}
and measures the corresponding quadrature
\hat{q}_{\theta_{x_{j}}}
. The continuous outcome is then discretized into a bit
o_{j}\in\{0,1\}
using the fixed periodic binning rule defined in Eq. (
5
). The experiment is thus described by the conditional probabilities
p(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{x})
. A behaviour

is Bell-local if it admits a local-hidden-variable decomposition
p(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{x})=\int\mathrm{d}\lambda\,p(\lambda)\prod_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}(o_{j}\mid x_{j},\lambda)
, and any violation of a Bell inequality derived from this assumption certifies multipartite Bell nonlocality.

Our central technical ingredient is an explicit expression for the full
N
-party behaviour generated by any finite-energy GKP-encoded state under this measurement scheme. Let
\rho_{L}^{\epsilon}
be the encoded state in Eq. (
3

M_{o}^{(x)}
is the single-mode binned-homodyne element for angle
\theta_{x}

\rho_{L}^{\epsilon}

(6)

where

are the logical Pauli coefficients from Eq. (
3

t_{k,o}^{(x)}:=\tr\left(M_{o}^{(x)}\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}\right)

N

Discussion & outlook
– Beyond fault-tolerant quantum computing, GKP encodings are emerging as natural building blocks for photonic quantum technologies, including long-distance quantum communication and repeater/network architectures that rely predominantly on Gaussian operations and homodyne readout

We have shown that finitely squeezed GKP resources enable sizeable multipartite Bell violations using only homodyne detection and fixed binning, yielding a simple benchmark compatible with high-efficiency homodyne detection and realistic noise.

Our main technical result, Eq. (

\ket{\text{W}_N}
is nonstabilizer, so preparing it from stabilizer inputs cannot be achieved with Clifford operations alone. Establishing experimentally feasible routes to (W)-type GKP entanglement within (or close to) the Gaussian toolbox therefore remains an interesting open direction, and is closely tied to the need for logical non-Clifford resources, which in the GKP code subspace require additional non-Gaussian ingredients beyond Gaussian protocols
Hahn et al. 2025
.

Appendix A
A no-go theorem for CHSH violation with Pauli local measurements

This appendix formalises a simple obstruction to bipartite CHSH violation that arises when the available dichotomic measurements are restricted to the (logical) Pauli observables. The result captures the essential reason why, in settings where the only accessible measurements are implementations of logical
X,Y
and
Z
(e.g., via quadrature homodyne detection followed by GKP-aligned binning), CHSH violations cannot be observed..

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\pm X,\pm Y,\pm Z\}
denote the set of nontrivial Pauli observables on a qubit, each with eigenvalues
\pm 1
. The CHSH expression for a two-qubit state
\rho
and dichotomic observables
A_{0},A_{1}
(Alice) and
B_{0},B_{1}
(Bob) is

S_{\textrm{CHSH}}(\rho;A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1}):=\tr(\rho\mathcal{B}_{\textrm{CHSH}})\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{B}_{\text{CHSH}}:=A_{0}\otimes(B_{0}+B_{1})+A_{1}\otimes(B_{0}-B_{1}).

(10)

Equivalently,
S_{\textrm{CHSH}}(\rho;A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1})=\langle A_{0}B_{0}\rangle+\langle A_{0}B_{1}\rangle+\langle A_{1}B_{0}\rangle-\langle A_{1}B_{1}\rangle
, where
\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle=\tr(\rho A_i \otimes B_j)
. Any local-hidden-variable model satisfies
|S_{\text{CHSH}}(\rho;A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1})|\leq 2
.
Before we proceed with the main theorem, we begin with two standard lemmas. The first lemma shows that the CHSH value is invariant under local unitary conjugation, provided the observables are transformed accordingly:

Lemma 1

(Invariance under local conjugation)
.

For any two-qubit state
\rho
, any unitaries
U_{A},U_{B}
, and any observables
A_{i},B_{j}
,

S_{\text{CHSH}}\quantity[(U_A\otimes U_B)\rho(U_A\otimes U_B)^\dagger; A_0, A_1, B_0, B_1]=S_{\text{CHSH}}\quantity[\rho; U_A^\dagger A_0U_A, U_A^\dagger A_1 U_A, U_B^\dagger B_0 U_B, U^\dagger_B B_1 U_B].

(11)

Proof.

This follows from cyclicity of the trace

\tr[(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\rho(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})^{\dagger}(A_{i}\otimes B_{j})]=\tr[\rho(U_{A}^{\dagger}A_{i}U_{A}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger}B_{j}U_{B})].

(12)

and linearity in the CHSH combination.
∎

Next, the second lemma shows that conjugation by a Clifford unitary preserves the Pauli set:

Lemma 2

(Pauli set is invariant under Clifford conjugation)
.

If
U
is a single-qubit Clifford unitary and
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then
U^{\dagger}PU\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

This is the defining property of the Clifford group as the normaliser of the single-qubit Pauli Group:
U^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}U=\mathcal{P}
up to phases, and for Hermitian Paulis the relevant phase reduces to
\pm 1
.
∎

We now state the main theorem:

Theorem 3

(No CHSH violation with Pauli measurements on a local-Clifford Bell pair)
.

. Let
\ket{\Phi^+}:=2^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11)})
and let
\rho
be any two-qubit state of the form:

\rho=(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\outerproduct{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})^{\dagger},

(13)

where
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
are single-qubit Clifford unitaries. Assume Alice and Bob are restricted to Pauli observables
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
. Then,

|S_{\textrm{CHSH}}(\rho;A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1})|\leq 2.

(14)

Proof.

Let
\rho
be as in Eq (
13
). Applying Lemma
1
gives

S_{\text{CHSH}}(\rho;A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1})=S_{\text{CHSH}}\quantity(\ketbra{\Phi^+}; A_0', A_1', B_0', B_1')

(15)

where
A_{i}^{\prime}=U_{A}^{\dagger}A_{i}U_{A}
and
B_{j}^{\prime}=U_{B}^{\dagger}B_{j}U_{B}
. By Lemma
2
,
A_{0}^{\prime},A_{1}^{\prime},B_{0}^{\prime},B_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
. Thus it suffices to prove that for any
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
one has

S_{\textrm{CHSH}}\quantity(\ketbra{\Phi^+};A_0, A_1, B_0, B_1)\leq 2.

(16)

We prove this statement with two steps:

1.

Step 1: Pauli-Pauli correlator on
\ket{\Phi^+}
.
First, recall the following identity

\langle\Phi^{+}|A\otimes B|\Phi^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\tr(AB^\top),

(17)

where the transpose is taken in the computational basis. Applying Eq. (
17
) to
A\in\{X,Y,Z\}
and
B\in\{X,Y,Z\}
and using
X^{\top}=X,Z^{\top}=Z,Y^{\top}=-Y
, and
\tr(PQ)=2\delta_{P,Q}
for
P,Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}
gives

\displaystyle\langle X\otimes X\rangle_{\Phi^{+}}

\displaystyle=+1,

\displaystyle\langle Y\otimes Y\rangle_{\Phi^{+}}

\displaystyle=-1,

\displaystyle\langle Z\otimes Z\rangle_{\Phi^{+}}

\displaystyle=+1,

(18)

and

\langle P\otimes Q\rangle_{\Phi^{+}}=0\>\>\text{whenever}\>\>P,Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}\>\>\text{and}\>\>P\neq Q.

(19)

2.

Step 2: CHSH bound under Pauli choices
. Let
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
. Define the four correlators
s_{ij}:=\langle A_{i}\otimes B_{j}\rangle
. By Eqs. (
A
)-(
19
), each
s_{ij}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
and
s_{ij}\neq 0
can occur only if
A_{i}
and
B_{j}
are the same Pauli axis (up to a sign). We consider two cases:

(a)

Case 1:
B_{1}=\pm B_{0}
. If
B_{1}=B_{0}
, then

S_{\text{CHSH}}=s_{00}+s_{10}+s_{01}-s_{11}=2s_{00},

(20)

since
s_{01}=s_{00}
and
s_{11}=s_{10}
. Hence,
|S_{\text{CHSH}}|=2|s_{00}|\leq 2
. If
B_{1}=-B_{0}
, then
|S_{\text{CHSH}}|=2s_{10}
and again
|S_{\text{CHSH}}|\leq 2.

(b)

Case 2:
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
are different Pauli axes. Then for each fixed
i\in\{0,1\}
,
A_{i}
can match at most one of the two distinct axes
\{B_{0},B_{1}\}
(up to a sign), so at most one of
s_{i0}
and
s_{i1}
is nonzero. Consequently,

\displaystyle|s_{00}+s_{01}|

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle|s_{00}-s_{01}|

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(21)

Indeed, if both terms in a pair are zero the bound is trivial. If exactly one is zero, then the pair equals
\pm 1
. They cannot both be nonzero because that would require
A_{i}
to coincide with both distinct Pauli axes. Using the triangle inequality and Eq. (
2b
),

|S_{\text{CHSH}}|=|s_{i0}+s_{i1}|\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad|s_{i0}-s_{i1}|\leq 1\>\>\text{for}\>\>i=0,1.

(22)

Thus
|S_{\text{CHSH}}|\leq 2
holds in both cases, proving that Eq. (
16
). Combining with the reduction given by Eq. (
15
) completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Observe that the reduction from
\rho
in Eq (
15
) to
\ket{\Phi^+}
uses the fact that Clifford conjugation preserves the Pauli set:
U^{\dagger}PU\in\{X,Y,Z\}
whenever
U
is a Clifford and
P\in\mathcal{P}
. This is false for general single-qubit unitaries, hence the assumption in Eq. (
15
) is essential for the theorem as stated. Finally, in GKP-based implementations, logical
X
,
Y
, and
Z
measurements are commonly realized by homodyne measurement of a quadrature (or a rotated quadrature), followed by modular binning into two outcomes aligned with the GKP lattice. When this procedure implements dichotomic observables that are well-approximated by the logical Pauli set, the above theorem explains the absence of CHSH violations in the bipartite case: CHSH violation requires measurement directions outside the Pauli axes (e.g.
\propto X\pm Z
), which are not available under a Pauli restriction.

Appendix B
Multipartite Bell inequalities

In this Appendix we define the multipartite Bell functionals used in the main text for the GHZ and
W
families. Throughout, each party
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
receives an input
x_{j}\in\{0,1\}
selecting one of two binned-homodyne POVMs
\{M_{0}^{(x_{j})},M_{1}^{(x_{j})}\}
, and returns an output bit
o_{j}\in\{0,1\}
, yielding the behaviour
p(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{x})
. Given a two-outcome POVM
\{M_{0}^{(x)},M_{1}^{(x)}\}
we associate the corresponding dichotomic observable

A^{(x)}:=\sum_{o\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{o}\,M_{o}^{(x)}=M_{0}^{(x)}-M_{1}^{(x)},

(23)

whose outcome is the
\pm 1
variable
a:=(-1)^{o}
. For a setting string
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, the full correlator is

E(\boldsymbol{x}):=\Bigl\langle\prod_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\Bigr\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{o}\in\{0,1\}^{N}}(-1)^{\sum_{j}o_{j}}\,p(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{x})=\tr\left[\rho\,\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}^{(x_{j})}\right],

(24)

where
A_{j}^{(x_{j})}
denotes the observable (
23
) acting on mode
j
. In our implementation, the settings correspond to homodyne angles that (in the ideal GKP limit) realise logical Pauli measurements, e.g.,
(Y,X)
or
(Z,X)
as specified below.

B.1
MABK inequality for GHZ-type resources

To witness GHZ-type full-correlation nonlocality we use the Mermin–Ardehali–Belinskii–Klyshko (MABK) family. Here each party chooses between two dichotomic observables
A_{j}:=A_{j}^{(0)},A^{\prime}_{j}:=A_{j}^{(1)}
, which in the main text correspond to the effective logical settings
(Y,X)
, i.e.,
x=0
is the binned-homodyne measurement at
\theta_{Y}
and
x=1
at
\theta_{X}
. Define the Bell operators
B_{N}
and
B^{\prime}_{N}
recursively by
B_{1}:=A_{1}
and
B_{1}^{\prime}:=A_{1}^{\prime}
, and,
for
N\geq 2
,

\displaystyle B_{N}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[B_{N-1}\otimes\left(A_{N}+A_{N}^{\prime}\right)+B_{N-1}^{\prime}\otimes\left(A_{N}-A_{N}^{\prime}\right)\Bigr],

(25a)

\displaystyle B_{N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[B_{N-1}^{\prime}\otimes\left(A_{N}+A_{N}^{\prime}\right)-B_{N-1}\otimes\left(A_{N}-A_{N}^{\prime}\right)\Bigr].

(25b)

The corresponding (dimensionless) Bell parameter is
S_{N}^{\textsf{M}}:=2^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\,\max\left\{\bigl|\langle B_{N}\rangle\bigr|,\ \bigl|\langle B_{N}^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|\right\}
, with
\langle B_{N}\rangle=\tr(\rho\,B_N)
(and similarly for
B_{N}^{\prime}
).
Any local-hidden-variable (LHV) model obeys
S_{N}^{\textsf{M}}\leq 2^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}
, so that
S_{N}^{\textsf{M}}>2^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}
certifies multipartite Bell nonlocality.

B.2
Cabello-type inequality for
W
-type resources

To probe nonlocality associated with
W
-type entanglement we use a Cabello/Hardy-type probability inequality
Cabello 2002
;
Heaney et al. 2011
, which only involves joint probabilities for a restricted set of measurement configurations. Here each party chooses between two binned-homodyne measurements corresponding to the effective logical settings
(Z,X)
, i.e.
x=0
is the setting at
\theta_{Z}
and
x=1
at
\theta_{X}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{0}:=(0,\dots,0)
and
\boldsymbol{1}:=(1,\dots,1)
, and let
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
denote the
N
-bit string with a single
1
at site
j
and zeros elsewhere. For each unordered pair
(i,j)
with
i<j
, define the setting string
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i,j)}
by
(x^{(i,j)})_{i}=(x^{(i,j)})_{j}=1
and
(x^{(i,j)})_{k}=0
for
k\neq i,j
(two
X
’s and the rest
Z
’s). We then define

\displaystyle S_{N}^{\textsf{C}}:=p(\boldsymbol{0}\mid\boldsymbol{0})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}p(\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{0})-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\Bigl[p(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{(i,j)})+p(\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{(i,j)})\Bigr]-\,p(\boldsymbol{0}\mid\boldsymbol{1})-p(\boldsymbol{1}\mid\boldsymbol{1}).

(26)

Any LHV model satisfies the bound
S_{N}^{\textsf{C}}\leq 0,
so that
S_{N}^{\textsf{C}}>0
certifies multipartite Bell nonlocality. As emphasized in the main text, (
26
) depends only on probabilities from the three classes of setting strings: all-
Z
(
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
), all-
X
(
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{1}
), and two-
X
settings
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i,j)}
, totaling
2+\binom{N}{2}
measurement configurations.

B.3
Distance to the polytope as a linear program

Beyond specific inequalities one can also verify nonlocality via the distance to the local polytope. Here we establish the distance from a distribution
\boldsymbol{p}
to the local polytope
\mathcal{L}
of classical correlations as a linear program.
For an
N
-partite Bell scenario, each party having
I

O

q(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{b})
must be decomposable as

q(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{b})=\sum_{\lambda}q_{\lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{N}q(o_{j}|b_{j}),\quad\text{for }\boldsymbol{o}\in\{1,\dots,O\}^{N},\>\boldsymbol{b}\in\{1,\dots,I\}^{N},

(27)

where
q(o_{j}|b_{j})
corresponds to the local distribution observed by party
j
and
q(\lambda)
is the probability distribution of the common hidden variable
\lambda
. By denoting
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{(OI)^{N}}
the real vector with entries

\boldsymbol{q}
given by Eq. (
27

\mathcal{L}_{N}

\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{p})
of any distribution
\boldsymbol{p}
from the local polytope. The distance can be defined as the following minimization

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{p})=\min

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{q}\|_{1}

(28)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{n-1}q_{\lambda}\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda},\quad q_{i}^{\lambda}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{f_{j}(b_{j},\lambda),o_{j}},\quad\sum_{\lambda=0}^{n-1}q_{\lambda}=1,\quad q_{\lambda}\geq 0.

where we have denoted by
f_{j}
the finite deterministic response functions of party
j
. Note that, since the number of inputs and outputs is finite, there is also a finite number
n
of deterministic strategies. Also note that the function
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{p})
can be stated as a linear program because all the constraints are linear and the objective function, the 1-norm, can be linearized through slack variables

Any classical distribution must have distance zero, while any positive distance indicates that the target distribution is unfactorizable as a LHV model.

Appendix C
Finite-energy GKP states

In this appendix, we derive the finite-energy wave functions and the related quantities which follows from it for a finite-energy GKP state.

C.1
Energy-filter matrix element

The central piece for describing the finite-energy GKP wavefunctions is to compute how
e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}
acts on a position eigenstate
\ket{x_0}_{x}
. To do so, we begin by computing the matrix element of the energy filter
\langle x|e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}|x_{0}\rangle
for
\epsilon>0
. Working with dimensionless quadratures, the number operator can be written as
\hat{n}=\hat{H}-\tfrac{1}{2}
, which implies
e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}=e^{-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}e^{-\epsilon\hat{H}}
. Consequently, one finds
Sakurai and Napolitano 2017
:
\langle x|e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}|x_{0}\rangle=e^{-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}K(x,x_{0};\epsilon)
, where
K(x,x_{0};\epsilon)\equiv\langle x|e^{-\epsilon\hat{H}}|x_{0}\rangle
denotes the Euclidean (imaginary-time) propagator of the harmonic oscillator. The propagator can be obtained via an eigenfunction expansion. The normalised energy eigenfunctions are

\psi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi^{\tfrac{1}{4}}\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}H_{n}(x)\quad\text{with}\quad n=0,1,2,\dots,

(29)

where
H_{n}
denote the Hermite polynomials. The corresponding eigenvalues are
E_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
. Since the functions
\psi_{n}
are real, the propagator can be written in the following form:

K(x,x_{0};\epsilon)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\epsilon E_{n}}\psi_{n}(x)\psi_{n}(x_{0})=\frac{e^{-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}+x_{0}^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(e^{-\epsilon})^{n}}{2^{n}n!}H_{n}(x)H_{n}(x_{0}).

(30)

Using the Mehler formula
Rainville 1960
, the sum appearing in Eq. (
30
) admits a closed-form expression:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}(x)H_{n}(x_{0})}{2^{n}n!}\ell^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\ell^{2}}}\exp\quantity( \frac{2x x_0 \ell- (x^2 + x_0^2) \ell^2}{1 - \ell^2}),

(31)

with
\ell=e^{-\epsilon}
. Then, substituting Eq. (
31
) into Eq. (
30
), the kernel takes the form

K(x,x_{0};\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\exp(-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2})}{\sqrt{1-\ell^{2}}}\exp(-\frac{x^2+x_0^2}{2})\exp\quantity( \frac{2x x_0 \ell- (x^2 + x_0^2) \ell^2}{1 - \ell^2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\exp(-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2})}{\sqrt{1-\ell^{2}}}\exp\quantity[ \frac{2x x_0 \ell- (x^2 + x_0^2) (1+\ell^2)}{1 - \ell^2}].

(32)

Introducing the following hyperbolic identities,

\displaystyle 1-\ell^{2}

\displaystyle=2e^{-\epsilon}\sinh\epsilon,

(33)

\displaystyle\frac{1+\ell^{2}}{1-\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\coth\epsilon,

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{2\ell}{1-\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sinh\epsilon},

(35)

the propagator can be simplified as follows:

K(x,x_{0};\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sinh\epsilon}}\exp\quantity[-\frac{(x^2 + x_0^2)\cosh\epsilon- 2x x_0}{2\sinh\epsilon}].

(36)

Finally, the matrix element of the energy filter reads

\langle x|e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}|x_{0}\rangle=\frac{e^{\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sinh\epsilon}}\exp\quantity[-\frac{(x^2 + x_0^2)\cosh\epsilon- 2x x_0}{2\sinh\epsilon}].

(37)

As a sanity check, note that in the limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, Eq. (
37
) tends to
\delta(x-x_{0})
. In the opposite limit,
\epsilon\to\infty
, one finds
K(x,x_{0};\epsilon)\to e^{-\epsilon/2}\psi_{0}(x)\psi_{0}(x)
and
\langle x|e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}|x_{0}\rangle\to\psi_{0}(x)\psi_{0}(x)
, corresponding to a projection onto the vacuum.

To reveal the structure relevant for GKP states, we complete the square in the exponent of Eq. (
37
):

\displaystyle-\frac{(x^{2}+x_{0}^{2})\cosh\epsilon-2xx_{0}}{2\sinh\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\cosh\epsilon}{2\sinh\epsilon}(x-x_{0}\sech\epsilon)^{2}-\frac{\sinh\epsilon}{2\cosh\epsilon}x_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}(x-x_{0}\sech\epsilon)^{2}-\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{4\Sigma^{2}},

(38)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\tanh\epsilon,

(39)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\coth\epsilon=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}.

(40)

The parameters
\sigma^{2}
and
\Sigma^{2}
do not directly correspond to the variances of the Gaussians. Instead, the peak variance is
2\sigma^{2}
, while the envelope variance is
2\Sigma^{2}
. The factor
\sech\epsilon
describes a contraction of the underlying lattice.

The ideal square-lattice GKP logical states are Dirac combs
\ket{\mu_L^{\text{ideal}}}\propto\sum_{s\in\mathbbm{Z}}\ket{x=(2s+\mu)\sqrt{\pi}}
. Applying the energy filter yields finite-energy states
\ket{\mu_L^{\epsilon}}\propto\ket{\mu_L^{\text{ideal}}}
. Their position-space wavefunctions are obtained via the integral transform with kernel given by Eq. (
37
), which, for Dirac combs, collapses to a discrete sum. Using Eq. (
C.1
), the resulting finite-energy codewords are normalisable and can be specified by their position-space wavefunctions
\psi_{\mu_{L}^{\epsilon}}(x):={}_{x}\langle{x\,|\,\mu_{L}^{\epsilon}\rangle}
, with

\displaystyle\psi_{0_{L}^{\epsilon}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{0}}}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\quantity[{-\frac{(x-2s\sqrt{\pi}\sech\epsilon)^2}{4\sigma^2}}-\frac{(2s\sqrt{\pi})^2}{4\Sigma^2}],

(41a)

\displaystyle\psi_{1_{L}^{\epsilon}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{1}}}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\quantity[-\frac{[x-(2s+1)\sqrt{\pi}\sech\epsilon]^2}{4\sigma^2} -\frac{[(2s+1)\sqrt{\pi}]^2}{4\Sigma^2}].

(41b)

C.2
Wigner functions of the GKP Pauli operators

We represent oscillator states in phase space via the Wigner transform

W_{A}(q,p):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dye^{ipy}\Big\langle q-\frac{y}{2}\Big|A\Big|q+\frac{y}{2}\Big\rangle,

(42)

with
(q,p)
the canonical quadratures. For the energy-filter model
e^{-\epsilon\hat{n}}
, the Wigner functions of the operators

\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}
admit a convenient closed form as a Gaussian mixture on a contracted square lattice:

W_{\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}}(q,p)=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\,G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}^{\epsilon}}(q,p),\qquad k=0,1,2,3.

(43)

Here
\boldsymbol{x}:=(q,p)^{T}
, and

G_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right]

(44)

is a centred two-dimensional Gaussian with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
.
For the finite-energy GKP states of Eq. (
41
), the covariance is isotropic and equals

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}^{\epsilon}=\sigma^{2}\,\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{2}\tanh(\epsilon)\,\mathbbm{1},

(45)

while the phase-space means are located on a contracted square lattice,

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}=\sech(\epsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\boldsymbol{m}\>\>\text{and}\>\>\boldsymbol{m}=\begin{bmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

(46)

The sets
\mathcal{M}_{k}
select one of four lattice cosets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m_{1}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2),\;m_{2}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\},

(47a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m_{1}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2),\;m_{2}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\},

(47b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m_{1}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2),\;m_{2}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\},

(47c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m_{1}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2),\;m_{2}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\}.

(47d)

and the sign pattern is

\displaystyle s_{0}(\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle=1,

(48a)

\displaystyle s_{1}(\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m_{2}/2},

(48b)

\displaystyle s_{2}(\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(m_{1}+m_{2})/2},

(48c)

\displaystyle s_{3}(\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m_{1}/2}.

(48d)

which is responsible for the alternating positive and negative peaks of the Wigner function.
Finally, the envelope weights are

c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}=\exp\left[-\frac{\pi}{4}\tanh(\epsilon)\big(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}\big)\right],

(49)

guaranteeing convergence of the lattice sum.

C.3
Loss and thermal-noise model

In the main text we model attenuation and Gaussian noise by coupling each signal mode to an environment mode on a beam splitter of transmissivity
\eta\in[0,1]
. The environment is taken to be either vacuum (pure loss) or a thermal state (thermal loss).
The thermal Wigner function is an isotropic Gaussian,
W_{\tau_{n_{\mathrm{th}}}}(q,p)=G_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}}(q,p)
, with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}=\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\mathbbm{1}
, where
n_{\mathrm{th}}
is the thermal mean photon number
n_{\mathrm{th}}
. Consequently, the vacuum corresponds to
n_{\mathrm{th}}=0
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}
. A key simplification for our purposes is that the the noisy-loss channel is a Gaussian channel
Weedbrook et al. 2012
, hence it maps each Gaussian component of a Wigner function to another Gaussian. More precisely, for the Gaussian kernel of Eq. (
44
) one has the transformation

G_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\ \longmapsto\ G_{\sqrt{\eta}\,\boldsymbol{\mu},\ \eta\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+(1-\eta)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}}.

(50)

Equation (
50
) can be understood directly at the level of first and second moments: in the Heisenberg picture the quadrature vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=(\hat{q},\hat{p})^{T}
transforms as
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto\sqrt{\eta}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{E}
, so that the mean rescales by
\sqrt{\eta}
and the covariance picks up an additive isotropic term weighted by
(1-\eta)
. In particular, the excess-noise contribution is proportional to
(1-\eta)n_{\mathrm{th}}
, as expected for noise injected through the unused port of the beam splitter. Applying (
50
) term-wise to the Gaussian-mixture representation (
43
) shows that the mixture structure of the finitely-squeezed GKP Pauli operators is preserved under loss and thermal noise. Defining

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{W}^{\,\epsilon}(\eta,n_{\mathrm{th}}):=\eta\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}^{\epsilon}+(1-\eta)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(\eta,n_{\mathrm{th}},\epsilon)\,\mathbbm{1},\>\>\text{with}\>\>\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(\eta,n_{\mathrm{th}},\epsilon)=\eta\,\sigma^{2}+(1-\eta)\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right),

(51)

and the rescaled lattice means
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\,\epsilon}(\eta):=\sqrt{\eta}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}=\sqrt{\eta}\,\sech(\epsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\boldsymbol{m}
, we obtain, for
k=0,1,2,3
, the following Wigner function

W_{{\eta,n_{\mathrm{th}}}(\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon})}(q,p)=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\,G_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\,\epsilon}(\eta),\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{W}^{\,\epsilon}(\eta,n_{\mathrm{th}})}(q,p).

(52)

For pure loss (vacuum environment) one simply sets
n_{\mathrm{th}}=0
, giving
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(\eta,0,\epsilon)=\eta\sigma^{2}+\tfrac{(1-\eta)}{2}
.

Appendix D
Single-mode overlap

Let
M_{o}^{(b)}
be the single-mode POVM element corresponding to setting
b
and binned outcome
o
and let the
N
-mode measurement operator for settings
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},...,b_{N})
and outcomes
\boldsymbol{o}=(o_{1},...,o_{N})
be the product POVM
M_{\boldsymbol{o}}^{(\boldsymbol{b})}:=M_{o_{1}}^{(b_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{o_{N}}^{(b_{N})}
. For any physical
N
-mode state
\rho
, Born’s rule gives
p(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{b})=\tr(\rho M_{\v o}^{\v b})
. For any physical
N
-mode state
\rho
that is an encoded qubit state (i.e.,
\rho=V_{\epsilon}^{\otimes N}\rho_{\text{qubit}}V_{\epsilon}^{\dagger\otimes N}
for some
N
-qubit state
\rho_{\text{qubit}}
), it admits a decomposition as linear combination of tensor-product operators built from the single-mode finite energy GKP Paulis
\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}
:
\rho=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{k\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{N}}c_{k}\sigma^{\epsilon}_{k_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma^{\epsilon}_{k_{N}}
, where
c_{k}\in\mathbbm{R}
and
\mathcal{N}
normalises the state. Hence, one can factorises the Born’s rule as

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{o}|\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{k}c_{k}\tr[(\sigma^{\epsilon}_{k_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma^{\epsilon}_{k_{N}})(M_{o_{1}}^{(b_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{o_{N}}^{(b_{N})})]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{k}c_{k}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\tr(M_{o_j}^{(b_j)} \sigma_{k_j}^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{k}c_{k}\prod_{j=1}^{N}t_{k_{j},o_{j}}^{(b_{j})}.

(53)

where
t_{k,o}^{(b)}:=\tr(M_{o_j}^{(b_j)} \sigma_{k_j}^{\epsilon})
is the single-mode overlap.

Let
\hat{q}_{\theta}:=\hat{q}\cos\theta+\hat{p}\sin\theta
denote the rotated quadrature, with generalised eigenstate
\ket{q_\theta}
. Since a setting
b
fixes an angle
\theta_{b}
, we write
M^{(b)}_{o}:=M^{(\theta_{b})}_{o}=\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q_{\theta}\outerproduct{q_\theta}{q_\theta}
, where the binning sets are the periodic union of intervals

R_{o}=\bigcup_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\quantity[ (2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}, (2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}].

(54)

Now, observe that the problem of computing the single-mode overlap reduces to computing a matrix element and integrating over bins. One can directly see this by linearity of the trace and the definition of
M_{o}^{(\theta)}
, i.e.,

t^{(\theta)}_{k,o}=\tr(\int_{R_o} \textrm{d}q_\theta\ketbra{q_\theta}\sigma_k^\epsilon)=\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q_{\theta}\langle q_{\theta}|\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}|q_{\theta}\rangle.

(55)

The next Lemma expresses the diagonal element
\langle q_{\theta}|A|q_{\theta}\rangle
as a marginal of the Wigner function

Lemma 4

(Wigner marginal at
\theta=0
)
.

Let
A
be a trace-class operator on a single bosonic mode, and define its Wigner function by

W_{A}(q,p):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,e^{ipy}\Big\langle q-\frac{y}{2}\Big|A\Big|q+\frac{y}{2}\Big\rangle.

(56)

Then for all
q\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p\,W_{A}(q,p)=\langle q|A|q\rangle.

(57)

Proof.

Integrating (
56
) over
p
and using
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\,e^{ipy}=2\pi\delta(y)
gives

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}pW_{A}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}p\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}ye^{ipy}\Big\langle q-\frac{y}{2}\Big|A\Big|q+\frac{y}{2}\Big\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy(2\pi)\delta(y)\Big\langle q-\frac{y}{2}\Big|A\Big|q+\frac{y}{2}\Big\rangle=\langle q|A|q\rangle.

(58)

∎

Lemma
4
treats the position basis
(\theta=0)
, while our measurement protocol uses homodyne angles
\theta_{b}
. The next corollary extends the marginal identity to these rotate quadratures, allowing us to express
\langle q_{\theta}|A|q_{\theta}\rangle
in terms of
W_{A}(q,p)
without redefining the Wigner function.

Corollary 5

(Rotated Wigner marginal)
.

Let
U_{\theta}:=e^{-i\theta\hat{n}}
be the phase-shift unitary. In the Heisenberg picture it acts as

\displaystyle U_{\theta}^{\dagger}\hat{q}U_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\hat{q}\cos\theta+\hat{p}\sin\theta=\hat{q}_{\theta},

(59)

\displaystyle U_{\theta}^{\dagger}\hat{p}U_{\theta}

\displaystyle=-\hat{q}\sin\theta+\hat{p}\cos\theta=\hat{p}_{\theta}.

(60)

Then for any trace-class operator
A
and all
q_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\langle q_{\theta}|A|q_{\theta}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p_{\theta}W_{A}\left(q_{\theta}\cos\theta-p_{\theta}\sin\theta,q_{\theta}\sin\theta+p_{\theta}\cos\theta\right).

(61)

Proof.

Since
|q_{\theta}\rangle=U_{\theta}^{\dagger}|q\rangle
, we have
\langle q_{\theta}|A|q_{\theta}\rangle=\langle q|U_{\theta}AU_{\theta}^{\dagger}|q\rangle
. Applying Lemma
4
to the operator
U_{\theta}AU_{\theta}^{\dagger}
gives

\langle q_{\theta}|A|q_{\theta}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}pW_{U_{\theta}AU_{\theta}^{\dagger}}(q_{\theta},p).

(62)

Now use covariance of the Wigner function under the symplectic transformation induced by (
59
). Writing
\boldsymbol{x}=(q,p)^{\top}
and introducing

S(\theta):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},

(63)

the Heisenberg action is
U_{\theta}^{\dagger}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}U_{\theta}=S(\theta)\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and Wigner covariance reads
W_{U_{\theta}AU_{\theta}^{\dagger}}(\boldsymbol{x})=W_{A}\left[S(\theta)^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\right]
. Thus, we obtain
W_{U_{\theta}AU_{\theta}^{\dagger}}(q,p)=W_{A}\left(q\cos\theta-p\sin\theta,q\sin\theta+p\cos\theta\right)
. Substituting this into (
62
) and renaming the integration variable
p\mapsto p_{\theta}
gives (
61
).
∎

We now have all the elements needed to state a theorem which provides a series representation for the single-mode overlap.

Theorem 6

(Single-mode overlap for binned homodyne on finite-energy GKP Paulis)
.

Let
\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}
denote the finite-energy single-mode GKP Pauli operators whose Wigner functions admit the Gaussian-mixture representation

W_{\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}}(q,p)=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\,G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}^{\epsilon}}(q,p),\qquad k=0,1,2,3.

(64)

with
\Sigma_{W}^{\epsilon}=\sigma^{2}I,\sigma^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\tanh\epsilon
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
on the contracted square lattice (
46
) and with
\mathcal{M}_{k},s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m}),c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
given in Eqs. (
47
)-(
48
)-(
49
), respectively.

Let
M_{o}^{(b)}
be the two-outcome POVM element corresponding to homodyne detection of the rotated quadrature
\hat{q}_{\theta_{b}}
followed by periodic binning into the sets
R_{0},R_{1}
[Eq. (
5
)],

M_{o}^{(b)}=\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q_{\theta_{b}}\outerproduct{q_{\theta_b}}{q_{\theta_b}}\quad\text{with}\quad o\in\{0,1\}.

(65)

Then, the single-mode overlap admits the explicit series representation

t^{(b)}_{k,o}=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{(2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}-\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}}}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}\right)-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{(2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}-\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}}}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}\right)\right],

(66)

where
\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}}
is the projection on the lattice mean onto the measured quadrature direction
\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}}=(\cos\theta_{b},\sin\theta_{b}).\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
and the series converges absolutely.

Proof.

We start by expressing
t^{(\theta)}_{k,o}
as a phase-space integral and use Corollary
5
with
A=\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}
:

t^{(\theta)}_{k,o}=\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q_{\theta}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{\theta}W_{\sigma_{k}^{\epsilon}}\left(q_{\theta}\cos\theta-p_{\theta}\sin\theta,q_{\theta}\sin\theta+p_{\theta}\cos\theta\right).

(67)

Substituting Eq. (
64
) into (
67
) gives

\displaystyle t^{(\theta)}_{k,o}=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\,\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q_{\theta}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{\theta}G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}\left(q_{\theta}\cos\theta-p_{\theta}\sin\theta,q_{\theta}\sin\theta+p_{\theta}\cos\theta\right).

(68)

The exchange of the sum and integrals in Eq. (
68
) is justified by the absolute convergence of the series, guaranteed by the Gaussian envelope
c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
. Since the covariance is isotropic, the Gaussian kernel satisfies the orthogonal transformation property: for any orthogonal matrix
O
,
G_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}(O\boldsymbol{x})=G_{O^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Taking
O=S(\theta)^{-1}
(which is orthogonal) and noting that
O^{\top}=S(\theta)
, we define the rotated mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m},\theta}^{\epsilon}:=S(\theta)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
. Then,
G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}\left(q_{\theta}\cos\theta-p_{\theta}\sin\theta,q_{\theta}\sin\theta+p_{\theta}\cos\theta\right)=G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m},\theta}^{\epsilon},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}(q_{\theta},p_{\theta})
. Writing
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m},\theta}^{\epsilon}=(\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}},\nu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}})^{\top}
and using the explicit Gaussian kernel,

G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m},\theta}^{\epsilon},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}(q_{\theta},p_{\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{(q_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}+(p_{\theta}-\nu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right].

(69)

Integrating over
p_{\theta}
gives the one-dimensional Gaussian density in
q_{\theta}
:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{\theta}G_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m},\theta}^{\epsilon},\sigma^{2}\mathbbm{1}}(q_{\theta},p_{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left[-\frac{(q_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]=:\varphi(q_{\theta};\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}},\sigma^{2}).

(70)

Note that the scalar
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}}
is the projection of
\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
onto the measured quadrature direction
\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta).\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
. Using the contracted-lattice form of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}
, this becomes
\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}}=\sech(\epsilon)\,\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\left(m_{1}\cos\theta+m_{2}\sin\theta\right)
. Hence, the single-mode overlap is reduced to a 1-D Gaussian probability over
R_{o}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle t^{(\theta)}_{k,o}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q_{\theta}\;\varphi(q_{\theta};\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}},\sigma^{2})=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\sum_{s\in\mathbbm{Z}}\int_{r^{(-)}_{s,o}}^{r^{(+)}_{s,o}}\textrm{d}q\,\varphi(q;\mu_{\theta,\boldsymbol{m}},\sigma^{2}),

(71)

where we used the fact that the integral over a countable union of disjoint sets equals the sum of integrals over each set
\int_{R_{o}}\textrm{d}q\,f(q)=\sum_{s\in\mathbbm{Z}}\int_{r^{(-)}_{s,o}}^{r^{(+)}_{s,o}}\textrm{d}q\,f(q)
. The integration limits are
r^{(\pm)}_{s,o}=(2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}\pm\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
. Note that the integral in Eq. (
71
) can be easily computed. Let
u=(q-\mu)/\sigma
, it follows that

\int_{r^{(-)}}^{r^{(+)}}dq\;\varphi(q;\mu,\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{(r^{(-)}-\mu)/\sigma}^{(r^{(+)}-\mu)/\sigma}\textrm{d}u\,e^{-u^{2}/2}=\Phi\left(\frac{r^{(+)}-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{r^{(-)}-\mu}{\sigma}\right),

(72)

where
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Finally, using
\Phi(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]
, the single-mode overlap can be expressed as an error-function series

t^{(b)}_{k,o}=(-1)^{\delta_{k,2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\epsilon}\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{m})\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{(2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}-\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}}}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}\right)-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{(2s+o)\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}-\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}}}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}\right)\right].

(73)

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

A remark is that the inner sum over
s
is an exact infinite series representation of the integral over
R_{o}
. Its rapid convergence follows from the Gaussian decay of
\varphi(q;\mu_{\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{m}},\sigma^{2})
, which suppresses contributions from intervals far from the mean.
