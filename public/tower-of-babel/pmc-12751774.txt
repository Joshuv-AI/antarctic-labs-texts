Microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ousman Mohammed, Habtu Debash, Melaku Ashagrie Belete, Alemu Gedefie, Mihret Tilahun, Zewudu Mulatie, Bruktawit Eshetu, Saba Gebremichael Tekele, Hussen Ebrahim, Daniel Gebretsadik Weldehanna, Agumas Shibabaw, Ermiyas Alemayehu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver the past decade, the incidence of chronic kidney disease among HIV-seropositive people has increased at an alarming rate. Microalbuminuria is believed to be a precursor of HIV-related renal disease; early detection with intervention may prevent end-stage renal disease. Therefore, the current study was aimed at determining the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV patients in Africa.MethodsWe accessed Google Scholar, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, EMBASE, the Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and ResearchGate to get the original research articles. We employed the I² and Cochran’s Q test to assess the presence of heterogeneity between studies. Publication bias was checked by using a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test. The pooled prevalence was estimated using the random-effects model. We ran the sensitivity analysis to see if there were outlier results in the included studies. Moreover, subgroup analysis was also done.ResultsThe current meta-analysis includes 16 studies with 3588 individuals. In Africa, the overall pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria in both adults and children was 22.25% (95% CI 17.38–27.13; I2 = 92.6). Microalbuminuria was observed to be 23.32% (95% CI: 17.36–29.29) in adult study groups and 20.72% (95% CI: 11.85–29.56) in children. Female HIV/AIDS patients exhibited a higher pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria (22.68% (95% CI: 7.43–37.94)) than male HIV/AIDS patients (15.84% (95% CI: 7.28–24.39)). Furthermore, the overall estimates of microalbuminuria among ART users and non-users were 22.32% (95% CI: 13.00–31.63) and 19.41% (95% CI: 14.88–23.94), respectively.ConclusionThe present review study found that over one-fourth of HIV-infected patients had microalbuminuria, indicating a considerable burden of subclinical kidney disease. Therefore, HIV patients need to be screened early for microalbuminuria to prevent further renal complications.Protocol registrationIt has been registered in the PROSPERO database with a registration number of CRD42023446664.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12210-9.

Background

Globally, the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) continues to pose a serious health threat, having claimed 680,000 lives so far. Estimates indicate that 37.7 million people were living with HIV in 2020, and more than 1.5 million acquired new cases each year, over two-thirds of whom (25.4 million) are in African regions [1, 2]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasingly recognized as a major contributor to mortality in HIV-infected patients. Over the past decade, its incidence among HIV-seropositive people has risen sharply, ranging from 4.4 to 44%, with Africa bearing the highest burden [3, 4].

HIV infection is associated with protean manifestations, and kidney disorders—acute or chronic—may occur during the course of infection [5]. Morbidity and mortality increase when immune complexes are deposited in renal tissues and directly infect renal epithelial and mesangial cells [6–8]. In addition, long-term use of antiretroviral drugs has been linked to HIV-associated nephropathy and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [9, 10]. Consequently, the high prevalence of HIV infection represents a significant health challenge in Africa [11, 12].

Microalbuminuria detection may be a crucial technique for the early diagnosis of CKD in HIV-infected people [13–16]. The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is a well-accepted early indicator of nephropathy that may be measured quickly and accurately [17, 18]. Microalbuminuria is considered a precursor of HIV-related renal disease, as patients are at risk of developing overt proteinuria, progressive renal decline, and ultimately ESRD [18, 19]. It has also been examined in relation to diabetic nephropathy, hypertension, obesity, and sickle cell disease, serving as an early biomarker of renal illness due to its association with subclinical structural and functional changes in the kidney [20–23].

Because microalbuminuria is relatively common and correlates with increased mortality risk, its detection provides valuable predictive information for HIV-infected patients [24]. Thus, early detection of microalbuminuria may be essential for interventions to minimize renal function loss in HIV/AIDS patients. Despite these clinical values, the magnitude of microalbuminuria in HIV/AIDS patients’ remains inconsistently reported throughout studies in Africa, with significant variations by region, study design, and patient characteristics. Furthermore, small sample sizes and heterogeneous findings limit individual studies, even though they provide useful information. Thus, to date, no comprehensive data exist on the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria in African HIV/AIDS patients. This study aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients. By consolidating available evidence, the findings might help health professionals, policymakers, and other stakeholders recognize the need for early detection and monitoring of HIV-related nephropathy to reduce the burden of ESRD.

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted the review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1) [25]. The protocol of this study was registered at PROSPERO with registration number CRD42023446664. We conducted a systematic literature search in the following databases: Google Scholar, PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and ResearchGate for all original articles pertaining to the prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients in Africa. The database search occurred from November 2024 to January 2025. The primary search phrases were directly connected to HIV and the kidney dysfunction indicator (i.e., HIV, microalbuminuria). The research question was targeted using a filtering method, including data on human subjects, HIV-infected individuals, English, and African studies, and a screening of reference lists for relevant articles. The search strategy made in PubMed was “microalbuminuria” AND “HIV/AIDS” OR “Human Immunodeficiency Virus” [MeSH Terms] OR Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome” AND each African country. We searched for observational studies, such as cross-sectional studies and cohort studies that examined the magnitude of microalbuminuria in HIV/AIDS patients in Africa. Despite searching for all types of observational studies, we found only cross-sectional studies and included them in our meta-analysis.

Selection criteria

The study was chosen in two steps. First, two reviewers looked through the database search titles, abstracts, and full text to determine eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. If a reviewer was unsure whether a title was eligible, it was not rejected but instead advanced to the next step of the selection procedure. The third independent reviewer then goes over the titles, abstracts, and whole text using the primarily mentioned inclusion criteria. We settled differences through discussion until we reached an agreement. The remaining articles were then evaluated for data extraction eligibility. To control duplicate references and text citations, EndNote 7 reference manager software was employed.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study inclusion criteria included the following: those studies published in English where the process of identifying microalbuminuria was well described; studies that report the prevalence of microalbuminuria; or those studies with sufficient information to estimate microalbuminuria prevalence.

Exclusion criteria

Available studies were excluded if they did not explain the process/criteria of microalbuminuria diagnosis and were inaccessible in full text, as were full-text articles published in a language other than English. Moreover, qualitative studies, case reports, clinical trials, case series studies, letters to the editor, and studies that either failed to describe the prevalence of microalbuminuria or lacked pertinent data for calculating microalbuminuria prevalence were also excluded.

Data extraction

Data were taken from full-text publications and entered using a form that was created specifically for Microsoft Excel. The first author’s name, the study’s design, the sample size, the year it was published, the nation where the study was conducted, the methods used to define microalbuminuria, the prevalence of microalbuminuria overall as well as the prevalence of microalbuminuria among people who had received ART versus those who had not received treatment, age, and gender were all included in the extracted data. Two reviewers (OM and EA) carried out the data extraction, and a third author (HD) independently confirmed it. Consensus-based talks and independent evaluation by a third author were employed to resolve any discrepancies, and efforts were made to contact the first writers for further information. Similarly, we considered the most recent, detailed, and large sample size investigations for studies published in different journals.

Quality assessment

Using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale adapted for cross-sectional studies and a quality assessment tool [26], two authors (OM and EA) independently appraised and evaluated each paper. They assessed studies based on factors such as the representativeness of the sample size, non-respondents, ascertainment of exposure, controls for the most important factor, controls for additional factors, assessment of the outcome response rate, and exclusion rate. The reviewers reviewed their quality appraisal scores and resolved any inconsistencies before assigning the final evaluation score. In the final analysis, we considered good-quality studies or higher (Supplementary Table 2).

Operational definition

Microalbuminuria was defined as a significant increase in the excretion of urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) within the specific range of 30–299 mg of albumin per g of creatinine.

Statistical analysis

For analysis, the required data was exported from Microsoft Excel to STATA version 14 software. A meta-analysis of the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria was carried out using a random-effects model, generating a pooled prevalence with 95% CIs. Heterogeneity among studies was estimated using Cochran’s Q and I2 statistics and is characterised as low, moderate, or high for 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively [27]. We also scrutinised forest plots of summary estimates from each study to determine whether we could identify any outliers or heterogeneity. The symmetry of funnel plots [28] and Egger’s test [29] were used to determine publication bias. Furthermore, sub-group analyses were performed based on sub-region, treatment status, gender, year of publication, and age. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was additionally conducted to assess the impact of single research on the combined estimate of the other studies [30]. For all computations, statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all calculations.

We identified records from PubMed (n = 43), Google Scholar (n = 122), EMBASE (n = 11), Web of Science (n = 13), ResearchGate (n = 35), Science Direct (n = 19), and Cochrane Library (n = 7). Our electronic database search offered 250 articles; of those, 137 non-duplicate papers were assessed. About 93 articles were removed since they did not address the research question. The full texts of the remaining 44 articles were reviewed. Subsequently, only 16 studies met our inclusion criteria, and the remaining 28 articles were rejected because the intended outcomes were not attained (Fig. 1).

Overview of included studies

A total of 3588 study participants were involved in all included studies [31–46]. Almost all the studies included in the current review used a cross-sectional study design and were conducted in hospital settings. Of the total 16 studies, 8 (50%) were conducted in Nigeria [31–38], 3 in Tanzania [39–41], 2 in the Congo [43, 44], 2 in South Africa [45, 46], and the remaining 1 was conducted in Uganda [42]. For diagnosing the presence of microalbuminuria, all the studies applied the excretion levels of the urine albumin-creatinine ratio within the particular range of 30–299 mg of albumin per g of creatinine. Thus, the overall reported prevalence of microalbuminuria in varying studies varies from 6.74% in children [35] to 38.6% in adult HIV/AIDS individuals [40]. Moreover, the sample size ranges from 50 [38] to 500 participants [31]. Regarding the mean age of the study participants, it varied from 4.07 to 46.70 years. Nine (56.2%) [31–34, 36, 40, 42, 44, 46] and 7 (43.8%) [35, 37–39, 41, 43, 45] of the studies were conducted on adult and child HIV-infected individuals, respectively. Four studies (25%) included both ART-taking and treatment-naïve groups, while 2 (12.5%) focused exclusively on ART-taking individuals and 5 (31.2%) on treatment-naïve HIV/AIDS individuals. In contrast, the treatment status was not reported for the remaining studies (Table 1).

The prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients in Africa

A total of 3588 participants in sixteen studies were used to estimate the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients. As indicated in the forest plot (Fig. 2), the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among all HIV/AIDS patients was found to be 22.25% (95% CI 17.38–27.13). The I2 test result indicated substantial levels of heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 92.6%, p < 0.001). Moreover, Cochran’s Q values for the heterogeneity test were 202.04 (degree of freedom, d.f. = 15). Thus, a subgroup analysis was done.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis based on sub-region, ART status, sex, study population (age), and publication year to investigate the sources of heterogeneity among the studies included in this meta-analysis. In four African sub-regions, the combined prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients ranged from 13.55% (95% CI: 6.80–20.29) in Central Africa to 29.90% (95% CI: 19.54–40.26) in South Africa. Except for studies done in Central Africa (P-value = 0.137), there was significant variation in the prevalence estimates between studies within each sub-region. Four African sub-regions showed significant heterogeneity as well (P-value = 0.026). Likewise, this study revealed that the overall estimated prevalence among ART-taking 22.32% (95% CI: 13.00–31.63) and treatment-naïve 19.41% (95% CI: 14.88–23.94) patients did not differ significantly with a P-value of 0.582 (Table 2).

Based on sex sub-group analysis, female HIV/AIDS patients had a slightly higher pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria 22.68% (95% CI: 7.43–37.94) than their male counterparts 15.84% (95% CI: 7.28–24.39). To see the prevalence of microalbuminuria over time, we divided the included studies into three groups (2006–2011, 2012–2017, and 2018–2022), based on the publication year. The highest pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria was observed in studies conducted between 2018 and 2022, at 25.94% (95% CI: 14.59–37.30), followed by 24.08% (95% CI: 11.32–36.85) in studies conducted between 2006 and 2011. Majority (62.5%) of the studies done from 2012 to 2017, thus a lower prevalence of 20.65% (95% CI: 14.29–27.01) was observed. Further, the subgroup analysis indicated that the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among adult study groups was 23.32% (95% CI: 17.36–29.29), as compared to 20.72% (95% CI: 11.85–29.56) in children. With a P-value of 0.633, the difference in adult and child heterogeneity was not statistically significant (Table 2).

Publication bias

Using funnel plots and Egger at a 5% significance level, publication bias was evaluated. The p-value for the overall prevalence of microalbuminuria among HIV/AIDS patients according to Egger’s regression test is 0.306. Additionally, the funnel plot (Fig. 3) was almost symmetrical. Therefore, the funnel plots and Egger tests showed no publication bias.

The sensitivity analysis revealed that omitting a single study from the aggregated burden estimates had no discernible effect. As a result of the random-effects sensitivity analysis, no one study had an effect on the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria (Table 3).

Discussion

Renal impairment, a sign of aggravated risk for cardiovascular disease (CV), is prevalent in HIV-positive patients. Patients with HIV frequently have microalbuminuria, which is an early indicator of renal HIV-related nephropathy and cardiovascular risk [20–23, 47]. As an increasingly frequent cause of ESRD, AIDS-related kidney disease is linked with significant morbidity and mortality in these patients [48–50].

This study describes a high pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria, defined as a mean urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) above 30 mg/g, in 22.25% of HIV-infected patients. Hence, the present review revealed that a significant proportion of individuals infected with HIV have microalbuminuria. Subsequently, the presence of subclinical abnormalities, particularly microalbuminuria, portends an increased risk of potentially more clinically relevant abnormalities, such as overt proteinuria, which leads to obvious renal impairment. In line with our finding, prior studies in HIV-positive patients reported a comparable incidence of microalbuminuria in the USA, ranging from 11% [51] to 20.2% [52]; Italy, 17% [53]; Jammu, India, 21.3% [49]; New Jersey, United States, 19.4% [54]; Pune, India, 17.6% [55]; and France, 18.1% [56]. Microalbuminuria in HIV-infected patients is predominantly caused by HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN), a condition in which direct infection of renal cells, particularly podocytes, impairs the glomerular filtration barrier, resulting in protein leakage into urine [5]. Chronic immune activation and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines exacerbate renal injury, while antiretroviral therapy, particularly tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), along with co-morbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and co-infections such as hepatitis B and C, increases the risk of microalbuminuria and kidney damage [6–10].

In contrast, the current prevalence is much lower than in a study done by Adedeji et al. [57] and Katia et al. [58], who reported higher prevalence rates of 51.9% and 49.7%, respectively. Moreover, the current pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria (22.25% among HIV/AIDS patients) is much lower than that among African diabetes patients (37.11%) [59]. These differences may be due to variations in study populations and underlying health conditions. In contrast, the current finding is higher than studies conducted in Norway (8.7% [60]) and Japan (13.2% [61]). Additionally, genetic variation is also one of the reasons for the differences; for instance, the Black population is known to be at higher risk for nephropathy due to variation in chromosome 22, which contains the MYH9 and ApoL1 genes [62].

Furthermore, the current study found that the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria was 23.32% in adult HIV/AIDS patients and 20.72% in child patients. Our results clearly demonstrate the need for physicians to closely monitor children with HIV to ensure that quality of life is achieved and maintained and to reduce the risk of HIV-associated kidney problems developing at a young age. The noted microalbuminuria of 20.72% among African children with HIV/AIDS was a little higher than the report from a previous study in the USA of 15% [63] and Chile of 11.1% [64]. Besides, the prevalence of microalbuminuria among adult patients was higher than 8.7% by Baekken et al. in Oslo, Norway [60], and 14% by New Heaven [65]. The present work is a meta-analysis of sixteen studies, whereas the Western studies were single observational studies, which may explain the disparity. Differences in microalbuminuria measurement methodologies and demographic variance also contribute to the findings of these investigations. A slightly higher rate of microalbuminuria among adult patients was in line with Islam et al. who reported that older age groups were more at risk of renal involvement as compared to younger patients [66]. This is explained by the combined impact of renal damage caused by HIV itself, an age-related decline in glomerular filtration rate, and the consequences of other chronic illnesses.

Regarding sex, this study revealed that female HIV/AIDS patients had higher prevalence rates of microalbuminuria (22.68%) compared to their male counterparts (15.84%). Males and females were found to have an equal risk of renal impairment, according to a prior study, which indicated no gender differences in the incidence of microalbuminuria [67]. Other studies showed a higher prevalence among male patients than females [59, 68, 69]. The exact reason for the observed phenomenon is unclear but may involve lower muscle mass in females leading to reduced creatinine excretion, which results in a heightened albumin-to-creatinine ratio despite similar albumin excretion rates [70]. Gender bias in therapy-seeking and adherence might be possible explanations.

The pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among ART-using patients was 22.32%, while a slightly lower proportion of 19.41% was observed among treatment-naïve HIV/AIDS patients. In agreement with the current work study in India, no significant difference in prevalence of microalbuminuria was observed in children on or not on ART [51, 71]. However, the previous studies demonstrated that recent ART was associated with microalbuminuria. For instance, one such study found that patients who were recently on ritonavir were more likely to have microalbuminuria compared to those who were not on ritonavir (19.8 vs. 7.4%) [72]. Hence, comparing this review study to the previous one, the rate of microalbuminuria was not significantly different between ART-taking and non-ART patients. Studies also indicated that, even though the use of ART improves patient quality of life, it is also associated with renal impairment [9, 10]. The development of effective therapies to reduce or reverse microalbuminuria in HIV-infected patients is crucial for justifying extensive screening programs. Currently, potential interventions focus on optimizing antiretroviral therapy (ART) to minimize nephrotoxicity and managing comorbid conditions that exacerbate renal damage. Moreover, studies suggest that early initiation of ART, particularly regimens with less nephrotoxic profiles, along with the use of angiotensin II receptor blockers, can help reduce microalbuminuria and prevent progression to more severe kidney disease [73, 74].

We also tried to conduct a subgroup analysis based on subregions and found significant differences among studies. Studies conducted in Western Africa found prevalence to be in a range of 6.74% to 35.6% (pooled estimate: 20.20%) [31–38]; in the eastern part, they reported 18.9% to 38.6% (pooled estimate: 26.66%) [39–42]. The highest prevalence was reported from South Africa, with a pooled estimate of 29.90% [45, 46], while Central Africa was reported to have the lowest prevalence rate of microalbuminuria, with a pooled estimate of 13.55% [43, 44]. In general, the observed differences in prevalence are supported by our subgroup analysis, which showed significant variation across subregions (heterogeneity between groups, p = 0.026), suggesting that geographical differences partially explain the variability among studies.

The current meta-analysis was unable to provide details on the associated risk factors connected to microalbuminuria due to limitations in the primary data. All the included studies were cross-sectional, which limits the ability to infer causality between HIV infection and microalbuminuria. Subgroup analysis on comorbidities, duration of HIV or ART, and ART regimen types could not be formally analyzed due to insufficient data. The included studies varied in methodological quality, sample size, and reporting standards, which may contribute to heterogeneity in the pooled estimates. In addition, potential residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as ART regimen types and coexisting medical conditions, could also affect the observed prevalence of microalbuminuria. Despite these limitations, this meta-analysis provides the results of multiple studies conducted in Africa, giving stronger support for the notion that microalbuminuria is common in HIV patients. Furthermore, it is the first study to present the pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria among adult and pediatric HIV patients using subgroup analysis.

Conclusion

The present review study found that over one-fourth of HIV-infected patients had microalbuminuria, indicating a considerable burden of subclinical kidney disease. Given the limitations of the included studies, early screening for microalbuminuria could be considered useful to identify patients at risk. Further research is needed to determine practical and effective screening approaches, particularly in resource-limited settings across Africa.
