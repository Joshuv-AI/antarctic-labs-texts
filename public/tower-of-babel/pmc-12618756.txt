Integrative Mendelian randomization and spatial transcriptomics analysis reveals causal links between plasma lipidome and non–small cell lung cancer
Authors: Baiquan Zhang, Jintao Liu, Qian Guo, Huanqin Wang, Lijun Miao, Lu Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the potential causal relationship between plasma lipidome and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach based on large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data.MethodsA two-sample bidirectional MR analysis was conducted using summary-level data from the GeneRISK and FinnGen GWAS. Genetic instruments were selected for 179 plasma lipid traits. Causal effects were estimated using inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, and Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) methods. Spatial transcriptomic profiling and machine learning–based gene prioritization were further employed to explore potential mechanistic links.ResultsMR identified 30 lipid species significantly associated with NSCLC risk, of which 17 demonstrated consistent causal effects across multiple sensitivity analyses. A subset of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine species exhibited inverse associations, indicating potential protective effects, while specific sterol esters, sphingomyelins, and triacylglycerols were positively associated with disease susceptibility. Plasmacytoma Variant Translocation 1 (PVT1) was first prioritized as a key lipid-associated gene by three machine learning algorithms and was subsequently validated by spatial transcriptomics, showing elevated expression in tumor-dense regions.ConclusionThis study reveals a causal link between distinct plasma lipid species and NSCLC risk, emphasizing the role of lipid structural diversity. PVT1 may act as a critical mediator connecting lipid dysregulation to lung tumorigenesis, offering novel biomarker and therapeutic target potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03823-2.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes about 85% of all lung cancer diagnoses, making it the most common histological variant globally [1]. Recent epidemiological data indicate that lung cancer continues to be the foremost cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [2]. The primary histological subtypes of NSCLC include adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma [3]. While tobacco use is still the principal risk factor for NSCLC, a growing incidence is observed among never-smokers, especially in specific geographic areas and among female patients. This trend underscores the necessity of identifying alternative risk factors that might play a role in the pathogenesis of NSCLC beyond conventional exposures. Although advancements in imaging, surgical methods, radiotherapy, targeted treatment, and immunotherapy have been considerable, the overall prognosis for NSCLC patients remains poor [4]. A significant number of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages of the disease, when curative therapeutic options are severely limited. This late detection is primarily due to the silent clinical course of early-stage NSCLC and the insufficient global implementation of screening strategies like low-dose computed tomography. Therefore, improving early detection among high-risk individuals and advancing the understanding of NSCLC etiology are critical for enhancing patient prognosis and treatment outcomes.

The involvement of lipid metabolic pathways in cancer biology has garnered growing attention in recent years. The plasma lipidome comprises a diverse array of lipid molecules that are fundamental for sustaining cellular energy balance, preserving membrane structure, and regulating intracellular signaling cascades. Key categories within the plasma lipidome include glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and sterol compounds [5]. Circulating lipid species participate actively in various physiological and disease-related mechanisms, notably inflammation, oxidative damage, and cancer advancement [6, 7]. Aberrant lipid metabolic activity has been linked to multiple cancer hallmarks, including uncontrolled cell division, resistance to programmed cell death, and disruption of signaling pathways [8]. Advances in lipidomics have enabled large-scale, quantitative characterization of the plasma lipidome across diverse biological specimens. These developments have substantially improved our understanding of how lipid metabolic dysregulation contributes to human diseases, particularly cancer [9]. Nevertheless, research directly assessing the relationship between plasma lipidome and NSCLC is still relatively limited. The bulk of current evidence stems from observational research, which is inherently susceptible to confounding variables and the risk of reverse causation [10, 11]. These methodological constraints hinder the establishment of definitive causal relationships.

Mendelian randomization (MR) has become an increasingly influential tool in genetic epidemiology, offering a robust framework to enhance causal inference within observational study designs [12, 13]. The MR framework utilizes genetic variants, most commonly single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified via genome-wide association studies (GWAS), as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer the causal impact of modifiable risk factors on disease endpoints [14]. Through the exploitation of the naturally randomized allocation of alleles during gamete formation, MR effectively simulates the randomization process observed in clinical trials, provided certain methodological assumptions are met [15]. If the core assumptions of MR are fulfilled—namely, relevance (genetic variants are significantly associated with the exposure), independence (variants are not linked to confounding variables), and exclusion restriction (variants influence the outcome exclusively via the exposure)—the method can yield valid and unbiased causal effect estimates [16].

In this study, we aim to investigate the potential causal relationship between specific plasma lipid species and the risk of NSCLC by integrating two-sample bidirectional MR, GWAS, and spatial transcriptomics. Unlike previous observational studies, our MR approach helps to reduce confounding and reverse causation, thereby improving causal inference. Additionally, we employ machine learning–based gene prioritization and spatial transcriptomics to explore possible molecular mediators underlying lipid–cancer associations. This integrative strategy is designed to uncover novel lipid biomarkers and mechanistic pathways involved in NSCLC development, ultimately contributing to early detection efforts and the identification of potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, a two-sample bidirectional MR analysis was performed utilizing summary-level data from publicly accessible GWAS to explore the potential causal links between the plasma lipidome and NSCLC (Fig. 1). Given that all datasets were anonymized and derived from open-access repositories, ethical approval and informed consent were not necessary for this analysis. To assess the stability and credibility of the MR findings, a comprehensive set of sensitivity analyses was conducted. Furthermore, both the MR-Steiger directionality test and reverse MR were applied to examine whether reverse causation might exist between plasma lipid species and NSCLC risk. According to the STROBE-MR guidelines for MR reporting, three core assumptions are essential for ensuring valid causal inference (Fig. 2): (1) Relevance, indicating that the genetic instruments are robustly linked to the exposure; (2) Independence, which requires that these instruments are unassociated with confounding variables; and (3) Exclusion restriction, implying that the genetic variants influence the outcome exclusively through the exposure under investigation [17].

GWAS summary data and human cell lines

Aggregated plasma lipidomic data were sourced from a GWAS involving 7,174 unrelated Finnish participants enrolled in the GeneRISK cohort. This study analyzed 179 lipid species across 13 lipid classes and 4 additional lipid categories, identifying genetic variants associated with both conventional and unconventional lipid species beyond traditional lipids [18]. SNPs associated with lipid traits at a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁵ were selected for analysis [19, 20]. GWAS summary statistics for NSCLC were obtained from the FinnGen study (Finn-b-C3_LUNG_NONSMALL), which included 1,627 NSCLC cases and 217,165 controls of European ancestry. This open-access dataset offers GWAS-derived summary statistics based on a European ancestry cohort. Comprehensive details regarding the study protocol, demographic features, genotyping methods, quality control procedures, and statistical approaches can be found in the corresponding GWAS publications [21].

The human NSCLC cell line A549 and the normal human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B were obtained from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Cells were cultured in the recommended growth medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Pricella) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Servicebio). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

IVs selection

To confirm the appropriateness of the IVs, a rigorous quality control protocol was employed in alignment with the three fundamental MR assumptions: relevance, independence, and the exclusion restriction criterion. First, SNPs strongly associated with the lipidome and NSCLC were identified using a suggestive significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶ [22]. Next, to reduce potential confounding from linkage disequilibrium (LD), clumping was performed using a 10 Mb window with an LD threshold set at r² < 0.001 [23]. Third, during the harmonization process, palindromic SNPs and those with ambiguous or inconsistent allele frequencies between the exposure and outcome datasets were excluded. Lastly, the strength of each IV was evaluated by calculating the F statistic, with values exceeding 10 considered indicative of sufficient instrument strength to avoid weak instrument bias [24]. Analysis method

This study employed multiple statistical approaches to conduct Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses, including inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger regression, the weighted median estimator (WME), as well as the weighted mode (WM) and simple mode (SM). Among these, IVW was used as the primary estimator for causal inference because of its high statistical efficiency, provided that all genetic instruments are valid and no directional pleiotropy is present. MR-Egger regression relaxes this assumption by allowing for directional pleiotropy, although this comes at the cost of reduced power. The weighted median estimator can provide consistent estimates even if up to 50% of the instruments are invalid, assuming the majority are valid. The mode-based approaches (weighted mode and simple mode) estimate causal effects based on clusters of instruments with similar effect sizes, offering robustness when the largest cluster of instruments is valid. By applying these complementary methods in combination, we aimed to evaluate the robustness and consistency of causal estimates under different assumptions. To assess heterogeneity across the genetic instruments, Cochran’s Q statistic was calculated. Horizontal pleiotropy was further examined using both the MR-Egger intercept test and the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) framework [27–29]. Additionally, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to evaluate whether the exclusion of any single SNP substantially altered the overall causal estimates [30, 31]. To strengthen causal inference, the MR-Steiger approach was also applied to determine whether the genetic instruments explained greater variance in the exposure than in the outcome, thereby providing evidence for the directionality of the associations [32, 33]. For integrative transcriptomic analyses—including single-cell and spatial transcriptomics—the instrumental SNPs were mapped to nearby genes within a ± 500 kilobase window of gene bodies. Genes identified in this step were then cross-referenced with known NSCLC-associated loci through Venn diagram analysis to identify overlapping candidates. Subsequent gene prioritization was conducted using three machine learning algorithms—Boruta, LASSO regression, and XGBoost—to rank candidate genes based on their feature importance scores. All MR-related analyses were implemented in R (version 4.2.1) using the TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO packages [27].

Spatial transcriptomic analysis

To investigate the spatial distribution of selected genes in NSCLC, we conducted spatial transcriptomics using a publicly accessible lung adenocarcinoma dataset (GSE179572-GSM5420754) [34] The raw spatial gene expression data were subjected to normalization and spatial registration to match the anatomical architecture of the tissue sections. Spot-level cell type annotations, with a diameter of approximately 55 μm, were derived through reference-based deconvolution using integrated immune and stromal cell atlases [35]. All analyses were conducted on the Sparkle platform (https://www.grswsci.top), which integrates 10x Genomics Visium spatial transcriptomic data from human tumor specimens [36]. To depict transcript distribution and evaluate gene expression variability within distinct tumor microenvironment regions, the SpatialPlot function from the Seurat R package (version 4.3) was utilized. Finally, to experimentally validate the transcriptomic findings, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to compare the expression of PVT1 between normal lung epithelial cells and NSCLC cell line.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from normal lung epithelial cells and NSCLC cell lines using the RNA Extraction Kit (AGbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed with 100 ng of total RNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit (AGbio, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted with the SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AGbio, China) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Gene-specific primers targeting PVT1 (AGbio, China) were used for amplification. Gapdh served as the internal control, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with normalization to the control group.

Results

The identification of IVs

A total of 2,790 SNPs that met stringent selection criteria were identified as IVs for plasma lipid traits. To assess the strength and validity of these IVs, F-statistics were calculated for each SNP; all values exceeded the conventional threshold of 10, indicating sufficient instrument strength and minimizing the risk of weak instrument bias. Detailed information on the selected IVs is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

MR analysis results

The causal effects of the lipidome on the risk of NSCLC were primarily assessed using the IVW method. The results indicated that sterol ester (SE), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), sphingomyelin (SM), phosphatidylinositol (PI), and triacylglycerol (TAG) may influence NSCLC risk (Fig. 3). Although some lipid species share similar structural backbones, their associations with NSCLC risk varied depending on the fatty acyl chain composition. Specifically, 11 lipid species, including SE (27:1/20:2), PEs (18:2_0:0 and 18:0_18:2), PCs (15:0_18:1, 15:0_18:2, 16:1_18:2, 18:0_20:2, 18:1_18:1, 18:1_20:2, O-16:0_16:0), and PI (18:0_20:3), were identified as protective factors. In contrast, SEs (27:1/20:4 and 27:1/20:5), PCs (20:4_0:0, 16:0_20:4, 16:0_20:5, 16:1_20:4, 18:0_20:4, 18:1_20:4, 18:2_20:4, O-16:0_20:4, O-16:1_20:4, O-18:0_20:4, and O-18:1_20:4), PEs (O-16:1_20:4 and O-18:1_20:4), SMs (d34:2 and d36:2), and TAGs (56:6 and 58:8) were identified as risk factors. Detailed results for all significant lipid species, including effect estimates, confidence intervals, and P values, are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Volcano plot analysis of MR for the association between lipid species and NSCLC risk

The volcano plot presents the results of MR analysis assessing the associations between lipid species and the risk of NSCLC (Fig. 4). The horizontal axis represents the effect size (beta coefficient), while the vertical axis shows the statistical significance as − log10(P value). The size of each point reflects the statistical power. The results revealed a clear bidirectional association pattern between lipid species and NSCLC risk. Lipid species located in the blue region on the left side were negatively associated with NSCLC risk, with the strongest protective associations observed for PI (18:0_20:3) and PE (18:2_0:0). In contrast, lipid species in the red region on the right side showed positive associations with NSCLC risk, including SE (27:1/20:4) and SM (d36:2).

Sensitivity analysis

Heterogeneity test

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (Table 1). Significant heterogeneity was detected for SE (27:1/20:2), PC (20:4_0:0), PE (18:0_18:2), PI (18:0_20:3), and TAG (58:8) (P < 0.05), suggesting potential heterogeneity in the causal estimates for these lipid species. Funnel plots generated using the MR-Egger and IVW methods generally showed symmetrical distributions for most lipid species (Supplementary Fig. 1). Mild asymmetry was observed for lipid species with significant heterogeneity, consistent with the results of Cochran’s Q test, suggesting potential heterogeneity.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of causal estimates. (Supplementary Fig. 3) For most lipid species, excluding any single SNP did not materially alter the causal estimates, indicating stable results. However, noticeable fluctuations were observed for PC species, specifically PCs (16:0_20:4, 16:1_20:4, 18:0_20:2, 18:0_20:4, 18:1_20:4, O-16:0_20:4), suggesting that certain SNPs may have disproportionately influenced the causal estimates for these exposures.

Reverse MR analysis

To assess the robustness and directionality of the observed causal relationships, the MR-Steiger directionality test (Table 3) and reverse MR analysis (Supplementary Table S3) were conducted. The MR-Steiger test supported the hypothesis that plasma lipid species were more likely to be upstream determinants of NSCLC rather than consequences of the disease. Reverse MR analysis was performed using NSCLC as the exposure and 179 plasma lipid species as the outcomes to evaluate potential bidirectional causal relationships. The analysis showed no evidence of a causal effect of NSCLC on the 30 plasma lipid species that were significantly associated in the primary analysis, suggesting no bidirectional causal relationship between NSCLC and these lipid species.

Prioritization and Spatial expression of shared genes

By intersecting lipid-associated genes from Mendelian randomization with NSCLC risk loci from GWAS datasets, we identified 21 shared genes (Fig. 5). Full gene names and annotations of the 21 overlapping genes are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Among these, Plasmacytoma Variant Translocation 1 (PVT1) was consistently prioritized across three independent feature selection algorithms—Boruta, XGBoost, and LASSO regression—as shown in Fig. 6A–D. Detailed feature importance scores, ranking metrics, and selection outputs from each model are provided in Supplementary Tables S5–S7 to support the consistency of PVT1 prioritization. Spatial transcriptomic visualization further revealed that PVT1 expression was predominantly concentrated in tumor-enriched regions, while its expression in stromal and immune cell areas remained comparatively low (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study employed a bidirectional two-sample MR design utilizing extensive GWAS summary-level data to investigate the potential causal association between plasma lipidome and susceptibility to NSCLC. The primary MR analysis revealed significant associations between 30 individual lipid species and the risk of NSCLC. After conducting a comprehensive set of sensitivity analyses—including Cochran’s Q test to assess heterogeneity, the MR-Egger intercept for detecting directional pleiotropy, and the MR-PRESSO test—17 plasma lipid species were found to exhibit stable and reproducible causal relationships with NSCLC risk.

Several lipid species—namely SEs (27:1/20:4, 27:1/20:5), PCs (16:0_20:5, 18:2_20:4, O-16:1_20:4, O-18:0_20:4), PEs (O-16:1_20:4, O-18:1_20:4), SM (d36:2), and TAG (56:6)—exhibited positive associations with NSCLC risk, indicating their potential involvement as lipid-based risk contributors. Conversely, PE (18:2_0:0) and a subset of PC species (15:0_18:1, 15:0_18:2, 16:1_18:2, 18:1_18:1, 18:1_20:2, O-16:0_16:0) were inversely correlated with NSCLC risk, suggesting a potentially protective lipidomic signature. This study utilized large-scale, publicly accessible GWAS summary statistics to conduct a two-sample bidirectional MR analysis, thereby improving causal inference validity and minimizing the risk of reverse causation bias. The application of complementary sensitivity analyses—such as MR-Egger regression, the MR-PRESSO global test, and the MR-Steiger directionality assessment—further reinforced the stability and reliability of the observed causal associations.

At the genomic level, a total of 23 intersecting genes were identified through the overlap between loci linked to lipid traits and genomic regions associated with NSCLC risk. Among these, the gene PVT1 was repeatedly ranked as a top candidate by several machine learning algorithms and subsequently confirmed by spatial transcriptomics, which demonstrated its elevated expression in tumor-dense areas. In addition, our qRT-PCR analysis further confirmed that PVT1 was markedly upregulated in the NSCLC cell line A549 compared with normal lung epithelial cells, providing supplementary experimental support for its involvement in tumor biology. This integrative evidence suggests that PVT1 may serve as a molecular mediator connecting aberrant lipid metabolism to the pathogenesis of NSCLC. Collectively, these results enhance our comprehension of how lipid-associated genetic factors contribute to NSCLC and highlight candidate molecular targets warranting further functional exploration.

In addition to class-level correlations, our MR analysis demonstrated that distinct lipid species belonging to the same class may exert divergent effects on NSCLC risk, emphasizing the critical role of molecular structure in lipid-related pathophysiological mechanisms. For example, among PC molecules, PC (15:0_18:1) and PC (18:1_18:1) were negatively associated with NSCLC risk, while PC (16:0_20:4) and its ether-linked counterpart PC (O 18:1_20:4) demonstrated positive associations with disease susceptibility. A comparable trend was identified within PE species, where PE (18:2_0:0), characterized by its high polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content, was inversely correlated with NSCLC risk, in contrast to its ether-linked form, PE (O 18:1_20:4), which showed a positive relationship.

The contrasting effects observed are likely attributable to variations in fatty acyl chain characteristics—including length, saturation level, and whether the linkage is ether or ester based—which influence membrane dynamics, lipid raft architecture, and protein–lipid interactions [37–39]. For instance, longer and more saturated acyl chains tend to reduce membrane fluidity and stabilize lipid rafts, thereby facilitating the clustering of oncogenic receptors such as EGFR and enhancing proliferative signaling. In contrast, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) introduce structural kinks into lipid bilayers, increasing membrane fluidity and susceptibility to lipid peroxidation, which can trigger ferroptosis and suppress tumor growth [40–42]. Ether-containing phospholipids such as PC(O 18:1_20:4) and PE(O 18:1_20:4) have been implicated in promoting membrane flexibility and reducing tension in metastatic cells, thereby supporting adaptation to oxidative or mechanical challenges and fostering invasiveness [43, 44]. Likewise, SM molecules like SM(d36:2)—which showed a positive correlation with NSCLC risk in our findings—are involved in ceramide pathway regulation and contribute to apoptosis evasion and enhanced tumor cell growth [45]. By contrast, PUFA-enriched ester-linked species such as PE(18:2_0:0) and PC(15:0_18:2) may exert anti-tumorigenic effects by enhancing oxidative damage and suppressing proliferative pathways [40, 41]. Such PUFA-containing lipids are thought to promote lipid peroxidation, alter cellular redox balance, and inhibit tumor-promoting signaling cascades [42]. Collectively, these mechanistic insights highlight that structural heterogeneity among lipid species is not merely a biochemical feature but a determinant of their pro- or anti-tumor functions, underscoring the importance of species-level resolution in cancer lipidomics.

The long non-coding RNA PVT1, situated on chromosome 8q24.21, is located within a genomic locus often co-amplified with the proto-oncogene MYC across multiple cancer types, including NSCLC [46]. PVT1 expression is significantly upregulated in both NSCLC tissues and cell lines, and shows strong associations with advanced TNM stages, lymphatic dissemination, and poor cellular differentiation, indicating its involvement in aggressive tumor phenotypes [47].

Functionally, PVT1 facilitates epithelial-mesenchymal transition through miR-200a sequestration, thereby promoting ZEB1 expression and enhancing the invasive capacity of tumor cells [48]. In addition, PVT1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin cascade, contributing to the maintenance of stem-like features and proliferative potential in cancer cells [49]. PVT1 has also been implicated in mediating chemoresistance—especially to cisplatin—possibly by modulating autophagic processes, regulating ATP-binding cassette transporter activity, and altering apoptosis-related signaling, thereby influencing therapeutic responsiveness [50]. Notably, PVT1 and MYC engage in a reciprocal regulatory circuit, wherein PVT1 enhances MYC protein stability and transcriptional activation, while MYC reciprocally promotes PVT1 transcription. This positive feedback loop has been documented in NSCLC and may play a pivotal role in maintaining oncogenic signaling pathways within the tumor microenvironment [46]. Additional evidence from other cancer types further substantiates the oncogenic potential of PVT1. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, PVT1 has been shown to preserve cancer stemness and inhibit CD8⁺ T cell infiltration through regulation of the miR-375/YAP1 pathway and DNA repair mechanisms [48]. In colorectal carcinoma, the PVT1-214 splice variant facilitates tumor progression by stabilizing Lin28 and suppressing let-7 microRNAs, thereby promoting a proliferative and stem-like cellular phenotype [51]. Collectively, these findings—along with the spatial enrichment of PVT1 in NSCLC tumor regions—support its potential role as a molecular bridge linking lipid metabolic disturbances to lung cancer development, warranting deeper exploration within lipid-related oncogenic networks. From a translational perspective, the identified lipid species and PVT1 may hold promise as early detection biomarkers or therapeutic targets, although further clinical validation is required to establish their utility.

There are several noteworthy limitations associated with this study. First, the lipidome-wide GWAS data were derived exclusively from Finnish-ancestry cohorts, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations with distinct genetic backgrounds and lipidomic profiles. Future replication studies in multi-ethnic or non-European cohorts are warranted to validate the robustness of the observed associations. Second, although we applied rigorous instrument selection and multiple sensitivity analyses, some associations observed in the MR analysis exhibited P-values close to the conventional significance threshold of 0.05. Given the large number of tests performed, we acknowledge the possibility of false-positive findings and recommend cautious interpretation of marginally significant results. Third, certain lipid species were instrumented by SNPs with limited variance-explaining capacity, which could reduce the precision of causal effect estimates and introduce weak instrument bias. Fourth, the spatial transcriptomics validation was conducted using data from lung adenocarcinoma samples only, which may not fully capture the molecular heterogeneity across all NSCLC subtypes, such as squamous cell or large cell carcinoma. Despite these constraints, our integrative framework—combining Mendelian randomization, gene prioritization algorithms, and spatial transcriptomic profiling—offers a multilayered perspective on lipid-mediated mechanisms in NSCLC and highlights novel candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Conclusions

This study utilized a two-sample bidirectional MR design based on large-scale GWAS data to assess the causal relationship between plasma lipid species and NSCLC risk. A total of 30 lipid species were initially found to be associated with NSCLC, of which 17 showed consistent causal effects across sensitivity analyses. These results highlight the importance of lipid structural diversity in cancer risk. Integrative analyses further identified PVT1 as a potential mediator linking lipid dysregulation to tumor development. In addition, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed the upregulation of PVT1 in NSCLC cells, providing experimental support for its potential role. These findings offer novel insights into the metabolic basis of NSCLC and suggest potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for future validation.
