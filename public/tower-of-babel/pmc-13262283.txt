Isolation of Burkholderia cepacia complex from pericardial fluid in a patient with lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis: A rare case report
Authors: K Mirlekar, M Saraf, A Sarage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurkholderia cepacia complex (BCC) comprises environmental non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli that primarily cause opportunistic infections in immunocompromised individuals. Pericardial infection due to BCC is exceedingly rare, particularly in patients with malignancy. Differentiating infectious from malignant effusions remains a diagnostic challenge, especially in tuberculosis-endemic regions.Case presentationWe report a case of a 50-year-old female presenting with chronic cough, fever, and progressive Grade III dyspnoea requiring oxygen support. Imaging revealed right pleural effusion and pericardial effusion. Cytological examination of pleural fluid demonstrated malignant cells, and immunohistochemistry confirmed lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis. The patient was also HBsAg positive with detectable viral load, representing an additional immunocompromised state.Pericardial fluid culture isolated Burkholderia cepacia complex, while pleural fluid cultures remained sterile. Tuberculosis workup, including Ziehl–Neelsen staining, GeneXpert testing, and adenosine deaminase levels, was negative. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing performed using CLSI 2024 interpretative criteria demonstrated susceptibility to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, levofloxacin, and meropenem; intermediate susceptibility to minocycline; and resistance to ceftazidime. The patient received targeted antimicrobial therapy along with chemotherapy and showed symptomatic improvement with reduced oxygen requirement.DiscussionIsolation of BCC from a sterile site without prior cardiac intervention raises important questions regarding pathogenesis. Dual immunocompromised states—lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis and chronic hepatitis B infection—likely facilitated opportunistic infection through hematogenous or contiguous spread. Clinical correlation and therapeutic response supported true infection rather than contamination.ConclusionsBCC can rarely cause pericardial infection in patients with malignancy. Comprehensive microbiological evaluation of pericardial fluid is essential even in confirmed malignant effusions to enable accurate diagnosis and targeted therapyKeywords: Burkholderia cepacia complex, Pericardial fluid infection, Lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis, Opportunistic infection, Pericardial effusion, Immunocompromised host, Non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli, Rare case report

Introduction

The Burkholderia cepacia complex comprises environmental non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli associated with opportunistic infections in immunocompromised hosts [1], [2] Pericardial involvement is extremely rare [3]. In tuberculosis-endemic regions, malignant and infectious effusions pose significant diagnostic challenges [4]. We report an unusual case of Burkholderia cepacia complex isolated from pericardial fluid in a patient with lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old female from Moregaon presented with dry cough for two months, fever for one month, and Grade III dyspnoea for three weeks. She had a 25-year history of tobacco (misri) use and occupational exposure to smoke from burning agricultural leaves.

On admission, the patient had cough, cold, body ache, and Grade III dyspnoea requiring oxygen support. Physical examination revealed bilateral crepitations.

CT chest demonstrated right-sided pleural effusion, lung atelectasis, mild cardiomegaly, and pericardial effusion.

Microbiology

Sputum culture showed no pathogenic growth.

Pleural fluid cultures demonstrated no microbial growth.

Aerobic blood cultures in pair were sent on the day of admission from Right cubital and Left cubital vein. There was no growth in the first 48 h and 5 days of incubation

Pericardial fluid culture yielded Burkholderia cepacia complex. The fluid was cultured on Blood Agar and Mac Conkey Agar. No special media was used.

Blood Agar -: White, smooth, slightly raised convex colonies with rounded and regular margins colonies. Approximately 1 mm in diameter.

Mac Conkey Agar -: Non lactose fermenting colonies with similar morphology.

Around 25–30 colonies grew on each culture media. Colony count method was not used for the pericardial fluid. Even a single colony would be significant. Colony count method is typically reported for samples like Urine, ET secretions where the standard loop is used for the culture.

The said growth was observed in 12 h of incubation. The Automated Vitek 2 Compact was used to identify the isolate. The Vitek 2 has been upgraded as per CLSI 2025 which does not give any susceptibility for this bacteria. Vitek identified the bacteria as Burkholderia Cepacia complex. No further species identification was done due to financial constraints. The Susceptibility testing was run manually thereafter and the antibiotics were reported in the next 18 h. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was interpreted cautiously, as Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2025 guidelines do not routinely recommend reporting susceptibility for certain antimicrobial agents against this organism [5]. However, testing was performed upon clinician request using CLSI 2024 interpretative criteria [6] to guide therapy.

The possibility of laboratory contamination was carefully evaluated. The organism was isolated from a normally sterile site obtained via aseptic ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis. Correlation with clinical findings and subsequent symptomatic improvement following targeted antimicrobial therapy supported its clinical significance rather than incidental contamination.

Pleural fluid cultures remained sterile, suggesting localized pericardial infection rather than disseminated pleuro-pericardial involvement. Isolation of an organism from a sterile body site along with compatible clinical presentation, exclusion of alternative etiologies, and therapeutic response fulfills accepted microbiological criteria for designation as a true pathogen.

Discussion

Burkholderia cepacia complex is an opportunistic non-fermenting Gram-negative bacillus known for intrinsic antimicrobial resistance and survival in moist environmental and healthcare settings [1], [2], [7]. Infection predominantly occurs in immunocompromised hosts.

In this case, two states were present. The primary factor was lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis, associated with impaired cellular immunity and malignant effusion formation. The second factor was chronic hepatitis B infection, which may further impair immune responses through chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation [8]. The coexistence of these conditions likely predisposed the patient to opportunistic infection.

The patient was HBsAg positive with a detectable viral load, indicating active Hepatitis B infection. Chronic HBV infection, particularly with ongoing viral replication, is associated with impaired T-cell–mediated immune responses, which may predispose to opportunistic infections. The patient was newly diagnosed and antiviral therapy was not initiated during hospitalization and appropriate hepatology follow-up for further evaluation and management of chronic HBV infection. This underlying immunological impairment may have contributed to the susceptibility and clinical course of Burkholderia cepacia complex infection in this patient.

A key clinical question remains how BCC entered the pericardial space in the absence of cardiac surgery or invasive procedures. Possible mechanisms include hematogenous dissemination from transient bacteremia or contiguous spread from adjacent pulmonary pathology. Negative blood cultures does not completely exclude a hematogenous route, as transient bacteremia may not always be captured. However, the isolation of Burkholderia cepacia complex exclusively from pericardial fluid, along with sterile pleural fluid, supports the possibility of hematogenous dissemination rather than contiguous spread from the lungs. Agricultural environmental exposure may also have contributed to colonization followed by opportunistic invasion.

An important diagnostic challenge was distinguishing true infection from contamination. Burkholderia cepacia complex is known to contaminate hospital environments, including medical devices, water systems, and solutions, sometimes leading to pseudo-outbreaks. However, in the present case, no additional cases were identified in the hospital during the same time period, and microbiological records did not reveal any clustering of similar isolates. Although environmental surveillance cultures were not performed, the absence of epidemiological linkage, coupled with consistent clinical findings and response to appropriate antimicrobial therapy, supports the diagnosis of a true infection rather than contamination. Further BCC can also exist as an environmental organism, several findings supported pathogenicity in this case.

Together, these factors strongly support true infection rather than laboratory contamination.

Malignant effusions are typically sterile unless secondary infection develops [9]. This case highlights the importance of routine microbiological analysis even when malignancy is confirmed. Early identification enabled targeted therapy guided by susceptibility testing performed under clinician request using CLSI 2024 criteria [6]

Tubercular pericarditis was excluded through negative molecular testing and low ADA levels, an essential consideration in endemic regions [4], [10].

Conclusions

Burkholderia cepacia complex can rarely involve the pericardial space in immunocompromised patients, as illustrated in this case of metastatic lung adenocarcinoma with coexisting Hepatitis B infection. The combination of malignancy-related immune suppression and chronic viral infection likely created a permissive environment for opportunistic infection.

The absence of prior cardiac intervention and sterile pleural fluid favors a hematogenous route of dissemination, although transient bacteremia could not be microbiologically confirmed. Importantly, multiple clinical and microbiological factors—including isolation from a sterile site, consistent clinical presentation, and response to targeted antimicrobial therapy—support true infection rather than contamination.

This case highlights the need for routine microbiological evaluation of pericardial fluid, even in patients with known malignant effusions, as early identification of uncommon pathogens can significantly influence management. Awareness of such rare presentations broadens the clinical spectrum of Burkholderia cepacia complex infections and highlights the importance of an integrated, multidisciplinary approach in complex immunocompromised hosts.
