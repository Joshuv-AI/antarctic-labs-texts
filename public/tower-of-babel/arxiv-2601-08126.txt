Abstract
. The Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem establishes pointwise convergence for integrable observables, but for f ∉ L 1 f\notin L^{1} , no normalization yields almost sure convergence. This paper investigates trimmed ergodic sums, where the largest observations are removed, for observables with polynomial tails ℙ ( f > t ) ≍ t − 1 / α \mathbb{P}(f>t)\asymp t^{-1/\alpha} in exponentially mixing dynamical systems. We prove trimmed strong laws of large numbers when α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 , extending known results from the i.i.d. case. Moreover, we establish distributional limit theorems for both lightly and intermediately trimmed sums in the regime α > 1 / 2 \alpha>1/2 , showing convergence to a non-standard law, which we describe explicitly, and a normal distribution, respectively. The proofs rely on approximating the trimmed sums by truncated ergodic sums and exploiting the system’s exponential mixing properties.

Part I
Results

1.
Introduction

A cornerstone of modern ergodic theory is the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem, which asserts that for an ergodic transformation
T
and an integrable observable
f\in L^{1},
the time averages converge almost surely to the spatial average. More precisely,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}(f)(x)}{N}=\mathbb{E}f\quad\text{for almost every }x,

where

S_{N}(f)(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f(T^{n}x).

A natural question, which we address for non-negative
f\notin L^{1}
, is what happens in the non-integrable case. One expects
S_{N}(f)
to grow faster than linearly, that is, faster than a constant multiple of
N
. Nevertheless, one might hope that, after renormalization by a suitable sequence
d_{N}\to\infty
, pointwise convergence still holds. However, Aaronson
[
Aar77
]
showed that such convergence is impossible: for any non-negative
f\notin L^{1}
and any normalizing sequence
d_{N}
,

(1)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}(f)(x)}{d_{N}}=\infty\quad\text{or}\quad\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}(f)(x)}{d_{N}}=0\quad\text{for almost every }x.

A heuristic explanation comes from order statistics: in the non-integrable case, the largest few terms typically dominate
S_{N}(f)
. Even a small shift in the position of an extreme value can therefore drastically change the sum, accounting for the oscillations in (
1
). This motivates the study of
trimmed sums
, where the largest terms are removed. For
N\geq 1
and
1\leq k(N)\leq N-1
, we define

(2)

S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f(T^{n}x)-\sum_{i=1}^{k(N)}f(x_{i}^{N}),

where
x_{1}^{N},\dots,x_{N}^{N}
are ordered so that

f(x_{1}^{N})\geq f(x_{2}^{N})\geq\dots\geq f(x_{N}^{N}).

The study of trimmed sums originates in classical probability theory, with contributions from Feller
[
Fel68
]
, Kesten
[
Kes93a
,
KM92
]
, Mori
[
Mor76
,
Mor77
]
, and others.

To help illustrate the concept, consider the function
f(x)=1/x
on the unit interval with a singularity at the origin. In this setting, the largest values of
f
occur at the orbit points
x_{1}^{N},\dots,x_{k(N)}^{N}
that lie nearest to the origin, and the trimmed sum is obtained by summing over the remaining points. However, this example also reveals a key conceptual nuance: if
f
is not monotonic-even when its only singularity remains at the origin-the points
x_{1}^{N},\dots,x_{k(N)}^{N}
need not be those closest to the singularity. This subtlety will play an important role in the subsequent analysis, and we revisit it in Section
3
.

Our focus is on establishing a
trimmed strong law of large numbers (SLLN)
: we seek sequences
d_{N}
and
k(N)
such that

(3)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}{d_{N}}=1\quad\text{for almost every }x.

The case where
k(N)=K
is constant is referred to as
light trimming
, while
k(N)\to\infty
with
k(N)=o(N)
is called
intermediate trimming
. Trimming a positive fraction of terms (i.e.,
k(N)\sim cN
) is uninteresting from our perspective, since it merely amounts to truncating
f
, reducing the problem to the classical SLLN.

In the most general sense, a trimmed SLLN always holds: for any non-negative
f
, there exist sequences
d_{N}
and
k(N)
such that (
3
) is satisfied (see
[
AS25
]
). While conceptually important, this result is of limited practical use, since it provides no control over the choice of
d_{N}
or, more crucially,
k(N)
. We therefore focus on functions with
polynomial singularities
, informally satisfying

\mathbb{P}(f>t)\asymp t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\quad\text{for some }\alpha>0.

A precise definition appears in Definition
1
. The functions
f
are characterized by a singularity order
\beta>0,
with
\alpha=\beta/d,
where
d
is the manifold dimension. For example, on
[0,1]
, consider
f(x)=x^{-\alpha}.

For brevity, we only sketch the relevant background, focusing on the i.i.d. case, which serves as a model for our results in exponentially mixing systems. We refer to
[
KS19
]
for a comprehensive survey. The key distinction lies between singularities of order
\alpha=1
and
\alpha>1
. Known results include:

•

If
\alpha=1
, then

(4)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{1}(f)(x)}{N\log N}=1\quad\text{for almost every }x,

as shown by Mori
[
Mor76
,
Mor77
]
and Kesten–Maller
[
KM92
,
KM95
]
.

•

If
\alpha>1
, then

(5)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}{N^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\quad\text{for almost every }x,

if and only if
\frac{k(N)}{\log\log N}\to\infty
while
k(N)=o(N)
. This was established by Haeusler
[
Hae93
]
and Haeusler–Mason
[
HM87
]
.

It is widely expected that (
4
) and (
5
) extend to sufficiently well-behaved dynamical systems. Indeed, the following dynamical counterparts are known:

•

for
\alpha=1
, (
4
) has been proved for
\psi
-mixing sequences
[
AN03
]
, the Gauss map
[
DV86
]
, and the doubling map
[
Sch18
]
;

•

for
\alpha>1
, (
5
) holds for systems with a spectral gap (such as piecewise expanding interval maps)
[
KS19
]
.

While these results are well established for i.i.d. sequences and certain specific dynamical systems, their validity for general exponentially mixing systems remains an open question. In this paper, we address this gap by establishing trimmed strong laws of large numbers for such systems. For
\alpha=1
, we prove that (
4
) holds in full. For
\alpha>1
, we prove a version of (
5
) under the trimming condition
k(N)/(\log N)^{\epsilon}\to\infty
for some
\epsilon>0
, which is close to the optimal
k(N)/\log\log N\to\infty
condition known for i.i.d. sequences.

Furthermore, we advance the theory by proving the corresponding distributional limit theorems. Once a trimmed SLLN is established, the natural next question concerns the size of second-order deviations. In this context, an untrimmed analogue arises from the distributional limit theorems of Gnedenko–Kolmogorov
[
GK68
]
for observables not in
L^{2}
. They show that, in the i.i.d. setting, if
f
has regularly varying tails, which is similar to our assumption polynomial singularities, then there are sequences
a_{N}
and
b_{N}
such that

\frac{S_{N}(f)-a_{N}}{b_{N}}\Rightarrow L\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

for a stable distribution
L
, where
\Rightarrow
denotes distributional convergence.

Trimmed versions of this model have also been studied; in particular, the lightly trimmed case has been investigated by several authors, including Darling
[
Dar52
]
, Arov–Bobrov
[
AB60
]
, Hall
[
Hal78
]
, and Teugels
[
Teu81
]
.
Their analysis of the maximal terms’ contribution to
S_{N}
demonstrates that, for
K\geq 1,
there exist sequences
a_{N}
and
b_{N}
such that

(6)

\frac{S_{N}^{K}(f)-a_{N}}{b_{N}}\Rightarrow L\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

where
L
is a non-degenerate limiting distribution. As shown by Kesten
[
Kes93b
]
, the normalizing sequences
a_{N}
and
b_{N}
do not depend on
K
, whereas the limiting distribution
L
does.

The intermediately trimmed case, where
k(N)\rightarrow\infty
but
k(N)=o(N)
, is of special interest because the limiting distribution is always normal. Csörő, Horvath, and Mason
[
CHM86
]
show that, for
k(N)\rightarrow\infty
with
k(N)=o(N)
, there exist sequences
a_{N},b_{N}
such that

(7)

\frac{S_{N}^{k(N)}-a_{N}}{b_{N}}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

where
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is the standard normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
. Subsequently, Csörő, Haeusler, and Mason
[
CHM88
]
provided an explicit characterization of the norming sequences
a_{N}
and
b_{N}
in this case.

In Theorems
9
and
11
, we prove dynamical analogues of (
6
) and (
7
) for systems that are exponentially mixing of all orders. Establishing these limit theorems in a dependent setting requires handling the interplay between extreme values (governing the trimming) and the system’s correlation structure. We show that the lightly trimmed sum converges to a non-standard distribution described in Section
8
, while the intermediately trimmed sum, when suitably normalized, converges to a standard normal distribution. Collectively, these results provide a nearly complete dynamical counterpart to the classical trimming theory for i.i.d. sequences with polynomial tails, extending it to a broad class of non-independent systems.

states the main theorems, and Section

. Sections

and

prove strong laws of large numbers under light and intermediate trimming, respectively. The tools for the stable law under light trimming are developed in Sections

, with the proof completed in Section

2.
Polynomial Singularities

Let
M
be a compact
d
-dimensional
C^{\kappa}
Riemannian manifold, and let
T:M\to M
be a
C^{\kappa}
diffeomorphism preserving a probability measure
\mu
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Riemannian volume
\mathrm{vol},
with a continuous density
\rho=\frac{\,\text{d}\mu}{\,\text{d}\mathrm{vol}}.

The system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is said to be
exponentially mixing of order
n
if there are constants
\kappa>0
,
C\geq 0
and
\gamma>0
such that, for all
n\geq 2
, functions
f_{0},\dots,f_{n-1}\in C^{\kappa}(M)
, and integers
0\leq k_{0}\leq\dots\leq k_{n-1}
, we have

(8)

\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}f_{j}\circ T^{k_{j}}\right)-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}f_{j}\right|\leq Ce^{-\gamma\min_{0\leq j\leq n-2}(k_{j+1}-k_{j})}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\|f_{j}\|_{C^{\kappa}}.

In particular, when
n=2
, the system is said to be
exponentially mixing
. If (
8
) holds for all
n\geq 2,
the system is called
exponentially mixing of all orders
.

Definition 1
.

Let
x^{*}\in M
, and let
f\in C^{\kappa}(M\setminus\{x^{*}\})
be a non-negative measurable function. We say that
f
has a power singularity of order
\mathrm{Ord}_{x^{*}}(f)=\beta
at
x^{*}
if the function defined by

g(x)=f(x)d(x,x^{*})^{\beta}\quad x\not=x^{*}

admits a
C^{\kappa}
smooth extension to
x^{*}
and
g(x^{*})>0
, where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the geodesic distance on
M
. In this case, we call
g(x^{*})=\mathrm{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)
the residue of
f
at
x^{*}
.

Remark 2
.

The function
f(x)=x^{-\alpha}
on
[0,1]
does not fully satisfy Definition
1
because its domain only gives a one-sided neighborhood of the singularity at
x^{*}=0
. On
\mathbb{T}^{1}
,
f
would have the required two-sided singularity. Nevertheless, all limit theorems below remain valid in this one-sided setting if
B_{1}
is replaced by
B_{1}/2=1
. This corresponds geometrically to only half of the tangential directions contributing to the singularity. Indeed, for
f(x)=x^{-\alpha}
we have
\mathrm{vol}(f>t)=t^{-1/\alpha}
, whereas for a function
h
with a proper power singularity of order
\alpha
,

\mu(h>t)\sim B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\mathrm{Res}_{x^{*}}(h)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Here
B_{1}=2
accounts for the full sphere of directions, so the one-sided case yields half this measure, i.e.
1
. This also explains the factor
1/2
difference between the constants in the i.i.d. case (
4
), (
5
) and those in Theorems
4
and
6
.

Under this condition, for
r>0
, there exists
\epsilon(r)>0
such that

(9)

(1-\epsilon(r))\mathrm{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)d(x,x^{*})^{-\beta}<f(x)<(1+\epsilon(r))\mathrm{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)d(x,x^{*})^{-\beta}

whenever
d(x,x^{*})\leq r,
and
\epsilon(r)\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow 0
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\epsilon
is
C^{\kappa}
smooth and monotonically decreasing as
r
approaches
0
.
Furthermore let
C_{0}>0
be a constant such that

(10)

f(x)\leq C_{0}d(x,x^{*})^{-\beta}\quad\forall x\in M.

We will also often denote
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{d}
.

Definition 3

(Slowly recurrent point)
.

A point
x^{*}\in M
is called
slowly recurrent
if, for every
A,C>0
, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
0<r<r_{0}
and positive integers
n\leq C|\log r|
, it holds that

\mu\left(B_{r}(x^{*})\cap T^{-n}(B_{r}(x^{*}))\right)\leq\mu(B_{r}(x^{*}))|\log r|^{-A}.

This notion of slow recurrence is closely related to the mixing properties of the system. Specifically, if the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing, then
\mu
-almost every point is slowly recurrent (see
[
DFL22
, Lemma 4.14]
).

We will establish trimmed limit theorems for functions that exhibit non-integrable power singularities.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing. Let
f
be a function with a power singularity of order
\operatorname{Ord}_{x^{*}}(f)=d
at a slowly recurrent point
x^{*}\in M
, and fix
K\geq 1
. Then it holds that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{K}(f)(x)}{N\log N}=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\quad\text{for almost every }x.

It is straightforward to verify that a lightly trimmed strong law of large numbers implies an untrimmed weak law. For the i.i.d. case, we refer to
[
Kes93a
]
, while the general case under mild additional conditions is treated in
[
AS25
, Remark 7]
.

Corollary 5

(Convergence in probability)
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
4
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu\left(\left|\frac{S_{N}(f)}{N\log N}-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0,\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

Theorem 6
.

Assume that the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing of all orders. Let
f
be a function with a power singularity of order
\operatorname{Ord}_{x^{*}}(f)=\beta>d
at a slowly recurrent point
x^{*}\in M
. Suppose
k(N)
is a monotone function and there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{k(N)}{(\log N)^{\epsilon}}=\infty.
Then it holds that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}{N^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha}}=\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)}{\alpha-1}B_{d}^{\alpha}\rho(x^{*})^{\alpha}\quad\text{for almost every }x,

where
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{d}
.

Remark 7
.

For i.i.d. observations, as discussed in the introduction, a stronger statement holds, namely a trimmed SLLN applies whenever

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{k(N)}{\log\log N}=\infty.

Probably this stronger statement is also valid in the exponentially mixing setting; however, proving it requires different and more sophisticated techniques. For now, we restrict our discussion to the statement of the theorem, reserving the stronger result for potential future work.

Remark 8
.

Analogously, one can derive intermediate trimming for the case
\operatorname{Ord}_{x^{*}}(f)=d
. The corresponding statement is as follows: if
k(N)\rightarrow\infty
and
k(N)=o(N)
then

(11)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}{N\log\left(\frac{N}{k(N)}\right)}=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\quad\text{for almost every }x.

However, given that Theorem
4
already establishes the strong law under light trimming, in what can be regarded as its strongest form with
K=1,
the result (
11
) is not the most interesting one. We therefore omit the proof and leave it as an exercise for the interested reader.

It should be noted, however, that (
11
) does not follow from the seemingly stronger light trimming result in Theorem
4
. Indeed, as shown in
[
AS25
]
, there exist functions for which trimming is not stable under removal of additional terms: an SLLN may hold for some trimming sequence
k(N)
, but fail if more terms are removed, say
k^{\prime}(N)>k(N)
.

Theorem 9
.

Assume that the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing of all orders. Fix
K\geq 1
, and let
x^{*}\in M
be a slowly recurrent point. Let

. Then there are constants

and a non-degenerate distribution

where
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{d}
. Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{a_{N}}{N}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

\displaystyle\text{if}\;\;\tfrac{1}{2}<\alpha<1,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{a_{N}}{N\log N}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)\,B_{d}\,\rho(x^{*}),

\displaystyle\text{if}\;\;\alpha=1,

\displaystyle a_{N}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{if}\;\;\alpha>1.

Remark 10
.

As mentioned in the introduction, similarly to the i.i.d. case studied by Kesten
[
Kes93b
]
, the normalizing constants
a_{N}
and
b_{N}
do not depend on the trimming number
K
, whereas the limit
Y
does.

Theorem 11
.

For
k(N)\to\infty
with
k(N)=o(N)
and a slowly recurrent point
x^{*}
let
f\in C^{\kappa}(M\setminus\{x^{*}\}
be a function with a power singularity of order
\operatorname{Ord}_{x^{*}}(f)=\beta>\frac{d}{2}

. Then there are constants

Part II
Proofs

3.
Large observations and closest points

Let us revisit an issue mentioned in the introduction: the conceptual relationship between trimming based on extremal function values and trimming based on geometric proximity to a singularity. For the canonical example
f(x)=1/x
, monotonicity ensures these two criteria select the same points. Consider, by contrast, a perturbed function such as
g(x)=1/x+\cos(1/x^{2})
, where oscillation can decouple the location of extreme values from proximity to the singularity. Despite this potential discrepancy, the asymptotic behavior often remains similar, yet a precise framework is required for proof.

We will prove that the trimmed sum is asymptotically equivalent to the sum obtained by removing the points closest to the singularity. Working with this geometric notion of trimming will provide a more streamlined foundation for the proofs that follow.

Consider
k
as either a constant
K
or a function
k(N)
.
Let
\hat{S}_{N}^{k}(f)(x)
denote the sum obtained by removing the
k
closest visits to
x^{*}
, formally defined as

(12)

\hat{S}_{N}^{k}(f)(x)=S_{N}(f)(x)-\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(\hat{x}_{l}),

where
\hat{x}_{l}
is the
l
th closest point to
x^{*}
among
\{x,\ldots,T^{N-1}(x)\}
. By definition of
S_{N}^{k},
it clearly holds that

S_{N}^{k}(f)\leq\hat{S}_{N}^{k}(f).

On the other hand, let
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
be the points that maximize
f
among
\{x,\ldots,T^{N-1}(x)\},
ordered by increasing distance to
x^{*}
so that

\displaystyle d(x_{1},x^{*})\leq d(x_{2},x^{*})\leq\cdots\leq d(x_{k},x^{*}),

\displaystyle f(x_{i})\geq f(T^{j}(x))\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq k,\,\,0\leq j\leq N-1,\,\,T^{j}(x)\not\in\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right\}.

Then we have

\hat{S}_{N}^{k}(f)(x)-S_{N}^{k}(f)(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(x_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(\hat{x}_{i}).

If
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}=\{\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}\}
then the sums coincide. Otherwise, let
1\leq m\leq k
be the number of points
x_{i}
not in
\{\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}\}.
Since no two points coincide - otherwise
x
would be periodic - there are also exactly
m
points
\hat{x}_{i}
not in
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}.
Rename those points such that
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\setminus\{\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}\},
and likewise for
\{\hat{y}_{i},\ldots,\hat{y}_{m}\}
, preserving the original order - although the order itself is not essential for the subsequent argument.

For any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, by construction we have
f(y_{i})\geq f(\hat{y}_{j})
since
y_{i}
was chosen among the largest values. On the other hand, by (
9
) we have

\displaystyle f(y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},y_{i})))\mathrm{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)d(x^{*},y_{i})^{-\beta}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k})))\mathrm{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)d(x^{*},\hat{y}_{j})^{-\beta}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k}))}{1-\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k}))}f(\hat{y}_{j}).

At the same time
\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\hat{y}_{i})\leq S_{N}^{k}(f)(x)
and therefore

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{N}^{k}(f)(x)-S_{N}^{k}(f)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(f(y_{i})-f(\hat{y}_{i}))

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k}))}{1-\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k}))}-1\right)\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\hat{y}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k}))}{1-\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k}))}-1\right)S_{N}^{k}(f)(x),

and we conclude

(13)

\hat{S}_{N}^{k}(f)(x)\leq\frac{1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k}))}{1-\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k}))}S_{N}^{k}(f)(x).

Lemma 12
.

Let
k(N)=o(N),
and suppose that
f
has a power singularity at
x^{*}
. Then, for almost every
x,
it holds that

d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})\rightarrow 0\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Let
G
be the set of generic points
x\in M
satisfying

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{N}\big({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{2^{-l}}(x^{*})}\big)(x)}{N}=\mu(B_{2^{-l}}(x^{*}))\quad\forall l\geq 1.

By the Ergodic Theorem,
\mu(G)=1
. For
x\in G
and
\varepsilon>0
, choose
l
sufficiently large so that
2^{-l}<\varepsilon
, then for
N
big enough it follows that

S_{N}\big({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{2^{-l}}(x^{*})}\big)(x)>k(N),

This implies that all
k(N)
closest points
\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k(N)}
lie in
B_{2^{-l}}(x^{*})
, so
d(\hat{x}_{k(N)},x^{*})\leq 2^{-l}
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
k(N)=o(N)
and suppose
f
has a power singularity at
x^{*}
. Then for almost every
x,

(14)

d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})\rightarrow 0\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Applying the same argument as in Lemma
12
to the sets
\{f>R\}
, where
R>0
, instead of balls, yields that almost surely
f(x_{k(N)})\rightarrow\infty
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Keeping in mind that
f(y)\leq C_{0}d(x^{*},y)^{-\beta}
for all points
y
, the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
k(N)=o(N)
and suppose that
f
has a power singularity at
x^{*}
. Then

d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})\sim d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})

for almost every
x.

Proof.

By definition
d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})\leq d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})
, so it suffices to show

(15)

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})}{d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})}\leq 1.

It holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)\left(1-\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)}))\right)}{d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq f(\hat{x}_{k(N)})\leq f(x_{k(N)})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)\left(1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k(N)}))\right)}{d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})^{\beta}}.

As
N\to\infty,

d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})\to 0
and
d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})\to 0
imply
\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)}))\to 0
and
\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{k(N)}))\to 0,
which in turn implies (
15
).
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
k(N)=o(N)
and suppose
f
has a power singularity at
x^{*}
. Then it holds that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}{S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}=1\quad\text{for almost every }x.

Proof.

Since
\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)\geq S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)
by definition, it suffices to show that

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}{\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)}\leq 1\quad\text{for almost every }x.

Given that
d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)}),d(x^{*},x_{k(N)})\rightarrow 0
as
N\to\infty,
the desired inequality follows directly from (
13
).
∎

4.
Truncation

For a given
\epsilon>0
we shall refer to a function of the form

g=f\cdot\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\epsilon}(x^{*})}\right)

as a
truncation
of
f
. In the proof of the main theorems, the key idea is to compare the trimmed ergodic sum with a standard ergodic sum of a suitable truncation of
f
. To this end, we provide below an estimate for the variance of ergodic sums associated with such truncations.

Lemma 16

(Mixing Estimate for Balls)
.

If the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing as defined in (
8
), then for any balls
B_{1},B_{2}\subset M
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\left|\mu(B_{1}\cap T^{-k}B_{2})-\mu(B_{1})\mu(B_{2})\right|\ll\max\left(\mu(B_{1}),\mu(B_{2})\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d}\cdot\frac{2\kappa}{2\kappa+1}}e^{-\frac{\gamma k}{2\kappa+1}}.

Lemma
16
is a standard consequence of exponential mixing and is therefore omitted.

Lemma 17

(Variance for the Truncation)
.

Suppose the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing and
\beta>\frac{d}{2}
.
For a sequence
\epsilon_{N}\rightarrow 0
, define
g_{N}=f\cdot\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\epsilon_{N}}(x^{*})}\right).
Then

\mathrm{Var}\left(S_{N}\left(g_{N}\right)\right)\ll N\epsilon_{N}^{-(2\beta-d)}.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, we omit the subscript
N
and write
\bar{g}=g-\mathbb{E}g.
we decompose the variance of the ergodic sum
S_{N}g
as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(S_{N}g)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(S_{N}\bar{g})^{2}

\displaystyle=N\mathbb{E}\bar{g}^{2}+\sum_{|j-k|\geq C|\log\epsilon|}\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot\bar{g}\circ T^{k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leq|j-k|<C|\log\epsilon|}\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot\bar{g}\circ T^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=I+II+III,

where
C>0
is a sufficiently large constant to be specified below. We proceed to estimate each term separately.

Step 1: Bounding
I.

We have

\mathbb{E}\bar{g}^{2}=\frac{d}{2\beta-d}\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\left(\epsilon^{-(2\beta-d)}-1\right)+o\left(\epsilon^{-(2\beta-d)}\right),

for
\beta>\frac{d}{2}.
Consequently,

I=N\mathbb{E}\bar{g}^{2}\ll N\epsilon^{-(2\beta-d)}.

Step 2: Bounding
II
via exponential mixing.

Let
0<\tilde{\epsilon}<\epsilon
to be chosen later.
We can construct a function
h\in C^{\kappa}(M)
such that
0\leq h\leq g
,
h=0
on
B_{\epsilon-\tilde{\epsilon}}(x^{*})
, and
h=g
on
M\setminus B_{\epsilon}(x^{*})
, satisfying

(16)

\|h\|_{C^{\kappa}}\ll\epsilon^{-\beta-\kappa}\tilde{\epsilon}^{-\kappa},

and

(17)

\|g-h\|_{L^{1}}\ll\epsilon^{d-\beta-1}\tilde{\epsilon}.

Denote
\bar{h}=h-\mathbb{E}h
. By (
16
), (
17
) and exponential mixing, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot\bar{g}\circ T^{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\ll\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{h}\circ T^{j}\cdot\bar{h}\circ T^{k}\right)\right|+\|\bar{g}\|_{\infty}\|\bar{g}-\bar{h}\|_{L^{1}}+\|\bar{h}\|_{\infty}\|\bar{g}-\bar{h}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\ll e^{-\gamma|j-k|}\|h\|_{C^{\kappa}}^{2}+\epsilon^{-2\beta+d-1}\tilde{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll e^{-\gamma|j-k|}\epsilon^{-2\beta-2\kappa}\tilde{\epsilon}^{-2\kappa}+\epsilon^{-2\beta+d-1}\tilde{\epsilon}.

Choosing
\tilde{\epsilon}=e^{-\frac{\gamma|j-k|}{2\kappa+1}},
we obtain

\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot\bar{g}\circ T^{k}\right)\right|\ll\epsilon^{-2\beta-2\kappa}e^{-\frac{\gamma|j-k|}{2\kappa+1}}.

Then, by setting
C>\frac{(d+1)(2\kappa+1)}{\gamma}
and considering
|j-k|=C|\log\epsilon|+i
with
i\in\mathbb{N},
it follows that

\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot\bar{g}\circ T^{k}\right)\right|\ll\epsilon^{-2\beta+d+1}e^{-\frac{\gamma i}{2\kappa+1}}.

Therefore, summing over all pairs
(j,k)
such that
|j-k|\geq C|\log\epsilon|,
we conclude that

II\ll N\epsilon^{-2\beta+d+1}.

Step 3: Bounding
III
via slow recurrence.

Since

III\ll\sum_{1\leq|j-k|<C|\log\epsilon|}\mathbb{E}\left(g\circ T^{j}\cdot g\circ T^{k}\right),

it suffices to prove that

\sum_{1\leq|j-k|<C|\log\epsilon|}\mathbb{E}\left(g\circ T^{j}\cdot g\circ T^{k}\right)\ll N\epsilon^{-2\beta+d}.

For
\hat{\epsilon}>\epsilon
to be chosen later, let
\hat{g}=g\cdot\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*})}\right).
For
1\leq|j-k|<C|\log\epsilon|,
we decompose the term
\mathbb{E}\left(g\circ T^{j}\cdot g\circ T^{k}\right)
as

\mathbb{E}\left(g\circ T^{j}\cdot g\circ T^{k}\right)=\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{B}+\mathfrak{C}+\mathfrak{D},

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}

\displaystyle:=\int_{T^{-j}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))}\hat{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot g\circ T^{k}\,\text{d}\mu,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}

\displaystyle:=\int_{T^{-k}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))}g\circ T^{j}\cdot\hat{g}\circ T^{k}\,\text{d}\mu,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left(\hat{g}\circ T^{j}\cdot\hat{g}\circ T^{k}\right),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}

\displaystyle:=\int_{T^{-j}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))\cap T^{-k}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))}g\circ T^{j}\cdot g\circ T^{k}\,\text{d}\mu.

Bounding
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}
: We apply Hölder’s inequality twice with conjugate exponents
p
and
q,
leading to

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{-j}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))}(\hat{g}\circ T^{j})(g\circ T^{k})\,\text{d}\mu\leq\mu(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\hat{g}^{q}\circ T^{j}\cdot g^{q}\circ T^{k}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\ll\hat{\epsilon}^{\frac{d}{p}}\left(\mathbb{E}\hat{g}^{pq}\right)^{\frac{1}{pq}}\left(\mathbb{E}g^{q^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q^{2}}}\ll\hat{\epsilon}^{-\beta+\frac{d}{p}+\frac{d}{pq}}\epsilon^{-\beta+\frac{d}{q^{2}}}.

The same argument applies to
\mathfrak{B}.

Bounding
\mathfrak{C}
: we use Cauchy-Schwartz to obtain

\mathfrak{C}\ll\mathbb{E}\hat{g}^{2}\ll\hat{\epsilon}^{-(2\beta-d)}.

Bounding
\mathfrak{D}
: By the slow recurrence property (see Definition
3
) with
1\leq|j-k|<2C|\log\hat{\epsilon}|
and
A>0
large,

\mu\left(T^{-j}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))\cap T^{-k}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))\right)\leq\mu(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))|\log\hat{\epsilon}|^{-A}\ll\hat{\epsilon}^{d}|\log\hat{\epsilon}|^{-A}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(T^{-j}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))\cap T^{-k}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}}(x^{*}))\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\mathbb{E}g\circ T^{j}\cdot g^{q}\circ T^{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\ll\hat{\epsilon}^{\frac{d}{p}}|\log\hat{\epsilon}|^{-\frac{A}{p}}\left(\mathbb{E}g^{pq}\right)^{\frac{1}{pq}}\left(\mathbb{E}g^{q^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q^{2}}}

\displaystyle\ll\hat{\epsilon}^{\frac{d}{p}}|\log\hat{\epsilon}|^{-\frac{A}{p}}\epsilon^{\frac{d}{q}-2\beta}.

For a big enough constant
A^{\prime}>0
, set
\hat{\epsilon}=\epsilon|\log\epsilon|^{\frac{2A^{\prime}}{2\beta-d}},
let
p=100,
and
A>100A^{\prime}+\frac{2dA^{\prime}}{2\beta-d}
. Then we have

\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D}\ll\epsilon^{-(2\beta-d)}|\log\epsilon|^{-A^{\prime}}.

Furthermore, since there are
O(N|\log\epsilon|)
pairs in the summation over
III
, we obtain

III\ll N|\log\epsilon|\left(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{B}+\mathfrak{C}+\mathfrak{D}\right)\ll N\epsilon^{-(2\beta-d)}|\log\epsilon|^{-A^{\prime}+1}.

Finally, combining the upper bounds for
I
,
II
, and
III
concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Lightly trimmed SLLN

In this section, we establish a trimmed SLLN for ergodic sums with a fixed trimmed number
K
and thereby provide a proof of Theorem
4
. In light of Lemma
15
, it suffices to show a SLLN for
\hat{S}_{N}^{K}
.

Suppose that the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing, and that the function
f
has a power singularity of order
\operatorname{Ord}_{x^{*}}(f)=d
. Define

\delta_{N}=\left(\frac{(\log\log N)^{6}}{N\log N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{N}=f\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}}\right).

The subsequent lemma provides bounds the probability that the trimmed sum differs from the original sum by more than a given threshold.

Lemma 18

(Trimmed Sum Approximation Error)
.

Under the hypothesis above, we have

(18)

\mu\left(\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>\frac{KC_{0}N\log N}{(\log\log N)^{6}}\right)\ll(\log N)^{-\frac{3}{2}},

where the constant
C_{0}
defined in (
10
).

Proof.

We observe that

\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|=\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f(T^{n}x){\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}}(T^{n}x)-\sum_{i=1}^{K}f(\hat{x}_{i})\right|.

If
S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})(x)=m\leq K,
then
\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{m}\in B_{\delta_{N}},
and consequently,

\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)(x)-S_{N}(f_{N})(x)\right|\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{K}f(\hat{x}_{i})\leq\frac{KC_{0}N\log N}{(\log\log N)^{6}}.

It follows that

\left\{\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>\frac{KC_{0}N\log N}{(\log\log N)^{6}}\right\}\subseteq\left\{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})\geq K+1\right\}.

Applying Markov’s inequality, we obtain for sufficiently large
N,

(19)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\left(\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>\frac{KC_{0}N\log N}{(\log\log N)^{6}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})\geq K+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})-1\right)\geq(K+1)K\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{(K+1)K}\mathbb{E}\left(\binom{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})}{2}\right).

We compute the variance of the binomial sum

\mathbb{E}\left(\binom{S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})}{2}\right)=\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq N-1}\mu\left(T^{-j}B_{\delta_{N}}\cap T^{-k}B_{\delta_{N}}\right).

and consider two cases based on the distance between indices
j
and
k.

Case 1 (Long Range): If
k-j>C\log N
for some large constant
C>0
, using Lemma
16
and the fact that
\mu(B_{\delta_{N}})\asymp(\log\log N)^{6}/(N\log N)
, we have

\mu\left(T^{-j}B_{\delta_{N}}\cap T^{-k}B_{\delta_{N}}\right)=\mu(B_{\delta_{N}})^{2}+O\left(\mu(B_{\delta_{N}})^{\frac{d-1}{d}\cdot\frac{2\kappa}{2\kappa+1}}N^{-\frac{C\gamma}{2\kappa+1}}\right).

Thus, the contribution from long-range pairs is
O\left((\log\log N)^{12}/(N^{2}(\log N)^{2})\right).

Since the number of such pairs is at most
N^{2}
, their total contribution is

O\left(\frac{(\log\log N)^{12}}{(\log N)^{2}}\right).

Case 2 (Short Range): By the slow recurrence property with
A=3
, for large
N
, we obtain

\mu\left(T^{-j}B_{\delta_{N}}\cap T^{-k}B_{\delta_{N}}\right)\ll\mu(B_{\delta_{N}})(\log N)^{-2}\ll\frac{(\log\log N)^{6}}{N(\log N)^{3}}.

The number of such pairs is at most
CN\log N
, so their total contribution is

O\left(\frac{(\log\log N)^{6}}{(\log N)^{2}}\right).

Combining both cases, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\binom{S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{B_{\delta_{N}}})}{2}\right)\ll\frac{(\log\log N)^{12}}{(\log N)^{2}}+\frac{(\log\log N)^{6}}{(\log N)^{2}}\ll(\log N)^{-\frac{3}{2}}.

Finally, we complete the proof using the inequality (
19
).
∎

Lemma 19

(Ergodic Sum of the Truncated Function)
.

There exists a sequence
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
such that

\mu\left(\left|S_{N}(f_{N})-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N\log N\right|>\epsilon_{N}N\log N\right)\ll(\log N)^{-1}(\log\log N)^{-4}.

Proof.

First note that

\mathbb{E}f_{N}\sim\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\log N.

Let
\varepsilon_{N}\to 0
be chosen such that

(20)

\left|\mathbb{E}f_{N}-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\log N\right|<\varepsilon_{N}\log N.

Letting
\epsilon_{N}=2\varepsilon_{N}+(\log\log N)^{-1}
, Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left|S_{N}(f_{N})-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N\log N\right|>\epsilon_{N}N\log N\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left|S_{N}(f_{N})-\mathbb{E}S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>\frac{\epsilon_{N}N\log N}{2}\right)\ll\frac{\operatorname{Var}(S_{N}(f_{N}))}{(\epsilon_{N}N\log N)^{2}}.

Now Lemma
17
yields

\operatorname{Var}(S_{N}(f_{N}))\ll N\delta_{N}^{-d}\ll\frac{N^{2}\log N}{(\log\log N)^{6}},

and we conclude

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\left(\left|S_{N}(f_{N})-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N\log N\right|>\epsilon_{N}N\log N\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{N^{2}\log N}{(\log\log N)^{6}(\epsilon_{N}N\log N)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll(\log N)^{-1}(\log\log N)^{-4}.

∎

Proof the Theorem
4
.

By Lemmas
18
and
19
, for

\omega_{N}=\epsilon_{N}+\frac{KC_{0}}{(\log\log N)^{6}}\to 0

we obtain

\mu\left(\left|\frac{\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)}{N\log N}-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\right|>\omega_{N}\right)\ll(\log N)^{-1}(\log\log N)^{-4}.

Now consider the subsequence defined by

N_{l}=\left\lceil\exp\left(\frac{l}{(\log l)^{2}}\right)\right\rceil,

from the above we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left|\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{l}}^{K}(f)}{N_{l}\log N_{l}}-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\right|>\omega_{N_{l}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\log N_{l})^{-1}(\log\log N_{l})^{-4}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l^{-1}(\log l)^{-2}

\displaystyle<\infty.

Therefore, by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, for
\mu
-almost every
x,

\lim_{l\to\infty}\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{l}}^{K}(f)(x)}{N_{l}\log N_{l}}=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*}).

For arbitrary
N\in[N_{l},N_{l+1}],
the monotonicity of the trimmed sum
\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)
implies

\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{l}}^{K}(f)}{N_{l+1}\log N_{l+1}}\leq\frac{\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)}{N\log N}\leq\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{l+1}}^{K}(f)}{N_{l}\log N_{l}}.

Taking the limit as
l\to\infty
, and observing that
\lim_{l\to\infty}N_{l+1}/N_{l}=1
, we conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)(x)}{N\log N}=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})

for
\mu
-almost every
x
. Finally, Lemma
15
implies that also

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{K}(f)(x)}{N\log N}=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})

∎

Proof the Corollary
5
.

Theorem
4
establishes that the normalized trimmed sum
S_{N}^{K}(f)/(N\log N)
converges almost surely to
\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})
. To pass from trimmed to untrimmed sums, we observe that the contribution from the
K
largest terms is negligible in probability:

\mu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}f(x_{i}^{N})>KN\log\log N\right)\leq K\mu\left(f>N\log\log N\right)\ll(\log\log N)^{-1}\to 0.

Therefore, the difference between
S_{N}(f)/(N\log N)
and
S_{N}^{K}(f)/(N\log N)
vanishes in probability, and the full sum shares the same limit in probability, that is

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu\left(\left|\frac{S_{N}(f)}{N\log N}-\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}\rho(x^{*})\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0,\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

∎

6.
Intermediately trimmed SLLN

In this section, we assume the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing of all orders and establish an intermediately trimmed SLLN for ergodic sums, thereby providing a proof of Theorem
6
. According to Lemma
15
, it is enough to prove the SLLN for
\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}
.

Let
\beta>d
and denote
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{d}
. The following two Lemmas are immediate consequences of the proof of Lemma
38
with balls instead of superlevel sets; see also Remark
40
.

Lemma 20
.

For
\epsilon_{N}\rightarrow 0
with
\epsilon_{N}^{-d}=o(N)
and
m\geq 2
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}S_{N}(\bar{g}_{N})^{m}\ll N^{\frac{m}{2}}\epsilon_{N}^{-m\beta+\frac{md}{2}},

where
g_{N}=f\cdot\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\epsilon_{N}}(x^{*})}\right)
and
\bar{g}_{N}=g_{N}-\mathbb{E}g_{N}
.

Lemma 21
.

For
\epsilon_{N}\rightarrow 0
with
\epsilon_{N}^{-d}=o(N)
and
m\geq 2
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}S_{N}(\bar{\chi}_{N})^{m}\ll N^{\frac{m}{2}}\epsilon_{N}^{\frac{md}{2}},

where
\bar{\chi}_{N}={\mathbf{1}}_{B_{\epsilon_{N}}(x^{*})}-\mu(B_{\epsilon_{N}}(x^{*}))
.

Lemma 22

(Trimmed Sum Approximation Error)
.

Let
k(N)\rightarrow\infty
be a sequence such that
k(N)=o(N)
, and let
r_{N}>0
be defined so that

\mu(B_{r_{N}}(x^{*}))=\frac{k(N)}{N}.

Furthermore let
f_{N}=f\cdot\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})}\right).
Then, for every
A>0
and small
\epsilon>0,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mu\left(\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>CN^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha-\epsilon}\right)\ll k(N)^{-A}.

Proof.

By the continuity of the density function of
\mu,
we have

r_{N}^{d}\sim\frac{k(N)}{B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N}.

Define the event

E_{N}=\left\{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})})<k(N)+k(N)^{1-\epsilon}\right\}\cap\left\{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*})})<k(N)\right\}.

On
E_{N}
, the difference between the trimmed sum and the truncated sum satisfies

\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|<C_{0}2^{\beta}r_{N}^{-\beta}k(N)^{1-\epsilon}<CN^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha-\epsilon},

where
C_{0}>0
is the constant from (
10
) and
C=C_{0}(2B_{d}\rho(x^{*})+10)^{\beta}.

It follows that

\left\{\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)-S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>CN^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha-\epsilon}\right\}\subset E_{N}^{c}.

The probability of the complement
E_{N}^{c}
is bounded by

(21)

\displaystyle\mu(E_{N}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\left|S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})})-k(N)\right|\geq k(N)^{1-\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle+\mu\left(\left|S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*})}\right)-N\mu(B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*}))\right|\geq k(N)-N\mu(B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*}))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\left|S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})})-k(N)\right|\geq k(N)^{1-\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle+\mu\left(\left|S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*})})-N\mu(B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*}))\right|\geq\tfrac{k(N)}{3}\right),

where in the last step we used the fact that
N\mu(B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*}))\sim 2^{-d}k(N)
.

By Lemma
21
, for any integer
m\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})})-k(N)\right)^{m}\right)

\displaystyle\ll k(N)^{\frac{m}{2}}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*})})-N\mu(B_{r_{N}/2}(x^{*}))\right)^{m}\right)

\displaystyle\ll k(N)^{\frac{m}{2}},

therefore, applying Markov’s inequality to (
21
), we deduce

\mu(E_{N}^{c})\ll k(N)^{-\frac{m}{2}+m\epsilon}+k(N)^{-\frac{m}{2}}\ll k(N)^{-A},

provided that
m>2A/(1-2\epsilon).
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 23

(Ergodic Sum of the Truncated Function)
.

For
\epsilon>0
and
A>0
it holds that

\mu\left(\left|S_{N}(f_{N})-\mathbb{E}S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>N^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha-\epsilon}\right)\ll k(N)^{-A},

Proof.

For
m\geq 2
, Lemma
20
yields

\mathbb{E}(S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})^{m})\ll N^{\frac{m}{2}}r_{N}^{-m\beta+\frac{md}{2}}\ll N^{m\alpha}k(N)^{\frac{m}{2}-m\alpha},

where
\bar{f}_{N}=f_{N}-\mathbb{E}f_{N}
. Using Markov’s inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left|S_{N}(f_{N})-\mathbb{E}S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>N^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha-\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\mathbb{E}(S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})^{m})}{N^{m\alpha}k(N)^{m-m\alpha-m\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\ll k(N)^{-\frac{m}{2}+m\epsilon}.

The desired result follows by selecting an integer
m>2A/(1-2\epsilon).

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Using Lemmas
22
and

, for

, we have

(22)

\mu\left(\left|\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)-\mathbb{E}S_{N}(f_{N})\right|>2CN^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha-\epsilon}\right)\ll k(N)^{-A},

where the constant

is the one specified in Lemma

.

Define the subsequence
\tilde{N}_{l}=\lceil 2^{l^{\epsilon}}\rceil
and construct

\frac{k(N_{l,j+2})}{k(N_{l,j})}\geq 2^{l^{-\epsilon}}.

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{k(N)}{(\log N)^{\epsilon}}=\infty
. By the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, and (

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{i}}^{k(N_{i})}(f)(x)}{\mathbb{E}S_{N_{i}}(f_{N_{i}})}=1\quad\text{ for almost every }x.

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{i}}^{k(N_{i})}(f)(x)}{N_{i}^{\alpha}k(N_{i})^{1-\alpha}}=c\quad\text{ for almost every }x.

\displaystyle k_{+}(N)

\displaystyle=\left\lceil 2^{l^{-\epsilon}}k(N)\right\rceil\quad\text{if}\quad\tilde{N}_{l}\leq N<\tilde{N}_{l+1}\quad\text{ and }

(29)

Rewriting this inequality as

\displaystyle\frac{N_{i}^{\alpha}k_{+}(N_{i})^{1-\alpha}}{N^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\hat{S}_{N_{i}}^{k_{+}(N_{i})}(f)}{N_{i}^{\alpha}k_{+}(N_{i})^{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)}{N^{\alpha}k(N)^{1-\alpha}}

7.
Returns to small balls

The main tool for analyzing distributional limits in the lightly trimmed case is the asymptotic Poisson nature of return times to small balls. This section is to establish this property.

The Poisson limit theorem for return times has been investigated extensively in the literature. One of the earliest results in a dynamical systems context is due to Pitskel
[
Pit91
]
, who proved it for mixing Markov chains. Subsequently, the result has been established under various mixing assumptions by many authors; for a comprehensive overview, we refer the reader to the survey
[
Hay13
]
, especially Section 5.1 for a discussion of the method of moments. More recently, slow recurrence together with exponential mixing of all orders has been employed in
[
DFL22
]
; see also
[
Aue25
]
.

Specifically,
[
Aue25
, Proposition 20]
implies that for
\mu
-almost every
x^{*}\in M
and any finite collection of times
0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{J}
, the finite-dimensional distributions converge weakly as
r\to 0
:

(30)

\left(S_{\lceil t_{1}/\mu(B_{r})\rceil}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r}}),\ldots,S_{\lceil t_{J}/\mu(B_{r})\rceil}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r}})\right)\;\Rightarrow\;\left(P(t_{1}),\ldots,P(t_{J})\right),

where
P(t)
denotes a Poisson random variable with mean
t
, and
\left(P(t_{1}),\ldots,P(t_{J})\right)
is a vector of independent Poisson variables. Moreover, a minor modification of the argument shows that the convergence in (
30
) also holds for points
x^{*}
that are slowly recurrent.
If
\rho(x^{*})>0,
the measure of
B_{r}
satisfies
\mu(B_{r})\sim B_{d}\rho(x^{*})r^{d}
, where
B_{d}
is the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball. Then (
30
) becomes

\left(S_{\lceil\frac{t_{1}}{B_{d}\rho(x^{*})r^{d}}\rceil}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r}}),\ldots,S_{\lceil\frac{t_{J}}{B_{d}\rho(x^{*})r^{d}}\rceil}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r}})\right)\Rightarrow\left(P(t_{1}),\ldots,P(t_{J})\right).

For the arguments that follow, we require a variant of this result in which the number of iterations is fixed while the radius of the balls varies. Although this version can be derived by the same methods, we include a proof for completeness.

Proposition 24
.

Suppose the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing of all orders. If
x^{*}\in M
is slowly recurrent and
\rho(x^{*})>0
, then for any
J\in\mathbb{N}
and time points
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{J}
, the finite-dimensional distributions converge weakly as
N\to\infty
:

(31)

\left(S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{1}}}}\right),S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{2}}}}\right),\ldots,S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{J}}}}\right)\right)\Rightarrow\left(P(t_{1}),P(t_{2}),\ldots,P(t_{J})\right)\quad

where
r_{N}^{t}=\left(\frac{t}{B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
,
P(t)
denotes a Poisson random variable with mean
t
, and
\left(P(t_{1}),\ldots,P(t_{J})\right)
is a vector of independent Poisson variables.

Proof.

By the method of moments, it suffices to show that for all
J\geq 1,

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{J},

(m_{1},\dots,m_{J})\in\mathbb{N}^{J}
,

(32)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\binom{S_{N,j}-S_{N,j-1}}{m_{j}}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{(t_{j}-t_{j-1})^{m_{j}}}{m_{j}!},

where
S_{N,j}=S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{j}}}(x^{*})}\right)
.

For
N\geq\max_{j}m_{j}
, we express the product in the left-hand side of (
32
) as

(33)

\prod_{j=1}^{J}\binom{S_{N,j}-S_{N,j-1}}{m_{j}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{j}}\xi_{N}^{j,i},

where

\xi_{N}^{j,i}={\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{j}}}(x^{*})\setminus B_{r_{N}^{t_{j-1}}}(x^{*})}\circ T^{k_{i}^{j}}

and the sum is over all
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1}^{1},\dots,k_{m_{1}}^{1},\dots,k_{1}^{J},\dots,k_{m_{J}}^{J})
with
1\leq k_{1}^{j}<\dots<k_{m_{j}}^{j}\leq N
for each
j
, and all
k_{i}^{j}
distinct across different
j
.

For a large constant
C,
let

\Delta_{N}=\Delta_{N,(t_{j}),(m_{j})}:=\left\{\textbf{k}=(k_{i,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,\ldots,J\\
i=1,\ldots,m_{j}\end{subarray}}\;\left|\;\begin{aligned} &1\leq k^{j}_{1}<\dots<k^{j}_{m_{j}}\leq N\;\textit{ for }j=1,\ldots,J\\
&k^{j}_{i}\neq k^{j^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}}\;\textit{ whenever }\;(j,i)\neq(j^{\prime},i^{\prime})\end{aligned}\right\}\right.\\

and

\Delta^{\prime}_{N}=\Delta^{\prime}_{N,(t_{j}),(m_{j})}:=\left\{\textbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}\;\left|\;\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,\ldots,J,\;i=1,\ldots,m_{j}\\
j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,J,\;i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,m_{j^{\prime}},\;(j,i)\not=(j^{\prime},i^{\prime})\end{subarray}}|k^{j}_{i}-k^{j^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}}|\leq C\log N\right\}\right..

Note that
\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{j}}\xi_{N}^{j,i}=0
if
k^{j}_{i}=k^{j^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}}
for some
(j,i)
and
(j^{\prime},i^{\prime})
with
j\neq j^{\prime}
. We then decompose the sum in (
33
) into two components

\prod_{j=1}^{J}{{S_{N,j}-S_{N,j-1}}\choose m_{j}}=M_{N}+R_{N},

where

(34)

M_{N}=M_{N,(t_{j}),(m_{j})}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{j}}\xi_{N}^{j,i},\;\;R_{N}=R_{N,(t_{j}),(m_{j})}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{j}}\xi_{N}^{j,i}.

We aim to prove that

(35)

\mathbb{E}M_{N}\rightarrow\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{(t_{j}-t_{j-1})^{m_{j}}}{m_{j}!}\;\;\textit{ and }\;\;\mathbb{E}R_{N}\rightarrow 0\;\;\;\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}m_{j}.
Let us first establish the asymptotic behavior of
M_{N}
.
For
\mathbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}^{\prime}
, the indices are well-separated. Analogously to Lemma
16
, exponential mixing of all orders yields

\displaystyle\left|\mu\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{J}\bigcap_{i=1}^{m_{j}}T^{-k_{i}^{j}}\left(B_{r_{N}^{t_{j}}}(x^{*})\setminus B_{r_{N}^{t_{j-1}}}(x^{*})\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\prod_{j=1}^{J}\mu\left(B_{r_{N}^{t_{j}}}(x^{*})\setminus B_{r_{N}^{t_{j-1}}}(x^{*})\right)^{m_{j}}\right|

\displaystyle=o\left(1\right).

Summing over
\textbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}\setminus\Delta^{\prime}_{N}
, and noting that

\mu\left(B_{r_{N}^{t_{j}}}(x^{*})\setminus B_{r_{N}^{t_{j-1}}}(x^{*})\right)\sim\frac{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}{N},

and

\Delta_{N}\setminus\Delta^{\prime}_{N}\sim\prod_{j=1}^{J}{N\choose{m_{j}}},

we obtain

(36)

\mathbb{E}M_{N}\sim N^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}m_{j}}\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}\setminus\Delta^{\prime}_{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}(t_{j}-t_{j-1})^{m_{j}}\sim\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{(t_{j}-t_{j-1})^{m_{j}}}{m_{j}!}.

Now we turn our attention to
R_{N}
. Let
A_{N}=B_{r_{N}^{t_{J}}}(x^{*})
. Note that
R_{N}
is supported on the set

U_{N}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}T^{-n}\left(A_{N}\cap\{\varphi_{A_{N}}\leq C\log N\}\right),

where
\varphi_{A_{N}}(x)=\min\{k\geq 1:T^{k}(x)\in A_{N}\}
is the first return time. By the slow recurrence condition at
x^{*}
,

\mu(U_{N})=o\left((\log N)^{-2\mathcal{M}}\right).

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\mathbb{E}R_{N}\leq\mu(\operatorname{supp}(R_{N}))^{1/2}\|R_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mu(U_{N})^{1/2}\|R_{N}\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus, to prove
\mathbb{E}R_{N}\rightarrow 0
, it suffices to show
\|R_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\ll(\log N)^{\mathcal{M}}
.
We bound
\|R_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
by noting that

R_{N}^{2}\leq\sum_{1\leq k_{1},\ldots,k_{2\mathcal{M}}\leq N}\prod_{i=1}^{2\mathcal{M}}{\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}}\circ T^{k_{i}}=S_{N,J}^{2\mathcal{M}},

where
S_{N,J}=S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}})
and

S_{N,J}^{2\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{m=1}^{2\mathcal{M}}m!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2\mathcal{M}}{m}{S_{N,J}\choose m}\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{2\mathcal{M}}{S_{N,J}\choose m},

where
C_{\mathcal{M}}=\max_{1\leq k=1,\ldots,2\mathcal{M}}m!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2\mathcal{M}}{m}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2\mathcal{M}}{m}
are the Stirling numbers of the second kind.

Fix
m\geq 1,
we adapt the previous notation for
t_{J},m
instead of
(t_{j}),(m_{j}).
Then
\Delta_{N}
denotes the set of
m
-tuples
1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{m}\leq N,
and
\Delta_{N}^{\prime}
is the subset of
\Delta_{N}
containing tuples where the minimum distance between any two distinct indices is at most
C\log N.
Analogously to (
34
), we decompose
\binom{S_{N,J}}{m}=M_{N,m}+R_{N,m}
, where
M_{N,m}
and
R_{N,m}
are sums of products of
{\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}}\circ T^{k_{i}}
over index tuples
\mathbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}^{\prime}
, respectively.

As in (
36
),
\mathbb{E}M_{N,m}
is bounded for each fixed
m
. It remains to show that

\mathbb{E}R_{N,m}\ll(\log N)^{2\mathcal{M}}

for all
m=1,\ldots,2\mathcal{M}
.

We partition
\Delta_{N}
by defining

s(\textbf{k})=\max\left\{s\geq 1\;\middle|\;\exists\,1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq N\text{ such that }\min_{r=1,\dots,s-1}|k_{i_{r+1}}-k_{i_{r}}|>C\log N\right\}.

Then
\Delta_{N}=\bigcup_{s=1}^{m}\Delta_{N}^{s}
, where
\Delta_{N}^{s}=\{\mathbf{k}:s(\mathbf{k})=s\}
, and
\Delta_{N}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{s=1}^{m-1}\Delta_{N}^{s}
.

For
s=1,\dots,m-1
and
1\leq l_{1}<\dots<l_{s}\leq N
with
\min_{r}|l_{r+1}-l_{r}|>C\log N
, define

A_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}}=\bigcap_{r=1}^{s}T^{-l_{r}}A_{N}.

By exponential mixing of all orders,
\mu(A_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}})\ll N^{-s}
. For
x\in A_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}},
consider

\mathfrak{K}_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}}(x)=\left\{\textbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in\Delta_{N}^{s}\;\left|\begin{aligned} &\exists\,i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\;\textit{ such that }k_{i_{r}}=l_{r},\;\forall r=1,\dots,s\\
&\textit{and }\;x\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}T^{-k_{i}}A_{N}\end{aligned}\right\}\right..

Then
|\mathfrak{K}_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}}(x)|\ll(\log N)^{m}
, since the remaining indices must lie within
C\log N
of some
l_{r}
. Furthermore, for any
\mathbf{k}\in\mathfrak{K}_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}}(x),
the support of the corresponding product satisfies

\operatorname{supp}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}}\circ T^{k_{i}}\right)\subset A_{l_{1},\dots,l_{s}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\Delta_{N}^{s}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}}\circ T^{k_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l_{1}<\ldots<l_{s}\leq N\\
\min_{r=1,\ldots,s-1}|l_{r+1}-l_{r}|>C\log N\end{subarray}}\int_{A_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}}|\mathfrak{K}_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}(x)|\,\text{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\ll(\log N)^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l_{1}<\ldots<l_{s}\leq N\\
\min_{r=1,\ldots,s-1}|i_{r+1}-i_{r}|>C\log N\end{subarray}}\mu(A_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}})

\displaystyle\ll(\log N)^{m}.

Summing over
s=1,\dots,m-1
yields
\mathbb{E}R_{N,m}\ll(\log N)^{m}\leq(\log N)^{2\mathcal{M}},
thereby completing the proof.
∎

To prepare for a trimmed version, we introduce an alternative representation of the limit in (
31
) using Poisson point processes.

Definition 25
.

A random countable set
\Lambda\subset[0,\infty)
is called a (standard) Poisson point process (PPP) if

•

For any interval
[t_{1},t_{2})\subset[0,\infty)
, the number of points in
\Lambda\cap[t_{1},t_{2})
follows a Poisson distribution with mean
(t_{2}-t_{1})
. That is, for
k\in\mathbb{N},

\mathbb{P}(\#(\Lambda\cap[t_{1},t_{2}])=k)=\frac{(t_{2}-t_{1})^{k}e^{-(t_{2}-t_{1})}}{k!}

•

For any collection of disjoint intervals
[s_{1},t_{1}),

[s_{2},t_{2}),

\dots,

[s_{n},t_{n})\subset[0,\infty)
, the counts

\#\left(\Lambda\cap[s_{1},t_{1})\right),\#\left(\Lambda\cap[s_{2},t_{2})\right),\dots,\#\left(\Lambda\cap[s_{n},t_{n})\right)

are independent.

Now the standard Poisson process
\mathcal{P}
is given by

\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}=\left(\Lambda\cap[0,t)\right)_{t\geq 0}.

For
K\geq 1
, the random set
\Lambda^{K}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda^{K}

\displaystyle=\Lambda\setminus\{\textit{the }K\textit{ closest points to }0\textit{ in }\Lambda\}

\displaystyle=\Lambda\setminus\argmin_{\{x_{1},\dots,x_{K}\}\subset\Lambda,\,\,x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{K}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}x_{j},

and is referred to as the
trimmed Poisson point process
. For simplicity, we set
\Lambda^{0}=\Lambda
.

Remark 26
.

Given a PPP
\Lambda
, there are iid standard exponentially distributed random variables
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
such that

\Lambda=\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1},

where
\xi_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_{j}
denotes the
k
-th arrival time. Then the corresponding trimmed PPP is
\Lambda^{K}=\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq K+1}
.

The trimmed version of Proposition
24
is the following.

Proposition 27
.

Let
K\geq 1
. Suppose the system
(M,\mathcal{B}(M),\mu,T)
is exponentially mixing of all orders. If
x^{*}\in M
is slowly recurrent and
\rho(x^{*})>0
, then for any finite collection of times
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{J},
the finite-dimensional distributions converge weakly as
N\to\infty
:

(37)

\left(\hat{S}_{N}^{K}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{1}}}}\right),\ldots,\hat{S}_{N}^{K}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t_{J}}}}\right)\right)\Rightarrow\left(\#(\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,t_{1})),\ldots,\#(\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,t_{J}))\right),

where
\Lambda^{K}
is a trimmed Poisson point process and
r_{N}^{t}=\left(\frac{t}{B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
.

Proof.

Given that

\hat{S}_{N}^{K}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t}}}\right)=\max\left\{S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}^{t}}}\right)-K,0\right\}

and

\#\left(\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,t)\right)=\max\left\{\#\left(\Lambda\cap[0,t)\right)-K,0\right\},

the desired conclusion follows immediately from Proposition
24
.
∎

8.
Summation over Poisson point processes

We investigate the asymptotic behavior of
\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,R)}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}
, for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, as
R\rightarrow\infty,
where
\Lambda^{K}
denotes a trimmed Poisson point process.

The case
\alpha>1
can be addressed directly.
Using the tail estimate
\mathbb{P}(\#(\Lambda\cap[n,n+1])>n^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}})\ll e^{-n^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}}
and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, we deduce that almost surely, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\#(\Lambda\cap[n,n+1])\ll n^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}.

It follows that

\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}\ll\sum_{n}n^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}<\infty,

which implies

(38)

\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,R)}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}\rightarrow\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}\quad\textit{as }R\rightarrow\infty,\;\textit{ almost surely}.

It remains to study the case
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
.

Proposition 28
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. Then there exist constants
c_{R}>0
and a non-degenerate distribution
Y
such that

\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,R)}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}-c_{R}\Rightarrow Y\quad\textit{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

Moreover, the
p
-th absolute moment of
Y
for any positive integer
p
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(|Y|^{p})\begin{cases}<\infty&\textit{if }p<\frac{K+1}{\alpha},\\
=\infty&\textit{if }p\geq\frac{K+1}{\alpha}.\end{cases}

Proof.

(i) To establish weak convergence, we first prove the existence of constants
c_{R}>0
and a distribution
\tilde{Y}
(without yet claiming non-degeneracy) such that

(39)

\Sigma_{R}-c_{R}\rightarrow\tilde{Y}\quad\textit{as }R\rightarrow\infty,\textit{ in }L^{p}\textit{ for all }1\leq p<\infty,

where
\Sigma_{R}=\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[1,R)}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}
.

For
\alpha>1
, we have already shown in (
38
) that

\Sigma_{R}\rightarrow\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K},x\geq 1}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}\quad\textit{as }R\rightarrow\infty,\;\textit{ almost surely}.

The limit is clearly in
L^{p}
for all
1\leq p<\infty
. Therefore, by the monotone convergence theorem, the above convergence also holds in
L^{p}
for all such
p
.

(ii)
Denote
c_{R}=\mathbb{E}\Sigma_{R}
, for every
k\geq 1
we will show

(40)

\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}-c_{R,R^{\prime}}\right|^{k}\right)\rightarrow 0\;\;\;\textit{as }R>R^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty,

where
\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}=\Sigma_{R}-\Sigma_{R^{\prime}}
and
c_{R,R^{\prime}}=c_{R}-c_{R^{\prime}}
. This convergence will imply that the sequence
\{\Sigma_{R}-c_{R}\}
is Cauchy in
L^{k}
, and hence converges in
L^{k}
to some
\tilde{Y}
. Clearly, if this holds,
\tilde{Y}
does not depend on
k
and (
39
) follows.
Let
\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}=\sum_{x\in\Lambda\cap[R^{\prime},R]}x^{-\alpha}
, for
R>R^{\prime}>1
be the untrimmed sum, and
c^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}
. We observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}\not=\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\#\{\Lambda\cap[0,R^{\prime}]\}<K\right)\ll(R^{\prime})^{K}e^{-R^{\prime}},

and consequently,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}-c_{R,R^{\prime}}\right|^{k}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}-c^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}\right|^{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\ll\sup|\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}-\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}|^{k}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}\not=\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll K^{k}(R^{\prime})^{K}e^{-R^{\prime}}.

Therefore, it suffices to prove (
40
) with
\Sigma^{*}_{R,R^{\prime}}
instead of
\Sigma_{R,R^{\prime}}
.

Furthermore, it is sufficient to verify (
40
) only for integer values
R=n
,
R^{\prime}=n^{\prime}
, and we may restrict our attention to even values of
k
, as the general case follows by standard moment comparison arguments.

(iii)
Let
N=|\Lambda\cap[n^{\prime},n)|
denote the number of points in
[n^{\prime},n)
, where
N\sim\text{Poisson}(n-n^{\prime})
. Given
N=k
, the arrival times
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k}
are independently and identically distributed according to a uniform distribution on
[n^{\prime},n),
then
\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{-\alpha}
and

c^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}\mid N\right]\right]=\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}x^{-\alpha}\,\text{d}x=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n^{1-\alpha}-(n^{\prime})^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha},&\alpha\neq 1;\\
\log n-\log n^{\prime},&\alpha=1.\end{cases}

The moment generating function of
\bar{Y}=\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}-c^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}
is defined as

M_{\bar{Y}}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\left(\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}-c^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}\right)}\right]=e^{-tc^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}}\mathbb{E}[e^{t\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}}].

We first compute
\mathbb{E}[e^{t\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}}]
using the law of total expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}}\mid N\right]\right].

As above we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}}\mid N=k\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}e^{t\xi_{i}^{-\alpha}}\right]=\left(\mathbb{E}e^{t\xi_{1}^{-\alpha}}\right)^{k}.

Since
\xi_{1}\sim\text{Uniform}[n^{\prime},n)
, we have

\mathbb{E}[e^{t\xi_{1}^{-\alpha}}]=\frac{1}{n-n^{\prime}}\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}e^{tu^{-\alpha}}\,\text{d}u:=M(t).

Thus,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M(t)^{N}\right].

Since
N\sim\text{Poisson}(n-n^{\prime})
, its probability generating function is
\mathbb{E}[z^{N}]=e^{(n-n^{\prime})(z-1)}
. Substituting
Z=M(t)
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}}\right]=e^{(n-n^{\prime})(M(t)-1)}.

Therefore,

M_{\bar{Y}}(t)=e^{-tc^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}}e^{(n-n^{\prime})(M(t)-1)}.

Substituting
\mu=\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}x^{-\alpha}\,\text{d}x,
we get

M_{\bar{Y}}(t)=\exp\left(\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}\left(e^{tu^{-\alpha}}-tu^{-\alpha}-1\right)\,\text{d}u\right).

It follows that the cumulant generating function is given by

K_{\bar{Y}}(t)=\log M_{\bar{Y}}(t)=\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}\left(e^{tu^{-\alpha}}-tu^{-\alpha}-1\right)\,\text{d}u.

The
k
-th cumulant is
\kappa_{k}=K_{\bar{Y}}^{(k)}(0)
. Noting that
K_{Y}(0)=0
, we further observe that
\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}]=K_{\bar{Y}}^{\prime}(0)=0
, which is consistent with the definition of centralization. For
k\geq 2
, define
g(t,u)=e^{tu^{-\alpha}}-tu^{-\alpha}-1
. Then

\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t^{k}}g(t,u)=(u^{-\alpha})^{k}e^{tu^{-\alpha}}.

Therefore

K_{\bar{Y}}^{(k)}(t)=\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}u^{-k\alpha}e^{tu^{-\alpha}}\,\text{d}u,\quad\text{and}\quad K_{\bar{Y}}^{(k)}(0)=\int_{n^{\prime}}^{n}u^{-k\alpha}\,\text{d}u.

This yields

\kappa_{k}=\begin{cases}0&\textit{if }k=1,\\
\frac{n^{1-k\alpha}-(n^{\prime})^{1-k\alpha}}{1-k\alpha}&\textit{if }k\geq 2,\,k\alpha\neq 1,\\
\log n-\log n^{\prime},&\textit{if }k\geq 2,\,k\alpha=1.\end{cases}

Higher-order moments of
\left(\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}-c^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}\right)
can be expressed in terms of cumulants
\kappa_{n}

as follows:

\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}-c^{*}_{n,n^{\prime}}\right]^{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}B_{k,m}(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{k-m+1}),

where
B_{k,m}
denotes the partial Bell polynomial, defined as

B_{k,m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-m+1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{k-m+1}=m,\\
m_{1}+2m_{2}+\cdots+(k-m+1)m_{k-m+1}=k\end{subarray}}\frac{k!}{m_{1}!m_{2}!\cdots m_{k-m+1}!}\prod_{j=1}^{k-m+1}\left(\frac{x_{j}}{j!}\right)^{m_{j}}.

Given that
\kappa_{1}=0
, all terms with
m_{1}\geq 1
vanish, and the summation is therefore restricted to the case
m_{1}=0
. This leads to

\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{n,n^{\prime}}-c_{n,n^{\prime}}\right]^{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{2},m_{3},\dots\geq 0\\
2m_{2}+3m_{3}+\cdots=k\end{subarray}}\frac{k!}{m_{2}!m_{3}!\cdots}\prod_{j\geq 2}\left(\frac{\kappa_{j}}{j!}\right)^{m_{j}}.

The dominant term corresponds to the case where
m_{2}=k/2
and
m_{j}=0
for all
j\neq 2
, which is expressed as

\frac{k!}{(\frac{k}{2})!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\kappa_{2}^{\frac{k}{2}}\ll\left(n^{1-2\alpha}-(n^{\prime})^{1-2\alpha}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}\to 0

as
n^{\prime}>n\to\infty,
for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}.

(iv)
All that remains to show is that
\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\in L^{p}
for
p<\frac{K+1}{\alpha}
, but
\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\notin L^{p_{0}}
for
p_{0}=\frac{K+1}{\alpha}
. Recall the points in
\Lambda
are denoted by
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots\}
. For any
p<\frac{K+1}{\alpha}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\right)^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\right)^{p}\;\left|\;\xi_{K+1}\in\left[\frac{1}{j},\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right.\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{K+1}\in\left[\frac{1}{j},\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{\alpha p}\mathbb{P}\left(\#\left(\Lambda\cap\left[0,\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right)\geq K+1,\#\left(\Lambda\cap\left[0,\frac{1}{j}\right)\right)\leq K\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{\alpha p}\sum_{i=0}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(\#\left(\Lambda\cap\left[0,\frac{1}{j}\right)\right)=i,\#\left(\Lambda\cap\left[\frac{1}{j},\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right)\geq K-i+1\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{\alpha p}\sum_{i=0}^{K}j^{-i-2(K-i+1)}\ll\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{\alpha p-K-2}<\infty,

therefore
\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\in L^{p}
.

Finally, to show that
\mathbb{E}(|\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}|^{p_{0}})=\infty
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\right)^{p_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\gg\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,1)}x^{-\alpha}\right)^{p_{0}}\;\left|\;\xi_{K+1}\in\left[\frac{1}{j},\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right.\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{K+1}\in\left[\frac{1}{j},\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\gg\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{\alpha p_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(\#\left(\Lambda\cap\left[0,\frac{1}{j}\right)\right)=K,\#\left(\Lambda\cap\left[\frac{1}{j},\frac{1}{j-1}\right)\right)\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\gg\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{\alpha p_{0}-K-2}=\infty.

∎

9.
Lightly trimmed distributional limits

This section will be devoted to proving Theorem
9
. As alluded to in the previous sections, this will again be easier for
\hat{S}_{N}^{K}
, where we simply remove the
K
closest points to
x^{*}
. However, unlike our proof for the SLLN, the argument in Lemma
15
is not quite enough to conclude a limit theorem for
S_{N}^{K}
, rather a quantitative estimate is needed. Recall that by definition
\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)\geq S_{N}^{K}(f)
.

Lemma 29
.

For any fixed integer
K\geq 1
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mu\left(\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}^{K}(f)>\epsilon N^{\alpha}\right)=0\quad\forall\epsilon>0.

Proof.

With the notation from §
3
, each
y_{i}
satisfies
d(x^{*},y_{i})>d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})
, since otherwise
y_{i}
would be included among the
K
closest points. Consequently,

\displaystyle f(y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},y_{i}))\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f))d(x^{*},y_{i})^{-\beta}

\displaystyle<(1+\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{K})))\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})^{-\beta},

Similarly, for each
\hat{y}_{i}
, we have
d(x^{*},\hat{y}_{i})\leq d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})
, and thus

f(\hat{y}_{i})\geq(1-\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})))\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})^{-\beta}.

The difference between the trimmed sums is

\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}^{K}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(f(y_{i})-f(\hat{y}_{i})\right).

Substituting the bounds above,

(41)

\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}^{K}(f)\leq K\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)\left(\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{K}))+\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K}))\right)d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})^{-\beta}.

Fix
\eta>0
and define the set

G_{N}(\eta)=\left\{x:d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})\geq\eta N^{-1/d},\ \max\left(\epsilon(d(x^{*},x_{K})),\epsilon(d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K}))\right)\leq\eta^{2\beta}\right\}.

On
G_{N}(\eta)
, (
41
) implies

\left(\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}^{K}(f)\right)N^{-\alpha}\leq 2K\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)\eta^{\beta}<\epsilon,

for
\eta
sufficiently small. By the Ergodic Theorem
d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{K})\asymp N^{-1/d}
and
d(x^{*},x_{K})\asymp N^{-1/d}
almost surely. Since
\epsilon(r)\to 0
as
r\to 0
, we have
\mu(G_{N}(\eta))\to 1
as
N\to\infty
. Therefore,

\mu\left(\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-S_{N}^{K}(f)>\epsilon N^{\alpha}\right)\leq 1-\mu(G_{N}(\eta))\to 0,

completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
9
.

(i) We show the claims of the Theorem for
\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)
by proving that

(42)

\frac{\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)-a_{N}}{N^{\alpha}}\Rightarrow Y\;\;\;\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

Given Lemma
29
, the corresponding result for
S_{N}^{K}(f)
follows with the same normalizing constants
a_{N}
.

(ii)
Denote
r_{N}=(B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N)^{-\frac{1}{d}}
. Let
R\geq 1
. For
N\geq m^{2}
(where
m
will be specified in part (iii)), define

\displaystyle f_{N}=f_{N,R}=f{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}(x^{*})},\;\textit{ and }\;\bar{f}_{N}=\bar{f}_{N,R}=f\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}(x^{*})}\right).

By the definition of
B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}},
we have

\mathbb{E}\left(S_{N}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}}\right)\right)=R+O\left(N^{-1/d}\right).

It follows that

(43)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f)\neq\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f_{N})+S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})\right)\leq\mu\left(S_{N}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}}\right)\leq K-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\left|S_{N}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}}\right)-\mathbb{E}S_{N}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}}\right)\right|\geq\mathbb{E}\left(S_{N}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}}\right)-(K-1)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}})\right)}{\left(R-(K-1)\right)^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{R},

where the final inequality follows from the proof of Proposition
24
, which shows that

\operatorname{Var}\left(S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}})\right)\ll R.

In the remainder of the proof, we will analyze
\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f_{N})
and
S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})
separately.

Applying Lemma
17
with
\epsilon=R^{1/d}r_{N}
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})\right)\ll N(R^{1/d}r_{N})^{-(2\beta-d)}\ll N^{2\alpha}R^{1-2\alpha}.

This implies that

(44)

\frac{S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})-\mathbb{E}S_{N}(\bar{f}_{N})}{N^{\alpha}}\to 0

in probability as
R\to\infty.

(iii) Partition the ball
B_{R^{1/d}r_{N}}(x^{*})
into
m
annular regions

A_{N}^{j}=\left\{x:\left(\frac{Rj}{m}\right)^{1/d}r_{N}\leq d(x,x^{*})<\left(\frac{R(j+1)}{m}\right)^{1/d}r_{N}\right\},\quad j=0,1,\ldots,m-1,

and introduce the trimmed counting process

\mathcal{N}_{N,j}^{K}=\hat{S}_{N}^{K}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}^{j}}\right).

By Proposition
24
, the joint convergence

(45)

\left(\mathcal{N}_{N,j}^{K}\right)_{j=0,\dots,m-1}\Rightarrow\left(\#\left\{\Lambda^{K}\cap\left[\frac{Rj}{m},\frac{R(j+1)}{m}\right)\right\}\right)_{j=0,\dots,m-1}\;\;\;\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty

holds. Set
c=\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)B_{d}^{\alpha}\rho(x^{*})^{\alpha}
. Define the bounds

\theta_{N,m,R,-}^{K}=c\left(\frac{mN}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{(j+1)^{\alpha}}\mathcal{N}_{N,j}^{K},

and

\theta_{N,m,R,+}^{K}=c\left(\frac{mN}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j^{\alpha}}\mathcal{N}_{N,j}^{K}.

From the joint convergence (
45
), we derive

N^{-\alpha}\theta_{N,m,R,\pm}^{K}\Rightarrow\theta_{m,R,\pm}^{K}\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

where

\displaystyle\theta_{m,R,-}^{K}=c\left(\frac{m}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{(j+1)^{\alpha}}\#\left\{\Lambda^{K}\cap\left[\frac{Rj}{m},\frac{R(j+1)}{m}\right)\right\}\;\textit{ and}

\displaystyle\theta_{m,R,+}^{K}=c\left(\frac{m}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j^{\alpha}}\#\left\{\Lambda^{K}\cap\left[\frac{Rj}{m},\frac{R(j+1)}{m}\right)\right\}.

Since

\left(1-\epsilon\left(R^{1/d}r_{N}\right)\right)c\left(\frac{mN}{R(j+1)}\right)^{\alpha}<f(x)<\left(1+\epsilon\left(R^{1/d}r_{N}\right)\right)c\left(\frac{mN}{Rj}\right)^{\alpha}

for
x\in A_{N}^{j}
and
1\leq j\leq m-1
and

f(x)>\left(1-\epsilon\left(R^{1/d}r_{N}\right)\right)c\left(\frac{mN}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\quad\text{for}\quad x\in A_{N}^{0},

we establish the lower bound

(46)

\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f_{N})\geq\left(1-\epsilon\left(R^{1/d}r_{N}\right)\right)\theta_{N,m,R,-}^{K},

and the probabilistic upper bound

(47)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\hat{S}_{N}^{K}(f_{N})>\left(1+\epsilon\left(R^{1/d}r_{N}\right)\right)\theta_{N,m,R,+}^{K}\right)\leq\mu\left(\left\{\exists k\leq N:T^{k}(x)\in A_{N}^{0}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{A_{N}^{0}}\right)\geq 1\right)\ll\frac{R}{m}.

(iv)
Using Proposition
28
(resp. (
38
)), there is a non-degenerate distribution
Y
and constants
c_{R}=c_{R}^{K}(\alpha)
, with
c_{R}=0
if
\alpha>1
, such that

\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{K}\cap[0,R)}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}-c_{R}\Rightarrow Y\;\;\;\textit{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

10.
Facts about distributional convergence

Let
(K,d_{K})
be a compact metric space, let
(\tilde{\vartheta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of Lipschitz functions on
K
dense in
C(K)
, and denote
\vartheta_{n}=\frac{\tilde{\vartheta}_{n}}{\|\tilde{\vartheta}_{n}\|_{Lip}}
. The metric

(48)

D_{K}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\sum_{n\geq 1}2^{-n}\left|\int_{K}\vartheta_{n}\,\text{d}\lambda-\int_{K}\vartheta_{n}\,\text{d}\lambda^{\prime}\right|,

for probability measures
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
, metrizes distributional convergence, in the sense that, for a sequence of measures
\lambda_{N}
, it holds that
\lambda_{N}\Rightarrow\lambda
if and only if
D_{K}(\lambda,\lambda_{N})\rightarrow 0
.

In our setting the metric space in question is
(K,d_{K})=([0,\infty],d_{[0,\infty]})
, where the metric on
[0,\infty]
is

d_{[0,\infty]}(r,s)=|e^{-r}-e^{-s}|.

Lemma 30
.

Let
X_{N},Y_{N},Z_{N},Y
are real valued random variables with

\displaystyle X_{N}\Rightarrow Y\;\textit{ and }\;Z_{N}\Rightarrow Y\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

If
X_{N},Y_{N},Z_{N}
are defined on a common probability space and

(49)

\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}<X_{N})\rightarrow 0\;\textit{ and }\;\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}>Z_{N})\rightarrow 0\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

then

Y_{N}\Rightarrow Y\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Let
\xi
be a continuity point of the distribution function of
Y.
Then

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}\leq\xi)\geq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}\leq\xi)-\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}>Z_{N})=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\xi),

and

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}\leq\xi)\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\leq\xi)+\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}<X_{N})=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\xi).

∎

11.
Intermediately trimmed distributional limits

Recall that
x_{i}
is the
i
th closest point to
x^{*}
among the
k(N)
points selected to maximize their
f
values, satisfying

\displaystyle d(x_{1},x^{*})\leq d(x_{2},x^{*})\leq\ldots\leq d(x_{k(N)},x^{*})

\displaystyle f(x_{i})\geq f(T^{j}(x))\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq k(N),\,0\leq j\leq N-1,\,T^{j}(x)\not\in\{x_{i},\ldots,x_{k(N)}\}.

And
\hat{x}_{i}
represents the
i
th closest point to
x^{*}
among all of
\{x,T(x),\ldots,T^{N-1}(x)\}
. With these definitions, the trimmed sum is given by

S_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)=S_{N}(f)(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{k(N)}f(x_{i}),

and the version defined in §
3
is given by

\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)(x)=S_{N}(f)(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{k(N)}f(\hat{x}_{i}).

To prevent the proof of Theorem
11
from being obscured by technical details, we first present a sketch of the proof in the simpler case of
\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}
, before proceeding with the full technical arguments. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\operatorname{Res}_{x^{*}}(f)=1
in this section.

For every
N\geq 1
let
r_{N}>0
be the unique radius so that

\mu(B_{r_{N}}(x^{*}))=\frac{k(N)}{N}.

Since
\mu(B_{r}(x^{*}))\sim B_{d}\rho(x^{*})r^{d}
, it follows that
r_{N}^{d}\sim\frac{k(N)}{B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N}
. Note that, denoting
\xi_{N}=d(x^{*},\hat{x}_{k(N)})
, it holds that
\xi_{N}\sim r_{N}
; in fact, as shall be shown later
\frac{\xi_{N}-r_{N}}{\sqrt{r_{N}}}
converges weakly to a centered normal distribution. Therefore, it is natural to split the trimmed sum as

\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}(f)=S_{N}\left(f\cdot\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})}\right)\right)+\hat{W}_{N},

where

\hat{W}_{N}=S_{N}\left(f\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k(N)}f(\hat{x}_{i}).

Applying the arguments of Lemma
38
to balls instead of superlevel sets (see also Remark
40
) shows that

\frac{S_{N}\left(\hat{f}_{N}\right)-N\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{N}}{\sqrt{k(N)}r_{N}^{-\beta}}

is asymptotically normal and, considering (
52
), independent of
\hat{W}_{N}/(\sqrt{k(N)}r_{N}^{-\beta})
, where
\hat{f}_{N}=f\left(1-{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})}\right)
.

Therefore, we concentrate on showing that
\hat{W}_{N}/(\sqrt{k(N)}r_{N}^{-\beta})
is asymptotically normal.

The key observation here is that either

•

if
\xi_{N}\leq r_{N}
, then all of the points
\hat{x}_{i}
lie in
B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})
, in this case
\hat{W}_{N}
is the sum of
f
over all the points in
B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})
that are not one of the
\hat{x}_{i}
,

•

or otherwise if
\xi_{N}>r_{N}
, then every point in
B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})
is one of the
\hat{x}_{i}
, in this case
-\hat{W}_{N}
is the sum of
f
over the remaining points.

In both cases, all of the points that are being summed over are between
r_{N}
and
\xi_{N}
, keeping in mind that
\xi_{N}\sim r_{N}
it follows that

(52)

\hat{W}_{N}\approx\frac{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})})-k(N)}{r_{N}^{\beta}}.

Remark
40
shows that
\frac{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})})-k(N)}{\sqrt{k(N)}}
converges to a standard normal distribution, and the proof is finished for
\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}
.

The main technical challenge in going from
\hat{S}_{N}^{k(N)}
to
S_{N}^{k(N)}
is that, if we simply try to split
S_{N}^{k(N)}
in the same way, setting

W_{N}=S_{N}\left(f\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{r_{N}}(x^{*})}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k(N)}f(x_{i}),

there is no analogue of (
52
).

Motivated by the proof of Lemma
13
, instead of balls we will use superlevel sets of
f
. Therefore, let

\Sigma_{R}=\{f\geq R\}\quad\textit{for }R>0,

and let
\lambda_{N}>0
be the unique value such that

\mu(\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}})=\frac{k(N)}{N}.

Clearly, it holds that
\lambda_{N}\sim B_{d}^{\alpha}\rho(x^{*})^{\alpha}N^{\alpha}k(N)^{-\alpha}\sim r_{N}^{-\beta}
.

Now, defining

(53)

W_{N}=S_{N}\left(f\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k(N)}f(x_{i}),

it
does
hold that

(54)

W_{N}\approx\lambda_{N}\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}})-k(N)\right),

as will be rigorously justified below, in direct analogy with (
52
).

We next collect some approximation properties of the superlevel sets
\Sigma_{R}
, which will be used in place of balls in several key arguments above, including Lemma
17
.

.

Let
R_{N}\rightarrow\infty
, then there are

such that

(55)

This is immediate from (

Lemma 35
.

For
R_{N}\rightarrow\infty
and
\epsilon_{N}\rightarrow 0
, there exist
C^{\kappa}
smooth functions

such that

{\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{R_{N}}}\leq h_{N}\leq{\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{R_{N}}-\epsilon_{N}}

Proof.

Let
\theta_{N}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
be a smooth function with

•

\theta_{N}(r)=1
if
r>R_{N}
,

•

\theta_{N}(r)=0
if
r<R_{N}-\epsilon_{N}
,

•

and
\|\theta_{N}\|_{C^{\kappa}}\ll\epsilon_{N}^{-\kappa}
.

We verify the required properties for
h_{N}=\theta_{N}\circ f
. Using (
55
), it follows that
h_{N}=1
on
\Sigma_{R_{N}}\supset B_{R_{N}^{-1/\beta}/2}(x^{*})
, and
h_{N}=0
outside
\Sigma_{R_{N}-\epsilon_{N}}\subset B_{2R_{N}^{-1/\beta}}(x^{*})

\|h_{N}\|_{C^{\kappa}}\ll\epsilon_{N}^{-\kappa}R_{N}^{1+\frac{\kappa}{\beta}}.

On the other hand, to estimate
\|{\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{R_{N}}}-h_{N}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\mu(R_{N}-\epsilon_{N}\leq f\leq R_{N})

and

as in Definition

. Using the product rule

\mu\left(\Sigma_{(1+\epsilon)\lambda_{N}}\right)\sim B_{d}\rho(x^{*})(1+\epsilon)^{-1/\alpha}\lambda_{N}^{-1/\alpha}\sim(1+\epsilon)^{-1/\alpha}\frac{k(N)}{N},

and therefore, for big enough

,

S_{N}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{(1+\epsilon)\lambda_{N}}}\right)-N\mu\left(\Sigma_{(1+\epsilon)\lambda_{N}}\right)\geq k(N)-\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)^{-1/\alpha}k(N)=c(\epsilon)k(N)

. Dividing by

and applying Corollary

\frac{S_{N}\bigl({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{(1+\varepsilon)\lambda_{N}}}\bigr)-N\mu\bigl(\Sigma_{(1+\varepsilon)\lambda_{N}}\bigr)}{\sqrt{k(N)}}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/\alpha}\bigr),

so the probability of exceeding

tends to

. Hence

(59)

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mu\left(W_{N}>0,W_{N}>(1+\epsilon)\lambda_{N}\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}})-k(N)\right)\right)=0,\quad\forall\epsilon>0.

For case (ii) we argue similarly: if
f(x_{i})<(1-\varepsilon)\lambda_{N}
for some
1\leq i\leq k(N)
, then
\Sigma_{(1-\varepsilon)\lambda_{N}}
contains at most
k(N)-1
of the orbit points, so
S_{N}\bigl({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{(1-\varepsilon)\lambda_{N}}}\bigr)\leq k(N)-1.
Then the same CLT argument gives

(60)

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mu\left(W_{N}<0,W_{N}>(1-\epsilon)\lambda_{N}\left(S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}})-k(N)\right)\right)=0,\quad\forall\epsilon>0.

Now define

X_{N}^{(1)}=Z_{N}^{(1)}=\frac{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}})-k(N)}{\sqrt{k(N)}},\qquad Y_{N}^{(1)}=\frac{W_{N}}{\sqrt{k(N)}\lambda_{N}},

and

X_{N}^{(2)}=Y_{N}^{(2)}=Z_{N}^{(2)}=\frac{S_{N}(f_{N})-N\mathbb{E}f_{N}}{\sqrt{k(N)}\lambda_{N}}.

Equations (
58
), (
59
) and (

) show that

,

,

satisfy the hypotheses of Lemma

.
Thus, once we prove

\left(X_{N}^{(1)},\,X_{N}^{(2)}\right)\Rightarrow\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\right)\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

, Lemma

will imply the corresponding convergence

\left(Y_{N}^{(1)},\,Y_{N}^{(2)}\right)\Rightarrow\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\right).

This is exactly the content of (A)–(C). We now turn to prove (

Lemma 38
.

With the notation above, it holds that

(62)

\left(\frac{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}})-k(N)}{\sqrt{k(N)}},\frac{S_{N}(f_{N})-N\mathbb{E}f_{N}}{\sqrt{k(N)}\lambda_{N}}\right)\rightarrow(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}))\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

where
(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}))
is a vector of independent random variables, and

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{2\alpha-1}

Proof.

Denote
g_{1}={\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}}
and
g_{2}=f_{N}
, the conclusion will follow once we show the following; for

(63)

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{M}\left(S_{N}(g_{1})-N\int_{M}g_{1}\,\text{d}\mu\right)^{m_{1}}\left(S_{N}(g_{2})-N\int_{M}g_{2}\,\text{d}\mu\right)^{m_{2}}}{k(N)^{\frac{m}{2}}\lambda_{N}^{m_{2}}}=\omega(m_{1},m_{2}),

where

\omega(m_{1},m_{2})=\begin{cases}(m_{1}-1)!!(m_{2}-1)!!\sigma^{m_{2}}&\textit{if both }m_{1}\text{ and }m_{2}\textit{ are even},\\
0&\textit{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Denote
m=m_{1}+m_{2}
and let
\epsilon>0
to be chosen later. Using Lemma
35
there is a
C^{\kappa}
smooth function
h_{1}
such that
{\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}}\leq h_{1}\leq{\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{N}}-\epsilon}

\|g_{1}-h_{1}\|_{L^{1}}\ll\epsilon\lambda_{N}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\quad\textit{and}\quad\|h_{1}\|_{C^{\kappa}}\ll\epsilon^{-\kappa}\lambda_{N}^{1+\frac{\kappa}{\beta}}.

Define

. Then

\|g_{2}-h_{2}\|_{L^{1}}\ll\epsilon\lambda_{N}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\quad\textit{and}\quad\|h_{2}\|_{C^{\kappa}}\ll\epsilon^{-\kappa}\lambda_{N}^{2+\frac{2\kappa}{\beta}}.

Therefore, the error term when replacing
g
by

(64)

\left|\int_{M}\prod_{j=1}^{2}S_{N}(\bar{g}_{j})^{m_{j}}\,\text{d}\mu-\int_{M}\prod_{j=1}^{2}S_{N}(\bar{h}_{j})^{m_{j}}\,\text{d}\mu\right|\ll\epsilon\lambda_{N}^{m_{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}N^{m},

\bar{g}_{j}=g_{j}-\int_{M}g_{j}\,\text{d}\mu

\bar{h}_{j}=h_{j}-\int_{M}h_{j}\,\text{d}\mu

By choosing

We expand the product

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{2}S_{N}(\bar{h}_{j})^{m_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{n}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\bar{h}_{1}\circ T^{n_{i}}\prod_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m}\bar{h}_{2}\circ T^{n_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{n}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bar{h}_{j(i)}\circ T^{n_{i}},

where

j(i)=\begin{cases}1&\textit{if }1\leq i\leq m_{1},\\
2&\textit{if }m_{1}+1\leq i\leq m.\end{cases}

Let
C>0

\textbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{m}

1\leq k=k(\textbf{n})\leq m^{2}+1
such that for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,m\},

(65)

|n_{i}-n_{i^{\prime}}|\leq C^{k}\log N\quad\text{or}\quad|n_{i}-n_{i^{\prime}}|>C^{k+1}\log N.

For

a cluster of

such that
n

\{i^{s}_{1},\dots,i^{s}_{l_{s}}\}

l_{s}
.

Equivalently, let
k
be the smallest integer for which the set
\{n_{i}\}
can be partitioned into
\eta
clusters of sizes
l_{s}
, each being a maximal subset where pairwise differences are at most

, and distinct clusters are separated by more than

By exponential mixing, we have

(66)

\displaystyle\left|\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bar{h}_{j(i)}\circ T^{n_{i}}\,\text{d}\mu\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\prod_{s=1}^{\eta}\int\prod_{r=1}^{l_{s}}\bar{h}_{j(i^{s}_{r})}\circ T^{n_{i^{s}_{r}}}\,\text{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle\ll e^{-\gamma C^{k+1}\log N}\prod_{s=1}^{\eta}\left\|\prod_{r=1}^{l_{s}}\bar{h}_{j(i^{s}_{r})}\circ T^{n_{i^{s}_{r}}}\right\|_{\kappa}.

Here, we write

as

for simplicity. And each

Since the time differences within each cluster are at most

\left\|\prod_{r=1}^{l_{s}}\bar{h}_{j(i^{s}_{r})}\circ T^{n_{i^{s}_{r}}}\right\|_{\kappa}\leq\prod_{r=1}^{l_{s}}\|\bar{h}_{j(i^{s}_{r})}\|_{\kappa}\|T\|_{\kappa}^{C^{k}\log N}\ll\left(N^{\kappa(\alpha+1)m}\|T\|_{\kappa}^{C^{k}\log N}\right)^{l_{s}}.

Consequently, the right-hand side of (

e^{-\gamma C^{k+1}\log N}N^{\kappa(\alpha+1)m^{2}}\|T\|_{\kappa}^{mC^{k}\log N}\leq N^{-100m},

which holds for sufficiently large

independent of

Considering (
64

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{\textbf{n}\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}^{m}}\prod_{s=1}^{\eta(\textbf{n})}\int_{M}\prod_{r=1}^{l_{s}}\bar{h}_{j(i^{s}_{r})}\circ T^{n_{i^{s}_{r}}}\,\text{d}\mu}{k(N)^{\frac{m}{2}}\lambda_{N}^{m_{2}}}=\omega(m_{1},m_{2}).

Replacing once more the smooth approximations

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{\textbf{n}\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}^{m}}\prod_{s=1}^{\eta(\textbf{n})}\int_{M}\prod_{r=1}^{l_{s}}\bar{g}_{j(i^{s}_{r})}\circ T^{n_{i^{s}_{r}}}\,\text{d}\mu}{k(N)^{\frac{m}{2}}\lambda_{N}^{m_{2}}}=\omega(m_{1},m_{2}).

Define

It holds that

(1) Case
\eta>\frac{m}{2}
: At least one cluster is a singleton.
Since

(2) Case
\eta<\frac{m}{2}
: If at least one cluster is a singleton, it can be treated as in the first case.
If no cluster is a singleton, then all

and
\eta<m/2
. Given that

\displaystyle\|\bar{g}_{1}\|_{L^{1}}\ll\lambda_{N}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}},\quad|\bar{g}_{1}|\ll 1,\quad\textit{ and}

\displaystyle\|\bar{g}_{2}\|_{L^{1}}\ll\lambda_{N}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}+1},\quad|\bar{g}_{2}|\ll\lambda_{N},

If

If

, then for each index

Corollary 39
.

For
t>0
it holds that

(74)

\frac{S_{N}({\mathbf{1}}_{\Sigma_{t\lambda_{N}}})-N\mu(\Sigma_{t\lambda_{N}})}{\sqrt{k(N)}}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}})\quad\textit{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

where
\mathcal{N}(0,t)
is a normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
t
.

Proof.

Given
t>0
, define
\tilde{\lambda}_{N}=t\lambda_{N}
and
\tilde{k}(N)=N\mu(\Sigma_{\tilde{\lambda}_{N}}).

From the scaling
\mu(\Sigma_{R})\sim B_{d}\rho(x^{*})R^{-1/\alpha}
, we obtain

\tilde{k}(N)\sim B_{d}\rho(x^{*})N(t\lambda_{N})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sim t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}k(N).

Applying Lemma
38
to the sequence
\tilde{\lambda}_{N}
, The first component of the convergence in (
62
) gives

\frac{S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{\Sigma_{\tilde{\lambda}_{N}}})-\tilde{k}(N)}{\sqrt{\tilde{k}(N)}}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Now rewrite

\frac{S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{\Sigma_{t\lambda_{N}}})-N\mu(\Sigma_{t\lambda_{N}})}{\sqrt{k(N)}}=\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{k}(N)}{k(N)}}\;\cdot\;\frac{S_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{\Sigma_{\tilde{\lambda}_{N}}})-\tilde{k}(N)}{\sqrt{\tilde{k}(N)}}.

Since
\tilde{k}(N)/k(N)\to t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}
and the second factor converges in distribution to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, Slutsky’s theorem gives (
74
).
∎

Remark 40
.
