Talagrand’s convolution conjecture up to log ⁡ log \log\log via perturbed reverse heat
Yuansi Chen

Abstract
We prove that under the heat semigroup ( P τ ) (P_{\tau}) on the Boolean hypercube, any nonnegative function f : { − 1 , 1 } n → ℝ + f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+} exhibits a uniform tail bound that is better than that by Markov’s inequality. Specifically, for any η > e 3 \eta>e^{3} and τ > 0 \tau>0 , ℙ X ∼ μ ​ ( P τ ​ f ​ ( X ) > η ​ ∫ f ​ 𝑑 μ ) ≤ c τ ​ log ⁡ log ⁡ η η ​ log ⁡ η , \displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\int fd\mu\right)\leq c_{\tau}\frac{\log\log\eta}{\eta\sqrt{\log\eta}}, where μ \mu is the uniform measure on the Boolean hypercube { − 1 , 1 } n \{-1,1\}^{n} and c τ c_{\tau} is a constant that only depends on τ \tau . This resolves Talagrand’s convolution conjecture up to a dimension-free log ⁡ log ⁡ η \log\log\eta factor. Its proof relies on properties of the reverse heat process on the Boolean hypercube and a coupling construction based on carefully engineered perturbations of this reverse heat process.



1
Introduction

In 1989, Talagrand (in Problem 1 and 2
[
15
]
) put forward a conjecture on the regularization effect generated by convolution applied to
L^{1}
functions on the Boolean hypercube. Consider the Boolean hypercube
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. Given
t\geq 0
, let
\xi_{t}
be a random vector on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, whose coordinates
\xi^{[i]}_{t}
are independent and identically distributed with

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\xi^{[i]}_{t}=1)=\frac{1+e^{-t}}{2},\quad{\mathbb{P}}(\xi^{[i]}_{t}=-1)=\frac{1-e^{-t}}{2}.

Consider the
heat semigroup

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined for any function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
by, for any
x\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
,

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x):={\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x\odot\xi_{t})\right],

(1)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise product. Let
\mu_{t}^{n}
denote the law of
\xi_{t}
, then
P_{t}
can also be written as a convolution
P_{t}f(x)=\int f(x\odot y)d\mu_{t}^{n}(y)=f\ast\mu_{t}^{n}
,
where the convolution operator
\ast
is defined via the multiplicative group structure on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. For this reason, the heat semigroup
P_{t}
was referred to as “convolution by a biased coin” in
[
15
]
.

Introduce the shorthand
\mu:=\mu_{\infty}^{n}
, which is the uniform measure on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. For
p\geq 1
,
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
, define its
L^{p}(\mu)
-norm to be
\left\|f\right\|_{p}:=\left(\int\left|f\right|^{p}d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
. Talagrand’s convolution conjecture, also restated on his website with the title “Regularization from
L^{1}
by convolution”
[
16
]
, aims to precisely quantify the regularization effect of the heat semigroup when applied to
L^{1}(\mu)
functions, as follows.

Conjecture 1

(
[
15
]
)
.

For every
\tau>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\tau}>0
only depends on
\tau
, such that for every nonnegative function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real_{+}
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\neq 0
, and any
\eta>1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\mu}(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1})\leq\frac{c_{\tau}}{\eta\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

In this conjecture, the regularization effect refers to the gain of a
1/\sqrt{\log\eta}
factor compared to Markov’s inequality while retaining the constant dimension-free. In this paper, we prove the following theorem, which resolves the conjecture up to a
\log\log\eta
factor, with a dimension-free constant.

Theorem 1
.

For every
\tau>0
, there exists a universal constant
c>0
, such that for every nonnegative function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real_{+}
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\neq 0
, and any
\eta>e^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\mu}(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1})\leq c\max\left\{1,\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\right\}\frac{\log\log\eta}{\eta\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

While
1/\sqrt{\log\eta}
is conjectured to be the extra factor, Talagrand also mentioned that “I do not know if it is true that
\lim_{\eta\to\infty}\psi(\eta)=0
”
[
16
]
, where
\psi(\eta):=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{f\geq 0}\left\{\eta{\mathbb{P}}(P_{\tau}f\geq\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1})\right\}
(see also Problem 1 in
[
15
]
). Our Theorem
1
is the first result with a dimension-free upper bound which confirms that
\lim_{\eta\to\infty}\psi(\eta)=0
.

Remark 1
.

Without loss of generality, by rescaling, we may assume
\left\|f\right\|_{1}=1
. Additionally, it suffices to prove the case for strictly positive functions first, and then obtain the general case by passing on limits.

Before we proceed to the main proofs, we make a few comments about its relation to classical results and summarize the progress on this conjecture.

1.1
A few comments on the conjecture

First, to bound the tail probability of
P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1}
, the first idea that comes to mind is Markov’s inequality. Since
{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mu}[P_{\tau}f(X)]={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mu}[f(X)]=\left\|f\right\|_{1}
, Markov’s inequality gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\mu}(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1})\leq\frac{1}{\eta}.

(2)

This bound holds for any
\tau\geq 0
. For
\tau>0
, Talagrand’s conjecture is a strengthening of Markov’s inequality when a heat semigroup is applied.

Second, from Eq. (
2
), one immediately obtains a bound with an extra
1/\sqrt{\log\eta}
factor if a dimension-dependent constant is allowed.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\mu}(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1})\leq\frac{1}{\eta}=\frac{\sqrt{\log\eta}}{\eta\sqrt{\log\eta}}\overset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{\sqrt{n\log 2}}{\eta\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

(i) follows from the trivial bound
f\leq 2^{n}\left\|f\right\|_{1}
(see Lemma
4
), as well as the fact that if
\eta>2^{n}
, then the probability on the left-hand side is
0
. Hence, allowing dimension-dependent constants in Conjecture
1
is not very interesting, unless the constants are much smaller than
\sqrt{n}
.

Third, as shown in Proposition 5 of
[
16
]
, if one aims for a uniform dimension-free tail bound which holds for any
f\geq 0
and any
\eta>2
, then the best bound one can hope for is
1/(\eta\sqrt{\log\eta})
up to a constant that depends on
\tau
. For this,
[
16
]
constructed an explicit lower bound by considering the product function
f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x_{i})
and a carefully chosen threshold
\eta
.

Fourth, one may ask why the normalization has to be with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}
rather than with
\left\|f\right\|_{p}
for
p>1
. As pointed out in
[
15
]
, as long as
p>1
,
P_{\tau}
is known to be a “regularizing” operator. Specifically, hypercontractivity for the uniform measure on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
implies that, for
q=1+e^{-2\tau}(p-1)
,
\left\|P_{\tau}f\right\|_{q}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{p}
. Then Markov’s inequality gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{p})\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{q}},

which is better than
1/(\eta\sqrt{\log\eta})
for large
\eta
as long as
p>1
. While hypercontractivity is powerful for
p>1
, it does not tell anything about the action of
P_{\tau}
on
L^{1}
functions. This leads to the original main motivation of the conjecture: to clarify the regularizing properties of
P_{\tau}
on
L^{1}
functions.

Finally, since the author comes from a background of studying isoperimetric inequalities, it is worth mentioning that Conjecture
1
is closely related to isoperimetric inequalities with extra logarithmic factors and the geometry of small sets (see Section 1
[
4
]
).

1.2
Remarks on the Gaussian counterpart

In an attempt to attack Conjecture
1
, a natural Gaussian counterpart of the conjecture, related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) semigroup, has been formulated
[
2
]
and studied.

Let
\gamma_{n}
denote the standard Gaussian measure in
n
with density
x\mapsto\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp(-\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}/2)
, with
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
being the Euclidean norm. For
p\geq 1
, let
L^{p}(\gamma_{n}):=\left\{f:\real^{n}\to\real\mid\int\left|f\right|^{p}d\gamma_{n}<\infty\right\}
. Given
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{n})
, the OU semigroup is defined by Mehler’s representation as

\displaystyle P_{t}^{\text{OU}}f(x):=\int f(e^{-t}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}y)d\gamma_{n}(y),\quad x\in\real^{n},t\geq 0.

The Gaussian counterpart of the conjecture asks whether it is true that for every nonnegative function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real_{+}
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\neq 0
, and any
\eta>1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\gamma_{n}}(P^{\text{OU}}_{\tau}f(X)>\eta\left\|f\right\|_{1})\leq\frac{c_{\tau}}{\eta\sqrt{\log\eta}},

(3)

for a constant
c_{\tau}
that only depends on
\tau>0
. In words, the Gaussian counterpart replaces the heat semigroup on the Boolean hypercube with OU semigroup, and the uniform measure on the Boolean hypercube with the standard Gaussian.
The Gaussian counterpart is fully resolved. Ball, Barthe, Bednorz, Oleszkiewicz and Wolff
[
2
]
first formulated this problem and provided a bound with a constant
c_{\tau,n}
that depends on the dimension
n
and an extra
\log\log\eta
factor in the numerator. Eldan and Lee
[
4
]
, using Föllmer’s process and a coupling construction via stochastic calculus, proved a dimension-free bound with an extra
\sqrt{\log\log\eta}
factor in the numerator. Following the main ideas in
[
4
]
, Lehec
[
10
]
refined their stochastic analysis to remove the extra
\sqrt{\log\log\eta}
factor, fully resolving the Gaussian counterpart.

In both
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
, they crucially use the second-order smoothness (or semi-log-convexity) brought by the OU semigroup, as follows: for any
f:\real^{n}\to\real_{+}
and
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log P_{t}^{\text{OU}}f\succeq-\frac{1}{2t}\mathbb{I}_{n}.

In fact, this semi-log-convexity is the only role of the OU semigroup in resolving the Gaussian counterpart of the conjecture. It is a well-known fact that the Gaussian counterpart of the conjecture follows from Talagrand’s Conjecture
1
by the central limit theorem. On the other hand, whether the Gaussian proof is helpful for proving Conjecture
1
is unknown.

There are several recent works which attempt to extend the success in the Gaussian setting to other measures and/or other semigroups.
[
8
]
gave an alternative proof of the Gaussian counterpart in dimension
1
.
[
9
]
studied another continuous analogue by replacing OU semigroup with perturbations of OU semigroup in dimension
1
, as well as other variants for the
M/M/\infty
queuing process on the integers and Laguerre semigroup in dimension
1
.

2
Preliminaries

We introduce a few basic facts about Boolean function analysis, general continuous-time Markov processes and the heat semigroup on the Boolean hypercube.

2.1
Boolean function analysis basics

Concepts are introduced without proofs. For a detailed exposition on Boolean function analysis, we refer interested readers to the book
[
12
]
.

Inner product for functions on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
.

For a pair of functions
g,h:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
, we define their inner product as

\displaystyle\left\langle g,h\right\rangle:=\int g(x)h(x)d\mu(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[gh].

Multilinear expansion of a function on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, define the monomial corresponding to
S
as
x^{S}:=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
,
with
x^{\emptyset}=1
by convention. With the above inner product,
\left\{x^{S}\mid S\subseteq[n]\right\}
form an orthonormal basis for the vector space of functions
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
(see e.g. Theorem 1.5 in
[
12
]
). Additionally, every function
g:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
can be uniquely expressed as a multilinear polynomial,

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{g}(S)x^{S},

(4)

where
\widehat{g}(S):=\left\langle g,x^{S}\right\rangle
is called the Fourier coefficient of
g
on
S
(see e.g. Theorem 1.1 in
[
12
]
). With the multilinear polynomial representation,
g
can be treated as a function on
n
. This allows us to define the derivative of
g
in the usual sense, for
i\in[n]

\displaystyle\partial_{i}g(x):=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(x+he_{i})-g(x)}{h}=\frac{g(\ldots,x_{i-1},+1,x_{i+1},\ldots)-g(\ldots,x_{i-1},-1,x_{i+1},\ldots)}{2}.

Additionally, the first Fourier coefficient satisfies, if
g\geq 0
, then
\hat{g}(\emptyset)=\left\|g\right\|_{1}=\int gd\mu=g(0)
.

Explicit form of the heat semigroup

Using the multilinear expansion, the heat semigroup (
1
) can be written as

\displaystyle P_{t}g(x)

\displaystyle=\int g(x\odot y)d\mu_{t}^{n}(y)=\sum_{y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{g}(S)x^{S}y^{S}\prod_{i\in[n]}\left(\frac{1+y_{i}e^{-t}}{2}\right)\overset{(i)}{=}g(e^{-t}x).

(5)

(i) follows by summing over
y
first. Vaguely speaking, applying the heat semigroup “moves” the input space from
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
to the inner hypercube
\left\{-e^{-t},e^{-t}\right\}^{n}
, which may benefit from additional regularity of a multilinear polynomial in the inner hypercube.

2.2
Continuous-time Markov processes

In this subsection, we introduce a few concepts related to Markov processes and Markov semigroups. Interested readers are referred to
[
1
]
for continuous-time Markov chains and
[
13
]
for a semigroup viewpoint for additional details.

Given a Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
X_{t}
taking values in a finite space
\Omega
. All Markov processes considered are càdlàg (right continuous with left limits).

Homogeneous case

When
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is time-homogeneous (i.e., the law of
X_{t}\mid X_{s}=x
stays the same as
X_{t-s}\mid X_{0}=x
,
\forall t\geq s\geq 0
), we define the associated
Markov semigroup

Q_{t}
as

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle Q_{t}h(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[h(X_{t})\mid X_{0}=x],

for every bounded function
h:\Omega\to\real
, and every
x\in\Omega
. It also acts on measures through the duality
\int hd(\nu P_{t})=\int P_{t}hd\nu
. Its
generator
is defined as (if the limit is well-defined)

\displaystyle Lh(x):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{Q_{t}h(x)-h(x)}{t}.

A measure
\nu
on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
is said to be
invariant
for
(Q_{t})
if
\int Q_{t}hd\nu=\int hd\nu,\forall t\geq 0
. The Markov property guarantees that it satisfies the semigroup property, namely,
Q_{t+s}=Q_{t}Q_{s},\forall 0\leq s\leq t
.

In-homogeneous case

When
X_{t}
is time-inhomogeneous, we work with a two-parameter semigroup (also called evolution system)
(Q_{s,t})_{0\leq s\leq t}
acting on bounded functions
\Omega\to\real
, such that

\displaystyle Q_{s,s}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle Q_{s,t}h(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[h(X_{t})\mid X_{s}=x].

The Markov property guarantees that it satisfies the two-parameter semigroup property
Q_{s,t}=Q_{s,r}Q_{r,t},0\leq s\leq r\leq t
. Additionally, on a finite space
\Omega
,
Q_{s,t}
can be seen as a matrix of size
\left|\Omega\right|\times\left|\Omega\right|
.
Its time-dependent generator
L_{t}
is defined via the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations (if the partial derivative with respect to
t
is well-defined):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{s,t}=Q_{s,t}L_{t},\quad\partial_{s}Q_{s,t}=-L_{s}Q_{s,t}.

2.3
Heat semigroup and its associated Markov process

In the heat semigroup definition in Eq. (
1
),
\xi_{t}
only specifies one-time marginals of the process. We introduce a Markov process which fulfills the one-time marginals via a jump stochastic differential equation (SDE), making the name “heat semigroup” consistent with our semigroup definition in Section
2.2
.

Let
E:=\real^{2}
, equipped with Lebesgue measure. Let
N
be a Poisson random measure (PRM) on
\real{}^{+}\times E
, which equips the probability space with a natural filtration
\left\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\in\real_{+}}
.
\widetilde{N}
is its associated compensated Poisson random measure and
\nu
is its intensity such that
\nu(dt,dz)=dtdz_{1}dz_{2}
. Let
f
be a function
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}=1
. Define
\nu_{f}:=f\cdot\mu
, which is probability measure with density
f
with respect to the uniform measure on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. Let
T>0
.

Forward jump process

We define the forward jump process through the following jump SDE,

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle\sim\nu_{f}=f\cdot\mu,

\displaystyle dU_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-2U_{t-}e_{i})\int_{E}\mathbf{1}_{z_{1}\in(i,i+1]}\mathbf{1}_{0<z_{2}<\frac{1}{2}}N(dt,dz).

(6)

Since the intervals
\left\{(i,i+1]\right\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
are disjoint, the driving noise in one coordinate is independent of that in another. Notation-wise, it is sometimes simpler to treat the driving noises as
n
independent Poisson random measures
N^{[i]}
on
\real{}^{+}\times\real
instead of
\real{}^{+}\times E
, and
i
-th coordinate evolves independently as follows

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle\sim f\cdot\mu

\displaystyle dU_{t}^{[i]}

\displaystyle=-2U_{t-}^{[i]}\int\mathbf{1}_{0<z<\frac{1}{2}}N^{[i]}(dt,dz).

(7)

We use these two notation interchangeably for convenience.

For any
T>0
, the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the SDE on
[0,T]
follows from the bounded total jump rate for pure jump process (see e.g., Chapter 4.7
[
6
]
). Additionally, since each coordinate of
U_{t}
evolves independently, the distribution of
U_{t}
is characterized by that in each coordinate. For the first coordinate, it follows a Poisson process with rate
1/2
. Its conditional mean has an explicit form, after solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[U_{t}^{[1]}\mid U_{0}^{[1]}=x\right]=xe^{-t}.

Then the transition probability follows since
U_{t}^{[1]}
only takes value in
\left\{-1,1\right\}
:
{\mathbb{P}}(U_{t}^{[1]}=1\mid U_{0}^{[1]}=x)=\frac{1+xe^{-t}}{2}
. In other words,
U_{t}\mid U_{0}=x
matches the distribution of
x\odot\xi_{t}
. Hence, the associated semigroup to
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is exactly the heat semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined in Eq. (
1
).

Next, we describe its generator and its marginal law. Its generator takes the form, for any test function
h:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
,

\displaystyle L^{U}h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(h(\varsigma_{i}(x))-h(x)\right).

(8)

In its matrix form,
L^{U}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{y=\varsigma_{i}(x)},\ x,y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. Additionally, the uniform measure
\mu
is the invariant measure for
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. For time
t>0
, the marginal law of
U_{t}
has measure
p_{t}:=\nu_{f}P_{t}
, which has an explicit form
p_{t}(x)=f(e^{-t}x)\mu(x)
. Conceptually, as
t
tends to
\infty
, one may think the process
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as creating a stochastic bridge from the probability measure
\nu_{f}
to the uniform measure
\mu
.

2.4
Time reversal of a Markov process

Given a Markov process
(X_{t})
on a finite space
\Omega
and its associated semigroup
(Q_{s,t})_{0\leq s\leq t}
with generator
L_{t}
. Let
\pi_{t}=\pi_{0}Q_{0,t}
be a family of probability measures of the law of
X_{t}
. The
time-reversed semigroup
(with respect to
(\pi_{t})
) is defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{s,t}(x,y):=\frac{\pi_{s}(x)}{\pi_{t}(y)}Q_{s,t}(x,y),\forall s\leq t,\forall x,y\in\Omega.

Its reversed generator satisfies the
flux equation

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{t}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{t}(x)}{\pi_{t}(y)}L_{t}(x,y),\text{ if }x\neq y,

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{t}(y,y)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x\neq y}\tilde{L}_{t}(y,x).

The time reversal has a pathwise interpretation. Define
\widetilde{X}_{t}:=X_{T-t}
for
t\in[0,T]
. Then
\widetilde{X}_{t}
is a Markov process with initial law
\pi_{T}
, with
\widetilde{Q}_{T-t,T-s}
as its associated semigroup and
\tilde{L}_{T-t}
as its generator. See e.g., Chapter 7
[
1
]
for more details about time-reversed Markov processes.

2.5
Time reversal of the heat process

Using the time reversal formula, we derive the time reversal of the heat process in Eq. (
2.3
).

Reverse jump process

Define
(V_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
as
V_{t}:=U_{T-t}
, which satisfies the following SDE

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=U_{T},

\displaystyle dV_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-2V_{t-}e_{i})\int\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(e^{-{(T-t)}}V_{t-})}N^{[i]}(dt,dz),

(9)

where the
score function
for
i
-th coordinate
S_{i}:[-1,1]^{n}\to\real
, considering multilinear expansion of
f
, is defined as

\displaystyle S_{i}(x):=\frac{x_{i}\partial_{i}f(x)}{f(x)},\quad\forall i\in[n].

(10)

The reverse jump rate is derived as the unique solution to the flux equation.
Recall that for the heat semigroup, the marginal law is
p_{t}(x)=f(e^{-t}x)\mu(x)
and
L^{U}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{y=\varsigma_{i}(x)}
. The flux equation provides an explicit form of the generator, for any function
h:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
,

\displaystyle L^{V}_{t}h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f(\varsigma_{i}(e^{-(T-t)}x))}{f(e^{-(T-t)}x)}(h(\varsigma_{i}(x))-h(x)).

Since
f>0
and the space
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
is finite, the jump rate is finite on
[0,T]
. The existence and uniqueness of the solution of the SDE follows from the bounded total jump rate for pure jump process (see e.g., Chapter 4.7
[
6
]
).

Unlike the forward process, the reverse jump process is time-inhomogeneous due to its time-dependent jump rates. In view of the jump rate, it is natural to introduce the
scaled reverse jump process

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}_{t}:=e^{-(T-t)}V_{t},

(11)

which lives in the inner cube
[-1,1]^{n}
and becomes a time-homogeneous Markov process.

Remark 2
.

In light of the recent trendy development on score-based diffusion models for sampling high dimensional natural images
[
14
]
via time-reversal of Ornstein-Ulhenbeck process, we remark that
(V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the Boolean hypercube analogue of the Gaussian diffusion models. Here, the Gaussian noise is replaced by Poisson jumps.

Note that as a vector,
S(\mathscr{V}_{t})=V_{t}\odot\nabla\log P_{T-t}f(V_{t})
. Its resemblance to Föllmer’s drift (or the score) in the Gaussian setting
[
4
]
is the reason we name it the score, too. It is known that Föllmer’s drift is a martingale in the Gaussian setting (see e.g. Section 2
[
4
]
). The score
S(\mathscr{V}_{t})
, in our definition, is also a martingale, which we show in Appendix (Lemma
13
).

3
Main proof

Fix
\tau>0
,
T>\tau
to be chosen and
{T_{o}}=T-\tau
. For
t\in[0,T]
, let
\rho_{t}=e^{-(T-t)}
. Based on Remark
1
, we restrict ourselves to strictly positive function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}=1
. To prove Theorem
1
, our high-level proof strategy is to construct a coupling, which is inspired by the main strategy in the Gaussian counterpart
[
4
]
. However, the coupling construction and technical details differ significantly due to the discrete nature of the Boolean hypercube
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. The first step is to show that it suffices to prove the following anti-concentration due to the heat semigroup.

Lemma 1

(Anti-concentration)
.

Let
\nu_{P_{\tau}f}
be the probability measure on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
with density
P_{\tau}f
with respect to the uniform measure
\mu
. There exists a universal constant
c>0
, such that for any
\eta>e^{3}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\sim\nu_{P_{\tau}f}}\left(P_{\tau}f(Y)\in(\eta,e\eta]\right)\leq c\max\left\{1,\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\right\}\frac{\log\log\eta}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

Theorem
1
directly follows from Lemma
1
, using the same proof in the Gaussian space (see Section 2
[
4
]
). A short proof is provided for completeness.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\mu}(P_{\tau}f(X)>\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{X\sim\mu}(P_{\tau}f(X)\in(e^{k}\eta,e^{k+1}\eta])

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e^{k}\eta}{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mu}\left[P_{\tau}f(X)\mathbf{1}_{P_{\tau}f(X)\in(e^{k}\eta,e^{k+1}\eta]}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e^{k}\eta}{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\sim\nu_{P_{\tau}f}}\left(P_{\tau}f(Y)\in(e^{k}\eta,e^{k+1}\eta]\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e^{k}\eta}c\max\left\{1,\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\right\}\frac{\log\log(e^{k}\eta)}{\sqrt{\log(e^{k}\eta)}}

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}2c\max\left\{1,\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\right\}\frac{\log\log(\eta)}{\eta\sqrt{\log(\eta)}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e^{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2ce}{e-1}\max\left\{1,\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\right\}\frac{\log\log(\eta)}{\eta\sqrt{\log(\eta)}}.

(i) follows from Lemma
1
. Let
q:x\mapsto\log\log(x)/\sqrt{\log(x)}
. (ii) follows from the fact that
q(\eta)\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{x>\eta}q
for
\eta>e^{3}
.
∎

The second step is to construct a coupling via the reverse jump process to prove Lemma
1
. While this idea is natural after reading the Gaussian proof
[
4
]
, one cannot apply the same type of perturbation because any drift perturbation moves the process outside the Boolean hypercube
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. New ideas are needed. We would like to construct a coupling of two Markov processes
(V_{t},W_{t})
such that, marginally at time
{T_{o}}
,
V_{{T_{o}}}
has law
\nu_{P_{\tau}f}
(already achieved according to the construction of the reverse process (
2.5
)),
W_{{T_{o}}}
has law
\nu^{W}
, and they satisfy the following two properties, vaguely speaking:

1.

the two measures
\nu_{P_{\tau}f}
and
\nu^{W}
are close in total variation (TV);

2.

with high probability,
P_{\tau}f(V_{{T_{o}}})
is strictly larger than
P_{\tau}f(W_{{T_{o}}})
, given that
P_{\tau}f(W_{{T_{o}}})\geq\eta
.

More precisely, these properties are formalized in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2

(Total variation control)
.

Let
\eta>e^{3}
. For any
0<\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists a coupling construction
(V_{{T_{o}}},W_{{T_{o}}})
, described in Section
3.1
, satisfying

\displaystyle d_{\text{\tiny{TV}}}\left(\nu_{P_{\tau}f},\nu^{W}\right)\leq\frac{ce^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\bar{\delta}\sqrt{\log\eta}.

Here
\nu^{W}
denotes the law of
W_{{T_{o}}}
and
c>0
is a universal constant.

Its proof is in Section
3.2
.

Lemma 3

(Approximate monotone coupling at tail)
.

Let
\eta>e^{3}
. For any
\frac{\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+0.91}{\log\eta}\leq\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists a coupling construction
(V_{{T_{o}}},W_{{T_{o}}})
, described in Section
3.1
, such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\log P_{\tau}f(V_{T_{o}})>\log\eta+1\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\log P_{\tau}f(W_{T_{o}})>\log\eta\right)-\frac{3}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

Its proof is in Section
3.3
. Lemma
1
easily follows from the above two lemmas.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Take
\bar{\delta}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+0.91}{\log\eta}
.
The TV distance control in Lemma
2
gives

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{P}}\left(P_{\tau}f(W_{T_{o}})>\eta\right)-{\mathbb{P}}\left(P_{\tau}f(V_{T_{o}})>\eta\right)\right|\leq\frac{ce^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\bar{\delta}\sqrt{\log\eta}.

Using this equation to eliminate
W_{T_{o}}
term in Lemma
3
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(P_{\tau}f(V_{T_{o}})\in(\eta,e\eta]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}+\frac{ce^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\bar{\delta}\sqrt{\log\eta}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\max\left\{1,\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\right\}\frac{\log\log\eta}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

∎

The rest of this section is organized as follows. In Section
3.1
, we introduce our coupling construction. In Section
3.2
and Section
3.3
, we prove Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
respectively.

3.1
Main coupling construction

The Föllmer process
[
7
]
, as the (time-reparametrized) time-reversed Ornstein-Ulhenbeck (OU) process, played an important role
for constructing a coupling in the Gaussian counterpart of Talagrand’s conjecture
[
4
]
. Following this idea, we perturb the reverse jump process (
2.5
) to construct a coupling. The perturbation is no longer on the drift, but on the jump rate. Recall that the reverse jump process
(V_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the following SDE

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=U_{T}

\displaystyle dV_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-2V_{t-}e_{i})\int\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(e^{-{(T-t)}}V_{t-})}N^{[i]}(dt,dz),

where the score function is
S_{i}(x)=\frac{x\partial_{i}f(x)}{f(x)}
.

Perturbed reverse jump process

Let
0<\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
. We define for
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle W_{0}

\displaystyle:=V_{0}

\displaystyle dW_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-2W_{t-}e_{i})\int\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-(1-\delta_{i}\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})S_{i}(e^{-(T-t)}V_{t-})}N^{[i]}(dt,dz),

(12)

where the stopping time is defined as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}:=\inf\left\{t\in[0,\infty)\mid\max\left\{\log P_{T-t}f(V_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta-1,\ \log P_{T-t}f(W_{t})\right\}>\log\eta\right\}\wedge{T_{o}},

(13)

and
\delta_{i}
is a shorthand for
\delta_{i}(e^{-(T-t)}V_{t-})
, where

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(x):=\bar{\delta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(x)>0}+\frac{1-2S_{i}(x)}{1-2\bar{\delta}S_{i}(x)}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(x)\leq 0}\right],\quad i\in[n],\quad x\in[-1,1]^{n}.

(14)

In words,
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
is the first time either the process
\log P_{T-t}f(W_{t})
hits value
\log\eta
or
\log P_{T-t}f(V_{t})
hits value
\log\eta+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+1
, with the convention that
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
takes value
{T_{o}}
if they never reach
\log\eta
. The jump perturbation rate is of order
\bar{\delta}
, but has two forms depending on whether the score
S_{i}
is positive or not.

Remark 3
.

In Eldan and Lee’s proof for the Gaussian counterpart
[
4
]
, a
\delta
perturbation is added to the score function, which results in a
\delta
perturbation of the drift of Föllmer’s process. Here, we cannot add perturbations to the drift as it moves out of
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. But the idea that we should perturb the score function, once the score on the Boolean hypercube is properly defined, remains valid and essential.

While a constant
\delta
perturbation of the score suffices in Eldan and Lee’s proof for the Gaussian counterpart
[
4
]
, our coupling construction takes full advantage of the perturbation along the stochastic process: the perturbation
\delta_{i}
is no longer a constant. It is state-dependent and coordinate-dependent.

The processes
(V_{t},W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
are coupled in that

•

they share the same initialization
W_{0}=V_{0}
;

•

they share the same Poisson random measure
N^{[i]},\forall i\in[n]
;

•

W
alone is not Markov, its jump rate depends on
V
.

To make sure the SDE solution is well-defined, we verify that the jump rate is nonnegative and never explodes. The following lemma checks the range of
S_{i}
.

Lemma 4

(Edge ratio bound)
.

For any nonnegative function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real_{+}
with
f\neq 0
, consider its multilinear expansion. Then for any
x\in(-1,1)^{n}
,
f
is upper and lower bounded pointwise

\displaystyle 0<\left\|f\right\|_{1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\left|x_{i}\right|)\leq f(x)\leq\left\|f\right\|_{1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+\left|x_{i}\right|).

Additionally, for any
i\in[n]
,
i
-th edge ratio is also bounded

\displaystyle\frac{1-\left|x_{i}\right|}{1+\left|x_{i}\right|}\leq\frac{f(\varsigma_{i}(x))}{f(x)}\leq\frac{1+\left|x_{i}\right|}{1-\left|x_{i}\right|},\quad\forall x\in(-1,1)^{n}.

Its proof is deferred to Section
4.1
. The next lemma verifies the existence and uniqueness of the SDE solutions.

Lemma 5
.

For
0<\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
, the coupled SDE for
(V,W)
in Eq. (
2.5
) and (
3.1
) has a unique solution. Additionally,
0\leq\delta_{i}\leq 2\bar{\delta},\forall i\in[n]
.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

First, since we consider strictly positive function
f
, the jump rate in Eq. (
2.5
) is always finite on a finite state space. Second, Lemma
4
gives

\displaystyle\frac{-\left|x_{i}\right|}{1-\left|x_{i}\right|}\leq S_{i}(x)\leq\frac{\left|x_{i}\right|}{1+\left|x_{i}\right|},\quad\forall x\in(-1,1)^{n}.

(15)

In particular, we obtain that for
t\in[0,{T_{o}}]
,
\frac{1-e^{-\tau}}{1+e^{-\tau}}\leq\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(e^{-(T-t)}V_{t-})\leq\frac{1+e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}
. Back to Eq. (
3.1
), we verify that the jump rate on the
i
-th coordinate of
W_{t}
is nonnegative and finite, as long as
0<\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
. For
t\in[0,T]
,
x=e^{-(T-t)}V_{t-}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(x)+\delta_{i}\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}S_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(x)+\bar{\delta}\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}S_{i}(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(x)\geq 0&\text{ if }S_{i}(x)>0,\\
\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(x)\right)\left[\mathbf{1}_{t\geq{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}+\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\frac{1}{1-2\bar{\delta}S_{i}(x)}\right]\geq 0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

The bounded total jump rate ensures the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the SDE (
3.1
) (Chapter 4.7
[
6
]
). Additionally, as long as
0<\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1-2S_{i}(x)}{1-2\bar{\delta}S_{i}(x)}\leq 2,

which leads to
0<\delta_{i}\leq 2\bar{\delta}
.
∎

Joint generator representation

The use of the stopping time
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
makes the joint process
(V_{t},W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
no longer Markov. To make it Markov again, we could enlarge the state space with a flag variable
I_{t}:=\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}
, taking value on
\left\{0,1\right\}
. Then the process
(V_{t},W_{t},I_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is Markov. However, to simplify notation, we decide to not do so. Applying Itô’s formula, for any function
h:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{2n}\to\real
, we have

\displaystyle dh(V_{t},W_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{t}h(V_{t-},W_{t-})dt+dM_{t}^{h},

where
M_{t}^{h}
is a local martingale.
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is a path-dependent and time-dependent operator adapted to
\mathscr{F}_{t}
, which we also call joint generator of
(V_{t},W_{t})
, with the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\bar{L}^{0}_{t}+\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}(\bar{L}_{t}-\bar{L}^{0}_{t}).

(16)

Here, with
\rho_{t}=e^{-(T-t)}
,

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{t}^{0}h(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[h(\varsigma_{i}(x),\varsigma_{i}(y))-h(x,y)\right]\left(\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)\right),

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{L}_{t}-\bar{L}_{t}^{0},

\displaystyle A_{t}h(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)\geq 0}\left[h(\varsigma_{i}(x),y)-h(\varsigma_{i}(x),\varsigma_{i}(y))\right]\delta_{i}(\rho_{t}x)S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)<0}\left[h(x,\varsigma_{i}(y))-h(x,y)\right][-\delta_{i}(\rho_{t}x)]S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)\Big\}.

We make a few remarks about
\mathcal{G}_{t}
.

•

In words,
\mathcal{G}_{t}
does,

–

before
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
(always
\leq{T_{o}}
), follow
\bar{L}_{t}
, the joint Markov semigroup with perturbation;

–

after
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
, follow
\bar{L}_{t}^{0}
, the joint Markov semigroup without perturbation.

•

The path-dependency of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
only comes from the stopping time in
\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}
.

•

Without perturbation, as captured in
\bar{L}_{t}^{0}
, the two processes jump together. Consequently,
\text{sign}(x\odot y)
is preserved along
\bar{L}_{t}^{0}
.

•

The perturbation part
A_{t}
always involves a jump on
y
.

3.2
Proof of Lemma
2
on the total variation control

In this subsection, we upper-bound the TV distance

\displaystyle d_{\text{\tiny{TV}}}\left(\text{Law}(V_{{T_{o}}}),\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}})\right)=\sup_{\phi:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}}\left|{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(V_{{T_{o}}})]-{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(W_{{T_{o}}})]\right|.

The main idea is to apply Duhamel’s formula from
0
to
{T_{o}}
by treating the joint generator (
16
) as unperturbed generator
\bar{L}^{0}_{t}
plus a small perturbation. A key observation on how the heat semigroup
P_{\tau}
helps in bounding TV distance is the following identity, which we prove later in Lemma
8
. For any test function
\phi:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(V_{{T_{o}}})-\phi(W_{{T_{o}}})]={\mathbb{E}}[P_{\tau}\phi(V_{T})-P_{\tau}\phi(W_{T})].

Intuitively, instead of working with arbitrary bounded test functions in the TV distance definition, we only have to deal with “smoothed” test functions due to
P_{\tau}
.

However, upon testing our proof strategy with a single-stage Duhamel’s formula, we realize that it cannot fully take advantage of the “smoothness” gained from
P_{\tau}
. We introduce a multi-stage Duhamel proof. We introduce
m
early-stopped perturbed processes, which interpolate between
(V_{t},V_{t})
and
(V_{t},W_{t})
as follows. Fix a partition of time intervals

\displaystyle 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{m}={T_{o}},

with
t_{k}-t_{k-1}=\upsilon
, to be chosen.

Early-stopped processes construction

For any
k\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,m\right\}
, define the early-stopped process
(V_{t},W_{t}^{(k)})_{t\in[0,T]}
, using the same PRM as
(V_{t},W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
:

•

on
[0,t_{k})
, the same dynamics as
(V_{t},W_{t})
is used;

•

on
[t_{k},T)
, the perturbation is switched off;

•

(V_{t})
is never changed, which is why we kept the same notation.

In other words, its (path-dependent) generator satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(k)}=\bar{L}^{0}_{t}+\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}}(\bar{L}_{t}-\bar{L}^{0}_{t}).

(17)

Just as how
(V_{t},W_{t})
is defined, these early-stopped processes are well-defined.
Note that

\displaystyle\text{Law}(V_{{T_{o}}})

\displaystyle=\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)}),\quad\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}})=\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(m)}).

By triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{\text{\tiny{TV}}}\left(\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)}),\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(m)})\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{\text{\tiny{TV}}}\left(\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(k-1)}),\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(k)})\right).

(18)

What remains is to apply Duhamel to each term on the RHS. Before completing the proof of Lemma
2
, we need three lemmas, with proofs deferred to Section
4.2
.

Lemma 6

(Basic level-1 inequality)
.

Let
h:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
. Consider its multilinear expansion. For any
x\in(-1,1)^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-x_{i}^{2})\left(\partial_{i}h(x)\right)^{2}\leq h(x)-h(x)^{2}.

This is a well-known consequence of Parseval’s inequality for biased Fourier analysis, see, e.g., Section 3 of
[
5
]
or Appendix 1 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 7

(Expected total squared score bound)
.

Let
n\geq 3,T\geq 100\log(n)
. For the reverse jump process (
11
), with
\mathscr{V}_{t}=e^{-(T-t)}V_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})^{2}dt\right]\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}\left(\log\eta+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+0.99\right),

where
\alpha=\frac{1-e^{-\tau}}{1+e^{-\tau}}
.

Intuitively, as a consequence of Itô’s formula applied to
\log f(\mathscr{V}_{t})
, this lemma expresses that the total squared score
\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})^{2}ds
is closely related to
\log f(\mathscr{V}_{t})-\log f(\mathscr{V}_{0})
, up to a multiplicative factor.

Lemma 8

(Reverse-time martingale identity)
.

For any
k\in[m]
and any
\phi:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\phi(W_{t_{k}}^{(k-1)})-\phi(W_{t_{k}}^{(k)})\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[P_{T-t_{k}}\phi(W_{T}^{(k-1)})-P_{T-t_{k}}\phi(W_{T}^{(k)})\right].

This lemma is the main manifestation of the gained smoothness from the heat semigroup
P_{\tau}
. We are now ready to complete the proof of Lemma
2
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Introduce the shorthand
Z_{t}^{(k)}:=(V_{t},W_{t}^{(k)})
. Let
R_{s,t}^{0}
and
R_{s,t}
be the Markov semigroups associated to the generators
\bar{L}_{t}^{0}
and
\bar{L}_{t}
respectively.

First, we derive Duhamel’s formula. Given a test function
h:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{2n}\to\real
, let
u_{t}(z)
be the post-switch process starting at time
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle u_{t}(z):=R_{t,T}^{0}h(z).

Note that by definition,
u_{t}
satisfies the Kolmogorov backward equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{t}}{\partial t}+\bar{L}_{t}^{0}u_{t}=0.

Consider
u_{t}(V_{t},W_{t}^{(k)})
, and apply Itô’s formula for
t\in[0,{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}]
to obtain

\displaystyle du_{t}(V_{t},W_{t}^{(k)})=\left(\frac{\partial u_{t}}{\partial t}+\bar{L}_{t}u\right)(V_{t-},W_{t-}^{(k)})ds+dM_{t}.

M_{t}
is a martingale. Integrating from
0
to
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}
, taking expectation and applying the optional stopping theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[h(Z_{{T_{o}}}^{(k)})]-{\mathbb{E}}[h(Z_{{T_{o}}})]={\mathbb{E}}[u_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}}(Z_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}}^{(k)})]-{\mathbb{E}}[u_{0}(Z_{0})]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}}(\bar{L}_{s}-\bar{L}^{0}_{s})u_{s}(Z_{s-}^{(k)})ds\right].

Doing the same for
k-1
, and noticing that
Z_{s}^{(k)}
is identical to
Z_{s}^{(k-1)}
until
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k-1}
, we obtain Duhamel’s formula

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[h(Z_{{T_{o}}}^{(k)})]-{\mathbb{E}}[h(Z_{{T_{o}}}^{(k-1)})]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k-1}}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}\wedge t_{k}}(\bar{L}_{s}-\bar{L}^{0}_{s})u_{s}(Z_{s-}^{(k)})ds\right].

(19)

Second, we examine the terms in the above integral and bound it. Recall that
A_{t}=\bar{L}_{t}-\bar{L}_{t}^{0}
,

\displaystyle A_{t}h(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)\geq 0}\left[h(\varsigma_{i}(x),y)-h(\varsigma_{i}(x),\varsigma_{i}(y))\right]\delta_{i}(\rho_{t}x)S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)<0}\left[h(x,\varsigma_{i}(y))-h(x,y)\right][-\delta_{i}(\rho_{t}x)]S_{i}(\rho_{t}x)\Big\}.

Thanks to Lemma
8
, we are only interested in test functions of the form
h(x,y)=\varphi(y)
, where
\varphi=P_{T-t_{k}}\phi
(we omit the
k
-dependency to simplify notation). Then

\displaystyle u_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[h(V_{{T_{o}}},W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(x,y)]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(x,y)]

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{y}u_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(x,\varsigma_{i}(y))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(x,y)]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}{\mathbb{E}}[\Delta_{i}\varphi(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(x,y)]

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{y}u_{s}(\varsigma_{i}(x),y)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\Delta_{i}\varphi(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(\varsigma_{i}(x),y)].

(i) follows from the fact that
W^{(0)}
has jump rates that only depend on
V
, so a flip at initialization results in a flip in the end.
Consequently, with
\mathscr{V}_{t}=\rho_{t}V_{t}
,

\displaystyle\quad A_{s}u_{s}(Z_{s-}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})\geq 0}\left[\Delta_{i}^{y}u_{s}(\varsigma_{i}(V_{s-}),W_{s-}^{(k)})\right]+\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})<0}\left[\Delta_{i}^{y}u_{s}(V_{s-},W_{s-}^{(k)})\right]\right\}\delta_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-}).

(20)

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
0\leq\delta_{i}\leq 2\bar{\delta}
from Lemma
5
, we bound the second sum in Eq. (
3.2
)

\displaystyle\quad\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})<0}\left[\Delta_{i}^{y}u_{s}(V_{s-},W_{s-}^{(k)})\right]\delta_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\bar{\delta}\ {\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Delta_{i}\varphi(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)})\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mid(V_{s},W_{s}^{(0)})=(V_{s-},W_{s-}^{(k)})\right]\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}^{2}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}2\bar{\delta}\left(\frac{4e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}^{2}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(i) applies the level-1 inequality pointwise from Lemma
6
using that fact that
\varphi=P_{T-t_{k}}\phi=\phi(e^{-(T-t_{k})}\cdot)
, which exploits the fact that the argument is in the inner cube. The first sum in Eq. (
3.2
) is bounded in the same way. Combining both bounds into Eq. (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}[h(Z_{{T_{o}}}^{(k)})]-{\mathbb{E}}[h(Z_{{T_{o}}}^{(k-1)})]\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4\bar{\delta}\left(\frac{4e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbf{1}_{s<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}^{2}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]ds

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}4\bar{\delta}\left(\frac{4e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(t_{k}-t_{k-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})^{2}ds\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\bar{\delta}e^{-\tau}}{\left(1-e^{-2\tau}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-{T_{o}}+k\upsilon}\sqrt{\upsilon}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})^{2}ds\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(i) applies Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The expected total squared score is bounded via Lemma
7
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{s-})^{2}ds\right]\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}\left(\log\eta+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+0.99\right).

Summing over
k
from
1
to
m
, using
m\upsilon={T_{o}}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{-{T_{o}}+k\upsilon}\sqrt{\upsilon}=\frac{\sqrt{\upsilon}}{1-e^{-\upsilon}}\left(1-e^{-{T_{o}}}\right)\overset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{1-e^{-1}}.

(i) follows because we choose
m=\left\lfloor{T_{o}}\right\rfloor
and
{T_{o}}>1
, then
1\leq\upsilon\leq 2
.

Finally, applying Lemma
8
and triangle inequality in Eq. (
18
), we have

\displaystyle d_{\text{\tiny{TV}}}\left(\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(0)}),\text{Law}(W_{{T_{o}}}^{(m)})\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{e^{-\tau}}{\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-e^{-2\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\delta}\sqrt{\log\eta}

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}}\bar{\delta}\sqrt{\log\eta}.

In fact, we also have an explicit bound on the constant,
c\leq 32
.

∎

Remark 4
.

The single-stage Duhamel proof is loose with a
\sqrt{T}
factor from the application of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. With our choice of
T
, it would be of order
\sqrt{\log n}
, making the final bound no longer dimension-free. The purpose of our multi-stage Duhamel proof is to save this extra
\sqrt{\log n}
factor.

Remark 5
.

A natural alternative idea to bound the TV distance is through a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence bound and Pinsker’s inequality. The KL divergence can be bounded via Girsanov’s theorem applied to jump processes (see e.g.
[
11
]
). However, to bound KL divergence this way requires a bound on
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(S_{i}(\rho_{t}V_{t})-S_{i}(\rho_{t}W_{t})\right)^{2}
, and the author was unable to obtain a good bound of this type. Intuitively, while in the Gaussian counterpart the perturbed process and the original process are closed in
L^{2}
distance, the discrete nature of the Boolean hypercube
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
makes them far apart in
L^{2}
distance. This is, according to the author, the main difficulty in working with the perturbed reverse process on the Boolean hypercube.

Another idea to bound the TV distance is to follow the proof in
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
. They crucially use the semi-log-convexity gained from the heat semigroup (see Eq. (5.6) in
[
10
]
), and the second-order Taylor expansion derived from it. As we show in Lemma
12
in Appendix, the heat semigroup on the Boolean hypercube also provides a similar semi-log-convexity and allows us to write a similar second-order Taylor expansion as follows

\displaystyle\log P_{\tau}f(W_{{T_{o}}})\geq\log P_{\tau}f(V_{{T_{o}}})+\left\langle\nabla\log P_{\tau}f(V_{{T_{o}}}),W_{{T_{o}}}-V_{{T_{o}}}\right\rangle-\frac{c_{\tau}}{2}\left\|W_{{T_{o}}}-V_{{T_{o}}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

for a constant
c_{\tau}
that only depends on
\tau
. However, again, the difficulty to proceed from here is that the author was unable to obtain a good dimension-free
L^{2}
bound on
\left\|W_{{T_{o}}}-V_{{T_{o}}}\right\|_{2}
, which is intuitively quite large as the distance between two points on the Boolean hypercube. Our whole proof was designed to avoid such
L^{2}
bounds.

3.3
Proof of Lemma
3
on the approximate monotone coupling at tail

In this section, we show that our coupling construction
(V,W)
makes
\log P_{\tau}f(V_{{T_{o}}})
strictly larger than
\log P_{\tau}f(W_{{T_{o}}})
conditioned on
\log P_{\tau}f(W_{{T_{o}}})
being large, via stochastic calculus. It is more convenient to work with scaled processes, with
\rho_{t}=e^{-(T-t)}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}V_{t},

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\rho_{t}W_{t}.

Then
P_{T-t}f(V_{t})=f(\mathscr{V}_{t})
and
P_{T-t}f(W_{t})=f(\mathscr{W}_{t})
.
Applying Itô’s formula to
\log f(\mathscr{V}_{t})
and
\log f(\mathscr{W}_{t})
, we obtain

\displaystyle d\log f(\mathscr{V}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})dt+\int\log\left(1-2S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})\right)\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})}N^{[i]}(dt,dz)\right],

\displaystyle d\log f(\mathscr{W}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[S_{i}(\mathscr{W}_{t-})dt+\int\log\left(1-2S_{i}(\mathscr{W}_{t-})\right)\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-(1-\delta_{i}\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})}N^{[i]}(dt,dz)\right],

where recall that
\delta_{i}\equiv\delta_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})
, with
0<\bar{\delta}<\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(x)=\bar{\delta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(x)>0}+\frac{1-2S_{i}(x)}{1-2\bar{\delta}S_{i}(x)}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(x)\leq 0}\right],\quad i\in[n],\quad x\in[-1,1]^{n}.

Introduce the shorthand
(v_{i},w_{i}):=(S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-}),S_{i}(\mathscr{W}_{t-}))
, where we omit the dependency on
t
as long as it is clear from the context. Then the two SDEs above become

\displaystyle d\log f(\mathscr{V}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[v_{i}dt+\int\log\left(1-2v_{i}\right)\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-v_{i}}N^{[i]}(dt,dz)\right],

\displaystyle d\log f(\mathscr{W}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[w_{i}dt+\int\log\left(1-2w_{i}\right)\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}-(1-\delta_{i}\mathbf{1}_{t<{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})v_{i}}N^{[i]}(dt,dz)\right].

(21)

To complete the proof of Lemma
3
, we need three lemmas which bound quantities in Eq. (
3.3
) with high probability. Their proofs are deferred to Section
4.3
.

Lemma 9
.

With probability at least
1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}
, we have

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{V}_{{T_{o}}})>\log f(\mathscr{V}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})-\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
is always upper bounded by
{T_{o}}
, from Eq. (
3.3
),
\log f(\mathscr{V}_{{T_{o}}})-\log f(\mathscr{V}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})
has nonnegative drift terms. Lemma
9
states that the noise term does not alter the nonnegativity too much.

Lemma 10
.

With probability at least
1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}
, we have

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{W}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})-\log f(\mathscr{W}_{0})<\left[2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}(1-\delta_{i})w_{i}v_{i}dt\right]+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta.

In words, Lemma
10
upper bounds
\log f(\mathscr{W}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})
with the corresponding score terms with high probability.

Lemma 11
.

With probability at least
1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}
, we have

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})-\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})<\left[-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\delta_{i}\left(\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\frac{1}{1-2v_{i}}\right)w_{i}v_{i}dt\right]+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta.

Roughly speaking, Lemma
11
shows that with high probability,
\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})
is slightly larger than
\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})
by a term proportional to
\bar{\delta}
. We are now ready to prove Lemma
3
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Define the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\log f(\mathscr{V}_{{T_{o}}})>\log f(\mathscr{V}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})-\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\log f(\mathscr{W}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}})-\log f(\mathscr{W}_{0})<\left[2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}(1-\delta_{i})w_{i}v_{i}dt\right]+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})-\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})<\left[-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}}\delta_{i}\left(\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\frac{1}{1-2v_{i}}\right)w_{i}v_{i}dt\right]+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta\right\}.

Applying Lemma
9
,
10
and
11
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3})\geq 1-\frac{3}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

(22)

The rest of proof is conditioned on all three events. Our choice of
\delta_{i}
allows us to connect the two quantities on the RHS. Recall that

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=\bar{\delta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\frac{1-2v_{i}}{1-2\bar{\delta}v_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\right],\quad i\in[n],\quad x\in[-1,1]^{n}.

Observe that

\displaystyle 1-\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-\bar{\delta})\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\left(1-\bar{\delta}\frac{1-2v_{i}}{1-2\bar{\delta}v_{i}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}=(1-\bar{\delta})\left[\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\frac{1}{1-2\bar{\delta}v_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\right]

\displaystyle\delta_{i}\left(\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\frac{1}{1-2v_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{\delta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\frac{1}{1-2\bar{\delta}v_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\right]\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{\bar{\delta}}{1-\bar{\delta}}(1-\delta_{i}).

(i) uses the expression of
1-\delta_{i}
in the first line. Consequently,

\displaystyle\delta_{i}w_{i}v_{i}\left(\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}>0}+\mathbf{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}\frac{1}{1-2v_{i}}\right)=\frac{\bar{\delta}}{1-\bar{\delta}}\left[(1-\delta_{i})w_{i}v_{i}\right].

This connects
\mathcal{E}_{2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{3}
, and allows us to obtain, under
\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}
,

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})-\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})<-\frac{\bar{\delta}}{1-\bar{\delta}}\left[\log f(\mathscr{W}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}})-\log f(\mathscr{W}_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta\right]+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta.

Rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})>\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})+\frac{\bar{\delta}}{1-\bar{\delta}}\left[\log f(\mathscr{W}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}})-\log f(\mathscr{W}_{0})\right]-\frac{1}{2(1-\bar{\delta})}\log\log\eta.

(23)

The event
\left\{\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})>\log\eta\right\}
implies that, based on our definition of stopping time (
13
),

\displaystyle\text{ either }\log f(\mathscr{V}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}})\geq\log\eta+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+1\text{ or }\log f(\mathscr{W}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}})\geq\log\eta.

If the first case holds at
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
, then by Lemma
9
, under
\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})\geq\log f(\mathscr{V}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}})-\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta\geq\log\eta+1.

If the second case holds at
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{T}}}
, then from Eq. (
23
), using Lemma
4
,
\log f(\mathscr{W}_{0})\leq\log(1+e^{-T})^{n}\leq 1/n^{99}\leq 0.01
if
T\geq 100\log n
and
n\geq 3
. For
\eta>e^{3}
,
\frac{\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+0.91}{\log\eta}\leq 0.49
. For
\bar{\delta}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta+0.91}{\log\eta}
, under
\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}
,

\displaystyle\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})>\log\eta+\frac{1}{1-\bar{\delta}}\left[\bar{\delta}(\log\eta-0.01)-\frac{1}{2}\log\log\eta\right]\geq\log\eta+1.

Combining both cases, we obtain the following implication

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}\cap\left\{\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})>\log\eta\right\}\implies\left\{\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})>\log\eta+1\right\}.

Together with Eq. (
22
), we conclude that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\log f(\mathscr{V}_{T_{o}})>\log\eta+1\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\log f(\mathscr{W}_{T_{o}})>\log\eta\right)-\frac{3}{\sqrt{\log\eta}}.

∎

Remark 6
.

The main difference between Poisson noise in the reverse heat process in the Boolean setting and Brownian motion in the reverse OU process in the Gaussian setting is that the former’s law is not symmetric. This is why (1) we have to design state-dependent perturbation, (2) we lower bound the unperturbed process by the perturbed process while it is in the reverse direction in the Gaussian counterpart
[
4
]
. Lower bounding the noise in Lemma
11
is more challenging than upper bounding it.

In view of the exponential martingale bound in Lemma
11
, it is reasonable to conjecture that one might be able to obtain a better bound to save the extra
\log\log\eta
factor via a similar strategy. One possibility is to obtain a better
L^{2}
bound on
\sum_{i}(w_{i}-v_{i})^{2}
and use a larger
\gamma
in Eq. (
28
) in the proof of Lemma
11
. However, as we discussed in Remark
5
, the author was unable to prove a good dimension-free
L^{2}
bound. Our current construction is designed to avoid the appearance of terms like
\sum_{i}(w_{i}-v_{i})^{2}
.

4.1
Proof related to smoothness

Here we prove Lemma
4
, which quantifies one form of smoothness brought by the heat semigroup.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Note that for
z\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, we may write

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}}f(y)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1+y_{j}z_{j}}{2}\right).

This gives another multilinear polynomial expansion, which takes value
f(y)
for any
y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. By the uniqueness of the multilinear polynomial expansion, we conclude that this polynomial agrees with the one in Eq. (
4
). It shows that for any
z\in[-1,1]^{n}
,
f(z)\geq 0
,
because
f(y)\geq 0
for
y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
and
1+y_{i}z_{i}\geq 0
. Additionally, for any
z\in(-1,1)^{n}
, because
1-\left|z_{i}\right|\leq 1+y_{i}z_{i}\leq 1+\left|z_{i}\right|
,

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\left|z_{i}\right|)\leq f(z)\leq\left\|f\right\|_{1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+\left|z_{i}\right|).

Now for
z\in[-1,1]^{n}
, write

\displaystyle f(z)=(1+z_{i})\underbrace{\sum_{y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n},y_{i}=1}f(y)\prod_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\left(\frac{1+y_{j}z_{j}}{2}\right)}_{=:R_{1}}+(1-z_{i})\underbrace{\sum_{y\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n},y_{i}=-1}f(y)\prod_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\left(\frac{1+y_{j}z_{j}}{2}\right)}_{=:R_{2}}.

Note that
R_{1}\geq 0
and
R_{2}\geq 0
, for the same reason why
f(z)\geq 0
. We have

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=(1+z_{i})R_{1}+(1-z_{i})R_{2}

\displaystyle f(\varsigma_{i}(z))

\displaystyle=(1-z_{i})R_{1}+(1+z_{i})R_{2}

Then if
z_{i}>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{f(\varsigma_{i}(z))}{f(z)}=\frac{1-z_{i}}{1+z_{i}}+\frac{\left[(1+z_{i})-\frac{(1-z_{i})^{2}}{1+z_{i}}\right]R_{1}}{(1-z_{i})R_{1}+(1+z_{i})R_{2}}\geq\frac{1-|z_{i}|}{1+|z_{i}|}.

If
z_{i}<0
,

\displaystyle\frac{f(\varsigma_{i}(z))}{f(z)}=\frac{1+z_{i}}{1-z_{i}}+\frac{\left[(1-z_{i})-\frac{(1+z_{i})^{2}}{1-z_{i}}\right]R_{2}}{(1-z_{i})R_{1}+(1+z_{i})R_{2}}\geq\frac{1-|z_{i}|}{1+|z_{i}|}.

Overall,
\frac{f(\varsigma_{i}(z))}{f(z)}\geq\frac{1-|z_{i}|}{1+|z_{i}|}
.
Finally, by considering
\varsigma_{i}(x)
in the place of
x
, we also obtain an upper bound.
∎

,

Proof of Lemma
6
.

We first introduce the basics of
p
-biased analysis on the Boolean hypercube. For a reference, see
[
3
]
or Section 8 of
[
12
]
. Let
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
, and let
\mu_{p}
be the following product measure on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, with

\displaystyle\mu_{p}(y):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+y_{i}(2p_{i}-1)}{2},

where the
i
-th marginal takes value
1
with probability
p_{i}
and value
-1
with probability
1-p_{i}
. For
i\in[n]
, define
\phi_{i}:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(y):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-2p_{i}}{\sqrt{p_{i}(1-p_{i})}}+y_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}(1-p_{i})}}\right).

(24)

For
S\subseteq[n]
, define
\phi_{S}:=\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{i}
, and
\phi_{\emptyset}:=1
by convention.

Proposition 1

(Proposition 31 in
[
3
]
)
.

(\phi_{S})_{S\subseteq[n]}
form an orthonormal basis for the space of functions on
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, equipped with the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mu_{p}}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{p}}[fg]
. Any function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\real
can be written as

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[\phi_{i}]^{2}

\displaystyle\partial_{j}S_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial_{i}f}{f}+x_{i}\left(-\frac{\partial_{i}f\partial_{i}f}{f^{2}}\right)&j=i\\
\quad x_{i}\left(\frac{\partial_{ij}f}{f}-\frac{\partial_{i}f\partial_{j}f}{f^{2}}\right)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence, the first part in the derivative becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla S_{i}(x),x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=S_{i}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{ij}(x)-S_{i}(x)\sum_{j}S_{j}(x),

\displaystyle=S_{i}(x)(1-S_{i}(x))+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}\left(R_{ij}(x)-S_{i}(x)S_{j}(x)\right)

where
R_{ij}(x)=\frac{x_{i}x_{j}\partial_{ij}f(x)}{f(x)}
and
R_{ii}=0
.

For the jump increments, using that
f
and
\partial_{i}f
are multilinear, we have

\displaystyle f(x-2x_{j}e_{j})

\displaystyle=f(x)-2x_{j}\partial_{j}f(x),

\displaystyle\partial_{i}f(x-2x_{j}e_{j})

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}f(x)-2x_{j}\partial_{ij}f(x),\text{ for }j\neq i

\displaystyle\partial_{i}f(x-2x_{i}e_{i})

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}f(x).

Then we deduce that

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}S_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{-x_{i}\partial_{i}f(x)}{f(x-2x_{i}e_{i})}-\frac{x_{i}\partial_{i}f(x)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-S_{i}(x)}{1-2S_{i}(x)}-S_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{-S_{i}(x)(1-S_{i}(x))}{\frac{1}{2}(1-2S_{i}(x))}.

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}S_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{i}(\partial_{i}f(x)-2x_{j}\partial_{ij}f(x))}{f(x)-2x_{j}\partial_{j}f(x)}-S_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{i}(x)-2R_{ij}(x)}{1-2S_{j}(x)}-S_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{i}(x)S_{j}(x)-R_{ij}(x)}{\frac{1}{2}(1-2S_{j}(x))}.

Since the jump rate is
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-2S_{i}(x)\right)
, the drift coming from centering the jumps cancel exactly with
\left\langle\nabla S_{i}(x),x\right\rangle
. We obtain that

\displaystyle dS_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int\Delta_{j}S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t-})\mathbf{1}_{0<z\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-2S_{j}(\mathscr{V}_{t-}))}\widetilde{N}^{[j]}(dt,dz).

Hence,
S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t})
is a local martingale. Since
S_{i}(\mathscr{V}_{t})
is also bounded by Lemma
4
, it is a martingale.
∎
