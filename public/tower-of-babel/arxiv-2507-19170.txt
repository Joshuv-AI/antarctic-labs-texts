Abstract
. We prove that certain suitably renormalized value functions associated with the d d -dimensional ( d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 ) N N -body problem corresponding to different limiting shapes of expanding solutions, under the assumption that the center of mass is at the origin, are viscosity solutions of the associated Hamilton-Jacobi equation. We analyze their singularities, defined as the initial configurations for which the minimizer of the associated variational problem is not unique. Moreover, we estimate the size of the closure of the singular set by proving its ℋ d ⁡ ( N − 1 ) − 1 \mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-1} -rectifiability, and we provide an upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of the set of regular conjugate points.

1.
Introduction

The Hamilton-Jacobi equation plays a fundamental role in the analysis of dynamical systems, particularly in Celestial Mechanics. The Newtonian
N
-body problem – describing the motion of
N
point masses under mutual gravitational attraction – provides a rich framework for studying viscosity solutions of the equation. Over the last two decades, substantial progress has been made in characterizing such solutions and connecting them to the geometric and variational properties of the
N
-body system (cf., e.g.,
[
19
,
20
,
18
]
).

We consider the classical Newtonian
N
-body problem in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geq 2
, where
N
point masses
m_{i}>0
occupy positions
r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Newton’s equation of motion for the
i
-th body can be written as:

m_{i}\ddot{r}_{i}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}m_{i}m_{j}\frac{r_{i}-r_{j}}{|r_{i}-r_{j}|^{3}}.

Since Newton’s equations are invariant under translation, we may fix the origin of our inertial frame at the center of mass of the system and consider the configuration space with zero barycenter:

\mathcal{X}=\left\{x=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}r_{i}=0\right\},

and denote by

\Omega=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\middle|\,r_{i}\neq r_{j}\text{ for all }i\neq j\right\}

the set of collision-free configurations, which is open and dense in
\mathcal{X}
. We write
\Delta=\mathcal{X}\setminus\Omega
for the collision set.

The Newtonian potential is given by:

U(x)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|r_{i}-r_{j}|},

so that the equations of motion take the more compact form:

(1.1)

\mathcal{M}\ddot{x}=\nabla U(x),

where
\mathcal{M}=\text{diag}(m_{1}I_{d},\ldots,m_{N}I_{d})
. The associated Lagrangian
L:T\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and Hamiltonian
H:T^{*}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
are defined by:

L(x,v)=\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(x),\qquad H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}\|p\|_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}^{2}-U(x),

where the norm is dual to the mass inner product on
T^{*}\Omega
, defined as

\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\langle r_{i},s_{i}\rangle,\qquad\text{for }x=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}),\ y=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})\in\mathcal{X},

with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denoting the standard scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We are interested in global solutions
v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
of the stationary Hamilton-Jacobi equation:

(1.2)

H(x,\nabla v(x))=h,

at a fixed energy level
h\geq 0
. Our goal is to address existence and regularity questions concerning viscosity solutions associated with expansive solutions of the
N
-body problem, obtained via minimization of a suitably renormalized action functional with prescribed initial data.

Definition 1.1
.

A motion
\gamma:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\Omega
is said to be
expansive
if all mutual distances diverge, that is,
|r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)|\rightarrow+\infty
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
for all
i<j
. Equivalently,
\gamma
is expansive if
U(\gamma(t))\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

From energy conservation, it follows that expansive motions can only exist at nonnegative energy levels, since the condition
U(\gamma(t))\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
implies
\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\to 2h
. To classify expansive motions, we define the minimum and maximum separation between bodies along a motion
\gamma
at time
t
:

r(t)=\min_{i<j}|r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)|,\qquad R(t)=\max_{i<j}|r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)|.

The following classical results, dating back to the 1970s, describe the asymptotic behavior of expansive motions. For positive functions
f
and
g
, we write
f\approx g
if there exist constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
\alpha\leq\frac{f}{g}\leq\beta
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(i)

(Pollard, 1967
[
26
]
) Let
\gamma
be a motion defined for all
t>t_{0}
. If
r(t)
is bounded away from zero, then
R(t)=O(t)
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
. Moreover,
R(t)/t\rightarrow+\infty
if and only if
r(t)\rightarrow 0
.

(ii)

(Marchal-Saari, 1976
[
23
]
) Let
\gamma
be a motion defined for all
t>t_{0}
. Then either
R(t)/t\to+\infty
and
r(t)\to 0
, or there exists a configuration
a\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\gamma(t)=at+O(t^{2/3})
. In particular, for
superhyperbolic
motions – i.e., motions such that
\limsup_{t\to+\infty}R(t)/t=+\infty
– the quotient
R(t)/t
diverges.

(iii)

(Marchal-Saari, 1976
[
23
]
) Suppose
\gamma(t)=at+O(t^{2/3})
for some
a\in\mathcal{X}
, and that the motion is expansive. Then for each pair
i<j
with
a_{i}=a_{j}
, we have
|r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)|\approx t^{2/3}
.

As a consequence, expansive motions cannot be superhyperbolic, and therefore must satisfy
\gamma(t)=at+O(t^{2/3})
for some asymptotic velocity
a\in\mathcal{X}
. Chazy (
[
5

) provided a complete classification of expansive motions with zero barycenter, based on the asymptotic growth rate of mutual distances. They fall into the following categories:

(H)

Hyperbolic
:
a\in\Omega
, with
|r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)|\approx t
for all
i<j
;

(P)

Completely parabolic
:
a=0
, with
|r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)|\approx t^{2/3}
for all
i<j
;

(HP)

Hyperbolic-parabolic

a\in\Delta
,
a\neq 0

To further refine this classification, we introduce the notion of
limit shape
, where we consider the diagonal action
S(t)\gamma=(S(t)r_{1},\ldots,S(t)r_{N})
.

Definition 1.3
.

A motion
\gamma(t)
is said to have a
limit shape
if there exists a family of similarity transformations
S(t)
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
S(t)\gamma(t)\to a\neq 0
as
t\to+\infty
.

For (half) hyperbolic motions, the limit shape is simply the asymptotic velocity
a=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\gamma(t)}{t}
; for (half) parabolic motions, if a limit shape exists, it must be a central configuration.

Definition 1.4
.

A configuration
b\in\mathcal{X}
is called a
central configuration
if it is a critical point of
U
restricted to the inertia ellipsoid

\mathcal{E}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\langle\mathcal{M}x,x\rangle=1\right\}.

A central configuration
b_{m}\in\mathcal{E}
is said to be
minimal
if it minimizes
U
over
\mathcal{E}
:

U(b_{m})=\min_{b\in\mathcal{E}}U(b).

generalized and refined earlier results by Maderna and Venturelli

For the parabolic case, the result of

1.1.
The Variational Setting and the Renormalized Action Principle

We now outline the variational approach used in
[
25
]
to prove the existence of hyperbolic, parabolic, and hyperbolic-parabolic motions, relying on the optimization of a suitably renormalized action functional.

For the
N
-body problem, the Hamiltonian
H
is defined on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{dN}
by

(1.6)

H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}\|p\|_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}^{2}-U(x),

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}
denotes the dual norm with respect to the mass inner product. The Lagrangian is defined analogously by

(1.7)

L(x,v)=\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(x).

Given two configurations
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
T>0
, we define the set of admissible paths:

\mathcal{C}(x,y,T)=\left\{\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathcal{X}\,\middle|\,\gamma\text{ is absolutely continuous},\,\gamma(a)=x,\,\gamma(b)=y,\,b-a=T\right\},

and the free-time path space:

\mathcal{C}(x,y)=\bigcup_{T>0}\mathcal{C}(x,y,T).

The Lagrangian action of a curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y,T)
is defined as

\mathcal{A}_{L}(\gamma)=\int_{a}^{b}L(\gamma,\dot{\gamma})\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{a}^{b}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\gamma)\right)\mathrm{d}t.

According to ofHamilton’s of least action principle, if a curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y,T)
minimizes the Lagrangian action, then it satisfies Newton’s equations at every instant
t\in[a,b]
for which
\gamma(t)
is collision-free. However, due to the existence of curves with isolated collisions and finite action (see
[
24
]
), minimizing paths not always correspond to genuine solutions. Marchal’s Principle (Theorem
1.11
) addresses this issue by ensuring that action-minimizing curves are free of collisions in the interior of the interval, thereby enabling the use of variational methods.

The idea of proving Marchal’s Principle via averaged variations originates from Marchal
[
22
]
, with more complete arguments provided by Chenciner
[
6
]
, and Ferrario and Terracini
[
13
]
.

Theorem 1.11

(Marchal
[
22
]
, Chenciner
[
6
]
, Ferrario and Terracini
[
13
]
)
.

Assume
d\geq 2
, let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, and let
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y)
be defined on some interval
[a,b]
. If

\mathcal{A}_{L}(\gamma)=\min\left\{\mathcal{A}_{L}(\sigma)\ \middle|\ \sigma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y,b-a)\right\},

then
\gamma(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in(a,b)
.

To state the Renormalized Action Principle, we introduce the following notion:

Definition 1.12
.

A curve
\gamma:I\to\mathcal{X}
is a
free-time minimizer
for the Lagrangian action at energy
h
if, for all intervals
[a,b],[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\subset I
and all curves
\sigma:[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\to\mathcal{X}
with
\gamma(a)=\sigma(a^{\prime})
and
\gamma(b)=\sigma(b^{\prime})
, we have:

\int_{a}^{b}L(\gamma,\dot{\gamma})\,\mathrm{d}t+h(b-a)\leq\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}L(\sigma,\dot{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t+h(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime}).

The main strategy is to construct solutions to the
N
-body problem of the form:

\gamma(t)=r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t)+x-r_{0}(1),

where
r_{0}
is a fixed reference path (linear in the hyperbolic case, parabolic self-similar in the parabolic case, or a hybrid in the hyperbolic-parabolic case),
\varphi
is a perturbation in a suitable function space, and
x
is the initial configuration. Specifically, we work with the space:

\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)=\left\{\varphi\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([1,+\infty),\mathcal{X}):\varphi(1)=0,\ \int_{1}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t<+\infty\right\},

endowed with the norm:

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}}=\left(\int_{1}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}.

This is the space in which the minimization of the renormalized Lagrangian action takes place. Let us recall two fundamental inequalities in the space
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
.

Proposition 1.13

(Cfr.
[
1
]
)
.

The space
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
is a Hilbert space, and
C_{c}^{\infty}(1,+\infty)
is dense in it.

Proposition 1.14

(
Hardy inequality
, Cf.
[
1
]
)
.

For every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, the following inequalities hold:

\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 4\int_{1}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(1.8)

\sup_{t\in[1,+\infty)}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t-1}\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

In order to cope with the nonintegrability of the Lagrangian of an expansive motion on the half-line
[1,+\infty)
, we introduce the following renormalization:

Definition 1.15

(Renormalized Lagrangian Action)
.

Given an initial configuration
x\in\mathcal{X}
and a reference path
r_{0}
, we define the
Renormalized Lagrangian Action
as the functional
\mathcal{A}_{x}:\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
given by:

\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\varphi(t)+r_{0}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\mathcal{M}\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

By applying the direct method in the calculus of variations to this functional, one obtains the existence of minimizers. The conclusion then follows from the following result, which relies on Marchal’s Theorem on the absence of collisions for minimal trajectories:

Theorem 1.16

(Renormalized Action Principle, Polimeni and Terracini, 2024
[
25
]
)
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
be an initial configuration, and let
r_{0}
be a reference path. If
\varphi^{\min}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
is a minimizer of the renormalized Lagrangian action, then the corresponding expansive motion

\gamma(t)=r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{\min}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)

is a free-time minimizer of the Lagrangian action and, in particular, solves Newton’s equations (
1.1
) for all
t\in(1,+\infty)
(or for all
t\in[1,+\infty)
if
x\in\Omega
).

One advantage of this variational framework is that it allows one to define a value function directly dependent on the initial configuration. As noted in
[
25
]
with a heuristic argument, a linear correction of this value function provides a viscosity solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation. In this paper, we rigorously prove this assertion and investigate the size and structure of the set of singular points of the value function.

1.2.
Viscosity solutions of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

Given the Hamiltonian (
1.6
), we study the supercritical Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(1.9)

H(x,\nabla v(x))=h,\quad x\in\Omega,

which, in the context of the
N
-body problem, takes the form

H(x,\nabla v(x))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2m_{i}}\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial r_{i}}\right|^{2}-\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|r_{i}-r_{j}|}=h.

In general, classical solutions of (
1.9
) may fail to exist due to the lack of compactness and regularity, or the development of singularities, especially in the presence of singular potentials such as in the
N
-body problem (cfr
[
9
,
18
]
). For this reason, it is natural to consider a weaker notion of solution – namely, that of viscosity solutions – which allows for meaningful interpretation of solutions even when the function
v
is not differentiable.

We now recall the definition of viscosity solutions used in this paper.

Definition 1.17
.

For a given
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define the sets

\begin{split}&D^{-}v(x)=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\liminf_{y\rightarrow x}\frac{v(y)-v(x)-\langle p,y-x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{\|y-x\|_{\mathcal{M}}}\geq 0\right\},\\
&D^{+}v(x)=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\limsup_{y\rightarrow x}\frac{v(y)-v(x)-\langle p,y-x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{\|y-x\|_{\mathcal{M}}}\leq 0\right\},\end{split}

which are referred to as the Fréchet superdifferential and subdifferential of
v
at
x
, respectively.

A function
v\in C(\mathcal{X})
is called a
viscosity supersolution
of (
1.9
) if, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

H(x,p)\geq h,\quad\forall p\in D^{-}v(x).

Similarly,
v
is a
viscosity subsolution
of (
1.9
) if, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

H(x,p)\leq h,\quad\forall p\in D^{+}v(x).

A function
v
is a
viscosity solution
of (
1.9
) if it is both a viscosity supersolution and a viscosity subsolution.

1.3.
Regularity of the value function in the finite horizon problem

Although in this paper we will mainly deal with stationary solutions of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, it is worth mentioning a few salient facts concerning the time-dependent case in a finite horizon setting. In optimal control and Hamilton-Jacobi theory, the value function plays a central role as it encodes the minimal cost to reach a given state. Understanding its regularity properties is crucial for both theoretical and numerical purposes. In particular, non-smoothness of the value function often corresponds to the presence of multiple minimizers or singularities in the dynamics. To address this, we rely on the foundational results of Cannarsa and Sinestrari.

In
[
4
]
, within a more general framework, Cannarsa and Sinestrari established several fundamental results on the regularity of viscosity solutions to Hamilton-Jacobi equations. Fix
t>0
, and let
\mathrm{AC}([0,t],\,\Omega)
denote the set of absolutely continuous arcs
\xi:[0,t]\to\Omega
. For a fixed
x\in\Omega
, define the functional

(1.10)

\mathcal{J}_{t}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}L(\xi(s),\dot{\xi}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+u_{0}(\xi(0)),\quad\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{t;x},

where

\mathcal{B}_{t;x}=\{\xi\in\mathrm{AC}([0,t],\Omega):\,\xi(t)=x\}.

The associated value function is given by

u(t,x)=\min_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{t;x}}\mathcal{J}_{t}(\xi).

In
[
4
, Theorem 6.4.3]
, it is proved that if
u_{0}
is continuous on
\Omega
, then
u(t,\cdot)
is locally semiconcave with linear modulus on
\Omega
for every
t>0
. Moreover, given
T>0
, it is shown in
[
4
, Theorem 6.4.5]
that
u
is a viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)+H(x,\nabla u(t,x))=0,&\text{in }[0,T]\times\Omega,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),&\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Remark 1.18
.

In
[
4
]
, the final endpoint of the trajectory is fixed. In contrast, in the present paper, we consider a reversed-time formulation, so that the configuration is fixed at the initial time. This reversal corresponds to the study of the backward Hamilton-Jacobi equation. Although the variational structure remains the same, the time orientation affects the interpretation of minimizers and the evolution of singularities.

Remark 1.19
.

Another important difference lies in the structure of the Lagrangian. In our setting, we consider the classical
N
-body problem, where the Lagrangian is of the form

L(x,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}m_{i}|v_{i}|^{2}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|r_{i}-r_{j}|},

which is quadratic in the velocities but includes singular potentials due to mutual interactions. While
[
4
]
allows for general smooth Lagrangians, we work within a singular setting where collision configurations may occur. However, in the domain
\Omega\subset\mathcal{X}
we exclude collisions, so that
L
remains smooth and the results from
[
4
]
can be adapted.

Cannarsa and Sinestrari aimed to investigate the singularities of the value function, which correspond to points where the minimizing arc of the associated variational problem is not unique. Furthermore, they showed that the value function inherits the same regularity as the data of the problem outside the closure of its singular set.

We now recall some relevant definitions. In what follows, for a point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
\xi(\cdot,z)
the characteristic curve
\xi
of the Hamilton-Jacobi problem with
z
as its starting configuration.

Definition 1.20
.

A point
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
regular
if there exists a unique minimizer of (
1.10
). All other points are called
irregular
.

Definition 1.21
.

A point
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
conjugate
if there exists
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\xi(t,z)=x
, the arc
\xi(\cdot,z)
is a minimizer of (
1.10
), and
\det(\xi_{z}(t,z))=0
.

We denote by
\Sigma
the set of irregular points and by
\Gamma
the set of conjugate points.

Cannarsa and Sinestrari provided a detailed description of the topological and measure-theoretic properties of the singular set
\Sigma
. In particular, they showed that its closure
\bar{\Sigma}
is also countably
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-rectifiable, meaning it has the same rectifiability as
\Sigma
. Additionally, they obtained the sharper estimate

\mathcal{H}^{n-1+\frac{2}{k-1}}(\Gamma\setminus\Sigma)=0,

where
k\geq 3
denotes the differentiability class of the data. This implies that the value function is as smooth as the problem data outside a closed rectifiable set of codimension one.

We now state their main result, adapted to our setting.

Theorem 1.22

(Cannarsa and Sinestrari, 2004
[
4
]
)
.

Let
n>1
, and suppose that
L\in C^{R+1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a positive Lagrangian, quadratic in the velocities, as in (
1.7
), and that
u_{0}\in C^{R+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
R\geq 1
. Then the set of conjugate points
\Gamma
is countably
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-rectifiable, and

\mathcal{H}^{n-1+\frac{2}{R}}(\Gamma\setminus\Sigma)=0.

In particular, if
L\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then the Hausdorff dimension satisfies
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma\setminus\Sigma)\leq n-1
.

Their proofs rely on a version of Sard’s theorem, stated as follows (
[
10
, Theorem 3.4.3]
).

Theorem 1.23
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
be a
C^{R}
map for some
R\geq 1
. For each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
, define

A_{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ |\ \mathrm{rk}(DF(x))\leq k\},

where
\mathrm{rk}(DF)
denotes the rank of the Jacobian matrix
DF
. Then

\mathcal{H}^{k+\frac{N-k}{R}}(F(A_{k}))=0.

In our setting, we aim to apply the same Sard-type theorem, carefully accounting for the differences between our problem and that considered by Cannarsa and Sinestrari.

1.4.
Busemann Functions, Horofunctions, and the Gromov Boundary in Hamilton-Jacobi Theory

As already highlighted in
[
20
]
, hyperbolic solutions fit naturally into the framework of the Gromov boundary and other compactifications of of the configuration space of the
N
-body problem, and for this reason have attracted a growing attention in the recent literature (see e.g.
[
3
,
8
,
11
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
25
]
and references therein). In particular, consider the noncollision configuration space
\Omega
endowed with the Jacobi-Maupertuis’ metric associated with the
N
-body problem (a position-dependent rescaling of the Euclidean metric). For a given energy level
h>0
, this metric
j_{h}
can be written in coordinates as n

g_{ij}(x)\;=\;2\,\big(h+U(x)\big)\,\delta_{ij}\,,

which is proportional to the standard Euclidean (mass-weighted) metric
g_{m}
. Free-time action minimizers are then precisely the minimal geodesics of
j_{h}
, and in particular our expanding solutions correspond to geodesic rays (i.e. geodesics defined for all
t\geq 0
). Each such geodesic ray can be thought of as converging to a point at infinity, and the collection of all these
endpoints at infinity
is the
ideal boundary
(or
Gromov boundary
) of
(\Omega,j_{h})
. To describe these boundary points more concretely, we recall the notion of
horofunction
, which is a convenient tool in metric geometry for capturing directions at infinity.

A
horofunction
is a type of function that arises in the study of the asymptotic geometry of metric spaces. It encodes how distances grow at infinity and plays a key role in defining boundaries at infinity (such as the Gromov boundary). More precisely, let
(X,d)
be a proper metric space (meaning all closed balls are compact), and fix a base point
x_{0}\in X
. For each point
x\in X
, consider the function

f_{x}(y)\;:=\;d(y,x)\;-\;d(x_{0},x)\,.

This
f_{x}(y)
measures the distance from
y
to
x
relative to the base point
x_{0}
(note that
f_{x}(x_{0})=0
by definition). As
x

goes to infinity
in
X
(i.e.
x
diverges without bound), the functions
f_{x}
may sub-converge (locally uniformly) to a limiting function. Any such limit

b(y)\;=\;\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{x_{n}}(y)

(for some sequence
x_{n}\to\infty
in
X
) is by definition a horofunction on
X
. The space of all horofunction limits (modulo additive constants) is called the
horofunction boundary
of
X
. In many cases, these horofunction boundaries coincide with the more abstract Gromov boundaries defined via geodesic rays. Intuitively, a horofunction
b
behaves like an
affine distance
from a point at infinity, and its sublevel sets are analogous to horospheres (level sets of distance from infinity).

Horofunctions in Hamilton-Jacobi theory.

In the context of the Jacobi-Maupertuis’ metric
j_{h}=2(h+U(x))\,g_{m}
on the configuration space
\Omega\subset E^{N}
(with
g_{m}
the mass inner product), horofunctions arise naturally as limits of rescaled action functionals. Let
\phi_{h}(x,y)
denote the action potential (i.e. the Jacobi-Maupertuis’ distance) between two configurations
x,y\in\Omega
at energy
h>0
. Now fix a particular
hyperbolic direction
given by a noncollision configuration
a\in\Omega
(for example,
a
could represent the normalized velocity-vector configuration of an hyperbolic solution). Consider a sequence of points
x_{n}\in\Omega
that tends to infinity in the direction of
a
, in the sense
x_{n}=\lambda_{n}\,a+o(\lambda_{n})
as
\lambda_{n}\to+\infty
. For each such
x_{n}
, define a function
b_{x_{n}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
by

b_{x_{n}}(y)\;:=\;\phi_{h}(y,\,x_{n})\;-\;\phi_{h}(x_{0},\,x_{n})\,.

Geometrically,
b_{x_{n}}(y)
is the extra action (or distance) required to go from
y
to
x_{n}
beyond that required from the reference point
x_{0}
to
x_{n}
. By construction
b_{x_{n}}(x_{0})=0
. If the sequence
(b_{x_{n}})_{n}
converges uniformly on compact sets, we obtain a horofunction
b_{a}
in the limit:

b_{a}(y)\;:=\;\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{x_{n}}(y)\,.

We call
b_{a}
the
horofunction directed by the configuration
a
. By standard arguments in calculus of variations and viscosity solutions, one can show that
b_{a}(x)
is a (weak) solution of the stationary Hamilton-Jacobi equation

H\!\big(x,\nabla b_{a}(x)\big)\;=\;h\,,

where
H(x,p)
is the Hamiltonian of the
N
-body system and
\nabla b_{a}(x)
denotes the gradient of
b_{a}
. In other words,
b_{a}
is a
canonical viscosity solution
of
H(x,\nabla S)=h
. Moreover, the minimizers of the action associated with
b_{a}
(sometimes called the
calibrating curves
of
b_{a}
) are precisely the expanding trajectories (hyperbolic motions) asymptotic to the configuration
a
. In the language of the Jacobi metric, those calibrating curves are geodesic rays of
(\Omega,j_{h})
that
point toward
the limiting direction
a\in\Omega
at infinity.

Geometric and dynamical meaning.

From a dynamical perspective,
in the framework of hyperbolic solutions
, horofunctions (and in particular the functions
b_{a}
defined above) have several important interpretations:

•

They provide canonical global viscosity solutions to the stationary Hamilton-Jacobi equation (selection principles often single out these specific solutions).

•

They encode the asymptotic behavior of action-minimizing trajectories or geodesic rays, effectively capturing the
direction
in which the orbit proceeds as
t\to+\infty
.

•

They coincide with the classical
Busemann functions
when one focuses on a particular geodesic ray. For instance, if
\gamma:[0,+\infty)\to\Omega
is a geodesic ray (e.g. the trajectory of an hyperbolic solution), one defines

b_{\gamma}(y)\;=\;\lim_{t\to+\infty}\Big(\phi_{h}\big(y,\gamma(t)\big)\;-\;\phi_{h}\big(x_{0},\gamma(t)\big)\Big)\,.

This
b_{\gamma}
is exactly the Busemann function associated with the ray
\gamma
, and it equals (up to an additive constant) the directed horofunction
b_{a}
where
a
is the asymptotic configuration (limit shape) of
\gamma(t)
as
t\to\infty
.

Thus, in the hyperbolic case, horofunctions can be viewed as describing
directions at infinity
in the configuration space, characterizing how the
N
-body system behaves asymptotically as time
t\to\infty
. In particular, each hyperbolic solution (geodesic ray) determines a unique horofunction (up to constant), and different asymptotic configurations
a
give rise to different points on the boundary at infinity of
(\Omega,j_{h})
.

It is worth noticing that the uniqueness result in
[
21
]
ensures that, in the hyperbolic case, our value function
v
, associated with the renormalized action minimal value, is a Busemann function. Moreover, it may be interesting that the linear correction in (
3.1
) is itself the Busemann function of the free particle.

Up to this point, our focus has been on the hyperbolic case. We now turn to the parabolic and mixed hyperbolic-parabolic settings, where the notions of horofunction and ideal boundary require further refinement. The parabolic case is particularly subtle, as the Jacobi–Maupertuis’ metric degenerates at infinity, and parabolic minimal rays converge only to central configurations. In this context, the ideal boundary must include all minimal central configurations, each represented by parabolic solutions of the form (
1.3
).

If the second term in the asymptotic expansion (
1.3
) were precisely of order
1/3
, then two distinct parabolic solutions would remain at bounded distance from each other at infinity. However, we are currently unable to establish this fact rigorously.

The mixed hyperbolic-parabolic case is even more complex. Here, the associated geodesic rays exhibit a two-term expansion at infinity as described in (
1.5
). Moreover, in the vicinity of such expansive solutions, the Jacobi-Maupertuis’ metric becomes equivalent to the Euclidean one. As a result, different choices of the minimal
a
-clustered central configuration
b_{m}
lead to diverging geodesic rays – although even the same choice of
b_{m}
may result in unbounded asymptotic behaviors. Therefore, any meaningful definition of the ideal boundary must be fine-tuned to distinguish among these cases.

Preliminary estimates

In this section, we establish some preliminary estimates that will be used later in the proof. We start by defining the space, for a given
T\in(1,+\infty]
,

\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)=\{\varphi\in H^{1,2}([1,T])\ :\ \varphi(1)=0,\ \int_{1}^{T}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t<+\infty\},

and the norm

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}=\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2},\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}([1,T]),\ \varphi(1)=0,

which satisfies

\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\sqrt{t},\quad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T).

The results of Proposition
1.14
can be extended as follows, showing that
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
is compactly embedded in the space
L^{2}(1,T)
equipped with proper weights.
To this end, for every
T\in(1,+\infty]
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we define
L^{2}(1,T;\mathrm{d}t/t^{2+\varepsilon})
as the space of functions
\varphi
such that

\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}t<+\infty.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
T\in(1,+\infty]
. Then, for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
and for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
, the following Hardy-type inequality holds

(2.1)

\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{4}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}\int_{1}^{T}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t,

that is, the space
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
is continuously embedded in the space
L^{2}(1,T;\mathrm{d}t/t^{2+\varepsilon})
. Besides,
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
is compactly embedded in the space
L^{2}(1,T;\mathrm{d}t/t^{2+\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

First, we prove that the embedding is continuous. For all fixed
T\in(1,+\infty)
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, this follows from

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t&=-\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{t^{1+\varepsilon}}\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\frac{\|\varphi(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{T^{1+\varepsilon}}+\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\langle\varphi(t),\dot{\varphi}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{1+\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\langle\varphi(t),\dot{\varphi}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{1+\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+2\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}\\
&\leq\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2},\end{split}

which gives the Hardy-type inequality (
2.1
). For
T=+\infty
, the thesis follows from the fact that the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(1,+\infty)
is dense in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
.

Now, let
\varepsilon>0
. For
T<+\infty
, the compactness follows from Rellich–Kondrachov Theorem. For
T=+\infty
, let
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
be a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, i.e. there exists
C>0
such that
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\leq C
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists a subsequence
(\varphi_{n_{k}})_{k}
converging pointwise to a function
\bar{\varphi}
on
[1,+\infty)
. Since, by (
1.8
),

\frac{\|\varphi_{n_{k}}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+\varepsilon}}\leq\frac{\|\varphi_{n_{k}}-\bar{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}}{t^{1+\varepsilon}}\leq\frac{C}{t^{1+\varepsilon}}\in L^{1}(1,+\infty),

we can use the Dominated Convergence Theorem to say that

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\varphi_{n_{k}}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2+\varepsilon}}\ \mathrm{d}t=0.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Clearly, for
T=+\infty
and
\varepsilon=0
, we obtain the same results of Proposition
1.14
.

2.1.
Uniform coercivity estimates

The aim of this subsection is to prove that the renormalized Lagrangian action satisfies uniform coercivity estimates with respect to
T
and
x
when the initial configuration belongs to a compact set. This will be crucial in the proof of the main theorems and will be used several times in the following arguments.

Notation 1
.

Fix
T>1
.
For a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
, we will write

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\varphi(t)+r_{0}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\mathcal{M}\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t.

To keep notations compact, in the case
T=+\infty
, with
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,+\infty]}
we mean the functional
\mathcal{A}_{x}
, defined on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Given a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
, fix
x\in\mathcal{K}
and
T\in(1,+\infty]
. Then, there exist positive constants
A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}
, which do not depend on
x
and
T
, such that for all
\varphi
belonging to a bounded subset of
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
, it holds

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)\geq A\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}-B\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+C.

Proof.

For fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
T=+\infty
, coercivity estimates have been proved in
[
25
]
.

Now, we fix
x\in\mathcal{K}
, with
\mathcal{K}
a compact subset of
\mathcal{X}
, and
T\in(1,+\infty]
. Let
\bar{k}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\|x\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\bar{k}
for all
x\in\mathcal{K}
. The goal is to prove that the renormalized Lagrangian action satisfies the coercivity estimates uniformly with respect to both
x
and
T
.

As usual, we study the three cases separately.

Hyperbolic case.

We write the action as

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)=\sum_{i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)_{ij},

where

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)_{ij}=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2M}|\dot{\varphi}_{ij}(t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+a_{ij}t+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}\,\mathrm{d}t,

M=\sum_{i=1,\dots,N}m_{i}
, and

\varphi_{ij}=\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{j},\ a_{ij}=a_{i}-a_{j},\ x_{ij}=x_{i}-x_{j}.

We then show uniform coercivity estimates of each term
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)_{ij}.

We have

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+a_{ij}t+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|+|a_{ij}|t+|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|t}\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

Then, by the convexity of
\frac{1}{t}
, we can use Taylor’s expansion and then Hardy inequality to obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|+|a_{ij}|t+|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|t}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\int_{1}^{T}-\frac{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|}{|a_{ij}|t^{2}}-\frac{|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}|t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\frac{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}-\int_{1}^{T}\frac{|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}|t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq-\frac{2}{|a_{ij}|}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\bigg(\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}-\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}|t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\frac{2}{|a_{ij}|}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+\frac{|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}|}.\end{split}

We have thus proved that

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)_{ij}\geq\frac{1}{2M}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}-\frac{2}{|a_{ij}|}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+\frac{|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}|},

from which the uniform coercivity with respect to
T
and
x
trivially follows.

Parabolic case.
In this case, it holds

\nabla U(r_{0})=\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})=-\frac{U_{min}}{\beta^{2}t^{4/3}}\mathcal{M}b_{m},

with
U_{min}=U(b_{m})
.

Following the same computations made in
[
25
, Section 4.1]
, we get

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)&=\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+\int_{1}^{T}U(\varphi(t)+\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\tilde{x})-U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})-\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}),\varphi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+U_{min}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\beta t^{2/3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}-\frac{1}{\beta t^{2/3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}+\frac{\langle\mathcal{M}b_{m},\varphi(t)\rangle}{\beta^{2}t^{4/3}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}-U_{min}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2\beta^{3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}t^{2}}+\frac{\langle\mathcal{M}\varphi(t),\tilde{x}\rangle}{\beta^{3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t-U_{min}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\langle\mathcal{M}b_{m},\tilde{x}\rangle\beta t^{2/3}+1/2\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{\beta^{3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

where we set
\tilde{x}=x-\beta b_{m}
.

The following inequalities hold:

•

-U_{min}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2\beta^{3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\geq-\frac{8}{18}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}.

•

\begin{split}-U_{min}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\langle\mathcal{M}\varphi(t),\tilde{x}\rangle}{\beta^{3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t&\geq-\frac{2}{9}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq-\frac{2}{9}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\frac{||\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{1/2}\\
&\geq-\frac{4}{9}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}.\end{split}

•

\begin{split}-U_{min}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\langle\mathcal{M}b_{m},\tilde{x}\rangle\beta t^{2/3}+1/2\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{\beta^{3}\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t&\geq-\frac{2}{9}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}\beta t^{2/3}+1/2\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq-\frac{2}{9}\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}\beta t^{2/3}+1/2\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{9}(2\beta-1).\end{split}

It follows

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)\geq\frac{1}{18}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}-\frac{4}{9}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{9}(2\beta-1),

and hence the proof for the parabolic case is completed.

Hyperbolic-Parabolic case.
As in
[
25
, Section 5]
, considering the cluster partition of the bodies determined by the equivalence relation (
1.4
), the renormalized Lagrangian action can be written as the sum of two terms as follows: for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
, we define

(2.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)&=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{A}_{K}(\varphi)+\sum_{K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{P},\ K_{1}\neq K_{2}}\mathcal{A}_{K_{1},K_{2}}(\varphi)\\
&=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}\bigg(\sum_{i,j\in K,\ i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\mathcal{A}_{K}^{ij}(\varphi)\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{P},\ K_{1}\neq K_{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i\in K_{1},\ j\in K_{2}}m_{i}m_{j}\mathcal{A}_{K_{1},K_{2}}^{ij}(\varphi)\bigg),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{K}^{ij}(\varphi)=&\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2M}|\dot{\varphi}_{ij}(t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+\beta_{K}b^{K}_{ij}t^{2/3}+\tilde{x}_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|\beta_{K}b^{K}_{ij}t^{2/3}|}\\
&+\frac{2}{9}\frac{\beta_{K}}{M}\frac{\langle b^{K}_{ij},\varphi_{ij}(t)\rangle}{t^{4/3}}\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{K_{1},K_{2}}^{ij}(\varphi)=&\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2M}|\dot{\varphi}_{ij}(t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+a_{ij}t+\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}t^{2/3}+\tilde{x}_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

where
\beta_{K}>0
,
b^{K}
is a minimal central configuration in the cluster
K
,
\beta_{K_{1,2}}b^{K_{1,2}}=(\beta_{K_{1}}b^{K_{1}},\beta_{K_{2}}b^{K_{2}})
with
b^{K_{i}}
minimal central configuration for the cluster
K_{i}
,
i=1,2
, and
\beta_{K_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
(see
[
25
, Section 5]
).

We start with the interaction term
\mathcal{A}_{K_{1},K_{2}}^{ij}
, which behaves like the renormalized Lagrangian action in the hyperbolic case, with the difference that here, in the denominator of the potential, there is also a term of order 2/3. By the triangular inequality, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{K_{1},K_{2}}^{ij}(\varphi)=&\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2M}|\dot{\varphi}_{ij}(t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+a_{ij}t+\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}t^{2/3}+\tilde{x}_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2M}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|+|a_{ij}|t+|\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}|t^{2/3}+|\tilde{x}_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|t}\ \mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

By the convexity of the function
\frac{1}{t}
, it holds

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|+|a_{ij}|t+|\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}|t^{2/3}+|\tilde{x}_{ij}|}\ \mathrm{d}t&\geq-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|^{2}}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{|\varphi_{ij}(t)|+|\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}|t^{2/3}+|\tilde{x}_{ij}|}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|^{2}}(3|\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}|+4\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+|\tilde{x}_{ij}|).\end{split}

So, it holds

\mathcal{A}_{K_{1},K_{2}}^{ij}(\varphi)>\frac{1}{2M}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}-\frac{4}{|a_{ij}|^{2}}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}+-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}|^{2}}(3|\beta_{K_{1,2}}b_{ij}^{K_{1,2}}|+|\tilde{x}_{ij}|).

To study the uniform coercivity of the term
\mathcal{A}_{K}^{ij}(\varphi)
, we notice that it has a similar expression to the renormalized action in the parabolic case. Thus, the same computations lead us to the conclusion

\mathcal{A}_{K}^{ij}(\varphi)\geq\frac{1}{18M_{K}}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}-\frac{4}{9}\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\|\tilde{x}_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{\|\tilde{x}_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{9}(2\beta_{K}-1).

This concludes the proof.

∎

2.2.
Distance of minimal solutions from collisions

As a consequence of the previous uniform coercivity estimates, we will prove that any expansive motion
\gamma(t)
with initial configuration in
\Omega
, given by the Renormalized Action Principle Theorem
1.16
, is far from collisions for all
t\in[1,+\infty)
, locally uniformly with respect to
x
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
\bar{x}\in\Omega
, there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}(\bar{x})
such that
\mathcal{U}(\bar{x})\cap\Delta=\emptyset
and there is a real constant
C>0
such that, for any
x\in\mathcal{U}(\bar{x})
, the motions
\gamma(t)
given by Theorems
1.5
,
1.6
and
1.7
with initial configuration
x
satisfy

(2.3)

d(\gamma(t),\Delta)\geq C\quad\forall t\in[1,+\infty),\ \text{uniformly with respect to }x.

Proof.

By Marchal’s Principle, we already know that the minimal curve
\gamma(t)=r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{\bar{x}}(t)+\bar{x}+r_{0}(1)
is free of collisions for
t\in[1,+\infty)
.

From uniform coercivity estimates Lemma
2.3
it follows that for any constant
R>0
there is a constant
C_{R}>0
such that for all
x\in B_{R}(0)
,
\|\varphi^{x}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\leq C_{R}
. Thus, we know that for any
R>0
there is
C_{R}>0
such that

\begin{split}|\gamma_{i}(t)-\gamma_{j}(t)|&\geq|(a_{i}-a_{j})t+(\beta b_{i}-\beta b_{j})t^{2/3}|-|\varphi_{i}^{x}(t)-\varphi_{j}^{x}(t)|-|\tilde{x}_{i}-\tilde{x}_{j}|\\
&\geq C^{\prime}t^{2/3}-C_{R}t^{1/2}-C^{\prime\prime}\\
&\geq 1\end{split}

for some
t\geq\tau=\tau(R)\geq 1
and for proper
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}>0
.

The fact that
|\gamma_{i}(t)-\gamma_{j}(t)|\geq 1
for all
i<j
and for
t\in[\tau,+\infty)
implies that there is a constant
C>1
such that
d(\gamma(t),\Delta)\geq C
. Indeed, the collision set
\Delta
can be represented as a union of hyperplanes:

\Delta=\bigcup_{i<j}\Delta_{ij},

with
\Delta_{ij}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:x_{i}=x_{j}\}
. It is easy to see that
d(\gamma(t),\Delta)=\min_{i<j}d(\gamma(t),\Delta_{ij})
.

Now, we want to prove that the inequality (
2.3
) holds also for
t\in[1,\tau]
. Suppose by contradiction that there is a sequence
({\bar{x}}_{n})_{n}\subset\mathcal{U}(\bar{x})
such that
d(\gamma_{n}(t),\Delta)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
for
t\in[1,\tau]
, where, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the curve
\gamma_{n}(t)
is minimal for the value function with initial configuration
{\bar{x}}_{n}
. By Ascoli-Arezelà’s Theorem, it can be proved that there is a subsequence
(\gamma_{n_{k}}(t))_{k}
converging uniformly over the compact subsets
[1,\tau]
. This implies that the subsequence converges also pointwise over compact sets.

Let
\gamma_{n_{k}}(t)\rightarrow\bar{\gamma}(t)\in\Delta
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
for
t\in[1,\tau]
. Then
\bar{\gamma}(1)\in\Delta
. Since
({\bar{x}}_{n})_{n}\subset\mathcal{U}(\bar{x})
, if we take the limit we obtain
\bar{\gamma}(1)\in\overline{\mathcal{U}(\bar{x})}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

3.
Hamilton-Jacobi equations

The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix
x\in\Omega
. The renormalized value function

(3.1)

v(x)=\min_{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)-\langle a,x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}

is a viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(3.2)

\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla v(x)\|_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}^{2}-U(x)=\frac{\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2}.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is in Section
3.3
, and needs a number of preliminary results, contained in this and the next subsections. Among these results, it is worth stressing out the following proposition, regarding the uniform convergence over compact sets of
v(T,x)
(defined through the functionals
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}
) to
v(x)
, as
T\rightarrow+\infty
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
x\in\Omega
and
T>1
, let
v(x)
be defined as in Theorem
3.1
, and let
v(T,x)
be defined as

(3.3)

v(T,x):=\min_{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

Then,
v(T,x)
and
v(x)
are continuous in
\Omega
, and, if
(T_{n})_{n}
is a sequence such that
T_{n}\to+\infty
, then

\lim_{n\to+\infty}v(T_{n},x)=v(x)\ \mbox{ uniformly on compact subsets of }\ \Omega.

The proof of Proposition
3.2
is a direct corollary of Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
.

3.1.
Finite horizon approximation

Define the functions
w:[1,+\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
w_{\infty}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
as the following value functions

(3.4)

w(T,x)=\min_{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi),\ \mbox{ and }\ w_{\infty}(x)=\min_{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi).

Given
T>1
, we say that
w(T,\cdot)
is a
finite-horizon
value function associated with
T
. Our aim, in this section, is to prove properties of these finite-horizon value functions, as well as their uniform convergence to
w_{\infty}
on compacts subsets of
\Omega
, as long as
T\to+\infty
.

Lemma 3.3

(Structure lemma)
.

For every
T\in(1,+\infty]
, we have

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)=Q_{T}(\varphi,\varphi)+P_{x,T}(\varphi),\ \mbox{ for }\ \varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T),

where

•

Q_{T}
is a positive definite quadratic form on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
;

•

P_{x,T}
is a functional on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
such that there exists
V:\Omega\times(1,+\infty)\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
for which

P_{x,T}(\varphi)=\int_{1}^{T}V(x,t,\varphi(t))\ \mathrm{d}t.

Moreover, for every compact subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\Omega
and
\hat{M}>0
, there exists
\hat{T}>1
,
C>0
and
\beta>1
such that

(3.5)

\left|V(x,t,\varphi(t))\right|\leq Ct^{-\beta}\ \mbox{ if }\ t\geq\hat{T},

for every
x\in\mathcal{K}
and
\varphi
such that
\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\hat{M}\sqrt{t}
.

Proof.

Hyperbolic case.
Since
\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}=\big(\int_{1}^{T}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\big)^{1/2}
is a norm, it is trivial that it is a definite positive quadratic form.

We can write down the second part of the action as

(3.6)

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)-\frac{\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}}{2}=\int_{1}^{T}\sum_{i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\bigg(\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t+\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}\bigg)\ \mathrm{d}t.

Since for all
s\in(0,1)

\begin{split}|a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})|&\geq|a_{ij}|t-s(\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\sqrt{t}+|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|)\\
&>|a_{ij}|t-(\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\sqrt{t}+|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|),\end{split}

for
x\in\mathcal{K}
, and
a\neq 0
, there is a
T_{1}=T_{1}(\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}},\mathcal{K})
such that, for all
t\geq T_{1}
,

(3.7)

|a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})|>|a_{ij}|t-(|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|+\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}\sqrt{t})>0.

Hence, for
t
large enough, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, it holds

\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t+\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\bigg[\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})|}\bigg]\ \mathrm{d}s.

By passing to the absolute value, we get

\begin{split}\Bigg|\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t+\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}-\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t|}\Bigg|&=\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}-\frac{[a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})](\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})}{|a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})|^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}s\Bigg|\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Using the inequality

\frac{|b+c|^{2}}{|b|^{2}-|c|^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{3},\qquad\text{for each }b,c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ such that }|b|\geq 2|c|,

which can easily be proved by elementary calculus, and knowing that there is a constant
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
large enough such that
|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|+\hat{M}\sqrt{t}\leq k^{\prime}\sqrt{t}
, we thus have

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}t+s(\varphi_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij})|^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}s&\leq\int_{0}^{1}3\frac{\hat{M}\sqrt{t}+|x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}{|a_{ij}t|^{2}-s|M\sqrt{t}+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{3k^{\prime}\sqrt{t}}{|a_{ij}|^{2}t^{2}-sk^{\prime}t}\ \mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

where the last term is dominated, at infinity, by a term
\frac{k^{\prime\prime}}{t^{\beta}}
with
k^{\prime\prime}
not depending on
T
and
x
, and
\beta>1
. This proves the statement in the hyperbolic case, with
V(x,t,\varphi)
given by the integrand function of the right-hand side of (
3.6
).

Parabolic case.
In the parabolic setting, we add and subtract the term
\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t
to the action, obtaining

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}(\varphi)=\int_{1}^{T}&\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\\
&+U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t)+\tilde{x})-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)),\varphi(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

We prove the statement taking into account

Q_{T}(\varphi,\varphi)=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,

and

P_{x,T}(\varphi)=\int_{1}^{T}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t)+\tilde{x})-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)),\varphi(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t.

First, exactly as observed for the case
T=+\infty
in
[
25
, Section 4.2]
, one can see that
\varphi\to Q_{T}(\varphi,\varphi)
is a norm on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}
. Indeed, as observed in
[
25
, Section 4.2]
, there exists
C^{\prime}
such that the following chain of inequalities is satisfied pointwise

-\frac{2}{9}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\leq\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}.

Then, by applying Hardy inequality on
[1,T]
, we get

C^{\prime\prime}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}^{2}\leq Q_{T}(\varphi,\varphi)\leq\frac{1}{18}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{T}},

for some positive
C^{\prime\prime}>0
.

Second, we prove the bound (
3.5
) for large values of time as follows. If
t
is large enough to satisfy (
3.7
), we have

\begin{split}&U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t)+\tilde{x})-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)),\varphi(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t)),\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{3}U(r_{0}(t)+\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\tau_{3}(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}))(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}),\varphi(t)+\tilde{x},\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}\rangle\tau_{1}\tau_{2}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\ \mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau_{3}\ \mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

Since
\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\hat{M}\sqrt{t}
, there exists
\tilde{t}>1
big enough such that, for all
t\geq\tilde{t}
,

\|r_{0}(t)+\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\tau_{3}(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x})\|_{\mathcal{M}}>0.

This implies that for all
t\geq\tilde{t}
it holds, from
[
25
, pag. 25]
,

\bigg|\langle\nabla^{3}U(r_{0}(t)+\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\tau_{3}(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}))(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}),\varphi(t)+\tilde{x},\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}\rangle\bigg|\leq\hat{C}\frac{\|\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}}{t^{8/3}}\leq\frac{\hat{C}^{\prime}(\hat{M},\mathcal{K})}{t^{7/6}},

where the constants
\hat{C},\hat{C}^{\prime}
do not depend on
T
and
x\in\mathcal{K}
.

Hyperbolic-parabolic case.

In the hyperbolic-parabolic setting, the renormalized Lagrangian action is decomposed as in (
2.2
) and the lemma is proved using the same arguments from the previous two settings.

∎

Lemma
3.3
is instrumental in proving the continuity of the value functions in (
3.4
).

Lemma 3.4
.

For every
T>1
,
w(T,\cdot)
is continuous in
\Omega
, and
w_{\infty}
is continuous in
\Omega
.

Proof.

We prove the statement for
w_{\infty}
. The result for the finite-horizon value functions
w(T,\cdot)
follows a fortiori.

Upper semicontinuity.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a sequence converging to
x
. Using the trivial fact that
\mathcal{A}_{x}
is continuous with respect to
x
, it holds

\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)=\limsup_{x_{n}\rightarrow x}\mathcal{A}_{x_{n}}(\varphi)\geq\limsup_{x_{n}\rightarrow x}w(x_{n})

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
. This plainly gives

w(x)\geq\limsup_{x_{n}\rightarrow x}w(x_{n}).

Lower semicontinuity.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a sequence converging to
x
. Consider any sequence
(\varphi^{x_{n}})_{n}\subset\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
of minimizers of
\mathcal{A}_{x_{n}}
. By the uniform coercivity estimates of Lemma
2.3
, we know that the sequence
(\varphi^{x_{n}})_{n}
is uniformly bounded. Besides, up to a subsequence, it converges uniformly on compact subsets of
[1,+\infty)
and weakly in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
to a function
\bar{\varphi}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
.

We decompose the action
\mathcal{A}_{x}
as in Lemma
3.3
. Being a quadratic form, we already know that
\varphi\mapsto Q_{\infty}(\varphi,\varphi)
is weakly lower semi-continuous on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
.

By previous arguments, we know that there exists
\bar{T}>1
such that on the interval
[\bar{T},\infty)
the integrand in
P_{x,\infty}(\varphi)
, here just
P_{x}(\varphi)
, is dominated by an
L^{1}
-function.

After dividing
P_{x_{n}}(\varphi^{x_{n}})
in two terms

P_{x_{n}}(\varphi^{x_{n}})=\int_{1}^{\bar{T}}V(x_{n},t,\varphi^{x_{n}}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\bar{T}}^{+\infty}V(x_{n},t,\varphi^{x_{n}}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,

we observe what follows. On the interval
[1,\bar{T}]
, directly by the fact that
\varphi^{x_{n}}
converges uniformly on
\bar{\varphi}
, and, thanks to (
3.5
), on the interval
[\bar{T},+\infty)
by the dominated convergence Theorem, we obtain

P_{x}(\bar{\varphi})=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}P_{x_{n}}(\varphi^{x_{n}}).

Putting this together with the lower semicontinuity of
Q
, we obtain

\begin{split}w_{\infty}(x)&\leq\mathcal{A}_{x}(\bar{\varphi})=Q(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\varphi})+P_{x}(\bar{\varphi})\\
&\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}Q(\varphi^{n},\varphi^{n})+\liminf_{n\to+\infty}P_{x_{n}}(\varphi^{n})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{A}_{x_{n}}(\varphi^{x_{n}})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}w_{\infty}(x_{n}).\end{split}

∎

Now, we prove the convergence of
w(T,x)
to
w_{\infty}(x)
.

Lemma 3.5

(Finite-horizon approximation)
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n}\subset(1,+\infty)
be a sequence of finite times such that
T_{n}\to+\infty
, as
n\to+\infty
.

Then,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}w(T_{n},x)=w_{\infty}(x)\ \mbox{ uniformly on compact subsets of $\Omega$}.

Proof.

Consider
\mathcal{K}
a fixed compact subset of
\mathcal{X}
. For every
x\in\mathcal{K}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, by the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}
, there exists
\varphi^{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T_{n})
such that

w(T_{n},x)=\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\varphi^{n}).

Let
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
be the following extension of
\varphi^{n}
to
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
:

\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)=\begin{cases}\varphi^{n}(t),\ &\mbox{ in }\ [1,T_{n}]\\
\varphi^{n}(2T_{n}-t),\ &\mbox{ in }\ (T_{n},2T_{n}-1]\\
0,\ &\mbox{ in }\ (2T_{n}-1,+\infty).\end{cases}

Clearly,
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\varphi^{n})
. Since
\|\tilde{\varphi}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{D}}=2\|\varphi^{n}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T_{n}}}
, and the uniform coercivity of the family of functionals
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}
, (see Section
2.1
), we deduce that
(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})_{n}
is a bounded sequence on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
. This implies that there exists
\bar{\varphi}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
such that
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}\to\bar{\varphi}
, weakly on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, uniformly on compact subsets of
(1,+\infty)
, and pointwise on
(1,+\infty)
.

We want to prove that

\mathcal{A}_{x}(\bar{\varphi})=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\varphi^{n}),

uniformly on compact sets with respect to
x
.

Hyperbolic case.
Like we did for the study of the coercivity for the hyperbolic case, we write the renormalized Lagrangian action as

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\sum_{i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{2M}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t),\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle+U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a)-U_{ij}(at)\ \mathrm{d}t,

where

U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a)=\frac{1}{|a_{ij}t+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|}.

Since
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
is a critical point of the action, it holds

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})[\psi]=0,\ \mbox{ for all }\ \psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T_{n}),

that is, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T_{n})
,

\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{M}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t),\dot{\psi}_{ij}(t)\rangle+\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a),\psi_{ij}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t=0.

Denoting
\bar{\varphi}^{n}=\bar{\varphi}\big|_{[1,T_{n}]}
, the above equation holds, in particular, for
\psi(t)=\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)
. Then

\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{M}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t),\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\dot{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle+\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t=0.

Starting with the potential term, it holds

\begin{split}\left|\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle\right|\leq C\frac{|\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)|}{|a_{ij}t+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{t^{1/2}}{|a_{ij}t+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}|^{2}},\end{split}

where the constants
C,C^{\prime}
depend only on the norms
\|\varphi_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}
, which are bounded, and, for
t\rightarrow+\infty
, the last term is dominated by the function
\frac{C^{\prime}}{t^{3/2}}\in L^{1}(1,+\infty)
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rightarrow 0
for
t\in(1,+\infty)
, it holds, by the dominated convergence Theorem, that

\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow 0,

and, in particular,

(3.8)

\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-a),\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow 0.

Since by the weak convergence of
(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t))_{n}
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
it holds

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t),\dot{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t=\int_{1}^{+\infty}|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}(t)|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t,

we can conclude that

(3.9)

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}|\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}(t)|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t=\int_{1}^{+\infty}|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}(t)|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t.

From (
3.8
) and (
3.9
),
since

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})&=\sum_{i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{2M}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t),\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle+U_{ij}(at+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a)-U_{ij}(at)\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\sum_{i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{2M}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t),\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}^{n}(t)\rangle+\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+s(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-a)),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{ij}(t)+x_{ij}-a_{ij}\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

we deduce

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\mathcal{A}_{x}(\bar{\varphi})

pointwise.

We can also state that the convergence is uniform on compact sets with respect to
x
. Indeed, writing

|\mathcal{A}_{x}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})-\mathcal{A}_{x}(\bar{\varphi})|=\bigg|\sum_{i<j}m_{i}m_{j}\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-a+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\theta\ \mathrm{d}t\bigg|,

we know that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there are
T_{\varepsilon}>1
and
N_{\varepsilon}
such that, for any
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
,

\int_{T_{\varepsilon}}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-a+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\theta\ \mathrm{d}t<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

This follows from the fact that, for
t\rightarrow+\infty
, there is a constant
C^{\prime\prime}
that does not depend on
x
such that

|\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-a+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}(t)\rangle|\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{t^{3/2}}.

In addition,
the fact that, for any
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
,

\int_{1}^{T_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U_{ij}(at+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-a+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)),\tilde{\varphi}_{ij}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}_{ij}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\theta\ \mathrm{d}t<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

follows from the continuity of
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
and
\bar{\varphi}
with respect to
x
, from the uniform convergence of
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
to
\bar{\varphi}
, and the fact that, if
n
is sufficiently large,
|at+\bar{\varphi}+x-a+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})|\geq\delta/2
, for some
\delta>0
.

Parabolic case.
In the parabolic case, we consider the renormalized Lagrangian action, as in Lemma
3.3

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\int_{1}^{T_{n}}&\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle+U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m})-U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\\
&-\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=Q_{T_{n}}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n},\tilde{\varphi}^{n})+P_{x,T_{n}}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}).\end{split}

Like in the previous case, the differential of the action is zero at
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
. It holds, in particular,

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}&\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t),\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t)-\dot{\bar{\varphi}}^{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\\
&+\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\\
&-\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t=0.\end{split}

Regarding the
potential
part, we get

\begin{split}&\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle+\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}))(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m})-\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\theta,\end{split}

and, if we do not consider the term
\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}))(x-\beta b_{m}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle
,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}&\langle(\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}))-\nabla^{2}(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}))\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{3}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\sigma\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}))(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x+\beta b_{m}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\theta\ \mathrm{d}\sigma\ \mathrm{d}\theta.\end{split}

We have

\begin{split}&\langle\nabla^{3}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\sigma\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m}))\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\theta\\
&\leq C\frac{\|\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\|\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\|\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{\|\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\sigma\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{4}}\\
&\leq C^{\prime}\frac{t^{3/2}}{\|\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\sigma\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{4}},\end{split}

where the constants
C,C^{\prime}
depend only on
\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}
or
\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{D}}
, which are bounded, and, for
t\rightarrow+\infty
, the last term is dominated by
\frac{C^{\prime}}{t^{7/6}}\in L^{1}(1,+\infty)
. Then, by the dominated convergence Theorem,

\begin{split}&\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m})-\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})-\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\rightarrow 0\quad\text{in }L^{1}.\end{split}

Arguing as in the hyperbolic case, we are able to prove that

\lim_{n}P_{x,T_{n}}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=P(\bar{\varphi}).

Now we study the remaining term. We notice that

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}&\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t),\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t)-\dot{\bar{\varphi}}^{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\|\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t),\dot{\bar{\varphi}}^{n}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

By the dominated convergence Theorem and the fact that
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}\rightharpoonup\bar{\varphi}
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, we have that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\langle\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t),\dot{\bar{\varphi}}^{n}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\bar{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\bar{\varphi}(t),\bar{\varphi}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t.

So,

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}\|\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\bar{\varphi}(t),\bar{\varphi}(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t.

Hence, with the notations of Lemma
3.3
, we have proved

\lim_{n}Q_{T_{n}}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n},\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=Q(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\varphi}).

Since

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{1}^{T_{n}}&\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&+\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}+\theta(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+x-\beta b_{m})),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)+\beta b_{m}\rangle\\
&-\langle\nabla U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\beta b_{m}t^{2/3})\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t),\tilde{\varphi}^{n}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t\ \mathrm{d}\theta,\end{split}

we can thus conclude that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T_{n}]}(\tilde{\varphi}^{n})=\mathcal{A}_{x}(\bar{\varphi}).

The proof of the uniform convergence over compact sets runs as in the hyperbolic case.

Hyperbolic-parabolic case.
As per usual, in the hyperbolic-parabolic case we use the decomposition of the renormalized Lagrangian action given by (
2.2
). Studying the two terms separately, we apply the same considerations that we made previously in the hyperbolic and parabolic cases and we get to the same conclusion.

∎

3.2.
Semiconcavity with linear modulus of the value function

By proving that the value function
v
is semiconcave with linear modulus, we also obtain its local Lipschitz continuity on the set
\Omega
.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any set
W\subset\subset\Omega
there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
x,z\in\mathcal{X}
with
x,x+z,x-z\in W
it holds

(3.10)

v(x+z)+v(x-z)-2v(x)\leq C\|z\|^{2}.

Proof.

From the uniform coercivity estimates, it follows that there is a constant
\hat{C}>0
such that
\|\varphi^{x}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\leq\hat{C}
for all
x\in W
.

Fixing a set
W\subset\subset\mathcal{X}\setminus\Delta
, we know from Lemma
2.4
that there is
\delta>0
such that

\inf_{t\geq 1}d(\gamma^{x}(t),\Delta)\geq\delta,

where
\gamma^{x}(t)=r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)
and
\varphi^{x}
realizes the minimum of the functional
\mathcal{A}_{x}
.

Since
v
is continuous over the set
\overline{W}
,
v
is bounded over
\overline{W}
: this means that we only have to prove (
3.10
) for small values of
\|z\|
.

For
\|z\|
sufficiently small, we have

\inf_{t\geq 1}d(\gamma^{x}(t)\pm sz,\Delta)\geq\frac{\delta}{2},\ \ s\in[0,1].

Thus

\begin{split}&v(x+z)+v(x-z)-2v(x)\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}L_{x+z}^{ren}(\varphi^{x}(t))+L_{x-z}^{ren}(\varphi^{x}(t))-2L_{x}^{ren}(\varphi^{x}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x+z-r_{0}(1))+U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-z-r_{0}(1))-2U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+sz),z\rangle-\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)-sz),z\rangle\ \mathrm{d}s\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}-2\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+(1-2\tau)sz)sz,z\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\tau\ \mathrm{d}s\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

where we denote
L_{x}^{ren}(\varphi^{x}(t))=\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi^{x}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\varphi^{x}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}
. The thesis follows from the fact that there is a constant
C^{\prime}>0
with the property that there is
\varepsilon>0
such that for
\|z\|<\varepsilon
and for any
x\in W

\|\nabla^{2}U(\gamma^{x}(t)+z)\|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{t^{2}},\quad\forall t\geq 1,

where we used the operator norm of a matrix.

∎

In particular, the semiconcavity of the value function implies that the function is locally Lipschitz continuous, see e.g.
[
4
]
.

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We prove Theorem
3.1
by means of the finite-horizon approximation of Proposition
3.2
, and the
stability principle
for viscosity solutions. To this end, the key idea is to show that each
v(T,x)
satisfies a proper differential equation, as proved in the following proposition.

We recall that, similarly to Definition (
1.17
), for a function
v:[1,+\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define the Fréchet superdifferential and subdifferential of
v
at
(T,x)
as

\begin{split}&D^{-}v(T,x)=\bigg\{(p_{T},p_{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}:\liminf_{(\bar{T},y)\rightarrow(T,x)}\frac{v(\bar{T},y)-v(T,x)-p_{T}(\bar{T}-T)-\langle p_{x},y-x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{\sqrt{(\bar{T}-T)^{2}+\|y-x\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}}\geq 0\bigg\},\\
&D^{+}v(T,x)=\bigg\{(p_{T},p_{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}:\limsup_{(\bar{T},y)\rightarrow(T,x)}\frac{v(\bar{T},y)-v(T,x)-p_{T}(\bar{T}-T)-\langle p_{x},y-x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{\sqrt{(\bar{T}-T)^{2}+\|y-x\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}}\leq 0\bigg\}.\end{split}

Given a function
F:[1,+\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we consider the equation

(3.11)

\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}(T,x)+H(x,\nabla v(T,x))+F(T,x)=0.

We say that a continuous function
v
is a viscosity supersolution of (
3.11
) if, for every
(T,x)
and
(p_{T},p_{x})\in D^{-}v(T,x)
, it holds

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+F_{*}(T,x)\geq 0,

where
F_{*}
is the
lower semicontinuous envelope
of
F
;
v
is called viscosity subsolution of (
3.11
) if, for every
(T,x)
and
(p_{T},p_{x})\in D^{+}v(T,x)
, it holds

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+F^{*}(T,x)\leq 0,

where
F^{*}
is the
upper semicontinuos envelope
of
F
.

If
v
is both a viscosity supersolution and a viscosity subsolution, then it is called a viscosity solution of (
3.11
).

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
(T,x)\in(1,+\infty)\times\Omega
. Then:

•

there exists a minimizer
\varphi_{x,T}
of
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}
on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
such that
v(T,x)
is a viscosity subsolution of

\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}(T,x)+H(x,\nabla v(T,x))+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\varphi_{x,T}(T)+x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-H(r_{0}(T),\dot{r}_{0}(T))\leq 0,

•

for all
\varphi_{x,T}

\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}

In particular, the function

\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}(T,x)=-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla v(T,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}+U(x)-\inf_{\varphi_{x,T}\in\mathcal{Z}}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\varphi_{x,T}(T)+x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-U(r_{0}(T)),

Moreover, it holds

(3.12)

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}(T,x)=0.

Taking Proposition
3.7

3.1

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

From the uniform coercivity estimates, fixed
x
, we have that
|\varphi_{x,T}(T)|\leq C\sqrt{T}

\left|\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\varphi_{x,T}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\right|\leq\|\ddot{r}_{0}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}C\sqrt{T}\to 0,\ \mbox{ as }\ T\to+\infty.

From a direct inspection,

\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-U(r_{0}(T))\to\frac{\|a\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}}{2},\ \mbox{ as }\ T\to+\infty.

Hence, from (
3.12
) and the uniform convergence of
v(T,x)
to
v(x)
over compact subsets of
\Omega
(Proposition
3.2
), using the stability principle of viscosity solutions (see e.g.
[
7
]
), we conclude that the uniform limit

satisfies in the viscosity sense the limit equation, that is, (
3.2
).

Although the stability principle stated in
[
7
]
is formulated for sequences of continuous equations, it still applies in our case, since the only term which may lack continuity, that is,

.
∎

The remainder of the section is then devoted to proving Proposition
3.7
.

We need several lemmas, involving supplementary value functions
u(T,x)
, defined for any
x\in\Omega
and
T>1
, by

(3.13)

\begin{split}u(T,x)&=\min_{\eta\in H^{1}([1,T]):\,\eta(1)=x}\int_{1}^{T}L(\eta(t),\dot{\eta}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\eta(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{T}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

where
\gamma

(3.14)

\begin{cases}\ddot{\gamma}=\nabla U(\gamma)\ \mbox{ a.e. in }\ (1,T)\\
\gamma(1)=x\\
\dot{\gamma}(T)=\dot{r}_{0}(T).\end{cases}

Notice that, in general, Problem (
3.14
) is not uniquely solvable.

Furthermore, by setting
\hat{\gamma}(t)=\gamma(T+1-t)
, we get a solution of

\begin{cases}\ddot{\hat{\gamma}}=\nabla U(\hat{\gamma})\ \mbox{ a.e. in }\ (1,T)\\
\hat{\gamma}(T)=x\\
\dot{\hat{\gamma}}(1)=-\dot{r}_{0}(T),\end{cases}

that satisfies

(3.15)

\begin{split}u(T,x)&=\int_{1}^{T}L(\hat{\gamma}(t),\dot{\hat{\gamma}}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\hat{\gamma}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\min_{\eta\in H^{1}([1,T]):\,\eta(T)=x}\int_{1}^{T}L(\eta(t),\dot{\eta}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\eta(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.\end{split}

Retracing the arguments made by Cannarsa and Sinestrari, we want to prove a similar inequality to the Dynamic Programming Principle (Theorem 1.2.2 in
[
4
]
), which translates into the following result.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{R}
,
T>1
, and
x\in{\Omega}
, and consider a curve
\overline{\gamma}\in H^{1}([1,T])
such that
\overline{\gamma}(T)=x
. Then, for all
T^{\prime}\in[1,T]
,

(3.16)

u(T,x)\leq u(T^{\prime},\overline{\gamma}(T^{\prime}))+\int_{T^{\prime}}^{T}L(\overline{\gamma}(t),\dot{\overline{\gamma}}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T^{\prime}),\tilde{\gamma}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},

where
\tilde{\gamma}\in H^{1}([1,T^{\prime}])
realizes the minimum in
u(T^{\prime},\overline{\gamma}(T^{\prime}))
and is such that
\tilde{\gamma}(T^{\prime})=\overline{\gamma}(T^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Fix
T^{\prime}\in[1,T]
and let
\tilde{\gamma}\in H([1,T^{\prime}])
such that
\tilde{\gamma}(T^{\prime})=\overline{\gamma}(T^{\prime})
. Setting

\xi(t)=\begin{cases}\tilde{\gamma}(t),\quad t\in[1,T^{\prime}]\\
\overline{\gamma}(t),\quad t\in[T^{\prime},T]\end{cases}

we have
\xi\in H^{1}([1,T])
and
\xi(T)=x
. Therefore,

\begin{split}u(T,x)&\leq\int_{1}^{T}L(\xi(t),\dot{\xi}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\xi(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{T^{\prime}}L(\tilde{\gamma}(t),\tilde{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t+\int_{T^{\prime}}^{T}L(\overline{\gamma}(t),\overline{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\tilde{\gamma}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{T^{\prime}}L(\tilde{\gamma}(t),\tilde{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t+\int_{T^{\prime}}^{T}L(\overline{\gamma}(t),\overline{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T^{\prime}),\tilde{\gamma}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T^{\prime}),\tilde{\gamma}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.\end{split}

Taking the infimum over all
\tilde{\gamma}\in H^{1}([1,T^{\prime}])
, we get (
3.16
).
∎

We now exploit Lemma
3.8
to prove the next result.

Lemma 3.9
.

For every
(T,x)\in(1,+\infty)\times\Omega
, we have the following:

•

for every
(p_{T},p_{x})\in D^{+}u(T,x)
, there exists a curve
\gamma
realizing
u(T,x)
in (
3.15
) such that, in the viscosity sense,

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 0;

•

for every
(p_{T},p_{x})\in D^{-}u(T,x)
and for every
\gamma
realizing
u(T,x)
in (
3.15
) it holds, in the viscosity sense,

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\geq 0.

In particular, the function

u(T,x)
defined in (
3.13
) satisfies, in the viscosity sense,

(3.17)

-\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}(T,x)=-H(x,\nabla u(T,x))+\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},

where
\mathcal{Y}
is the set of curves
\gamma\in H^{1}([1,T])
,
\gamma(1)=x
that realize
u(T,x)
.

Before starting the rigorous proof of Lemma
3.9
by proving that
u(T,x)
is a viscosity solution of (
3.17
), we give the heuristic idea.

By taking a point of differentiability
x
and formally differentiating the value function with respect to
x
, we obtain

\nabla u(T,x)=-\mathcal{M}\dot{\gamma}(1).

Indeed, denoting
\psi(t):=\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial x_{i}}(t)
and with
e_{i}
the
i
-th element of the canonical basis, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}(T,x)&=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\gamma}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\psi(t)+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\dot{\psi}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\psi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\psi(t)\bigg|^{T}_{1}-\int_{1}^{T}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\bigg)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\gamma}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\bigg)\psi(t)\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\psi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\psi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{\gamma}(1),\psi(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\psi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-m_{i}\dot{\gamma}(1)\cdot e_{i},\end{split}

which follows from integration by parts. Besides, using integration by parts and the conservation of the energy, we denote
\phi(t):=\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}(t)
and compute

\begin{split}\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}(T,x)&=L(\gamma(T),\dot{\gamma}(T))+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\gamma}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\phi(t)+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\dot{\phi}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\dot{\gamma}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\phi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=L(\gamma(T),\dot{\gamma}(T))+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(T),\dot{\gamma}(T))\phi(T)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(1),\dot{\gamma}(1))\phi(1)\\
&-\int_{1}^{T}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\gamma}}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\bigg)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\gamma}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\bigg)\phi(t)\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\dot{\gamma}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\phi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

and therefore

\begin{split}\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}(T,x)&=-\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\gamma(T))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}(1)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\gamma(1))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-H(x,\nabla u(T,x))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.\end{split}

Now, we prove the same result in a rigorous way.

Proof of Lemma
3.9
.

To simplify the computations, we consider the formulation of
u(T,x)
given by the last line in (
3.15
), so that the end-point of
\gamma
realizing
u(T,x)
is
x
.

Take
T>1
,
x\in\Omega
and
(p_{T},p_{x})\in D^{+}u(T,x)
. Then, for every
z\in\mathcal{X}
,

\limsup_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u(T-h,x-hz)-u(T,x)+h(p_{T}+\langle z,p_{x}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}})}{h\sqrt{1+\|z\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}}\leq 0,

which is equivalent to

\limsup_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u(T-h,x-hz)-u(T,x)}{h}\leq-p_{T}-\langle z,p_{x}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

Setting
\zeta(t)=x+(t-T)z
, with
z\in\mathcal{X}
, we can apply (
3.16
) to get

\begin{split}u(T,x)&\leq u(T-h,\zeta(T-h))+\int_{T-h}^{T}L(\zeta(t),\dot{\zeta}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T-h),\gamma_{h}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=u(T-h,x-hz)+\int_{T-h}^{T}L(x+(t-T)z,z)\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T-h),\gamma_{h}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

where
\gamma_{h}
realizes the minimum in
u(T-h,\zeta(T-h))
. This implies

\begin{split}\limsup_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u(T-h,x-hz)-u(T,x)}{h}&\geq\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}-\frac{1}{h}\int_{T-h}^{T}L(x+(t-T)z,z)\ \mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{h}\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T-h),{\gamma_{h}}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-L(x,z)+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

where we used the fact that

(3.18)

\gamma_{h}(1)\rightarrow\gamma(1)\text{ as }h\rightarrow 0^{+},

with
\gamma
a minimizer realizing
u(T,x)
. Notice that not every
\gamma
minimizer is in general obtained as a limit of
\gamma_{h}
.

We can thus conclude that

p_{T}+\langle p_{x},z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-L(x,z)+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 0,\ \mbox{ for all }\ z\in\mathcal{X},

which, taking
z=p_{x}
, yields

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 0,

for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}
such that (
3.18
) holds.

Hence, we have

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 0,

that is to say, that
u(t,x)
is a viscosity sub-solution of

\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}+H(x,\nabla u(T,x))+\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=0.

To prove that
u
is a viscosity supersolution, suppose that
\gamma
is a minimizer for the value function
u(T,x)
with
x=\gamma(T)
and
w=\dot{\gamma}(T)
. Then,

\begin{split}u(T-h,{\gamma}(T-h))-u(T,x)&\leq\int_{1}^{T-h}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T-h),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\int_{1}^{T}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t+\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-\int_{T-h}^{T}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t+\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T-h),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.\end{split}

This implies

\begin{split}\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u(T-h,{\gamma}(T-h))-u(T,x)}{h}&\leq\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}-\frac{1}{h}\int_{T-h}^{T}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{h}\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T)-\dot{r}_{0}(T-h),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-L(x,w)+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.\end{split}

Given
(p_{T},p_{x})\in D^{-}u(T,x)
, from the fact that
u(T,x)
is Lipschtiz continuous (see Section
3.2
), it holds

\liminf_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u(T-h,{\gamma}(T-h))-u(T,x)}{h}\geq-p_{T}-\langle p_{x},w\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

Then

p_{T}+\langle p_{x},w\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-L(x,w)+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\geq 0,

which leads to

p_{T}+H(x,p_{x})+\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\geq 0.

for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}

As a consequence,
u(T,x)
is a viscosity supersolution of

\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}+H(x,\nabla u(T,x))+\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=0.

We have thus proved that the value function
u
solves, for a fixed
T>1
, the equation

-\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}(T,x)=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(T,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}-U(x)+\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}

in the viscosity sense.
∎

We finally make use of the following relation between
v(T,x)
and
u(T,x)
.

Lemma 3.10
.

For every
T>1
and
x\in\Omega
, it holds

v(T,x)=u(T,x)-\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}+U(r_{0}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),r_{0}(T)-r_{0}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

Proof.

We recall that
v(T,x)=w(T,x)-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}
, as defined in (
3.3
), with
w(T,x)
defined in (
3.4
).

The statement is then true if we prove that

u(T,x)=w(T,x)+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}+U(r_{0}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),r_{0}(T)+x-r_{0}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

This follows from the next chain of identities:

\begin{split}u(T,x)&=\int_{1}^{T}L(\bar{\gamma}(t),\dot{\bar{\gamma}}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\bar{\gamma}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\dot{\bar{\varphi}}(t),\dot{r}_{0}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+U(r_{0}(t)+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),r_{0}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),\bar{\varphi}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),x-r_{0}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(r_{0}(t)+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\bar{\varphi}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),r_{0}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),x-r_{0}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

which gives

\begin{split}u(T,x)&=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\bar{\varphi}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(r_{0}(t)+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\bar{\varphi}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\\
&+U(r_{0}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),r_{0}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),x-r_{0}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

and therefore

u(T,x)=w(T,x)+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{r}_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(r_{0}(t))\ \mathrm{d}t-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),r_{0}(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}-\langle\dot{r}_{0}(T),x-r_{0}(1)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

Above, we have taken advantage of the fact that if
\bar{\gamma}
realizes
u(T,x)
, then
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)\ni\bar{\varphi}=\bar{\gamma}-r_{0}(t)-x+r_{0}(1)
realizes
w(T,x)
. This follows from the fact that
\bar{\varphi}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\ddot{\varphi}=\nabla U(r_{0}+\varphi+x-r_{0}(1))\ \mbox{ a.e. in }\ (1,T)\\
\varphi(1)=0\\
\dot{\varphi}(T)=0,\end{cases}

that is,
\varphi
is a critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{x,[1,T]}
on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,T)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

This follows from (
3.17
), after one observes that
\nabla v(T,x)=\nabla u(T,x)
in the viscosity sense.

We claim that

(3.19)

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}-\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\gamma(T))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=-\frac{\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2}.

If (
3.19
) is proved, then, since

\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}(T,x)=-\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(\gamma(T))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}

in the viscosity sense, we conclude that

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}(T,x)=-\frac{\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2},

in the viscosity sense.

We prove our claim (
3.19
):

•

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\|\dot{\gamma}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\|\dot{r}_{0}(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}
.

•

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}U(\gamma(T))=0
. This follows from the fact that, for
T
sufficiently large and for
x
belonging to a compact set, due to the uniform coercivity estimates (cfr. Section
2.1
), there is a positive constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
which does not depend on
T
and
x
such that

\begin{split}U(\gamma(T))&=\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|r_{0,ij}(T)+\varphi_{ij}(T)+x_{ij}-r_{0,ij}(1)|}\\
&\leq\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|r_{0,ij}(T)|-|\varphi_{ij}(T)|-|x_{ij}-r_{0,ij}(1)|}\\
&\leq\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|r_{0,ij}(T)|-cT^{1/2}-|x_{ij}-r_{0,ij}(1)|},\end{split}

and the last term goes to zero when
T\rightarrow+\infty
.

•

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=0
. To prove this, we can study the three cases separately. In the hyperbolic case,
\ddot{r}_{0}(T)=0
for all
T\geq 1
. In the parabolic case,

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}-\frac{2}{9}\langle\beta b_{m}T^{-4/3},\beta b_{m}T^{2/3}+\varphi(T)+x-\beta b_{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}-\frac{2}{9}\frac{1}{T^{2}}=0,

and in the hyperbolic-parabolic case,

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(T),\gamma(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}-\frac{2}{9}\langle\beta b_{m}T^{-4/3},aT+\beta b_{m}T^{2/3}+\varphi(T)+x-a-\beta b_{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=0,

since
\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\langle\beta b_{m}T^{-4/3},\varphi(T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=0
by the fact that, for
T
sufficiently large, there is a positive constant
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
which does not depend on
T
and
x
such that
\|\varphi(T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq c^{\prime}T^{1/2}
.

∎

3.3.1.
Alternative direct proof of Theorem
3.1

Theorem
3.1
can also be proved with more direct computations, simply by showing that Definition
1.17
is satisfied by the value function. For the sake of completeness, we also show this alternative proof.

Given
\varphi^{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
realizing
v(x)
, set
\gamma=r_{0}+\varphi^{x}+x-r_{0}(1)
. Since the conservation of the energy implies

H(\gamma(1),\dot{\gamma}(1))=\frac{\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2},

to prove Theorem
3.1
it is enough to show that
\|\nabla v(x)\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\|\dot{\gamma}(1)\|_{\mathcal{M}}
, in the viscosity sense, where
\gamma(t)
is an arbitrary expansive motion starting at
x
.

Let
x\in\Omega
and
z\in\mathcal{X}
. For
h>0
, let
x_{h}=x+hz
, and
\varphi^{h}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
be a minimizer of
\mathcal{A}_{x_{h}}
. It holds,
v(x_{h})=\mathcal{A}_{x_{h}}(\varphi^{h})-\langle a,x_{h}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}
. Since
\varphi^{h}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, we have

v(x_{h})-v(x)\geq\mathcal{A}_{x_{h}}(\varphi^{h})-\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi^{h})-\langle a,x_{h}-x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

Hence, since
r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{h}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+shz
is free from collisions, due to the minimality of
\varphi^{h}
, we have

\begin{split}v(x_{h})-v(x)&\geq\int_{1}^{+\infty}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{h}(t)+x_{h}-r_{0}(1))-U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{h}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))\,\mathrm{d}t-h\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}h\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{h}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+shz),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t-h\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\end{split}

that is

\frac{v(x_{h})-v(x)}{h}\geq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{h}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+shz),z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

As we observed in the proof of the continuity of
v
(see Proposition
3.2
), there exists
\bar{\varphi}
minimizer of
\mathcal{A}_{x}
such that
\varphi^{h}\to\bar{\varphi}
pointwise on
[1,+\infty)
, uniformly on
[1,T]
, for every
T>1
, and weakly on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
. In particular, we have
\varphi^{h}+shz\to\bar{\varphi}
pointwise on
[1,+\infty)
and uniformly on every
[1,T]
. It then suffices to observe that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T_{\varepsilon}>1
such that

\left|\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{h}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+shz),z\rangle\right|\leq\frac{\|z\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{(1-\varepsilon)\|r_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}},\ \mbox{ for }\ t\geq T_{\varepsilon},

to apply dominated convergence Theorem and conclude that, as
h\to 0^{+}
,

(3.20)

\begin{split}\frac{v(x_{h})-v(x)}{h}&\geq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)),z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1)\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)),z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1)\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\ddot{\gamma}(t),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1)\\
&=-\langle\dot{\gamma}(1),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1),\end{split}

where
\gamma(t)=r_{0}(t)+\bar{\varphi}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)
. Therefore, if
p\in D^{+}v(x)
, then the following chain holds:

\langle p,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\geq\limsup_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{v(x_{h})-v(x)}{h}\geq-\langle\dot{\gamma}(1),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\ \ \mbox{ for every }\ z\in\mathcal{X}.

By choosing
z=-p
, we then obtain

(3.21)

-\|p\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\geq\langle\dot{\gamma}(1),p\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},

Instead, by choosing
z=\dot{\gamma}(1)
, we have

(3.22)

\langle\dot{\gamma}(1),p\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\geq-\|\dot{\gamma}(1)\|_{\mathcal{M}}.

Putting together (
3.21
) and (
3.22
), we conclude that

(3.23)

\|p\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\leq\|\dot{\gamma}(1)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+2U(\gamma(1))

by the conservation of the energy and Theorem
1.7
.

On the other hand, if
\varphi^{x}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{A}_{x}
, so that
v(x)=\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi^{x})-\langle a,x\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}
, we have

v(x_{h})-v(x)\leq\mathcal{A}_{x_{h}}(\varphi^{x})-\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi^{x})-h\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}.

With similar arguments as above, we then get

\begin{split}\frac{v(x_{h})-v(x)}{h}&\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x_{h}-r_{0}(1))-U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x+shz-r_{0}(1)),z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)),z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1),\ \mbox{ as }\ h\to 0^{+}\\
&=\int_{1}^{\infty}\langle\ddot{\gamma}^{x}(t),z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle a,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1),\end{split}

that is,

(3.24)

\frac{v(x_{h})-v(x)}{h}\leq-\langle\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+o(1),\ \mbox{ as }h\to 0^{+}.

Hence, if
p\in D^{-}v(x)
, then

(3.25)

\langle p,z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\liminf_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{v(x_{h})-v(x)}{h}\leq-\langle\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\ \mbox{ for every }\ z\in\mathcal{X}.

As in the arguments to obtain (
3.23
), by putting together the inequalities from choosing
z=-p
and
z=\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1)
in (
3.25
), we get

\|p\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\geq\|\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+2U(\gamma^{x}(1)),

by the conservation of the energy and Theorem
1.7
.

To conclude, we proved that, in the viscosity sense,

\|\nabla v(x)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+2U(x),

that is

(3.26)

H(x,\nabla v(x))=\frac{\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2}.

Remark 3.11
.

En passant
, in the proof of (
3.26
), we also proved that if for
x\in\Omega
there is a unique
\varphi^{x}
minimizing
\mathcal{A}_{x}
on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, then
v
is differentiable at
x
, and

\nabla v(x)=-\mathcal{M}\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1),

where
\gamma^{x}(t)=r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)
.

Indeed, (
3.20
) and (
3.24
) together, in this case, imply

-\langle\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{v(x+hz)-v(x)}{h}\leq-\langle\dot{\gamma}^{x}(1),z\rangle_{\mathcal{M}},\ \mbox{ for every }\ z\in\mathcal{X}.

4.
Fine regularity results on
v

In this section, which concerns regularity results on the value function
v(x)
, we treat the
size
of two special sets of points: the set of irregular points and the set of the so-called conjugate points.

4.1.
Irregular and conjugate points

Definition 4.1

(Irregular points)
.

We say that a configuration
x\in\Omega
is
regular
if
\mathcal{A}_{x}
admits a
unique
minimum
\varphi^{x}
on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
. All other points are called
irregular
. We denote by
\Sigma
the set of irregular points.

As observed in Remark
3.11
, the set
\Omega\setminus\Sigma
corresponds to points in which
v
is
C^{1}
, and the Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
3.2
) is satisfied in the classical sense.

Retracing the work by Cannarsa and Sinestrari
[
4
]
, we want to define the set of
conjugate
points in a similar way to what has been done in
[
4
]
, taking into consideration that we are working with a renormalized Lagrangian action that is defined over a set of curves whose dominium is the half-line
[1,+\infty)
.

We recall from
[
25
]
that over the set of non-collisional configurations, the differential
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)
is continuous to the dual space
(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*}
, and it takes the following form:

(4.1)

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)[\psi]=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\dot{\varphi}(t),\dot{\psi}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t)+x-r_{0}(1)),\psi(t)\rangle-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\psi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t.

By composing
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)
with the inverse of Riesz’s isomorphism
\mathcal{R}_{i}^{-1}:(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*}\to\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, we can define a map
F:U\subset\Omega\times\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)\to\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)

as:

\begin{split}F(x,\varphi)&:=\mathcal{R}_{i}^{-1}\circ\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)\\
&=\varphi+\mathcal{R}_{i}^{-1}[\nabla U(r_{0}+\varphi+x-r_{0}(1))-\mathcal{M}\ddot{r}_{0}].\end{split}

Two comments on
F
. First, zeros of
F
correspond to couples
(\bar{x},\bar{\varphi})
in which
\bar{\varphi}
is a critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{x}}
. Second, if the differential
D_{\varphi}F(\bar{x},\bar{\varphi})
is invertible, then the Implicit Function Theorem implies that there is
\mathcal{\bar{U}}\times\mathcal{\bar{V}}
a neighborhood of
(\bar{x},\bar{\varphi})\in\Omega\times\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, such that for every
x\in\mathcal{\bar{U}}
there is one and only one critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{x}
on
\mathcal{\bar{V}}
, say
\varphi^{x}\in\mathcal{\bar{V}}
, which depends on
x
with maximal regularity.

A sufficient condition to prove that
D_{\varphi}F(x,\varphi)
is invertible is to prove that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)
is coercive. In contrast, the set of conjugate points heuristically consists of points such that the linearized problem degenerates. Hence, the following definition.

Definition 4.2

(Conjugate points)
.

We define the set of
conjugate points

\Gamma
as the subset of points
x\in\Omega
such that

•

the set of minimizers of
\mathcal{A}_{x}
is isolated on the set of critical points,

•

\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi^{x})\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty);(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*})
is not invertible, for every
\varphi^{x}
minimizer of
\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi^{x})
,

where
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x}
is the bilinear form corresponding to
D_{\varphi}F(x,\varphi^{x})
, given by

(4.2)

\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi^{x})[\psi,\zeta]=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle\dot{\psi}(t),\dot{\zeta}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x}(t)+x-r_{0}(1))\psi(t),\zeta(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t.

Our goal is to obtain an estimate on the dimension of the set of conjugate points
\Gamma
.

4.2.
Rectifiability of
\Sigma\setminus\Gamma

In this and the next sections, we make use of the following right-tail
T
-actions:

(4.3)

\mathcal{A}_{x,[T,+\infty)}(\varphi)=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t)+x-r_{0}(T))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\varphi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t,

where
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
.

Proposition 4.3
.

For all
T>1
, and for any
x\in\Omega
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
, the differential
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x,[T,+\infty)}(\varphi)
is a compact perturbation of an invertible operator.

Proof.

We observe that
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x,[T,+\infty)}
is as in (
4.1
), but with the integral evaluated in
[T,+\infty)
. Using Riesz isomorphism, we can write

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{x,[T,+\infty)}(\varphi)=\mathbf{1}+\mathcal{R}_{i}^{-1}[\nabla U(r_{0}+\varphi+x-r_{0}(1))-\mathcal{M}\ddot{r}_{0}].

We will divide the proof accordingly to the class of expansive motion under consideration.

Hyperbolic case.
The statement follows once one observes that
P_{x,T}^{H}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty);(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty))^{*})
, defined by

P_{x,T}^{H}(\varphi)\ :\ \psi\rightarrow\int_{T}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U(\gamma(t)),\psi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t,

is compact. Consider
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
. As we already observed throughout the paper, up to subsequences,
\varphi_{n}\to\bar{\varphi}
, for some
\bar{\varphi}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
, where the convergence is, in particular, pointwise in the whole half-line
[T,+\infty)
. Hence, for every fixed
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
such that
\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}=1
, we have

\begin{split}P_{x,T}^{H}(\varphi_{n})[\psi]-P_{x,T}^{H}(\bar{\varphi})[\psi]&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U(\gamma_{n}(t)-\nabla U(\bar{\gamma}(t)),\psi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\gamma_{n}(t)+s(\varphi_{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)))(\varphi_{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)),\psi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

Since, for
t
large enough and for every
s\in[0,1]
,

\left|\langle\nabla^{2}U(\gamma_{n}(t)+s(\varphi_{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)))(\varphi_{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)),\psi(t)\rangle\right|\leq\frac{C\,\|\varphi_{n}(t)-\bar{\varphi}(t)\|\,\|\psi(t)\|}{t^{3}}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{t^{5/2}}\in L^{1}(T,+\infty),

by the Dominated Convergence Theorem and the pointwise convergence of
\varphi_{n}
to
\bar{\varphi}
, we deduce that

\|P_{x,T}^{H}(\varphi_{n})-P^{H}_{x,T}(\bar{\varphi})\|_{(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty))^{*}}=\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\left|P_{x,T}^{H}(\varphi_{n})[\psi]-P_{x,T}(\bar{\varphi})[\psi]\right|\to 0,\ \mbox{ as }\ n\to\infty,

that is,
P_{x,T}^{H}(\varphi_{n})
is convergent in
(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty))^{*}
, which means that
P_{x,T}^{H}
is compact.

Parabolic case.
In this case, we need to start from
\mathcal{A}_{x,[T,+\infty)}(\varphi)
written as follows

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{x,[T,+\infty)}(\varphi)&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\,\varphi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{T}^{+\infty}U(\gamma(t))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)),\varphi(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\,\varphi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

where

\varphi\mapsto\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\,\varphi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

is a norm, coming from a positive quadratic form which is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}
, as shown in
[
25
, Section 4.2]
. Hence, by Lax-Milgram Theorem, its differential is invertible. Setting

\mathcal{F}(\varphi)=\int_{T}^{+\infty}U(\gamma(t))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)),\varphi(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\,\varphi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,

it holds

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\varphi)[\psi]&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\langle\nabla U(\gamma(t))-\nabla U(r_{0}(t)),\psi(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\gamma(t)+\sigma_{1}(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}))(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}),\psi(t)\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t))\varphi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\left[\nabla^{3}U(\gamma(t)+(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}))(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}+\sigma_{1}(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x})\right]\,\varphi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{1}\ \mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad\qquad+\mbox{ lower order terms }.\end{split}

Since, for
t
sufficiently large, we have

\left|\langle\left[\nabla^{3}U(\gamma(t)+(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}))(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x}+\sigma_{1}(\varphi(t)+\tilde{x})\right]\,\varphi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle\right|\leq\frac{C\,\|\varphi(t)\|^{2}\,\|\psi(t)\|}{\|r_{0}(t)\|^{4}},

with the same arguments used in the proof of the hyperbolic case for the operator
P_{x,T}^{H}
, we conclude that
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty);(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty))^{*})
is compact.

Hyperbolic-Parabolic case.
The proof exploits the Lagrangian action decomposition that follows from the cluster partition of the bodies that has been presented in
[
25
]
. In particular, the renormalized Lagrangian action is written as the sum of two terms, where one describes the movement of the bodies inside each cluster and the other describes the motions of couples of bodies that belong to different clusters. Since the first term describes a parabolic motion and the second describes a hyperbolic one, the computation will be similar to the two cases above.

Theorem 4.4
.

If the set
\{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty):\,\varphi\ \mbox{ is a minimizer of the renormalized value function v(x)}\}
is not finite, then
x\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

Assuming
x\notin\Gamma
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
, we have that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x}
is invertible, that is, for every
J\in(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*}
there exists a unique
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
such that
J[\cdot]=\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)[\psi,\cdot]
in
(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*}
. This implies that the Implicit Function Theorem applies to the
0-
level of

F(x,\varphi):=\mathrm{d}\,\mathcal{A}_{x}(\varphi)\in(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*},

and, therefore, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}_{x}\times\mathcal{U}_{\varphi}
of
(x,\varphi)
in
\Omega\times\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
such that, for every
z\in\mathcal{U}_{x}
, there exists a unique
\bar{\varphi}=\bar{\varphi}^{z}=\bar{\varphi}(z)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varphi}
critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{z}
. Thus, there are at most countably many minimizers, and they are isolated.

Suppose that the number of such minimizers is not finite, and let
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
be the sequence of minimizers. Then, up to subsequences, there exists
\bar{\varphi}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
such that
\varphi_{n}\to\bar{\varphi}
weakly and pointwise. By Proposition
4.3
, we can conclude that
\bar{\varphi}
must be a critical point and, in particular, it is a minimizer. It follows that

0=\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\mathcal{D}}-\|\bar{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{D}}+2\,\int_{1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi_{n}(t)+x-r_{0}(1)+s(\bar{\varphi}(t)-\varphi_{n}(t)),\bar{\varphi}(t)-\varphi_{n}(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t

and then, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we obtain
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\to\|\bar{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{D}}
. This in turn implies
\|\varphi_{n}-\bar{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\to 0
, which contradicts the fact that
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
is discrete in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
.

.

4.3.
Bound on the dimension of the set of conjugate points

4.3.1.
Definition of a local flow

Given
x_{0}\in\Gamma
, consider
\varphi^{x_{0}}
a corresponding minimizer of
\mathcal{A}_{x_{0}}
on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
. Associated to
\varphi^{x_{0}}
is
\gamma^{x_{0}}
, defined by
\gamma^{x_{0}}(t)=r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x_{0}}(t)+x_{0}-r_{0}(1)
. Extend
\gamma^{x_{0}}
to the interval
(1-\varepsilon,1]
as the unique smooth solution of (
1.1
). Still denote by
\gamma^{x_{0}}
this extension, defined in
(1-\varepsilon,+\infty)
.

The Hessian of the action is defined as the bilinear form
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x_{0}}(\varphi)
given in (
4.2
). Following
\varphi(t)
, and consequently
\gamma(t)
, we consider a set of bilinear forms on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)\times\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{x_{0}}(T),[T,+\infty)}(\tilde{\varphi})[\psi,\eta]&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\langle\dot{\psi}(t),\dot{\eta}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t)+\tilde{\varphi}(t)+\gamma^{x_{0}}(T)-r_{0}(T))\psi(t),\,\eta(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\langle\dot{\psi}(t),\dot{\eta}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi^{x_{0}}(t)+x_{0}-r_{0}(1))\psi(t),\,\eta(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

where
\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi^{x_{0}}|_{[T,+\infty)}-\varphi^{x_{0}}(T)
. Notice that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x_{0}}(\varphi)
is not invertible, as, by hypothesis,
x\in\Gamma
.

Remark 4.7
.

We recall that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x_{0}}(\varphi)
is invertible if
\forall\ J\in(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*}
there is a unique
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
such that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x_{0}}(\varphi)[\psi,\cdot]=J[\cdot]
in
(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty))^{*}
(or, using Riesz’s isomorphism,
\mathcal{R}_{i}^{-1}\circ\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{x_{0}}(\varphi)[\psi,\cdot]=\mathcal{R}_{i}^{-1}\circ J[\cdot]
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(1,+\infty)
).

By Lax-Milgram’s Theorem,
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{x_{0}}(T),[T,+\infty)}(\tilde{\varphi})
is invertible if it is coercive, that is, if there is a constant
\alpha>0
such that

(4.5)

\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{x_{0}}(T),[T,+\infty)}(\tilde{\varphi})[\psi,\psi]\geq\alpha\|\psi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)},

for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
.

Now, we give the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Given
x_{0}\in\Gamma
there exist
{T}_{0}>1
and
C>0
such that (
4.5
) holds for every
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}({T}_{0},+\infty)
.

Proof.

We distinguish the hyperbolic case from the parabolic one to prove the claim.

Starting with the hyperbolic case, we notice that

\langle\nabla^{2}U(at)\psi(t),\psi(t)\rangle\geq-C\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{\|\gamma^{x_{0}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}}\geq-\frac{C}{\|a\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}T}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}},

for some constant
C>0
and for
t\geq T
. Then

\int_{T}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\gamma^{x_{0}}(t))\psi(t),\psi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{T}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-\frac{C^{\prime}}{T}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\geq\bigg(1-\frac{4C^{\prime}}{T}\bigg)\int_{T}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t.

Choosing
T
big enough so that
1-\frac{4C^{\prime}}{T}>0
, we obtain the coercivity of
L_{x_{0},T}
.

To prove (
4.5
) in the parabolic case, we use the inequality

\langle\nabla^{2}U(\gamma^{x_{0}}(t))\psi(t),\psi(t)\rangle\geq-U_{min}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{\|\gamma^{x_{0}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}},

which implies

\begin{split}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\gamma^{x_{0}}(t))\psi(t),\psi(t)\rangle&\geq-U_{min}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{\|r_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}}\frac{1}{\big(1+\frac{\|\varphi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{\|r_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}}+\frac{\|x-r_{0}(1)\|_{\mathcal{M}}}{\|r_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}}\big)^{3}}\\
&\geq-U_{min}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{\|\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{3}}\\
&=-\frac{2}{9}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}},\end{split}

being
r_{0}(t)=\beta b_{m}t^{2/3}
. Then,

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{x_{0}}(T),[T,+\infty)}(\tilde{\varphi})[\psi,\psi]&\geq\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}^{2}-\frac{2}{9}\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}^{2}-\frac{8}{9}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{9}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}^{2}.\end{split}

∎

Let
T_{0}>1
be a time such that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{x_{0}}(T_{0}),[T_{0},+\infty)}
is coercive, as proved in Lemma
4.8
. Denote
z_{0}=\gamma^{x}(T_{0})
. By the Implicit Function Theorem, there are two neighborhoods
\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d(N-1)}
and
\mathcal{U}_{\varphi^{z_{0}}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T_{0},+\infty)
, and there is a function
\varphi:\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}_{\varphi^{z_{0}}}
such that
\varphi(z)=\varphi^{z}
is the unique critical point of the functional
\mathcal{A}_{z,(T_{0},+\infty)}
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T_{0},+\infty)
, that turns out to be its minimum. Moreover,
z\mapsto\varphi^{z}
is smooth, and the function

, defined by

is smooth and such that

\nabla u_{T_{0}}(z)=-\mathcal{M}\,\dot{\varphi}^{z}(T_{0})
.

Up to consider a ball
B_{R}=B_{R}(z_{0})\subset\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}
, by continuity, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that it is well defined

\xi=\xi(z,t)
, for every
z\in B_{R}
,
t\in(1-\varepsilon,T_{0}]
, as the unique solution of

(4.6)

\begin{cases}\ddot{\xi}=\nabla U(\xi),\quad 1-\varepsilon<t\leq T_{0}\\
\xi(z,T_{0})=z\in{B}_{R}\\
\dot{\xi}(z,T_{0})=-\mathcal{M}^{-1}\,\nabla u_{T_{0}}(z)+\dot{r}_{0}(T_{0}).\end{cases}

We briefly comment on the existence of
\varepsilon
. If
z=z_{0}
, then
\xi(z_{0},1)=x_{0}\notin\Delta
. Hence, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that we can extend
\xi
backward up to the time
1-\varepsilon
. By continuity, it is possible to extend the arguments in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

Fix
x
such that there exist
z\in B_{R}
and

, for which
\xi(z,s)=x
. By translating by

the flow
\xi
, we can define

, that satisfies

(4.7)

\begin{cases}\ddot{\varphi}=\nabla U(r_{0}+\varphi)-\ddot{r}_{0},\quad 1-\varepsilon<t\leq T_{0}\\
\dot{\varphi}(T_{0})=-\mathcal{M}^{-1}\,\nabla u_{T_{0}}(z)\\
\varphi(s)=x-r_{0}(s).\end{cases}

Clearly,

, so that
\varphi(z,\cdot)
given in (

)
can be extended as the unique solution of
\eqref{eq:NDsystemT0}_{1}
and
\eqref{eq:NDsystemT0}_{2}
such that

It results that
\varphi(z,\cdot)
is a critical point of

(4.8)

\begin{cases}&\mathcal{J}_{x,s}(\varphi)=\displaystyle{\int_{s}^{T_{0}}\frac{\|\dot{\varphi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{2}+U(r_{0}(t)+\varphi(t))-U(r_{0}(t))-\langle\ddot{r}_{0}(t),\varphi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t+u_{T_{0}}(\varphi(T_{0})+r_{0}(T_{0}))},\\
&\varphi\in H^{1}(s,T_{0}),\,\varphi(s)=x-r_{0}(s),\,\varphi(T_{0})+r_{0}(T_{0})\in B_{R}.\end{cases}

Remark 4.9
.

If

admits a unique minimizer

, over

4.3.2.
Spectral theory along the flow

Thanks to the definition of
\xi(z,T)
and
\varphi(z,T)
(
4.6
)-(
4.7
), we can consider, for every
z\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
and
T\in(1-\varepsilon,+\infty)
, the functional

\mathcal{A}_{\xi(z,T),[T,+\infty)}
, over
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
, defined in (
4.3
). The Hessian of this functional evaluated at
\tilde{\varphi}(z,\cdot)=\varphi(z,\cdot)|_{[T,+\infty)}-\varphi(z,T)
is the bilinear form over
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)\times\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
given by

\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\xi(z,T),[T,+\infty)}(\tilde{\varphi}(z,\cdot))[\psi,\eta]=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\langle\dot{\psi}(s),\dot{\eta}(s)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,T))\psi(s),\,\eta(s)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s.

Given
z
and
T
, we observe that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\xi(z,T),T}(\tilde{\varphi}(z))[\psi,\psi]
corresponds to the numerator of the Raileigh quotient

\mathcal{R}_{a}(\psi):=\frac{\int_{T}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\psi(t),\psi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t}{\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t},\ \ \psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty),

of the Sturm-Liouville problem

(4.9)

\begin{cases}-\ddot{\psi}(t)+\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\psi(t)=-\frac{\lambda}{t^{3}}\psi(t)\ \mbox{ a.e. in }\ (T,+\infty)\\
\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty).\end{cases}

Fix
z,T
. We already observed, since (roughly speaking)
\nabla^{2}U(\xi)\approx\nabla^{2}U(r_{0})
for large values of
t
, that there exists
\hat{T}>T
such that
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\xi(z,\hat{T}),\hat{T}}(\tilde{\varphi}(z,\cdot))
is coercive on
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(\hat{T},+\infty)
. Moreover, since
\xi(z,\cdot)
is far from collisions, there exists
C>0
such that

\inf_{\eta:\,\|\eta\|=1}\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\eta(t),\,\eta(t)\rangle\geq-\frac{C}{t^{3}},\ \mbox{ for every }\ t\in[T,\tilde{T}].

As a consequence, by using Lemma
4.8
, there exist
\mu_{0}>0
and
\tilde{\alpha}>0
such that

\begin{split}\int_{T}^{+\infty}&\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\psi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle+\mu_{0}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{T}^{\tilde{T}}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\psi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle+\mu_{0}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{\tilde{T}}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\psi(t),\,\psi(t)\rangle+\mu_{0}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\int_{T}^{\tilde{T}}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-C\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}+\mu_{0}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\alpha\,\int_{\tilde{T}}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\int_{T}^{\tilde{T}}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\alpha\,\int_{\tilde{T}}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\tilde{\alpha}\|\psi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)}\ \mbox{ for every }\ \psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty),\end{split}

where
\alpha
is given in Lemma
4.8
.

Hence, for every
f\in L^{2}((T,+\infty);\mathrm{d}t/t^{3})
there exists a unique
\psi
such that

(4.10)

\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\xi(z,T),[T,+\infty)}(\tilde{\varphi}(z,\cdot))[\psi,\eta]+\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\langle\mu_{0}\psi(t),\eta(t)\rangle}{t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\langle f(t),\eta(t)\rangle}{t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t,\ \mbox{ for every }\ \eta\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty).

As a consequence, it results defined an operator
\mathcal{F}:L^{2}((T,+\infty);\mathrm{d}t/t^{3})\to L^{2}((T,\infty);\mathrm{d}t/t^{3})
such that
\mathrm{im}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
, and also such that
\mathcal{F}(f)=\psi
solving (
4.10
). From Lemma
2.1
,
\mathcal{F}
is compact (and positive), and hence standard techniques imply that (
4.9
) admits a sequence of eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\lambda_{n}\leq\cdots\to+\infty,

such that

(4.11)

\lambda_{1}(\xi(z,T),T)=\min_{\psi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty),\ \psi\not\equiv 0}\frac{\int_{T}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))\psi(t),\psi(t)\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t}{\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t}.

We define the function
\Lambda
as:

\Lambda(z,t)=\lambda_{1}(\xi(z,t),t).

Remark 4.10
.

By the variational formulation of eigenvalues as minmax, we can write the
k
-th eigenvalue as

\Lambda_{k}(z,T)=\min_{M\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty),\ \dim(M)=k}\max_{M\setminus\{0\}}\mathcal{R}_{a}(\psi),

where
\mathcal{R}_{a}(\psi)
is the quotient in (
4.11
).

Remark 4.11
.

Given
z\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
, we have that
t\mapsto\Lambda(z,t)
is strictly decreasing. Indeed, for every fixed
\delta>0
, if we extend by zero in
(t,t+\delta)
an eigenfunction associated with
\Lambda(z,t+\delta)
, we obtain a function
\bar{\psi}
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
such that
\mathcal{R}(\bar{\psi})=\Lambda(z,t+\delta)
. Hence, by the characterization of
\Lambda(z,t)
given by (
4.11
) we have
\Lambda(z,t)\leq\Lambda(z,t+\delta)
. Moreover,
if
\Lambda(z,t+\delta)=\Lambda(z,t)=\lambda
, for some
\delta>0
, one can extend by zero an eigenfunction of (
4.9
) associated with
\lambda
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(t+\delta,+\infty)
to an eigenfunction of (
4.9
) associated with
\lambda
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(T,+\infty)
. This is impossible since the eigenfunctions of (
4.9
) cannot be zero in an open set of their domain, thanks to unique continuation due merely to the continuity of
\nabla^{2}U(\xi(z,t))
. Hence, for every
z\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
, there exists a unique
t
such that
\Lambda(z,t)=0
.

Remark 4.12
.

We notice that there exists
\mathcal{U}_{x}
, a neighborhood of
x
, such that

\Gamma\cap\mathcal{U}_{x}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,y=\xi(z,t),\,\mbox{ for some }\,(t,z)\in(1-\varepsilon,T_{0})\times\mathcal{B}_{0}\,\mbox{ such that }\,\Lambda(z,t)=0\}.

For every

, if
(z,t)
is such that
\xi(z,t)=x
, then
\xi(\xi(z,t-t^{\prime}),t^{\prime})=x
.

Remark 4.13
.

For every
\mathcal{W}
transversal section to
\xi
containing
z_{0}
, we can define

\xi_{\mathcal{W}}(z,t)=\xi(z,t),\ \mbox{ for every }\ z\in\mathcal{W},t\in(1-\varepsilon,T_{0}).

Consequently, we can define

Again,

is strictly decreasing. Hence, for every

, there exists a unique

such that

.

4.3.3.
Case
\Lambda(z_{0},1)
simple

Since
\lambda_{1}(x_{0},1)<\lambda_{2}(x_{0},1)
, then there exists
\delta
such that
\Lambda(z_{0},t)
is simple (i.e.
\lambda_{1}(\xi(z_{0},t),t)<\lambda_{2}(\xi(z_{0},t),t)
) for

. Let
t\mapsto\psi(t,T)
be the first eigenfunction in (
4.9
) associated with
\Lambda(T):=\Lambda(z_{0},T)
such that

\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\dot{\psi}(t,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t=1.

Denoting
\psi_{T}(t,T):=\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\psi(t,T)
, we use (
4.11
) to compute
\Lambda(T)
:

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T}\Lambda(T)&=-\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+\int_{T}^{+\infty}2\langle\dot{\psi}_{T}(t,T),\dot{\psi}_{T}(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+2\langle V(t)\psi_{T}(t,T),\psi_{T}(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+2\langle\psi_{T}(t,T),\dot{\psi}_{T}(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\bigg|_{T}^{+\infty}+2\int_{T}^{+\infty}-\langle\ddot{\psi}(t,T),\psi_{T}(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+\langle V(t)\psi_{T}(t,T),\psi(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-2\langle\psi_{T}(T,T),\dot{\psi}_{T}(T,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}+2\lambda_{1}(T)\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\langle\psi(t,T),\psi_{T}(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

From the boundary condition and the fact that we supposed
\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\dot{\psi}(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t\equiv 1\ \forall T
, it follows

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T}\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{\|\dot{\psi}(t,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t=0,

which implies

\int_{T}^{+\infty}\frac{2\langle\psi(t,T),\psi_{T}(t,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}}{t^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}t=0.

Besides, from the boundary condition, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\psi(T,T)=\dot{\psi}(T,T)+\psi_{T}(T,T)=0.

We can thus conclude that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T}\Lambda(T)=-\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}-2\langle\psi_{T}(T,T),\dot{\psi}(T,T)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=-\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+2\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\|\dot{\psi}(T,T)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}>0.

This implies that we can apply the Implicit Function Theorem to obtain that the function
t(z)
, defined for all
z\in\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}
such that

\Lambda(z,t)=0\ \mbox{ if and only if }\ (z,t)=(z,t(z)),

is smooth. The same holds if one considers
\Lambda_{\mathcal{W}}
instead of
\Lambda
, implying that for every transversal section
\mathcal{W}
,
\Lambda_{\mathcal{W}}(z,t)=0
if and only if
t=t_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
is smooth on a neighborhood of
z_{0}
in
\mathcal{W}
, that is,
\mathcal{V}_{z_{0}}=\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}\cap\mathcal{W}
.

Therefore,
\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x}
is the image through
\xi_{\mathcal{W}}
of a graph of a smooth function.
Since
\Gamma^{\prime}=\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{k}
, where

(4.12)

\Gamma_{k}=\{x=\xi(z,t):\,z\in\mathcal{W},\,\lambda_{1}(\xi(z,t),t)\leq\lambda_{2}(\xi(z,t),t)+1/k\},

then

-rectifiable. Indeed, if we define

On the other hand, fixed

Differentiating (

such that
\Phi(z,1)
has rank at most
d(N-1)-2
.

Hence, by Sard-type lemma applied as in
[
4
, Lemma 6.6.1]
to the function
F
, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x})\leq\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(F(\{z:\,\mathrm{rk}\,F_{z}(z)\leq d(N-1)-2\}))=0
, for every
\varepsilon>0
. This, in particular, implies that
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x}
is (at least)
(d(N-1)-1)
-rectifiable. Again, since
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
is a closed set, then there are (at most) countably many
x_{k}
such that
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x_{k}}
, where each
\mathcal{U}_{x_{k}}
is a proper neighborhood of such
x_{k}
.

∎

4.5.
Estimate on the Hausdorff measure of
\Gamma\setminus\Sigma

Fix
x_{0}\in\Gamma\setminus\Sigma
, and let
z_{0}=\gamma^{x_{0}}(T_{0})
. Then, for every
z\in B_{R}(z_{0})
, they are defined

\theta(z)\ \mbox{generator of }\mathrm{Ker}\,\Phi(z,t(z)),\quad v(z)=\dot{\xi}(z,T_{0}).

Observe that
\theta(z)
and
v(z)
are not parallel. Since
\eta(\cdot)=\dot{\xi}(z,\cdot)
satisfies

\begin{cases}\ddot{\eta}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\nabla^{2}U(\xi)\eta\\
\eta(T_{0})=v(z)\\
\eta\in\mathcal{D}^{1,2}(1,T_{0}),\end{cases}

then it must hold
\eta(t)=\Phi(z,t)\,v(z)
. If
v(z)=k\,\theta(z)
, for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\eta(t(z))=0
, and hence
\dot{\xi}(z,t(z))=0
. This contradicts at
t=t(z)
the conservation of the mechanical energy, since
h\geq 0
and
U>0
.

Now, consider the vector space of dimension
d(N-1)-2
, orthogonal to
\theta_{0},v_{0}
, and denote it by
W_{0}
. For each
w\in W_{0}
, we can define
\eta_{w}=\eta_{w}(s)
by

\begin{cases}\dot{\eta}_{w}=\theta(\eta_{w}(s))\\
\eta_{w}(0)=z_{0}+w.\end{cases}

We can then define
\mathcal{W}
a
(d(N-1)-1)
-dimensional sub-manifold of
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, directly through the parameterization
U\ni(w,s)\mapsto\sigma(w,s)=\eta_{w}(s)
. It is plain to verify that
\partial_{s}\sigma(w,s)=\theta(\eta_{w}(s))
and that
\partial_{w}\sigma(w,0)\in W_{0}
. This in turn implies that
v(\eta_{w}(0))\notin T_{\eta_{w}(0)}\mathcal{W}
. As a consequence, up to defining
\sigma
in a subset of
U
,
v(\eta_{w}(s))\notin T_{\eta_{w}(s)}\mathcal{W}
, for every
(w,s)\in\mathcal{W}
.

Starting from the chart
(U,\sigma)
, we compute

\partial_{s}\hat{\xi}(w,s,t(w,s))=\partial_{s}(\xi(\sigma(w,s,t(w,s))))=\partial_{z}\xi(\sigma(w,s,t(w,s)))\,\partial_{s}\sigma(w,s,t(w,s))=\\
\partial_{z}\xi(z,t)\,\theta(z)+\partial_{t}\xi(z,t(z))\,\langle\nabla t(z),\theta(z)\rangle.

For the second addendum of the last line above, we can adapt
[
4
, Proposition 6.6.8]
to our case by proving that for every
z\in\mathcal{V}_{z_{0}}\subset\mathcal{B}_{0}
such that
\xi(z,t(z))\notin\Sigma
, it holds
\langle\nabla t(z),\theta(z)\rangle=0
. Indeed, as in
[
4
]
, if
\langle\nabla t(\bar{z}),\theta(\bar{z})\rangle\neq 0
, in view of
[
4
, Lemmas 6.6.6 and 6.6.11]
, it holds that for a
\rho>0
, the equation

\xi(z,t(\bar{z}))=\bar{x}+s\,w

does not admit any solution
z\in B_{\rho}(\bar{z})
, if
s<0
is small enough,
w
is a generator of
\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(\bar{z},t(\bar{z}))^{\perp}
, and
\bar{x}=\xi(\bar{z},t(\bar{z}))
. Hence, as in the proof of
[
4
, Proposition 6.6.8]
, but involving
\mathcal{J}_{\xi(\bar{z},t(\bar{z})),t(\bar{z})}
given in (
4.8
) instead of
J_{\bar{t}}
, there exists a sequence of arcs
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
where every
\varphi_{n}
minimizes
\mathcal{J}_{x_{n},t(\bar{z})}
with
x_{n}=\xi(\bar{z},t(\bar{z}))\pm(1/n)\,w\to\xi(\bar{z},t(\bar{z}))
, and is such that
\varphi_{n}(T_{0})+r_{0}(T_{0})\notin B_{\rho}(\bar{z})
. Up to a subsequence, passing to the limit for
n\to\infty
, we obtain another minimizer of
\mathcal{J}_{\xi(\bar{z},t(\bar{z})),t(\bar{z})}
given in (
4.8
), that is a contradiction.

This proves that
\mathrm{d}\xi_{\mathcal{W}}(z,t(z))\theta(z)=0
, and hence
\mathrm{rk}(\mathrm{d}\xi_{\mathcal{W}}(z,t(z)))\leq d(N-1)-2
.

The application of the Sard-type Lemma on Banach manifolds
[
27
]
implies that

\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x})\leq\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\{\xi_{\mathcal{W}}(z,t(z)):\,z\in\mathcal{W}\})=0,

for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Now, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider

\Gamma_{k}=\{x\in\Gamma^{\prime}:\,\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\lambda_{1}(x)+1/k\},

that is a closed subset of
\Gamma
. Hence, for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}

\Gamma_{k}\cap B(0,n)=\bigcup_{x\in\Gamma_{k}}(\Gamma_{k}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x}\cap B(0,n))=\bigcup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\in\Gamma_{k}}(\Gamma_{k}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x_{i}}\cap B(0,n)),

where
B(0,n)
is the ball centered at the origin with ray
n
. Therefore,

\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{k}\cap B(0,n))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{k}\cap\mathcal{U}_{x_{i}}\cap B(0,n))=0.

It follows

\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{k})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{k}\cap B(0,n))=0

and

\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma^{\prime})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{k})=0.

Since
we already proved that
\mathcal{H}^{d(N-1)-2+\varepsilon}(\Gamma^{\prime\prime})=0
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma\setminus\Sigma)\leq d(N-1)-2.
