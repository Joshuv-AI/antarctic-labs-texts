Association Between Pre-Transplant Psoas Sarcopenia and Perioperative Outcomes in Lung Transplant Recipients: A Single-Centre Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Garance Vaillant, Nathalie Zappella, Elie Kantor, Kinan El Husseini, Loukbi Saker, Enora Atchade, Philippe Montravers, Linda Hajouji, Hervé Mal, Pierre Mordant, Antoine Khalil, Jonathan Messika, Vincent Bunel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLung transplantation (LTx) is the solid organ transplantation with the comparatively worst prognosis. Therefore, improving candidate selection and risk stratification for these patients has become a high-priority research focus. Incorporating objective markers of frailty such as sarcopenia in the pretransplant evaluation may help achieve this goal. We evaluated the association between CT-measured psoas muscle sarcopenia and early LTx outcomes.MethodsWe performed a retrospective study including patients who underwent LTx from 2014 to 2018 at our institution with available chest and abdominal CT scans in the year prior to LTx. The psoas cross-sectional area was measured, and previously established sex-specific cutoffs were used to define sarcopenia. We compared sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic LTx recipients regarding 1-year mortality and perioperative outcomes.ResultsA total of 140 patients were included, who received LTx primarily for interstitial lung disease (ILD) (n = 75, 53%) or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (n = 54, 39%). Forty-six (33%) were sarcopenic. We found no association between psoas sarcopenia and 1-year all-cause mortality (multivariable P = .13) nor perioperative complications or FEV1 at 1 year post-LTx. Exploratory subgroup analysis revealed an association between psoas sarcopenia and 1-year all-cause mortality in patients with ILD (multivariable P = .042).ConclusionsPre-established sex-specific cutoffs for psoas sarcopenia did not reach statistical significance for 1-year mortality or perioperative outcomes in the overall cohort; given the limited statistical power, a clinically meaningful association cannot be excluded. An exploratory signal in ILD patients warrants further investigation. Future studies may benefit from disease-specific threshold validation and the integration of muscle strength and physical performance alongside muscle mass assessment.

INTRODUCTION

Despite progress in recent years, lung transplantation (LTx) remains the organ transplant with the worst median survival (6.7 years in 2019).1,2 To improve these results, enhancing candidate selection is critical. Clinicians aim to select patients who are in relatively good general condition despite advanced severe chronic respiratory failure. The International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines published in 2021 may help clinicians in the selection process;3 however, some criteria may be less robust than others, such as strict age cutoffs4–6 or nutritional status surrogates like body mass index (BMI). Validating new objective criteria for nutritional and muscular health could help improve candidate selection.7 Sarcopenia is one such marker, which can be quantified through physical performance tests and muscle mass measurement on imaging. Muscle mass quantification using CT scans can rely on 3D CT reconstruction of some muscles,8 or more simply on the measurement of the cross-sectional area of the psoas,9 which provides a reliable estimate of total body skeletal muscle volume,10 but suffers from a lack of standardization for clinical use.11 In the context of LTx studies, pretransplant sarcopenia has been assessed on chest or abdominal CT using a variety of techniques and muscle groups.12–18 Recently, Hsu et al.16 established sex-specific fixed cutoffs for psoas cross-sectional area to define sarcopenia in LTx candidates.

In this study, we aimed to retrospectively apply these cutoffs to LTx recipients in our institution, to determine whether psoas sarcopenia was associated with poorer post-LTx outcomes.

METHODS

Study design

We retrospectively reviewed all patients who underwent LTx at Bichat University Hospital in Paris from January 2014 to December 2018. Patients were included if they had an available thoracic-abdominopelvic CT scan performed less than 1 year before LTx. Exclusion criteria were age <18 years at the time of LTx, retransplantation, or death within the first day after LTx. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Institutional Review Board [00006477] of HUPNVS, Paris 7 University, AP-HP and EudraCT/IRB: 2017 A01714-49). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Storage of data from our patients was consistent with requirements outlined in the WMA Declaration of Taipei.

Data collection

Data were collected retrospectively from the electronic health records and from a prospectively maintained clinical database at our institution. Perioperative data included age, sex, BMI, frailty index (quantified using the deficit accumulation model proposed by Mitnitski et al.19 in which a frailty index is computed as the proportion of health deficits spanning multiple physiological domains), type of respiratory disease, history of diabetes, use of corticosteroids >5 mg per day or steroid pulse in the year before LTx, long-term oxygen therapy, non-invasive ventilation, 6-min walk distance, right heart catheterization data before LTx, biological analysis before LTx, median time from CT scan to LTx, pulmonary rehabilitation before LTx, use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) before LTx, and time on the LTx waiting list and LTx procedure details.

Psoas sarcopenia assessment

We assessed psoas sarcopenia using techniques previously validated by our team.20 Briefly, psoas cross-sectional area was measured at the L4 lumbar vertebral level, where both transverse processes were visible, using the most recently performed CT scan available prior to LTx. Investigators were blinded to the patients’ identities. Measurements were manual, circling the edge of the left and right psoas, and expressed in square centimetres standardized to patient height squared (cm2/m2). A senior radiologist (LS) reviewed 100% of the CT scans, and each clinician (1 senior and 1 junior) reviewed 50% of the scans. We used the mean of the radiologist and clinician measurements (previously reported intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.93).20 Sarcopenia was defined according to the sex-specific cutoffs determined by Hsu et al.16 under 6.4 cm2/m2 for women and 7.8 cm2/m2 for men.

Outcome definitions

The primary judgement criterion was 1-year mortality. Secondary criteria included the following perioperative outcomes: grade 3 primary graft dysfunction (PGD) at 72 hr; post-LTx use of ECMO and duration; ventilatory weaning (length of mechanical ventilation [MV], use and duration of tracheotomy); pneumonia occurrence in the intensive care unit (ICU) defined by use of antibiotics according to the clinician’s assessment; thoracic wall and abdominal complications requiring surgery in the ICU; need for continuous renal replacement therapy; acute cellular rejection defined by administration of steroid pulse; alloimmunization requiring treatment;21 airway complications requiring interventional bronchoscopy and dehiscence visualized by bronchoscopy according to ISHLT guidelines;22 ICU length of stay, ICU readmission, and ICU death. We also recorded forced expiratory volume in 1 sec at 1 year after LTx and number of hospital days in the first year.

Statistical analysis

Continuous parametric variables are described with mean ± SD and non-parametric variables with median (interquartile range). The normal distribution of variables was assessed with QQ plots. We used Student’s t-test to compare continuous parametric variables and Wilcoxon’s rank sum test for continuous nonparametric variables. Categorical variables are described with number (%). A chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables, depending on sample size. Survival analysis involved the log-rank test, Kaplan–Meier curves and a Cox model for multivariable analysis. For multivariable analysis, covariables were selected according to clinical relevance and statistical significance (P < .1). Schoenfeld residuals were used to test the proportional-hazards assumption in the Cox model. Variables defined as clinically correlated were removed from the model. Results are presented as hazard ratios with 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses were performed, stratifying on respiratory disease in the 2 main disease groups (interstitial lung disease (ILD) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)). Data were analysed using R statistical software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied; all subgroup and sensitivity analyses were pre-specified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

RESULTS

Population, pre- and post-LTx data

From 2014 to 2018, 226 patients underwent LTx at Bichat University Hospital; 140 patients were included in the study (Figure 1). The mean age was 55 ± 9 years, and 96 (69%) patients were men. The most common diagnosis before LTx was ILD (n = 75, 53%), followed by COPD (n = 54, 39%).

Flow Chart of Patients Undergoing Lung Transplantation (LTx) From January 2014 to December 2018 at Bichat University Hospital (Paris). Abbreviation: CT: computed tomography.

The median time from CT scan to LTx was 120 [51-227] days. The cross-sectional area of the psoas was 6.33 ± 1.35 cm2/m2 in female patients, 8.94 ± 1.77 cm2/m2 in male patients and varied between disease groups in male patients (P < .01) (Figure 2). Forty-six (33%) patients were sarcopenic. Women accounted for 50% of the sarcopenic population but only 22% of non-sarcopenic patients (P < .001). Patient demographics and LTx data are presented in Table 1. More patients had undergone pulmonary rehabilitation in the year before LTx in the sarcopenic than in the non-sarcopenic group (P = .0014). Patients had spent 91 ± 124 days on the waiting list. LTx was performed according to the French high-emergency allocation procedure for 17 (12%) patients. Seven (5%) patients had ECMO as a bridge to LTx. Many patients had bilateral LTx (n = 82, 59%). When unilateral LTx was performed, the right lung was more often transplanted (n = 35, 60%). The mean surgery time was 388 ± 118 min; 71% of patients required ECMO during surgery. Body mass index was significantly lower for sarcopenic recipients (22 ± 4.6 vs 25.1 ± 4.2 kg/m2, P < .001).

Survival analysis

The all-cause 1-year mortality rate was 26%: 28% in the sarcopenic group and 24% in the non-sarcopenic group (P > .9) (Figure 3, Table 2). Multivariable analysis using variables significantly associated with mortality on univariable analysis showed an independent association between male sex and mortality but no significant association between mortality and sarcopenia (HR 1.77 [0.84-3.74], P = .13), BMI, pre-LTx corticosteroid use, pre-LTx systolic pulmonary artery pressure or post-LTx use of Rituximab as a surrogate for alloimmunization (Table 3).

Secondary outcomes

Perioperative and functional outcomes are presented in Table S1. The sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups did not differ in ICU stay duration, grade 3 PGD occurrence, pneumonia occurrence, surgical complications, renal failure, rejection events, or airway complications. At 1 year, FEV1 reached similar levels in sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients (70 ± 23% predicted vs 65 ± 21% predicted, respectively). In the first year following LTx, sarcopenic patients spent 100 [60-136] days in hospital vs 84.5 [51.3-128] days for non-sarcopenic patients (P = .42).

Subgroup analyses

Exploratory subgroup multivariable analysis of ILD and COPD patients involved the variables sex, BMI, surface of the psoas in cm2/m2 (sarcopenia) and systolic pulmonary artery pressure. We added to the model pre-LTx use of corticosteroids, post-LTx use of rituximab and forced vital capacity for ILD patients and forced expiratory volume in 1 sec for COPD patients (Tables S2 and S3). Psoas sarcopenia before LTx was associated with overall mortality during the first year post-LTx for ILD patients (P = .042) but not COPD patients (P > .9).

DISCUSSION

In our study, previously proposed cutoffs defining pre-LTx psoas sarcopenia were not associated with 1-year overall mortality nor complications after LTx; only in an exploratory subgroup analysis of ILD patients was sarcopenia predictive of increased mortality.

Considering the importance of muscle mass and quality in LTx and in other thoracic surgery candidates,23 these results are overall disappointing. However, post-hoc power analysis using the Schoenfeld formula indicated that with 36 events and a sarcopenia prevalence of 33%, the study had 36% power to detect a hazard ratio of 1.77 at a 2-sided α of .05. Approximately 425 patients would have been required for 80% power. The minimum detectable hazard ratio with 80% power was 2.70, suggesting the study was substantially underpowered for moderate effect sizes. These results are nonetheless coherent with previous studies assessing LTx outcomes according to pretransplant sarcopenia defined by CT cross-sectional area of muscles, which relied on various muscle groups and surface thresholds for defining sarcopenia and reported inconsistent findings.24

Psoas sarcopenia (cross-sectional area at L4) did not impact 1-year mortality according to Weig et al.13 as in our study. Conversely, Hsu et al.16 showed a large difference in 3-year survival between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic LTx recipients when sarcopenia was defined by their cutoffs. Unfortunately, we did not confirm these results when using the same sex-specific cutoffs in our LTx recipient population.

Several key factors may explain our results. First, the difference in typology of patients (older age and higher proportion of ILD patients in our study), 1-year mortality (15% vs 26% in our study), and median time from CT scan to LTx (2.8 months vs 4 months in our study) could limit the generalizability of these cutoffs.

Secondly, markers of frailty, like BMI, albumin level, and frailty score were within normal limits in both our groups, so sarcopenia may have been relatively mild in affected patients. Screening for LTx according to the 2021 ISHLT guidelines may exclude patients with abnormal BMI and albumin levels and thus truly frail and sarcopenic patients.3 In addition, half of the patients in the sarcopenic group had undergone physical rehabilitation before LTx, which could have influenced muscle mass and LTx prognosis. However, because of missing data, we do not know whether pulmonary rehabilitation was performed before or after the CT scan.

Third, we show high variability between ILD and COPD patients regarding psoas cross-sectional area, despite relatively homogeneous ages. In COPD patients, quadriceps strength but not abdominal muscle strength tends to be reduced,25 but we evidenced more sarcopenia in these patients. It is conceivable that the differences in disease evolution between individuals with ILD and COPD could influence the kinetics of sarcopenia, and the differences shown in subgroup analyses raise the question of disease-specific sarcopenia definitions, even though these analyses were exploratory in nature and lacked statistical power.

More broadly, these observations raise a fundamental question about the transferability of externally derived cutoffs. Muscle mass and strength evolve dynamically throughout life11 and peak between the ages of 30 and 40 years11 before declining more rapidly for muscle strength.26 Initial muscle mass varies with age but also with birth weight27 and ethnic origin.28 Throughout life, muscle mass is influenced by diet29 and intensity of physical activity, which may allow muscle mass to peak at age 30 but also may limit its decline. Using a single fixed threshold across this heterogeneity risks systematic misclassification in both directions: underestimating sarcopenia in patients with high baseline muscle mass (eg, younger COPD males) and overestimating it in those with constitutionally lower mass. Data-driven, disease-specific, or centre-adapted thresholds, derived from sufficiently large prospective registries, may ultimately be required before CT-based psoas sarcopenia can serve as a reliable clinical decision tool in LTx candidate evaluation.

Third, we question whether psoas cross-sectional area measured on a CT scan is a reliable surrogate for total-body skeletal muscle mass. In a cohort of patients with ovarian cancer, psoas cross-sectional area at L3 was not representative of total skeletal muscle area, which includes all muscles on a CT scan slice.30 Therefore, the cross-sectional area of a muscle might not be representative of whole-body sarcopenia. Inclusion of a larger muscle area may reduce the burden of measurement error and increase the reliability of the measurement. However, in the LTx population, results are inconsistent in terms of mortality and early complications when total skeletal muscle area is used.12,18

The difficulty of evidencing a prognostic effect in our study and others could also be altogether explained by an incomplete assessment of sarcopenia through CT scans alone, as this notion encompasses muscle strength and physical performance in addition to muscle mass. Indeed, Rozenberg et al.31 in a population of 50 LTx recipients, found that LTx candidates had low quadriceps strength and low physical performance rather than low grip strength and, even more so, low muscle mass as measured by bioelectrical impedance. As well, the greater the number of muscle deficits (grip strength, quadriceps strength, muscle mass, physical performance), the longer the post-LTx hospital stay. Individual low quadriceps strength but not low muscle mass was associated with length of hospitalization stay. These results illustrate the complexity of sarcopenia and the heterogeneity of the LTx recipient population.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-centre study with unavoidable missing data. Many patients could not be included due to the absence of a recent CT scan, as systematic data were not collected for excluded candidates; the direction and magnitude of any resulting selection bias cannot be formally assessed. In addition, more patients in the sarcopenic group had pulmonary rehabilitation before LTx, which may have influenced muscle mass and LTx prognosis. Unfortunately, because of the retrospective design, we were unable to determine whether pulmonary rehabilitation occurred before or after the CT scan.

The multivariable Cox model included 6 covariates with 36 events, yielding an events-per-variable ratio below the commonly recommended thresholds. This may limit model stability and the precision of individual coefficient estimates; results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Formal assessment of multicollinearity using variance inflation factors confirmed that simultaneous inclusion of BMI and CT-defined sarcopenia did not introduce collinearity (all VIF < 1.20), supporting the validity of their joint modelling. We did not adjust for age, which is an important determinant of muscle mass, but the 2 groups did not differ significantly in mean age, and age was not associated with mortality in univariable analysis in our cohort. Nonetheless, age remains a clinically relevant confounder of both muscle mass and post-transplant outcomes; future adequately powered studies should strive to include age in multivariable models. Furthermore, because of the retrospective design, we were unable to determine whether pulmonary rehabilitation occurred before or after the CT scan used to define sarcopenia. If rehabilitation followed the scan, muscle mass deficit may have been attenuated before lung transplant, suggesting that sarcopenia prevalence and its prognostic effect may be underestimated in our cohort.

Another limitation was the heterogeneity of the population in terms of underlying diagnosis (ILD vs COPD) and ethnic origin (not recorded in French medical records), which prevents full adjustment for all factors influencing muscle mass. Furthermore, all-cause mortality as a primary outcome is clinically relevant but non-specific: because post-transplant death is attributable to many competing causes, detecting a survival difference is inherently difficult in small samples. Finally, although manual assessment of psoas cross-sectional area demonstrated satisfactory inter-rater agreement, measurement accuracy may be improved by automated contouring software that excludes vascular and adipose components from the muscle boundary.

On the other hand, we chose to use sarcopenia cutoffs determined in another study rather than define these cutoffs using ad hoc percentile or median values in our own population, which could have magnified the statistical effect but would have reduced generalizability. Secondly, to our knowledge, this is the largest cohort study to evaluate psoas sarcopenia as a prognostic factor in LTx. Finally, we assessed an original patient-centric outcome, the number of hospital days in the year after LTx, which is a relatively new concept in the LTx field.32

CONCLUSION

In our cohort, the pre-established sex-specific cutoffs used to define psoas sarcopenia did not identify LTx recipients at increased risk of morbidity or mortality; given the study’s limited power, this should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of absence of effect. Whether alternative, population-adapted thresholds would perform better remains an open question, particularly given the exploratory association observed in ILD patients. Applying externally derived cutoffs to heterogeneous transplant populations may be inherently limited, and measurement of psoas cross-sectional area alone may be insufficient to capture global sarcopenia. Future studies in LTx recipients would benefit from prospective validation of disease-specific thresholds and from integrating a multimodal assessment combining CT-derived muscle mass (automated total skeletal muscle index ± psoas area), objective strength (handgrip) and a brief performance test (SPPB or 4-m gait speed), measured within a defined pre-transplant window.
