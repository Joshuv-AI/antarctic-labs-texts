Identification of cancer-associated fibroblast signature genes for potential biomarkers in cholangiocarcinoma: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Sheng Hu, Lu Chen, Lixin Liu, Xiaowen Zhang, Chuqi Xia, Ya Zhu, Hao Zou, Tao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), integral components of the tumor microenvironment, substantially influence cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) progression and clinical outcomes. This study aimed to identify CAF-related genes implicated in CCA pathogenesis.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CCA and non-tumorous samples were extracted from the Gene Expression Omnibus GSE107943 dataset, then intersected with cancer-associated fibroblast signature genes (CAFSGs) to derive DE-CAFSGs. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was subsequently conducted to determine causally linked biomarkers. The diagnostic efficacy of these biomarkers was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using data from GSE107943 and the TCGA-CHOL cohort. Functional annotation of representative DE-CAFSGs was performed through single-gene gene set enrichment analysis. The expression trends of these biomarkers were preliminarily explored using RT-PCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsA total of 135 DE-CAFSGs were identified, among which GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 emerged as CCA-associated biomarkers. GRN and PLXNC1 were identified as risk-associated, while ORMDL3 exhibited a protective association. Each demonstrated robust discriminatory capacity, with area under the ROC curve values exceeding 0.8 in both datasets. Enrichment analysis revealed significant associations with pathways including retinol metabolism, cytochrome P450-mediated drug metabolism, and the complement and coagulation cascades.ConclusionGRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 represent key CAF-associated genes in CCA, shedding light on potential molecular mechanisms of disease development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04286-1.Keywords: Cholangiocarcinoma, Cancer-associated fibroblast signature genes, Differentially expressed analysis, Mendelian randomization, Enrichment analysis1

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), including intrahepatic and periportal distal biliary epithelial malignancies [1], continues to exhibit rising incidence and mortality worldwide [2–4]. Early-stage CCA is frequently asymptomatic, resulting in late-stage diagnosis for the majority of patients, of whom only approximately 35% qualify for curative surgical resection [5]. For unresectable or metastatic cases, palliative gemcitabine-cisplatin (GC) chemotherapy remains the standard regimen, although median overall survival typically remains under 12 months. The asymptomatic onset and anatomical inaccessibility of early lesions further hinder timely diagnosis [6]. Nevertheless, advanced molecular diagnostics such as mutation profiling and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) have gained relevance in clinical practice [7, 8]. Integration of genomic characterization with adjuvant therapeutic strategies offers potential to improve patient outcomes, particularly given the role of genetic heterogeneity in therapeutic resistance. In recent years, Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a widely applied tool in exploring causality in complex diseases, including cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and diabetes mellitus [9–11]. Although MR applications in CCA remain limited, preliminary investigations have highlighted its utility in identifying etiological associations. Notably, MR analysis has implicated genetically predicted cholelithiasis as a risk factor for CCA [12]. MR serves as a robust genetic epidemiological method for predicting and elucidating the heritable risk factors underlying CCA. By leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables, MR enables causal inference regarding CCA pathogenesis, offering insights conducive to the development of early diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. This study applies MR to delineate the role of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in CCA progression, with the aim of revealing the molecular mechanisms that drive tumor development. CAFs, a distinct stromal cell population within the tumor microenvironment (TME), actively participate in tumor proliferation, metastasis, inflammatory modulation, and tissue remodeling. In CCA, they contribute to tumor aggressiveness by secreting a repertoire of cytokines and structural proteins—including fibronectin, collagen, and matrix metalloproteinases—that collectively promote cellular proliferation, invasion, and neovascularization [13, 14]. Experimental evidence indicates that suppression of CAF expansion and functional activity impedes CCA progression. Accordingly, pharmacological agents and immunotherapies directed against CAF-specific targets are under investigation as promising therapeutic modalities.

MR offers a robust framework for understanding causal relationships in genetic epidemiology by employing genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), thereby mitigating confounding and reverse causality. For valid inference, IVs must adhere to three core assumptions: relevance (a robust association with the exposure), independence (no association with confounders), and exclusion restriction (no direct effect on the outcome other than through the exposure) [15]. Figure 1 illustrates the criteria for genetic variants to qualify as valid IVs: (1) a statistically significant association with the exposure; (2) independence from confounding variables influencing the exposure-outcome relationship; and (3) an effect on the outcome mediated exclusively through the exposure [16]. Earlier MR studies suffered from limited sample sizes and insufficient genetic variation, impacting their validity. Advancements in genotyping technologies and the aggregation of large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) datasets have substantially enhanced the feasibility and precision of MR analyses. Nevertheless, current MR investigations into risk factors for CCA have largely been restricted to ethnic predispositions and gut microbiota, leaving critical molecular targets insufficiently explored [12, 17].

MR analysis explored the causal effects of DE-CAFSGs on CCA. The genetic variant in question meets three conditions: ① IVs must be related to exposure; ② IVs must not be associated with any confounders that may influence the association between exposure and outcome; ③ IVs can only be associated with outcome through exposure

The objective of this study was to identify potential CAFs-associated biomarkers in CCA by integrating transcriptomic sequencing with GWAS summary data for MR analysis, and to genetically assess the causative relationships involved. The investigation further examined the biological pathways implicated in these associations. Collectively, the findings offer a conceptual framework and empirical reference for advancing CCA research.

Materials and methods

Access of datasets and target genes

The expression matrix from the GSE107943 [18] training set was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), comprising intrahepatic CCA tumor samples (n = 30) and adjacent normal liver tissues (n = 27). For validation, the Cancer Genome Atlas-CCA (TCGA-CHOL) cohort was incorporated, consisting of transcriptomic data from 35 CCA patients and 9 non-tumor controls. Additionally, 596 cancer-associated fibroblast signature genes (CAFSGs) were compiled based on prior studies [19], with co-culture experiments indicating that CAFs primarily originate from substrate-invading cells [20]. The E-MTAB-6389 transcriptome dataset of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), obtained from the EMBL-EBI repository (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress/studies/E-MTAB-6389), including 109 tissue samples (31 controls; 78 cases), served as a supplementary validation set.

Differentially expressed CAFSGs (DE-CAFSGs) identification and enrichment analysis

Firstly, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CCA samples and controls in GSE107943 were identified using the R package “limma”, applying thresholds of |log2FoldChange|≥ 1 and adj. P < 0.05 [21]. Results were visualized via volcano plots and heat maps. Enrichment analyses were subsequently conducted for both the DEGs and 596 CAFSGs using the R package “clusterProfiler” [22], referencing the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (adj. P < 0.05).

Data summary and pre-processing based on GWAS

MR analyses were performed to investigate the association between DE-CAFSGs and CCA, utilizing expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data for DE-CAFSGs as the exposure and GWAS data for CCA as the outcome, both obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The CCA dataset (ieu-b-4915) included 372,366 individuals and 7,687,713 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). The ‘extract_instruments’ function in the R package ‘TwoSampleMR’ [23] was employed to identify SNPs significantly associated with the exposure (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸). Subsequently, IVs in linkage disequilibrium were pruned using the clumping parameters (clump = TRUE; r2 = 0.001; kb = 10,000) to retain only independent genetic variants.

MR analysis between DE-CAFSGs and CCA

Following harmonization of effect equipotential and effect size via the “harmonize_data” function, and subsequent exclusion of IVs using the “lasso_feature_selection” function, MR analysis was performed utilizing five algorithms within the “multiple” function framework: MR Egger [24], weighted median [25], simple mode, weighted mode [26], and inverse variance weighted (IVW) [27]. Causal inference between DE-CAFSGs and CCA primarily relied on IVW estimates, with results visualized through scatterplots, forest plots, and funnel plots. Sensitivity analyses were then applied to evaluate the robustness of MR findings, including assessments of heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, and leave-one-out (LOO) analysis. In this context, genes exhibiting statistically significant causal associations with CCA were designated as biomarkers.

Statistical efficacy analysis in MR analysis

MR statistical power analysis was conducted to evaluate the associations between three exposure variables (eqtl-a-ENSG00000030582, eqtl-a-ENSG00000172057, and eqtl-a-ENSG00000136040) and CCA-related outcomes using the ‘pwr’ package. The detection capacities of five MR methods—MR Egger, weighted median, inverse variance weighted (multiplicative random effects) (IVW), simple mode, and weighted mode—were systematically assessed to determine their sensitivity in identifying significant outcome associations.

Diagnostic performance analysis of pivotal biomarkers

The diagnostic value of MR-identified biomarkers in CCA was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis performed via the ‘pROC’ [28] package across GSE107943, E-MTAB-6389, and TCGA-CHOL datasets. Corresponding AUC values were calculated to quantify classification accuracy. Additionally, expression differentials between CCA samples and matched controls were examined within GSE107943 and TCGA-CHOL to support diagnostic relevance.

Establishment of the nomogram

To evaluate the discriminatory performance of selected biomarkers between control and disease groups, a nomogram model was developed using the ‘rms’ package in R. Model calibration was assessed via calibration plots generated with the ‘regplot’ package, while decision curve analysis (DCA), implemented through the ‘ggDCA’ package, quantified the net clinical benefit of the nomogram relative to each individual biomarker.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for biomarkers based on Spearman correlation analysis

To investigate the functional relevance and pathway associations of the identified biomarkers in CCA, single-gene GSEA [29] was conducted using the “GSEA” function in R. The analysis utilized KEGG gene sets from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) [30], guided by Spearman correlation coefficients between each biomarker and other genes in the GSE107943 dataset (adj.P < 0.05).

Specimen collection

Inclusion criteria included histopathological confirmation of CCA following curative-intent resection with clear surgical margins and no administration of preoperative adjuvant therapy. Exclusion parameters included: (i) histological ambiguity between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and CCA or the presence of concurrent neoplasms, (ii) a prior malignancy diagnosis within the preceding 5 years, and (iii) compromised tissue integrity due to marked autolysis, suboptimal fixation, or nucleic acid degradation. In accordance with these criteria, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) CCA samples and matched peritumoral non-neoplastic tissues were obtained from two patients. Tumor specimens were collected by selecting two to three representative regions from each hepatectomy sample, with sampling guided by lesion size. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval No. PJ-2021-216), and the study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, as mandated by national regulations.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

To preliminarily explore the mRNA expression levels of biomarkers, RT-PCR experiments were conducted. Total RNA was extracted from cryopreserved tissue using TRIzol (Ambion, Cat. No. 15596018CN) under RNase-free conditions. Reverse transcription of mRNA was performed on an S1000™ Thermal Cycler (BIO-RAD) employing the SweScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), strictly following the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting cDNA was diluted 5–20-fold in RNase/DNase-free ddH2O and subsequently used for mRNA quantification via the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Supermix for qPCR (gDNA digester plus) (Edson, Shanghai, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. mRNA expression was evaluated using RT-qPCR, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Amplification was conducted in 10 μl reaction volumes for 40 cycles on a CFX96 Real-Time Fluorescence PCR Instrument (BIO-RAD, Cat. No. XLFZ006), with thermal cycling conditions set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 80 s, followed by 40 cycles of annealing at 55 °C for 20 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s.

Western blot (WB)

The WB was performed to preliminarily detect the protein expression of biomarkers. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat. No. G2002-30ML), and concentrations were measured with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat. No. P0009). Electrophoretic separation of histones was performed via SDS-PAGE, followed by wet transfer onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Germany, Cat. No. K2MA8350E, 0.45 μm, 26.5 cm × 3.75 m). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat. No. CR2302110) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GRN (Bioss, Beijing, China, Cat. No. bs-0823R; 1:2000), PLXNC1 (Bioss, Beijing, China, Cat. No. bs-7492R; 1:1000), and ORMDL3 (Abmart, America, Cat. No. PH14016S; 1:1000). After rewarming, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied. Protein bands were visualized via chemiluminescence using a gel documentation system. Quantitative analysis was performed with ImageJ, and expression levels were graphically represented using GraphPad Prism.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm and subsequently deparaffinized with xylene and graded ethanol. Sections underwent incubation with primary antibodies (GRN, 1:100; PLXNC1, 1:100; ORMDL3, 1:500), followed by the application of a reaction enhancement solution and visualization using a DAB detection kit (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China, Cat. NO. ZLI-9019). Hematoxylin was applied for nuclear counterstaining, and neutral gel was used to seal the slides. Slide evaluation was performed independently by two investigators blinded to clinical data. Digital images of stained sections were acquired using a scanner at 200 × magnification, capturing the entire field of view with a 50 μm scale bar. Hematoxylin-stained nuclei appeared blue, whereas positive expression of GRN, ORMDL3, and PLXNC1 was indicated by brownish-yellow staining. Quantitative analysis was executed via ImageJ-pro-plus software, and histogram data were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Results

Identification of 135 DE-CAFSGs mainly enriched in regulation of fibrinolysis and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway

A total of 4,956 DEGs were identified in GSE107943 when comparing CCA samples to controls, including 2,820 upregulated and 2,136 downregulated genes (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table S1). Heat map visualization of the top 20 up- and downregulated DEGs highlighted distinct transcriptional patterns (Fig. 2b). Intersection of these DEGs with previously reported CAFSGs yielded 135 DE-CAFSGs subjected to subsequent enrichment analysis (Fig. 2c). Functional annotation revealed 330 GO terms—comprising 271 biological processes (BPs), 15 cellular components (CCs), and 44 molecular functions (MFs)—as well as 3 KEGG pathways. Notable GO categories included nuclear division, chromosomal region, and regulation of fibrinolysis, while KEGG analysis implicated the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, axon guidance, and complement and coagulation cascades (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Table S2–S3).

The training cohort underwent functional and pathway enrichment analysis. d The results of the Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of DE-CAFSGs were classified into three functional categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC and Molecular Function (MF). e Results of pathway enrichment analysis of DE-CAFSG by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)

Three DE-CAFSGs were causally associated with CCA supported by sensitivity analysis

Among the 135 DE-CAFSGs evaluated through MR analysis, only GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 demonstrated a causal relationship with CCA based on the IVW method (Supplementary Table S4). Detailed variant annotations were provided in Supplementary Table S5. According to Table 2 (The causal relationship between biomarkers and CCA in MR analysis), GRN (P = 0.0003; OR = 1.00081; 95% CI 1.0004–1.0013) and PLXNC1 (P = 0.461; OR = 1.00018; 95% CI 1.0000–1.0004) conferred increased risk, whereas ORMDL3 (P < 0.0001; OR = 0.9998; 95% CI 0.9997–0.9999) was associated with reduced susceptibility to CCA under the IVW model. Consistency between the scatter plot of causal estimates and the IVW-derived results was observed (Fig. 3a). Forest plots further supported the overall IVW estimates (Fig. 3b). Funnel plot symmetry of the top instrumental variables for each exposure implied adherence to Mendel’s law of random assortment (Fig. 3c). Sensitivity assessments were conducted to evaluate result robustness. Cochran's Q test and MR-Egger intercept confirmed the absence of heterogeneity (GRN: P = 0.7165; PLXNC1: P = 0.8758; ORMDL3: P = 0.9650) and horizontal pleiotropy (GRN: P = 0.4427; PLXNC1: P = 0.9901; ORMDL3: P = 0.8524) (Table 3: The results of heterogeneity test, Table 4: The results of horizontal pleiotropy). Additionally, LOO analysis did not introduce substantial bias into the causal estimates (Fig. 3d). Collectively, GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3, identified as causally linked to CCA, were selected as candidate biomarkers for subsequent analyses.

Statistical efficacy analysis of MR

The inverse variance weighted (multiplicative random effects) (IVW) method exhibited consistently high statistical power across all exposure variables. In contrast, MR Egger displayed markedly lower sensitivity, with power estimates frequently approaching 0.05, reflecting limited detection capability within this dataset. The weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods demonstrated variable performance, achieving substantial power under certain conditions. These discrepancies in statistical efficacy likely stem from the differing methodological sensitivities to underlying data structures. GRN, PLXNC1 and ORMDL3 took on decent diagnostic performances

Analysis of ROC curves revealed that GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 obtained AUC values exceeding 0.8 in both GSE107943 and TCGA-CHOL datasets (Fig. 4a, b), indicating robust predictive accuracy. In E-MTAB-6389, AUC values for the same genes surpassed 0.7, supporting their diagnostic relevance (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, expression analysis based on GSE107943 and TCGA-CHOL showed elevated levels of GRN and PLXNC1 in CCA samples relative to controls, whereas ORMDL3 exhibited reduced expression. Similar trends were observed in E-MTAB-6389, where PLXNC1 and ORMDL3 displayed significant differential expression compared to control samples (Fig. 4f).

GRN, PLXNC1 and ORMDL3 were mainly enriched in metabolism

To elucidate the functional roles of GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 in CCA progression, single-gene GSEA was performed using the KEGG gene set. All three biomarkers exhibited predominant enrichment in pathways related to retinol metabolism, drug metabolism, cytochrome P450 activity, and the complement and coagulation cascades (Fig. 6). GRN additionally appeared to influence steroid hormone biosynthesis and the catabolism of branched-chain amino acids including valine, leucine, and isoleucine. PLXNC1 was linked to peroxisome-associated metabolic processes. ORMDL3 showed involvement in xenobiotic metabolism mediated by cytochrome P450, along with participation in the degradation of valine, leucine, and isoleucine (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

CCA, the second most prevalent primary hepatic malignancy after HCC, is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, contributing to low resectability rates and unfavorable clinical outcomes. Recent evidence indicates that CAFs contribute to CCA progression through both autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, particularly by enhancing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes [31]. Cadamuro et al. [32] report that CCA cells enhance the activation of signaling pathways such as PDGFR-β, RHO-GTPase, JNK, AKT, ERK [33], and IL-6/STAT3, which in turn modulate resistance to chemotherapy [34]. Through the secretion of platelet-derived growth factor D (PDGF-D), CCA cells actively recruit CAFs, although the downstream regulatory dynamics remain insufficiently defined. Previous studies on prognostic gene profiling and biomarker identification in CCA often adopt divergent methodologies or do not address CAF-associated molecular features. This investigation applies MR analysis to examine the association between CAFs and CCA, aiming to uncover CAF-related biomarkers and characterize their regulatory landscape. Diagnostic validity was assessed through ROC curve analysis, yielding enhanced specificity and reproducibility. Quantitative expression levels of the candidate biomarkers were preliminarily evaluated in tumor and peritumoral tissues via RT-PCR, WB, and IHC, thereby reinforcing the analytical outcomes.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were primarily associated with retinol metabolism, xenobiotic processing via cytochrome P450 (CYP450), and the complement and coagulation cascades—pathways closely implicated in tumorigenic processes. Retinol, a vital micronutrient, exerts its physiological effects predominantly through its active metabolite, retinoic acid (RA), which regulates gene expression involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis by binding to nuclear receptors retinoic acid receptors (RARs) and retinoid X receptors (RXRs) [35]. Dysregulation or altered expression of RARs and RXRs has been documented in multiple malignancies, including HCC and prostate cancer [36, 37], potentially through AKT/p53 signaling-mediated suppression of apoptosis [38]. The retinol metabolic network influences antitumor immunity by modulating dendritic cell maturation and T-cell activation, thereby altering immune cell infiltration and functional dynamics within the TME [39]. In CCA, retinol metabolism is markedly disrupted [40]. Downregulation of retinoic acid receptor responder 1 (RARRES1) expression significantly promotes EMT, enhancing cancer cell motility and invasiveness [41]. All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), a natural retinol derivative, has demonstrated capacity to inhibit proliferation and invasion while inducing apoptosis in CCA cells, potentially via caspase pathway activation and cellular differentiation. These mechanisms highlight ATRA’s therapeutic relevance in CCA management [42]. GRN may influence AKT reactivation through modulating lysosomal function [43, 44]. However, there is currently no direct evidence demonstrating that GRN regulates CCA progression via retinol metabolism, which requires further experimental validation. CYP450 enzymes are integral to Phase I metabolic processes involving endogenous and exogenous substrates and exhibit a strong association with tumor susceptibility. Among DEGs in malignant biliary strictures, the CYP450 metabolic pathway emerged as the most significantly enriched signaling axis [45]. This study provides preliminary evidence implicating CYP450-mediated drug metabolism in CCA pathogenesis. Previous investigations identified specific isoforms, including CYP2A6 and CYP2E1, as contributors to cholangiocarcinogenesis and tumor progression under chronic cholangitis conditions via oxidative stress-induced DNA lesions [46]. Overexpression of CYP3A5 accelerates the catabolism of chemotherapeutic agents such as gemcitabine and 5-FU, thereby diminishing the therapeutic efficacy of adjuvant regimens [47]. Meanwhile, ORMDL3 has been found to regulate the activity of certain CYP450 subtypes through modulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, although the precise mechanisms remain to be further investigated [48]. The complement and coagulation cascades interact at multiple regulatory junctures in thrombotic and inflammatory contexts and consistently rank among the most enriched pathways in functional genomics analyses [49]. As a component of humoral immunity, the complement system comprises a network of proteins essential for host defense [50], mediating processes such as inflammation and apoptosis [51, 52]. The coagulation cascade, a key regulator of hemostasis, also intersects with immune signaling, and cross-talk between these systems has been implicated in several malignancies. Prognostic relevance of this pathway has been documented in renal cell carcinoma [53], melanoma, bladder cancer [54], HCC [55], and CCA [56]. Proposed mechanisms include coagulation-driven enhancement of tumor invasion and metastasis, and complement-mediated suppression of immune cytotoxicity. Within the TME, platelets have been shown to potentiate metastatic potential through activation of the coagulation cascade [57], PLXNC1 may regulate the aforementioned responses by promoting complement-mediated platelet activation through semaphorin 7A (SEMA7A) [58, 59].

In this study, MR analysis was conducted using 135 DE-CAFRGs as exposure variables and CCA as the outcome. GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 exhibited statistically significant causal relationships with CCA and have been identified as potential biomarkers. GRN, the gene encoding progranulin (PGRN), is located on chromosome 17q21.31. Following signal peptide cleavage, mature PGRN undergoes further proteolysis to generate multiple bioactive peptides (~ 6 kDa). Both full-length and cleaved forms participate in processes including tissue repair and cellular development, and show elevated expression across several malignancies such as ovarian, lung, colorectal, and liver cancers [60–63]. In an O. viverrini-induced CCA hamster model, Upontain et al. [64] reported enhanced GRN expression in biliary epithelial cells. PGRN has been implicated in cell cycle regulation by modulating G1 phase progression and inhibiting apoptosis, and may impact EMT in CCA cells via the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade [65]. The PI3K-Akt pathway plays a functional role in CCA tumorigenesis and invasion. KLF4, a transcription factor, has been shown to regulate this pathway by modulating COL3A7 expression, thereby affecting CCA cell proliferation and migration [66]. Validation using human CCA specimens revealed significantly elevated GRN expression in ICC tissues relative to adjacent non-tumor tissues, as confirmed by RT-PCR, WB, and IHC. In parallel, GRN has been associated with neurodegenerative disorders, where loss-of-function mutations diminish PGRN levels and lead to lysosomal impairment [67]. CCA cells exhibit elevated expression of lysosome-associated genes, including GRN, to enhance lysosomal function in response to increased demands for proliferation and invasion [68]. Enhanced V-ATPase activity in lysosomes has been shown to reverse the intra- and extracellular pH gradient, a condition that promotes tumor cell survival and proliferation [69]. Hydrolytic enzymes within lysosomes, particularly tissue protease B, contribute to extracellular matrix degradation, thereby supporting tumor invasion and metastatic dissemination [70]. Upregulation of GRN is postulated to promote CCA progression by reinforcing proliferation and invasion through EMT activation and lysosomal functional enhancement.

Located at 12q23, the PLXNC1 gene encodes a large transmembrane protein of the PLEXIN family, serving as a receptor for semaphorin 7A (SEMA7A) and mediating processes such as cell adhesion and metastasis. PLXNC1 has been implicated in the progression of various malignancies, including melanoma, pancreatic cancer, HCC, and colorectal cancer [71]. Elevated PLXNC1 expression in CCA tissues relative to normal controls correlates with the activation of EMT, TNF-α, and IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathways [72]. In tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), PLXNC1 modulates polarization and functional states, thereby impacting immune evasion [73]. Additionally, its interaction with integrins and ligands such as SEMA7A regulates intercellular adhesion and promotes tumor cell migration [73, 74]. Prior studies suggest that PLXNC1 expression exhibits context-specific variation: it is elevated in peritumoral regions under chronic inflammatory conditions, correlating with inflammation severity [75], while significantly lower expression is observed in normal tissues [76]. Additionally, PLXNC1 expression in peritumoral liver tissues often mirrors that in tumor regions, potentially due to shared immune microenvironmental elements [77]. In this study, PLXNC1 emerges as a promising CAF-related biomarker, supported primarily by evidence from MR analysis and its robust diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.8) across multiple independent cohorts. Previous studies have shown that PLXNC1 is upregulated in various malignancies, such as gastric and liver cancers, and promotes tumor progression by regulating pathways like IL6/STAT3 [72, 73]. In our preliminary exploratory experiments, PLXNC1 showed an upward trend in tumor tissues; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited validation sample size. Consequently, the potential of this marker is currently supported mainly by computational and literature evidence, which nonetheless provides partial support for its potential role in CCA. In the future, we will perform further validation in larger cohorts that include histologically normal control tissues to better clarify the clinical translational potential of PLXNC1.

ORMDL3 functions as a key modulator of sphingolipid metabolism, suppressing sphingolipid biosynthesis by inhibiting serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) activity within the endoplasmic reticulum [78]. This regulatory mechanism has been implicated in processes such as cell growth, proliferation, migration, invasion, and autophagy [79].

ORMDL3 suppresses type I interferon signaling, thereby impairing anti-tumor immune responses [78]. Elevated ORMDL3 expression exhibits an inverse association with type I interferon-stimulated genes (CCL5 and CXCL10) and CD8 + T cell activation markers (PRF1, GZMA, GZMB) within the TME [80], implying that ORMDL3 modulates immune infiltration and effector cell function, ultimately supporting tumor progression [81–83]. Experimental evidence by Sun et al. demonstrated that ORMDL3 knockdown in HCC cells enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of sorafenib by inhibiting autophagy via the PERK-ATF4-Beclin1 pathway [84]. While ORMDL3 expression in CCA remains uncharacterized in earlier reports, a related study identified significant upregulation of ORMDL1 in CCA relative to normal tissues [79]. Notably, integrative analysis of RT-PCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry from resected CCA samples consistently revealed elevated ORMDL3 expression compared to matched peritumoral controls. This divergence may be attributed to intrinsic differences between the MR study cohort and surgically obtained specimens, particularly in tumor subpopulation profiles. Additionally, the influence of tumor-adjacent microenvironmental alterations—common in highly invasive and heterogeneous malignancies such as CCA—may partially account for expression discrepancies observed across datasets. According to MR analysis, an OR < 1 for ORMDL3 suggests a potential inverse association with inflammatory mediators, indicating its interaction with immune-related factors. Future studies utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing or spatial transcriptomics will help elucidate the cell-type-specific expression and regulation of ORMDL3 across distinct cellular components of the cholangiocarcinoma tumor microenvironment—such as cancer cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and immune cells—thereby enabling a more precise interpretation of its seemingly contradictory genetic effects and expression patterns. Collectively, GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 serve as CAF-associated biomarkers implicated in CCA pathogenesis and may influence cellular phenotypes, although their mechanistic roles remain to be fully defined. Notably, detection of DE-CAFSGs can be achieved using routine blood or tissue specimens, eliminating the reliance on advanced instrumentation or highly specialized protocols, thereby reducing diagnostic complexity and cost compared to conventional histopathological or serological assessments. Sundeep Singh et al. reported an AUC of 0.7 for CA19-9 in biliary cancer detection when evaluating the diagnostic and prognostic utility of conventional CCA markers [85], while the AJCC staging system yielded an AUC of 0.82 for predicting CCA prognosis [86]. Diagnostic AUCs of 0.84 and 0.83 were observed for single-marker assays using CEA and CA125, respectively [87]. In contrast, GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 demonstrated AUCs exceeding 0.7 across three independent cohorts, with most values surpassing 0.9, indicating enhanced discriminatory capacity in distinguishing CCA patients from healthy controls. In parallel, although a separate study developed a serological assay targeting tumor antigens, its diagnostic performance was limited by suboptimal sensitivity and specificity [88], highlighting the need for more robust biomarker panels.

This study employed MR to explore the potential causal association between DE-CAFSG and CCA. The analytical foundation of MR is rooted in Mendel’s second law, which posits the independent segregation of gene pairs during gametogenesis [89]. Leveraging SNPs as instrumental variables reduces susceptibility to confounding and mirrors the random allocation principle underlying RCTs [90]. Moreover, the fixed nature of germline genetic variation—established at conception and unaffected by disease status—supports the temporal precedence of exposure, thereby minimizing reverse causality bias and enabling inference over extended observational windows [91, 92]. Compared with RCTs, MR offers a cost-effective and time-efficient alternative, particularly when evaluating modifiable or adverse risk factors that are otherwise difficult to manipulate in experimental settings [89]. Nonetheless, methodological limitations persist. Horizontal pleiotropy, wherein genetic variants influence outcomes through pathways unrelated to the exposure, compromises the exclusion restriction assumption and undermines causal interpretation [93]. Additionally, MR is applicable only when exposure variables are supported by robust genetic instruments [94, 95]. Genetic variants generally exert minimal influence on most exposures, often yielding false negatives and limiting statistical power [92]. Incorporating multiple variants associated with the risk factor as instrumental variables significantly enhances statistical robustness [96]. This investigation assessed the genetic association between DE-CAFSGs and CCA through large-scale GWAS datasets and a two-sample MR framework [96, 97]. Analytical methods included the MR Egger test to assess pleiotropy [24], along with Cochran’s Q and IVW to evaluate IV heterogeneity. LOO analysis was conducted to ensure result stability [98]. Predictive validity of biomarkers was supported by ROC curve construction and the inclusion of diverse genetic variants within the instrumental variables.

In summary, GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 were identified as genes associated with CAFs in CCA, suggesting their potential utility as molecular indicators for early diagnosis and therapeutic targeting. This study offers several methodological strengths. First, MR analysis is employed to minimize bias from confounding variables and reverse causation. Second, the integration of large-scale GWAS datasets improves statistical robustness. Third, multiple analytical approaches are adopted to enhance result reliability. However, the functional roles of GRN, PLXNC1, and ORMDL3 in CCA pathogenesis remain unclear, and the mechanistic involvement of several key genes lacks full experimental validation. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power to detect subtle association, This limitation stems from the rarity of resectable CCA and the stringent inclusion criteria. While our cohort was carefully matched for key confounders, the restricted sample size could introduce selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to broader population. And the analysis is based on a single population cohort, which may restrict extrapolation due to inherent protocol-specific limitations, selection bias, and endpoint predefinition. Moreover, the use of peritumoral tissue as a control group may introduce confounding effects arising from microenvironmental interactions, given the complex biology of CCA. Future research should emphasize larger and more diverse sample sets, improved control group selection—preferably histologically confirmed normal bile duct tissue—standardized data acquisition protocols, and multi-center validation to enhance clinical applicability. Additionally, a more integrative approach including proteomic, metabolomic, transcriptomic, and genomic profiling is warranted to achieve a comprehensive understanding of CCA pathophysiology and to support the development of reliable biomarkers for early detection, individualized therapy, and outcome prediction.
