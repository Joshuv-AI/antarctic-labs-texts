Compositional design and formulation study of hydroxyapatite–chitosan nanocomposite-modified conventional glass ionomer cement: influence of composition and loading on mechanical, interfacial, and antibacterial properties
Authors: Neven S Aref, Abeer A El-Fallal, Mohamed A El-Farrash, Fikry M Reicha, Essam E Al-Wakeel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo prepare hydroxyapatite/chitosan (HA/CTS) nanocomposites at four weight ratios (85/15, 70/30, 50/50, 30/70) and two concentrations (3 and 5 wt.%), incorporate them into conventional glass ionomer cement (GIC), and evaluate their effect on compressive strength, shear bond strength to dentin, and antibacterial activity.Materials and methodsHA/CTS nanocomposites were synthesized by co-precipitation and characterized by TEM and FTIR. Nine groups were prepared (n = 10): one unmodified control (Group I) and eight modified groups (Groups II–IX). Compressive strength was tested per ISO 9917–1:2007; shear bond strength to human dentin and antibacterial activity (agar diffusion against Streptococcus mutans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli) were also evaluated. One-way and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD were applied (P ≤ 0.05).ResultsTEM confirmed nanoscale particles (7–50 nm) with dispersion improving at higher chitosan content. FTIR verified dual-phase incorporation and Ca2+–NH2 coordination bonding. Compressive strength (F(8,81) = 177.509; P < 0.0001) and shear bond strength (F(8,81) = 202.262; P < 0.0001) differed significantly among groups. A significant ratio × concentration interaction was confirmed for both properties (P < 0.0001). Only the 70/30 nanocomposite at 3 wt.% exceeded the control compressive strength (133.44 vs. 115.02 MPa; P < 0.05). All eight modified groups showed significantly higher shear bond strength than the control (6.04 MPa), with the 85/15 at 5 wt.% achieving the highest value (13.30 MPa). No inhibition zones were detected in any group.ConclusionsThe 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.% optimizes compressive strength, while the 85/15 at 5 wt.% optimizes dentin adhesion. Overall, the 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.% demonstrated the most favorable balanced performance profile across both mechanical outcomes. The absence of detectable antibacterial activity under the present agar diffusion testing conditions may be related to chitosan immobilization within the nanocomposite matrix and the diffusion limitations of the assay method. Findings suggest the potential for application-specific optimization of HA/CTS-modified GIC under controlled in vitro conditions, pending further long-term and in vivo validation.Clinical significanceHA/CTS nanocomposite modification enhanced the mechanical and adhesive performance of conventional GIC under the present experimental conditions. The observed improvement in compressive and shear bond strength across the modified formulations may support improved marginal integrity in esthetic GIC restorations. Two application-specific optimal formulations were identified based on the functional demands of the restoration site, with the 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.% representing the most favorable overall formulation combining enhanced compressive strength with clinically acceptable dentin adhesion; however, further long-term and in vivo validation remains necessary before definitive clinical translation.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Glass ionomer cement, Hydroxyapatite, Chitosan, Nanocomposite, Compressive strength, Shear bond strength, Esthetic restorative dentistry, Antibacterial activity

Introduction

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) is a self-adhesive restorative material widely used in pediatric and adult dentistry because of its fluoride-releasing and anticariogenic properties. Since its introduction by Wilson and Kent in 1972, GIC has been progressively refined to improve its physical properties and broaden its clinical applications [1–3].

Its sustained clinical relevance can be attributed to a unique combination of properties. GIC chemically bonds to tooth structure, releases and recharges fluoride over time, and exhibits favorable biocompatible properties that distinguish it from resin-based composites [4–6].

Because of their resilience, low polymerization shrinkage, and thermal compatibility with dental tissues, GICs and their modifications are widely used as liner materials beneath restorations such as dental composites, particularly in sandwich techniques. These characteristics support their role as an effective dentin substitute [7–9].

Despite its advantages, conventional GIC still exhibits inferior mechanical performance compared with resin composites. Its relatively low strength and susceptibility to moisture imbalance during early setting may result in surface degradation and microcracking [5, 6, 10, 11]. These limitations have motivated continued efforts to reinforce GIC while preserving its biological advantages.

Hydroxyapatite (HA) is a widely studied dental biomaterial because of its excellent biocompatibility and similarity to the mineral phase of enamel and dentin. However, conventional HA is limited by relatively poor mechanical properties associated with its porous structure. In contrast, nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) has attracted considerable attention because of its enhanced physicochemical and biological properties. Its nanoscale size provides higher surface area and reactivity, improving its bioactivity [12].

Importantly, nHA serves as an effective source of calcium and phosphate ions, promoting remineralization of demineralized enamel, particularly in early carious lesions. Its structural similarity to natural apatite crystals also improves integration with dental hard tissues [13]. Incorporation of nano-hydroxyapatite into glass ionomer cement has been reported to enhance physical and biological properties while reducing microleakage and cytotoxic effects [14, 15].

Chitosan is a naturally derived polysaccharide produced by deacetylation of chitin, the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth [16]. In dentistry, chitosan has attracted attention because of its biocompatibility and antimicrobial properties, which may reduce bacterial activity associated with dental caries. It has also been reported to enhance the biological performance and antibacterial behavior of glass ionomer cements. Additionally, chitosan promotes wound healing and tissue regeneration, supporting its potential use in restorative and surgical applications [17–19].

Recently, a study reported that ultra-mild universal adhesive-modified chitosan/hydroxyapatite nanocomposite exhibited enhanced bonding efficiency to caries-affected dentin as well as its bioactive potential, without compromising either physical or chemical characteristics of the adhesive [20]. Hydroxyapatite/chitosan (HA/CTS) nanocomposite systems have been introduced as promising biomimetic materials for remineralization and tissue regeneration applications, integrating the collagen-binding properties of chitosan with the bioactive and mineralization potential of nano-hydroxyapatite [21, 22].

Several studies have examined HA or chitosan additions to GICs independently, reporting variable fluoride release, mechanical and antibacterial improvements that depend heavily on particle size, concentration, and preparation route [14, 15, 23–25]. Although previous studies have reported the incorporation of hydroxyapatite, chitosan, or HA/chitosan-based systems into glass ionomer cements, most investigations evaluated single formulations, isolated concentrations, or limited mechanical outcomes. A systematic comparative evaluation of multiple HA/CTS nanocomposite compositional ratios at different loading concentrations within a unified factorial experimental design has not been comprehensively investigated. In particular, the interaction between nanocomposite composition and concentration, and its influence on compressive strength, dentin adhesion, and antibacterial behavior, has not been comprehensively characterized. Therefore, the present study was designed not merely as an additive modification study, but as a formulation optimization and interaction-analysis investigation aimed at identifying composition-dependent performance behavior in HA/CTS-modified GIC systems.

The present study was therefore designed to prepare and characterize HA/CTS nanocomposites at four weight ratios (85/15, 70/30, 50/50, and 30/70) and two concentrations (3 and 5 wt.%), incorporate them into conventional GIC, and evaluate their mechanical, adhesive, and antibacterial performance against three clinically relevant bacterial strains. A secondary aim was to determine whether a statistically significant interaction exists between nanocomposite composition and concentration in governing the overall material performance.

The null hypothesis of this study was that incorporation of hydroxyapatite/chitosan nanocomposites at different ratios and concentrations would have no significant effect on the compressive strength, shear bond strength, or antibacterial activity of conventional glass ionomer cement, and that no interaction would exist between composition and concentration.

Methods

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was carried out using G*Power software (version 3.1, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) based on a one-way ANOVA design with nine groups. At α = 0.05, a power of 1 − β = 0.80, and a large-to-very-large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.45) consistent with the effect magnitudes reported in comparable in vitro GIC studies evaluating modified restorative biomaterials [26]. Ten specimens per group were therefore used for compressive strength and shear bond strength testing to provide a margin above the calculated minimum. For antibacterial evaluation, 5 specimens per group per bacterial strain were tested (15 specimens for each group for the three bacterial strains used in the study), yielding 135 specimens in total. Representative specimen from each group was used for the FTIR analysis. Additionally, a sample from each experimental nanocomposite ratio was analyzed by TEM and FTIR.

Preparation of the hydroxyapatite/chitosan nanocomposite

The HA/CTS nanocomposite was synthesized by the co-precipitation method, [27, 28] which was selected for its ability to produce nanoscale particles with intimate phase mixing and reproducible composition. Chitosan solution was initially prepared and subsequently served as the reaction medium during hydroxyapatite precipitation, allowing in situ formation of the HA/CTS nanocomposite through the co-precipitation process. Four HA-to-chitosan weight ratios were prepared — 85/15, 70/30, 50/50, and 30/70 — to allow systematic investigation of compositional effects on the physicochemical and biological properties of the resulting material. These ratios were selected to provide a compositional transition from hydroxyapatite-dominant to chitosan-enriched formulations while maintaining sufficient inorganic content for reinforcement and acceptable handling characteristics.

A 10 wt.% phosphoric acid solution was then added to this chitosan solution. Characterization of the prepared nanocomposites

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Nanoparticle morphology and size were examined using a JEM-2100 TEM (JEOL, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Representative micrographs were obtained for each HA/CTS nanocomposite ratio to verify nanoscale dimensions and to visualize crystallite morphology and phase distribution within the composite matrix.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Chemical structure and phase interactions were characterized using a Nicolet iS10 double-beam dispersive FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Electron Corporation, UK) over 500–4000 cm−1. Spectra were recorded for pure chitosan, pure hydroxyapatite, all four nanocomposite ratios, and each of the nine GIC groups (control and eight modified) to identify characteristic absorption bands and confirm successful nanocomposite incorporation into the cement matrix.

Experimental design

The experimental design of this in vitro study is presented in Fig. 1. Following synthesis and characterization, each of the four HA/CTS nanocomposites was incorporated into the powder component of a conventional glass ionomer cement at two weight concentrations (3 wt.% and 5 wt.%), resulting in eight modified formulations. An unmodified glass ionomer cement served as the control group.

Consort flow diagram

The study was designed as a comparative formulation-based evaluation to investigate the influence of nanocomposite composition and loading on the mechanical and interfacial performance of glass ionomer cement and to identify the most favorable formulation. The selected loading concentrations (3 wt.% and 5 wt.%) represented intermediate incorporation levels intended to balance reinforcement potential with adequate nanocomposite dispersion within the GIC matrix.

This design yielded nine experimental groups; Group I: Control, Group II: 85/15 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.%, Group III: 85/15, HA/CTS nanocomposite at 5 wt.%, Group IV: 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.%, Group V: 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 5 wt.%, Group VI: 50/50 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.%, Group VII: 50/50 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 5 wt.%, Group VIII: 30/70 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.%, and Group IX: 30/70 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 5 wt.%.

The manufacturer-recommended powder-to-liquid ratio was maintained throughout. The synthesized HA/CTS nanocomposite powder was hand-blended with the conventional GIC powder before addition of the manufacturer-supplied liquid to ensure uniform distribution within the cement matrix.

Compressive strength testing

Ninety cylindrical specimens (10 per group) were prepared in split Teflon molds (4 mm diameter × 6 mm height) in accordance with ISO 9917–1:2007. Shear bond strength between glass ionomer and dentin

Antibacterial activity assessment

Antibacterial efficacy was evaluated against Streptococcus mutans (oral swab), Staphylococcus aureus (nasal swab), and Escherichia coli (oral ulcer swab) by the agar diffusion method [31]. A total of 135 disc-shaped specimens (5 specimens per group per strain; 5 mm diameter × 3 mm thickness) were prepared in Teflon molds. Bacterial strains were cultured overnight in selective media at 37 °C. Each 100 mm Petri dish was prepared with 15 mL of blood agar (pH 7.5) combined with 100 µL of inoculum. After solidification, discs were placed on the agar surface and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Inhibition zones were measured at three equidistant points by digital calipers; net zone size was obtained by subtracting the specimen diameter (5 mm) from the mean of the three measurements. Absence of any zone was recorded as zero, indicating no detectable antibacterial activity.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) and standard errors (SE). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way ANOVA was applied across all nine groups for compressive strength and shear bond strength outcomes; where a significant F-value was obtained, pairwise comparisons were made using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test at P ≤ 0.05. A two-way ANOVA was additionally applied to the eight modified groups to assess the main effects of HA/CTS nanocomposite ratio (Factor A) and concentration (Factor B), and their interaction (A × B), on compressive and shear bond strength. The unmodified control was excluded from the two-way ANOVA as it belongs to neither factorial dimension. Antibacterial activity zones data were not subjected to statistical analysis, as zero inhibition was recorded for all groups across the three bacterial strains, precluding any meaningful inferential comparisons. Effect size interpretation was additionally considered using eta-squared (η2) for one-way ANOVA outcomes and partial eta-squared (partial η2) for two-way ANOVA effects to support a more balanced evaluation of the magnitude of the observed effects beyond statistical significance alone. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were calculated for group means.

Results

Characterization of the HA/CTS nanocomposites

Transmission electron microscopy

TEM analysis confirmed nanoscale particle dimensions across all four HA/CTS nanocomposite ratios, with sizes ranging from approximately 7 to 50 nm (Fig. 2). A clear compositional trend was evident: as the chitosan proportion increased, particle agglomeration decreased and dispersion improved.

Transmission electron micrographs of the four HA/CTS nanocomposites. a 85/15; b 70/30; c 50/50; d 30/70 HA/CTS. Scale bar = 50 nm

The 85/15 HA/CTS nanocomposite (Fig. 2a) showed densely packed spherical clusters at × 40,000– × 50,000 magnifications, with particle sizes of 10.71 to 49.06 nm. The 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite (Fig. 2b) presented a similar morphology but with greater interparticle spacing, reflecting partial surface passivation by the higher chitosan content; annotated sizes ranged from 10.71 to 49.06 nm at × 50,000 magnification. At equimolar proportions, the 50/50 HA/CTS nanocomposite (Fig. 2c) underwent a notable morphological shift, displaying elongated, needle-like crystallites embedded within a continuous chitosan matrix at × 15,000 magnification, with dimensions of 6.59 to 47.08 nm. The 30/70 HA/CTS nanocomposite (Fig. 2d) produced the most dispersed distribution, with individual HA crystallites (6.79–38.23 nm) appearing as isolated dark contrast regions within a dominant chitosan matrix at × 30,000 magnification. These TEM observations support successful nanoscale integration between hydroxyapatite crystallites and the chitosan matrix within the synthesized nanocomposites. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that increasing chitosan content systematically suppresses agglomeration and drives a morphological transition from clustered spherical particles toward individually dispersed crystallites.

FTIR spectroscopy of the prepared nanocomposites

FTIR spectra of all four nanocomposites (Fig. 3) confirmed successful incorporation of both phases across all ratios. The characteristic hydroxyapatite phosphate stretching bands (1042–1074 cm−1) and bending doublets (559–604 cm−1) were consistently present alongside chitosan amide I (1646–1654 cm−1) and amide II (1563–1564 cm−1) bands.

FTIR spectra of the four prepared HA/CTS nanocomposites recorded over 500–4000 cm⁻1. Spectra are stacked vertically for comparison. (Spectra 1) 85/15 HA/CTS; (Spectra 2) 70/30 HA/CTS; (Spectra 3) 50/50 HA/CTS; (Spectra 4) 30/70 HA/CTS

With increasing chitosan content, C–H stretching bands (~ 2924–2926 cm−1) and C–N stretching bands (~ 1319–1321 cm−1) became progressively more prominent, being undetectable in the 85/15 and 70/30 spectra but clearly resolved in the 50/50 and 30/70 formulations. The gradual shift of the amide I band from 1646 cm−1 (85/15) toward higher wavenumbers with increasing chitosan content, together with the reduction in relative phosphate band intensity in the 30/70 spectrum, confirm progressive Ca2+–NH2 coordination bonding between the HA and chitosan phases across all ratios.

FTIR spectroscopy of the experimental groups

FTIR spectra of the control GIC (Spectrum 1 — Group I) and all eight modified groups (Spectra 2–9, Groups II–IX) are shown in Fig. 4. All modified formulations retained the defining GIC absorption profile — O–H stretching (3448–3453 cm−1), polyacrylate C–O band (~ 1459 cm−1), and Si–O/Al–O glass network band (~ 1077–1081 cm−1). Additional C–H stretching bands attributable to the chitosan component were detected in Groups II, III, VII, VIII, and IX, with intensity increasing with higher chitosan content and loading concentration. These spectral changes confirm successful incorporation of the HA/CTS nanocomposite into the GIC matrix in all eight modified groups.

Eta-squared analysis (η2 = 0.946) demonstrated a very large overall group effect for compressive strength, indicating that most of the observed variance was attributable to experimental group differences. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for all group means are presented in Table 3.

Group IV (GIC + 70/30 HA/CTS, 3 wt.%) achieved the highest mean compressive strength of all groups (133.44 ± 6.48 MPa), and was the only modified group to significantly exceed the unmodified control (Group I: 115.02 ± 5.70 MPa; P < 0.05). All other modified groups fell significantly below the control (P < 0.05 for all), with the lowest values recorded for Group VIII (55.05 ± 4.65 MPa) and Group VI (55.96 ± 6.74 MPa). Tukey post-hoc analysis showed that Groups II, VII, and IX were statistically indistinguishable (subgroup ‘c’), as were Groups III and V (subgroup ‘d’) and Groups VI and VIII (subgroup ‘e’).

Two-Way ANOVA — effect of ratio and concentration

Two-way ANOVA on the eight modified groups revealed significant main effects for both HA/CTS nanocomposite ratio (F(3,72) = 110.302; P < 0.0001) and nanocomposite concentration (F(1,72) = 10.504; P = 0.0018), and most importantly a highly significant ratio × concentration interaction (F(3,72) = 267.184; P < 0.0001). This interaction confirms that the effect of concentration on compressive strength was not consistent across all ratios; it varied substantially depending on the relative proportions of HA and chitosan. Shear bond strength between glass ionomer and dentin

Antibacterial activity

No measurable antibacterial activity was detected in any group against Streptococcus mutans, Staphylococcus aureus, or Escherichia coli following 48 h of incubation at 37 °C. No inhibition zones were observed around any specimen in any group for any of the three tested strains.

Discussion

This study set out to explore how incorporating HA/CTS nanocomposites at different compositional ratios and concentrations affects the mechanical, adhesive, and antibacterial behavior of conventional GIC. What emerged was not a simple dose–response relationship, but the results revealed a composition-dependent picture with practical implications for how GIC might be tailored for specific clinical applications.

Characterization and structure–property relationships

Transmission electron microscopy

Increasing chitosan content influenced the morphology of the HA/CTS nanocomposite. Previous studies have shown that chitosan can regulate hydroxyapatite crystal development and promote interaction between the inorganic and polymeric phases, with a reduction in crystal size observed as chitosan content increases. In addition, compositional variation in HA/CTS nanocomposite systems has been reported to affect composite formation and morphology, as confirmed by electron microscopic analyses [28, 32, 33]. From a mechanical standpoint, this compositional effect on dispersion is significant: more homogeneous nanoparticle distribution within the cement matrix reduces internal porosity and supports effective stress transfer, thereby helping to maintain structural integrity, while agglomeration can create localized voids and stress concentration points within the set cement that act as sites of fracture initiation and mechanical weakness [34, 35].

The morphological transitions observed across ratios from densely clustered spheres (85/15 and 70/30) to elongated needle-like forms (50/50) and individually dispersed particles in a polymer matrix (30/70) — reflect the progressively greater templating and directing influence of chitosan as its proportion increases. These structural differences translate directly into the contrasting mechanical behaviors observed in subsequent testing.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR confirmed the coexistence of both phases and, critically, the presence of chemical interaction between them. The hydroxyapatite phosphate bands and chitosan amide bands were consistently observed together across all ratios [27], and the progressive shift of the amide I band along with the emergence of C–H and C–N stretching features with increasing chitosan content suggest Ca2⁺–NH₂ coordination bonding, a well-characterized interaction in HA/chitosan systems [28, 33]. This is mechanistically important: these interactions indicate that the two phases are chemically integrated rather than simply mixed, which can improve stress transfer between the organic polymer matrix and the inorganic HA phase when the nanocomposite is embedded within the GIC.

Compressive strength

Overall interaction between composition and concentration

The compressive strength results of the unmodified GIC fell within the expected range for conventional materials, consistent with previously reported values [36–38].

A statistically significant interaction between nanocomposite composition and concentration was observed, indicating that the effect of filler loading varied depending on the HA/CTS ratio. This interaction suggests that no single concentration can be considered universally optimal, as material performance is governed by the interplay between composition and loading. The observed interaction effect was associated with a substantial effect magnitude, supporting the practical influence of HA/CTS composition and concentration on compressive strength performance.

Optimal formulation: 70/30 HA/CTS at 3 wt.%

In the present study, among all different prepared HA/CTS ratios, 70/30 HA/CTS ratio showed the most significant ratio for improving compressive strength. This ratio has the highest degree of chemical and mechanical interlocking, hydrogen bonding (between NH2 and OH) and chelation (between NH2 and Ca) of chitosan and hydroxyapatite which characterized it by the highest compressive strength among the prepared ratios [28]. The optimal formulation was observed in Group IV (70/30 HA/CTS at 3 wt.%), which significantly exceeded the control (+ 16%). From a clinical perspective, the 18.42 MPa improvement achieved by Group IV above the unmodified control exceeds the + 10–15 MPa range generally considered meaningful for practical enhancement of GIC compressive performance. Both Group I (115.02 MPa) and Group IV (133.44 MPa) surpass the ≥ 100 MPa threshold considered appropriate for moderate occlusal stress applications, while Groups VI and VIII (≈55 MPa) fall below the ≥ 70 MPa minimum for non-load-bearing restorations and are therefore not recommended at the tested parameters. For shear bond strength, all nine groups exceeded the ≥ 5 MPa minimum threshold considered acceptable for clinical retention under physiological conditions, and the optimal Group III formulation (13.30 MPa) represents a 2.2-fold increase above the control and a 2.7-fold increase above this minimum threshold. This may reflect a balance between the dominant HA phase and the selected concentration (3 wt.%). The HA phase provides reactive Ca2⁺ and PO₄3⁻ ions for interfacial interactions with the GIC polyacrylate network, while the lower concentration helps maintain sufficient nanoparticle dispersion and maximize interfacial contact area. During the GIC setting reaction, polyacrylic acid interacts with calcium and phosphate ions released from the HA nanoparticle surface, contributing to the formation of an interfacial matrix that bridges the organic polymer and inorganic phases. At this concentration, the interaction is likely to occur more effectively, whereas at higher concentrations, increased agglomeration may reduce its efficiency [39, 40].

Effect of higher concentration on HA-dominant ratios

At higher concentrations (5 wt.%), a marked reduction in compressive strength was observed for HA-dominant ratios, particularly in the 70/30 group, which showed a decrease of 62.44 MPa. Beyond an optimal loading threshold, nanoparticle agglomeration prevents effective interaction with the poly acid matrix, thereby reducing the reinforcing benefit of HA incorporation [15]. Such changes may also interfere with the acid–base setting interactions within the GIC matrix [39]. This behavior is consistent with another study [41], who reported that micro HA addition at standard powder/liquid ratios reduced GIC compressive strength, and with another investigation [40], which demonstrated a concentration threshold beyond which nHA incorporation becomes detrimental.

Effect of higher chitosan content

In contrast, the chitosan-dominant ratios (50/50 and 30/70) showed compressive strength values below the control group at both concentrations tested. However, within these groups, higher compressive strength was observed at 5 wt.% than at 3 wt.%, suggesting a concentration-dependent partial recovery effect.

The overall reduction in compressive strength relative to the unmodified GIC may be attributed to the higher chitosan content interfering with the GIC matrix. As chitosan content increases relative to hydroxyapatite, the available polyacrylic acid may be insufficient to effectively bond to the increased nanocomposite particle surface area, weakening the interfacial bonding between particles and the ionomer matrix [42]. This is consistent with previous findings showing that higher chitosan content in GIC is associated with progressive reduction in compressive strength and widening of surface cracks [43].

The slight strength improvement observed at 5 wt.% compared to 3 wt.% within these chitosan-dominant groups may reflect a partial compensating effect, whereby the amino and hydroxyl groups of chitosan form hydrogen and ionic bonds with the polyacrylic acid chains of the GIC matrix at higher loading, increasing cross-link density and internal cohesion, and supporting resistance to crack initiation and propagation [42]. At 3 wt.%, however, this quantity appears insufficient to establish either effective mineral-phase reinforcement from HA or meaningful polymer network cohesion from chitosan, resulting in the lowest compressive strength values recorded across all experimental groups — Groups VI and VIII.

This pattern reflects a fundamental principle in composite biomaterial design: that filler dispersion, matrix integrity, and interfacial chemistry must be simultaneously optimized, and that no single concentration can be universally beneficial across formulations of differing composition [35, 40, 44].

Shear bond strength between glass ionomer and dentin

The universal improvement in shear bond strength across all eight modified groups represents one of the most practically meaningful findings of this study. Reliable dentin adhesion is an important prerequisite for marginal integrity and long-term restorative performance, and the observation that every HA/CTS formulation tested — regardless of ratio or concentration — significantly outperformed the unmodified GIC (P < 0.05 for all) highlights the consistency of the adhesion-enhancing effect under the present experimental conditions.

Mechanisms of enhanced dentin adhesio

Both components of the nanocomposite contribute to improved adhesion through distinct but complementary mechanisms. Hydroxyapatite is highly analogous to the mineral phase of dentin in terms of composition and crystal structure, enabling ionic exchange and enhanced chemical bonding at the cement–dentin interface [4]. During setting, the carboxyl groups of polyacrylic acid form ionic bonds with calcium ions from both the GIC glass and the hydroxyapatite on the tooth surface. This contributes to the formation of an interfacial matrix that reinforces the transition zone between the material and the tooth substrate [45]. The 85/15 ratio, with its high HA content, likely increases the density of these interfacial bonding sites. The incorporation of hydroxyapatite into GIC has been shown to enhance bonding through increased availability of reactive calcium and phosphate ions at the cement–dentin interface [14], which explains the consistently superior shear bond strength observed at both concentrations for this ratio.

Chitosan contributes an additional adhesion mechanism through its cationic nature at physiological pH, enabling electrostatic interaction with negatively charged dentin collagen and residual hydroxyapatite [28]. Ibrahim et al. [46] demonstrated that chitosan-modified GIC at concentrations of 5–10% v/v did not adversely affect bonding to dentin, confirming that chitosan’s interaction with the dentin substrate is compatible with adhesive interface integrity. The present findings suggest that this adhesion-compatible effect of chitosan is preserved even when incorporated within a co-precipitated HA/CTS nanocomposite rather than applied as a solution-phase modifier.

Optimal formulation and interaction effect

Group III (85/15 at 5 wt.%: 13.30 ± 0.40 MPa — more than double the control) exhibited the highest bond strength values, and statistical analysis confirmed a significant interaction between composition and concentration (F(3,72) = 85.743; P < 0.0001), indicating that the effect of concentration on adhesion is composition-dependent. For the 85/15 ratio, increasing concentration likely enhances adhesion by providing a greater density of reactive calcium ions from both the GIC glass and the incorporated HA nanocomposite available for interaction with the tooth substrate [4, 14, 45].

In contrast, for the 70/30 and 50/50 ratios, higher filler loading (5 wt.%) may result in insufficient polyacrylic acid to effectively bond to the increased nanocomposite surface area, potentially weakening the interfacial bonding between the particles and the ionomer matrix and limiting effective adaptation at the bonding interface [42]. This observation aligns with findings reported by Khudhair and Khalaf [47], who showed that chitosan incorporation into resin-modified GIC improved shear bond strength at low concentrations, while higher concentrations adversely affected the integrity of the adhesive interface. For the 30/70 ratio, bond strength plateaued across both concentrations, suggesting that beyond a certain chitosan content, further increases in loading do not yield additional adhesion benefits. Overall, the interaction effects demonstrated meaningful practical influence on dentin adhesion behavior under the present experimental conditions.

Antibacterial activity

The absence of inhibition zones for all tested groups was an unexpected finding, given the well-documented antimicrobial properties of chitosan. However, this result can be explained by the specific physicochemical state of chitosan within the HA/CTS nanocomposite and the inherent limitations of the test method used.

Immobilization of chitosan within the nanocomposite matrix

Chitosan’s primary antibacterial mechanism depends on the protonation of its free –NH₂ groups under acidic conditions, generating cationic sites capable of electrostatically interacting with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, increasing their permeability and ultimately causing cell death [48]. However, the FTIR findings of the present study suggest that a significant proportion of these –NH₂ groups are involved in Ca2⁺–NH₂ coordination bonding within the nanocomposite structure [28]. This interaction may reduce the availability of free amine groups responsible for antibacterial activity. Since metal ion complexation with amino groups is known to reduce their availability for electrostatic interaction with bacterial surfaces [48], this coordination likely diminishes the free amine content available for antimicrobial activity. In addition, the embedding of the nanocomposite within a solid GIC matrix further limits chitosan availability at the specimen surface. A previous study reporting antibacterial effects of chitosan-modified GIC has typically involved chitosan in its free solution form incorporated into the liquid phase [46], a configuration that preserves direct surface contact with bacteria, in contrast to the co-precipitated solid nanocomposite system used in the present study.

Limitations of the agar diffusion method

The agar diffusion assay detects only agents that diffuse freely through agar gel; it is inherently insensitive to contact-dependent or surface-bound antimicrobial mechanisms [49]. Therefore, the absence of inhibition zones in the present study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of complete antibacterial inactivity of the modified materials. Chitosan’s mode of action in solid dental materials depends primarily on direct surface contact with bacterial membranes rather than diffusion, which explains why inhibition zones may be absent even when antibacterial potential exists. This limitation has been demonstrated in studies on chitosan-modified dental composites, where no inhibition zones were observed by agar diffusion testing despite the material containing an established antimicrobial agent [49].

Clinical implications for antibacterial potential

Although no direct antibacterial effect was demonstrated under the current testing conditions, the potential cariostatic contribution of the material should not be dismissed. Chitosan has been shown to inhibit Streptococcus mutans adsorption to hydroxyapatite through an anti-adhesion mechanism — with reductions in bacterial attachment ranging from 47 to 66% — an effect that operates independently of diffusion and would therefore not be detected by the agar diffusion method [50]. In addition, the intrinsic fluoride-releasing capacity of conventional GIC contributes independently to the prevention of secondary caries [4].

These findings suggest that the antibacterial potential of HA/CTS-modified GIC may be more effectively expressed under conditions involving direct surface contact or biofilm formation. Future studies employing biofilm models, colony-forming unit (CFU) analysis, MTT viability assays, and extended evaluation periods are required to provide a more comprehensive and clinically relevant assessment of antibacterial behavior.

Clinical significance

The findings of this study have relevant clinical implications. The 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.% may be considered a promising formulation for applications where compressive strength is a primary requirement, such as core build-ups and stress-bearing restorations in primary teeth. The observed improvement relative to unmodified GIC suggests potential for enhanced performance under moderate loading conditions.

The universal improvement in shear bond strength across all modified groups is of considerable clinical importance. The 85/15 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 5 wt.% demonstrated the highest adhesion values, indicating that targeted nanocomposite modification may enhance interfacial integrity at the restoration–dentin interface, which is critical for marginal sealing and resistance to secondary caries.

However, no single formulation simultaneously optimized both compressive strength and dentin adhesion. The 70/30 formulation at 3 wt.% demonstrated the most favorable compressive performance, whereas the 85/15 formulation at 5 wt.% showed the highest dentin adhesion under the present experimental conditions. These findings highlight the composition-dependent nature of HA/CTS-modified GIC behavior and support the value of systematic formulation optimization prior to more extensive restorative material characterization. Nevertheless, the 70/30 HA/CTS nanocomposite at 3 wt.% demonstrated the most favorable overall performance profile, combining the highest compressive strength with clinically acceptable dentin adhesion values that significantly exceeded the unmodified control, suggesting it as the most promising candidate for applications requiring balanced mechanical and adhesive performance. Future investigations incorporating additional mechanical, physicochemical, biological, and long-term aging assessments are required to further validate the performance of the identified formulations.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, this was an in vitro investigation conducted under controlled laboratory conditions that do not fully replicate the clinical environment, including thermal cycling, occlusal loading, enzymatic activity, moisture variation, and biofilm formation. Accordingly, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to long-term clinical performance, and in vivo validation is required.

Second, mechanical properties were evaluated at a single time point (24 h), and the long-term durability of the observed improvements under aging conditions, thermocycling, and wear has not been assessed.

Third, several clinically relevant material properties were not evaluated, including flexural strength, diametral tensile strength, surface microhardness, wear resistance, water sorption, solubility, and fluoride release. These factors are essential for a comprehensive assessment of restorative material performance.

Fourth, antibacterial evaluation was limited to the agar diffusion method at a single time point. More sensitive approaches, such as biofilm-based models, CFU counting, and MTT assays over extended periods, would provide a more accurate representation of antibacterial performance. Accordingly, the present antibacterial findings should be considered preliminary and method-dependent rather than definitive evidence of complete antibacterial inactivity.

Finally, the biocompatibility of the modified materials was not assessed. Evaluation of cytotoxicity, cellular response, and tissue compatibility is essential before any clinical application can be considered.”
