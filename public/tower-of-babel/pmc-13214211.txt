Introduction

Coarctation of the aorta accompanied by a subclavian artery aneurysm is a rare clinical entity, typically involving the left subclavian artery. Bilateral subclavian artery aneurysms in this context are exceedingly rare, with only six cases reported in the literature to our knowledge.

The combination of aortic coarctation and achalasia has not been previously documented. Surgical repair of aortic coarctation carries several risks, including neurological complications such as injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve (occurring in 1–5% of cases) and, less frequently, the vagus nerve. Damage to the vagus nerve can impair esophageal motility, potentially leading to an achalasia-like disorder. We present a case of a patient with this unique triad of conditions, conidering that we assumed this patient’s achalasia was caused by vagus nerve injury during the initial surgery.

Case Report

A 30-year-old male presented with a three-month history of progressively worsening compressive neck pain and dyspnea. Thirteen years earlier, he had undergone surgical repair of an aortic coarctation. At the time of the initial surgery, a 3 cm aneurysm of the right subclavian artery and a 1.8 cm aneurysm of the left subclavian artery were also noted but were not repaired (Fig. 1). Post-operatively, the patient developed hoarseness and symptoms suggestive of gastroesophageal reflux disease. His medications included beta-blockers and antiplatelet agents, and he had no other significant medical history.

A contrast-enhanced multi-slice computed tomography (MSCT) angiogram was performed. It showed a left subclavian artery aneurysm measuring 2.1 cm and a significantly enlarged right subclavian artery aneurysm measuring 5.8 cm. The large right aneurysm extended from the vessel’s origin to its exit from the thoracic cavity, causing compression of the trachea and esophagus. The scan also revealed dilation of the esophagus distal to the site of compression (Fig. 2). A subsequent contrast esophagogram confirmed esophageal dilation with a characteristic “bird’s beak” tapering of the distal esophagus, consistent with a diagnosis of achalasia (Fig. 3). Flexible laryngoscopy demonstrated complete left vocal cord paralysis and partial right vocal cord paralysis, consistent with a left recurrent laryngeal nerve injury.

After a multidisciplinary consultation, surgical intervention for the right subclavian artery aneurysm was prioritized due to the high risk of rupture. Further investigation and management of the esophageal symptoms were deferred until after the patient’s recovery from vascular surgery. The patient provided informed consent after understanding the high risks of morbidity and mortality associated with the procedure.

A surgical approach via a partial sternotomy extended into the right supraclavicular fossa was undertaken. The brachiocephalic artery, right common carotid artery, and the right subclavian artery proximal and distal to the aneurysm were isolated and controlled. The neck of the aneurysm was ligated and sutured. A vascular bypass was then created from the right common carotid artery to the distal right subclavian artery using an 8 mm Dacron graft (Fig. 4). The procedure was complicated by significant intraoperative bleeding due to severe adhesions to the supraclavicular veins.

Post-operatively, the patient was monitored in the surgical intensive care unit for 24 h. He was successfully extubated, and vocal cord function was confirmed to be stable, avoiding the need for a tracheostomy. The patient was transferred to the vascular surgery ward and discharged on the 8th postoperative day with significant amelioration of his compressive symptoms. Examination at discharge revealed a palpable radial pulse in the right upper limb, with a warm extremity, no edema or discoloration, and normal sensorimotor function.

Histological examination of the resected aneurysm wall showed focal atherosclerotic changes without evidence of underlying inflammatory or connective tissue disease.

The patient resides in a remote area, which prevented him from attending follow‑up appointments and completing the necessary investigations for managing achalasia and continued surveillance of the subclavian aneurysm on the contralateral side.

Discussion

The combination of aortic coarctation and subclavian artery aneurysms is extremely rare. A literature search by Hiller et al. [1] identified only seven similar cases between 1965 and 1999. Since the first report by Mozzi et al. [2], five additional cases of bilateral subclavian artery aneurysms associated with aortic coarctation have been documented [3–6]. Another recent case was presented by Acosta et al. At the 2023 European Society of Cardiology conference in a 40-year-old male with Takayasu arteritis. The primary mechanism for aneurysm formation in most cases is believed to be high shear stress on the wall of the aortic arch and its branches, except in cases associated with specific systemic diseases like Takayasu arteritis [6] or fibromuscular dysplasia.

Both surgical and endovascular approaches are valid for treating subclavian artery aneurysms. In our case, open surgical repair was chosen over an endovascular approach due to the unavailability of specific endovascular materials, high cost, and the high risk of type II endoleak given the aneurysm’s involvement with the vertebral artery and other branches.

Achalasia is an uncommon esophageal motility disorder. Several studies have investigated the relationship between autonomic nervous system dysfunction, esophageal motor innervation, and achalasia [7, 8]. Ohlson et al. Have even proposed considering achalasia as a vagal disease [9]. While external compression can mimic achalasia, this was ruled out by CT imaging in our patient. The presence of hoarseness, left vocal cord paralysis, dysphagia, and reflux following the initial cardiac procedure strongly suggests iatrogenic nerve injury as the underlying cause. Further investigations, such as gastroscopy and esophageal manometry, were deliberately delayed to avoid the risk of aneurysm rupture during manipulation.

Neurological complications during cardiac surgery are uncommon. However, due to their close anatomical proximity to the aortic arch and its branches, the recurrent laryngeal and vagus nerves are particularly vulnerable. Injury to these nerves can result in hoarseness and, as this case suggests, potentially in achalasia.

Considering that we presumed this patient’s achalasia resulted from vagus nerve injury during the initial surgery, in the absence of prior medical records and with no operative details available in the hospital archives, including potential complications from the first procedure, as well as the vocal cord paralysis likely caused by recurrent laryngeal nerve injury.

Noting that the only known information about the initial surgery is the type of incision, as a lateral thoracotomy scar was observed, and that the patient did not require cardiopulmonary bypass(membrane oxygenator).

Conclusion

This case highlights a previously unreported triad of aortic coarctation, a progressive subclavian artery aneurysm, and achalasia. This emphasizes the critical importance of meticulous dissection and nerve preservation during surgery on the aortic arch and its branches. In patients presenting with dysphagia after such procedures, a neurological etiology should be considered in the differential diagnosis.
