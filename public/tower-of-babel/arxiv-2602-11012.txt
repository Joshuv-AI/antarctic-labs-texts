Rational Points in Hyperbolic Regions and Multiplicative Diophantine Approximation on Manifolds
Sam Chow, Rajula Srivastava, Niclas Technau, Han Yu

Abstract
. We establish the convergence theory of multiplicative Diophantine approximation for all non-degenerate, smooth manifolds. We also settle said convergence theory for all affine subspaces satisfying a highly generic and essentially optimal Diophantine condition. This answers a question of Beresnevich and Velani from 2005, while simultaneously sharpening results of Kleinbock and Margulis on the strong extremality of non-degenerate manifolds, and of Kleinbock on the strong extremality of affine subspaces.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

1.1.
Introduction

1.1.1.
Simultaneous and multiplicative approximation

We investigate rational approximations to points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed. It follows from Dirichlet’s approximation theorem that if
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then there exist infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

{\mathrm{max}}\{\|qx_{1}\|,\ldots,\|qx_{n}\|\}<q^{-1/n},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the distance to
\mathbb{Z}
. This describes the rate at which
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
can be simultaneously approximated by rationals of the same denominator. By Khintchine’s theorem
[
32
,
33
]
, if
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
is monotonic and
\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)^{n}=\infty
then, for almost all
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exist infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

{\mathrm{max}}\{\|qx_{1}\|,\ldots,\|qx_{n}\|\}<\psi(q).

This is the cornerstone of the metric theory of diophantine approximation. The result is sharp, for if the series converges then the set of such
{\mathbf{x}}
has measure zero.

We see from the discussion above that if
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then there exist infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|qx_{1}\|\cdots\|qx_{n}\|<1/q.

This describes the multiplicative rate at which
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
can be approximated by rationals of the same denominator. Famously, Littlewood’s conjecture asserts that if
n\geqslant 2
then
1/q
can be replaced by
c/q
here, for any
c>0
. Gallagher’s theorem
[
25
]
asserts that if
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
is monotonic then almost no/every
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|qx_{1}\|\cdots\|qx_{n}\|<\psi(q)

if the series

(1.1)

\sum_{q\geqslant 1}\psi(q)(\log q)^{n-1}

converges/diverges.

1.1.2.
Diophantine approximation on manifolds

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an analytic submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This comes with an induced Lebesgue measure, and one can ask for analogues of the aforementioned theorems of Khintchine and Gallagher. This is an old problem which appears to have had its genesis in a 1932 paper by Mahler
[
37
]
, in which metric diophantine approximation on the Veronese curve
\{(t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{n})\}
is discussed.

There is often a difference in behaviour according to whether
\mathcal{M}
is flat or curved. To be precise, we say that
\mathcal{M}
is
non-degenerate
if it is not contained in any proper affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The definition can be extended to sufficiently smooth manifolds that are not necessarily analytic, see
[
35
]
and Definition
1.3
below.

The Khintchine divergence theory was resolved for
C^{3}
, non-degenerate planar curves in
[
8
]
, and for all analytic, non-degenerate manifolds in
[
4
]
. The assumption of analyticity was removed for curves in
[
11
]
, and recently for all non-degenerate manifolds in
[
6
]
.

The corresponding convergence theory was solved for
C^{3}
, non-degenerate planar curves in
[
43
]
. It was recently settled for all non-degenerate manifolds by Beresnevich and Yang
[
13
]
, making essential use of the quantitative non-divergence estimates developed in
[
35
]
and
[
15
]
.

In the complementary setting of affine subspaces, the convergence theory requires a diophantine assumption on the parametrising matrix. See the recent article of Huang
[
30
]
, which built on the work of Kleinbock
[
34
]
and Huang–Liu
[
31
]
.

Naturally, diophantine approximation on manifolds is closely related to the density of rational points near manifolds. The latter also has a long and distinguished history, and continues to be vigorously investigated
[
14
,
17
,
24
,
26
,
27
,
29
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
42
]
.

1.2.
Main results

The analogue of Gallagher’s theorem has been much more elusive. Badziahin and Levesley
[
3
]
settled the convergence theory for non-degenerate planar curves. In
[
12
, Problem S2]
, released in 2005, Beresnevich and Velani posed the problem of generalising this. In the flat setting there has been substantial progress, for fibres
[
9
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
44
]
, planar lines
[
21
,
30
]
, and affine hyperplanes
[
22
]
. For hypersurfaces with
n\geqslant 5
and non-vanishing Gaussian curvature, the first and fourth named authors recently established the Gallagher property in full
[
22
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say

{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is

\psi
-multiplicatively approximable if

(1.2)

\prod_{i\leqslant n}\|qx_{i}\|<\psi(q)

holds for infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\mathcal{W}^{\times}_{n}(\psi)
be
the set of
\psi
-approximable vectors
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Definition 1.2
.

A Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
convergent Gallagher
if, for any non-increasing function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow[0,1)
,

\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)(\log q)^{n-1}<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu(\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\times}(\psi))=0.

In this paper, we are interested in the set of
\psi
-approximable points on a smooth, flat or curved (non-degenerate) submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For an integer
1\leqslant d\leqslant n
, consider

(1.3)

V\subseteq U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

so that
V
is closed set and
U
a bounded, open set.
Our primary object of interest is the manifold

\mathcal{M}=\{\mathbf{f}({\mathbf{x}}):{\mathbf{x}}\in V\},

embedded by a sufficiently smooth function

\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Note that we allow
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
to be linear. In this ‘flat’ case, we denote the manifold by
\mathcal{L}
. Let us now describe the ‘curved’ setting precisely.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
1\leqslant l\leqslant n-d
be an integer. We say that
{\mathbf{x}}

is an
l
-non-degenerate
point of
\mathcal{M}
if the partial derivatives of

\mathbf{f}
at
{\mathbf{x}}
of order in
[1,l]
span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that
\mathbf{f}({\mathbf{x}})
is non-degenerate if it is
l
-non-degenerate for some
l\leqslant n-d
.

Remark 1.4
.

The non-degeneracy condition is an infinitesimal version of
\mathcal{M}
not lying in any proper
affine hyperplane, i.e. the linear independence of
1,f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
over
\mathbb{R}
. For example,
if the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
are analytic — in other words, the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is analytic — then the linear independence of
1,f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
over
\mathbb{R}

in the domain
U
is equivalent to all points of
\mathcal{M}
being
non-degenerate.

We establish the following results.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a smooth, non-degenerate manifold of dimension
d\geqslant 1
. Then the natural Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{M}
is convergent Gallagher.

Remark 1.6
.

We establish this indirectly. We use a fibring trick (Appendix
A
) to reduce the theorem to that concerning smooth, non-degenerate curves (Theorem
1.14
). More precisely, we will prove a fibring lemma (Lemma
A.1
) for
sufficiently smooth,
non-degenerate manifolds which shows that any
d
-dimensional smooth, non-degenerate manifold
\mathcal{M}
can be decomposed into smooth, non-degenerate curves. Such a result has been well known and often used for
analytic
manifolds and curves, see
[
5
,
41
]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
1\leqslant d<n
be positive integers.
Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
d
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying Hypothesis D.
Then the induced Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{L}
is convergent Gallagher.

Remark 1.8
.

Hypothesis D is a diophantine condition on the defining parameters of
\mathcal{L}
that will be described in §
3
. We will see that it is almost sharp, and that it is generic.

1.3.
Further remarks and open problems

Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
are already new, for example, for curves/lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}.
While these results account for most of the manifolds, there are still some cases that remain unexplored.

Conjecture 1.9
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an affine subspace satisfying Hypothesis D. Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{L}
be a smooth manifold which is non-degenerate when viewed as a manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{\dim\mathcal{L}}\simeq\mathcal{L}.
Then the induced Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{M}
is convergent Gallagher.

Remark 1.10
.

This is closely related to an ‘inheritance’ property discussed in
[
34
]
, where it was shown that analytic manifolds inherit the extremality from their supporting affine subspaces, i.e. the smallest containing affine subspace. In the case
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the conjecture specialises to Theorem
1.5
.

Another curious problem concerns the monotonicity condition on the approximation function
\psi
. Notice that the convergent Gallagher property is satisfied by the ambient Lebesgue measure, i.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, without the monotonicity condition. It is unclear whether the monotonicity condition is necessary in the convergence theory of manifolds.

Question 1.11
.

In Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
, is it possible to drop the monotonicity condition on
\psi
?

Remark 1.12
.

For simultaneous approximation,
[
30
, Theorem 2.3]
provides a sufficient condition for the non-monotonic convergence property for affine subspaces. In our multiplicative context, it is likely that a non-monotonic version of flat Theorem
1.7
can be supplied. However, the non-monotonic analogue of the curved Theorem
1.5
is largely open. There are some existing results along these lines, e.g.
[
10
,
22
,
28
]
.
However, we do not know of any example of a non-degenerate manifold which fails the non-monotonic convergence theory, either simultaneous or multiplicative.

Finally, we note that our method is robust enough to handle variations of the approximation problem, e.g. weighted approximation and, in some cases, inhomogeneous approximation. We choose not to pursue this level of generality in this paper.

Remark 1.13
.

While finishing this paper, we became aware of related work of Beresnevich, Datta,
and Yang
[
7
]
on weighted and multiplicative Diophantine approximation for non-degenerate manifolds.
Their approach uses dynamical methods and the geometry of numbers, building upon
[
4
,
13
]
.

Organisation

We discuss some main ideas of the proofs in §

For Theorem

For Theorem

Theorem 1.14
.

If the curve
\mathcal{C}
from

Theorem

then follows from Theorem

.

2.
Outline of the proofs

Our proofs blend many different ideas from previous works
[
22
,
30
,
38
,
40
]
. To gain a coherent understanding, we first discuss the central idea through both the proofs. After that, more specific techniques diverge for the flat and curved cases.

2.1.
A high-level overview

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth manifold, and let
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{M}
. Let
Q>0
be a large integer. We wish to study the distribution of
\|q{\mathbf{x}}\|
for
q\in[Q,2Q]
. Given the nature of the approximation problem — Khintchine or Gallagher — we find a target set
\mathcal{T}_{Q}\subset[0,1]^{n}
controlled by the approximation function at height
Q
, i.e.
\psi(Q)
, and estimate the target-hitting statistic

\{q\in[Q,2Q]:\|q{\mathbf{x}}\|\in\mathcal{T}_{Q}\}.

For the Khintchine problem, we choose
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
to be a Euclidean ball of radius
\psi(Q)^{1/n}
. For the Gallagher problem, we choose
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
to be a hyperbolic region whose Lebesgue measure is
\psi(Q)\log^{n-1}(1/\psi(Q))
. We further decompose
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
into a union of rectangles of various dimensions. Existing methods, e.g.
[
13
,
38
,
40
]
, enable us to handle the case when
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
is a rectangle that is not too small, or a finite union of such rectangles.
In the case of counting rational points near curved manifolds, this was done
for the first time by the second named author
[
40
]
, albeit under a rigid geometric condition on the manifold. To study the Gallagher problem, one needs to study the target-hitting statistics in a uniform manner for all possible rectangular
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
. It is this uniformity that requires new inputs. In this paper, we illustrate how to handle it via Fourier analysis.

Considering only
\|q{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q\in[Q,2Q]}
for each point
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{M}
, hardly gives us any new information.
To take advantage of the structure of
\mathcal{M}
, we instead consider a suitable neighbourhood of the point
{\mathbf{x}}
. Let
T_{\mathbf{x}}
be the affine subspace tangent to
\mathcal{M}
at
{\mathbf{x}}
. For
\delta>0
, consider the
\delta
-ball
B_{\delta,{\mathbf{x}}}
around
{\mathbf{x}}
in
\mathcal{M}
. Owing to the smoothness of
\mathcal{M}
, the sets
T_{{\mathbf{x}}}
and
B_{\delta,{\mathbf{x}}}
are equivalent at scale
\delta^{1/2}
, i.e. their Hausdorff distance is
O(\delta^{1/2})
. We choose a suitable value of
\delta
according to
Q
. Instead of looking at
\|q{\mathbf{x}}\|,
we look at
\|qB_{\delta,{\mathbf{x}}}\|
, which is essentially
\|qT_{{\mathbf{x}}}\|
up to a suitable cutoff.

Since
T_{\mathbf{x}}
is linear, our first task is then the study of diophantine approximation for points in affine linear subspaces. When
\mathcal{M}
is flat, we shall see that under a mild condition (Hypothesis D), the sequence
\|qT_{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q\in[Q,2Q]}
effectively equidistributes in
[0,1]^{n}
, uniformly across a large collection of rectangular
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
. From here, we obtain the Gallagher convergence theory for such affine subspaces. This is carried out in §
3
.

In the curved setting, we can decompose
\mathcal{M}
into small and approximately flat pieces. It would be convenient if all such flat pieces were to satisfy Hypothesis D, but this is not true in general. To overcome this issue, we assume that
\mathcal{M}
is non-degenerate and invoke the quantitative non-divergence estimate for non-degenerate manifolds — due to Bernik–Kleinbock–Margulis, see Theorem
4.10
— which states, roughly speaking, that most points
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{M}
satisfy a certain diophantine condition involving
{\mathbf{x}}
and
T_{\mathbf{x}}
. We decompose
\mathcal{M}
according to the quantitative non-divergence rate; such a decomposition is referred to as sub-level set decomposition, and its simultaneous approximation version was introduced in
[
38
]
. Recall that we need to handle target-hitting statistics uniformly for many rectangular
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
. The decomposition is in general different for different rectangular
\mathcal{T}_{Q}.
For a uniform treatment, one needs to carefully track this dependency. This technical issue will be taken care of in §
4.5
. The uniformity is illustrated in §
4.6
.

These operations furnish a family of decompositions — one for each rectangular
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
— of
\mathcal{M}
into small almost-linear pieces, and after dropping a small number of them, we can assume that all these almost-linear pieces satisfy a certain diophantine property. We exploit the diophantine property to obtain equidistribution for
\|q\cup_{{\mathbf{x}}}T_{{\mathbf{x}}}\|_{q\in[Q,2Q]}.
This is done in §
4.8
via Fourier analysis in a similar manner to
[
38
]
. In doing so, we also bookkeep the dependence between the shape of
\mathcal{T}_{Q}
and the decomposition on
\mathcal{M}
. From here, we are able to establish the convergence Gallagher property for non-degenerate manifolds.

Remark 2.1
.

We restrict attention to non-degenerate manifolds because we rely on the quantitative non-divergence estimate of Bernik, Kleinbock, and Margulis. We believe that the latter should hold for relatively non-degenerate manifolds in diophantine affine subspaces, potentially leading to inroads into Conjecture
1.9
.

2.2.
The flat case, i.e. Theorem
1.7

We start by expressing
\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\times}(\psi)
as a lim sup set of hyperbolic regions indexed by
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Collecting together
q\in(Q,2Q]
for
Q
a power of two, we index by
Q
and eventually apply the first Borel–Cantelli lemma. This reduces our task to bounding the induced Lebesgue measure of

\bigcup_{Q\leqslant q\leqslant 2Q}\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{B}:(q\mathbf{t},q(A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}))\in\mathcal{T}\},

where
\mathfrak{B}
is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}:\|x_{1}\|\cdots\|x_{n}\|\leqslant\psi(Q)\}
. We can cover
\mathcal{T}
by axis-aligned rectangles
\mathcal{A}
that are not too small. These steps are executed in §
3.4
.

Next, we observe that
q\mathbf{t}
vanishes on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
whenever
\mathbf{t}
is
q^{-1}
times an integer vector. This enables us to decompose the flow
(q\mathbf{t},q(A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}))
into small regions
\wp
. Here
\wp
begins at
(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{p})
, where
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\mathbf{p}
is some

O(Q)
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of
\mathbf{b}
and the columns of
A
. This reduces our task to counting how many small regions
\wp
intersect
\mathcal{A}
. The key observation is that
\wp
can only intersect
\mathcal{A}
if
\mathbf{p}
lies in a certain projection of
\mathcal{A}
. These steps are executed in §
3.3
.

Finally, we need to bound how many times
\mathbf{p}
lies in this projected set. The latter is efficiently contained within an axis-aligned rectangle, and the requisite count matches the natural equidistribution heuristic. Using smooth weights and Fourier series, this expectation is realised by the contribution of the zero frequency. Our smooth weights are blessed with rapid Fourier decay, and bounding the contributions from the non-zero frequencies boils down to dual diophantine approximation for
\mathbf{b}
and the columns of
A
. Hypothesis D implies a gap principle which enables us to successfully bound these contributions. These steps are executed in §
3.2
.

2.3.
The curved case, i.e. Theorem
1.5

Using a fibring trick (Appendix
A
) and the non-degeneracy of
\mathcal{M}
, we first reduce matters to a model class of smooth, non-degenerate curves in §
4.1
.
Next, as described in §
4.2
, to estimate the number of

\psi(t)
-good multiplicative approximations
near

\mathcal{C}
for each integer
t
, we need to
count lattice points in
non-isotropic
dyadic rectangles around
\mathcal{C}
of dimensions
{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}=(2^{-z_{1}},\ldots,2^{-z_{n}})
and volume

2^{-z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}}\asymp\psi(2^{t})\asymp 2^{-t(1+o(1))}.

In §
4.3
, we pass to this non-isotropic counting function
and in §
4.4
, we reduce the proof of Theorem
1.14
to that of our main counting result, Theorem
4.5
. Our next aim is to show that, modulo a set of small measure, all of the relevant non-isotropic rectangles around
\mathcal{C}
contain the expected number of rational points.
We pass to a smooth counting function in §
4.5
and make some final technical reductions in §
4.6
.

After two Poisson summations,
this counting function can be reinterpreted
as
the Fourier transform of
the surface measure of

\mathcal{T}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{1+n}

given by
(y,x)\mapsto y(1,{\mathbf{f}}(x))
, where
{\mathbf{f}}
is as in (
1.4
).
With this in mind, we smoothly decompose the curve
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}

using non-standard bump functions adapted to small scales depending on
t
and
{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}
. This is carried out in §
4.7
.

The set
\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}
is the
portion of
\mathcal{C}
which lifts to the part of
\mathcal{T}
where the Fourier transform of its surface measure
does not have rapid decay.
Building on the work of Schindler and two of the authors
[
38
]
,
we reinterpret this as a sub-level set estimation problem in §
4.7.2
. To bound the measure of
\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}
, we use the seminal work
of Bernik, Kleinbock and Margulis from
[
15
]
on quantitative non-divergence in the space of lattices (see Theorem
4.10
). This is carried out in §
4.7.1
.

To establish good Fourier decay of the surface measure on the complementary part
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}
,
we estimate a family of oscillatory integrals whose amplitudes are the aforementioned bump functions whose derivatives blow up as in (
4.28
) and (
4.44
).
This is achieved in §
4.8
. The broad strategy here is similar to that in
[
38
]
. However, the non-isotropic/multiplicative nature of problem at hand requires more delicate estimates, leading to new constraints on the parameters in the quantitative non-divergence estimate and the scale of the bump functions, all of which are functions of
{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}
and
t
. In §
4.9
, we choose these parameters and combine our arguments to prove Theorem
4.5
.

3.
The flat setting

In this section, we first discuss Hypothesis D — including its sharpness and genericity — and
prove Theorem
1.7
.

3.1.
Diophantine hypothesis

Let
\mathcal{L}
be
d
-dimensional. We assume that the projection of
\mathcal{L}
onto any axis-aligned subspace of dimension
d
is surjective. Then
\mathcal{L}
has the Monge parametrisation

(3.1)

\mathcal{L}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{t}\\
A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}\end{pmatrix}:\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\},

where

A=({\mathbf{a}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{a}}^{(d)})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-d)\times d}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d}.

Moreover, by our projection-surjectivity assumption,
\mathcal{L}
has such a parametrisation in each of the
n!
many orderings of the standard basis on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To elaborate, in (
3.1
) we treated the first
d
coordinates as being special. However, we can deem any choice of
d
coordinates to be special, resulting in different Monge forms. For example, in the case
(n,d)=(2,1)
, we can present a planar line as
y=ax+b
or
x=a^{-1}y-a^{-1}b.

Hypothesis D will be described in terms of Hypothesis
D(c,{\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2})
, for certain parameters
c,{\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, we write

|\mathbf{m}|={\mathrm{max}}\{|m_{j}|:1\leqslant j\leqslant k\},\qquad H(\mathbf{m})=\prod_{j\leqslant k}{\mathrm{max}}\{1,|m_{j}|\}.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be as described at the beginning of this section. Let
c>0
, and let
{\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2}\in(0,\infty]
.
We say
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
Hypothesis
D(c,{\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2})

if, for any Monge parametrisation and any
T\geqslant 1
, the inequality

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{max}}\{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}\|,\ldots,\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(d)}\|,\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\mathbf{b}\|\}\geqslant cT^{-\sigma_{1}}

holds whenever

(3.3)

\mathbf{0}\neq{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d},\qquad|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\leqslant T^{\sigma_{2}},\qquad H({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\leqslant T.

Now we can describe what it means for

\mathcal{L}
to satisfy Hypothesis D.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be as described at the beginning of this section. Hypothesis D asserts that there exist

c>0\quad\text{and}\quad{\gamma}\in\left[\frac{n-d}{n},1\right)

such that Hypothesis
D(c,\gamma(1+d{\sigma}_{2}),{\sigma}_{2})
holds for all

\frac{1}{n-d}\leqslant{\sigma}_{2}\leqslant 1.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be as described at the beginning of this section, and suppose Hypothesis
D(c,{\sigma}_{1},\infty)
holds for some
c>0
and some

{\sigma}_{1}\in\left[1,\frac{n}{n-d}\right).

Then Hypothesis D holds.

Proof.

Put

{\gamma}={\sigma}_{1}\frac{n-d}{n}\in\left[\frac{n-d}{n},1\right),

and let
{\sigma}_{2}\geqslant 1/(n-d)
. Then

{\gamma}(1+d{\sigma}_{2})\geqslant\frac{{\sigma}_{1}(n-d)}{n}\left(1+\frac{d}{n-d}\right)={\sigma}_{1},

so Hypothesis
D(c,\gamma(1+d{\sigma}_{2}),{\sigma}_{2})
holds.
∎

We now discuss some special cases in which the condition simplifies.

Example 3.4
.

Consider the case
d=n-1
of affine hyperplanes. Then

A=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
is a row vector of non-zero reals. Our Monge form is

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d},a_{0}+a_{1}t_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}t_{d}),

where
a_{0}=b
. Kleinbock
[
34
]
considered the case of strong extremality, namely
\psi(q)=q^{-\tau}
for
\tau>1
. By
[
34
, Corollary 5.7]
, it is necessary to assume that

\omega(A,b):=\inf\{{\sigma}\geqslant 0:{\mathrm{max}}\{\|qa_{j}\|:0\leqslant j\leqslant d\}\gg_{\sigma}q^{-{\sigma}}\}\leqslant n.

In this case, since
n-d=1
we always have
{\sigma}_{2}=1
, and Hypothesis D reduces to having

\omega(A,b)<n

in any ordering of the standard basis.

Note that Kleinbock allows some of the
a_{i}
to vanish. Our method is also robust enough to achieve this, but we defer the discussion until after the proof, see Remark
3.11
.

Example 3.5
.

In the case
(n,d)=(2,1)
of planar lines, the assumption that (
3.2
) holds for some
{\sigma}_{1}<2
with
{\sigma}_{2}=1
matches Huang’s threshold
[
30
, Theorem 2.4]
.

We now show that Hypothesis D is almost sharp. A vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
very well multiplicatively approximable
(VWMA) if it satisfies the following equivalent conditions
[
35
, §1]
:

(i)

For some
\tau>1
, there are infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\|qx_{1}\|\cdots\|qx_{n}\|<|q|^{-\tau}.

(ii)

For some
\tau>1
, there are infinitely many
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

\|\mathbf{m}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}\|<H(\mathbf{m})^{-\tau}.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be as described at the beginning of this section. Let
\gamma>1
and
{\sigma}_{2}\geqslant 1/(n-d)
. Assume that, for infinitely many positive integers
T
, there exists

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

such that

{\mathrm{max}}\{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}\|,\ldots,\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(d)}\|,\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\mathbf{b}\|\}<T^{-{\gamma}(1+d{\sigma}_{2})}

and

|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\leqslant T^{{\sigma}_{2}},\qquad H({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\leqslant T.

Then all points on
\mathcal{L}
are VWMA, and in particular
\mathcal{L}
is not convergent Gallagher.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{t}\\
A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}\end{pmatrix}.

Put

v=\frac{1+{\gamma}}{2}(1+d{\sigma}_{2}),

and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be sufficiently small in terms of
A
and
\mathbf{t}
. By assumption, for some arbitrarily large
T
, there exist
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}
with

0<|\mathbf{q}|\leqslant T^{{\sigma}_{2}},\qquad H(\mathbf{q})\leqslant T

and

|p_{j}+\mathbf{q}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(j)}|<\varepsilon^{2}T^{-v}\qquad(1\leqslant j\leqslant d+1),

where
{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)}=\mathbf{b}
.

Write
(A,\mathbf{b})=(a_{i,j})
and
t_{d+1}=1
. By the triangle inequality,

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leqslant n-d\\
j\leqslant d+1\end{subarray}}(p_{j}+q_{i}a_{i,j})t_{j}\right|<\varepsilon T^{-v}.

With
\mathbf{m}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d},q_{1},\ldots,q_{n-d})
, we therefore have

\displaystyle|\mathbf{m}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}+p_{d+1}|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\sum_{j\leqslant d+1}p_{j}t_{j}\right)+\sum_{i\leqslant n-d}q_{i}\left(b_{i}+\sum_{j\leqslant d}a_{ij}t_{j}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leqslant n-d\\
j\leqslant d+1\end{subarray}}(p_{j}+q_{i}a_{i,j})t_{j}\right|<\varepsilon T^{-v}.

Furthermore, since
|\mathbf{q}|\leqslant T^{{\sigma}_{2}}
,

H(\mathbf{m})\ll_{A,\mathbf{t}}|\mathbf{q}|^{d}H(\mathbf{q})\leqslant T^{1+d{\sigma}_{2}}=:T_{0}.

With

\tau=\frac{v}{1+d{\sigma}_{2}}=\frac{1+{\gamma}}{2}>1,

we have

\|\mathbf{m}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}\|<\varepsilon T_{0}^{-\tau}\ll_{A,\mathbf{t}}H(\mathbf{m})^{-\tau}.

As
T_{0}
can be arbitrarily large, we see that
{\mathbf{x}}
is VWMA.
∎

Next, we show that Hypothesis D is generic.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
1\leqslant d<n
be integers, and let
\gamma\geqslant 1
. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the set of matrices

A=({\mathbf{a}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)})\in[0,1]^{(n-d)\times(d+1)}

such that there are infinitely many
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}
for which

(3.4)

{\mathrm{max}}\{\|\mathbf{q}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}\|,\ldots,\|\mathbf{q}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)}\|\}<H(\mathbf{q})^{-{\sigma}_{1}},

where

(3.5)

|\mathbf{q}|=H(\mathbf{q})^{{\sigma}_{2}},\qquad{\sigma}_{1}=\gamma(1+d{\sigma}_{2}).

Then

{\mathrm{\dim_{H}}}(\mathcal{W})=(d+1)(n-d-1)+\frac{d+2}{1+{\gamma}(d+1)}\leqslant(n-d)(d+1)-d.

Proof.

We begin with the upper bound

{\mathrm{\dim_{H}}}(\mathcal{W})\leqslant(d+1)(n-d-1)+\frac{d+2}{1+{\gamma}(d+1)},

adapting a classical covering argument
[
23
]
. Put

m=d+1,\qquad m^{\prime}=n-d.

For
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}}
, let

R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m}:\mathbf{q}^{T}A+\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}\}

and, for
{\delta}>0
, let

\Delta(R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}},{\delta})=\{A\in[0,1]^{m^{\prime}\times m}:{\mathrm{dist}}(A,R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}})<{\delta}\}.

Note that if (
3.4
) holds then, for some
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, we have

{\mathrm{dist}}(A,R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}})<H(\mathbf{q})^{-{\sigma}_{1}}|\mathbf{q}|^{-1},

and so
A\in\Delta(R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}},H(\mathbf{q})^{-{\sigma}_{1}}|\mathbf{q}|^{-1})
. Consequently, for any
Q\geqslant 2
,

\mathcal{W}\subseteq\bigcup_{|\mathbf{q}|\geqslant Q}\bigcup_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}{\Delta}(R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}},H(\mathbf{q})^{-{\sigma}_{1}}|\mathbf{q}|^{-1}).

Let

(3.6)

\tau>m(m^{\prime}-1)+\frac{1+m}{1+{\gamma}m}.

The implied constants below depend at most on
m,m^{\prime},\tau
.

Given
\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}
, let us write
{\delta}=H(\mathbf{q})^{-{\sigma}_{1}}|\mathbf{q}|^{-1}
. Then

\#\{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}:{\Delta}(R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}},{\delta})\neq\emptyset\}\ll|\mathbf{q}|^{m}.

Now
{\Delta}(R_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}},{\delta})
is covered by a collection
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}
of balls of radius
{\delta}
, where

\#\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\ll{\delta}^{m(1-m^{\prime})}.

By (
3.5
), we have

{\sigma}_{2}=\frac{\log|\mathbf{q}|}{\log H(\mathbf{q})},\qquad\delta^{-1}=|\mathbf{q}|^{\gamma d+1+\gamma{\sigma}^{-1}_{2}}.

Now

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(\mathcal{W})

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|\geqslant Q}|\mathbf{q}|^{m}{\delta}^{\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|\geqslant Q}|\mathbf{q}|^{m+({\gamma}d+1+{\gamma}{\sigma}_{2}^{-1})(m(m^{\prime}-1)-\tau)}

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{q}|^{(mm^{\prime}-\tau)({\gamma}d+1)-m{\gamma}d}H(\mathbf{q})^{-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma}},

and so

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(\mathcal{W})

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{q_{m^{\prime}}\geqslant Q}\>\sum_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{m^{\prime}-1}\leqslant q_{m^{\prime}}}q_{m^{\prime}}^{(mm^{\prime}-\tau)({\gamma}d+1)-m{\gamma}d}(q_{1}\cdots q_{m^{\prime}})^{-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{q_{m^{\prime}}\geqslant Q}q_{m^{\prime}}^{(mm^{\prime}-\tau)({\gamma}d+1)-m{\gamma}d-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma}}(\log q_{m^{\prime}}+q_{m^{\prime}}^{1-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma}})^{m^{\prime}-1}.

Taking
Q\to\infty
furnishes
\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(\mathcal{W})=0
, as long as

(3.7)

(mm^{\prime}-\tau)({\gamma}d+1)-m{\gamma}d-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma}<-1

and

\displaystyle(mm^{\prime}-\tau)({\gamma}d+1)-m{\gamma}d-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(m^{\prime}-1)(1-(\tau-m(m^{\prime}-1)){\gamma})<-1.

These inequalities hold as long as

\tau>{\mathrm{max}}\left\{mm^{\prime}+\frac{1-m({\gamma}d+{\gamma})}{1+{\gamma}d+{\gamma}},m(m^{\prime}-1)+\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{1+{\gamma}d+m^{\prime}{\gamma}}\right\}.

Since
m=d+1
and
m+m^{\prime}-1=n
, we require that

\tau>{\mathrm{max}}\left\{mm^{\prime}+\frac{1-{\gamma}m^{2}}{1+{\gamma}m},m(m^{\prime}-1)+\frac{1+n}{1+n{\gamma}}\right\},

i.e.

\tau>{\mathrm{max}}\left\{m(m^{\prime}-1)+\frac{1+m}{1+{\gamma}m},m(m^{\prime}-1)+\frac{1+n}{1+{\gamma}n}\right\}.

As
{\gamma}\geqslant 1
and
n\geqslant m
, the first term dominates. Thus, by (
3.6
), our requirement is met and indeed
\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(\mathcal{W})=0
. Therefore

\displaystyle{\mathrm{\dim_{H}}}(\mathcal{W})

\displaystyle\leqslant m(m^{\prime}-1)+\frac{1+m}{1+{\gamma}m}

\displaystyle=(d+1)(n-d-1)+\frac{d+2}{1+{\gamma}(d+1)}.

The lower bound follows by considering only vectors of the form

\mathbf{q}=(0,\dots,0,q).

For such vectors,

{\sigma}_{2}=1,\qquad{\sigma}_{1}=\gamma(1+d).

Specialising

(m,n,\boldsymbol{{\beta}},{\lambda})=(1,d+1,\mathbf{0},{\gamma}(d+1))

in
[
36
, Theorem 1]
, we obtain some
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
that is the Cartesian product of a set
\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)(n-d-1)}
, where

{\mathrm{\dim_{H}}}(\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime})=\frac{d+2}{1+{\gamma}(d+1)}.

From here, we see that

{\mathrm{\dim_{H}}}(\mathcal{W})\geqslant{\mathrm{\dim_{H}}}(\mathcal{W}^{\prime})=(d+1)(n-d-1)+\frac{d+2}{1+{\gamma}(d+1)}.

∎

3.2.
Equidistribution on tori

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
A,\mathbf{b}
satisfy (
3.2
) whenever we have (
3.3
), where

c>0,\qquad 1\leqslant{\sigma}_{1}\leqslant n,\qquad\frac{1}{n-d}\leqslant{\sigma}_{2}\leqslant 1.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\left\{q_{1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}+\cdots+q_{d+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)}\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}:\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1},\>0\leqslant q_{j}\leqslant Q-1\>\forall j\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\mathbb{T}^{n-d},

where
{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)}=\mathbf{b}
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be an axis-aligned rectangle in
\mathbb{T}^{n-d}
, and let
Q\in\mathbb{N}
. If

{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})\geqslant\varepsilon Q^{\varepsilon-1/{\sigma}_{1}}

for some
\varepsilon>0
, and each side length of
\mathcal{S}
is at least
\varepsilon{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})^{{\sigma}_{2}}
, then

\#\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{P}\ll_{n,c,\varepsilon}{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})Q^{d+1}.

By symmetry, the lemma also holds with

\{q_{1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}+\cdots+q_{d+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)}:\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n-d},\>|q_{j}|\leqslant Q\>\forall j\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{n-d}

in place of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
Q
is arbitrarily large in terms of
n,c,\varepsilon
. Now
consider the vectors

\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{j\leqslant d+1}q_{j}{\mathbf{a}}^{(j)}\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n-d},

as
\mathbf{q}
varies.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be the probability measure obtained by placing a Dirac mass
Q^{-d-1}
at
\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{q}}
, for each
\mathbf{q}
.
For each
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-d
, let
r_{j}
be the side length of
\mathcal{S}
in direction
j
.
Let
\varpi
be a bump function adapted to
\mathcal{S}
, so that
\varpi\geqslant 1
on
\mathcal{S}
and
\|\varpi\|_{1}\leqslant 2{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})
,
and the support of
\varpi
is contained in an axis-aligned rectangle of length
2r_{j}
in direction
j
, for each
j
.

By Plancherel,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle\leqslant Q^{d+1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n-d}}\varpi({\mathbf{x}}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}}\hat{\varpi}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

The Fourier coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=Q^{-d-1}\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{P}}e(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}})=Q^{-d-1}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle=Q^{-d-1}\sum_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{d+1}=0}^{Q-1}e(-\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}},q_{1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}+\cdots+q_{d+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)}\rangle)

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\leqslant d+1}Q^{-1}\sum_{q=0}^{Q-1}e(-q{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(j)})\ll\prod_{j\leqslant d+1}\min\{1,(Q\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(j)}\|)^{-1}\}.

By the rapid decay of
\hat{\varpi}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\#\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{P}}{Q^{d+1}}\ll{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}\\
|\xi_{j}|\leqslant Q^{\varepsilon/n}/r_{j}\>\forall j\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|.

Now consider the points

(3.9)

({\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}^{(d+1)})\in\mathbb{T}^{d+1},

for non-zero vectors
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}
with
|\xi_{j}|\leqslant Q^{\varepsilon/n}/r_{j}
for all
j
.
The assumption (
3.2
), applied with

T=\prod_{j\leqslant n-d}(Q^{\varepsilon/n}/r_{j}),

implies the following gap principle: if
\mathbf{z}
is such a point, or the difference between two distinct such points, then

\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}\gg{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})^{{\sigma}_{1}}Q^{-\varepsilon{\sigma}_{1}(n-d)/n}.

Since

{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})\gg Q^{\varepsilon-1/{\sigma}_{1}},

it follows that

(3.10)

\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}\gg Q^{\varepsilon d/n-1}.

To finish, we cover
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
by cubes of side length
Q^{\varepsilon d/n-1}
. By our gap principle (
3.10
), any such cube contains at most
O(1)
many of the points (
3.9
). Thus, by (
3.8
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{0}\neq{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}\\
|\xi_{j}|\leqslant Q^{\varepsilon/n}/r_{j}\>\forall j\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle\ll\left(\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\frac{1}{1+qQ^{\varepsilon d/n}}\right)^{d+1}\ll 1.

∎

3.3.
Target-hitting estimate

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be an axis-aligned rectangle in
[0,1)^{n}
, with side length
r_{j}
in direction
j
for each
j
, where

0<r_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant r_{n}\leqslant 1.

Assume that
r_{d+1}=(r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n})^{{\sigma}_{2}}
. Let
A,\mathbf{b}
satisfy (
3.2
) whenever we have (
3.3
), where

c>0,\qquad 1\leqslant{\sigma}_{1}\leqslant n.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
be such that

{\lambda}_{n}(\mathcal{R})^{\chi}\geqslant\varepsilon Q^{\varepsilon-1/{\sigma}_{1}},

for
\chi=1/(1+d{\sigma}_{2})
.
Then

{\lambda}_{d}(\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{B}:\exists q\in[Q,2Q]\quad(q\mathbf{t},q(A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}))\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n}\in\mathcal{R}\})\ll_{n,c,\varepsilon,A,T}Q{\lambda}_{n}(\mathcal{R}),

for any ball
\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius

T\geqslant 2
centred at the origin.

Proof.

Define the projection

\displaystyle\pi:[0,1)^{n}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{n-d}

\displaystyle({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbf{y}-A{\mathbf{x}}.

Then
\pi(\mathcal{R})\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, for some axis-aligned rectangle
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{n-d}
where, for each coordinate
d+1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, the side length of
\mathcal{S}
in direction
j
is at least
r_{j}
and
O_{A}(r_{j})
.

For
q=Q,Q+1,\ldots,2Q
, let

\mathcal{L}_{q}=\{(q\mathbf{t},q(A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b})):\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{B}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Let
I_{d}
be the
d
-dimensional identity matrix.
We cover
\mathcal{L}_{q}
by a union of small regions

\wp=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{m}\\
\mathbf{p}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}\\
A\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}:\mathbf{z}\in[0,1)^{d}\right\},

where
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}

\displaystyle=A\mathbf{m}+q\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}:=\left\{q_{1}{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}+\cdots+q_{d}{\mathbf{a}}^{(d)}+q_{d+1}\mathbf{b}:-2TQ\leqslant q_{1},\ldots,q_{d+1}\leqslant 2TQ\right\}.

Let
N
be the total number of these small regions
\wp
such that
\wp\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n}
intersects
\mathcal{R}
,
as we range over
q=Q,Q+1,\ldots,2Q
. The key observation is that if
\wp
intersects
\mathcal{R}+\mathbb{Z}^{n}
then its starting point
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{m}\\
\mathbf{p}\end{pmatrix}
lies in

(\{\mathbf{0}\}\times\pi(\mathcal{R}))+\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

Therefore

N\ll_{T}\#((\pi(\mathcal{R})\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n-d})\cap\mathcal{P})\leqslant\#\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{P},

where

\mathcal{P}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n-d}
.

Observe that

r_{1}\cdots r_{n}=(r_{1}\cdots r_{d})(r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n})\leqslant(r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n})^{d{\sigma}_{2}}r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n},

whence

r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n}\geqslant(r_{1}\cdots r_{n})^{\chi}.

Note also that

\frac{1}{n-d}\leqslant{\sigma}_{2}\leqslant 1.

Since

{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})\gg r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n}\geqslant(r_{1}\cdots r_{n})^{\chi}\gg Q^{\varepsilon-1/{\sigma}_{1}}

and

r_{d+1}=(r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n})^{{\sigma}_{2}}\gg{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})^{{\sigma}_{2}},

we may apply Lemma
3.8
, giving

N\ll{\lambda}_{n-d}(\mathcal{S})Q^{d+1}\ll r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n}Q^{d+1}.

For each
\wp
, the set of
\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{B}

3.4.
Completing the proof of Theorem

In this subsection, we prove Theorem

. Let

{\gamma}\in\left[\frac{n-d}{n},1\right)

and

be as in Hypothesis D. Let

By assumption, if
({\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2})\in\mathcal{G}
then

holds.
Let

Let

be monotonic, with

\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)(\log q)^{n-1}<\infty.

We claim we may assume that for
({\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2})\in\mathcal{G}
,

\psi(q)^{1/(1+d{\sigma}_{2})}>q^{(\varepsilon-1)/{\sigma}_{1}}\qquad(q\in\mathbb{N}).

To see this, note that if
({\sigma}_{1},{\sigma}_{2})\in\mathcal{G}
then

(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1+d{\sigma}_{2}}{{\sigma}_{1}}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{{\gamma}}>1.

Hence, replacing
\psi(q)
by
\tilde{\psi}(q):=\psi(q)+q^{(\varepsilon-1)/\gamma}
yields a monotonic function such that

\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\times}(\tilde{\psi})\supseteq\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\times}(\psi),\qquad\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\psi}(q)<\infty.

This justifies the claim.

Let
\mu_{d}
be the induced Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{L}
. Let

, and let

be the ball of radius

centred at the origin.
By symmetry, it suffices to prove that

\mu_{d}(\mathcal{W})=0,

where
\mathcal{W}
is the set of

such that

\|qx_{1}\|\cdots\|qx_{n}\|<\psi(q),\qquad\|qx_{1}\|\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\|qx_{n}\|

has infinitely many solutions
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
Q=1,2,4,8,\ldots
, we consider the hyperbolic region

\mathcal{T}_{Q}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}:\|x_{1}\|\cdots\|x_{n}\|\leqslant\psi(Q),\quad\|x_{1}\|\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\|x_{n}\|\}.

We have

\mathcal{W}\subseteq\limsup_{Q\in\{1,2,4,8,\ldots\}}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{Q},

where

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{Q}=\{(\mathbf{t},A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}):\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{Q}\},

wherein

\mathcal{E}_{Q}=\bigcup_{Q\leqslant q\leqslant 2Q}\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{B}:(q\mathbf{t},q(A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}))\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{Q}\}.

We can cover
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
by

many sets of the form

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})=\bigcup_{Q\leqslant q\leqslant 2Q}\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{B}:(q\mathbf{t},q(A\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{b}))\,\,\textnormal{mod}\,\,\mathbb{Z}^{n}\in\mathcal{R}\}.

Here

is an axis-aligned rectangle, with side length

and

{\lambda}_{n}(\mathcal{R})\asymp\psi(Q).

We apply Lemma

given by

r_{d+1}=(r_{d+1}\cdots r_{n})^{{\sigma}_{2}},\qquad{\sigma}_{1}={\gamma}(1+d{\sigma}_{2}).

This furnishes

The Cauchy condensation test now delivers

Remark 3.11

4.3.
A Counting Problem

Theorem

will be a consequence of an estimate

rational points contained in certain
anisotropic neighbourhoods
of subsets of

For

and

we define the counting function

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)=\#\left\{({\mathbf{a}},q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}:2^{t}\leqslant q<2^{t+1},\quad\exists x\in\mathcal{G}\textrm{ with }\eqref{eq proximity cond}\right\},

where (

\Big|f_{i}(x)-\frac{a_{i}}{q}\Big|<\frac{\delta_{i}}{q}\textrm{ for }1\leqslant i\leqslant n.

This is to say that

will follow from the following theorem which achieves two objectives: count rational points in a neighbourhood of a ‘good’ portion of the curve

{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}
and

.
Previously in
[
38
]
, a similar strategy
was used for the Khintchine-type convergence theory. Here, the neighbourhoods
of the curve in which the rational points need to be counted are boxes with widely different side lengths,
rather than hypercubes. This introduces new difficulties and forces us to revisit the method and
introduce a couple of new ideas. Firstly, we discretise the possible side lengths into
logarithmically many dyadic choices, possibly reparametrising so that the first coordinate is
placed where the smallest side length is. Secondly, the usage of the non-divergence estimates
will need to be sensitive to different parameters (in fact the size of the smallest side).
Thirdly, we carefully inflate the dyadic scales — should they be too small —
to a size in which we can just handle the dyadic rectangles.

In light of Lemma

We argue by induction on the ambient dimension

To show this, let

be given.
For

and

4.5.
Smooth counting functions

To establish Theorem
4.5
,
we smooth our counting functions.
We introduce smooth cut-offs

{\omega},w:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]

such that:

•

The support of
{\omega}
is contained in
[1/2,4]
, and
{\omega}(x)=1
for

.

•

The support of

, we have

for

, and
w(x)=w(-x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We shall also require a family of smooth weights adapted to
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{I}
and
\kappa\in(0,1)
be such that the
\kappa
neighbourhood of
\mathcal{G}
is contained in

, where

is as in
(
1.4
).
A smooth weight

is

if its support is contained in
U
and it is identically
1
on the
\kappa
neighbourhood of
\mathcal{G}
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n})\in\mathcal{D}(t)
.
For
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathbb{I}
, let

be a smooth weight which is
(\mathcal{G},\kappa)
adapted for some small enough

. Recall the constant

from (
4.4
). For

, we define

(4.17)

N_{j,\mho}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)=\sum_{(q,a)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}({\mho}\circ f_{j}^{-1})\left(\frac{a}{q}\right){\omega}\left(\frac{q}{2^{t}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}w\bigg(\frac{\|q(f_{i}\circ f_{j}^{-1})(\frac{a}{q})\|}{4L\delta_{i}}\bigg).

The following lemma details how
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},4{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)
can be dominated by the smooth count

, where the index

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\rho
and
L
be as in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) respectively.
Let

be such that

, and let
\mho
be
(\mathcal{G},4L\delta_{j}/2^{t})

Then, for any

,

and
{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n})\in\mathcal{D}(t)

(4.18)

\#\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},4{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)\leqslant N_{j,\mho}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t).

Proof.

Define

\mathcal{S}_{j}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)=\Big\{(a,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:2^{t}\leqslant q\leqslant 2^{t+1}\text{ and }\eqref{eq something}\text{ holds}\Big\},

where
(
4.19
)

refers to the system of inequalities

(4.19)

\min_{x\in\mathcal{G}}\Big|x-f_{j}^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{q}\right)\Big|<\frac{4L\delta_{j}}{q},\quad\left\|qf_{i}\circ f_{j}^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{q}\right)\right\|<4L\delta_{i}\textrm{ for }1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n.

Using the support properties of the smooth cutoff functions defined above, it is straightforward to check that

(4.20)

\#\mathcal{S}_{j}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)\leqslant N_{j,\,\mho}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t).

Let
\pi_{j}:\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},4{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be the projection map

\pi_{j}({\mathbf{a}},q)=(a_{j},q).

We claim that
\pi_{j}
maps
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},4{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)

injectively to
\mathcal{S}_{j}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)
.
This claim, combined with (
4.20
), will imply the desired estimate (
4.18
).

To prove the claim, let
({\mathbf{a}},q)\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},4{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)
. Then

2^{t}\leqslant q<2^{t+1}
, and there exists
x_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
such that

(4.21)

\Big|f_{i}(x_{0})-\frac{a_{i}}{q}\Big|<\frac{4\delta_{i}}{q}\quad\textrm{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant n.

Let
C:=C_{\rho}
be as in (
4.4
).
For
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
with
i\neq j
, using the triangle inequality, the mean value theorem for
f_{i}\circ f_{j}^{-1}
, and (
4.21
), we get

\displaystyle\left|f_{i}\circ f_{j}^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{q}\right)-\frac{a_{i}}{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|f_{i}\circ f_{j}^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{q}\right)-f_{i}(x_{0})\right|+\left|f_{i}(x_{0})-\frac{a_{i}}{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left|\frac{a_{j}}{q}-f_{j}(x_{0})\right|+\frac{4\delta_{i}}{q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4C\delta_{j}}{q}+\frac{4\delta_{i}}{q}<\frac{4L\delta_{i}}{q},

where we have used

to get the last inequality.
Arguing similarly and using the mean value theorem for

\left|x_{0}-f_{j}^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{q}\right)\right|<\frac{4L\delta_{j}}{q}.

This shows that
\pi_{j}
maps
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{G},4{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)

\mathcal{S}_{j}(\mathcal{G},{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},t)

To see that this mapping is injective, let

and

(say). Further, let

be such that

(4.22)

\left|f_{i}(x_{1})-\frac{a_{i}}{q}\right|<\frac{4\delta_{i}}{q},\qquad\left|f_{i}(x_{2})-\frac{b_{i}}{q}\right|<\frac{4\delta_{i}}{q},\qquad\textrm{ for }1\leqslant i\leqslant n.

Using the triangle inequality and the above for

, we get

\left|f_{j}(x_{1})-f_{j}(x_{2})\right|<\frac{8\delta_{j}}{q}.

For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
with
i\neq j
, we again use the triangle inequality, the mean value theorem for
f_{i}\circ f_{j}^{-1}
and (
4.22
) to estimate

\displaystyle\left|\frac{a_{i}}{q}-\frac{b_{i}}{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|f_{i}(x_{1})-\frac{a_{i}}{q}\right|+\left|f_{i}(x_{1})-f_{i}(x_{2})\right|+\left|f_{i}(x_{2})-\frac{b_{i}}{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{8\delta_{i}}{q}+\frac{8C\delta_{j}}{q}\leqslant\frac{4L\delta_{i}}{q}.

Because
\Xi
is large, see (
4.7
),
we are ensured
L\delta_{i}\leqslant L2^{-10}L^{-1}<1/4

and hence

for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant j
. This shows that

is injective, thus
completing the proof of the claim and also the lemma.
∎

N^{{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
equals
M({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)

plus the smooth exponential sum

E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q):={\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}^{\times}\sum_{(q,a,{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\times\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}\left(\frac{a}{q}\right){\omega}\left(\frac{q}{Q}\right)B({\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})e(q\langle{\mathbf{F}}(a/q),{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime}\rangle),

where
B({\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})
is as defined in (
4.34
),
up to a rapidly-decaying error in
Q
. Formally,

(4.36)

N^{{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)=M({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)+E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)+O_{w,\eta,A}(Q^{-A})

for any
A>1
.

Proof.

We use the Fourier expansion of

x\mapsto w(\|x\|/\delta_{i})

for
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
, see

[
42
, Lemma A.3]
.
This gives

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}}\left(\frac{\|qF_{2}(a/q)\|}{\delta_{2}}\right)\cdots{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}}\left(\frac{\|qF_{n}(a/q)\|}{\delta_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}\Big(\delta_{i}\widehat{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}}}(0)+\delta_{i}\sum_{1\leqslant|j_{i}|\leqslant J}\widehat{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}}}(\delta_{i}j_{i})e(j_{i}qF_{i}(a/q))+O_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}},\eta,A}(Q^{-A})\Big)

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}\widehat{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}}}(0)^{n-1}+{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}B({\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})e\left(q\langle{\mathbf{F}}(a/q),{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime}\rangle\right)+O_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}},\eta,A}(Q^{-A}).

Substituting this into the
definition of
N^{{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}}
yields
(
4.36
).

We still need to verify the upper bound in
(
4.35
).
To this end, note that

{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}\ll 1_{[-4,4]}

and
\omega\ll 1_{[-4,4]}
,
where
1_{[-4,4]}
is the indicator function
of
[-4,4]
.
Replacing

{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}
and
\omega
by
O(1_{[-4,4]})
,
and using that there are
O(Q^{2})
many
fractions
a/q
with
q\ll Q
in
[-4,4]
,
produces the required bound.
∎

4.8.2.
Pruning the error term

It remains to show that

E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)

is indeed an error term.
To this end, we write it as an average of oscillatory integrals.
To estimate the oscillatory integrals, we shall need to keep track of the terms arising from repeated
integration by parts, keeping in mind the amplitude blowup. Recall that our weight function
{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}
is
r
-rough,
see (
4.28
).

The basic setup is as follows. Let
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\psi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
be real-valued functions such that

\psi^{\prime}\neq 0
on the support of
h
.
We define the differential operator
L

via

(4.37)

Lh=-\>\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\frac{h}{\psi^{\prime}}\right).

Its formal adjoint operator is given by

L^{*}=-\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
.
Note that it has the property

(2\pi\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{-1}L^{*}e(\lambda\psi)=e(\lambda\psi)
.
Thus, we can express an
N
-fold integration by parts as

(4.38)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e(\lambda\psi(x))h(x)\mathrm{d}x=(2\pi\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{-N}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e(\lambda\psi(x))(L^{\circ N}h)(x)\mathrm{d}x

for
N\geqslant 1
.
We can express
L^{\circ N}(h)
efficiently using the following lemma.

Lemma 4.13

(Lemma A.2 in
[
2
]
with
n=1
)
.

Let
N
be a positive integer,
h\in C_{c}^{N}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\psi\in C^{N+1}({\mathbb{R}})
. Suppose that

\psi^{\prime}\neq 0
on the support of
h
. Then
there exists an integer
V=V(N)\geqslant 1
and complex coefficients
c_{N,1},\ldots,c_{N,V}
so that

L^{\circ N}h=\sum_{\nu=1}^{V}c_{N,\nu}h_{N,\nu},

where

h_{N,\nu}=\frac{h^{(\kappa_{0})}}{|\psi^{\prime}|^{\kappa_{0}}}\prod_{l=1}^{M}\frac{\psi^{(\kappa_{l}+1)}}{|\psi^{\prime}|^{\kappa_{l}+1}},

for some non-negative integer
\kappa_{0}

and strictly positive integers

\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{M}
satisfying

(4.39)

\kappa_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{M}\kappa_{l}=N.

Since
\kappa_{l}>0
for each
l\leqslant M
, Equation (
4.39
) implies that

(4.40)

\kappa_{0}+M\leqslant N.

We now turn our attention to the analysis of

E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
. Let

{\mathbf{j}}=(j_{1},{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

For a large enough constant
C_{\mathbf{F}}>0
, depending only on
{\mathbf{F}}
and to be decided shortly, we set

(4.41)

\mathcal{J}:=\bigg\{(j_{1},{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}:\begin{array}[]{ll}&0\leqslant|j_{1}|\leqslant C_{\mathbf{F}}J_{\textrm{max}},\quad{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0},\\
&|j_{2}|\leqslant J_{2},\ldots,|j_{n}|\leqslant J_{n}\end{array}\bigg\}.

We define the truncated error term

E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
to be

{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant|c|\leqslant C_{\mathbf{F}}J_{\textrm{max}}\\
{\mathbf{j}}\in\mathcal{J}\end{subarray}}B({\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e\left(yQ[\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}({x})-c]\right){\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}({x})\,y\cdot{\omega}(y)\,\mathrm{d}{x}\,\mathrm{d}y,

where

\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)=\langle(x,{\mathbf{F}}(x)),{\mathbf{j}}\rangle=xj_{1}+\langle{\mathbf{F}}(x),{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime}\rangle.

The next lemma shows that up to an acceptable error, it is
E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
which decides the order of magnitude of
E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
N\geqslant 4
.
Assume that

(Cond 3)

r\geqslant Q^{\eta-1}\delta_{\textnormal{min}}.

Then

E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)=E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)+O_{N}({{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}J_{\textnormal{max}}^{n+1}Q^{2}(QrJ_{\textnormal{max}})^{-N}+{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q^{-2N}).

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
38
, Lemma 4.5]
,
except here we operate only under the weaker condition (
Cond 3
).
This turns out not to be an issue. The shape of the error term is also slightly different. We give the details in Appendix
C
for completeness.
∎

Suppose

(Cond 4)

\delta_{\textrm{min}}\geqslant 2Q^{-\eta-1}.

Observe that, when this and (
Cond 3
) are satisfied, we have

(4.42)

J_{\textrm{max}}\leqslant Q^{1+2\eta},\qquad QrJ_{\textrm{max}}\geqslant Q^{2\eta}.

Thus, by Lemma
4.14
and choosing
N>\frac{n+2}{\eta}
with
\eta<\frac{1}{2}
,

(4.43)

E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)=E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)+O_{n,\eta}({{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q).

4.8.3.
Bounding the error term

The final lemma of this subsection establishes that
E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
is indeed an acceptable error term.

Lemma 4.15
.

Assume (
Cond 3
) and (
Cond 4
) are true.
Suppose that

(Cond 5)

\Delta\geqslant Q^{\eta-1}

and

(Cond 6)

Q^{\eta-1}K^{-1}\leqslant r\leqslant Q^{-\eta}K\delta_{\textnormal{min}}\leqslant 1.

Then

E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)\ll_{\eta}{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q.

Proof.

We first bound
E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
.
Fix
{\mathbf{j}}\in\mathcal{J}
as defined in (
4.41
).
Notice that for any point
x\in{\mathrm{supp}}({\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}})
,
at least one of the inequalities

\|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)\|<\Delta,\qquad|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime}(x)|<K

is false.
This motivates us to decompose

E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)
based on the size of

|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime}(x)|
with respect to
K
.
Let
b:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a smooth weight,
supported on
(-1,1)
and
identically equal to
1
on

[-1/2,1/2]
.
For each
{\mathbf{j}}=(j_{1},{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{J}
and

c\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leqslant|c|\leqslant C_{\mathbf{f}}J_{\textrm{max}}
, we split

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}ye(yQ[\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)-c]){\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}({x}){\omega}(y)\mathrm{d}{x}\mathrm{d}y=I(c,{\mathbf{j}})+II(c,{\mathbf{j}}),

where

\displaystyle I(c,{\mathbf{j}})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}ye(yQ[\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)-c]){\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}(x){\omega}(y)b(K^{-1}\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime}(x))\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle II(c,{\mathbf{j}})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}ye(yQ[\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)-c]){\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}(x){\omega}(y)(1-b(K^{-1}\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime}(x)))\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

We deal with the second integral first.
The amplitude function in
II(c,{\mathbf{j}})

is supported on the set where

|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime}({x})|\geqslant K/2
, which lets us integrate by parts in the
{x}
variable, keeping
y
fixed.
Let
L
be the differential operator defined in (
4.37
) with
\psi=\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}
. Set

h={\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}\cdot[1-b(K^{-1}\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime})].

Using Lemma
4.13
, we get that
L^{\circ N}h=\sum_{\nu=1}^{V}c_{N,\nu}h_{N,\nu}

with each
h_{N,\nu}
of the form

\frac{h^{(\kappa_{0})}}{|\psi^{\prime}|^{\kappa_{0}}}\prod_{l=1}^{M}\frac{\psi^{(\kappa_{l}+1)}}{|\psi^{\prime}|^{\kappa_{l}+1}}\quad\textrm{ with }\kappa_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{M}\kappa_{l}=N.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
. By the Leibniz rule, we have

(4.44)

\|h^{(\kappa)}\|_{\infty}\ll(K^{-1}Q^{\eta}\delta_{\textrm{min}}^{-1}+r^{-1})^{\kappa}\ll r^{-\kappa},

where we used
r\leqslant\delta_{\textrm{min}}KQ^{-2\eta}
to get the last estimate.
Combining this with the assumed lower bound on

|\Phi_{\mathbf{j}}|
, we get

\left\|\frac{h^{(\kappa_{0})}}{(\psi^{\prime})^{\kappa_{0}}}\right\|_{\infty}\ll(rK)^{-\kappa_{0}}.

Since

\|\psi^{(\kappa+1)}\|_{\infty}\ll Q^{\eta}\delta_{\textrm{min}}^{-1}
, and using the lower bound on

|\psi^{\prime}|=|\Phi_{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime}|
, we get

\left\|\frac{\psi^{(\kappa_{l}+1)}}{(\psi^{\prime})^{\kappa_{l}+1}}\right\|_{\infty}\ll Q^{\eta}\delta_{\textrm{min}}^{-1}K^{-(\kappa_{l}+1)}.

We combine (
4.39
), (
4.40
) and the above observations to conclude

\displaystyle\|h_{N,\nu}\|_{\infty}\ll(rK)^{-\kappa_{0}}\prod_{l=1}^{M}(Q^{\eta}\delta_{\textrm{min}}^{-1}K^{-(\kappa_{l}+1)})

\displaystyle=r^{-\kappa_{0}}Q^{M\eta}K^{-N}(\delta_{\textrm{min}}K)^{-M}

\displaystyle\leqslant r^{-\kappa_{0}}K^{-N}(Q^{-\eta}\delta_{\textrm{min}}K)^{-N+\kappa_{0}}.

Recalling that
1\leqslant(Q^{-\eta}\delta_{\textrm{min}}K)^{-1}\leqslant r^{-1}
, and noting that
(rK)^{-1}\leqslant Q^{1-\eta}

by condition (
Cond 6
), we obtain

\|h_{N,\nu}\|_{\infty}\ll r^{-\kappa_{0}}K^{-N}r^{-N+\kappa_{0}}\ll Q^{(1-\eta)N}.

Thus
Q^{-N}\|L^{\circ N}h\|_{\infty}\ll_{N,d}Q^{-N\eta},

and integrating by parts
N
many times with respect to
{x}
using (
4.38
), yields

II(c,{\mathbf{j}})\ll Q^{-N\eta}.

We now bound
I(c,{\mathbf{j}})
. Observe that the support of
{\Omega_{\Delta,K,\mathrm{g}}}\cdot b(K^{-1}\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime})
is contained in the intersection of the complement of
\mathfrak{S}_{\Delta,K}

with
\{\|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<K\}
. By the definition of
\mathfrak{S}_{\Delta,K}
, we conclude that for any
c\in\mathbb{Z}
,

|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}({x})-c|\geqslant\|\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}({x})\|\geqslant\Delta\geqslant Q^{\eta-1}

on this set. Thus, we integrate by parts in the
y
variable with

\psi(y)=y[\Phi_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)-c]).

Since

|\omega^{(\kappa)}(y)|\ll_{\kappa}1
and
\psi^{(\kappa+1)}(y)=0
for any
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
, we conclude that

I(c,{\mathbf{j}})\ll_{N}Q^{-N\eta}

for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Summing over
{\mathbf{j}}\in\mathcal{J}

and
0<|c|<C_{{\mathbf{F}}}J
gives

E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)\ll_{N}{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q^{2}\sum_{0\leqslant|c|\leqslant C_{\mathbf{F}}J_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\mathcal{J}}Q^{-N\eta}\ll_{N}{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q^{2}J_{\textnormal{max}}^{n+1}Q^{-N\eta}.

Recalling (
4.42
)
and choosing

delivers

E_{\mathrm{tr}}({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)\ll_{\eta}{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}^{\times}Q.

In view of (
4.43
), we
finally have
E({\boldsymbol{{\delta}}},Q)\ll_{\eta}{{\boldsymbol{{\delta}}}}^{\times}Q
.

Appendix A
Reduction to Curves via fibring

Lemma A.1

(Fibring lemma for smooth, non-degenerate manifolds)
.

Let

{\mathbf{g}}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}):U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a smooth function in
d
real variables defined on an open neighbourhood of
\mathbf{0}
. Suppose that
{\mathbf{g}}
is non-degenerate of order
l
on
U
, and put
p=l+2
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small in terms of
\mathbf{g}
. Then,
for any
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|\mathbf{h}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon
,
the curve
\phi_{\mathbf{h}}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with coordinates given by

\phi_{\mathbf{h},i}(t):=g_{i}(t^{1+p^{d}}+h_{1},t^{p+p^{d}}+h_{2},\dots,t^{p^{d-1}+p^{d}}+h_{d})

is non-degenerate of order
L
, where
L
depends on
l
and
d
.

A similar lemma for
analytic
, non-degenerate manifolds and
\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0}
was established in Sprindzhuk’s survey
[
41
, pp. 9-10]
and re-proven in a streamlined way in
[
5
, Appendix C]
, again for analytic, non-degenerate manifolds. Lemma
A.1
applies in the more general
smooth
, non-degenerate setting. We make use of the following technical lemma from
[
5
]
, which will allow us to express multi-dimensional derivatives of
{\mathbf{g}}
in terms of higher-order, single-variable derivatives of
\phi_{\mathbf{h}}
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
0<p_{0}<p
be positive integers and let
e_{p}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{\geqslant 0}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
be given by

(A.1)

e_{p}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}(p^{j-1}+p^{d})\,.

Let

(A.2)

S_{p_{0}}:=\{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}^{d}:\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}\leqslant p_{0}\}\,.

Then

•

e_{p}
maps
S_{p_{0}}
into
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
injectively, and

•

e_{p}(S_{p_{0}})\cap e_{p}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}^{d}\setminus S_{p_{0}})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

See
[
5
, Lemma 12]
.
∎

We can now prove the fibring lemma for smooth, non-degenerate manifolds. Our approach is similar to the one in
[
41
]
, except we no longer have the relatively straightforward equivalence of
{\mathbf{g}}
being non-degenerate with the component functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
and the constant function
1
being linearly independent. We need to explicitly compute the multi-order derivatives of
{\mathbf{g}}
, match them with appropriate derivatives of
\phi_{\mathbf{h}}
, and prove they are linearly independent. Another technical obstruction is that we can no longer expand our functions into infinite power series. Instead, we use a multivariable Taylor series expansion up to the appropriate order.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Since
{\mathbf{g}}
is non-degenerate of order
l
, there exist multi-indices

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}=(\beta_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\beta_{d}^{(1)}),{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}=(\beta_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,\beta_{d}^{(2)}),\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}=(\beta_{1}^{(n)},\ldots,\beta_{d}^{(n)})

in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}^{d}
, with
1\leqslant\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\|_{1}\leqslant l
for all
k
, such that the vectors

\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}}{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{0}),\ldots,\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}}{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{0})

span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the following, we make frequent use of the
abbreviation

{\mathbf{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}:=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}

for
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

and
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{\geqslant 0}
