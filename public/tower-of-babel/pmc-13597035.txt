Reducing distress during vaccine injections: 2026 clinical practice guideline update

ABSTRACT
Background:Distress (pain, fear) is the most common adverse reaction associated with vaccination. This 2026 guideline updates the 2015 Help ELiminate Pain in Kids & Adults (HELPinKids&Adults) Collaborative recommendations for reducing vaccination-related distress.Methods:We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to select the clinical questions and critical and important outcomes, and to determine the certainty of evidence and strength of the recommendations. We aligned guideline processes and reporting with the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II tool. The intended audience includes clinicians and organizations that deliver vaccinations, and vaccine recipients and their support persons. Guideline panel members representing different knowledge user groups, including vaccine recipients and support persons, voted on clinical questions and outcomes. We categorized interventions into 5 domains of distress management, the 5Ps: process (education and implementation), procedural (injection techniques), physical (body position and activity), pharmacologic (medication), and psychological (thoughts and behaviour). Five systematic reviews combined evidence from randomized and quasi-randomized controlled trials identified using database (up to Jan. 31, 2025) and hand searching, supplemented by contacting experts. Evidence summaries were presented at a guideline meeting, and voting panel members drafted recommendations using the GRADE evidence-to-decision framework, considering certainty of the evidence, magnitude of effect, and contextual factors. We applied the Guidelines International Network principles to manage competing interests.Recommendations:In the 5Ps framework, process-related interventions apply to all user groups. Procedural recommendations are directed at clinicians and organizations responsible for delivering vaccinations. Physical, pharmacologic, and psychological recommendations are targeted at vaccine recipients and support persons and incorporate choice. We developed 16 recommendations across the 5Ps framework, directed to different user groups. Of these, 7 are strong recommendations in favour of interventions (e.g., multicomponent process interventions, oral nutritive and non-nutritive interventions for infants), primarily supported by moderate- to high-certainty evidence. The remaining 9 are conditional recommendations based on low to very low certainty of evidence: 4 in favour of specific interventions (e.g., reducing fear cues) and 5 advising against other interventions (e.g., video or movie distraction in infants).Interpretation:This guideline presents evidence-based strategies to manage vaccination-related distress. Implementing recommendations can improve the quality of vaccine delivery and promote person-centred care by aligning interventions with vaccine recipient and support person preferences.

Fear and pain — collectively referred to as distress — are among the most common adverse effects related to vaccine injections.1 Although substantial evidence supports effective strategies for reducing this distress, such approaches have not been systematically incorporated into vaccination programs or delivery practices,2–7 despite the existence of clinical practice guidelines.8,9 Acutely, unmitigated distress causes unnecessary suffering and increases the risk of immunization stress–related responses (e.g., dizziness, fainting) that can lead to injuries from falling.10,11 Over time, both vaccine recipients and observers may develop needle-related fears and traumatic associations.11

Varying degrees of needle fear exist from low to high levels, accompanied by anxiety, distress, and impairment; at one extreme is a phobia of needles, a mental health diagnosis.12 Across this spectrum, needle fear is highly prevalent, affecting approximately 33% to 63% of children and 14% to 63% of adults.12–17 Pain- and fear-related concerns contribute to vaccine hesitancy and refusal across the lifespan, influencing about 10% of individuals.13,16,18,19 People who are neurodiverse, have disabilities, or belong to underserved and equity-deserving communities are at heightened risk of experiencing distress, further deterring vaccination.18,20,21 Integrating evidence-based strategies to minimize distress can improve the experiences of vaccine recipients and onlookers and prevent the iatrogenic harms of vaccination that erode confidence in vaccination and compromise population health.11,22–24

In 2015, the Help ELiminate Pain in Kids & Adults Collaborative (HELPinKids&Adults) — a national interdisciplinary team of clinicians, scientists, policy-makers, and lay people interested in improving practices — created a guideline on reducing distress during vaccination.8 An update was deemed necessary for several reasons. New research published in the interim addressed previously identified research gaps, particularly related to the applicability of guideline recommendations.25 New developments in guideline methodology provided opportunities to refine the presentation and improve clarity for users.26,27 Downward trends in vaccine confidence and suboptimal uptake affecting both adults and children — particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic — make this update timely for providers and organizations delivering vaccinations that want to follow best practices to promote more positive vaccination experiences for everyone.28–33 This 2026 guideline updates the 2015 HELPinKids&Adults recommendations for reducing vaccination-related distress across the lifespan.

Scope

The recommendations apply to individuals of all ages who receive vaccine injections across vaccination settings and focus on interventions to reduce vaccine injection–related distress. The target audience encompasses all knowledge users involved in vaccination, including clinicians administering vaccines and organizations providing vaccination services, vaccine recipients, and their support persons. The audience of the 2015 guideline8 has now been expanded beyond clinicians to include all knowledge users, reflecting the critical role each group plays in managing distress and advancing best practices. This broader scope supports the integration of evidence-based approaches into policies and procedures, backed by appropriate oversight and evaluation.

The scope excludes interventions specific to management of high levels of needle fear (i.e., excessive fear leading to avoidance or refusal)12 and the treatment of local and systemic reactions that may occur in the hours to days after vaccination. For needle fear that is inadequately addressed with interventions in the guideline update, our related clinical practice guideline12 provides recommendations and clinical resources related to exposure-based therapy — a well-established evidence-based intervention for anxiety and phobias — typically delivered by a mental health professional with expertise in cognitive behavioural therapy in either individual or group settings, using stepped care approaches to improve accessibility.12,34,35 Although we did not explicitly exclude individuals with developmental and intellectual disabilities in this guideline update, they were typically under-represented in the primary research. Readers are directed to extant literature exploring clinical considerations and pathways to supplement recommendations, including the use of sedation, for care of individuals with high needle fear, or intellectual or developmental disabilities.20,36,37

We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to develop recommendations40 (Box 1). We integrated factors from the GRADE evidence-to-decision (EtD) framework43 into our decision-making, including priority of the problem, certainty of evidence, desirable effects (benefits), equity, acceptability, and feasibility (Appendices 2 to 6, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content). That vaccine recipients and support persons have the opportunity to choose from a range of recommended intervention(s) was a central principle throughout.

Grading of recommendations

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation system provided the framework for formulating recommendations and good practice statements.40–42

Good practice statements

We created good practice statements in situations in which the panel agreed that it was necessary to include the message; the indirect evidence was deemed sufficient to lead to large net positive effects; summarizing the evidence was considered unnecessary and a poor use of time; the rationale connecting the indirect evidence was clear; and clear and actionable guidance could be provided.

Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to define magnitude of effect on distress: a reduction in the SMD < 0.2 was considered trivial, SMD ≥ 0.2 small, SMD ≥ 0.5 moderate, and SMD ≥ 0.8 large.44

The panel also endorsed 3 good practice statements41,42 that address ongoing monitoring activities to support implementation, and the use of a small number of interventions that fell outside the current update (Box 2 and Appendix 7, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content).

The 5P framework

We organized interventions to reduce vaccine injection–related distress across the 5P framework.8 The process domain includes strategies related to education and implementation (e.g., clinician-directed education). Procedural interventions refer to approaches used to administer the vaccine injection (e.g., injecting vaccines in order of increasing distress). The physical and pharmacologic domains respectively address body position and physical activity (e.g., breastfeeding in infants), and medication use (e.g., topical anesthetics). Psychological interventions involve thoughts and behaviours (e.g., distraction with a toy) that can influence the individual’s experience.

Appendix 8 (available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content) presents the conceptual model underpinning the guideline recommendations within the 5P framework, illustrating their relevance across distinct knowledge user groups. Process interventions apply to all user groups, whereas procedural interventions are directed specifically toward clinicians and organizations responsible for vaccination delivery. Physical, pharmacologic, and psychological interventions are intended for vaccine recipients and their support persons, who may choose among these options according to individual needs and preferences. Clinicians and vaccination-delivery organizations are expected to respect and support these choices. The overarching outcome is an improved vaccination experience for all.

Process interventions

Vaccine delivery is complex and involves multiple knowledge user groups, including clinicians and organizations responsible for delivering vaccines, as well as vaccine recipients and their support persons who receive vaccination services. Individuals in all these groups can positively or negatively influence the distress experienced by vaccine recipients.66–73 Currently, no evidence exists that the relevant user groups are systematically educated about evidence-based management of distress,4,6,7,74–78 and distress-reducing interventions are not consistently integrated in vaccination delivery.2–7,79 Process interventions encompass education and implementation for all user groups.

We recommend education of all knowledge user groups — clinicians, organizations responsible for vaccination, vaccine recipients, and support persons — about distress-reducing interventions and use of distress-reducing interventions for vaccine injections (strong recommendation, moderate certainty of evidence).

The guideline development process included 4 clinical questions related to process interventions. One focused on multicomponent (complex) interventions engaging all user groups — clinicians, organizations, support persons, and vaccine recipients — and the other 3 focused on individual user groups. A summary of the evidence and the factors prioritized in the decision-making process is provided below, with additional notes available in the EtD (Appendix 2).

Multicomponent interventions

Research shows that a multicomponent or complex intervention — including education for all knowledge user groups and the integration of distress-reducing strategies into vaccination delivery — reduces distress in vaccine recipients (standardized mean difference [SMD] −0.21, 95% confidence interval [CI] −0.31 to −0.10; high certainty of evidence).80 This SMD corresponds to an approximate mean difference of 0.5 points on a scale of 0 to 10 (whereby 0 = no distress and 10 = maximum distress), assuming a standard deviation of 2.2. Research also shows an increase in the use of evidence-based interventions (SMD 0.6, 95% CI 0.15 to 1.04; moderate certainty of evidence).80 These findings are based on a single, well-defined complex intervention: the CARD system (Comfort–Ask–Relax–Distract),25 which has been evaluated across infant, child, and adult vaccine recipients in diverse vaccination settings.80 In addition, across studies evaluating implementation outcomes, multicomponent interventions have positive impacts on acceptability, feasibility, and vaccination experience.22–24,70,81–86 Some studies show a positive impact on vaccination uptake.87,88

Individual user groups

Benefit was also demonstrated in research targeting individual user groups (clinician, support person, vaccine recipient).80 Interventions targeting clinicians increased the use of distress-reducing interventions (SMD 0.66, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.85; low certainty of evidence). Interventions targeting support persons such as parents or caregivers reduced distress (SMD −0.30, 95% CI −0.50 to −0.10; moderate certainty of evidence), increased the use of distress-reducing interventions (SMD 0.67, 0.17 to 1.17; low certainty of evidence), and increased knowledge about these interventions (SMD 0.25, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.34; moderate certainty of evidence). Educating vaccine recipients specifically reduced distress (SMD −0.8, 95% CI −1.23 to −0.37; low certainty of evidence).

As each knowledge user group contributes to the overall vaccination experience, strategies that involve all groups working together offer the most potential for real-world impact. In contrast, interventions targeting single user groups are often context specific and may lack the collaborative foundation required to sustain long-term improvements. Health care providers and organizations are encouraged to apply multiple, complementary strategies to reduce distress, while inviting vaccine recipients and their support persons to select and combine interventions that meet their needs and desired level of comfort. This multicomponent, integrated approach reflects the interconnected nature of vaccination delivery and enhances both the effectiveness of distress relief and the acceptability of care.24,82

Systems are currently in place to educate knowledge users about vaccines and vaccination delivery; this education should be expanded to include evidence-based interventions to reduce distress. Vaccine recipients should also have access to safe vaccination experiences, which includes the use of interventions that prevent distress-related harm. Strategies to mitigate distress enhance the safety of vaccine administration and should be considered essential components of immunization delivery9 — on par with other equipment and supplies. Recipients should be offered a choice among the most evidence-based interventions that align with their individual needs and preferences, consistent with current models of health care delivery that emphasize involvement and equity and help build trusting relationships.52,61,89–93

The panel elected to develop a single core recommendation in favour of multicomponent interventions. This collective recommendation integrates the evidence80 with a priority to include all user groups, supporting the adoption of distress-reducing interventions across the full spectrum of vaccination delivery. In making this decision, the panel highlighted distress management as a fundamental component of vaccination delivery and the right of vaccine recipients.9,94 The panel also considered the infrastructure already available to educate and integrate distress-reducing interventions, as well as the positive impact on vaccination experiences.80

Procedural interventions

The way vaccines are prepared and administered can affect the distress of vaccine recipients and their support persons. Clinicians and organizations involved in delivering vaccinations can adopt methods that minimize distress and promote more positive vaccination experiences. The summary of evidence and other information influencing the recommendations are provided below and in Appendix 3.

We recommend performing vaccine injections without aspiration and using a rapid injection technique for recipients of all ages (strong recommendation, low certainty of evidence).

Vaccines have historically been administered using a technique that involves aspiration, followed by a slow injection (i.e., the “traditional” method). Aspiration refers to applying negative pressure to the plunger after needle insertion to check for inadvertent entry into a blood vessel. Compared with this traditional approach, a pragmatic technique — no aspiration, combined with fast injection — results in significantly lower distress (SMD −0.77, 95% CI −1.26 to −0.27).95 Removal of 1 study from our analysis because of methodological concerns96 increased the certainty of evidence to moderate and increased the effect size (SMD −1.09, 95% CI −1.61 to −0.57).

The observed benefit of a pragmatic injection technique is likely a result of diminished tissue damage from shorter dwelling time and less shearing action from the needle.51 Additional undesirable factors associated with aspiration include increased complexity of the intervention and longer procedure duration. Aspiration is technically more complex for clinicians because it requires the use of both hands, preventing stabilization of the limb during injection and increasing the risk of injury if the recipient moves. Aspiration also lengthens the procedure compared with the pragmatic technique (approximately 5 to 10 s v. 1 to 2 s) and cannot be performed with autodisable syringes — which are widely used.97,98

We performed a separate analysis examining injection speed alone (i.e., fast injection, a component of the pragmatic injection technique, v. slow), which demonstrated a trend toward lower distress and supported the results of the combined approach (SMD −0.27, 95% CI −0.51 to 0.01).95

Although theoretical concerns have been raised about the potential for intravascular injection if aspiration is omitted from the vaccine injection process, the sites used for vaccine injections in the arm or thigh do not include large blood vessels, and evidence to support any harm is lacking.99 The pragmatic technique is currently an accepted practice in many countries, including Canada.100

The panel made a strong recommendation in favour of not aspirating and of using a fast injection technique, based on low certainty of evidence. This seemingly discordant recommendation (i.e., strong recommendation with low certainty of evidence) was informed by results from our systematic review showing reduced distress, as well as a sensitivity analysis in which exclusion of a methodologically problematic study increased the certainty of evidence to moderate.95 Additionally, the panel considered the greater efficiency of a pragmatic approach — both in terms of clinician training requirements and procedure duration — along with its long-standing use in clinical practice, which supports its acceptability and feasibility without documented evidence of harm.

We recommend administering vaccines in order of increasing distress, with the least distressing vaccine given first for recipients of all ages (strong recommendation, moderate certainty of evidence).

Multiple vaccines are often administered during the same visit, and the sequence of administration can influence overall distress because of vaccine-specific characteristics (e.g., pH, dose volume), injection-related factors (e.g., site, route of administration), or both.51 Evidence shows that beginning with the less distressing vaccine reduces overall distress (SMD −0.73, 95% CI −0.93 to −0.52).95

Given our systematic review95 and ease of implementation (acceptability and feasibility), the panel recommended that when multiple vaccines are required, they be administered in ascending order of expected distress — that is, begin with the least distressing vaccine and progress to the most distressing. The panel acknowledged that whereas this intervention is straightforward in principle, limited research exists to guide the optimal order of many vaccines administered in the same sitting, which may affect feasibility. Moreover, vaccine formulations can change over time, and there is uncertainty about the transferability of published results to newer formulations. Some examples of vaccines that have been demonstrated to cause more distress and therefore should be administered last if part of a sequence of injections include M-M-R II, Prevnar, and Gardasil.101–103

We suggest reducing exposure to fear cues before vaccine injections in vaccine recipients of all ages (conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence).

Needles are a specific, well-established fear cue that can heighten anticipatory distress in vaccine recipients; their visibility before injection may act as a conditioned stimulus, triggering anxiety and fear responses associated with pain.104–106 Furthermore, this fear can generalize over time to other stimuli.107 More broadly, the visibility of medical equipment can affect individuals’ stress and trust in health care providers,108 and patients have reported not wanting to see it.109,110

Research shows that if the patient does not see the needle during vaccination, it may reduce distress (SMD −0.26, 95% CI −0.57 to 0.06).95 Reducing the visibility of needles can be achieved using physical barriers (e.g., needles can be stored behind tabletop dividers and covered until the moment of injection) and layout (e.g., positioning vaccine recipients to face away from needles). These findings provide indirect support for minimizing fear-related cues before vaccination. When appraising the limited evidence, the panel considered extant literature on generalization of fears and medical equipment visibility, and determined that these results apply to preparing and storing vaccine doses, as well as setting up of vaccination spaces to minimize buildup in distress before vaccine injections.

These aforementioned strategies align with broader environmental design principles aimed at creating emotionally safe and supportive vaccination settings and preventing immunization stress–related responses.10,21,105 Key principles include minimizing exposure to fear-inducing stimuli, preserving privacy, reducing sensory overload, and promoting calm through layout and flow.10,21,105 This also includes reducing use of fear-inducing words, excessive reassurance, and false suggestions.53,54 Together, these approaches help create a less threatening environment that supports emotional safety and reduces distress for vaccine recipients.

Given the evidence review95 and consistent with known pathways involved in fear development — such as direct conditioning, vicarious observational learning, and the receipt of negative information106 — as well as considerations of acceptability and feasibility, the panel suggested minimizing exposure to fear cues before vaccine injections. This recommendation applies specifically to reducing anticipatory distress and does not address whether individuals choose to look at the needle during injection, nor does it concern preferences regarding the presence of a support person during vaccination. Personal preferences should be respected, as some individuals find observing the needle or having a trusted support person present during the vaccine injection to be helpful coping strategies.81,111 The panel also noted that these principles apply not only to the individual receiving the injection, but also to others waiting nearby, who may experience increased distress from visual and auditory fear cues.21,105–107

Physical interventions given orally

Infants (aged 0 to 12 mo)

Various interventions can be administered to infants (aged 0 to 12 mo) by mouth to reduce distress. These interventions work in multiple ways, including promoting calmness from proximity to support persons (caregivers), activation of endogenous opioid pathways, and distraction.112

We recommend caregivers be offered the opportunity to provide oral nutritive and non-nutritive interventions to infants (aged 0 to 12 mo) receiving vaccinations, in the form of their preferred option of breastfeeding, bottle-feeding, other sweet-tasting liquids (expressed breast milk, oral rotavirus vaccine, sucrose solution, glucose or dextrose solution), and pacifiers (strong recommendation, very low to moderate certainty of evidence).

The summary of evidence and clinical considerations are summarized below, with additional information provided in Appendix 4. Note that in this section, although we use the term “breastfeeding” to maintain consistency with terminology in the primary research, alternative terms may be more appropriate in specific contexts (e.g., “chestfeeding”). Breastfeeding during vaccination reduces distress in infants (SMD −2.38, 95% CI −2.90 to −1.87; moderate certainty of evidence).113 Breastfeeding is also effective when administered before injection — defined as breastfeeding that is stopped 5 or more minutes beforehand (SMD −1.19, 95% CI −1.76 to −0.62; low certainty of evidence).113 Bottle-feeding during injection with either expressed breast milk or formula also reduces distress (SMD −1.65, 95% CI −2.66 to −0.64; low certainty of evidence).113

Beyond breastfeeding and bottle-feeding, other sweet-tasting liquids given immediately before injection may lessen distress, including oral rotavirus vaccine and expressed breast milk (SMD −1.47, 95% CI −3.02 to 0.08; moderate certainty of evidence).113 Placebo-controlled trials demonstrate the effectiveness of sucrose solution (SMD −1.02, 95% CI −1.47 to −0.58; moderate certainty of evidence) and glucose or dextrose solutions (SMD −0.78, 95% CI −1.11 to −0.45; moderate certainty of evidence) given immediately before injection.113 Non-nutritive sucking during injection using a pacifier is also effective (SMD −1.98, 95% CI −3.88 to −0.08; very low certainty of evidence).113

Breastfeeding, bottle-feeding, and non-nutritive sucking are ideally commenced before injection and continued during and afterward. The timing required for the infant to commence sucking is minimal, making these interventions feasible. Suggested resources include access to a chair and privacy, which are considered standard in vaccination settings. Caregivers may also provide resources to support bottle-feeding and non-nutritive sucking with a pacifier. Infant thumb-sucking is an alternative to pacifier use.

In the case of oral rotavirus vaccine, if it is part of the regimen of vaccines to be given, it can be administered before injectable vaccines instead of giving exogenous sweet-tasting substances. Sucrose and glucose or dextrose solutions are commercially available. Sucrose can also be compounded on the spot, by mixing table sugar and clean water (about 1 teaspoonful in 10 mL).62 A small amount of these oral substances — typically 1 to 2 mL — is administered orally 1 to 2 minutes before vaccine injection. Sweet-tasting solutions may also be combined with pacifiers.113 Implementation considerations include the acceptance of interventions by both infants and caregivers. For example, infants or caregivers may refuse specific interventions.

The panel issued a strong recommendation to include all interventions listed above and to offer a choice among them. Although the systematic reviews found that several interventions were supported by low or very low certainty of evidence,113 the panel prioritized considerations of acceptability and equity during decision-making. Limiting recommendations to a narrow set of higher-certainty interventions could disadvantage infants or caregivers who are unable or unwilling to use them (e.g., non-breastfed infants or infants not receptive to nutritive interventions) and would not reflect real-world practice. The panel also considered indirect evidence demonstrating effectiveness across other needle-related procedures as well as the feasibility of use.112 The panel further noted that this recommendation does not pertain to the use of sweet-tasting substances more broadly.

Pharmacologic and physical interventions administered at the injection site

Several interventions to reduce distress are available, applied at or near the injection site. These include pharmacologic interventions that reduce pain sensation, and physical interventions that introduce a competing touch sensation (counterstimulation) or cause numbing from cold.62 Many interventions are applied by clinicians administering vaccinations, but some can also be applied by vaccine recipients and their support persons; hence, training of the individual delivering the intervention is required. Across all ages, the panel stressed the importance of choice for vaccine recipients and their support persons, given that differences in sensory processing can make certain interventions aversive or intolerable for some individuals.20,62 The recommendations are provided below for infants (aged 0 to 12 mo) and older individuals (children aged > 12 mo and adults) separately; additional data informing the recommendations are included in Appendix 5.

Infants (aged 0 to 12 mo)

We recommend caregivers be offered the choice to use topical anesthetics or manual tapping at the injection site for infants (aged 0 to 12 mo) receiving vaccine injections (strong recommendation, high to moderate certainty of evidence).

Topical anesthetic creams, gels, and patches that are applied to the skin contain local anesthetics that block the transmission of nociceptive nerve signals.62 Placebo-controlled trials show that topical anesthetics reduce distress during vaccine injections in infants (SMD −0.78, 95% CI −1.00 to −0.55; high certainty of evidence).114

Manual tapping (or the Helfer skin tap technique39) involves applying rhythmic tapping near the injection site. It can be delivered by the clinician administering vaccinations using the ventral aspect of the noninjecting hand or by another person. In included research, it was typically commenced just before vaccine injection and continued during injection and described as 15 to 16 taps, or up to 2 to 3 minutes of tapping. Research demonstrates a reduction in distress (SMD −1.69, 95% CI −2.42 to −0.97; moderate certainty of evidence).114

Some planning is required to accommodate the skin application time for topical anesthetics, which allows the medication to penetrate the skin. Commercial products included in trials vary in application times (20 min for lidocaine [e.g., Maxilene], 40 min for tetracaine [e.g., Ametop], and 60 min for lidocaine and prilocaine [e.g., Eutectic Mixture of Local Anesthetics — EMLA]). Plastic wrap can be wrapped around the limb to cover the cream completely while waiting, providing a simpler alternative to commercial dressings (e.g., Tegaderm, OpSite) and also preventing recipient discomfort that may occur when dressings are removed.

Topical anesthetics can be applied before arrival at the clinic or while waiting for vaccination. Research demonstrates feasibility of application while waiting, across vaccination settings including outpatient clinics, schools (mass vaccination clinics), and community pharmacies.22–24,115 We found no documented cases of systemic toxicity from using topical anesthetics for vaccinations, and no evidence that they interfere with the effectiveness of vaccines.62 Costs can be reduced by stocking multidose tubes.

Manual tapping, in contrast, can be delivered immediately before vaccination, requires minimal planning, and takes a maximum of 2 to 3 minutes to perform.

The panel issued a strong recommendation in favour of offering both topical anesthetics and manual tapping for infants, as these interventions are supported by high or moderate certainty of evidence and are demonstrated to be feasible. The recommendation emphasizes choice for caregivers.

We suggest not offering the following physical interventions at the injection site to caregivers for vaccine injections in infants (aged 0 to 12 mo): manual or device-delivered pressure, vibration (with or without cold), cold, vapocoolant, and manual rubbing (conditional recommendation, low to very low certainty of evidence).

Although we found demonstrated benefit for all physical interventions included in this conditional recommendation, except for manual rubbing (associated with a trivial effect on infant distress114), all were associated with low or very low certainty of evidence.

The panel decided to conditionally recommend against these interventions for reducing distress in infants. In formulating this recommendation, the panel prioritized evidence certainty in the decision-making process, given the availability of many alternative distress-reducing options for this age group that were supported by higher certainty of evidence. Additional considerations specific to infants included limited data on the tolerability of these interventions and the potential for discomfort during application62 — such as with ice or vibration.

Adults and children older than 12 months

We recommend offering topical anesthetics at the injection site for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months and allowing recipients and their support persons to choose whether to use them (strong recommendation, moderate certainty of evidence).

Placebo-controlled trials show that pharmacologic management of distress with topical anesthetics is effective in children and adults (SMD −0.59, 95% CI −0.93 to −0.25).114 Feasibility of use has been shown in diverse vaccination contexts, including schools, community pharmacies, and mass vaccination clinics.22–24,82 Topical anesthetics may be preferred by individuals who are more distressed during vaccination, such as children, and are routinely available in hospital settings for pediatric needle procedures.116 If topical anesthetics are applied at the clinic while vaccine recipients are waiting for vaccination, having distraction items and activities available can help prevent a buildup of anxiety that can occur during the waiting period, which may be more likely in individuals who want to use topical anesthetics.

The panel made a strong recommendation in favour of offering topical anesthetics to adults and children older than 12 months, considering the certainty of evidence, feasibility, and impacts on health equity. Extending their use to nonhospital settings and to individuals of all ages upholds high-quality, person-centred care by ensuring that effective distress-reducing options are available across health care settings, while respecting the diverse needs and preferences of vaccine recipients and their support persons. Considerations can be made for individuals with sensory differences in terms of application and removal methods (see related recommendation for infants).

We suggest offering the following physical interventions targeting the injection site for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months — manual tapping, vibration (with or without cold), manual or device-delivered pressure, cold, and vapocoolant — and allowing recipients and their support persons to choose their preferred option (conditional recommendation; low certainty of evidence).

Various physical interventions involving tactile stimulation (touch or cold sensation) at or near the injection site — delivered through manual or device-mediated approaches — were included in the systematic review;114 all were associated with a low certainty of evidence. Manual approaches include manual tapping (SMD −1.25, 95% CI −1.86 to −0.63) and thumb-applied pressure (SMD −0.3, 95% CI −0.78 to 0.17).114 Pressure can also be applied using an external device (SMD −0.43, 95% CI −0.94 to 0.08).114 A vibration device can be used alone (SMD −0.76, 95% CI −1.22 to −0.31) or combined with cold (SMD −0.63, 95% CI −0.95 to −0.32). Cold sensation may also be provided with ice or a cold pack (SMD −0.46, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.18) or a vapocoolant (SMD −0.38, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.02).114

Interventions in this category are typically delivered immediately before and during injection and require minimal time to implement (typically < 1 min). Manual techniques are usually performed by the clinician administering vaccinations. Multiple individuals can apply techniques that require assistive devices. Devices incur additional costs but the costs are low, and most devices are reusable (e.g., cold packs, vibration devices) after cleaning between users. Batteries are needed to operate vibration devices, and refrigeration is required to keep items cold (e.g., cold pack).

The panel issued a conditional recommendation for injection-site interventions in this category. In contrast to the approach used for infants (aged 0 to 12 mo), the panel adopted a more liberal approach to offering physical interventions with a low certainty of evidence for older individuals (children aged > 12 mo and adults). This is because of the limited number of options available overall for this age stratum, the feasibility of the interventions, and the high value placed on vaccine recipient and support person autonomy (choice), as well as on offering diverse distress-reducing intervention options to increase equity.

We suggest not offering manual rubbing near the injection site for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months (conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence).

Although manual rubbing near the injection site was associated with a benefit (SMD −0.53, 95% CI −1.01 to −0.05), the certainty of evidence was very low.114 Given this and the availability of many alternative physical interventions for this population with higher certainty of evidence, the panel made a conditional recommendation against manual rubbing near the injection site to reduce distress.

Psychological interventions involving distraction

Distraction involves transferring attention away from pain to something else, ideally something more pleasant or interesting. A common explanation for the mechanism of action is that attention is a limited resource (i.e., when someone is distracted, fewer resources are available for perceiving pain), although learning theory and neurobiological changes have also been discussed.117 Distraction can take many different forms; however, for all distraction interventions, consideration must be given to who (if anyone) is trained in the use of the strategy and whether others are supporting its use. Although tremendous variability exists in the timing that has been studied, distraction should start before and continue during and for a short time after the injection. Across all ages, the panel stressed choice for vaccine recipients and their support persons to respect individual preferences. The summary of evidence and factors prioritized for decision-making are included below and in Appendix 6, organized by vaccine recipient age.

Infants (aged 0 to 12 mo)

We suggest that caregivers be offered the option of using their choice of the following distraction interventions — toys and singing — for infants (aged 0 to 12 mo) receiving vaccine injections (conditional recommendation, low certainty of evidence).

Using a toy (with or without a battery) (SMD −3.68, 95% CI −5.12 to −2.25) and singing (SMD −0.6, 95% CI −1.21 to 0.02) can be used to reduce distress during vaccine injections in infants.118 A variety of age-appropriate options (e.g., rattle, lullaby) are available to support caregivers who wish to use these interventions to reduce distress in their infants.

Toys are relatively low cost and can be reused after cleaning; caregivers likely need to support the infant’s continued engagement with the toy, and novelty may be important.119 Caregivers can sing to their infants (e.g., a lullaby) before, during, and after the injection, with no additional resources required.

The panel made a conditional recommendation in favour of distraction via toys and singing. This recommendation reflects the panel’s prioritization of evidence certainty and recognition of the availability of other distress-reducing interventions in this population, supported by higher-quality evidence. The panel additionally noted that distraction interventions should be paired with physical closeness and soothing behaviours from caregivers to support infant distress regulation.120

We suggest not offering distraction interventions that involve auditory stimulation using a device and videos or movies to caregivers for vaccine injections in infants (aged 0 to 12 mo) (conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence).

In our systematic review, distraction interventions supported by very low certainty of evidence included auditory interventions with a device (e.g., white noise, other sounds, music) (SMD −1.39, 95% CI −2.26 to −0.52) and videos or movies (SMD −1.67, 95% CI −3.56 to 0.23).118

Given the availability of alternative distraction strategies for infants, as well as other interventions across other P categories — such as physical and pharmacologic approaches — with higher certainty of evidence, the panel issued a conditional recommendation against the use of auditory interventions delivered via devices or distraction via a video or movie. Furthermore, the panel acknowledged the difficulty of assessing the acceptability of these interventions to infants (i.e., preference uncertain).

Adults and children older than 12 months

We recommend offering distraction with music for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months and allowing recipients and their support persons to choose whether to use it (strong recommendation; moderate certainty of evidence).

Music distraction reduces distress in vaccine recipients older than 12 months (SMD −0.38, 95% CI −0.66 to −0.11).118 Music can be delivered in different ways, including loudspeakers and headphones. Consideration of age-appropriate options for music delivery is required for vaccination settings when offering this intervention. Vaccine recipients may also be invited to use their own devices. For individuals wearing headphones, plans should be made to communicate with them so they do not have to continually put the headphones on and off, which would disrupt the distraction.121

The panel issued a strong recommendation in favour of offering music distraction because of the certainty of evidence and feasibility of use, while maintaining choice. It should be noted that this recommendation is not an endorsement of devices or “screen time.” It refers specifically to the therapeutic use of music as a distraction technique during vaccine injections, which are infrequent and time-limited events.

We suggest offering the following distraction interventions for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months — digital and nondigital activities or games, virtual reality, breathing activities with toys, toys (with or without a battery), and videos or movies — and allowing recipients and their support persons to choose their preferred option (conditional recommendation, low to very low certainty of evidence).

Various distraction interventions, delivered using both nondigital and digital approaches, were included in the systematic review, based on low to very low certainty of evidence.118 Nondigital approaches include using a toy (with or without a battery) (SMD −1.37, 95% CI −2.11 to −0.63; very low certainty of evidence), engaging in breathing activities with toys (e.g., blowing a pinwheel or party blower) (SMD −0.82, 95% CI −1.38 to −0.26; very low certainty of evidence), and playing activities or games (e.g., drawing, role-playing) (SMD −0.86, 95% CI −2.01 to 0.29; very low certainty of evidence). Digital approaches include playing activities or games (e.g., video games) (SMD −0.74, 95% CI −1.35 to −0.12; low certainty of evidence), virtual reality (SMD −0.51, 95% CI −0.73 to −0.29; low certainty of evidence), and watching videos or movies (SMD −1.03, 95% CI −1.43 to −0.62; very low certainty of evidence).118

The set-up time is relatively short (< 1 min) for most of these interventions. Some basic equipment is required; in many cases, it is already available or can be easily acquired with minimal resources. Alternatively, vaccine recipients and support persons can choose to use their own supplies. The feasibility of various distraction interventions has been well documented across vaccination settings.22–24,81,82,85,122 An exception may be virtual reality games, which typically involve more substantial costs, potentially limiting their appropriateness and practicality.

The panel conditionally recommended offering the aforementioned distraction interventions based on the certainty of evidence, feasibility, and strong emphasis on acceptability to vaccine recipients and support persons (choice), necessitating a diverse range of options to promote equity and person-centred care. The panel adopted a more liberal stance with respect to evaluating the evidence for children and adults (v. infants) because of the limited number of distress-reducing intervention options supported by a higher certainty of evidence in this age group and the ability of this population to communicate their preferences.

We suggest not offering verbal distraction and simple breathing interventions (e.g., coughing) for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months (conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence).

Distraction interventions in the systematic review with very low certainty of evidence included verbal distraction (SMD −0.27, 95% CI −1.02 to 0.47) and simple breathing interventions like coughing or taking a deep breath and blowing out (SMD −0.08, 95% CI −0.49 to 0.32).118

Although these methods may seem feasible and accessible, unintended consequences may occur that undermine distress management. For example, in the case of verbal distraction, individuals may unintentionally engage in distress-promoting behaviours, such as offering reassurance, using fear-provoking language, or making false suggestions, which are not helpful or can even escalate distress.53,54,56 For simple breathing interventions, the point estimate (i.e., SMD) does not meet the minimum benefit threshold, suggesting a subclinical effect.

The panel issued a conditional recommendation against the use of verbal distraction (small effect) and simple breathing interventions (trivial effect), given the availability of alternative interventions with higher certainty of evidence and higher effect sizes — both among available distraction interventions and across other P domains such as physical and pharmacologic approaches. We note that this recommendation does not apply to a vaccine recipient’s request to talk to them about something they like (i.e., “directed” verbal distraction), which respects autonomy. This recommendation also does not apply to slow, deep breathing (i.e., diaphragmatic or belly breathing), a distinct intervention123 that involves repeated deep and slow inspiration and expiration, which was inadequately evaluated in 1 trial in the systematic review.118 Deep breathing is very well accepted in other contexts123–125 and should not be confused with what has been evaluated in the vaccination literature.

Psychological interventions involving memory reframing

Adults and children older than 12 months

Memory reframing interventions involve emphasizing positive aspects of vaccine injection procedures (e.g., use of coping strategies), correcting overly negative memories of distress, and promoting self-efficacy.126

We suggest not offering memory reframing for vaccine injections in adults and children older than 12 months (conditional recommendation, low certainty of evidence).

The systematic review demonstrated a trivial effect of the intervention on distress (SMD −0.16, 95% CI −0.67 to 0.36).118

The panel made a conditional recommendation against the use of memory reframing, primarily from a subclinical effect on distress.

Methods

We used the GRADE40–42 approach to select clinical questions and key outcomes, assess certainty of evidence, and formulate recommendations and good practice statements. The guideline process and reporting align with the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II).127 It is also consistent with the methods used by the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization,128 which plans to use this guideline to inform an update to its 2015 recommendations on mitigating pain at the time of vaccination.9

Composition of participating groups

An independent team (HELPinKids&Adults Collaborative) was assembled, comprising 28 members from across Canada who participated in a variety of groups (described below) related to guideline development, based on their expertise, perspective, and interest (see Appendix 9, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content, for predefined roles and activities, and Appendix 10, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content, for membership).

The Oversight Committee, consisting of 5 individuals, was responsible for overall direction, priorities, membership, group processes, methodological guidance, and approval of the final guideline. It comprised 2 co-chairs — an INGUIDE GRADE–certified clinical practice guideline methods specialist (E.L.) and the HELPinKids&Adults scientific lead (A.T.) — and 3 content experts (N.E.M., V.S., C.M.M.). Members of the Oversight Committee selected individuals to serve as members of the guideline panel but did not participate in decision-making activities of the panel. The methodologist oversaw all aspects of the guideline development process, including management of competing interests.

The Evidence Working Group comprised individuals with content and methods expertise (A.T., V.S., C.M.M., E.U.) who searched for, synthesized, and presented evidence summaries to the guideline panel, and drafted the guideline manuscript.

The guideline panel comprised both voting and nonvoting subgroups. Voting members included 20 individuals from across disciplines and sectors involved in vaccination: clinicians (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, child life specialists), scientists (infectious diseases, vaccinology, pharmacology), policy-makers (government), educators (postsecondary and health care organizations), and patient or public members (individual vaccine recipients, support persons). Panel members were responsible for voting on included clinical questions and outcomes and drafting clinical practice guideline recommendations and considerations. Three voting members shared a vote with a colleague from the same organization (E.A. and C.J. — Public Health Agency of Canada; J.C. and S.S. — British Columbia Centre for Disease Control; M.G. and E.K. — First Nations Health Authority [British Columbia]); hence, 20 voting members contributed to the equivalent of 17 votes. Nonvoting panel members included 2 content experts (R.P.R., M.N.) who provided feedback during guideline development.

Patient preferences were incorporated through guideline panel membership, which included 2 public representatives — 1 individual with a fear of needles and 1 parent of a child with a history of repeated painful procedures — as well as individual guideline panel members who are part of equity-deserving populations and serve in roles that promote equity (including across age, gender, geographical location, and socioeconomic status). We incorporated patient views in all group discussions of the evidence base. Additionally, we integrated patient preferences documented in original research into the evidence review process via the GRADE EtD framework.43 All panel members, including patient representatives, received education about the processes used to develop guideline recommendations and good practice statements.

Selection of priority topics

We derived 47 clinical questions from those in our 2015 guideline8,129 and an unpublished scan of studies published in the interim. In a vote by eligible panel members, the draft questions were approved, with no changes proposed.

Clinical questions followed the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) framework.130 The population included individuals of all ages undergoing vaccination, clinicians, or support persons, as applicable to the clinical question. Interventions consisted of distress-reducing modalities during vaccination, spanning the 5P framework: process (education and implementation), procedural (injection techniques), physical (body position and activity), pharmacologic (pain medicine), and psychological (thoughts and behaviours).8 We established age divisions (0 to 12 mo and > 12 mo) for certain interventions to account for their mechanisms of action or developmental differences.131 The comparators included placebo (prioritized when possible), no treatment, usual care, or an active intervention if an add-on trial.

Voting panel members ranked the relative importance of candidate outcomes, prioritizing the target population (i.e., vaccine recipient), consistent with our previous approach.8 Distress was selected by voting panel members as the critical outcome across all clinical questions; it was operationalized as pain, fear, or both, in individuals of all ages.131 For clinical questions involving process interventions, critical outcomes also included the use of distress-reducing interventions and knowledge of distress-reducing interventions. The use of a single continuous measure of distress across all ages was new to this guideline update and allowed us to include more studies in a single meta-analysis, streamlining the presentation and increasing clarity for users, consistent with GRADE methodology. Consistent with our previous work, we used a low threshold of effect (SMD ≥ 0.2)44 as important, given that distress is an iatrogenic harm.8

Literature review and quality assessment

Recommendations were based on 5 systematic reviews80,95,113,114,118 that combined eligible studies from our earlier work129 with new studies published in the interim. The Evidence Working Group conducted the reviews; methodological details are available in a separate manuscript.131 Briefly, we developed a new search strategy that combined pain, fear, anxiety, and syncope terms with both needle and study design terms to capture articles published since 2015. We tested it to ensure suitable articles were identified and then ran it in MEDLINE, Embase, APA PsycInfo, CINAHL, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global (2015 to Jan. 31, 2025). Eligible studies were English-language randomized and quasi-randomized controlled trials evaluating distress-reducing interventions for vaccines administered by injection into the arm or leg in individuals of any age. We scanned references of included articles and systematic reviews and consulted experts to identify additional relevant articles. We assessed quality using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (version 1.0).132 Box 1 provides information about the approach we used to assess certainty of evidence for the overall body of evidence base using GRADE.

Development of recommendations

We drafted recommendations at a hybrid (virtual and in-person) meeting of the guideline panel and the Oversight Committee (Sept. 8, 2025). GRADE evidence summaries and training materials were provided before the meeting, along with clarification and reinforcement during the meeting. In developing the recommendations, we considered the prioritized factors from the GRADE EtD framework43 (Box 1): priority of the problem, certainty of evidence, desirable effects (benefits), equity, acceptability, and feasibility (explanations in Appendix 11, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content).

We typically made strong recommendations when the certainty of evidence according to GRADE was either high or moderate, and made conditional recommendations when certainty of evidence was low or very low; exceptions could be made (e.g., when other evidence-based interventions of higher certainty could create inequities) and are noted where applicable. To support a recommendation in favour of an intervention, a minimum threshold for effectiveness — defined as an SMD of at least 0.244 — had to be met for critically important outcomes (e.g., distress). Effect sizes that did not meet this threshold (i.e., SMD < 0.2) were considered trivial and not clinically important.

We gave little importance to variability in the value of the outcomes because acceptance of needle-related distress is widespread in society, despite being associated with well-documented harms.6,133–136 No substantial safety concerns were raised in the evidence base for the included interventions; therefore, safety was not a primary consideration when weighing the benefits of the interventions (v. the risks or harms). We gave costs low importance because they are minimal for most interventions, and because distress management is regarded as part of good immunization practice;9 hence, costs are the responsibility of organizations and clinicians providing vaccination services.

We developed good practice statements according to guidance from GRADE;41,42 details are provided in Box 1 and Appendix 7.

We used a consensus process to agree on recommendations and good practice statements. However, we had set an a priori minimum cut-off of 80% for a strong recommendation and a simple majority for a conditional recommendation,137,138 if we were unable to achieve consensus. For all recommendations, a consensus was achieved by all voting panel members.

External review

We shared a draft of the guideline with external organizations that were suggested by guideline team members, for general review using the AGREE Global Rating Scale instrument.139 Organizations that provided feedback are listed in Appendix 12 (available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content), along with a summary of their comments.

Suggestions were primarily related to the provision of additional methodological details and results, reorganization and simplification of the presentation of the recommendations, and inclusion of knowledge translation tools and cost information to support implementation.

Finally, several external reviewers questioned draft recommendations related to oral interventions in infants, which included 2 separate recommendations reflecting the certainty of evidence of included strategies (moderate v. low or very low). We subsequently combined these recommendations into a single strong recommendation, with unanimous agreement from the panel.

We made other changes to address the feedback, and then the revised guideline was rereviewed and approved by the panel.

Management of competing interests

We applied the Guidelines International Network principles for managing conflicts of interest140 (Appendix 13, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content). We sought intellectual and financial conflicts from all members at the beginning of the project, during drafting of the guideline recommendations, and during guideline manuscript review. Conflicts were reviewed by the Oversight Committee and adjudicated by one of the co-chairs (E.L.). A priori, we decided that individuals with subject matter expertise (i.e., academic faculty with research programs involving pain, fear, and related therapeutic areas) would be excluded from all voting activities because of potential intellectual conflicts (A.T., V.S., C.M.M., N.E.M., R.P.R., M.N.). The methodologist chaired the meeting in which recommendations were formulated, to ensure that the procedures were followed. In particular, individuals with relevant financial or intellectual conflicts were excluded from voting on relevant recommendations; however, they could participate in discussions and answer questions. One voting panel member was excluded from voting on process intervention questions because of their extensive involvement (as a knowledge user) on process intervention–related research (L.M.B.).

We received funding for the project from the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (FRN 159905). The funding agency had no input into the project. The funding was used to pay for project support staff, travel and hospitality costs for the guideline meeting, and publication costs. Two voting guideline panel members representing patient and public interests received an honorarium at the conclusion of the process. The remaining members were not remunerated.

Implementation

A need exists for organizations and clinicians delivering vaccinations to implement these recommendations widely, across diverse populations and geographic regions, to realize up-to-date, safer, and more responsive care for vaccine recipients and their support persons. To do this effectively, they must accept distress management as core to quality immunization9 and integrate it within vaccination program delivery and evaluation.141 As with other innovations in vaccination, effective integration requires allocation of appropriate resources for training, supplies, and oversight.

Substantial advances have been made in the applicability of guideline recommendations since the 2015 version,8 facilitating the transfer of knowledge into practice. The earlier guideline lacked a framework to support implementation across all recommendations. In contrast, systematic reviews for this update identified multicomponent interventions that integrate strategies and engage all user groups,80 thereby addressing this gap and providing important evaluation data for guideline implementation.142

The evidence for multicomponent interventions focuses primarily on the CARD system, a knowledge translation tool developed by selected team members (A.T., C.M.M., N.E.M., L.M.B.) to support integration of the 2015 guideline.25 Relevant to both the 2015 guideline and the current update, CARD offers a structured approach to vaccination planning, delivery, and evaluation that integrates numerous evidence-based interventions and accepted methods of promoting practice change, including educating the relevant knowledge users; continuously monitoring, evaluating, and adapting implementation; and promoting collaboration.143 It distinguishes the roles of those involved in vaccination and fosters collaboration to improve the vaccination experience.38 Vaccine recipients and their support persons “play their CARDs” by choosing coping strategies framed around CARD, and health care providers and organizations use the “4Es” checklist (Education–Environment–Engagement–Evaluation) to guide the support of these choices and implement other evidence-based interventions from the guideline (Appendix 14, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content).38

A robust evidence base has been amassed — including randomized and nonrandomized controlled trials involving approximately 20 000 vaccine recipients — that shows the real-world effectiveness of CARD in reducing vaccination-related distress.22–24,81–83,85,144 This evidence also encompasses key implementation outcomes, such as acceptability and feasibility across relevant user groups.22–24,70,81–86 Vaccine recipients have reported improved experiences and more positive attitudes toward vaccination with CARD, and clinicians reported vaccination interactions to be time neutral or time saving in studies that assessed these outcomes. In nonrandomized trials focusing on populations with historically lower vaccination coverage, including those with high levels of fear, CARD was associated with increased vaccine acceptance.87,88

CARD tools are freely available at www.cardsystem.ca and include downloadable materials, a Web-based health game to teach children about CARD,145 and a training e-module for clinicians and organizations.38 We have included an implementation toolkit in Appendix 15, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content. This includes a CARD 4E tip sheet for clinicians and organizations (Appendix 16, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content) and CARD checklists to solicit coping preferences in vaccine recipients and their support persons — 1 for infants (Appendix 17, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content) and 1 for adults and children older than 12 months (Appendix 18, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content). Templates for other tools — such as wall posters that provide education, posters that serve as visual distractions, and distraction activities like colouring pages and mazes — are provided online to support implementation. Monitoring and feedback surveys are available to track compliance and capture vaccine recipient symptoms and experiences, helping to ensure that implementation goals are met. Most tools are available in English and French. These resources were developed to address common implementation barriers and facilitators related to people, environment, evidence, and logistics146 (see also Appendix 19, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content, for a high-level summary of barriers and potential solutions).

CARD provides a well-developed, practical, and adaptable approach. Costs associated with implementation include staff training, materials (e.g., posters, surveys, and distress-reducing items), monitoring, and evaluation. Start-up costs may range from several hundred to several thousand dollars, depending on the scope of implementation, such as whether implementation leaders and champions are involved to provide support, monitoring, and evaluation, and the characteristics of the population being served. For example, pediatric populations typically request a greater number of distress-reducing interventions than adult vaccine recipients do. Revisions to policies and procedures may be necessary to support professional role changes and environmental and workflow modifications that enable implementation of specific recommendations, such as educating vaccine recipients and support persons, reducing fear cues, and using topical anesthetics or sweet-tasting solutions.

It is particularly important to consider the timing of education of vaccine recipients and their support persons. Providing education ahead of time allows for logistical and emotional preparation, such as wearing clothing that permits easy access to the limb and bringing preferred coping items. In settings where anticipatory guidance is feasible, such as school-based or scheduled clinic visits, advanced education is often preferable. In contrast, in walk-in or high-throughput environments, same-day education may be more practical, supported by clear signage and workflow adjustments. Regardless of timing, educational interventions are typically brief, making integration feasible across vaccination settings.

We note that while we applied age stratification (0 to 12 mo v. > 12 mo) to summarize the evidence and simplify guidance, overlap may be present in the literature base across ages. Together with substantial differences in developmental trajectories and individual circumstances, appropriate use of some interventions may exist outside of the recommendations (e.g., breastfeeding in child > 12 mo).

Ongoing costs can be partly offset by discontinuing non–evidence-based practices in accordance with the Choosing Wisely Canada framework (https://choosingwiselycanada.org) — for example, administering vaccines in the same rather than alternate limbs24,85,147 and eliminating alcohol skin antisepsis before injection,24,64 both of which are preferred by vaccine recipients and reduce resource use. Familiarity with CARD and enhanced coping can also confer long-term benefits to the vaccination process, including reduced procedure duration and improved efficiency.

Several summaries have been developed to support communication of the guidelines across different audiences, including a policy brief, and targeted summaries for the general public, educators, and media (Appendix 20, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content).

We will conduct regular literature scans to assess whether an update is warranted within the next 5 to 10 years. Key factors in the decision for a future update will include the emergence of new evidence with potential to influence recommendations, methodological advances in guideline methodology, perceived importance of the topic, and the availability of research funding. An interim search of newly published studies since we conducted our supporting systematic reviews (i.e., Feb. 1 to Nov. 20, 2025) identified several new studies; the findings, however, remain consistent with our systematic reviews.80,95,113,114,118

Other guidelines

This national guideline update builds on our 2015 version8 by streamlining structure, simplifying age stratification, and prioritizing distress as the critical outcome. Strong recommendations for overlapping interventions were retained, whereas refinements included reclassifying within the 5P framework, consolidating related strategies, aligning recommendations with user groups, and introducing the evidence-based CARD system to support implementation. These changes promote person- and family-centred care, enhance clarity and usability, and ensure practice is guided by updated evidence. We prioritized interventions supported by new or updated evidence within the vaccination context, causing some strategies to be removed or consolidated into broader categories or good practice statements. Additional details outlining differences are provided in Appendix 21, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260123/tab-related-content. With respect to relevant international guidelines, the WHO adapted the 2015 Canadian guidelines to reflect a global context, leading to some differences in the interventions recommended based on cost and feasibility.9

We reviewed 4 related guidelines published after 2015 — 1 international guideline148 and 3 national guidelines (Canada,149 United States,150 The Netherlands151); these addressed pain management in children undergoing minor procedures more broadly but did include needle procedures. Distress-reducing interventions in these guidelines largely overlapped with our guideline update (e.g., topical anesthetics, breastfeeding, sucrose, distraction, education), but some were absent (e.g., manual tapping) and others added (e.g., hypnosis, sedation), which may reflect differences in context and the relevant evidence base. Two guidelines applied GRADE methodology but were indirect to vaccine injections; one pertained to pediatric cancer and the other to topical anesthetics.148,151 None used a single measure of effect or clearly defined a minimum threshold for interpretation, and none provided a detailed implementation framework and guidance on how to incorporate the choices of health care recipients and their support persons.

Our updated guideline distinguishes between choice (for vaccine recipients and their support persons) and use (for clinicians and organizations). This distinction reflects a deliberate effort to promote person- and family-centred care and equity. It stands in contrast to traditional guideline and distress management approaches, whereby clinicians and organizations evaluate symptoms and select interventions based on their own preferences, not necessarily supported by research evidence or care recipient and support person input, and assume responsibility for decisions on behalf of recipients.7,85,152,153

Gaps in knowledge

The recommendations in this guideline are constrained by the available evidence. Gaps in evidence exist for research involving adult populations, individuals with neurocognitive or neuro-developmental diversity, and those with underlying conditions.

An important future direction involves evaluating guideline implementation in under-represented populations, particularly equity-deserving populations. It will also be essential to monitor and evaluate the broader impacts of guideline implementation over time — not simply immediate distress responses — to understand outcomes at a societal level, including acquisition of needle fear and acceptance of vaccination and other injectable therapies. Formal economic analysis has not been performed to determine the cost–benefit and presents another important area for future research.

Limitations

We did not compare the relative effectiveness of different distress-reducing interventions, as that was beyond the scope of this guideline. Instead, our primary aim was to identify evidence-based options that can be used and combined, considering the values and preferences of vaccine recipients and their support persons. Future research could address these questions; for example, through network meta-analyses.

The literature search was restricted to English-language studies, which may have led to the exclusion of potentially relevant studies. In some cases, study authors could not be contacted to verify methodological details for identified articles, resulting in the exclusion of additional potentially relevant studies. Sample sizes were also reduced in studies with treatment arms that contributed data to multiple analyses in the same meta-analysis. Together, these factors may have reduced the precision of estimated treatment effects for included interventions.

Given that we divided results into 2 age stratifications (0 to 12 mo and > 12 mo) to capture developmental and intervention considerations, this approach precluded a detailed evaluation of the interventions across the age spectrum. We note, however, that the available research does not provide a clear distinction across ages.

The evidence base comprised studies conducted across diverse settings and populations with varying levels of training and expertise of the relevant knowledge user groups. Although this diversity may enhance generalizability, it also introduces uncertainty regarding what can be achieved in any clinical practice. Local monitoring and evaluation are strongly encouraged to ensure intended goals are met.

Consistent with our previous guideline,8 the scope of the clinical questions was intentionally limited to interventions deemed relevant and feasible for routine vaccinations. The focus remained on strategies applied before and during vaccination, rather than after the procedure, to emphasize prevention of iatrogenic harm. Feasibility considerations led to the exclusion of studies involving nonvaccination needle procedures (e.g., veni-puncture, venous cannulation), neonatal-specific interventions (e.g., skin-to-skin care),112 and one-off or nonroutine techniques (e.g., eliciting grasp reflex). Finally, the scope did not include other types of immunization (e.g., monoclonal antibodies), although certain recommendations may nonetheless be applicable.

Conclusion

Vaccine injection–related distress should be viewed as a predictable and manageable consequence of vaccination, rather than an inevitable event. This guideline updates our 2015 recommendations for reducing distress during vaccine injections and presents evidence-based interventions across the 5P framework: process, procedural, physical, pharmacologic, and psychological. Building on earlier work, the guideline introduces new tools and strategies supported by research, offering practical guidance for health system leaders, clinicians, vaccine recipients, and their support persons to improve current vaccination delivery practices with the goal of creating more positive vaccination experiences that reduce fear and support future vaccination.
