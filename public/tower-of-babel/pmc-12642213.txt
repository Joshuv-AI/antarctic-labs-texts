Effect of lower-limb fatigue on kinematic variables during an unanticipated block among adolescent female volleyball players
Authors: Seyedeh Haniyeh Hosseini, Mehdi Gheitasi, Seyed Mohammad Hosseini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies investigating the effects of muscle fatigue on lower limb kinematics in adolescent volleyball players have reported contradictory findings. Thus, the present study aimed to assess the effect of lower-limb fatigue on the kinematic variables during an unanticipated block among adolescent female volleyball players.MethodsGiven the inclusion criteria, 20 adolescent female volleyball players were selected through a convenience sampling method. In the pre-test stage, each subject performed an unanticipated single-leg jump-landing maneuver, the data of which were recorded by using a motion analyzer. Then, the intervention of a volleyball-specific lower-limb fatigue protocol was applied and the players were re-evaluated.ResultsThe results represented a significant decrease in knee and hip joint flexion, as well as a significant increase in ankle inversion at the initial contact after applying the fatigue protocol(P ≤ 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed in terms of the other kinematic variables of the knee, ankle, and hip joints at the initial contact and landing peak(P ≥ 0.05).ConclusionsReduced hip and knee flexion, along with increased ankle inversion, were identified as high risk movement strategies observed at initial contact. These findings suggest that fatigue may influence the proper kinematic pattern of lower-limb, leading to changes in movement control during landing and consequently increasing the risk of knee injuries in athletes.Trial registration numberConsidering that our research is semi-experimental, it does not need a clinical trial number.

Introduction

Volleyball players are at risk of injury due to the high volume of jump training in specific movement tasks such as spike, block, frequent jump, and landing [1, 2]. Lower limb injuries constitute 60% of all volleyball injuries, which are more common in ankle and knee joints than other joints [3]. Regarding this sport, 63% of all recorded injuries and 61% of knee joint ones are related to the landing task [4], occurring acutely following a block or attack without hitting another player [5].

Identifying injury risk factors and formulating strategies for injury prevention can be addressed as the main components to prevent sports injuries [6]. The role of neuromuscular fatigue, as one of the potentially modifiable variables, has attracted the attention of researchers in recent years [7]. In sports activities, fatigue is a multidimensional and complicated phenomenon [8], representing a decrease in sensory and cognitive functions [9]. Generally, it is defined as a reduction in the muscle’s ability to produce the desired force and causing neuromuscular changes, including a decline in muscle strength [10] the body’s capability to maintain stability [11], change in muscle activation [12], proprioception [13], and movement asymmetry in the joints, especially the knee joint during the landing task [14].

According to Fidai et al. [15], an elevation in fatigue level is related to more dynamic knee valgus, also, Bard et al. evaluated the effect of fatigue on the movement pattern of jumping and landing and introduced gender and fatigue as two risk factors for ACL injury [16], but in some studies, including Kim et al., it has been reported that fatigue does not affect the angles of the lower limbs [17].

Frequent jumping and landings can lead to lower limb fatigue due to their high demand and during a volleyball match, an athlete can perform around 200 jumps [18, 19]. This issue affects athletes’ performance, leading to an alteration of proper landing technique [20]. The relevant injuries are associated with landing movement refers to a set of several biomechanical factors, that appear highly related to knee and ACL injury [21–23]. Any malalignment in the lower limb (e.g., knee valgus and internal rotation, knee valgus angle, knee flexion angle, and ankle deviation, as well as the combined effect of hip flexion and internal rotation) during landing makes the athlete susceptible to the knee joint injuries [24].

As indicated, the results of some studies show a significant relationship observed between fatigue and ACL [15, 16]. It is also specified that reaction task maneuvers are closer to competition conditions than anticipated ones because the players should perform the movement in the shortest possible time immediately after detecting the stimulus, thus resulting in adopting an adverse neuromuscular control pattern during landing combined with fatigue, which leads to injury [25].

However, none of the studies assessed the above-mentioned relationship in unanticipated block conditions among adolescent female volleyball players who are exposed to fatigue due to frequent jumps and landings. During adolescence, individuals experience a vulnerable musculoskeletal phase due to heightened neuromuscular fatigue, coinciding growth spurts, and puberty [26, 27].

Accordingly, in light of existing uncertainties, the present study aimed to investigate the effect of lower limb fatigue on kinematic variables during unanticipated blocks among adolescent female volleyball players. Drawing upon previous research [15, 28], it is hypothesized that fatigue induces significant alterations in lower limb kinematics within the studied population.

Methods

This study was conducted using a single-group pre-post test design with an intervention, due to the challenges design and constraints in recruiting a comparable control group that met the same inclusion criteria. The statistical population included 20 adolescent female volleyball players (aged 13–16) from clubs in Tehran, Iran (Table 1). The inclusion criteria were a history of volleyball activity during the past three years, as well as not performing any intense physical activity up to 48 h before implementing the fatigue protocol and not suffering from joint diseases in the lower limb (e.g., osteoarthritis). Additionally, no history of any injury in the last 12 months, lower limb surgery or fracture (ankle, knee, hip, and pelvis), and chronic disorders in lower limbs (e.g., chronic ankle instability or patellofemoral pain syndrome) can be mentioned as some of the inclusion criteria. The others were not having significant postural disorders, especially in the lumbo-pelvic-hip complex, knee, ankle, and foot.

The sample size was determined using G*Power software version 3.1, with an alpha of 0.05, a power of 0.8, an effect size of 1.25, and based on previous studies [29]. The initial sample size was calculated to be 8 participants, but to account for potential missing data or participant dropouts, a total of 20 eligible participants were recruited.

After coordinating with female volleyball clubs and teams in Tehran province, the subjects were referred to the Laboratory of Pathology and Corrective Exercises at the Faculty of Sport Sciences and Health of Shahid Beheshti University of Tehran, Iran. A meeting was held before the main test, during which the players completed individual and parent consent forms, as well as individual and demographic information. Further, they got familiar with test procedures and objectives. Regarding the main test session, the subjects first took warming-up (10 min stretching and dynamic exercises for the upper and lower body) training, and their dominant leg was determined with the soccer shoot [30, 31], followed by recording their maximum vertical jump by using a Sargent vertical jump device. Briefly, the players were first asked to touch the highest point of the board while standing and still, and then make three vertical jumps, the greatest value of which was recorded as the maximum jump height. The difference between the highest height recorded in the standing position and the vertical jump was considered as the maximum vertical jump.

Task protocol

In the pre-test phases, the players were positioned at the center of the designated area behind a wide strip, at the height of 2.24 m. The reason for using a strip instead of a net was the placement of markers on the pelvic (ASIS), which could obstruct the view of the markers by the cameras and potentially cause them to get caught in the 5-centimeter gaps of the net during the jump and landing. Additionally, a random movement direction device was used, which consisted 2 lights: a blue light on the right and a red light on the left. These lights were positioned at a height of 1.50 m, right in front of the players, and the reason for using this device was to better simulate match conditions (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Random orientation device

Random orientation device

After positioning the players in the designated area, they assumed the specific volleyball block stance. Upon the illumination of either light, they would take a step to the side in the direction of the light and execute the technique. It is essential to note that the landing should be performed on the dominant leg. The players were required to perform this task three times consecutively, with the direction of movement being randomly determined by the sequence of lights turning on. The reason for randomizing the direction was to prevent the players from adapting to a fixed direction. The sequence of lights turning on is followed by six patterns: right-left-right, right-right-left, left-right-right, left-right-left, right-left-left, and left-left-right. In the next phase, the volleyball-specific functional fatigue protocol was applied, and the players were repositioned in the designated area and performed the task.

Lower-limb fatigue protocol

Before performing the fatigue protocol, the maximum vertical jump of each subject was measured on the Sargent vertical jump device [11]. In the next stage, the lower limb length of each individual was used to determine the distance between two legs in the lunge movement.

The functional fatigue protocol applied in this study included several repeated cycles until reaching fatigue [11]. Each cycle involves the three stations of the Modified Southeast Missouri (SEMO) agility test, 10 fixed lunges, and 10 vertical jumps in rapid succession.

Modified Southeast Missouri agility drill

The first station, a modified SEMO agility test, is performed in a 3.6 × 5.7 square, which includes a series of forward sprints, diagonal backpedaling, and side shuffling (Fig. 2). At the completion of this station, the subjects immediately started the lunge station.

SEMO agility table

Stationary lunges

The subjects immediately started the lunge station by stepping forward five times with the right leg and five times with the left one. The distance between the two legs equaled the recorded lower limb length, and the pieces of tape on the ground denoted the distance between the origin and the target. The players should execute each lunge in 2 s. To achieve the certain target, the lunge movement should be performed as 90˚ of the hip and knee flexion of the front leg along with keeping the trunk upright and returning the leg to the starting point after finishing the movement.

Quick jumps

In the third station, the individuals instantly started 10 quick two-leg vertical jumps equivalent to 50% of the maximum jump height determined by the Sargent vertical jump device, followed by landing on two legs. They were requested to stand near the wall and hold their hands and arms above the head towards the wall (similar to the block movement). Further, the subjects should be continuously encouraged verbally during the fatigue protocol [11].

After completing each three stations as one cycle of the fatigue protocol, the volleyball players immediately returned to the first station and they started a new cycle until fatigue was reached. Furthermore, base time measurement was used to specify the subject fatigue. In this regard, they were asked to implement the protocol with the maximum power and high intensity two times, and the best obtained time was recorded as the base time for each individual. Regarding each player, the cycle in which the time increased by 50% compared to her base time was assumed as her fatigue point [11].

Kinematic data collection

The kinematic data were collected using a 3D motion analyzer system from Motion Analysis Corporation, equipped with eight Osprey infrared cameras with a sampling rate of 240 Hz. The system was also equipped with cortex software. Further, the retroreflective markers of 9 mm diameter were placed bilaterally on the skin surface, anterior and posterior superior iliac spines, sacrum, lateral and medial femoral epicondyle, lateral midthigh, lateral mid shank, medial and lateral malleolus, second metatarsal head, and posterior calcaneus according to the Helen Hayes marker set [32]. The analyzed parameters included joint angles of the hip, knee, and ankle in the sagittal, frontal and horizontal planes, extract at the moment of the initial contact and at the peak landing. The moment of landing (initial contact of the foot with the ground) was obtained by considering the coordinates of the second metatarsal head marker position along the z-axis in the frame with the minimum spatial distance from the ground. Peak knee flexion was also used to determine peak landing. Furthermore, a Butterworth 6 Hz low-pass filter was utilized to filter the data [33].

Kinematic data analysis

Kinematic data were processed utilizing MATLAB 2019 software. Joint angles of the hip, knee, and ankle were computed across the sagittal, frontal, and horizontal planes. The analysis was performed by a biomechanics specialist employing custom-written MATLAB scripts. Joint angles were extracted at both the initial contact and peak landing phases.

Statistical analysis

The data were examined for normality by employing the Shapiro-Wilk test and statistically analyzed by using SPSS 26 software. The dependent tow-tail t-test was employed for the comparison of the data in the pre-test and post-test phases. In this study, Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion and implications

The present study evaluated the effect of lower-limb fatigue on kinematic variables during an unanticipated block among adolescent female volleyball players. The findings of this study indicated that fatigue significantly reduced hip and knee flexion while increasing ankle inversion at initial contact, potentially resulting in a harder landing and higher risk of fatigue related injuries.

Although a multi-planar fatigue protocol was implemented in this study to better simulate match and training conditions, most of the observed changes occurred within the sagittal plane. Nevertheless, based on the findings, it appears that lower-limb fatigue can generally alter the biomechanical nature of the lower extremities.

The variation in kinematic parameters may be attributed to muscle fatigue and less effect on decreasing impact forces through eccentric activities [34, 35]. In this study, after applying the fatigue protocol, a reduction in hip and knee flexion was observed both at initial contact and the landing peak; however, this reduction was statistically significant only at initial contact (hip: pre-test 20.11, post-test 18.24; knee: -14.64, post-test − 12.40), which is consistent with the results of some studies [28, 36]. Reduction in flexion is potentially recognized as an injury mechanism, as it leads to an increased load applied to the passive structures of the knee joint during landing [34, 35]. For example, this kinematic alteration, through the elevation of anterior shear forces, may increase the risk of injury to one of the most critical passive components of the knee, namely the anterior cruciate ligament [37].

The various studies examining the landing after a block in volleyball have reported a wide range of vertical ground reaction force (VGRF) values based on the movement task, jump height, and players’ weight [38–40].

Another effect of reduced knee flexion during landing is increase in ground reaction forces, which, in turn, may raise the risk of injury to passive structures, including ACL [41, 42]. Previous studies have shown that the highest vertical ground reaction forces(VGRF) experienced by athletes during landing occur within a knee flexion range of 0 to 25 degrees, indicating an inverse relationship between decreased knee flexion and increased VGRF [43]. Based on the previous findings, the reduction in hip and knee flexion observed in the present study may lead to an increase in vertical ground reaction forces during landing.

Active trunk flexion in the sagittal plane can be addressed as one of the factors enhancing knee and hip flexion during landing among athletes [44]. Regarding the volleyball players, this is not optimally possible due to their presence near the net and single-leg landing, as well as holding their hands above the head during block [38, 45], and it is possible in combination with fatigue, and unpredictable conditions, they may experience a harder landing and be at a greater risk of knee injuries. However, the results of the present study are not in line with those of some research that revealed higher or fixed flexion following fatigue [17, 46]. It seems that the conditions of these studies were inadequate for altering the nature of lower limb kinematics due to the use of local fatigue protocols, which mainly target specific muscles. Further, some researchers believed in the ability of the larger muscles of the lower limb to compensate for the function of the intended muscle in this fatigue model, leading to no biomechanical changes [17].

The limitation in knee joint flexion is associated with greater valgus torque in the frontal plane, which is known as a poor movement strategy that may lead to an elevated risk of injury to the passive structures of the knee joint [47, 48]. In the present study, although the observed increase in knee valgus following the fatigue protocol did not reach statistical significance, this trend may indicate alterations in neuromuscular control as a result of fatigue, aligning with the findings of Fidai et al., who reported an increase in valgus among adolescent athletes [15].

It is noteworthy that one possible reason for the consistency between the current study and the findings of Fidai et al. could be the potential effects of maturation, considering the age group under study, as maturation may, in combination with fatigue, impair lower limb postural control during landing and alter knee joint biomechanics [15]. Indeed, previous studies have reported that adolescent players experiencing more than 20% fatigue are more likely to exhibit greater dynamic knee valgus compared to less fatigued athletes [15], thereby reinforcing this hypothesis.

Furthermore, while an increase in knee valgus was observed at initial contact, a slight reduction was noted at peak landing, which may be attributed to increased hip abduction or a compensatory protective strategy adopted by fatigued players to maintain the knee closer to an optimal alignment [49].

On the other hand, our findings are inconsistent with certain previous studies, which may be attributed to the different fatigue protocols utilized, as protocols involving repetitive movements predominantly in the sagittal plane may not be adequate for assessing the frontal plane kinematics of the joint [50].

Although the risk factors for injury have been separately analyzed in the previous studies, knee joint injuries occur due to a combination of the various variables. An increase in the internal rotation of the tibia, hip, and knee joints can be mentioned as another important biomechanical factor in knee injuries.

However no significant changes in the direction of internal rotation were observed in the hip and knee joints during initial contact in the present study, at the peak landing, a shift from external hip rotation (-0.05) to internal hip rotation (2.93) was noted, which is consistent with the findings of Mclean et al. [51]. Though this change was not statistically significant, the increase in dynamic knee valgus as a result of increased hip internal rotation or a combination of decreased hip flexion and increased internal rotation may impose compensatory stresses on the knee joint, potentially predisposing passive structures, including the ACL, to injury [52, 53].

However, some researchers have reported contradictory results [37, 54], which may be attributed to the unpredictability of movement direction in the present study, which is much closer to the match conditions than the predicted ones.

On the other hand, before performing the fatigue protocol, an internal rotation of 0.76 was detected in the knee at the landing peak. Nevertheless, it had an external rotation of -0.03 in the post-test, which may be related to an increase in the hip internal rotation. In such conditions, the subjects created more external rotation in the knee to place the lower limb in a position close to the natural alignment when landing [54].

In sports activities accompanied by landing, a distal-to-proximal loading pattern is observed for the lower limb, in which the foot and ankle joints are first loaded, followed by transmitting the applied force to the knee and hip joints [55]. Therefore, the imbalance during landing can affect how forces are transferred from distal to proximal on the knee joint, indicating the important role of the ankle in knee mechanics and possible ACL injuries [49].

The results of the present study represented no significant relationship between ankle kinematics (e.g., pronation or foot external rotation) and knee injury after fatigue. At the initial contact, ankle plantar flexion slightly increased after fatigue, which may lead to more shock absorption in the lower limb and higher energy loss [55, 56]. The previous studies have demonstrated the greater energy loss of the lower limb in soft-landing techniques (plantar flexion, and hip and knee flexion) compared to the hard landings, which potentially decreases the risk of passive structures [57]. Accordingly, the strategy of consciously increasing hip, knee flexion, and ankle plantar flexion during landing should be discussed with volleyball players in order to reduce the risk of knee injuries [55].

Further, a significant increase in ankle inversion was observed following fatigue at initial contact. A higher inversion may elevate the risk of lateral ankle sprain, which is directly related to a rise in plantar flexion. this issue is attributed to greater VGRF around the subtalar joint, as well as the probability of placing most of the center of lateral pressure, especially at the initial contact [58, 59]. Thus, acute fatigue can negatively influence the possible risk factors of lateral ankle sprain and increase the risk of ankle injuries among volleyball players. In terms of this parameter, the results of the present study are in line with those of Verschueren et al. [60] and Herbaut et al. [59]. This kinematic variation may be related to the disturbance in joint position sense change after performing the fatigue protocol.

However, the results are inconsistent with those of Thomas et al. [61], in which no significant increase in inversion was observed after fatigue. This difference can be ascribed to the sports activity level of the players who recreationally were active females since this statistical population cannot provide an accurate insight into the kinematic changes in the foot among high-level athletes.

Furthermore, foot toe-in is associated with higher inversion, knee internal rotation, and hip abduction, an increase which can lead to more risk of knee injuries [62]. The toe-in necessitates foot internal rotation. In the present study, foot internal rotation was enhanced following more inversion at the initial contact after fatigue. However, the increase was not significant enough to cause greater toe-in, higher pressure on the knee, and risk creation. Based on the results of the present study, the lower-limb fatigue in the ankle may not vary the kinematic variables related to the knee injuries as already mentioned.

Conclusion

Regarding the present study, most of the hypotheses were not statistically significant. However, less hip and knee flexion angle at the initial contact and landing peak, as well as more ankle inversion, revealed slight kinematic changes due to fatigue. The occurrence of these movement patterns after fatigue appears to have a limited role in predicting knee injuries; therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes and a control group are required to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of fatigue on knee injuries.
