Development and internal validation of an intrapartum caesarean risk prediction model to guide rural obstetric transfer decisions: a population-based study using BORN Ontario data
Authors: Kheira Jolin-Dahel, Steven Hawken, Liam Bruce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and internally validate a clinical prediction model estimating the probability of emergency caesarean delivery among low-risk pregnancies using routinely available intrapartum variables. The model is intended to support clinicians in recommending transfer decisions in rural obstetric settings without onsite caesarean capacity.DesignRetrospective cohort study with multivariable logistic regression and internal validation using bootstrap resampling.SettingProvince-wide birth registry in Ontario, Canada.ParticipantsSingleton, hospital births from low-risk pregnancies between 1 April 2012 and 31 March 2020, captured in the Better Outcomes Registry and Network Ontario. Exclusions included previous caesarean, major comorbidities and other high-risk conditions.Primary outcomeEmergency caesarean delivery during labour.ResultsAmong 611 644 low-risk pregnancies, 66 482 (10.9%) resulted in emergency caesarean delivery. Key predictors included abnormal fetal health surveillance, nulliparity, gestational hypertension, polyhydramnios area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and oxytocin augmentation. The optimism-corrected AUC was 0.86, indicating strong discrimination. Calibration was generally good, though the model underestimated caesarean risk in patients first admitted to level I hospitals.ConclusionsThis study presents a proof-of-concept prototype with strong discrimination, though calibration of the full model was suboptimal in level I hospitals, the intended setting for application. While not clinically deployable in its current form, the model lays the groundwork for a translational pathway that will require local recalibration, external validation and contextual adaptation before implementation. Threshold-based clinical utility (eg, positive/negative predictive value or net benefit) was not assessed and will be evaluated during external validation and local recalibration.

Introduction

In Canada, most rural and remote communities do not have local intrapartum services, forcing many pregnant individuals to travel long distances for delivery.1 This lack of nearby obstetric care has been linked to higher rates of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes; for example, rural women who live several hours from a maternity unit experience significantly increased odds of perinatal and neonatal mortality and unplanned out-of-hospital birth, as well as greater medical intervention rates (such as labour inductions).2 It also disrupts continuity of care and imposes heightened emotional and financial stress on families.3 Additionally, urgent obstetric transfers often mean that a labouring person must leave behind their family support and cultural birthing practices, contributing to an isolating and challenging perinatal experience.3

Many experts advocate maintaining rural intrapartum services even in facilities without onsite surgical capabilities.4 Studies suggest that, with proper emergency preparedness and transfer protocols, birth outcomes in these settings can be comparable to those of hospitals with immediate caesarean section access.2 5–7 However, the risk of delays in emergency transfers remains a major concern, contributing to stress among providers and hesitancy in offering local intrapartum care.8 9 A clinical decision model that predicts the likelihood of successful vaginal delivery could be valuable for both patients and providers, helping guide transfer decisions and reducing uncertainty in rural obstetric care.

Although numerous obstetric prediction models have been developed, most target specific subpopulations (eg, models for predicting the success of labour induction or VBAC).10 11 The few models available for general obstetric populations often rely on antepartum factors or require measurements that are not easily accessible to rural healthcare providers. To our knowledge, only one existing model developed by Schuit et al uses intrapartum variables.12 However, this model was designed for European tertiary care centres, where patients receive obstetrician-led care, making it less applicable to North American rural and remote settings. Furthermore, it depends on fetal ECG data, which are not readily available in most primary care settings, and it remains paper-based, limiting its practicality in modern clinical workflows.

To address this gap, we developed and internally validated a prototype prediction model intended to inform transfer decision-making for low-risk pregnancies in rural and remote settings. We emphasise that this is a proof-of-concept prototype designed to explore feasibility, not a clinically deployable tool. Its purpose is to establish a foundation for a translational pathway, moving from prototype development to local recalibration, external validation in diverse populations and ultimately, careful implementation within rural clinical workflows. Using data from the Better Outcomes Registry and Network (BORN), the model estimates the probability of emergency caesarean delivery during labour and lays the foundation for future clinical implementation. This large-scale dataset allows the incorporation of key intrapartum variables that may influence birth outcomes, ensuring that the model is both clinically relevant and applicable to real-world settings. All included variables are either known prior to or assessable at the time of presentation in labour. The model is intended to support intrapartum decision-making after admission to a Level I facility, when patients who initially meet low-risk criteria begin to develop signs of complications. At this point, clinicians must decide whether to continue local management or initiate transfer to a higher-level hospital with caesarean capability. By focusing on variables available at the time of labour, the model directly targets the moment of greatest uncertainty for rural providers, balancing the benefits of maintaining local care with the need for timely access to surgical intervention. This distinguishes it from antepartum risk tools that rely on earlier less context-specific predictors

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the BORN Ontario, a province-wide, population-based birth registry that captures >99% of births in Ontario.13 The cohort used for modelling consisted of pregnancies that resulted in a singleton, live birth in a hospital with labour to individuals with low-risk pregnancies and planned vaginal delivery between 1 April 2012 and 31 March 2020. Low-risk pregnancies were defined via the Provincial Council for Maternal and Child Health level I (1 b) criteria.14 Specifically, singleton pregnancies with cephalic presentation were delivered at equal to or greater than 36 weeks gestation to individuals aged 18−39 years (inclusive) with a prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) <40 kg/m², no previous caesarean section deliveries, and none of the conditions or complications identified in table 1.

Data source

Since 2012, the BORN Ontario has collected information on pregnancy, births, and newborns for nearly all births in Ontario, Canada, for the purpose of facilitating and improving care.13 The available data include maternal demographics, health behaviours, pregnancy complications and interventions, and birth outcomes, which are collected from medical records, clinical forms and patient interviews. Gestational age was recorded at birth and used as a proxy for gestational age at admission. In low-risk term populations, these values are usually within hours of each other and thus expected to have negligible impact on prediction. Our dataset captures hospital deliveries only. We were not able to identify individuals who may have planned a home birth but subsequently transferred to hospital during labour.

Measures

Outcomes

The outcome of interest was emergency caesarean delivery, which was defined as an unplanned caesarean section that occurred during labour. The BORN registry includes variables for delivery type (spontaneous vaginal delivery, assisted vaginal delivery and caesarean delivery), labour type (laboured and no labour), and caesarean section type (planned and unplanned caesarean section).

Covariates

Model covariates were chosen on the basis of clinical knowledge and literature,15–17 with a focus on predictive ability. The predictors included in the model were gestational age at the time of birth (weeks+days), maternal age at the time of labour/admission (years), gestational hypertension diagnosed during pregnancy (yes/no), gestational diabetes diagnosed during pregnancy (yes/no), polyhydramnios (yes/no), prelabour rupture of membranes (yes/no), type of labour (induced or spontaneous), amniotomy used to induce labour (yes/no), oxytocin used to induce labour (yes/no), prostaglandin used to induce labour (yes/no), parity, nulliparous (yes/no), gravida, nitrous oxide used for pain management during labour (yes/no), opioids used for pain management during labour (yes/no), epidural or spinal anaesthesia used as pain management during labour (yes/no), atypical or abnormal fetal surveillance (yes/no), meconium (yes/no), fever >38.5°C (yes/no), amniotomy used to augment labour (yes/no), oxytocin used to augment labour (yes/no), smoking at the time of labour/admission (yes/no), and prepregnancy BMI. While induced labour can also be augmented in clinical practice, augmentation methods are captured only in BORN data when labour is spontaneous. In addition to these covariates, the following interaction terms were chosen a priori for inclusion in the model on the basis of clinical reasoning: type of labour and gestational age; nulliparous and maternal age; type of labour and prepregnancy BMI; type of labour and gestational hypertension; and type of labour and nulliparous.

To enhance clinical usability and align with real-world decision-making in early labour, a simplified model was specified by excluding variables that may be inconsistently reported, difficult to obtain at presentation, or reflective of later intrapartum events. These included prepregnancy BMI, smoking status and atypical or abnormal fetal surveillance. This simplification was performed based on clinical and implementation considerations rather than p value-based variable selection, consistent with recommendations for principled model development. The simplified model was then refitted and its performance assessed to determine whether discrimination and calibration remained adequate for potential clinical use.

Statistical analyses

Cohort characteristics were first summarised by mode of delivery (caesarean vs vaginal). Analysis was performed via frequency distributions for categorical variables and medians and IQRs for numerical variables. Missing data were handled with multiple imputation by fully conditional specification via the aregImpute algorithm in the Hmisc package.18–20 More details about the multiple imputation process are included in online supplemental file 1.

For each of the 20 imputed datasets, a multivariate logistic regression model was fitted including all hypothesised covariates, with delivery method (caesarean or vaginal) as the binary outcome. To allow for nonlinearity in certain numerical predictors, gestational age, prepregnancy BMI and interactions involving these variables were modelled with restricted cubic splines (six knots).21 To obtain the final prediction model, the regression coefficients and standard errors from each imputation were combined via Rubin’s rules.22

Predictor effects were estimated using multivariable logistic regression. For spline-modelled variables (gestational age, BMI and related interactions), overall effects were summarised with partial effects plots and 95% confidence bands. The main effect of induced labour was absorbed in the spline intercept and therefore not listed separately in the coefficient table. Predictor importance was quantified using Wald χ² statistics (minus df), with joint contributions reported for variables modelled with nonlinear or interaction terms.

Model discrimination was assessed using optimism-corrected area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), averaged across imputations, using the validate function in the rms package.23 The AUC was obtained by calculating the average of (D+1)/2 across 200 bootstrap test resamples, where D represents Somers’ D in each resample. Optimism was corrected in each imputation by subtracting the average difference in the AUC between the original dataset and each bootstrap resample. Calibration was assessed graphically using bias-corrected calibration plots generated using the calibrate function in the rms package23; the primary plot was produced from the first imputed dataset and compared with others for consistency. The calibration curves were produced by performing lowess smoothing on the observed and predicted probabilities averaged across 200 bootstrap iterations. Probabilities were binned using quantiles—every 5th percentile from the 5th to the 95th was used as representative points for calibration.

Calibration plots were also produced for subgroups in the data to examine heterogeneity between different perinatal and birthing levels of care and different fiscal years of delivery. To account for potential within-patient correlations (in the case of multiple pregnancies for the same person), a sensitivity analysis was conducted by refitting the logistic regression model to the first imputed dataset via generalised estimating equations,24 with person-level identifiers as clusters.

In addition to the full model, a clinically simplified model was developed by excluding variables that are difficult to obtain at presentation or represent later intrapartum findings, as described above. The same modelling, validation and calibration procedures were applied to this simplified model.

Given the intended application of the model in level I hospitals, performance of both the full and simplified models was examined within this subgroup. In addition, to address potential context-specific calibration differences, an exploratory analysis was performed in which the simplified model was refitted using only pregnancies first admitted to level I hospitals and internally validated using the same bootstrap procedures.

The data were analysed in RStudio via R V.4.5.3.25 Multiple imputation was performed via the Hmisc package18 (V.5.2-5), logistic regression modelling was performed via the rms package26 (V.8.1-1) and sensitivity analysis was performed via the geepack package24 (V.1.3.13).

This study corresponds to a type 1b prediction model study under the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD)15 17 classification, indicating model development with internal validation. Internal validation was performed via bootstrap resampling across 20 multiply imputed datasets to assess model performance and correct for optimism. Calibration and discrimination metrics were averaged across imputations to reflect overall model behaviour. External validation is planned in future work.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Cohort description

The study included 611 644 low-risk pregnancies from 1 131 488 total pregnancies in Ontario (2012–2020) after applying exclusion criteria (figure 1). Of these, 89.1% delivered vaginally and 10.9% by caesarean. Baseline characteristics are shown in table 2, with additional details in online supplemental table 1. Compared with vaginal births, caesarean deliveries were more common among nulliparous women (88.5% vs 45.2%), those with higher prepregnancy BMI (median 24.6 vs 23.4) and those whose labours were induced (45.8% vs 24.2%). Caesarean deliveries were also more often associated with epidural analgesia (86.9% vs 64.4%) and abnormal fetal surveillance (59.9% vs 18.0%). Other demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly similar between groups.

The most influential predictors were atypical/abnormal fetal surveillance and parity (figure 2). Model performance

Full model performance

The full model demonstrated strong discrimination, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.86. Calibration was good overall, with the bias-corrected calibration curve (figure 3A) closely aligned with the 45° line. The distribution of predicted probabilities was broad across the population. However, when examined in level I hospitals, the model underestimated the probability of caesarean delivery (figure 3B), suggesting differences in risk patterns compared with the overall cohort. Calibration remained stable across fiscal years (online supplemental figure 2).

Simplified model performance

A clinically simplified model excluding prepregnancy BMI, smoking status and atypical/abnormal fetal surveillance was developed to enhance applicability in early labour. The simplified model maintained good discrimination, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.81 in the overall cohort. Calibration remained good, with bias-corrected calibration curves closely aligned with the ideal line (figure 3C).

To address concerns regarding calibration in the intended clinical setting, an exploratory analysis was performed in which the simplified model was refitted using only pregnancies first admitted to level I hospitals. This level I-specific model demonstrated improved calibration and discrimination within this subgroup, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.83 (figure 3D).

Sensitivity analysis

Because some individuals contributed more than one pregnancy, a sensitivity analysis was performed using generalised estimating equations to account for clustering by patient. Coefficient estimates from this model were nearly identical to those from the primary logistic regression, with all GEE estimates falling within the 95% CIs of the main analysis (online supplemental file 1). This indicates that the independence assumption did not materially affect the results.

Discussion

We developed and internally validated a prototype model to estimate the likelihood of emergency caesarean delivery in low-risk pregnancies using data available during labour. The model was designed to support decision-making in level I rural centres without caesarean capability, where eligibility criteria already exclude patients with significant comorbidities such as high BMI, advanced maternal age or prior uterine surgery. Our exclusions reflect these real-world thresholds. While higher-risk individuals may still present unexpectedly, they fall outside the model’s intended scope, and future work should develop complementary tools for such scenarios.

The model functions as a proof-of-concept prototype rather than a deployable clinical tool. It establishes a foundation for external validation, simplification, and eventual integration into rural workflows. The need for such timely, evidence-based transfer tools has been emphasised repeatedly in the rural obstetric literature.27 28

The model achieved strong predictive accuracy (AUC=0.86) in distinguishing between vaginal and caesarean deliveries. The most influential predictors included atypical or abnormal fetal surveillance, nulliparity, gestational hypertension, oxytocin augmentation and polyhydramnios. These variables are consistent with prior evidence linking intrapartum fetal distress, nulliparity, hypertensive disorders, oxytocin exposure and polyhydramnios to increased caesarean risk.29–3334 Importantly, the model was developed using variables typically available at the time of labour admission, supporting its potential future application as an intrapartum decision-support tool.

Atypical or abnormal fetal surveillance emerged as the strongest individual predictor of caesarean delivery, consistent with prior literature linking abnormal fetal heart rate patterns to operative delivery.30 However, its utility for transfer decision-making may be limited because these findings often arise later in labour, when urgent delivery planning may already be required. In addition, interpretation and documentation of fetal surveillance can vary across providers and clinical settings. For these reasons, fetal surveillance was excluded from the simplified model, along with other variables that may be difficult to obtain early in labour.

The simplified model demonstrated slightly lower overall discrimination (AUC=0.81), an exploratory level I-specific refit demonstrated improved calibration and discrimination within this subgroup (AUC=0.83), suggesting that reliance on earlier, more consistently available clinical variables and context-specific recalibration may improve applicability in time-sensitive rural settings.

Although the full model demonstrated strong overall discrimination (AUC=0.86), it underestimated the risk of caesarean delivery in patients admitted to level I hospitals, the primary intended setting for use. This highlights limitations in generalisability when applying province-wide models to rural contexts. In an exploratory analysis, a simplified model refitted using only pregnancies presenting to level I hospitals demonstrated improved calibration and discrimination within this subgroup, suggesting that local recalibration or context-specific adaptation may improve performance in rural settings. These differences may reflect unmeasured system-level factors, such as variation in transfer thresholds and clinical decision-making across sites.

These findings underscore that the model remains a prototype and is not yet suitable for clinical deployment. External validation and assessment across diverse rural and level I settings will be required before implementation to ensure adequate performance across different clinical contexts.

Strengths

This study leverages a large, population-based registry (BORN Ontario) that captures nearly all hospital births in the province, providing a robust and representative sample for model development. The use of routinely collected clinical variables available at labour onset ensures that the model is grounded in real-world data that could feasibly be incorporated into clinical workflows. Rigorous internal validation using bootstrap resampling strengthens the reliability of the reported estimates, and model development adhered to TRIPOD recommendations for transparent reporting. Importantly, the model addresses a clinically meaningful and underexplored problem: supporting transfer decisions in rural intrapartum care, where timely and accurate risk prediction is critical to balancing patient safety and local access to services.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be emphasised for interpretation and future research.

First, while the model achieved strong discrimination, it was only internally validated. External validation in other populations and geographic settings will be essential before clinical use. Second, calibration was suboptimal in Level I hospitals, the very settings where the tool is intended for use, meaning local recalibration and contextual adjustment are mandatory. Factors such as staffing, availability of surgical backup and time of day/week (which were not captured in BORN) may explain some of this miscalibration. Third, although we selected predictors that are usually known at admission or during labour, gestational age was recorded at birth in the registry and served as a proxy for gestational age at admission. In most cases, this discrepancy is minor, but it limits the precision of ‘real-time’ applicability and should be addressed in prospective datasets. Similarly, abnormal fetal health surveillance and oxytocin augmentation often arise later in labour, which may limit their value as early transfer triggers. Future work should explore earlier or dynamic predictors. Fourth, neonatal outcomes were not included in this analysis. Because transfer decisions aim to optimise both maternal and neonatal safety, future models should incorporate neonatal morbidity and mortality to fully capture clinical utility.

Finally, while exclusions were clinically justified to reflect Level I eligibility, they reduce generalisability to broader obstetric populations, including those with high BMI, prior caesarean or significant comorbidities. Geographic and cultural factors, particularly in Indigenous and remote communities, were also not specifically examined. Moreover, although our cohort was large and population-based, it may not reflect the unique characteristics of patients in remote northern and Indigenous communities. These populations often face different risk profiles, care pathways and cultural contexts. Therefore, external validation and local adaptation will be essential to ensure the model is appropriate and relevant in these settings. We did not assess threshold-based clinical utility (eg, positive/negative predictive value, decision thresholds or net benefit), which will be required to determine bedside usefulness beyond clinician judgement in future validation work.

Together, these limitations highlight that the model is best viewed as a proof-of-concept prototype requiring refinement, validation and adaptation before integration into clinical workflows.

Future directions

Future research should focus on external validation of this model in diverse geographic and clinical settings, particularly in Indigenous and remote communities where access to obstetric care is limited.

Conclusions

This internally validated prediction model may inform future efforts to improve decision-making in rural obstetric settings. Further research is needed to assess external validity, clinical integration and impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes.
