Flower development and morphoanatomy of Dendrophthora costaricensis Urb. (Viscaceae) and its implications in the taxonomy of Phoradendreae
Authors: Alejandro V Yañez-Macias, Emmanuel Martínez-Ambriz, Sonia Vázquez-Santana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDendrophthora is a genus of stem-parasitic plants that are commonly known as mistletoes. It exhibits a notable morphological similarity to Phoradendron, which makes differentiation difficult. Traditionally, the two genera are grouped within the tribe Phoradendreae. Molecular analyses have called into question the monophyly of each genus but confirm the monophyly of Phoradendreae. The unique distinction between genera is the number of locules in the anther. However, this feature introduces uncertainties when defining the two genera. Therefore, this study examines the development and floral morphoanatomy of Dendrophthora costaricensis to identify characters that distinguish it from Phoradendron. Standard histological and scanning electron microscopy techniques were employed. Staminate flowers of D. costaricensis have monosporangiate anthers throughout development, while the pistillode develops structures resembling a style and stigmodium. In pistillate flowers, the carpels are arranged in a ring with an internal placenta before ovule formation; there is no vestigial androecium. As the carpels close, a sessile stigma and two ategmic ovules develop, aligned in the dorsal-ventral plane of the flower. For the first time, a nectary has been observed in the gynoecium and pistillode of Dendrophthora. Anther wall development is monocotyledonous and basic, with dehiscence through an apical pore. We describe the development of pollen and unusual embryo sacs. While similarities exist in the development and floral morphoanatomy of the two genera, the presence of monosporangiate anthers and an Allium type of embryo sac development are distinctive features of Dendrophthora. Nonetheless, more research on floral development in Phoradendreae is needed for accurate comparisons.Keywords: Mistletoe, Dendrophthora costaricensis, Development, Ategmic ovules, Placental-Nucellar Complex, Ectopic embryo sac

Introduction

Parasitic plants are a specialized group of angiosperms that obtain essential resources from other plants through a specialized organ called the haustorium (Těšitel 2016). They are found across 292 genera, comprising 4,750 species, collectively accounting for 1.6% of all angiosperm species (Nickrent 2020). The classification of parasitic plants has been complex due to their diverse origins within angiosperms, resulting in various classification schemes over time. Traditional classifications typically categorize them based on their photosynthetic ability as hemiparasites (partial photosynthesis) versus holoparasites (no photosynthesis), their reliance on the host as facultative (partial dependence) versus obligate, and/or their site of attachment as root versus stem parasites (Heide-Jørgensen 2013). Teixeira-Costa and Davis (2021) proposed a new classification of parasitic plants based on the life cycle, identifying five functional groups: euphytoid parasites, parasitic vines, obligate root parasites, endoparasites, and mistletoes. Mistletoes are stem parasites that belong to the order Santalales in the families Loranthaceae, Misodendraceae, Santalaceae s. str., Amphorogynaceae and Viscaceae (Mathiasen et al. 2008; Vidal-Russell and Nickrent 2008; Nickrent et al. 2010). The key features of this functional group include aerial seed germination, development on host stems, and a terminal haustorium (Teixeira-Costa and Davis 2021). With approximately 1,580 species, mistletoes are distributed across many habitats worldwide. Loranthaceae and Viscaceae are the groups that contain the most mistletoe species (Stevens 2001; Cruzado-Lima 2013).

The family Viscaceae contains seven genera: Dendrophthora, Phoradendron, Arceuthobium, Viscum, Korthalsella, and Ginalloa (Kuijt and Hansen 2015). Engler (1894) grouped Dendrophthora and Phoradendron within the tribe Phoradendreae due to morphological similarity, especially in their inflorescence type (Kuijt 1961, 2003; Nickrent et al. 2019). Distinguishing morphologically between Dendrophthora and Phoradendron is difficult (for more details, see Kuijt 1959, 1996; Ashworth 2000a; Dettke and Caires 2021). The only difference between the two genera is the number of anther locules: one in Dendrophthora and two in Phoradendron (Kuijt 1961, 2003; Dettke and Caires 2021).

The genus Dendrophthora contains 129 species, which mainly parasitize dicotyledons (Martínez-Quesada 2022). This genus is distributed from southern Mexico to Bolivia and throughout the Caribbean (Kuijt and Hansen 2015). Dendrophthora species are characterized by being small, monoecious or dioecious shrubs, with olive-green or yellowish colors, and are generally glabrous. The vegetative characteristics of the genus include simple leaves, which are either sessile or have a very short petiole, often somewhat succulent, and in many species, reduced to scaly leaves on part or all the plant. Many species have one or more pairs of cataphylls at the base of lateral branches and/or inflorescences. The phyllotaxy is generally opposite and decussate. The stems are terete and usually articulated, though in some species, they may be winged, furrowed, or significantly compressed. There is a terminal haustorium present, which does not expand laterally through the endophytic system and does not produce emerging buds from that system (Kuijt 1961).

Among the reproductive characteristics of Dendrophthora, solitary inflorescences occur on the axils of lateral branches and/or leaves, or in groups forming compound inflorescences; in a few species, terminal inflorescences are present. The inflorescence is an indeterminate spike, with one or more sterile internodes acting as a peduncle and one or more internodes (named as article according to Suaza-Gaviria et al. 2017) bearing partially sunken flowers along their axes. The flowers are arranged in regions of the inflorescence called floral areas, generally two per article. These floral areas exhibit different flower arrangement patterns, including uniseriate, biseriate, triseriate, and multiseriate (Fig. 2 in Nickrent et al. 2019). The flowers are sessile, diclinous, and monochlamydeous, with three (four) deltoid petals. In staminate flowers, the anthers are sessile and adnate to the petals, and the style is rudimentary. In pistillate flowers, a semi-inferior ovary is present, without anthers or anther rudiments. The fruit is typically a white or yellowish berry with persistent petals. The seed contains a green endosperm, abundant viscin, and a dicotyledonous embryo (Kuijt 1961; Kuijt and Hansen 2015).

The challenge of using morphology to determine inter- and intrageneric relationships within Phoradendreae led Ashworth (2000a, 2000b, 2017) to use molecular markers to assess these relationships. Upon analyzing regions of nuclear ribosomal DNA and chloroplast DNA, Ashworth (2000a, 2017 respectively) found that Phoradendreae is monophyletic. However, Phoradendron is paraphyletic, with Dendrophthora nested within it. In addition, there are doubts about the monophyly of Dendrophthora. Despite the taxonomic implications of Ashworth’s work, a nomenclatural change in Phoradendreae has not been a feasible proposal. This is because limited sampling of species and the difficulty of correctly differentiating and identifying species in these genera have led to considerable uncertainty (Kuijt 2013; Ashworth 2017). In contrast, studies of floral development have proven to be complementary to molecular data for understanding relationships among organisms, as they can identify new characters (Wanntorp and Ronse De Craene 2009). Furthermore, the study of anatomical and other characteristics must be considered before proposing a taxonomic restructuring in Phoradendreae (Ashworth 2000a).

Although morphological and molecular analyses have been performed in Phoradendreae, the study of floral characters remains incomplete. There is considerably more information in the literature on Phoradendron, including the embryology (Billings 1932, 1933) and the morphoanatomy of flowers and fruits (Gómez-Sánchez et al. 2011; Polli et al. 2016; Yañez-Macias 2023), while information on Dendrophthora is much more limited. The work by York (1913a, b) is the only reference on the development and morphology of pistillate flowers, the embryo sac, and the fruit of Dendrophthora. The development and anatomy of staminate flowers have not been studied in Dendrophthora. Another notable example is the work of Bhandari and Vohra (1983), which primarily considers the characteristics of Phoradendron for describing the embryology of Phoradendreae. Additionally, some aspects of embryology in Phoradendron (Billings 1933) and Dendrophthora (York 1913a, b) appear to be questionable and require further investigation (Maheshwari 1937, 1948). For example, modifications in the ovules and stamens structure in Dendrophthora compared to other angiosperms complicate its embryological study (Maheshwari 1937). In addition, researchers have been more attracted to the embryological study of pistillate flowers due to their unusual characteristics. Meanwhile, no detailed study of the androecium has yet been conducted, which would resolve some ambiguities in the anther morphology. In particular, the number of microsporangia for anther (one or two) and their dehiscence type (transversal or poricidal) are described inconsistently between authors and without evidence to support their descriptions (Eichler 1868; Kuijt 1987; Endress and Stumpf 1990). This lack of reproductive information for Dendrophthora, especially in the staminate flowers, limits comparisons with Phoradendron and complicates the taxonomic delimitation of the two genera. To complete the missing information mentioned above, this paper offers a detailed description of the flowers of Dendrophthora costaricensis, focusing on the development and morphoanatomy of staminate and pistillate flowers. We compare and compile this floral information with that of other species of Dendrophthora and Phoradendron.

Materials and methods

Collection of inflorescences

To locate Dendrophthora costaricensis in Mexico, we visited the following herbaria: CHIP, HEM, CH, MEXU, IZTA, and XAL. We also reviewed specialized literature and websites, including JSTOR, Tropicos, World Flora Online, and Naturalist to identify localities and coordinates.

Inflorescences with flowers at different developmental stages of D. costaricensis were collected in Xico, Veracruz, Mexico, in October 2023. We photographed D. costaricensis at the collection sites, and herbarium specimens were prepared and deposited in XAL and MEXU herbaria. Inflorescences at various stages were collected and fixed in FAA (10:50:5:35, formaldehyde: ethanol (95%): acetic acid: distilled water).

Flower processing for histology and micromorphology analysis

After fixation in FAA, part of the material was dehydrated in 70% ethanol for observation and photography of inflorescence and flower morphology using an EZ4 HD Stereo Microscope with an integrated 3.0-megapixel CMOS camera. For histological sections, the material was dehydrated in a graded ethanol series under a vacuum chamber and embedded in Paraplast (Sigma-Aldrich). It was then cut into longitudinal and transverse Sects. 10–12 μm thick with an American Optical 820 rotary microtome. Histological sections were stained with safranin-fast green. Additional samples were embedded in medium-grade LR-White (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Fort Washington, PA, USA), cut into 1–3 μm thick cross-sections with a JMT-MT-990 ultramicrotome, and stained with 1% toluidine blue (Márquez-Guzmán et al. 2016). Histological sections were photographed using an Olympus Provis AX70 optical microscope equipped with an Evolution MP5.1 digital camera.

To observe the callose wall of the microspores during microsporogenesis, we applied 1% decolorized aniline blue to histological sections of the anthers and examined them under an Olympus Provis AX750 fluorescence microscope. To detect starch, Lugol’s solution was applied to histological sections of anthers and PNC, and photomicrographs were captured with an Olympus Provis AX70 optical microscope equipped with an Evolution MP5.1 digital camera. Finally, images were edited using Adobe Photoshop CS6 software.

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), samples were dehydrated in increasing ethanol concentrations up to 100%, critical point-dried in a desiccator (CPD-030 Bal-Tec), mounted on metal sample holders, and coated with gold using an ion sputter (Denton Vacuum Desk-II). Electron micrographs were captured with a JEOL JSM-5310LV scanning electron microscope.

Results

Inflorescence and flower traits of D. costaricensis

Dendrophthora costaricensis is a small shrub that parasitizes the stems of a Miconia species (Melastomataceae) (Fig. 1a). The inflorescences usually develop singly in leaf axils. However, they can appear in groups of three (triplets). They are green, as are the flowers (Fig. 1b). The inflorescences (15.03 ± 2.32 mm long, n = 40) consist of a peduncle (2.55 ± 0.44 mm long, n = 40) and two articles, each with a pair of bracts fused at the base: (1) the basal article (7.79 ± 1.34 mm long, n = 40), bears distal staminate flowers and proximal pistillate flowers; and (2) the apical article (4.69 ± 1.15 mm long, n = 40), is shorter than the basal article and contains only pistillate flowers (Fig. 1c). These articles only have two flower zones (Fig. 1c-d).

The pattern of flower arrangement varies. In the basal article, a triseriate (Fig. 1c) and biseriate (Fig. 1e) pattern occurs, sometimes together in the same article. In the apical article, a uniseriate pattern is observed when only two or three flowers are present (Fig. 1c-e), but it becomes irregular with an increasing number of flowers. During fruit development, it is difficult to identify a clear seriation pattern in both articles, which is described as irregular (Fig. 1f). In the basal article, each flower zone contains nine to 12 staminate flowers and six pistillate flowers, while in the apical article, each zone has four to five flowers. Staminate flowers have a perianth with three to four petals, sessile anthers adnate to the petals, and a pistillode in the center (Fig. 1g-h). Pistillate flowers in both articles have a perianth with three to four petals, a semi-inferior ovary, and a sessile stigma, with no parts of the androecium remaining (Fig. 1g, i).

Development of staminate flowers

The staminate flowers of D. costaricensis are only located at the basal article of the inflorescence, along which there are flowers at different stages of development (Fig. 2a). A single whorl of the perianth is considered a corolla (see below). Flowers begin their development at the base of the inflorescence, above the bract axil (Fig. 2b-c). The inflorescence articles increase in size through intercalary growth at their bases, causing flowers to mature farther from the initial floral primordia, which are positioned more proximally within the inflorescence (Fig. 2a). The floral primordium is flattened and slightly radial; it is partially embedded in the inflorescence (Fig. 2b-c). Development of the sporangiate whorls is observed after the formation of three (or four) petals and the closure of the floral apex by these petals (Fig. 2c-d). At this stage, petals exhibit valvate aestivation (Fig. 2b, i). Three anther primordia arise simultaneously, opposite the petals, at the periphery of the floral apex and close to the point where the petals join the receptacle. Simultaneously, the center of the floral apex differentiates into the pistillode primordium, closely associated with the anther primordia (Fig. 2d-e).

The development of the pistillode and anthers continues until the pistillode develops structures similar to the style (rudimentary ovary) and stigmodium, while the anthers reach the microspore mother cell stage. At the same time, nectarostomata differentiation occurs in the receptacle and stigmodium. At this point, the petals have already developed a clear cuticle on the abaxial epidermis (Fig. 2f). From this stage until flower anthesis, all floral organs remain in close proximity (Fig. 2g). When the anthers are removed, the receptacle can be seen to develop lobes due to pressure from the anthers (Fig. 2h). Externally, changes minor and barely perceptible, except for their size (Fig. 2i). A notable change occurs in the abaxial epidermis of the petals, whose cells bulge, and their anticlinal walls become sinuous (see comparison of the flower buds in Fig. 2b and i). The space between the anticlinal walls suggests the presence of a thick cuticle (Fig. 2i). Additionally, the cells of the abaxial epidermis are smaller towards the short pedicel. The pedicel contains three vascular bundles, each supplying a petal (Fig. 2j). At flower anthesis, the petal tips curve, and the anthers collapse after releasing pollen, exposing the receptacle, which functions as a nectary (see below, Fig. 2k). The pistillode does not widen further during development, nor are ovules observed; only the elongation and widening of its apex occur (Figs. 2f and 3b).

Androecium and development of the anther wall

The androecium of D. costaricensis consists of stamens with sessile anthers, adnate to the adaxial side of petals. Therefore, in this and subsequent sections, we will refer only to the organs that comprise the androecium, specifically the anthers. It is also important to note that, because of the kidney-shaped anthers, the layers forming the anther wall can be seen overlapping in cross-sections. As a result, the description of the anther wall formation was conducted using both cross and longitudinal sections to provide a more detailed explanation. In the earliest stages of the anther, the epidermis and a group of subepidermal cells with dense cytoplasm, corresponding to archesporial cells, can be observed (Fig. 3a-b). The archesporial cells then divide periclinally, forming the primary parietal layer and the primary sporogenous cells (Fig. 3b). The primary sporogenous cells undergo mitosis to produce secondary sporogenous cells, while the primary parietal cells divide periclinally to form secondary parietal layers (outer and inner) (Fig. 3c-d). At this stage, a single microsporangium is present in the anther. In the anther wall free to the petal and at the anther apex, the tapetum and middle layers form by the periclinal division of cells in the inner parietal layer, and the endothecium is differentiated from the outer parietal layer (Fig. 3e).

In the lateral sections of the anther, there is a periclinal division of both secondary parietal layers. However, this occurs only in some sections and is not constant throughout the anther wall (Fig. 3e-f). It is important to note that although in some cases a five-layered anther wall appears, this results from the curvature of the anther and not from the aforementioned divisions (Fig. 3g). In regions of the anther far from the base of the pistil, the anther wall consistently has only four layers (Fig. 3g-h). Furthermore, the region of the anther adnate to the petal also differentiates into a middle layer and tapetum (Fig. 3g, i). This area adjacent to the petals can be clearly distinguished from the petal tissue during the formation of unicellular pollen grains (Fig. 4c). After the formation of the anther layers, no additional divisions are observed during microsporogenesis and microgametogenesis, so the anther wall is composed of the epidermis, endothecium, one or two middle layers, and tapetum. Longitudinal sections show that the anther wall retains all four layers at its apex, although the endothecium layer has smaller cells than those in the lateral zones (Fig. 3j-k, o). This is likely because it is situated in the region of anther dehiscence.

The tapetum is of the secretory type; during microsporogenesis, it exhibits dense cytoplasm and uninucleate cells (Fig. 3h, k-l, n-o). During pollen formation, the tapetum accumulates starch granules and begins to degrade (Fig. 4a-e). At the time of anther dehiscence, starch is still detectable as a remnant of the tapetum (Fig. 4f-g). The middle layer(s) collapses after the formation of the tetrads (Fig. 3o). The endothecium in the apical part of the anther collapses similar to the middle layer (Fig. 4a). At the same time, in the rest of the wall, the cells of the endothecium increase in size, becoming large that in the other layers (Fig. 4c). Before anther dehiscence, the endothecium develops fibrous bands (Fig. 4f). The anther dehiscence zone is located at its apex (Fig. 4g-h). In the region adnate to the petal, the layer of cells behind the middle layer develops fibrous thickenings such as those of the endothecium (Fig. 4f-g). The epidermis persists throughout development; however, during anther dehiscence, it collapses (Fig. 4f-h). Each dehiscent anther has a single locule (Fig. 5a). The presence of a single locule is due to the anthers having a single microsporangium.

Microsporogenesis and microgametogenesis

Microsporogenesis begins once the four (or five) layers of the anther wall have formed, a stage at which the formation of microspore mother cells occurs (Fig. 3g-i). The microspore cells undergo meiosis I, which occurs without wall formation, resulting in the formation of a binucleate cell (Fig. 3j-l). Each binucleate cell (dyad) formed is isolated from the others by the formation of a callose wall (Fig. 3m). Cytokinesis occurs simultaneously after meiosis II, forming tetrahedral tetrads (rarely isobilateral), with the wall developing between the microspores (Fig. 3n, p). Microgametogenesis begins after the microspores are released from the tetrad, and the tapetum starts to degrade (Fig. 4a-b). Before mitosis, most pollen grains lack starch granules in their cytoplasm, except for those near the tapetum (boxes in Fig. 4a-b). Before the formation of fibrous thickenings in the endothecium, mitosis occurs in the pollen grain, producing a generative cell and a vegetative cell. The generative cell is distinguished by its attachment to the pollen wall (Fig. 4e). Once the thickenings in the endothecium are observed, starch granules appear in all pollen grains (box in Fig. 4f). At the time of anther dehiscence, the pollen grains are bicellular (box in Fig. 4g). The pollen grains are tricolporate, operculate. The shape of pollen is rounded triangular, with a rugulate exine and a margo present in the colpi (Fig. 4i-j).

Micromorphology and anatomy of staminate flowers

The staminate flower morphoanatomy was described using the largest buds observed, as anther dehiscence occurred before flower opening (Fig. 5a-b). The anthers release pollen through an irregular apical pore (Figs. 2k and 5a-b). The staminate flower is characterized by a perianth composed of three (or four) petals, sessile anthers adnate to the petals, and the pistillode, which functions as a nectary (Figs. 2k and 5a). Longitudinal sections reveal the insertion of the petals into the receptacle, with the pistillode positioned above it (Fig. 5b). The petals and pistillode contain idioblasts with druses, visible under polarized light (Fig. 5c). Cross sections show the close proximity of all floral organs, as well as the different layers of petal parenchyma distinguished by staining differences (Fig. 5d-f). At anthesis, the pistillode and the empty anthers are collapsed (Fig. 5g). The dehiscent anther is bowl-shaped (Figs. 2k and 5a and h).

The base of the petals, at the level of the anthers, is broader than the apices. The petal base contains five layers of parenchyma cells, while the tips only have three layers (Fig. 5b). The petals consist of an abaxial epidermis with bulging cells and a thick cuticle. In contrast, the adaxial epidermis has smaller cells and a less prominent cuticle (Fig. 5e-f). The petal parenchyma is divided into two regions: (1) the subepidermal area beneath the abaxial epidermis, consisting of two layers of vacuolated cells, and (2) the subepidermal area beneath the adaxial side, which contains three layers of smaller cells with dense cytoplasm. The innermost layer of this region has idioblasts with druses (Fig. 5f). In the adnation region between the anther and petal, it is more difficult to distinguish clearly defined layers (Fig. 5e). Similarly, near the petal apex, cells in both areas are of similar size and shape. However, vacuolated cells can still be distinguished from those with denser cytoplasm (Fig. 5b). Each petal has a vascular bundle that may branch toward its apex. The petals share the vascular bundle with the anthers (Fig. 3e). A notable feature is the presence of one sclereid per petal, positioned centrally and aligned with the branching of the vascular bundle (Fig. 5g).

The pistillode is arranged centrally in a slightly trilobed receptacle. The pistillode consists of two parts: (1) a rudimentary elongated ovary that lacks a locule or ovules; and (2) a slightly swollen tip resembling a stigmodium (Figs. 2k, 5b and h and 6b). The pistillode lacks vascular bundles. The receptacle has three lobes that alternate with the anthers (Figs. 2h and k and 5h). The receptacle consists of a single-layered epidermis with nectarostomata (see below; Fig. 5i), and has parenchymal tissue composed of isodiametric cells, some of which contain idioblasts with druses (Fig. 5j). The nectarostomata have swollen subsidiary cells that partially cover the ostiole cells and have a reduced substomatal chamber (Fig. 5i-j). The rudimentary ovary is triangular in cross-section because of pressure from the three anthers. This structure is composed of a single-layered epidermis and parenchymal tissue, with some idioblasts containing druses (Fig. 5k-l). The stigmodium shares traits with the rest of the pistillode, although its epidermal cells are smaller. A notable feature is the presence of three nectarostomata on the stigmodium, usually arranged alternately with the anthers. These nectarostomata are paracytic, arranged transversely, and have swollen subsidiary cells that cover the ostiole cells (Fig. 5l-m). Due to the nectar remnants observed on the receptacle (Fig. 5i) and the stigmodium (Fig. 5m), we consider that the receptacle and stigmodium act as a nectary, with nectarostomata as secretory structures. The staminate flower at anthesis is polysymmetrical (= actinomorphic), with at least three planes of symmetry.

Development of pistillate flowers

Pistillate flowers develop at both the basal and apical parts of the inflorescence. At the basal part, they form after the staminate flowers and are located closer to them (Fig. 6a-b). The pistillate flowers go through the same developmental stages as staminate flowers before the formation of the sporangiate whorls. The floral primordium is flattened and partly embedded in the inflorescence tissue (Fig. 6c-d). Next, three or four petals emerge from the edges of the floral meristem as projections that grow toward the center of the bud (Fig. 6d). No stamen formation or remnants are visible at any developmental stage. During gynoecium development, a free central placenta and carpels grow around the placenta as an annular primordium (Fig. 6e). The annular primordium of the gynoecium lacks apices that would indicate the number of carpels it contains (Fig. 6e-f). The carpels continue to develop until they completely cover the placenta (Fig. 6f-h). As the annular primordium of the gynoecium begins to close, two ovules (each one with reduced nucellus) develop at the base of the ovary locule, one on each side of the placenta, in the dorsoventral plane of the flower (Fig. 6h). At this stage, the flower clearly lacks a pedicel, being sessile on the inflorescence axis (Fig. 6h). With the closure of the carpels and the formation of the ovary, a sessile, slightly bilobed stigma begins to form, and the ovary takes on a semi-inferior position due to the expansion of the receptacle (Fig. 6i-j). Stomatal differentiation occurs in the ovary near the petals, suggesting this part of the ovary functions as a nectary (Fig. 6i). Stigma formation occurs alongside megasporogenesis, specifically during the embryo sac elongation stage (Fig. 6j). As the gynoecium closes, the inner margins of the annular primordium form the transmitting tissue, which extends from the stigma to the base of the ovary, surrounding the placenta and ovules (Fig. 6j). When the petals open at anthesis, they expose the stigma and nectary (see below), with nectarostomata arranged in a ring around the stigma (Fig. 6k).

Gynoecium and development of the Placental-Nucellar Complex (PNC)

The development of ovules is unique among angiosperms (see below) and is therefore discussed in its own section here. Before ovule formation, the placenta is located at the base of the unilocular ovary (Fig. 6f). As the gynoecium closes, an ovule develops on each side of the placenta, one per carpel, positioned in the flower’s dorsoventral plane (Fig. 7a). Each ovule consists of a nucellus made up of a few parenchyma cells, surrounded by a nucellar epidermis; it is ategmic (lacking integuments) and is in close contact with the inner ovary epidermis (Fig. 7a-c). Due to the unique structure of the ovules and placenta, this is called the Placental-Nucellar Complex (PNC, as defined by Ross 2002 for Arceuthobium). Cross sections of the PNC show an oval shape (Fig. 7d), while longitudinal sections reveal a cordate form, with the broadest part representing the connection to the placenta, with the ovary (Fig. 7a-c, h). The structure of the PNC remains unchanged during megasporogenesis, only increasing in size along the dorsoventral plane of the flower (Fig. 7). During megagametogenesis, the nucellar cells near the embryo sacs collapse, until it becomes difficult to distinguish the PNC from the rest of the ovary tissue (Fig. 8).

Megasporogenesis and megagametogenesis

Within the PNC, two archesporial cells differentiate, one in each nucellus, positioned hypodermically (Fig. 7a). Each archesporial cell develops directly into the megaspore mother cell without forming any parietal cells (Fig. 7b-c). During later development, a delay often occurs in the development of the two embryo sacs (Fig. 7d). After meiosis I and the formation of a cell wall between the chalazal cell (nearest the center of the PNC) and the micropylar cell of the dyad (close to the nucellar epidermis), early degeneration of the micropylar cell is observed before meiosis II (Fig. 7e). Meanwhile, the chalazal cell begins to store starch as a reserve before meiosis II (Fig. 7e-f). With meiosis II, two megaspores form without a cell wall separating them, both containing abundant starch granules (Fig. 7g-h). Both megaspores contribute to the formation of the embryo sac. Immediately after meiosis II, a cross-section shows that the two megaspores are positioned equidistant from the nucellar epidermis (Fig. 7d, g). At this stage, the micropylar cell can still be seen degenerating. Subsequently, longitudinal sections reveal that one nucleus is closer to the nucellar epidermis, while the other is nearer the center of the PNC (Fig. 7h). During megagametogenesis, the two megaspores undergo mitosis, forming an embryo sac with four nuclei (Fig. 8a). Next, the embryo sac extends toward the base of the flower, carrying the two micropylar nuclei (near the nucellar epidermis), while the two chalazal nuclei (near the center of the PNC) stay in the same position (Fig. 8b-c). At this stage, the differentiation of a tissue called the sclereids plate in the receptacle is also observed (see floral morphoanatomy) (Fig. 8b). Both embryo sacs continue to grow toward the receptacle, and just a few layers before reaching the sclereids plate, they change their growth direction and begin to elongate outside the PNC, very close to the inner epidermis of the ovary and through the transmitting tissue (Fig. 8d). During this growth, the embryo sac consumes its starch reserves and acquires a hooked or J shape (relative to the main axis of the flower) at the four-nucleus stage; the embryo sacs are ectopic (Fig. 8d), growing outside the nucellus. The upper part of Figs. 8e-i (cross sections) shows an embryo sac with two chalazal nuclei at the base of the PNC (Fig. 8f), and the presence of two micropylar nuclei in the part that grew into the transmitting tissue, one proximal and one distal to the base of the flower (Fig. 8g, i). Later, the more advanced embryo sac has only five nuclei (Fig. 8d-i). Although the divisions that gave rise to these nuclei could not be observed, the following can be assumed: the two embryo sac nuclei that remain in the PNC divide and give rise to the antipodal cells (possibly three that quickly degenerate) (Fig. 8f), and a polar nucleus that begins to migrate toward the end of the embryo sac outside the PNC (Fig. 8e). In the part of the embryo sac that develops into the transmitting tissue, a polar nucleus (Fig. 8g) and the egg cell (Fig. 8h) are formed by the proximal micropylar nucleus, while the two synergids are formed by the distal micropylar nucleus (Fig. 8i).

Both embryo sacs are hook-shaped and are in close contact with each other within the PNC (Fig. 8j-k). In a perfectly longitudinal section, the two embryo sacs appear almost as a continuous structure, resembling the letter W (Fig. 8l). The five nuclei seen in the previous stage are organized into four cells: two synergids, one egg cell, and a binucleate central cell (Fig. 8m-q). These cells are positioned near each other at the apex of the embryo sac (Fig. 8m-n). It is also clear that the apex of the embryo sac is located at the boundary between the transmitting tissue and the parenchymal tissue of the ovary (Fig. 8o, q).

Micromorphology and anatomy of the pistillate flower

The pistillate flower was described using the largest buds and flowers in anthesis (Fig. 9a-b). From a frontal view, the flower shows three (occasionally four) petals, the nectary at the top of the ovary, and the sessile, bilobed stigma (Fig. 9a). The flower features a semi-inferior ovary, surrounded by receptacle tissue except at the top. The ovary’s internal structure is compact, with a very small locule, and due to the intrusive growth of the embryo sacs into the ovary wall makes it difficult to distinguish the PNC from the inner wall (Fig. 9b). Groups of sclereids are present in the perianth and receptacle. Additionally, a sclereids plate can be seen at the base of the gynoecium, just below the PNC. Idioblasts with druses are distributed throughout the floral tissues (Fig. 9c). The petals are narrow at their tips and become more layered toward the base. The abaxial epidermis is single-layered, with a very thick cuticle on its outer periclinal and anticlinal walls, while the adaxial epidermis, also single-layered, has smaller cells with an inconspicuous cuticle (Fig. 9d-e). The parenchymal tissue of the petal consists of four to six cell layers, thicker toward the base, and each petal contains only one vascular bundle (Fig. 9d-e). In the petals, the cells of the abaxial epidermis are separated by their anticlinal walls, which have sinuous edges (Fig. 9f), whereas the adaxial epidermis has cells without such separation and with polygonal shapes (Fig. 9g).

The stigma is sessile, slightly bilobed, and moist; its secretions may crystallize (Fig. 9g-h). The stigmatic papillae are short and globose, with an inconspicuous cuticle. The transmitting tissue beneath these papillae consists of small, rectangular cells with dense cytoplasm, extending around the PNC (Fig. 9b, h). The outer epidermis of the semi-inferior ovary is unstratified, with a conspicuous cuticle and slightly globose cells (Fig. 9g-h). Nectarostomata are present and arranged in a ring surrounding the stigma. Nectar can be observed on the ovary and its nectarostomata (Fig. 9i). The nectarostomata are paracytic, sometimes positioned above the epidermis level, with globose subsidiary cells that partially enclose the guard cells (Fig. 9i-j). Evidence of the secretory function of this area includes the parenchymal tissue with denser cytoplasm, located nearer to the vascular bundles of the flower (Fig. 9b). The inner epidermis of the ovary is unstratified; however, periclinal and anticlinal divisions are observed (Fig. 7a-b).

At the lower part of the flower, corresponding to the receptacle, the epidermis is single-layered with small, globose cells that decrease in size toward the base of the flower (Fig. 9b, k-l). The parenchymal tissue at this level originates from the receptacle and ovary, which are difficult to distinguish from each other. The outermost region of the parenchyma contains groups of brachysclereids. Toward the center of the flower, the size of the parenchyma cells gradually diminishes. In the center of the cross sections, the transmitting tissue can be identified by small cells with very dense cytoplasm (Fig. 9k). The vascular tissue consists of three vascular bundles originating from the inflorescence that surround the sclereids plate at the flower’s base (Fig. 9m). These bundles branch toward the apex of the flower, with about 8 to 10 visible in some cross sections (Fig. 9k). Only one or two vascular bundles reach the petals (Fig. 9e). The vascular bundles form xylem with thin vessels exhibiting scalariform thickenings and phloem. The vascular tissue is located at the transition from the receptacle to the ovarian parenchyma, although this transition is diffuse (Fig. 9n). Just below the PNC, a tissue composed of cells resembling large, short sclereids with thick and pitted walls can be distinguished, retaining their nuclei and cytoplasm. This sclereids plate is innervated by a vascular bundle (Fig. 9o-p). Above the sclereids plate, the PNC is visible, although the growth of the embryo sacs makes it hard to distinguish from the rest of the ovary (Fig. 9b, q). The PNC and the ovary wall may be distinguished by staining their cuticles (Fig. 9q). Pistillate flowers are monosymmetrical (= zygomorphic). The plane of symmetry is defined by the two ovules and the stigma lobes (the symmetry plane of the gynoecium), aligning with the flower’s dorsoventral plane. The perianth consists of one distal and two proximal petals, or vice versa, relative to the inflorescence axis, though petal arrangement may vary further and can cause the flower to be slightly asymmetrical (Fig. 9a, d).

Discussion

Inflorescences

The inflorescence structure of D. costaricensis examined in this work differs from that reported by Kuijt (1961, 2011). The first noticeable difference is the arrangement of the flowers in both articles. According to Kuijt (1961, 2011), all articles of the inflorescence (from two to four) have staminate flowers at the tip and pistillate flowers toward the base. In contrast, we observed this pattern only in the basal article, with the apical article containing only pistillate flowers. Another difference is the pattern of flower arrangement. Kuijt (2011) describes it as triseriate to irregular, observed only in the basal article, whereas the apical article shows the flowers in uniseriate to irregular arrangement. These differences should be verified in the type specimen and other specimens identified as D. costaricensis to determine whether they are previously unnoticed features in this species.

Flower development

The literature on floral development in Phoradendron and Dendrophthora is limited (York 1913a; Billings 1932, 1933; Suaza-Gaviria et al. 2017). One interesting aspect is that the flowers in Phoradendreae do not develop in the axils of the bracts but at some distance from them. Due to the intercalary growth of the segment, it “shifts”, allowing the development of new floral primordia at its base (York 1913a; Billings 1933; Suaza‐Gaviria et al. 2017). However, our observations coincide with previous reports, as the floral primordia in both genera are similar in shape and in the closure of the floral apex. D. costaricensis has flattened and slightly radial floral primordia as D. avenia (Trel.) Kuijt and P. nervosum Oliv. (Figures 2H-K and 4F-I in Suaza‐Gaviria et al. 2017). Another point to note is the simultaneous formation of the perianth organs in P. nervosum (Fig. 4I in Suaza‐Gaviria et al. 2017). Although the pattern of perianth formation in D. costaricensis was not observed, it is likely to be simultaneous, as this pattern is associated with valvate aestivation (Ronse De Craene 2008).

D. costaricensis presents staminate and pistillate flowers that differentiate during the development of the androecium and gynoecium. The sporangiate whorls in Dendrophthora emerge after the perianth closes (York 1913a), similar to Phoradendron (Billings 1932, 1933). In both genera, the staminate flowers have a functional androecium and a nonfunctional gynoecium, a pistillode, making them type I unisexual flowers (by abortion). Meanwhile, the pistillate flowers only develop the gynoecium, making them type II unisexual flowers (by inception) (Kuijt 2003; Mitchell and Diggle 2005).

The differential development between staminate and pistillate flowers in D. costaricensis appears to be related to their position in the inflorescence, due to the consistency in the flower distribution pattern. Floral development in D. costaricensis is possibly determined by differential hormone concentrations in the inflorescences, as these contribute to differentiation processes in unisexual flowers and to their distribution within the inflorescences (Diggle 2003). In the monoecious Cucumis sativus L., the development of the androecium and gynoecium in the flowers is induced by differences in ethylene concentration in the stems, in their receptors, and the promoting signal induced by these receptors (Perl-Treves and Rajagopalan 2006). High ethylene concentration in the distal part of the stem triggers the formation of the gynoecium and inhibits the development of the androecium in the flower buds, resulting in the differentiation of pistillate flowers. In the proximal zone, low ethylene concentration triggers androecium formation and inhibits gynoecium development, resulting in the differentiation of staminate flowers. Furthermore, the intercalation of pistillate and staminate flowers occurs at the interface of these zones, similar to what is observed in the inflorescence of D. costaricensis. However, this phenomenon of intercalation of floral development in inflorescences has not been explained in C. sativus or in other monoecious species with a similar pattern to that of D. costaricensis (Diggle 2003; Perl-Treves and Rajagopalan 2006). Therefore, studies focused on the effect of hormones on floral development in Phoradendreae could shed light on the mechanisms that influence the diversity of flower distribution in this tribe, which includes both dioecious and monoecious species (Kuijt 1961, 2003).

Regarding the androecium in Dendrophthora, the anther primordia arise after the formation of the perianth and become adnate to it, as observed in the development of the androecium of Phoradendron (Billings 1932) and Arceuthobium (Cohen 1968). As in Phoradendron, no remnants of the androecium are found in mature pistillate flowers (P. brachystachyum (DC.) Oliv., P. carneum Urb., and P. forestierae B.L. Rob. & Greenm.; Gómez-Sánchez et al. 2011). However, there seem to be exceptions where androecium remnants are present in pistillate flowers (P. macrophyllum (Engelm.) Cockerell (= P. flavescens var. macrophyllum) and P. villosum (Nutt.) Nutt. ex Engelm.; Billings 1932).

The development of the gynoecium differs between staminate and pistillate flowers of Dendrophthora. In staminate flowers, the gynoecium develops from a central primordium in the developing flower, without differentiation into carpels and without ovule development. This resembles development in staminate flowers of Phoradendron (Billings 1932) and other mistletoe species like Antidaphne viscoidea Poepp. & Endl. (González et al. 2021) and Arceuthobium (Cohen 1968).

In pistillate flowers, the gynoecium develops from a ring-shaped primordium that surrounds the placenta/floral apex. This is a typical developmental pattern in families with free-central placentation (Endress 2015) and is seen in other members of Viscaceae, such as Korthalsella (Rutishauser 1935) and Arceuthobium (Cohen 1970). However, in Dendrophthora, Phoradendron, and Viscum, the gynoecium has been described as forming from two carpels (York 1913a; Billings 1933; Steindl 1935). The interpretation of these works may be the result of the use of medial sections of the flower buds without considering the three-dimensional structure of the floral organs. In Dendrophthora (York 1913a), the development is described from cross sections of the developing gynoecium, and what is described as carpels may be the apices observed on the dorsal and ventral side of the ring-shaped gynoecium primordium (Fig. 6E of the present study). These apices are not actually carpel apices but result from a structural need of the gynoecium during its closure (Endress 2015). In Phoradendron and Viscum, the interpretation of two carpels forming the gynoecium could have resulted from analysis of longitudinal sections of flower buds (Billings 1933; Steindl 1935) with results similar to those seen in Fig. 6H of this work. However, no studies on flower development in Phoradendron (and Viscum) have used scanning electron microscopy to confirm this assertion.

Floral morpho-anatomy

The perianth organs in Dendrophthora are referred to as petals in this work, based on Wanntorp and Ronse De Craene (2009). However, in the description given by Kuijt (1961) for the genus, they are referred to as sepals. This variation in the terminology of the perianth of Dendrophthora, as in other members of Viscaceae, is because the perianth has morphological and anatomical characteristics of petals (non-overlapping organs, no stomata, one vascular bundle) and sepals (broad base, protection of internal organs, green color) (Table 1 in Ronse De Craene 2008). The combination of these features in the perianth is common in the core eudicots. It is caused by (1) the influence of the androecium on perianth development and (2) the acquisition of protection and attraction functions by the petals (Ronse De Craene 2008; Endress 2010). The previous discussion also applies to the perianth of Phoradendron.

The distribution of vascular bundles differs between the two types of flowers present in D. costaricensis. In staminate flowers of Dendrophthora, the distribution and number of vascular bundles (three) are similar to those described for P. macrophyllum (Kuijt 1959). However, in the latter, the vascular bundles do not bifurcate. In pistillate flowers of D. costaricensis, the presence of three vascular bundles and a sclereids plate are consistent with observations in D. opuntioides (L.) Eichler and D. flageliformis (Lam.) Krug & Urb. (= D. gracile) (York 1913a) and P. pauciflorum Torr. (Kuijt 1959). Although the connection between the vascular bundles and the sclereids plate was not observed in the present work, it was seen during the early stages of fruit development. In this work, we use the term sclereids plate to replace the term tracheid plate used by York 1913a, b; Billings (1933) in Phoradendreae. Likewise, we consider the sclereids plate to be similar to the terminal sclereids present in leaves of some angiosperms. Both the terminal sclereids and the sclereids plate in Phoradendreae are positioned at the end of the vascular bundles, with a highly sclerosed cell wall with pits (Rao and Mody 1961). The presence of terminal sclereids in the gynoecium of Phoradendreae makes sense when assuming the carpels are organs derived from a leaf ground plan (Scutt et al. 2006). Although the function of the sclereids plate in Phoradendreae is unclear, it is proposed to act as a barrier that prevents the embryo sacs from growing beyond this region and as a tissue involved in nutrient flow to the seed during fruit formation (York 1913b; Billings 1933).

The presence of nectarostomata in the receptacle and pistillode of staminate flowers and gynoecium of pistillate flowers of D. costaricensis is a new characteristic for the genus. However, the presence of nectarostomata in the gynoecium of Phoradendron has been reported previously (Yañez-Macias 2023). The nectarostomata in D. costaricensis do not have a preferred orientation at the receptacle (staminate flower) or in the ovary (pistillate flower), but they do show a transverse orientation in the stigmodium of staminate flowers. The transverse orientation of stomata is rare among angiosperms but is common within Santalales, particularly among hemiparasitic species (Rudall 2023). However, the transverse orientation of stomata had only been described in vegetative structures and never in floral organs. Transversely oriented stomata have been associated with xerophytic and hemiparasitic species as a response to the changes in water availability experienced by these plant groups (Rudall 2023). However, in this work, we assume that the receptacle and pistillode of staminate flowers and the upper region of the ovary on pistillate flowers function as nectaries in Dendrophthora, as observed in other genera of Viscaceae (Arceuthobium, Gregor et al. 1974; Korthalsella, Sultan 2014; Viscum, Thomas et al. 2023; and references therein; and Phoradendron, Gómez-Sánchez et al. 2011). The receptacle (in staminate flowers) is slightly trilobed, and there is a ring of nectarostomata on the ovary (in pistillate flowers). These features are shared with Phoradendron (Gómez-Sánchez et al. 2011; Yañez-Macias 2023), which also agrees with the nectary structure described in Santalales (Endress 2010). Furthermore, due to the size of the flower, the supply to the nectary can be provided by the vascular bundles of the petals, without the nectary requiring its own vascular bundles (Bernardello 2007). Another important aspect to consider is the presence of druses in the parenchymal tissue of the nectary and in the surrounding tissue, as their presence has been associated with the sequestration of calcium to avoid the inhibition of plasma membrane ATPases and the sucrose transport coupled to them (Chatt et al. 2021). Likewise, the structure described as a pistillode in Dendrophthora functions as a secretory structure, analogous to the nectar gland in staminate flowers of Phoradendron (Trelease 1916).

Anthers

This study provides the first detailed account of anther development and structure in Dendrophthora. The characteristics of anthers in Dendrophthora had not been previously studied, despite their taxonomic importance and the fact that they are the only feature that has enabled differentiation between genera within Phoradendreae (Kuijt 2003). Nevertheless, Ashworth (2000a) presents a cladogram in which P. piperoides (Kunth) Trel., P. crassifolium (Pohl ex DC.) Eichler, and P. mairaryense Ule (= P. sulfuratum) with bisporangiate anthers are grouped with species of Dendrophthora (including D. costaricensis, with monosporangiate anthers). While this could suggest problems in defining the genera, Kuijt (2003) also points out issues associated with using molecular analyses in the taxonomy of Phoradendron, stating that even future molecular studies may not resolve these challenges.

Meanwhile, embryological characters have proven to be key to delimiting some genera and families (Bhojwani et al. 2015). Within Viscaceae, notable modifications in anther structure are important for taxonomic delimitation between genera (Kuijt et al. 1979). Phoradendron has stamens with a short filament, adnate to the petals, bisporangiate anthers, and poricidal dehiscence (one pore per microsporangium) or longitudinal openings (P. californicum Nutt.) (Billings 1932; Endress and Stumpf 1990). Meanwhile, the stamens of Dendrophthora are described as sessile anthers, adnate to the petals, and unilocular (Eichler 1868). However, there is ambiguity in the characteristics of the androecium in Dendrophthora. For example, the use of the term “locule” in descriptions of Dendrophthora (and several other angiosperm groups) can be confusing, as authors sometimes use this term interchangeably to refer to either the microsporangium or the locules formed during anther dehiscence (Green 1980). In the original description of the genus, Eichler (1868) mentions anthers with two contiguous microsporangia and transverse dehiscence. However, other studies report a single microsporangium (Kuijt 1987) or two microsporangia with dehiscence through pores (Endress and Stumpf 1990). Despite claims about the androecium of Dendrophthora, none of the works provide evidence to support these assertions. In the present study, we demonstrate that Dendrophthora has stamens with sessile, monosporangiate anthers that dehisce by apical pore (Table 1). The presence of a single microsporangium and poricidal dehiscence in Dendrophthora contrasts with the original description of the genus (Eichler 1868).

Monosporangiate anthers in Dendrophthora are noteworthy, as this characteristic had previously only been described and mentioned as a peculiarity of Arceuthobium (Munro et al. 2014). However, the anthers in Dendrophthora are simpler in structure than those in Arceuthobium and the rest of angiosperms. In the work of Ashworth (2000a), the clade formed by P. piperoides and P. crassifolium appears as the sister clade to Dendrophthora, implying that monosporangiate anthers evolved from bisporangiate anthers. Nevertheless, P. mairaryense, a species with bisporangiate anthers, is nested within Dendrophthora. This complicates the explanation of the transition from bi- to monosporangiate anthers in Phoradendreae.

Determining the type of anther wall development was challenging in Dendrophthora. The highly reduced structure and morphology of the anther complicate its study. The anther structure of Dendrophthora matches that reported by Johri et al. (1992) for Viscaceae: epidermis, fibrous endothecium, one or two middle layers, and parietal tapetum (Table 1). At the apex and in the region free to the petal, the wall development matches the monocotyledonous type (according to Davis 1966). In contrast, in some lateral sections of the anther, the basic developmental pattern was observed. This variation in anther wall development in Dendrophthora is related to the shape of the anther itself, leading to differences in the arrangement of cell layers throughout the wall. The presence of more than one type of wall development in the anther has been previously described in other angiosperms (García 2003 and references therein), indicating a certain lability of anther development in some families. In the region adnate to the petal, the tapetum, middle layer, and endothecium are differentiated in the same way, though not derived from the archesporial tissue. The origin of the anther layers in the part adnate to the petal results from the radial field produced by the sporogenous tissue, which induces periclinal divisions in the adjacent cells (Scott et al. 2004). The events that occur during microsporogenesis and microgametogenesis are similar between D. costaricensis and Phoradendron species, with some differences (Table 1). In this study, specific pollen characteristics of D. costaricensis are described, although the palynological description is incomplete. Barth (1972) points out the difference in pollen between Dendrophthora and Phoradendron regarding the structure of the exine (tectate sexine without detachments vs. psilate sexine with detachments). However, only a few species were evaluated (D. elliptica (Gardner) Krug & Urb., P. piperoides, and P. crassifolium), and some characteristics differ from those reported for other Phoradendron species (Kuijt 2003; Gómez-Sánchez et al. 2011) and in the present study (Table 2). This suggests that certain palynological features are variable within Phoradendreae (e.g., exine sculpture) and that additional comparative palynological studies are needed to assess the diversity of these traits in Phoradendreae.

Placental-Nucellar Complex (PNC)

The term Placental-Nucellar complex describes embryological structures in Santalales. Specifically, this term refers to a reduced protuberant, erect, orthotropous, and ategmic ovular structure, with basal or central placentation as observed in Arceuthobium (Ross 2002). However, the above description has some flaws in applying orthotropous and ategmic terms to a structure that is an incomplete ovule, but the fusion of the nucellar tissue of two ovules and the tissues of the placenta. In Balanophoraceae, the term PNC is used more broadly, for species in which the boundaries between the placenta and reduced ovules are blurred (Table 1 in González et al. 2019). In some cases, there is a fusion, to varying degrees, of the PNC with the ovary wall (as Lophopytum; Sato and González 2016).

In this work, we define the PNC as a basal ovary structure in which the distinction between a free central placenta and the reduced ovules (sessile, with reduced nucellar tissue and ategmic) is blurred (Table 3). This definition excludes structures with an ovarian papilla or mamelon has multicellular archesporial tissue, as in Eubranchion (Bhandari and Indira 1969) or Ligaria (Stella and Ferrucci 2025), where the tissue is purely nucellar.

This definition of PNC is based on two trends observed in the Santalales order: (1) reduction and no clear boundaries between ovules and placenta (central free; Stevens 2001) and (2) fusion of the ovules, placenta, and ovarian wall (Sato and González 2016). The above is corroborated by the expression of genes related to ovule development, especially AINTEGUMENTA (ANT) and BELL1 (BEL1), both in ategmic ovules and the ovary tissue that surrounds them in Santalum species and Phoradendron leucarpum (Brown et al. 2010). Furthermore, it can be assumed that the PNC is a transitional structure during development, between a free central placenta with slightly differentiated unitegmic ovules, as in Thesium, and the total fusion of carpels, placenta, and ovules, as in Balanophora (Fig. 9 in Sato and González 2016).

The presence of the PNC in Santalales is poorly documented. The PNC is mentioned in Viscaceae (Simpson 2019) and in some members of Balanophoraceae (González et al. 2019; see references therein; Table 3). However, the presence of PNC in other families of Santalales is obscured by the use of different terms, such as lobed mamelon, placenta stalk, placental column, and funiculus to refer to the placenta and ovules, for example, in families such as Santalaceae s. str. Amphorogynaceae, Loranthaceae, Misodendraceae, and Shoepfiaceae (Smith and Smith 1943; Johri et al. 1992; Kuijt and Hansen 2015; Table 3). These terms describe structures that fit the PNC definition. In our opinion, standardizing these embryological terms is essential to clarifying the evolutionary trends of gynoecia reduction across the order (González et al. 2019).

The development of PNC in Dendrophthora follows the same pattern as observed in Phoradendron: the presence of two sessile ovules on the free central placenta, the absence of integuments, and the reduced ovary locule (York 1913a; Billings 1933). One noteworthy feature is the arrangement of the ovules in Dendrophthora and Phoradendron. In Dendrophthora the ovules are arranged on the dorso-ventral plane of the flower (which coincides with the axis of the inflorescence), while in Phoradendron, they are positioned on the lateral plane (perpendicular to the inflorescence axis). These statements are based on the material we have personally examined. However, Billings (1933) mentions that the ovules of P. villosum, and P. macrophyllum show the same arrangement as observed in Dendrophthora. It is important to examine the arrangement of ovules in other species within Phoradendreae and determine whether this is a distinguishing characteristic between the genera or if it varies widely within the group.

Another aspect to evaluate is the development of the embryo sac in Phoradendreae, which had remained incomplete until this study was conducted (Table 4). On one hand, the development of the embryo sac in D. costaricensis aligns with development in D. opuntioides and D. flagelliformis (York 1913a) in certain aspects: the accumulation of starch during early development, the ectopic growth of embryo sacs outside the PNC tissue, and the contact between embryo sacs in the chalazal region. Despite these similarities, mature embryo sacs in D. opuntioides and D. flagelliformis have two antipodal cells, two polar nuclei, two or three synergids, and one egg cell, which contrasts with the observations in D. costaricensis (Table 4). Based on personal experience, this discrepancy may arise from confusion between the nuclei of the embryo sac and those of the ovary cells, which collapse as the embryo sac develops. The comparison with embryo sac development in Phoradendron is partial (Table 2) because it is unknown whether development is bisporic or tetrasporic (Maheshwari 1955). However, in P. macrophyllum, the development of the embryo sac is described as bisporic-Endymion type, with the mature embryo sac eight-nucleated (Bhandari and Vohra 1983), and the chalazal ends of the embryo sacs never meet (Billings 1933). In contrast, D. costaricensis has an Allium-type embryo sac development, five-nucleated, with chalazal ends in close contact. These characteristics appear to be key in separating Phoradendron and Dendrophthora, although embryological studies in both genera, including a greater number of species, are required to corroborate their taxonomic importance.

The growth of embryo sacs into the ovarian tissue observed in Phoradendreae can be explained by an inter- and intragametophytic “competition”. In the first case, we observe that “competition” between embryo sacs (each originating from a different nucellus) in Phoradendreae resembles the “competition” between embryo sacs in the multi-archesporial ovule of Trimenia (Trimeniaceae; Bachelier and Friedman 2011). In both Phoradendreae and Trimenia, this competition among embryo sacs results in only one embryo sac developing into the seed. In the second case (intragametophytic competition), the “competition” occurs between the products of the two functional megaspores in bisporic embryo sacs. In this case, both megaspores can develop an egg cell and later an embryo (Haig 2020 and references therein). In Phoradendreae, the growth of the embryo sac must serve as a mechanism that favors the products of one megaspore over the other during embryo sac maturation. A key observation supporting this is the absence of nuclei at the chalazal ends of the embryo sacs in all flowers of D. costaricensis, similar to other species with bisporic embryo sac development (Haig 2020 and references therein).

Conclusions

The study of Dendrophthora had been limited to its taxonomy, and the embryological and morphoanatomical characteristics of the flower had not been studied to verify their taxonomic importance. The difference between the genera Dendrophthora and Phoradendron had been limited solely to the number of anther locules. This study allowed us to identify new characteristics in the genus. The presence of a single microsporangium, embryo sac development bisporic Allium type, and the ectopic embryo sacs found at their chalaza ends are features unique to the genus Dendrophthora. For the first time, the presence of nectarostomata in the gynoecium in pistillate flowers, and the receptacle and pistillode of staminate flowers, is described as a new characteristic in Dendrophthora. However, studies of additional Dendrophthora species are required to verify the presence of these characteristics across the genus and confirm their taxonomic relevance. Otherwise, the floral structure and development are similar between the genera, although some aspects need to be (re)investigated in Phoradendron. The orientation of the ovules in the PNC, as well as palynological characteristics, appear to have taxonomic significance within Phoradendreae but require further investigation.
