A hybrid-Hill estimator enabled by heavy-tailed block maxima
Cláudia Neves, Chang Xu

Abstract
When analysing extreme values, two alternative statistical approaches have historically been held in contention: the seminal block maxima method (or annual maxima method, spurred by hydrological applications) and the peaks-over-threshold. Clamoured amongst statisticians as wasteful of potentially informative data, the block maxima method gradually fell into disfavour whilst peaks-over-threshold-based methodologies were ushered to the centre stage of extreme value statistics. This paper proposes a hybrid method which reconciles these two hitherto disconnected approaches. Appealing in its simplicity, our main result introduces a new universal limiting characterisation of extremes that eschews the customary requirement of a sufficiently large block size for the plausible block maxima-fit to an extreme value distribution. We advocate that inference should be drawn solely on larger block maxima, from which practice the mainstream peaks-over-threshold methodology coalesces. The asymptotic properties of the promised hybrid-Hill estimator herald more than its efficiency, but rather that a fully-fledged unified semi-parametric stream of statistics for extreme values is viable. A finite sample simulation study demonstrates that a reduced-bias off-shoot of the hybrid-Hill estimator fares exceptionally well against the incumbent maximum likelihood estimation that relies on a numerical fit to the entire sample of block maxima.



Keywords:

Aggregated data, domain of attraction, extreme value theory, peaks-over-threshold, regular variation theory, semi-parametric estimation.

1
Introduction

Despite there being various approaches by which extreme values can be statistically analysed, these have historically been categorised into two main strands that eventually evolved in separate ways:
methods for maxima over fixed-length intervals and methods for exceedances (or peaks) over a high threshold. The former relates the oldest group of extremal models, arising from the seminal work of Gumbel (1958) as block maxima (BM) models to be fitted to the largest observation collected from each of the large samples (blocks) of identically distributed observations. The peaks-over-threshold’s (POT’s) strand of development seems to have deployed from
Todorovic and Zelenhasic, 1970
. The impacts of adopting either a BM method or the POT approach are still being thrashed out. Few studies have looked at the issue and many that have are aimed at a judicious selection of one approach over the other – a challenge more often than not won by the POT; see
Bücher and Zhou, 2021
– rather than addressing them with a reconciliatory stance. A common problem is that studies fail to ensure that POT is not applying a BM method as well. While the BM method lends itself well to lower-frequency, long-span data, the POT approach is preferable for high-frequency sequences such as daily or hourly observations. This is typically the narrative to discerning between approaches for practical applications. The nuance though is reflected on daily maxima, just to give an example, where there is a level of aggregation in the data and only maximum daily temperatures have been observed. Another example is when the interest is in hourly maximum windspeed or wind gusts. To the best of our knowledge, the only closely related approach for statistical inference in this direction is the work by
Wager, 2014
. But, so too, their relatively elaborate subsampling procedure entails that the resulting extreme values estimation must concede to the provision of a block size
m(n)
going to infinity with the sample size
n
. In our proposed hybrid approach, the large enough block size
m
for the extreme value theorem to hold within each block is a catalyst instead of a requirement. This is found to translate into significant gains to the estimation of extreme value characteristics, especially that of a positive extreme value index. Since our estimators do not depend on the choice of the block size, and indeed are robust to changes in
m
, our findings add significantly to the current body of knowledge.

This paper addresses the estimation of the positive extreme value index
\gamma>0
, regarded as a measure of tail-heaviness, in a principled way that conflates two hitherto separate statistical inference tenets in extreme value statistics, both BM and POT methodologies. We begin with the classical BM method which slices the dataset into non-overlapping blocks and models only the maximum value from each block to the asymptotically prescribed extremal value distribution. Since it discards sub-maximal observations, the BM framework is robust to measurement errors and short-term irregularities, making it well suited to datasets with lower temporal resolution or long historical spans. But while shorter blocks do not fully mitigate seasonal cycles and impair within-block convergence to the limiting extreme value distribution, larger blocks reduce the effective sample size and lead to a wastage of information. A similar criticism applies to de-clustering methods. According to
Coles, 2001
, results can be sensitive to the arbitrary choices made in cluster determination, especially running at a loss of information as a consequence of discarding all data except the cluster maxima, with missed opportunities for modelling within-cluster behaviour since there may be short-range dependence within blocks but not between blocks
(
Katz et al., 2002
)
.

Closely related to Hill’s estimation, it can be deduced from a possible extension to the case
r=0
of Theorem 2.2 in
Ferreira and de Haan, 2015
that the Hill’s statistic is a valid estimator of
\gamma>0
. But if employed using the entire sample of BM, as current practices advise, the asymptotic variance of this estimator results very large with no closed form expression for this variance as a function of
\gamma
. Figure

tally to a sample of size

These preliminary simulation results evidence that it is preferable to have fewer observations within each block and many more BM for minimising the MLE’s mean squared error.

On the other hand, the inadequacy of small block sizes for the extreme value theorem to hold is offset by the added flexibility for setting a larger thershold in the hybrid-Hill estimation, with augmented capacity for accurate inference that actually takes places in the tail region. Eventually, for smaller block sizes nearing a single data-point, the BM method seamlessly defaults to the mainstream POT approach ascribed to
m=1
. The assumptions involved, laid out just next in Section
1.1
, do allow every
m\geq 1

By construction, the hybrid method discards those lower block maxima that might be too small to be considered representative of extremal behaviour and would otherwise hinder a a proper GEV parametric fit. One such applied situation relates the contribution of very dry months to assessing extreme monthly rainfall, whose lowest maxima values would scupper every attempt to find a plausible GEV-fit to the whole sample of BM. Not for nothing, avoiding this pitfall is deemed a necessity, formalised in Condition 3.2 of
Bücher and Segers, 2018
, albeit in a time-series context.

To fix ideas, let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n},\dots
be a sequence of independently and identically distributed random variables (i.i.d.) with the same distribution function (d.f.)

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\rightarrow{\infty}}}P\bigl\{a_{n}^{-1}\max(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq x\bigr\}=\displaystyle{\lim_{n\rightarrow{\infty}}}F^{n}\bigl(a_{n}x\bigr)=G(x),

(1.1)

for every continuity point of

. Then,
G(x)=G_{\gamma}\bigl((x-1)/\gamma\bigr)
and

is the Generalised Extreme Value (GEV) distribution function given by

G_{\gamma}(x)=\exp\bigl\{-(1+\gamma x)^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}\bigr\},

for all

belongs to the domain of attraction of the Fréchet distribution with distribution function
\exp\{-x^{-1/\gamma}\}
,
x\geq 0
, for some extreme value index
\gamma>0
. Let us note that if there is a limiting distribution to the i.i.d. partial sequence of linearly normalised maxima then that distribution in the limit must be max-stable. In particular, if ascribed to
\gamma>0

(

;

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\frac{1}{-t\log F\bigl(a(t)x\bigr)}=x^{1/\gamma}.

(1.2)

We now deviate from the mainstay expansion

applied to the left hand-side of (

) for the establishing the dual extreme value types theorem that stipulates the Generalised Pareto distribution as the limit of POT observables

This paves the way for a characterisation of max-domains that is very similar to that for pot-domains of attraction towards the prescribed Pareto limit, eventually unifying the two. While the domain of attraction condition (
1.2
) appertains the unknown distribution
F
generating the raw data, our identified links between POT and BM conditions will arise from analogous considerations around the tail distribution function of block maxima
1-F^{t}
. Crucially, the proposed Hill estimator of
\gamma>0
stems from this recasting for obtaining the universality class that encloses both BM and POT domains of attraction, analysed one at a time in
Bücher and Zhou, 2021
. This is the key insight to the aimed universal statistical methodology for extreme values: inference conditional on larger block maxima, yet still predicated on the negative-log transform of the initial distribution function
F
as in (
1.2
).

Here and throughout, the block maxima (BM) are represented by the sequence of random variables
\{M_{i}^{(m)}\}_{i=1}^{k}
defined, for
m=1,2,\ldots
and
i=1,\ldots,k

(k\in\mathbb{N})
as

M_{i}^{(m)}:=\mathop{\max}\limits_{(i-1)m<j<im}X_{j}\,,

(1.3)

and constitute an i.i.d. sample taken from the distribution
F^{m}
.
It is worth our while to highlight at this point that the distribution
F^{m}
is in the same max-domain of attraction of the initial distribution function
F
: the two distribution functions are regularly varying in the tail with the index
-1/\gamma<0
and
1-F^{m}
has second-order parameter governing the rate of that convergence matching that of

(see Appendix

Beirlant et al., 2004

;
Reiss and Thomas, 2008
)
.

Through the blocking mechanism (

) for obtaining a sample made up of

upper order statistics of the partial BM sequence. For every block-length

be the order statistics of

\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}\equiv\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}(k_{0},k):=\frac{1}{k_{0}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k_{0}-1}\log M^{(m)}_{k-i:k}-\log M^{(m)}_{k-k_{0}:k}\,.

(1.4)

1.1
Structure of the paper

We shall assume throughout that
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is an i.i.d. sequence whose common distribution function
F
satisfies the primary extreme value condition (
1.2
). Then, by continuity of the limiting Fréchet distribution function and monotonicity of the left hand-side of (
1.1
), the convergence in (
1.2
) is uniform, whereby

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\,\displaystyle{\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}}\,\Bigl|\frac{1}{-t\log F(a(t)x)}-x^{1/\gamma}\Bigr|=0.

(1.5)

Equivalent to (
1.5
) is the next condition formulated in terms of the quantile-type function

V:=\bigl(1/(-\log F)\bigr)^{\leftarrow}=F^{\leftarrow}(e^{-1/t}),

where
←
denotes generalised inverse.

Condition A1.
Assume that: (i)
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\log V(tx)-\log V(t)=\gamma\log x
, for all
x>0
; (ii)
for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
there is a
t_{0}=t_{0}(\varepsilon,\delta)
, such that for
t\geq t_{0}
and
x\geq 1
,

\Bigl|\frac{V(tx)}{V(t)}-x^{{\gamma}}\Bigl|\leq\varepsilon x^{{\gamma}+\delta}.

We then say that
V
is regularly varying with index
\gamma
, i.e., it satisfies

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\frac{V(tx)}{V(t)}=x^{\gamma},

for all
x>0
, and use the notation
V\in RV_{\gamma}
.

Together with the max-stability property of the limiting extreme value distribution
G_{\gamma}
, the uniform relation (
1.5
) brings to light the coincidence between the classes of distributions
F
and
F^{t}
induced by each of their specific max-domains of attraction conditions. What is involved in unifying the two classes stems from the asymptotically equal rescaling by
V(t)\sim a(t)
, as
t\rightarrow\infty

(cf.
Resnick, 1987
, Proposition 2.12, p.108)
.

This paper is organised as follows.
Firstly, we introduce a cursory semi-parametric estimator of
\gamma>0
, whose asymptotic behaviour encompassed
in Section
2
can be read off from Condition A1 in self-evident way. Since the goal is to widen the focus to the tail distribution function underlying the BM picked up from blocks of size
m
, we devise a new set of extreme value conditions which will unveil the Pareto-tail as the proper limit for the regularly varying
1-F^{m}
upon inner normalisation by
V(m(t-1/2))
.

Condition B.
With
V
satisfying Condition A1, assume the following holds for every
m\geq 1
:

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-F^{m}\bigl(xV(m(t-1/2))\bigr)}{1-F^{m}\bigl(V(m(t-1/2))\bigr)}=x^{-1/\gamma},

(1.6)

for all
x>0
.
Since
V
is nondecreasing, there are accompanying uniform bounds in the sense of Proposition B.1.10 of
de Haan and Ferreira, 2006
: for each
\varepsilon,\delta>0
there is
t_{0}=t_{0}(\varepsilon,\delta)
such that for

,
tx\geq t_{0}

\biggl|\frac{1-F^{m}\bigl(xV(m(t-1/2))\bigr)}{1-F^{m}\bigl(V(m(t-1/2))\bigr)}-x^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}\biggl|\leq\varepsilon\max(x^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}-\delta},\,x^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\delta}).

Proposition
7
in Appendix

ascertains that Condition B does provide a universal and complete characterisation of max-domains of attraction, irrespective of

being set fixed or assumed going to infinity with

.

In order to make matters simple and furnish an extreme-value condition that is fit for our purposes, we proceed to the estimation on the basis of Condition B, since the normalisation with

makes it possible for inference to stretch back into the tail region so as to harness intermediate BM values in addition to the extreme. This renders a semi-parametric approach at the intersection of BM and POT methodology in a fruitful and impactful way for applications.
In Section
3
, the hybrid-Hill estimator presented in (
1.4
) is engendered on the empirical counterpart of Condition B. To heap on the evidence that BM and POT approaches are effectively unified, we note that such a development conforms to a wealth of already published work around the asymptotic properties of the original Hill’s estimator

and more widely to large sample properties of semi-parametric estimators specialised in (log-)exceedances above a high threshold.

The speed of convergence in Condition A1, and thus that of (

Condition A2 will prove instrumental to capturing the approximation bias, a deterministic pre-asymptotic bias, that such deviations from the limiting Pareto-tail instil in the H2 estimator. Of relevance in this regard is Lemma

Condition A2
: Suppose there exists a function

ultimately of constant sign and

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\frac{\frac{V(tx)}{V(t)}-x^{\gamma}}{A(t)}=x^{{\gamma}}\frac{x^{\rho}-1}{\rho},

for all

Consequently, Theorem B2.18 of

We then say that the quantile-type function

1.3
N.N.I.I.D. random variables or I.N.N.I.D. random variables

The standard practice of identifying clusters of high-level exceedances with the intention of concentrating on cluster maxima for data analysis is a form of data aggregation that not only is permissible but also seamlessly covered by the hybrid semi-parametric framework. This is one situation under the general umbrella of not necessarily independent identically distributed (N.N.I.I.D.) random variables which exerts further reduction of the block size through the approximate equality

P(M_{i}^{(m)}>x)\approx 1-F^{m\theta}(x),

where
0<\theta\leq 1
is the so-called extremal index. In this case, Condition B-(
1.6
) still holds for every
i=1,2,\ldots,k
.
Furthermore, Condition B remains valid under mild mixing conditions such as Leadbetter’s condition
D
.

The new setting of regular variation for characterising maxima taken from a heavy-tailed distribution, encapsulated in Conditions A1 and B and including their respective second order conditions (details in Appendices
B
and
C
) inherits very many of the existing asymptotic theory for independent not necessarily identically distributed (I.N.N.I.D.) random variables in a plentiful manner. It is a straightforward exercise to assemble Condition B into the theorem of convergence of heteroscedastic extremes in
de Haan, 2015
, from which result a stream of statistical methods for a trend in extremes will emanate
(
de Haan et al., 2015
;
Einmahl et al., 2022
, see e.g.)
.

The now proposed hybrid-estimation methodology encompasses all the preceding settings.
The fact that the distribution function
F^{m\theta}
may still be a plausible approximation to
\prod_{j=1}^{\theta m}F_{j}
, for every
\theta^{-1}\geq 1
, so long as the deviations of
F_{j}
from one another do not place them in different max-domains of attraction, adds significance to the hybrid framework.
Notably, due to the lack of asymptotic theory for non-stationary BM, current practice has been limited to the use of the GEV distribution as an emulator of the random process generating BM and non-stationarity is modelled by allowing its parameters to change with time and other covariates
(
Dupuis and Trapin, 2023
, see e.g.)
. The hybrid semi-parametric estimation fills in this gap in current knowledge, since as a result of fusing BM and POT approaches into a single strand, most of the extreme values modelling techniques that have carried the hallmarks of each approach are now interchangeable.

Theorem 1

3
Hybrid-Hill (H2) estimator based on
k_{0}
tail-related BM

In this section, we investigate the asymptotic properties of the
Hill, 1975
estimator, now cast at the intersection of BM and POT approaches for inference on a positive extreme value index
\gamma>0
. As with the cursory estimator (
2.1
), this configures a semi-parametric approach, amenable to a wide range of applications for its generality and data-driven simplicity.
Indeed, a key implication of Condition B, and one of impending practical consequence, is that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-F^{m}\bigl(xV(m(t-1/2))\bigr)}{1-F^{m}\bigl(V(m(t-1/2))\bigr)}\,\frac{dx}{x}=\gamma,

(3.1)

for
\gamma>0
. (cf. Lemma
8
in Appendix
A
). By analogous reasoning to that which have led to the cursory estimator in Section
2
, we proceed by replacing
t
with
k/\theta_{k}\rightarrow\infty
, as
k\rightarrow\infty
, while also replacing the d.f.
F^{m}
of the
k
independent BM with its empirical counterpart, namely the tail empirical distribution function given by

\overline{F_{k}^{m}}(x):=1-F_{k}^{m}(x)=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{M_{i}^{(m)}>x\}},

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Adopting
\widehat{V}(m(k/\theta_{k}-1/2))=M^{(m)}_{k-k_{0}:k}

1.4

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-F_{k}^{m}\bigl(x\widehat{V}(m(k/\theta_{k}-1/2))\bigr)}{1-F_{k}^{m}\bigl(\widehat{V}(m(k/\theta_{k}-1/2))\bigr)}\,\frac{dx}{x}=\int_{M^{(m)}_{k-k_{0}:k}}^{\infty}\frac{\overline{F_{k}^{m}}(u)}{\overline{F_{k}^{m}}\bigl(M_{k-k_{0}:k}\bigl)}\,\frac{du}{u}=\frac{k}{k_{0}}\int_{M^{(m)}_{k-k_{0}:k}}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F_{k}^{m}(u)\bigr)\,\frac{du}{u}.

Next, we establish its consistency and asymptotic normality in the framework issued by Condition B and its second order strengthening borne out by A2.

3.1
Consistency

Theorem 4
.

For any
m\geq 1
, assume that the distribution function
F^{m}
underlying the BM sample satisfies Condition B. Suppose a sequence of positive constants
(\vartheta_{k})
satisfying
\sqrt{k}\vartheta^{-3}_{k}\rightarrow 0
, as

If
k_{0}=k_{0}(k)\rightarrow\infty
is such that
\vartheta_{k}\,k_{0}=o(\sqrt{k})

\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}\equiv\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}(k_{0},k)\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{P}}\,\gamma\,,

as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

To prove Theorem
4
we first need a distribution-free result amenable to the universality class of distributions induced by the new extreme value condition (

also draws the forethought on other possible unified approaches to the modelling of extreme value features in higher dimensions (recall discussion in Section

Lemma 5
.

For every
k\geq 2
and constants

\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{U_{i}^{*}\leq s\}}

and the sample
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}\}
of i.i.d unit Fréchet random variables with common distribution function

\displaystyle{\sup_{(\vartheta_{k}(k+1))^{-1}<s\leq 1-\exp\{-(\vartheta_{k}\log k)^{-1}\}}}\sqrt{k}\Bigl|Z_{k-[ks]:k}-\Bigl(\frac{1}{1-U^{*}_{k-[ks]:k}}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\Bigl|\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{a.s.}}\,0,

\sqrt{k}\vartheta_{k}^{-3}\rightarrow 0

Remark 1

The proof of Lemma

Proof of Theorem

\bigl\{M^{(m)}_{k-i:k}\bigr\}_{i=0}^{k-1}\displaystyle{\mathop{=}^{d}}\Bigl\{V\Bigl(mZ_{k-i:k}\Bigr)\Bigr\}_{i=0}^{k-1}.

Since

(3.2)

\displaystyle\log M^{(m)}_{k-i:k}-\log M^{(m)}_{k-k_{0}:k}\displaystyle{\mathop{=}^{d}}\log V\bigl(mZ_{k-i:k}\bigl)-\log V\bigl(mZ_{k-k_{0}:k}\bigl)

\displaystyle\log V\bigl(mZ_{k-i:k}+\frac{m}{2}\bigl)-\log V\bigl(mZ_{k-k_{0}:k}+\frac{m}{2}\bigl)-\Bigl[\log V\bigl(mZ_{k-i:k}+\frac{m}{2}\bigl)-\log V\bigl(mZ_{k-i:k}\bigl)\Bigl]

3.2
Asymptotic normality

This section is primarily aimed at the asymptotic normality for the proposed hybrid-Hill estimator. A secondary objective is to lay the groundwork for the reduced-bias estimation developed later on in Section

Theorem 6
.

Assume Conditions B and A2 and define
g(t):=t-1/2
. Then, for all
m=1,\ldots,n
, there exists a function
\tilde{A}
, ultimately of constant sign and satisfying
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{A}(t)=0
, such that

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\frac{\log V(mg(tx))-\log V(mg(t))-\gamma\log x}{\tilde{A}(mt)}=\frac{x^{\tilde{\rho}}-1}{\tilde{\rho}},

(3.4)

for all
x>0
, locally uniformly. Moreover,
|\tilde{A}|\in RV_{\tilde{\rho}}
with
\tilde{\rho}:=\max(\rho,-1)
. Let
k_{0}=k_{0}(k)
in the conditions of Theorem
4
be an upper intermediate sequence, i.e.
k_{0}\rightarrow\infty
and

, as

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\sqrt{k_{0}}\tilde{A}\Bigl(\frac{n}{k_{0}}\Bigl)=\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then, the H2 estimator is asymptotically normal, namely

\sqrt{k_{0}}\bigl(\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}-\gamma\bigl)\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{d}}\,N\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{1-\tilde{\rho}},\gamma^{2}\Bigl).

Remark 3
.

The asymptotic normality of
\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}
does not depend explicitly on the block length
m
since it only affects the second order convergence relating the approximation bias.

Proof.

The first part of the theorem follows readily from Lemma
9
in Appendix

(given in Theorem

5

\sqrt{k_{0}}(\hat{\gamma}^{\textrm{H2}}-\gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\approx}}\,\sqrt{k_{0}}\Bigl\{\frac{1}{k_{0}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k_{0}-1}\log V\Bigr(m\bigl(\frac{Y_{k-i:k}}{Y_{k-k_{0}:k}}Y_{k-k_{0}:k}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl)\Bigr)-\log V\Bigl(m\bigl(Y_{k-k_{0}:k}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl)\Bigr)-\gamma\Bigr\}.

\displaystyle{\sup_{x\geq 1}}\,x^{-(\tilde{\rho}+\delta)}\biggl|\Bigl(\frac{\log V(mg(tx))-\log V(mg(t))-\gamma\log x}{\tilde{A}(mt)}-\frac{x^{\tilde{\rho}}-1}{\tilde{\rho}}\Bigr)\biggr|\leq\varepsilon\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{k_{0}}\Bigl\{\frac{1}{k_{0}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k_{0}-1}\gamma\log\frac{Y_{k-i:k}}{Y_{k-k_{0}:k}}-\gamma+\tilde{A}(mY_{k-k_{0}:k})\Bigl[\frac{1}{k_{0}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k_{0}-1}\frac{(Y_{k-i:k}/Y_{k-k_{0}:k})^{\tilde{\rho}}-1}{\tilde{\rho}}

(3.5)

is approached by continuity (cf. Appendix

). The supplementary document to this paper lists a number of distributions and their indices of regular variation, which can serve as an aid to what comes next.

•

For
\tilde{\rho}=-1
, the deterministic component of the asymptotic bias is primarily determined by

b_{k}=\frac{\gamma}{4}\sqrt{k_{0}}\Bigl|\Bigl(\frac{k}{k_{0}}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl)^{-1}\Bigr|,

and becomes negligible for
k_{0}\ll k^{2/3}
. Given the wide range of distributions possessing
\tilde{\rho}=-1
, including Fréchet, Pareto, Cauchy as well as quite a few Burr distributions, this upper bound for enabling asymptotically null bias is in a certain sense distribution-free. A list of distributions with
\tilde{\rho}=-1
is given in Table 1, in the supplement.

•

For
-1<\tilde{\rho}\leq 0
, the interest is in finding the largest possible
k_{0}
(hence minimising the variance) such that
\sqrt{k_{0}}\tilde{A}(n/k_{0})
is arbitrarily close to zero. We define
L(t):=t^{-\tilde{\rho}}\tilde{A}(t)\in RV_{0}
, whereby there exists a positive function
a
such that
t^{-1}a(t)\in RV_{-1}
and

L(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{a(s)}{s}\,ds.

Invoking Karamata’s theorem for integration of regularly varying functions
(
de Haan and Ferreira, 2006
, Theorem B.1.5)
, we find that

\frac{L(t)}{a(t)}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{s^{-1}a(s)}{t^{-1}a(t)}\,ds\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{t\rightarrow\infty}\,}\infty.

This implies that whenever the slowly-varying part of
\tilde{A}
satisfies
L(t)\rightarrow c\in(0,\infty)
, minimisation through finding the zeros of its derivative is rendered useless due to the necessarily
a(t)=o(1)
. That is,
a(t)\ll L(t)
meaning that the works on
a
is not enough to keep
L
in check, eventually. This partly explains the excessively large turning points reported in
Neves and Fraga Alves, 2004
. Hence, we proceed via the direct method and write
t_{k}=k/k_{0}\rightarrow\infty
and
\sqrt{k_{0}}\tilde{A}(t_{k})=\sqrt{k}\,t_{k}^{-1/2+\tilde{\rho}}L(t_{k})
. If
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}L(t)
is positive and finite, then we may set
\sqrt{k}\,t_{k}^{-1/2+\tilde{\rho}}\rightarrow\beta\in[0,1)
which, solving for
k_{0}
, yields
k_{0}\sim\beta^{2/(1-2\tilde{\rho})}k^{1-1/(1-2\tilde{\rho})}\rightarrow\infty
. The role of the block size
m
is that of a catalyst in the convergence whereby we can replace
k
with
mk
thus making it possible to conduct the H2 estimation on a wider range of
k_{0}
in a optimal way. We note that if
\tilde{\rho}=-1
, we obtain
k_{0}\sim(\beta k)^{2/3}
, the same power of
k
that staves off the bias in the previous item. If
L(t)=o(1)
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, then we seek the largest
k_{0}
such that
t_{k}^{1/2+\tilde{\rho}}/(mk)^{-1/2}\rightarrow 0
, i.e.
k_{0}\ll(mk)^{-2\tilde{\rho}/(1-2\tilde{\rho})}
. As it tallies with the previously obtained
k_{0}\ll(mk)^{2/3}

\tilde{\rho}=-1

for

We are now ready to devise a similar approximation to (
6.1

H_{k}(s)^{\leftarrow}=\bigl(M(Q_{k}(s)\bigl)^{\leftarrow}=\frac{1}{k}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{U_{i}^{*}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{s}}\}}\displaystyle{\mathop{=}^{a.s.}}\,\frac{1}{k}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{U_{i}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{1}{s}}\}},

), through application of the non-increasing Vervaat’s lemma

Resnick, 2007

\Bigl|\sqrt{k}\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{U_{i}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{1}{s}}\}}-(1-e^{-\frac{1}{s}})\Bigl)-B_{k}(e^{-\frac{1}{s}})\Bigl|=o_{p}(1),

uniformly for

Next, we replace

for writing

\displaystyle{\sup_{t\geq\,\vartheta_{k}\log k}}\sqrt{k}\Bigl|\frac{1}{k}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{U_{i}\geq 1-\exp(-\frac{1}{t-1/2})\}}-\frac{1}{k}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{(1-U_{i})^{-1}\geq t\}}\Bigl|\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{a.s.}}\,0

Proof.

The proof will be tackled by working essentially with the positive function

h(t):=t^{1/\gamma}\bigl(1-F(t)\bigr)\in\textrm{ERV}(\rho),

with auxiliary function
\alpha^{\star}(t):=t^{1/\gamma}\bigl(1-F(t)\bigr)\alpha(t)
that need not tend to zero. That is, for all
x>0
,

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\frac{(tx)^{1/\gamma}\overline{F}(tx)-t^{1/\gamma}\overline{F}(t)}{\alpha^{\star}(t)}=\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow{\infty}}}\frac{h(tx)-h(t)}{\alpha^{\star}(t)}=\frac{x^{\rho}-1}{\rho}.

For devising the analogous first order extended regular variation
(cf.
de Haan and Ferreira, 2006
, App. B2)
with intervening
F^{m}
, we note that
1-F^{m}(t)=\bigl(1-F(t)\bigr)\bigl(F^{m-1}(t)+F^{m-2}(t)+\ldots+F(t)+1\bigr)\sim m(1-F(t))
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Hence,

\frac{(tx)^{1/\gamma}(1-F^{m}(tx))-t^{1/\gamma}(1-F^{m}(t))}{t^{1/\gamma}B(t)}\approx\frac{h(tx)-h(t)}{t^{1/\gamma}B(t)/m}\;\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{t\rightarrow\infty}\,}\,\frac{x^{\rho}-1}{\rho},

by assumption, with auxiliary function given in terms of
\alpha(t)=B(t)/\bigl(m(1-F(t))\bigr)\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, setting

\beta(t)=\frac{m(1-F(t))}{1-F^{m}(t)}\alpha(t)\sim\alpha(t)

fulfils the second order condition on
1-F^{m}
, i.e.

\frac{(tx)^{1/\gamma}(1-F^{m}(tx))-t^{1/\gamma}(1-F^{m}(t))}{t^{1/\gamma}(1-F^{m}(t))\beta(t)}\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{t\rightarrow\infty}\,}\,\frac{x^{\rho}-1}{\rho},

for all
x>0
, with
\beta(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
∎
