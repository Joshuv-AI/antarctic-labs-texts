Abstract
. We tackle the challenge of integrating large language models (LLMs) with external recommender systems to enhance domain expertise in conversational recommendation (CRS). Current LLM-based CRS approaches primarily rely on zero- or few-shot methods for generating item recommendations based on user queries, but this method faces two significant challenges: (1) without domain-specific adaptation, LLMs frequently recommend items not in the target item space, resulting in low recommendation accuracy; and (2) LLMs largely rely on dialogue context for content-based recommendations, neglecting the collaborative relationships among entities or item sequences. To address these limitations, we introduce the CARE (Contextual Adaptation of Recommenders) framework. CARE customizes LLMs for CRS tasks, and synergizes them with external recommendation systems. CARE (a) integrates external recommender systems as domain experts, producing recommendations through entity-level insights, and (b) enhances those recommendations by leveraging contextual information for more accurate and unbiased final recommendations using LLMs. Our results demonstrate that incorporating external recommender systems with entity-level information significantly enhances recommendation accuracy of LLM-based CRS by an average of 54 % 54\% and 25 % 25\% for ReDial and INSPIRED datasets. The most effective strategy in the CARE framework involves LLMs selecting and reranking candidate items that external recommenders provide based on contextual insights. Our analysis indicates that the CARE framework effectively addresses the identified challenges and mitigates the popularity bias in the external recommender.



1.
Introduction

A conversational recommender system (CRS) helps users achieve recommendation-related goals through multiple rounds of conversation
(
Jannach et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Pramod and Bafna 2022
)
. Compared with traditional recommendation systems that mainly use entity-level information (
e.g., item name, ID or attributes
) as input, CRS adopts conversational data (
e.g., dialogue history
) as input, fully aligning to real-world scenarios
(
Li et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2023
)
. Aside from the input difference, CRS aims to accomplish two tasks: 1) recommending items based on both the user’s query or interests, and 2) generating high-quality conversational responses
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2022
)
.

Figure 1
.
CRS input example (ReDial Dataset) with conversational, contextual and entity-level inputs. (
Blue
: item entities;
Red
: attribute entities)

A key challenge for CRS is effectively leveraging dialogue history to generate accurate recommendations
(
He et al. 2023
;
Lei et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2018a
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, CRS utilizes two main types of information to model user preferences. First, entity-level information can be extracted from the dialogue history, including attribute entities (e.g., “user enjoys horror movies”) or item entities (e.g., “historical movies watched by the user”). Second, contextual information is gathered from implicit preferences expressed in natural language (e.g., “user’s opinion for a specific movie”). These two types of information are integrated throughout the conversation and work together to guide the CRS in generating relevant recommendations.

With the advent of large language models (LLMs), studies have explored their effectiveness as conversational recommenders
(
Dai et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2023
;
Palma et al. 2023
)
. Leveraging their superior language capabilities, LLMs show impressive results in zero-shot CRS applications. However, two key challenges remain due to the limitations of LLM-based CRS:
1) item space discrepancy
and
2) item information negligence
(§
3.1
).
The issue of
item space discrepancy
arises when
LLMs are pre-trained on data that differs from the domain-specific item space, causing LLMs to generate out-of-domain items, which fall outside the relevant target domain
(
He et al. 2023
;
Xi et al. 2024
)
.
The problem of
item information negligence
stems from the zero-/few-shot learning setting, where LLMs lack access to item-specific training data
(
Yang and Chen 2024
;
Gao et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, they lack comprehensive user histories for items and attributes, leading them to provide recommendations in cold-start scenarios predominantly.
Consequently, these models cannot leverage collaborative patterns among users and items.
Instead, they focus on content-based queries from the dialogue history and rely heavily on contextual information in the dialogue history to make recommendations
(
He et al. 2023
;
Tay et al. 2022
;
He et al. 2024
)
.
These two limitations have been acknowledged in prior studies (e.g.,
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2023
)
), but remain unaddressed in subsequent CRS research.

Inspired by advances in traditional recommender systems that integrate LLMs with external collaborative filtering techniques
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
, we propose the
Contextual Adaptation of RecommEnders (CARE)
framework.
CARE bridges LLMs and external entity-level recommenders via contextual adaptation, using item entities as intermediaries.
Specifically, we first train a lightweight domain-specific recommender based on entity sequences extracted from dialogue history
(
Zou et al. 2022a
;
Zou et al. 2022b
)
. This recommender acts as a domain expert, providing recommendations within the target domain based on sequential modelling (challenge 1). However, operating solely at the entity level limits its ability to capture nuanced user intentions from a dialogue context.
For example, although both statements—
“I like horror movies and I want one”
and
“I like thrillers but I don’t want to watch tonight!”
—express a taste for scary films, they reflect opposite intentions regarding immediate viewing options.
Therefore, we then introduce various methods to adapt the recommender’s output to LLMs, enabling LLMs as re-rankers to engage contextually by selecting and reranking the entity-based recommendations using dialogue history.
By leveraging both entity-level and contextual information, CARE familiarizes LLMs with item-specific information before generating final recommendations (challenge 2).

In conclusion, this approach employs external recommenders as domain experts to enrich a general LLM with item-specific information. The LLM then uses contextual information to refine these outputs, helping alleviate recommender biases such as popularity bias
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2023
)
or context-agnostic rankings. Our CARE framework integrates these two components through various strategies. Experimental results show that CARE significantly outperforms both training-based and LLM-based methods in recommendation accuracy, while our analysis further confirms CARE’s effectiveness in addressing the identified challenges and mitigating popularity bias.
Our main contributions are summarised:

•

We target two identified but unsolved challenges in LLM-based CRS:
item space discrepancy
and
item information negligence
.

•

We introduce the CARE framework, integrating LLMs with external recommenders to leverage both entity-level and contextual information for conversational search and recommendation.

•

We demonstrate CARE’s superior performance and effectiveness in identified challenges on two CRS benchmark datasets.

2.
Related Work

Conversational Recommendation System (CRS)
aims to model user preferences and generate recommendations through multi-round of conversations
(
Jannach et al. 2021
;
Gao et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. The current research focus has shifted from the
attribute-based approaches
, where the system and users exchange items or attribute entities using a template
(
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Lei et al. 2020
)
, into
conversational approaches
, where the system interacts with users through natural language
(
Li et al. 2018b
;
Deng et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
. The majority of CRS in conversational approaches use language models (LMs; e.g.,
DialoGPT
)
for learning-based preference modelling
(
Li et al. 2018a
;
Hayati et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
. Particularly, LMs are trained to generate recommendations or system responses using word or entity embeddings encoded from the conversations or external knowledge graphs
(
Li et al. 2018a
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2020
)
.

Large Language Models for CRS.
LLMs have shown promising performance in CRS as zero-/few-shot conversational recommenders using item-based
(
Palma et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
)
or conversational inputs
(
He et al. 2023
;
Sanner et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023d
)
to generate recommendation results. However, the performance of LLMs largely depends on their internal knowledge and their capability will notably diminish in domains with scarce knowledge
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023d
)
. Thus, LLMs are integrated with external agents
(
Feng et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2023
)
to either directly provide the necessary knowledge resources or retrieve the essential knowledge resources from external knowledge base to improve their performance in domain-specific CRS tasks
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Xi et al. 2024
)
. However, there is limited research in integrating traditional recommender systems to improve the item-specific capability for conversational recommendation tasks
(
He et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2022b
)
.

Sequential Modelling in CRS.
Sequential modelling is well-suited for CRS applications for two reasons: (1) entities in CRS are mentioned in a sequential flow that leads to recommendations, and (2) CRS often faces a cold-start scenario, where user profiles are unseen, limiting the application of collaborative filtering methods
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Zou et al. 2022b
;
Li et al. 2018a
;
Xi et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2022b
)
. Compared to RNN models, transformers have shown superior performance in sequential recommendation
(
Liu et al. 2016
;
Sun et al. 2019
)
. Motivated by the work in sequential recommendation
(
Sun et al. 2019
)
, the transformer-based sequential modelling framework is also applied to CRS by encompassing the entities mentioned in CRS conversations as a sequence using positional embedding
(
Zou et al. 2022b
)
or knowledge-aware positional encoding
(
Petruzzelli et al. 2024
)
. However, the existing sequential modelling methods only use entity-level information while we introduce the framework to incorporate contextual information in modelling user preferences.

Seq=[e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{n}]
.
As this paper limits its scope to the recommendation task in CRS, the target function for CRS is expressed as

i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{n}
,

Cov

3.1.
Main Problems and Key Challenges

3.1.1.
Item Space Discrepancy.

We illustrate the issue of item space discrepancy in Figure
3
. LLMs generate zero-shot recommendations based solely on their internal knowledge in their item space
1
1

1

Item space refers to the full set of items known to the model.
(left circle in red), which can significantly differ from the target item space (right circle in green) for domain-specific CRS tasks
(
He et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
)
. The overlap between these spaces represents where LLMs can make accurate recommendations (blue dots). The red dot indicates recommendations outside the target item space, while the white cross highlights target space items that the LLM cannot recommend due to lack of exposure or limited knowledge.

While recommending out-of-domain items may occasionally produce creative outputs, like AI-generated content (AIGC for recommendation)
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Yoon et al. 2024
)
, in real-world services such as e-commerce or movie platforms, these recommendations often misalign with the platform’s offerings. This can lead to user disappointment and a degraded overall experience
(
He et al. 2023
;
Zou et al. 2022b
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

3.1.2.
Item-specific Information Negligence.

LLMs excel at mapping user descriptions or queries to target items
(
He et al. 2023
;
Tay et al. 2022
)
.
However, a key limitation is their lack of understanding of the item-specific information from the item entities mentioned by the users or system
(
He et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
)
.
To investigate this, we follow the approach in
(
He et al. 2023
)
and evaluate LLM performance by separating different input types with or without item entities (Figure
1
): (1) the full conversation (
CRS_FULL_Conversation
), which includes the complete dialogue history; (2) the entity-level item sequence (
CRS_ITEM_ONLY
), consisting solely of item entities (
Seq
); and (3) a context-only input (
CRS_COV_ONLY
), where item entities were excluded from the dialogue history (
Cov-Seq
). The results in Figure
3
demonstrate that LLMs primarily base their recommendations on contextual information, neglecting the item-specific information in the item entity sequence. To fill the gap, we propose CARE-CRS to integrate both contextual and item-specific information for conversational recommendation.

4.
CARE-CRS

The CARE-CRS framework (Figure
4
) consists of two parts: 1) external recommenders for sequential modelling and 2) LLMs for conversational recommendations. Part 1
processes dialogue history by first extracting entities to form an entity sequence. The external recommender leverages this entity-level data to generate candidate items. In Part 2, the LLM is prompted to use the dialogue history and provided candidate items to generate, select, or rerank items based on dialogue context.

4.1.
Transformer-based Recommender for Sequential Modelling

The external recommender system takes entity sequences as input. To generate this sequence, we first apply string-matching scripts to align the entities in the dialogue (e.g., movies, actors, attributes) with the ones in knowledge graphs
(
Bizer et al. 2009
;
Zou et al. 2022b
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
. All matched entities are then concatenated to form the final entity sequences.
We do not explicitly distinguish positive from negative entities for two reasons: 1) the external recommender merely generates an initial candidate set, and language models subsequently re-rank items based on contextual cues such as user preference
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2021
)
, as detailed in §
4.2.2
, and 2) a sentiment analysis of experimented datasets (Table
1
) shows that most entities are associated with neutral or positive preferences. Consequently, we include all extracted entities uniformly in the sequence.

4.1.1.
Recommender Structure

While the external recommender can be implemented using any system with entity-level inputs and outputs, we adopt a sequential model to capture entity dynamics better and choose a transformer architecture for its superior performance, as shown in prior works
(
Petruzzelli et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2022b
;
Liu et al. 2016
;
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
, Notably, we deliberately exclude LLMs from the recommender to preserve a clear modular separation from the LLM-based re-rankers. The model has three components: an embedding layer, a self-attention layer, and an output layer and is trained using a cloze objective
(
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2019
)
, where the final item in a sequence is replaced with a special token
[MASK]
, and the model learns to predict the masked item.

The embedding layer computes each entity’s embedding combing graph embedding
h_{K}
and positional embedding
h_{P}
for final embedding
h_{i}^{0}
as
h_{K}+h_{P}
, where graph embeddings encode the entity from knowledge graph
(
Kipf and Welling 2016
;
Schlichtkrull et al. 2018
)
and positional embeddings encode the position of entities in the sequence, as in
(
Petruzzelli et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2022b
)
.
The self-attention layer uses a Multi-head Self-attention mechanism to learn the final sequential representation
H
, formulated as:

(2)

H=\text{Self-Attention}(h_{1}^{0},h_{2}^{0},...,h_{n}^{0},\text{{[MASK]}})

The output layer projects the final hidden states of sequential representation
H
to the item space
Out_{k}
using two layers of neural networks, where
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
are the weights and biases of the feed-forward networks.

(3)

\text{Out}_{k}=[\text{out}_{1}\dots,\text{out}_{k}];~\text{out}_{i}=\text{GELU}(HW_{1}^{T}+b_{1})W_{2}^{T}+b_{2}

To better integrate with LLMs, the recommender’s outputs
Out_{k}
, are converted into a candidate set in text format, which is then incorporated into the assemble prompt for contextual adaptation with LLMs (§
4.2.2
). The number of items
k
in the candidate set varies across strategies, and we discuss this parameter in §
5.3
.

4.2.
Prompting LLMs for Contextual Adaptation

Without external recommenders, the existing approach uses instructional prompts to guide LLMs in generating recommendation outputs from dialogue history
(
He et al. 2023
)
. As shown in Figure
4
(Part 2), a prompt consists of three components:
1)
role prompt (
P_{R}
)
that defines the system’s role,
2)
format prompt (
P_{F}
)
that constrains the output format for future post-processing, and
3)
task prompt (
P_{T}
)
that specifies the task and function of the system.
The final prompt,
P_{CRS}=P_{R}+P_{T}+P_{F}
, integrates these three components. The overall process is formulated as follows:

(4)

i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{n}=LLM(~P_{CRS},Cov)=LLM(~(P_{R}+P_{F}+P_{T}),Cov)

In our setup, to better integrate external recommenders, we update the task prompt
P_{T}
from two key perspectives: 1) Recommender Adaptation and 2) Contextual Engagement. These updates enable the LLMs to effectively leverage contextual information, facilitating their ability to generate, select, or rerank the entity-level recommendations derived from an external recommender system.

4.2.1.
Adaptation of Recommender as Domain Expert.

LLMs demonstrate significant potential when integrated with external sub-systems to form agent-based
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
or expert-guided systems
(
Ge et al. 2024
)
. Adaptation refers to the challenge of effectively introducing and describing these external systems so that LLMs can better understand and collaborate with them as domain experts
(
Wang et al. 2023c
)
(Recommender Adaptation in Figure
4
Part 2). We design three methods to adapt the recommender systems as domain experts: 1) Direct Prompting, 2) Recommender Description, and 3) Self-Reflection. Each approach modifies the task prompt
P_{T}
in the LLM to facilitate this adaptation.

•

Direct Prompting:
Direct introduce the recommender as a domain expert without any description of its function or input/output format.

Task Prompt (
P_{T}^{m=1}
):

“To help you with the recommendation, we introduce a domain expert who provides some recommendations based on the training data and you can use the domain expert’s recommendations as examples to generate your output”

•

Description of Recommender:
Briefly describe how the recommender works to model the sequential inputs and generate the candidate items.

Task Prompt (
P_{T}^{m=2}
):
“To help you with the recommendation, we introduce a domain-expert which is a recommender for sequential modelling that uses the entities mentioned in the dialogues to generate a ranking list of items.”

•

Self Reflection:
Step by step lead the LLM to examine all the resources of the recommender (e.g., code, paper or data samples). After each resource, we ask LLM to self-reflect, correcting or generating a new prompt to describe the recommender
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. We repeat these steps until the LLM is confident about its output and fix it as the task prompt
P_{T}^{m=3}
for self-reflection.

Task Prompt (
P_{T}^{m=3}
):
“To help you with the recommendation, you can access an advanced recommendation system that specializes in enhancing conversational recommendations by leveraging both the sequence of entities mentioned in a conversation and external knowledge embedded in knowledge graphs. This system generates diverse suggestions based on the entity sequence. The system will return a list of movie recommendations that are entity-relevant, diverse, and informed by the sequence provided and external knowledge from knowledge graphs.”

4.2.2.
Contextual Engagement for Conversational Recommendations.

After introducing the recommender as a domain expert, we propose contextual engagement strategies that enable LLMs to learn item space information from the expert and incorporate contextual data to refine its recommendations
(
He et al. 2023
;
Hou et al. 2024
)
. This allows LLMs to generate target domain-specific recommendations and correct external recommender errors, such as
1) context-agnostic rankings, where the recommender ignores the explicit preference over an item in the context
or
2) popularity bias, where the statistically popular item is over-ranked
, both are common issues in learning-based approaches
(
Zou et al. 2022b
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
. We introduce three contextual engagement strategies: 1) expansion, 2) reranking, and 3) selection-then-reranking, modifying prompt
P_{T}
after recommender adaptation.

•

Expansion:
Provide fewer example recommendations than output and ask the model to generate more, offering guidance with minimal restrictions.

Task Prompt (
P_{T}^{s=1}
):

To help … you may use the domain expert’s recommendations as
examples
to generate additional recommendations based on the dialogue history.
You
can
generate items beyond the domain expert’s recommendations.

•

Reranking:
Provide the same number of recommendations as the desired output and ask the model to rerank the candidate items using dialogue history.

Task Prompt (
P_{T}^{s=2}
):

To help … you need to
rerank
the recommendations, placing the domain expert’s suggestions in the appropriate order based on your understanding of the dialogue history. You
cannot
generate items beyond …

•

Selection-then-Reranking:
Show a larger set of recommendations and ask the model to select from them, rerank the selected items, to form a ranked list.

Task Prompt (
P_{T}^{s=3}
):

To help … you need to
select
the most appropriate items from the domain expert’s recommendations and
rerank
them in a ranked order based on dialogue history. You
cannot
generate items beyond …

Given the dialogue history
Cov
and the top-K candidate items from the external recommender
Out_{k}
as inputs, LLM is prompted to generate
n
outcomes with adaptation methods
m
, contextual engagement strategies
s
, using the final prompt
P_{CARE}=(P_{R}+(P_{T}^{m}+P_{T}^{s})+P_{F})
as formulated in:

(5)

\begin{split}i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{n}&=LLM(~P_{CARE},Cov,Out_{k})\\
&=LLM(~(P_{R}+(P_{T}^{m}+P_{T}^{s})+P_{F}),Cov,Out_{k})\end{split}

)

5.1.2.
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate our model and baselines on their recommendation abilities using metrics HIT@K, MRR@K and NDCG@K with K in
\{5,10\}
, as commonly adopted in
(
Deng et al. 2023
;

5.2.2.
Training Efficiency.

In contrast to learning-based baselines like UniCRS, which fully fine-tune a language model such as DialoGPT
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
with 762M parameters, our approach leverages a zero-shot LLM inference through API without fine-tuning. The only training involved is for the external recommender, which has 2 layers and 2 attention heads using a transformer structure, totalling 2.7 M parameters—less than 1% of the size of the baseline models. As our overall framework is modular, both the external recommender and the LLMs can be replaced with newer models. Future work can further enhance training efficiency by using alternative pre-trained recommendation systems in the target domain, eliminating the need for additional training. Additionally, the external recommender’s output can be shared with different LLMs through a unified interface, allowing further exploration of performance improvements.

5.3.
Discussion on CARE-CRS Methods and Strategies (RQ2)

We propose several methods for contextual adaptation between LLMs and recommenders in §

5.3.1.
Contextual Engagement Strategies: Better selection than a generation for LLM-based CRS

Figure

2
). ReDial contains three times more item entities than INSPIRED, but nearly half the average entities per dialogue, indicating fewer attribute entities in the conversations. Because attribute entities provide a richer context for LLMs, zero-shot LLMs perform better on INSPIRED, while the external recommender—focusing on item information—is more effective in ReDial (Table
6
). This conclusion is further substantiated by Table
3

Despite dataset differences, similar trends emerge across the contextual engagement strategies.
ST1
underperforms due to generating irrelevant items that are outside the target domain(§
5.4
), while
ST2
and
ST3
consistently outperform both the zero-shot LLM and external recommender by leveraging reranking or selection based on context. The consistency among the three strategies shows that the CARE framework is robust across different types of datasets, whether item- or attribute-rich. This also suggests that LLMs perform better when selecting rather than generating recommendations in domain-specific tasks. While choosing from the entire item space is infeasible,
ST2
and
ST3
effectively integrate entity-level recommendations from the external recommender as candidate items and improve with the LLM’s contextual refinement, changing in two ways: (1) filtering items based on user preferences expressed in the context (e.g., selecting lower-ranked but highly relevant items) and (2) mitigating popularity bias (e.g., removing statistically popular but irrelevant items from the top position).

5.4.
Discussion of CARE-CRS on CRS Challenges and Bias (RQ3)

We further analyze the performance of the CARE framework in tackling key challenges and biases, such as item space discrepancy in LLMs and popularity bias in external recommenders. As integrating external entity-level recommender targets the challenge of item-specific information negligence, we omit the discussion in this section.

5.4.1.
Item Space Discrepancy from LLMs.

In Figure
9
, we report the ratio of recommended items falling outside the target domain for each method. Because LLM-based recommendations are generated in free text, we map each recommended item to a target-domain item via Levenshtein distance; any item whose distance exceeds a threshold
\vartheta
is classified as out-of-domain. Zero-shot LLMs often generate items that do not exist in the target domain (
item space discrepancy
, §
3.1
), whereas external recommenders, which are trained to rank items solely within the target domain, exhibit no such discrepancy.

Our proposed ST2 (rerank) and ST3 (selection-then-rerank) mitigate this issue by restricting generation to a given candidate set, unlike ST1 and zero-shot LLMs, which freely produce items beyond the candidate set. As shown in Figure
9
, both ST2 and ST3 significantly reduce out-of-domain outputs and consequently improve recommendation accuracy over ST1 (Figure
6
). Between these two strategies, ST3 selects and reranks from a larger candidate pool but achieves the same low out-of-domain rate while further boosting accuracy. These results validate that the selection-then-rerank strategy is effective within the CARE-CRS framework.

5.4.2.
Popularity Bias from Recommender.

As discussed in §
4.2.2
, popularity bias remains a significant challenge in learning-based recommendation systems
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2023
)
. Entity-level recommenders trained on historical data often reinforce this bias by consistently ranking the same popular items at the top. Figure
10
presents a box plot of item popularity to their ranking position, showing that external recommenders (with high mean and low variance) consistently rank the same popular items at the top positions. In contrast, with contextual engagement in the CARE framework, the model
explicitly understands the user’s intention and preferences from the dialogue history. As a result, they can either select lower-ranked items and rerank them to higher positions or remove popular items from the top position to form the final ranking list.

As a real example from the testing data in the ReDial dataset, Figure
4
illustrates the CARE-CRS framework with a case study in popularity bias. Even though the user prefers horror and thriller movies in the dialogue history, the external recommender still ranks
Monna
(a fantasy movie) first due to its historical popularity in training data, which is irrelevant to the context. However, with our contextual adaptation methods, the LLM can remove this popular item from the top position by excluding
Monna
from the final ranking list. Consequently, it reranks other relevant movies such as
The Exorcist
(horror) and
Disturbia
(thriller) by elevating them to top positions, which aligns better with the user’s stated interests and contextual information. Statistically, our approach (low mean, high variance) in Figure
10
reflects a notable reduction in popularity bias and yields more diverse recommendations, ultimately enhancing recommendation accuracy.

6.
Conclusion

This paper presents a new framework that facilitates the integration of LLMs with external recommenders via candidate items, aiming to address two key limitations of LLM-based CRSs: 1) item space discrepancy, and 2) item information negligence.
We employ a transformer-based recommender to perform entity-level sequential modelling and generate a candidate set of items. Subsequently, LLMs are guided to select and rerank these candidates based on conversational context through a contextual adaptation process. This design enables seamless and training-efficient collaboration between LLMs and external recommenders.
Experimental results and analysis demonstrate that our framework significantly improves recommendation accuracy for both open- and closed-source LLMs, while effectively mitigating the identified limitations and reducing popularity bias.
