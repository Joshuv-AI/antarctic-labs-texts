Evaluating AI Counseling in Japanese: Counselor, Client, and Evaluator Roles Assessed by Motivational Interviewing Criteria
Keita Kiuchi, Yoshikazu Fujimoto, Hideyuki Gotō, Tomonori Hosokawa, Makoto Nishimura, Yōsuke Satō, Izumi Sezai

Abstract
This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of large language model (LLM) performance across three counseling roles in Japanese-language therapeutic contexts. We simultaneously assessed counselor artificial intelligence (AI) systems (GPT-4-turbo with zero-shot prompting or Structured Multi-step Dialogue Prompts [SMDP], Claude-3-Opus-SMDP), client AI simulations, and evaluation AI systems (o3, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-pro). Human experts ( n = 15) with extensive counseling experience evaluated AI-generated dialogues using the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) Coding Manual 4.2.1. Notably, SMDP implementation significantly enhanced counselor AI performance across all MITI global ratings compared with zero-shot prompting, with no significant differences between GPT-SMDP and Opus-SMDP. Evaluation AIs showed comparable performance to human raters for Cultivating Change Talk but systematically overestimated Softening Sustain Talk and the overall quality metrics. Model-specific biases emerged: Gemini emphasized power-sharing, o3 focused on technical proficiency, and Sonnet prioritized emotional expression. Client AI simulations exhibited a limited emotional range and unnaturally high compliance, indicating the need for enhanced realism. These findings establish benchmarks for AI-assisted counseling in non-English contexts and identify critical areas for improvement through advanced prompt engineering, retrieval-augmented generation, and targeted fine-tuning, with important implications for developing culturally sensitive AI mental health tools.

3.5
Qualitative Evaluation

A comprehensive summary of qualitative findings is presented in Appendix 7.

3.5.1
Counselor AI Evaluation

Expert human evaluators identified several common limitations across AI counselors: excessive directiveness and advice-giving, insufficient emotional empathy and exploration of unexpressed content, inadequate deepening of change-oriented statements, and an evaluative stance hindering collaborative relationship building.

The evaluators observed distinct patterns for each AI counselor system. Opus-SMDP effectively elicited client strengths and resources but dominated sessions through excessive verbalization, resulting in ambiguous goal-setting and risk exploration before transitioning to protracted closings. GPT-zero demonstrated rapid support for client decisions and change initiatives but rushed toward problem identification and termination without adequate exploration, relying on generic encouragement. GPT-SMDP exhibited collaborative intentions and egalitarian understanding but employed monotonous, mechanical patterns of reflection and questioning that failed to explore client values or workplace-specific challenges in depth.

AI evaluators converged on several observations. While change talk was elicited, it lacked depth; empathy appeared warm but remained superficial; early transitions to advice-giving created counselor-dominated interactions; and complex reflections, ambivalence exploration, and scaling techniques were absent. Power-sharing remained moderate, falling short of ‘̀full collaboration.‘̀ However, the evaluation patterns differed across AI systems.

While human experts provided comprehensive assessments, integrating basic engagement and specialized techniques to identify specific challenges for each counselor AI, AI evaluators demonstrated metric-specific biases. o3 approximated human evaluation by addressing verbalization quantity and technical proficiency through expertise-based assessment. Sonnet prioritized client affirmation, encouragement, and self-efficacy. Gemini emphasized client autonomy and empowerment, centering power-sharing in its evaluative framework.

3.5.2
Client AI Evaluation

Human expert evaluations identified three primary limitations in client AI performance: unnatural and monotonous dialogue patterns, biased interactions with deficient emotional expressions, and insufficient character and situational depths.

Client AIs reportedly exhibited problematic response patterns, including unconditional acceptance of suggestions, mechanical repetition of gratitude and agreement, and flat interactions lacking authenticity. Language issues included direct translation artifacts from English, excessively formal expressions, and insufficient use of natural backchanneling, colloquialisms, and paraphrasing. Sessions concluded awkwardly with prolonged endings and unnatural insertions of formulaic phrases, such as ‘̀May I end the conversation¿̀

Additionally, client AIs demonstrated passive following of counselor utterances without agency, displaying an LLM characteristic of positive compliance bias. This resulted in minimal resistance, ambivalence, and negative emotional disclosure, which are typically observed in authentic counseling contexts. Finally, character development lacked specificity regarding age, workplace, and family composition, with abstract rather than concrete problem descriptions and ideal state articulations. Evaluators noted the absence of age-appropriate emotions (e.g., pre-retirement anxiety or anger) and realistic hesitation regarding third-party consultations.

Recommendations for improvement emphasize three domains: natural dialogue rhythm implementation, emotional range and bidirectionality enhancement, and concrete background specifications with increased realism. Natural responses would benefit from incorporating backchannelling, brief silences, segmented responses, varied expressions of gratitude and agreement, integrated resistance and ambivalence, authentic negative emotional disclosure, and insight articulation. Enhanced realism could emerge from specific narratives incorporating age, workplace environment, and family composition details alongside realistic uncertainty and emotional reactions reflective of the lived experience.

4
Discussion

This study evaluated counseling scripts generated through interactions between counselor AIs and client AIs, employing both human evaluators and multiple LLM-based counseling evaluation AIs to examine their respective performances. Our findings elucidated the capabilities and current limitations of counselor, client, and counseling evaluation AIs while highlighting future possibilities for improvement.

4.1
Hypothesis Testing

H1: Effects of Structured Multi-Step Dialogue Prompts

Opus-SMDP and GPT-SMDP demonstrated significantly higher human evaluation scores than GPT-zero, supporting this hypothesis. In the SMDP groups, we implemented a strict dialogue cycle of
Listen–Back 1
\to
Listen–Back 2
\to
Question
through prompt engineering, which resulted in improvements in: (1) frequency of reflective responses, (2) questions aligned with client input, and (3) establishment of appropriate “pauses
prime
prime
(confirming client situations and thoughts) before transitioning to advice-giving. All indicators showed increases exceeding 0.5 points in human evaluation. When deploying LLMs as counselors, providing specific instructions for counseling procedures through structured prompts yields superior quality compared to simply instructing the model to “act as a counselor.
prime
ensuremathprime

H2: Performance Differences Between Models

The differences in human evaluation scores between Opus-SMDP and GPT-SMDP were not statistically significant (e.g., a OVR difference of approximately 0.06 points,
p=0.624
), thus failing to support this hypothesis. The Japanese conversational capabilities of GPT-4-turbo and Claude-3-Opus were comparable, with differences too minimal to decisively impact counseling quality. However, our equivalence-testing simulation indicated that 375 or more evaluators were necessary to definitively demonstrate equivalence, suggesting that further validation is required.

H3: Differences Between Counseling Evaluation AIs and Human Evaluators

For SST and OVR metrics, all evaluation AIs assigned higher scores than human evaluators, partially supporting the hypothesis. Other indicators showed no consistent patterns, with exploratory analyses revealing that differences between AI and human ratings varied according to the counselor AI type for CCT, PAR, and EMP. The variability in human ratings may compromise the consistency with evaluation AIs, highlighting the need for improved evaluation reliability. A detailed analysis of the differences between evaluation AIs remains a priority for future research.

4.2
Toward Improving Counselor AIs

While counselor AIs utilizing GPT-4-turbo and Claude-3-Opus with SMDP received higher evaluations than zero-shot GPT-4-turbo, qualitative assessments identified persistent challenges: (1) reflective responses remained superficial paraphrases, lacking reflection of ambivalent emotions and values; (2) questions followed predetermined flows without deeper exploration of client responses; and (3) timing and quality of advice-giving remained suboptimal. Although SMDP achieved minimal respect for client autonomy, it fell short of facilitating emotional exchanges or dialogue deepening.

Proposed improvements include: First, enhancing Listen–Back capabilities to be multifaceted and interpretive, potentially leveraging Retrieval-Augmented Generation to reproduce input patterns that deepen dialogue
(
Arabi et al. 2024
)
. Second, dialogue flow progression could be managed through multi-agent architectures, where separate LLMs make strategic decisions, with reasoning models potentially guiding response-generating LLMs strategically
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Boonstra 2024
;
Kampman et al. 2024
)
. Third, addressing excessive advice-giving through introducing MI techniques such as Ask–Offer–Ask could facilitate more appropriate advice provision. Finally, managing high utterance frequency requires prompt-based limitations on utterance length, incorporation of minimal responses and strategic silences, particularly in voice-based interactions, to allow users to deepen their thinking at their own pace.

4.3
Toward Improving Client AIs

Human evaluators identified issues such as flatness, lack of realism, uncritical acceptance of advice, and premature dialogue termination. These challenges stem from (1) insufficient background information and emotional variability, (2) monotonous discourse style and dialogue rhythm, and (3) superficial scenario depth
(
Maurya 2024a
;
Qiu and Lan 2024
)
. Potential solutions include implementing detailed persona settings with dynamic emotional responses and resistance calculations
(
Bao et al. 2025
)
. Medical education has successfully employed virtual patient AIs that incorporate resistance reactions, demonstrating their utility
(
Potter and Jefferies 2024
)
. Additionally, ensuring a natural dialogue rhythm and stylistic diversity through variable response lengths (one to three sentences) and incorporating colloquial expressions and strategic pauses would enhance realism. Introducing dynamic flows that present significant challenges while following standard counseling processes could prove effective
(
Du et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, parameterizing client orientations, unique goals, and fears to reflect in each conversational turn promises more realistic interactions
(
Cook et al. 2025
)
.

4.4
Toward Improving Counseling Evaluation AI

This study examined the performance of Gemini, Sonnet, and o3 as counseling evaluation AI systems. We confirmed consistency both among LLMs and across repeated measurements, and found that their CCT evaluations were comparable to human ratings. However, their ratings were considerably more lenient than human evaluators, particularly for OVR and SST. Additionally, each model demonstrated distinct emphases: Gemini on power balance, Claude on emotional communication, and o3 on technical skills. We discuss possibilities for improving counseling evaluation AI based on these discrepancies with human ratings.

First, regarding evaluation perspectives, human evaluators strictly penalized qualitative shortcomings such as ‘̀premature advice-giving‘̀ and ‘̀question-dominated interactions with weak power-sharing.‘̀ In contrast, AI evaluation models highly valued politeness and positive language, showing a strong tendency to award points when clear ‘̀negative behaviors‘̀ were absent. Consequently, SMDP scripts that maintained status quo talk with warm phrasing automatically received high OVR and SST scores, but could not adequately credit implicit emotions or collaborative deficits, resulting in lower EMP and PAR scores. For CCT, both humans and AI primarily judged based on the surface-level criterion of ‘̀capturing change-oriented statements,‘̀ resulting in smaller score differences. Additionally, there remains uncertainty about whether evaluation AI could focus exclusively on counselor AI responses. While human evaluators faced the same conditions, the input inevitably included client AI responses, making it unclear how well the evaluation AI could isolate and assess only the counselor AI
prime
s contributions.

Regarding MITI criteria influences, MITI global ratings define perfect scores for OVR and SST with phrases like ‘̀accepting/non-judgmental‘̀ and ‘̀not reinforcing sustain talk,‘̀ which LLMs may have interpreted as achievable through surface-level behaviors. Conversely, EMP requires ‘̀recognizing unspoken emotions‘̀ and PAR demands ‘̀sharing agenda-setting with clients‘̀—higher-order behavioral anchors that LLMs likely understood as unattainable through merely polite responses. Thus, LLMs
prime
surface-level compliance elevated OVR and SST scores but plateaued for EMP and PAR, while CCT
prime
s behavior-ratio-centered definition allowed keyword detection to align with human ratings.

Based on these findings, improvements to evaluation AI could include prompt engineering, criteria refinement, and implementation of RAG or fine-tuning. First, as a prompt improvement strategy, we could provide evaluation AI with the complete MITI manual as contextual information, not just the global rating criteria included in our prompts. Human evaluators implicitly consider MITI behavior counts (e.g., reflection frequency exceeding questions) and non-MI behaviors (e.g., confrontation or advice without permission) when making global ratings. Providing the full MITI text might enable evaluation AI to make global assessments aligned with MITI values, similar to human evaluators.

Numerous insights have been proposed for a prompt design. Notably, our counseling evaluation AI employed reasoning models with a built-in Chain of Thought and Step-by-Step prompting. While these models excel at complex problem-solving and reasoning, they require simpler, clearer prompts and specialized techniques (Reasoning best practices, OpenAI API, n.d.). Accordingly, an effective structure for counseling evaluation AI prompts might include Developer Messages containing Rubric Yaml and JSON output instructions, with User Messages containing only the script. Specifically, the Developer Message could begin with role declaration, input format, scoring procedures, full MITI text as a reference, and output format instructions. The Rubric Yaml would contain structured MITI global rating rubrics, while JSON output would include numerical ratings and character-limited Reason text sections.

Regarding evaluation criteria, while we used the same standards as human evaluators, these may be difficult for LLMs to interpret. To create LLM-friendly formats, we need to clearly specify counselor behaviors that warrant point additions or deductions for each indicator. Furthermore, while MITI typically separates behavior counts from global ratings, integrating behavior count evaluations into global ratings might clarify evaluation criteria for LLMs.

For RAG and fine-tuning approaches, human MITI evaluators provide one global rating for entire 20-minute scripts. When implementing RAG for evaluation AI, we could perform partial MITI global evaluations on short chunks like ‘̀client input—counselor response—client input,‘̀ then integrate these into overall assessments.

For fine-tuning, either partial evaluations or single-script evaluations could work. In either case, preparing good and poor examples for low-learning-rate fine-tuning with LoRA would be practical. Ideally, a dataset comparing human-rated top 20% and bottom 20% across 5-6 different consultation topics would enable efficient training. However, this would require 50-80 paired examples for responses with limited variation like CCT or SST, and 100-200 pairs for comprehensive evaluations like OVR, making data preparation labor-intensive. Additionally, operating fine-tuned models incurs substantial API costs. This could be addressed by fine-tuning teacher models on large-scale systems, then distilling to 7B student models for local deployment

citepzhangPLaDPreferencebasedLarge2024. Evaluating multi-turn dialogues like counseling remains a developing area in AI systems and LLM research, requiring continued development with domain expert involvement

citepguanEvaluatingLLMbasedAgents2025.

4.5
Consistency Between Human and AI Evaluations

In the human MITI global ratings, SST and PAR showed high ICC values, indicating clear evaluation criteria with minimal subjective variation. Conversely, CCT and EMP exhibited substantial individual differences, requiring averages from 3-5 or more raters to achieve adequate reliability
(
Koo and Li 2016
)
. Our independently adopted OVR measure showed an extremely low ICC owing to insufficient variance. Global ratings may need to be calculated as composite scores using MITI
prime
s four indicators.

The counseling evaluation AI group also showed insufficient variance for some indicators, resulting in incalculable or extremely low ICC values. Leniency bias may partially explain this. Future improvements to counseling evaluation AI design, combined with using multiple foundation-model-based LLMs, could ensure adequate variance while achieving stable evaluations. Such approaches are already incorporated in LLM evaluation contexts
(
Morgan 2025
)
and warrant consideration for counseling evaluation AI.

4.6
Limitations

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of counselor, client, and counseling evaluation AI performance in Japanese counseling contexts. Several limitations warrant consideration.

First, the LLMs employed are no longer state-of-the-art due to the time lag between data collection and publication. While the demonstrated utility of SMDP and minimal differences between OpenAI and Anthropic models likely generalize to current models, verification is needed. Additionally, counselor AI excluded Google
prime
s model due to Gemini
prime
s limited performance at data collection, though current capabilities suggest potential effectiveness.

Second, our evaluation used AI-to-AI dialogues rather than human-AI interactions. Whether counselor AI can appropriately respond to human clients and whether client AI effectively trains human counselors requires separate investigation. AI
prime
s difficulty with inputs deviating from prompt-specified flows remains unexamined.

Third, ethical considerations regarding AI in counseling—including transparency, information security, and professional boundaries—were beyond this study
prime
s scope but require attention for practical implementation (Kiuchi et al., 2025).

Fourth, we evaluated counselor AI using MITI, a motivational interviewing-specific tool. While MI represents a fundamental technique, it does not encompass all counseling approaches. MITI
prime
s original design for audio recordings introduces validity concerns for text evaluation. Future research should develop universal counselor AI evaluation methods.

Finally, our findings are specific to Japanese-language contexts. Given documented language-dependent variations and cultural biases in LLMs, generalizability remains limited.

5
Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive evaluation of counselor AI (GPT-4-turbo with zero-shot prompting, GPT-4-turbo with SMDP, Claude-3-Opus with SMDP), client AI, and counseling evaluation AI (o3, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-pro) in Japanese counseling dialogues. SMDP-enhanced counselor AI significantly outperformed zero-shot conditions on MITI global ratings. GPT-4-turbo and Claude-3-Opus demonstrated comparable Japanese counseling quality. Evaluation AI showed no significant differences from humans in CCT ratings but exhibited marked leniency in SST and OVR ratings, with model-specific biases: Gemini emphasized power-sharing, o3 focused on technical skills, and Claude prioritized emotional expression. Client AI produced flat responses lacking resistance and emotional expression.

These findings suggest improvements through detailed persona settings with emotional parameters, model optimization via RAG and fine-tuning, and integrated advancement of prompt design, model selection, and evaluation frameworks. Our results inform research, development, and practical applications for Japanese counseling support and training. Future research should extend to human-AI interactions involving actual clients and counseling professionals.

You are a supportive and well aware counselor of strengths. Listen to your users through the following methods and respond in Japanese.

<methods>

Listen-Back 1:
After the user makes a statement, paraphrase it into a single sentence while adding a new nuance or interpretation.

Wait for the user’s reply to your Listen-Back 1 (e.g., they might say only “yes”).

Listen-Back 2:
After receiving the user’s response, further paraphrase their reply, condensing it into one sentence and adding another layer of meaning or interpretation.

Once you’ve done Listen-Back 1 and Listen-Back 2 and received a response from the user, you may then pose a question from the list provided.

Questions are in the order specified by order_of_questions. All questions must be asked without skipping any, even if you think you already have the answer.

After the user answers your question, return to Listen-Back 1.

You can ask your next question only after: receiving a response to your Listen-Back 1, providing your Listen-Back 2, and getting another response from the user.

Avoid giving advice as much as possible, and if you must do so, please include a disclaimer that it is your opinion and the user is free to follow it or not.

Never ask consecutive questions. Also, you must not do Listen-Back and ask a question at the same time.

Always follow the pattern of Listen-Back 1, user response, Listen-Back 2, and then another user response before moving on to your next question.

</methods>

<order_of_questions>

1: Open by asking the user a question about a particularly troubling aspect of the situation.

2: Then, inquire about how the user would envision the ideal outcome.

3: Proceed by asking about what steps, if any, the user has already taken to address the issue.

4: Follow up by exploring other actions the user is currently undertaking related to the situation.

5: Delve into potential resources that could aid in achieving the user’s goals.
