Comparative Outcomes of Rectangle Recession Versus Eponychial Folding Flaps for Nail-Plate Length After Fingertip Amputation
Authors: Mulakala Sravya Keerthi, Arvindraj Mahadevan, Satish Kumar, Arun Prasath, R. Santharam, C. Balasubramanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFingertip amputations affect the distal phalanx, pulp, and nail complex, with considerable impact on both function and appearance. Although various flap techniques are used for soft-tissue coverage, restoration of apparent nail-plate exposure is often neglected. This study aimed to compare the functional and aesthetic outcomes of two apparent nail-plate exposure techniques, the rectangle recession flap and the eponychial folding flap, in patients with fingertip amputations. Neither technique creates new nail tissue; both modify the proximal nail-fold to expose a greater proportion of the already-present nail-plate.MethodsThis was a prospective comparative study conducted from June 2023 to June 2024 at a tertiary care specialty hospital. Sixty patients with fingertip amputations were randomized using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes into the rectangle recession flap group (group A, n = 30) or the eponychial folding flap group (group B, n = 30). A biostatistician was involved in both sequence generation and statistical validation. Patients were assessed at regular intervals over a 3-month postoperative period. Primary outcome measures included apparent gain in visible nail-plate exposure (as a percentage of the contralateral normal), scar quality, sensation, and overall aesthetic satisfaction using validated scoring systems.ResultsThe study revealed significant differences between the two techniques in terms of apparent gain in visible nail-plate exposure and functional outcomes. Patients who underwent the eponychial folding flap technique demonstrated superior outcomes, particularly in terms of visible nail-plate exposure, scar quality, appearance, and postoperative pain.ConclusionsBoth techniques are viable options for increasing apparent visible nail-plate exposure in fingertip reconstruction; however, the eponychial folding flap provides superior outcomes in terms of visible nail-plate exposure, scar quality, and aesthetic restoration, and may be preferred in appropriate cases.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.

Fingertip injuries involving the nail-plate and nail bed are common and frequently result from workplace machinery accidents, motorcycle chain crush injuries, and door hinge trauma. The fingernail contributes to fine object manipulation, counterpressure during pinch, protection of the distal phalanx, and overall aesthetic appearance of the fingertip.1

Sommer and Brown2 suggested that when more than 25% of the nail bed distal to the eponychium remains intact, preservation of the nail is beneficial. Fassler3 similarly advised that when the residual nail bed measures less than 5 mm, nail ablation and revision amputation are appropriate. Shortening or loss of the nail bed may result in an unsatisfactory appearance and altered fingertip perception, even when soft-tissue coverage is adequate.4

Despite these guidelines, patients with borderline residual nail bed length may retain sufficient germinal matrix to permit nail regrowth, and reconstructive strategies aimed at improving the appearance of nail-plate exposure may be considered in selected cases. In such situations, techniques that modify the proximal nail-fold can increase the visible portion of the nail-plate without restoring lost nail tissue.

In 1998, Bakhach5 introduced the eponychial flap, a technique based on the longitudinal arterial branches of the dorsal distal digital arch. Both Bakhach5 and Adani et al6 reported satisfactory cosmetic outcomes using eponychial flap techniques for distal fingertip amputations. In parallel, a variety of local flaps including the Atasoy flap, Kutler flap, cross-finger flap, and Venkataswami flap have been described to provide soft-tissue coverage of the exposed distal phalanx, although these techniques primarily address pulp reconstruction rather than nail length or appearance.7, 8, 9, 10

The present study aimed to compare the eponychial folding flap and the rectangle recession flap techniques, with specific emphasis on their ability to increase the apparent exposure of the nail-plate and to evaluate associated aesthetic and functional outcomes following fingertip amputation. It is important to note at the outset that neither technique creates new nail tissue; both work by modifying the proximal nail-fold to expose a greater portion of the already-present nail-plate. The outcome is therefore best described as an improvement in apparent visible nail-plate exposure, not true nail regeneration.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This prospective, single-center study was conducted over a 12-month period from June 2023 to June 2024. A total of 60 patients with fingertip amputations were included and allocated into two equal groups (n = 30 each): group A (rectangle recession flap) and group B (eponychial folding flap). Institutional ethics committee approval was obtained prior to the commencement of the study. Informed oral and written consent was obtained from all participants; in cases involving minors, consent was obtained from a parent or guardian.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Patients were randomized using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, in keeping with CONSORT recommendations for allocation concealment. Sequence generation was performed using simple randomization in blocks of four, using computer-generated random numbers prepared by a biostatistician (Department of Biostatistics, SRM Institute of Science and Technology) who was not involved in patient care or surgical procedures. The sealed envelopes were stored securely by the biostatistician and were only opened at the time of surgery, immediately after induction of anesthesia, by the operating surgeon. The allocation was therefore concealed from the surgeon at the time of initial preoperative assessment and clinical decision-making; the assigned technique was revealed only at the point of incision planning. Complete blinding of the operating surgeon to the surgical technique is inherently not feasible in procedural studies; however, objective outcome assessments (nail-plate exposure measurement, Vancouver Scar Scale scoring) were performed by a coinvestigator not involved in the operative procedure, who was blinded to group allocation at follow-up.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients of all age groups were eligible for inclusion if they presented with fingertip amputations (transverse, dorsal oblique, or volar oblique) retaining more than 25% of the nail bed distal to the eponychium, with an intact eponychium and germinal matrix. Additional inclusion criteria were: (1) presentation within 48 hours of injury; (2) absence of significant crush injury or scarring of the proximal nail-fold; (3) no comminuted distal phalanx fracture precluding nail unit reconstruction; and (4) patient consent and willingness to participate in follow-up. Exclusion criteria included fingertip injuries with more than 75% loss of the nail complex, delayed presentation beyond 48 hours, severely crushed fingertips with comminuted distal phalanx fractures, crush injuries involving the proximal nail-fold, or significant scarring of the eponychial flap.

Preoperative assessment

Initial assessment was performed in the emergency department. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs were obtained to evaluate bony involvement. Clinical examination included documentation of residual nail bed length, presence or absence of the nail-plate, and evaluation of the pulp, hyponychium, paronychium, and distal phalanx. The extent of soft-tissue loss was compared to the contralateral digit. Surgical planning was based on the type and severity of the amputation.

Operative technique

Rectangle recession flap technique (group A)

This technique was performed under regional anesthesia with tourniquet control. A rectangle measuring 0.5–0.6 cm proximal to the distal border of the eponychium was marked. The skin within this rectangle was de-epithelialized, with the flap height adjusted to the required extent of nail-plate exposure (not exceeding 4 mm) and matched to the contralateral digit. Two longitudinal periungual incisions were made on either side of the nail-fold to release lateral attachments. Lateral undermining in the subcutaneous plane was performed alongside these incisions to adequately mobilize the flap and allow proximal advancement without excessive tension. The distal eponychial flap was slid proximally and sutured in place using interrupted 4-0 nonabsorbable or absorbable sutures (Figs. 1A and 2). The nail-plate, if present, was used as a splint. Eponychial folding flap technique (group b)

This technique was performed under regional anesthesia with tourniquet control. Two longitudinal parallel incisions were made on either side of the eponychium extending 3–4 mm proximally. The distal portion of the eponychial flap (approximately 2–3 mm) was elevated from the dorsal surface of the nail-plate by blunt dissection, maintaining the plane between the eponychium and the dorsal nail-plate surface. Critical to this step is that dissection remains superficial to the germinal matrix and its cul-de-sac (the ventral floor of the proximal nail-fold), thereby preserving germinal matrix integrity. The distal 2–3 mm of the elevated flap was de-epithelialized and this elevated segment was plicated proximally upon itself (inward folding), effectively shortening the eponychial fold and exposing the underlying proximal nail-plate. The plicated flap was secured using 4-0 nonabsorbable/absorbable sutures (Figs. 1B and 3). The remnant nail-plate was preserved wherever possible and used as a temporary stent to prevent adhesion between the eponychial fold and the nail bed. In cases where no intact nail-plate was available, a nonadherent dressing was placed to maintain the eponychial space.Figure 3Intraoperative steps of the eponychial folding flap technique. These nail-fold modification techniques are typically combined with various regional or local flaps (cross-finger flap, V-Y advancement flap, or split skin graft) for associated soft-tissue coverage, and are performed under regional/general anesthesia with tourniquet control. The procedure can also be carried out using Wide-Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet.

Operative time is defined as the duration from the first surgical incision related to nail reconstruction to completion of skin closure of the nail unit component, excluding anesthesia induction and closure of associated soft-tissue flaps.

Postoperative care and follow-up

Dressings were changed on postoperative days 3, 7, and 10. Flap discoloration and wound dehiscence were monitored for flap viability; sutures were removed on day 10. All outcome assessments were performed at 3-month follow-up. No patients in our cohort experienced compromised flap viability.

Outcome measures

The following standardized definitions were used throughout the study: (1) Residual nail bed length: the length of the nail bed (sterile and germinal matrix combined) remaining after amputation, measured intraoperatively from the hyponychium to the proximal nail-fold, in millimeters. (2) Visible nail-plate exposure: the length of the nail-plate visible distal to the eponychium at the time of assessment. (3) Apparent gain in visible nail-plate exposure: the difference in visible nail-plate exposure between the immediate postoperative and preoperative states, in millimeters (Figs. 4 and 5).Figure 4Intraoperative and follow-up photographs of the rectangle recession flap technique. Follow-up assessments at three months included: final apparent gain in visible nail-plate exposure, scar evaluation using the Vancouver Scar Scale, pain assessment using the Numeric Rating Scale-11 (NRS-11, 0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain), aesthetic satisfaction (patient-reported appearance score, 0–10), and functional assessment with the Purdue Pegboard Test. The Purdue Pegboard Test was included as a safety end point to confirm that the nail-fold modification did not adversely affect overall dexterity. It is acknowledged that this instrument is influenced by multiple factors including pulp bulk, joint motion, and concomitant flap type, making it a nonspecific measure for a nail-fold procedure per se.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp). All statistical analyses were performed and validated by a certified biostatistician (Department of Biostatistics, SRM Institute of Science and Technology). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Baseline comparability between groups was assessed using the chi-square test (or Fisher exact test where appropriate) for categorical variables, and the independent samples t test for continuous variables. For comparison of means between groups, the independent samples t test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. The chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used for categorical variables, as appropriate. Ninety-five percent CIs were calculated for the primary outcome. Cohen's d was used to report effect size for the primary continuous outcome. A post hoc power analysis was performed using G∗Power 3.1. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Concomitant flap distribution

As shown in Table 1, the distribution of concomitant reconstructive flaps (cross-finger flap: 11 vs 15; V-Y flap: 18 vs 15; split skin graft: 1 vs 0 in group A vs B, respectively) was compared using chi-square analysis, yielding P = .32, confirming no statistically significant difference between groups. A sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who received V-Y flaps (the largest subgroup, n = 33) confirmed that the directional findings for nail elongation and appearance scores were preserved in this more homogeneous subgroup.

Power analysis

A post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary outcome (apparent gain in visible nail-plate exposure). Using the observed effect size (Cohen's d = 1.22), n = 30 per group, and alpha = 0.05 (two-tailed), the achieved statistical power was 0.99, confirming that the study was adequately powered to detect the observed difference despite the absence of a prospective power calculation.

Discussion

The fingertip is anatomically defined as the portion of the finger distal to the insertion of the flexor digitorum superficialis and extensor tendons on the distal phalanx.11 The nail should protrude at least 2 mm from the eponychium to ensure precision grip and a favorable appearance.12 The dorsal surface comprises the nail-fold, nail bed, and nail-plate. Additional components include the eponychium (soft-tissue proximal to the nail), paronychium (lateral nail folds), and hyponychium (a keratinous plug beneath the nail's distal edge). The germinal matrix is responsible for approximately 90% of nail growth and plays a critical role in nail regeneration.13

It is essential to state clearly that both the rectangle recession flap and the eponychial folding flap do not create new nail tissue. These techniques modify the proximal nail-fold to increase the proportion of the already-present nail-plate that is visible distal to the eponychium. The outcome is therefore best described as an improvement in apparent visible nail-plate exposure, not true nail regeneration. This conceptual distinction is of paramount importance for accurate interpretation of the results and patient counseling.

Restoring adequate nail-plate exposure is vital, as a shortened nail bed can result in reduced pulp stability, compromised fine motor dexterity, and unsatisfactory cosmetic outcomes.14 In this study, the eponychial folding flap provided significantly greater apparent visible nail-plate exposure (mean 3.27 mm vs 2.66 mm; P < .001; mean difference 0.61 mm, 95% CI, 0.35–0.87 mm; Cohen's d = 1.22), aligning with the observations of Bakhach and Adani.5,6

Pain scores assessed using the NRS-11 were significantly lower in the eponychial folding flap group on postoperative day 12 (mean 0.47 vs 1.07; P = .007), likely because of the minimal dissection and reduced tissue manipulation involved. In terms of aesthetic outcomes, the eponychial folding flap achieved a significantly higher patient-reported appearance score (mean 8.83 vs 7.63; P < .001), supporting its superiority for cosmetic restoration.

Scar evaluation using the Vancouver Scar Scale showed no significant difference between the groups (P = .409), indicating that both techniques yielded comparable scar maturation. Similarly, functional outcomes assessed by the Purdue Pegboard Test were equivalent, confirming that both approaches did not adversely affected manual dexterity. The Purdue Pegboard Test was used as a safety end point in this study; the absence of a between-group difference is expected and reassuring, but does not reflect an efficacy advantage of either nail technique specifically, given the instrument's sensitivity to multiple hand factors beyond the nail-fold modification. Future studies may consider the peg test described by Noel et al15 as a complementary dexterity tool, particularly in patients immobilized with orthoses after surgery.

The operative time difference (2 minutes shorter in group B) is statistically significant but clinically negligible. The choice of technique should be guided by anticipated aesthetic and functional outcomes rather than operative speed.

Both techniques in this study were combined with concomitant reconstructive flaps (cross-finger flap, V-Y flap, or split skin graft) for soft-tissue coverage. The distribution of flap types between groups was statistically comparable (P = .32). Nevertheless, we acknowledge that concomitant flap type may influence pain scores, scar quality, pulp contour, and patient satisfaction, and therefore represents a potential confounding variable. A sensitivity analysis restricted to V-Y flap recipients (n = 33) preserved the directional findings. Future studies with larger samples should perform adjusted analyses to more definitively isolate the contribution of the nail-fold procedure from that of the associated soft-tissue reconstruction.

The discussion of nail-fold modification techniques should also be contextualized within broader reconstructive algorithms for distal fingertip and nail complex restoration. The clover flap described by Pangrazi et al16 represents an important advancement, providing composite soft-tissue and nail-related reconstruction in a single-stage procedure for suitable fingertip defects. While the Pangrazi clover flap and fold-modification techniques address different aspects of fingertip reconstruction, local tissue rearrangement versus fold-based nail-plate exposure, they may serve complementary roles: fold-modification techniques are applicable when the nail-plate is preserved but concealed, whereas the clover flap addresses composite soft-tissue and nail defects. Understanding these distinctions is important for selecting the most appropriate reconstructive strategy for each patient.

Although this study primarily included adult patients, fingertip injuries are also common in pediatric populations, where long-term nail appearance and fingertip development are critical considerations. Effective reconstructive strategies in children must be adapted to accommodate ongoing growth and functional maturation.15

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results: (1) Follow-up duration: The follow-up period was 3 months. For nail-related outcomes including nail curvature, plate symmetry, proximal fold contour, and scar maturation, a minimum of 6–12 months is generally required for reliable evaluation. Our 3-month data therefore represent early outcomes only; long-term durability cannot be established from this study alone. A prospective 12-month follow-up study is planned. (2) Sample size: The sample size was not calculated prospectively; however, post hoc power analysis demonstrated adequate power (0.99) for the primary outcome. (3) Concomitant flap confounding: The type of associated soft-tissue reconstruction may influence secondary outcomes including pain, scar, and dexterity, despite comparable group distributions. (4) Blinding: Complete blinding of the operating surgeon is not feasible in a surgical technique comparison study. Patient-reported outcomes are subject to reporting bias. No blinded independent observer assessment of aesthetic outcomes was performed; future studies should incorporate this. (5) Single-center design limits external generalizability.

Both the rectangle recession flap and the eponychial folding flap are viable techniques for improving apparent visible nail-plate exposure in fingertip reconstruction following amputation. Importantly, neither technique creates new nail tissue; both expose the concealed proximal nail-plate by modifying the proximal nail-fold. The eponychial folding flap demonstrated statistically superior outcomes in terms of apparent nail-plate exposure (mean difference 0.61 mm; 95% CI, 0.35–0.87 mm; P < .001), aesthetic satisfaction, and postoperative pain, without compromising scar quality or functional recovery. The rectangle recession flap remains a valid alternative, particularly when the eponychial folding flap is technically not feasible. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and adjusted analyses for concomitant reconstruction are recommended to validate these findings.
