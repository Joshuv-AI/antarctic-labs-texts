Chylothorax and chylous ascites in AIDS-associated Kaposi sarcoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Qunqun Jiang, Kai Peng, Zhongwei Zhang, Xiaoping Chen, Qian Du, Liping Deng, Yong Xiong, Zhiyong Ma, Yongxi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKaposi sarcoma (KS) is one of the most common tumors in patients with AIDS, while the occurrence of chylous effusions in KS patients is rare and often indicates a poor prognosis. This report present a case of successfully treated AIDS-associated KS complicated by both chylothorax and chylous ascites, along with a review of the relevant literature.Case presentationA 31-year-old male patient, who was recently diagnosed with HIV infection and oral KS at our clinic center, returned two weeks post-discharge. He reported significant enlargement of the oral tumor, increased abdominal girth with accompanying abdominal distension and pain, and a dry cough over the past week. After eliminating other potential etiologies for the chylous effusion, we associated it with the progression of KS. Prompt initiation of systemic chemotherapy combined with antiretroviral therapy is critical for improving outcomes.Keywords: Kaposi sarcoma, Chylothorax, Chylous ascites, Case report, Literature review

Background

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is an angioproliferative malignancy caused by infection with Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), also known as human herpesvirus 8 (HHV8) [1, 2]. It represents one of the most common AIDS-defining malignancies and predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with uncontrolled HIV infection [3]. KS typically presents with cutaneous lesions but can also involve mucosal surfaces and visceral organs, most frequently the lungs and gastrointestinal tract [1, 2]. While the epidemiological and virological aspects of KS are well established, its varied clinical manifestations and complications continue to pose significant management challenges.

Chylothorax and chylous ascites are rare but serious complications in patients with KS, associated with considerably elevated morbidity and mortality [4–6]. The presence of chylous effusions often signifies advanced disease and underscores the critical need for timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary management. In this report, we present a case of successfully treated AIDS-related KS complicated by both chylothorax and chylous ascites, and review the pertinent literature to highlight diagnostic and therapeutic considerations.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old male patient with a two-month history of fever and rash was admitted to our center on September 22, 2023. Physical examination revealed variously sized skin lesions on the face, upper limbs, and back, which were crateriform and protruding. Additionally, a small purplish-red nodule, approximately 1 cm by 1 cm in size, was noted on the hard palate of the oral cavity. The patient presented with a peak temperature of 39.8 °C. Laboratory tests confirmed an HIV-positive serology, with an initial viral load of 5.3 × 10⁶ copies/mL and a CD4 count of 10 cells/µL. Given his profoundly low CD4 count, the patient was initiated on cotrimoxazole prophylaxis to prevent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. Blood and bone marrow cultures identified the presence of penicillium marneffei, prompting antifungal treatment with amphotericin B. After a two-week course of treatment, the regimen was switched to oral itraconazole for maintenance. Following resolution of the patient’s fever and rash, a biopsy of the oral nodule was performed, which confirmed Kaposi’s sarcoma. Considering the patient’s severely immunocompromised state and the fact that the KS at presentation was limited to a single oral lesion, a joint decision was made with the patient to prioritize the immediate initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and closely monitor for KS progression, deferring systemic chemotherapy at that time. The patient was discharged on October 18 and began antiretroviral therapy(ART) with Biktarvy (bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide) on October 30.

Later, the patient returned to our center on November 2, 2023, reporting an enlargement of the oral nodule, difficulty chewing, and new symptoms including significant abdominal distension, abdominal pain, and dry cough within the past week. Upon readmission, the previously noted purplish-red nodule on the hard palate had grown significantly in size to approximately 3 cm by 3 cm, showing surface erosion (Fig. 1a). A new purplish-red nodule, approximately 1 cm in diameter, was found on the abdominal wall, protruding from the skin surface without pain and non-blanching upon pressure (Fig. 1e). The patient’s lower lung fields showed reduced breath sounds, and the abdomen was distended with tenderness and positive moveable dullness.

Routine blood tests indicated moderate anemia, with hemoglobin levels at 76 g/L. Liver and kidney functions were largely normal, except for a low albumin level (24.3 g/L). Electrolyte potassium (K+) was measured at 3.23 mmol/L. Inflammatory markers showed normal procalcitonin levels, while interleukin-6 was 35.0 pg/mL. The patient’s chest computed tomography(CT) shows solid nodules in both lungs, bilateral pleural effusion with atelectasis of the right lung; abdominal CT shows slightly enlarged liver and spleen, a large amount of ascites in the abdominal and pelvic cavity; multiple lymph nodes behind the peritoneum, some of which are slightly enlarged (Fig. 2a, d, g). Subsequently, thoracentesis and paracentesis were performed, and the fluid obtained from both cavities appeared milky white, suggesting a consideration of chylous leakage (Fig. 3). The pleural fluid analysis showed a positive mucin clot test; the nucleated cell count was 1613 cells/uL, predominantly mononuclear, protein 48.6 g/L, and glucose was normal. The ascitic fluid analysis also indicated a positive mucin clot test; nucleated cell count is 342 cells/uL, predominantly mononuclear; ascitic fluid protein was 43.3 g/L; glucose level was normal. The bacterial smears, acid-fast staining, microbial cultures, and mycobacterium tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (GeneXpert) tests for both pleural and ascitic fluids were all negative. Additionally, cytological examination did not reveal any tumor cells, and pathogenic microorganism metagenomic next-generation sequencing(mNGS) was also negative. Unfortunately, triglyceride levels could not be measured due to assay limitations.

The patient exhibited an enlargement of oral lesions associated with KS, along with the emergence of new skin lesions and the accumulation of pleural effusion and ascites, with no other pathogen infections and tumour detected, leading us to consider progression of the KS. Consequently, while continuing antiretroviral therapy (ART), the patient was initiated on systemic chemotherapy with liposomal paclitaxel at a dose of 150 mg/m², along with intermittent drainage to relieve symptoms associated with the pleural effusion and ascites. Following the first cycle of chemotherapy, the pleural effusion and ascites remained stable without progression. The patient subsequently received the second and third cycles of chemotherapy on a 14-day interval. Before the second chemotherapy session, a chest and abdominal CT scan showed a significant reduction in pleural effusion and ascites (Fig. 2b, e, h), with noticeable shrinkage of the oral and skin lesions (Fig. 1b, f). The CT scan before the third chemotherapy session indicated the nearly complete resolution of pleural effusion and ascites (Fig. 2c, f, i), with further regression of the oral and skin lesions (Fig. 1c, g). Following the third chemotherapy session, the patient elected not to continue with further chemotherapy treatments. Six months after treatment initiation, a telephone follow-up was conducted. The patient reported effective disease control with significant clinical improvement. Oral and abdominal wall lesions had substantially regressed, leaving only mild residual pigmentation (Fig. 1d and h). No new lesions had emerged, and there was no recurrence of pleural effusion or ascites. Laboratory studies showed an undetectable viral load (< 20 copies/mL) and the CD4 count had increased to 118 cells/uL.

Discussion

Although KS frequently infiltrates internal organs such as the lungs and gastrointestinal tract, cases presenting with chylothorax and chylous ascites are seldom reported and typically have a poor prognosis. In this article, we present a case of successful management of an AIDS patient with KS who developed both chylothorax and chylous ascites. The patient’s condition was successfully managed with antiretroviral therapy, systemic chemotherapy, and intermittent thoracoabdominal paracentesis for drainage.

Chylothorax and chylous ascites refer to the accumulation of chyle within the pleural or peritoneal cavities, typically caused by obstruction or damage to the lymphatic drainage system, leading to the leakage of chylomicrons into these spaces. This situation can occur due to various causes, such as traumatic injuries, iatrogenic complications, malignancies, idiopathic conditions, infectious diseases, or inflammatory processes [7, 8]. Chyle contains a high concentration of triglycerides, proteins, and lymphocytes. The development of chylous effusions can lead to nutritional depletion, a decrease in immune function, and an increased susceptibility to infections, significantly increasing both mortality and morbidity [9]. Reported mortality rates for chylous ascites range between 40% and 70%, while the 90-day mortality rate for chylothorax varies from < 10% to 82% [7]. Cases of KS associated with chylous effusions are rarely reported. Our search of previous literature on PubMed revealed only 18 cases, among which 17 were HIV-positive patients and one was an HIV-negative patient; 13 had chylothorax, 2 had chylous ascites, and 3 had both. After treatment, 6 patients survived while 12 died, resulting in a mortality rate of approximately 66.7% (Table 1) [4–6, 9–23].

The pathogenesis of chylothorax or chylous ascites in patients with KS remains incompletely understood. Potential mechanisms include direct lymphatic invasion or obstruction by KS lesions. In particular, involvement of the thoracic duct or retroperitoneal lymph nodes may disrupt normal chyle transport, a hypothesis supported by autopsy studies demonstrating KS infiltration of these structures [15]. Additionally, extensive visceral KS involvement—such as in the bronchial tree, omentum, or gastrointestinal tract—may contribute to lymphatic leakage [18]. In the context of AIDS-associated KS, chylous effusions may also arise as part of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) following initiation of antiretroviral therapy [1, 9]. However, it is essential to note that people living with HIV are at increased risk of other conditions that can cause chylous effusions, including tuberculosis, nontuberculous mycobacterial infections (e.g., Mycobacterium avium complex), and lymphoma. Therefore, a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation to exclude these alternative etiologies is necessary before attributing chylous effusions solely to KS.

In this case, the patient began ART on October 30, 2023, and was readmitted on November 2, 2023. At that time, he reported having experienced symptoms such as chest tightness and abdominal distension for approximately one week. Therefore, it is inferred that the chylous ascites developed prior to the initiation of ART, thus they are not considered to be related to an immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome. The microbiological and cytological examinations of the patient’s pleural and ascitic fluids were all negative, there is no evidence of other causes leading to chylous effusion. Given the patient’s enlarging oral primary lesion and new abdominal wall lesions, we consider the chylous effusions to be associated with the progression of KS. Although further biopsies or lymphangiography were not conducted to confirm our suspicions, the patient’s condition significantly improved after starting systemic chemotherapy targeting KS, which supported our diagnostic considerations.

The clinical management of KS is determined by the patient’s clinical presentation, disease staging, immune status, and comorbidities. Combined antiretroviral therapy (cART) is the mainstay of treatment for AIDS-related KS, with response rates to cART monotherapy ranging from 20% to 76%. Systemic chemotherapy should be considered for cases with extensive or painful skin disease, visceral involvement, or rapidly progressive disease [1].

Chylothorax and chylous ascites have long been perplexing clinical issues, with no standardized and consistently effective treatment. Besides treatments targeting the underlying cause, reported treatments include thoracentesis and abdominocentesis for drainage, ligation of the thoracic duct, pleurodesis, pleurectomy, thoracoabdominal shunting, use of somatostatin analogs, and dietary modifications, but patient’s outcomes are inconsistent [7]. Accordingly, a comprehensive review of the relevant literature was performed. Natarajan and Miller reported a case of bilateral chylothorax due to visceral KS that did not improve with repeated aspiration or talc pleurodesis but eventually responded to paclitaxel chemotherapy [12]. Cipriano and colleagues described a patient with AIDS and widespread KS who had both chylous ascites and pleural effusion. Despite undergoing regular treatment with liposomal doxorubicin chemotherapy, antiretroviral therapy, periodic puncture drainage, and nutritional support, the patient’s condition failed to improve [9]. Cherian et al. depicted a case of chylothorax in an HIV-associated KS patient that showed improvement after four months of systemic chemotherapy, a medium chain triglyceride (MCT) diet, percutaneous chest tube drainage, and talc pleurodesis treatment [10].

Collectively, the literature reveals that, apart from symptomatic supportive therapy, timely systemic chemotherapy is a common factor among KS patients with chylous effusions who achieved a favorable prognosis [10, 12, 14, 20–22]. This underscores the critical role of chemotherapy in managing this condition. Systemic chemotherapy is indicated for patients with extensive disease, visceral involvement, or symptoms not controlled by local therapy or antiretroviral therapy (ART). First-line regimens include liposomal doxorubicin or paclitaxel. The historical combination of doxorubicin, bleomycin, and vincristine (ABV regimen) remains an option, though its use has declined due to greater toxicity compared to liposomal agents. Other chemotherapeutic options include vinblastine, etoposide, and gemcitabine, often used in combination with ART. The choice of regimen is highly individualized and must be made by an oncologist based on the patient’s specific condition, comorbidities, and immune status. Guided by this principle, in our case, the patient also achieved a positive outcome after being treated with chemotherapy in addition to antiretroviral therapy and local symptomatic treatment.

A limitation of this report is that triglyceride levels were not measured in the effusions, which is a definitive biochemical test for chylous effusion. However, the classic milky appearance, along with the high protein content and lymphocytic predominance, provides strong supportive evidence for the diagnosis. This combination of findings is highly characteristic of chylous leakage, as outlined in established literature.

Conclusions

For patients with known HIV/AIDS and Kaposi sarcoma, the development of chylous effusions should be recognized as a sign of disease progression. Both the present case and previous literature suggest that a combination of systemic chemotherapy and antiretroviral therapy may contribute to disease control and improved outcomes in certain cases. Therefore, early diagnosis and prompt initiation of systemic therapy are critical. Further large-scale, collaborative studies remain necessary to establish more effective and standardized treatment strategies.
