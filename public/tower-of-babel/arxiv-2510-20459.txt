Mass-Radius relationship and Gravitational Wave Emission from Spheroidal Magnetized Quark Stars
Rajasmita Sahoo, Arunkarthiheyan Thiyagarajan, Asutosh Panda, Somnath Mukhopadhyay

Abstract
In this work we have studied the masses, equatorial radii and gravitational wave strain amplitudes of strongly magnetized oblate spheroidal quark stars. We have used an anisotropic Equation of State (EoS) based on the MIT bag model, extended to incorporate the effects of strong magnetic fields and the resulting pressure anisotropy due to the breaking of spatial rotational symmetry. Our analysis includes both magnetized free strange quark matter (MSQM) and magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phases. The masses and equatorial radii are computed using the stellar structure equations in presence of the γ \gamma -metric. We also computed the ellipticity, the gravitational redshift, the mass quadrupole moment and the tidal deformability for two values of bag constants: 65 M ​ e ​ V / f ​ m 3 MeV/fm^{3} and 75 M ​ e ​ V / f ​ m 3 MeV/fm^{3} , for both MSQM and MCFL phases. Additionally, using the mass quadrupole moment, we have calculated the gravitational wave strain amplitudes for isolated deformed rotating magnetized quark stars.



1
Introduction

Compact stars are extraordinary astrophysical laboratories where elementary particles interact under extreme conditions of density, pressure and magnetic fields. Among the various classes of compact stars, strange quark stars-hypothetical stars composed entirely of deconfined up
(u)
, down
(d)
and strange
(s)
quarks, which offer a unique opportunity to explore the inner structure of matter governed by the strong nuclear force. This idea, originally proposed by Bodmer and later developed by Witten, suggests that the strange quark matter could represent the true ground state of hadronic matter at zero pressure, provided the baryon density is sufficiently high
Bodmer 1971
;
Witten 1984
. The transition from hadronic matter to strange quark matter represents a first order phase transition predicted by quantum chromodynamics under extreme conditions
Iwasaki 2004
. Two distinct scenarios are considered likely for this transition. The first occurs at high temperatures and low baryon chemical potentials, conditions believed to have existed in the early universe, leading to the formation of a quark-gluon plasma. The second, more relevant to astrophysical compact objects occurs at low temperatures and extremely high baryon densities, several times the nuclear saturation density
n_{0}=0.16\;fm^{-3}

Bombaci et al. 2011
. In such environments, the energy density becomes sufficient to deconfine quarks from nucleons. The generation of strangeness through weak interactions then leads to a chemically equilibrated mixture of
u
,
d
and
s
quarks. If this conversion extends throughout the star’s interior, the resulting configuration may stabilize as a self-bound strange quark star
Itoh 1970
;
Alcock et al. 1986
. It has been conjectured that such a transition could occur rapidly after a core-collapse supernova, bypassing the neutron star phase altogether. Strange quark stars are of great interest in theoretical astrophysics because they are believed to be very dense objects with small sizes
\sim 8
km and masses close to
1.5\;M_{\odot}
. These properties are difficult to explain using standard neutron star models, suggesting that strange quark stars might be an alternative way to explain certain cold, dense stars that do not fit into regular models of matter
Prakash et al. 2003
;
Abubekerov et al. 2008
;
Gangopadhyay et al. 2013
;
Rawls et al. 2011
. At very high densities, quark matter behaves differently from normal matter, and the equations of state that describe it are usually softer, especially because of the strange quarks in strange quark matter. This softness limits how massive these stars can be. However, recent measurements of very massive pulsars-like PSR J0348+0432
(2.01\pm 0.04\;M_{\odot})
, PSR J0740+6620
(2.08\pm 0.07\;M_{\odot})
, PSR J2215+5135
(2.27^{+0.17}_{-0.15}\;M_{\odot})
, and PSR J0952-0607
(2.35\pm 0.17\;M_{\odot})
, challenge these soft models, as they are too massive for many of the current theories
Antoniadis 2013
;
Miller et al. 2021
;
Linares et al. 2018
;
Romani et al. 2022a
. To explain these massive stars, some models use stiffer equations of state for quark matter, which allow for higher mass stars and keep the idea of strange quark stars as a possibility
Alford and Reddy 2003
;
Baldo et al. 2003
;
Lai and Xu 2011
;
Bonanno and Sedrakian 2012
;
Chu et al. 2017
. A key factor that may explain the existence of such high mass compact stars is the presence of strong magnetic field. Many compact stars, particularly magnetars, possess extremely strong magnetic field, with surface magnetic field strength exceeding
10^{15}
Gauss and the core magnetic field value possibly reaching beyond
5\times 10^{18}
Gauss
Mihara et al. 1990
;
Thompson and Duncan 1993
. This extreme magnetic field not only modifies the Equation of State (EoS) through Landau quantization but also introduces pressure anisotropy due to the anisotropic nature of the stress-energy tensor. In such a case, the pressure perpendicular to the magnetic field
(P_{\perp})
differs from the pressure parallel to it
(P_{\parallel})
, thereby breaking the spherical symmetry of the star and inducing axisymmetric deformation
Ferrer et al. 2010
;
Bali et al. 2014
;
Konno et al. 1999
;
Chatterjee et al. 2015
;
Alvear Terrero et al. 2019
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
. Recent studies, such as Ref.
Terrero et al. 2021
, the authors investigated magnetized strange quark stars by assuming a constant magnetic field throughout the stellar interior. Using this simplification, they constructed the equation of state (EoS) and analyzed the stellar structures using the
\gamma
-metric formalism, which accounts for pressure anisotropy. Their study found that the maximum mass at a constant magnetic field strength of
B=1\times 10^{18}
Gauss was around
1.83\;M_{\odot}
. However, such results based on unpaired magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) may underestimate the possible stiffness of the EoS at higher densities. At such extreme densities, quark pairing becomes energetically favorable, the system may transition into a more stable phase known as the color-flavor locked (CFL) phase, in which all quark flavors and colors form Cooper pairs analogous to those in electronic superconductors
Alford et al. 1999
;
Alford et al. 1998
;
Alford et al. 2008
.
A particularly intriguing feature of the CFL phase is the role of the rotated photon, denoted
\tilde{A}_{\mu}
. In QCD, although color superconductivity breaks the conventional electromagnetic
U(1)_{em}
gauge symmetry and color
SU(3)
symmetry
Alford et al. 1999
, a residual unbroken
U(1)
symmetry remain - a linear combination of the ordinary photon
(A_{\mu})
and a gluon field (specially
G_{\mu}^{8}
, the
8^{th}
gluon), that is not broken by the quark pairing. The rotated photon
\tilde{A}_{\mu}=A_{\mu}\cos\theta+G^{8}_{\mu}\sin\theta
associated with this symmetry remains massless and long-ranged like ordinary photon, unlike in QED-based superconductors where the photon acquires a mass via the Meissner effect. The corresponding electromagnetic coupling constant is
\tilde{e}=e\cos\theta
, where the mixing angle
\theta
arises due to the combination of the electromagnetic and color gauge fields, depends on the pairing (gap) structure and is given as
\cos\theta=g/\sqrt{e^{2}/3+g^{2}}
, where
g
is the QCD (strong) coupling constant and
e
is the electromagnetic coupling constant for the CFL phase. As a result, magnetic field
\tilde{B}
associated with this rotated electromagnetic field can penetrated the CFL phase, unlike in conventional superconductors where magnetic field is expelled. This crucial difference means the CFL phase does not exhibit a standard Meisser effect, and instead allows the external magnetic field to exist inside the superconducting quark matter. This fundamentally alters the magnetic response of quark stars in comparison to neutron stars or ordinary superconductors. Furthermore, the electromagnetic coupling is much smaller than the strong coupling
(e<<g)
, which implies
\cos\theta=1
. Therefore, one can reasonably approximate the rotated and ordinary electromagnetic fields are being nearly
\tilde{e}\tilde{B}=eB

Alford et al. 2000
;
Gorbar 2000
;
Fukushima and Warringa 2008
. In addition to magnetic field and pairing effects, a consistent treatment of the CFL phase must enforce strict charge neutrality conditions to ensure physical stability inside compact stars
Alford and Rajagopal 2002
. These conditions apply to both electromagnetic and color charges. Electromagnetic charge neutrality demands that the net electric charge density vanishes, which is typically achieved by adjusting the quark chemical potentials and when necessary, including electrons to balance any residual charge
Rajagopal and Wilczek 2001a
. However, color charge neutrality ensures that all color charge densities vanish, preventing the development of color fields that would destabilize the system
Buballa and Shovkovy 2005
.
To ensure color neutrality, color chemical potentials, such as
\mu_{3}
and
\mu_{8}
are introduced. These are associated with the diagonal generators
T_{3}=diag(1/3,1/3,-2/3)
and
T_{8}=diag(1/3,1/3,-2/3)
of the
SU(3)
color group and must be chosen in such a way that the corresponding color charge densities vanish.
The chemical potential for each quark flavor
i\in[u,d,s]
is then determined by its electric charge and color charges according to:
\mu_{i}=\mu_{B}-Q_{i}\mu_{e}+T_{3i}\mu_{3}+T_{8i}\mu_{8}
, where
\mu_{B}
is the baryon chemical potential,
\mu_{e}
is the electron chemical potential, and
Q=diag(2/3,-1/3,-1/3)
is the electric charge matrix. A careful analysis of the neutrality conditions in the CFL phase shows that, in the regime
eB<\mu_{B}^{2}
, the color chemical potential simplify:
\mu_{3}=0
,while
\mu_{8}=-m_{s}^{2}/\mu_{B}
, where
m_{s}
is the strange quark mass. This approximation reduces the complexity of the system while preserving the essential physics of color and electric neutrality
González Felipe et al. 2011a
. In Ref.
González Felipe et al. 2011a
, the authors studied the magnetized CFL phase in compact stars and solved the TOV equations, obtaining a maximum mass
M<2\;M_{\odot}
for a pairing gap parameter
\Delta=100\;MeV
under an extremely strong magnetic field of
1\times 10^{19}
Gauss. In Ref.
Manreza Paret et al. 2015
, the authors investigated both magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) and the magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phase in the context of magnetized quark stars, using an anisotropic equation of state (EoS) that accounts for the pressure anisotropy induced by strong magnetic fields. They found that the maximum mass significantly increases in the MCFL phase with pairing gap
\Delta=100\;MeV
, exceeding
M>3\;M_{\odot}
at magnetic field strength of
B=5\times 10^{17}
Gauss, compared to the MSQM case. The upper limit of magnetic field strength considered was
B=1\times 10^{18}
Gauss. The TOV equations were solved assuming that all variables depend only on the perpendicular (equatorial) radius. Also, in Ref.
Goswami et al. 2023
, the authors found that the maximum mass of quark star in the color-flavor locked (CFL) phase is approximately
3.61\;M_{\odot}
, considering variable strange quark mass
(m_{s})
and density-dependent bag constant.
In this work, we investigate the stellar structure of spheroidal magnetized quark stars by employing an anisotropic equation of state (EoS) for two distinct phases of quark matter: magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) and the magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phase. For our analysis, we consider two representative values of the bag constant,
B_{bag}=65\;MeV/fm^{3}
and
75\;MeV/fm^{3}
, to explore the effect of confinement pressure. For the MCFL phase, we further include the effects of color superconductivity by assuming constant pairing gap parameters
\Delta=30\;MeV
and
50\;MeV
, which significantly influence the EoS and star stability under strong magnetic fields. In our study, we set the upper limit of the magnetic field strength to
B=1.5\times 10^{18}
Gauss, and the magnetic field is taken to be density-dependent inside the dense deformed magnetized quark star and also include the pressure anisotropy induced by the strong magnetic field and solve the modified TOV equations using the
\gamma
-metric formalism, which accounts axisymmetric deformation
Terrero et al. 2021
.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we present the formulation of the anisotropic EoS for MSQM followed by the MCFL phase in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we solve the stellar structure equations using the obtained anisotropic EoSs, incorporating a density-dependent magnetic field profile discussed in Section
5
. In Section
6
, we present and analyze our results, and finally, we conclude our work in Section
7
.

2
Anisotropic Equation of State of Magnetized Free Strange Quark Matter

In this case, we consider the magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) consisting of free up, down and strange quarks along with electrons in the presence of strong magnetic field. The matter is beta-equilibrated and charge neutral. The magnetic field
B
is assumed to be aligned along the z direction
(B=(0,0,B))
, causing quarks and electrons to occupy quantized Landau levels in the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field
B
. For the anisotropic EoS, we use the phenomenological MIT bag model, which treats quarks as quasi-free particles confined within a hypothetical "bag". This model effectively captures the principles of asymptotic freedom and confinement through the Bag constant B
bag
, a binding energy term that contributes to the total energy density while reducing the quark pressure
Chodos et al. 1974
. For our calculations, we adopt fixed quark masses and charges,
m_{u}=2.2
MeV,
m_{d}=4.8
MeV,
m_{s}=95
MeV with corresponding electric charges,
e_{u}=+\frac{2}{3}e
and
e_{d}=e_{s}=-\frac{1}{3}e
. The rest mass of electron is
m_{e}=0.511
MeV.

The total energy of a charged fermion (quark or electron) is quantized into Landau levels and given by
Strickland et al. 2012
;
Mukhopadhyay et al. 2017
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
,

E_{\nu,p_{z}}^{(i)}=[{p_{z}^{2}c^{2}}+m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})]^{1/2}.

(1)

where
i\in[u,d,s,e]
denotes the particle species,
p_{z}
is the component of the momentum along the magnetic field, and
m_{i}
is the rest mass of the particle. The Landau level index is defined as
\nu
=
n+\frac{1}{2}+s_{z}
, where
n=0,1,2,...
is the Landau levels and
s_{z}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
is the spin projection along the field direction. The dimensionless magnetic field
B_{D}^{(i)}=\frac{B}{B_{c}^{(i)}}
, where
B
is the magnetic field strength and
B_{c}^{(i)}
=
\frac{m_{i}^{2}c^{3}}{e_{i}\hbar}
is the critical magnetic field for each species.

The density of states (including spin degeneracy) for each particle species
i\in[u,d,s,e]
is given by,

\sum_{\nu}\frac{2\pi}{h^{2}}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}g_{\nu}\int\limits\frac{dp_{z}}{h}.

(2)

where
g_{\nu}
is the spin degeneracy factor, with
g_{\nu}=1
for
\nu=0
and
g_{\nu}=2
for
\nu\geq 1
. At absolute zero temperature, the Fermi-Dirac distribution function simplifies to,

f(E)=\begin{cases}1,\;\mbox{for }E\leq E_{F}^{(i)}\\
0,\;\mbox{for }E>E_{F}^{(i)}\end{cases}

(3)

where,
E_{F}^{(i)}
is the Fermi energy of the species
i
, defining the maximum occupied energy level. This condition imposes a constraint on
p_{z}
, such that:

p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)=\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{{\left(E_{F}^{(i)}\right)}^{2}-m_{i}^{2}c^{4}\left(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)}\right)}.

(4)

For each species, the Landau level index is constrained by the condition,

\nu\leq\nu_{max}^{(i)}
=
\dfrac{1}{2B_{D}^{(i)}}\left(\dfrac{{\left(E_{F}^{(i)}\right)}^{2}}{m_{i}^{2}c^{4}}-1\right)

ensuring that only a finite number of Landau levels are populated at a given magnetic field strength.
Since magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) consists of fluid of free quarks and electrons in the presence of strong magnetic field, the equation of state (EoS) for magnetized SQM can be written as derived in Refs.
Strickland et al. 2012
;
Mukhopadhyay et al. 2017
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
. The number density
n_{i}
of any charged fermion species
i
(quark or electron) in MSQM phase as,

\displaystyle\begin{split}n_{i}&=\dfrac{2\pi}{h^{2}}f_{i}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(E)\frac{dp_{z}}{h},\\
&=\dfrac{4\pi}{h^{2}}f_{i}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\int\limits_{0}^{p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)}\frac{dp_{z}}{h},\\
&=\dfrac{4\pi}{h^{3}}f_{i}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\,{p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)}\\
\end{split}

(5)

where,
f_{i}
is denoted as the flavor degeneracy factor. For each quark,
f_{u,d,s}=3
and for electron,
f_{e}=1
.
The energy density
\epsilon_{i}
of each particle species (quark or electron) in the presence of magnetic field in MSQM phase is given as,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\epsilon_{i}&=\dfrac{2\pi}{h^{2}}f_{i}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(E)E_{\nu,p_{z}}^{(i)}\frac{dp_{z}}{h},\\
&=\dfrac{4\pi}{h^{2}}f_{i}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\times\int\limits_{0}^{p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)}\sqrt{{p_{z}^{2}c^{2}}+m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})}\,\frac{dp_{z}}{h},\\
&=\dfrac{2\pi}{h^{3}}f_{i}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\,\Bigg(E_{F}^{(i)}\;\;p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)+m_{i}^{2}c^{3}\times\\
&(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})\log_{e}\left(\frac{E_{F}^{(i)}+p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)c}{\sqrt{m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})}}\right)\Bigg).\\
\end{split}

(6)

The parallel pressure (z-direction) of each particle species (quark or electron) in the direction of the magnetic field in MSQM phase is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\parallel,i}&=f_{i}B_{D}^{(i)}\frac{2\pi m_{i}^{2}c^{2}}{h^{3}}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\int\limits_{p_{z}=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{c^{2}p_{z}^{2}}{E_{\nu,p_{z}}^{(i)}}f(E)dp_{z},\\
&=f_{i}B_{D}^{(i)}\frac{4\pi m_{i}^{2}c^{2}}{h^{3}}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\times\int\limits_{0}^{p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)}\frac{c^{2}p_{z}^{2}}{\sqrt{{p_{z}^{2}c^{2}}+m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})}}\,dp_{z},\\
&=f_{i}\dfrac{2\pi}{h^{3}}m_{i}^{2}c^{2}B_{D}^{(i)}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\,\Bigg(E_{F}^{(i)}\;p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)-m_{i}^{2}c^{3}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})\\
&\log_{e}\left(\frac{E_{F}^{(i)}+p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)c}{\sqrt{m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})}}\right)\Bigg).\end{split}

(7)

The perpendicular pressure (x- and y- directions) of each particle species (quark or electron) in the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field in MSQM phase is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\perp,i}&=f_{i}\frac{m_{i}^{2}c^{4}}{2}{\left(B_{D}^{(i)}\right)}^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi m_{i}^{2}c^{2}}{h^{3}}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\times\int\limits_{p_{z}=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{2\nu}{E_{\nu,p_{z}}^{(i)}}f(E)dp_{z},\\
&=f_{i}{m_{i}^{2}c^{4}}{\left(B_{D}^{(i)}\right)}^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi m_{i}^{2}c^{2}}{h^{3}}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\times\\
&\int\limits_{0}^{p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)}\frac{2\nu}{\sqrt{{p_{z}^{2}c^{2}}+m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})}}\,dp_{z},\\
&=f_{i}{m_{i}^{2}c^{3}}{\left(B_{D}^{(i)}\right)}^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi m_{i}^{2}c^{2}}{h^{3}}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{\nu_{max}^{(i)}}g_{\nu}\times\\
&2\nu\,\log_{e}\left(\frac{E_{F}^{(i)}+p_{z,F}^{(i)}(\nu)c}{\sqrt{m_{i}^{2}c^{4}(1+2\nu B_{D}^{(i)})}}\right).\end{split}

(8)

The system must satisfy the conditions of
\beta
-equilibrium, charge neutrality, and total baryon number density
(n_{B})
conservation
Felipe et al. 2008
;

\begin{split}\mu_{u}+\mu_{e}=\mu_{d}=\mu_{s},\\
\frac{2}{3}n_{u}-\frac{1}{3}(n_{d}+n_{s})-n_{e}=0,\end{split}

n_{B}=\frac{1}{3}(n_{u}+n_{d}+n_{s}).

(9)

where,
\mu_{i}
is the fermionic chemical potential,
i\in[u,d,s,e]
. We assume that the neutrinos escape freely from the system, implying they have no influence on the
\beta
-equilibrium conditions.
For a given baryon number density
n_{B}
and specific central magnetic field strength, these equations along with Eqs. (
5
) can be solved to obtain the chemical potentials and compute all the thermodynamic quantities of the system. To construct the EoS for MSQM phase, we adopt the MIT Bag Model. In this, the equation of state includes the bag constant (representing the vacuum energy) and also incorporates the magnetic field contributions. Specifically, the magnetic field adds an energy density term
\epsilon_{B}=\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}
, along with anisotropic pressure components:
P_{\perp}^{B}=\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}
and
P_{\parallel}^{B}=-\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}
, which arise due to the presence of the anisotropy in the magnetic field itself
Das and Mukhopadhyay 2012
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
.

\epsilon_{T}^{MSQM}=\sum_{i=u,d,s,e}\epsilon_{i}+B_{bag}+\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi},

(10)

P_{\parallel T}^{MSQM}=\sum_{i=u,d,s,e}P_{\parallel,i}-B_{bag}-\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi},

(11)

P_{\perp T}^{MSQM}=\sum_{i=u,d,s,e}P_{\perp,i}-B_{bag}+\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}.

(12)

3
Anisotropic Equation of State of Magnetized Color-Flavor Locked (MCFL) Quark Matter

In magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phase, the baryon number density
n_{B}
is assumed to be equal to the number density of each quark flavor along with the contribution of the pairing energy term. This condition naturally arises from minimizing the system’s energy while ensuring both color and electric charge neutrality. Notably, due to this perfect charge balance among quarks, electrons are not present in the MCFL phase. The charge neutrality condition for the MCFL phase is given as
González Felipe et al. 2011a
;
Manreza Paret et al. 2015
:

n_{u}+\frac{2\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}}{\pi^{2}}=n_{d}+\frac{2\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}}{\pi^{2}}=n_{s}+\frac{2\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}}{\pi^{2}}=n_{B}

(13)

where,
n_{i=u,d,s}
are the number density of quarks given by Eq. (
5
). The extra term
\left(\frac{2\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}}{\pi^{2}}\right)
comes from pairing energy in MCFL phase. The EoS for MCFL phase is written as follows
González Felipe et al. 2011a
;
Manreza Paret et al. 2015
;
Rajagopal and Wilczek 2001b
:

\epsilon_{T}^{MCFL}=\sum_{i=u,d,s}\epsilon_{i}+B_{bag}+\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}-\frac{3\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}^{2}}{\pi^{2}},

(14)

P_{\parallel T}^{MCFL}=\sum_{i=u,d,s}P_{\parallel,i}-B_{bag}-\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}+\frac{3\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}^{2}}{\pi^{2}},

(15)

P_{\perp T}^{MCFL}=\sum_{i=u,d,s}P_{\perp,i}-B_{bag}+\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}+\frac{3\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}.

(16)

for
i\in[u,d,s]
, the terms
\epsilon_{i}
,
P_{\parallel,i}
and
P_{\perp,i}
represent the energy density, the parallel pressure, and the perpendicular pressure of each unpaired quark in the MSQM phase, as given in Eq. (
6
), Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
8
) respectively. The last term
\left(\frac{3\Delta^{2}\mu_{B}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)
in the EoS accounts for the free energy cost associated with quark pairing, which is compensated by the formation of Cooper pairs and
\Delta
is the pairing gap parameter. The quantity
\mu_{B}=(\mu_{u}+\mu_{d}+\mu_{s})/3
represents the baryon chemical potential.
In our calculations, we consider two values of gap parameter,
\Delta=30\;MeV
and
50\;MeV
, assuming no dependence on magnetic field.

4
Stability Condition for Anisotropic Magnetized Strange Quark Matter

The stability of magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) and it’s superconducting phases is usually tested by comparing the energy per baryon (E/A) at zero pressure with the stability of the most stable nucleus
{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}
, whose energy per baryon is about
930
MeV
Paulucci and Horvath 2008
;
Wen 2013
;
González Felipe et al. 2011b
. The stability condition is expressed as,

\left(\frac{E}{A}\right)_{P=0}<\left(\frac{E}{A}\right)_{{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}}\simeq 930\;MeV

(17)

If this inequality condition is satisfied, the corresponding phase of quark matter is stable, otherwise the matter remains metastable. We have plotted the energy per baryon of different phases of quark matter in Fig.

B_{bag}=65\;MeV/fm^{3}
, the MSQM, CFL, and the MCFL phases with pairing gaps
\Delta=30
and
50

{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}
line compared to SQM. At
B_{bag}=75\;MeV/fm^{3}
, the curves shift upward, reducing the overall stability, however the MSQM and MCFL phase with gaps
\Delta=30
and
50

\Delta=30
and
50

{{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}}

B_{bag}=75\;MeV/fm^{3}
, the stability weakens but the MCFL phase with
\Delta=50
MeV approaches the threshold.

The stability of magnetized quark matter strongly depends on the quark masses, the bag constant, the pairing gap parameter, and the magnetic field strength. In this work, both the quark masses and the gap parameters have been considered at relatively small values. As a consequence, the SQM and the CFL phases do not fully satisfy the stability condition, since the reduction in free energy is not sufficient to bring E/A below the
{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}
value. However, if both the quark masses and the gap parameters are increased, then the energy per baryon decreases accordingly. In such cases, both the SQM and the CFL phases can also satisfy the stability inequality. The presence of strong magnetic field further favors the MSQM and MCFL phases by enhancing their stability through Landau quantization, which lowers the energy per baryon and explains why the MSQM and MCFL phases emerge as the most stable configurations under the considered conditions.

5
Stellar structure equations for Anisotropic Magnetized Quark Stars

Due to the breaking of spherical symmetry caused by pressure anisotropy in the presence of strong magnetic field, the star changes to spheroidal axisymmetric shape. To study the stellar structure of such deformed magnetized quark stars for both magnetized SQM and magnetized CFL phases within the framework of general relativity, we employ the
\gamma
-metric formalism, which effectively accounts for deviations from spherical symmetry and accommodates the axisymmetric nature of the spacetime geometry.
The spacetime geometry around the deformed compact object can be then described by the following line element (
G=c=1
)
Zubairi and Weber 2017
;
Zubairi et al. 2017
;
Terrero et al. 2021
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
;

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle-e^{2\nu(r)}dt^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right)^{-\gamma}dr^{2}

(18)

\displaystyle+r^{2}sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}.

Thus, the components of the metric are expressed in terms of the equatorial radius
r
, the polar radius
z
, and the gravitational mass
m
.
The stellar structure equations of anisotropic magnetized quark stars are given as:
Terrero et al. 2021
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
;

\displaystyle\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^{2}\gamma\epsilon_{T}

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{\parallel T}}{dz}=-\frac{\left(\epsilon_{T}+P_{\parallel T}\right)\left[\frac{r}{2}+4\pi r^{3}P_{\parallel T}-\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}\right]}{\gamma r^{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{\perp T}}{dr}=-\frac{\left(\epsilon_{T}+P_{\perp T}\right)\left[\frac{r}{2}+4\pi r^{3}P_{\perp T}-\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}\right]}{r^{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}}

The stellar structure equations can be numerically solved in a manner similar to the spherically symmetric TOV equations by using the anisotropic EoS. Starting at the center of the star with a chosen central baryon number density, we determine the central energy density
\epsilon_{T0}=\epsilon_{T}(r=0)
, parallel and perpendicular pressures
P_{\parallel T0}=P_{\parallel T}(r=0)
,
P_{\perp T0}=P_{\perp T}(r=0)
and the deformation parameter
\gamma={P_{\parallel T0}}/{P_{\perp T0}}
, which we take constant throughout the star. Integration continues outward until the smaller of the two pressures (here
P_{\parallel T}
) vanishes, defining the polar radius
Z
, where
P_{\parallel T}(Z)=0
. The equatorial radius is then given by
R=\frac{Z}{\gamma}
, and the total mass is
M=m(r=R)
. Notably, setting
B=0
restores spherical symmetry with
P_{\parallel T}=P_{\perp T}
and
\gamma=1
, recovering the standard TOV equations for non-magnetized compact stars.

6
Numerical calculations using Density-dependent Magnetic Field

As discussed in
Terrero et al. 2021
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
, integrating the differential equations for parallel and perpendicular pressures from Eqs. (
5
) yields two corresponding energy densities
\epsilon_{\parallel T}
and
\epsilon_{\perp T}
, obtained through interpolation at each step. This reflects an anisotropic mass distribution. To incorporate this anisotropy into the stellar structure equations, the energy density can be expressed accordingly, as outlined in
Terrero et al. 2021
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
,

\displaystyle\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^{2}\gamma\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\parallel T}+\epsilon_{\perp T}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{\parallel T}}{dz}=-\frac{\left(\epsilon_{\parallel T}+P_{\parallel T}\right)\left[\frac{r}{2}+4\pi r^{3}P_{\parallel T}-\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}\right]}{\gamma r^{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{\perp T}}{dr}=-\frac{\left(\epsilon_{\perp T}+P_{\perp T}\right)\left[\frac{r}{2}+4\pi r^{3}P_{\perp T}-\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}\right]}{r^{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{\gamma}}

We use the average energy density to update the right-hand side of Eq. (
6
), preserving the effects of mass density anisotropy. Eq. (
6
) is then solved numerically with the specified boundary conditions to obtain the masses and radii of deformed magnetized quark stars, incorporating a chemical potential-dependent magnetic field. The central baryon number density is expressed in units of
n_{0}
, the nuclear saturation density.
At absolute zero temperature, the chemical potential is equivalent to the Fermi energy
(\mu=E_{F})
. We model the magnetic field profile within a deformed magnetized quark star as a function of the chemical potential of the strange quark
(\mu_{s})
instead of as a density dependent function and is defined by the relation similar to the one given in
Bandyopadhyay et al. 1997
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 1998
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
:

B_{D}\left(\mu_{s}\right)=B_{S}+B_{0}\left[1-exp\left(-\alpha\left(\frac{\mu_{s}}{\mu_{0s}}\right)^{\beta}\right)\right]

(21)

where
B_{D}
(in units of
B_{c}
) is the magnetic field strength at a given chemical potential of strange quark
\mu_{s}
,
B_{S}
(in units of
B_{c}
) represents the surface magnetic field,
\mu_{0s}
is the central chemical potential of strange quark. The constants
\alpha
,
\beta
and
B_{0}
determine the shape and strength of the magnetic field profile. For our study, we choose
\alpha=0.8
,
\beta=0.9
to ensure a smooth and nearly flat magnetic field profile near both the surface and the center. We kept the surface magnetic field strength at
10^{15}
Gauss, consistent with observational constraints. Once the central magnetic field strength is specified, the parameter
B_{0}
is determined accordingly. In our analysis, we set the upper limit of the central magnetic field to
1.5\times 10^{18}
Gauss. This upper limit is closely associated with the violation of the virial relation.
As we know, the baryon number density
n_{B}
is not zero at the surface of magnetized quark star
(i.e.,n_{B}\neq 0)
. Therefore, the modified chemical potential-dependent magnetic field is defined as:

B_{D}\left(\mu_{s}\right)=B_{S}+B_{0}\left[1-exp\left(-\alpha\left(\frac{\mu_{s}-\mu_{s,min}}{\mu_{0s}-\mu_{s,min}}\right)^{\beta}\right)\right]

(22)

where,
\mu_{s,min}
denotes the chemical potential of strange quark corresponding to the surface baryon number density and
\mu_{s}
varies from
\mu_{s,min}
to
\mu_{0s}
. Eq. (
22
) provides the correct variation of the magnetic field from the surface to the center of the deformed magnetized quark star.

7.1
Deformation and Ellipticity

The variation of the deformation (
\gamma
-factor) with total central energy density for different central magnetic field strengths
B_{cen}=[0,5\times 10^{17},7\times 10^{17},1\times 10^{18},1.5\times 10^{18}]
Gauss at a fixed bag constant
B_{bag}=65\;MeV/fm^{3}
is shown in Fig.
7
, panel (
7(a)
). As seen in the figure, the
\gamma
-factor decreases (i.e, deformation increases) with increasing central magnetic field strength. In the absence of magnetic field
(B=0)
, the star remains spherically symmetric, corresponding to
\gamma=1
, and hence shows no deformation. The most significant deviations from spherical symmetry occur at intermediate central energy densities.
At lower central energy densities, the
\gamma
-factor is extremely small, such configurations should not be considered. At higher central energy densities, the
\gamma
-factor approaches unity, indicating that the star becomes nearly spherical again. This behavior highlights that the combined effect of magnetic field pressure and matter pressure leads to maximum deformation at intermediate central energy densities.
The effect of the central magnetic field and a fixed bag constant on the deformation of the star can be analyzed using the ellipticity
\varepsilon
, which is defined as
Rizaldy and Sulaksono 2018
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\sqrt{1-\gamma}

(23)

As discussed earlier, for a spherically symmetric configuration,
\gamma\sim 1
, which results in an ellipticity
\varepsilon\sim 0
, indicating no deformation.
In contrast, for the most deformed case,
\gamma\sim 0
, leading to an ellipticity
\varepsilon\sim 1
, signifying maximum deformation. This relationship highlights the direct correspondence between the degree of deformation and the ellipticity, with
\varepsilon
measuring how far the star deviates from spherical symmetry. The mass as a function of ellipticity for different central magnetic fields
B_{cen}=[5\times 10^{17},1\times 10^{18}]
Gauss is shown in Fig.
7
, panel (
7(b)
). The plot includes results for two fixed bag constants
B_{bag}=65MeV/f^{3}
and
75\;MeV/fm^{3}
illustrating how the mass varies with ellipticity for two different values of the bag constant. At a particular central magnetic field strength, the ellipticity decreases with increasing bag constant, indicating that the deformation becomes less as the bag constant increases. In other words, a higher bag constant results in a more compact and less deformed star configuration. Similarly, at a fixed bag constant, the ellipticity increases with increasing central magnetic field strength, indicating greater deformation of the star. It is observed that the maximum mass of the star is achieved only at lower ellipticity values, i.e., when star is only slightly deformed. As the deformation becomes more significant (i.e., as ellipticity increases), the maximum mass decreases, suggesting that highly deformed stars may become gravitationally unstable and undergo collapse. This highlights the limitation of the
\gamma
-metric formalism. Thus the
\gamma
-metric formalism is suitable only for small deviations from spherical symmetry and may not be applicable for modeling of stars with extreme deformation.

7.2
Gravitational Redshift

Gravitational redshift is a crucial phenomenon predicted by Einstein’s General Theory of Relativity and is one of it’s fundamental observational tests. It occurs because the frequency of light or radiation is affected by the gravitational potential of the emitting source. When photons are emitted from the surface of a dense compact object, such as a neutron star or black hole, and travel to a region of weaker gravitational potential, they lose energy. This energy loss results in a stretching of photon’s wavelength-an effect known as redshift. In case of magnetized quark stars, which are extremely dense and posses strong magnetic fields, the gravitational redshift effect can also be significant for deformed magnetized strange quark star. The gravitational redshift for a spheroidal static compact object is given as
Zubairi et al. 2015

7.3
Mass Quadrupole Moment

The mass quadrupole moment describes how a star’s mass distribution deviates from spherical symmetry. In deformed magnetized quark stars, strong magnetic field cause anisotropic pressures that deform the star, generating a nonzero quadrupole moment. This deformation makes such stars potential continuous sources of gravitational waves.
The mass quadrupole moment for the spheroidal star is given as
Herrera et al. 1998
,

Q=\frac{\gamma}{3}M^{3}(1-\gamma^{2})

(25)

For spherically symmetric stars, there is no deformation, i.e.
\gamma=1

Q=0
. As a result, such stars do not emit gravitational waves, since gravitational wave emission required a time-varying quadrupole moment. We plotted the mass quadrupole moment

7.4

E_{rot}=I\Omega^{2}

\varepsilon_{tidal}=\Omega^{2}

Pérez et al. 2024

7.5
Gravitational Wave Amplitude

The gravitational wave amplitude (or strain amplitude) is a measure of the fractional change in distance between two points caused by the passage of a gravitational wave. It quantifies the observable strength of the wave and in the quadrupole approximation, is given by
Flanagan and Hughes 2005
:

h_{0}=\frac{2G}{c^{4}}\frac{1}{r}\ddot{Q}

(27)

where
\ddot{Q}
is the second time derivative of the mass quadrupole moment,
G
is the gravitational constant,
c
is the speed of light, and
r
is the distance to the observer. This formula implies that gravitational waves are emitted by sources with a time-varying, non-zero mass quadrupole moment. A slightly deformed magnetized quark star possesses such a quadrupole moment due to anisotropic pressure caused by strong magnetic field. This deformation breaks the star’s spherical symmetry and leads to a non-zero quadrupole moment, making it a potential emitter of gravitational waves. The strength of the emitted gravitational waves i.e., the strain amplitude depends directly on the deformation and the dynamics of the mass distribution. The magnitude of gravitational wave amplitude for a slightly deformed rotating star is given by the equation
Bonazzola and Gourgoulhon 1996
:

h_{0}=\frac{6G}{c^{4}}Q\frac{\Omega^{2}}{r}

(28)

where
\Omega
is the angular velocity,defined as
\Omega=\frac{2\pi}{P}
, with
P
being the rotation period of the star. By substituting the expression for
Q
from Eq. (
25
) and the angular velocity
\Omega
into the formula for
h_{0}
, we obtain the gravitational wave amplitude for a slightly deformed magnetized quark star,

h_{0}=9.16\gamma(1-\gamma^{2})\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{ms}{P}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{kpc}{r}\right)

(29)

8
Summary and Conclusions

In this work, we investigated the stellar properties of spheroidal magnetized quark stars by considering two phases of dense quark matter: magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) and magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phases. Both phases were modeled using the phenomenological MIT bag model, extended to incorporate the anisotropy induced by strong magnetic fields. Magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM), composed of unpaired quarks, requires the presence of electrons to satisfying both
\beta
-equilibrium and charge neutrality conditions. In contract, the magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phase consists of paired quarks that inherently satisfy the charge neutrality condition without the need for electrons. In our approach, the magnetic field inside the star is not assumed to be uniform; instead, it varies as a function of Fermi energy of quarks rather than being density-dependent, making the model more physically realistic. The presence of strong magnetic field introduces pressure anisotropy, causing the pressure components parallel and perpendicular to the magnetic field direction to differ significantly. In particular, the perpendicular pressure exceeds the parallel pressure. As a result, the star deviates from spherical symmetry and obtained spheroidal configuration. To account for the deformation induced by the Fermi energy-dependent magnetic field, we employ the
\gamma
-metric formalism to solve the modified Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV) equations. This approach allows to obtain the stellar properties of deformed magnetized quark stars in magnetized SQM and magnetized CFL phases, incorporating the effects of anisotropy and deviation from spherical symmetry. For our calculations, we considered two different values of the bag constants,
B_{bag}=65\;MeV/fm^{3}
and
75\;MeV/fm^{3}
, along with a range of central magnetic field strengths given by
B_{cen}=[0,5\times 10^{17},7\times 10^{17},1\times 10^{18},1.5\times 10^{18}]
Gauss for magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) and two different pairing gap parameters
\Delta=30\;MeV
and
50\;MeV
for magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phase. This framework allows us to examine the influence of the bag constant, strong magnetic fields and pairing gaps on the structure and physical properties of deformed magnetized quark stars in both magnetized SQM and magnetized CFL phases. We computed the masses and radii of deformed magnetized quark stars for two distinct phases of magnetized dense quark stars: MSQM and MCFL phases. At a fixed bag constant, both the maximum mass and equatorial radius increase with increasing central magnetic field strength, while higher bag constants result in more compact configurations due to softer equation of state (EoS). Compared to MSQM, magnetized quark stars with MCFL phase exhibit stiffer equation of state (EoS) due to quark pairing, yielding higher masses and radii. Stars with masses up to the maximum value are stable against the radial oscillations, as they satisfy
\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\epsilon_{T0}}\right)>0
. Beyond the maximum mass, the stars are unstable to radial oscillations because
\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\epsilon_{T0}}\right)<0
. This indicates that the stability is ensured only in the region where the stellar mass increases with increasing central energy density. Due to the presence of strong magnetic field-modeled as dependent on the Fermi energy and the resulting pressure anisotropy, the quark stars attain significantly large masses. Therefore, it becomes essential to investigate other physical properties of deformed magnetized quark stars, such as ellipticity, gravitational redshift, mass quadrupole moment, tidal deformability, and gravitational wave amplitude for both magnetized SQM and magnetized CFL phases. The gravitational redshift increases with the central magnetic field strength, when analyzed as a function of both mass and the equatorial radius. This is noticeable for both phases. Notably, magnetized quark stars exhibit higher gravitational redshifts compared to their non-magnetized counterparts, highlighting the significance of the influence of strong magnetic fields on the spacetime curvature near the stellar surface. Compared to magnetized SQM, the gravitational red shift is more pronounced in MCFL stars due to their greater compactness. The tidal deformability increases with increasing central magnetic field strength, reflecting enhanced deformation. The mass quadrupole moment depends on both the stellar mass and the degree of deformation. For a given magnetic field strength and bag constant, the quadrupole moment reaches its maximum value for stars with intermediate masses. Furthermore, the magnitude of the mass quadrupole moment decreases with increasing bag constant, indicating that a softer equation of state suppresses stellar deformation. The value of mass quadrupole moment is enough to find out the gravitational wave amplitude for the deformed magnetized quark stars. But deformed magnetized quark stars in magnetized CFL phase with large gap parameter exhibits higher quadrupole moments than magnetized SQM phase, making them more efficient gravitational wave emitters. In this study, the mass quadrupole moment is found to be sufficient for determining the gravitational wave amplitude of deformed magnetized quark star. The resulting gravitational wave amplitude is found to be of the order of
10^{-24}
, increasing with magnetic field and maximized for intermediate-mass stars in both phases. These finding underscore the significant influence of magnetic fields, bag constants, and quark pairing on the structure, stability, and observational signatures of deformed magnetized quark stars in both magnetized strange quark matter (MSQM) and magnetized color-flavor locked (MCFL) phases.
