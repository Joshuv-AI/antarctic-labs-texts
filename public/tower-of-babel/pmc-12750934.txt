Low discontinuation rate of tirzepatide treatment in Japanese patients with diabetes mellitus; importance of traditional Japanese diet
Authors: Takahiro Hiraide, Yoshihiko Suzuki, Satoko Yamamoto, Soroku Yagihashi, Motoaki Sano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tirzepatide, a novel dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist, has demonstrated robust efficacy in clinical trials; however, discontinuation rates due to gastrointestinal adverse events have been reported at 6–10% in Western populations. In contrast, our real-world study of 219 Japanese patients revealed a markedly lower discontinuation rate of approximately 1.3%. Dietary questionnaires indicated that patients experienced reduced appetite for high-fat and high-calorie foods while maintaining consumption of low-carbohydrate traditional Japanese foods such as fish and meat, which may have contributed to the low discontinuation rate.

To the editor

Tirzepatide is a novel dual agonist for the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptors, which has been demonstrated as a safe and effective treatment for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Despite its efficacy, previous large randomized controlled trials in European and American countries have shown that approximately 6% to 10% of patients discontinued tirzepatide treatment due to adverse events, primarily gastrointestinal events [1, 2]. Loss of appetite for a high-fat and high-calorie diet may be beneficial for weight loss, but nausea or vomiting after consuming a high-fat diet al.so becomes a significant issue during tirzepatide treatment.

In Japan, tirzepatide treatment became available from 2023. We analyzed 219 Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus; 74.9% were male, with a mean age of 59.2 ± 9.8 years old. There were no patients with frail or sarcopenia. Patients visited our institution every two weeks for close monitoring and underwent a questionnaire on dietary habits with their consent after initiating tirzepatide. Tirzepatide treatment was discontinued in only 3 (1.3%) patients due to gastrointestinal adverse events. Eighty (36.5%) patients experienced gastrointestinal adverse events: 9 (4.1%) had nausea or vomiting, 22 (10.0%) had constipation, 6 (2.7%) had diarrhea, 14 (6.4%) had gastritis, 176 (80.3%) had decreased appetite, and 33 (15.1%) had severe (more than 50%) decreased appetite (Fig. 1A). Changes in dietary habits were observed in 202 (92.2%) patients and were most frequent with 5 mg to 7.5 mg injections of tirzepatide (Fig. 1B). Regarding eating behavior, 97 (44.2%) decreased their intake of staple food (rice), 89 (40.6%) decreased their intake of high-fat diet, and 36 (16.4%) decreased their intake of fish or meat (Fig. 1C). Out of 202 patients, 137 patients (67.8%) maintained the same number of meals, whereas 58 patients (28.7%) reduced their meal frequency, which was considered an effect of decreased appetite. Only 16 patients (7.3%) actively requested dietary counseling. According to the questionnaire responses, the reasons included: (1) they felt special guidance was unnecessary because their food intake was naturally adjusted; (2) they felt less dependent on nutritional counseling since no self-restraint was required; and (3) their successful experience of weight loss enhanced confidence in self-management.

Although the rates of adverse gastrointestinal events were similar to those in previous reports, the discontinuation rate due to gastrointestinal events was low in Japanese patients. This discrepancy in the discontinuation rate of tirzepatide may arise from differences in dietary habits. The real-world data regarding the ratios of carbohydrates, protein, and fat intake differ between Japan and the U.S.: carbohydrates: 53.6%, protein: 15.7%, and fat: 27.9% in Japan [3], while carbohydrates: 46.9%, protein: 16.0%, and fat: 35.0% in the U.S [4]. The main differences lie in carbohydrate and lipid intake targets, reflecting cultural eating habits and national health status.

Tirzepatide causes changes in dietary preferences, reducing the preference for high-fat diets and sweet tastes [5]. Kennedy SF, et al. reported that the total food craving scores significantly decreased in the tirzepatide group compared with placebo, and subscale analysis revealed significant reductions in cravings sweets, carbohydrates/starches, and fast-food fats, compatible with our study [6]. Recent studies have shown that mice treated with tirzepatide exhibited reduced body weight and food consumption of high-fat diets [7, 8]. GLP-1 receptor agonists induced the decrease in gastrointestinal motility at all levels of the gastrointestinal tract [9]. Endogenous GLP-1 normally contributes to postprandial slowing of gastric emptying; blocking GLP-1 receptors accelerates gastric emptying and increases antral motility. GLP-1 receptor agonists prolong gastric retention of solids and liquids, with individual variability. This slowing of gastric emptying reduces postprandial glucose excursions, but can also lead to gastrointestinal adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, regurgitation, and in some cases, retention of gastric contents [10]. Although the mechanism behind the loss of appetite is not fully understood, this shift in dietary habits may encourage the consumption of healthier foods such as soba and sushi in Japan, given that the U.S. diet tends to align more closely with Japanese food and also helps control excess sugar and fat intake. For example, patients at our clinic have avoided ohtoro (fatty tuna) and opted for tuna with less oily ingredients when consuming sushi. Traditionally, Japanese food has been considered healthier than Western food. With the introduction of tirzepatide, Western individuals with diabetes may have more opportunities to incorporate Japanese foods, such as soba noodles and sushi, into their diets. The lower treatment discontinuation rate in Japan may be partly attributable to the availability of traditional Japanese dietary options, which are generally lower in fat and sugar.

This study has several limitations. Since we changed the dose of tirzepatide and other drugs for diabetes mellitus (such as SGLT2 inhibitor, metformin, and insulin) according to the weight change of patients, we did not evaluate the change in body weight induced by tirzepatide alone. Moreover, body composition, waist circumference, 24 h food recall questionnaire could not be assessed.

We believe that Westerners undergoing tirzepatide treatment may adapt to a diet similar to the Japanese diet, which could be a helpful strategy for continuing tirzepatide treatment when patients experience gastrointestinal events.
