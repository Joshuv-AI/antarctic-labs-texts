Evaluation of a Novel Antegrade-to-Retrograde Femoral Sheath Conversion System: A Preclinical, Cadaveric Study
Authors: Ramakrishnan Ranganath, Kerry K Gilbert, Randy Hickle, Anuva Iyer, Ryan Um, Rex Johnson, Randall Torrence, Daniel I Sessler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripheral arterial disease often affects the lower extremities, is frequently bilateral, and typically involves multiple leg segments. Symptomatic disease is usually treated endovascularly. Antegrade access may be necessary in some cases and can be challenging compared with retrograde common femoral artery access. The ability to convert a conventional retrograde access to antegrade access would thus be valuable. We developed and tested a novel system, named as antegrade-to-retrograde converter. Our aim was to establish antegrade superficial femoral artery access from initial retrograde entry and reconvert to retrograde access.MethodsWe tested the antegrade-to-retrograde converter bilaterally in 15 fresh (unembalmed) human cadavers. We used the converter and its accessories to convert an initial retrograde common femoral artery access to an antegrade orientation in the superficial femoral artery and then to reconvert to retrograde access.ResultsThere were 5 men and 10 women cadavers, with an average age of 79 (range, 56-93) years. We were able to obtain antegrade wire access after initial retrograde common femoral artery puncture in 100% (30/30) of the procedures. We were similarly able to obtain retrograde wire access after successful antegrade access during all 30 attempts. During antegrade conversion, usable superficial femoral artery access was achieved in 27 of 30 (90%) attempts, with the remaining resulting in profunda femoris artery access. The primary end point was therefore achieved in 90% of the procedures.ConclusionsThe antegrade-to-retrograde converter may be useful for peripheral vascular interventions. Studies in patients need to be performed.Keywords: antegrade access, chronic limb-threatening ischemia, femoral access, retrograde access

Introduction

More than 200 million people worldwide have clinically meaningful peripheral arterial disease, usually in the legs.1 Arterial disease in the legs affects 12% to 20% of Americans older than 60 years.2 Among them, ∼10% to 15% present with chronic limb-threatening ischemia.3 Multisegment disease involving the aortoiliac, femoropopliteal, and infrapopliteal segments is common. Consequently, multisegment interventions are often necessary.

Endovascular techniques are generally preferable because subsequent amputations are less likely than with open surgical approaches.4 However, treating multiple locations often requires multiple arterial puncture points, each of which increases risk for access site complications. For complex infrapopliteal disease, antegrade access increases procedural success and may be the only suitable access in patients who have prior aortic endografts, bilateral common iliac stents, or anatomically hostile aortic bifurcations.5

Antegrade common femoral artery access is more difficult and riskier than retrograde access. Consequently, there have been various attempts to convert an initial retrograde access to an antegrade access.6, 7, 8 However, success has been variable, and none has been widely adopted. Direct antegrade access of the superficial femoral artery under ultrasound guidance is possible but poses a risk of pseudoaneurysm formation because of the lack of support from the femoral head during hemostasis. Consequently, the most common approach to antegrade access remains ultrasound or fluoroscopic cannulation of the common femoral artery. But, with either approach, owing to the anatomy of the bifurcation of the common femoral artery, wires usually enter the profunda femoris artery instead of the superficial femoral artery, and wire control is challenging because there is little room to maneuver the wire without losing access.

Contralateral retrograde access (up-and-over approach) to the common femoral artery thus remains the easiest and safest option, but in patients who have complex infrapopliteal disease, procedural success is compromised by increased distance to the target lesions.7 It would therefore be helpful if there was a reliable method to convert an initial retrograde common femoral access to an antegrade superficial femoral access and, if necessary, flip back to a retrograde access especially in patients with multisegment disease. Flipping back to a retrograde orientation may also allow safe use of most vascular closure devices.9

We therefore developed and describe a novel system, named as the antegrade-to-retrograde converter (ARC) device. In this initial cadaver study, our primary end points were as follows: (1) successful establishment of usable antegrade access to the superficial femoral artery after initial retrograde entry into the ipsilateral common femoral artery; and (2) reestablishment of retrograde access to the common femoral artery through the original arteriotomy. Our safety end points were vessel dissection, vessel perforation, and loss of vessel access (no wire remaining in vessel before end of procedures). We defined usable access as the placement of a standard 6F sheath in the intended direction.

Materials and methods

Our study was performed on fresh unembalmed human cadavers that were donated through a Willed Body Program to the Institute of Anatomical Sciences at the Health Sciences Center at the Texas Tech University in Lubbock, Texas, as shown in the Central Illustration. Because this was a cadaver study, institutional review board approval was neither needed nor sought.

Cadaver procedure for femoral arterial access flipping (left). The antegrade-to-retrograde converter (ARC) device and its accessories (right).

The cause of death was obtained from the certification document provided at the time of death. All cadavers tested negative for HIV, hepatitis B and C viruses, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and Clostridium difficile. Cadavers were excluded when they had prior bilateral femoral-popliteal or aortobifemoral bypass, a common femoral artery stent, known occlusion of the distal external iliac artery within 7 cm of the inferior epigastric artery, occlusion of the superficial femoral artery within the proximal 7 cm, or occlusion of the common femoral artery.

The right carotid artery was surgically accessed by the cadaver laboratory staff, and the arterial system was intermittently pressurized to ∼125 mm Hg with tap water using a Porti-Boy embalming machine (Mopec). The arterial tree was pressurized for about a minute during initial access with ultrasound guidance, during antegrade or retrograde wiring, and at the end of the procedure to wash out contrast medium to facilitate identification of perforations and dissections. We avoided continuous pressure because it causes abdominal compartment syndrome, paradoxical vessel collapse, and risked rupturing vessels.

With the arterial system pressurized, common femoral artery depths and diameters were measured using 2D ultrasound (GE vivid, 8L-RS transducer; GE Healthcare), bilaterally in perpendicular axes. We simultaneously recorded the degree of calcification using ultrasound and fluoroscopy (GE OEC; GE Healthcare). Calcification was considered severe when apparent on fluoroscopy, moderate when only apparent on ultrasound in a circumferential pattern, and mild if only apparent on ultrasound and not circumferential.

Prototypes of the ARC device and its accessories were built by Lightning Cath LLC. Components of the ARC device are illustrated in Supplement Figure S1. The bend in the shaft of the ARC device, which incorporates the turn-back lumen, is illustrated in Supplement Figure S2. The ARC device supports introduction of 6F and 7F sheaths.

All ARC-related procedures were done by single author (R.R.), an experienced interventional cardiologist with interest in peripheral vascular interventions and considerable experience in vascular ultrasound. He is also the inventor of the ARC system. The procedures that follow were conducted bilaterally on all 15 cadavers.

After carotid artery pressurization with 125 mm Hg of pressure, we used ultrasound guidance to access the common femoral artery with an 18-gauge needle, and a 0.025-inch stiff Amplatz wire was passed into the abdominal aorta, with proper wire position confirmed fluoroscopically in an anteroposterior projection. The arterial tree was then decompressed. The ARC device was prepared by injecting tap water through the turn-back and straight-through lumens. The device was then inserted over the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire far enough that the turn-back lumen was within the common femoral artery, and the intraluminal position of the turn-back lumen was confirmed by injecting 2.5 mL of iodinated contrast (Omnipaque) in an anteroposterior projection using fluoroscopy (Figure 1). The ARC device was positioned such that the elbow of the device was intraluminal, just distal to the arteriotomy. We used an anteroposterior projection to limit scatter and ambient radiation. In some cases, with hip arthroplasties, we used a 30° right anterior oblique or left anterior oblique projection.

(A) The antegrade retrograde converter device is placed retrograde on a 0.025 Amplatz wire. (B) Contrast injection confirming intraluminal position of the turn-back lumen.

For the next step, we again pressurized the carotid artery at 125 mm Hg and then introduced the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire through the red port, which projected it antegrade. Under fluoroscopy, we passed the wire into the middle of the superficial femoral artery when possible. The ARC device was rotated in the longitudinal axis along the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire if needed, under fluoroscopy, to selectively wire the superficial femoral artery (Figure 2). We then depressurized the carotid artery.

Antegrade wiring with customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire after initial retrograde access.

The ARC device was exteriorized by pulling it back until the turn-back lumen exit port was exteriorized and the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire was directly visible exiting the device. The wire was pinched and removed from the device while maintaining the antegrade wire in the superficial femoral artery. Thereafter, the device was completely removed from the body while retaining the retrograde 0.025-inch Amplatz wire in the external iliac artery and the antegrade customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire in the superficial femoral artery, both entering through the same common femoral artery arteriotomy. The arterial tree was then depressurized.

Using both wires, the curved balloon sheath was inserted into the common femoral artery (Figure 3). This sheath has a 60° curve at its proximal 5 mm and a balloon at its proximal end, which is located 6 mm from the tip. The sheath tip straightens when both wires are introduced in it (Figure 4). The curved balloon sheath was pushed on both wires so that it is well within the vessel. It is possible to insert the curved balloon sheath well within the artery, even though the wires are headed in the opposite direction, because the 0.018-inch nitinol wire is very soft and gets dragged retrograde, allowing this process. Once the curved balloon sheath was sufficiently intraluminal, the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire was removed, which allowed the curved balloon sheath to assume a 60° bend. The balloon was inflated using tap water and locked in its inflated position. The curved balloon sheath was then pulled back, until there was resistance due to the inflated balloon. The 0.018-inch customized nitinol wire can be pulled back, if necessary, at this stage, to remove any slack that may have been introduced during the insertion of the curved balloon sheath.

(A) The 0.025 Amplatz wire and the 0.018 customized Nitinol wire entering through the same arteriotomy but travelling in opposite directions. (B) The curved balloon sheath introduced on both wires.

The curved balloon (CuB) sheath.

At that point, we introduced the tracking catheter on the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire and passed it into the superficial femoral artery (Figure 5). The customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire was removed and a 110-cm, V18 wire (Boston Scientific) was introduced through the tracking catheter. The curved balloon sheath was deflated, and the sheath and tracking catheter were removed, leaving the V18 wire in place. A 6F sheath was introduced on the stiff wire, thus obtaining access to the superficial femoral artery in the antegrade orientation, which was confirmed by injecting iodinated contrast medium into the sheath and cine angiography in an anteroposterior projection (Figure 6).

The tracking catheter (3 radio-opaque markers) being inserted through the curved balloon sheath, on the 0.018-inch customized nitinol wire and traveling in the antegrade direction.

(A) The V-18 wire is seen in the antegrade direction. (B) A standard 6 F sheath has been inserted In the antegrade direction.

We pressurized the carotid artery at 125 mm Hg and reintroduced the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire through the sheath in the antegrade direction and then removed the sheath. The ARC device was then reintroduced antegrade over the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire. Thereafter, the 0.018-inch nitinol was reintroduced into the red port in a retrograde direction into the external iliac artery (Figure 7). The device was removed with the 0.025-inch wire while retaining the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire in the retrograde direction. Finally, a stiff micropuncture sheath was introduced on the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire to reestablish retrograde access (Figure 8). The 0.018-inch nitinol wire was removed, and a 0.035-inch J-wire introduced for the placement of a standard 6F sheath.

The antegrade-to-retrograde converter device being used to flip from antegrade-to-retrograde access.

(A) The 0.018 customized Nitinol wire has been inserted in the retrograde direction (B) A micro-sheath introducer has been inserted in the retrograde direction

To evaluate vessel dissection, we used the Porti-Boy embalming machine again at 125 mm Hg, to wash out contrast from the common femoral artery and external iliac artery and fluoroscopically imaged in an anteroposterior direction and oblique projections. Dye hang up or extravasation was considered evidence of dissection or vessel perforation. Similarly, any evidence of vessel dye staining would indicate dissection. At the end of each procedure, contrast was injected in the common femoral artery to identify extravasation. Loss of vessel access was defined by loss of all wire access to the initial arteriotomy any time after initial successful wire access.

Results

There were 5 men and 10 women cadavers, with an average age of 79 years (range, 56-93 years). The average BMI was 22 kg/m2 (range, 14-29 kg/m2). The average depth of the common femoral artery was 14 mm (range, 5-21 mm) and of the diameter was 8 mm (range, 5-11 mm); 23% had evidence of moderate to severe calcification of the common femoral artery.

Based on available records, about half (53%) of the cadavers had coronary artery disease. Other conditions that were considered contributors to death included peripheral artery disease (33%), chronic kidney disease (14%), and tobacco abuse (40%). Attributed causes of death were cardiovascular causes (42%), cancer (21%), septic shock with multiorgan failure (14%), advanced dementia (14%), and respiratory failure due to COPD (7%).

We were able to obtain antegrade wire access after initial retrograde common femoral artery puncture in 100% (30/30) of the procedures. We similarly obtained retrograde wire access, after successful antegrade access, during access flip, in 100% of the procedures (30/30). During antegrade conversion, usable superficial femoral artery access was achieved in 90% (27/30) of our attempts, with the remaining resulting in profunda femoris artery access. In 2 of these cases, the access point in the common femoral artery was low and just above the flow divider, making it impossible to turn the wire into the superficial femoral artery. In 1 case, the superficial femoral artery could not be wired because of ostial stenosis. The primary end point was therefore achieved in 90% of the procedures.

During antegrade-to-retrograde conversion, the retrograde sheath was successfully positioned in 90% (27/30) of the attempts, but we lost vessel access in 10% (27/30) of the attempts. Losses were all during flipping from established antegrade access back to retrograde access. In 2 cases, there appeared to be some external iliac disease that prevented adequate wire purchase. One loss resulted from operator error, with accidental wire removal during device exteriorization. No perforations or dissections were detected. There was no appreciable oozing from the access sites during any of the procedures.

Discussion

The current approach for antegrade femoral artery access continues to be ultrasound access or direct fluoroscopic access when arteries are severely calcified. But, with either approach, the wire usually enters the deep femoral artery instead of the superficial femoral artery owing to the anatomy of the bifurcation of the common femoral artery and limited space for maneuvering a guide wire. Direct antegrade access of the superficial femoral artery with ultrasound is possible but can cause pseudoaneurysms owing to inadequate support from the femoral head during hemostasis. Access via the common femoral artery therefore appears to be the safest option. The ability to convert retrograde common femoral artery access to an antegrade superficial femoral artery access thus provides procedural advantages.

An interesting design feature of the ARC device is the 130° angle at the elbow (Figure 3). This angle facilitates the convenient positioning of the device in the common femoral artery in a very stable fashion. It also allows the operator to access the artery in the usual 40° to 45° angle and helps wiring in the antegrade direction because the 130° bend compliments the 90° turn of the turn-back lumen, achieving a 180° turn of the 0.018-inch customized nitinol wire with reference to the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire. Owing to this geometry, we do not need to access the artery more vertically or perpendicularly, which risks posterior wall puncture.

An important feature of the ARC device is that it allows operators to manipulate a customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire by rotating the ARC device along its long axis, which in turn permits selective wiring of the superficial femoral artery under fluoroscopy. Similarly, the 30° bend at the tip of the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire allows selective wiring of the superficial femoral artery. Steerability allows operators to keep the wire in the main vessel.

The customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire is extremely flexible, which allows it to navigate the sharp direction change within the turn-back lumen. This flexibility also permits exteriorization of the device without loss of wire position. However, its extreme flexibility also makes it unsupportive for any sheath placement, and it must be replaced by a stiff standard stainless steel 0.018-inch wire to permit sheath placement. Escalation to a stiff wire is facilitated by the curved balloon sheath and the tracking catheter. The tracking catheter can be inserted through the curved balloon sheath on the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire, permitting escalation to a stiff 0.018-inch wire that supports introduction of a 6F sheath.

The curved balloon sheath is designed to work in femoral arteries with diameters as small as 6 mm. Most of the arteries that we measured had diameters in the 7- to 11-mm range, averaging 8.1 mm. The ARC device will therefore suit in most adults. The curved balloon sheath has an outer diameter of 6F and is hydrophilic for easy insertion. It can be inserted on both wires and assumes its 60° bend only when the 0.025-inch Amplatz wire is removed. The balloon at the tip of the curved balloon sheath measures 6 by 3 mm and is donut shaped, which reduces the risk of it being accidentally removed from the artery.

For the tracking catheter to follow the customized 0.018-inch nitinol wire in the antegrade direction, the catheter had to turn within the common femoral artery as close to the arteriotomy as possible. Forces applied to the tracking catheter while inserting it into the curved balloon sheath therefore had to consistently be directed in an antegrade direction. Two mechanisms contributed to this: (1) the inflated balloon in the curved sheath allowed the curved sheath to be retracted to the arteriotomy; and (2) the 60° bend at the tip of the curved balloon sheath directed the force antegrade. We found that when the tracking catheter was inserted through the curved balloon sheath, it was capable of tracking the 0.018-inch customized nitinol wire, even when the wire was only in the proximal to middle of the superficial femoral artery, as much as 10 cm from the ostium of the superficial femoral artery. This suggested that the ARC device may provide antegrade access even when the proximal-to-mid superficial femoral artery is occluded. The ARC device may thus allow intervention in both limbs at multiple segments through a single vascular access.

Caution

The presence of atherosclerotic disease and severe calcification in the common femoral artery and its bifurcation can pose significant difficulty with wiring. The risk of dissection and perforation can be high in these circumstances. This may also be a contraindication for the use of this device.

It is also important to emphasize that all above-mentioned maneuvers are done under fluoroscopy, and the operator’s hands may receive more direct radiation. Special leaded gloves would be mandatory to limit this risk. Road mapping was not used during this study owing to unavailability. This could also potentially reduce x-ray exposure.

Limitations

Even fresh unembalmed cadaver tissue differs considerably from living tissue. This could presumably complicate comparison between cadaver trials and clinical practice. For example, fresh cadavers often have congealed blood in the vascular space, and it is not possible to detect thrombosis as a complication of a procedure. Furthermore, cadavers lack pulsatile blood flow, which possibly limited our ability to evaluate contrast hang up and thus our ability to diagnose perforation and dissection.

Conclusions

The ARC changes retrograde-oriented femoral access to antegrade-oriented access and back again. The ability to change vessel access orientation seems likely to facilitate treatment of multisegment peripheral arterial disease. Our results in cadavers are encouraging and suggest that studies in living humans would be a reasonable next step.
