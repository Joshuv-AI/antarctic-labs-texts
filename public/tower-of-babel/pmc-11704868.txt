Real-World Status in the Treatment of Latent Tuberculosis Infection in People Living with HIV in Korea
Authors: Hyun-Ha Chang, Hyun-Ju Nam, Hyun Sook Kim, Kyung-Hwa Park, Sohyun Bae, Yoonjung Kim, Shin-Woo Kim, Sook In Jung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective study analyzed medical records of 1,392 people living with HIV (PLWH) diagnosed with latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) at two provincial central hospitals from 2011 to 2022. LTBI was diagnosed in 152 patients (10.9%) patients aged ≥18 years. Among the 113 patients who initiated treatment, 96 (85.0%) completed isoniazid therapy, while 17 (15.0%) discontinued due to patient refusal, liver function test abnormalities, and other reasons. During a mean follow-up period of 55.0±31.0 months, two cases of active tuberculosis were reported in both the treatment non-completion group (3.6%) and the completion group (2.1%). This study provides recent real-world insights into LTBI treatment among PLWH in Korea.Keywords: People living with HIV, Latent tuberculosis, Treatment, Outcomes

Active tuberculosis (TB) develops in approximately 10% of people living with HIV (PLWH) each year from latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), necessitating routine testing for LTBI in all PLWH to prevent progression to active disease [1,2]. Notably, the Korea demonstrates one of the highest incidences of active tuberculosis among the 38 member countries of the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, with 39 cases per 100,000 persons reported in 2022 [3,4]. Despite this high incidence, there are no existing reports on the outcomes of LTBI treatment specifically among PLWH in Korea [5], and even the World Health Organization (WHO) lacks data on the incidence of tuberculosis in this population. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the real-world LTBI treatment landscape among PLWH in Korea and identifying potential factors hindering effective LTBI treatment.

A retrospective cohort study was conducted through the review of medical records from PLWH diagnosed with LTBI between January 1, 2011, and July 12, 2022, across two provincial central hospitals located in Daegu and Gwangju. All LTBI diagnoses were confirmed using the interferon-gamma release assay for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Specifically, the QuantiFERON®-TB Gold in-tube test was utilized, and results were interpreted in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Due to the unavailability of rifapentine in Korea, all patients were offered isoniazid (INH) monotherapy (5 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 300 mg, orally daily) for either 6 or 9 months. Patients with suspected or confirmed active TB at the time of testing or those with a history of prior treatment for active TB were excluded from the study. Completion of LTBI treatment was defined as receiving INH therapy for more than 24 weeks, categorizing these individuals into the treatment completion group. The non-completion group included patients who discontinued treatment prematurely or remained untreated.

Among 1,392 PLWH aged ≥18 years, 152 patients (10.9%) were diagnosed with LTBI during the study period. The cohort was predominantly male (95.4%), with a mean age of 46.5 (±13.0) years. Of the 152 patients diagnosed with LTBI, 39 (25.7%) did not undergo treatment due to patient refusal or other contraindications to INH therapy. Among the remaining 113 patients who initiated INH treatment, 96 (85.0%) successfully completed the full 6- or 9-month course (8.3% and 91.7% of patients, respectively). Seventeen patients (15.0%) discontinued treatment prematurely, with reasons including patient refusal (6 cases), elevated liver function tests (6 cases), dizziness (1 case), rash (1 case), nausea (1 case), and other causes (2 cases). For patients who discontinued treatment, the mean duration of INH therapy was 68.4±41.1 days, with a range of 2 to 139 days.

There were no statistically significant differences in underlying comorbidities, including diabetes, chronic liver disease, chronic renal disease, central nervous system disorders, or malignancies, between the treatment completion and non-completion groups. Additionally, baseline CD4+ T cell counts and HIV RNA levels at the time of LTBI diagnosis did not differ significantly between these groups (Table 1). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the duration from HIV diagnosis to LTBI diagnosis or from LTBI diagnosis to the initiation of INH treatment (Table 1). During a mean follow-up period of 55.0±31.0 months, active TB occurred in two patients (3.6%) in the treatment non-completion group; notably, both cases involved individuals who had not initiated treatment, and both presented as pulmonary TB. In the treatment completion group, 2 cases of active TB (2.1%) were observed—one case of pulmonary TB and one case of lymphadenitis (P=0.501). Although the time from LTBI diagnosis to the onset of active TB was longer in the treatment completion group compared to the non-completion group (91.2±4.4 weeks vs. 38.5±43.1 weeks), this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.227) (Table 1).

These findings provide valuable insights into the management of LTBI in PLWH in Korea. First, the study identified a substantial LTBI diagnosis rate of 10.9% among PLWH, underscoring the critical need for routine LTBI screening in this high-risk population. Although the prevalence of tuberculosis among PLWH has been documented, there is a lack of country-specific data on the prevalence of LTBI in this group, as well as limited information on the proportion of PLWH with LTBI who are receiving appropriate treatment. Houben et al. utilized mathematical modeling to estimate the global burden of LTBI in the general population, reporting a global prevalence of 23.0% (95% uncertainty interval,-20.4–26.4%) in 2014, corresponding to approximately 1.7 billion people [6]. Notably, the WHO South-East Asia, Western Pacific, and African regions exhibited the highest prevalence rates, collectively accounting for around 80% of the global LTBI burden [6]. Given the frequent travel between these regions and Korea, the incidence of LTBI in Korea is expected to increase.

Current international guidelines recommend that all individuals diagnosed with HIV be evaluated for LTBI, irrespective of their epidemiological risk of tuberculosis exposure [2]. However, even in high-resource settings such as the United States and the United Kingdom, the rate of tuberculosis screening among PLWH has been reported to be suboptimal. In the past decade, only 47% to 69% of PLWH attending care clinics underwent initial screening, and among those diagnosed with LTBI, only 42% initiated appropriate treatment [7,8,9,10,11,12]. Similarly, our study revealed significant barriers to initiating and completing LTBI treatment. A quarter of diagnosed patients did not begin treatment, and 15% of those who initiated therapy failed to complete it. These findings are alarming given the elevated risk of progression to active tuberculosis in PLWH.

No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the treatment completion and non-completion groups. This suggests that non-medical factors—such as patient education, access to support systems, and management of treatment-related adverse events—may be crucial determinants of adherence and treatment completion in this population. Recently, the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency published educational materials aimed at enhancing public understanding of LTBI treatment. Clinicians should leverage these resources, and public health authorities should actively promote them through policy initiatives. To mitigate treatment discontinuation due to adverse events such as gastrointestinal disturbances and liver dysfunction, the introduction of alternative regimens, such as rifapentine-based therapies recommended by various international guidelines, is essential [1,2]. Making these therapies available in Korea could substantially improve LTBI treatment outcomes.

While the rate of progression to active tuberculosis was lower in the treatment completion group compared to the non-treatment group (2.1% vs. 3.6%), this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small sample size and relatively short follow-up period. Larger-scale studies with extended follow-up durations are required to definitively evaluate the efficacy of LTBI treatment in this high-risk population.

This study provides a recent real-world assessment of LTBI treatment among PLWH in Korea. Despite the limitations of a small sample size, our findings highlight the significant proportion of patients who either refuse treatment initiation or discontinue therapy. Future research should focus on conducting nationwide prospective cohort studies to systematically analyze the barriers to LTBI treatment in this vulnerable population.
