Contemporary endodontic practices among endodontic specialists in Saudi Arabia: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Ghaliah Alsawah, Abdullah AlShaikh, Reem Barakat, Afnan Alotaibi, Lama Almunasser, Yasmin Alawaji, Rahaf Almohareb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess and compare contemporary evidence-based endodontic practices and continuing education preferences among endodontists.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between November 2023 and January 2024 through in-person recruitment of endodontic residents and practicing endodontists from governmental and private sectors, as well as at a national dental conference. The questionnaire included 4 demographic items and 21 items evaluating clinical practices, satisfaction, and preferences for continuing education. Questions were presented in multiple-choice and Likert-scale formats. Descriptive statistics were calculated, and associations were examined using chi-square, Fisher’s exact and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 115 endodontists met the inclusion criteria (residents: 30.4%, n = 35; specialists/consultants: 69.6%, n = 80). Most participants had fewer than 10 years of experience and worked in governmental institutions (78.3%, n = 90), although over half practiced in multiple settings. Cold testing and periapical radiographs were used universally. Sodium hypochlorite irrigation, electronic apex locators, and rotary instrumentation were used by nearly all respondents. Obturation was most commonly performed using warm vertical compaction and/or single-cone techniques (83.5%, n = 96). Calcium silicate-based sealers (93.9%, n = 108) and system-matched gutta-percha (97.4%, n = 112) were widely preferred. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) was used by 80% (n = 92), and consultants were more likely to request it routinely (p = 0.002). Consistent use of magnification and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution was reported by 46.9% (n = 54) and 49.6% (n = 57), respectively. Satisfaction with instrumentation and obturation was moderate. Certification or fellowship programs were the preferred formats for continuing education.ConclusionsEndodontics residents, specialists and consultants reported strong adoption of several core evidence-based technologies. However, variation remains in routine CBCT use, magnification, and adjunctive EDTA irrigation. Introduction

Endodontics is a dental specialty concerned with the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of pulpal and periapical diseases. Its scope includes root canal treatment (RCT), retreatment, and apicoectomy procedures intended to eliminate infection, promote healing, and preserve natural teeth. Over the past two decades, advances such as nickel-titanium rotary systems, electronic apex locators, magnification, evidence-based irrigation protocols, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), and calcium silicate-based sealers have improved the precision and predictability of clinical outcomes [1–6].

Position statements and clinical practice guidance from the American Association of Endodontists, the European Society of Endodontology, and the British Endodontic Society emphasize aseptic technique, effective irrigation and disinfection, accurate working-length determination, three-dimensional obturation, and timely coronal restoration as foundations of high-quality endodontic care [7–9]. Despite strong evidence supporting contemporary endodontic approaches, integration into routine practice remains uneven across geographic regions and among practitioners with varied training backgrounds. Recent international and transnational surveys have reported variability in the adoption of newer technologies and guideline-recommended practices, highlighting gaps between recommendations and clinical implementation [10–12].

In Saudi Arabia, the dental sector has expanded rapidly, accompanied by growth in postgraduate endodontic programs and an increasing number of trained specialists. Recent workforce data estimate approximately 1,336 registered endodontists in 2024, equivalent to about 4.1 per 100,000 population, reflecting continued development of training pathways [13, 14]. Earlier Saudi studies have focused primarily on endodontic residents or mixed practitioner groups (e.g., general dental practitioners and endodontists). As such, this limits conclusions about contemporary practice patterns among fully trained endodontists [6, 15–17]. The Saudi Commission for Health Specialties classifies endodontic specialists into four ranks, namely consultant, senior registrar, registrar, and training resident, based on their qualifications and clinical experience.

Continuing education (CE) is essential to maintain evidence-based practice, refine clinical skills, and integrate emerging technologies. However, participation may be limited by barriers such as workload, time constraints, and financial or geographic limitations [18, 19]. Newer CE modalities, including online and distance learning, may help overcome these barriers [20, 21]. Nevertheless, evidence on CE engagement patterns and format preferences among endodontic residents and specialists in Saudi Arabia remains limited [22].

The present study aimed to assess the extent to which evidence-based practices and modern technologies are used by endodontists and to compare clinical behaviors, satisfaction, and CE preferences across professional rank and experience. For the purpose of this study, participants were classified according to the Saudi Commission for Health Specialists as residents, specialists, and consultants. The specialist category included senior registrars and registrars.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was registered, and ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Princess Norah Bint Abdulrahman University (IRB: 24–0527).

A questionnaire was developed based on previously published instruments examining endodontic practices among dental practitioners [10, 23]. Items were adapted to align with current clinical guidelines and recommendations. Three endodontists with more than five years of experience reviewed the questionnaire for relevance, representativeness, and simplicity. It was then piloted among eight endodontists and residents to assess clarity and response time. Redundant or ambiguous items were modified or removed.

The questionnaire began with an informed consent item following an explanation of the study’s purpose. It included 4 demographic questions and 21 items about endodontic practices, including diagnosis, isolation, materials, instruments and devices, techniques, satisfaction, and preferences for CE. Most items were single- or multiple-choice. Questions about CE preferences used a Likert-scale format. A sample of the questionnaire is provided in Appendix 1 as supplementary material.

The survey was administered using Google Forms. All items were set as required in the survey platform, and participants were prompted to complete any unanswered items before submission. A convenience sampling approach was used. Between November 2023 and January 2024, visits were carried out at private and governmental dental clinics and hospitals in Riyadh, where endodontists were approached in person and invited to complete the survey. Additional responses were sought during the Saudi International Dental Conference in January 2024, where attendees were invited to participate by scanning a QR code linked to the questionnaire. In total, 320 dental practitioners were invited to participate.

The sample size was calculated based on the reported population of endodontists in Saudi Arabia [13] using the proportion formula, n₀ = Z²p(1 − p)/d², and adjusted for finite population using n = n₀ / [1 + (n₀ − 1)/N]. Because prevalence estimates for emerging endodontic technologies varied across prior studies, a conservative expected proportion of 50% was assumed. The minimum sample size was 169 participants, based on a 90% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. A total of 202 responses were received, of which 87 were from general practitioners and were excluded, leaving 115 valid responses for analysis (Fig. 1).

STROBE flow diagram of participant selection, recruitment, and inclusion in the final analysis

Data were exported to Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were calculated. Likert-scale data were non-normally distributed according to the Shapiro–Wilk test (p < 0.0001) and were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test [24]. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to assess associations between variables. Items were analyzed individually, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The survey response rate was 36% (n = 115). 60% of respondents were female (n = 69). Postgraduate residents represented 30.4% of participants (n = 35), while consultants or specialists comprised 69.6% (n = 80). Although many respondents worked in multiple settings, most practiced in governmental institutions (78.3%, n = 90), followed by private (37.4%, n = 43) and educational (32.2%, n = 37) sectors. Most participants (75.7%, n = 87) had 1 to 10 years of clinical experience (Table 1).

Regarding magnification, 46.9% (n = 54) reported consistent use of magnification (loupes and/or an operating microscope), and 20% (n = 23) used loupes exclusively. Nearly all respondents (97.4%, n = 112) used an electronic apex locator for working-length determination and rotary files for instrumentation. Sodium hypochlorite was used by all respondents as the primary irrigant, and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was used as an adjunct irrigant by 49.6% (n = 57) (Table 2). A significant association was observed between years of experience and EDTA use (p ≤ 0.0001), with clinicians having fewer than 15 years of experience reporting higher usage (Table 4).

In terms of obturation techniques, vertical compaction and single-cone techniques were reported by 83.5% (n = 96), whereas lateral condensation was reported by 30.4% (n = 35). Calcium silicate–based sealers were preferred by 93.9% (n = 108), followed by resin-based (65.2%, n = 75) and eugenol-based sealers (5.2%, n = 6). System-matched gutta-percha cones were used by 97.4% (n = 112); 4% taper cones were used by 67.8% (n = 78), whereas 6% taper cones were used by 6.1% (n = 7) (Table 2). Half of the centers (50.4%, n = 58) supplied gutta-percha cones and sealers. Access to apex locators was reported by 98.3% (n = 113), rotary motors by 93% (n = 107), and obturation devices by 86.1% (n = 99) (Table 5).

Regarding satisfaction, 59.1% (n = 68) were satisfied with their instrumentation technique, and 49.6% (n = 57) with their obturation technique. The main factors influencing the selection of instrumentation and obturation techniques were ease of use, 77.4–82.6% (n = 89–95), and time efficiency, 84.3–87% (n = 97–100) (Table 5).

Among CE formats, certification or fellowship programs received the highest mean rating (6.50 ± 0.97). Lower mean scores were observed for journal clubs and study groups (3.97 ± 1.78) and virtual reality or simulation-based training (4.38 ± 1.61) (Table 6). Participants with less than five years of experience preferred certification and fellowship programs (p = 0.024), whereas those with more than 15 years of experience showed stronger preferences for online courses and webinars (p = 0.007) and certification/fellowship programs (p = 0.024) (Table 7).

Discussion

This study investigated contemporary endodontic practice patterns among residents and specialists practicing across governmental, private, and academic sectors in Saudi Arabia. The higher proportion of female participants compared with some international surveys may reflect shifts in gender distribution within dentistry and specialty training [10, 14]. Most respondents were early- to mid-career clinicians (75%, n = 87) with ≤ 10 years of experience and predominantly employed in governmental institutions (78.3%, n = 90), consistent with recent workforce analyses describing the strong role of the public sector [13, 14].

Diagnostic practices showed strong reliance on conventional pulp sensibility testing, with near universal use of thermal testing and frequent use of electric pulp testing. The combined application of these methods is consistent with evidence demonstrating improved diagnostic accuracy compared with either modality alone [24, 25]. In contrast, bitewing radiographs were used less frequently. This pattern likely reflects a continued emphasis on periapical imaging in endodontic decision making, despite evidence supporting the value of bitewings for detecting proximal caries and early pulpal involvement that may influence treatment planning [26–28]. The limited use of bitewings suggests a gap not in imaging availability but in diagnostic protocol standardization and training priorities. Similar trends have been reported in a Japanese survey, which highlighted the need for enhanced clinician training in bitewing interpretation and policy updates to support their routine use [26].

CBCT imaging was widely available but was generally used selectively rather than routinely. This pattern is consistent with international guidelines that recommend CBCT primarily for complex cases, retreatment, or situations in which conventional radiography is inconclusive [1, 29]. Consultants were more likely to request CBCT on a routine basis, which may reflect hierarchical clinical decision-making structures in which more experienced clinicians assume greater responsibility for advanced imaging decisions. Rather than indicating overuse, this pattern likely represents cautious adoption influenced by radiation considerations and institutional imaging policies. Similar trends have been reported in Swiss, German, and other international surveys, which also documented higher CBCT utilization among more highly trained endodontists [10, 30, 31].

Participants reported universal adoption of core components of modern endodontic practice, including rubber dam isolation, electronic apex locators, and rotary instrumentation. This aligns with contemporary evidence and international survey findings indicating widespread adoption among specialists [2, 3, 10, 12]. In contrast, only about half of clinicians reported consistent use of magnification, which is lower than rates reported in the United States and Europe [10, 12, 32]. This difference likely reflects variation in equipment availability across practice settings, particularly between institutional clinics and private practices, as operating microscopes require substantial financial investment and infrastructural support. These findings suggest that access to equipment, rather than lack of awareness or training, is a primary determinant of microscope utilization.

Rotary nickel–titanium instrumentation was the predominant choice, consistent with evidence that NiTi systems enhance shaping efficiency and reduce procedural errors compared with hand instrumentation [2]. Recent surveys from Spain, Latin America, and multinational cohorts have similarly documented widespread adoption in specialist practice [10, 12].

Sodium hypochlorite was universally used as the primary irrigant, confirming adherence to established biological principles of chemomechanical debridement [4, 33, 34]. In contrast, adjunctive use of EDTA was reported by only about half of participants, a rate lower than that described in several international surveys [10, 12]. Because EDTA plays an important role in smear layer removal and improving dentin surface conditions [4, 5], inconsistent use may affect sealer penetration and adaptation, particularly with calcium silicate–based sealers [33], which were highly preferred by respondents. This discrepancy suggests that clinicians may be adopting new obturation materials faster than the corresponding irrigation protocols required to optimize their performance. Targeted continuing education focusing on irrigation protocols may therefore improve treatment standardization and clinical outcomes.

Chlorhexidine use was limited. Although it provides antimicrobial benefits, it possesses negligible capacity for organic tissue dissolution compared with sodium hypochlorite, which supports the continued role of sodium hypochlorite as the primary irrigant [34].

Obturation practices reflected a shift toward contemporary techniques, with frequent use of single-cone and thermoplasticized methods and widespread preference for calcium silicate–based sealers. These trends parallel international practice patterns and indicate rapid incorporation of newer materials into clinical care [10, 12, 17]. However, only about half of respondents reported consistent availability of gutta-percha cones and sealers at their centers. This finding highlights an important systems level issue: technology adoption alone does not ensure consistent practice if procurement processes limit material availability. In large institutional settings, supply logistics may influence technique selection as much as clinician preference or training.

Despite growing evidence that single- and multiple-visit root canal treatments achieve comparable clinical outcomes, most practitioners in the present study reported a preference for two-visit protocols. This preference may reflect case complexity, institutional scheduling practices, or prevailing clinical philosophies rather than outcome-based considerations alone [35].

Access to major endodontic devices was high; however, only about half of respondents reported consistent availability of gutta-percha cones and sealers at their centers, suggesting that procurement and supply processes can still limit clinical efficiency. This is consistent with prior reports describing practical challenges in dental material ordering and supply within governmental dental facilities in Saudi Arabia [36].

Clinician satisfaction with instrumentation and obturation systems was moderate, and technique selection was primarily influenced by ease of use and time efficiency. This suggests that practical clinical considerations remain major drivers of decision-making even among specialists. Educational programs may therefore benefit from emphasizing workflow efficiency alongside biological principles, as adoption of techniques is closely linked to operatory time and clinical practicality [10, 12, 15–17].

Regarding continuing education, clinicians demonstrated a strong preference for structured certification or fellowship programs over informal educational activities. Less experienced clinicians favored formal, credential-oriented programs, whereas more experienced practitioners preferred flexible learning formats such as online courses and webinars. These findings suggest that continuing education initiatives may be more effective if tailored according to career stage, with early-career clinicians prioritizing comprehensive training programs and senior clinicians favoring accessible, update focused formats. These preferences are consistent with previous studies indicating that clinicians tend to value structured, credential-bearing and practice oriented educational models [19, 37–39].

The main limitation of the present study is that it did not achieve the calculated sample size. Although the achieved sample provides reasonable precision for overall descriptive estimates, it was likely underpowered for subgroup analyses by education level, years of experience, or practice setting. Therefore, subgroup comparisons should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory. Furthermore, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed.

Within these limitations, the findings suggest substantial reported adoption of evidence-based endodontic technologies among surveyed clinicians. However, variability in irrigation protocols, magnification use, and material availability indicates that practice patterns are shaped not only by clinical knowledge but also by institutional resources, procurement systems, and training pathways. Future initiatives should therefore address protocol standardization and material accessibility in addition to technological implementation.

Conclusions

In this national cohort, endodontic residents and specialists demonstrated broad adoption of core evidence-based diagnostics and technologies. Variation was observed in CBCT usage patterns, adjunctive EDTA use, consistent use of magnification, and reliable supply of consumables. Strengthening standardized clinical pathways, improving supply reliability, and aligning CE offerings with clinicians’ preferences may help enhance endodontic care practices and quality across diverse practice settings.
