Manganese-nickel cobaltite/graphitic carbon nitride/cerium oxide nanocomposite for vanillin sensing in food samples
Authors: Ali Ramzannezhad, Shahram Ghasemi, Shayan Safari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vanillin is a widely used flavoring agent in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries; however, its excessive intake may pose potential health risks, necessitating sensitive and reliable detection methods. In this work, a novel and cost-effective electrochemical sensor was developed based on Mn0.5Ni0.5Co2O4 (MNC) spinel decorated with g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticles (NPs). The effect of different loading ratios of g-C3N@CeO2 NPs on the sensor performance was systematically evaluated to identify the optimal composition. The incorporation of g-C3N4@CeO2 NPs significantly enhanced the electroactive surface area, accelerated electron transfer kinetics, and improved the electrocatalytic activity toward vanillin oxidation. The synthesized nanocomposites were characterized by spectroscopic, structural, morphological, and electrochemical techniques, confirming the successful fabrication and improved electrochemical properties. Under optimized conditions, the sensor exhibited a low limit of detection (LOD) of 6 nM and a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 19 nM, along with two linear response ranges of 0.006-0.4 µM and 1-1000 µM. Additionally, a wide detection range up to 4000 µM was achieved. The sensor demonstrated excellent selectivity, reproducibility, and long-term stability. Its practical applicability was verified by the accurate determination of vanillin in real food samples, yielding satisfactory recovery and precision. These findings highlight the potential of surface-engineered spinel/NPs nanocomposites as efficient platforms for sensitive electrochemical sensing applications.

Introduction

Vanillin (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde) is one of the most widely used flavoring agents in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries owing to its distinctive aroma and low toxicity at regulated concentrations1–3. Beyond its pleasant sensory properties, vanillin also serves as an important intermediate in fine chemical synthesis and exhibits notable antioxidant and antimicrobial activities4. However, excessive intake of vanillin has been linked to adverse health effects, including liver dysfunction, gastrointestinal disorders, and allergic responses5. Therefore, accurate and reliable monitoring of vanillin levels in commercial products is essential to ensure consumer safety and regulatory compliance6. Various analytical techniques have been developed for vanillin determination, such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)7, gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy (GC-MS)8, capillary electrophoresis9, and UV-visible spectrophotometric10. Although these methods offer high sensitivity and accuracy, they often involve laborious sample preparation, sophisticated instrumentation, lengthy analysis times, and high operational costs, limiting their practicality for routine and on-site monitoring11,12. In addition to conventional techniques, fluorescence-based sensing methods have been explored for vanillin detection due to their high sensitivity, rapid response, and simplicity13. Fluorescent probes, especially those based on nanomaterials, provide low detection limits and allow visual detection. However, fluorescence sensing suffers from photobleaching, background interference in complex matrices, and limited long-term stability, and the need for optical instrumentation may restrict practical applicability14. In comparison, electrochemical sensors offer robustness, cost-effectiveness, miniaturization potential, and enhanced stability, making them more suitable for reliable vanillin detection in complex food samples. Electrochemical sensors based on carbon nanotubes, graphene derivatives, metal oxides, and composite nanomaterials have shown promising performance for vanillin determination, providing rapid response and high sensitivity15–18. However, challenges remain in terms of LOD, selectivity, and long-term stability, particularly in the presence of interfering species19–21. To address these limitations, advanced electrode materials with enhanced electrocatalytic activity and structural stability are needed. Spinel-type metal oxides, especially ternary and quaternary variants, have attracted considerable attention as electrode materials due to their rich redox chemistry, high surface area, and tunable electronic structure22. The Mn0.5Ni0.5Co2O4 (MNC) spinel integrates multiple Mn/Ni/Co redox centers that facilitate efficient electron transport and robust electrocatalytic activity23,24. However, despite its spinel structure and multiple redox centers, pristine MNC still exhibits suboptimal electrical conductivity and a tendency toward particle agglomeration, which can limit its electrochemical performance. To address these limitations, a dual-modification approach was employed. First, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), a two-dimensional semiconductor rich in nitrogen sites, was incorporated as a conductive and high-surface-area scaffold. Its layered structure mitigates MNC aggregation and fosters interfacial coupling and synergistic catalytic effects25–27. Second, cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs) were introduced to enhance redox activity. Owing to their unique oxygen storage/release capability and facile Ce3+/Ce4+ transitions, CeO2 NPs accelerate charge transfer and create additional electroactive sites28,29. The integration of g-C3N4 and CeO2 NPs with MNC spinel is expected to improve intrinsic conductivity, enlarge the electroactive surface, and establish multiple cooperative catalytic centers. Consequently, the resulting hierarchical nanohybrid architecture enhances sensitivity, lowers the LOD, and improves selectivity for vanillin detection, even in complex matrices30. In this work, a novel hierarchical MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle is designed and employed as an electrochemical sensing platform for vanillin detection. The synergistic coupling of the spinel oxide with graphitic carbon nitride and cerium oxide nanoparticles enhances electrical conductivity, increases electroactive surface area, and creates multiple cooperative redox sites. The decoration ratio of g-C3N4@CeO2 NPs was systematically optimized to achieve superior sensing performance. As a result, the developed sensor exhibits a low LOD, a wide linear response range, excellent selectivity, and long-term stability. Its practical applicability was successfully demonstrated through vanillin determination in real food samples, underscoring its potential for reliable food safety monitoring.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and instruments

All chemicals, including Mn(CH3COO)2.4H2O (≥ 99%), Ni(CH3COO)2.4H2O (≥ 98%), Co(CH3COO)2.4H2O (≥ 98%), citric acid (≥ 99%), ethylene glycol (≥ 99%), urea (≥ 99%), Ce(NO3)2.6H2O (≥ 99%), NaHCO3 (≥ 99%), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4), and vanillin (≥ 99%), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and used without further purification.

Synthesis of MNC spinel nanoparticles

The Mn0.5Ni0.5Co2O4 spinel nanoparticles were synthesized via a sol-gel method. Mn(CH3COO)2.4H2O (0.96 g), Ni(CH3COO)2.4H2O (0.98 g), and Co(CH3COO)2.4H2O (3.91 g) were dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water with continuous magnetic stirring. Citric acid (5.42 g) was then added as a chelating agent to complex the metal ions. Subsequently, ammonia solution (25 wt%) was added dropwise to adjust the pH to 7. Ethylene glycol (40 mL) was introduced as a cross-linking agent to promote gel formation. The resulting mixture was heated on a hot plate at 120 °C for 8 h to form a homogeneous gel. The gel was dried in an oven at 100 °C overnight, and the dried precursor was calcined in a muffle furnace at 450 °C for 5 h with a heating rate of 2 °C min− 1. The obtained black powder was designated as the Mn0.5Ni0.5Co2O4 spinel nanocomposite.

Synthesis of g-C3N4 nanosheets

Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) nanosheets were synthesized through thermal decomposition of urea in a covered alumina crucible. In a typical procedure, 1 g of urea was placed in the crucible and heated in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 2 h under air, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. After cooling naturally to room temperature, the resulting yellow bulk g-C3N4 was collected and ground into a fine powder. To obtain nanosheets, the bulk g-C3N4 powder was dispersed in deionized water and ultrasonicated for 2 h. The upper supernatant containing exfoliated g-C3N4 nanosheets was carefully collected, washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C overnight31.

Synthesis of CeO2@g-C3N4 nanosheets

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, 2g of g-C3N4 nanosheets were dispersed in 90 mL of ethanol via ultrasonication for 1.5 h. Subsequently, 2 g of Ce(NO3)2.6H2O was added to the dispersion. Then, 0.25 g of NaHCO3 was introduced, which acts as a mild base, facilitating the hydrolysis of Ce2+ ions to form CeO2 nanoparticles and helping to control particle growth and dispersion on g-C3N4 nanosheets. The mixture was continuously stirred at room temperature for 8 h. The resulting product was collected by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried in an oven at 40 °C overnight. Finally, the dried powder was calcined at 350 °C for 2 h.

Fabrication of MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle

Three different loading ratios of g-C3N4/CeO2 NPs on MNC spinel were prepared. This was done to investigate the effect of nanoparticle loading on electrochemical performance. In a typical procedure for the 1:1 ratio, 5 mg of g-C3N4@CeO2 NPs was dispersed in 5 mL ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) via ultrasonication for 60 min. Separately, 5 mg of MNC spinel powder was ultrasonically dispersed in 5 mL ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) for 30 min. The two suspensions were combined and magnetically stirred at room temperature for 12 h to promote hetero-assembly of the spinel onto the CeO2 NP/g-C3N4 nanosheets.

For the higher loading ratios (2:1 and 3:1), the same procedure was followed, using 10 mg and 15 mg of CeO2 NPs/g-C3N4, respectively, while keeping the spinel mass constant at 5 mg. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged, washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C overnight. Finally, the powders were annealed at 300 °C for 2 h in air to strengthen interfacial contact and prevent particle coarsening (Fig. 1b).

Fabrication of modified electrode

The surface of the glassy carbon electrode (GCE) was sequentially polished with Al2O3 powder on an emery pad, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol, and then dried at room temperature (25 °C). The MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanocomposite was dispersed in deionized water, and an optimized volume (3 µL) of the suspension was drop-cast onto the polished electrode surface. The modified electrode was subsequently dried at 60 °C before being employed in electrochemical measurements.

Material characterization

FTIR spectroscopy of MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2nanoparticle

FTIR spectra were collected in the range of 500–4000 cm-1 using a Perkin-Elmer 1730 spectrometer. The pellets for measurement were obtained by blending the sample with KBr and pressing the mixture into disks under hydraulic pressure.

X-ray diffraction of MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle

XRD measurements were carried out on a Rigaku Ultima diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). The diffraction data were collected within the 2θ range of 10–80°, using a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 10° min− 1.

Microstructural characterization by FESEM and TEM

The surface morphology of the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle was examined using a TESCAN Mira4 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM). In addition, a Philips EM 208 S transmission electron microscope (TEM) was employed to investigate the internal structure and nanoscale features of the sample.

Electrochemical investigations

Electrochemical measurements were carried out using an Autolab 302 N potentiostat (Metrohm, Netherlands). The techniques employed included EIS, CV, DPV, and amperometric analysis to comprehensively evaluate the electrochemical behavior of the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle.

Results and discussion

Characterization of MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of the samples, including pure g-C3N4, g-C3N4@CeO2 NPs, MNC, and MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle, are shown in Fig. 2a. For pristine g-C3N4, the characteristic band at 741 cm− 1 can be attributed to out-of-plane bending vibrations of N–H32. The absorption peaks at 1372 cm− 1 and 1643 cm− 1 can be assigned to the stretching modes of C–N and C = N groups within the triazine framework33. The broad absorption band at 3465 cm− 1 is assigned to surface –OH groups and hydrogen-bonded functionalities34. Upon the incorporation of CeO2 NPs into g-C3N4, a slight red-shift and decrease in the intensity of the C–N/C = N vibrations are observed, suggesting electronic interactions between Ce4+ species and the π-conjugated g-C3N4 network35. The broadening of the hydroxyl band at 3465 cm− 1 further suggests the contribution of surface hydroxyl groups originating from CeO2, serving as active adsorption and reaction sites36. For MNC, the spectrum mainly shows weak absorption features in the 500–700 cm− 1 region, corresponding to M–O lattice vibrations of the spinel phase37. The absence of C–N and C = N bands confirms the phase purity of the synthesized oxide38. In the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle, the spectrum clearly shows the coexistence of the characteristic g-C3N4 and CeO2 bands, accompanied by distinct changes in intensity and position. The attenuation of the C–N/C = N vibrations compared with pristine g-C3N4 suggests strong interfacial interactions between the spinel and g-C3N4 frameworks39. Moreover, the enhanced and broadened hydroxyl band around 3465 cm− 1 indicates abundant surface functional groups and synergistic coupling among the nanohybrid components40.

XRD analysis

The XRD pattern of the MNC sample is presented in Fig. 2b. All major diffraction peaks can be indexed to a typical cubic spinel structure consistent with NiCo2O4 (JCPDS NO. 20–0781)41, belonging to the Fd3m space group. The characteristic reflections located at corresponding 2θ values are assigned to the (111), (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystal planes, confirming the formation of a well-crystallized spinel phase.

The absence of additional diffraction peaks indicates that no detectable crystalline impurity phases such as NiO or Mn3O4 are present within the resolution limit of the XRD instrument, suggesting the successful formation of a single-phase mixed metal oxide42. The MNC composition can be considered as a substituted spinel structure derived from NiCo2O4, where partial incorporation of Mn ions into the lattice leads to slight structural modification. Two weak reflections observed at 2θ ≈ 44 and 75° can be attributed to higher-index planes of the spinel lattice, consistent with the calculated lattice parameters (a ≈ 8.23–8.30 Å)43. Minor shifts in diffraction peak positions compared to the standard pattern are attributed to the substitution of Co ions by Mn and Ni ions, whose different ionic radii induce lattice distortion and internal strain within the spinel framework. Such peak shifts and variations are commonly reported in multi-cation spinel oxides due to cation redistribution between tetrahedral and octahedral sites43. The relatively broad full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peaks indicates nanocrystalline domains. These results confirm the successful formation of a well-integrated nanocomposite structure.

Study of morphological features

The surface morphology of the synthesized MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle was examined by FESEM at various magnifications, as shown in Fig. 3 (a-c). The images reveal a hierarchically porous morphology composed of interconnected nanoparticles and wrinkled sheet-like structures. As shown in Fig. 3a, the composite exhibits a rough surface with well-dispersed aggregates, indicating homogeneous integration of the MNC spinel phase within the g-C3N4 framework. This uniform distribution prevents particle agglomeration and exposes a large number of electroactive sites. In Fig. 3b, the nanostructure becomes more compact and interconnected, suggesting the formation of a continuous conductive network that facilitates rapid charge transport throughout the nanocomposite. The g-C3N4 framework provides structural stability and promotes efficient electron migration, both of which are crucial for enhancing sensing performance. At higher structural detail, Fig. 3c suggests the presence of CeO2 NPs dispersed on the MNC/g-C3N4 matrix. These nanoparticles establish intimate interfacial contact and form multiple heterojunctions, enabling accelerated redox kinetics and improved electron transfer efficiency. The elemental composition was verified through EDX (Fig. 3d). The pronounced peaks of C, N, and O confirm the g-C3N4 framework, while the signals of Mn, Ni, and Co indicate the presence of the mixed spinel phase. Characteristic Ce peaks further demonstrate the uniform dispersion of CeO2 NPs. The quantitative data (inset table) reveal carbon and nitrogen as the dominant constituents, consistent with the g-C3N4 backbone, whereas lower atomic percentages of Ce, Mn, Ni, and Co suggest a homogeneous distribution rather than aggregation. The absence of unexpected impurity peaks, aside from Au originating from the conductive coating, confirms the high purity of the sample.

The images reveal thin, crumpled nanosheets serving as a conductive scaffold on which fine MNC particles are uniformly anchored (Fig. 4a and b). This configuration forms a well-connected nanohybrid architecture that prevents particle aggregation and ensures close interfacial contact among the components. As shown in Fig. 4c and d, the darker contrast regions correspond to MNC domains tightly embedded within the lighter g-C3N4 framework, indicating strong electronic interaction between the two phases. Such intimate interfaces promote efficient charge migration, an essential factor for achieving high electrochemical response. In Fig. 4e, uniformly dispersed CeO2 NPs are observed as distinct dark spots decorating the nanohybrid surface. Their homogeneous distribution and nanoscale dimensions suggest effective hybridization within the MNC/g-C3N4 matrix, contributing additional redox-active centers and facilitating rapid electron exchange. Optimization of active material loading

To optimize the loading of the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle on the GCE, different suspension volumes (2, 3, 4, and 5 µL) were drop-cast and evaluated in a PBS solution (pH = 7) containing 10 mM vanillin. Although smaller volumes produced higher current responses, the signals lacked stability due to uneven surface coverage. A loading of 3 µL yielded a more uniform and stable film, delivering a reliable electrochemical response. When the loading exceeded this, the peak current declined—likely because the thicker film impeded electron flow and mass transport across the electrode–electrolyte interface. Accordingly, 3 µL was chosen as the optimal loading for all subsequent electrochemical tests (Fig. 5a).

Electrochemical performance of MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 NP

EIS was used to probe the interfacial charge-transfer behavior of the electrodes. Figure 5b shows Nyquist plots for bare GCE, MNC, g-C3N4@CeO2 NP, and the ternary MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle, recorded in 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− with 0.1 M KCl over the frequency range of 100 kHz to 1 Hz. The impedance data were fitted using a modified Randles equivalent circuit including solution resistance (Rs), charge-transfer resistance (Rct), a constant phase element (CPE) for nonideal double-layer capacitance, and a Warburg element (W) to account for diffusion at low frequencies. In this model, the diameter of the high-frequency semicircle corresponds to the charge-transfer resistance Rct, which directly reflects the efficiency of interfacial electron-transfer kinetics. The bare GCE exhibited the largest semicircle with an Rct of ~ 95 kΩ, indicating sluggish electron transfer due to limited electroactive surface. Modification with MNC significantly reduced the semicircle diameter, yielding an Rct of ~ 68 kΩ, highlighting the contribution of the spinel structure and multiple redox-active centers to improved charge conduction. The g-C3N4@CeO2 NP-modified electrode showed an Rct of ~ 85 kΩ, lower than that of bare GCE but higher than MNC, suggesting that while CeO2 NPs introduce oxygen vacancies and defect sites to improve conductivity, the semiconducting g-C3N4 component still limits charge transfer. In contrast, the ternary MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle achieves the lowest Rct (~ 55 kΩ). The percentage reduction in Rct was calculated based on the relative decrease in charge-transfer resistance compared to the reference electrode, providing a quantitative measure of the improvement in interfacial electron-transfer kinetics. These reductions demonstrate that the ternary nanohybrid more efficiently lowers the kinetic barrier for electron transfer than each individual component. The significantly reduced Rct reflects a synergistic effect arising from the high intrinsic conductivity and redox activity of MNC, the π-conjugated pathways of g-C3N4, and the defect-rich surfaces of CeO2 NPs which together establish a more conductive and electronically coupled interface. Although Warburg features appear at low frequencies for all electrodes, indicating diffusion-influenced processes, the dominant improvement originates from the reduced charge-transfer resistance, which directly translates into faster electron-transfer kinetics and enhanced electrochemical performance.

CV measurements were performed on bare GCE, MNC, g-C3N4@CeO2 NPs, and the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 NP-modified electrode in 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]4−/3−with 0.1 M KCl (Fig. 5c). The unmodified GCE displayed weak and broad redox peaks, consistent with slow charge-transfer kinetics. After modification with MNC spinel oxide, the current response improved modestly, likely due to mixed-valence states of Mn, Ni, and Co enhancing electronic conductivity. Further coating with g-C3N4 decorated by CeO2 NPs resulted in higher currents and sharper peak reversibility, which can be attributed to the conductive g-C3N4 layers and the catalytic contributions of CeO2 NPs. Among all electrodes, the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 nanoparticle exhibited redox current and narrowest potential separation, indicating the most efficient interfacial electron transport. The excellent performance of this hybrid is ascribed to strong interfacial coupling and synergism among the spinel oxide, carbon nitride, and NPs, which enhance the electroactive surface area, supply abundant catalytic sites, and streamline charge transfer. These results affirm the nanohybrid’s promise as an electrode modifier for electrochemical applications.

CV responses of bare GCE and GCE modified with MNC: g-C3N4@CeO2 NP at various ratios (1:1, 1:2, 1:3) were measured over the potential range from − 200 mV to 600 mV (Fig. 5d). The unmodified electrode showed minimal redox current, indicative of limited conductivity and slow electron transport. Modification with MNC: g-C3N4@CeO2 NP markedly increased the peak currents, underscoring synergistic effects among the spinel matrix, CeO2 NPs, and g-C3N4. Of the tested ratios, the 1:2 composition exhibited the highest anodic and cathodic currents, implying optimal site accessibility and superior electrocatalytic performance. The 1:1 ratio resulted in moderate enhancement, while the 1:3 ratio experienced a slight decline, likely due to the aggregation of NPs that reduced the number of accessible active sites. Well-defined redox peaks indicate reversible electron transfer, and the quasi-rectangular mid-potential region suggests pseudocapacitive behavior and surface charge storage. Overall, these observations confirm that modifications with MNC: g-C3N4@CeO2 NP significantly improve electrode characteristics. The 1:2 ratio provides the best balance between conductivity and active site exposure, maximizing electrocatalytic activity and storage capacity, making this nanohybrid architecture attractive for applications in electrochemical sensing and energy devices. Despite the higher current response observed for the g-C3N4@CeO2 NP compared to the spinel, the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of the spinel remains lower. This apparent discrepancy arises from the different electrochemical information provided by CV and EIS measurements. The enhanced current of g-C3N4@CeO2 is mainly attributed to the increased electrochemically active surface area and abundant surface-active sites, whereas the intrinsic charge transfer kinetics at the electrode-electrolyte interface are more favorable for the spinel structure, resulting in a lower Rct.

Elucidated molecular mechanism for vanillin detection

The MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 NP coating on the GCE establishes a cooperative sensing interface in which each component plays a distinct role in the electrochemical oxidation of vanillin. Vanillin molecules are first adsorbed onto the π-conjugated domains of g-C3N4 through π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding, facilitating their accumulation near the electrode surface. The electrochemical oxidation of vanillin is generally attributed to a two-electron and two-proton process involving the phenolic hydroxyl group, leading to the formation of the corresponding quinone-type species. CeO2 NPs, enriched with oxygen vacancies, act as electron-transfer mediators by reversibly cycling between Ce3+ and Ce4+ states, thereby facilitating electron shuttling during the oxidation process. Meanwhile, the ternary spinel MNC provides abundant multivalent redox-active sites (Mn, Ni, and Co), which lower the activation energy for vanillin oxidation and promote reaction kinetics. The synergistic integration of molecular adsorption, electron mediation, and catalytic oxidation results in enhanced oxidation currents and improved interfacial charge-transfer characteristics, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 6.

Electrochemical behavior of vanillin

Figure 7a shows the CV responses of bare GCE, MNC/GCE, g-C3N4/GCE, g-C3N4@CeO2 NP/GCE, and MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2/GCE in a 300 µM vanillin solution, recorded in neutral phosphate buffer (PBS, pH = 7) at a scan rate of 100 mV s− 1. A clear divergence in electrochemical performance appears among the electrodes, highlighting how effective surface modification can promote the redox behavior of vanillin. The bare GCE exhibits a weak broadened anodic peak, indicative of slow electron-transfer kinetics and limited catalytic efficiency toward vanillin oxidation. MNC or g-C3N4 on GCE moderately enhance peak currents, reflecting their intrinsic conductivity and catalytic activity over bare GCE. Still, both modified electrodes show relatively low current densities, indicating a constrained ability to drive vanillin oxidation fully. A more pronounced enhancement occurs with the g-C3N4@CeO2/GCE, which displays a distinct, anodic peak, suggesting a cooperative effect between g-C3N4 and CeO2 NPs that enhances electron mobility and supplies additional electroactive sites. This composite structure enhances interfacial charge transfer and supports more efficient vanillin oxidation. Among all tested electrodes, MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2/GCE shows the highest anodic current with a clear and sharp peak. This exceptional electrocatalytic behavior stems from the synergistic integration of the MNC spinel and the conductive g-C3N4@CeO2 network, which together maximize active site density, enhance charge conduction pathways, and accelerate redox kinetics of vanillin. The strong response and improved reversibility confirm that this hierarchical hybrid acts as an efficient catalytic platform that outperforms all other electrodes.

Various concentrations of vanillin

The CV curves of the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 modified GCE were recorded in vanillin at concentrations ranging from 50 to 300 µM (PBS, pH = 7, scan rate: 100 mV s− 1) (Fig. 7b). The anodic peak current increases progressively with increasing vanillin concentration, indicating an enhanced oxidation response. A slight positive shift in the anodic peak potential is observed at higher concentrations, which can be attributed to increased polarization and mass-transport limitations at the electrode surface. Nevertheless, the linear dependence of peak current on concentration suggests that the vanillin oxidation process is predominantly diffusion-controlled under the studied conditions.

A clear linear relationship between vanillin concentration and anodic peak current is illustrated in Fig. 7c. The regression equation y = 0.06465x + 2.0115 with a correlation coefficient R2 = 0.99 attests to the high precision of the electrochemical response. This strong linearity implies consistent active site availability and efficient electron-transfer pathways, ensuring reliable quantification of vanillin across diverse concentrations. These results emphasize the reliability of the nanohybrid electrode for analytical applications.

Scan rate dependence

The cyclic voltammograms of MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2/GCE, recorded over scan rates from 10 to 110 mV s− 1 in the presence of 300 µM vanillin, show a systematic increase in both anodic and cathodic peak currents with increasing scan rate (Fig. 7d). The nearly proportional relation between current and scan rate suggests a diffusion-controlled mechanism, while the preservation of peak shapes indicates consistent electrode kinetics. These findings confirm that the nanohybrid nanostructure enables efficient charge transfer and maintains stable electrochemical activity even under varying dynamic conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 7e, CV analysis demonstrates a linear dependence between the anodic peak current (ip) and the square root of the scan rate (v1/2), confirming a diffusion-controlled electrochemical process. The linear fit of ip versus v1/2 yielded an excellent correlation (R2 = 0.99), confirming the reliability and reproducibility of the diffusion process. Error bars representing the standard deviation from three independent measurements were included to reflect experimental consistency. The small intercept (-1.0482 µA) and the near-unity correlation coefficient reflect the high reversibility and stability of the redox process.

Influence of pH on electrochemical performance

Figure 8a shows the CVs of the g-C3N4@CeO2-decorated MNC spinel electrode recorded in PBS with pH values ranging from 5 to 9. The electrochemical response exhibits a strong dependence on the solution pH, both in terms of peak current intensity and redox potential position. At lower pH (5 and 6), the electrode displays relatively broad and weak peaks with moderate current intensities (5.97 and 7.84 µA, respectively), suggesting sluggish electron transfer and limited accessibility of active sites. The peak current reaches its maximum at neutral pH (pH = 7, 9.39 µA), indicating optimal proton-electron coupling and efficient charge transfer. At slightly alkaline conditions (pH = 8 and 9), the current decreases to 7.40 and 4.22 µA, respectively, implying a non-linear relationship between hydroxide concentration and catalytic activity. This decline at higher pH can be attributed to competitive adsorption of hydroxyl species, which partially block active sites, thereby limiting charge transfer despite the higher availability of OH− ions.

Stability, selectivity, repeatability and reproducibility

Amperometry was chosen for these tests due to its high temporal resolution and suitability for monitoring long-term stability and selectivity.

Stability

The amperometric signal of the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 NP-modified electrode (Fig. 9a) exhibits two sharp, reproducible rises in current at 500 s and 600 s following vanillin injections. These spikes rapidly settle into a stable baseline, persisting unchanged until 2000 s, providing clear evidence of fast electron transfer, abundant catalytic sites, and resistance to surface fouling. The minimal drift and flat baseline indicate that the nanohybrid architecture offers a durable interfacing layer capable of sustained performance, making it suitable for real-sample vanillin detection.

Selectivity

Selectivity testing (Fig. 9b) was conducted by introducing common electroactive interferents over 800 s, namely phenol (Ph), hydroquinone (HQ), acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), ascorbic acid (AA), catechol (CC), uric acid (UA), and glucose (Glu), into the sensing solution. Each added species elicited only a negligible current relative to vanillin, confirming low cross-reactivity. In contrast, subsequent vanillin injections produced clear, strong signals that far exceeded the baseline interference level. This highly discriminative behavior underscores the selective recognition afforded by the synergy between the MNC spinel and CeO2 NPs.

Repeatability (intra-day)

Five consecutive DPV measurements of 40 µM vanillin performed on the same day resulted in a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 2.8%, demonstrating excellent short-term stability.

Reproducibility (inter-day)

Measurements performed over three consecutive days using freshly prepared electrodes yielded an RSD of 3.6%, confirming consistent fabrication and reliable performance. Moreover, all current responses remained within ± 5% of the mean value, further validating the robustness of the sensing interface for practical and reliable quantification in analytical settings.

Food matrix analysis

The applicability of the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 NP-modified electrochemical sensor was assessed in real food samples, including milk, ice cream, and cake. Stock vanillin solution (1 mM in methanol) was prepared, and samples were spiked with appropriate amounts to obtain different concentrations. Each spiked sample was mixed with methanol, vortexed for 2 min, sonicated, and centrifuged. The supernatants were then reconstituted in 0.1 M PBS (pH = 7) to the desired final volume for electrochemical measurements (Fig. 9c). Vanillin standards at concentrations of 20, 40, and 60 µM were spiked into the samples, and the resulting peak currents were recorded using DPV. All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the mean values with RSD% were reported to evaluate reproducibility. Table 2 summarizes the vanillin determination results in the three food matrices. Recovery values ranged from 88 to 107%, while RSDs varied between 2.6 and 3.3%, confirming satisfactory accuracy and precision. The more complex cake matrix exhibited slightly lower recoveries and higher variability compared to milk and ice cream. For liquid samples, recoveries between 92 and 106% confirm the sensor’s linear response and sensitivity across the studied concentration range. Although satisfactory recovery and precision were achieved, further validation using standard analytical techniques such as HPLC will be considered in future work to confirm the accuracy of the proposed method.

Conclusions

In this work, a novel ternary spinel-based nanohybrid was engineered by integrating MNC with g-C3N4 and CeO2 NPs and employed as an advanced electrochemical sensing interface. Comprehensive physicochemical analyses confirmed the structural integrity, nanoscale morphology, and synergistic hybridization of the composite. Electrochemical studies using CV and DPV demonstrated remarkable electrocatalytic activity toward vanillin oxidation. The sensor showed a low LOD of 6 nM and two linear response ranges (0.006-0.4 µM and 1-1000 µM), enabling sensitive quantification from trace to high concentrations. Reproducibility was confirmed by low RSD values, and accuracy was validated through recovery tests in complex food matrices. For milk, ice cream, and cake samples, recoveries ranged from 92 to 110%, with RSDs below 5%, demonstrating reliable performance in real-world conditions. Overall, the MNC/g-C3N4@CeO2 NP-modified electrode provides an efficient, stable, and highly selective platform for vanillin determination. Its excellent analytical performance, combined with cost-effectiveness and facile preparation, underscores the potential of this hybrid nanomaterial for next-generation electrochemical sensors in food safety and quality control.
