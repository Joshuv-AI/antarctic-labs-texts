2.1.
Construction of Distribution via Maximum Entropy Principle

We fix the degree

in the Fermat equation
x^{n}+y^{n}=z^{n}

such that its
n
-th absolute moment is constant.

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{n}]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|^{n}p(x)dx=\mu

Just as the second moment constraint (
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
) corresponds to kinetic energy and leads to the Gaussian distribution in physics, we regard the
n
-th moment here as the generalized energy of this system. Consequently, the statistical manifold constructed hereafter will inherently possess the exponent
n
of the Fermat equation as its metric structure.

There are infinitely many probability distributions
p(x)
that satisfy the constraint condition
\mu

This principle asserts that among the distributions satisfying the given constraints, one should choose the one with the maximum entropy. This implies selecting the distribution with the highest uncertainty—that is, the most statistically neutral distribution—without introducing any subjective bias beyond the information contained in the constraints.

Based on Jaynes’ Maximum Entropy Principle, we uniquely determine the probability density function that maximizes the Shannon entropy
H[p]=-\int p(x)\ln p(x)dx
under this constraint.The constraints to be considered here are the following two:

•

Normalization condition of probability:
\int p(x)dx=1

•

Energy constraint:
\int|x|^{n}p(x)dx=\mu

To solve this constrained variational problem, we introduce Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{0}
(for the normalization term) and
\lambda
(for the energy term), and construct the Lagrangian functional
\mathcal{L}[p]

\mathcal{L}[p]=-\int p(x)\ln p(x)dx-\lambda_{0}\left(\int p(x)dx-1\right)-\lambda\left(\int|x|^{n}p(x)dx-\mu\right)

We apply the stationary condition for entropy maximization
\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta p}=0
.

\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta p}=-\ln p(x)-1-\lambda_{0}-\lambda|x|^{n}=0

Solving this for
p(x)
yields the following form:

p(x)=\exp\left(-1-\lambda_{0}-\lambda|x|^{n}\right)

Here, we absorb the constant terms into a normalization constant
C=\exp(1+\lambda_{0})
and define
\theta=-\lambda
as a parameter corresponding to the physical inverse temperature (where
\theta<0
is required by the convergence condition). Consequently, the resulting probability density function becomes the following generalized normal distribution:

p(x;\theta)=\exp\left(\theta|x|^{n}-\psi(\theta)\right)

Here,
\psi(\theta)=\ln Z(\theta)
is the potential function (free energy), which plays a crucial role in determining the subsequent geometric structure. This form guarantees that the constructed model belongs to the exponential family, thereby ensuring the existence of a dually flat structure.

First, we provide the preliminaries.

Statistical Embedding and Invariance

The geometric structure in the space of probability distributions must not depend on the method of representation of observed data (choice of parameters or data transformations that do not lose information). To formulate this mathematically, we introduce the concept of an invariant metric.

Definition: Invariant Metric and Information Monotonicity

A metric
g
on a statistical manifold is said to be invariant if, for any mapping
T:X\to Y
by a sufficient statistic, the metric does not contract (possesses information monotonicity). In particular, for reversible transformations that do not lose information from data (transformations by sufficient statistics), the distance on the manifold must be preserved. In other words, this is a requirement that the distance between distributions must not change merely by relabeling the data.

Chentsov’s Theorem and its Extension

The geometric structure of a statistical model should possess universality independent of the representation format of the observed data. N.N. Chentsov (1972) imposed ”information monotonicity” as an axiom, which states that the metric does not increase under Markov mappings (probabilistic data transformations independent of parameters). Under this requirement, it has been proven that for discrete distributions, the only Riemannian metric that is invariant, particularly under transformations by sufficient statistics (reversible Markov embeddings), is the Fisher information metric, up to a constant scalar factor.

Theorem: Chentsov’s Theorem”

On a manifold consisting of probability distributions over a finite set, the Fisher information metric is the only Riemannian metric, up to a constant scalar factor, that is invariant under Markov embeddings (probabilistic data transformations independent of parameters).”
