Bile‐Derived Exosomal miR‐196a/‐196b as Diagnostic Biomarkers Associated With Tumor Progression in Biliary Tract Cancer
Authors: Naotaka Kugiyama, Katsuya Nagaoka, Takehisa Watanabe, Michihiro Yoshida, Tadashi Toyohara, Cheng Pan, Kenyu Hashimoto, Fumiya Otsuka, Shinya Ushijima, Yukiko Uramoto, Hajime Iwasaki, Motohiro Yoshinari, Shunpei Hashigo, Kazuki Hayashi, Itaru Naitoh, Fumihiro Okumura, Hiromu Kondo, Kaiki Anbe, Hiromitsu Hayashi, Yoshihiro Komohara, Yasuhito Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBile‐based liquid biopsy presents a promising approach for identifying novel biomarkers for biliary tract cancer (BTC) given its proximity to tumor sites. The aim of this study was to identify bile‐derived exosomal microRNAs (miRNAs) with diagnostic potential for BTC. The candidate miRNAs were initially screened by RNA sequencing of exosomal RNA from the bile samples of 10 BTC patients and 10 controls, resulting in the identification of miR‐196a, miR‐196b, and miR‐424. Subsequent validation using TaqMan PCR in an independent cohort (50 BTC, 53 controls) confirmed that miR‐196a and miR‐196b were specifically elevated in the bile of BTC patients, with miR‐196a also elevated in early‐stage BTC. MiR‐196a achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.79 for the diagnosis of BTC. The combination of miR‐196a and CA19‐9 increased the AUROC to 0.86, and further improvement to 0.88 was observed upon the addition of CEA. Furthermore, serum levels of these miRNAs were analyzed in 64 BTC patients and 79 controls; serum miR‐196a levels were significantly elevated in patients with metastatic BTC. Analysis of public datasets confirmed that both miR‐196a and miR‐196b were significantly upregulated in invasive BTC tissues compared to normal biliary epithelium and biliary intraepithelial neoplasia. Functional assays demonstrated that miR‐196a and miR‐196b promoted the proliferation and invasion of cholangiocarcinoma cells (p < 0.05). In conclusion, bile‐derived exosomal miR‐196a and miR‐196b are novel BTC‐specific biomarkers. MiR‐196a may contribute to early BTC detection, enhance diagnostic accuracy in combination with serum markers, and assist in detecting metastasis.

Introduction

Biliary tract cancer (BTC) includes intrahepatic, perihilar, and distal cholangiocarcinomas (CCAs), and gallbladder carcinoma (GBC). The incidence of CCA has been increasing worldwide [1, 2, 3, 4]. Based on the anatomical origin, CCA is classified into intrahepatic CCA (iCCA) and extrahepatic CCA (eCCA), and each subtype exhibits distinct molecular and genetic characteristics [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Serum biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 (CA19‐9) play a key role in BTC diagnosis [4, 12, 13]. However, the specificity of these markers is limited as they can be elevated in other malignancies, as well as benign conditions. Therefore, it is crucial to identify new biomarkers of BTC for improving diagnostic accuracy.

Advances in liquid biopsy technologies have gained considerable attention for cancer diagnosis, with particular focus on cell‐free DNA (cfDNA), circulating tumor cells, exosomes, and microRNAs (miRNAs) as targets for minimally invasive tumor profiling [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Bile is a promising biofluid for BTC diagnosis due to direct contact with the tumor cells [12, 19, 20]. Several studies have reported that bile‐derived cfDNA has a higher mutation concordance with tumor tissues and superior detection sensitivity compared to plasma biomarkers [21, 22, 23]. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is usually performed in patients with eCCA and GBC, thus enabling bile‐based liquid biopsy.

Several studies on bile‐based liquid biopsy have focused on the diagnostic and prognostic utility of miRNAs [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Extracellular miRNAs are stable and have been shown to play a role in cancer invasion and metastasis [29, 30, 31]. Furthermore, miRNAs contained in bile‐derived exosomes are involved in intercellular signaling and tumor progression [32, 33, 34, 35]. However, the clinical significance and potential utility of bile‐derived exosomal miRNAs in BTC have not been fully elucidated.

In this study, we investigated the exosomal miRNAs in bile samples from eCCA and GBC patients who underwent ERCP to identify novel BTC‐specific biomarkers.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort

BTC patients were enrolled from multiple hospitals across Japan, including Kumamoto University Hospital, Nagoya City University Hospital, Gifu Prefectural Tajimi Hospital, Nagoya City University West Medical Center, and Toyokawa Municipal Hospital, from September 2016 to March 2024. The diagnosis of BTC was based on histopathological confirmation. Bile samples were collected from the patients by ERCP. The control group included patients undergoing ERCP for non‐cancerous conditions, such as bile duct stones, benign biliary stricture, and other benign diseases. BTC‐specific exosomal miRNAs were identified through a discovery analysis using bile‐derived exosomal small RNA from 10 BTC patients and 10 controls, followed by RNA sequencing. For validation, bile‐derived exosomal small RNA was extracted from an independent cohort of 50 BTC patients and 53 controls, and the expression levels were quantified using TaqMan PCR (Figure 1a). Exclusion criteria for bile sample analysis were as follows: cases after endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST), cases with bleeding, and cases with severe cholangitis with purulent bile.

Serum samples were obtained from BTC patients either on the same day as ERCP or at the initial visit. For the control group, serum samples of patients with bile duct stones and benign biliary stricture, and of healthy subjects who underwent routine checkup were obtained from the Kumamoto University Hospital Biobank. The serum samples of 64 BTC patients and 79 controls were analyzed for exosomal miRNAs by TaqMan PCR. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kumamoto University (acceptance number 2458), and all participants provided informed consent.

Collection of Biological Samples

Bile samples were collected by ERCP without contrast agent injection and immediately centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min to remove cellular debris and stored at −80°C. Serum samples were separated by centrifugation at 1500 g for 5 min and stored at −80°C until use.

Exosome Isolation and Small RNA Extraction

Exosomes were isolated using the EVAGLAX spin column (AGC Inc.), a nanoporous glass‐based device, as previously reported [32, 36]. Briefly, 1 mL bile or serum was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, and 200 μL of the filtrate was loaded onto the spin column. Following centrifugation at 6000 g for 10 min, 500 μL QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen) was added directly onto the spin column, and the captured exosomes were lysed by centrifugation. Prior to RNA extraction, 3.5 μL synthetic single‐stranded cel‐miR‐39‐3p (1.6 × 108 copies/μL, UCACCGGGUGUAAAUCAGCUUG, Thermo Fisher) was added as a spike‐in control to normalize miRNA quantification. Total RNA, including small RNAs, was then extracted using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Each sample was mixed with 140 μL chloroform by vigorous shaking for 15 s, incubated at room temperature for 3 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4°C for 15 min. The aqueous phase was then aspirated, mixed with 420 μL 100% ethanol, and loaded onto the RNeasy MinElute spin column. After washing with RWT buffer (700 μL) and RPE buffer (500 μL), the RNA fraction was eluted in 25 μL RNase‐free water.

RNA Sequencing and Small RNA Quantification

Small RNA libraries were prepared using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Qiagen) and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500/550 platform with single‐end 75 bp reads. The concentration and quality of small RNA were assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA 6000 Pico Kit and Small RNA Kit. Data quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.11.9) and MultiQC (v1.12). The reads were normalized by the TMM method using CLC Genomics Workbench 22.0.2.

Reverse Transcription and Taqman PCR

Reverse transcription (RT) was performed using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 2 μL RNA was reverse‐transcribed with primers specific for miR‐196a (Assay ID: 241070, Thermo Fisher), miR‐196b (Assay ID: 002215, Thermo Fisher), miR‐424 (Assay ID: 000604, Thermo Fisher), and cel‐miR‐39 (Assay ID: 000200, Thermo Fisher) using the TaqMan MicroRNA Assay kit. The reaction conditions were as follows: 16°C for 30 min, 42°C for 30 min, and 85°C for 5 min. TaqMan PCR was performed using 4 μL cDNA (three‐fold dilution) and 16 μL of a prepared master mix, which included TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix II (Thermo Fisher), miRNA‐specific primers, and nuclease‐free water. The reaction conditions were as follows: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles (for bile) or 50 cycles (for serum) of 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 60 s. Cel‐miR‐39 was used as a spike‐in control, and relative miRNA expression levels were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

The human cholangiocarcinoma cell line HuCCT1 was kindly provided by Dr. Hiromitsu Hayashi, Kumamoto University (Kumamoto, Japan). The cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)‐1640 medium (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma‐Aldrich).

RNA Extraction From Cultured Cells

Total RNA was extracted from the cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were lysed directly in the culture dish with 1 mL TRIzol reagent per well. To normalize miRNA quantification, 3.5 μL synthetic single‐stranded cel‐miR‐39‐3p (1.6 × 108 copies/μL, UCACCGGGUGUAAAUCAGCUUG, Thermo Fisher) was added as a spike‐in control after dissolving the RNA in QIAzol reagent. Thereafter, 0.2 mL chloroform was added per mL TRIzol reagent, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, and RNA was precipitated by adding 0.5 mL isopropanol. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 10 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The RNA pellet was washed with 300 μL of 75% ethanol and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was removed, and the RNA was dissolved in 20 μL RNase‐free water. The RNA concentration was adjusted to 20 ng/μL, and 2 μL was used for RT‐PCR as described above.

Transfection of miRNA Mimics and Inhibitors

HuCCT1 cells were seeded at 60% confluence in 6‐well plates and transfected with miRNA mimics or inhibitors using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To overexpress specific miRNAs, the cells were transfected with hsa‐miR‐196a‐5p miRCURY LNA miRNA Mimic (GeneGlobe ID: YM00470616), hsa‐miR‐196b‐5p miRCURY LNA miRNA Mimic (GeneGlobe ID: YM00471776), or Negative Control 5 miRCURY LNA miRNA Mimic (GeneGlobe ID: YM00479904) (QIAGEN). For miRNA inhibition, the cells were transfected with hsa‐miR‐196a‐5p miRCURY LNA miRNA Inhibitor (GeneGlobe ID: YI04104666), hsa‐miR‐196b‐5p miRCURY LNA miRNA Inhibitor (GeneGlobe ID: YI04101328), or Negative Control A miRCURY LNA miRNA Inhibitor (GeneGlobe ID: YI00199006) (QIAGEN) at the final concentration of 100 nM. The miRNA mimic or inhibitor was diluted in Opti‐MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mixed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent. The cells were incubated with this transfection mixture for 24 h at 37°C in a humidified CO2 incubator. MiRNA expression levels were analyzed by TaqMan PCR as described above.

Cell Proliferation Assay

HuCCT1 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at the density of 5000 cells per well, and transfected as described above. After 24 h, the transfection medium was replaced with complete RPMI‐1640 (+ 10% FBS), and the cells were cultured for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, DOJINDO) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 1 h at 37°C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell Invasion Assay

HuCCT1 cells were transfected with miRNA inhibitors as described above and seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well into the upper chambers of Matrigel‐coated Transwell inserts (8‐μm pore size, Corning, 24‐well) in serum‐free medium. The lower chambers were filled with complete medium as the chemoattractant. After 24 h, the invaded cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed, stained with 0.1% Crystal Violet, and counted under a light microscope. Cells were quantified in three randomly selected fields per insert, and the experiment was performed in triplicate.

Analysis of miRNA Microarray Data

The miRNA microarray data of non‐neoplastic epithelium (normal), biliary intraepithelial neoplasia (BilIN), and invasive tumor (invasive) tissues from 12 distal cholangiocarcinoma (dCCA) cases (total 36 samples) was extracted from the GSE140001 dataset from gene expression omnibus (GEO) database [37]. The microarray data was analyzed using R. The readNanoStringRccSet function from the R package NanoStringNCTools was used to load the RCC files into a demoData object. The data were normalized using the normalize function with nSolver method, followed by quality control using the setQCFlags function. The matrix of the normalized count of each miRNA was extracted with assayDataElement, and then fitted to a linear model using the lmFit function from package limma, along with a design matrix representing the comparison of paired BilIN versus normal, invasive versus normal, or BilIN versus invasive within each dCCA case. The differentially expressed genes were screened using the eBayes function.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with non‐normal and normal data distribution were compared between two groups using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and Student's t‐test, respectively. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test. In the discovery analysis, differentially expressed miRNAs were identified using a volcano plot, and those with p < 0.05 were selected. p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and the corresponding false discovery rate (FDR) values were calculated. Candidate biomarkers were selected from the top three miRNAs with the highest log2 fold change (Log2FC) in BTC. Correlations between exosomal RNA levels and serum total bilirubin or C‐reactive protein were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

For TaqMan PCR data analysis, the bile and serum samples with values below the detection limit were assigned respective values of 1 × 10−8 and 1 × 10−9, which corresponded to one‐tenth of the lowest detected value in each sample type. The expression levels were log10‐transformed, and group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between miRNA levels and BTC, adjusting for age, sex, total bilirubin, and CRP. In addition, stratified analyses according to total bilirubin levels (cutoff: 2.0 mg/dL) and sensitivity analyses excluding patients with total bilirubin > 5.0 mg/dL were performed. The diagnostic performance of each miRNA was evaluated by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The optimal threshold of the ROC curve was determined using the Youden's index, and the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) along with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. The areas under the ROC curve (AUROC) were calculated using log10‐transformed expression values. To assess the combined diagnostic performance of multiple miRNAs or serum biomarkers, the adjusted AUROC values were estimated by multivariable logistic regression analysis. For models including CEA or CA19‐9, only cases with complete data were included, and cases with missing values were excluded. To further evaluate the incremental diagnostic value of adding miR‐196a and CEA beyond CA19‐9 alone, likelihood ratio tests, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and decision curve analysis were performed using logistic regression models. Likelihood ratio tests were used to compare the goodness of fit of nested logistic regression models and to determine whether the addition of miR‐196a significantly improved model fit beyond CA19‐9 alone. Continuous NRI was used to assess improvement in discrimination without predefined risk categories, and decision curve analysis was used to evaluate the net benefit of the models across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad Software Inc.), STATA (version 18, StataCorp LLC), or R (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All p‐values were two‐sided test, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Exosomal‐miR‐196a/‐196b Are Elevated in the Bile of BTC Patients

The clinical characteristics of BTC patients (n = 10) and controls (n = 10) included in the discovery analysis are shown in Table 1. The median quantity of exosomal small RNAs in the bile of BTC patients was 551 pg/μL compared to 231 pg/μL in the healthy controls (p = 0.03, Figure 1b). RNA levels were not significantly correlated with serum total bilirubin or C‐reactive protein (CRP) (Figure S1). Furthermore, RNA sequencing identified multiple exosomal miRNAs that were upregulated in the bile samples of BTC patients (Table 2), of which miR‐196a, miR‐196b, and miR‐424 were selected as biomarker candidates based on their high Log2FC (Figure 1c).

The candidate miRNAs were validated using bile samples from an independent cohort of 50 BTC patients and 53 controls. The clinical characteristics of both groups are shown in Table 3. The median serum levels of CEA (3.85 vs. 2.25 ng/mL, p = 0.04) and CA19‐9 (102.3 vs. 22.2 U/mL, p = 0.04) were significantly higher in BTC patients compared to those in controls. TaqMan PCR confirmed the upregulation of miR‐196a and miR‐196b in BTC patients, whereas miR‐424 levels were similar in both groups (Figure 1d). The median expression level (95% CI) of miR‐196a was 1.8E‐4 (2.6E‐5–4.9E‐4) in BTC patients and 5.6E‐6 (2.1E‐6–1.4E‐5) in controls (p < 0.0001), with detection rates (DRs) of 96% and 79%, respectively. For miR‐196b, the median (95% CI) expression was 1.7E‐5 (1.8E‐6–4.7E‐5) in BTC patients and below the limit of detection (LOD) (LOD–4.0E‐6) in controls (p = 0.004), with DRs of 68% and 43%, respectively. To exclude the influence of confounding factors, multivariable logistic regression was performed, adjusting for age, sex, total bilirubin, and CRP. This analysis identified both miR‐196a and miR‐196b as independent factors associated with BTC (miR‐196a: OR 1.83 (95% CI 1.30–2.72), p = 0.001; miR‐196b: OR 1.31 (95% CI 1.02–1.71), p = 0.037) (Table 4). Furthermore, stratified analyses according to serum total bilirubin levels (cutoff: 2.0 mg/dL) showed similar trends, although statistical significance was limited by the reduced sample size in the subgroups (Table S1). Sensitivity analyses excluding patients with marked hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin > 5.0 mg/dL) confirmed that both miRNAs remained significantly elevated in BTC patients (Table S2).

Furthermore, miR‐196a was significantly elevated in early‐stage BTC (cStage I and II, n = 24; median (95% CI): 1.2E‐4 (1.4E‐5–1.2E‐3), DR: 100%) compared with controls (p = 0.002). miR‐196a levels in early‐stage BTC were comparable to those in advanced‐stage BTC (cStage III and IV, n = 26; median (95% CI): 2.1E‐4 (1.8E‐5–8.9E‐4), DR: 92%), whereas miR‐196b did not show a significant increase in early‐stage BTC (Figure 1e). Taken together, miR‐196a and miR‐196b are BTC‐specific exosomal miRNAs that are elevated in bile, and miR‐196a is consistently elevated in both early and advanced stages.

The Combination of Exosomal miR‐196a and CA19‐9 Enhanced the Diagnostic Accuracy for BTC

The diagnostic performance of bile‐derived exosomal miRNAs (miR‐196a and miR‐196b) and serum tumor markers (CEA and CA19‐9) for BTC was evaluated by ROC analysis. The AUROC (95% CI) was 0.79 (0.69–0.88) for miR‐196a and 0.80 (0.71–0.89) for CA19‐9, indicating comparable diagnostic performance. On the other hand, miR‐196b and CEA showed lower AUROC values of 0.71 (0.61–0.81) and 0.72 (0.62–0.83), respectively (Figure 2a,b). Furthermore, the combination of miR‐196a and miR‐196b resulted in an AUROC of 0.78 (0.68–0.88), showing no improvement over miR‐196a alone (Figure 2b). In contrast, combining miR‐196a with CA19‐9 increased the AUROC to 0.86 (0.79–0.94), and the combination of miR‐196a, CA19‐9, and CEA further improved the AUROC to 0.88 (0.80–0.95) (Figure 2b–d). To further evaluate the incremental diagnostic value beyond CA19‐9, likelihood ratio tests, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and decision curve analysis were performed. Adding miR‐196a significantly improved model performance compared with CA19‐9 alone (Tables S3 and S4). Decision curve analysis also demonstrated greater net benefit for models including miR‐196a across clinically relevant threshold probabilities (Figure S2). These findings indicate that combining miR‐196a with serum tumor markers can improve the diagnostic accuracy for BTC.

Serum Exosomal miR‐196a Is Elevated in Metastatic BTC

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of exosomal miRNAs, we analyzed their expression in the serum samples from 64 BTC patients and 79 controls. The clinical characteristics of both groups are shown in Table 5. Serum miR‐196a levels tended to be higher in BTC patients compared to the controls (median (95% CI): 5.9E‐7 (2.6E‐8–3.3E‐6) vs. LOD (LOD–4.7E‐7), p = 0.07), with DRs of 66% and 49% respectively (Figure 3a). Interestingly, higher serum levels of miR‐196a were detected in metastatic BTC patients (cStage IV, n = 30) compared to controls (median (95% CI): 3.0E‐6 (9.2E‐8–1.2E‐5) vs. LOD (LOD–4.7E‐7), p = 0.02; DRs 77% vs. 49%, Figure 3b). On the other hand, serum miR‐196b levels showed no significant difference between BTC patients and controls (median (95% CI): LOD (LOD–8.1E‐7) vs. LOD (LOD–2.9E‐7), p = 0.94; DRs: 44% vs. 47%, Figure 3c), and miR‐196b levels in the metastatic BTC patients remained comparable to that in controls (median: LOD (LOD–8.1E‐7) vs. LOD (LOD–2.9E‐7), p = 0.98; DRs: 40% vs. 47%, Figure 3d). These findings suggest that exosomal miR‐196a is elevated in metastatic BTC and may serve as a potential serum biomarker for advanced stage of the disease.

Exosomal miR‐196a/‐196b Promote Tumor Progression in Invasive BTC

We analyzed miR‐196a and miR‐196b expression in BTC tissues using the GEO database. Both miRNAs were significantly upregulated in invasive CCA compared to normal biliary epithelium and BilIN (Figure 4a). To investigate their functional roles, we transfected HuCCT1 cholangiocarcinoma cells with miR‐196a, miR‐196b, or control mimics. Transfection with miR‐196a or miR‐196b significantly increased intracellular miRNA levels (Figure 4b), but did not affect proliferation rates compared to the control (Figure 4c). This may be attributed to the high endogenous expression of these miRNAs in cholangiocarcinoma cells, which might have limited additional proliferative benefit. Conversely, inhibition of miR‐196a or miR‐196b significantly reduced intracellular miRNA levels in the HuCCT1 cells (Figure 4d), which led to decreased proliferation (Figure 4e). Furthermore, the invasion capacity of the inhibitor‐treated cells was also significantly reduced (Figure 4f). These findings suggest that miR‐196a and miR‐196b drive cholangiocarcinoma invasion and proliferation in invasive BTC.

Discussion

We identified bile‐derived exosomal miR‐196a and miR‐196b as promising diagnostic biomarkers for BTC. Notably, exosomal miR‐196a was elevated in the bile of early‐stage BTC patients and enhanced diagnostic accuracy when combined with serum‐based tumor markers. MiRNA levels partially overlapped between BTC and controls, suggesting limited utility as standalone biomarkers while still contributing to BTC diagnosis. Furthermore, exosomal miR‐196a was elevated in the serum of metastatic BTC patients. Thus, exosomal miRNAs may improve BTC detection through bile‐based liquid biopsy and may have potential as serum biomarkers for advanced BTC.

Serum CA19‐9 is a primary biomarker for BTC, with a reported sensitivity of 72% and specificity of 84% for CCA [38]. Consistent with this, the sensitivity and specificity of CA19‐9 were respectively 68% and 83% for BTC in our study. However, CA19‐9 is undetectable in 5%–10% of individuals without the Lewis antigen and is often elevated in biliary obstruction and cholangitis [13, 38]. In addition, its diagnostic accuracy is lower for early‐stage BTC [39]. On the other hand, bile‐derived exosomal miR‐196a exhibited diagnostic ability comparable to CA19‐9 for BTC, with sensitivity of 76%, specificity of 74%, and an AUC of 0.79. Exosomal miR‐196a was also significantly elevated in all early‐stage BTC cases, suggesting its utility for early diagnosis. Moreover, the combination of bile‐derived exosomal miR‐196a with serum CA19‐9 improved the AUC for BTC diagnosis to 0.86, and adding CEA further increased the sensitivity to 84%, specificity to 80%, and the AUC to 0.88. Incremental utility analyses also demonstrated additional value beyond CA19‐9 alone. Thus, integration of exosomal miR‐196a with serum tumor markers may improve the diagnostic accuracy of BTC.

Bile‐derived miRNAs are feasible biomarkers for BTC diagnosis due to the ready availability of bile samples from eCCA and GBC patients undergoing ERCP. Several studies have identified bile‐derived miRNAs for BTC or CCA diagnosis through microarray‐based screening, and the reported AUCs range from 0.65 to 0.98. In a small‐cohort study, three miRNAs (miR‐9, miR‐145, miR‐944) showed high diagnostic performance, with AUCs of 0.77–0.98 [24]. Likewise, miR‐451a and miR‐3619‐3p (AUC 0.73–0.75), miR‐412, miR‐640, miR‐1537, and miR‐3189 (AUC 0.78–0.81), and miR‐30d‐5p and miR‐92a‐3p (AUC 0.65–0.73) have shown diagnostic potential for BTC [25, 26, 32]. In this study, we utilized RNA sequencing to identify the BTC‐specific miRNAs. Apart from miR‐196a and miR‐196b, the previously reported miR‐145 was also upregulated in BTC patients, whereas most other miRNAs did not exhibit significant elevation. Thus, identification of relevant miRNAs in bile samples depends on the detection method, indicating the need to explore optimal detection approaches and miRNA combinations for bile‐based liquid biopsy in BTC.

MiR‐196 is upregulated in various cancers and contributes to tumor initiation and progression [40, 41, 42]. Le et al. [43] also reported the diagnostic value of miR‐196a through ERCP brush cytology specimens in pancreatic and biliary cancers. In our analysis, the GEO database confirmed the upregulation of miR‐196a and miR‐196b in BTC patients. However, these miRNAs were not significantly elevated in BilIN, a pre‐neoplastic lesion. These findings suggest that miR‐196a and miR‐196b may be upregulated during malignant transformation rather than in pre‐neoplastic lesions. A previous study showed that miR‐196 promotes the proliferation and invasion of BTC cells via the HAND1/Wnt/β‐catenin axis [42]. Consistent with this, miR‐196a and miR‐196b inhibitors suppressed the proliferation and migration of cholangiocarcinoma cells in vitro, suggesting that miR‐196 plays a key role in BTC progression. Furthermore, while miR‐196 has been identified as a serum biomarker in colorectal, pancreatic, and gastric cancers [41, 44, 45], its potential as a serum biomarker in BTC has not been reported. In our study, serum miR‐196a was significantly elevated in metastatic BTC, whereas no significant increase was observed in non‐metastatic BTC. In contrast, miR‐196b did not show a significant increase even in metastatic BTC. These findings suggest that miR‐196a may have potential as a biomarker for disease progression and treatment monitoring, while its utility as a diagnostic biomarker may be limited.

This study has several limitations that ought to be considered. First, given the retrospective design and limited sample size of the study, larger prospective studies are needed to validate our findings. Second, most control samples for bile analysis were obtained from patients with benign biliary diseases, such as bile duct stones, rather than healthy individuals. This may have influenced the comparison between BTC and controls. Third, since bile collection requires ERCP, our study focused on eCCA and GBC patients, and excluded iCCA cases. Therefore, the applicability of bile‐derived exosomal miR‐196a and miR‐196b in the diagnosis of iCCA remains unclear. Fourth, some samples were undetectable by TaqMan PCR, which may have affected the accuracy of comparisons. Using more sensitive methods, such as droplet digital PCR, could improve analytical reliability. Fifth, although we adjusted for total bilirubin and CRP, residual confounding related to biliary obstruction and inflammation cannot be completely excluded.

In conclusion, exosomal miR‐196a and miR‐196b are promising biomarkers for BTC diagnosis and disease monitoring. Notably, miR‐196a showed potential for early diagnosis, enhanced diagnostic accuracy when combined with serum tumor markers, and detection of advanced disease. These findings suggest that exosomal microRNAs in bile may contribute to BTC diagnosis and disease monitoring. A schematic summary of these findings is shown in Figure 5.
