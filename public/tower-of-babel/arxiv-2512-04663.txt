Fermionic neural Gibbs states
Jannes Nys, Juan Carrasquilla

Abstract
We introduce fermionic neural Gibbs states (fNGS), a variational framework for modeling finite-temperature properties of strongly interacting fermions. fNGS starts from a reference mean-field thermofield-double state and uses neural-network transformations together with imaginary-time evolution to systematically build strong correlations. Applied to the doped Fermi–Hubbard model, a minimal lattice model capturing essential features of strong electronic correlations, fNGS accurately reproduces thermal energies over a broad range of temperatures, interaction strengths, even at large dopings, for system sizes beyond the reach of exact methods. These results demonstrate a scalable route to studying finite-temperature properties of strongly correlated fermionic systems beyond one dimension with neural-network representations of quantum states.

I
Method

We generate fermionic thermal states through the thermofield double (TFD) formalism
27
;
28
;
32
;
61
. In the TFD approach, thermal properties of a system are computed via a single imaginary-time evolution of an entangled purification of the thermal density matrix.
We represent this state by a variational ansatz constructed by supplementing well-understood mean-field states with neural-network transformations
52
;
40
;
50
;
63
acting on two identical copies of the system.

I.1
Mean-field thermofield doubled fermions

The starting ingredient in our approach is the construction of a finite-temperature mean-field reference state, which also lays the groundwork for extending the formalism to interacting fermions in the next subsection. Consider a mean-field Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
with orthonormal eigenstates
\ket{\mu}\equiv\hat{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\ket{\Omega}
with energies
\epsilon_{\mu}
and vacuum
\ket{\Omega}
. In the TFD formalism, we introduce a double Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. Fermionic operators in the
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
Hilbert space are denoted
\tilde{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}
where we use
\hat{\phantom{o}}
and
\tilde{\phantom{o}}
to denote operators in the physical and auxiliary thermofield space, respectively. We impose the anti-commutation relations
\{\hat{c}_{\mu}^{(\dagger)},\tilde{c}_{\nu}^{(\dagger)}\}=0
. A thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta=1/T
is given by

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(\beta)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mu}\left[u_{\mu}(\beta)+v_{\mu}(\beta)\hat{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\right]\ket{\Omega}.

(1)

With
w_{\mu}(\beta)=e^{-\beta\epsilon_{\mu}/2}
we have
u_{\mu}(\beta)=1/\sqrt{1+w_{\mu}(\beta)^{2}}
, and
v_{\mu}(\beta)=w_{\mu}(\beta)u_{\mu}(\beta)
, where the latter yields the Fermi-Dirac occupation
|v_{\mu}|^{2}
.

This form resembles a Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) state of physical-auxiliary fermion pairs, where
P_{\mu}^{\dagger}=\hat{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}
is a geminal creation operator and creates pairs of physical- and auxiliary-mode fermions. The finite-temperature expectation value of an operator
\hat{O}_{\beta}
can be obtained as (see also Ref.
61
and Appendix
E
for Monte Carlo estimators)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat{O}}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\matrixelement{\Psi(\beta)}{\hat{O} \otimes\tilde{\iden}}{\Psi(\beta)}}{\innerproduct{\Psi(\beta)}{\Psi(\beta)}}

(2)

We consider systems in the canonical ensemble, and hence projecting both systems in Eq. (
1
) onto a fixed charge sector with
N
fermions, yields
27
(up to normalization from hereon)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}\ket{\Psi(\beta)}

\displaystyle=[\Gamma^{\dagger}(\beta)]^{N}\ket{\Omega}=\ket{\mathrm{AGP}}

(3)

with
\Gamma^{\dagger}(\beta)=\sum_{\mu}w_{\mu}(\beta)P_{\mu}^{\dagger}
and
\ket{\mathrm{AGP}}
, an antisymmetric geminal power state
39
;
50
, see Appendix
C
.

Finally, we consider the projection of this mean-field thermal state onto a specific Fock state. In particular, consider a fermion pair in mode
p
(with creation operator
\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{p}
) and thermofield mode
q
(i.e.
\ket{p, \tilde{q}}=\hat{f}_{p}^{\dagger}\tilde{f}_{q}^{\dagger}\ket{\Omega}
),

\displaystyle\matrixelement{p, \tilde{q}}{\Gamma^\dagger(\beta)}{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}w_{\mu}(\beta)\matrixelement{p}{\hat{c}_\mu^\dagger}{\Omega}\matrixelement{\tilde{q}}{\tilde{c}_\mu^\dagger}{\Omega}

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}w_{\mu}(\beta)\phi_{\mu}(p)\tilde{\phi}_{\mu}(q)

(5)

\displaystyle=:\varphi(p,q)

(6)

where
\phi_{\mu}(p)=\matrixelement{p}{\hat{c}_\mu^\dagger}{\Omega}
, and we expressed the systems in a conjugated orbital basis
\tilde{\phi}_{\mu}=\phi_{\mu}^{*}
.
A many-body thermal mean-field state for a Fock state
\ket{x, \tilde{y}}
occupying modes
(p_{1},...,p_{N},\tilde{q}_{1},...,\tilde{q}_{N})
is fully parametrized by the temperature-dependent pair matrix
[\varphi]_{p,q}:=\varphi(p,\tilde{q})
.,

\displaystyle\innerproduct{x, \tilde{y}}{\Psi(\beta)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}e^{-\beta E(\mathcal{S})/2}\innerproduct{x}{\mathcal{S}}\innerproduct{\tilde{y}}{\mathcal{S}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}[\varphi]_{p_{1},\tilde{q}_{1}}&\dots&[\varphi]_{p_{1},\tilde{q}_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
[\varphi]_{p_{N},\tilde{q}_{1}}&\dots&[\varphi]_{p_{N},\tilde{q}_{N}}\end{pmatrix}

(8)

Here,
p=(l,\sigma)
will typically refer to an occupied mode with spin
\sigma
and orbital (or site)
l
. For spin-conserving Hamiltonians, the determinant factorizes into two spin contributions.
The set
\mathcal{S}
runs over all combinations of
N
occupied states, and we used the Cauchy-Binet identity
4
to obtain the thermal pair orbitals in Eq. (
6
).

I.2
Correlated thermofield fermions

The analytic mean-field expression of the thermal state in Eq. (
8
) serves as the foundation for incorporating many-body correlations and constructing an ansatz for correlated fermionic thermal states. We define our correlated fermionic neural Gibbs states (fNGS) by first building correlations into the mean-field state at a fixed temperature. We generalize the mean-field formalism by lifting the pair orbitals to matrix functions that depend on the many-body configurations
[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]_{p,q}
. We will focus on a correlated fNGS ansatz inspired by neural backflow
52
(yielding a second quantized form of the neural Pfaffian
40
or neural AGP
50
used to capture pairing in superfluids)

\displaystyle\innerproduct{x, \tilde{y}}{\Psi(\beta)}=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]_{p_{1},\tilde{q}_{1}}&\dots&[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]_{p_{1},\tilde{q}_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]_{p_{N},\tilde{q}_{1}}&\dots&[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]_{p_{N},\tilde{q}_{N}}]\end{pmatrix}e^{-J(x,\tilde{y})}

(9)

where
\varphi
is a neural network, and
J
is a Jastrow-like factor capturing both inter- and intra-species interactions. The details of the model with inter and intra-attention mechanisms are detailed in Appendix
D
, and we discuss alternative ansatze in Appendix
G
. We will refer to our neural architecture as the BiViT, (an adaptation of the ViT
92
). Crucially, our model generates neural representations that capture both inter- and intra-species correlations.
Alternative strategies to the neural backflow transformations
52
are possible, such as hidden fermion pairs, a generalization of Ref.
69
.

I.3
Variational imaginary time evolution

Variational methods such as tensor networks often prepare Gibbs states via imaginary-time evolution from an infinite-temperature purification
88
;
99
;
41
. Although most effective at high temperatures, this approach suffers from accumulated numerical errors when cooling toward low temperatures, and several refinements have been proposed to mitigate these issues
57
;
43
;
87
. Similar difficulties arise in variational Monte Carlo, where direct cooling from infinite temperature remains challenging even in spin systems
61
.
Here, we adopt a different strategy inspired by Ref.
36
. Instead of cooling from infinite temperature, we begin from a thermal state of a solvable mean-field Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
at inverse temperature
\beta_{0}
, characterized by geminal orbitals
\varphi_{0}
. From this reference state
\ket{\Psi_0(\beta_0)}
in Eq. (
8
), we reach the correlated thermal state of the target Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
by imaginary-time evolution with the work operator

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\hat{H}\otimes\tilde{\mathbb{I}}-\tfrac{\beta_{0}}{\beta}\hat{\mathbb{I}}\otimes\tilde{H}_{0},

(10)

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(\beta)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}W}\ket{\Psi_0(\beta_0)}.

(11)

Unless stated otherwise, we take
\beta=\beta_{0}
, with further cooling achievable via evolution under
\hat{H}\otimes\tilde{\mathbb{I}}
. The resulting TFD state is symmetric under exchange of the physical and auxiliary systems, a property that can be imposed on the fNGS ansatz (see Appendix
D
).

To implement this procedure efficiently, we develop a Taylor-root expansion projected imaginary-time evolution (tre-pITE) method, a high-order scheme for the imaginary-time propagator based on complex-time detours, following Ref.
62
. When applicable, accelerate the projected imaginary
-
time evolution by choosing each time step
\delta\tau
adaptively:
\delta\tau=\sqrt{I_{c}/\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\smash{\hat{H}}\right]}
(see Appendix
H
). In addition, we propagate the variational parameters with a momentum
-
like predictor across time steps, extrapolating the velocity extracted from the two most recent time-step solutions, which gives a reliable initial guess for the next optimization. Full details of the scheme are provided in Appendix
H
.

Few other works have attempted to model electronic thermal states with fermionic NQS. As a first example, Ref.
96
proposed a variational density-matrix approach based on a neural canonical transformation to study the finite-temperature electron gas in continuous space.
Other works on traditional VMC have studied electronic systems in the TPQ/METTS formalism
85
;
14
, which requires evolving a large number of states, achievable with non-neural approaches.

III
Conclusions and outlook

We have introduced fermionic neural Gibbs states (fNGS), a variational framework that combines thermofield doubling with neural transformations of geminal pair orbitals to represent correlated fermionic thermal ensembles. Starting from a mean-field reference and evolving with a work operator via tre-pITE, the approach reaches target temperatures efficiently while preserving antisymmetry and scalability. Benchmarks on the doped
t
-
V
and Fermi-Hubbard models show that fNGS reproduces thermal energies over wide temperature and interaction ranges, captures spin correlations, and scales beyond exact methods. fNGS emerges as a practical tool for finite-temperature studies of strongly correlated fermions in >1 dimensions, and it charts a clear path to translating modern fermionic neural-quantum-state techniques into a method for understanding thermal phenomena.

Looking ahead, several extensions are natural: (i) extensions to continuum/continuous space approaches where ground-state ansatze are available, see e.g. Refs.
63
;
40
(ii) response and real-time dynamics from purified fNGS for transport and spectroscopy
61
(iii) and larger or more expressive neural models to move into the low-temperature regime relevant to e.g. cuprates.

In the context of (iii), it is important to realize that even understanding which types of neural networks and optimization techniques yield accurate ground-state approximations is a very active field of research (see e.g. Ref.
49
;
74
;
25
;
21
;
47
;
48
;
69
;
70
;
63
for recent progress and benchmarks), and advancements therein will directly impact the accuracy of the current method as well in the future.
Another possible extension is to consider alternative cooling approaches, such as the exponential cooling approach used in the exponential tensor renormalization group (XTRG)

13
.

(15)

Appendix B
Thermal Hartree-Fock

The hopping matrix
T
is defined by
T_{ij}=-t
on
\langle i,j\rangle
and
0
otherwise.
Our thermal Hartree-Fock (THF) ansatz introduces, for each spin
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
, two rectangular matrices
46
:

•

Y^{\phi}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N_{\sigma}}
, whose reduced QR decomposition yields an isometry

Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}
with
{Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}}^{\dagger}Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}=\mathbb{I}
, and

•

Y^{d}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{\sigma}}
, whose reduced QR yields
Q^{d}_{\sigma}
and the site occupations

d_{i\sigma}=\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\sigma}}\big|(Q^{d}_{\sigma})_{ia}\big|^{2},\qquad 0\leq d_{i\sigma}\leq 1,\qquad\sum_{i}d_{i\sigma}=N_{\sigma}.

(19)

The Slater state for spin
\sigma
is built from the
N_{\sigma}
orthonormal columns of
Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}
. Its one-body density is

\rho_{\sigma}=Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}{Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}}^{\dagger}
and

\bra{\Phi_\sigma}K\ket{\Phi_\sigma}=\mathrm{Tr}\big[{Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}}^{\dagger}KQ^{\phi}_{\sigma}\big]
for any one-body matrix
K
. Given the densities
d_{i\sigma}
, the spin-resolved mean-field Hamiltonians are (see Eq. (
18
))

K_{\uparrow}=T+\mathrm{diag}\big(Ud_{\downarrow}\big),\qquad K_{\downarrow}=T+\mathrm{diag}\big(Ud_{\uparrow}\big),

(20)

and the free-energy functional minimized at temperature
T=1/\beta
is

\mathcal{F}[Q^{\phi}_{\uparrow},Q^{\phi}_{\downarrow},d_{\uparrow},d_{\downarrow}]=\sum_{\sigma}\bra{\Phi_\sigma}K_{\sigma}\ket{\Phi_\sigma}-U\sum_{i}d_{i\uparrow}d_{i\downarrow}-\frac{1}{\beta}S[d_{\uparrow},d_{\downarrow}],

(21)

The first term is the band energy of the Slater states in the self-consistent potentials, the second removes the double counting of the on-site interaction (the energy shift in the previous section), and the last accounts for thermal mixing via the occupations
d_{i\sigma}
. We introduced the sitewise Fermi-Dirac entropy

S[d_{\uparrow},d_{\downarrow}]=-\sum_{i,\sigma}\big[d_{i\sigma}\ln d_{i\sigma}+(1-d_{i\sigma})\ln(1-d_{i\sigma})\big].

(22)

Fixing particle numbers
(N_{\uparrow},N_{\downarrow})
is built in through the column counts of
Y^{\phi}_{\sigma}
and
Y^{d}_{\sigma}
.

Although our free-energy functional uses Fermi-Dirac occupations and an entropy term, i.e. it looks grand-canonical, the
Hartree-Fock stationarity equations for the orbitals are identical to the canonical case. The particle-number constraint enters only via a Lagrange multiplier (the chemical potential) that fixes the occupations at the Fermi level. The self-consistent single-particle orbitals
Q^{\phi}_{\sigma}
that diagonalize the mean-field matrices
K_{\sigma}
are
unchanged by whether one enforces
N_{\sigma}
explicitly (canonical) or through
\mu_{\sigma}
(grand-canonical) for the
same target densities. Only the occupation numbers differ by the choice of
\mu
, not the orbitals themselves.

Thermal state as an AGP

For a mode set
\mathcal{M}

M=|\mathcal{M}|

Variational model

We start by encoding the single and relative site properties. The occupation per site

\displaystyle=W\cdot\textrm{LayerNorm}(v_{i})

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]_{(i,\sigma),(j,\tau)}

Observables

Expectation values follow from

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat O}_{\beta}=\frac{\matrixelement{\Psi(\beta)}{\hat O \otimes\tilde{\iden}}{\Psi(\beta)}}{\innerproduct{\Psi(\beta)}{\Psi(\beta)}}.

(49)

We estimate this quantity with Monte Carlo sampling in the doubled configuration space. Let

\displaystyle\pi(x,\tilde{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\absolutevalue{\Psi(x,\tilde y)}^{2}}{\sum_{x^{\prime},\tilde{y}^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{\Psi(x',\tilde y')}^{2}},

(50)

\displaystyle O_{\mathrm{loc}}(x,\tilde{y})

Appendix F
Monte Carlo sampling in a doubled space

Sampling is performed using the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, employing a composite transition kernel comprising multiple proposal rules. The first rule, termed ‘site exchange,’ facilitates nearest-neighbor transitions. This includes: (i) single-electron hopping, (ii) spin exchange between two electrons, and (iii) doublon hopping. In the doubled space, this rule is applied by randomly selecting either the physical or the auxiliary system for the update.

The second rule, ‘diagonal exchange,’ is particularly useful at high temperatures. It executes symmetric single-electron moves simultaneously on both the physical and auxiliary systems. Therefore, we restrict proposals to moves that are valid for both configurations at the same time.

Finally, to restore symmetry between the two systems, we employ a global ‘swap rule’ that attempts to exchange the physical and auxiliary configurations entirely.

The above rules are respectively performed with a 79%, 20%, and 1% probability rate.

Appendix G
Alternative ansatz

For completeness, we introduce an alternative ansatz based on considering the physical+auxiliary system in its entirety as an interacting fermionic system with
2N
electrons. If we consider the TFD system, a generic assumption for the wave function would be

\displaystyle\Psi(x,\tilde{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}w_{\mathcal{S}}(x,\tilde{y})\det{\Phi(x, \tilde{y})}_{\mu,k}

(53)

where we bundle the physical and unphysical coordinates into a larger index
k=(p,\tilde{q})
. This represents an antisymmetric wave function on the doubled system, with orbitals spanning both systems. Since the orbitals are expected to be the same on both systems, i.e. the orbitals
\mu
come in pairs, we can perform a decomposition, where the set
\mathcal{S}
splits up into two sets. This procedure is similar to defining spin-orbitals, and taking into account that physical and unphysical fermions cannot occupy each other’s modes.

\displaystyle\Psi(x,\tilde{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}w_{\mathcal{S}}(x,\tilde{y})\det{\Phi(x, \tilde{y})}_{\mu,p}\det{\Phi^*(\tilde{y}, x)}_{\mu,q}

(54)

If we further assume that
w_{\mathcal{S}}(x,\tilde{y})=\prod_{\mu}^{N}w_{\mu}(x,\tilde{y})
we can connect this ansatz to ours

\displaystyle\Psi(x,\tilde{y})

\displaystyle=\det{\sum_\mu w_\mu(x, \tilde{y}) [\Phi(x, \tilde{y})]_{\mu, p} [\Phi(\tilde{y}, x)]_{\mu, q}^*}

(55)

which resembles a Bogoliubov-de Gennes determinant.
Note that in the above
w
is sample dependent.
This can be cast into the form

\displaystyle\Psi(x,\tilde{y})

\displaystyle=\det{\varphi(x, \tilde{y})}_{pq}

(56)

where
\varphi
is Hermitian and

\displaystyle[\varphi(x,\tilde{y})]

\displaystyle=[\Phi(x,\tilde{y})]^{T}\textrm{diag}[w(x,\tilde{y})][\Phi(\tilde{y},x)]^{*}

(57)

Note that the form in Eq. (
55
) reduces as follows to the mean-field pair orbitals

\displaystyle[\Phi(x,\tilde{y})]_{\mu,p}\to[\Phi]_{\mu,p},\quad w_{\mu}(x,\tilde{y})\to e^{-\beta\epsilon_{\mu}/2}

(58)

When approaching a ground state, we have

\displaystyle\det{\sum_\mu[\Phi(x)]_{\mu, p} [\Phi(\tilde{y})]_{\mu, q}^*}

(59)

Or, in other words, the dependency of the orbitals on the other system vanishes. At infinite temperatures
\beta\to 0
, we get

\displaystyle\det{\sum_\mu\delta_{\mu, p} \delta_{\mu, q}}

\displaystyle\propto\det{ \delta_{p,q} }

(60)

or any permutation between the matching of
\mu
and
p
.
This illustrative discussion demonstrates how the low- and high-temperature constraints are automatically built into our ansatz, since the latter forms a special case of our more general ansatz used in this work.

Appendix H
Imaginary time evolution

H.1
Projections

We use a second-order consistent Taylor-root expansion of the imaginary-time propagator, as introduced in Ref.
62
.
We approximate the target state
\hat{V}\ket{\phi}
by maximizing the fidelity of a trial state
\ket{\psi}
with respect to it,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[\ket{\psi},\hat{V}\ket{\phi}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\matrixelement{\psi}{\hat V}{\phi}}{\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}}\frac{\matrixelement{\phi}{\hat V^\dagger}{\psi}}{\matrixelement{\phi}{\hat V^\dagger\hat V}{\phi}}.

(61)

Monte Carlo estimators for this objective follow the constructions in Ref.
53
, with the variance-reduced, control-variate form of Ref.
80
(see also Ref.
24
for a pedagogical and systematic review on fidelity estimators and their gradients):

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[f_{1}(x)\right]F_{2}+F_{CV},

(62)

\displaystyle F_{CV}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[g_{1}(x)\right]G_{2}-1\right),

(63)

where

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{[\hat{V}\phi](x)}{\psi(x)},

\displaystyle f_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi(y)}{[\hat{V}\phi](y)},

\displaystyle g_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{f_i(x)}^{2},

(64)

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\hat V \phi}^{2}}\left[f_{2}(y)\right],

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\hat V \phi}^{2}}\left[g_{2}(y)\right].

(65)

Rao-Blackwellization is used to further cut variance, as in Refs.
53
;
24
. The control-variate contribution is biased for finite samples, but its bias vanishes as
F\to 1
. We used the short-hand notation

\displaystyle[\hat{V}\phi](x)=\matrixelement{x}{\hat V}{\phi}=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\matrixelement{x}{\hat V}{x'}\phi(x^{\prime}).

(66)

To eliminate sampling from
\hat{V}\ket{\phi}
, we adopt self-normalized importance sampling using configurations from
\ket{\phi}

61
;
62
. For any
f(y)
that stands for
f_{2}
or
g_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\hat V \phi}^{2}}\left[f(y)\right]\;\to\;\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\phi}^{2}}\left[w(y)f(y)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\phi}^{2}}\left[w(y)\right]},\qquad w(y)=\absolutevalue{\tfrac{[\hat V \phi](y)}{\phi(y)}}^{2}.

(67)

Near convergence one may instead draw
y\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}
since
\ket{\psi}\approx\hat{V}\ket{\phi}
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\hat V \phi}^{2}}\left[f(y)\right]\;\to\;\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[w(y)f(y)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[w(y)\right]},\qquad w(y)=\absolutevalue{\tfrac{[\hat V \phi](y)}{\psi(y)}}^{2}\approx 1.

(68)

However, we opt to sample from
\absolutevalue{\phi}^{2}
instead, since we will see that the gradients have
F_{2}
included as an overall magnitude factor, and can therefore be computed every e.g.
n=5
’th step to reduce computational cost.

When restricting
\ket{\psi}
to a variational family
\ket{\psi(\theta)}
, gradients of (
61
) can be estimated as in Ref.
53
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta_{k}}F

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[O_{k}(x)^{*}\left(f_{1}(x)-F_{1}\right)\right]F_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[\Delta O_{k}(x)^{*}f_{1}(x)F_{2}\right],

(69)

with
O_{k}(x)=\partial_{\theta_{k}}\log\psi(x)
and

\displaystyle\Delta O_{k}(x)=O_{k}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[O_{k}(x^{\prime})\right].

(70)

Since
F_{2}
is a global factor, the update direction is unaffected by it. We therefore prefer the asymmetric fidelity in (
61
), where the operator acts only on
\ket{\phi}
, avoiding additional importance sampling from
\ket{\psi}
required by symmetric or Padé-type schemes
24
that can introduce (finite sample) biases in the gradient direction. This becomes particularly important for fermionic systems where many small contributions and zeros occur.
An alternative, CV-aware gradient form is

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta_{k}}F=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[O_{k}(x)^{*}\left(f_{1}(x)F_{2}-F\right)\right],

(71)

where
F
denotes the control-variate adjusted fidelity.

The quantum geometric tensor is estimated by

\displaystyle S_{kk^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[\Delta O_{k}(x)^{*}\Delta O_{k^{\prime}}(x)\right].

(72)

If gradient estimates become unstable, for example, when

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{O_k(x)}\gg\absolutevalue{\psi(x)},

(73)

we introduce an auxiliary sampling density
q(x)
and reweight all expectations over
x
using self-normalized importance sampling,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}}\left[f(x)\right]\;\to\;\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[w(x)f(x)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[w(x)\right]},\qquad w(x)=\frac{\absolutevalue{\psi(x)}^{2}}{q(x)},

(74)

with the same weights applied to the metric
S_{kk^{\prime}}
. In particular, for small systems and/or high interactions, we find it useful to sample with umbrella sampling

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle\propto e^{-\alpha V(x)}

(75)

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=\log\absolutevalue{\Psi(x)}

(76)

where the standard form is
\alpha=2
corresponding to sampling from the Born distribution. This improves the gradient estimation when the wave function becomes peaked or fermions become localized. In an ideal scenario,

H.2
Adaptive evolution

We introduce a cheap adaptive form of tre-tVMC (which in the imaginary-time domain we refer to as tre-pITE, in accordance with Ref.
61
, referring to Taylor-root expansions projected imaginary-time evolution) that increases the time step
\delta\tau
to render the evolution more efficient. As an efficient approach, we estimate the infidelity of the time-evolved state, i.e.
e^{-\tau\hat{H}}\ket{\phi}
, and the unoptimized state
\ket{\phi}
, as a measure for the change expected in the Hilbert space. We estimate the leading contribution to the infidelity between
e^{-\delta\tau\hat{H}}\ket{\phi}
and
\ket{\phi}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\delta\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\absolutevalue{\mel{\phi}{e^{-\dtau\hat{H}}}{\phi}}^{2}}{\norm{\ket{\phi}}^{2}\norm{e^{-\dtau\hat{H}}\ket{\phi}}^{2}}

(77)

\displaystyle=\delta\tau^{2}\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\hat{H}\right],

(78)

and choose
\delta\tau=\sqrt{I_{c}/\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\hat{H}\right]}
. Here
\hat{H}
denotes either the work operator or the physical Hamiltonian, depending on the step.
We defined a cutoff
I_{c}
of maximum initial infidelity. This additionally implies that
\delta t\propto N^{-1}

95
. Notice that
\hat{H}
can represent the work operator or simply the Hamiltonian applied to the physical part. This scheme is particularly powerful for small systems and is currently only used for those examples.

H.3
Fast convergence through parameter dynamics

A second significant improvement to the method is to approximate the dynamics in parameter space throughout the evolution. Said differently, we estimate the velocity
\theta_{t}
from the parameter solutions
\theta_{t}
and
\theta_{t+\delta t_{1}}
, after evolving from
t
to
t+\delta t_{1}
. We use this to obtain an initial guess of the solution
\theta_{t+\delta t_{1}+\delta t_{2}}
at time
t+\delta t_{1}+\delta t_{2}
, when evolving over a time
\delta t_{2}

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{t+\delta t_{1}}-\theta_{t})/\delta t_{1}

(79)

\displaystyle\theta_{t+\delta t_{1}+\delta t_{2}}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t+\delta t_{1}}+\dot{\theta}_{t}\delta t_{2}

(80)

In practice, this often results in significant speedups, reaching faster convergence.

Summary

At
T=\infty
in the
canonical
ensemble,
N_{\uparrow}
and
N_{\downarrow}
are fixed, so sites are sampled without replacement. This induces weak negative correlations between distinct sites:

\displaystyle C_{ss}(\mathbf{d}\neq 0)=-\frac{C_{ss}(0)}{N-1}\neq 0

(105)

Consequently, the connected structure factor at zero momentum vanishes.
Equivalently

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{0})=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{i}\hat{S}_{i}^{z}\right)=0

(106)

because the total magnetization is fixed.
In the
grand-canonical
ensemble, site occupations are independent at
T=\infty
, hence
C_{ss}(\mathbf{d}\neq 0)=0
and the connected
S(\mathbf{q})
is flat.
