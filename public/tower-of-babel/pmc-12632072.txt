Physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic analysis of castor (Ricinus communis L.) under foliar selenium treatment
Authors: Guangai Deng, Xiaoming Zhang, Yalijuan Wu, Fenglan Huang, Zhiyong Wang, Xiaojing Zhao, Mu Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Selenium (Se) plays a dual role in plants as a beneficial micronutrient at low concentrations and a potential toxin at high levels. However, the mechanisms underlying its regulatory effects in castor remain unclear. Here, we investigated the effects of foliar-applied sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) on the physiological and molecular responses of castor (Ricinus communis L.) using transcriptomic analysis.ResultsWithin the concentrations range of 20–40 mg/L, Se significantly enhanced SOD, CAT, and APX activities and upregulated the related genes. These treatments activated multiple hormone signaling pathways—including jasmone acid, salicylic acid, auxin, ethylene, and abscisic acid—and stimulated phenylpropanoid biosynthesis by upregulating key genes such as Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase-like (PAL), Cinnamoyl-CoA reductase 1-like (CCR), and Cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD). Additionally, several Se transporter gene families—phosphate transporters (PHTs), aquaporins (NIPs), and ABC families—were upregulated, facilitating Se uptake and detoxification. Conversely, a high Se concentration (60 mg/L) suppressed most antioxidant enzyme activities and related antioxidant gene expression, indicating toxicity. Overall, moderate Se application enhanced castor’s stress tolerance and metabolic activity by coordinating antioxidant defense, hormone signaling, and secondary metabolism. In contrast, excessive Se induced physiological stress and gene repression.ConclusionsThis study identified the optimal Se concentration (20–40 mg/L), which significantly improved the growth of castor and contributed to understanding the effects of Se treatment on the expression of genes in castor, providing valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms of Se in alleviating abiotic stress in castor plants and inform optimal Se application strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07701-5.

Introduction

Selenium (Se) is an essential micronutrient for human health, playing pivotal roles in immune modulation, antioxidant defense, anticancer and antiviral activities, antitumor effects, anti-aging processes, and cardiovascular protection [1], Se deficiency has been closely linked to numerous health disorders, including Keshan disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, prostate cancer, and autoimmune thyroiditis [2]. As humans cannot synthesize Se endogenously, dietary intake—especially through Se‑enriched plant foods—is vital for maintaining optimal Se status [3]. Due to the highly uneven soil Se distribution, over one billion people in more than 40 countries—including China, Africa, and Eastern Europe—face risks of Se deficiency and related health issues [4]. Therefore, exogenous Se supplementation in crops has emerged as a key agronomic strategy to enhance Se concentration in edible tissues and combat Se malnutrition [5]. Inorganic Se in soil primarily exists as selenite (SeO₃²⁻, Se(IV)) and selenate (SeO₄²⁻, Se(VI)) [6], Soil and foliar applications of these inorganic forms are the main methods used in Se biofortification [7]. However, soil-based Se fertilization faces several limitations from the standpoint of crop Se accumulation. The bioavailability of Se in soils is strongly influenced by soil properties such as redox potential, pH, and organic matter content [6]. Selenite tends to bind to soil minerals or accumulate in non-edible tissues, limiting its mobility, transformation, and bioavailability [8]. Selenate, on the other hand, is prone to leaching under irrigation or rainfall, and sulfur-selenium antagonism further inhibits Se uptake by plants [9]. Consequently, soil Se fertilization is often suboptimal. In contrast, foliar Se application bypasses soil and microbial barriers, enabling more efficient Se uptake and accumulation via accelerated translocation through the xylem and phloem [10]. Thus, foliar Se application is considered a more effective biofortification technique, especially in regions with low soil Se availability.

Although foliar‑applied selenate typically results in higher total Se accumulation in plant tissues, its conversion to organic Se forms is significantly lower than that achieved with selenite, making selenite the preferred choice for foliar biofortification [11, 12]. After being absorbed by plant, selenite is rapidly assimilated into organic Se within plants, whereas selenate must first be reduced to selenite via ATP sulfurylase–mediated steps before assimilation; the reduction of selenate to selenite is the rate-limiting step in Se metabolism [13, 14]. For instance, Cheng et al. reported higher conversion of Se into protein-bound forms in barley under sodium selenite than under sodium selenate treatments [15]. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that foliar application of inorganic Se during the potato tuber bulking stage led to Se accumulation of 78.8 µg per 300 g fresh tuber, demonstrating a safe and effective strategy for producing Se‑rich potatoes [11, 12]. In rice, foliar Se application increased grain Se content while simultaneously improving starch yield and quality, achieving dual goals of high yield and nutritional enhancement [16]. Apart from its role in improving plant nutritional content, foliar Se application also alleviates various abiotic stresses. Treatment with 40 mg/L Na2SeO3 effectively mitigated drought-induced damage in wheat by promoting the uptake of Se, iron (Fe), and sodium (Na), thereby enhancing stress tolerance and improving grain quality [17]. Biosynthesized nano‑Se sprays reduced heavy‑metal toxicity (Cadmium (Cd), Lead (Pb), Mercury (Hg)) in Brassica chinensis by inhibiting metal uptake and enhancing antioxidant defense systems [18]. Under cold stress, 2.5 mg/L Na2SeO3 increased chlorophyll content and antioxidant enzyme activity in Capsicum annuum L., reducing lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [19]. Similarly, foliar nano‑Se enhanced antioxidant capacity and osmotic regulation in strawberry plants under salt stress, ameliorating salt-induced damage [20]. Selenium has also shown biotic stress relief: 30 mg/L nano‑Se acted as a natural fungicide against wheat leaf spot [21], and 100 mg/L effectively inhibited Alternaria alternata, the pathogen responsible for tomato leaf spot [22]. However, supra‑optimal Se doses can be phytotoxic, leading to photosynthetic inhibition, oxidative stress, and leaf necrosis [23].

Castor (Ricinus communis L.) is a non-edible oilseed crop widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions; its seeds are rich in ricinoleic acid and have significant value across industrial, pharmaceutical, chemical, and energy sectors [24, 25]. At the same time, castor exhibits strong tolerance to stresses such as drought and salinity-alkalinity and can grow steadily on poor marginal lands, highlighting its cultivation potential under the backdrop of climate change and land degradation [26]. In recent years, numerous studies have elucidated the physiological and molecular responses of castor to salinity–alkalinity [26], heavy metals [27], and temperature [28], thereby providing gene resources for genetic engineering and molecular improvement. On this basis, nutrient management has emerged as a rapid, synergistic approach complementary to genetic improvement. Among nutrients, Se confers both nutritional and stress-mitigating effects and its foliar application, which bypasses soil barriers and improves use efficiency, has become a promising strategy to enhance photosynthetic performance, redox homeostasis, and yield stability in plants. Despite the economic importance of castor, the molecular basis of the species’ responses to Se, particularly the pathways governing Se uptake, transport, assimilation, and signaling, remains insufficiently defined, and systematic investigations are limited. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) is a powerful approach to resolve plant transcriptional regulation under nutrient or stress conditions, enabling the discovery of Se-responsive differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and key regulators involved in Se handling and hormone signaling [29]. Here, we performed RNA-seq on castor leaves subjected to graded foliar Na2SeO3 treatments to: (i) profile the global transcriptional response to exogenous Se; (ii) identify DEGs implicated in Se uptake and assimilation; and (iii) elucidate regulatory modules by which Se modulates antioxidant defense, hormone signaling, and stress acclimation. These results provide a molecular framework for understanding Se function in castor and offer guidance for the rational design of precise Se biofortification and stress-management strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

The castor CSR181 seeds were provided by the Tongliao Agricultural Science Research Institute. Healthy, fully mature seeds were selected for germination. Prior to germination, the seeds were surface-sterilized by soaking in a 10% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) solution for 10 min and then rinsed 4–6 times with distilled water to remove any residual sterilant. The sterilized seeds were placed on moist filter paper at 25°C in the dark. After 3 d, uniform seedlings were selected and transplanted into the 7 × 7 cm square nursery pots with vermiculite as the germination substrate. All castor seedlings were cultured in a cultivation room with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, a temperature of 25°C, and 60% humidity. Foliar application of Na2SeO3 was initiated approximately two weeks after germination. Four concentrations were used: 0 mg/L (CK), 20 mg/L (Low-Se), 40 mg/L (Medium-Se), and 60 mg/L (High-Se) in deionized water. Sprays were applied once daily for 10 consecutive days, using small aliquots in multiple passes to achieve uniform wetting of both leaf surfaces and to prevent oversaturation and visible runoff to the soil. Each treatment was replicated with three biological replicates. Leaf tissue was collected 24 h after the final spray application, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent physiological assays and RNA extraction.

Measurement of plant growth and physiological parameters

Approximately 0.50 g of fresh tissue was excised from the third fully expanded true leaf of castor. Pigments were extracted in 80% (v/v) aqueous acetone in the dark overnight at 4°C. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was used for spectrophotometry (UV-Vis, SM1200; AZZOTA, Claymont, DE, USA) with 1 cm quartz cuvettes. Absorbance was recorded at 663 nm (A663), 646 nm (A646), and 470 nm (A470) against an 80% acetone blank [30].

After adequate light acclimation, photosynthetic parameters-including net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr)-were measured between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. on the third fully expanded true leaf under different treatments, using a LI-COR 6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA).

Fresh leaf samples (1 mm × 1 mm) from control and treated plants were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.05 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.1) for 3 h. The samples were then post-fixed in osmium tetroxide, which also functioned as a contrasting agent for ultrastructural analysis. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series (30%−100%, v/v), they were embedded in Spurr’s epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome, post-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined under a JEOL transmission electron microscope (JEOL USA, Inc, Peabody, MA).

The antioxidant enzyme activities in leaves treated with Low, Medium, High concentrations of Na2SeO3 for 10 days were analyzed. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), along with the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), were determined using commercial biochemical assay kits (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Twelve RNA samples from castor leaves were extracted using E.Z.N.A plant RNA kit (R6827-01, Omega Bio-tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and RNA integrity was assessed with the RNA Nano 6000 assay kit and the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The reference genome for Ricinus communis L. (version GCF_019578655.1) was obtained from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_019578655.1/).

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control to remove low-quality reads and adapter contamination, yielding clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml) [31], and transcript assembly, and annotation were performed using StringTie (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/) [32]. Gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (v1.5.0-p3) and expressed as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (v1.20.0) with thresholds of adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 and absolute log₂ fold change >1. GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the identified DEGs as previously described [33]. All RNA-seq data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI SRA under accession number PRJNA1292991.

To validate the sequencing quality of the transcriptome data, the selected genes were examined by quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). Total RNA was extracted using the Plant RNA Kit (OMEGA Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA; Cat. R6827-01), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized with the HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; Version 2024.08, Cat. AR108-100T). Primer 5.0 was used to design the gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1). The ACTIN2 gene of castor was used as an internal reference gene. The qPCR was performed as per the directions for ChamQTM Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. Three biological replicates and three identical reactions were performed on each sample. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analyses were conducted using the online tools available on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com/page/tools/). Data are expressed as the mean ± standard errors of three biological triplicates. Statistical analyses were carried out using Excel 2019. One-way ANOVA was performed to compare the differences among different treatments with a significance level of p-value < 0.05, using SPSS (version 20.0, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data visualizations were performed in R (v4.5.0) using RStudio (v2024.12.01) as the integrated development environment.

Results

Physiological changes in castor under Se stress

To assess Se-induced stress in castor leaves, a range of foliar Na2SeO3 concentrations was applied, and leaf development and photosynthetic performance were quantified. As shown in Fig. 1A, compared to the CK, the Low-Se treatment generally maintained leaf growth or showed slight improvement. Medium-Se induced visible chlorosis and mild tissue injury, with small necrotic patches on the leaf surface. High-Se caused the most pronounced damage, including marked growth inhibition and evident tissue destruction. Exogenous Na2SeO3 markedly altered the antioxidant and oxidative status of castor leaves. SOD displayed a biphasic dose–response, with a significant increase in activity at Low- and Medium-Se. Medium-Se increased SOD activity by ~ 12-fold compared to CK (Fig. 1B). POD activity increased consistently across treatments, rising 404.7% (P < 0.01) at Medium-Se, with no inhibition at High-Se (Fig. 1C). CAT followed a similar pattern to SOD, increasing at Low-to-Ledium levels but decreasing significantly at High-Se (Fig. 1D). APX was maximally induced at Medium-Se (P < 0.01; Fig. 1E). Consistent with elevated oxidative burden, MDA rose by 51.4% (P < 0.01) at Medium-Se (Fig. 1F), while H₂O₂ surged by 369.7% and 355.6% at Medium- and High-Se, respectively (Fig. 1G).

Consistent with these phenotypes, Fig. 1H and O shows that Low-Se significantly increased chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll content, and yielded the highest Pn, while Gs and Tr changed only modestly, whereas Ci decreased, suggesting enhanced light harvesting and photochemistry under minimal non-stomatal limitations. In contrast, Medium-Se reduced chlorophyll levels compared to both CK and Low-Se, with a decline in Pn despite higher Gs and Tr, indicating that non-stomatal factors (e.g., impaired thylakoid electron transport or RuBP regeneration) were limiting photosynthesis. High-Se further decreased pigment levels (especially chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll); whereas Gs and Tr remained elevated, Pn recovered only partially and remained lower than Low-Se, showing that increased stomatal opening cannot compensate for injury to the photosynthetic apparatus. Carotenoids were lowest at Low-Se and increased at Medium and High-Se but did not return to CK levels, indicating persistent oxidative demand.

Transmission electron microscopy revealed a clear concentration-dependent response of chloroplast ultrastructure to Na2SeO3. Compared to CK, chloroplasts in Low-Se maintained intact double envelopes and well-organized thylakoid membranes with dense grana (Gr) and continuous stroma lamellae, and only sparse inclusions (Fig. 2A and F). Medium-Se already showed early stress indicators—including thicker chloroplast rims, increased electron-dense granules, and more frequent/enlarged starch granules (SG) and plastoglobules (PG), accompanied by localized undulation and loosening of thylakoid stacks (Fig. 2G and L). High-Se exhibited the most severe alterations, including oversized electron-lucent SG, abundant PG, disrupted and uneven lamellar organization, and occasional concentric membrane–like/autophagic structures in the surrounding cytoplasm. Collectively, High-Se imposed pronounced oxidative and membrane stress.

Transcriptome analysis of castor under Se stress

Quality assessment of RNA-seq data

The sequencing data of castor under different Na2SeO3 concentrations yielded a total of 76.71 Gb of clean data, with each sample exceeding 5.89 Gb (Supplementary Table S2). After filtering, the proportion of Q20 bases (quality score > 20) was over 99%, and Q30 bases ranged from 96.92% to 97.13%. The overall sequencing error rate was under 0.012%, and GC content ranged from 41.31% to 42.36% (Supplementary Table S2). Pearson correlation coefficients among the three replicate samples were above 0.86 (Supplementary Figure S1), and the correlations between replicates were higher than those between non-replicates, indicating good reproducibility and reliability of the sequencing results, which are suitable for further analysis. The RNA-seq expression data were validated using qRT-PCR. The expression of seven randomly selected genes from the DEGs was detected using qRT-PCR and compared with the RNA-seq results. The gene expression trends obtained from qRT-PCR were consistent with those observed in the RNA-seq analysis (Supplementary Figure S2), confirming the accuracy and reliability of the RNA-seq data.

DEGs analysis and functional annotations

To identify DEGs in castor under various concentrations of Na2SeO3, three pairwise comparison groups were established. DEGs were defined based on an adjusted P-value (Padj) ≤ 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change (|log₂FC|) ≥ 1.0. After treatment with Low-, Medium-, and High-Se concentration treatments, the numbers of upregulated DEGs were 4,561, 4,537, and 4,892, respectively, while downregulated DEGs totaled 4,643, 4,534, and 4,794 (Fig. 3A and C). A Venn diagram revealed that 1,334 DEGs were commonly shared across all three treatment groups in the leaves (Fig. 3D).

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed for DEGs identified under Low-, Medium-, and High-Se treatments (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S3). In GO terms, DEGs from all doses were commonly enriched in “response to stimulus” (GO:0050896, biological process) and “membrane” (GO:0016020, cellular component), indicating a broad stress-responsive remodeling of membrane-associated processes. Dose-specific signals also emerged: “DNA-binding transcription factor activity” (GO:0003700) was uniquely enriched at Low-Se (Fig. 4A), consistent with early transcriptional priming, whereas “oxidoreductase activity” (GO:0016491) was enriched at Medium- and High-Se (Fig. 4B), reflecting heightened redox turnover at stronger exposure. At High-Se, additional enrichment of “hormone-mediated signaling pathway” (GO:0009755) and “plasma membrane” (GO:0005886) points to intensified hormone signaling and transport processes under elevated Se (Fig. 4C).

KEGG analysis revealed a clear concentration dependence. At Low-Se, DEGs were significantly enriched in photosynthesis-antenna proteins, plant hormone signal transduction, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and the MAPK signaling pathway, suggesting enhanced light harvesting and signaling under mild Se (Fig. 4D). At Medium- and High-Se, enrichment shifted toward phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction, with High-Se additionally engaging diterpenoid biosynthesis and plant-pathogen interaction-a pattern consistent with stronger defense/phenylpropanoid routing at higher doses (Fig. 4E and F). Across all three doses, the recurrent appearance of sulfur metabolism, ABC transporters, glutathione metabolism, and amino acid metabolism among the top 20 pathways underscores a conserved thiol-centered detoxification/translocation axis central to Se tolerance, detoxification, and movement.

DEGs involved in photosynthesis and chloroplast ATP synthesis pathway

We examined the expression of genes related to PSI, PSII, and chloroplast ATP synthase under Low, Medium, and High-Se conditions (Fig. 5A). PSI-related transcripts-including core subunits (psaB, psaD, psaG, psaK) and light-harvesting complex proteins (LHCA/LHCB)-were predominantly downregulated, especially at Medium and High-Se (Fig. 5B), indicating a reduction in light capture and PSI activity. PSII genes showed a biphasic response: several psbA isoforms and psb27 were upregulated at Low-Se, whereas psbB, psbC, psbW, psbQ were strongly repressed at Medium and High-Se, which aligns with the inhibition of the PSII core. Gene related to ATP synthase (atpD, atpB, atpF) and petA were upregulated at Low-Se but repressed at higher Se levels; petF was consistently upregulated, while petE declined progressively with Se concentration (Fig. 5B). Regulatory genes CIA2 and CIAO3 were strongly induced at High-Se (Fig. 5B), suggesting a compensatory response aimed at maintaining plastid function. Overall, Se treatment induced a concentration-dependent reprogramming of photosynthetic pathways. Low-Se transiently enhanced energy metabolism/repair, whereas Medium and High-Se suppressed core photosynthetic complexes and chloroplast bioenergetics, ultimately compromising photosynthetic performance.

Antioxidant-related DEGs

To elucidate how selenium regulates the antioxidant system in castor, we analyzed the expression of 49 antioxidant-related DEGs under Low-, Medium- and High-Se treatments (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table S4). Among these three treatments, Medium-Se induced the most pronounced transcriptional response, with the highest number of upregulated genes, suggesting a concentration-dependent activation pattern. These DEGs were involved in several key antioxidant pathways, including GST, GPX, APX, CAT, SOD, POD, and MDHAR. Notably, members of the GST family, such as GSTU7 and GST23, as well as several POD genes, were strongly induced, particularly at Medium-Se levels. GPX and APX genes also showed elevated expression, while CAT and SOD genes displayed concentration-specific patterns, with moderate induction mainly at Medium and High-Se. In contrast, certain genes, such as SODC4 and MDHAR4, were consistently downregulated, regardless of treatment level. Overall, Se treatment activated the antioxidant defense system in a concentration-dependent manner, with Medium-Se optimizing the transcriptional activation of key antioxidant enzymes, particularly those from the POD and GST families, thereby enhancing the plant’s oxidative stress response.

Selenium metabolism and transport of DEGs

Selenium treatment induced dose-dependent transcriptomic reprogramming in castor leaves, particularly in pathways related to Se transport, detoxification, and metabolism (Supplementary Table S5). Thirty-seven ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters spanning subfamilies ABCA–ABCG were differentially expressed, with most showing dose-responsive upregulation (Fig. 6B). Notably, ABCA7, ABCB11, ABCB4, ABCC14, ABCC12, and ABCG1 were consistently induced across all treatments. In contrast, ABCB19 was transiently upregulated at Medium-Se (log₂FC = 1.49) but declined at the highest dose, while ABCC8 was strongly induced only at High-Se. Within the ABCG subfamily, PDR-type transporters displayed divergent regulation, suggesting specialized roles in Se efflux and detoxification. Beyond ABC transporters, several other membrane transporters were dynamically regulated (Fig. 6C). SULTR3.5 and PHO1.3 peaked at Medium-Se, while NRT2.5, NRT3.1, and NIP5.1/NIP6.1 were upregulated at Low and Medium-Se but suppressed at High-Se, indicating a shift in nitrogen and water transport under excess Se. Metal transporters exhibited contrasting trends, with Nramp6 induced at Medium-Se, while Nramp3 was repressed. Amino acid transporters, especially lysine-histidine transporters (e.g., LHT-like 8), responded robustly, with LHT-like 8 showing the highest induction at Low-Se (log₂FC = 4.51), and remaining elevated throughout the treatments. These results are consistent with Se-induced changes in amino acid homeostasis. Among ion transporters, HKT1 and Ca²⁺-ATPase12 were consistently upregulated, whereas CAX2/CAX4 and ATPase4 were downregulated, indicating perturbations in K⁺/Ca²⁺ and membrane transport. In line with these shifts, key enzymes involved in Se assimilation and sulfur metabolism were regulated in a dose-dependent manner. APS and APR were activated at Low-Se, while OAS-transferases and cysteine synthase reached their maximum at High-Se, and HMT showed moderate induction at Medium-Se. Notably, selenoprotein-like (SL) genes were consistently downregulated across all treatments, suggesting a reduced demand for selenoprotein biogenesis. While this suggests a potential transition in Se metabolism, the exact mechanisms of organic-Se detoxification-such as the conversion of inorganic Se to organic forms like SeMet or MeSeCys-remain insufficiently understood, will require further investigation in future work.

DEGs involved in phytohormone signaling and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway

To understand how Se modulates phytohormone signaling in castor, we analyzed hormone-related transcripts under Low, Medium, and High-Se (Fig. 7; Supplementary Table S6). In the auxin pathway, broad repression was observed: 27 out of 35 DEGs, including multiple IAA and SAUR genes, were downregulated, although three SAUR loci were upregulated (log₂FC > 0.72), indicating mixed regulation of auxin-responsive elements (Fig. 7A). Ethylene signaling was strongly activated: 28 out of 35 ERF transcription factors (e.g., ERF017, ERF109, ERF113) were induced (log₂FC up to 8.28), and biosynthetic genes (ACS1, ACO) were upregulated, especially at Medium- and High-Se (Fig. 7B). In the brassinosteroid pathway, receptor-proximal components (BRI1, BSK2) were suppressed (log₂FC < − 1.0), whereas two XTH genes were induced (log₂FC > 2.0), suggesting a decoupling between receptor signaling and downstream cell-wall remodeling (Fig. 7C). For ABA, three of four ABA 8′-hydroxylase genes (e.g., CYP707A1, CYP707A2) were upregulated at Low and Medium-Se (log₂FC > 0.5), while one variant was repressed at High-Se (Fig. 7D).

This reprogramming aligns with stress acclimation at Low-Se and reinforced defense/lignification with metabolic reconfiguration at High-Se.

Discussion

Effects of selenium stress on leaf photosynthesis

Previous research has shown that Low-Se promotes plant growth, while excessive Se has an inhibitory effect on plant development [34]. Low-Se treatment significantly increased the content of chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids in plants [35]. In this study, Low-Se significantly enhanced the photosynthetic pigment content in castor, improving chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids. This finding aligns with earlier reports that Low-Se mitigates chlorophyll degradation caused by abiotic stress, such as salt stress in Brassica napus L. Selenium influences ion transporters and channels, which play key roles in the uptake and translocation of essential nutrients like Mg and K, both critical for chlorophyll biosynthesis [36]. As a result, Low-Se enhances chlorophyll synthesis. In contrast, High-Se induces chlorosis and tissue damage, reduces chlorophyll content, and decreases net photosynthesis, despite an increase in stomatal conductance (indicating non-stomatal limitations). High-Se treatment also represses PSI/PSII and ATP synthase transcripts (Fig. 5B), suggesting a disruption of chloroplast bioenergetics. These findings indicate that while Low-Se supports photosynthetic activity, High-Se impairs it through oxidative damage and bioenergetic disruption.

Antioxidant response of R. communis L. to selenium stress

Abiotic stress often leads to the excessive production of ROS, which can cause oxidative damage unless efficiently neutralized by the antioxidant defense system [37]. Antioxidant enzymes play a critical role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [38].

Selenium modulates both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants [39]. However, this modulation is dose-dependent-beneficial at low-to-moderate levels yet prone to exacerbate ROS formation at excessive levels [40]. In this context, Low-Se and Medium-Se treatments increased the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT, and APX, indicating enhanced ROS scavenging capacity. However, at High-Se, SOD and CAT activities declined, implying that excessive Se can inhibit or destabilize these systems through inactivation, cofactor depletion, or oxidative modification. On the other hand, POD activity increased steadily, peaking at the highest Se concentration, suggesting that POD plays a compensatory role when CAT and APX are impaired [41, 42].

Excessive ROS production also leads to lipid peroxidation, with MDA serving as a key marker of oxidative damage [43]. In agreement with previous studies, Se stress resulted in MDA accumulation in Brassica rapa and apple [29, 44]. In our system, the strongest MDA increases occurred under High-Se, aligning the biochemical proxy of oxidative damage with the physiological/photosynthetic impairments. At Low-Se, SOD upregulation reflects ROS-driven activation of defense genes [45]. However, at higher Se concentrations, CAT activity increased in response to elevated H₂O₂ levels [46]. Transcriptomic analysis revealed higher expression of genes related to the ascorbate-glutathione cycle (e.g., APX, MDHAR, DHAR) at Low-Se and Medium-Se compared to High-Se, indicating that mild Se enhances the recycling of electron donors and H₂O₂ scavenging capacity, whereas excessive Se weakens these pathways [47, 48]. Collectively, Se elicits a dose-dependent reconfiguration of antioxidant defenses: Low-Se primes multi-enzyme ROS scavenging, whereas High-Se overloads the system, dampening core enzymes and shifting detoxification toward POD-dominant pathways, concomitant with increased lipid peroxidation.

Transporters of selenium

Consistent with transporter specificities, uptake of selenite is largely mediated by PHT [49]. PHT1 family members markedly enhance Se(IV) uptake in rice [50]. The silicon influx aquaporin NIP2;1 also facilitates Se(IV) transport [51]. Furthermore, aquaporins have been identified as important mediators of SeMet uptake [52]. In our study, the PHT1 family members (PHT1.3, PHT1.9) and several NIP genes were significantly upregulated under Se treatment, supporting their roles in Se uptake and translocation. In addition, NRT1.1B has been reported to transport SeMet and elevate organic Se in rice [53]. NRT2.5 was significantly upregulated in castor under Se stress, consistent with the findings in foxtail millet [54], suggesting that NRT2 is a potential key player in Se transport in plants. Within plant cells, Se can be stored in organic forms such as methyl-SeCys. LHTs are high-affinity amino acid transporters with notable metal-chelating capacity under heavy-metal stress [55] They are capable of mediating the uptake of various amino acids, including SeCys and SeMet, into root epidermal cells and leaf mesophyll cells, thereby promoting photosynthesis and plant growth [56]. In our study, LHT1 and LHT8 exhibited the highest expression levels under Low and Medium-Se, indicating their role in Se uptake and amino acid transport, particularly under stress. Because transport routes determine the chemical form entering leaves, PHT/NIP-driven selenite influx (more redox-active) is expected to impose greater oxidative pressure than uptake of organic Se species via LHT/NRT; thus, the observed transcriptional profile helps explain the dose-dependent oxidative outcomes.

Multiple ABC transporters were reported to be involved in the detoxification of multiple heavy metals/metalloids. For example, ABCC are responsible for vacuolar sequestration of phytochelatin (PC)-Cd(II), PC-Hg(II) and PC2-As(III) in Arabidopsis [57, 58]. ABCB25/ATM3 exports Cd²⁺ from mitochondria to the cytosol, and ABCG36/PDR8 serves as a plasma-membrane efflux pump [59, 60]. In castor leaves, ABCA7, ABCC3, ABCB11, ABCG1, ABCG36, and ABCG29 were upregulated at Low and Medium-Se, suggesting enhanced vacuolar sequestration or cellular efflux as key Se detoxification routes. Similar ABC activation has been reported in Lolium perenne [61], Medicago sativa [62] and Chinese cabbage (Brassica chinensis var. pekinensis cv. ‘Suzhouqing’) [63]. By contrast, Na2SeO3 and SeNPs primarily induced root ABC-transporter genes with minor leaf effects in rice [64], pointing to species- and tissue-specific divergence within the ABC family. Collectively, these lines of evidence outline a coherent model in which Se assimilation (ATPS–APR–SiR/OASTL), selective influx routes (PHT, NIP, NRT, LHT), and ABC-mediated sequestration/efflux together shape Se handling in castor, with dose-dependent transcriptional adjustments underpinning uptake and detoxification.

Response of plant hormone signaling pathways and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway to selenium

Plant hormone signaling regulates growth, development, and stress acclimation [65]. ROS not only act as early stress signals but also reshape hormone biosynthesis and turnover, thereby attenuating production or accelerating catabolism [66, 67]. Consistent with reports that Se perturbs hormonal networks and can induce JA and SA pathways [68, 69], our study showed that jasmonate (JA) and salicylic acid (SA) branches were activated in response to Se stress. The upregulation of LOX2S and the key JA transcription factor MYC2 indicates enhanced JA biosynthesis and signaling, while increased TGA2 expression supports SA-pathway engagement (Fig. 7) [70]. Interestingly, plant hormones such as JA and ABA can also indirectly promote Se uptake by upregulating sulfate transporter genes [71]. ABA plays a central role in maintaining photosynthesis, regulating leaf growth, and maintaining ROS homeostasis [72]. Its precursor, carotenoids, are actively mobilized under Se stress to scavenge ROS [73]. We observed that the expression of ABA pathway genes CYP26A and PP2C gradually decreased with increasing Se concentrations (Fig. 7), suggesting suppression of ABA signaling during Se-induced oxidative stress. Given that ABA often upregulates antioxidant capacity and constrains ROS via stomatal and metabolic control [74], its attenuation under High-Se likely removes a brake on ROS accumulation and contributes to the non-stomatal photosynthetic inhibition. Auxin signaling displayed a dose-dependent, biphasic pattern: Low-Se elevated expression of IAA-related genes, whereas High-Se suppressed AUX/IAA transcripts, consistent with findings in rice roots and tomato leaves [75, 76]. Ethylene biosynthesis and perception were generally upregulated—including ACO and ETR—in agreement with prior evidence that Se(IV) elevates ACS activity and enhances stress acclimation [77].

Beyond hormone signaling, Selenium can also activate the expression of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway [78]. In our results, DEGs encoding PAL, 4CL, CCR/CCR1 family memebers, CAD, POX, and LAC were significantly enriched (Fig. 7G). As the entry enzyme, PAL commits carbon to cinnamate and downstream p-coumaroyl-CoA, supplying precursors for lignin and flavonoids [79]. The coordinated induction of lignin-biosynthetic genes suggests reinforced cell-wall lignification, a structural defense that restricts pathogen ingress and mitigates abiotic damage [80]. This lignification is biochemically coupled to ROS dynamics because peroxidases consume H₂O₂ during lignin polymerization [81]; thus, phenylpropanoid activation provides both mechanical reinforcement and a sink for excess H₂O₂ under High-Se. These results concur with reports that Se augments lignin deposition and cell-wall strength in rice and promotes endodermis formation/lignification in peanut, thereby improving Se tolerance [82, 83].

Proposed model of selenium uptake, assimilation, and stress responses in castor

Figure 8 illustrates a model for selenium uptake, assimilation, and dose-dependent stress responses in castor leaves. Selenium enters the plant primarily through PHT, NIP, NRT, and LHT transporters, and is then processed in the chloroplasts, where it is converted to SeCys and SeMet. At Low-Se, antioxidant systems are activated, maintaining chlorophyll content and PSI/PSII function, while JA, SA, and ET signaling pathways are engaged to enhance stress tolerance. As selenium concentration increases, ROS overproduction leads to lipid peroxidation and damage to the thylakoid membranes. The transcription of PSI/PSII and ATP synthase genes is repressed, ABA signaling is attenuated, and phenylpropanoid flux shifts toward lignin synthesis. Although ABC transporters help detoxify Se through vacuolar sequestration and efflux, these defenses are overwhelmed at High-Se, resulting in non-stomatal photoinhibition and visible tissue injury.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the regulatory effects of foliar-applied Na2SeO3 at varying concentrations on the physiological, biochemical, and gene expression responses of castor. Low-Se concentration (20–40 mg/L) significantly activated antioxidant enzyme systems and multiple plant hormone signaling pathways, enhancing phenylpropanoid metabolism and improving plant stress tolerance and metabolic activity. The upregulation of Se transporter genes further indicates that the plant coordinates Se uptake and detoxification through multiple mechanisms to maintain internal homeostasis. In contrast, High-Se concentration (60 mg/L) inhibited antioxidant systems and related gene expression, exhibiting clear toxic effects, which highlights the necessity of strict dosage control. Overall, Se exhibits a typical biphasic regulatory role in castor, with complex and multilayered influences on plant stress response mechanisms. This study enriches our understanding of Se-regulated networks in plants and provides theoretical and practical guidance for its rational application to enhance castor stress resistance. Future research should explore the interaction effects of Se with other environmental factors to promote sustainable and efficient cultivation of castor and related crops.
