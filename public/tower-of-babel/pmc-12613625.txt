The stroke that unveiled a silent threat: rupture and thrombosis of hydatid cyst in the left ventricle- a case report
Authors: Ounci Es-Saad, Ayoub Abetti, Hajar Belghit, Abdellahi Darraz, Rajae Alkouh, Smael Labib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac hydatid cysts are rare, even in endemic regions, and are typically asymptomatic. Systemic embolism resulting from rupture of a cardiac hydatid cyst is exceptionally uncommon, and thrombosis within a cyst has not previously been reported.Case presentationWe describe the case of a 7-year-old girl who presented with left hemiplegia and aphasia secondary to an ischemic stroke. Neuroimaging revealed a right middle cerebral artery infarction, while cardiac imaging identified a heterogeneous mass at the apex of the left ventricle. Further investigations confirmed a ruptured and partially thrombosed hydatid cyst. Additional infarctions in the spleen and kidneys supported a diagnosis of systemic embolization.Surgical management was performed via a left pleural approach due to pericardial adhesions. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established, and aortic cross-clamping was used to prevent embolization. A pre-declamping transverse aortotomy revealed and allowed removal of the germinative membrane. The patient recovered well postoperatively, with resolution of aphasia but persistent hemiplegia. Histopathology confirmed hydatid disease.ConclusionThis case represents the first reported instance of a ruptured and thrombosed cardiac hydatid cyst causing systemic embolization. It underscores the critical role of intraoperative aortic inspection before declamping to prevent secondary embolic events.

Introduction

Hydatidosis, or cystic echinococcosis, is a zoonotic parasitic disease that occurs accidentally in humans after ingesting eggs of the dog tapeworm (Echinococcus Granulosus) [1]. This infestation is a major public health concern in rural areas of Morocco, as well as in many low- and middle-income countries [2, 3].

The parasite can spread to any organ through the lymphatic or systemic circulation, with a particular affinity for the liver and lungs, which are affected in 65% and 25% of cases, respectively [3]. However, cardiac hydatid cysts are rare, even in endemic regions, with an incidence ranging from 0.5% to 2% of hydatidosis cases. Among these, the left ventricle is the most commonly affected site [4].

Systemic embolisms, including ischemic strokes secondary to the rupture of a left ventricular hydatid cyst, are extremely rare [5]. Moreover, thrombosis of a hydatid cyst has not yet been documented. Here, we report a case of systemic embolism revealing a ruptured and partially thrombosed hydatid cyst in the left ventricle and its surgical management. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case in the literature.

Case presentation

A 7-year-old female patient from a rural area, with no significant pathological or family history, was admitted to the emergency room for the management of left hemiplegia associated with aphasia, which had appeared two days earlier. On initial examination, her vital signs were stable, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 10/15 and a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 16. The cardiovascular examination was unremarkable except for tachycardia.

A brain computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a subacute infarction in the territory of the right middle cerebral artery, leading to temporal and subfalcine cerebral herniation (Fig. 1). Both cerebral and cervical angiography showed no vascular abnormalities.

The electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated sinus arrhythmia and negative T waves in aVF, lead II, and from V3 to V6. Transthoracic echocardiography in both four- and two-chamber views revealed a heterogeneous, round mass at the apex of the left ventricle, measuring 4.5 cm × 4.8 cm (Fig. 2A) with the presence of a small shunt between the mass and the left ventricle (Fig. 2B). A long, mobile, echogenic filamentous structure was attached to the mass and extended into the left ventricle through a small opening (Fig. 2C). However, the left ventricle was of normal size and had good systolic function, with no valvular or congenital heart disease observed. The right ventricle also exhibited normal function.

A thoraco-abdominal-pelvic CT scan revealed a large aneurysmal sac (36 × 74 × 42 mm) located at the anterolateral wall of the left ventricle, containing two cystic lesions (Fig. 3A). These findings initially suggested a hydatid cyst within the left ventricle. The mass extended toward the left pleural cavity, reaching the chest wall. Additionally, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated small (≤ 2 cm) cortical wedge-shaped infarcts in both kidneys and multiple splenic infarctions (Fig. 3B, C). Global renal function remained normal throughout hospitalization, with neither an increase in serum creatinine nor a decrease in urine output. Whole-body imaging excluded hydatid cysts at other sites.

These findings were confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Furthermore, the imaging also showed rupture of the cyst into the left ventricular lumen (Fig. 4A), which was filled with a mass (Fig. 4B), strongly suggesting a ruptured and partially thrombosed hydatid cyst.

To confirm the diagnosis, hydatid serology was performed and returned positive, while other laboratory tests were normal. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of a left ventricular hydatid cyst was established, and medical treatment with oral albendazole (15 mg/kg) was initiated. Given the high risk of intraoperative hemorrhagic complications due to the ischemic stroke, a joint decision was made with the patient’s family to delay surgery for seven days.

The operation was performed via a median sternotomy with a longitudinal pericardiotomy. Perioperative assessment revealed that the pericardium was highly adherent to the left ventricle. Given this situation, Cardiopulmonary bypass was established using bicaval venous and ascending aortic cannulations, aorta was clamped and cardioplegic arrest was achieved.

This approach allowed for safer manipulation of the heart, minimizing the risk of systemic embolism and cyst injury. Due to the strong adhesion of the pericardium to the left ventricle, dissection was deemed impossible.

Consequently, the cyst was approached via the left pleural route. Upon opening the left pleura, a large cyst prolapsing into the pleural cavity was observed. To minimize the risk of contamination, the pleural cavity was covered with compresses soaked in 20% hypertonic saline. The cyst was then punctured following the injection of hypertonic saline. Subsequently, after identification of the phrenic nerve, the cyst and pericardium were opened through a single incision, necessitated by the firm adherence of the pericardium to the left ventricle, then the cyst was emptied. The daughter vesicles (Fig. 5) and thrombotic material (Fig. 6) were removed; however, the germinative membrane was absent. Further exploration revealed a 2 cm communication between the residual cavity and the left ventricle. A left atriotomy was performed, which confirmed the absence of vesicles or germinative membranes within the left ventricle. To ensure a thorough assessment, a horizontal aortotomy was performed at the level of the ascending aorta, where the germinative membrane was finally located (Fig. 7) and removed (Fig. 8). After extensive irrigation with hypertonic saline, the rupture was closed using separate sutures reinforced with a pericardial patch. Additionally, the residual cavity, left atriotomy, and aortotomy were closed using continuous 5 − 0 polypropylene sutures. The patient was extubated one hour after surgery and discharged 10 days later (Table 1). Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst. Postoperatively, Albendazole (15 mg/kg/day) was prescribed for six months. One month after surgery, the aphasia had resolved; however, hemiplegia persisted.

Discussion

Cardiac hydatid cysts are a rare manifestation of Echinococcus granulosus infection. This parasitic disease may reach the heart via the coronary circulation, a patent foramen ovale, or the pulmonary circulation [4, 6]. Although hydatid cysts can develop in any cardiac chamber, the left ventricular wall is most frequently involved (60%). This predilection may be explained by the thicker myocardial wall and the rich vascular supply from the coronary circulation [3, 4, 7].

Patients with cardiac hydatid cysts frequently remain asymptomatic for extended periods, owing to the slow growth of the cysts, which typically does not exceed 1 cm per year [3, 7, 8]. When clinical manifestations occur, they are often nonspecific and depend on factors such as cyst size, location, number, and interaction with adjacent structures [2–5, 8, 9]. The most commonly reported symptoms include chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, and cough, which may result from compression of the coronary arteries or pericardial stretching [4, 7].

Delayed diagnosis can result in cyst rupture, leading to life-threatening complications such as anaphylactic shock or sudden cardiac death [4, 5, 8]. Rupture may occur into the pericardial space, potentially resulting in pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, or chronic constrictive pericarditis [2, 5, 8, 9]. Alternatively, rupture may be intracavitary, as in the present case, where the hydatid cyst of the left ventricle ruptured into the ventricular lumen [5, 8, 9]. This complication is particularly dangerous due to the release of hydatid fluid into systemic circulation, which can trigger anaphylactic shock or systemic embolization [5, 8, 9]. Embolism may occur through migration of daughter vesicles or the germinative membrane, potentially causing ischemic stroke, abdominal aortic occlusion, limb ischemia, or renal and splenic infarctions [5, 8–10]. Acute ischemic stroke as the initial presentation of a cardiac hydatid cyst is extremely rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature [5, 7–10]. In the present case, stroke was the initial manifestation, with subsequent findings of bilateral renal and splenic infarctions.

In most cases, systemic embolization is attributed to cyst content migration, particularly daughter vesicles or the germinative membrane [5, 8]. However, in a rare instance, ischemic stroke was linked to a left intraventricular thrombus secondary to ventricular dysfunction caused by an unruptured intramyocardial cyst [7]. In this case, embolization may have resulted from either thrombus formation, cyst contents, or a combination of both, given that the hydatid cyst was both ruptured and partially thrombosed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such presentation described in the literature.

Electrocardiographic abnormalities, such as Q waves and T-wave inversion, may be observed in affected patients [9]. Chest radiographs may reveal cardiomegaly or a calcified cardiac mass [11]. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the first-line imaging modality for diagnosing cardiac hydatid cysts, allowing assessment of their morphology, hemodynamic impact, and complications [5, 7, 8]. Features suggestive of hydatid origin include an echolucent mass containing daughter vesicles, detached membranes, or a multivesicular appearance [2–4, 11]. In atypical cases, cysts may appear as solid or calcified masses, mimicking cardiac tumors [4, 11]. In such instances, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide detailed characterization of the lesion and its relationship with surrounding cardiac structures [2, 5, 7, 8]. Serological testing supports the diagnosis, although a negative result does not exclude the disease [3, 4, 8, 11]. In general, the presence of a cardiac cystic lesion with positive serology is sufficient to confirm the diagnosis [3].

The treatment of choice for cardiac hydatid cysts (whether symptomatic, asymptomatic, or unruptured) is surgical excision, which offers the potential for complete cure and excellent prognosis [5, 7–12]. While superficial cysts can be resected on a beating heart, with or without cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), the safest technique remains excision under CPB with aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegic arrest [3, 8, 12]. This approach minimizes the risk of cyst rupture and facilitates complete intracardiac evaluation [3, 12].

To further reduce the risk of dissemination, manipulation of the heart should be avoided before aortic clamping [8]. Nevertheless, embolic events may still occur intraoperatively, particularly following declamping and cardiac reactivation. This phenomenon has been reported in two cases where the germinative membrane was absent from both the cyst and the left ventricular cavity [5, 8]. A similar situation was encountered in the present case. However, to prevent membrane migration, a transverse aortotomy at the level of the ascending aorta was performed prior to declamping, which allowed retrieval of the membrane.

The most widely adopted surgical technique involves cyst puncture, injection of a protoscolicidal agent, aspiration of contents, and subsequent cystectomy [3, 9, 12]. This method enables safe removal while preserving adjacent anatomical structures such as valves and conduction tissue, and also reduces the risk of dissemination [12]. For optimal results, adjunctive medical therapy with albendazole (15 mg/kg/day) or mebendazole is recommended both pre- and postoperatively for a duration of 6 to 24 months [2, 4, 8]. This strategy is believed to significantly reduce recurrence and dissemination risks. Long-term follow-up is essential to detect new lesions or recurrence, particularly in infarcted areas [8, 9, 11]. Monitoring should include periodic serological tests and imaging studies such as echocardiography, brain MRI, and abdominopelvic CT over a five-year period [8, 9].

This is a single-case observation; causality between thrombosis and embolization remains conjectural. Neurological recovery and recurrence surveillance will require long-term assessment.
