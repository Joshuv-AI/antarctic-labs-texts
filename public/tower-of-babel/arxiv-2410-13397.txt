Quantum digital signature based on single-qubit without a trusted third-party
Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
Digital signatures are a powerful cryptographic tool widely employed across various industries for securely authenticating the identity of a signer during communication between signers and verifiers. While quantum digital signatures have been extensively studied, the security still depends on a trusted third-party. To address this limitation and enhance the applicability in real-world scenarios, here we propose a novel quantum digital signature protocol without a trusted third-party to further improve the security. We note that a quantum one-way function can work appropriately in digital signature due to the intrinsic non-cloning property for quantum states. Secret keys in the protocol are constituted by classical private keys and quantum public keys because we assume that no user is trusted in the protocol. We prove that the protocol has information-theoretical unforgeability. Moreover, it satisfies other important secure properties, including asymmetry, undeniability, and expandability.

Our protocol can be implemented only with single qubit physical operations, which does not require any entangling operation.
Although it is impossible to perfectly identify

In addition, there is a possibility that

2.3
Definitions for security analysis

As an alternative concept of one-way function, we introduce the following concept.

Definition 2

(Information-theoretic quantum one-way function)

A function
f
from classical information to quantum states
is called an information-theoretic quantum one-way function
when it is hard to identify the input of the function from
a limited number of copies of the output state of the function.

We define the quantum states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
are the eigenstates of
\sigma_{z}
corresponding to eigenvalues
1
and
-1

|+\rangle
and
|-\rangle
are the eigenstates of
\sigma_{x}
corresponding to eigenvalues
1

-1

where
\sigma_{z}
and

Hence, in our protocol, we employ the function from

l

Definition 3

(Forging attack)

Assume that a signature protocol generates a message
m=\{m_{1},...,m_{l}\},m_{a}\in\{0,1\}
, and a signature
s=\{s^{1},...,s^{l}\}
.
The following operation by an adversary is called a forging attack;
The adversary generates
pairs
\{m^{\prime}_{1},s^{\prime 1}\},\ldots,\{m^{\prime}_{l},s^{\prime l}\}
, where
\{m^{\prime}_{1},...,m_{l}^{\prime}\}=m^{\prime}\neq m

so that the verifier accepts the pair
\{m^{\prime}_{1},s^{\prime 1}\}
as a correct pair of
a message and a signature.

Since we assume that
the adversary can collude with a limited number
k

we define information-theoretic unforgeability as follows.

Definition 4

(Information-theoretic unforgeability)

We say that the signature protocol
is
(k,\epsilon)
-information-theoretically unforgeable
when an adversary having
k
copies of the public key succeeds forging attacks with at most probability of
\epsilon
.

Definition 5

(Denying attack)

The following operation by a signer is called a denying attack;
The signer makes the verifier to reject the signature sent by the signer.

Definition 6

(Expandability)

When the length of the message in the protocol is
l
,
we denote the sum of the bits and qubits consumed in one turn
(from key generation to verification for one message) by
C(l)
.
The protocol is expandable when
the relation
C(l)\leq bl
holds with a constant

for a sufficiently large integer

Security analysis

In this section, we demonstrate that our protocol satisfies
three security requirements:
unforgeability, undeniability and expandability.

Unforgeability

Our security analysis on unforgeability
is based on the following asymmetry.
The secret keys used in the protocol consist of the private key and the public key.
In the protocol, the private key is
l
eigenvalue pairs
\left\{VP_{1},\ldots,VP_{l}\right\}
and
l
basis pairs

\left\{BP_{1},\ldots,BP_{l}\right\}
, the public key is
l
eigenstate pairs
\left\{SP_{1},\ldots,SP_{l}\right\}
.
We note that one can directly prepare the quantum states
SP_{a}
if he knows the eigenvalue pairs
VP_{a}
and the basis pairs

BP_{a}
.
However, it is not so easy to know the private key
VP_{a}
and
BP_{a}
from
SP_{a}
because he cannot know the measurement basis for each eigenstate.
Therefore, the map from the private key
VP_{a}
and
BP_{a}
to the public key
SP_{a}
works as an information-theoretic quantum one-way function.
The security of our protocol is based on this function.
However,
when an adversary, Jack, colludes with
too many participants holding public keys,
he can get the information of the corresponding private key.
Our security analysis depends on

1-P\left(S_{a,c}\right)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j+1,n}^{1/2}
, which implies

P(S)\leq P(S_{a})=\prod_{c=1}^{\lambda}P\left(S_{a,c}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j+1,n}^{1/2}\right)^{\lambda}.

(4)

Undeniability

The undeniability of our protocol is automatically satisfied since the communications between Alice and Bob only exist
in Key generation phase and Signing phase. Once Alice sends the public key, message, and signature to Bob, Bob can always
accept the signature.

Expandability

In the protocol presented in Section
3
,
we denote the bits consumed for the private key and the signature as
C_{1}(l)
and
C_{2}(l)
respectively, and
the qubits consumed for the public key as
C_{3}(l)
.
Now we have
C_{1}(l)=4\lambda l
,
C_{2}(l)=2\lambda l
,
C_{3}(l)=2\lambda l
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle C(l)=C_{1}(l)+C_{2}(l)+C_{3}(l)=8\lambda l.

(5)

Since
\lambda
is a constant, according to Definition 4, the expandability is also satisfied.

5
State identification

In this section, we assume that the adversary Jack has
n
copies of a quantum state uniformly
distributed on
|1\rangle,|0\rangle,|+\rangle,|-\rangle
and discuss the minimum identification error probability for
the quantum state.

5.1
Minimum error

The security analysis of our protocol
requires the maximum value of the state identification
of the following type of problem setting.
We choose the eigenvectors

|e_{0}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)

and
|e_{1}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)

of
Y
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|e_{0}\rangle+|e_{1}\rangle),\quad\frac{1+i}{\sqrt{2}}|+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|e_{0}\rangle+i|e_{1}\rangle)

(6)

\displaystyle i|1\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|e_{0}\rangle-|e_{1}\rangle),\quad\frac{1-i}{\sqrt{2}}|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|e_{0}\rangle-i|e_{1}\rangle).

(7)

Therefore, our possible states form
the state family
\{|f_{x}\rangle\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}
, where

|f_{x}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|e_{0}\rangle+e^{x\pi i/2}|e_{1}\rangle)
.
We consider estimation problem under this state family.
Given the true parameter
x
and our estimate
\hat{x}
,
we employ the following error function:

\displaystyle R(x,\hat{x}):=\frac{1-\cos(x-\hat{x})\frac{\pi}{2}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}(e^{\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}}+e^{-\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}})

(8)

That is, when the true parameter is
x
,
and the estimate by Jack is
\hat{x}
,
the probability that the information sent by Jack coincides with
Bob’s measurement outcome is
1-R(x,\hat{x})
.
Therefore, to consider Jack’s optimal attack,
we need to minimize the average of
R(x,\hat{x})
.

In the one-copy case,
we need to consider the following minimization

\displaystyle\min_{\{\Pi_{\hat{x}}\}}\sum_{x=0}^{3}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}R(x,\hat{x})\Tr\Pi_{\hat{x}}|f_{x}\rangle\langle f_{x}|,

(9)

where
\{\Pi_{\hat{x}}\}
is a POVM over one-qubit system.
In the
n
-copy case,
we need to consider the following minimization

\displaystyle\min_{\{\Pi_{\hat{x}}\}}\sum_{x=0}^{3}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}R(x,\hat{x})\Tr\Pi_{\hat{x}}(|f_{x}\rangle\langle f_{x}|)^{\otimes n},

(10)

where
\{\Pi_{\hat{x}}\}
is a POVM over
n
-qubit system.
For
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}
, We define

\displaystyle\alpha_{j,n}:=\sum_{k:k=j\hbox{ \rm mod}4}{n\choose k}.

(11)

Then, we obtain
the minimum identification error probability as follows.

Theorem 2

We have the following relation

\displaystyle\min_{\{\Pi_{\hat{x}}\}}\sum_{x=0}^{3}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}R(x,\hat{x})\Tr\Pi_{\hat{x}}(|f_{x}\rangle\langle f_{x}|)^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j+1,n}^{1/2}.

(12)

5.2
Proof of Theorem
2

To prove Theorem
2
, we employ
the covariant state estimation theory
[
35
, Chapter 4]
,
[
36
, Chapter 4]
.

We consider the representation
V_{x}
of the group
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
as

\displaystyle V_{x}:=|e_{0}\rangle\langle e_{0}|+e^{x\pi i/2}|e_{1}\rangle\langle e_{1}|.

(13)

Then, the state family
\{(|f_{x}\rangle\langle f_{x}|)^{\otimes n}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}

and the error function
R(x,\hat{x})
satisfies
the group covariance with respect to the representation
V_{x}^{\otimes n}
.
The vector
|f_{x}\rangle^{\otimes n}

belongs to the
n
-th symmetric subspace
{\cal H}_{n}
, which has the dimension
n+1

and is spanned by

\{|g_{k,n}\rangle\}_{k=0}^{n}
, where

\displaystyle|g_{k,n}\rangle:={n\choose k}^{-1/2}(\underbrace{|e_{0}\rangle\cdots|e_{0}\rangle}_{k}\underbrace{|e_{1}\rangle\cdots|e_{1}\rangle}_{n-k}+permutation).

(14)

The state
|f_{x}\rangle^{\otimes n}
is written as

\displaystyle|f_{x}\rangle^{\otimes n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}{n\choose k}^{1/2}e^{kx\pi i/2}|g_{k,n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\sum_{k:k=j\hbox{ \rm mod}4}\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}{n\choose k}^{1/2}e^{kx\pi i/2}|g_{k,n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\alpha_{j,k}^{1/2}e^{jx\pi i/2}|\beta_{j,n}\rangle,

(15)

where the normalized vector
|\beta_{j,n}\rangle
is defined as

\displaystyle|\beta_{j,n}\rangle:=\alpha_{j,k}^{-1/2}\sum_{k:k=j\hbox{ \rm mod}4}{n\choose k}^{1/2}|g_{k,n}\rangle.

(16)

Hence, the state

|f_{x}\rangle^{\otimes n}
belongs to the subspace

{\cal K}_{n}

spanned
by
\{|\beta_{j,n}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}
.

The representation on
{\cal K}_{n}

is given as

\displaystyle U^{(n)}_{x}:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}e^{jx\pi i/2}|\beta_{j,n}\rangle\langle\beta_{j,n}|.

(17)

Hence, the optimal estimator is given as a covariant measurement over

{\cal H}_{n}
.
That is, the optimal POVM has the form

\{U^{(n)}_{\hat{x}}TU^{(n)}_{-\hat{x}}\}_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}
,
where
T\geq 0
and

\langle\beta_{j,n}|T|\beta_{j,n}\rangle=1/4
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}
.
Then, to characterize the off-diagonal part of
T
,
we introduce

\displaystyle a_{j,j^{\prime}}:=4\langle\beta_{j,n}|T|\beta_{j^{\prime},n}\rangle,

(18)

which satisfies
|a_{j,j^{\prime}}|\leq 1
.

In the following, we consider

(e^{\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}}+e^{-\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}})

instead of

R(x,\hat{x})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}(e^{\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}}+e^{-\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}})
.
That is, we maximize

\sum_{x=0}^{3}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}(e^{\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}}+e^{-\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}})\langle f_{x}|^{\otimes n}\Pi_{\hat{x}}|f_{x}\rangle^{\otimes n}
.

Using the relation

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}(e^{-\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}}+e^{\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}})e^{(j-j^{\prime})\hat{x}\pi i/2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{ when }j-j^{\prime}=1,3\hbox{ mod }4\\
0&\hbox{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x=0}^{3}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}(e^{\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}}+e^{-\frac{(x-\hat{x})\pi i}{2}})\langle f_{x}|^{\otimes n}\Pi_{\hat{x}}|f_{x}\rangle^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}(-e^{\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}}+e^{\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}})\langle f_{0}|^{\otimes n}\Pi_{\hat{x}}|f_{0}\rangle^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}(e^{-\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}}+e^{\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}})\langle f_{0}|^{\otimes n}a_{j,j^{\prime}}U^{(n)}_{\hat{x}}|\beta_{j,n}\rangle\langle\beta_{j^{\prime},n}|U^{(n)}_{-\hat{x}}|f_{0}\rangle^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\sum_{\hat{x}=0}^{3}(e^{-\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}}+e^{\frac{\hat{x}\pi i}{2}})e^{(j-j^{\prime})\hat{x}\pi i/2}a_{j,j^{\prime}}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j^{\prime},n}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}:j-j^{\prime}=1,3}a_{j,j^{\prime}}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j^{\prime},n}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}:j-j^{\prime}=1,3}\frac{1}{2}(a_{j,j^{\prime}}+a_{j^{\prime},j})\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j^{\prime},n}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}:j-j^{\prime}=1,3}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j^{\prime},n}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\alpha_{j,n}^{1/2}\alpha_{j+1,n}^{1/2}.

(21)

The equality holds when
a_{j,j^{\prime}}=1
.
Therefore, we conclude (
12
).

6
Conclusion and future works

Quantum digital signature plays an important role in constructing advanced quantum information systems. A basic task
required in many quantum information systems is the authentication of the identity of a user.
A trusted third-party is necessary for many known quantum digital signature schemes
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
To break through the bottleneck of
the security, we propose a qubit-based quantum digital signature protocol without the third-party. On this basis, our protocol satisfies unforgeability
when there are not so many copies of the public keys in the adversary’s hand.
It also satisfies other significant security requirements: undeniability and expandability.
We conclude the security comparison with preceding studies in Table
1
.
In addition,
our protocol can be implemented only with single qubit physical operations, which does not require any entangling operation.
It should be mentioned
that our protocol can be further improved because there is room for reduction of
C(l)
.
The security analysis
for the combination of quantum digital signatures with other quantum information systems needs to be developed. Hence, in
the future, our work focus on how to solve the above problems.
In addition,
although our protocol is given as a simple combination of qubit technologies,
it requires quantum memories.
Implementation of quantum memories is a key technology for our protocol.
