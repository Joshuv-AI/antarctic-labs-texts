Comparative evaluation of the degree of conversion of an 8th-generation bonding agent when applied to normal dentin or caries-affected dentin, pre-treated with MMP inhibitors – An in vitro study
Authors: Neil Vincent Lewis, Shalini Aggarwal, Prasanna Dhatavkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe degree of conversion of dentin bonding agents contributes to the success of final restorations in adhesive dentistry. It is clinically relevant, as it is directly related to the overall bond strength that maintains these restorations in place and prevents marginal deterioration. However, Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) present within the dentin substrate have been known to have a deleterious effect on the bond stability, due to their collagenolytic activity. Inhibition of the effects of MMPs has been studied, and the use of a pre-treatment protocol that includes MMP inhibiting agents before bonding procedures has been explored, and has been found to increase bond strengths. However, the effect that MMP inhibitors have on dental adhesives has not yet been explored, particularly when being used in caries-affected-dentin (CAD). This study aims to report the degree of conversion of an 8th-generation dentin bonding agent in sound dentin (SD) vs caries-affected dentin after pre-treatment using MMP inhibitors.Materials & methods60 dentin discs were divided into groups of sound and affected dentin, and pretreated with either Cranberry extract or mulberry extract was applied to half of the discs in each group, followed by the application of an eight-generation bonding agent to the pre-treated discs, which were then light cured. The degree of conversion of the adhesive was calculated using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy.ResultsThe degree of conversion was found to be the highest in the samples of sound dentin which were pre-treated with Mulberry extract.ConclusionPre-treatment of the dentin with MMP inhibitors led to an increase in the degree of conversion in sound dentin but not in CAD.

Introduction

Dental adhesives can be either etch and rinse, or self-etch. The recent self-etch adhesives are set apart from etch and rinse systems as they comprise an acidic primer/adhesive mixture that can be applied either on enamel or smear layer-covered dentin: this procedure voids any inconsistency between an etched collagen network and the resin infiltration. These unique systems, reduce the technique sensitivity involved with the previous etch-and-rinse systems.1

Dentin adhesives consist primarily of hydrophobic and hydrophilic monomers, present in varying percentages, and these blends then form amphiphilic compounds that allow for adhesion between the hydrophobic restorative material, and the dentin substrate which is wet and poses a challenge during bonding procedures. Self-etching systems have been known to have greater percentages of hydrophilic monomers when compared to previous systems.2, 3, 4, 5, 6

Despite their simplified bonding procedure, these dental adhesives have been known to show lower immediate bond strengths and stability of the adhesive interface, which results in an accelerated aging phenomenon, affecting the hybrid layer. This is attributed to the relatively increased number of hydrophilic monomers in their formula. The resultant adhesive interface is semi-permeable and permits fluid to cross due to osmotic pressure, thus, degrading the interface due to a water-meditated phenomenon.7

In clinical settings, the dentin substrate presents a challenge in adhesion as clinicians are inclined to perform conservative cavity preparations. These preparations include sound dentin (SD) and to some extent caries caries-affected dentin (CAD). When compared, SD is known to be a more stable substrate and outperforms CAD in both the self-etch and etch and rinse systems when tested for immediate bond strengths. Due to the carious process, CAD has lower mineral content and shows apatite crystal dissolution, and collagen degradation by bacterial and host-mediated enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cysteine cathepsins. These morphological alterations to CAD may reduce the performance of bonded restorations due to poor dentin hybridization.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

MMPs are zinc and calcium-dependent endopeptidases naturally present within the dentin matrix during teeth development. They are activated in acidic environments such as those caused by the caries process and subsequently by applying acidic etchants during bonding procedures. Several strategies have been implemented to inhibit MMP activity to promote tensile and micro-tensile bond strengths of the resin dentin interface.16

Studies have indicated that the adhesive permeability of self-etch adhesives is correlated with the presence of unreacted monomers within the hybrid layer and higher permeability has been associated with a low degree of conversion (DC). The degree of conversion is the rate of polymerization of monomeric C=C bonds to polymeric C-C bonds that form after curing. It is known that the DC is directly interlinked to the physical properties of the polymer achieved. Clinically, including CAD in cavity preparations is the more conservative option and avoids removal of dentinal substrate that is affected by caries, but shows no bacterial presence. CAD is known to have poorer bond strengths with dental adhesives, and thus every effort is made to ensure that the bonding strengths in this substrate are improved by all possible means. The application of MMP inhibitors raises the bond strengths in both SD and CAD. As the adhesion to the dentin substrate provides a challenge, this study aimed to evaluate whether a higher DC can be achieved when an 8th generation bonding agent (G-Premio Bond) is applied to different dentin substrates, with or without a pre-treatment protocol (MMP inhibitors).7,17

Materials and methods

In this in-vitro experimental study, dentin discs required for the study were obtained from freshly extracted human teeth. This procedure was implemented according to the standard guidelines following ethical consent from the institutional review board (DPU/484/27/2021).

Sample size calculation and grouping

In this study, the sample size calculation was done using OpenEpi™ software, utilizing a 95 % confidence interval and 80 % power of study. As per the requirement of this study, a total of sixty (60) samples were needed. This formula is used in the software to calculate sample size n:

The samples consisted of two groups; a sound dentin (SD) group and a caries affected dentin (CAD) group.

Preparation of the dentin pre-treatment solution

Two experimental solutions i.e., Cranberry extract (CRE) and Mulberry extract (MLE) solutions were used in the designed protocol. The pre-treatment solutions were prepared separately by adding 6.5g of powdered extract in 100 ml of deionized water to achieve two individual MMP-inhibiting solutions of 6.5 % CRE and 6.5 % MLE.18

Specimen storage and preparation to obtain sound dentin surface

Sixty (60) extracted non-carious teeth were obtained and stored in 0.1 % thymol solution due to its antibacterial properties. Sixty (60) dentin discs were sectioned after the removal of occlusal enamel to expose the sound dentin surface. Sectioning was performed mesiodistally to the cementoenamel junction, using a water-cooled diamond saw (Isomet 1000 Saw; Buehler Ltd, Lake Bluff, IL).18 To achieve standardization of the experimental surface, the discs were sectioned into 2 mm thickness, and nail varnish was applied to the outer circumferential part of the dentin disc to aid in the identification of the experimental surface.

Simulation of an artificial carious lesion using a chemical model

In the CAD group, to simulate the demineralization of dentin that occurs in carious lesions, a chemical model was used, which consisted of a demineralizing solution (DS) bath containing 6 % citric acid (pH = 2.2). The specimens would then be submerged in the solution for a fixed time (96h), after which they are removed, washed with deionized water, and air-dried.19

Pre-treatment protocol using MMP inhibiting solution

The bonding protocol in the experimental groups incorporated a ‘dentin pre-treatment’ phase, which consisted of applying MMP inhibiting solutions after etching the specimen for 15 s using orthophosphoric acid (Etching gel Prime Dental ™). Using a micro-brush applicator (ORO, Reach Global India Pvt. ©) as a carrier, the MMP inhibiting solution was applied and agitated, then dried using compressed air, followed by a second application. The samples were then processed for the degree of conversion analysis and graph plotting. Laboratory photographs of the pre-treatment protocol are provided in Fig. 2. Degree of conversion using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy

In the control groups, after achieving a dry etched surface, an 8th-generation dental adhesive (GC G-Premio Bond, GC Corporation™, Tokyo, Japan) was applied according to the manufacturer's instructions. The uncured adhesive applied to the sample surface was placed on the ATR- FTIR spectrometer (Alpha II Bruker Optics™ GmbH & Co. KG Ettlingen, Germany), and absorption spectra were obtained. The adhesive was then light cured (blue light 420–480 nm) using a light curing unit (Woodpecker ™ Light Cure LED C, 1000–1200mW/cm-2, Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instruments Co., Ltd) for 10 s, and absorption spectra were obtained again. The values of unconditioned SD and CAD were evaluated to establish the baseline values of the control groups. The same protocol was executed to assess the degree of conversion of the test groups (6.5 % CRE and 6.5 % MLE).

ATR-FTIR analysis and degree of conversion calculation

The absorption spectra of the dental adhesive of each specimen were obtained before and after curing by slightly pressing the central part of the targeted surface to the ZnSe crystal (1 × 1mm2) of an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) cell which was attached to a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Alpha II Bruker Optics™ GmbH & Co. KG Ettlingen, Germany). The spectra were obtained under the 1000-4000 cm-1wavenumber range at a resolution of 4cm-1 and the spectral peak values were used to calculate the degree of conversion. The spectra and peak values were obtained for the sound dentin (SD) group and caries affected dentin (CAD) group and assessed using software (OriginLab © Corporation). Results and statistical analysis

After the calculation of all samples tested; the DC data was statistically analyzed and group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and the statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Mann-Whitney U test was applied to find out the significant mean differences between individual groups (Fig. 5a–b). In the SD group, the DC was observed to be highest when compared to the CAD group overall. Inter-group comparison within the SD group showed that the DC was maintained in the CRE test group, whereas it was found to have significantly increased in the MLE test group. Contrary to these findings, in the CAD group, the DC had been maintained in the CRE test group, but, it had not improved in the MLE test group. It was seen that the application of MMPs increased the DC in the SD groups but this was not seen in the two CAD groups, where only CRE treated group could maintain its DC, but showed no increase.

Discussion

This research was carried out to evaluate the degree of conversion seen in an 8th generation adhesive (GC- G Premio Bond®) with and without the intervention of MMP inhibitors as a pre-treatment protocol on the substrates – Sound dentin (SD) and Caries affected dentin (CAD). In polymers, the degree of conversion (DC) refers to the conversion of monomeric carbon-carbon double bonds to polymeric carbon-carbon single bonds. In dental adhesives, the monomers are converted to polymers when induced by the application of a curing light. The value of this monomer-polymer conversion rate represents the degree of conversion (DC), which forms the basis of all the physical properties of any dental adhesive. The DC is influenced by a host of factors, chief amongst these, is the nature of the monomer itself, and the environment in which it is placed.7 Also, any unreacted monomers within the adhesive layer, are bound to leach out, leading to permeability within the hybrid layer, thereby decreasing its longevity.

Shallow resin tag formation and a low conversion rate, both contribute to the decreased longevity of the adhesive layer, even if the collagen impregnation is achieved satisfactorily. A lower DC leads to the formation of a porous hybridoid structure, with a reduction in its sealing ability all along the adhesive interface, extrapolating to a reduced clinical performance of the dental adhesive (self-cure and multi-step adhesive systems).1,7

One-step self-etch adhesives show the highest permeability in their adhesive layers as opposed to multi-step adhesive systems. Whilst using the dental adhesive, any failure in adhering to the manufacturer's instructions, such as the recommended film thickness, number of layers, curing technique and curing/not curing in between layers all contribute to a poorly polymerized adhesive layer. The bond instability caused by a substandard DC result in reduced tensile bond strength and will manifest clinically in the entire spectrum ranging from discolouration at the tissue-material interface to debonding of the restoration.23

Standardization of specimens and the simulation of artificial carious lesion: chemical model vs microbiological model

Artificial caries lesions mimic the clinical scenario in which treatment will be executed. A well-formed and reproducible in vitro lesion is necessary to establish the assessment and comparison of experimental treatment outcomes. In this study, specimens were stored in thymol solution. Immersion of teeth specimens in a 0.1 % thymol solution has been shown to completely eradicate Streptococcus mutans. Thymol causes the perforation of cell membranes, resulting in bacterial cell death, though its use cannot ensure the eradication of all pathogens. Although, sectioning and polishing of teeth specimens to expose an internal fresh dentin surface could potentially remove contamination before treatment and provide a standardised surface for artificial caries simulation.24, 25, 26

The development of chemical in vitro models to simulate carious lesions was aimed at providing a simplified reproducible model for caries. These systems can create lesions histologically with comparable depths that are similar to those found in naturally occurring caries and microbiological models that contain bacteria and degradative enzymes.27

Microbiological models that are used to artificially simulate carious lesions are produced using biofilms obtained from plaque and saliva to mimic the oral environment. Due to microbiological models being more complex and technique-sensitive, their characterization, culture, and standardization pose a challenge. Oral biofilms are complexly composed of a variety of bacterial species which may be challenging to cultivate. They are not easily regulated by accurate control of acid, pH values, or mineral contents in a laboratory setting. Lack of consistency and reproducibility can be a limitation of microbiological models when compared with chemical models.28, 29, 30, 31, 32

Role of the substrate in DC

In addition to the above factors, the condition of the substrate and its pre-treatment too, bear influence on the degree of conversion. The adequacy of the etched enamel, as well as the appropriate conditioning of dentin, contributes to the final DC. When dentin is the substrate, its molecular and sub-molecular interaction with adhesives would also vary, as per its condition, whether sound, caries infected, or caries affected. Sound dentin and caries-affected dentin have markedly different chemical compositions. This establishes itself in the nano-architecture, due to the depletion (moderate, mild, severe) of the mineral content in each type, resulting in a marked change of the physical properties, microstructure, and atomic emission spectra of CAD when compared to SD.33,34

CAD shows a predominance of organic material, in the form of collagen fibres and fibrils. The integrity of which is questionable when demanding an intact bonded surface able to sustain restorations in clinical practice. Whilst considering the resin dentin bonding stability in CAD, the durability and overall strength of the adhesive layer formed by the dental adhesive is of utmost importance. Therefore, the DC of the dental adhesive equally contributes to the success of restorations placed within CAD.

The usage of MMP inhibitors as a pre-treatment protocol in bonding procedures has been known to improve properties of the hybridised dentin (SD), when evaluated micro-morphologically and for various physical properties.35,36 In CAD, studies have shown to improve the mechanical properties (TBS/μTBS) of restorations after a similar pre-treatment protocol.37 Up until now though, the efficacy of the dental adhesive by gauging its DC with or without a pre-treatment protocol using MMP inhibitors had not been explored.

Role of MMPs and MMP inhibitors in bonding and degree of conversion

MMPs are known zinc and calcium-dependent endopeptidases present in the extracellular matrix of dentin. Previous studies indicate that their presence causes collagenolytic activity/collagen degradation.38,39 As stable collagen porosities and network lattices are needed for the effective penetration of dental adhesives in bonding procedures, it is imperative to preserve the integrity of collagen within the dentin substrate. The use of MMP inhibitors as a pre-treatment protocol have been previously studied and are known to stabilize or reduce MMP activity as they compete to bind to zinc and calcium ions, thus inactivating MMPs. This inactivation has been shown to improve the overall stability of collagen leading to better and efficient bond strengths of dental adhesives.16 These effects are translatory to the degree of conversion of these adhesives.

The results of this study showed that there was a statistically significant difference in the degree of conversion that an 8th-generation self-etch adhesive could achieve when it was applied to either sound dentin or caries affected dentin. Additionally, when the MMP inhibitor was applied there was an increase in the degree of conversion in the SD samples. This was not the case in the CAD samples, where the samples treated with either of the two MMP inhibitors showed no increase in the degree of conversion, and the samples which were pre-treated with either mulberry extract showed a lower degree of conversion than the CAD that was pre-treated with cranberry extract. This could be explained by the high moisture content of the CAD, which was then further inundated with water molecules from the MMP inhibitors, lowering the concentration of the monomers in the dental adhesive, and therefore lowering the DC.

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy in determining the degree of conversion

The ATR-FTIR spectroscopy technique was used in this study model as it measures the effect of light absorbance in the polymerization process. The technique is also capable of measuring the rate of polymerization in real time.40 When compared to the complimentary conventional Raman or micro-Raman spectroscopy technique, it has proven to be more accurate in determining absorbance in smaller cross sections/spatial resolutions. Water content in dentin bonding agents plays a role as water is a strong IR(Infrared) absorber and a weak Raman scatterer. IR spectroscopy relies on the direct absorption of light because of transitions between vibrational (or rotational) states and must be performed in the IR region of the spectrum.41 ATR-FITR can identify and quantify the polymerization rates and further it can trace how dental monomers react to dental substrates.42

The in vitro model of this study suggests that lateral investigations are needed to determine the quality of the hybrid layer formed within these variables, and their subsequent bond strengths tested. Within the limitations imposed by the in vitro model of this study, it could be inferred that, in clinical situations, even cavities with a higher percentage of caries affected dentin, could be restored after the application of MMP inhibitors, which would increase the remaining dentin thickness and improve the prognosis of the restorative procedure.

There are very few studies that have examined the effect of MMP inhibitors on the dentin substrate, sound dentin, and the degree of conversion of the bonding agent. There have been no studies done thus far to examine the degree of conversion when MMP inhibitors are applied to CAD.

Conclusion

The degree of conversion is an important measure of the performance of a dental adhesive and this study shows that the DC is improved using MMP inhibitors, prior to the application and light curing of the bonding agent. The use of MMP inhibiting agents such CRE and MLE in sound dentin was seen to maintain and improve the degree of conversion of an 8th-generation self-etch adhesive and thus it leads to the conclusion within the limitations of this study that clinically hybridization of CAD is a distinct possibility.42, 43, 44, 45
