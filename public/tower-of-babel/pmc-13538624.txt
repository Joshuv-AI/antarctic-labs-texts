Acoustic higher-order topological metals with bound corner states in the continuum
Authors: Xingyu Chen, Zhenhang Pu, Hailong He, Liping Ye, Jiuyang Lu, Manzhu Ke, Weiyin Deng, Zhengyou Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two-dimensional topological insulators for first order and higher order, characterized respectively by in-gap edge states and corner states, have attracted tremendous research interest. Different from them, two-dimensional topological metals exhibit topological boundary states in the absence of bulk bandgaps. While first-order topological metals with anti-chiral or anti-helical edge states have been implemented, higher-order topological metals still lack systematic experimental investigation. Here, we propose a practically accessible approach to two-dimensional higher-order topological metals and demonstrate their experimental realization in phononic crystals. The metallic bulk states, gapped anti-helical edge states, and topological corner states, are unambiguously demonstrated via airborne sound experiments. As the key feature of such topological metal, the topological corner states are embedded in the continuous spectrum of metallic bulk bands, namely the bound corner states in the continuum. Our findings evidence higher-order topological states in metallic phases, and may promote the development of next-generation devices for acoustic wave manipulation.

Introduction

Topological phases of matter present a defining frontier in modern physics, with origins traceable to the discovery of two-dimensional (2D) topological insulators in the 1980s1–4. As a paradigmatic topological insulator, a quantum Hall insulator requires the breaking of time-reversal symmetry and is characterized by chiral edge states in the bulk bandgap5,6. In time-reversal-invariant 2D systems, quantum spin Hall insulator is featured with helical edge states (HESs) in the bandgap7,8, enabling spin-filtered one-way edge transport as illustrated in Fig. 1a. During the past decade, higher-order topological insulator has sparked sustained academic interest since its proposal9–12. This refers to a gapped system hosting in-gap topological states on boundaries of codimension larger than one, which typically originate from the quantized multipole moments of bulk bands or the mass sign reversal of edge bands. For instance, the quantum spin Hall insulator is found to support 0D in-gap corner states in-between the gapped edge bands when subjected to an in-plane Zeeman field13,14, manifesting the second-order topology (Fig. 1b). To date, the notion of higher-order topological states has been transplanted from condensed matter systems to artificial structures15–20 such as photonic crystals and phononic crystals (PCs), paving the way toward robust sensing devices in a wide variety of quantum and classical wave systems21–24.Fig. 1Schematics of four topological phases in two dimensions.a Quantum spin Hall insulator with HESs. b Higher-order topological insulator with in-gap corner states. c Topological metal with anti-HESs. d HOTM with BCICs. More recently, research focus has increasingly shifted to topological metals25–31, a gapless topological phase exhibiting conduction and valence bands intersecting without isolated closing singular points or lines at the Fermi level. 2D first-order topological metals can host new types of edge states, including the anti-chiral edge states25 and anti-HESs30, which have been observed in photonic crystals27 and PCs31, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the anti-HESs show counterintuitive spin-filtered edge transports that propagate in the same direction on opposite edges. Based on these advances, it is natural to expect a higher-order topological metal (HOTM) phase exhibiting bound corner states in the continuum (BCICs) bulk band32, see Fig. 1d. Embedded in the continuous spectrum of metallic bulk bands but remaining intact, such BCICs offer new possibilities for higher-order topological materials without requiring spectral isolation. Nevertheless, the high promise of HOTMs contrasts sharply with slow progress, due to a scarcity of testable theoretical models to enable practical implementation.

Here, we report a practically accessible approach to HOTMs and present its first observation in PCs. Leveraging the layer pseudospin degree of freedom, we engineer a bilayer model containing two sets of oppositely oriented chiral hoppings. Via a unitary transformation, the chiral hoppings can be equivalently converted into intrinsic spin-orbit couplings with staggered signs and a synthetic in-plane Zeeman field. As such, the desired HOTM is realized in a simple model with real-valued hoppings only, which is easy-to-perform in diverse systems. Through numerical simulations and airborne sound experiments, the metallic bulk bands, the anti-helical edge transport, and the BCICs are unambiguously verified, collectively demonstrating the acoustic HOTM across hierarchical levels. Unlike most of the reported topological bound states in the continuum33–38, which are typically realized via spectral tuning, the BCICs in our metallic model are naturally embedded in the continuum bulk bands, requiring no intentional engineering. Our findings extend the scope of topological physics in metallic systems and may herald opportunities to design innovative acoustic devices.

To verify the existence of gapped edge states, we compute the edge band structure of a nanoribbon with zigzag edges along the xx direction, as shown in Fig. 2c. While remaining the bulk bands gapless, the Zeeman field opens up an energy gap between two branches of edge bands; in each branch both spin-up and spin-down edge states are involved with opposite group velocities, as indicated by the color scale. Of note, for each spin sector, the edge states exhibit identical group velocity direction regardless of whether they reside on the top or bottom edges, implying the anti-helical edge transport in the presence of the edge bandgap. On the other hand, as the top and bottom edges are terminated by different sublattices, their effective edge Hamiltonians host mass terms with opposite signs (see Supplementary Note 1). According to the Jackiw-Rebbi mechanism39, corner states are topologically ensured as domain wall modes between the two distinct edges with opposite mass signs. Armchair edges are omitted due to the absence of edge states, which prevents the formation of mass domain walls and thus precludes the emergence of corner states (see Supplementary Note 5).

To confirm the above prediction, we examine a rhombic nanoflake and display the energy spectrum in Fig. 2d. The nanoflake possesses two edges terminated by sublattices A and the other two by sublattices B, with the same zigzag configurations as the nanoribbon in Fig. 2c. As expected, two BCICs appear, as highlighted in orange, which are embedded in the bulk band but situated in the edge bandgap. More explicitly, the wavefunctions of three representative eigenstates (marked by the arrows) are exemplified in Fig. 2e: state I represents a BCIC, which is localized solely at the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$120^\circ$$\end{document}120∘ corners, interpolating the edges with opposite mass signs; state II is a bulk state next to the BCICs in the spectrum, exhibiting a similar energy but extended spatial distribution; state III denotes an edge state whose energy lies in the energy range of anti-HES bands, i.e., the shaded regions in Fig. 2c, d. These results unambiguously identify our model as a HOTM with BCICs. Crucially, as the two key elements enabling the HOTM phase, the staggered spin-orbit couplings and the in-plane Zeeman field (specifically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{y}$$\end{document}By) are simultaneously achieved by the oppositely oriented chiral hoppings in our bilayer structure. If the interlayer hoppings share the same chirality, conventional uniform spin-orbit couplings are effectively formed, driving the system into an insulating phase40. Discussion

To summarize, we have developed a practically accessible model and presented the first experimental evidence of HOTMs using a bilayer PC, thereby advancing the progress in this field despite its great theoretical promise. As the key signature of the HOTM phase, the BCICs exhibit distinct spectral configurations from known corner states in topological insulators13; compared to previous topological corner states in the continuum in PCs36,37,44, the BCICs are generated via a different mechanism and naturally reside within the bulk continuum without engineering. In addition, by extending the established progression from topological insulators to 3D semimetals47–55, we can further stack our model to realize 3D HOTMs with hinge states. Our work is broadly applicable to a variety of systems, such as electric circuits, mechanical, photonic, and cold atom systems, and may have broader potential applications in topological devices spanning classical and quantum systems.
