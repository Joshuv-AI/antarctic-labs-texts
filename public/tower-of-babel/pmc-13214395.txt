Angiotensin II impairs the remyelination mediated by brain endothelial cell-derived exosomes
Authors: Yiwen Yuan, Suzhen Liang, Ao Guan, Jian Sun, Weiwei Shen, Qiang Dong, Min Guo, Mei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite matter hyperintensities (WMH), characterized by myelin loss and axonal injury, are largely driven by hypertension, their most significant modifiable risk factor. The role of endothelial cell-derived exosomes (EC-Exos) in oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPCs)-mediated remyelination within WMH remains critical but poorly defined. This study investigated the function of EC-Exos in remyelination by comparing exosomes from angiotensin II-treated brain microvascular endothelial cells (AngII-Exos; modeling hypertensive conditions) with those from untreated cells (BMEC-Exos; modeling normotensive conditions). In vitro, BMEC-Exos promoted OPCs differentiation, evidenced by increased myelin basic protein (MBP) expression and reduced immature NG2+ OPCs. AngII-Exos lost this pro-differentiation capacity, instead suppressing OPCs proliferation and inducing immature OPCs accumulation. In vivo, the focal demyelination mouse model showed that BMEC-Exos injected into the lateral ventricle migrated to lesions, enhancing MBP intensity and reducing NG2+ areas, while AngII-Exos failed to promote remyelination. Lipidomic analysis revealed AngII-Exos exhibited altered fatty acid profiles, including reduced levels of essential myelin-related lipids (e.g., FFA 18:1, 22:0) and elevated FFA 22:6, impairing their ability to supply lipid precursors for OPCs maturation. These findings demonstrate that Ang II disrupts EC-Exos lipid composition, compromising their pro-remyelination effects on OPCs, which may represent a critical pathological mechanism underlying hypertension-associated white matter damage. Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) are frequently observed in brain MRI scans of middle-aged and elderly individuals and are commonly associated with various forms of white matter damage. Large-scale, long-term clinical cohort studies have demonstrated that WMH is strongly correlated with adverse outcomes, including stroke, dementia, and increased mortality rates [1, 2]. Therefore, slowing the progression of white matter damage is considered a crucial strategy for reducing the incidence of stroke and dementia. Among the many risk factors contributing to WMH severity and progression, hypertension is identified as an independent, modifiable risk factor. Effective blood pressure control has been shown to significantly ameliorate white matter lesions [3]. Among antihypertensive treatments, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) and angiotensin II (Ang II) receptor blockers (ARBs) are believed to exert more favorable effects on the outcomes of brain diseases such as stroke and dementia [4]. These findings suggest that Ang II, a key molecule involved in the pathophysiological processes of hypertension-induced white matter damage, play a pivotal role in its progression. However, the molecular mechanism linking Ang II to white matter damage remains not yet fully explained.

Pathologically, white matter damage is characterized by myelin loss and associated axonal injury. Under healthy conditions, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) mediate myelin regeneration by proliferating, migrating to injury sites, and differentiating into mature oligodendrocytes to restore myelin at demyelinated regions [5]. During remyelination in adulthood, the interaction between OPCs and the “oligovascular niche” is a dynamic process where blood vessels serve as both physical scaffolds for migration and sources of critical biochemical signals [6]. In adult demyelinated brains, increased OPC density around the vasculature was identified, with approximately 17% of oligodendrocytes and their precursors in direct contact with vascular basement membranes [7]. Brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) are the essential cellular constituents of cerebral blood vessels, constituting the inner lining of the blood-brain barrier. Recent advancements in the study of BMECs have shifted the understanding of these cells from being static barriers to dynamic components of the neurovascular unit. BMECs are increasingly recognized as key regulators of brain homeostasis and contributors to the progression of remyelination [8]. It was reported that endothelial cells play a crucial role in promoting OPCs migration and differentiation during development, partly through the secretion of factors such as Cxcl12, TGFβ1, and Ang1 [6, 9, 10]. However, how BMECs regulate OPCs-mediated myelin regeneration in adult remyelination remains poorly understood.

Endothelial cells possess a robust capacity for exosome secretion, a process that is subject to regulation by a variety of physiological and pathological insults, such as ischemia and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [11–13]. Exosomes, which are nano-sized extracellular vesicles, have recently emerged as critical signaling carriers in the central nervous system (CNS) [14]. Exosomes deliver biological cargo from donor cells to recipient cells, where they influence diverse cellular functions. Despite their recognized role in CNS communication, no study has yet investigated whether exosomes derived from endothelial cells under normal and hypertensive conditions can influence white matter damage or the OPCs-mediated remyelination process. To address this knowledge gap, the present study explores the effects of endothelial cell-derived exosomes on OPCs under normal and hypertensive conditions. Additionally, lipidomics analyses of exosomes were conducted to investigate the underlying mechanisms mediating their effects on myelin regeneration.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of primary BMECs

Primary BMECs were isolated from 4-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Shanghai Jiesijie Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.). Following anesthesia with isoflurane and decapitation, brains were rapidly extracted and placed in ice-cold high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Meninges were carefully removed and then the cerebral cortex was dissected and minced into approximately 1 mm³ pieces. These tissue blocks were transferred to a 50 ml centrifuge tube and digested with a digestion buffer (0.1% collagenase type II; 100 U DNAse dissolved in 25 mL high-glucose DMEM) by repeated pipetting. The homogenate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with continuous shaking. The enzymatic digestion was terminated by the addition of high-glucose DMEM. Following centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min at room temperature (RT), the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in a 20% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution. This suspension was then centrifuged at 1000 g for 20 min at 4 °C to separate the microvessels. The white layer containing the microvessels was carefully aspirated, and the supernatant was discarded. The digestion and BSA gradient centrifugation steps were repeated once to further purify the BMECs. The isolated microvessels were then resuspended in an endothelial cell culture medium (ECM, ScienCell kit, 5% fetal bovine serum, FBS; 100× endothelial cell growth supplement; 100× penicillin-streptomycin; basal medium, 1001) and plated in 6 cm tissue culture dishes. The culture medium was replaced after 3 days and subsequently every 2–3 days. The purity of BMECs is > 90% as shown in the additional file 1 Fig. 1.

Isolation of OPCs

Primary OPCs were isolated from neonatal pups. Following anesthesia with ice and decapitation, brains were rapidly extracted and placed in the ice-cold DMEM/F12 medium. Meninges were removed, and the brain tissue was minced into a 50 ml centrifuge tube containing 20% FBS supplemented culture medium. The tissue was further homogenized by repeated pipetting and distributed into T75 flasks with 9–10 ml of the F12 medium with 20% FBS. Mixed glial cultures were maintained for 10–14 days, with medium changes performed every 3–4 days. Mixed glial cells were then trypsinized (using Trypsin-EDTA, 0.25%, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25200072), and the cell suspension was centrifuged at 350 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer, centrifuged, and then incubated with Anti-O4 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, ratio 9:1 cells to beads) at 4 °C for 15 min. Following incubation, cells were washed and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. Magnetic separation was performed using LS Separation Columns (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-401) and a MidiMACS™ Separator (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-301) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The positively selected fraction containing OPCs was collected. OPCs were plated at a density of 7–10 × 10⁴ cells/cm² on tissue culture plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (PDL, Thermo Fisher Scientific industries, A3890401). OPCs were cultured in the OPC culture medium (ScienCell kit, 1601, OPCM, 500 µL 100× N2, Gibco, 17502048; 1 mL 50× B27, Gibco, 17504044; 30 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, PeproTech, 100-18B-10UG), 30 ng/mL platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF, PeproTech, 100–13 A-10UG) in 50 mL DMEM/F12 medium).

Ang II treatment and exosome isolation

A fresh Ang II stock solution (1 mg/mL in PBS, 1000×) was prepared prior to each experiment. For cell treatment, the stock was diluted into the culture medium at 1:1000 (v/v) to achieve a final concentration of 1 µg/mL. This concentration was selected based on established protocols for endothelial cell mechanistic studies[15–18] to ensure maximal receptor occupancy and to compensate for the rapid proteolytic degradation of the peptide in the static in vitro environment.

Primary mouse BMECs were treated at 70–80% confluence: the control group received standard endothelial cell medium, while the Ang II group was incubated with medium containing 1 µg/mL Ang II. After 24 h, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS (37 °C) and then cultured in medium with exosome-depleted serum (5% exosome-free FBS, VivaCell, C3801-0100) for another 48 h. The conditioned medium was collected and sequentially centrifuged: first at 3000 g for 15 min to remove cellular debris, then at 18,000 g for 45 min to remove larger microvesicles. Exosomes were finally pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C using a Beckman Coulter Optima L-100 XP ultracentrifuge. The resulting exosome pellet was resuspended in PBS for downstream applications.

Exosome qualification

Isolated exosomes were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) as we previously reported [19, 20]. For TEM, exosome suspensions were applied to an electron microscope copper mesh, fixed and stained with Phosphotungstic acid solution, and visualized using a TEM (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Purified exosomes were also characterized using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300, NanoSight Ltd.). Samples were diluted 1:100 in PBS and analyzed in triplicate.

Mouse Ang-II ELISA Kit (Elabscience, E-EL-M2612) was used to evaluate the Ang-II levels in exosomes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each group (Control and Ang‑II), exosomes were collected from approximately 2 × 10⁷ endothelial cells. The exosome pellet was resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors, lysed by sonication, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was normalized, and samples were diluted to a final concentration of 10 µg/mL for the assay. A volume of 50 µL of diluted sample (0.5 µg total protein per well) was loaded into each well.

Exosome treatment in vivo and in vitro

All exosomes were isolated from BMECs of the same isolation batch and passage. For each experiment, cells were split into untreated (control) and AngII‑treated groups, and exosomes from both groups were collected in parallel under identical conditions. This approach ensured that comparisons were always made between exosomes derived from the same original cell population.

Exosome doses were normalized to the number of source endothelial cells. For in vitro studies, exosomes equivalent to those secreted by 5 × 106 BMECs were applied per 1 cm² OPC culture. For in vivo administration, each mouse received exosomes derived from 5 × 107 BMECs. This standardization ensured consistent and comparable exosome exposure across all experiments.

Chemical demyelination model induction and intracerebroventricular (ICV) exosome injection

Demyelinating lesions were generated in the corpus callosum (CC) of 6–8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice through stereotaxic unilateral injection of 1 µL of 2% lysolecithin (LPC) into the CC (1.0 mm lateral, 1.0 mm rostral to Bregma, and 1.5 mm deep). For exosome tracing, PKH67 dye was used to label exosomes according to the manufacturer’s instructions (PKH67 Fluorescent Labeling Kit, Sigma-Aldrich, MIDI67) [19]. Mice were randomized into the exosomes injection group and the control group. On day 7 post-injury, exosomes were administered via ICV injection. The coordinates for the right lateral ventricle were established as follows: anteroposterior (AP) at -0.22 mm from bregma and mediolateral (ML) at + 1.0 mm from the midline. The target site on the skull was then marked. A cranial drill was used to create a burr hole at this marked position. A microsyringe needle was lowered to a depth of 2.5 mm below the dura mater (dorsoventral (DV) = -2.5 mm). An exosome suspension of 10 µL, derived from 5 × 107 endothelial cells, was infused into the ventricle at a rate of 0.2 µL/min. The needle was kept in place for an additional 5 min post-infusion to minimize reflux before being slowly retracted. The scalp was sutured using interrupted stitches, and the mice were allowed to recover in a warmed cage.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from isolated exosomes and cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffers (mixed with protease phosphatase inhibitors at a 100:1 volume ratio). Cell lysates were sonicated on ice and centrifuged at 14000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble material. The supernatants containing the total protein were collected and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Separated proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skimmed milk in TBST. Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following specific primary antibodies: anti-CD63(1:1000; Abcam, Ab59479), anti-GAPDH(1:1000; Yisheng, 30201ES20), anti-CD31(1:1000; Abcam, ab222783) (QuickBlock™ Western primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized with chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad, ChemiDocTM XRS + System).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed as previously described [21]. In brief, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline followed by a fixative (4% PFA/0.25% glutaraldehyde). After overnight post-fixation, 200 μm vibratome sections were prepared. Regions of interest were dissected, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and processed through graded ethanol dehydration and Epon embedding. Following toluidine blue staining for orientation, ultrathin sections were contrasted with uranyl acetate and imaged on a JEOL JSM-6490 SEM.

OPC differentiation assay

OPCs were seeded into 48-well plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (PDL) and maintained in the Neurobasal medium for 7 days. The medium was then replaced with a differentiation medium for 2 days, after which exosomes derived from BMECs (5 × 10⁶ BMECs/300 µL) were added to the culture for 48 h. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and labeled for further analysis.

OPC proliferation assay

For proliferation assays, differentiated OPCs were treated with 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) from an EdU cell proliferation kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, C0071S) and exosomes (concentration specified as mentioned previously) for 24 h. OPCs were plated onto glass coverslips and, after one day, were co-incubated with BMECs-derived exosomes (5 × 10⁶ BMECs/300 µL) and the thymidine analog EdU in a proliferation medium for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained for EdU following the manufacturer’s instructions. DAPI was used to visualize nuclei. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified using fluorescence microscopy.

OPCs migration assay

The migration of OPCs was assessed using transwells. Both sides of the upper compartment membrane and the bottom surface of the lower compartment were pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (PDL). OPCs (1 × 10⁵ cells) were seeded into the top compartment of each insert with 200 µL of the neurobasal medium, while the bottom wells were filled with 500 µL of neurobasal medium. The medium was renewed every 3 days. Seven days later, exosomes derived from 1.25 × 10⁷ endothelial cells were added to the bottom wells. After 48 h, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The inserts were washed three times with PBS, air-dried, and cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed using a wet cotton swab. The remaining cells on the lower surface were stained with 1 mL of crystal violet solution, pre-filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and analyzed.

Immunostaining

Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. Cells were permeabilized and blocked simultaneously with a blocking solution (5% sheep serum containing 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies: anti-Olig2 (1:200, Abcam, ab109186), anti-CC1 (mature oligodendrocytes, 1:200, Abcam, ab16794), anti-MBP (1:200, Abcam, Ab7349), anti-NG2 (1:500, MilliporeSigma, AB5320) at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained DAPI (Life Technologies).

Brain tissues were harvested from mice anesthetized with 4% chloral hydrate via intraperitoneal injection. Mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were harvested, sequentially dehydrated in 15% and 20% sucrose overnight at 4 °C, washed in PBS, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned into 20 μm-thick slices. For myelin visualization, sections were stained with Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemistry, sections underwent antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer, permeabilization with 0.6% Triton X-100, and blocking in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100, 5% BSA, and 5% goat serum. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by PBS washes and incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution). Immunoreactivity was visualized using the avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (ABC) method (Vector Laboratories) followed by development with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were acquired using a light microscope (Olympus). For immunofluorescence on tissue sections, the procedure would follow a similar blocking and primary antibody incubation, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (as described previously), DAPI counterstaining, and mounting with fluorescent mounting medium. Primary antibody including: anti-CD31 (1:100, ab222783, Abcam), anti-Olig2 (1:200, ab109186, Abcam), anti-MBP (1:500, ab7349, Abcam), anti-NG2 (1:500, AB5320, MilliporeSigma), anti-GFAP (1:500, Abcam, ab4674), and anti-Iba1 (1:500, Abcam, ab178846).

Exosome lipid extraction and data processing

A mixture of lipid standards was prepared, and all lipid extraction reagents were pre-cooled on ice for 20 min. After purification, the exosome pellets were rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen. The exosomes were resuspended in an appropriate amount of double-distilled water, followed by the addition of the lipid standards mixture. Pre-cooled isopropanol (IPA) was then added, and the mixture was vortexed for 30 s to ensure thorough mixing.

The mixture underwent three cycles of freezing in liquid nitrogen and thawing at room temperature, with vortexing for 1 min after each cycle. Subsequently, the mixture was sonicated in an ice bath for 10 cycles (80 Hz for 30 s and 45 Hz for 30 s), with a 5-minute pause after every 5 cycles, followed by vortexing for 30 s. The mixture was left to stand on ice for 45 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. Then the supernatant was carefully transferred to a new EP tube. The remaining pellet was re-extracted with an appropriate amount of pre-cooled IPA, repeating the freezing, thawing, sonication, and centrifugation steps, with the standing time on ice reduced to 10 min. Supernatants from both extractions were combined, dried under a nitrogen stream at room temperature (approximately 1.5 h), and stored at -80 °C for further analysis.

Data acquisition and processing were conducted using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS). Chromatographic separation was achieved using BEH HILIC, Phenomenex C18, and Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 columns to generate untargeted lipidomics data. The content of various lipid molecules in exosomes was quantified using the internal standard method, calculated as follows:

Lipid content = Peak area of lipid molecule / Peak area of corresponding lipid standard × Concentration of standard.

Missing values were imputed using the random forest method, and the data were normalized by summation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess sample clustering, data quality, and outlier exclusion. Lipid classification was followed the LIPID MAPS Lipid Classification System.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R packages and GraphPad Prism software. Single-variable statistical tests were conducted at the lipid class and subclass levels using a moderated Student’s t-test, with p-values corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR). All the data from mice are represented as the mean ± SD and data from cells are represented as mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were applied, while comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Exosomes partially mediate the effects of endothelial cell-derived supernatants on OPCs maturation

To investigate the effects of exosomes derived from BMECs under hypertensive versus normotensive conditions on OPCs, we performed a set of parallel experiments using two types of exosomes: exosomes from AngII-treated BMECs (AngII-Exos) mimic hypertensive conditions, and untreated BMECs (BMEC-Exos) that mimic normotensive conditions. The conditioned media (CM) from primary BMECs with (AngII CM) or without (BMEC-CM) AngII treatment were collected. Exosomes were removed from the above CM by ultracentrifugation to harvest exosome-free conditioned media (EFCM). These CMs were added to OPCs, and the maturation of OPCs was evaluated after 24 h. Immunofluorescence analysis using CC1 as a marker for mature oligodendrocytes revealed that BMEC-CM significantly increased the proportion of CC1-positive mature oligodendrocytes. This effect was substantially diminished upon exosome depletion (BMEC-EFCM), demonstrating that BMEC-Exo promotes OPCs maturation. Notably, AngII treatment abrogated the pro-maturation effect of BMEC-CM on OPCs. Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of AngII-CM on OPCs maturation was attenuated upon exosome depletion (AngII-EFCM) (Fig. 1a, b). These results indicate that AngII-Exo may lose the ability to promote OPCs maturation.

AngII-Exo inhibits OPCs maturation and proliferation, while promoting migration

To further explore the function of AngII-Exo on OPCs, exosomes were purified and examined. Electron microscopy revealed a typical cup-shaped morphology and NTA confirmed a size distribution of around 100 nm. Neither morphology nor size was altered by AngII treatment (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). However, AngII treatment reduced exosome production, as evidenced by lower CD63 levels in the AngII-treated group, which was from equivalent numbers of endothelial cells as the control (Fig. 2a–c). ELISA analysis revealed no significant difference in Ang II levels between BMEC-Exo and AngII-Exo (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Purified exosomes were applied directly to OPCs for 48 h to test the earlier differentiation. Immunofluorescence staining was performed using NG2 to identify OPCs and MBP to detect mature oligodendrocytes. We observed that BMEC-Exo significantly reduced the proportion of NG2+ OPCs and significantly increased the proportion of MBP+ OPCs, indicating that BMEC-Exo promotes OPCs differentiation, consistent with the results observed with BMEC-CM. In contrast, AngII-Exo lost this function in promoting OPCs maturation (Fig. 2d–f). This phenomenon was further validated in the later stages of OPCs maturation by adding exosomes three days after initiating T3-induced differentiation. Similarly, we observed a significantly increased proportion of MBP+ mature OPCs and decreased NG2+ OPCs in the BMEC-Exo group (Fig. 2d, g, h), suggesting that BMEC-Exo promotes OPCs maturation during mid-to-late differentiation. In addition, AngII-Exo had no influence on OPCs maturation either (Fig. 2d–h).

In OPCs-mediated myelin regeneration, robust proliferation and migration of immature OPCs are critical processes[5]. Therefore, we assessed the effects of BMEC-Exo on OPCs proliferation and migration. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified as a measure of proliferation. We observed that AngII-Exo significantly reduced OPCs proliferation, while BMEC-Exo had no significant effect (Fig. 2i, j). Transwell assays were used to evaluate the migration of OPCs. Results indicated that AngII-Exo promoted OPCs migration, whereas BMEC-Exo had no significant effect (Fig. 2k, l). Altogether, these results indicate that AngII-Exo inhibits OPCs maturation and proliferation while promoting migration.

Establishment and evaluation of the LPC-induced demyelination model

The chemical demyelination mouse model was established by unilateral stereotactic injection of 2% LPC into the corpus callosum (Fig. 3a). Seven days post-injection, Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining revealed significant demyelination in the injection area, accompanied by a reduction in MBP immunostaining, showing demyelination within the lesioned region (Fig. 3b). TEM further demonstrated axonal damage alongside pronounced demyelination, characterized by thinned and irregular myelin sheaths (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, we observed infiltration of astrocytes and microglia, along with the decrease in MBP signal in the injured area (Fig. 3d). These pathological features are consistent with the typical characteristics of the model. In this model, we observed NG2-labeled OPCs clustered around blood vessels labeled with CD31-positive endothelial cells in the peri-lesional area (Fig. 3e). Higher-magnification views confirmed that immunopositive OPCs were also distributed in proximity to blood vessels (Fig. 3f), recapitulating the spatial association observed in human demyelinated tissue. This suggests a close relationship between endothelial cells and OPCs in demyelinating conditions.

BMEC-Exo promotes the maturation of OPCs in regions of myelin regeneration, while AngII-Exo loses this function

To investigate the role of BMEC-Exo in myelin regeneration in vivo, we injected exosomes from different treatment groups into the lateral ventricle seven days post-LPC-induced demyelination (Fig. 3a). The control group received PBS. To track exosome distribution in vivo, purified exosomes were labeled with PKH67. One day post-injection, exosomes started diffusing along the corpus callosum from the lateral ventricles. By four days post-injection, exosome labeling was clearly visible throughout the corpus callosum and within the lesion area (Fig. 4a). Given that day 10 post-demyelination is a critical time point for OPCs differentiation and early myelin regeneration in the LPC model, and that PKH67-labeled exosomes were enriched in the demyelinated corpus callosum by day 11 (4 days post-injection), we analyzed myelin regeneration at this time point. In the mice receiving BMEC-Exo, although the number of Oligo2+ mature OPCs in the lesion was not significantly altered, MBP fluorescence intensity was significantly increased, indicating increased maturation of OPCs. While no significant change was observed in the mice receiving AngII-Exo (Fig. 4b–d). Furthermore, BMEC-Exo significantly reduced the density of NG2+ OPCs within the lesion, indicating a decrease in local immature OPCs (Fig. 4e, f). Consistent with the in vitro observations, these results suggest that AngII-Exo failed to promote OPCs maturation, thereby effectively suppressing the BMEC-Exo-induced local myelin regeneration.

Altered lipid composition in endothelial exosomes with AngII intervention

Based on the above results, BMEC-Exo promotes OPCs’ maturation but does not influence their proliferation and migration. AngII-treatment reduced exosome release; these AngII-Exo lost the function to induce OPCs maturation, while promoting the proliferation and migration of OPCs. These results indicate that AngII-Exo has distinct characteristics that are different from BMEC-Exo. During the maturation of OPCs, lipid metabolism significantly influences key processes, including cellular migration [22] and the progression towards myelination [23]. Thus, we analyzed the lipid composition of BMEC-Exo and AngII-Exo. Lipidomic analysis identified 35 subclasses and 1292 molecular species, with glycerolipids (primarily triglycerides [TG], 548 species) and glycerophospholipids (primarily phosphatidylcholine [PC] and phosphatidylethanolamine [PE], 465 species) being the most abundant. The number and proportion of each lipid subclass are detailed in Additional file 1: Fi. S3. Furthermore, differences in the proportion of several subclasses were observed between BMEC-Exo and AngII-Exo. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed a clear separation between the two groups, suggesting distinct lipid profiles (Fig. 5a).

To identify specific lipids potentially involved in modulating OPCs, we analyzed the different lipid content between BMEC-Exo and AngII-Exo. Following false discovery rate (FDR) correction, the total content of phosphoglycerides (PG, corrected P = 0.04) and eicosanoids (corrected P < 0.0001) was significantly lower in AngII-Exo than in BMEC-Exo. We further revealed 17 differentially abundant lipids (corrected P < 0.05) between the two groups, primarily within the fatty acid and free fatty acid (FFA) subclasses (Table 1; Fig. 5b–c), comprising a total of 11 lipids. Notably, among these 17 differentially abundant lipids, only FFA (22:6) was increased in the AngII group, while the other 16 lipids were decreased. The decrease in these lipids in AngII-Exo may account for their reduced capacity to promote OPCs differentiation and mutation.

Discussion

High blood pressure is the most significant modifiable risk factor for WMH progression, with substantial clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of blood pressure management in attenuating WMH advancement [3, 24, 25]. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms encompass dual pathways: WMH stemming from small vessel atherosclerosis, and parenchymal deterioration resulting from blood-brain barrier (BBB) compromise [26–28]. Recent research has revolutionized our understanding of the BBB, revealing its role as a dynamic mediator of peripheral-central interactions. Endothelial cells, serving as fundamental BBB components, maintain brain homeostasis through complex bidirectional signaling mechanisms [8]. Our investigation highlights that AngII-Exo exhibits distinct variations in lipid content and function compared to BMEC-Exo. We found novel evidence that BMEC-Exo significantly enhances OPCs maturation and remyelination, underscoring their biological significance. However, this effect diminishes upon treatment with AngII-Exo. Instead, these exosomes induce OPCs recruitment while suppressing their proliferation. These findings illuminate the pivotal role of endothelial cells in OPCs-mediated myelin regeneration and demonstrate their capacity to dynamically modulate the brain microenvironment in response to systemic alterations.

Myelin regeneration, documented across both experimental models and clinical studies, proceeds through a precise cascade: OPCs proliferation, enhanced motility, targeted recruitment to demyelinated regions, and subsequent differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes capable of axonal ensheathment [29, 30]. In pathological models of myelin damage and repair, LPC-induced localized myelin loss provides clear time points for assessing demyelination and subsequent regeneration in vivo, offering high reproducibility and translational potential [31–33]. In our study, the LPC model demonstrated robust demyelination and axonal injury seven days post-intervention, validating the experimental paradigm. In recent years, increasing studies have demonstrated that the physical interaction of OPCs with blood vessels exerts vital function during remyelination. Following demyelination injury, OPCs were sequestered around vessels, where heightened and sustained adhesion appeared to trap these cells in a precursor state, ultimately inhibiting their differentiation and myelin regeneration [34, 35]. The observed perivascular aggregation of OPCs at injury sites corroborates the widespread vessel-OPCs association across diverse demyelination models [36]. In the LPC-induced localized demyelination model of the corpus callosum, OPCs migrated to the injury site, differentiated, and initiated early myelin regeneration by day 10 post-injury [22, 37]. We administered fluorescently labeled BMEC-Exo via lateral ventricle injection on day 7 post-injury and observed their distribution in vivo. Intriguingly, the BMEC-Exo not only reached the injured corpus callosum but also exhibited preferential accumulation along its structure. While the specificity of this distribution remains unclear. Further studies are required to confirm whether this preferential distribution is specific to endothelial cell-derived exosomes or is influenced by the method of lateral ventricle injection. The SVZ, a crucial region for OPCs genesis and storage, also showed BMEC-Exo enrichment, which implies the physiological relevance of OPCs and BMEC-Exo.

Consistent with the in vitro study, BMEC-Exo promoted OPCs maturation in the injured corpus callosum, as evidenced by the increased mature and reduced immature OPCs markers. In contrast, AngII-Exo exhibited no pro-maturation effects on OPCs. While T3-loaded neural stem cell exosomes have been reported to promote myelin regeneration in the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model, they require complex engineering for effective outcomes [38]. Our findings suggest that unmodified BMEC-Exo significantly promotes OPCs maturation, suggesting a potential role in myelin regeneration for demyelinating diseases. Furthermore, engineering BMEC-Exo, for example, by loading them with T3 or other myelin-promoting molecules, could enhance their regenerative capacity. Myelin sheaths formed by oligodendrocytes provide critical physical and metabolic support for neurons in the central nervous system, enabling rapid nerve impulse conduction along axons [39]. Lipids constitute 70% of the dry weight of myelin, with cholesterol and sphingolipids being the major components [40]. OPCs require the rapid acquisition of large amounts of lipids to generate the extensive membrane needed for myelin formation during remyelination. Compared to other membranes, myelin contains significantly higher proportions of long-chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, specifically oleic acid (18:1) and nervonic acid (24:1). These specific fatty acids are critical for the structural integrity of the myelin sheath [41, 42]. Interestingly, our study demonstrated significantly lower FFA (18:1), FFA (22:0), and FFA (24:0) and significantly higher FFA (22:6) in AngII-Exo compared to the BMEC-Exo. Proper myelin formation depends on endogenous fatty acid synthesis in oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system [43, 44]. These results suggest that BMEC-Exo may promote OPCs differentiation and maturation by supplying the fatty acid precursors necessary for myelin formation. Conversely, a deficit in essential fatty acids in AngII-Exo may impair their ability to support OPCs maturation and demyelination.

Moreover, two oxidized metabolites of linoleic acid, 9-HODE and 13-HODE, are endogenous activators of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) [45]. Activation of PPARγ has been shown to promote OPCs maturation in animal models of cerebral hemorrhage [46]. In our study, AngII-Exo exhibited reduced levels of 9-HODE, which may have diminished PPARγ activation, contributing to the observed suppression of OPCs maturation. Notably, sphingosine-1-phosphate, a lipid previously reported to influence OPCs migration, did not differ significantly between the two exosome groups. This suggests that the mechanism by which AngII-Exo promotes OPCs migration but inhibits proliferation may involve non-lipid components, such as proteins or nucleic acids. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis and to explore the role of newly identified differential lipids in OPCs migration and proliferation.

Ang II disrupts endothelial lipid homeostasis, which may consequently reshape the biogenesis and cargo of endothelial exosomes. Metabolomic profiling reveals that Ang II exposure significantly dysregulates pathways central to lipid handling, including glycerophospholipid metabolism and fatty acid biosynthesis [47]. This is mechanistically supported by its upregulation of key regulators such as LOX-1, which enhances the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) [48], and CD36, which facilitates fatty acid transport and exacerbates lipid peroxidation [49]. Given that exosomes are generated via the endosomal pathway, wherein intraluminal vesicles bud into multivesicular bodies (MVBs), such aberrant lipid metabolism likely alters the lipid composition of endosomal and MVB membranes, leading to the selective packaging of pathological lipid species into exosomes, thereby modifying their lipid cargo. Ang II directly stimulates extracellular vesicle release via Rho kinase-dependent blebbing from lipid rafts, yielding vesicles enriched in cholesterol-rich microdomain markers [50]. In line with this, our data show that Ang II significantly alters the levels of multiple free fatty acids and glycerophospholipids in endothelial exosomes. These findings provide a foundation for future studies to dissect the precise mechanisms by which Ang II on endothelial exosomal composition and function.

Ang II influences white matter integrity through multiple mechanisms. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis in a mouse model of AngII-induced hypertension demonstrated that Ang II disrupts endothelial transport, impairs oligodendrocyte differentiation, alters lipid metabolism in mature oligodendrocytes, and leads to deficits in axonal conduction [51]. Although the direct effects of Ang II on OPCs remain incompletely understood, Ang II is known to directly regulate multiple central nervous system cell types, including endothelial cells and glial cells, which in turn influence OPCs’ behavior through complex intercellular mechanisms [52–55]. Our study identifies a previously unrecognized regulatory mechanism of OPCs during remyelination, thereby advancing the understanding of OPCs regulation in myelin repair.

The concentration of Ang II (1 µg/mL) used in the present in vitro experiments is much higher than the dose used in patients. This concentration is employed to model endothelial AngII receptor activation, as the rapid degradation of Ang II in static culture systems necessitates a higher initial bolus to sustain effective receptor stimulation over the experimental period. Accordingly, this concentration should not be directly extrapolated to clinical settings. Future studies using animal models of chronic hypertension or analyzing endothelial-derived circulating exosomes from patient cohorts will be essential to validate the clinical relevance of these mechanistic observations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that endothelial cell-derived exosomes influence the process of OPCs regeneration to myelin. Under normal conditions, endothelial cell-derived exosomes promote OPCs differentiation and remyelination. However, under hypertensive conditions, this ability of endothelial cell-derived exosomes is lost, potentially due to the reduction in fatty acids that is essential for myelin synthesis and OPCs maturation, thereby impeding the remyelination process. This study reveals a novel role of endothelial cell-derived exosomes in remyelination, offering new insights underlying WMH.
