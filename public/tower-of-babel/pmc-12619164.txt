The association between weight control behaviors and health literacy among adolescents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maryam Heydari, Shahzad Pashaeypoor, Reza Negarandeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global rise in adolescent obesity underscores the need for effective weight control behaviors (WCBs) as an integral part of adolescents’ lifestyles. Health literacy (HL), widely recognized as a key determinant of health outcomes, is an important factor in supporting informed health decisions. Given the potential influence of HL on health behaviors, this study aims to examine the association between WCBs and HL among adolescents in Tehran, Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from September to November 2021 across ten health service centers in southern Tehran. Adolescents were recruited via multistage random sampling. Data were collected using validated self-administered questionnaires assessing demographics, WCBs (healthy, unhealthy, and very unhealthy), and HL using the Health Literacy in School-Age Children scale. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and behaviors. A generalized linear model was used to adjust for age and BMI. In all tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong the 240 adolescents included (mean age 14.25 ± 1.29 years; 48.8% female), 73.8% had a normal BMI, 7.1% were underweight, and 19.1% were overweight/obese. Nearly all (97.1%) reported at least one healthy WCB; 65.5% reported at least one unhealthy, and fewer than 5% reported very unhealthy behaviors. The mean HL score was 34.23 ± 4.48. Higher HL was associated with healthy WCBs, with odds increasing 3.4-fold from low to high HL (p < 0.001). No significant associations were detected between HL and unhealthy or very unhealthy WCBs.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the relevance of HL in promoting healthy WCBs among adolescents. The study highlights the potential value of developing strategies and programs to enhance HL, particularly for adolescents with higher BMI or those at risk of unhealthy behaviors, to support healthier lifestyle choices. Future longitudinal research is needed to further clarify the relationship between HL and WCBs over time.Keywords: Weight loss, Health literacy, Adolescents, Cross-sectional study, Iran

Background

Adolescent obesity is an acute global health crisis [1]. The prevalence of overweight or obesity among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years increased from 2% to 8% between 1990 and 2022—representing more than a fourfold increase [2]. This upward trend is also observed in Iran [3], where approximately 11.9% of children and adolescents are classified as obese, with a higher prevalence among boys (13.6%) than girls (10.2%) [4]. Adolescent obesity poses both short- and long-term health risks [5], including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and mental health disorders. Adolescence, a critical developmental stage marked by rapid physical, cognitive, and emotional changes, is pivotal for establishing lifelong health behaviors [6]. Unhealthy dietary patterns, inactivity, and maladaptive weight control strategies developed during this period may persist into adulthood [7], increasing obesity-related morbidity and mortality. Moreover, obesity in childhood and adolescence often tends to persist into adulthood [8], underscoring the urgency of early intervention. During adolescence, body dissatisfaction is common and can adversely affect overall health [9]. Obesity is also linked to psychological distress, including stress related to body image and weight management, which may influence adolescents’ weight control behaviors (WCBs) and hinder their ability to adopt or maintain a healthy lifestyle [10].

WCBs encompass a range of strategies aimed at managing body weight, from healthy approaches such as balanced diets and regular physical activity to harmful methods such as fasting and laxative use [11–14]. Neumark-Sztainer et al. (2012) classified WCBs into healthy, relatively unhealthy, and very unhealthy behaviors [15]. While healthy behaviors promote well-being, unhealthy ones—reported by more than one-third of adolescent girls—can result in serious physical, emotional, and social consequences [13, 16, 17]. A study in China found that 59.43% of children engaged in weight control efforts, with dietary improvement (45.11%) and increased physical activity (49.91%) being more common than dieting (7.19%) or weight loss medication use (2.28%) [18]. In Iran, limited research has been conducted on WCBs, and overall patterns remain poorly understood. For example, Aflakseir et al. reported that 56.9% of adolescents in Shiraz engaged in weight loss behaviors, with nearly half citing aesthetic rather than health-related motivations [19]. These behaviors are shaped by multiple cognitive and contextual factors [20], including health literacy (HL), which influences adolescents’ decision-making and risk perceptions.

HL, defined as the ability to access, interpret, and apply health information, is a key determinant of health outcomes [21, 22]. Strengthening HL during adolescence—when peer influence is strong and self-efficacy is developing—may support healthier choices and reduce engagement in risky behaviors [23]. Research suggests that adolescents with higher HL tend to experience better health outcomes and are more likely to engage in self-management and preventive behaviors [24]. Although most HL studies have focused on adults [25], emerging studies increasingly emphasize its importance in shaping adolescent health trajectories. For example, Loer et al. (2020) found that factors such as age, gender, education level, social support, and self-efficacy are associated with HL [26]. Similarly, Upton et al. (2025) conducted a systematic scoping review and reported that inadequate HL is associated with obesogenic behaviors, higher BMI, and lower levels of physical activity [27]. In Iran, Varmazyar et al. (2024) [28] reported that inadequate HL is related to overweight and obesity among female adolescents, largely due to poor nutritional literacy and unhealthy food choices.

Despite the growing recognition of HL as an important factor in adolescent health, its association with WCBs—especially unhealthy practices—remains underexplored. This gap is particularly notable in low- and middle-income countries such as Iran. Olyani and Peyman (2021) observed that Iranian adolescents with limited HL often face difficulties interpreting and applying health information, which may contribute to disparities in obesity-related outcomes [29]. The present study addresses this gap by examining the association between HL and WCBs among adolescents in southern Tehran. Understanding this relationship can help inform targeted interventions to promote healthier lifestyle choices and reduce obesity-related risks in this population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional descriptive-correlational study was conducted between September and November 2021 in comprehensive health service centers located in the southern districts of Tehran, Iran. This region was selected because it encompasses inhabitants from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and is served by our affiliated institution, Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Moreover, the South Tehran Health Service Centers cover a resident population of approximately 1,530,122 people, making it a suitable setting for the study. To ensure high-quality reporting of observational research, the study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [30].

Participants

The study population comprised adolescents aged 12–16 years who visited the selected health service centers during the study period. The inclusion criteria were as follows: being 12–16 years old, residing in the southern districts of Tehran, and willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included the presence of physical disabilities, underlying medical conditions associated with obesity (e.g., diabetes, hypo- or hyperthyroidism, or polycystic ovary syndrome), and the use of steroid medications.

Sampling procedure

A multistage random sampling method was used. First, comprehensive health service centers in southern Tehran were identified across five districts (10, 11, 16, 17, and 19). From each district, two centers were randomly selected using a computer-generated randomization process. A list of eligible adolescents was subsequently obtained from the Health Information System of these centers. Each adolescent was assigned a unique number, and a total of 249 participants were randomly selected using a random number table.

Measurement tools

Three validated instruments were used for data collection:

Demographic Questionnaire: The questionnaire consisted of two sections: (1) demographic characteristics (age, gender, educational level, and parental education and occupation) and (2) anthropometric characteristics (height and weight). The students’ height and weight were measured by the researcher, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). For descriptive analysis, BMI was categorized according to WHO recommendations as follows: <18.5, underweight; 18.5–24.9, normal weight; 25.0–29.9, overweight; and ≥ 30.0, obesity.

Weight Control Behaviors Questionnaire: Developed in Persian by Hoseini Esfidarjani et al. (2020) [31], this instrument consists of 15 items assessing WCBs across three categories: four healthy (reducing the consumption of high-fat foods, reducing sugar intake, increasing the consumption of fruits and vegetables, exercising and physical activity), seven relatively unhealthy (prolonged hunger, following low-calorie or very low-calorie diets, following diets promoted in the media, restricting to one or a few specific food groups, eliminating one or more food groups, skipping a main meal, using weight loss devices such as slimming belts and massagers) and four very unhealthy WCBs (use of weight- loss pills, laxatives, diuretics and intentional vomiting). The responses are binary (“yes” or “no”), and the number of participants’ WCBs is calculated separately for each category. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire were established in a previous study conducted by a member of the current research team. Validity was assessed using content validity methods, and reliability was evaluated through test-retest and internal consistency approaches [31]. As the study population in the previous research was identical to that of the current study, we relied on the previously established psychometric properties.

Health literacy in school-age children (HLSAC) questionnaire: This instrument, which is based on the conceptualization of HL by Paakkari and Paakkari (2016) [32], includes 10 items that assess five components: theoretical knowledge, practical knowledge, critical thinking, self-awareness, and citizenship. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale, resulting in a total score ranging from 10 to 40. Scores ranging from 10 to 25 indicate low HL, 26 to 35 indicate average HL, and 36 to 40 indicate high HL. The original questionnaire was in English; therefore, it was translated into Persian. Its validity was evaluated using qualitative content validity and face validity, whereas reliability was assessed through Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.798) for internal consistency and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.788). The full psychometric properties of this questionnaire have been published in a separate paper [33].

Data collection procedure

The researcher visited the selected health centers to obtain participants’ contact information through the Health Information System. Adolescents and their parents were contacted by phone, informed about the study objectives, and invited to participate. Upon consent, an appointment was scheduled with the adolescent and one parent. During the appointment, the participants received detailed information about the study. The questionnaires were self-administered by the adolescents in a quiet setting under the researcher’s supervision, to address any questions or concerns.

Sample size

The following formula was adopted to estimate the sample size:

The required sample size was initially estimated to detect a minimum correlation of 0.20 between HL and WCBs, with a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05) and 90% power. The correlation coefficient of r = 0.2 represents a small but meaningful effect size that the study aimed to detect between the two variables. After accounting for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, the final required sample size was set at 249 participants. This calculation was based on the use of Pearson or Spearman correlation tests, depending on the data distribution, as originally planned to examine the associations between variables. Although the statistical analysis was later changed to a generalized linear model (GLM) to control for up to ten covariates, the initial sample size calculation was deemed sufficient, as the required sample size for a GLM with this number of predictors is generally smaller [34].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25. Descriptive statistics—including frequency, percentage, median, mode, mean, standard deviation (SD) and interquartile range—were used to summarize participant characteristics. Accordingly, inferential analysis was performed using a generalized linear model (GLM) to control for confounding variables in the association between HL levels and the number of WCBs. A GLM was chosen because it does not require normally distributed residuals, making it robust in many practical situations. Potential confounding variables were determined based on prior knowledge, previous research, and the results of bivariate analyses. Accordingly, age and BMI were included in the model to isolate the association between the main variables by adjusting for their influence. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 249 adolescents assessed, 9 with incomplete questionnaires were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 240. The participants were aged 12 to 16 years, with a mean age of 14.25 ± 1.29 years. Among them, 117 (48.75%) were female, and 123 (51.25%) were male. In terms of educational level, 33 (13.75%) of the adolescents were in sixth grade, 31 (12.92%) were in eighth grade, 89 were in ninth grade (37.08%), 77 were in tenth grade (32.08%), and only 10 (4.17%) were in seventh grade. The mean BMI of the participants was 20.4 ± 3.5. Most participants (177; 73.8%) were normal weight, 17 (7.1%) were underweight, and 46 (19.1%) were classified as overweight or obese—32 (13.3%) overweight and 14 (5.8%) obese. Discussion

Most adolescents in this study reported engagement in several healthy WCBs, while fewer reported multiple unhealthy or very unhealthy behaviors. Only a small proportion indicated no healthy behaviors, and very few reported high levels of unhealthy or very unhealthy practices. This overall trend points toward healthier practices in this population.

These results differ from those of Hoseini et al. (2022) [31], who used a similar questionnaire but a different operational approach. Hoseini classified adolescents by the presence or absence of any behavior within each group (i.e., whether a participant reported at least one healthy, unhealthy, or very unhealthy WCB), whereas we counted the number of behaviors in each group per participant, which provides a more nuanced measure of WCBs. When we applied Hoseini’s presence/absence method to our data, 97.1% of the participants reported at least one healthy WCB, 65.6% at least one unhealthy WCB, and 4.3% at least one very unhealthy WCB. By contrast, Hosseini’s sample of older adolescent girls showed 17.5% healthy, 60.6% unhealthy, 15.8% very unhealthy, and 6.1% reporting no WCBs. Compared with their results, our participants displayed a greater prevalence of healthy behaviors and fewer very unhealthy ones. Differences in age, sex, and particularly the operational approach to WCBs likely explain most of the discrepancies between the studies. Furthermore, Abera et al. (2025) [16] reported a 38% prevalence of unhealthy WCBs among 526 female adolescents in Ethiopia, suggesting that a substantial proportion of adolescents engage in unhealthy behaviors. Their questionnaire included nine unhealthy WCBs, and adolescents reporting at least one were classified as engaging in unhealthy behaviors. In contrast, in the present study, 34.5% of the adolescents reported no unhealthy behaviors. This difference may stem from variations in study populations, such as inclusion of only girls, as well as cultural, socioeconomic contexts, or differences in how unhealthy WCBs were defined by different questionnaires. Park et al. (2024) [35] used the Jung Hui-seop’s WCBs scale [36], which consists of four areas: exercise, dietary, drug, and behavioral therapy. The total score ranged from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating greater engagement in WCBs. The participants’ average score was 42.20 ± 8.27, reflecting moderate overall engagement. While both Park’s study and the present study measured WCBs, the methods and reporting differ. Nevertheless, both studies show that adolescents engage in some WCBs, while still leaving room for improvement.

A U.S.-based study conducted in California in 2014 [37] reported that 29% of 404 adolescents engaged in at least one unhealthy method, with very few reporting more than three. By comparison, 65.5% of our participants reported at least one unhealthy behavior. This difference may reflect variations in classification systems: the U.S. study combined unhealthy and very unhealthy behaviors, whereas our study treated them as separate categories, which likely contributed to the observed variation. Further research is needed to clarify these discrepancies and develop culturally appropriate interventions to promote healthy WCBs in adolescents.

In terms of HL, the mean HL score was 34.23 ± 4.48. Over half of our participants demonstrated moderate HL, whereas 42.1% had high HL, and only 2.9% had low HL. These findings contrast with those of Panahi (2017) [38], who reported that 74.5% of adolescents aged 15–18 years had limited HL. Similarly, Caldwell (2020) [39] reported a mean HL score of 3.78, suggesting possible limitations in HL among adolescents. The observed differences may be due to various factors, including the use of different instruments, unique sample characteristics, and cultural or socioeconomic contexts.

For example, Paakkari (2018) [40] employed the same instrument as the present study (HLSAC) in a large sample of 3,833 adolescents (aged 13–15), finding that 10% had low HL, 57% had moderate HL, and 33% had high HL—closely mirroring our results. MOHaney (2020) [41] reported a mean score of 33.67 ± 4.62, with 41.2% classified as having high HL through the HLSAC tool. Chu-Ko (2021) [42] used the Chinese HL Survey Questionnaire in Taiwan and found that approximately 30% of 918 junior college students had insufficient or problematic HL, with a mean score of 36.15 ± 6.21 Similarly, You and Ahn (2025) [43] measured a mean HL score of 12.54 ± 2.57 out of 16 among 149 middle school students These comparisons highlight that our study’s HL results are generally consistent with prior research using the same instrument, whereas variations across studies reflect differences in sample characteristics and context.

Despite variations in reported HL levels across studies, a consistent finding is that HL is associated with adolescent health behaviors. HL has emerged as a significant predictor of health-promoting behaviors. Adolescents with adequate HL are more likely to engage in healthy behaviors [43], as they better understand and apply health information in daily life [44]. In the present study, a significant association was observed between HL and healthy WCBs. However, no significant associations were found between HL and unhealthy or very unhealthy WCBs. This lack of association might be explained by the low prevalence of unhealthy and very unhealthy behaviors in the sample. The finding that most adolescents had a normal BMI could also play a role. As one of the first studies to examine HL and WCBs in adolescents, direct comparisons with previous research are limited. However, findings from adult populations provide additional perspective. For instance, one study among urban Iranian adults reported a significant association between HL and a range of health-promoting behaviors, with HL emerging as an important factor linked to healthier lifestyle practices [45]. While our study identified this relationship primarily in relation to healthy WCBs among adolescents, the findings from adults highlight the broader relevance of HL as a determinant connected to positive health behaviors. Considering both adolescent and adult evidence, it becomes clear that strengthening HL may be an important element in supporting healthier choices throughout the life course. These findings emphasize the importance of examining WCBs as part of adolescent lifestyles, particularly in relation to obesity prevention.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is among the few investigations to examine the association between HL and WCBs, addressing a literature gap. Second, the use of a validated instrument for measuring HL (HLSAC questionnaire) enhances the reliability. Third, detailed WCBs classification allowed a nuanced understanding of adolescent behaviors. Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference; future longitudinal or cohort studies are needed to provide deeper insights. Additionally, responses might be influenced by adolescents’ mental or emotional states during data collection which is beyond the control of researchers. Furthermore, the sampling strategy, restricted to comprehensive health centers in southern Tehran, limits generalizability; these findings may not be representative of all adolescents in Tehran, and the results should be interpreted with caution when comparing them to the broader adolescent population of the city. Numerous variables can influence WCBs. Many of these factors were unmeasurable or could not be included in our analysis, although we attempted to account for the most notable confounding variables, such as age and BMI, in the regression models. Socioeconomic status and specific cultural attitudes toward weight and body image, among others, may confound the observed associations.

Conclusions

In the present study, no significant association was found between HL and engagement in unhealthy or very unhealthy WCBs. However, higher levels of HL were associated with greater engagement in healthy WCBs. These results indicate a relationship between HL and healthy WCBs, but due to the cross-sectional design, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. These findings highlight the potential value of supporting adolescents’ HL to encourage healthier behavioral choices. Health policymakers may consider implementing educational programs to improve HL. Future research could explore specific strategies, such as school-based interventions incorporating nutrition literacy, physical activity guidance, and behavioral skills. Since this study aimed to examine adolescents in the southern districts of Tehran, the sample included all BMI levels. Future studies should focus on adolescents with higher BMI, lower body satisfaction, or those at risk of unhealthy behaviors, employing longitudinal designs to better understand how HL and WCBs are associated over time.
