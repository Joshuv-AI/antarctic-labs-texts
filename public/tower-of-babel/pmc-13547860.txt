Underfunded and undervalued: community-based health promotion practitioners’ experiences of Western Australia’s prevention sector
Authors: Ruth Wallace, Leesa Costello, Elizabeth J Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevention is a central policy focus of contemporary health promotion, offering cost-effective strategies to reduce disease burden and improve population well-being. Despite this, Australia allocates a small proportion of its health budget to prevention, with funding models characterized by short-term cycles and competitive tendering. While the consequences for public health are well documented, the impact on the health promotion workforce remains underexplored. Workforce health and stability are critical for program delivery, ethical practice, continuity of relationships with communities, and the capacity to sustain long-term prevention efforts. This qualitative study examined the experience of 19 community-based health promotion practitioners in Western Australia (WA), working primarily in nongovernment, local government, and private prevention settings. In-depth interviews, analyzed using codebook thematic analysis, identified four interconnected themes: persistent funding shortages and inter-agency competition; short-term, cyclical funding and administrative burden; perceived prioritization of treatment over prevention; and consequences for workforce well-being, program continuity, and ethical decision-making. Findings reveal that funding instability undermines program sustainability and creates job insecurity, stress, and attrition, while also generating ethical dilemmas and a paradox in which practitioners tasked with promoting health work under conditions that erode their own health, well-being, and professional values. This study contributes qualitative insight into how funding structures are experienced and negotiated by community-based prevention practitioners. It has the potential to inform policy and funding reforms to strengthen and support a more sustainable health promotion workforce.Keywords: health promotion, preventative health, workforce sustainability, health system funding, qualitative research, Australia

Background

Australia’s health promotion efforts are increasingly shaped by rising rates of noncommunicable disease (NCD) and associated risk factors, highlighting the need for sustained investment in upstream prevention. Community-based health promotion offers compelling value for money, with reported median returns on investment far exceeding costs (Masters et al. 2017). Building and sustaining a skilled health promotion workforce is therefore essential (Battel-Kirk et al. 2011), given the central role of prevention in addressing the social determinants of health (Zaid and Liamputtong 2025). Despite this evidence, funding for prevention remains fragmented and short-term, constraining program continuity and limiting impact (Shiell et al. 2024). Australian studies have long shown that episodic and project-based funding arrangements constrain the development and sustainability of the health promotion workforce, with implications for long-term preventative practice (Shilton et al. 2001, Smith et al. 2016).

It is important to position the intent of this study alongside the complex nature of the Australian public health system at the start of this paper. Baum (2015, p. 530) notes that the boundaries between definitions of public health and health promotion are “neither fixed nor constant.” Indeed, the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion is considered the “foundation of the ‘new’ public health” (p. 38), which emerged in the 1980s. Health promotion, closely aligned with primary prevention, addresses the social and environmental conditions that shape health. It is both a values-driven discipline with its own theoretical, ethical, and practice traditions (Potvin and Jourdan 2021) and a central, upstream strategy within the broader public health sector (Biehl et al. 2026). Given this conceptual and practice-based overlap, the terms public health, health promotion, and prevention are used relatively interchangeably in this paper, reflecting their shared values-driven and upstream orientation (Baum 2015, Potvin and Jourdan 2021). Prevention is treated as a related but distinct construct spanning primary prevention (intervening before disease or injury occurs), secondary prevention or early intervention (addressing problems at an early stage), and tertiary prevention or treatment (managing established disease) (Department of Health 2021). This distinction is analytically important: our findings demonstrate, particularly in Theme 3, that practitioners working at different levels of prevention face markedly different challenges in securing funding, legitimacy, and resources.

Framing health promotion as values-driven sits in tension with the dominant policy discourse of “prevention,” which is frequently articulated with language around risk, burden, and behavior change. Critical health promotion scholarship has cautioned that such framings can reproduce individualizing and biomedical logics that the Ottawa Charter’s vision of upstream, structural action sought to move beyond (O’Hara and Taylor 2022), particularly where prevention discourse intersects with risk surveillance (Taylor et al. 2014) or privileges behavior change over empowerment-oriented approaches (Fry 2020). We use “burden,” “risk,” and “prevention” in places throughout this paper to reflect the language of the funding and policy environment in which our participants work, rather than as an endorsement of these framings.

Recent developments in Australia’s public health landscape include the establishment of an Australian Centre for Disease Control and the appointment of WA’s first Minister for Preventative Health (Australian Centre for Disease Control 2025, Public Health Association Australia 2025). These initiatives signal a growing recognition that chronic disease prevention improves individual health and strengthens communities—by supporting participation in education, employment, and civic life (Department of Health 2021, Nutbeam and Muscat 2021). Prevention also reduces pressure on health and social care systems and delivers substantial economic benefits (Shiell et al. 2024, The Australian Prevention Partnership Centre 2025).

Chronic NCDs are the leading cause of death in Australia, accounting for 90% of all deaths and 85% of years lost to ill-health or premature mortality (Dickson et al. 2023, Islam et al. 2023). Individuals living with chronic conditions often experience pain, disability, and psychological distress and are less likely to enter or remain in the workforce (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025a). The economic burden is substantial, with $42 billion attributed to avoidable risk factors in 2018–19 alone (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025b). Prioritizing NCD prevention is, therefore, both a public health and economic imperative.

Many risk factors—such as poor diet, physical inactivity, and alcohol and tobacco use—are shaped by broader social determinants of health, including environments that do not support health and well-being (The Sax Institute 2025). Even modest reductions in these risk factors can markedly reduce disease burden (Howse et al. 2021). The principle that prevention outweighs cure remains supported by evidence consistently demonstrating that multi-sectoral, multifaceted strategies are both cost-saving and effective (Merkur et al. 2013); although what counts as effective prevention, structural or individualized, remains contested. Across high-income countries, a systematic review found a median return of AUD $14.30 for every AUD $1 invested in public health interventions—effectively a 14-fold return (Masters et al. 2017).

Australia’s National Preventive Health Strategy (Department of Health 2021) positions prevention as the cornerstone of public health, aiming to reduce disease burden, address health inequalities, and prepare for emerging threats. It sets an ambitious target: “Investment in preventive health will rise to be 5% of total health expenditure across Commonwealth, state and territory governments by 2030” (p. 9). However, progress remains slow. In December 2024, the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (2024) reported that only 2.3% of Australia’s total health expenditure for 2022–23 was allocated to public health (excluding COVID-19-related spending), placing Australia mid-table among OECD countries (OECD data cited in Shiell et al. 2024) and well short of the 5% target.

This national picture is mirrored at the state level. In WA, where this study was conducted, ∼75% of the 2025–26 state health budget was allocated to hospital services, whereas only 9.7% went to secondary and tertiary prevention, which includes early detection, intervention, treatment, and rehabilitation (Government of Western Australia 2025a, 2025b). In stark contrast, only 0.3% was directed toward primary prevention initiatives aimed at stopping disease or injury before it occurs (Government of Western Australia 2025a).

Practitioners in the health promotion sector often work outside the clinical system—in schools, communities, and nongovernment organizations (Nutbeam and Muscat 2021)—and rely on short-term, external funding that is widely acknowledged as “inadequate, siloed, sporadic, short-term, and piecemeal” (Musolino et al. 2022, Shiell et al. 2024, p. 2, Slevin and Coombe 2024). Unlike the medical profession, their work is preventative by design yet often undervalued in funding and policy decisions—a concern echoed in recent position statements from the Council of Academic Public Health Institutions Australia (CAPHIA 2025a, 2025b), which emphasize the foundational role of public health (including health promotion) and the urgent need for sustained investment in workforce development and education. This persistent undervaluing undermines the Australian Health Promotion Association and Public Health Association of Australia’s (2018) assertion that a “well trained and resourced health promotion and prevention workforce is essential” (p. 5).

Funding constraints have direct implications for the health promotion workforce, contributing to recruitment challenges and difficulties retaining experienced staff in short-term roles due to perceived job insecurity (Department of Health 2014). Despite widespread recognition of the importance of workforce stability for preventive systems, there remains limited empirical research focused specifically on employment conditions within the Australian health promotion workforce. Job insecurity is a psychosocial hazard that can negatively affect psychological well-being, potentially contributing to anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and sleep disorders (Safe Work Australia n.d., Stratton et al. 2021, Pires 2025)—an ironic consequence given the sector’s mission to promote and protect population health. The prevalence of unstable contracts may also drive skilled professionals away from the field, as seen in other sectors (Science and Technology Australia 2022, Tan et al. 2025).

While the consequences of underfunding for preventative health are internationally acknowledged, its impact on the health and well-being of staff employed in the preventative health/health promotion workforce, including in Australia, remains underexplored. Hence, this qualitative study aimed to explore the experiences of WA community-based health promotion practitioners working in the prevention sector.

Method

Study design

This study employed a qualitative interview-based research design to explore the experiences of WA community-based health promotion practitioners, with a focus on funding environments, workforce conditions, and systemic barriers to effective practice. A secondary aim reported separately (Cook et al. 2024) examined practitioner insights into the skills and competencies required by future public health graduates in a post-COVID-19 context.

The study was initially conceptualized as a focused ethnography; however, early field engagement indicated this approach was impractical, as many participants were extremely time-constrained and face-to-face engagement was perceived as burdensome. Although the intent of a focused ethnography is for the researcher to spend only a short time in the field (Knoblauch 2005, Kelly 2022), even asking for an hour or two felt like an imposition. Hence, participants were invited to participate in a one-off face-to-face or online interview via Microsoft Teams instead of the ethnographic engagements initially planned. This shift reflects the realities of conducting qualitative research in a busy, under-resourced sector and highlights the importance of methodological flexibility. We note that our inability to conduct the originally planned focused ethnography was itself a product of the precarious, time-poor conditions that formed the focus of this study. In this sense, the methodological pivot reflects an early manifestation of the very conditions we sought to examine.

Statement of ethics approval

This study received full ethical approval from Edith Cowan University’s Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC no. 2023-04760-WALLACE). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Researcher positionality

The research team included academics with professional experience in public health and health promotion, including practice-based experience within the sector. This positionality facilitated access and contextual understanding but also required careful attention to power dynamics and assumptions. It also informed the study’s critical realist stance (discussed later), whereby the researchers recognize underlying structural realities, such as workforce shortages, funding insecurity, and policy constraints. Reflexive practices, peer debriefing and participant review of transcripts, and the manuscript itself were used to support analytical transparency as well as reflexivity and ethical conduct.

Participant recruitment and eligibility

Community-based health promotion practitioners were recruited through the lead researcher’s professional networks. Eligibility was determined based on participants’ primary role involving the design, delivery, or coordination of population-level prevention or health promotion activities, rather than clinical service delivery. Eligible participants were employed in not-for-profit, nongovernment organizations, private enterprises, or Local Government Authorities where prevention was a core function of their role.

Unfortunately, it was not feasible to recruit participants from state or federal government departments due to time constraints and complex research governance processes. That is, the multiple layers of oversight, approval, and risk management required to ensure the research aligned with public sector responsibilities, in addition to university ethics approval (Kolstoe and Pugh 2024), made recruitment impracticable within the study timeframe. The absence of state and federal government employees as participants meant the sample was weighted toward community-based and contract-funded roles—a factor considered in the interpretation of the findings.

Potential participants received an email invitation and a participant information letter outlining the study’s purpose and procedures.

Data collection and analysis

In-depth interviews ranged from ∼45 to 90 minutes in duration and were conducted between February and March 2024. At the beginning of the face-to-face interviews, participants completed a brief demographic survey on paper. Online interviewees were emailed the same survey. The lead researcher used a semi-structured guide (Supplementary File) that explored participants’ journey from graduation into their current role, the nature of their current role and associated responsibilities, and their experiences in the sector, including challenges faced and views on the future of prevention. Additional questions (reported separately) examined participants’ thoughts on the employability skills required by graduates, advice for students on building those employability skills, and the intersection between practice and academia. The analysis reported here draws on interview questions focused on funding environments, organizational context, workforce experiences, and perceived impacts on practice and well-being.

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed using Microsoft Teams, with verbal consent obtained at the start of each session. Interviews continued until analytic sufficiency was reached, whereby no substantively new patterns relevant to the study aims were identified across successive interviews (Lim 2026).

Following Braun and Clarke’s (2021) call for researchers to locate their epistemological position explicitly, we approached the data from a critical realist standpoint, treating participants’ accounts as meaningful reports of their experiences within a real, structurally constrained system. While Braun and Clarke are most closely associated with reflexive thematic analysis, they recognize that thematic analysis encompasses a family of approaches. In this study, a codebook approach was adopted as a pragmatic strategy, appropriate for a multi-researcher team seeking systematic coding while retaining reflexive engagement with the data.

Transcripts were first cleaned and uploaded to NVivo14 for analysis. Wallace and Cook inductively developed an initial codebook from a subset of transcripts informed by the study aims. Coding proceeded iteratively, with regular discussion to refine code definitions and explore alternative interpretations. NVivo14 facilitated data organization, comparison across transcripts, and the examination of patterns within the dataset. This enabled an ongoing refinement of the codebook, culminating in several distinct but interconnected themes.

Participant identities were anonymized using pseudonyms, and complete anonymity was prioritized given the small population from which the sample was drawn (as per Heaton 2022). Member checking occurred at two stages: first, each participant reviewed their transcript to confirm its accuracy; second, they reviewed the drafted manuscript to confirm the accuracy of the researchers’ interpretation of their experiences and to ensure they were comfortable that their identities were protected. Participants were, at this second stage, invited to choose their pseudonym for this paper. This practice aims to enhance participant agency and authenticity in the research process as it acknowledges that naming is an ethical and relational act, which shapes identity and power (Itzik and Walsh 2023, Pretorius and Patel 2025).

Results

Nineteen community-based practitioners working in preventative health roles across WA participated. Most identified as female (n = 14) and lived and worked in metropolitan Perth (n = 14). Fourteen worked in nongovernment (NGO) or not-for-profit (NFP) organizations (largely charities reliant on mixed grant, donation, membership, and fee-for-service income), three worked in local government (LGO), and two in private enterprise. All but three held tertiary qualifications, with five trained outside public health (e.g. journalism, business, education, art therapy, and law). Participants represented a range of career stages, from early-career to senior/organizational management. Their roles spanned program delivery, coordination, and leadership.

The analysis of interview data regarding barriers to effective practice identified four interconnected themes that together illustrate a central paradox. That is, practitioners tasked with promoting population health are required to do so within conditions that undermine their own well-being, ethics, and capacity for long-term change.

Theme 1: scarcity as a structuring force that fragments collaboration

Across NGO, NFP, and private enterprises, participants identified funding as the most significant constraint influencing their capacity to deliver health promotion. Jess described organizations as “hugely understaffed” relative to the “scope of work [we] have to do,” with funding “probably the biggest challenge.” Others conveyed the severity of this constraint more succinctly: “We operate on the smell of an oily rag” (Alex); “It’s a big, wicked challenge” (Nina).

Scarcity was closely tied to workforce limitations and was widely understood as influencing how organizations functioned and interacted. Irene noted that “a lack of funding […] ties into the diminishing workforce as well,” while Eric characterized competition for limited resources as “ruthless,” with agencies “fighting for the same piece of the funding pie.” Although collaboration was consistently seen as desirable, participants emphasized that competitive tendering often undermined it in practice. As Alex reflected, “Community alliances [were a] great idea in theory,” but Ella explained, “you’re both going to compete [for] the same thing and then you’ve got a massive conflict of interest.” Doris highlighted how smaller, community-based organizations are disproportionately disadvantaged despite their local knowledge and responsiveness, observing that “you’re constantly competing with the really big organizations [with] infrastructure [and] greater outreach […] We still really need smaller organizations who […] know the local area really well.”

Participants also linked funding scarcity to political priorities and short policy cycles. Charlie explained that “if something is hot on the political agenda [funding] flows […] and staff [are] available,” but noted that this attention was often fleeting, marked by “ebbs and flows”. As Alex elaborated, politicians were seen to prioritize outcomes that could be quantified, such as “how many doctors they’ve put on […] or how many beds,” rather than supporting “long term sustainable change [which is impossible to show short-term].”

This theme demonstrates that practitioners perceived funding scarcity as influencing not only their organizations’ work and priorities but also the capacity for collective action in a sector where agencies were often competing for the same limited funding. Participants indicated that smaller organizations were disproportionately affected under these conditions.

Theme 2: living in short horizons: how funding cycles collapse long-term thinking

Participants consistently described short-term grants and cyclical contracting as incompatible with strategic planning, sustainability, and rigorous evaluation. Many reported operating with persistent uncertainty about the future of their roles and programs. As Francis explained, “we get a specific short-term fund [and] don’t even know if it will be [there] after six months.” This lack of security constrained program design and delivery, with Phoenix noting “we can’t think long-term […] maybe at most six months.” Evaluation was repeatedly characterized as “expensive” (Molly) and difficult to resource within small, time-limited grants, despite being widely recognized as essential to demonstrating impact.

Where longer contracts, such as 3- or 5-year agreements, were secured, they were described as a “luxury” (Elizabeth) yet still associated with substantial administrative burden. Participants highlighted the cumulative workload of frequent reporting to multiple funders—half-yearly, annual, and end-of-contract—often undertaken alongside the preparation of new grant applications. Ella described the strain of constantly writing grant applications “that may or may not be successful,” reflecting that “if you don’t get that money […] then what do you do next?” Procurement processes were similarly experienced as restrictive, particularly in behavior change programs, where time-intensive compliance requirements were described as “limiting and stifling” (Eric).

Short-term funding cycles were also linked to constrained innovation and reduced flexibility in daily practice. Participants reported that funding requirements often limited their ability to trial new approaches or respond flexibly to local needs. For example, several practitioners felt that funder specifications narrowed the scope of what could be attempted, even when alternative approaches were perceived as more appropriate or effective. As Phoenix explained, “we have to stick with what they want […] they are not open to […] new things that we think would be great to do.” Others described being forced to modify delivery models to fit tight timelines, despite recognizing that meaningful behavior change required sustained engagement over longer periods.

This theme demonstrates that practitioners perceived short-term funding cycles as limiting their ability to plan, evaluate, and sustain health promotion programs. Participants described ongoing uncertainty, administrative burden, and constrained opportunities for innovation under these funding conditions.

Theme 3: doing prevention in a system that rewards cure

Participants consistently described a perceived imbalance in health system priorities and resourcing, with a strong emphasis on biomedical and treatment-focused services at the expense of upstream prevention. Sarah-Jane framed this disparity starkly: “Billions of dollars spent on treatment every year. Bugger all on prevention.” Several participants contrasted the relative security of treatment-oriented services with the precarious positioning of health promotion work. Phoenix, for example, noted that, within their organization, “core” medical services received ongoing funding, while health promotion projects attracted sporadic, short-term support, “sometimes for as short as three months.”

This imbalance was widely experienced as a missed opportunity for meaningful prevention. Participants distinguished (primary) prevention from early intervention and treatment (i.e. secondary prevention), emphasizing that upstream work addressing social determinants was most vulnerable to marginalization within existing funding logics. Jess reflected that “there’s definitely a lot more […] funding put into treatment […] than prevention,” which she felt was “too late” given the long-term nature of behavior change and the social determinants shaping health outcomes. Participants suggested that this funding logic constrained their ability to intervene early, privileging responses after problems had escalated to clinical or crisis points.

Practitioners working in alcohol and other drugs services highlighted this tension particularly clearly. Francis described being “too busy at the tertiary end” to adequately resource prevention, noting the difficulty of “not sacrificing the tertiary end but doing more in the prevention space.” Others pointed to the mismatch between the complexity of the problems they sought to address and the simplicity of the program logics imposed through funding arrangements. As Alex explained, “we deal with complex social policy problems [that] need complex policy solutions. Except we don’t – we always seem to devolve back to really simple program logic and hope that it works.” Several participants framed these challenges as “wicked problems” (Molly), requiring sustained, coordinated, and upstream action rather than short-term, measurable outputs.

This theme demonstrates that practitioners perceived prevention as receiving less funding and priority than treatment-focused services. Participants described this imbalance as limiting opportunities for upstream prevention and reducing the capacity of organizations to address the broader determinants of health.

Theme 4: promoting health while absorbing harm: the paradox of prevention work

Funding instability was experienced most acutely through job insecurity, attrition, and stress. Short-term contracts were described as the norm, with the workforce “always under threat” and staff “shed wherever […] there’s a budget shortfall” (Irma). Participants repeatedly emphasized the difficulty of retaining skilled staff when funding lapsed. This persistent uncertainty shaped both individual career decisions and organizational capacity to plan for continuity. Doris asked, “How are you supposed to hold onto staff?”, noting that “skills and experience [are] flying off elsewhere” to more stable roles, often outside the prevention sector.

Underemployment further intensified these pressures. Several participants noted their organizations were only able to offer part-time roles due to funding constraints, despite staff needing full-time work. Doris explained that “they’re needing full-time work, which we’ve not been able to offer,” while those who remained experienced escalating workloads. In some cases, practitioners were “working part-time hours and trying to manage an entire organization” (Doris), compounding stress and increasing the risk of burnout.

Funding instability also disrupted service continuity, with direct consequences for communities. When grants were not renewed, participants described how their organization capacity diminished, which in turn curtailed programs and limited access to support. Jess explained that limited funding “reduced capacity considerably” for education initiatives, leaving communities “affected and suffer[ing]” (Doris). Others highlighted the challenges of navigating referral pathways in already stretched systems. As Rose described, “I just can’t get them… the help that they need,” even when referrals were made to specialist services, which also had long wait lists. Managers, such as Ella, described absorbing additional operational responsibilities to maintain services while simultaneously seeking alternative funding.

The personal and emotional toll of these conditions was evident across accounts. Participants spoke of the distress associated with caring for staff and communities while lacking the structural means to offer security or continuity. “I’m someone who cares for my staff,” Eric explained; they’re like “family,” and being unable to extend contracts or sustain programs “really keeps me awake and [is] quite frustrating too.” The invocation of “family” here reflects an organizational culture of care and relational responsibility, intensifying moral strain when managers are unable to protect staff from insecurity or loss. For example, participants described the emotional impact of losing staff who did not want to leave but felt compelled to seek stability elsewhere. Doris reflected there was “a lot of emotion because they’re not wanting to go,” while Alex described his experience as “awful” when attempts to reshuffle roles to retain staff ultimately failed.

Anticipation of contract nonrenewal further shaped workforce dynamics. Some staff saw “the writing [..] on the wall” and proactively sought other employment before funding ended. Jess recounted supporting staff through this process, noting that while it was “really good” they secured alternative employment, it left organizations grappling with the practical challenge of filling positions for only a few remaining months. “We had four months to the end of a contract to finish,” they explained, “how were we going to employ people for such a short amount of time?” These accounts reflect a form of moral strain, as practitioners carried responsibility for staff and community well-being without the structural capacity to act in alignment with their values and concerns.

Participants described modifying programs and seeking alternative funding sources to maintain services. Short-term funding cycles were seen to constrain longer-term planning and program development. As Francis explained, “sometimes it prevents us from working better and thinking better [because, with] a specific short-term fund, […] we don’t know [how long we’ll be] there.” At the same time, funding constraints prompted participants to seek ways of sustaining programs, including adopting lower-cost delivery approaches and diversifying revenue through partnerships. For example, Jess described efforts to combine grant funding with partnerships with “corporates [and] mining companies,” while others emphasized the ethical considerations involved in such decisions. Ella explained that alignment with organizational values was critical, noting, “You can’t go to a business and organizations that don’t align with your organization [for example,] we wouldn’t go to Coco-Cola and say let’s do a healthy eating campaign.”

This theme demonstrates that practitioners perceived funding instability as affecting workforce well-being, staff retention, and service continuity. Participants also described making changes to sustain programs and seeking alternative funding sources, which sometimes raised ethical concerns relating to organizational values and partnerships.

Discussion

The findings presented above illustrate how chronic underfunding and short-term funding arrangements influence the everyday realities of health promotion practice in WA. Considered together, the four themes clarify the central paradox: a sector mandated to enhance population health operates under conditions that generate stress, insecurity, and attrition within its own workforce.

This discussion situates these findings within the broader public health, preventative health, and health promotion literature, examining how funding structures, political priorities, and system design contribute to this paradox and considering the implications for workforce sustainability, ethical practice, and policy reform.

Interpreting the findings in context

The findings suggest that short-term, cyclical funding undermines strategic planning, innovation, and rigorous evaluation, limiting organizations’ capacity to achieve sustained population-level health outcomes. Evidence from the Australian Prevention Partnership Centre indicates that such funding mechanisms weaken outcomes and limit the ability to accumulate learning over time (Mastersson and Ananthapavn 2025). Participants’ concerns about a systemic tilt toward treatment rather than upstream prevention echo wider observations that governments struggle to invest in the “unseen” enabling work of prevention—community development, empowerment, and systems reform—that spans portfolios beyond health (Shiell et al. 2024). Internationally, UK analyses show that a preventative approach across public services improves healthy life expectancy (Hoddinott et al. 2024), reinforcing the need for a health-in-all-policies lens to avoid policy contradictions, such as planning decisions that promote unhealthy environments. Leading public health advocates have called for a shift away from hospital-centric models toward social supports and community-led programs (Brennan 2025). Yet, political cycles remain short, and the benefits of prevention often take decades to materialize, making such investments appear politically risky (Hoddinott et al. 2024). Underfunding persists in part because prevention delivers benefits that are delayed and diffuse, while political and budgetary incentives reward visible, short-term treatment outputs that can be claimed within electoral cycles. Legislative safeguards to protect the promised 5% spend on prevention have been proposed to prevent reversals under future governments (Australian Medical Association 2023). The new Australian Centre for Disease Control has signalled prevention of NCDs as a key function, which may help consolidate a more coherent and long-range approach to prevention governance in Australia (Australian Centre for Disease Control 2023). However, public health is inherently global in nature: the social, economic, environmental, and commercial determinants of health transcend national boundaries, meaning that isolated national efforts are constrained unless embedded within coordinated, international approaches to prevention and system reform (Thomas et al. 2025). As articulated in the Ottawa Charter, sustained population-level gains depend on local investment as well as collective global action to create supportive environments for health (World Health Organization 1986). It is worth returning here to the terminological choice flagged in the Introduction: while public health, health promotion, and prevention were treated as closely related at the level of framing this study, the findings show these are not equivalent in practice. Participants’ accounts of the disparity between primary prevention, early intervention, and treatment reveal precisely where disinvestment is most acute within this broader system, a distinction that collapsing these terms at the outset risks obscuring.

Funding and program design

Increasing the amount of funding is necessary but insufficient as a solitary strategy; how funds are structured and governed and how programs are evaluated is what matters (Klug et al. 2026). Participants emphasized that fragmented, short-term funding cycles created uncertainty and diverted time away from program delivery toward repeated grant applications—a finding that aligns with Crane et al.’s (2024) observation that inadequate resourcing and short funding horizons undermine the capacity to generate evidence and demonstrate impact. Multiyear contracts, streamlined reporting, and ring-fenced evaluation budgets would enable cumulative impact assessment (Shiell et al. 2024). However, as shown in recent analysis of local public health plans in WA, mandating evaluation may be critical to understanding impact and ensuring accountability (Klug et al. 2026). The findings also show that, while collaboration was seen as important for achieving public health outcomes, it was difficult to put into practice when community-based organizations are competing for the same tenders, echoing Alderwick et al.’s (2021) argument that collaboration is essential yet structurally inhibited. Furthermore, smaller organizations were seen as disproportionately disadvantaged due to limited infrastructure and reach, reinforcing the need for funding models that support equity across agencies (Department of Health 2014, Burroughs et al. 2025).

Systems-level action

Behavior change strategies, commonly used in public health, were seen as incompatible with short funding horizons because sustained engagement is required for meaningful outcomes (Matthews et al. 2024, Nadal et al. 2024). These practitioner perspectives are consistent with systems thinking guidance from The Australian Prevention Partnership Centre (2014), recent CAPHIA position statements (CAPHIA 2025a, 2025b), and research on the built environment’s influence on health (Henry et al. 2024), all of which highlight that prevention success depends on coordinated, cross-sector action rather than discrete, time-limited projects. The findings also align with recent policy discourse emphasizing holistic collaboration over competition for limited resources (Alderwick et al. 2021). Insights from local government planning further reinforce this point, demonstrating that successful implementation relies on well-coordinated collaboration, clear roles and responsibilities, and partnerships that facilitate access to health data and planning resources (Klug et al. 2026). Shiell et al. (2024) stress that evaluating current funding models is essential to improve transparency and strengthen preventative health agencies. However, persistent calls for “healthy lifestyles” without addressing the underlying determinants (e.g. poverty, housing shortages, and environments that do not support health and well-being) reflect a lack of systems-level thinking and a persistent trend of inaction (Shiell et al. 2024).

Workforce sustainability

Funding instability was consistently linked to job insecurity, with downstream effects on morale, staff retention, and organizational capacity. Participants described persistent difficulties retaining experienced staff and recruiting into very short-term positions. These conditions intensified workloads for remaining staff and generated ethical concerns about the sustainability of services. Such experiences align with evidence that job insecurity is associated with poorer mental health among Australian workers (Ervin et al. 2024, Irvine and Rose 2024) and is particularly consequential at key life-course decision points, such as purchasing a home or starting a family (Chesters and Cuervo 2019).

There is a clear risk of a skills exodus as practitioners seek more stable employment elsewhere. This pattern mirrors allied health attrition linked to insecure work (Foster et al. 2025) and is conceptually comparable to the “brain drain” documented across other health professional sectors (Kamarulzaman et al. 2022). Stabilizing the workforce will likely require the provision of secure roles, clear career pathways, and adequately resourced supervision, both to mitigate burnout and to safeguard practitioner well-being.

Globally, the health promotion sector has internationally recognized competencies and an accreditation program for courses and practitioners administered by the International Union on Health Promotion and Education (IUHPE 2026), which “provides a valuable mechanism for capacity development across different health promotion contexts and systems” (Battel-Kirk et al. 2021, p. 48). In Australia, health promotion is not a regulated profession. Twelve Australian university health promotion programs have achieved IUHPE accreditation (two of those are in teach-out) (IUHPE 2026). For individual practitioners, accreditation is voluntary rather than a requirement for employment. Of the 5100 Health Promotion Officers employed in Australia (Jobs and Skills Australia 2026), there are 267 Registered Health Promotion Practitioners (AHPA 2026)—approximately 5%. However, this proportion likely understates the true scale of the workforce, and many roles involving health promotion work may not be formally described or classified as such. Hence, the system is best characterized as emerging rather than fully institutionalized.

Workforce sustainability therefore also depends on recognized competency frameworks and professional pathways that support progression, supervision, and retention, ensuring prevention careers remain viable rather than contingent. Without structural reform, continued attrition and loss of institutional knowledge risk eroding sector capacity, undermining the continuity and long-term effectiveness of community prevention programs.

Ethical dilemmas

In this context of constrained resources, participants described tensions between maintaining programs and services and ensuring that funding sources aligned with health promotion values. This was particularly evident in discussions of corporate partnerships, where concerns were raised about accepting support from organizations whose activities conflicted with health promotion goals. These concerns are reflected in the literature on the commercial determinants of health, which shows how corporate interests can influence public health agendas and decision-making within the sector (Moodie et al. 2013, Thomas and Daube 2023, van Tulleken et al. 2024). Conflicts of interest can also affect which issues are prioritized and limit critical scrutiny of industries whose activities may harm population health (van Tulleken et al. 2024, Lunnay et al. 2026).

Importantly, these tensions are structurally produced rather than simply matters of individual judgment. Funding models characterized by short-termism and uncertainty create conditions in which organizations may feel compelled to pursue partnerships that they would otherwise reject. Consistent with Health Promotion International’s policy position discouraging engagement with unhealthy commodity industries (Thomas and Daube 2023) and broader policy concerns regarding unhealthy political and commercial influence (Public Health Association Australia 2021, van Tulleken et al. 2024, Lunnay et al. 2026), these findings suggest the need for clearer sector-wide guidance, stronger conflict-of-interest frameworks, and alternative funding pathways that reduce reliance on ethically contentious partnerships. Without such structural supports, pragmatic decisions made in response to funding insecurity risk undermining the credibility and foundational values of health promotion practice.

Strengths and limitations

The qualitative design of this study enabled rich, nuanced accounts of practitioners’ experiences, offering insights that quantitative approaches often overlook (Greenhalgh et al. 2023). Rigor was strengthened through peer debriefing, which ensured interpretive consistency across authors, and member checking, where participants reviewed transcripts and the draft manuscript to confirm reporting accuracy and safeguard their confidentiality—both strategies enhance confirmability in qualitative research (Ahmed 2024).

Several limitations warrant consideration. Recruitment constraints meant that the study did not capture perspectives from all segments of the preventative health workforce in WA, particularly practitioners employed in state government settings. As a result, the findings primarily reflect the experiences of community-based practitioners working in nongovernment, local government, and private sector contexts. Inclusion of government-employed practitioners may have provided additional insights into how funding and policy priorities are negotiated within government systems and may have highlighted different experiences of workforce security and precarity.

The self-selected nature of the sample may have introduced bias, as participants with strong views on funding instability could have been more motivated to participate. Additionally, the use of purposeful and convenience sampling limits transferability beyond the study context. While five participants were trained outside public health, examining how interdisciplinary training shaped practice perspectives was beyond the scope of this analysis. Finally, as with all qualitative research, the findings are interpretative and shaped by researcher positionality; they should be understood as contextually grounded rather than generalizable to the entire preventative health sector.

Future directions

Two priorities emerge from this study. First, there is a need for longitudinal and mixed-methods research to track workforce trajectories, such as retention, mobility, and mental health impacts, under differing funding regimes, and to examine how contract length and governance arrangements affect organizational capacity and program continuity. Second, policy-focused studies should assess the impact of funding model reforms, for example, multiyear commitments, embedded evaluation budgets, and partnership incentives, and explore ways to operationalize health-in-all-policies approaches across planning, education, and social services.

Beyond research, these findings also highlight the importance of sector-led advocacy to shift the funding conditions that drive workforce insecurity. Health promotion practitioners and peak bodies are well placed to mobilize policy influence in support of multiyear funding, secure roles, and clearer workforce development pathways, including strengthened professional recognition and registration processes where relevant. Positioning workforce stability as a prerequisite for sustained prevention impact may help reframe prevention funding as essential system infrastructure rather than discretionary program expenditure.

Analysis of local public health planning further highlights the importance of building capacity, strengthening partnerships, and providing access to evidence-based resources to support integrated and comprehensive public health initiatives (Klug et al. 2026). Collectively, these priorities should inform structural solutions to stabilize the prevention workforce and accelerate sustainable population-level gains.

Conclusion

This study highlights how systemic underfunding, short-term funding cycles, and a preference for treatment-focused services undermine program sustainability and workforce well-being in WA’s community-based and contract-funded prevention sector. These conditions create job insecurity, stress, and attrition, threatening the sector’s capacity to deliver long-term health gains. In doing so, the findings illuminate a central paradox of prevention work: practitioners tasked with promoting population health are required to operate within conditions that undermine their own well-being, ethical practice, and capacity for long-term change.

Recent policy commitments—including the pledge to increase preventative health spending to 5% of the health budget and the establishment of the Australian Centre for Disease Control—signal progress, but funding models must also change. Multiyear contracts, streamlined reporting, and embedded evaluation resources are essential to enable strategic planning and cumulative impact, as strongly advocated in CAPHIA’s position statements (2025a, 2025b). Equally critical is investment in workforce stability through secure roles and career pathways, ensuring prevention is supported by a skilled and sustained workforce. Prevention is widely recognized as cost-effective and central to population health; realizing this promise requires structural reform that values both the work and the workforce behind it.
