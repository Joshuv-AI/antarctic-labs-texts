Exploring the mechanism of seed shattering in Psathyrostachys juncea through histological analysis and comparative transcriptomics
Authors: Yuru Lv, Lan Yun, Miaomiao Jia, Yixin Mu, Zhiqiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeed shattering (SS) negatively impacts seed yield in Psathyrostachys juncea. Understanding and improving the SS trait requires elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of SS and identifying the key genes involved.ResultsThis study presents a comprehensive analysis of the abscission zone (AZ) structures at four developmental stages in two P. juncea genotypes. High-SS P. juncea (H) exhibited a significantly higher SS rate than low-SS P. juncea (L) at all four developmental stages. Anatomical analysis revealed that the degree of lignification in the AZ cell walls is related to the integrity of the abscission structure. The degradation of the AZ in H occurred earlier and was more severe compared to L. At different developmental stages of the AZ, H exhibited higher cellulase and polygalacturonase activities and higher abscisic acid contents compared to L. Conversely, L showed higher lignin, cytokinin, auxin, and gibberellin contents than H. Transcriptomic analysis identified key metabolic pathways related to SS in P. juncea, such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, fructose and mannose metabolism, galactose metabolism, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions. The integration of morphological, histological, physiochemical, and metabolic data led to the identification of critical genes, including AUX1, CKX, ABF, GH3, 4CL, CCoAOMT, BGAL, Gal, and PG. The roles of these genes were involved in the regulation of plant hormones and in the synthesis and degradation of cell walls within the AZ.ConclusionsThis study provides an in-depth understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of SS in P. juncea through comparative transcriptomic analysis. The SS in P. juncea may result from the degradation of the cell wall regulated by cell wall hydrolases genes. The genes identified in this study provide a basis for the genetic improvement of SS traits and serve as a reference for research on other grass species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05881-0.Keywords: Psathyrostachys juncea, Seed shattering, Abscission zone, Transcriptome analysis, Cell wall hydrolase

Introduction

Psathyrostachys juncea is a diploid (2n = 2x = 14) plant of the Triticeae Dumort in the Gramineae family. It is a cross-pollinated perennial forage grass native to central and northern Asia. Due to introduction and domestication, its distribution has spread to East Asia, Europe, North America, and China [1]. This species is characterized by tufted leaves, high tillering capacity, and well-developed root systems, making it a valuable forage with high nutritional value [2]. P. juncea thrives in arid and cold environments and is used in natural grasslands and artificial pastures in cold and dry regions [3]. However, the species exhibits high seed shattering (SS) [1]. In wild plants, SS is an adaptive trait that enables seed dispersal into the surrounding environment, facilitating self-propagation and population maintenance [4]. In agricultural production, reducing SS is beneficial as it allows for efficient collection of mature seeds and prevents yield loss due to excessive SS [5].

The detachment of plant organs, including flowers, fruits, and seeds, is a natural phenomenon. The formation, development, and degradation of the abscission zone (AZ) directly cause organ abscission in plants. The AZ is composed of one or several layers of small cells characterized by dense cytoplasm and abundant starch grains [6]. Upon receiving endogenous signals, the AZ triggers the degradation of cell walls, ultimately leading to organ detachment. Understanding the synthesis and degradation processes of cell walls is therefore essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying plant organ abscission. Plant cell walls are divided into the primary wall (PW), middle lamella (ML), and secondary wall (SW). The PW is a dynamic three-dimensional network composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and small amounts of structural proteins. Pectin is also found in the ML [7]. Structural proteins in the cell wall cross-link with other components, influencing its mechanical properties [8]. The SW forms inside the PW, consisting of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, and primarily occurs in the tubular cells of vascular tissues and fiber cells. Lignin strengthens plant cell walls by reinforcing tubular structures. Understanding the synthesis and degradation of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and lignin is essential for elucidating the mechanisms of organ abscission. These processes are regulated by gene expression.

The SH5 gene in Oryza sativa regulated the development of the AZ and the synthesis of lignin [9]. The OsXTH8 gene, encoding xyloglucan endo-transglucosylase/hydrolase, was involved in the cell wall modification process in the AZ of O. sativa [10]. In Sorghum bicolor, the SpWRKY gene regulated the biosynthesis of the AZ cell wall [11]. The TaqSH1 gene in Triticum aestivum controlled the development of the AZ [12]. Research on SS in Gramineae has primarily focused on crops, with significant progress in understanding the functions of these genes. However, studies on grass species are notably lacking. In grass species, the AZ of inflorescences is typically located at the base of the spikelet and at the junction between the floret and the floret pedicel [13]. SS in Bromus catharticus and Panicum coloratum primarily occurs through the breaking of the spikelet axis [14]. In Elymus sibiricus, Elymus nutans, and Leymus chinensis, SS occurs through floret detachment [15]. In P. juncea, SS mainly involves the detachment of the spikelet axis [1]. This study analyzed the AZ at the junction between the spikelet and the rachis in P. juncea. Morphological, histological, physiochemical, and transcriptomic analyses were conducted on the AZ of high-SS genotype (H) and low-SS genotype (L). A more systematic and comprehensive understanding of the SS mechanism will be provided, which will aid in future improvements of SS traits in P. juncea.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Based on a previous evaluation of the single-plant SS rate (SHT) in P. juncea, 10 plants each from the H and L groups were selected for experimental studies (Supplementary Table 1). These plants were cultivated in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia (40°44' N, 111°50' E, altitude 1048 m).

SS phenotyping and histological analysis of spikelet rachis

Indicators for both P. juncea genotypes were measured on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 after heading [16]. SHT was measured using Steven's [17] method for evaluating SS in Gramineae forage grasses. Samples for paraffin sections and SEM were taken from the middle of the spike. The target for paraffin sections was the junction between the spikelet and the rachis, and for SEM, the breakage point. The experimental procedures followed the methods described by Kantar [18], Htun [19], and Konishi [20].

Physiochemical analysis of AZ

Samples for cellulase (CE), polygalacturonase (PG), abscisic acid (ABA), trans-zeatin (ZA), auxin (IAA), gibberellin (GA3), and lignin (Lig) were collected from 10 selected P. juncea plants. Samples were taken from the junction between the spikelet and the rachis, and from the rachis itself (each about 1 mm in length, due to the short spikelet axis and ease of SS). Samples from the same genotype and developmental stage were pooled in equal amounts and divided into three biological replicates. CE and PG were measured using Comin assay kits (Comin Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The concentrations of all plant hormones were determined using an ACCHROM S3000 high-performance liquid chromatography system, performed by Comin Biotech Co., Ltd. Data plotting and statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and RNA sequencing

A portion of the mixed samples was sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. for RNA extraction and quality control. Subsequently, library construction and second- and third-generation sequencing were performed.

RNA from different samples was mixed and subjected to damage repair using the SMRTbell Template Prep Kit (PACBIO, USA). Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SMARTer PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (Clontech, USA). The amplified full-length cDNA was purified with AMPure PB beads (PACBIO, USA), and SMRT dumbbell adapters were ligated. The cDNA was purified again with PB beads to obtain the sequencing library. Finally, sequencing was performed on the Sequel II sequencing system (PACBIO, USA).

Oligo-attached beads paired with polyA bases to isolate mRNA from the total RNA. The mRNA was randomly fragmented into approximately 300 bp segments. After reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA, adaptors were ligated to the cDNA fragments. The adaptor-ligated products were size-selected and purified to obtain the final library. Sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq X Plus sequencer (Illumina, USA).

Quality control and annotation analysis

Third-generation sequences were filtered to remove polymerase reads shorter than 50 bp and sequences with accuracy below 0.90. CCS sequences were extracted, and redundant sequences were removed using the collapse tool. Sequence completeness was assessed using BUSCO software, resulting in the final set of full-length transcripts. For second-generation raw data, quality control was performed using fastp software [21], removing adapter sequences and low-quality sequences shorter than 20 bp to obtain clean data. All clean data were assembled de novo using Trinity software [22]. The assembly results were evaluated with TransRate software [23], and redundant sequences were removed using CD-HIT software [24]. Sequence completeness was again assessed with BUSCO software [25]. Second- and third-generation transcriptome data were integrated using TAMA software. The long-read information from the third-generation data optimized the assembly results of the second-generation data, producing more complete transcripts. Annotation was performed using Diamond software by aligning the transcripts with six major databases (Swiss-Prot, Pfam, NR, COG, GO, KEGG).

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

Gene abundances were quantified using RSEM software [26] and normalized by the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) method [27]. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software [28]. Genes with |log2 FC|≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using Goatools software, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Python scipy software. A p-value (p-adjust) < 0.05 was considered significantly enriched.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA).

To ensure accuracy, expression data (24 samples from AZ tissues of two P. juncea genotypes at four developmental stages) and phenotypic data were prepared. The phenotypic data included SHT, Lig, CE, PG, ABA, ZA, IAA, and GA3. After correcting and normalizing the expression data, DEGs with FPKM > 1 were selected. SS gene identification was performed using the WGCNA analysis method [29].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Ten DEGs related to SS in P. juncea were selected for validation. Total RNA was isolated from the AZ tissues using the MJZol RNA extraction kit (Majorbio) following the manufacturer's protocol. The quality and quantity of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and the integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cowin Bio, China) and diluted tenfold for qRT-PCR validation. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) in a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. Each sample included three replications. Actin was used as the reference gene [30]. Primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (Supplementary Table 2) and synthesized by BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. The relative expression levels of the DEGs were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [31]. Data analysis was performed using the R language.

Histological analysis of AZ structure in P. juncea

Histological analyses of spikelet axes in two P. juncea genotypes revealed structural changes in the AZ (Fig. 2). Abscission layer cells in both genotypes were smaller and elliptical or irregular compared to surrounding cells (Fig. 2b). These cells stained red, indicating higher lignification than surrounding tissue cells. Lignification of abscission layers increased progressively, highest in L28. The abscission layer in L remained intact, while cracks appeared in H28, suggesting lignification of plant cell walls relates to the integrity of the abscission layer. The fracture surface of the spikelet axis in P. juncea was uneven and non-planar (Fig. 2c). Abscission layers in both genotypes degraded to varying degrees over development. In L7 and L14, cell outlines of abscission layers were clear, with distinct intercellular spaces. However, in L21 and L28, cell outlines became less distinct. In H7, some regions of the abscission layer showed indistinct intercellular spaces. In H14, cell outlines blurred, and by H21 and H28, the surface of the abscission layer had become smooth, indicating more severe degradation.

Transcriptome sequencing of AZ in two P. juncea genotypes

To better understand the relationship between the developmental timing of the AZ and SS in different P. juncea genotypes, transcriptome sequencing was performed on the AZ tissues of two P. juncea genotypes. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows that the 24 sequenced samples were divided into 8 groups, exhibiting good reproducibility and no outliers. All samples were suitable for further analysis.

Numerous DEGs were observed in the two P. juncea genotypes on days 7, 14, and 21 after heading. By day 28, the number of DEGs was lowest (Supplementary Fig. 2). qRT-PCR validation of RNA-seq data

To verify the reliability of the transcriptome data, ten SS-related genes (CKX, TGA, PG, PYL, XET, EG, PAL, 4CL, ETR, MYC) were selected for qRT-PCR validation (Supplementary Fig. 5). These genes are involved in key pathways associated with seed shattering: CKX participates in the "Zeatin biosynthesis" pathway, TGA in the "Plant hormone signal transduction" pathway, and PG in the "Pentose and glucuronate interconversions" pathway. Additionally, PYL, ETR, and MYC are involved in the "MAPK signaling" pathway, XET in the "Xyloglucan metabolic process," EG in the "Cellulose catabolic process," and PAL and 4CL in the "Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis" pathway.

The qRT-PCR results revealed varying expression levels of these genes across the four developmental stages of the AZ in the two P. juncea genotypes. For instance, PG and EG exhibited significantly higher expression in the H during the later stages (21 and 28 days after heading), correlating with increased cell wall degradation. Conversely, PAL and 4CL showed higher expression in the L during the same stages, indicating enhanced lignin biosynthesis and cell wall stabilization. While minor discrepancies between FPKM values and relative expression levels were observed for certain genes at specific stages (e.g., TGA in early stages), the overall expression trends were consistent. These results confirm the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing data and underscore its utility in identifying key genes regulating SS.

Discussion

Histological and physicochemical differences of the AZ

The detachment of fruits and seeds is a complex, coordinated process involving changes in cell structure, metabolism, and gene expression [32]. In this study, H and L with significant differences in SHT were selected for morphological, histological, physicochemical, and transcriptomic analyses. Previous studies showed that abscission is induced by AZ development and related to the degree of AZ degradation [20, 33]. In wild rice, the abscission layer forms during flowering and degrades after pollination [34]. Our results revealed that the abscission layer in H14 began to degrade during flowering, with severe degradation in H21 and H28. In contrast, the abscission layer in L21 only began to degrade during flowering. AZ degradation in H occurred earlier and was more severe in H28 compared to L28. This is consistent with Fig. 1c, where SHT in H28 was significantly higher than in L28. Scanning electron microscopy of the AZ in E. sibiricus on days 21 and 28 after heading showed that the spikelet fracture surface of high-shattering genotype was smooth, indicating a higher degree of AZ degradation compared to low-shattering genotype [35]. In Stylosanthes guianensis, the AZ is at the junction between the seed and the stem. In high-shattering genotype, the abscission layer surface was smooth 14 days after flowering, indicating severe degradation leading to layer separation [36]. These findings are consistent with our results, where the degradation of the abscission layer in H was greater than in L.

Lignin primarily exists in the thickened secondary cell walls, enhancing plant rigidity and compressive strength, thus providing structural support. It is the second most abundant macromolecular organic substance in plants after cellulose [37, 38]. In the secondary cell walls of Gramineae plants, approximately 20% of lignin fills the gaps between polysaccharides [39]. Our findings indicated that the degree of lignification in L28 was higher than in H28, with cracks appearing in the abscission layer of H28. This suggests that a higher number of lignified cells in the abscission layer can better stabilize its structure. As shown in Fig. 1c, the SHT of H28 was significantly higher than that of L28, and the lignin content in L28 was significantly higher than in H28 (Fig. 2a). These results mutually validate that the high SS rate in P. juncea may be due to the lower lignin content in the AZ, resulting in insufficient support of the spikelet axis. In rice, inhibiting the biosynthesis of lignin to reduce its content in the AZ and surrounding tissues increases SS [9]. This is consistent with our findings in P. juncea, indicating that the content of lignin in the abscission layer is one of the reasons for the differing SS traits in P. juncea.

SS involves the degradation of cell wall components driven by hydrolases like CE and PG [4]. Cellulose, providing mechanical support and protection, can be hydrolyzed by CE, influencing SS [40]. Pectin, maintaining plant cell structure and function, can be hydrolyzed by PG [41]. Dissolving pectin between adjacent cell walls in the AZ can cause protoplast damage [42]. Studies have shown that PG positively regulates fruit ripening, abscission, and pod shattering [43, 44]. Reducing PG activity can minimize seed yield losses before harvest [45]. CE is critical for loosening plant cell walls during organ abscission [40]. As E. sibiricus seeds mature, CE and PG activities in the AZ increase, with higher levels in high-shattering genotype [16, 35]. In this study, CE and PG activities were higher in H than in L. PG activity peaked in both H and L on day 21. By day 28, PG activity in H slightly decreased, possibly due to nearing seed maturity and slowed physiological responses, while PG activity in L remained relatively unchanged. Our findings align with previous studies, showing that significant increases in CE and PG during seed development lead to more severe AZ cell wall degradation [46, 47]. Analyzing Figs. 1 and 2 suggests that higher cell wall hydrolase activity results in more severe AZ tissue degradation, leading to higher SS rate.

Differences in plant hormones in AZ tissues

Plant organ abscission results from the interaction of various hormones, each playing different roles at different growth stages. Hormone imbalances near the AZ can lead to organ detachment [48]. IAA regulates organ growth, seed production, and fruit ripening. Removing tomato floral organs causes IAA deficiency in the pedicel AZ, leading to the upregulation of IAA-related transcription factors [49]. The expression of IAA transport genes is related to the abscission of tomato flowers and fruits [50], indicating that IAA transport and distribution in the AZ play a crucial role in pedicel abscission [51]. Spraying 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) during fruit development prevents AZ formation in the fruit peduncle, reducing abscission [52]. These findings suggest that IAA significantly inhibits plant organ abscission. Our results showed that as P. juncea seeds matured, IAA levels in the AZ decreased (Fig. 3c), and AUX1 expression was downregulated (Fig. 10). Higher IAA contents in L likely result in lower SS rate (Fig. 1c), consistent with other plant studies. GA3 promotes cell elongation and inhibits maturation and senescence throughout the growth cycle. Studies on GA3's impact on abscission have varied results. Applying GA3 to Lupinus luteus AZ promoted leaf abscission, with higher GA3 levels in naturally abscised leaves [53]. GA3 also promoted flower abscission in Vitis vinifera [54]. Conversely, spraying orange trees with GA3 increased IAA and GA3 contents, inhibiting abscission [55]. Another study found no effect of GA3 on citrus abscission [56]. Lower GA3 contents (Fig. 3c) and downregulated GA3 expression (Fig. 10) might contribute to higher SHT in H. Further research on GA3's role in abscission is needed to support agricultural production. ABA is a growth-inhibiting hormone that accelerates abscission. Norflurazon (NFZ), an ABA antagonist, significantly inhibited cotyledon abscission in cotton seeds. The degree of cotyledon abscission in NFZ-treated plants correlated positively with ABA concentration applied afterward [57]. ABA contents in Lupinus luteus were significantly related to flower abscission [58]. ABA contents in the AZ of P. juncea increased as seeds matured, with ABF (ABA responsive element binding factor) upregulated in H. This suggests that upregulated ABF leading to higher ABA contents in H may promote SS in P. juncea. Zeatin, a natural cytokinin (CK), inhibits mango fruit abscission when applied [59]. CKs inhibit seed abscission in Arabidopsis [60]. ZA contents in P. juncea increased, indicating CK involvement in seed maturation. However, at day 28, nearing seed maturation, ZA contents suddenly dropped, with L having higher ZA contents than H. This suggests that high ZA contents may inhibit SS, consistent with previous studies. Additionally, the expression trend of CKX (cytokinin dehydrogenase) (Supplementary Fig. 5) was consistent with ZA contents changes (Fig. 3c).

Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis

Inhibiting lignin biosynthesis increases SS in rice [9]. The expression of lignin synthesis genes is positively correlated with lignin content in the cell wall, affecting SS in S. guianensis by providing cell wall support [36]. PAL/PTAL, encoding phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, plays a crucial role in cell differentiation, lignin, and flavonoid synthesis [61]. In the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway of P. juncea (Fig. 6), PAL-1 was upregulated in H, while PAL-2 was upregulated in L. PTAL was downregulated in L. CYP98A, and CYP84A encode cytochrome P450, essential for the biosynthesis and metabolism of phenylpropanoids, terpenes, indoles, and other compounds necessary for plant growth [62]. The upregulation of cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (HCT), flavone O-methyltransferase (COMT), and caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase (CCoAOMT) results in lignin deposition and secondary cell wall thickening [63, 64]. In P. juncea, 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL), CCR, HCT, and CCoAOMT were upregulated in L28. Figure 2b, 3-a, and 1-c show that L28 exhibited strong lignified cell structures, high lignin content, and low SS. These findings suggest that the above four genes are involved in lignin synthesis in the AZ of P. juncea, promoting cell wall thickening and reducing SS, consistent with studies in other plants. Upregulating cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) in melons leads to lignin accumulation, helping plants resist drought conditions [65]. Conversely, CAD and COMT were upregulated in H and downregulated in L, possibly inhibiting lignin synthesis. Lignin monomers are catalyzed by peroxidase (POD) to form macromolecular lignin [66]. The cell wall-degrading enzyme (BcGS1) can activate defense mechanisms in tomato cells, preventing damage. Under the action of BcGS1, tomato cells produce more hydrogen peroxide, which enhances POD activity, indicating that POD can prevent cell damage by controlling lignin synthesis [67]. Caffeoylshikimate esterase (CSE) in dicotyledonous plants such as Arabidopsis and Alfalfa converts caffeoyl shikimic acid to caffeic acid, which is used in lignin monomer biosynthesis [68]. POD and CSE were upregulated in P. juncea on day 28, suggesting that more lignin is produced during seed maturation to prevent SS.

Fructose and mannose metabolism

Carbohydrate transport influences cellulose synthesis [69]. Phloem distributes photosynthetic products and transmits signals in plants [70]. Carbohydrates transported through the phloem are primarily in the form of sucrose [71], which can be converted into fructose and glucose. Fructose and glucose regulate osmotic potential and turgor pressure in cells and influence plant growth and development [72]. Fructose and glucose are converted into fructose-6P and glucose-6P by hexokinase (HK) and fructokinase (FRK), respectively, entering glycolysis or other metabolic pathways [73]. FRK is a key enzyme and signal regulator in fructose metabolism. Studies in Arabidopsis show that inhibiting FRK expression restricts plant growth and cellulose synthesis [74]. Inhibiting the tomato FRK genes SlFRK3 and SlFRK2 simultaneously results in thin and collapsing cell walls [75]. Co-inhibition of SlFRK1 and SlFRK2 reduces xylem area, decreases xylem vessel number, and results in smaller phloem fiber cells with thinner cell walls [76]. These findings indicate the critical role of FRK in plant cell wall metabolism. In P. juncea, HK and FRK were downregulated in H (Fig. 7), suggesting that low HK and FRK activity leads to fewer carbohydrates in AZ cell walls, resulting in insufficient cell wall support and increased SS. Phosphomannose isomerase (PMI) plays a crucial role in carbohydrate metabolism and cell wall precursor synthesis pathways [77]. PMI was significantly downregulated in H28, possibly due to seed maturation, leading to slower cell wall metabolism and fewer carbohydrates, resulting in insufficient cell wall support and increased SS. Nudix hydrolases (NUDX) are specific hydrolases. In P. juncea, NUDX was upregulated in H and downregulated in L, potentially promoting carbohydrate hydrolysis in H and contributing to cell wall degradation. Xylose isomerase (XI), also known as glucose isomerase (GI) [78], catalyzes the interconversion of D-glucose and D-fructose in P. juncea. Sorbitol dehydrogenase (SDH) is a key enzyme in sorbitol degradation, oxidizing sorbitol to produce fructose, which plays a critical role in carbohydrate conversion [79]. SDH was also identified in P. juncea. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBP) mediates fructose signal transduction during Arabidopsis growth [80]. Phosphofructokinase (PFK) catalyzes the conversion of fructose-6P to fructose-1,6P2. Triose phosphate isomerase (TPI) primarily catalyzes the equilibrium between glyceraldehyde-3P and glycerone-P [81]. FBP, PFK, and TPI exhibited different expression patterns at various developmental stages in the two P. juncea genotypes.

Galactose metabolism

Galactose stabilizes and strengthens pectin, enhancing cell wall elasticity and providing mechanical support for wall contraction and expansion [82]. Galactinol synthase (GOLS) regulates the accumulation of raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs) in plants [83]. GOLS catalyzes the first step of RFO biosynthesis, followed by galactosyltransferase (GT) synthesizing various oligosaccharides from galactinol [84]. Overexpressing aspen GolS3 in Arabidopsis increased the number of xylem vessels and the thickness of the gelatinous layer in fibers, with higher cellulose and lower lignin content. This suggests that GolS overexpression may affect sugar storage and distribution, altering the composition and structure of secondary cell walls [85]. GOLS and GT were also identified in P. juncea (Fig. 8). GOLS was significantly upregulated in L7, while GT was significantly upregulated in H14. This suggests that RFO accumulation requires the mutual regulation of various enzymes, displaying promotive or inhibitory effects, and further functional validation is needed. UDP-glucose 4-epimerase (UGE) is crucial for synthesizing polysaccharide precursors [86]. UGE balances and regulates nucleotide sugar levels, potentially influencing monosaccharide composition [87]. AtUGE2 and AtUGE4 in Arabidopsis are involved in cell wall biosynthesis [88]. The rice bz1 mutant exhibited altered cell wall structure and abnormal chloroplast development, affecting cell wall strength and leaf color [89]. UGE in P. juncea was significantly upregulated in L21, possibly contributing to more polysaccharide synthesis, thereby reinforcing the cell wall in the AZ of L. Alpha-galactosidase (α-Gal) belongs to glycoside hydrolases, α-Gal enzymes catalyze the hydrolysis of galactomannan, galactolipid, glycoprotein, and others [90]. α-Gal plays a crucial role in fruit ripening and leaf development by participating in cell wall metabolism (dissociation and expansion) [91]. Fruit softening during ripening results from the coordinated hydrolysis of cell wall polysaccharides by multiple enzymes and proteins, including α-Gal, beta-galactosidase (BGAL), polygalacturonase (PG), pectin methylesterase (PME), pectate lyase (PL), cellulase (CE), xyloglucan endotransglycosylase (XET), α-L-Arabinofuranosidase (α-AF), and expansins (EXP) [92, 93]. In soft tomato fruit, α-Gal activity significantly increased [94]. CsGAL2 promotes rapid fruit growth during cucumber maturation by dissociating the cell wall [95]. In P. juncea, Gal was downregulated in H and significantly upregulated in L14 and L21, indicating that this gene might inhibit cell wall degradation in the AZ. In melon, the expression of CmBGAL3-11 was positively correlated with BGAL activity, suggesting that CmBGAL3-11 contributes to melon softening [96]. Mdβ-GAL18 in apples also reduced fruit firmness [97]. The expression of BGAL in H first increased and then decreased over time, suggesting that BGAL activity in the AZ is related to seed development time, and further validation of this gene's expression and function is needed. Phosphoglucomutase (PGM) is involved in cell wall synthesis and plays a crucial role in sucrose metabolism [98]. PGM was also identified in P. juncea. PGM catalyzes the conversion of glucose-1P to glucose-6P, which then enters the pentose phosphate pathway [99].

Pentose and glucuronate interconversions

Cell wall degrading enzymes primarily cause cell separation in the plant AZ. Polygalacturonase (PG), a key hydrolase in the plant AZ, is involved in pectin degradation, leading to cell wall disintegration. In tomatoes, three PG genes (TAPG1, TAPG2, TAPG4) expressed in the AZ of leaves and flowers have been identified [100]. Subsequent studies found that silencing both PG and EXP1 genes in tomatoes could reduce fruit cracking rates [101]. In P. juncea, PG expression was significantly upregulated in H21 (Fig. 9). The pattern of PG expression changes mirrored PG activity (Fig. 3b), suggesting that SS may result from increased pectin degradation. PL is also a key enzyme in pectin degradation [102]. In L7, PL expression was significantly upregulated, potentially inhibiting SS during seed maturation in P. juncea. PME, an enzyme that hydrolyzes pectin, often coexists in different tissue-specific types within the same plant [103]. PME expression was significantly upregulated in H28, possibly causing cell wall damage due to pectin degradation, increasing the likelihood of SS. UDP-glucose dehydrogenase (UGDH) is involved in cell wall polysaccharide synthesis and acts as a central hub in polysaccharide metabolism [104]. Overexpression of LgUGDH increased hemicellulose content and thickened cell walls in transgenic Arabidopsis stems [105]. Overexpression of NtUGDH in Alfalfa resulted in higher xylose (essential for hemicellulose synthesis) and Lig content compared to wild-type plants [106]. UGDH was downregulated in H and significantly upregulated in L7 and L14, suggesting that UGDH may strengthen the cell wall in the AZ of L while affecting polysaccharide synthesis in H. In plants, D-Glucuronate (D-GlcA) is converted to D-Glucuronate-1P by glucuronokinase (GlcAK), which plays an important role in pectin synthesis [107]. In P. juncea, GlcAK expression was upregulated in H21 but significantly downregulated in H28, while upregulated in L28. This suggests that as P. juncea seeds near maturity, the AZ in L requires more carbohydrate synthesis to strengthen the cell wall and further inhibit SS. UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (UDP) serves as a glucosyl donor for the synthesis of sucrose, cellulose, and pectin [108]. UGP2 expression was significantly upregulated in L28, suggesting that during seed maturation in L, more carbohydrate synthesis is needed to enhance the cell wall's resistance to SS.

The differentially expressed genes identified may play a role in the SS of P. juncea, requiring further validation of their specific functions. These genes show diverse temporal expression patterns, indicating that the combined action of specific genes may regulate SS in different P. juncea genotypes. This offers a valuable selection of genes for future research into the SS mechanism in P. juncea.

Conclusions

The H exhibited significantly higher SS rates across all developmental stages compared to the L. Earlier and more severe degradation of the abscission layer in H was associated with lower lignification of cell walls, while L had higher lignin content, contributing to structural stability. Elevated CE and PG activities and ABA levels in H contrasted with higher Lig, CK, IAA, and GA3 levels in L, reflecting distinct physiological profiles. Transcriptomic analysis highlighted critical pathways, such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and sugar metabolism, and identified key SS-regulating genes (AUX1, CKX, ABF, GH3, 4CL, CCoAOMT, BGAL, Gal, PG). These findings suggest that SS in P. juncea is primarily driven by cell wall degradation regulated by hydrolase genes. This study provides novel insights into the mechanism of SS in P. juncea, offering a foundation for genetic improvement and a reference for other grass species.
