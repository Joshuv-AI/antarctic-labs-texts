A Rare Case of Cutaneous Metastasis From Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) and the Possible Role of GALR2 as a Biomarker: A Case Report
Authors: Ianny Brum Reis, Milena Moraes de Carvalho, Danilo Paschoal Ferrarezi, Natalie Aparecida Rodrigues Fernandes, Camyla Rodrigues Nascimento, Álvaro Formoso Pelegrini, Aldenora Maria Ximenes Rodrigues, Alex de Freitas Porsani, Flávio Ferlin Arbex, Carlos Rossa Junior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a rare case of cutaneous metastasis from oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). The primary tumor showed low expression of Galanin, CD68, GALR2, and Collagen IV, with increased FOXP3 and Vimentin. Systemic analysis revealed reduced CD8+ cells and increased Tregs. The patient died following aggressive disease progression.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), primarily oral (OSCC), is a high‐mortality malignancy correlated to tobacco and alcohol [1, 2, 3, 4]. Prognosis depends on HPV status and staging; advanced OSCC has a ~50% 5‐year survival rate and high recurrence [2, 5, 6]. While lymph node involvement is common (20%–40%), cutaneous metastasis is rare (0.7%–10%) [7, 8]. This study reports a rare OSCC cutaneous metastasis, evaluating the expression of Galanin Receptor‐2 (GALR2), a receptor with debated oncogenic or tumor‐suppressive roles [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], beside local and systemic biomarkers, comparing results to a non‐metastatic cohort.

Case History

A 54‐year‐old male Brazilian patient (P10), with a history of smoking and ethanol consumption, was diagnosed with HNSCC (pT4a pN3b Mx), affecting the floor of the mouth and lateral side of the tongue (left side). Partial pelviglossectomy with left radical neck dissection was performed approximately 5 months after diagnosis (on July 2022). The tumor measured 6 × 3 cm and the final pathologist's report indicated well‐differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. Immunohistochemistry (P16) showed that the lesion was HPV‐positive and there were neoplasm‐compromised adjacent striated muscles and mandibular bone, widespread angiolymphatic invasion, focal tumor necrosis, and absence of perineural invasion. The surgical margins of the specimen, including mandibular bone margins, were determined to be clear of neoplastic cells except for the posterior aspect of the soft tissue (mucosal) margins, that showed local foci of increased cellular infiltrate. Lymph node dissection was performed and 10 of 27 nodes were positive, with involvement at levels 2 (n = 3), 3 (n = 4), and 4 (n = 3). The extranodal extension was at level 4, with tumoral extravasation into the sternocleidomastoid muscle.

Post surgery, the patient received 5 cycles of chemotherapy (Cisplatin 100 mg/m2; 170 mg IV, every 3 weeks) and radiotherapy as adjuvant/curative intent (66 Gy in 35 fractions). The treatment was followed from August to October 2022, and was completed as planned. Approximatelly 11 months after completing adjuvant treatment, the patient developed a cutaneous metastatic lesion in the mid‐thoracic dorsal region, slightly left of the midline, at approximately the T5‐T6 level. The lesion presented as a cutaneous implant without features of a primary skin neoplasm. Metastatic lesions were detected in the skin (neck and upper mid back). Resection of skin lesions was performed. The histopathological and clinical findings confirmed that the lesions were not consistent with a primary skin tumor. Consequently, a senior pathologist confirmed the diagnosis as OSCC metastasis, given the patient's primary tumor history. The lesion resected from the neck was a well‐differentiated squamous cell carcinoma infiltrating derma (neoplastic implant pattern), without connection with the epidermis, no angiolymphatic or perineural invasion, and no areas of tumor necrosis. The lesion resected from the back measured 2 cm and was defined as ulcerated invasive well‐differentiated squamous cell carcinoma with angiolymphatic invasion, no perineural invasion and areas of tumor necrosis. The lesions were treated with local surgical resection, followed by systemic chemotherapy (Cisplatin 100 mg/m2; 170 mg IV, every 3 weeks for 3 cycles). In January 2024, the patient developed systemic progression with cervical recurrence and multiple cutaneous implants, and subsequently died, approximately 18 months after the initial surgical treatment and 6 months after detection of the first distant metastasis.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigation and Treatment

Primary tumor and venous blood were collected at the primary tumor resective surgery and analyzed at the State University of São Paulo. Tissue sample was fixed in buffered ethanol and embedded in paraffin for subsequent sectioning. One section was stained with hematoxylin/eosin (Figure 1A–E) and the other serial sections were used for immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence. Histological hematoxylin/eosin‐stained sections of metastatic lesions resected from the skin of the back of the patient were provided by the attending pathologist (Figure 1F–H).

Total RNA was extracted using an affinity column system (RNAqueous, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using random hexamers as primers and a recombinant moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was amplified using TaqMan Expression Assays for the constitutive gene 18S and using SYBR Green qPCR master mixes (Applied Biosystems) for the target genes. Expression of GALR2, Galanin, FOXP3, and CD68 was determined in tumor samples and GALR2 expression was assessed in PBMC. 18S RNA was used for normalization of target gene expression (information of qPCR primers in Table S1). Data was analyzed by the Δ(ΔCt) method. Gene expression of the reported case was compared with the expression of the same genes in a sample cohort of 22 or less (depending on sample availability, with a maximum of 22 samples included for comparison) HNSCC patients with similar clinical stage classification (T3/T4—oral cavity and/or oropharynx) in treatment in the same hospital. They also presented with angiolymphatic invasion (N1+; n = 10), focal tumor necrosis (n = 6), and positive margins (n = 9). Additionally, 2 patients were HPV/p16‐positive, 3 were HPV/p16‐negative, and the remaining 17 patients were not tested for HPV/p16 status.

Conclusion and Results

In comparison with the cohort of patients with similar stage tumors, the reported case had lower expression of Galanin, CD68, and FOXP3 in the tumor. Notably, expression of GALR2 in the primary tumor and PBMC of the reported case was lower than that of the cohort of T3/T4 HNSCC patients. Expression of Vimentin was increased in comparison with the cohort, although no significant difference was observed in the expression of E‐cadherin and N‐cadherin (Figure 2).

The reported case had a reduced expression of collagen IV (determined by conventional, enzyme‐based immunohistochemistry) in comparison with the other OSCC cases with T3/T4 tumors (6.5% vs. 16.72% average positive staining area in the comparison cohort) (Figure 3D–F). Reduced expression of collagen IV has been reported as a marker of tumor aggressiveness in ovarian cancer, in which collagen IV expression is downregulated compared to benign tissue [15].

Discussion

The most common primary site of metastasis in HNSCC is the lung, followed by bone and liver [16]. Cutaneous metastasis from head and neck cancer is uncommon; it corresponds to 0.7%–10% of metastatic cancers, and it is seen more commonly in laryngeal cancer [17]. Distant metastasis is defined as tumor that has spread to other organs or systems [18] and is associated with an aggressive phenotype. Here we report a case in which distant metastasis presentation occurred, with overall survival of 15 months and only 4 months after presentation with cutaneous metastasis. Thus, we retrospectively looked for biomarkers indicative of aggressiveness by assessing selected candidate aspects in primary tumor and peripheral blood samples obtained at primary tumor resection. To obtain perspective and insights into the relevance of the selected aggressiveness biomarker candidates, we compared information from the case presented to a cohort of other HNSCC cases with similar clinical staging at diagnosis and treated at the same hospital but without cutaneous metastasis. In this cohort, 14 patients exhibited ongoing survival; 3 deaths were attributed to disease progression, and 3 resulted from other etiologies.

It is intriguing to consider the mechanisms that may have allowed cells from a primary tumor in the floor of the mouth/tongue to find suitable ‘soil’ in the epidermis. This phenomenon requires several steps initiating with the migration and intravasation of cancer cells into blood vessels. First, these cells have to separate from their neighboring cells and basement membrane in the primary tumor. For this, they will acquire mesenchymal‐like properties via a process called epithelial mesenchymal transition. The cells begin to exhibit a migratory phenotype, which allows detachment of the primary tumor and invasion of the underlying stroma. Thus, these cells must intravasate into circulation at the primary tumor site, survive the microenvironment of peripheral blood and systemic circulation and transport to target organs. Cancer cells have to migrate through the vessel wall into the interstitial space and extravasate into the dermis and migrate towards the epidermis, all the while undergoing shifts between mesenchymal and epithelial phenotypes themselves [19, 20]. However, migrating to a new tissue is no guarantee that the cells will survive this new environment and grow to form a secondary tumor. Factors such as apoptosis, hypoxia caused by insufficient vascularization and attacks by cells from the immune system are possible obstacles to the development of these cells in the new site. These steps involve complex biological mechanisms that include many chemotaxis mediators, cell‐matrix chemical ligand‐receptor interactions and intracellular signaling networks [18]. These aspects make this case presentation both intriguing and relevant for the search for biomarkers associated with metastatic tumor dissemination.

Some features observed in this case may have favored this process: macrophages may ‘pave the way’ and serve as chaperones in the intravasation and extravasation processes, a depleted/exhausted systemic immune response can be permissive for tumor cell survival in the circulation and the microvasculature and dermis may have expressed chemotactic signals ‘driving’ the neoplastic cells to this specific microenvironment. Identifying biomarkers associated with this process may yield various opportunities to interfere and prevent metastatic tumor dissemination and also serve as a prognosis indicator, helping define a more suitable treatment strategy in aggressive and likely recurrent/metastatic tumors.

The case reported presented cutaneous metastasis approximately 11 months after completing adjuvant treatment, which is the standard of care at the hospital. Despite being diagnosed at an advanced stage, which is usually associated with worse prognosis, it is interesting to describe selected features that may serve as biomarkers of a more aggressive/metastatic evolution in comparison to a cohort of patients presenting tumors of similar clinical staging. Reports of such rare cases have a role in generating insights into such biomarkers or phenotypical characteristics that can contribute to this process and increase awareness of potential aggressiveness, which may be considered in the initial treatment planning. We assessed different candidate markers of tumor aggressiveness in the reported case, using a cohort of other OSCC patients diagnosed with similar stage tumors, but without distant metastasis, for relative comparison. Nevertheless, it is important to be aware of the inherent limitations of the study design (case report) in terms of the ‘N’ of 1 case and the consequent inadequacy of generalizing the possible inferences. Also, although the reported case with cutaneous mutation was HPV+, we do not systematically assess HPV status in the samples of the patient cohort, which has demographic characteristics associated with HPV− cases: predominantly male, aged 50+, history of heavy alcohol/tobacco use. Moreover, it is not possible to assert precisely the moment of disease onset and evolution until the effective clinical diagnosis, and despite the absence of distant metastasis it is possible that some of the cases in the comparison cohort had a more aggressive local evolution.

In carcinomas, a dynamic interaction occurs at the interface between tumor cells and the surrounding extracellular matrix components. Collagen IV is one of the major components of the basement membrane [21] and normal basement membrane is one of the biological barriers to tumor invasion and metastasis [22]. Studies have shown that collagen IV has cell adhesion function and is involved in the process of tumor invasion and metastasis [23, 24, 25], which has been reported in colorectal [22], breast [26], gastrointestinal [22], and ovarian cancers [15]. We could not find any study on collagen IV expression in OSCC and its correlation with tumor aggressiveness. Our results are similar those of Bar et al. [15], which reported that collagen IV is downregulated in ovarian carcinomas in comparison with benign tissue, and that its expression is inversely correlated with stage and markers of malignancy. The low levels of collagen IV in the reported case may be indicative of the invasive phenotype of the tumor, with subsequent progression to cutaneous metastasis, but more studies are needed. Interestingly, we observed that expression of Vimentin in the reported case was high in relation to the distribution of expression in the comparative cohort, which may suggest an accentuated EMT status at the initial presentation (despite the histopathological assessment of ‘well‐differentiated squamous cell carcinoma’), which, speculatively, may be related with increased aggressiveness.

Immune cell infiltration in the tumor, particularly by macrophages, is a well‐defined prognostic indicator. Bone marrow‐derived macrophages are the most common type of tumor‐infiltrating immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and are vital mediators of the antitumor immune response [27, 28]. Studies showed that CD68 is overexpressed in tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) and reported that high levels of CD68 are associated with a higher tumor grade, larger tumor size, increased Ki‐67 positivity, and other malignant features associated with tumor progression and aggressiveness [29, 30]. Our results showed a high infiltration of CD68+ cells at the invasive front, which was not different from the comparison cohort of cases. This characteristic was not particular to this case, but the prevalence of CD68+ cells was noticeably greater in tumor invasive areas in comparison with similar areas in the comparison cohort. This may suggest that macrophages are functioning as chaperones to the neoplastic cells, facilitating their invasion. There is evidence suggesting that these roles are defined by the macrophage phenotype, which may vary in different areas of the tumor microenvironment [31].

The influence of the status of the immune response assessed systemically on the prognosis and aggressiveness of HNSCC is relatively less studied, but it may yield important insights into the prognosis of tumor behavior in a more rapid manner than traditional histological methods. It is important to note that all blood samples analyzed in this report were collected at the time of resection of the primary tumor. In this rare case of OSCC with cutaneous metastasis, total leukocyte count was reduced, including the prevalence of CD8+ cells, both active and exhausted. In late‐stage HNSCC total leukocyte counts are reduced compared to age‐matched healthy individuals, and a decreased proliferative response of T lymphocytes has been associated with a worse prognosis [32], suggesting that the systemic status of the immune response may reflect the tumor burden and/or aggressiveness. There is a report indicating that the activation marker CD69 is reduced in peripheral blood in T cells of HNSCC patients, and both T‐helper (CD4+) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CD8+) sub‐populations are decreased in the peripheral blood [33]. Notably, an active immune response, characterized by increased prevalence of CD8+ T cells and Th1‐type cells and cytokines, has been associated with a better prognosis [34, 35].

There are controversial reports of GALR2/galanin axis role as an oncogene or tumor suppressor in HNSCC. An in vivo study reported that GALR2 stimulates tumor angiogenesis in HNSCC via p38‐mediated inhibition of TTP resulting in increased RNA stability and enhanced cytokine secretion [10]. Other groups reported that activation of the GALR2 signaling pathway suppresses cell proliferation via induction of apoptosis in a number of tumor types, including HNSCC [12, 14]. Interestingly, GALR2 is the only galanin receptor expressed by leukocytes [36] and its activation is associated with immunosuppressive effects [10, 36] and perineural invasion [11] in an oncogene‐type role. On the other hand, GALR2 overexpression induced caspase‐3‐dependent apoptosis and inhibited cell proliferation in HNSCC cells that contained mutant p53 [13]. In the reported case, the reduced expression of GALR2 both in the tumor and PBMC in comparison with a cohort of HNSCC patients with tumors of similar stage and no distant metastasis is not consistent with a biomarker of tumor aggressiveness.

This aggressive case of OSCC presenting rare distant metastatic dissemination was associated with low GALR2 expression, low collagen IV positivity and depleted systemic immune response: low total leukocyte count, reduced prevalence of CD8+ cells and increased relative prevalence of Tregs. From a translational perspective, identifying phenotypic and systemic profiles at the time of primary tumor resection offers a valuable opportunity to refine personalized clinical management, and our findings highlight a signature of high biological aggressiveness that could justify earlier, multi‐modality therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, establishing risk profiles could directly optimize post‐treatment surveillance and imaging follow‐up protocols. Although these candidate biomarkers require validation in larger prognostic cohorts for their relevance as biomarkers of OSCC aggressiveness, translating these bench findings into risk‐stratification tools provides clinicians with objective data for realistic patient counseling and shared decision‐making regarding aggressive follow‐up versus quality of life.
