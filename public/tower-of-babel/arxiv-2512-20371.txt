Non linear Regge trajectories of quarkonia from holography
Nelson R. F. Braga, Yan F. Ferreira

Abstract
We propose a holographic model for quarkonia using the WKB approximation with the Langer correction to properly reproduce nonlinear Regge trajectories of the form m n 2 = β ​ ( n + c 0 ) 2 = 3 + c 1 m_{n}^{2}=\beta(n+c_{0})^{2/3}+c_{1} . This form is expected from previous studies involving the solution of Cornell Potential for heavy quark anti-quark interactions using a model based on the quadratic form of the spinless Salpeter-type equation (QSSE). The model fits experimental masses with very good accuracy. As a by product, the corresponding decay constants also show a reasonable agreement with the results obtained from experimental data.

I
Introduction

From semiclassical arguments, supported by the phenomenology of light mesons, it is expected that the squared masses of such particles should grow linearly with their radial excitation quantum number
n

12
. For heavy mesons, on the other hand, the authors of
10
, by employing the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization approach to a model based on the quadratic form of the spinless Salpeter-type equation (QSSE) with the Cornell potential, show that the squared masses of quarkonia should grow with
n^{2/3}
when
n
is large. The experimental masses of charmonium and bottomonium states are fitted in Ref.
10
with an equation of the form

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}=β(n+c_{0})^{2/3}+c_{1}.

(1)

The fit obtained shows good agreement with experimental values, especially when compared to the linear fit.

The AdS/CFT correspondence
16
motivated many different AdS/QCD holographic models with the purpose of describing hadronic properties. The general idea is to modify the gravitational background with the introduction of mass scales. Some of the first models that appeared are the hard-wall
20
, that was used to calculate masses of glueballs
1
and the soft-wall model, that produces linear Regge trajectories for light mesons
13
. More recently, the tangent model was introduced in order to fit not only masses, but also the decay constants of heavy mesons. This framework was used in order to describe the thermal dissociation of such particles in a quark-gluon plasma with temperature, density, magnetic field, and angular momentum
2
;
4
;
5
;
6
;
9
;
7
.

In this work, we use the WKB approximation, considering the Langer correction, to build an AdS/QCD model that reproduces a Regge trajectory of the form (
1
). The model is shown to provide also decay constants with a reasonable agreement with the results obtained from experimental data.
The structure of the present article is as follows: in Sec.
II
we review the general form of holographic models with a dilaton background and the particular case of the soft-wall. Then, in the following sections we show a sequence of steps aimed to reproduce a Regge trajectory of the form (
1
). As the first building block of the model, in Sec.
III
we explain how to add a term of mass shift that will be generate the parameter
c_{1}
. In Sec.
IV
, we make some remarks on the application of the WKB method in holography and develop a method to approximate the integral that appears in the WKB quantization condition for large values of masses. These remarks will be important through the following sections. Then we reproduce the exponent of
2/3
using the WKB approach explained before. At the end of this section, we add the last building block to the model, introducing a new parameter to it that will be responsible for reproducing the parameter
c_{0}
in (
1
). Finally, in Secs.
VI
and
VII
, we present the Numerical Results and conclusions, respectively.

II
Holographic AdS models with a dilaton background

II.1
A quick review

In the type of AdS/QCD models considered here, vector mesons are represented by an abelian vector field
V_{m}
in five dimensions subjected to the action

\displaystyle S=-\frac{1}{4g_{5}^{2}}\int ḏ^{4}x\int_{0}^{∞}ḏz\,\sqrt{-g}\,\={e}^{-ϕ(z)}F^{mn}F_{mn},

(2)

where
F_{mn}
is related to the field
V_{m}
through
F_{mn}=∂_{m}V_{n}-∂_{n}V_{m}
and the indices are contracted with the metric of an
\mathrm{AdS}_{5}
space, given by

\displaystyle ḏs^{2}=\frac{R^{2}}{z^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-ḏt^{2}+ḏ\bm{x}^{2}+ḏz^{2}}}\right)},

(3)

where
R
is the radius of the AdS and the function
ϕ(z)
is referred as dilaton. In the soft-wall model,

\displaystyle ϕ(z)=ϕ_{\mathrm{SW}}(z)\equiv κ^{2}z^{2}.

(4)

Taking a Fourier component of the field
V_{m}
representing a particle at rest, we write
V_{m}=ε_{m}v(z)\={e}^{-\^{\i}ωt}
, where
ε_{m}
is a unitary vector representing the polarization of the field and
ω
is the energy of the particle. Since the particle is at rest, we identify the energy of the particle with its mass
m
, so that
ω=m
. The equations of motion produced by the action (
2
) in this case is

\displaystyle ω^{2}v(z)-{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{1}{z}+ϕ^{\prime}(z){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}v^{\prime}(z)+v^{\prime\prime}(z)=0.

(5)

It is convenient to define the function
ψ
as

\displaystyle ψ(z)=\sqrt{\frac{R}{z}}\={e}^{-ϕ(z)/2}v(z),

(6)

so that the equation of motion (
5
) assumes the form of a Schrödinger-like equation with eigenvalues
m_{n}^{2}
:

\displaystyle-ψ^{\prime\prime}(z)+V(z)ψ(z)=m_{n}^{2}ψ(z),

(7)

with

\displaystyle V(z)=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+\frac{1}{2z}ϕ^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1}{4}ϕ^{\prime}(z)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}ϕ^{\prime\prime}(z).

(8)

Eqs. (
5
) and (
7
) are valid for any dilaton
\phi
. When a dilaton is given, the simple evaluation of the right hand side of (
8
) gives the potential
V
. At this point there are two different approaches that one can follow. Either choose the form of the dilaton and calculate the corresponding function
V(z)
or follow the opposite procedure.

For the particular case of the soft-wall model
ϕ
is given by (
4
), so that

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{SW}}(z)=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+κ^{4}z^{2}.

(9)

The normalized
1

V_{m}
. Therefore, saying that the field is normalized is equivalent to saying that the value of the integral
\int(R/z)\={e}^{-ϕ(z)}|v(z)|^{2}ḏz=\int|ψ(z)|^{2}ḏz
is 1.

7
) in the soft-wall case are

\displaystyle ψ_{n}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2κ}{n+1}}(κz)^{3/2}\={e}^{-κ^{2}z^{2}/2}L_{n}^{1}(κ^{2}z^{2}),

(10)

with the masses of the states with radial excitation number
n
given by

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}=4κ^{2}(n+1)

(
n=0,1,2...
)
.

(11)

II.2
Heavy mesons

The soft-wall model is therefore able to find Regge trajectories of the form
m_{n}^{2}\sim n
. However, since this model has only one parameter,
κ
, the squared mass of the ground state,
m_{0}^{2}=4κ^{2}
, is not independent of the slope of the slope of the Regge trajectory
m_{n+1}^{2}-m_{n}^{2}=4κ^{2}
. For light mesons, it is not a problem, but heavy mesons like charmonium or bottomonium do not respect this property. We can fit their squared masses with a curve of the form
m_{n}^{2}=a+bn
or, with more convenient parameters,

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}=M_{q}^{2}+4κ^{2}(n+1)

(
n=0,1,2...
)
.

(12)

To interpret the parameter
M_{q}
, note that the mass of a bound state of 2 quarks can be written as the sum of the masses of its constituents,
\textstyle\sum_{q}m_{q}
, plus the binding energy
E_{n}
between them. Therefore,

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}=M_{q}^{2}+4κ^{2}(n+1)={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\!\textstyle\sum_{q}m_{q}+E_{n}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!2}.

(13)

Now, consider that we could turn the binding energy off, making
E_{n}=0
. In this case, the masses of all states should collapse into one single value independent of
n
: the sum of the masses of the constituent quarks. For the expression
M_{q}^{2}+4κ^{2}(n+1)
not to depend on
n
,
κ
must be zero. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,M_{q}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{κ=0}=\textstyle\sum_{q}m_{q},

(14)

meaning that, when
κ=0
, the parameter
M_{q}
is the the sum of the mass of the constituent quarks. We can not keep this assertion if
κ
is not zero, but we can assume that
M_{q}
and
\textstyle\sum_{q}m_{q}
should still be intimately related also in this case.

The question that now arises is this: can we build a holographic model with a new dilaton that is capable of reproducing the spectrum given by Eq. (
12
)? The answer to this question is positive, and we explain how to do it in the next section.

The tangent model of Refs.
2
;
3
;
4
;
5
;
6
;
8
, attempts to shift the masses and does this with satisfactory success. In that model, a term of the form
Mz
is added to the dilaton. This term helps with shifting the predicted masses, making them closer to experimental values. This method, however is not accurate. In particular, the masses of bottomonium and charmonium states present a considerable discrepancy with respect to the experimental data. The success of that model lies on the reasonable fit of the decay constants of charmonium and bottomonium states, which is necessary to describe the thermal properties of these particles. In the next section, however, we will show an improved way of shifting the masses based on Eq. (
12
), solving the so called Schrödinger inverse problem. That means: we show how to find a potential that generates a given mass spectrum.

III
Incorporating the heavy quark masses in the holographic approach

The field equation for the soft-wall model has the form

\displaystyle-ψ_{n}^{\prime\prime}+V_{\mathrm{SW}}ψ_{n}=4κ^{2}(n+1)ψ_{n}\,.

(15)

We want to find a dilaton
ϕ_{I}
that leads to a spectrum of squared masses with the form
M_{q}^{2}+4κ^{2}(n+1)
, appropriate to heavy mesons, so that the corresponding field equation must have the form

\displaystyle-ψ_{n}^{\prime\prime}+V_{I}ψ_{n}={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}M_{q}^{2}+4κ^{2}(n+1){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}ψ_{n},

(16)

where
V_{I}
is the new Schrödinger-like potential generated by the this new dilaton
ϕ_{I}
.

Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) have the same form. They are equal if one imposes

\displaystyle V_{I}(z)=V_{\mathrm{SW}}(z)+M_{q}^{2}=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+M_{q}^{2}+κ^{4}z^{2}.

(17)

Using Eq. (
8
) one finds the dilaton
ϕ_{I}
that generates the potential above. The general solution is

\displaystyle ϕ_{I}(z)=κ^{2}z^{2}-2\ln{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}U(M_{q}^{2}/4κ^{2},0,κ^{2}z^{2})+c_{1}\,L(-M_{q}^{2},-1,κ^{2}z^{2}){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}+c_{2},

(18)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants,
U(a,b,x)
is the Tricomi confluent hypergeometric function, and
L(n,α,x)=L_{n}^{(α)}(x)
is the generalized Laguerre function.

This dilaton goes to a constant when
z
goes to zero. According to the appendix of
7
, this constant can be incorporated into the
\mathrm{AdS}
radius, making the value of
ϕ(0)
arbitrary. For convenience, we choose
c_{2}
such that
ϕ(0)=0
. This gives

\displaystyle ϕ_{I}(z)=κ^{2}z^{2}-2\ln{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}Γ{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+M_{q}^{2}/4κ^{2}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}U(M_{q}^{2}/4κ^{2},0,κ^{2}z^{2})+c_{1}\,L(-M_{q}^{2},-1,κ^{2}z^{2}){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}.

(19)

For recovering the soft-wall model when
M_{q}
is zero, we set
c_{1}=0
, which gives,

\displaystyle ϕ_{I}(z)=κ^{2}z^{2}-2\ln{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}Γ{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+M_{q}^{2}/4κ^{2}}}\right)}U(M_{q}^{2}/4κ^{2},0,κ^{2}z^{2}){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}.

(20)

IV
Obtaining Non linear Regge trajectories

Considering a model based on the quadratic form of the spinless Salpeter-type equation (QSSE) with a Cornell potential, it was found in Ref.
10
that the radial Regge trajectories for quarkonia are not linear. For large
n
they behave as
m_{n}^{2}\sim n^{2/3}
. Masses of charmonium and bottomonium were then fitted to the expression:

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}=β(n+c_{0})^{2/3}+c_{1}.

(21)

It was shown in
10
that the mass spectra obtained from this model finds good agreement with experimental results, even for light mesons.

We show in this section how to use the WKB approximation to build a holographic model that reproduces the same Regge trajectory (
21
). Other approaches to non-linear Regge trajectories in holography, some of them also using the WKB approximation, can be found in
18
;
17
;
19
. In this paper, however, we will look not only to the leading order in large
n
(
m_{n}^{2}\simeq βn^{2/3}
in our case), but, instead, we will use the Langer correction to properly obtain the terms
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
from (
21
).

IV.1
WKB approximation

In the first-order WKB approximation with two turning points, the quantization condition for the equation

\displaystyle-ψ^{\prime\prime}(z)-{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}V(z)+m^{2}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}ψ(z)=0

(22)

is given by

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{a}\sqrt{-Q(z)}\,dz={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}n+1/2{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}\pi,

(23)

where
Q(z)=V(z)-m^{2}
,
n
is an integer, and the points
a
and
b
are, respectively, the largest and the smallest turning points,
i.e.
, the values of
z
for which
Q(z)=0
.

However, because of the presence of the term
3/4z^{2}
in the Schrödinger-like potential (
8
), the WKB approximation is not directly applicable. The WKB procedure produces a solution for
z
close to zero and another solution for
z
close to the turning point
b
. The behaviors of these two parts should match in between.
2
2

2

Formally, the large
z
behavior of the WKB solution at small
z
should coincide with the small argument behavior of the solution close to the turning point
b
.
The derivation of equation Eq. (
23
) depends on this match, known as the connection condition. In the present case, however, these behaviors do not agree. Indeed, the
3/4z^{2}
term is analogous to the angular momentum term
ℓ(ℓ+1)/r^{2}
in the quantum two-body problem, where the same issue occurs. In order to make the WKB approximation applicable to the quantum two body problem, one introduces the Langer transformation
15
, which modifies the radial variable, mapping the origin
r=0
into
-∞
. The wave function is also transformed in order to keep (
22
) with the form of a Schrödinger equation. After the changes, the regularity condition at the origin
r=0
becomes a normalization condition at
-∞
. In the notation of our problem, the Langer transformation reads

\displaystyle\qquad z=\={e}^{x}/κ\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad ψ(z)=\={e}^{x/2}\tilde{ψ}(x),

(24)

where
κ
is a parameter from
V
with dimension of mass.

With this, Eq. (
22
) becomes

\displaystyle-\tilde{ψ}^{\prime\prime}(x)+{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\={e}^{2x}}{κ^{2}}V(\={e}^{x}/κ)+\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\={e}^{2x}}{κ^{2}}m^{2}}}\right)}\tilde{ψ}(x)=0.

(25)

The quantization condition for the new problem is

\displaystyle\int_{\ln(κb)}^{\ln(κa)}\sqrt{-\tilde{Q}(x)}\,dx={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}n+1/2{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}\pi,

(26)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(x)=\frac{\={e}^{2x}}{κ^{2}}V(\={e}^{x}/κ)+\frac{1}{4}-\={e}^{2x},

(27)

and with
{\ln(κa)}
and
{\ln(κb)}
being the turning points of
\tilde{Q}(x)
. By reverting the change of coordinates defined in (
24
), the quantization condition takes the same form as in Eq. (
23
) but now with

\displaystyle Q(z)=U(z)-m^{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad U(z)=V(z)+\frac{1}{4z^{2}}.

(28)

Note that
a
and
b
are the turning points of
Q(z)
and that the
“corrected”
potential
U
differs from the potential
V
by
1/4z^{2}
. This difference is known as the Langer correction.

As the integral in the left-hand side of (
23
) may not be solvable with a closed form in general, it will be useful to expand it in powers of
1/m
, that is considered to be small in the WKB approach. For this reason, we will make use of the series expansion

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}x^{n},

(29)

where
(a)_{n}=Γ(a+n)/Γ(n)
is the Pochhammer symbol, to write

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{a}\sqrt{-Q(z)}\,dz=\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\,m\!\int_{b}^{a}\frac{U^{n}}{m^{2n}}\,dz.

(30)

As an example (that will be useful in the following sections), let
α
be a positive constant and consider a potential of the form

\displaystyle V(z)=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+κ^{2+α}z^{α}\quad\implies\quad U(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}}+κ^{2+α}z^{α}.

(31)

In this case,

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{a}\sqrt{-Q(z)}\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{m^{2n-1}}\int_{b}^{a}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}}}\right)}^{\!k}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(κ^{2+α}z^{α}}}\right)}^{n-k}\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\binom{n}{k}\frac{κ^{(2+α)(n-k)}}{m^{2n-1}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}F_{n,k}(a)-F_{n,k}(b){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)},

(32)

where

\displaystyle F_{n,k}(z)=\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt\begin{matrix}\dfrac{z^{αn-(2+α)k+1}}{αn-(2+α)k+1},&\;\text{if }αn-(2+α)k+1\neq 0,\\

(33)

is a primitive of
z^{αn-(2+α)k}
. The turning points

and
b
are solutions of

\displaystyle a\simeq\frac{1}{κ}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{m}{κ}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!2/α}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}1-\frac{1}{α}{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{κ}{m}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}}^{\!2+4/α}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b\simeq\frac{1}{m}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}1+\frac{1}{2}{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{κ}{m}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}}^{\!2+α}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}.\qquad

Given this, collecting the powers of

. The leading contribution from

\hskip 40.00006pt{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\binom{n}{k}\frac{κ^{(2+α)(n-k)}}{m^{2n-1}}F_{n,k}(a){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{k=0}\\

(35)

To calculate the series, we substitute
\frac{1}{αn+1}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{αn}ḏx

) from the right to the left. This gives

{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\binom{n}{k}\frac{κ^{(2+α)(n-k)}}{m^{2n-1}}F_{n,k}(a){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{k=0}\simeq{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{m}{κ}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!1+2/α}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}x^{α}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{n}ḏx\\

b^{αn-(2+α)k+1}
. These terms give

\displaystyle-{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\binom{n}{k}\frac{κ^{(2+α)(n-k)}}{m^{2n-1}}F_{n,k}(b){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{k=n}\simeq\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\frac{1}{2n-1}m^{0}+\mathcal{O}(m^{-2-α}).\quad

(38)

Now we separate the
n=0
term and substitute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n-1}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{2n-2}ḏx

(
n\neq 0
)

(39)

to obtain

-{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}\binom{n}{k}\frac{κ^{(2+α)(n-k)}}{m^{2n-1}}F_{n,k}(b){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{k=n}\simeq-1+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}x^{2}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!n}-1{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}ḏx\\
=-1+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}-1{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}ḏx=-\frac{π}{2}.

(40)

The next order in
b
comes from setting
k=n-1
, but it is clearly smaller than
m^{0}
.

Using the results (
32
), (
36
) and (
40
), one approximates the quantization condition as

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{a}\sqrt{-Q(z)}\,ḏz\simeq\frac{\Beta(1/α,3/2)}{α}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{m}{κ}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!1+2/α}-\frac{π}{2}={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)}π.

(41)

Therefore,

\displaystyle m_{n}={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{απ}{\Beta(1/α,3/2)}(n+1)}}\right]}^{α/(2+α)}κ.\hskip 50.00008pt

(
n=0,1,2...
)
.

(42)

As a consistency check for the procedure, note that Eq. (
42
) agrees with with the exact soft-wall result found in Eq. (
11
) when
α=2
.

IV.2
Regge Trajectories with power 2/3

From Eq. (
42
), one notes that a Schrödinger-like potential of the form
V(z)=3/4z^{2}+κ^{2+α}z^{α}
produces a Regge trajectory that goes as
m\sim(n+1)^{α/(2+α)}
. So, a trajectory of the form
m^{2}\sim(n+1)^{2/3}
is obtained by choosing
α=1
. This gives

\displaystyle V_{I\!I}(z)=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+κ^{3}z

(43)

and

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}\simeq{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3π}{2}}}\right)}^{\!2/3}(n+1)^{2/3}κ^{2}.

(44)

At this point we have the correct large
n
behavior for the trajectory, but not yet the shift of masses in the first state of heavy mesons due to the heavy quark masses. In Sec.
III
we have shown how to obtain a shift in the masses. We just have to add a term
M_{q}^{2}
to the Schrödinger-like potential. So, we change the potential to

\displaystyle V_{I\!I}(z)=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+κ^{3}z+M_{q}^{2},

(45)

so that

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}\simeq{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3π}{2}}}\right)}^{\!2/3}(n+1)^{2/3}κ^{2}+M_{q}^{2}.

(46)

And the dilaton that produces this potential is, again, the solution of (
8
), with
V
given by (
45
). This time, however, the solution to (
8
) is not analytic, but we can find its large and small
z
behaviors, which are

\displaystyle ϕ_{I\!I}(z)

\displaystyle\simeq-2\ln{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}c_{1}\sqrt{z}\Ai\!{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}κz+M_{q}^{2}/κ^{2}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}+c_{2}\sqrt{z}\Bi\!{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}κz+M_{q}^{2}/κ^{2}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}

(47)

for
z
large, and

\displaystyle ϕ_{I\!I}(z)\simeq-2\ln{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c_{3}+\frac{1}{2}M_{q}^{2}z^{2}\ln(κz)}}\right)}

(48)

for small
z
. The symbols
\Ai
and
\Bi
represent the Airy functions.

We will choose the solution with
c_{2}=0
. This way we avoid problems, that also appears in the soft-wall model with the negative sign for the dilaton, such as the appearance of a null mass state. The large
z
behavior of the dilaton is, then

\displaystyle ϕ_{I\!I}(z)\simeq\frac{4}{3}(κz)^{3/2}+\frac{2M_{q}^{2}}{κ^{2}}\sqrt{κz}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\!{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(κz}}\right)},

(49)

where we expanded the Airy function for large values of its argument.

As in Sec.
III
, we can set
ϕ(0)=0
for convenience. We do this by choosing
c_{3}=1
, so that

\displaystyle ϕ_{I\!I}(z)\simeq-M_{q}^{2}z^{2}\ln(κz)

(50)

for small
z
.

This analysis allows us to find the dilaton by numerically solving Eq. (
8
) using the fact that the dilaton should match the large and small
z
behaviors of Eqs. (
49
) and (
50
) as boundary conditions. The result is plotted in Fig.
4
, in the Sec.
VI
of Numerical Results.

IV.3
Adjusting the parameter
c_{0}

Recall that our objective is to obtain a Regge trajectory of the form

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}=β(n+c_{0})^{2/3}+c_{1}.

(51)

At this point, we have
β=(3π/2)^{2/3}κ
,
c_{1}=M_{q}^{2}
and
c_{0}
is the fixed and universal parameter
c_{0}=1
. However, from the fits in
10
, one notices that
c_{0}=1
is far from the fits of
c_{0}
in
10
. The final step, then, to build a model that yields
m_{n}^{2}=β(n+c_{0})^{2/3}+c_{1}
will be to add a new term to the Schrödinger-like potential, thus promoting
c_{0}
to an adjustable parameter.

The term that does this in the potential must be of the form
1/\sqrt{z}
, so that the new and final form of the potential is

\displaystyle V(z)=\frac{3}{4z^{2}}+\frac{Aκ^{2}}{\sqrt{κz}}+M_{q}^{2}+κ^{3}z,

(52)

where
A
is a dimensionless constant.

We will set
M_{q}
to zero and recover it later by summing it to the squared masses at the end, shifting the masses of the model with
M_{q}=0
, as we saw in Sec.
III
.

Now we must update the WKB calculations of Sec.
IV.1
. The integral that appears in the quantization condition now is

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{a}\sqrt{-Q(z)}\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{∞}{\sum}^{\prime}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{k_{1}!\,k_{2}!\,k_{3}!}\frac{1}{m^{2n-1}}\int_{b}^{a}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{1}{z^{2}}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!\!k_{3}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{Aκ^{3/2}}{\sqrt{z}}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!\!k_{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(κ^{3}z}}\right)}^{\!k_{1}}\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{∞}{\sum}^{\prime}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{k_{1}!\,k_{2}!\,k_{3}!}\frac{A^{k_{2}}κ^{k_{1}+3k_{2}/2}}{m^{2n-1}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}F_{n;k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}(a)-F_{n;k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}(b){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)},

(53)

where

\displaystyle F_{n;k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}(z)=\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt\begin{matrix}\dfrac{z^{-2k_{3}-k_{2}/2+k_{1}+1}}{-2k_{3}-k_{2}/2+k_{1}+1},&\;\text{if }-2k_{3}-k_{2}/2+k_{1}+1\neq 0,\\
\ln{κz},&\;\text{if }-2k_{3}-k_{2}/2+k_{1}+1=0.\end{matrix}

(54)

We used the multinomial theorem, so that the summation with the prime is performed over
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
and
k_{3}
ranging from
0
to
n
and with the constraint
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=n
. The expressions for the turning points change to

\displaystyle\qquad a\simeq\frac{m^{2}}{κ^{3}}-\frac{A}{m}\qquad\quad\text{and}\qquad\quad b\simeq\frac{1}{m}+\frac{Aκ^{3/2}}{2m^{7/2}}.\qquad

(55)

The first contribution from
a
to the integral (
57
) comes from setting
k_{1}=n
and
k_{2}=k_{3}=0
. Its calculation is very similar to what was done in (
36
), but the the order
1/m
in the expression for
a
now lefts a contribution of order
m^{0}
in
m^{1-2n}F_{n;n,0,0}(a)
. This smaller contribution, however, vanishes in the final summation. The result is

\hskip 40.00006pt{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,\sum_{n=0}^{∞}{\sum}^{\prime}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{k_{1}!\,k_{2}!\,k_{3}!}\frac{A^{k_{2}}κ^{k_{1}+3k_{2}/2}}{m^{2n-1}}F_{n;k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}(a){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{k_{1}=n,k_{2}=k_{3}=0}\\
\simeq\sum_{n=0}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{n!}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{m^{3}}{κ^{3}}-Am^{0}}}\right)}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{m^{3}}{κ^{3}}.\hskip 40.00006pt

(56)

A new contribution from
a
is now present, it comes from
k_{1}=n-1
,
k_{2}=1
and
k_{3}=0
:

\hskip 40.00006pt{\!\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\,\sum_{n=0}^{∞}{\sum}^{\prime}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{k_{1}!\,k_{2}!\,k_{3}!}\frac{A^{k_{2}}κ^{k_{1}+3k_{2}/2}}{m^{2n-1}}F_{n;k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}(a){\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right|}_{k_{1}=n-1,k_{2}=1,k_{3}=0}\\
\simeq\sum_{n=1}^{∞}\frac{(-1/2)_{n}}{(n-1)!}\frac{2A}{2n-1}\frac{m^{3}}{κ^{3}}=-\frac{Aπ}{2}.\hskip 40.00006pt

(57)

The contribution from
b
is
-π/2
, exactly the same as the one found in Eq. (
40
). Then, we write the quantization condition as

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{a}\sqrt{-Q(z)}\,ḏz\simeq\frac{2}{3}\frac{m^{3}}{κ^{3}}-\frac{Aπ}{2}-\frac{π}{2}={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)}π.

(58)

Therefore, recovering the term
M_{q}^{2}
, the 1st order WKB approximation for the spectrum of squared masses is

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{3π}{2}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n+1+A/2}}\right)}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}^{2/3}κ^{2}+M_{q}^{2}\hskip 50.00008pt

(
n=0,1,2...
)
.

(59)

Hence, by comparing this to the Regge trajectory of Eq. (
51
), we see that this model gives

\displaystyle β=(3π/2)^{2/3}κ^{2},\qquad\quad c_{0}=1+A/2,\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{1}=M_{q}^{2}.

(60)

V
Comparison to the QSSE model

In Ref.
10
one finds an expression for the parameter
β
from Eq. (
51
). However, there are no expressions for the parameters
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
. From the WKB approach with the Langer correction and with the method of collecting the leading orders in powers of
1/m
explained here, one can write an equation analogous to (
32
) and (
53
) for the QSSE model, and find expressions for the QSSE versions of the parameters
β
,
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
as well. This procedure gives

\displaystyle m_{n}^{2}={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}12πm_{q}σ{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n+3/4}}\right)}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}^{2/3}+4m_{q}^{2}\hskip 50.00008pt

(
n=0,1,2...
)
,

(61)

so that we identify

\displaystyle β=(12πm_{q}σ)^{2/3},\qquad\quad c_{0}=3/4,\qquad\quad\text{and}\qquad\quad c_{1}=4m_{q}^{2},

(62)

where
m_{q}
is the constituent quark mass and
σ
is the string tension found in the linear term of the Cornell potential.

Comparison of (
60
) to (
62
) allows us to interpret our holographic parameters
κ
and
M_{q}^{2}
. This confirms that
M_{q}
can, indeed, be interpreted as the sum of the masses of the constituent quarks, since

\displaystyle c_{1}=M_{q}^{2}=4m_{q}^{2}\implies M_{q}=2m_{q}.

(63)

Additionally, we see that
κ
is related to the string tension through

\displaystyle β=(12πm_{q}σ)^{2/3}=(3π/2)^{2/3}κ^{2}\implies κ=(8m_{q}σ)^{1/3}.

(64)

Finally, we left
A
as a free parameter, but if we want the large
m
behavior of our model to perfectly match the one given by the Cornell potential in the QSSE, we can set
A=-1/2
, so that

\displaystyle c_{0}=1+A/2=3/4.

(65)

VI
Numerical Results

In this section we show the results found for the best fits and the values of the normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) defined as

\displaystyle\textrm{NRMSE}=100\%\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{N}\sum\!\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\rule{0.0pt}{9.90276pt}_{\,i\,=\,1}^{N}$}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}}{\mean(\hat{y}_{i})}}}\right)}^{\!2}},

(66)

where
N
is the number of experimental points, the
y_{i}
’s are the values of masses or decay constants predicted by the model, and the
\hat{y}_{i}
’s are the experimental values of masses or decay constants.

Two different possibilities were considered. The first one,
that leads to the best fit of experimental masses, is to fix
A=-1/2
and look for the values of
M_{q}
and
\kappa
that minimize the error. We will refer to this as the two parameter case. In this case, the 1st order WKB approaches of the AdS/QCD model and of the QSSE model find a perfect match.

The second case analyzed, a three parameter case, corresponds to
leaving the parameter
A
free and look for the set of values
of
A
,
M_{q}
and
κ
that leads to the smallest error for the combined sets of masses and decay constants. To calculate the errors for this set, we define the combined normalized root mean square error (CNRMSE) as

\displaystyle\textrm{CNRMSE}=100\%\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{N}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\sum\!\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\rule{0.0pt}{9.90276pt}_{\,n\,=\,0}^{N_{m}-1}$}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{m_{n}-\hat{m}_{n}}{\mean(\hat{m}_{n})}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!\!2}+\sum\!\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\rule{0.0pt}{9.90276pt}_{\,n\,=\,0}^{N_{\!f}-1}$}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right.\hskip-1.0pt}\frac{f_{n}-\hat{f}_{n}}{\mean(\hat{f}_{n})}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right)}^{\!\!2}{\hskip-1.0pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\rule{0.0pt}{3.22916pt}}}\right]}},

(67)

where the
m
’s are the masses, the
f
’s are the decay constants,
N_{m}
is the number of measured masses,
N_{\!f}
is the number of measured decay constants, and
N=N_{m}+N_{\!f}
is the total number of experimental points.

All the experimental values were taken from the Particle Data Group (PDG)
21
and we followed
14
to use
ψ(4660)
as the
5S
state of the vector charmonium, but we don’t have an experimental value for its decay constant.

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we developed a holographic model for heavy vector mesons that incorporates features that are present in the phenomenology of quarkonia states. Starting from the soft-wall model, we discussed in section III how to introduce a mass shift to account for the constituent quark masses.
