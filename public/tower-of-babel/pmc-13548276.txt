Infection prevention and control measures during the COVID-19 pandemic in sub-Saharan Africa: a multinational cross-sectional study
Authors: Lena Böff, Kouassi Julien Momou, Jean Florent Rafamatanantsoa, Francis Mosala, Felix Reichert, Idesbald Boone, Sounan Fidèle Touré, Pati Pyana, Lantonirina Ravaoarisoa, Sagesse Nduenga, Anna-Lisa Behnke, Andele Conradie, Khaled Ferchichi, Marek Fuchs, Sophie Müller, Chinwe Lucia Ochu, Giuseppina Ortu, Francisco Pozo-Martin, Angela Schuster, Ann Christin Vietor, Sabrina Weiss, Tim Eckmanns, Bamourou Diané, Steve Ahuka, Zely Randriamanantany, Chantal Akoua-Koffi, Sara Tomczyk, Tochi Okwor, Charbel El Bcheraoui

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the implementation of infection prevention and control (IPC) measures and associated factors in healthcare in low-resource settings during the COVID-19 pandemic.DesignMultinational cross-sectional study.SettingThe study was conducted from February to November 2022 in Côte d’Ivoire, Democratic Republic of Congo, Madagascar and Nigeria.ParticipantsA total of 6749 healthcare workers (HCWs) at 324 healthcare facilities (HCFs) were enrolled from different levels of care and types.Primary and secondary outcomesStandardised HCW and HCF questionnaires assessed COVID-19-related exposures and IPC measures and descriptive analyses were conducted overall and by country and HCF. Partial proportional odds models were used to assess factors associated with HCW compliance to hand hygiene and mask wearing.ResultsAmong 324 HCFs, the reported presence of IPC programmes ranged from 51.4% (n=111) at primary non-hospitals to 83.3% (n=18) at tertiary HCFs. More than half reported no patient or HCW screening (57.4%, n=186). Only 19.8% (n=64) reported handrub at point of care in every room. Among 6749 enrolled HCWs, 54.0% were working in high-risk patient care. More HCWs reported sufficient availability of masks (62.7%, n=4231) compared with respirators (28.5%, n=1926). HCW compliance with hand hygiene and mask wearing, respectively, was improved by presence of an IPC programme (OR: 1.3, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.5; OR: 1.4, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.6), IPC training received by the HCW (OR: 1.5, 95% CI 1.3 to 1.7; OR: 1.3, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.5) and availability of handrub and masks, respectively (OR: 5.9, 95% CI 4.1 to 8.4; OR: 2.3, 95% CI 2.0 to 2.7).ConclusionsWe conducted a large survey including HCFs across levels of care and type in urban and rural regions in sub-Saharan Africa. Critical gaps in IPC programmes and access to IPC equipment during the COVID-19 pandemic hindered HCW compliance with recommended IPC practices. To improve general infection control and pandemic preparedness in low-resource settings, continued focus on strengthening IPC programmes and ensuring access to materials/equipment is essential.

Introduction

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are the backbone of health service delivery. They help prevent and control pathogen transmission within healthcare systems and the broader population, while being exposed to potential sources of infection.1 2 During the COVID-19 pandemic, their role as an indispensable frontline workforce became even more evident globally.3–9 Women make up a particularly important proportion of HCWs globally, often working in more risk-exposed positions.10 11

Infection prevention and control (IPC) measures are a vital instrument of resilient health systems to protect the health of both HCWs and patients.12 Evidence-based IPC procedures have been shown to be the primary approach to reducing healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), antimicrobial resistance and the transmission of emerging pathogens, which have a serious impact on mortality, morbidity and health systems worldwide.13–16 Appropriate IPC measures, sufficient supply of personal protective equipment (PPE) and context-adapted training are essential to enabling HCWs to operate safely while ensuring patient safety.13 17–19

To promote IPC improvement, the WHO issued guidelines on the core components of IPC programmes at the national and acute healthcare facility (HCF) level in 2016.20 During the COVID-19 pandemic, these IPC recommendations were adapted for use in WHO guidance on COVID-19 management along with HCF recommendations for standard and transmission-based precautions.21–23

However, the implementation of IPC core components in low- and middle-income countries can be a challenge. In many of these settings, a higher burden of HAIs has been demonstrated.24 Global surveys have highlighted gaps such as budget constraints, lack of established surveillance and insufficient built environment including availability of materials and equipment.25 26 This is particularly true across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where facilities have reported low hand hygiene implementation scores and serious gaps in IPC infrastructure.26 27 In a multinational survey, the SSA region scored lowest on perceived IPC preparedness during the COVID-19 pandemic.28

Providing sufficient IPC materials such as PPE is a minimum requirement for IPC. During the COVID-19 pandemic, HCWs reported shortages in the provision of critical PPE like masks and gloves in a global survey, ranging from 64.2% of surveyed HCWs in low-income countries to 27.4% in high-income countries.28 29 In addition to the availability of PPE, evidence reviews have shown that other factors like IPC education and training approaches in facilities, IPC guidelines and protocol implementation strategies, personal characteristics including professional category, experience, workload and age, and structural factors including level of care and water supply in HCFs also have an impact on HCW compliance with IPC measures.30–39 Furthermore, access to basic handwashing facilities including soap and water in the general population was about 20% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Côte d’Ivoire and around one third in Nigeria, illustrating the general lack of built environment components that also affect the provision of safe healthcare services in these settings.40 However, many of these IPC-focused studies in SSA were limited to single urban tertiary HCF studies, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive IPC assessment across HCF level and type of care and urbanicity to inform actionable recommendations.

Leveraging the circumstances created by the COVID-19 pandemic to inform future preparedness plans, we assessed the implementation of the core components of IPC programmes and PPE availability through a transnational study in 324 HCFs from four SSA countries. Specifically, we examined the compliance of HCWs with IPC measures such as hand hygiene and mask wearing as well as key factors affecting their compliance. We thus aimed to improve understanding of gaps in IPC implementation and preparedness across diverse healthcare settings in SSA.

Material and methods

Study design and population

The study took place in 2022 as part of the multinational BCHW project (Burden of COVID-19 among healthcare workers),41 a mixed-method seroepidemiological multicentre study across four SSA countries: Côte d’Ivoire (CIV), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Madagascar (MDG) and Nigeria (NIG). Enrolment of HCFs and HCWs in target regions and districts took place between February and November 2022, although data collection periods varied in relation to each country’s respective COVID-19 epidemic waves (online supplemental figure 3) and vaccination coverage (online supplemental figure 4).

As previously described, the sample size was calculated based on the main outcome of seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2, equalling 761 HCWs for each of the initially planned three sampling timepoints and regions.41 First, target regions were selected in each country based on logistics, security concerns, consideration of COVID-19 burden and scarcity of data on seroepidemiology (ie, inclusion of remote areas). Next, we aimed to use a multistage stratified random sampling with subregions as the primary sampling unit, HCF as the secondary sampling unit, and HCW as the tertiary sampling unit. After considering accessibility and safety, certain areas were excluded from selection and some sampling approaches had to be adapted (online supplemental tables 2, 5 and figure 5). Namely, CIV had to purposively sample districts and HCFs. In CIV, data were collected in three regions over one time point. In DRC, data were collected in two regions over four survey time points, each covering a certain geographical area. In MDG, data were collected in four regions over two survey timepoints. In NIG, data were collected in two regions over a period of 7 months. Only MDG and NIG included private HCFs. In MDG and NIG, tertiary care facilities included university hospitals as well as private HCFs, while in CIV and DRC, uniquely public HCFs up to the secondary level of care were included, given the more remote and rural nature of the regions and districts selected.42 The study population comprised all consenting HCWs aged 18 years or above. Following the WHO definition, HCWs thereby included staff involved in direct patient care, personnel with indirect patient contact via biological fluids or potentially contaminated objects, administrative staff and auxiliary workers.43

Patient and public involvement

There was no active involvement of study participants in study design, data analysis and writing.

Data collection

Two questionnaires were developed through a systematic stepwise process. Initial questionnaire drafts were adapted from evidence-based WHO instruments and designed in exchange with a survey methodology expert to ensure appropriate question formulation and consistent structure. The HCW questionnaire (online supplemental table 3) was developed based on the WHO interim guidance on risk assessment in the context of COVID-19 and included questions on sociodemographic characteristics, occupational and private exposures to SARS-CoV-2, IPC measures at the workplace, and COVID-19 test history and vaccination status.43 The HCF questionnaire (online supplemental table 4) included modified questions from the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) which measures the implementation of the core components of IPC programmes and additional questions on the HCF’s COVID-19 pandemic-specific measures such as strategies for testing and rational use of PPE.44 The instruments were drafted in English and French, reviewed by each of the SSA research partners and pilot tested with approximately 90 HCWs across all four countries. The pilot feedback was then used to revise the assessment instruments accordingly (eg, alignment with country-specific contexts such as HCW profession terminology as well as improvement of skip logic for clarity). Pilot results were not included in the final analysis. The final HCW questionnaire was administered to each participating HCW by trained data collectors (ie, who were also HCW by background). The training for data collectors consisted of interviewing techniques, including standardised questioning and neutral prompting, and IPC theory (eg, SARS-CoV-2 exposures, HCF and HCW risk factors and PPE) to improve the reliability of interviews. The interviews were conducted in the official administrative language of each country (ie, English (NIG) or French (CIV, DRC and MDG)). If the participant did not speak this language, the data collector would translate the questions into the local language. Participants did not receive any financial or non-financial incentives for participation. The HCF questionnaire was completed once per facility by the manager of each HCF included in the study.

Data were collected using Open Data Kit (ODK).45 Only completely filled questionnaires could be uploaded and stored on ODK servers. Data were anonymised within each SSA partner country, then combined from all countries for quality control. During the data collection period, regular data quality checks were conducted using data management pipelines developed in R. These assessed the number of conducted interviews, duration of interviews, completeness of the reported variables and the plausibility of basic demographic information. Identified inconsistencies were fed back to and discussed with local study coordinators, who regularly observed interviews on site and could intervene if necessary. During data processing, free-text responses were categorised and categorical variables were re-grouped consistently across the four countries.

Definition of variables

HCFs were categorised into public or private type of care, according to their legal ownership. HCF level of care (tertiary, secondary or primary) was defined by scope and specialisation of provided health services,42 while a difference was made between health centres covering basic care (primary non-hospital) and hospitals not meeting the criteria for secondary care (primary hospital). As healthcare systems varied in each country, the categorisation followed the specific national context and interpretation (online supplemental table 5).

For questions about a HCW’s exposure to SARS-CoV2, COVID-19 patients were defined as those who had laboratory-confirmed or suspected COVID-19 disease. A HCW’s contact with a COVID-19 patient in the healthcare setting included talking to, caring for, touching or working in the room of COVID-19 patients, with close contact defined as within less than 1 m lasting over 15 min (online supplemental table 3).43 HCWs’ professional activities were grouped into ‘high’, ‘medium’ and ‘low’ risk groups based on healthcare professional group and type of reported patient contact (online supplemental table 6). Facility departments were divided into risk areas according to the likelihood of exposure to the virus, using input from all country partners (online supplemental table 7). Questions related to the exposures, contacts and IPC practices of HCWs in the private setting were also assessed.

IPC policies and practices on HCF level were assessed on a binary ‘Yes/No’ scale, except isolation capacity, where frequency was assessed and then recoded into ‘Yes’ (‘Always’ and ‘Most of the time’) and ‘No’ (‘Sometimes’, ‘Rarely’ and ‘Never’) for analysis purposes. Among HCWs, sufficient availability of PPE (including masks, respirators, face shields, gloves and goggles) in the facility departments in the 3 months prior to the survey was assessed on a binary ‘Yes/No’ scale. Individual HCWs reported the frequency of sufficient availability of alcohol-based handrub on an ordinal frequency scale (‘Always’, ‘Most of the time’, ‘Occasionally’, ‘Rarely’, ‘Never’) (online supplemental table 3).

Individual hand hygiene behaviour of HCWs in relation to IPC recommendations was assessed according to a modified version of the WHO five moments of hand hygiene (online supplemental table 3).46 HCWs reported frequency of hand hygiene performed during these exposures on an ordinal frequency scale (‘Always’, ‘Most of the time’, ‘Occasionally’, ‘Rarely’, ‘Never’ and ‘Not applicable’). HCWs who reported consistently performing hand hygiene ‘Always’ in all relevant moments were classified as ‘Full compliance’, those who reported ‘Most of the time’ were classified as ‘Moderate compliance’ and those who reported ‘Occasionally’ or less frequently in at least one relevant moment were grouped as ‘Low compliance’. If no moments applied, compliance was ‘Not applicable’. The frequency of mask wearing in the HCF was assessed on an ordinal frequency scale from ‘Never’ to ‘Always’ and similarly recoded to ‘Full compliance’ (ie, always wearing a mask), ‘Moderate compliance’ (ie, wearing mask most of the time) or ‘Low compliance’ (ie, wearing mask occasionally or less frequently; online supplemental table 4).

Statistical analysis

Data were managed and analysed using R in V.4.1.3 (2022-03-10).47 48 Data cleaning used the tidyr49 and dplyr48 packages. HCWs recruitment over the survey period was descriptively compared with national infection numbers (online supplemental figure 3).50 Absolute and relative frequencies of HCFs, individual HCWs characteristics, and reported exposures (ie, categorical variables) were described overall and stratified by country and HCF. Radar plots (fmsb package) visualised the setting and implementation of IPC core components and measures at HCF level.51 Ordinal-scaled frequency and compliance of IPC measures were visualised using ggplot bar charts and heat plots.52 Proportions of PPE availability among different strata were compared by Fisher’s exact tests.

Based on a literature review and expert opinions, we identified factors with a potential impact on hand hygiene and mask wearing compliance. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) were drafted using dagitty.net to clarify causal dependencies and define minimal covariate adjustment sets for estimating effects of identified exposures of interest on respective outcomes (online supplemental figures 1 and 2).53 Country was set as covariate to account for differences in national health systems. Given the ordinal scale of the compliance outcomes, we ran separate partial proportional odds (PPO) models for every exposure-outcome combination, using the VGAM package.54 55 Models were adjusted by the sets of covariates identified with the respective DAG and exposure variables and covariates deviating from the proportional odds (PO) assumption were permitted to vary in their effect across outcome categories (eg, low to moderate compliance and moderate to full compliance) (online supplemental tables 24 and 25).56 For variables following the PO assumption, singular coefficients were reported. ORs and 95% CIs were reported along with forest plots. Using complete case analyses, HCWs who reported that the five moments of hand hygiene were not applicable to their work were excluded.

Given the dynamic COVID-19 pandemic situation during the year of enrolment, a sensitivity analysis of results comparing the period from February to August 2022 (ie, a reported break in infection waves across countries) to the overall study sample was performed (online supplemental figure 3).

Overall, 54.0% of HCWs reported working in high-risk patient care. The proportion of female HCWs was higher in the high-risk (69.5%) and medium-risk (55.7%) compared with low-risk (42.3%) professional groups (online supplemental tables 11–13). Slightly less HCWs in the high-risk group indicated participation in an IPC training (49.2%), though this proportion of HCWs in the high-risk professional group was higher in NIG (71.4%), DRC (27.8%) and CIV (27.2%) compared with the proportion in other risk groups, respectively. In MDG, approximately half of HCWs in all three risk groups indicated participation in an IPC training (online supplemental table 13). In all countries, more male HCWs working in the high-risk group had participated in a previous IPC training than women working in this group (online supplemental table 12).

HCF IPC implementation

At the HCF level, the implementation of IPC measures during the COVID-19 pandemic varied across countries and HCF level and type (figure 1; online supplemental tables 8–10). Overall, the implementation of selected WHO IPC core components assessing the presence of an IPC programme, committee, team and guidelines generally increased with increasing HCF level of care (figure 1a). The reported presence of an IPC programme ranged from 51.4% (111/216) in primary non-hospital HFCs to 83.3% (15/18) for IPC programmes in tertiary HCFs. There was great variability in the reported implementation of COVID-19 control measures across countries and HCF level of care (figure 1b). More than half of HCFs reported no patient or HCW screening (57.4%, n=186); this ranged from 71.3% (n=154) in primary non-hospital HCFs to 44.4% (n=4) in tertiary HCFs (online supplemental table 9). The presence of a separate COVID-19 ward was indicated by at least 50% of secondary and tertiary facilities in DRC, MDG and NIG whereas HCFs in CIV did not have this capacity. Mask policies for HCWs were in place in more than half of HCFs across all countries (53.8% in CIV, 58.3% in NIG, 76.7% in DRC and 82.3% in MDG) and in higher percentages of primary non-hospital (73.1%) and tertiary (77.8%) than of primary hospital (57.1%) and secondary HCFs (65.8%). For most HCFs in CIV, MDG and NIG, mask policies required the use of at least a surgical mask, whereas the majority of HCFs in DRC allowed textile masks. Less than 40% of all HCFs across level of care indicated the availability of handrub at the point of care in every room, while this ranged from 12% in public to 31% in private HCFs (online supplemental table 8–10).

Availability of PPE

The availability of PPE and handrub reported by HCWs also varied by country (figure 2; online supplemental tables 13–16). Only 28.5% (n=1926) of HCWs reported that respirators were available, ranging from 17% in DRC to 34% in NIG by country and 20% of primary to 41% of tertiary HCFs by level of care (online supplemental tables 14 and 15). Whereby, more than half of HCWs (62.7%; n=4231) reported that surgical masks were available. HCWs in primary non-hospital (60%) and primary hospital HCFs (58%) reported lower mask availability than those in secondary (64%) and tertiary (68%) HCFs. The availability of gloves reported by HCWs varied from 39% in CIV to 71% in NIG. Handrub was reported as ‘always available’ by 58.7% (n=3962) of HCWs, although only 19.8% (n=64) of HCFs reported it at the point of care in every room. This availability of handrub reported by HCWs also ranged by country (64.7% to 68.1% in CIV, MDG and NIG compared with 20.5% in DRC). Nonetheless, the majority of HCFs (more than 90% in MDG and NIG, 83% in CIV, 70% in DRC) indicated the availability of handwashing facilities with soap and running water in each ward (figure 2). This also ranged from 85% of primary, 97% of secondary and 100% of tertiary HCFs (online supplemental figures 7, 8 and tables 14, 15). Higher availability of masks, gloves and handrub was reported for private HCFs (p<0.0005). In DRC and NIG, HCWs in the high-risk group indicated higher availability of masks and handrub than those in the low-risk group (online supplemental figure 6).

Mask and hand hygiene compliance

More than 50% of HCWs in MDG and NIG, approximately one third of HCWs in CIV, and 13% of HCWs in DRC reported ‘always’ wearing a mask in the HCF (figure 3; online supplemental tables 17–22). Reported hand hygiene varied by country, ranging from 26% and 31% of HCWs in DRC indicating ‘always’ practising hand hygiene before and after touching patients to 61% and 68% of HCWs in NIG, respectively. Across all countries, higher percentages of HCWs reported always performing hand hygiene after touching a patient or an aseptic procedure than before a respective moment. The percentage of HCWs specifically exposed to COVID-19 patients who reported ‘always’ performing hand hygiene was higher than among HCWs in general (online supplemental table 23).

HCWs working in HCFs with an IPC programme in place showed higher compliance of both hand hygiene compliance and mask use (figure 4) (p<0.0005). Likewise, HCWs in a facility with IPC programmes had 1.3 (95% CI 1.2 to 1.5) and 1.4 (95% CI 1.2 to 1.6) times the odds of higher hand hygiene and mask compliance levels, respectively, compared with HCWs without IPC programmes in their HCF (figure 5). Compared with those reporting handrub ‘never’ available, HCWs who reported that handrub was ‘always available’ in their HCFs had significantly higher odds of moderate to full compliance with hand hygiene (OR: 5.9, 95% CI 4.1 to 8.4) and low to moderate compliance (OR: 5.1, 95% CI 3.5 to 7.5). Where handrub was available ‘most of the time’ compared with ‘never’ available, the odds of moderate to full compliance were still 5.6 times (95% CI 3.8 to 8.3) higher. HCWs who reported available masks also had 2.3 (95% CI 2.0 to 2.7) higher odds of mask compliance compared with those reporting no available masks. HCWs who reported having received IPC training since the beginning of the pandemic had significantly higher odds of greater compliance with both hand hygiene (OR: 1.5, 95% CI 1.3 to 1.7) and mask wearing (OR: 1.3, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.5); increased compliance was also seen among HCWs in higher professional risk groups (figure 5). HCWs in tertiary HCFs reported significantly 1.7 times higher odds (95% CI 1.4 to 2.0) for moderate to full mask wearing compliance compared with HCWs in primary non-hospital HCFs. HCWs in private HCFs had slightly higher odds for moderate and full handrub compliance compared with those in the public sector (OR: 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.6). Results of the sensitivity analysis restricted to February to August 2022 (ie, a relative break in reported COVID-19 infection waves across participating countries) were broadly consistent with the overall analysis, with no substantial differences in the direction or magnitude of the observed associations.

Discussion

Our study broadly examines the implementation of the IPC core components, PPE availability and compliance with hand hygiene and mask use reported by HCWs and HCFs in different settings across four SSA countries during the COVID-19 pandemic. We summarised findings from more than 6700 HCWs and 320 HCFs of varying HCF levels of care and types. Importantly, our findings extend existing IPC evidence beyond urban tertiary referral HCFs to more remote territories of participating countries, where IPC infrastructure constraints may be significant despite these facilities representing the main point of care for many populations.

The reported implementation of IPC core components and acute containment measures varied across countries but generally increased with higher HCF level of care and in private ownership.20 Although a majority of secondary and tertiary HCFs reported the presence of an IPC programme, fewer had specific measures such as screening strategies or the needed elements of a built environment for safe healthcare provision. Although the WHO IPC core components clearly outline the importance of equipment availability at the point of care, we found that handrub was only partially available in selected departments or rooms across HCFs and countries.13

In our study, 49%–69% of HCWs across countries reported the availability of surgical masks, and only 17%–34% reported available respirators. Similar PPE shortages and inequities across profession risk groups and levels of care have been documented in other COVID-19 IPC surveys in low-resource settings,28 suggesting persistent gaps in the distribution of IPC materials to settings often with the highest risks. A narrative review of a COVID-19 initiative involving primary HCFs in 22 African countries likewise identified sustainable PPE supply as a critical priority.57 Together, these findings emphasise the importance of ongoing global investments in sustainable and equitable IPC supply chains, alongside strengthened local institutional prioritisation of IPC across all HCF levels of care. In response to such challenges, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) adopted an IPC legal framework for the African region in 2023 to support strengthened IPC and universal health coverage.58 Given the limited availability of hand rub reported in our study, local production of IPC products may represent a viable strategy to help address persistent shortages.59

Compared with previous studies in the surveyed countries, we found some comparable estimates of reported compliance with selected hygiene measures. In single HCF studies prior to the pandemic, 70.1% of HCWs in Nigeria reported usually wearing gloves before handling patients or patients’ care products, while 58.5% always practised hand hygiene after touching patients, similar to our findings.32 38 During the COVID-19 pandemic, a study among Congolese HCWs in referral and university hospitals reported that 55% of HCWs complied with good hygiene practices and 55% used PPE consistently during contact with patients with respiratory symptoms.37 We observed lower proportions of HCWs reporting always wearing masks when exposed to COVID-19 patients, which may reflect the inclusion of more primary HCFs in remote areas with potentially lower IPC capacity and PPE availability, as well as our stricter definition of compliance based on HCWs reporting ‘always’ implementing IPC practices.

Notably, the implementation of an IPC programme in the HCF, the completion of IPC training since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic and the uninterrupted availability of handrub and masks significantly increased the odds of HCW compliance with both hand hygiene and mask wearing compared with the counter scenario, and in line with previous studies.30 This underscores that IPC capacity during health emergencies is not only dependent on technical guidance, but also on broader health system resilience, including equitable supply chains, workforce training and sustained institutional support across all levels of care. In particular, effective IPC programmes can play a foundational role. A central goal of IPC programmes is the prevention of nosocomial transmission among patients, HCW and their close contacts. Future research could assess the frequency and transmission pathways of HAIs in relation to the IPC measures implemented by HCFs in these regions. WHO estimates that effective IPC programmes at HCFs can improve IPC practices and reduce HAIs by 70%.60 A systematic review evaluating the evidence on IPC interventions in HCFs in Africa also found that continuous training and the availability of hand hygiene supplies are critical drivers of IPC compliance.61 Ongoing investments are needed for the sustainable development of WHO IPC core components such as IPC programmes for better preparedness, readiness and response to health emergencies.62

Although hand hygiene after contact with patients or potentially infectious fluids is a protective measure for the next patient the HCW cares for, it can also be seen as a self-protection measure for the HCW. In our study, a higher proportion of compliance with hand hygiene recommendations was reported in the moments following exposure to risk than for pre-contact measures, which is consistent with other studies.35 39 63 For the interruption of pathogen transmission and patient safety, IPC procedures that are protective for both the patient and HCW are necessary. Routine HCF multimodal approaches to IPC including IPC education and training, workplace reminders as well as audit and feedback can help increase the necessary hand hygiene awareness.61

The proportion of HCWs in the study reporting awareness of exposure to COVID-19 patients was relatively low across most countries. Awareness of one’s exposure depends on the availability of tests and established patient screening procedures, which were not consistently implemented at HCFs, especially at lower levels of care, and may therefore have resulted in exposure misclassification. In addition, differences in surveillance capacity and communication of local epidemic information may have influenced HCWs’ perceived risk and adherence to IPC measures across settings.

The proportion of women in the study sample and especially in the high-risk profession group was higher than men. Globally, women make up 70% of the health and social care workforce, with as many as 90% of nurses identifying as female.11 Performing a major part of such patient care tasks, women were often shown to account for higher proportions of COVID-19 infections among HCWs.10 We also found a gender gap in access to IPC training, stressing that women and men must be equally provided with the necessary IPC resources and education to enable them to protect themselves, their peers and patients. Furthermore, a higher scarcity of PPE items was found among high risk professions and women made up the majority of such patient-facing roles, so this could also represent gender inequities in risk exposure.64

Although we aimed to conduct a robust survey with a standardised protocol across four SSA countries, several limitations should be noted. First, the logistical constraints and security considerations related to the inclusion of more remote HCFs necessitated adapting the multistage stratified random sampling strategy which may have affected the representativeness of our findings across countries (online supplemental table 2). Notably, there was a predominance of primary-level HCF study sites included, but this also reflected the healthcare infrastructure in the included regions, given that smaller HCFs in rural settings often represent the main accessible points of care. In addition, data collection periods varied across countries in relation to each country’s varying COVID-19 epidemic waves and vaccination programmes (online supplemental figures 3 and 4). These differences in epidemiologic context and individual protection may have influenced HCWs’ risk perception and reported IPC practices and should be considered when comparing country results. Next, although interviewers were trained in a standardised manner prior to data collection across all countries, they may have deviated across the different local settings, such as when questionnaire translation was needed into other local languages beyond English and French, which could have affected HCW question perception. For example, this may have played a role in the differences seen in the level of reported ‘not applicable’ responses from HCWs across countries. Lastly, our study assessed self-reported information on IPC implementation, access and compliance rather than direct observational audits. Self-reported behavioural data are inherently susceptible to recall, social desirability and misclassification bias and may overestimate compliance compared with observational assessments.65 This may partly explain instances where high reported compliance with hand hygiene or mask use co-occurred with reported shortages of corresponding IPC materials such as hand disinfectants or face masks. While direct observation is also recommended by WHO to monitor IPC performance, such as hand hygiene,66 67 conducting standardised observational audits with trained IPC auditors across more than 320 HCFs in four SSA countries was not operationally feasible within the scope of this survey. Nevertheless, the systematic questionnaire development process,43 interviewer training and data quality control likely improved the consistency of the collected data relative to other studies using non-standardised self-reported survey instruments.30

Conclusions

Our study showed that essential gaps in IPC core component implementation and PPE availability were critical determinants for HCWs in SSA to adequately perform their essential tasks during the COVID-19 pandemic. When frontline HCWs are provided with the support of an effective IPC programme, continuous training and necessary supplies, they are empowered to implement improved IPC for HCW and patient safety. We hope these findings from a comprehensive IPC assessment in SSA can help to further inform local and global decision makers working on IPC improvement and preparedness.
