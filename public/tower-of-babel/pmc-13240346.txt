Shared Experiences and Care Improvement Priorities for Multimorbidity Management: An Experience‐Based Co‐Design Study
Authors: Binu Koirala, Chitchanok Benjasirisan, Arum Lim, Katie E Nelson, Lyndsay DeGroot, Rebecca Wright, Cheryl R Dennison Himmelfarb, Patricia M Davidson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMultimorbidity—coexistence of two or more chronic conditions in the same individual—is a growing global healthcare challenge. Despite recognition of the difficulties in managing multimorbidity, there is a limited understanding of lived experiences and care improvement priorities as identified by key stakeholders.ObjectivesThe study aimed to explore lived experiences of patients with multimorbidity, their family caregivers, and interprofessional healthcare workers, and to identify shared care improvement priorities. These findings were synthesized into a multimorbidity management toolkit.MethodsAn experience‐based co‐design study with six stages was implemented, considering human‐centered design. Participants included patients aged 50 years and above living with heart failure and multimorbidity, family caregivers, and multidisciplinary healthcare professionals recruited in Maryland, USA. Following study preparation and ethical approval (Stage 1), in‐depth individual interviews (Stages 2 and 3) were conducted, and the results were discussed during co‐design events and working group meetings (Stages 4 and 5) to reach a consensus on care priorities and specific strategies. The study findings were summarized into a multimorbidity management toolkit that was shared with participants at a celebration event (Stage 6). Data analysis included descriptive statistics and inductive thematic methods.ResultsForty‐six individual interviews were conducted: 24 patients (mean ± SD age 71 ± 9 years; 54% women; with an average of 8 morbidities), 7 family caregivers (mean ± SD age 64 ± 14 years; 57% women), and 15 healthcare professionals (mean ± SD age 36 ± 10 years). A total of 18 participants attended two co‐design events. Multiple working group meetings were organized to receive diverse input. Four shared care improvement priorities emerged: (1) communication, (2) care coordination and follow‐up care, (3) mental and emotional support, and (4) health education for patients and caregivers.ConclusionPerson‐centered care models that emphasize shared strategies to improve communication, care coordination, education, and emotional support, as outlined in the toolkit, may enhance multimorbidity management and patient outcomes.Keywords: co‐design, EBCD, healthcare, management, multimorbidity

Introduction

Multimorbidity is typically defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions in the same individual at the same time [1]. Multimorbidity poses a significant challenge in individual and population health, as its prevalence is increasing due to a global aging population, improved longevity, and ongoing health inequalities [2]. The higher prevalence of multimorbidity is associated with higher morbidity, healthcare utilization and healthcare costs, and mortality [1]. The recent growing concern about the prevalence and burden of multimorbidity has highlighted the limitations of current healthcare models which are primarily organized around “disease” or “system” specific care needs [1]. These fragmented, disease‐specific models are poorly equipped to support continuous learning, adaptation, and integration across conditions that are essential for managing complex, multimorbid populations within modern healthcare systems [3].

Cardiovascular disease is one of the leading causes of death globally and an exemplary condition with multimorbidity. A typical individual living with heart failure has an average of four chronic conditions [4]. However, the current healthcare model and research tend to focus on singular risk factors or conditions in isolation. There is a need for a conceptual shift that helps understand and manage multiple chronic conditions that is based on patient and family‐centered holistic care models rather than disease or system‐specific care models [1]. Specifically, the identification of shared care improvement priorities by working together with individuals living with multimorbidity, their family caregivers, and a multidisciplinary healthcare team will be crucial in designing effective multimorbidity management models of care [5, 6]. This shift aligns closely with the principles of a Learning Health System (LHS), which seeks to integrate clinical care, patient experience, and real‐world data into continuous cycles of learning and improvement [3]. A recent qualitative study among individuals living with multimorbidity from critical care units, their family caregivers, and healthcare providers highlighted the need for more studies in understanding lived experiences and care improvement priorities to design and tailor interventions/models of care with the highest impact and sustainability potential [6]. Additionally, it is not only important to identify the problem but also to identify approaches to tackle those problems from the perspectives of key stakeholders in multimorbidity care.

Participatory approaches are increasingly being used to address knowledge gaps and inform care by involving end users in the exploration of their experiences and needs [7]. Evolving evidence highlights that partnerships between users, patients, and the public in completing service improvement activities not only improve quality and effectiveness but also lead to improvement in the outcomes, safety, quality, and cost. Participatory approaches such as Experience‐Based co‐design (EBCD) [8] and human‐centered design (HCD) [9], which use key stakeholders' experiences and needs in considering service improvement activities, may be useful for understanding shared improvement priorities in multimorbidity management [6, 10].

Although the challenges of efficiently and effectively managing multimorbidity have been recognized [11], potential effective strategies are not well identified [12, 13]. Hence, the study used EBCD and HCD methods to identify the shared experiences and care improvement priorities of those living with multimorbidity, their family caregivers, and interprofessional healthcare teams with experience managing patients with multimorbidity. The goal of the study was to develop a multimorbidity management toolkit identifying shared improvement priorities and specific strategies to achieve those priorities. These priorities and strategies could be vital in designing future multimorbidity research and models of care.

Methods

Study Design

The study used an experience‐based co‐design (EBCD) method, which is a methodological approach that encourages the collective participation of patients, caregivers, and healthcare staff, aiming to improve the quality of care [8]. The EBCD process consists of six stages [14]. The six stages are: (1) setting up; (2) engaging staff and gathering experiences; (3) engaging patients and gathering experiences; (4) co‐design event; (5) co‐design working groups; and (6) celebration event (details in Section 2.3). Stages 2 and 3 used a qualitative descriptive design to elicit experiences and perspectives from the study participants [15].

EBCD is intertwined with principles of HCD, which emphasizes human needs at every stage of the study design process, such that interventions will directly address these needs [9]. The study specifically focused on the HCD principles of storytelling and priority ranking, which were part of EBCD activities, to understand experiences and identify multimorbidity care improvement priorities from key stakeholders. Collectively, individuals with multimorbidity, family caregivers, and multidisciplinary healthcare teams synthesized improvement priorities and specific strategies, summarizing it as a multimorbidity toolkit. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB00244792).

Setting and Sample

The study was conducted at a large, urban hospital in Maryland, United States. The hospital serves a diverse population, including those with multimorbidity. Participants for the study were identified using purposive and convenience sampling. The inclusion/exclusion criteria were as follows: Patients—individuals living with at least 2 chronic conditions defined as the presence of heart failure and any other chronic conditions, aged 18 years and older, English speakers, and who have been admitted to the hospital were approached for study participation. Family caregivers—English‐speaking family caregivers of those living with two or more chronic conditions and who were discharged from the hospital were approached for the study. Healthcare professionals and other stakeholders—full or part‐time attending physicians and residents, nurse practitioners, registered and licensed nurses, social workers, case managers, researchers, and other diverse healthcare team members who have experience working with people living with multimorbidity were approached for the study.

Study Procedures

The study followed the six‐stage process of EBCD [8] to collectively explore the individual experiences/emotions and identify shared care improvement priorities for multimorbidity management by working together with the patients living with multimorbidity, family caregivers, and healthcare providers. The process initiated with the preparation for the study, which involved developing relationships with the clinical partners, getting ethical approval, and identifying the point of contact for recruitment, data collection, and other study‐related activities (Stage 1). Individual narrative interviews were conducted among multidisciplinary healthcare providers and key stakeholders (Stage 2), patients living with multimorbidity, and their family caregivers (Stage 3). The in‐depth individual interviews used a storytelling approach to explore participants' experiences, the context of care, and care improvement priorities. A short film was then developed outlining key themes from analysis of the interviews. Two co‐design events were organized (Stage 4), whereby diverse participants could view the film and engage in discussion to validate interview findings, identifying missed experiences, and ensure collective agreement for improvement priorities. The priority ranking exercise at the end of the co‐design events was valuable in helping understand the urgency for each improvement strategy identified. Follow‐up working group co‐design meetings were organized (Stage 5), which included participation from diverse participants and team members to identify strategies for achieving the improvement priorities. All key findings and inputs for care improvement priorities as well as specific strategies to achieve those priorities were summarized into a multimorbidity management toolkit, the final output of the study. The toolkit was shared with all study participants and clinical partners in a celebration event to receive final feedback (Stage 6). Those who were not able to attend co‐design events, working group meetings, or celebration events received updates and a copy of the final toolkit via email. All feedback was incorporated in the finalized version of the multimorbidity toolkit.

Data Collection and Management

All interviews with patients, caregivers, and healthcare workers were conducted in‐person or virtually via Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant Zoom meetings. Interviews were recorded after obtaining the participants' consent and were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service (Production Transcripts). Participants ‘demographic characteristics were collected before interviews, and patients’ comorbidity data were collected from their electronic health records. All study‐related data, including recordings and transcripts, were stored on an encrypted institutional server that required authorization to access it. Only approved study team members were authorized to access it. Recruitment and interviews continued until the study team agreed on achieving information power [16], yielding a diverse representation of experiences and perspectives. Information power was defined based on the different dimensions of the study, such as samples with relevant characteristics and experiences for the study were recruited (sample specificity), study team members experienced in the proposed research and practice area conducted the interviews (quality of dialogue), and sufficient data and a similar pattern was identified in the data during cross‐case analysis which were relevant to study aims [16]. For the co‐design events and working group meetings, all patients, family caregivers, and health care providers involved in the study were invited. Specifically in working group meetings, study team members additionally reached out to a larger group of healthcare providers other than those involved in interviews to validate the findings and receive diverse input. These larger groups included working group meetings of multidisciplinary providers within the health system and clinical leadership team meetings. During these meetings, the study team presented the progress of the study and preliminary aggregate findings and received input from the larger group to validate these findings and identify specific strategies to achieve shared priorities in a group discussion. This step help us ensure reliability and applicability of shared priorities and feasible implementation approaches that are relevant to health system change. Participants received a $25 e‐gift card as compensation for their time and effort in completing the interview or participating in the co‐design and celebration events.

Data Analysis

Data collection and analysis were iteratively conducted throughout Stages 2–6 of the EBCD process. Qualitative interview data (Stages 2 and 3) were analyzed using thematic analysis [17] by four study team members (KN, CB, AL, LD). All were trained in qualitative methodologies and used a two‐phase approach: (1) qualitative description to generate initial codes and (2) inductive thematic analysis to examine latent constructs. Data were managed using F4analyse, a qualitative data management software, to enable independent coding as well as cross‐comparisons for determining consensus. CB and AL independently coded all transcripts; KN and LD coded 20% of the transcripts to achieve intercoder reliability [18]. Codes were created by combining deductively based on literature regarding multimorbidity and inductively from the data produced by participants' interviews. The preliminary codes and themes were reviewed by the study team, and then the codebook was iteratively refined to account for coding discrepancies. Thematically similar codes were consolidated where appropriate to create overarching themes. Once themes were identified by consensus, a second round of coding was performed. Finally, themes were defined based on discussions with the entire study team and connections to existing literature [19]. To ensure the rigor of the qualitative analysis, the study team attempted to adhere to the four criteria of trustworthy qualitative research: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [20]. Subsequently, the final themes were presented in the film at the co‐design events to seek participants' feedback and elucidate their feedback.

Qualitative analysis continued during and after the co‐design events and working group meetings (Stages 4 and 5). In the co‐design events, participants validated the findings and agreed on the shared improvement priorities. Their additional feedback were added to the analysis and grouped under each relevant improvement priority. All finalized improvement priorities and strategies were compiled into the multimorbidity toolkit, which was shared as the final output from the study.

Results

In total, individual interviews were conducted with 24 patients living with multimorbidity, seven family caregivers, and 15 multidisciplinary healthcare providers and other stakeholders. Among healthcare workers, the majority were women (80%), with a mean (±SD) age of 36 (±10) years and an average (±SD) of 9 (±8) years of experience caring for patients with heart failure and multimorbidity. Communication Between Health Care System and Patients/Families

The interview findings underscored the critical role of communication between healthcare workers and patients/families in effectively managing multimorbidity. This communication dynamic primarily revolved around interactions between patients, family caregivers, and healthcare workers. Many patients and caregivers reflected on their experiences and expressed appreciation for healthcare workers who invested time in addressing their inquiries and conveying information in accessible language, particularly during hospital stays (caregivers). One participant emphasized the significance of receiving updates on a loved one's condition and appreciated clear communication about what to expect next in care planning (patient).

Furthermore, the interviews highlighted the importance of communication within interdisciplinary healthcare teams to ensure continuity of care, even in instances where patients missed appointments. One healthcare worker voiced concerns about sharing experiences in tracking patients who were lost to follow‐up, signaling a need for robust systems to monitor and address such instances effectively (consultant‐provider). The healthcare workers recognized the importance of tailoring discussions to patients' priorities, particularly regarding transitions in managing heart failure and multimorbidity. The findings also underscored the need to engage patients actively in decision‐making processes, ensuring that their preferences and goals are central to care planning. Additionally, most of the participants highlighted the value of electronic health communication tools, such as MyChart, in facilitating communication between patients and healthcare workers.

Health Education for Patients and Family Caregivers

The interviews revealed insights into the need for comprehensive health education for patients and family caregivers within the context of managing heart failure and multimorbidity. Some patients expressed overall satisfaction with the level of education they received, particularly regarding medication management, but some patients admitted to gaps in understanding and had to rely on healthcare providers for guidance and decision‐making, as stated, “I'm not a doctor, and I will take your advice as to what you think is best.” (patient) Some noted the absence of a responsive healthcare contact for their health inquiries, which made them “get on the internet and see what [they] can find” (caregiver).

Meanwhile, healthcare workers emphasized the significance of tailoring education to patients' health literacy levels, recognizing that some patients admitted for heart failure lacked a fundamental understanding, as “they have no idea what heart failure is” (clinical staff—provider). Some healthcare workers also emphasized the need to assess and address patient‐specific barriers to comprehension, advocating for a patient‐centered approach to education (licensed clinical social worker—provider). Additionally, healthcare workers expressed concerns regarding workload and time constraints within the healthcare system. They emphasized the “need to optimize the time to spend with this patient one‐on‐one to do the education and not in the last 30 min before they're walking out the door” (nurse practitioner—provider), urging for strategies to integrate early education into patient care workflows. This sentiment was also echoed by a clinical dietitian, who highlighted the pivotal role of nurses in identifying educational needs and coordinating timely education before discharge (clinical dietitian—provider). Overall, the findings underscored the importance of comprehensive health education, specifically tailored to the needs, conditions, and literacy levels of patients and their family caregivers, to manage multimorbidity effectively.

Care Coordination and Follow‐Up Care

The interview findings revealed the complex nature of care coordination among patients living with multimorbidity as a result of the complexity of the diseases and navigating the healthcare system. Patients emphasized that they “have multiple problems [they] need multiple doctors who will talk to each other” (patient), and several echoed the feeling of disconnect within healthcare teams, particularly between different specialists involved in their care (patient). They envisioned a seamless system where information could be easily shared among all involved parties, such as “interactive medical records” (patient), regardless of HIPAA protection (caregiver), to enable more coordinated and efficient care delivery.

Healthcare workers mentioned that the complexity and advancement of healthcare systems could impede care continuity for patients (nurse—provider). In response to such challenges, the healthcare workers recognized the importance of “transition guide nurses” (nurse practitioner—provider) or navigators in facilitating continuity of care, particularly during critical periods such as hospital discharge. Additionally, healthcare providers acknowledged the need to assist patients and caregivers in identifying their support networks beyond medical providers alone (nurse—provider).

Mental and Emotional Support

The interview reflected the mental and emotional burden experienced by individuals living with multimorbidity, particularly after receiving significant diagnoses like heart failure. For many patients, such diagnoses necessitated substantial life changes or the abandonment of long‐held plans, leading to psychological distress. One patient shared the traumatic experience of having their aspirations sidelined and facing uncertainty about the future, highlighting the mental strain associated with managing such conditions (patient). Patients also highlighted the loneliness and emotional distress experienced during hospitalization, underscoring the need for regular interaction with supportive professionals or volunteers. They expressed a desire for companionship and emotional support during their hospital stay and after discharge, emphasizing the importance of holistic care that addresses mental well‐being alongside physical health.

From healthcare workers' perspectives, they acknowledged the emotional toll that heart failure and any chronic condition exacts on both patients and caregivers, emphasizing the loss of independence and its ripple effect on quality of life. They recognized the difficulty patients face in coping with a condition that often arises despite their best efforts, underscoring the importance of providing comprehensive support beyond medical interventions (licensed clinical social worker—provider). Some healthcare workers advocated for earlier involvement of palliative care, hospice, or mental health consultations to address the significant psychological burden faced by patients. By integrating these services into care plans, they aimed to alleviate distress and improve overall well‐being.

However, despite the recognized importance of mental support, patients noted its relative neglect compared to other aspects of care. One patient noted receiving more attention from a nutritionist than a psychologist (patient), while a caregiver reported a lack of psychological support, highlighting a gap in mental health services within healthcare settings (caregiver). Patients highlighted the significance of family, friends, and religious communities in providing encouragement, prayers, and practical assistance, which served as sources of strength and solace during challenging times (patient). Additionally, caregivers who were friends of the patient played a crucial role in providing emotional support, offering face‐to‐face presence and comforting gestures, even if/when unable to assist physically. One patient mentioned support groups emerged as valuable sources of emotional support, providing opportunities for individuals to connect with others facing similar challenges, share experiences, and offer mutual support and advice. They found solace in these groups, finding comfort in the understanding and camaraderie of peers navigating similar journeys (patient).

Shared Improvement Priorities and Specific Strategies Identified From Co‐Design Events and Working Groups (Stages 4 and 5)

A total of 18 participants attended the codesign events, 12 study participants and six study team members, respectively. During the co‐design events, participants ranked improvement priorities based on their experiences and needs. Participants ranked communication as the top priority for improvement, followed by care coordination and follow‐up care, mental and emotional support (both ranked equally), and health education. Multiple working group meetings helped identify potential specific strategies to achieve improvement priorities. During working group meetings, study team members also presented ongoing findings of the study to larger multidisciplinary care providers, including physicians and the clinical leadership team within the health system. These presentations facilitated additional validation of the study findings and helped receive diverse input on improvement priorities and specific strategies to achieve them. Summaries around each improvement priority are presented below.

Communication

Participants emphasized the importance of setting clear expectations and goals at the beginning to ensure that providers, patients, and family members are aligned. They also highlighted the need for respect based on cultural backgrounds and education, as decision‐making styles can vary significantly. Moreover, they stressed the necessity of providing accessible communication channels after discharge, such as phone or online portals, for patients to contact their hospital/healthcare providers with questions or concerns.

Care Coordination and Follow‐Up Care

Discussion included the importance of facilitating communication between inpatient, outpatient, and home care providers before, during, and after transitions or discharge. Functional limitations and social determinants of health may impact the availability, accessibility, and usability of available services. Identifying a primary contact, such as a primary care provider, case manager, or social worker, who can offer care coordination and follow‐up support is important. Patients should be guided to a central number for scheduling appointments. Additionally, recognizing that many patients are also caregivers for spouses and other family members can complicate care coordination and follow‐up.

Mental and Emotional Support

The participants raised the point that most patients managing multiple chronic conditions are also caregivers. This dual responsibility can increase stress levels. Functional limitations can lead to changes in work, family, and social roles, contributing to mental health challenges. Involving social workers in the care team as early as possible and continuing their support after transitions or discharge can help provide valuable assistance and identify resources for patients and their caregivers after discharge.

Health Education

Participants mentioned that extensive education at the time of discharge may not be effective. It is crucial for multidisciplinary team members to assess and provide health education at every interaction with patients and their families—including after discharge.

Celebration Event (Stage 6) and Finalized Multimorbidity Management Toolkit

All improvement priorities and strategies for achieving them identified from EBCD Stages 1–5 were summarized in a multimorbidity management toolkit. A final celebration event was organized, inviting all study participants to receive feedback on the toolkit and study process. Additional inputs during the celebration event helped refine the final toolkit (Appendix 1) [21].

Discussion

This EBCD study explored shared experiences and identified care improvement priorities for multimorbidity management from the perspectives of patients, family caregivers, and healthcare providers. Four major improvement priorities were identified: the critical importance of effective communication between the healthcare system and patients/families, the significance of robust care coordination and follow‐up support, the mental and emotional support required, and the need for comprehensive, patient and family‐centered health education.

The findings emphasize the importance of communication between the healthcare system and patients/families, as well as across multidisciplinary care teams. Effective communication with healthcare teams is crucial to maintaining patients' health and quality of life, particularly after discharge. In‐hospital communication also plays a vital role in enhancing patient outcomes by ensuring all team members and patients are informed and aligned in their treatment approaches. A systematic review indicated that communication interventions before discharge significantly reduce readmission rates, improve adherence to treatment regimens, and increase patient satisfaction in the short term [22]. Additionally, another systematic review highlighted communication failures as persistent issues in managing patients with multimorbidity, even with technological advancements like shared health records [23]. This calls for the need for improved in‐hospital and after‐discharge communication strategies between healthcare systems, patients/families, and across multidisciplinary care teams to ensure cohesive and comprehensive care delivery. The findings shed light on the evolving understanding among healthcare workers regarding patient‐centered communication and the potential benefits of shared decision‐making. Specifically, for those living with multimorbidity, the complex care system should also be considered when designing approaches for effective communication [6]. The study also highlighted the value of electronic health communication tools, such as MyChart, in facilitating communication between patients and healthcare workers. This underscores the potential of technology to enhance communication in healthcare delivery.

Effective care coordination and follow‐up care are essential for multimorbidity management. Individuals living with multiple chronic conditions have multiple care providers and need multiple care approaches; hence, challenging care coordination and follow‐up care [6]. This study emphasized the crucial role of nurse navigators or transition guide nurses in facilitating continuity of care, especially during transitions from hospital to home. Evidence supports that nursing activities, such as assessing comprehensive patient and family needs, developing tailored care plans, establishing relational continuity of care, planning end‐of‐life care, and supporting patient activation, can help smooth the transitional process [24]. These activities build trust between patients and healthcare providers, increase a sense of security, and make patients feel cared for and respected [25]. Importantly, the effectiveness of nurse care coordination on patient‐reported and health service outcomes is greatly influenced by several key factors. For example, frequent contact and in‐person interactions can improve adherence to treatment plans and patient satisfaction. Continuous follow‐up and monitoring of disease status allow for early detection of complications, reducing healthcare costs. Additionally, applying behavior change theory encourages patients to adopt healthier lifestyles, leading to improved outcomes [26]. These aspects need to be carefully balanced to ensure the coordination program is effective without placing an undue burden on patients. Additionally, the study results highlighted the importance of identifying support networks in addition to having multidisciplinary care team members to promote the continuity of care. One profession that was frequently mentioned during the co‐design events was the social worker. Social workers are involved in offering support and psychosocial interventions, linking patients to health and social services, providing referrals, and coordinating interactions between patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers [27]. These activities help patients smoothly transition within or between hospital‐based and community‐based care settings.

The findings revealed that patients often experience loneliness and emotional distress during hospitalization, which can be especially pronounced when they receive a new diagnosis. Additionally, patients with multimorbidity may feel easily exhausted by social activities, but their social needs should not be ignored. Studies have shown the importance of distress screening (using tools like the Patient Health Questionnaire‐2) [28] and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire‐2 [29], mental health referrals, and providing resources for patients, especially when visitation is limited [30, 31]. Additionally, interventions such as video calls, which gained prominence during the COVID‐19 pandemic, allowed patients to connect with family and friends safely [32]. Furthermore, providing what they need to practice their faith can also support patients' emotional well‐being [33]. Considering the mind‐heart‐body connection is crucial, as psychological health impacts cardiovascular and overall health [31]. Interventions such as stress management programs and mindfulness‐based interventions have shown significant improvements in anxiety, perceived stress, depression, and systolic blood pressure [31]. Importantly, just understanding or trying to understand what patients and their family members are going through is critical to helping them. Addressing the mental and emotional aspects of multimorbidity is as important as managing physical symptoms, as it can lead to better overall health outcomes.

Health education should be tailored to patients' literacy levels, social determinants of health, and specific health conditions. The study indicated that there are already several health education activities and discharge education has improved a lot yet there is a need for tailored education that is based on the need and priorities of the patients and their caregivers as well as the importance of timing for that education. According to a systematic review, one‐fourth of patients with heart failure have inadequate health literacy, with literacy levels varying based on geography, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status [34]. The presence of multimorbidity further complicates self‐care when living with chronic conditions [35]. For example, patients may face competing priorities and conflicting treatment recommendations, making it challenging to adhere to all aspects of their care. Additionally, fragmented care coordination between different healthcare providers can lead to inconsistent advice. Therefore, there is a need to develop educational materials with an equity lens to ensure they are clear, accessible, understandable, usable, and based on the needs. A statement from the American Heart Association recommends considering message content, readability, usability, text appearance, visuals, and overall layout and design when educating patients [36]. Resources that are easy to read and that incorporate images have been suggested to promote memory retention and enhance patient understanding [36]. This highlights the necessity of developing patient‐centered health education, ensuring that both patients and caregivers fully understand how to manage multimorbidity [6].

Overall, the synergy between EBCD and HCD was a promising method for understanding real‐life experiences and identifying shared care improvement priorities that are based on the interests of key stakeholders. The findings of the study were consistent with similar studies before [6] yet provided alternative processes using EBCD and yielded an updated toolkit which may prove useful for clinical teams. The study and toolkit highlighted the need for person‐family centered care models rather than disease/system‐specific. Individual‐focused, tailored approaches were highlighted as a vital need to enhance health and clinical outcomes. The study highlights that considering shared experiences and care priorities is important in designing models of care and future trials that are responsive, effective, and sustainable for multimorbidity management.

From an LHS perspective, this study represents a foundational phase of system learning by transforming lived experience into structured, actionable knowledge that can inform evidence‐based effective models of care, interventions for research, educational approaches, and policy initiatives [21]. The multimorbidity toolkit developed through this process functions as a translational bridge between experiential knowledge and actionable system change by specifying concrete priorities and strategies that can be operationalized within patient education processes, patient‐provider interactions, clinical workflows, team structures, and system‐level change for multimorbidity care. Although this study focused on the understanding, planning, and design phase of multimorbidity care rather than system‐level implementation, it provides essential guidance for organizational and clinician‐level change, including clarifying approaches for communication among care settings and diseases, optimizing care coordination and follow‐up care, opportunities for structured patient and caregiver education, and embedding emotional support and social work into routine care. These elements can inform the development of education models, care pathways, and quality improvement as well as research that allows healthcare organizations to integrate the toolkit into practice and iteratively evaluate its impact through ongoing learning cycles.

To facilitate translation of the co‐designed toolkit into routine practice, the study recommends concrete clinician and organizational changes that are feasible within most health systems. For example, clinician actions include routine use of shared decision making, teach‐back and goal‐setting conversations by bedside nurses and clinicians, documentation of patient goals and education needs in an EHR “care team” template, and early social work screening with streamlined referral pathways to behavioral health, social services, or palliative care. Organizational supports could establish a named transition navigator (or repurposed discharge coordinator) and a centralized post‐discharge scheduling workflow. One of the examples of the next step in research from this study is the development and implementation of the care coordination and symptom management intervention (COORDINATE) program [37]. Currently, the study team is pilot testing the COORDINATE intervention and planning for larger implementation research. The intervention protocol has been published [37], and the pilot study findings will be published elsewhere. The study identified the design infrastructure with shared care improvement priorities and strategies to achieve them, which could inform subsequent system‐level implementation, testing, and system‐wide scaling of effective multimorbidity care approaches. The next phase of implementation of COORDINATE highlights the “continuity” aspect of the learning cycle of LHS by applying and evaluating evidence and innovation with a goal to improve quality and value in health care [3].

Findings should be interpreted within the context of the study's limitations. Firstly, this was a single‐site study, which may limit the generalizability of the results. The diversity of the sample may be restricted and might not reflect the experiences of a more varied population. Additionally, the exclusion criteria, which omitted patients who did not speak English or had cognitive impairments, may have overlooked important perspectives. There may be specific needs for patients with language barriers and cognitive impairments that require further assistance and exploration. Traditionally, co‐design events were held in person. However, like this study, using virtual platforms for these meetings has become more prevalent, especially during the COVID‐19 pandemic because of its convenience [38, 39, 40]. This shift has presented both advantages and challenges. Despite the convenience of the virtual format events, a limited number of participants attended the first co‐design event. Hence, multiple co‐design events were organized to include diverse participation. Moreover, the absence of in‐person interaction sometimes hindered the development of close connections that can enhance collaborative efforts and excluded participants without digital access from joining the events. On the positive side, the virtual format facilitated participation by reducing barriers associated with physical attendance, such as travel and time constraints. This inclusivity allowed a broader range of stakeholders to contribute to the discussions. Despite these challenges, the events successfully created an inclusive environment that promoted idea generation, demonstrating the potential of virtual platforms in fostering collaboration while highlighting areas for improvement in maintaining personal connections. The identification of the multimorbidity management toolkit further embodies the core principle of the LHS by transforming experiential knowledge into structured, actionable system change strategies. The implementation and evaluation of the toolkit in the next steps, such as evaluation of the COORDINATE program, further highlights the continuity characteristics of the LHS framework and will provide evidence and innovation with a goal to improve quality and value in health care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study offers valuable insights into the experiences, improvement priorities, and action items (i.e., toolkit) for multimorbidity management from the perspectives of patients, family caregivers, and multidisciplinary healthcare providers. By focusing on communication, care coordination, health education, and addressing the mental and emotional needs of patients, healthcare providers can significantly improve the health outcomes of those living with multimorbidity. Leveraging interdisciplinary approaches, integrating technological solutions, and enhancing support structures can move healthcare systems toward more holistic and patient and family‐centered care experiences for individuals managing complex chronic conditions.
