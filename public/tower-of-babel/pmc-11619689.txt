Association between C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index and depressive symptoms in American adults: results from the NHANES 2005 to 2010
Authors: Chaojuan Huang, Hongtao You, Yuyang Zhang, Zhiwei Li, Mingxu Li, Xingliang Feng, Naiyuan Shao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe novel serum C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) has been identified as an ideal parameter that integrates inflammation and insulin resistance, which are potential mechanisms underlying depressive symptoms. Our research aimed to investigate the association between CTI and depressive symptoms.MethodsOur cross-sectional investigation utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey conducted between 2005 and 2010. The integrated CTI was calculated as 0.412 × Ln (C-reactive protein) (mg/dL) + Ln [triglyceride (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2]. The severity of depressive symptoms was evaluated through the continuous Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scores, and the categorical definition of depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 score ≥ 10) reflected moderate to severe symptoms. Survey-weighted linear and logistic regression models were conducted to establish the correlation between CTI and PHQ-9 scores, and between CTI and depressive symptoms. Moreover, subgroup analyses, interaction tests, and smoothed curve fitting were performed to scrutinize the steadiness of the results.ResultsA total of 5,954 participants were enrolled in our study, including 477 with depressive symptoms and 5,477 without. The results revealed a significant positive relationship between CTI and PHQ-9 scores (β: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.25,0.55, p < 0.001) and depressive symptoms (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.06,1.61, p = 0.02). Additionally, individuals in the fourth quartile of CTI exhibited a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms than those in the first quartile (PHQ-9 score: β: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.39,1.26, p < 0.001; depressive symptoms: OR: 2.00, 95% CI:1.19,3.36, p = 0.01). Smooth curve fitting and subgroup analyses consistently demonstrated the positive relationship.ConclusionsElevated CTI was correlated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms, underscoring CTI as a potential clinical indicator for identifying and stratifying depressive symptoms.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06336-4.

Background

Depression is a common mental disorder characterized by a persistently depressed mood, loss of pleasure or interest, feelings of worthlessness, difficulty concentrating or making decisions, and even suicidal ideation or attempts lasting for more than two weeks [1]. These symptoms significantly impair psychosocial functioning and role performance [2]. Epidemiologic surveys by the World Health Organization reported that nearly 350 million populations grappled with depression in 2020, with the prevalence continuing to rise annually [3]. Ranking among the foremost global mental health burdens, depression is predicted to become the top cause of disease burden worldwide by 2030 [4]. Owing to the high probability of recurrence, disability, and suicide, depression imposes a significant burden on individuals, families, and societies, raising serious public health concerns worldwide [3]. Currently, pharmacotherapy is the most widely used treatment for depression, but it has limited effectiveness, numerous side effects, and is restricted in certain populations. Therefore, identifying trustworthy and readily available biomarkers for depression risk stratification is crucial, facilitating early prevention and intervention.

Depression is driven by various complicated factors, including genetic, psychological, and neurobiological etiologies [5]. Accumulating evidence has highlighted the underlying mechanisms of inflammation and insulin resistance (IR) in the occurrence and progression of depression. C-reactive protein (CRP), an indicator of systemic inflammation, might be involved in depression by affecting neuroendocrine function, neurotransmission, and neuroplasticity. Research from cross-sectional studies indicated a relationship between raised CRP levels and depression risk [6]. Additionally, prospective clinical trials have identified the anti-depressive effects of anti-inflammatory treatments, noting that patients with CRP levels exceeding 3.0 mg/L derive more benefits from such therapies [7]. IR is a pathological state characterized by impaired insulin responsiveness, a core characteristic of metabolic syndrome, and has been linked to multiple neuropsychiatric disorders, for instance, Alzheimer’s disease [8] and depression [9]. Central IR influences the release of dopamine, signal transduction in neural circuits, and mitochondrial energy cycles, consequently impacting mood and cognition [10]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is an accessible and dependable measure that reflects IR compared to gold-standard technology. According to a study of 13,350 American participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), the TyG index is positively relevant to depressive symptoms [9]. Moreover, the antidepressant light therapy could produce greater influence on depressive patients with lower insulin sensitivity compared to those with higher insulin sensitivity [11]. Inflammatory factors could trigger downstream effects and impair insulin signaling [12]. IR may cause metabolic disturbance, increase oxidative stress, and amplify inflammatory responses [13, 14]. The vicious cycle between inflammation and IR mutually aggravates and ultimately lead to depressive symptoms [15].

Currently, the comprehensive C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) is established on CRP and the TyG index, simultaneously reflecting inflammation and IR status. The CTI was first proposed in 2022 by Ruan et al. [16] and has since been broadly employed in clinical cancer studies. In accordance with one previous study from NHANES, CTI significantly stratified risk for cancer mortality [17]. Moreover, the novel CTI is an accessible, cost-effective, and rapid index derived from biochemical examinations. Previous research primarily focused on individual CRP or the TyG index and utilized small sample sizes [18, 19], thus restricting the reliability and generalizability of the conclusions. Nevertheless, the correlation between the integrated CTI and depressive symptoms remains largely unexplored. Considering the aforementioned limitations, our study intends to comprehensively explore the association between CTI and depressive symptoms. The publicly available, extensive NHANES datasets of the American population build a solid foundation for our investigation.

We hypothesized a significant positive association between CTI and depressive symptoms, with a progressive and consistent relationship observed across increasing CTI quartiles. Our exploration could enhance the risk stratification of depressive symptoms and provide novel insights into preventing depressive symptoms through joint interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

NHANES is a cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health, nutrition, and socioeconomic status of the American population. To ensure the representation of the population, NHANES employed a sophisticated multistage probability sampling design. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) organized this program in the United States and releases databases in a two-year cycle. This study incorporated publicly available data from 2005 to 2010.

A sum of 31,034 participants were initially enrolled. Our exploration was restricted to subjects over 18 years old (N = 18,381). A series of sample selection processes were applied according to the exclusion criteria: (1) absence of Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scores (N = 2,557); (2) incomplete laboratory tests for CTI (including CRP, triglyceride, and fasting glucose) (N = 8,235); (3) pregnant female participants (N = 411); (4) missing data on available covariates: marital status (N = 252); poverty income ratio (PIR) (N = 509); education (N = 6); body mass index (BMI) (N = 62); hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (N = 24); creatinine (N = 1); smoking (N = 217); alcohol (N = 8); Diabetes Mellitus (DM) (N = 22); chronic kidney disease (CKD) (N = 31). Consequently, our study included 5,954 adult participants with complete information in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Assessment of CTI (exposure)

As previously mentioned [17], CTI was defined based on the biochemical data of blood samples, including CRP, triglyceride, and fasting glucose; among them, triglyceride and fasting glucose were utilized to reflect IR. Biochemical samples were collected in the morning after fasting for eight hour and delivered to a laboratory authenticated by NCHS. CRP, triglyceride, and fasting glucose were tested through latex-enhanced nephelometry on the Behring Nephelometer, the enzymatic assay on the Roche Cobas 6000 chemistry analyzers, and the oxygen rate method on the Beckman DxC800, respectively. The CTI was determined using the following equation: CTI = 0.412 × Ln (CRP) (mg/dL) + Ln [triglyceride (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2]. The units for these three variables were milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL). In the preliminary analysis, the continuous CTI variable was considered and later categorized into quartiles for subsequent analyses.

Assessment of depressive symptoms (outcome)

The assessment of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks is conducted through self-reported PHQ-9 questionnaire [20]. The PHQ-9 scoring system consists of nine items, each scored from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’), with a cumulative score ranging from 0 to 27 [21]. Depressive symptoms were defined as a PHQ-9 score ≥ 10, a threshold indicating moderate to severe symptoms based on established guidelines [22, 23]. This threshold has been clinically validated with a sensitivity and specificity of up to 88% for identifying depressive symptoms.

Assessment of potential covariates

Our investigation selected four categories of variables as covariates: sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral features, comorbidities, and laboratory tests. (1) Sociodemographic characteristics, age was stratified into < 60y/≥60y; sex was sorted into female/male; race was grouped into non-Hispanic White/non-Hispanic Black/Mexican American/other Hispanic/other races; PIR was categorized into < 1.3/1.3–3.5/≥3.5; educational level was classified as above high school/high school/below high school; and marital status was divided into living alone/married or living with a partner. (2) Behavioral features, including BMI, smoking status, and alcohol use. BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m2) and was separated into < 25/25–30/≥30. Smoking status was stratified into never-smokers (smoking fewer than 100 cigarettes lifelong and regular smoking), former smokers (smoking more than 100 cigarettes lifelong and currently not smoking), or current smokers (smoking more than 100 cigarettes lifelong and currently smoking). Alcohol use was sorted into no consumption (< 12 alcoholic drinks during the past year) and consumption (≥ 12 alcoholic drinks during the past year) [24]. (3) Comorbidities, consisting of hypertension, DM, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), and CKD. Hypertension was diagnosed through systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medication [25]. DM was defined by fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, random glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or the use of hypoglycemic therapy [26]. Hyperlipidemia was assessed by a total cholesterol value ≥ 240 mg/dL or undergoing lipid-lowering therapy. CVD was identified through self-reported congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, or heart attack. CKD was quantified by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 according to the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration creatinine Eq. (2009) [27]. (4) Laboratory tests, including uric acid, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), HbA1c, creatinine, and eGFR. In the NHANES protocol, biochemical measurements and PHQ-9 scores were collected within the same survey cycle, with the time lag between these measurements ranging from 0 to 7 days.

Statistical analysis

All data utilized in our investigation were appropriately weighted to ensure nationally representative data of the American population, taking into account the complicated sampling methodology of the NHANES program. Descriptive statistics compared general features of the populations with and without depressive symptoms. Continuous variables were exhibited as weighted mean ± standard deviation, and group differences were examined employing weighted linear regression. Categorical variables were described as frequency (weighted proportion), and group differences were compared employing weighted chi-square tests. Conditioning on potential confounding factors, multivariable linear and logistic regression analyses were carried out to identify the independent association between CTI and PHQ-9 scores and depressive symptoms. The corresponding results were depicted as β, odds ratios (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Crude Model 1 in the regression analyses did not take into account any adjustments for confounders. Adjustments for age, sex, race, PIR, educational level, and marital status were made in Model 2. Further adjustments for BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension, DM, hyperlipidemia, CVD, CKD, as well as uric acid, total cholesterol, HDL, HbA1c, creatinine, and eGFR were included in Model 3. The continuous variable CTI was converted into a categorical variable (quartiles). The Cochran-Armitage trend test with weighted regression was applied to validate the correlation trends between CTI quartiles and PHQ-9 scores and depressive symptoms. Additionally, the generalized additive model combined with a smooth fitting curve was constructed to intuitively visualize the nonlinear association between CTI and depressive symptoms.

Furthermore, to investigate the steady influence of CTI on PHQ-9 scores and depressive symptoms across various contexts, subgroup analyses were stratified by age, sex, race, educational level, marital status, BMI, smoking status, and presence of hypertension, DM, hyperlipidemia, CVD, and CKD. Subgroup membership, CTI, and depressive symptoms were incorporated as multiplicative interaction terms in the fully adjusted Model 3 to estimate potential interactive influences. Two-tailed tests were conducted, and the threshold for significance was established at 0.05. All statistical analyses were accomplished through EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com) and R software version 4.2.0. The weighted dataset was applied in our analysis to minimize data fluctuations.

Results

Characteristics of study participant

Detailed group differences in clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Eventually, 5,954 subjects were enrolled, including 477 with depressive symptoms and 5,477 without. With respect to sociodemographic characteristics and behavioral features, females (64.29% vs. 49.36%, p < 0.0001), individuals living alone (48.19% vs. 33.54%, p < 0.0001), those with higher BMI (29.55 ± 0.42 kg/m2 vs. 28.64 ± 0.12 kg/m2, p = 0.04), lower PIR (2.13 ± 0.10 vs. 3.16 ± 0.04, p < 0.0001), and lower educational attainment were more prone to experience depressive symptoms. Smoking status and alcohol use also differed significantly between the two groups (p < 0.05). Regarding comorbidities, our results reported that individuals with depressive symptoms exhibited higher prevalence rates of hypertension (48.05% vs. 35.94%, p < 0.001), DM (21.82% vs. 14.56%, p < 0.001), CVD (16.45% vs. 8.05%, p < 0.0001), and CKD (16.13% vs. 13.08%, p = 0.03) than those without depressive symptoms. Additionally, among the laboratory tests, participants with depressive symptoms had significantly increased levels of HbA1c, TyG, CRP, and CTI, whereas levels of creatinine, uric acid, and HDL were significantly decreased. Additionally, the distributions of PHQ-9 scores in the depressive symptom group have been presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Association between CTI and depressive symptoms

Table 2 demonstrates a consistent positive correlation between CTI and depressive symptoms. The continuous CTI variable was positively related to PHQ-9 scores in all models. The effect sizes were 0.48 (95% CI: 0.35,0.60) in crude Model 1, 0.48 (95% CI: 0.36,0.61) in minimally adjusted Model 2, and 0.40 (95% CI: 0.25,0.55) in fully adjusted Model 3. Furthermore, each standard deviation increased in continuous CTI led to a 38% elevation in the likelihood of depressive symptoms (OR:1.38, 95% CI: 1.21,1.57) in crude Model 1. The connection weakened with additional confounder adjustments but remained significant in Model 2 (OR:1.34, 95% CI: 1.17,1.54) and Model 3 (OR:1.30, 95% CI: 1.06,1.61).

When continuous CTI data were stratified into quartile groups, a progressively stronger positive association with depressive symptoms was observed from quartile 1 to quartile 4, with the highest quartile of CTI showing the highest risk of depressive symptoms. Specifically, the fully adjusted β for the PHQ-9 scores increased with each higher CTI quartile: 0.16 (95% CI: -0.14,0.46) in quartile 2, 0.47 (95% CI: 0.05,0.88) in quartile 3, and 0.83 (95% CI: 0.39,1.26) in quartile 4. CTI quartiles were also positively related to depressive symptoms. With the lowest quartile of CTI serving as a reference, the fully adjusted ORs for depressive symptoms increased across CTI quartiles: 1.00 (reference), 1.34 (95% CI: 0.90,1.98), 1.68 (95% CI: 1.00,2.83), and 2.00 (95% CI: 1.19,3.36), respectively. Furthermore, linear trend tests exhibited a statistically significant association between increasing CTI levels and depressive symptoms (p for trend < 0.05).

Additionally, smooth curve analyses were performed in the current investigation to elucidate the correlation trends between CTI, PHQ-9 scores, and depressive symptoms across the CTI range. As exhibited in Fig. 2A and B, the plots indicated a positive linear correlation between increasing CTI levels and higher PHQ-9 scores, as well as an elevated risk of depressive symptoms.

Subgroup analyses

To estimate the reliability and stability of the linkage between CTI and depressive symptoms in fully adjusted Model 3, subgroup analyses combined with interaction tests were carried out. A consistently positive correlation between CTI and PHQ-9 scores was observed across different stratification factors, including sex, age, educational level, marital status, and comorbidities such as hypertension, DM, and hyperlipidemia (Table 3). As presented by the forest plot (Fig. 3), the interaction tests across all subgroups did not yield statistically significant P values (p < 0.05), implying that stratification factors consistently affect the relationship between CTI and PHQ-9 scores in the same direction. The results of stratified analyses between CTI and depressive symptoms are demonstrated in Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2. Notably, the association was not as robust as the relationship with PHQ-9 scores. Additionally, significant interactions were observed in subgroup analyses involving smoking status and CKD, indicating that the association between CTI and depressive symptoms was more pronounced in former smokers and individuals without CKD.

Discussion

Employing NHANES data in this comprehensive cross-sectional analysis, we identified a positive relationship among CTI, PHQ-9 scores, and depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted regression model with all covariates considered. Moreover, the fully adjusted logistic regression model revealed that individuals in higher CTI quartiles exhibited a heightened likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms. The smooth curve analyses presented a similar linear trend. The consistent findings between CTI and PHQ-9 scores were corroborated by subgroup analyses across different stratification factors, except for race, BMI, smoking status, CVD, and CKD. Notably, we identified significant interactions between smoking status and the presence of CKD regarding the CTI - depressive symptom association.

It is noteworthy that this study represents a pioneering effort to illustrate a well-established and consistent relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms. Consistent with our findings, previous research engaging 6,293 American participants from the 2017–2020 NHANES declared that each standard deviation increases in continuous CRP contributed to a 10% elevation in depressive symptoms susceptibility. When compared to the lowest CRP quartile, the highest CRP quartile had a fully adjusted OR of 1.39 for depressive symptoms [6]. To explore the relationship between CRP levels and specific depressive symptoms, Lin et al. [28] utilized data from the NHANES general population cohort, stratifying participants into five independent samples based on biennial data release cycles. Their analysis consistently demonstrated a statistically significant association between CRP levels and the fatigue subscale of the PHQ-9 across all five independent samples as well as the overall cohort. Another cross-sectional study conducted by Shi et al. found that subjects in the highest TyG quartile had a higher risk of depressive symptoms compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR = 1.46) [9]. However, the results are not consistently aligned. One prior study involving 4,688 subjects from the Korea NHANES in 2016 reported a linkage between TyG and depressive symptoms instead of CRP [29]. Contradictory findings may stem from variations in population characteristics, sample size, research design, and assessment methods. Hence, future longitudinal studies with sizeable, multi-ethnic cohorts are warranted. Compared to individual markers CRP or TyG index, the composite CTI simultaneously integrates the information of inflammation and IR status, reflecting the synergetic and interplay effect of the above two on depressive symptoms pathogenesis. Multiple studies have documented the intricate interplay between metabolic disturbances and inflammation in the pathophysiology of depressive symptoms [30]. The positive feedback between these factors contributes to both the onset and exacerbation of depressive symptoms [15]. In supporting this, clinical trials of antidepressant treatment have found that non-responders showed aggravation of IR [31]. Conversely, treatment responders presented improvement in IR, and alteration in IR mediated the connection between CRP and treatment response. Our study underscores the potential of the composite CTI as an effective tool for identifying and stratifying depressive symptoms risk. The integration and optimization of inflammation and IR might provide powerful management strategies for depressive symptoms. Currently, depressive symptoms diagnosis primarily relies on self-reported questionnaires and subjective assessments. The recall bias or social desirability bias potentially hinder early detection and intervention efforts [23]. Incorporating the objective laboratory examination of CTI into the diagnostic framework for depressive symptoms might help identify at-risk individuals and facilitate timely interventions to improve disease outcomes and prognosis.

After adjusting for multiple confounding factors, our results consistently demonstrated a positive association between CTI and depressive symptoms, whether CTI was treated as a continuous or categorical variable. The inclusion of covariates in our analysis was guided by their established or potential relevance to the relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms. For example, the PIR, a key indicator of socioeconomic status, was included due to its well-documented association with depressive symptoms. A meta-analysis of 26 studies highlighted that individuals in populations with greater income inequality face a higher risk of depressive symptoms compared to those in populations with lower income inequality [32]. Behavioral features and comorbidities, which are recognized as potential confounders of CTI and depressive symptoms, were also included. Laboratory parameters were selected based on prior research [23, 33] indicating their potential influence on the CTI-depression relationship. However, information on medical and pharmacological interventions, which are known to mitigate depressive symptoms [34, 35], was unavailable in the NHANES dataset. Future studies should aim to incorporate these variables for a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship. The use of both continuous and categorical variables for the PHQ-9 and CTI enables a comprehensive analysis of their associations. Continuous PHQ-9 scores maximize statistical power and allow for the exploration of nuanced linear relationships with CTI. Meanwhile, categorizing depressive symptoms based on a threshold facilitates clinical interpretation, albeit with a potential loss of granularity. Similarly, treating CTI as a continuous variable reflects its full variability, while quartile stratification provides opportunities to examine subgroup comparisons and identify potential threshold effects.

Subgroup analysis demonstrated consistently strong associations between CTI and PHQ-9 scores across most stratification factors, underscoring the robustness and credibility of our findings. The consistency of the relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms across stratification factors was weaker compared to PHQ-9 scores. Notably, the continuous PHQ-9 score was dichotomized utilizing a cut-off value of 10. The continuous data originally contained complete information; however, some information may have been lost due to data transformation, potentially explaining the observed discrepancy. The relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms did not change in the stratified analysis by sex, implying that the clinical application of CTI was consistent regarding sex. Furthermore, significant interactions between smoking status and CKD were observed in the relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms. Accumulating evidence has put forward that smoking behaviors could aggravate systemic inflammation levels [36] and IR status [37]. Notable correlations between CTI and depressive symptoms were observed in former and never smokers, instead of in current smokers. Current smokers often experience greater psychological stress, and subjects with depressive symptoms tend to have higher smoking rates [38, 39]. Harmful components of cigarettes could produce pathophysiological effects that overshadow the real relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms. Former smokers typically adopt healthier lifestyles; however, the detrimental effects of cigarettes may persist post-cessation. Thus, compared to never smokers, the correlation was more pronounced in former smokers. Similarly, a growing body of literature has suggested a linkage between CKD, inflammation, IR, and depressive symptoms. The synergistic effects of inflammation and IR are widely recognized as pivotal in CKD [40]. Relying on data gathered from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, another observational study demonstrated bidirectional positive correlations between CKD and depressive symptoms [41]. However, our research revealed that this relationship became non-significant among participants with CKD, possibly due to the small sample size within this subgroup (13.28% for CKD). Overall, our exploration indicated that smoking status and CKD might mediate the correlation between CTI and depressive symptoms.

The mechanisms that underlie the linkage between CTI and depressive symptoms have recently garnered research attention. Motivational and motor deficits are general symptoms of depressive symptoms associated with impaired cortico-striatal reward and mesolimbic dopaminergic circuits. Inflammation could contribute to reduced dopamine synthesis and release, alter dopaminergic circuits, and thereby influence depressive behavior [42]. Bekhbat et al. [43] conducted a clinical trial that modulated reward circuitry via levodopa supplementation in depressive patients with elevated inflammation. They further observed that improvements in depressive symptoms were negatively related to the functional connectivity of the reward circuitry following levodopa therapy in subjects with CRP levels > 2 mg/L. Insulin receptors are broadly distributed in the brain, where insulin can modulate signal transduction in dopaminergic mesolimbic pathways [44]. As mentioned above, the combined effects of inflammation and IR on dopaminergic circuits may represent an essential mechanism of depressive symptoms. Additionally, inflammation and IR could impair the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, axonal transport, signal projection, and mitochondrial energy metabolism, consequently contributing to depressive symptoms [45, 46]. The literature on the exact mechanisms linking CTI to depressive symptoms remains inconclusive; future research directions include in-depth animal studies and longitudinal clinical trials. CTI biomarkers provide potential therapeutic strategies from the perspective of anti-inflammatory, insulin-sensitizing, and dopaminergic agents. Patients with elevated inflammatory markers or metabolic dysregulation often exhibit poorer responses to conventional antidepressant therapies and are more likely to experience treatment resistance [11]. Our findings suggest that individuals with immunometabolic disturbances, as indicated by elevated CTI, may benefit from targeted therapies. Future research should explore the predictive value of CTI for treatment response and its potential role in guiding personalized therapeutic strategies, including anti-inflammatory and metabolic treatments.

Unlike previous studies, our research comprehensively explored the association between CTI and depressive symptoms using both continuous and categorical CTI variables, along with various measures to evaluate depressive symptoms. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the PHQ-9 is a validated screening tool that assesses the frequency and severity of depressive symptoms but does not establish a clinical diagnosis of depression. A structured diagnostic interview, incorporating exclusion criteria and functional impairment assessments, is required for a definitive diagnosis [47]. Secondly, our study excluded participants with incomplete covariate data, such as PIR, to minimize confounding from population admixture, however, may introduce selection bias. Future studies should consider retaining these variables in their continuous form to capture more nuanced and precise associations. The sample size of participants with depressive symptoms is relatively small in our research. However, The NHANES dataset is designed to be representative of the American population, and the prevalence of depressive symptoms observed in our sample closely aligns with epidemiological findings. Thirdly, the dichotomization of continuous variables, such as age and alcohol consumption, while practical for subgroup analyses, may have reduced the granularity of the findings and led to some loss of information. And that the absence of specific variables related to psychotherapy or counseling interventions limits the ability to comprehensively evaluate their potential modifying effects on the relationship between CTI and depressive symptoms. Fourthly, the inherent nature of cross-sectional research can merely indicate correlation rather than exact causality. Finally, our research was restricted to a specific American population, minimizing confounding due to population admixture but limiting generalizability to other nations and ethnicities.

Conclusions

This NHANES-based study demonstrated a compelling correlation between CTI and depressive symptoms, showing a progressively higher susceptibility to depressive symptoms as CTI levels increased. It holds significant public health value for clinicians to consider assessing CTI in high-risk patients for depressive symptoms. Accordingly, our study emphasizes CTI as a promising instrument for early detection and intervention of depressive symptoms. Future investigations are warranted to determine the causality between CTI and depressive symptoms and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
