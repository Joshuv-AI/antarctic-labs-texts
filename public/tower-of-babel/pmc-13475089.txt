Comparison of double-blind and open-label decline rates in lecanemab and donanemab trials in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Alok K Dwivedi, Bruno P Imbimbo, Jesus Abanto, Alberto J Espay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhase 3 trials of the anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies lecanemab and donanemab in Alzheimer’s disease demonstrated modest slowing of cognitive decline over 18 months. Subsequent open-label extensions (OLE) suggested greater long-term benefit based on comparisons with historical untreated cohorts, which are vulnerable to selection and attrition bias.MethodsWe simulated longitudinal Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes trajectories using mixed-effects models calibrated to reproduce published means, variances and attrition patterns from the Clarity-AD and TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trials, separately for early- and delayed-start cohorts and in combined analyses. Observed annualised slopes were also directly compared between double-blind (DB) and OLE phases. Placebo effects were estimated by comparing DB placebo arms with matched untreated historical cohorts derived from published natural history data.ResultsSimulations closely reproduced reported trajectories. In both trials, the early-start cohorts showed significantly faster annualised decline during OLE than during DB treatment (lecanemab ∆=0.53; 95% CI 0.40 to 0.66; donanemab ∆=0.66; 95% CI 0.41 to 0.91), whereas delayed-start cohorts showed no meaningful phase-related differences. OLE decline rates approximated those observed in DB placebo groups. In the lecanemab trial, DB placebo participants declined more slowly than matched untreated controls; decline in the donanemab trial varied by baseline severity.ConclusionsDespite selective retention favouring slower progressors, both lecanemab and donanemab accelerated cognitive decline during OLE phases and paralleled placebo-level declines.

However, OLE analyses are highly vulnerable to selection bias, attrition bias and the absence of contemporaneous placebo controls.

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies targeting aggregated amyloid-β have been introduced as potential disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The pivotal placebo-controlled (double-blind (DB)) phase 3 trials of lecanemab (Clarity-AD)1 and donanemab (TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2)2 showed modest absolute differences in the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) at 18 months compared with placebo, corresponding to a slowing of decline of about 25% to 35%. Both trials subsequently launched long-term open-label extensions (OLEs) of 48 months for lecanemab and 36 months for donanemab, which have been recently published.3 4 These data suggested progressively greater benefit with continued open-label treatment, although when comparing participants who remained in long-term follow-up with untreated historical cohorts, such as those participating in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI).

OLEs are particularly susceptible to selection bias, as only a self-selected subset of trial participants remain in the long-term follow-up.5 Participants who tolerate treatment, experience fewer adverse events or decline more slowly are disproportionately represented, whereas those who are harmed or worsen no longer count,6 artificially inflating the effect size of an intervention.7 In addition, placebo effects during the DB phase may attenuate observed treatment–placebo contrasts, further complicating interpretation of long-term outcomes.

To address these limitations and in recognition of the policy by drug manufacturers against sharing individual-level data for independent analyses, we conducted both simulation-based and observed-data analyses to compare the rate of CDR-SB decline during the DB and OLE phases of the lecanemab and donanemab trials. Our objectives were twofold. First, we compared the slopes of CDR-SB decline during the DB and OLE phases of the lecanemab and donanemab trials directly within the treated groups, separately for early, delayed start and combined cohorts, that is, separate from external controls. Second, we estimated the magnitude and direction of placebo effects during the DB phase by comparing placebo arms with simulated untreated historical cohorts with similar baseline disease severity. This framework allows assessment of whether the apparent long-term benefit reflects sustained disease modification or instead conceals accelerating clinical decline once placebo effects dissipate.

Methods

Overview of the simulation framework

We conducted longitudinal simulations of CDR-SB trajectories using mixed-effects models, separately for the early- and delayed-start cohorts, calibrated to reproduce published trial-level summary statistics, baseline characteristics, dropout patterns and reported effect sizes from the Clarity-AD and TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 studies. We also simulated data from both studies, reporting DB (treated and placebo) and combined OLE-phase (early- and delayed-start cohorts) data using random- and fixed-effects models, after matching sample sizes, dropout rates, baseline characteristics and effect sizes. Simulations were designed to generate participant-level data closely matching observed mean trajectories, variances and attrition patterns across trial phases. All simulations were conducted using aggregated data (means, SD and figures) from trials rather than individual-level data from the sponsors.

Trial populations and follow-up periods

We simulated individual-level data for 1734 participants (859 in the early-start cohort and 875 in the delayed-start cohort) from the lecanemab study and for 1634 participants (794 in the early-start cohort and 840 in the delayed-start cohort) from the donanemab study over the 36-month follow-up period. The data from these participants included repeated measurements at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 months by accounting for the random dropout rate as reported in published studies. In addition, we also simulated individual-level data from reports that presented combined data from early and delayed cohorts. For lecanemab, we generated 48-month follow-up data that included 1734 participants randomised during the 18-month DB phase1 and 478 participants completing the subsequent 30-month OLE.3 For donanemab, simulations included 1736 participants in the DB phase2 and 417 participants completing the 18-month OLE.4 These numbers reflect reported enrolment and retention rates and account for selective continuation into OLE phases. From the reported mean, SD and range of CDR-SB, we initially generated baseline data assuming a normal distribution, separately for each group. Using these baseline CDR-SB data and the reported mean change in CDR-SB, we generated follow-up CDR-SB data for each patient using a normal distribution at each follow-up time point by incorporating intra-class correlation and residual variance closely matching the observed pattern in published data. After simulating the CDR-SB value at each visit, we randomly removed CDR-SB data to incorporate the dropout rate at each visit. A similar step is also followed for generating other simulation data for validating primary findings.

Modelling untreated historical controls

To estimate placebo effects, we simulated 3-year CDR-SB trajectories at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 months using random effects models from an untreated cohort of 792 individuals with early AD, separately for CDR 0.5 and CDR 1. To compare data from combined cohorts, we also simulated 4-year CDR-SB trajectories for an untreated cohort of 792 individuals with early AD, based on published summary statistics from the longitudinal study by Williams et al.8 This cohort provides a well-characterised natural history of CDR-SB progression across baseline CDR scores of 0.5 and 1.0. After generating simulated individual-level data from the untreated cohort, we randomly selected a matched control group reflecting the baseline CDR distribution (80% from CDR 0.5 and 20% CDR 1) of the placebo arms in both trials. The overall untreated cohorts reflect similar baseline disease severity. Mean slopes and variability parameters were extracted directly from the published literature.

Data extraction and calibration

Numerical values were obtained from peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations, and publicly available company materials. When individual data points were not reported, values were digitised from figures using WebPlotDigitizer. Simulation parameters were iteratively calibrated to ensure close agreement between simulated and published trajectories during the DB phase for both treatment and placebo arms.

Statistical analysis

All simulations incorporated participant-level random intercepts and slopes to account for within-subject correlation over time. Annualised rates of CDR-SB change were estimated for DB and OLE phases, separately for early-start, delayed-start and combined cohorts. Differences in slopes were evaluated using interaction terms between time and trial phase and linear contrasts using linear mixed-effects models. Comparisons were expressed as annualised CDR-SB change with 95% CIs. To minimise reliance on modelling assumptions, we validated our findings by directly comparing observed annualised slopes of decline between DB and OLE phases using unpaired t-tests. The results from simulation data and observed data analysis, separately for early and delayed start cohorts, were further confirmed in additional sensitivity analyses by directly extracting data and comparisons from combined cohorts using random effects models as well as fixed effects models. For establishing the placebo effect, the CDR-SB decline in the placebo group from the DB phase was compared with the simulated data of untreated controls stratified by CDR and overall cohorts using random effects and fixed effects models. Sensitivity analyses were further performed by comparing the placebo arm of the DB phase to the overall control cohort to assess the robustness of placebo effect estimates. The statistical codes for data simulation using random-effects models, separately for the early- and delayed-start cohorts and the untreated control cohort, are also included in the online supplemental file appendix A. Analyses were performed in Stata 19 with statistical significance set at p <0.05.

Results

Reproduction of published trial trajectories

Simulated CDR-SB trajectories closely reproduced the published trial results for both early- and delayed-start cohorts, as well as the untreated control cohort (online supplemental table 1). The estimated effect size using simulated and observed data for lecanemab (−0.49, 95% CI−0.62 to −0.36; and −0.44, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.16) and donanemab (−0.58, 95% CI −0.79 to −0.37; and −0.65, 95% CI −0.93 to −0.37) closely matched the reported effect sizes in the DB phase (−0.45, 95% CI −0.67 to −0.23; and −0.6, 95% CI −0.9 to −0.3) of published trials. Moreover, the simulated data also showed a distribution quite close to that of the combined cohorts of trials and untreated controls (online supplemental tables 2 and 3).

Comparison of DB and OLE slopes in the lecanemab study

In the early-start cohort, the annual rate of CDR-SB decline was significantly faster during the OLE phase than during the DB phase (−1.30 vs −0.78 points/year; ∆=0.53; 95% CI 0.40 to 0.66) (figure 1A). In contrast, in the delayed-start cohort, the decline rate in the OLE phase was modest compared with the DB phase (−1.21 vs −1.01 points/year; ∆=0.19; 95% CI 0.06 to 0.32), indicating convergence of slopes once all participants were receiving active treatment (figure 1A). Although no meaningful difference, the annual decline was faster in the delayed-start cohort of the OLE phase compared with the early start cohort of the DB phase (∆=0.10; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.21). These findings were consistent in both simulated and observed-data analyses (online supplemental table 5).

In combined-cohort analyses, the annualised slope of CDR-SB decline during the DB phase was −0.64 points per year (95% CI −0.76 to −0.53; p<0.001). During the OLE phase, this steepened to −1.13 points per year (95% CI −1.20 to −1.07), representing a statistically significant acceleration of decline (p<0.001). Notably, the OLE slope did not differ from the placebo slope observed during the DB phase, which was −1.24 points per year (−1.24; 95% CI −1.35 to −1.13; p=0.46) (figure 1B).

Comparison of DB and OLE slopes in the donanemab study

A similar pattern was observed for donanemab. In the early-start cohort, the annual rate of decline accelerated during the OLE phase compared with the DB phase (−1.73 vs −1.07 points/year; ∆=0.66; 95% CI 0.41 to 0.91) (figure 2A). In the delayed-start cohort, however, decline rates were similar between phases (−1.27 vs −1.36 points/year; ∆=−0.09; 95% CI −0.33 to 0.14), indicating convergence of slopes once all participants were receiving active treatment (figure 2A). The annual decline was faster in the delayed-start cohort of the OLE phase compared with the early start cohort of the DB phase (∆=0.46; 95% CI 0.25 to 0.67). These findings were confirmed in observed-data analyses (online supplemental table 4).

In combined-cohort analyses, the annualised slope of DB decline was −1.12 points per year (95% CI −1.24 to −1.00; p<0.001), while the OLE steepened to −1.67 points per year (95% CI −1.86 to −1.49). Also here, the OLE slope was statistically indistinguishable from the DB placebo slope of −1.55 points per year (95% CI −1.62 to −1.48; p=0.206). Results were consistent in fixed-effects models (online supplemental table 5) (figure 2B).

Placebo effects relative to untreated controls

In the lecanemab trial, participants assigned to placebo during the DB phase declined significantly more slowly than matched untreated historical controls (∆=0.62 CDR-SB points/year; 95% CI 0.48 to 0.76; p<0.001), regardless of baseline CDR stratum (figure 1B). In contrast, in the donanemab trial, the DB placebo group did not differ significantly from matched untreated controls (∆=0.07 points/year; 95% CI −0.15 to 0.28; p=0.53), particularly among participants with baseline CDR=0.5 (online supplemental table 6) (figure 2B). These findings were robust in fixed-effects models using both stratified and overall untreated control cohorts (online supplemental tables 6 and 7).

Discussion

In contrast with the perception of increasing benefit with longer exposure to monoclonal antibodies suggested by the recently published OLE studies,3 4 we observed that (a) both trials in their OLE phases exhibited a greater and steeper decline compared with the decline observed in the DB phase of these trials and (b) the annual rate of decline in the OLE phase approximated the rate observed in the placebo arm of the DB phase, suggesting no sustained disease-modifying effect during open-label treatment.

The steepened slope of decline observed in treated subjects in the OLE period, despite increasing enrichment with slower-progressing participants, is not explained by the natural history of AD, which shows a largely linear CDR-SB progression over 5 years in individuals with baseline CDR scores of 0.5 or 1 and does not significantly change in subjects who progress from CDR 0.5 to CDR 1.0.8 Instead, the similar slopes of decline between OLE and DB placebo suggest that the modest benefit observed during the DB phase may largely reflect placebo effects rather than sustained disease modification.

The lack of a disease-modifying effect was supported by Eisai’s delayed-start analysis: when comparing participants who received lecanemab during the 18-month core trial with those who initially received placebo and then crossed over to lecanemab for 18 months, the trajectories of the two groups ran in parallel. By 36 months, instead of separating as expected with disease modification, the curves appeared to be converging.9 The same phenomenon has been observed in the donanemab delayed-start trial.10

Because comparisons with historical cohorts are highly vulnerable to selection bias,5 and there are no alternative proper comparisons against an active intervention, we compared the decline slopes between the OLE and DB phases directly within the treated groups. Further, we compared the placebo-treated data from the DB phase with publicly available historical control data from a large study8 to illustrate that placebo-treated participants may show more favourable outcomes than untreated external controls. This comparison suggests that the use of untreated ADNI participants as control groups, as performed in two recently published papers on the OLE studies of lecanemab and donanemab,3 4 may be inappropriate because it does not account for the potential beneficial effects of trial participation, expectation, close monitoring and placebo exposure.

A similar simulation-based approach was recently applied to published randomised trial datasets to evaluate whether amyloid reduction can serve as a valid surrogate for clinical benefit in anti-amyloid therapy trials.11 Using simulated data calibrated to reproduce the characteristics and outcomes of the original randomised studies, the authors concluded that the association between plaque reduction and cognitive outcomes was substantially weaker and more uncertain than suggested by the aggregate-trial correlation reported in a prior secondary analysis.12 The accompanying methodological discussion emphasised the importance of within-trial analyses when individual-level participant data are not publicly available, rather than relying on surrogate extrapolations or indirect assumptions. Greater data transparency in AD trials, including timely access to de-identified participant-level datasets to permit independent validation of therapeutic claims, would reduce the need for such indirect analyses.13

Despite selective retention (approaching half the original cohorts: 56% lecanemab; 53% donanemab), which enriches OLE cohorts with slower progressors6 and inflates benefit estimates,7 the slopes of decline of lecanemab and donanemab in the DB phase did not level out or flatten in the OLE phase, but steepened, indicating an acceleration of cognitive decline. While this is inconsistent with progressive slope attenuation, alternative explanations include waning placebo effects after DB unblinding, regression to the mean, trial context changes or selective retention. The published OLE articles themselves acknowledge survivorship enrichment, which strengthens the rationale for our modelling approach.

One of the primary limitations of the simulated slopes calibrated to reproduce the mean changes, variances and overall trajectories reported in published cohorts was the assumption of random attrition to incorporate the dropout rate at each follow-up visit. If the real-world OLE attrition is non-random, then the resulting estimates may be biased. To address this concern, we directly compared the slopes in OLE vs DB using aggregated data analysis instead of the simulation framework. Another limitation of simulation modelling is the potential underestimation of intraclass correlation estimates. However, our sensitivity analyses based on fixed effects modelling yielded results consistent with the primary findings, supporting the robustness of the observed longitudinal trends. In our analysis, published historical cohorts are used solely for contextualisation of placebo magnitude. Our primary inference relies on within-trial DB vs OLE comparisons, not historical contrasts. Thus, the conclusions do not depend on cross-study matching. We also confirmed the results using combined cohort data analysis by simulating data with random- and fixed-effects models. Data extraction from multiple published reports, including abstracts and articles, and comparisons between OLE and DB based on simulation data using random- and fixed-effects models, along with observed data analyses, support the robustness of the findings.

In conclusion, these findings challenge claims of increasing long-term benefit based on comparisons with historical untreated cohorts.14 More broadly, they caution against inferring disease-modifying effects from OLE data without contemporaneous controls and highlight the importance of within-trial comparisons to disentangle true treatment effects from placebo responses and selection bias. Altogether, the combination of modest cognitive and clinical efficacy of lecanemab and donanemab observed in the 18-month, double-blind, effect-controlled studies in subjects with early AD, and the apparent acceleration of clinical decline during the subsequent OLE phases of these trials, raises doubts about the true clinical utility of these drugs. This concern is particularly relevant considering that the modest cognitive benefits are associated with a massive reduction in brain amyloid load, the biological target of these drugs. The apparent disconnect between the marked brain Aβ clearance and the lack of sustained long-term clinical modification questions whether brain amyloid reduction can meaningfully alter the long-term clinical trajectories of patients with early AD.
