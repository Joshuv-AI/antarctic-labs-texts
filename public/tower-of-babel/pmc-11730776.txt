Extending the concept of “obstetric violence” to post-partum experiences: cautions regarding the “first ever” pill for post-partum depression
Authors: Alicia Ely Yamin, Lisa Cosgrove
Published: 2024

Introduction

Feminists have long articulated ways in which power operates within and beyond health systems to constitute identities and discourses. Indeed, the hegemonic power of biomedicine in the delivery room and beyond “is a common but unnamed element, and its hegemony seems to erase the need to report its existence”.1 As a result, certain women and minoritised sexual groups are systematically disadvantaged in terms of both access and treatment. Further, broader political and economic processes are inextricably entangled with structures of reproductive governance, defined as “the mechanisms through which different historical configurations of actors [ ] use legislative controls, economic inducements, moral injunctions, direct coercion, and ethical incitements to produce, monitor and control reproductive behaviours and practices”2.

The growing identification of “obstetric violence”, first in Latin America and subsequently far more widely around the world3,4 and in supra-national human rights forums’ jurisprudence,5 has been a pivotal site for contesting the power asymmetries encoded into the biomedical paradigm and examining reproductive governance. Statutory definitions vary somewhat but the legislation in Argentina defines obstetric violence as “[v]iolence exercised by health personnel on the body and reproductive processes of pregnant women and gestating persons, expressed through dehumanizing treatment, medicalization abuse, and the conversion of natural processes of reproduction into pathological ones … ”.6 A key aspect of deploying “obstetric violence”,7 as opposed to the discourses of “disrespect and abuse”8 or, in the US, the more common “obstetric mistreatment”9 has been to challenge the epistemic paradigm of pregnancy and childbirth as a medical condition or event, as opposed to a natural process.10

Until now, little attention has been paid to post-partum obstetric violence beyond egregious acts such as detentions in health facilities for non-payment of fees.11 We argue here for expanding consideration of the post-partum period to include inappropriate pharmaceuticalisation, with a focus on the US context. Just as overcrowding of facilities in low- and middle-income settings can produce the conditions that foster obstetric violence, so too can neglect of social determinants of health. For example, in the US, the organisation of a highly privatised and financialised health system promotes an over-reliance on pharmaceutical solutions.

According to the Centres for Disease Control, approximately one out of eight women in the US now suffer from post-partum depression (PPD), a seven-fold increase between 2000 and 2015.12 Nearly two of three maternal deaths in the US occur during the postpartum period. Suicide and drug overdoses are among the leading causes of pregnancy-related deaths in the US, defined as death during pregnancy, labour or within the first year of childbirth.13 Although non-Hispanic white women are still mostly diagnosed in absolute numbers, studies show that those identifying as Native American, Pacific Islander or Black are disproportionately affected by post-partum depressive symptoms and that they are increasingly being diagnosed at higher rates than White women.14 Newly available pharmaceutical solutions15 may appear as a solution to lack of access, especially for women of colour who are not treated for PPD with the standard of care as often as White women.16 However, the biomedical framing shifts attention from the socio-political causes of emotional distress, which is crucial for reproductive justice. This framing has significant consequences for individual women, public health and how we address the multi-faceted impacts of systemic racism in our health system.

In the US, the reproductive justice movement developed in the early 1990s when a group of Black feminists organised. Shifting attention away from a narrow individualised focus on bodily autonomy, their aim was to focus on how women of colour experience oppression in the health system.17–19 Reproductive justice, which is consonant with much of sexual and reproductive rights activism in the international arena, understands reproductive health in the context of the social determinants of health and intersectionality, and concomitantly, the political economy of decision-making over women’s bodies.20 Reproductive justice calls for all reproductive subjects to have the ability to decide if, when, and how to have children, and the right to parent children in safe, healthy, and sustainable conditions. We argue here that the reduction of reproductive subjects’ experiences to biochemical disorders, together with the lowering of regulatory standards for the pharmaceutical industry, threaten to perpetuate a subtle but damaging form of obstetric violence, which detracts from structural concerns of reproductive justice. Taking the recent approval of the “first-ever” pill for post-partum depression, zuranolone (brand name Zurzuvae) as a case example, we maintain that prescribing this medication without robust informed consent about its potential harms represents a subtle but dangerous form of obstetric violence through the pharmaceuticalisation of PPD treatment.

Obstetric violence as a structural issue

The term obstetric violence is often reflexively rejected by the obstetric community as tarnishing the entire profession when in fact abuses are only committed rarely by the proverbial “bad apples”.21 Such resistance fundamentally misunderstands the nature of obstetric violence. Individual providers may deliberately perpetrate obstetric violence.7 However, the complex nature of such violence necessitates a systemic and nuanced approach precisely to move away from civil liability (e.g. tort liability in the US) that frames these issues in terms of inter-personal conflicts and malpractice. When taken-for-granted practices in medicalised childbirth are denaturalised and understood as violations of reproductive agency, it becomes possible to draw attention to structural factors and informal and background norms that pervade health systems, which systematically deprive reproductive subjects of agency.22 For example, women are often not provided with necessary information to make fully informed choices, due to harmful “gender stereotypes on the role of women” as “merely passive subjects with a reproductive role”.5 In the US, obstetric violence is closely linked with obstetric racism, as the patterns of discrimination in the overall society permeate our health system as well as all other social institutions.23 The understanding of “obstetric racism” as a structural phenomenon that systematically causes injury to certain populations has grown increasingly accepted, and validated metrics have been developed to measure it.23

Obstetric violence often arises out of the political and economic organisation of our social world and the frameworks for reproductive governance that we come to accept. Underfunded health systems across the world produce staffing shortages, overcrowding and incentives for quick deliveries to free up beds, or, in the worst case, a “maternity ward where women deliver on the floor, alone, in unhygienic conditions”.24 In the US, funding shortages and the hollowing out of health networks through privatisation and financialisation especially affect women and disadvantaged minorities.13 Maternal health generally is exceptionally poor in the US compared with other high-income countries in the Organization for Cooperation and Economic Development (OECD). In 2022, 22.3 US women per 100,000 died either during pregnancy or within a year of giving birth. By comparison, the United Kingdom reported 5.5 maternal deaths per 100,000. However, while White women had a maternal mortality rate of 19 per 100,000 in the US, Black women had a rate of 49.5 per 100,000.13

Further, since the overturning of Roe v Wade in 2022, increasing restrictions on abortion coverage are further fostering maternal health deserts, impacting poor, Black and rural women disproportionately.25,26 These factors operate on a structural level, through “systemic deficiencies that create a disrespectful or abusive environment” at the micro-level, including poor treatment or conditions that are then normalised.24 Studies in the US suggest that such treatment in the intra-partum period fosters psycho-social effects in the post-partum period.1

There are profound racial and class inequities embedded in the US health system at both the macro-level of financing and the meso-level of care delivery. These inequalities take a particular toll on women and minoritised reproductive subjects who too often experience poverty, marginalisation and exclusion from US society through contacts with the health system during pregnancy, childbirth and the post-partum period. In this context, the assurance of full, prior, and informed consent becomes an especially important tool to curb obstetric violence at the micro-level. Unfortunately, as Sadler et al note, there are critical flaws “in the assumption that women fully understand their options and are always able to make free, adequate choices”.27,28 In international human rights law, this gap has been addressed by requiring a standard of “active information” where providers retain a duty to “provide the necessary information for individuals to be able to exercise [all their] rights” such as access to different forms of health services and the ability “to take free, full, well-informed decisions … concerning their own body and sexual and reproductive health”.29 As the Inter-American Court of Human Rights has recognised, duties of active information are particularly essential when there are sharp asymmetries of power between physicians and patients, due to language, education, class, race, and other factors.28

Extending the implications of obstetric violence to post-partum experiences in the US

These two truths can co-exist: on the one hand, severe post-partum depression and psychosis occur and must be treated with medical as well as other forms of care. PPD creates tremendous strains on a woman and can interfere with her ability to take care of herself as well as her baby. A health system in which women of colour are routinely policed and punished calls for considering equity in access to all forms of support at every point in the continuum of reproductive care, including the post-natal period.16 On the other hand, constructing post-partum experiences in terms of “disease” can displace societal factors and may normalise denying agency to reproductive subjects, which undermines reproductive justice.10

Biomedical solutions distinguish “baby blues” – a normal reaction to an extreme transition – from PPD, which is deemed pathological. To receive a PPD diagnosis, a woman must meet the criteria for Major Depressive Disorder with symptoms during pregnancy or within four weeks of birth.30 The widely used screening tools for PPD, the PHQ-9 and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), entirely disregard contextual, financial, institutional, or relational factors implicated in the experience of depression and anxiety during pregnancy and after childbirth. Since 2010, PPD diagnoses in the US have increased far more rapidly in discriminated and structurally disadvantaged populations such as Black, Asia-Pacific Islander and Latina women, than in White women.31

A PPD diagnosis is often profoundly stigmatising and feeds into the powerful tropes that entangle race and gender in US society. The problem is identified as a psychiatric disorder that constructs the woman as a “bad mother”, and converts political failures into personal defects that are disproportionately affecting women of colour. Women with PPD are said to “display hostility and negligence, have lower tolerance, and are less responsive to their infants’ needs”.32 PPD is said to not only disrupt mother-infant bonding, “but [it] also impairs the cognitive, behavioural, and social-emotional development and physical health of the child”.33

Women of colour are more likely to be treated punitively in the healthcare system when they are considered “bad mothers”, and thus are more likely to be forced into the family regulation system under the auspices of “early intervention”, “child protection” and the like. However, providing medications without providing psycho-social support and addressing the environments in which diverse people parent does not ameliorate stigmatisation of perinatal mental health issues. It may, however, deflect attention from the ways in which structural racism pervades our health care system and facilitates paternalistic and punitive “interventions”.

In medicalising PPD, the stereotype of the “good” mother’s selfless devotion to her baby is prioritised over the conditions under which a woman is parenting.16–18 Any previous professional interests, financial contributions to one’s household, and the web of relationships that impact the mother-baby relationship (circumstances of pregnancy, abusive partners or family, poverty, marginalisation, and structural discrimination) are shunted aside.34 Likewise, a lack of community or extended family supports, which can exacerbate feelings of isolation and despair, is disregarded as merely a background factor when making a biomedical diagnosis. Common advice for women suffering from PPD in the US includes: “try to get some help from your family, friends, or a support group”; “ask a relative to watch your baby for a few hours”; and “ask a friend to cook a meal for your family or to help with chores”.35

By contrast, a reproductive justice paradigm centred in human rights would call for greater attention to developing enabling environments for women to flourish in the post-partum period.36 Studies on PPD and the effects of accessible social protections show that “paid and longer maternity leaves tend to be associated with a reduction of postpartum depression symptoms”.32 Unlike its OECD counterparts, in the US there is neither universal healthcare nor publicly funded, guaranteed maternity leave. By comparison, Norway provides 86 weeks paid maternity leave and the United Kingdom provides 39 weeks of paid maternity leave.13 Yet compared to women in the other countries, US women are unlikely to have social supports during the post-partum period. In other high- and even some middle-income countries, new mothers are regularly visited by home nurses or midwives to provide care, support and education, and these visits are covered by the health system.13 In the US, as Dr Werner of Tufts Medical Center points out, “Best case, postpartum people have two visits after they deliver, [generally at the doctor’s office, not at home] … And many of them lose insurance weeks [or] months after delivering”. Similarly, providing broader labour protections than exist in the US has been shown to be effective in improving labour force participation among persons with mild to moderate mental illness symptoms more broadly, which in turn improves quality of life and functioning.37 Post-partum women who do not have access to adequate labour protections, maternity leave, and/or affordable child care cannot earn livelihoods and are at higher risk of evictions. In light of the housing crisis in the US – exacerbated by pandemic pressures and no longer abated by the Emergency Rental Assistance Program – studies have also proposed that lessening the risk of eviction (also disproportionately borne by disadvantaged racial groups) is crucial in decreasing widespread existing psycho-social health burdens in US society.38

For its part, the World Health Organization (WHO) embraces the need to adopt a patient-oriented perspective,39 including addressing social determinants of mental health in all cases – access to education, employment, housing and social benefits – in order to support all persons experiencing mental health conditions.40 Yet these holistic approaches have been displaced by what Dainius Purus, former UN Special Rapporteur on the right to health, called a “reductionist biomedical model” which has come to dominate “clinical practice, policy, research agendas, medical education and investment” in mental health generally.41 In the case of PPD, this over-medicalisation has potentially iatrogenic effects on individual women and creates social harm to all women, and women of colour in particular, which makes it appropriate to use the term obstetric violence.

Challenging pharmacological “solutions” to PPD

The increasing frequency of PPD diagnoses is likely to accelerate quickly with the advent of pharmacological solutions, which allow prescribing physicians to present a quick fix “solution” to a patient’s evident suffering. Until now, the standard of care for PPD has been therapy and anti-depressants (SSRIs).42 However, in 2016 the United States Preventive Services Task Force recommended universal depression screening for women who are pregnant and during the post-partum period, which will likely exacerbate a medicalised and one-size-fits-all approach.43 Because questionnaire-based screening is based on a de-contextualised checklist approach, rather than a sound and thorough diagnostic interview, researchers have found that screening for depression results in high false positive rates, over- or inappropriate treatment, and in the prescription of medication.44 Indeed, Canada and the UK, looking at the same evidence for depression screening, recommended against it because of over-diagnosis and over-treatment concerns.45

In 2019, the FDA approved brexanolone/Zulresso, an intravenous injection to treat PPD, which required a 60-hour hospital stay in a facility approved to administer the drug, and insurance pre-approval. In 2023, the FDA also approved the pill zuranolone to treat PPD, marketed under the brand name Zurzuvae.46, 47 Zuranolone is meant to be taken by patients daily for two weeks, and acts much faster than a typical SSRI antidepressant coupled with therapy.

On the one hand, all women, regardless of ability to pay, should have access to effective mental health treatment. Zurzuvae, manufactured jointly by Biogen and Sage Therapeutics, currently costs approximately $US 16,000 for the two-week course, which is out of reach without insurance coverage. There is an active advocacy campaign urging state Medicaid agencies to make sure that low-income patients have easy access to Zurzuvae. Lobbying groups seek to have Medicaid-managed care plans, as well as private plans, waive prior authorisation requirements and other restrictions, such as “fail-first” approaches that call for patients to try already approved treatments first.48 There is no question that incorporating zuranolone into Medicaid plans and removing need for prior authorisation would exponentially expand access for low-income women. This is important because untreated mental health issues during the perinatal period pose health risks including increased risk of illicit drug misuse or an exacerbation of an underlying psychiatric condition.

On the other hand, rushing to expand demand for the drug may have serious iatrogenic consequences, in addition to displacing attention from the underlying drivers in our laws and policies, and institutional arrangements. Clinical trial data demonstrated a clear potential for abuse due to zuranolone’s “gabaminergic”, or stress-reducing, effects. Moreover, although zuranolone is being marketed as a “neurosteroid” – the definition of which remains somewhat opaque – and not a benzodiazepine, there appear to be significantly understated safety concerns regarding addiction. The FDA reported that (1) “the [human abuse potential] study demonstrates … positive reinforcing effects … similar to that of alprazolam [a benzodiazepine]” and (2) “Zuranolone produced dose-dependent euphoric effects indicating [ ] abuse potential.”49 Further, other studies have raised alarms about the heightened risk of substance use disorder among women who experience a major depressive episode, including PPD.50

It is noteworthy that the Department of Health and Human Services made a recommendation to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) to place zuranolone in schedule IV of the Controlled Substances Act.51 Unlike antidepressants, which are not included on the list of controlled substances, the DEA accepted this recommendation and placed zuranolone in the same category as benzodiazepines. Moreover, there is some evidence from biochemical and electrophysiological studies to suggest that zuranolone is pharmacodynamically more similar to a barbiturate than a benzodiazepine.52 If that proves to be the case, there should be even greater concern about the potential for abuse, dependence, and toxicity, as barbiturates have a narrow therapeutic window and can be fatal in overdose.51

In addition to significant concerns about zuranalone’s safety, there are also limited data assessing its efficacy, which was approved through a rapid “Priority Review”,53 the abuse of which has led the FDA to be accused of regulatory capture in other instances. Zuranolone was not “compared to standard applications”, and it is unknown whether zuranolone is safer and more effective than antidepressants and psychotherapy54 or whether zuranolone would be effective on women with less severe depression. Regulatory approval was based on only two clinical trials submitted to the FDA, both of which only included women who had severe PPD.54 Further, the control group in the study was not treated with a “standard of care” condition, arguably violating ethical obligations of non-maleficence, since “the median length of time to response expected with these interventions [antidepressants and psychotherapy] does not justify withholding treatment from the control group”.54

These safety and efficacy issues raise substantial concerns about the ways in which physicians may prescribe zuranalone, the populations that are targeted, and the informed consent processes that are afforded. When women are not made aware of the full array of risks, including sedation, dizziness, and serious abuse potential, as well as contra-indications for breast-feeding,54 they are effectively denied agency over their bodies and lives at a time of heightened vulnerability. That denial of agency, which is framed by a context of legal and policy indifference to the social and economic conditions of new mothers, should be construed as a form of obstetric violence. Likewise, not being provided with unbiased and balanced information about the limited efficacy data and significant safety concerns of zuranolone, coupled with information about the current standard of care, is also a violation of meaningful informed consent. Without systemic accountability mechanisms in place to ensure standards of “active information” about zuranalone – and the alternatives – prescription of zuranalone may lead to widespread iatrogenic harms and violations of reproductive agency.

Conclusion

Identifying obstetric violence in the US, far from a misnomer, highlights inappropriate practices in pregnancy, intra-partum and post-partum periods that are too often normalised in the US health system.21 While “obstetric violence” is not enshrined in legislation in the US, scholars have highlighted its existence within the healthcare system at every stage of the reproductive process, and its relationship with structural racism.55

We have argued that addressing the complex drivers of psychic distress in the post-partum period requires an array of strategies, which extend far beyond individual medical treatment to changing social institutions to better support those performing social reproduction.56 Reproductive justice calls for strategies that enhance the agency of women and other reproductive subjects over their reproductive choices, understood as connected to the wider conditions of their lives.

At the bedside, our analysis suggests that we must avoid spreading a subtle but dangerous form of obstetric violence through pharmaceuticalisation of PPD treatment. It is imperative that clinicians are appropriately trained and provide post-partum women with information on the limited efficacy data and safety concerns noted in the FDA report. Indeed, all reproductive subjects have the right to a robust informed consent process whereby they are given information in accessible formats about the risks of zuranolone, as well as alternative treatments.

At a health systems level, zuranolone raises difficult questions about structural violence in the practice of both obstetrics and psychiatry, and the role of the pharmaceutical industry in shaping both demand and supply of “solutions”. Righting reproductive governance, and health governance more broadly, will require ending undue industry influence on regulatory bodies. Such influence has led to the over-estimation of new drugs’ efficacy/benefits while under-reporting – or hiding – adverse effects and harms.57 “Priority review” and other policy changes have led to “decreasing regulatory standards, shortening approval times, and increasing industry involvement in FDA decision-making”.58, 59 Indeed, the case of zuranolone illustrates the need for “broader discussions about how regulators define treatment benefits”.60

At a societal level, PPD stands at the intersection of political economies and moral economies of social reproduction. Promoting pharmaceutical solutions without consideration of the context of women’s lives is antithetical to the conceptual framework of reproductive justice. Indeed, reproductive justice calls for tackling the multiple ways in which the US health system currently reinforces intersecting patterns of discrimination and exclusion based on class, race and gender, not deflecting attention through the dispensing of pharmaceutical solutions without consideration of the context of women’s lives.61 At a time when women’s reproductive agency is under assault in the US,62 with particular impacts on low-income women of colour,63,64 we must not fail to see the ways in which limiting people’s choices during pregnancy is linked to the ways in which we construct ideals of motherhood and limit diverse women’s choices after delivery.
