Scale without Conformal Invariance in bottom-up Holography
Lavish Chawla, Mario Flory

Abstract
In holography, the isometry group of the bulk spacetime corresponds to the symmetries of the boundary theory. We thus approach the question of whether (and when) scale invariance in combination with Poincaré invariance implies full conformal invariance in quantum field theory from a holographic bulk perspective. To do so, we study bulk spacetimes that include a warped extra dimension and in which the isometry group corresponds to scale without conformal invariance. Firstly, we show that the bulk Weyl tensor plays a pivotal role in distinguishing those metrics exhibiting conformal invariance (Weyl=0) from those merely exhibiting scale invariance (Weyl ≠ \neq 0). Based on this, we then prove the following theorem: For putative boundary theories with n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 dimensions, the bulk metric can not exhibit scale without conformal invariance if its warped extra dimension is compact and the null energy condition is required to hold. For n = 1 n=1 , we discuss that a more general ansatz for the bulk metric must be made, a detailed analysis of which is left for future research. 3.3
Topology of the Base Space

Equation (
39
) shows that the metric
h_{ij}
must have at least one Killing vector,
J^{i}
. The following is a well-known fact:

Box 3
.

Classification of 2d metrics

In 2 dimensions, a metric can have either

•

no Killing vectors,

•

one Killing vector,

•

or three linearly independent Killing vectors

in any infinitesimal neighbourhood. The latter case corresponds to a maximally symmetric metric with constant curvature
\bar{R}
.

The case of exactly two linearly independent Killing vector fields does not exist, because the Ricci scalar
\bar{R}
has to be constant along flows of the Killing vector fields. But in two dimensions, two linearly independent Killing vector fields would imply the curvature to be constant in all directions, which in 2 dimensions is enough to imply that the spacetime is locally maximally symmetric.
There are some results in the mathematical literature concerning the classification of 2d-metrics admitting exactly one Killing field locally, see for example
Bryant et al. (2007)
;
Matveev (2012)
. However, the big issue that we are facing is the issue of topology.

Consider for instance the case of vanishing
\bar{R}
everywhere. That means locally the metric
h_{ij}
of the base space is just the metric of the flat plane, with three linearly independent Killing vector fields in any small enough neighbourhood. However, as stated in Box
2
, we demand the base space to have the topology of a cylinder. Folding up the flat space to a cylinder preserves two linearly independent Killing vectors globally, but one of the Killing vector fields can not be extended globally in a single valued manner any more.
We will from now on only consider those Killing vector fields to be valid which can be extended globally over the whole base space in a single valued manner
10
10

10

This seems to be a common attitude in the GR community, for instance the BTZ black hole in 3d is well known to be constructed by folding up the (maximally symmetric) AdS
3
space, yet it is commonly described as having only two Killing vectors
Banados et al. (1993)
.
.

There is an additional restriction arising from the non-trivial topology that we have to consider.

There is now one final issue relating to topology that needs to be considered. Essentially, we want to know whether and how we can apply results like the Gauss-Bonnet theorem to the base space as this will be a powerful tool later in this paper. J^{i}
is a Killing vector, then clearly so is
\lambda\cdot J^{i}
with some constant
\lambda\neq 0

\bar{R}
can’t depend on such an overall scaling choice. This is indeed the case with (

\displaystyle\bar{\square}\log(\lambda^{2}J^{2})=\bar{\square}\log(J^{2})+\bar{\square}\log{\lambda^{2}}=\bar{\square}\log(J^{2}).

(58)

Of course, not every metric in 2 dimensions has a Killing vector, but there will always be conformal Killing vectors at least locally because of the conformal flatness of the metric. In appendix

is only a conformal Killing vector.

Lastly, it is clear from our result (

. But is the converse also true?
We assume that in appropriate coordinates, a metric of the form (

), with a Killing vector like (

), covers the

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{J}J^{2}=0.

The same holds true for

, but not

\displaystyle\bar{R}\equiv 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 0=\bar{\square}\log(J^{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\partial_{\theta}h^{\theta\theta}\sqrt{h}\,\partial_{\theta}\log(J^{2})

(60)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow const.\equiv h^{\theta\theta}\sqrt{h}\,\partial_{\theta}\log(J^{2}).

(61)

For a non-degenerate Euclidean metric

, but

is a total derivative of a periodic function, and so is either zero everywhere or switches sign somewhere. Hence (

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\log(J^{2})=0\Rightarrow J^{2}=const.

(62)

Consequently, at least in the cases of relevance to us, we find

\displaystyle\bar{R}=0\Leftrightarrow J^{2}=const.

(63)

3.5
Null Energy Condition

As we indicated in our main conjecture in box
2
, we will use the
null energy condition
(NEC) to judge whether a bulk metric is physically valid or not. The NEC states that for any null vector
n^{\mu}
, we have
Curiel (2017)

\displaystyle 0\leq T_{\mu\nu}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}.

(65)

As we are dealing with null vectors, we can replace
T_{\mu\nu}
with
G_{\mu\nu}
or
R_{\mu\nu}
when assuming Einstein’s equations. It will be beneficial to write this in a form adapted to our warped product ansatz explained earlier in section
3.1
. Specifically, we decompose the null vector as

\displaystyle n^{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{n}^{a}\\
\bar{n}^{i}\\
\end{array}\right),

where
\bar{n}^{i}
is a vector in the base space and
\hat{n}^{a}
is a vector in the (tangent space of the) fiber. This implies

\displaystyle 0\equiv n^{\mu}g_{\mu\nu}n^{\nu}=S\hat{n}_{a}\hat{n}^{a}+\bar{n}^{i}h_{ij}\bar{n}^{j}\Rightarrow\hat{n}_{a}\hat{n}^{a}=-\frac{\bar{n}^{i}h_{ij}\bar{n}^{j}}{S}.

(68)

Next, we use that in the warped product ansatz (
2
), one finds
Nozawa (2008)

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X\eta_{ab}&0\\
0&r_{ij}\\
\end{array}\right)

with, for the case of a two-dimensional fiber on which we are focusing at the moment,

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=-[\sqrt{S}\bar{\square}\sqrt{S}+(\bar{\nabla}\sqrt{S})^{2}]

(71)

\displaystyle=-S[\bar{\nabla}q+2q^{2}],

(72)

and

\displaystyle r_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{R}}{2}h_{ij}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{S}}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}\sqrt{S}

(73)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{R}}{2}h_{ij}-2(\bar{\nabla}_{i}q_{j}+q_{i}q_{j}).

(74)

The NEC hence simplifies to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq T_{\mu\nu}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}

(75)

\displaystyle=R_{\mu\nu}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}

(76)

\displaystyle=X\hat{n}_{a}\hat{n}^{a}+\bar{n}^{i}r_{ij}\bar{n}^{j}

(77)

\displaystyle=\left(r_{ij}-\frac{X}{S}h_{ij}\right)\bar{n}^{i}\bar{n}^{j}\ \ \forall\bar{n}^{j}.

(78)

So, the question becomes whether

\displaystyle t_{ij}

\displaystyle\equiv r_{ij}-\frac{X}{S}h_{ij}

(79)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\bar{R}}{2}+\frac{\bar{\square}\sqrt{S}}{\sqrt{S}}+\frac{(\bar{\nabla}\sqrt{S})^{2}}{S}\right]h_{ij}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{S}}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}\sqrt{S}

(80)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\bar{R}}{2}+\bar{\nabla}q+2q^{2}\right]h_{ij}-2\bar{\nabla}_{i}q_{j}-2q_{i}q_{j}

(81)

is positive semi-definite or not for a given
S
and
h_{ij}
and at any possible location on the base space manifold.

Of course, the base space is Euclidean with a positive definite metric so locally one can always choose a coordinate system where
h_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
, and
t_{ij}
can be treated like a regular
2\times 2
matrix. Such a matrix can be characterized by its trace and determinant:

\displaystyle\text{tr}[t_{ij}]\geq 0\text{ and }\det[t_{ij}]\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow t_{ij}\text{ is positive semi-definite}\Leftrightarrow\text{NEC is satisfied,}

(82)

\displaystyle\text{tr}[t_{ij}]\leq 0\text{ and }\det[t_{ij}]\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow t_{ij}\text{ is negative semi-definite,}

(83)

\displaystyle\det[t_{ij}]<0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow t_{ij}\text{ is indefinite.}

(84)

The trace is easy to determine:

\displaystyle\text{tr}[t_{ij}]=t^{i}_{j}=\bar{R}+\frac{2(\bar{\nabla}\sqrt{S})^{2}}{S}=\bar{R}+2q^{2}.

(85)

To solve for the determinant, we make use of two identities that are valid for
2\times 2
-matrices:

\displaystyle\det\left[A+B\right]

\displaystyle=\det[A]+\det[B]+\text{tr}[A]\text{tr}[B]-\text{tr}[AB],

(86)

\displaystyle\det[B]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{tr}[B]^{2}-\text{tr}[B^{2}]\right).

(87)

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\det[t_{ij}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\bar{R}+\frac{2(\bar{\nabla}\sqrt{S})^{2}}{S}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{S}\left(\bar{\square}\sqrt{S}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{S}(\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}\sqrt{S})(\bar{\nabla}^{i}\bar{\nabla}^{j}\sqrt{S})

(88)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\bar{R}+2q^{2}\right)^{2}+(\bar{\nabla}q+q^{2})^{2}-2(\bar{\nabla}_{i}q_{j}+q_{i}q_{j})(\bar{\nabla}^{i}q^{j}+q^{i}q^{j}).

(89)

AdS
{}_{3}\times
S
1
example:

With what we already know, it is easy to find that

\displaystyle t_{ij}=2q^{2}h_{ij}-2q_{i}q_{j},\ \text{tr}[t_{ij}]=\frac{2}{L^{2}},\ \det[t_{ij}]=0.

(90)

Clearly, the NEC is satisfied everywhere.

B
, we will briefly discuss the weak energy condition (WEC) in warped product spacetimes of the type that we are investigating, however we will show that it doesn’t provide any additional information or constraints on the spacetime compared to the NEC. In appendix

depends on the number of dimensions of the fiber.

4
Role of the Weyl tensor

4.1
Form of the Weyl tensor

In the earlier paper
Flory (2018)
it was already noticed that, but not fully understood why, the bulk Weyl tensor plays a pivotal role in determining whether the bulk isometry group can be extended to full conformal invariance. This may seem natural at first, after all it is well known
Stephani et al. (2003)
that the Weyl tensor is invariant under conformal rescalings of the metric, and vanishes for conformally flat metrics. See e.g.
Iorio et al. (1997)
for information on the relations between scale, Weyl, and conformal invariance.
However, when we talk about scale or conformal invariance in the present work, we always mean scale and special conformal transformations in a putative holographically dual boundary theory, not scale or conformal transformations in the bulk. Hence, upon further consideration, it is not obvious to us at all why the Weyl tensor should have been expected to be so important.
As we will show in this section, the vanishing or not-vanishing of the Weyl tensor allows us to distinguish between those metrics in which scale invariance is extended to full conformal invariance, and those metrics that correspond to genuine scale- without conformal invariance.

To begin, we note that for a generic metric, the Weyl tensor

\displaystyle C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\equiv R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{\alpha[\gamma}R_{\delta]\beta}-g_{\beta[\gamma}R_{\delta]\alpha}\right)+\frac{1}{3}Rg_{\alpha[\gamma}g_{\delta]\beta}

(91)

depends on the metric and up to second derivatives of it, and consequently can be quite complicated. For a metric of the warped product form (
2
) however, the Weyl tensor can be written in a form

\displaystyle C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\phi\,\tilde{C}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta},\ \ C_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}C^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{3}\phi^{2},

(92)

where only the scalar

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\bar{R}+\bar{\square}\log(S)

(93)

\displaystyle=\bar{R}+\frac{2\bar{\square}\sqrt{S}}{\sqrt{S}}-\frac{2(\bar{\nabla}\sqrt{S})^{2}}{S}

(94)

\displaystyle=\bar{R}+2\bar{\nabla}q

(95)

depends on derivatives of the metric
Nozawa (2008)
. It is clear from (
95

57
) that
\phi
is a total derivative. By contrast, the ”reduced Weyl tensor”
\tilde{C}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
only depends on metric components directly, and consequently takes on a relatively simple form in any coordinate system (see Appendix D of
Nozawa (2008)
). This also means that

\displaystyle C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=0\Leftrightarrow\phi=0.

(96)

{}_{3}\times

that if scale invariance is already present, then

implies full conformal invariance, and vice versa. However, first we establish an identity which will be very useful later. Since

\displaystyle 0=q^{i}\bar{\nabla}_{i}(J^{k}q_{k})

This result elegantly connects the Weyl tensor, the NEC, and the base space Killing vector

Using (

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=-\bar{\square}\log(J^{2})+\bar{\square}\log(S)

On the other hand we can write,

\displaystyle J^{i}J_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{1}{2}S\bar{\nabla}^{i}\mathcal{K}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{2}S\bar{\nabla}_{i}\mathcal{K}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left(-\frac{1}{2}S\bar{\nabla}^{i}\mathcal{K}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{2}S\bar{\nabla}_{i}\mathcal{K}\right)=\mathcal{K}S

Let us manipulate (

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}^{i}J_{i}

\displaystyle=-\bar{\nabla}^{i}\left(\frac{2\mathcal{K}}{\bar{\nabla}^{j}\mathcal{K}\bar{\nabla}_{j}\mathcal{K}}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\mathcal{K}\right)

\displaystyle u^{\lambda}\nabla_{\lambda}(p^{\mu}q_{\nu})=\mathcal{L}_{u}(p^{\mu}q_{\nu})=\mathcal{L}_{u}(p^{\mu})q_{\nu}+p^{\mu}\mathcal{L}_{u}(q_{\nu}).

\displaystyle J^{i}\bar{\nabla}_{i}(\bar{\nabla}^{j}\mathcal{K}\bar{\nabla}_{j}\mathcal{K})=2\bar{\nabla}^{j}\mathcal{K}\bar{\nabla}_{j}(\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{J}{\mathcal{K})}

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}^{i}J_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\mathcal{K}^{2}\bar{\square}\log\mathcal{K}}{\bar{\nabla}^{i}\mathcal{K}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\mathcal{K}}

4.6
Petrov Classification

In this section, we discuss the algebraic classification of the Weyl tensor using the Newman–Penrose (NP) formalism
Newman and Penrose (1962)
. This involves projecting the Weyl tensor onto a complex null tetrad {
l_{\mu},k_{\mu},m_{\mu},\bar{m}_{\mu}
}— a complete basis consisting of two real
\{l_{\mu},k_{\mu}\}
and a complex-conjugate pair
\{m_{\mu},\bar{m}_{\mu}\}
of null vectors, which satisfy the following properties:

\displaystyle l_{\mu}l^{\mu}=k_{\nu}k^{\nu}=m_{\rho}m^{\rho}=\bar{m}_{\sigma}\bar{m}^{\sigma}=0\,,

\displaystyle l_{\mu}m^{\mu}=l_{\nu}\bar{m}^{\nu}=k_{\rho}m^{\rho}=k_{\sigma}\bar{m}^{\sigma}=0\,,

\displaystyle-l_{\mu}k^{\mu}=m^{\nu}\bar{m}_{\nu}=1\,,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=-l_{\mu}k_{\nu}-k_{\mu}l_{\nu}+m_{\mu}\bar{m}_{\nu}+\bar{m}_{\mu}m_{\nu}\,,

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}=-l^{\mu}k^{\nu}-k^{\mu}l^{\nu}+m^{\mu}\bar{m}^{\nu}+\bar{m}^{\mu}m^{\nu}\,.

(126)

Projecting the Weyl tensor onto this basis yields five complex scalars
\{\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},\Psi_{3},\Psi_{4}\}
, which collectively encode its independent components. These are known as Weyl scalars and are defined as follows
Newman and Penrose (1962)
;
Stephani et al. (2003)
(see also appendix E of
Frolov and Novikov (1998)
):

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}\equiv C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}l^{\mu}m^{\nu}l^{\rho}m^{\sigma}\,,\;\;\Psi_{1}\equiv C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}l^{\mu}k^{\nu}l^{\rho}m^{\sigma}\,,

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2}C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}l^{\mu}k^{\nu}(l^{\rho}k^{\sigma}-m^{\rho}\bar{m}^{\sigma}),

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}\equiv C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}l^{\mu}k^{\nu}k^{\rho}\bar{m}^{\sigma}\,,\;\;\Psi_{4}\equiv C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\bar{m}^{\mu}k^{\nu}\bar{m}^{\rho}k^{\sigma}\,.

(127)

The classification of the Weyl tensor is based on which of these Weyl scalars vanish and is referred to as the Petrov classification. This serves as a practical method for determining the Petrov type of a spacetime. Another approach, which more closely resembles Petrov’s original method, involves solving an eigenvalue problem after projecting the Weyl tensor onto the space of bivectors (see
Stephani et al. (2003)
for more details).

To determine the Petrov type of the four-dimensional bulk spacetime (
2
) under consideration, we construct a complex null tetrad that satisfies (
126
).
But before doing so, to simplify the calculations, we can use the fact that every 2D metric is conformally flat, and choose a coordinate system
\{u,v\}
(without loss of generality) for our base space such that the bulk metric locally takes the following simpler form

14
14

14

These are called
isothermal coordinates
on the base space, and can locally be constructed by solving the Beltrami equation. However, it is unclear to us whether in general this construction extends to the entire base space in a single valued manner due to the non-trivial topology. As the Petrov classification is only concerned with local curvature, we do not have to worry about this issue right now. The metric ansatz (

) and some of its properties were studied in this coordinate system in
Rykała (2024)
.
:

6.1

6.2
Synge g-method, spacetime (reverse-)engineering and geometrization

Our strategy in this paper has been to construct a spacetime with certain geometrical properties which we considered to be interesting or desirable (ansatz (
2
) with SwCI isometry group and cylindrical base space), and then check the physicality of this spacetime by computing the energy momentum tensor assuming Einstein’s equations and using the NEC as a criterion. This approach to dealing with Einstein’s equations has sometimes been referred to as the
Synge g-method
after
Synge (1960)
, and is often critically compared with the more traditional approach of making an ansatz for both matter content and spacetime symmetries and then solving Einstein’s equations as a set of coupled PDEs. This approach is a common way to consider various science-fiction inspired concepts like wormholes
Visser (1995)
, warpdrives
Santiago et al. (2022)
or tractor beams
Santiago et al. (2021)
from a hard-science perspective, and thus maybe the term of
spacetime (reverse-)engineering
(following
Santiago et al. (2021)
) would be more appropriate.

Whatever one may call it, the strategy is fundamentally the same in those papers and ours, and so we might ask what we could learn from this body of literature, or vice versa.
Checking the NEC (or WEC) for an exactly given metric may seem trivial, but for generic ansätze, where entire functions of the coordinates appearing in the metric may be left unspecified a priori (like
S(w,\theta)
was in our example), checking the NEC (or WEC) for the entire class of metrics at once may be non-trivial (see e.g.
Santiago et al. (2022)
). In our work, we were able to establish relatively good control over the NEC thanks to the many isometries and the warped product structure (
2
) which allowed us to break everything down to a two dimensional problem and the
relatively
easy to check condition (
82
). Whether this trick can be applied to other spacetime engineering problems would have to be seen on a case by case basis.
Considering a transfer of methods in the other direction, there have been some exciting developments concerning numerical tools for the analysis and optimization of warp drive geometries
Helmerich et al. (2024a)
;
Helmerich et al. (2024b)
;
Fuchs et al. (2024)
. If we were to extend our ansatz (
2
) e.g. to higher base space dimensions or different topology, it might be interesting to see whether such numerical toolkits can be adapted in order to search (by hand or in some automated way) for physical metrics exhibiting SwCI.

Suppose a physical metric with desirable properties (such as SwCI) were to be found under more general conditions than the ones where our no-go theorem applies. Then, the next step would be to not only show that the NEC is satisfied, but to show what exact matter configuration gives rise to the necessary energy-momentum-tensor. This task is known as
geometrization
, and some broad results can be found in
Krongos and Torre (2015)
. One may also replace the NEC with such geometrisation conditions, and try to prove e.g. statements on whether or not SwCI can be realised in a spacetime sourced by only a scalar field or only a gauge field for instance. We can already deliver one statement of this type (see appendix
C
for a detailed discussion): If the spacetime is sourced by a scalar field, then we will necessarily have
\phi=0
and thus SwCI will always be extended to full conformal invariance. Another no-go result for when the bulk matter is described by the specific low energy bosonic action of String/M-theory under a certain gauge condition is already given in
Nakayama (2010a)
;
Nakayama (2010b)
.

6.3
Relaxation of the NEC

Of course, we might also consider relaxing the NEC as defining test of the physicality of a spacetime. While the NEC is satisfied by most classical matter configurations, it is well known that it can be violated by quantum effects such as the Casimir effect

;
Visser (1995)
. In realistic systems, the negative Casimir energy tends to be literally outweighed by the positive mass of the plates needed to generate the effect

;
Visser (1995)

. Consequently, instead of strictly enforcing the NEC
