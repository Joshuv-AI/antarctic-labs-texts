Introduction

Universal health coverage (UHC) means that all people have access to the full range of quality health services they need, when and where they need them, without financial hardship. It covers the full continuum of essential health services, from health promotion to prevention, treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care across the life course. The latest UHC global monitoring report shows that improvements to health services coverage have stagnated since 2015 while the proportion of the population that faced catastrophic levels of out-of-pocket health spending has increased.1 What can be done to reverse these trends?

There are certainly several pathways to progress, but evidence points to the fact that countries with primary healthcare (PHC)-oriented health systems are better equipped to progress towards UHC, health security and well-being.2,4 Using a PHC approach entails ensuring that all efforts to strengthen health systems are being shaped in consideration of three integrated and interrelated components: the need to provide integrated health services and essential public health functions, the need to ensure multisectoral policy and action, and the need to empower people and communities as actors of their health.

As often, the critical question will be: How to get there and (re)orient health systems towards a PHC approach?

How a comprehensive use of legal instruments contributes to health system reorientation

A review of 40 years of implementation experience of the PHC approach highlighted the role of political will and governance as a major enabling factor for reorienting health systems—stressing specifically the role of legislation for health.5

Legislation is made of a vast array of legal and regulatory instruments that perform different functions—establishing fundamental rights that underpin the social contract (eg, constitution, international treaties), translating rights into entitlements (eg, health benefits), establishing mandates and hierarchy lines (eg, statutes, decentralisation laws), harmonising delivery and quality of care (eg, standards and regulation) or introducing public health interventions (eg, sin taxes, prohibition of harmful products). These instruments working together constitute the legal and regulatory framework of a country, an essential tool to inform, formalise and operationalise political commitments.

The experience of Thailand, one of the top performers in UHC, illustrates how political will and legislation successfully come together to advance progress. In Thailand, the 30-Baht scheme was initially introduced during an election campaign in 2000 and then piloted in a few provinces over a 2-year period.6 Shortly afterwards, the scheme was formalised by adopting the National Health Security Act that laid the basis of UHC for all Thai citizens. In addition to this act, the country used legal tools not only to regulate the cost of services and enhance health financing but also to institutionalise a high-level multisectoral approach by creating a governing structure involving inter alia the prime minister and various ministries, thus formalising mechanisms to better integrate services and foster multisectoral action, two key dimensions of the PHC approach.7 8

Similarly, in Uruguay, the health reform to establish an Integrated National Health System was implemented through an array of laws responding to identified problems and reflecting the PHC approach. Some of the issues faced by the country included inequity in the benefit provision across institutions, delays in payment of salaries, high-priced medications, unemployment.9 To respond to these, the country adopted a series of laws establishing the National Health Fund to address financial protection, decentralising the Health Services Administration to enable better empowerment and financial protection of the people and creating an integrated national health system (SNIS).10

The previous examples illustrate how legislation, when used as a coherent combination of these legal and regulatory instruments, can be used to spearhead and institutionalise PHC-health system reorientation in a way that responds to the challenges, preferences and capacities of the countries.

Why the potential of legal tools is largely underused

Despite its important contributions to health progress, findings from existing studies and countries’ insights suggest that the use of legal tools to advance health is largely untapped in some countries.11 12 Why is that?

One reason could be that the contribution of laws to reorienting health systems towards PHC principles and values is neither sufficiently documented nor recognised. While some work has been done on the role of legislation for specific health topics (eg, road safety, tobacco control, sexual and reproductive health), much more investment is needed to promote awareness of the role of legislation for health system strengthening in general and reorienting health systems towards PHC in particular.

Another reason could lie in the underutilisation of holistic approaches to health legislation strengthening. This speaks to the need to leverage synergistically all functions of legislation across the health system—those that address ‘visible’ measures (eg, specific public health interventions such as health benefits, tobacco bans) and those that provide the ‘less visible’ arrangements that will enable implementation and foster system’s efficiency (eg, coherent mandates, sufficient means and accountability mechanisms). Still, too often, legislative measures introduced in countries are taken in isolation and for specific political or societal reasons which can prove ineffective when missing their integration into the overall system. Overlooking the needed comprehensiveness of legal reforms can create barriers, fragmentation, unnecessary administrative burdens and possibly contradictions; further eroding the trust in the importance of legal interventions for health.

A third reason could be the reluctance of policy-makers to tackle the (perceived) length and complexity of the law-making process. Well-conducted reforms often require time, meaningful stakeholders’ engagement, adequate management of conflicting interests and priorities, and a strong evidence and information base13 including on cost-effectiveness of interventions, implementation feasibility and availability of resources. All these are essential ingredients but can also affect the policy-makers’ appetite to embark on major reforms. Additionally, benefits from a reform are quite often visible after a few policy cycles, meaning that lawmakers need to be willing to do what is in the best interest of the population, even if it may be unpopular at the time and if others may reap the political benefits from it.

Finally, whether because they underestimate the potential of legal instruments or because they are reluctant to embark on the complex process that law-making is, policy-makers can be tempted to focus their efforts on national health policies, strategies and plans (NHPSP), a technocratic process that they know very well and over which they exercise greater control. However, while this can be perceived as a quick fix likely to achieve faster progress, when not coupled with appropriate legal frameworks, these plans and strategies too are likely to encounter frequent implementation challenges.

The way forward to harness the power of comprehensive legal frameworks for PHC

While many challenges can be faced by the countries on the path to health reforms, harnessing the power of laws is not a step the countries can afford to miss—an enabling legal system largely contributes to fit-for-purpose institutional arrangements and is a key determinant to the sustainability and scale of successful policy implementation.

To assist countries overcome the challenges faced more investments are needed. There is a need to document what works (legal interventions needed to (re)orient health systems around a PHC approach), how legal processes may be successfully navigated (political economy of legal reforms) and how implementation can be sustained. The path to progress is certainly one that requires incremental changes and would greatly benefit from legal monitoring and evaluation to assess progress and impact over time.

Stakeholders committed to reorienting health systems towards PHC, including the WHO, should, therefore, collaborate to enhance country capacities to harness the power of legal instruments through dimensions such as:

Conclusion

Similar to legal reforms, all this will not happen overnight, but it is critical to increase investment in this area of ‘legal determinants of health’15 and further promote, integrate and document the legal dimensions of health reforms needed to reorient health systems along PHC principles and values. Country examples highlight how legal and regulatory tools used in synergies offer a potent strategic lever to reorient health systems. It is high time for countries, development partners and other stakeholders to scale up efforts to better understand, use and integrate this lever in health system strengthening and research endeavours.
