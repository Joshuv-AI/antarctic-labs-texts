Why stop at extubation?—the difference between enhanced recovery and on table extubation after paediatric cardiac surgery
Authors: Tim Murphy, Louise Davies, Barny Allan, Tuan Chen Aw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There remains ongoing interest in the optimal approach to the perioperative management of a subset of paediatric cardiac surgical patients characterised by lower complexity surgery, in less complex patients, the outcomes from which may be reasonably predictable. Immediate postoperative management may feature timely separation from mechanical ventilation within a specified short period after completion of surgery, together with varying approaches to the entire process of admission for surgery covering the preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative phases of care, and including time spent in an intensive care unit. The term ‘fast track’ became the first established descriptor of the management approach for this subset of patients. Over time, there have been further processes of evolution of the approach to perioperative management, across the range of specialties that are involved in the care of children undergoing surgical correction of a congenital heart defect. The most formalised and protocolised approach to these phases of care is, in its most modern descriptor, now more commonly referred to as ‘enhanced recovery after surgery’ (ERAS). This review explores the history, evolution, evidence base and current status of the approaches to managing such straightforward paediatric cardiac surgical cases and in particular the difference between ERAS compared to models of care in which primary emphasis is placed on management of intubation and subsequent extubation of the trachea, and which have been variously referred to as ‘early extubation’ or ‘on table extubation’.

Introduction

The process of care for children undergoing surgery for congenital cardiac defects has developed significantly since the earliest reports of success with a range of procedures including ligation of patent ductus arteriosus, the Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt, repair of tetralogy of Fallot and staged palliation of single ventricle defects including the Norwood procedure for hypoplastic left heart syndrome (1,2). There has been considerable progression in diagnostic techniques, surgical and catheter-based interventions and anaesthetic and intensive care management, and corresponding progression from early goals such as simply seeking to maximise survival to an altogether more sophisticated approach to both delivering and measuring improved perioperative outcomes after paediatric cardiac surgery. Anaesthetic techniques have undergone processes of development and refinement with the ultimate aim of contributing to the reduction of avoidable perioperative morbidity and mortality, and the provision of the best possible outcome and experience for patients. One particular field of development has centred upon the approach to, and management of, admission to the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) in the immediate post-operative period, and in particular, the use or avoidance of mechanical ventilation once the surgical procedure has been completed. Reduction in unnecessary ventilation time potentially reduces the incidence of its associated recognised complications such as airway oedema, nosocomial infection, delirium and delayed enteral nutrition (3). Drawing upon experience in our respective institutions the authors aim, in this review article, to shine light on the critically important issue of post-operative ventilation and subsequent management and to clarify the significant differences between ‘on table extubation’ (OTE) and ‘enhanced recovery after surgery’ (ERAS). We examine some of the reasons behind the ongoing interest in ‘on table extubation’ of selected paediatric cardiac surgical patients as a goal in itself, as opposed to widespread implementation of the altogether more comprehensive approach to ‘enhanced recovery after paediatric cardiac surgery’.

Predictability, case suitability and outcome measurement

There is a range of paediatric cardiac surgical cases that are suitable for an ERAS/OTE approach to management. These are characterised by lower complexity surgery predictably resulting in a complete repair of the relevant defect without significant sequelae, for example repair of atrial septal defect, usually in Risk Adjustment in Congenital Heart Surgery (RACHS) categories I to 3 (4); some patients undergoing palliative procedures for single ventricle defects may also be suitable. The expected period of cardiopulmonary bypass is relatively short. Most patients are electively admitted from home, are in a stable physiological pre-operative condition and are free of major disability or impairment, as such being classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I to III (5); some institutions also have minimum age or weight criteria for inclusion. When assessing the impact of implementation of an ERAS/OTE approach in retrospective analysis, outcomes might be compared to those of a well-matched cohort of patients managed within the institution prior to the implementation of the ERAS/OTE programme. Specific outcomes from the ERAS/OTE approach for paediatric cardiac surgical patients that are reported within the literature include the incidence or rate of extubation within the operating room (OR) or within a specified short period of time after leaving it; reintubation rate; ICU and total hospital length of stay (LOS); incidence of complications such as severe post operative nausea and vomiting or episodes of severe pain; compliance with components of the ERAS protocol; potential cost savings and patient/parental experience and satisfaction with process of care (6-10). There are a limited number of reports of outcomes of patients prospectively identified as suitable for OTE (11), such as the achieved versus expected rate of extubation. There are a significant number of more recently published studies which assessed the contribution of regional local anaesthetic techniques such as fascial plane blocks to the ERAS/OTE approach (12).

Admission to an ICU after paediatric cardiac surgery

Completion of many cardiac surgical procedures, particularly those incorporating the use of cardiopulmonary bypass, is recognised to expose patients to the risk of a number of adverse sequelae including, but not limited to, the many manifestations of the inflammatory response, renal dysfunction, pulmonary dysfunction, neurological injury and haemodynamic instability including dysrhythmias and a low cardiac output state. These incidence of such complications correlate predictably with increasing duration of cardiopulmonary bypass, complexity of surgery, patient comorbidity and complexity, and decreasing patient age and weight (13,14). Such complications are ideally managed in an ICU where management with inotropes, ventilation and renal support may be safely and effectively delivered. Standard management following cardiac surgery such as ensuring satisfactory haemostasis, administration of sedation and/or analgesia, achievement of normothermia, and management of fluid balance, invasive lines, drains and temporary pacing wires are also typically managed in an ICU or high dependency unit (HDU). A fundamental part of the delivery of such complex therapies may also include the provision of mechanical ventilation and the associated requirement for deeper sedation. Historically, particularly in paediatric practice, there were perceived to be additional risks associated with managing awake children in the immediate post-operative period; post-operative ventilation was also predictably necessary as the anaesthetic approach to perioperative management became more reliant upon the benefits of a high-dose, longer-acting opiate technique (15). Although the majority of paediatric cardiac surgical procedures do still require admission to an ICU for immediate post-operative management, there has been considerable development over time in the overall approach to immediate post-operative management and a rethinking of the role of post-operative intubation and ventilation.

History and the importance of terminology

Over time a variety of descriptors for approaches to perioperative management have entered into use. Such descriptors or expressions have sometimes been used interchangeably and yet, over the same period of time, their meanings have evolved, and certain important differences merit analysis. Such expressions include ‘fast track’, ‘enhanced recovery after surgery’, ‘on table extubation’ and ‘early extubation’. At a basic level there is clearly some similarities amongst these approaches in terms of minimisation of post-operative ventilation and time spent in ICU/HDU and, ultimately, timely and safe hospital discharge.

In 1980, Barash et al. (16) reported on the outcomes associated with an ‘early extubation’ protocol for 197 consecutive paediatric cardiac surgical patients of whom half were younger than three years of age; 57% of the cardiac surgical procedures were completed on cardiopulmonary bypass; 72% of patients were extubated immediately at the end of surgery (this would equate to ‘on table extubation’ in later literature), of whom one (0.7%) died. There were 16 deaths reported amongst the 55 patients (29%) who were not immediately extubated, such mortality figures being very considerably higher than those reported in modern healthcare systems such as the National Health Service in the United Kingdom, where the latest reported 30-day survival after paediatric cardiac surgery was 98.7% (17), with these results achieved in an era when the case mix and complexity have changed very significantly over time (18).

Simultaneously, practice evolved in adult cardiac anaesthesia and ‘fast tracking’ gained considerable traction, yielding encouraging results relating to duration of post-operative mechanical ventilation, and ICU/overall hospital LOS (19). Within 30 years of Barash et al.’s study, Mittnacht and Hollinger (20) were reporting generalised outcomes from a ‘fast tracking’ approach in paediatric cardiac anaesthetic practice, and they pointed to significant benefits from shortening the period of postoperative mechanical ventilation. Looking specifically at the process of extubation, they performed a meta-analysis of six studies published between 1997 and 2008 including over 1,700 paediatric patients demonstrating the safety, feasibility and acceptability of extubation either immediately at the end of surgery—‘on table extubation’, or within a pre-determined short period of time after admission to ICU (‘early extubation’). It is notable that ‘new’ studies on this approach to extubation are still being published in contemporary literature, even though convincing evidence has supported it for more than two decades.

Mittnacht and Hollinger concluded their analysis by drawing attention to the necessities of a successful ‘fast track’ approach including careful patient selection and the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to its delivery. They also alluded to the need to conduct further studies to demonstrate specific measurable benefits from such a ‘fast track’ approach to management compared to earlier or more traditional approaches. In many ways these closing remarks were particularly prescient as the evidence base regarding specific, measurable benefits from Enhanced Recovery Programmes (ERP or ERAS) after paediatric cardiac surgery, from prospectively conducted studies, is still in its infancy.

The term ‘Enhanced Recovery’ originated in 1995 in adult gastrointestinal surgery, where Kehlet and colleagues first described a comprehensive multi-disciplinary approach to patient care which aimed to streamline the recovery process by optimising various factors before, during and after surgery. This differed from previous approaches to the management of the adult lower gastrointestinal surgical patient in that multimodal interventions were implemented in a co-ordinated way, across a number of teams (relating to perioperative nutrition, fluid therapy, feeding, mobilisation, anaesthetic and analgesic management, surgical technique and tube/drain management), with a wide-ranging goal to reduce perioperative organ dysfunction and augment timely recovery (21,22). The concept of ‘Enhanced recovery’ gained considerable traction in adult practice and over time (perhaps some 20 years later) began to be increasingly implemented in paediatric surgical specialties (23).

In 2010, Howard et al. published the results of their institutional ‘fast track’ approach to 178 uncomplicated paediatric cardiac surgical cases in patients over six months of age at Great Ormond Street Hospital, London, UK (6). One of the primary aims of their programme was to circumvent the automatic need to spend the first post-operative night after cardiac surgery on an ICU, and the authors defined successful fast tracking as the ability to achieve this. For this to occur there were mandated a range of necessary institutional changes including fast track pathway practitioners and staff on the paediatric cardiology ward (where the cardiac HDU was located) who were able to deliver care to surgical patients within a matter of hours of leaving the OR and ICU. In this study, five different types of surgery were eligible for fast tracking: repair of atrial septal defect, repair of ventricular septal defect, relief of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction +/− conduit replacement or pulmonary valve replacement, relief of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and ‘other’; 78% of the patients were successfully ‘fast tracked’ and the results demonstrated a reduction in hospital LOS compared to the period before the programme was implemented.

The first institution to report successful implementation of a formal ERAS approach for paediatric cardiac surgical patients was Boston Children’s Hospital, MA, USA (7); this study of 155 patients was followed by similar reports relating to 88 and 121 patients in institutions in the United Kingdom (Bristol) and Australia (Brisbane) respectively (8,9). The Australian study bore marked similarity to the 2010 study by Howard et al. (6) in terms of discharge from ICU on the same day as admission, although the programme was given the more modern and contemporary descriptor ‘Enhanced Recovery’ rather than ‘Fast track’. In essence there now appears to be two different approaches to ‘Enhanced Recovery’ after paediatric cardiac surgery in which institutions may either opt for patients to spend their first postoperative night in an ICU, or for there to be a brief period of ICU admission followed by discharge to a ward-based HDU environment within a few hours of ICU admission—provided of course that the HDU facility has the appropriate staffing and skill mix, and that the patient is suitably straightforward and uncomplicated. One modification that the Bristol ERAS programme has instituted is that non-bypass cases (such as vascular ring surgery) are now admitted directly to HDU post-operatively, rather than spend any time at all on ICU. The benefits and disadvantages of these subtly different approaches might provide opportunities for further research in the future (24). There have been several more publications relating to the programme in Boston (25,26) and overall, studies consistently demonstrate a reduction in ICULOS of between 25% and 80% (this larger reduction achieved when patients are moved out of ICU and into HDU before the end of the working day) together with very high rates of success (up to nearly 90%) in achieving extubation either on the table or within a short specified time frame (6–8 hours) after completion of surgery. While some reports fail to demonstrate a reduction in overall hospital LOS, the Australian study showed a reduction of 20% (from 5 to 4 days) in median HLOS for 95 patients matched with 95 controls managed before the ERAS programme was implemented. The studies also clearly demonstrated that a comprehensive ERAS approach was initially suitable for lower complexity and more predictable surgical cases such as atrial septal defect repair, sub aortic obstruction relief, or pulmonary valve/conduit replacement. Once programmes have gained institutional traction, it becomes possible to extend inclusion criteria to younger, smaller patients and for more complex surgeries such as repair of tetralogy of Fallot or closure of an isolated ventricular septal defect.

Against this background of the modern era of enhanced recovery after paediatric cardiac surgery, in 2021 the American Association for Thoracic Surgery (AATS) published a consensus document regarding paediatric cardiac ERAS which contained the critically important statements ‘comprehensive ERPs in pediatric cardiac surgery that incorporate preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative elements of care can be effective to improve outcomes’ and ‘multidisciplinary stakeholder involvement is recommended to enact and sustain programmatic change in perioperative pediatric cardiovascular care’ (27). This document included a total of 28 recommendations which cover aspects of the entire perioperative period from prior to hospital admission up to and including discharge. Of these 28 recommendations only two relate specifically to intraoperative anaesthetic management and only one of those to the process of extubation, with the recommendation being for extubation within a period of 6 hours of admission to ICU—i.e., allowing for both OTE and extubation within a short period of time after admission to ICU. It is clear that this document envisages a striking distinction between OTE and ERAS, with one and 27 associated recommendations respectively. Further evidence relating to this important distinction was provided from the results of a survey of 42 European paediatric cardiac surgical programmes published in 2025 by Meier et al. (28); 16 centres (38%) followed an ERAS ‘concept’ (focussing principally on the process of extubation) but only three (7%) had a formalised programme covering the entire hospital admission.

Recent institutional data compared

The institution, Bristol Royal Hospital For Children (BRHC), in which three of this review’s authors practice (T.M., B.A., L.D.) has had an ERAS programme in place since 2019, the initial results of which were published in 2022 (8). From ongoing unpublished audit of outcome data, the median ICULOS for a recent cohort of 115 patients managed between August 2024 and November 2025 was 1.15 days, which includes patients extubated both on the table and in ICU within 6 hours. The total median hospital LOS from admission to discharge was 6.14 days—which included time spent on the preoperative ward and time spent in the OR (29).

At The Royal Brompton Hospital (RBH), the remaining author of this review (T.C.A.) and colleagues have progressively and successfully implemented a strategy of isolated OTE or early (within 6 hours) extubation such that, from 2013 to 2025, the percentage of patients extubated in this way has risen from 24.4% to 59% (30). This corresponds to a period in which progressively more anaesthetists became comfortable and familiar with, and recognised the benefits of, timely/OTE; interestingly, there followed a further period of intra-institutional change whereby this practice of earlier extubation transferred on to adult cardiac surgical patients. The proportion of paediatric patients subject to timely extubation has likely now reached its maximum, given that the remaining patients are of sufficient complexity that postoperative ventilation on ICU is mandatory. The intensive care LOS has, however, remained much the same in the most recent period 2023–2025 with a current median ICULOS of 1.3 days for patients extubated within 6 hours of admission and 2.3 days for those extubated on the table, and is therefore greater than the corresponding figure for BRHC. This data would suggest that extubation alone does not automatically guarantee a relatively shorter stay in ICU. After discharge from PICU, most recent RBH data demonstrates an additional period on the postoperative ward of between 5.1 and 6.2 days.

While there appears to be a significant difference between the two institutions, it should be borne in mind that the OTE cohort does not necessarily precisely match the ERAS cohort: there are a number of cardiac surgical patients included in OTE analysis—for example those undergoing Fontan completion for single ventricle anatomy and physiology, where OTE is thought to be associated with better haemodynamic performance in the immediate postoperative period—who might be extubated on the table at the end of surgery but who have a greater LOS [10–11 days (31)], compared to the ERAS cohort in whom this figure is around 6 days. For wider context, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons recently reported postoperative LOS data from 113 US institutions for the period 2017 to 2021 which were stratified according to complexity score [Society of Thoracic Surgeons-European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) 1 (lowest) to STAT 5 (highest) (32)]. Median LOS data were as follows: 9.08 days (STAT 1), 19.27 days (STAT 2), 18.46 days (STAT 3), 32.52 days (STAT 4) and 51.16 days (STAT 5) (33). It is uncommon for patients with higher STAT ranking (such as 4 or 5) to ever be suitable for ERAS or for a proactive or early extubation strategy.

ERAS and OTE are therefore definitely not the same thing, and an OTE cohort of patients might well have a specific risk and complexity profile which makes comparing them to an ERAS cohort very misleading. An ERAS cohort will likely entirely comprise OTE patients but not every patient who is an OTE candidate will fit the simplicity and predictability profile that is the hallmark of the ERAS cohort, and the terms should not be used interchangeably.

The similarities and differences between extubation and enhanced recovery

There are clearly important similarities within both OTE and ERAS methodology and looking purely at the important process of management of the endotracheal tube, OTE and ERAS closely resemble one another. Undoubtedly, a specific and highly developed anaesthetic approach is required successfully to achieve sustained extubation of children undergoing cardiac surgery, and anaesthetists accordingly play a key role in delivering both OTE and ERAS. In addition to an approach to managing the endotracheal tube there is need for optimisation of fluid strategy, temperature management, transfusion and coagulation management, analgesic management including the use of a regional local anaesthetic technique, and nausea and vomiting prophylaxis which all fall within the remit of the anaesthetist managing the case. The AATS document effectively draws attention to the critical differences between approaches directed mainly towards achieving timely extubation purely as an end in itself, and essentially delivered by the anaesthetist managing the case, and an altogether more comprehensive, institutionally-delivered, ERAS programme. The term ‘OTE’ lends itself to some further clarification or refinement as a descriptor of the moment of extubation as it may refer to removal of the endotracheal tube either while the patient is still on the operating table in the OR, or immediately after surgery is fully completed, or within a specified period of time after departure from the OR. It also remains to be clarified whether it makes any demonstrable difference at what particular point within a given time frame (such as 6 to 8 hours) following completion of surgery that endotracheal tube removal actually occurs.

In order to gain institutional traction for an OTE programme there undoubtedly needs to be co-operation between the anaesthetists managing the cases on the one hand, and the surgeons and intensivists on the other. To achieve this limited degree of co-operation might not however require much in the way of time and effort. From an institutional perspective, establishing an OTE programme might be viewed as a component of efficient perioperative management with potential positive effects amongst the rest of the team (as has been demonstrated with the data from RBH). Establishing the practice of isolated OTE represents a highly visible progression in terms of institutional culture within the OR, and can efficiently be implemented by a small, highly committed team, the outcome from which can be visible and easily measured. By contrast for ERAS to work, the decision to manage the endotracheal tube and the PICU admission is made within a framework to which many more teams and individuals are required to be committed, since ERAS protocols cover not only what happens within the OR, but also what happens beforehand (e.g., case selection, patient and family education, preoperative optimisation), what happens on the ICU, and how the patient is managed once they are in a position to be discharged from ICU. For ERAS delivery to be successful, all the relevant teams need to behave in a pre-agreed, consistent and co-ordinated way, which takes a significant amount of organisation and planning, together with long term commitment if the programme is to succeed over the long term. ERAS culture also reduces the specific importance of any one individual (for example, the anaesthetist removing the endotracheal tube) and spreads responsibility for delivery of care more widely. In this way, dependence on any one particular individual is removed. When looking at the processes of and strategies for change management within an institution delivering care for a paediatric congenital heart disease (CHD) cohort, setting up an OTE culture is easier than setting up an ERAS culture, and the measurable end points and performance metrics are potentially easier to identify, measure and publicise.

Evidence from the wider paediatric ERAS literature for non-cardiac surgical specialties consistently demonstrate that this takes significant time and ongoing institutional leadership and investment (34). Establishing an ERAS programme is a much more sizeable undertaking for an institution with a much greater commitment from a far broader range of specialties, and there are clearly barriers to institutions being able to move from a more simple extubation-driven culture to a more complex ERAS culture (35,36). There is also a learning curve for Centres who have instituted an ERAS programme which requires ongoing commitment in terms of time, audit cycle and outcome/performance metric measurement, education and refinement of approach, case selection and pathway modification (37).

Using data from the authors’ institutions, if a typical ERAS patient spends a total of approximately 140 hours in hospital from admission to discharge, the time spent under the care of the anaesthetist in the OR, at approximately 5 hours, is less than 4% of that total (29). ERAS envisages systematic, organised, co-ordinated and multidisciplinary care for the remaining 96% of entire hospital LOS, beginning with the approach to identifying and agreeing which patients are suitable for an ERAS approach and then how all aspects of subsequent care can then be safely delivered. Important postoperative details such as optimised nutrition, analgesic and symptom management, mobilisation and physical rehabilitation and efficient discharge planning and follow-up are included within a typical ERAS protocol, none of which are automatic sequelae to the simple removal of the endotracheal tube at the end of surgery or shortly thereafter.

Conclusions

There is clearly significant overlap between the mindset of the team that supports immediate or time-specified extubation of a child undergoing cardiac surgery compared to the institution that has designed and implemented an enhanced recovery after cardiac surgery programme. However, OTE is relatively more focussed on the actions of an individual (the anaesthetist), whereas ERAS is focussed on the actions of an entire team and covers the entire perioperative period from before admission until time of discharge. OTE and ERAS cohorts overlap in terms of patient demographics but are by no means identical. It might therefore be the case that the provision of the best possible care to this particular subset of the entire paediatric cardiac surgical population (i.e., relatively straightforward and predictable surgery performed in patients with limited or no co-morbidities) might be achievable if predicated on a shift in emphasis away from purely the process of extubation. Such best possible care might be achievable when teams collectively agree on case selection, preoperative preparation and education, intraoperative and postoperative management on ICU, HDU and the cardiology ward, built on a well thought-through, evidence-based and consistent approach to the delivery of care. ERAS, as opposed to OTE, has the capacity to remove obstacles to achieving gains in efficiency and patient and family satisfaction, and with wider implementation of ERAS, at the cost of greater institutional effort and commitment, it may become clearer which patients will benefit most from this approach.
