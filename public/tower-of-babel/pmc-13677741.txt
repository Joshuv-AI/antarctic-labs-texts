A modular synthetic promoter toolkit for programmable gene control in activated and chronically stimulated T cells
Authors: Giuliano Bonfá, Giovanna Martino, Assunta Sellitto, Juan Luis Blazquez-Roman, Fabiana Tedeschi, Antonio Rinaldi, Caio Raony Farina Silveira, Francesca Piro, Fabio Caliendo, Loris Melchiorri, Daniela Perna, Rita Tarnai, Christoph Heuser-Loy, Fabio Mastrogiovanni, Timea Vadász, Freddie Starkey, Evangelos Nikolados, Filippo Menolascina, Luca Gattinoni, Velia Siciliano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthetic circuits that couple transgene expression to endogenous transcription factor (TF) activity enable cellular response to changing functional states. In T cells, antigen stimulation results in dynamic changes of transcription factor expression programs. Harnessing these endogenous TF dynamics for programmable gene control remains an important challenge in engineered T cells. Here, we develop a library of synthetic promoters (SPs) responsive to seven TFs identified through transcriptomic analysis of T-cell activation programs. These promoters couple endogenous TF activity to transcriptional output across a broad dynamic range of inducibility and basal expression. We quantitatively profile promoter performance in HEK293 and Jurkat cells, evaluate them for orthogonality, and integrate them into Boolean logic gate architectures and network topologies to minimize basal transcriptional leakiness and improve ON/OFF ratios. We test selected synthetic promoters in primary human CD8⁺ T cells, under endogenous TF regulation, and we further characterize the NR4A2-responsive promoter subset in vitro and in tumor-infiltrating engineered T cells in a melanoma xenograft model, demonstrating dynamic and reversible activation.Together, these results provide a modular SP toolkit of TF-responsive synthetic promoters for programmable transcriptional control in engineered T cells.

Introduction

Realizing the potential of mammalian synthetic biology for biomedical applications requires the construction of genetic circuits with predictable behaviors1,2, and the ability to control their expression with high robustness to prevent off-target activations3,4. Over the years, synthetic biology has introduced innovative tools and strategies to achieve cell-specific and spatiotemporal control of therapeutic gene expression. These include transcriptional and post-transcriptional systems, chemogenetics, optogenetics, and feedback-based circuit topologies5–9. Transcriptional regulation remains the most widely employed strategy, particularly when integrating genetic payloads into target cells. However, the use of strong constitutive promoters can lead to gene expression levels that risk dose-dependent side effects, raising significant safety concerns10,11. Alternatively, genetic circuits can be regulated by artificial transcriptional systems (ATS) that employ either engineered transcription factor (TF) and cognate synthetic promoters, or Zinc finger, Talen and CRISPR-based TFs to target endogenous promoters. The amplitude of promoter activation can be tuned using activation domains (AD) of varying strength, offering safer and more precise gene regulation to desired levels12–16. An alternative strategy is to couple transgene expression to dynamic cellular states through endogenous transcription factor (TF) activity17,18. In this context, synthetic promoters (SPs) can be engineered to respond to defined TF families19, with design considerations that enable compactness, specificity, and orthogonality20–22. This places SPs in a privileged position compared to endogenous promoters, which often exhibit crosstalk due to the presence of enhancers or promiscuous binding sites. The impact of SPs for cancer immunotherapy has already been demonstrated to select immunomodulatory circuit activation in a tumor-specific fashion, potentially enhancing safety for clinical applications.In cancer immunotherapy, NFAT-based promoters have been used to control cytokine expression in engineered T cells in vitro and in vivo, while NR4A-based promoters responsive to antigen-induced T-cell activation have thus far been characterized only in vitro19,21,23–25. These studies highlight the potential of synthetic promoters to improve the safety of engineered T-cell therapies and the need for greater stringency to avoid severe toxicities10,26,27. T cells undergo dynamic transcriptional remodeling in response to activation and persistent antigen stimulation. In solid tumors, the highly immunosuppressive TME and chronic antigen exposure are associated with progressive alterations in TF expression programs28–33. Transcriptional programs associated with T cell activation and prolonged stimulation substantially overlap, and the abundance of many transcription factors changes progressively rather than in a state-specific manner34–36.

Consequently, there is a need for modular TF-responsive promoter systems capable of sensing endogenous transcription factor activity under different stimulation contexts, without relying on transcription factors uniquely associated with discrete functional states.

Here, we design and systematically characterize a library of synthetic promoters responsive to transcription factors whose expression is modulated by antigen stimulation of T cells.

Using bioinformatic analysis of transcriptomic datasets from activated and exhausted T cells28,37,38 we identify candidate TFs and establish a modular platform to engineer ~80 SPs responsive to seven TFs. To systematically characterize their behavior, we evaluate OFF-state basal activity (leakiness) and inducibility in HEK293 cells and in Jurkat cells.

Further, we integrate the SPs into engineered circuit architectures to improve ON/OFF ratios39 and construct AND and OR logic gates to compute combinatorial TF input processing. Using a transfer learning strategy, we explore the predictability of SP strength.

To assess biological relevance beyond cell lines, we first evaluate selected synthetic promoters in primary human CD8⁺ T cells by transient nucleofection of SP reporter plasmids following T-cell activation, thereby assessing promoter activity under endogenous transcription factor regulation. We then validate NR4A2-responsive SPs under acute and persistent stimulation conditions, demonstrating dynamic and reversible activation.

Finally, we demonstrate reporter activation in tumor-infiltrating engineered T cells in a melanoma xenograft model, supporting the applicability of TF-responsive SPs in physiologically relevant contexts.

Together, this study provides a systematically characterized and biologically informed library of TF-responsive synthetic promoters exhibiting a broad operational range of transcriptional behaviors. As a modular, platform-oriented framework, this work complements existing regulatory strategies in engineered T cells and provides a foundation for the future development of programmable, context-dependent gene control.

Results

Design of a modular architecture for rapid construction of synthetic promoters

To design transcription factor–responsive synthetic promoters, we first carried out a bioinformatic analysis to identify TFs whose expression is modulated following T-cell antigen stimulation. To this end, we explored publicly available gene expression data and generated a list of 197 TFs always upregulated in exhausted vs effector/memory T cells across chronic infections and cancer37,38,40. We then refined our analysis using RNA-Seq data from positional papers in the field of T cell and CAR-T cell immunology40–43. Among the differentially expressed TFs we selected a subset of seven TFs (IRF4, BATF, MAF, GATA3, NR4A2, EOMES and IKZF2) based on: (i) expression in the early stages of activation, functional effector/memory and in dysfunctional cells, and (ii) their well characterized role in promoting or augmenting dysfunctional states28–30,33,44 (Supplementary Fig. 1, 2 A, B and Supplementary Data 1). The SPs are composed of transcription factor binding sites (TF-BSs), and a minimal core promoter (minP) (Supplementary Fig. 2C). TF-BSs were identified from the TFBS Data Bank (TFBS-DB) and selected based either on reported binding of the TF, or on the likelihood of being targeted by the TF. TF-BSs were cloned in 5x tandem repetition, upstream the minP module, alternating each TF-BS with spacers of 10–20 bps (Supplementary Data 2 and 3)19,45. We chose to use five tandem repeats of each transcription factor binding site (TF-BS) as a design compromise informed by prior synthetic promoter studies and by practical considerations for promoter screening. Previous work has shown that multimerization of TF-BSs is generally required to achieve measurable and robust inducible activity from minimal promoters, while excessive repetition can increase basal activity, introduce context-dependent effects, or reduce portability across cell types19,46.

To investigate the impact of minP on SP leakiness and activation, we initially combined TF-BSs with four different minP (YB-TATA, miniCMV, miniTK, LateADEp). A mCherry fluorescent protein was inserted downstream of the SPs as an output. Since each TF can recognize different DNA-responsive elements, by using this combinatorial modular design, we generated a library of over 80 SPs sensors (Supplementary Fig. 2C; Supplementary Data 2).

All minP except miniCMV showed minimal basal activity both in HEK293 and Jurkat cells in the absence of TF-BSs (Fig. 1A–C). Conversely, when combined to the TF-BSs we observed a broad range of background noise, and the YB_TATA emerged as the least leaky in combination with the responsive elements (Fig. 1D–H and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Among the SPs targeting IRF4/BATF, we observed low background noise with TF-BS derived from BRAF endogenous promoters SP61-SP64 (Fig. 1G, H and Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Of these, the lowest basal activity was achieved in combination with YB_TATA (SP61), while coupling with miniCMV (SP62C) resulted in higher expression in the OFF state in both HEK293 and Jurkat cells. Notably, high background noise was associated with two distinct groups of SPs in HEK293 and Jurkat cells: AP1-derived BS (SP21-24) in HEK293 and IRF4 responsive element-derived BS (GAAA, SP65B-68) in Jurkat cells (Fig. 1G, H and Supplementary Fig. 3B, C).

In the library of MAF SPs, both HEK293 and Jurkat cells showed minimal basal activity with SPATA20 promoter-derived BS (SP37-40), and the combination with YB_TATA and LateADEp exhibited the lowest noise levels (Fig. 1G, H and Supplementary Fig. 3D, E). Conversely, the highest background was associated with two groups of SPs in HEK293 and Jurkat cells: STAT3-derived BS (SP41-44) in HEK293, and CD69-derived BS (SP33-36) in Jurkat cells (Fig. 1G, H; and Supplementary Fig. 3D, E). However, it was interesting to observe that in Jurkat cells the leakiness of MAF-responsive SPs was not as high as the other SPs. Last, all SPs targeting GATA3 exhibited minimal noise in HEK293 cells, whereas in Jurkat cells the background activity was observed in all combinations, albeit to a different extent (Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Fig. 3F, G).

Collectively, these data indicate that YB_TATA and lateADEp generally support lower basal activity across the tested architectures. Since long-term expression in engineered cells is crucial to exploit therapeutic interventions, it is critical to minimize viral genetic elements to reduce the risk of silencing. As lateADEp has adenoviral origins51, we then opted for YB_TATA to build the next set of SPs responding to NR4A2, EOMES and IKZF2.

NR4A2-responsive SPs showed low basal activity in HEK293 and Jurkat cells (Fig. 1I–K andSupplementary Fig. 4A–E) except for SP77A (NEG1, 4x binding sites) that exhibited high leakiness in Jurkat cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A–E). Like NR4A2, EOMES- and IKZF2-responsive SPs showed low basal activity in general for both cell lines tested (Fig. 1I–K and Supplementary Fig. 4A–C, F–I).

Altogether, this initial characterization enabled the design of transcriptional sensors with low leakiness when coupled to YB_TATA. In contrast, miniCMV consistently exhibited higher basal activity, regardless of the specificity of the TF-BSs, generating high noise in the OFF state when combined with certain TF-BSs (e.g., SP22, SP42, SP50, SP54 and SP66) (Fig. 1G, H). Notably, noise in the OFF state was cell-dependent, underscoring the importance of conducting preliminary screening designs in bona fide in vitro setups.

Transcription factor–responsive synthetic promoters exhibit inducible activity across a broad activation range

We next evaluated SP activation by co-expression of the cognate TF in HEK293 and Jurkat cells, followed by quantification of the mCherry reporter. Most of the IRF4/BATF, MAF, NR4A2, and EOMES SPs induced the expression of mCherry reporter as compared to the control lacking the TF (no-TF), in both cell lines (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Figs. 5–9, 10B, C, 11B–E). In contrast, reporter expression was lower for GATA3-responsive promoters in HEK293 cells, with a milder effect in Jurkat cells (Supplementary Fig. 10D, E). GATA3 TF regulates histone modifications and can function as either an activator or repressor depending on the cellular context52; this may partially explain the observed differences. The IRF4/BATF SPs were tested in the presence of either TFs or both, resulting in different induction patterns. In some designs (SP05-5X_IL10P1_BS and SP69A-3X_IKZF2_BS), IRF4 but not BATF alone activated the SPs, and co-expression of both resulted in increased mCherry levels (Supplementary Fig. 6B). In other designs (e.g. SP61-5X_BRAF_BS) we observed mild activation with individual TFs and a synergistic effect with co-expression of both (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Figs. 6B). Some SPs, such as SP13A (3X_IL10P3_BS) and SP65B (GAAA), showed induction only by IRF4, especially in HEK293 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Indeed, most of the TF-BSs selected to sense IRF4/BATF are composed of responsive elements that sense both TFs, whereas GAAA BS is specific only for IRF4 binding, explaining this induction pattern. We also selected a single responsive element for BATF (SP21, AP1-derived), but it displayed a high basal level with no differential induction (Supplementary Fig. 6B). This effect may be explained by the fact that this motif is shared and can be recognized by additional members of the AP1 TF family. Instead, SP25 (CTLA-4) showed low induction in the presence of single TFs and a synergistic expression when they were co-expressed in HEK293 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6B). The SPs responsive to MAF showed a more consistent activation both in HEK293 and Jurkat cells, in particular with SPATA20 (SP37-40) and RNF212 (SP45-48) BSs, in combination to all core promoters tested (Supplementary Fig. 10B-C), and again when coupled to YB_TATA and lateADEp the leakiness was the lowest (Supplementary Fig. 10B-C). Highest reporter activation was observed for SP45 (RNF212) (Fig. 2A-B). SP33 (CD69) and SP37 (SPATA20) showed moderate activation in HEK293 cells while SP41 (STAT3) was active in the absence of MAF. Different from HEK293 cells, SP33 and SP37 did not show induction in Jurkat, whereas SP41 exhibited mild activation, highlighting the different behaviors in different cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 10B, C).

All NR4A2 SPs showed an ample range of activation, with the highest reporter levels achieved by SP73 (CON1) in both HEK293 and Jurkat cells (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Fig. 11B, C). Since NR4A-based systems represent well-established approaches for sensing acute TCR signaling21, we compared the performance of our best NR4A2 promoter SP73 with the architecture previously reported21 under the same experimental setting in HEK293 cells. SP73 exhibited higher inducible activity than the other NR4A promoter in both HEK293 and Jurkat cells (Supplementary Fig. 24A, B). These results underscore that by modulating synthetic promoter architecture, it is possible to achieve an operational range of transcriptional behaviors that can be adapted to the desired application. EOMES-responsive SPs displayed a comparatively lower range of activation in Jurkat cells as compared to HEK293 cells. Conversely, IKZF2 SPs did not show a functional response between ON and OFF states (Supplementary Fig. 11F, G). Notably, the overall range of activation observed in Jurkat cells was lower than in HEK293 cells. We therefore quantified TF expression levels following transfection (HEK293) and electroporation (Jurkat). qPCR analysis revealed substantially lower TF transcript levels in Jurkat cells compared to HEK293 cells (Supplementary Fig. 35).

To further determine whether promoter activation reflected successful TF delivery, we generated TF-P2A-GFP constructs (or TF-P2A-BFP for BATF to allow simultaneous analysis with IRF4) and co-electroporated them together with the corresponding synthetic promoters in Jurkat cells. Promoter activity was quantified by flow cytometry after gating separately on TF-reporter–positive and –negative populations (Supplementary Fig. 41A). Across all TF-responsive promoters tested, mCherry expression was consistently enriched within the TF-reporter–positive population, whereas TF-reporter–negative cells showed little or no reporter activation (Supplementary Fig. 41B). Likewise, cells electroporated with a GFP control plasmid in the absence of TF co-expression exhibited minimal mCherry expression (Supplementary Fig. 41C). Together, these experiments demonstrate that promoter activation in Jurkat cells depends on successful TF expression and indicate that the lower activation amplitudes observed in bulk Jurkat populations largely reflect heterogeneous TF delivery rather than non-specific promoter behavior.

To enable direct comparison across the entire promoter library, subsequent fold induction analysis was performed using the original bulk Jurkat electroporation dataset, without gating on TF-reporter–positive cells or using the TF–P2A–GFP constructs (Supplementary Figs. 12–17). Fold induction, calculated relative to the condition lacking the cognate TF, reflected the broad range of activation and promoter leakiness observed across the different SPs. As expected, promoters with higher basal activity generally exhibited lower fold induction despite robust reporter expression, highlighting the contribution of OFF-state leakiness to the overall dynamic range (Fig. 2C–J and Supplementary Figs. 12–17). Among the best-performing promoters, IRF4/BATF-responsive SP61/SP64, MAF-responsive SP37/SP45, and NR4A2-responsive SP73 exhibited the highest fold inductions in the respective screening platforms, whereas EOMES-responsive SP87 and SP88 displayed more context- and TF expression-dependent activation profiles.

Since transcription factor binding site (TF-BS) consensus sequences may be shared among different TFs, we next assessed the specificity of the synthetic promoters by testing potential cross-activation with non-cognate TFs. To this end, we performed an orthogonality analysis in HEK293 cells using the best-performing synthetic promoters (SPs), systematically co-expressed with all seven transcription factors evaluated in this study. This analysis allows us to assess intrinsic promoter–TF specificity in a controlled and modular context while minimizing the influence of endogenous, lineage-specific transcriptional programs. The resulting orthogonality matrix showed that activation was predominantly observed with the cognate transcription factor for each SP, with only limited cross-activation by non-cognate TFs (Fig. 2K). Since GATA3-responsive promoters did not display inducible activity under the tested conditions, they were not included in the primary matrix but were presented separately in Supplementary Fig. 36. Cross-activation by non-cognate TFs was generally minimal. Low-level activation was observed only in a limited number of cases following MAF overexpression. However, this activation remained substantially lower than the induction achieved with the cognate TF (except for IKZF2, which showed minimal activation overall) and did not alter the relative ranking or functional interpretation of the promoters. Overall, these data demonstrate a high degree of functional specificity of the synthetic promoter library across the TF panel tested.

Finally, we explored whether promoter performance could be partially inferred from models trained on external promoter datasets. Given the limited size of our experimental dataset (n = 82), we applied a transfer learning strategy using approximately 25,000 human promoters from the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (EPD) as the training dataset (Supplementary Fig. 18A–C and Supplementary Note 1). The predicted promoter ranking showed a moderate correlation with the experimentally observed ranking (ρ = 0.49; Supplementary Fig. 18D), indicating that features learned from large promoter datasets can partially generalize to synthetic promoter libraries. Although predictive performance remains limited, these results suggest that transfer learning may assist prioritization of promoter candidates when experimental datasets are too small to support de novo model training.

Control of therapeutic gene expression and release by the NR4A2-responsive promoter SP73

Transcriptional sensors that couple T cell activation to defined gene outputs may enable controlled expression of immunomodulatory molecules. As proof of concept, we evaluated whether the NR4A2-responsive promoter SP73 could drive inducible expression of selected cytokine payloads53. SP73 was placed upstream IL12p70, a cytokine known to enhance T cell activation and function54. SP73–payload constructs were transiently delivered to HEK293 and Jurkat cells together with NR4A2 or control plasmids, and secretion was quantified by ELISA 48hs later. In the absence of NR4A2, basal cytokine production remained low, whereas co-expression of NR4A2 resulted in increased secretion (Supplementary Fig. 22). These results demonstrate that NR4A2-responsive synthetic promoters can be configured to drive inducible secretion of immunomodulatory molecules, supporting their potential utility in engineered T cell applications.

In the AND gate configuration, we used a split synthetic transcription factor (GAL4-VP16) whereby each of the parts (VP16 and GAL4) are fused to N7 and N8 dimerizing tags respectively55. VP16 is a strong transcriptional activation domain derived from the herpes simplex virus (HSV). When fused to GAL4 (GAL4-VP16), it activates the transcription of a blue fluorescent protein (EBFP) upon binding to UAS enhancer regions. VP16-N7 was placed downstream of the NR4A2-SP73, whereas the GAL4-N8 was placed downstream of the MAF-SP45 (Fig. 3A). Thus, GAL4-VP16 reconstitution when both TFs are present triggers the UAS promoter and results in EBFP expression both in HEK293 and Jurkat cells (Fig. 3B, C). We obtained similar results using a split GFP. Here, the GFP1-10 fragment was placed downstream NR4A2-SP73, while the GFP11 helix was fused to β-actin to improve stability and was placed downstream MAF-SP45. When both NR4A2 and MAF are expressed, the split parts reassemble to form the full fluorescent GFP protein (Supplementary Fig. 19).

In the OR gate configuration, we used the same split synthetic transcription factor (GAL4-VP16), with VP16-N7 placed downstream of both NR4A2- and MAF-SP, while GAL4-N8 was placed downstream of a constitutive promoter, shEF1α. Thus, the full GAL4-VP16 complex is formed when either NR4A2 or MAF is present (Fig. 3D). We confirmed OR functionality in both HEK293 and Jurkat cells, with EBFP expression as a function of either of the two TFs upregulation (Fig. 3E, F). The design of logic gates by our synthetic sensors demonstrates that they can be exploited for programmable and precise regulation of genetic circuits with higher therapeutic potential.

We next sought to design circuits configuration to reduce the background output activation in the OFF state. We took as an example MAF responsive SP45 which exhibited highest leakiness and activation in HEK293 cells (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 20E).

To mitigate basal activation, we implemented the CASwitch, a synthetic gene circuit based on combining two well-known network motifs: the Coherent Feed-Forward Loop (CFFL) and Mutual Inhibition (MI), forming what we term the Coherent Inhibitory Loop (CIL). CIL was previously demonstrated to reduce leakiness while maintaining high expression, without modifying either the TF or the promoter39. The CASwitch is based on the activity of CasRx endoribonuclease that binds and cleaves the direct repeats (DR) in the 3’UTR of the mRNA output, causing mRNA degradation56. In the original settings, CasRx is regulated by a rtTA-3G-controlled pCMV/TO promoter repressed by doxycycline (Dox) (X-Y repression in Supplementary Fig. 20A). rtTA-3G also activates the reporter gene that includes the DR motif (X-Z activation in Supplementary Fig. 20A). Since CasRx irreversibly binds the DR, there is a mutual inhibition between CasRx and the reporter (Y-Z in Supplementary Fig. 20A).

By re-adapting the original architecture of the CASwitch, we developed two designs that include MAF-SP45. In the first design (SP-CIL), SP45 induces the expression of the rtTA-3G, initiating the regulatory cascade (Fig. 4A). In the second design (SP-MI), we removed the X-Y repression to obtain a Mutual Inhibitor (MI) topology by placing CasRx under a constitutive CMV promoter, while the reporter EGFP-DR was controlled by MAF SP45. Validation of NR4A2-responsive synthetic promoters in engineered primary human CD8⁺ T cells

To evaluate the functionality of synthetic promoters (SPs) in primary T cells, we generated lentiviral constructs in which NR4A2-responsive promoters with distinct binding site configurations drive expression of an mCherry reporter. The lentiviral backbone also includes a second transcriptional unit constitutively expressing dLNGFR to identify engineered cells (Fig. 5C). Primary human CD8⁺ T cells isolated from healthy donor buffy coats were transduced and then subjected either to single, acute stimulation (1S) or repeated stimulation every 48 h (2D) using anti-CD3/anti-CD28 Dynabeads (see Materials and Methods) to assess promoter activation under different stimulation regimens. mCherry expression was quantified by flow cytometry.

Consistent with the screening performed in cell lines, NR4A2-responsive SPs displayed distinct activation amplitudes. In acutely stimulated cells (1S), SP73 showed an initial modest activation at day 4, which progressively decreased at day 6 and day 8 (Fig. 5D). In contrast, SP78 did not show detectable activation in the 1S condition (Fig. 5D). Persistently stimulated cells (2D) show rapid and sustained increase of mCherry already at day 4 (after two rounds of stimulation) for both SP73 and SP78 (Fig. 5E, G and Supplementary Fig. 27). SP75 exhibited a comparable proportion of mCherry+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 26A, B), but lower fluorescent intensity (Supplementary Figs. 26C, 27). In contrast, SP74, SP76, and SP77 did not show detectable activation (Supplementary Fig. 26A, B), consistent with results observed following transient delivery. As expected, the control promoter lacking NR4A2 BS (SP01) did not show reporter activation (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 27A, B, 37A, B), and no promoter activation was observed in the absence of stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 27C). In parallel, expression of activation- and dysfunction-associated markers was analyzed at day 8 relative to baseline (day 0). Persistently stimulated T cells (2D) exhibited increased NR4A2 levels as well as elevated expression of CD39, LAG3, TIM3, and PD-1 compared to acutely stimulated cells (Fig. 5F), consistent with sustained stimulation. We also assessed TOX expression, given its reported role as a regulator of T cell exhaustion and its potential relevance for synthetic promoter design. TOX levels were increased in both 1S and 2D conditions relative to unstimulated cells; however, absolute expression levels remained modest (Supplementary Fig. 25), consistent with previously published reports57.

NR4A2 promoter activation is dynamic and reversible

Dynamic ON–OFF behavior is a critical feature of inducible promoters. We therefore tested whether SP73 and SP78 promoter activation was reversible or became constitutively active following stimulation. Using the experimental scheme outlined in Fig. 5C, anti CD3/CD28 dynabeads were withdrawn at defined time points after two (2DNB4), three (2DNB6), or four (2DNB8) rounds of stimulation, and mCherry expression was monitored by flow cytometry. Following stimulus withdrawal, mCherry expression progressively declined (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Figs. 29, 31 for SP73 and Supplementary Fig. 28A for SP78), indicating that SP activation depends on continued stimulation.

To assess re-inducibility, resting cells from the 1S and 2DNB4 conditions were re-exposed to anti-CD3/anti-CD28 stimulation at day 8. Upon re-stimulation, mCherry expression was restored (Fig. 5I and Supplementary Fig. 30). SP78 exhibited similar reversible dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 28), demonstrating that multiple NR4A2-responsive promoter architectures support stimulation-dependent and reversible control of gene expression in primary human CD8⁺ T cells. In contrast, the control promoter lacking NR4A2 BS (SP01) did not show reporter activation, consistent with previous results (Supplementary Fig. 37C, D).

These data suggest that SP73-driven reporter activity more closely parallels NR4A2 expression dynamics (Fig. 7C, G) than overall activation or dysfunction marker expression.

Analysis of spleen-derived T cells showed a similar temporal pattern. NR4A2 expression increased at day 14 but remained overall low, accompanied by near-baseline levels of SP73-driven mCherry (Supplementary Fig. 38). Dysfunction-associated markers also increased at day 14 and partially declined by day 28 (Supplementary Fig. 34).

Together, these data indicate that SP73-driven reporter activity is detectable in vivo and varies according to tumor-associated stimulation and NR4A2 expression levels.

Discussion

In this study, we show that rational, target-directed design of synthetic promoters enables a versatile approach to achieve inducible transcriptional activity in response to T cell-associated transcription factors (TFs). Achieving tunable gene expression in the context of dynamically regulated TFs requires both activation specificity and minimal basal activity. We therefore implemented systematic design and screening strategies to reduce promoter leakiness and optimize fold induction. To ensure specificity and evaluate leakiness, we inferred reported and predicted binding sites for each TF and engineered compact promoter architectures. Inducible responses were observed for multiple synthetic promoters, whereas others exhibited limited or no activation under the tested conditions. These differences likely reflect context-dependent transcriptional regulation and TF cofactor requirements not fully recapitulated in the screening platforms. Integration of chromatin accessibility profiling or ChIP–seq–based validation may further refine promoter–TF pairing strategies. Although initial library screening relied on transient plasmid delivery in HEK293 and Jurkat cells, concordant ranking of NR4A2-responsive promoters was preserved following lentiviral integration in primary CD8⁺ T cells. These findings indicate that relative promoter performance can be maintained across delivery modalities, whereas absolute activation levels remain influenced by TF abundance and cellular context. This underscores both the broader applicability of the approach and the importance of systematic evaluation across diverse cell types and delivery systems.

To investigate combinatorial TF input integration, two of the best-performing SPs responsive to MAF and NR4A2 were incorporated into Boolean gate configurations. Additional design modifications were implemented to reduce basal activation and increase ON-OFF dynamic range (e.g., SP45 responding to MAF). Although these constructs do not exhibit strict digital logic behavior, they illustrate that TF-responsive promoters can be assembled into higher-order circuit architectures. This modular design approach enables rational tuning of transcriptional responses and may support adaptation to diverse cellular contexts.

A key advantage of constructing TF-specific SP libraries incorporating multiple binding site variants is the resulting diversity of activation strength. This range enables selection of promoters across a spectrum of transcriptional outputs tailored to specific applications. Importantly, the objective was not to generate promoters that strictly discriminate between discrete T cell functional states, but rather to establish a platform of TF-responsive modules whose activity reflects dynamic transcriptional programs associated with activation and prolonged stimulation.

Several challenges remain prior to therapeutic translation. Circuit optimization would benefit from high-throughput cloning, automated screening, and computational modeling approaches22,57. Previous studies have identified SPs with enhanced cell-state specificity through large-scale screening and machine learning pipelines in cancer and organoid systems. Here, we explored a transfer learning–based modeling strategy trained on the Eukaryotic Promoter Database. Although predictive performance was moderate, this analysis illustrates that external promoter datasets can partially inform prioritization of small synthetic promoter libraries without de novo model training. Importantly, the primary conclusions of this study are supported by experimental validation.

HEK293 and Jurkat cells served as scalable platforms for systematic screening of the promoter library, despite their well-recognized translational limitations. To address the heterogeneous TF delivery associated with Jurkat electroporation, we generated TF-reporter constructs that enabled promoter activity to be quantified specifically within TF-expressing cells. Promoter activation was confined to GFP⁺/BFP⁺ cells, confirming that SP activation depends on successful TF delivery and supporting the interpretation of the Jurkat screening results. Although reduced TF abundance in Jurkat cells contributed to lower activation amplitudes compared with HEK293 cells, the relative behavior of representative promoters was preserved, indicating that the screening strategy captures intrinsic promoter properties despite differences in cellular context.

To further establish biological relevance, we evaluated representative synthetic promoters responsive to multiple TFs in primary human CD8⁺ T cells under endogenous transcription factor regulation following transient nucleofection. Promoters responsive to MAF, IRF4/BATF, EOMES, and NR4A2 were activated by endogenous transcriptional programs, extending validation beyond the NR4A2-responsive subset and supporting the broader applicability of the library.

NR4A2-responsive SPs were subsequently selected for deeper characterization following lentiviral integration in primary human CD8⁺ T cells, where dynamic and reversible activation was observed under acute and persistent CD3/CD28 stimulation. Validation was further extended to antigen-specific in vitro and in vivo settings. In vitro co-culture of engineered CD8⁺ T cells expressing an NY-ESO-1–specific TCR resulted in activation of the NR4A2-responsive promoter upon tumor antigen exposure. In a melanoma xenograft model, reporter activity was detected in tumor-infiltrating T cells and decreased following tumor regression. Together, these findings support the in vivo responsiveness of TF-directed synthetic promoters while indicating the need for broader validation across tumor models and immune contexts. Synthetic gene circuits have shown therapeutic potential by detecting extracellular disease-associated signals9,58,59. In contrast, the present strategy emphasizes sensing intracellular transcriptional states to couple endogenous activation programs to defined output. With appropriate modifications of promoter inputs and therapeutic payloads, this platform could be extended to additional dysfunctional cellular contexts.

In summary, this work establishes a systematically characterized library of transcription factor–responsive synthetic promoters that can be incorporated into modular gene circuit architectures. As a modular toolkit, it expands the repertoire of regulatory strategies available for engineered T cells and provides a foundation for programmable, context-dependent control of gene expression.

Methods

Ethics

This study was conducted in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations. Human primary cells were obtained from healthy donors with ethical approval granted by Azienda Ospedaliera Universitaria Federico II (Protocol N. 8925) and Universitätsklinikum Regensburg (Protocol N. 20-2040-101). Animal procedures were approved by the Government of Lower Franconia (Protocol N. RUF-55.2.2-2532-2-1610).

Transcription Factors (TFs) and TF-Binding Sites (TF-BSs) selection

A universal epigenetic and transcriptional gene signature of T cell dysfunction in mouse and human has been identified by exploring deposited gene expression data (GSE41867, GSE86042) and ATAC-Seq data (GSE164978 GSE97646 GSE86881); the analysis yield 2844 transcripts of genes, up-regulated in dysfunctional vs effector or memory CD8+ cells in chronic infections and cancer, confirmed at the chromatin level. By categorizing all these genes, we identified 197 TFs, whose expression has been further investigated in T cell and CAR-T cells datasets of exhaustion (GSE240851, GSE179613, GSE160174, and https://dice-database.org). By ranking the most relevant TFs in these datasets, we selected a subset of seven TFs (IRF4, BATF, MAF, GATA3, NR4A2, EOMES and IKZF2) to sense by designing novel Synthetic Promoters (SPs). The TFs selected were based on their expression in the early stages of activation, functional effector/memory, and in dysfunctional cells, and on their well characterized role in promoting or augmenting dysfunctional states26–28,31,42. To design SPs, we selected transcription factors binding sites (TF-BSs) from the literature and the TFBS Data Bank (TFBS-DB) web tool (http://tfbsdb.systemsbiology.net/)60 which uses robust ATAC-Seq data and motif repositories such as JASPAR, SELEX and TRANSFAC.

Plasmids library generation

Design and generation of synthetic promoters for screening

The SPs are composed of three main modules: the TF-BSs, a minimal core promoter (minP), and a reporter gene. TF-BSs were cloned in 5x tandem repetition, upstream of the minP module, alternating each TF-BS with spacers of 10–20 bps. We combined TF-BSs with four different minP (YB-TATA, miniCMV, miniTK, LateADEp). Constructs for each minP, where no TF-BSs were inserted, were also created as minP basal activity controls. An mCherry fluorescent protein was inserted downstream of the SPs as an output.

These modules composing the SP and reporter gene were cloned into the plasmid backbone pT2A-MCS-pPGK-H2B-mCherry-pA. Originally, this backbone plasmid encodes nuclear mCherry under the regulation of the constitutive promoter PGK, followed by an SV40 polyA signal. We replaced the PGK and the H2B sequences with the synthetic promoter sequence. Each module was flanked by two restriction sites, allowing the easy combination of all parts (NotI-5xTF-BSs+spacers-HindIII-minP-PacI). Each module was assembled via oligo annealing reactions and ligated into the linearized vector. First, constructs of control for each minP (no TF-BSs) were created. From this last, all the others SPs were created by replacing the TF-BS region via the NotI and HindIII sites with new TF-BSs by digestion-oligo annealing-ligation. During the cloning process, some sequences were more prone to recombination, resulting in SPs with less than 5x BS. We kept these versions and tested them. All general structures and sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 2–4.

Generation of transcription factor expression plasmids

For the TF expression plasmids, we used the pCDNA3.1(+) backbone under the control of the CMV promoter. The coding sequences were obtained from different sources. IRF4, GATA3, NR4A2, BATF and IKZF2 were obtained from Addgene (pDONR223-IRF4-WT, Addgene #81782; GATA3-pCDNA3.1, Addgene #115271; cDNA3.1-hNurr1-NE, Addgene #102363; pCDNA3.1-mBATF, Addgene #34575; cDNA3-IKZF2-V2-V5, Addgene #107391). IRF4 was subcloned into the pCDNA3.1-mBATF backbone. The MAF plasmid was obtained from GenScript (c-MAF transcript variant 2, OHu31519). The EOMES plasmid was obtained from OriGene (NM_001278182, SC337262) and subcloned into the pCDNA3.1(+) backbone using Golden Gate assembly.

To enable identification of TF-expressing cells by flow cytometry, fluorescent TF reporter constructs were generated. For all TFs except MAF, whose cloning into this configuration was unsuccessful, the TF coding sequence was linked to GFP through a self-cleaving P2A peptide. For BATF, BFP was used instead of GFP to allow simultaneous analysis with IRF4. TF-P2A-fluorescent reporter constructs were generated by PCR amplification of the coding sequences followed by assembly into the pCDNA3.1(+) backbone using BsaI-HF Golden Gate assembly or In-Fusion cloning.

Generation of SPs driving circuits and payload release

The circuits designed for Boolean Logic Gates, Feed-Forward Loop and payload release were created by implementing the combinatorial modular design MTK (Mammalian Tool Kit61), a Golden Gate-based cloning toolkit designed for the versatile, reversible, and fast assembly of DNA vectors and their implementation in mammalian models. Modular parts can be assembled into transcriptional units and further integrated into complex circuits60. To adapt the SPs to the MTK, we split the promoter into two different parts: MTK2a (5xTF-BSs+spacers) and 2b (minP YB_TATA), keeping as many original sequences and adapting it to match the original MTK overhangs. Some parts of the MTK library were ordered from Addgene (https://www.addgene.org/browse/article/28197510/) or generated from oligos and PCR fragments. Domestication of new parts was done by Esp3I Golden Gate assembly into a chloramphenicol-resistant destination vector and by selection of white colonies. From the library of parts, transcriptional units (TUs) were built by assembling Parts (1-5) with a BsaI-HF Golden Gate reaction and selection of ampicillin-resistant white colonies, which generated plasmids ready for introduction into mammalian cells. MTK2a parts from MAF-SP45 and NR4A2-SP73 were created and combined to other modules to create new SP constructs. Since each TF can recognize different DNA-responsive elements, by using this combinatorial modular design we generated a library of over 80 SP sensors (Supplementary Data 2). The N7 and N8 sequences were obtained from plasmids kindly donated by Dr. Roman Jerala and Dr. Mojca Benčina55. The VP16, GAL4, shEF1a, and pUAS were obtained from internal plasmids. The GFP1-10 sequence was obtained from pcDNA3.1-GFP(1-10) (Addgene #7021962 and GFP11 was inserted in primers and domesticated as a MTK part. The β-actin sequence was obtained from MTK library (Addgene). The sequences of rtTA3G, EGFP-DR, and CasRx were obtained from plasmids kindly donated by Dr. Diego di Bernardo39. The sequence of IL12p70 was obtained from pLN540 (FuGW-G8p-IL12-P2A-CCL21-1Pe) (Addgene #10520119). For the plasmid SP91, the sequence for 8xNR4A from Guo et al., 202221 was designed and created by oligo annealing and cloned upstream a lateADEp minimal promoter to express mCherry, using as backbone the vector pYTK095 (Addgene #65202). These sequences were domesticated as MTK parts, confirmed by sequencing, and used to assemble and create the described transcription units used in this paper. All DNA fragments were either chemically synthesized or PCR-amplified from existing plasmids. All oligos or DNA syntheses were performed by Sigma-Aldrich or Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA), respectively. Plasmids were sequence-verified by Sanger Sequencing (Genewiz, Germany). Plasmids for transfection and electroporation were prepared by high-quality standard procedures and purified using a Plasmid Midi/Maxiprep Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

For lentivirus transductions, TFBSs-YB_TATA-mCherry of the SP73-SP78 (NR4A2-responsive SPs) or NoTFBSs-YB_TATA-mCherry of SP01 (no TF-BSs control) sequences were cloned in a pFUGW lentiviral backbone (Addgene #14883) as first transcription unity (TU) followed by a constitutive second TU containing the promoter shEF1a and codifying for either EGFP (co-culture and in vivo experiments) or dLNGFR (in vitro polyclonal activation and reversibility experiments).

Cell lines maintenance

HEK 293FT (ATCC-CLR-1573) and Jurkat Clone E-6 (ATCC-TIB-152) cells were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). HEK 293FT cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY), and Jurkat cells were maintained in RPMI1640 (Life Technologies). All media were supplemented with 10% FBS (Life Technologies), 100 U/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco), 2mM L-Glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1x Non-Essential Amino Acids solution (HyClone). Cells were incubated at 37 °C under conditions of 100% humidity and 5% CO2.

The human melanoma cell line SK-Mel-23 (kindly provided by Dr. Steven A. Rosenberg, NIH, USA) expressing NY-ESO-1 was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2).

Cell transfection and electroporation

Transfections in HEK 293FT cells were performed in a 24-well plate for flow cytometry analysis. 7×105 cells per well were seeded approximately 24 h before transfection in complete media. Cells were transfected with PEI transfection reagent according to manufacturer’s instructions using 200-400 ng DNA diluted in Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium (Gibco) before being mixed and incubated for 15 minutes prior to addition to the cells. Cells were detached and prepared for flow cytometry analysis 48 h after transfection. For SP screening, the synthetic promoters were transfected together with expression plasmids encoding for the SP-specific TF plus a plasmid encoding for GFP using the following concentrations: 100 ng pSP, 50 ng pCMV-TF, 20 ng pCMV-EGFP. Jurkat cells were electroporated in a 96-well plate with Neon Transfection System 10 μl Neon Tip (Life Technologies). A total of 1.3 μg of DNA was prepared in a 0.5 mL tube. Meanwhile, 5 × 104 cells/well were centrifuged in PBS at 200 x g for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were suspended in suspension buffer R, added to the DNA tube, and gently mixed. The DNA and cell mixtures were picked with the appropriate Neon Tip, electroporated (pulse voltage 1422 v, pulse width 10 ms, pulse number 3) and cultured in RPMI medium without antibiotics. Cells were collected and prepared for flow cytometry analysis 48 h after transfection.

Jurkat cells were nucleofected with SP–mCherry reporter plasmids together with the corresponding transcription factor (TF)–P2A–GFP expression construct. For IRF4-BATF co-expression, a BATF–P2A–BFP was built. Nucleofection was performed with the 4D-Nucleofector system (Lonza), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 105 cells were nucleofected in a 96-well plate with a 2:1 molar ratio of TF to SP plasmid DNA and cultured in antibiotic-free RPMI medium. As a control for promoter basal activity, cells were nucleofected with the SP–mCherry reporter together with the GFP control plasmid (pgMAX; Lonza) in place of the TF expression construct. Cells were harvested 24 h after nucleofection and analyzed by flow cytometry. Synthetic promoter activity was quantified as mCherry expression within the TF-reporter–positive and –negative populations or, for control samples, within the pgMAX-positive population.

Flow cytometry

All cells were acquired with a BD CELESTA™ version 8.0.1.1 cell analyzer (BD Biosciences) 48 h after transfection/electroporation. HEK 293FT cells were detached with 50 μl of Trypsin and resuspended in 150 μl of FACS Buffer (1xPBS, 1% FBS and 2 mM EDTA), while Jurkat cells were analyzed directly. For each sample, >10000 singlet events were collected and a compensation matrix created using unstained cells (wild type) or single-color controls (EBFP only, EGFP only and mCherry only). Population of live cells and single cells were selected according to FSC/SSC parameters, followed by a gate strategy selecting the fluorescent cells sequentially (see Supplementary Fig. 5). Data containing percentage and Geometric Mean were extrapolated from this analysis and used for calculation of SP basal and inducible activity. Data analysis was performed with FlowJo™ v10.9.0 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Basal activity, absolute and fold induction calculation of SPs characterization

Background activity (OFF-state activity) of SPs was represented by extrapolating mCherry Geometric Mean Fluorescence Intensity (gMFI) after sequential gate analysis inside the transfected/electroporated positive cells (FSC/SSC»Single cells»EGFP+ cells»mCherry+ cells) performed in FlowJo™ (see also Supplementary Fig. 5 for Gate Strategy and OFF-state representative dot plots). For heatmaps, log2 was applied to the gMFI. Absolute induction of SPs was represented by a normalization between reporter mCherry and transfection control EGFP, where both percentage and gMFI were considered and calculated for each condition using the following formula: AI = (mCherry gMFI x %mCherry+ cell)/(EGFP gMFI x %EGFP+ cells), where mCherry gMFI is extracted from double EGFP+mCherry+ cell population, %mCherry+ cells is extracted from EGFP+ population, EGFP gMFI is extracted from single EGFP+ population and %EGFP+ cells is extracted from single cells. To these numbers, we multiplied to 1 × 106 and applied log 10 for better visualization (relative units, RU). In Fig. 2A, B, which summarizes the best-performing SPs for each TF, we used a simplified calculation based on the mCherry gMFI and the percentage of mCherry+ cells within the transfected cell population (AI = mCherry gMFI x % mCherry+ cells). This approach was adopted to provide a clear and intuitive representation of promoter activity for comparison purposes in this summary figure. Again, to these numbers we multiplied to 1 × 106 and applied log10 for better visualization (relative units, RU). A similar approach was used in the logic gate circuit characterization, where a calculation was used based on the EBFP gMFI and the percentage of EBFP+ cells (or GFP gMFI and percentage of GFP+ cells) within the transfected cell population. The calculation of Fold-induction was calculated by dividing the AI of inducible conditions ( + TF) by non-inducible (no TF). For heatmaps of Fold Induction, log2 was applied to the Fold Induction.

ELISA assays

Supernatants were harvested from HEK 293FT or Jurkat cells 48 hours after transfection/electroporation with SP plasmids guiding the expression of IL12p70 in the presence or absence of the plasmid that expresses the TF. These molecules were quantified by Lumit® IL12 (Human) Immunoassay (Promega) according to manufacturer instructions.

Primary human CD8 + T cells isolation, transduction and chronic stimulation

All T cell experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines (Ethical approval from Azienda Ospedaliera Universitaria Federico II Protocol number# 8925 and Universitätsklinikum Regensburg protocol n° 20-2040-101). Human CD8+ T cells were isolated from buffy coats of blood donors by magnetic sorting, activated with Dynabeads™ Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 at 1:1 beads: cells ratio, and cultured in R10 medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (Life Technologies), 2 mM GlutaMax (Gibco), 10 mM HEPES, 100 U/ml Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco) and 1% Non-Essential Amino Acids (HyClone)) in the presence of IL-2 (30 U/ml).

To assess synthetic promoter (SP) activation by endogenous transcription factors, primary human CD8⁺ T cells undergoing two rounds of CD3/CD28 stimulation were nucleofected with SP–mCherry reporter plasmids using a 4D-Nucleofector system (Lonza), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were activated with Dynabeads at a 2:1 beads: cells ratio for 3 days, then a second stimulation round at a 1:1 beads: cells ratio was applied for an additional 24 h; at day 4, 1–2 × 10⁶ cells were nucleofected with 1–2 μg of plasmid DNA using the P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector® X Kit and program EO-115. Following nucleofection, cells were kept in culture for 18 h in the absence of beads, then returned to the previous stimulation conditions and cultured for an additional 30 h. Synthetic promoter activity was evaluated at 48 h post-nucleofection by flow cytometric quantification of mCherry expression, while endogenous transcription factor expression was assessed by intracellular immunostaining and flow cytometry.

Lentivirus transduction was carried out in 6-well plates in stimulated T cells after 24–48 h from stimulation. Cells were transduced with lentivirus for SPs at a MOI of 5 and kept at a concentration of one million cells/ml. Cells were re-stimulated with Dynabeads every two days for 8 days to induce chronic stimulation, with previously added beads removed and replaced with fresh beads at each restimulation.

Continuous antigen exposure assay and real-time IncuCyte® imaging

Retroviral particles encoding the NY-ESO-1–specific TCR were generated in 293GP cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), and viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h. In vivo functional assay in NSG mice

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines. NOG IL-15 mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Sug Tg(CMV-IL2/IL15)1-1Jic/JicTac, Taconic Biosciences) were used to assess NR4A2-responsive synthetic promoter activity in engineered CD8⁺ T cells during tumor challenge. SK-Mel-23 melanoma cells were harvested at 70–80% confluency, and 1 × 10⁶ viable cells ( > 95%) in 100 µL were injected subcutaneously per mouse. Tumor growth and clinical status were monitored twice weekly. Mice were 8 weeks of age at the time of tumor implantation. They were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions in a temperature- and humidity-controlled animal facility (12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with ambient temperature maintained at 22 °C ± 2 °C and relative humidity at 45–65%). In accordance with the approved protocol, the maximal permitted tumor burden for mice bearing subcutaneous tumors was a mean tumor diameter — calculated as (length + width)/2 — of 1.5 cm (in line with Workman et al., Br J Cancer 2010, and GV-SOLAS guidelines). In no experiment was the maximal permitted tumor size or burden exceeded.

CD8⁺ T cell isolation, activation, and transduction were performed as described above. According to tumor size, transduced CD8⁺ T cells were resuspended at 7.5–10 × 106 cells/mL in PBS, and 1.5 × 106 cells in 200 µL were administered intravenously per mouse. Mice were monitored regularly for tumor progression and clinical signs.

Tumors and spleens were harvested at day 14 and day 28 post-adoptive transfer. Maximal tumor size and burden were not exceeded. Tumors were excised, weighed, and dissociated using the Human Tumor Dissociation Kit (130-095-929, Miltenyi Biotec) and the gentleMACS™ Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell suspensions were filtered, centrifuged, and resuspended in MACS buffer.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were enriched using the EasySep™ Human CD45 Positive Selection Kit (100-0105, STEMCELL Technologies). Spleens were mechanically dissociated, red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer, and cells were washed in MACS buffer. Tumor and spleen cells were counted and resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells per sample for downstream flow cytometric analysis.

Cells were stained with viability dye and antibodies against CD8, TCR, GFP, and exhaustion-associated markers (including PD-1, TIM-3, TIGIT, CD39, NR4A2, and LAG-3) as described above. Following acquisition, gating was performed to identify live, single CD8⁺ TCR⁺ GFP⁺ cells. Within this population, terminally exhausted (Ttex) CD8+ T cells were identified through sequential gating: LAG-3⁺TIGIT⁺ first selection, followed by CD39⁺ PD-1⁺ cells population selection and NR4A2⁺TIM-3⁺ cells.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated independently 2–5 times. To compare multiple connected conditions, statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparison analysis. For comparisons between two conditions, a one-tailed unpaired t test was used. For fold change data, a two-tailed One-sample t test was used to determine significant differences from the hypothetical baseline value of 1. The threshold for significance was set to 0.05 for the global ANOVA and t test P values.
