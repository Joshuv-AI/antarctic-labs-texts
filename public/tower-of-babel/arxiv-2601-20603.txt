A Canonical Characterization of Normal Functions
Peter V. Dovbush, Steven G. Krantz

Abstract
We characterize normal families in the unit ball as those families of analytic functions whose restrictions to each complex line through the origin are normal. We then generalize this result to a characterization of normal functions according to behavior on analytic discs. A simple proof of an old theorem of Hartog’s that a formal power series at 0 in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} is convergent if its restriction to each complex line through the origin is convergent are given.

Invariant Families in the Unit Ball

The Bergman metric.

In a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
we consider the Hilbert space of holomorphic functions:

L_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}(\Omega)=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{O}(D):\|\varphi\|_{D}^{2}=\int_{D}|\varphi|^{2}dV<\infty\right\}

with the scalar product

(\varphi,\psi)=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\bar{\psi}dV

(
dV
is the volume element).
We shall only consider domains for which this space is nontrivial; we shall call them domains of bounded type (for example, all bounded domains are of this type, but the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is not).

If
\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\in A}
is a complete orthonormal system for
L_{h}^{2}(\Omega)
, the Bergman kernel of
\Omega
is given by the expansion

K_{\Omega}(z,\xi)=\sum_{\alpha\in A}\phi_{\alpha}(z)\overline{\phi_{\alpha}(\xi)}

This formula is far from optimal to find closed forms of
K_{\Omega}
for
\Omega
arbitrary.
Nonetheless, it is well-known (e.g. by choosing a specific complete orthonormal system) that the function
\Phi(z):=\log K_{\Omega}(z,z),z\in\Omega
, is strictly plurisubharmonic, so

\omega_{\Omega}=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\log K_{\Omega}(z,z)

defines a Kähler form on
\Omega
.
The bilinear form

ds^{2}=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}\ln K}{\partial z_{\mu}\partial\bar{z}_{\nu}}dz_{\mu}d\bar{z}_{\nu}=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{n}g_{\mu\nu}dz_{\mu}d\bar{z}_{\nu}

(2.1)

is called the Bergman form of the domain
\Omega
.
The associated Kähler metric
g_{\Omega}
is called the Bergman metric of
\Omega
.

It is remarkable that if
h:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\Omega_{2}
is a biholomorphism of bounded domains, the Bergman kernel for
\Omega_{1}
transforms as

K_{\Omega_{1}}(z,z)=|\det(J(h)(z))|^{2}K_{\Omega_{2}}(h(z),h(z))

where
J(h)
is the Jacobian matrix of
h
, which implies that biholomorphisms between bounded domains are isometries with respect to the Bergman metrics.

The most basic and notorious model is given by the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, for which

K_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(z,z)=\frac{n!}{\pi^{n}}\frac{1}{\left(1-\|z\|^{2}\right)^{n+1}}\,,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
For the ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid|z|<r\}
by formula (
2.1
)

ds^{2}_{r}=(n+1)\left\{\frac{|dz|^{2}}{r^{2}-|z|^{2}}+\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{z}_{\mu}z_{\nu}dz_{\mu}d\bar{z}_{\nu}}{\left(r^{2}-|z|^{2}\right)^{2}}\right\}\,,

where
|dz|^{2}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}\left|dz_{\nu}\right|^{2}
.
For
n=1
and
r=1
the Bergman metric coincide with the Poincarè metric in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
.

The interested reader can refer to
10
for further literature on the Bergman kernel and metric.

For every function
\varphi
of class
C^{2}(\Omega)
we define at each point
z\in\Omega
a hermitian form

L_{z}(\varphi,v):=\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial z_{k}\partial\overline{z}_{l}}(z)v_{k}\overline{v}_{l}\,,

and call it the
Levi form
of the function
\varphi
at
z
.
Since
\varphi
is real this form is Hermitian
\left(\partial^{2}\varphi/\partial z_{\mu}\partial\bar{z}_{\nu}=\bar{\partial}^{2}\varphi/\partial z_{\nu}\partial\bar{z}_{\mu}\right)
and hence it takes real values on all vectors
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

If
\psi:U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a holomorphic mapping and
\psi\in C^{2}
in a neighborhood of the point
f(z)
, then

L_{z}(\varphi\circ\psi,v)=L_{\psi(z)}\left(\varphi,\psi_{*}v\right)

where
\psi_{*}=d\psi
is the differential of the mapping
\psi
at
z
.
The last property shows that the Levi form is invariant relative to biholomorphic mappings.

For a holomorphic function
f
in
\Omega
, set

f^{\sharp}(z):=\sup_{|v|=1}\sqrt{L_{z}(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v)}

(2.2)

This quantity is well defined since the Levi form

is nonnegative for all

.
In particular, for

) takes the form

f^{\sharp}(z):=\frac{|f^{\prime}(z)|}{1+|f(z)|^{2}}\,.

(2.3)

The quantity

is called

of

We say that a holomorphic function

Definition 2.4
.

We say that a subset

Theorem 2.5
.

If a subset
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C})
is an
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
-invariant normal family, then there exists a constant

such that

L_{z}(\log(1+|f|^{2},v)\leq C\cdot ds^{2}(z,v)

(2.5)

for every

Proof.

Let

C(z)=\sup_{\left|v\right|=1,f\in\mathcal{F}}L_{z}(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v)/ds^{2}(z,v)

for each
z\in\mathbb{B}
, and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
|\cdot|
is the length by a flat metric of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

We first prove
C(0)<\infty
for a point

.

Suppose that
C(0)=\infty
.
Then there exist sequences
\left\{f_{j}\right\}\subset\mathcal{F}
and

\left\{v_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid\left|v_{j}\right|=1\right\}

of vectors such that

L_{0}(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v_{j})\geq j^{2}ds^{2}(0,v_{j})\,,

(2.6)

where we may assume that
\left\{v_{j}\right\}
converges to

.
From (
2.6
), we see that

L_{0}(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v)\geq j\cdot\alpha\,,

(2.7)

where
\alpha
is a positive constant.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C})
, some subsequence
\left\{f_{j_{k}}\right\}
of
\left\{f_{j}\right\}
exists such that

and

as

.
Consequently

.
This contradicts (

) and hence

We next prove that

is constant on
\mathbb{B}
.
From the definition of

we see that

L_{z}(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v)\leq C(z)\cdot ds^{2}(z,v)

for every

and every

.
As

is

-invariant,

for every

Hence

.
Similarly

and so

.
As

is homogeneous,

is constant on
\mathbb{B}
and the theorem is proved.

3
Hartogs Theorem

Alexander
2
gives a characterization of normal families of analytic functions in
\mathbb{B}
: A family
\mathcal{F}
of analytic functions on
\mathbb{B}
is normal if and only if the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
to every complex line through the origin is normal.
This result is a “radial” analogue to a theorem of Nishino
12
who proved that a family
\mathcal{F}
of analytic functions on a domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is normal if the restriction to each coordinate line (of the form
z_{1}=\lambda
or
z_{2}=\lambda
) is normal.

Nishino proves a separate normality theorem not only for families of holomorphic functions but also for families of meromorphic functions.

We acknowledge that there have been prior follow-ups to Nishino’s paper, notably a direct generalization by Toshiaki Terada
16
; and an extension to mappings by Theodore J. Barth
3
.
Moreover, Nishino’s results were anticipated by Renato Caccioppoli
4
.

We prove the following theorem

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of analytic functions on
\mathbb{B}
.

(a)

An analytic function
f
on
\mathbb{B}
is normal if and only if the restriction of
f
to every complex line through the origin is normal.

(b)

If the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
to each complex line through the origin is normal, then
\mathcal{F}
is normal.

Proof.

(a)\Rightarrow
Put
z=\lambda c
, where
c\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
|c|=1
.
Define
g(\lambda)=f(\lambda c)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
.
Then

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(\lambda c)}{\partial z_{k}}c_{k}\,.

If
f
is normal in
\mathbb{B}
, then (
2.5
) yields

\displaystyle|g^{\sharp}(\lambda)|^{2}=\frac{\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(\lambda c)}{\partial z_{k}}c_{k}\,\right|^{2}}{(1+|f(\lambda c)|^{2})^{2}}=

\displaystyle L_{c\lambda}\left(\log(1+|f|^{2}),c\right)\leq M\left\{\frac{|c|^{2}}{1-|\lambda|^{2}}+\frac{\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{n}\bar{c}_{\mu}c_{\nu}}{(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{2}}\right\}\leq

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{2M}{(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{2}},\quad(|\lambda|<1)\,.

(The last inequality follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.)

Hence, by Theorem
1.5
, the restriction of
f
to every complex line through the origin is normal.

\Leftarrow
Suppose that the restriction of
f
to some complex line through the origin is not normal.
Then (
3.1
) does not hold, since
(1-|\lambda|^{2})|g^{\sharp}(\lambda)|^{2}
cannot be bounded by Theorem
1.5
, and Theorem
2.5
does not apply.
Hence,
f
is not a normal function in
\mathbb{B}
.

(b)\Rightarrow
Put
z=\lambda c
, where
c\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
|c|=1
.
Define
g(\lambda)=f(\lambda c)
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
.
Then

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(\lambda c)}{\partial z_{k}}c_{k}\,.

If
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
\mathbb{B}
, then (
2.5
) yields

|g^{\sharp}(\lambda)|^{2}=L_{c\lambda}\left(\log(1+|f|^{2}),c\right)\leq\frac{M}{(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{2}}

(3.2)

Hence, by Theorem
1.5
, the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
to every complex line through the origin is normal.

\Leftarrow
Suppose that the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
to some complex line through the origin is not normal.
Then (
3.2
) does not hold for any
M
and Theorem
2.5
does not apply.
Hence,
\mathcal{F}
is not a normal function in
\mathbb{B}
.
∎

Let
\mathbb{C}\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\right\}
be the ring of formal power series of
n
variables
z=\left(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\right)
over
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
be the ring of convergent power series of
n

z=\left(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\right)
over
\mathbb{C}
.

K. Weierstraß based the theory of holomorphic functions on convergent power series.
Therefore it is of interest to have theorems which imply the convergence of a power series.
Here, we give a simple proof of an old theorem of Hartogs
8
that a formal power series at 0 in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is convergent if its restriction to each complex line through the origin is convergent.
An elementary proof, valid for fields more general than the complex numbers, has been given by Stoll

.

As an immediate consequence of the Theorem

, we have

Corollary 3.2

(Hartogs
8
)
.

Let
F=\sum c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}
be a formal power series in
n
complex variables.
If the restriction of
F
to each complex line through the origin is convergent, then
F
is convergent.

Proof.

Put
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{m}\right\}
where
f_{m}=\sum\left\{c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}:|\alpha|\leq m\right\}
.
By Theorem
3.1

\mathcal{F}
is normal at 0, from which it follows easily that
F
is convergent.
∎

Main Result

The origins of the concept of the Kobayashi metric lie in Schwarz’s lemma in complex analysis.

Definition 4.1
.

The Kobayashi metric on
\Omega
is the function
F_{K}:\Omega\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
defined by

F_{K}(z,v)=\inf\left\{\alpha\mid\alpha>0,\exists\varphi\in\Omega(\mathbb{D})\text{ with }\varphi(0)=z,\varphi^{\prime}(0)=v/\alpha\right\}\,.

Since the automorphism group of the ball
\mathbb{B}
is transitive and includes the full group of unitary rotations (hence any complex tangent direction at 0 can be mapped to any other), it follows that any biholomorphically invariant metric on
\mathbb{B}
(Bergman metric for example) is a constant multiple to the Kobayashi metric.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a domain.
Let
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function.
Let
f^{\prime}
be the matrix
\left(\partial f/\partial z_{j}\right)
, i.e. the holomorphic Jacobian.
We say that
f
is normal provided that
f^{\prime}
is bounded from the Kobayashi metric on
\Omega
to the spherical on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
.
More explicitly, we require that for all
z\in\Omega
and all
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
it holds that

L_{z}\left(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v\right)\leqslant C\cdot(F_{K}(z,v))^{2}\,.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function.
A holomorphic function
f
on
\Omega
is normal if and only if we have that the family
\mathcal{F}=\left\{g=f\circ\varphi\mid\text{ for all }\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega\right\}
is normal on the disc
\mathbb{D}
.
Then
f
is normal on
\Omega
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
Suppose that
f
is a normal function on
\Omega
.
Then

\displaystyle|g^{\sharp}(\lambda)\cdot\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)|^{2}

\displaystyle=L_{\varphi(\lambda)}\!\left(\log(1+|f|^{2}),\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\cdot\left(F_{K}(\varphi(\lambda),\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda))\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\,\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)|^{2}}{(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{2}}\,.

(The last inequality follows from the distance-decreasing property of the Kobayashi metric.)
It follows that

|g^{\sharp}(\lambda)|\leq\,\frac{\sqrt{C}}{1-|\lambda|^{2}}\,.

By Theorem
1.5
, the family
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
\mathbb{D}
.

\Leftarrow
Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a normal family on
\Omega
.
By Theorem
2.1
, we have

L_{\varphi(0)}\!\left(\log(1+|f|^{2}),\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right)=\frac{\left|f^{\prime}(\varphi(0))\cdot\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right|^{2}}{(1+|f(\varphi(0))|^{2})^{2}}\leqslant C

for all
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
.

Now fix
z\in\Omega
and a Euclidean unit vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Choose a map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
such that
\varphi(0)=z
,
\varphi^{\prime}(0)
is a positive multiple of
v
, and

F_{K}(z,v)\geqslant\frac{1}{2\|\varphi^{\prime}(0)\|}\,.

It follows that

L_{z}\!\left(\log(1+|f|^{2}),v\right)\leq\,\frac{C}{|\varphi^{\prime}(0)|^{2}}\leq 4C\,\left(F_{K}(z,v)\right)^{2}\,.

Hence,
f
is a normal function on
\Omega
.
∎

5
Concluding Remark

Smoothly bounded domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
generically have no holomorphic automorphisms; so our definition of normality is different from the one given above on
\mathbb{B}
.
On the other hand, Definitions
4.2
and results in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are clearly the natural generalizations of the result in
\mathbb{C}^{1}
.
Some theorems related to those in the present work may be found in the book
7
.
