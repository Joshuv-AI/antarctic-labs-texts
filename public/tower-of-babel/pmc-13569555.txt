Analysis of textual responses to explore barriers, enablers, and motivators for cultivating research capacity: a statewide study of hospital pharmacy staff
Authors: Nadia Izzati Zamri, Nazanin Ghahreman-Falconer, Jacinta L Johnson, Sally B Marotti, Elizabeth McCourt, Sidony Miller-Waugh, Laetitia Hattingh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStudies show that embedding research within healthcare services is associated with improved patient outcomes, enhanced workforce satisfaction, and increases in system performance. However, limited evidence exists regarding the research culture and capacity among hospital pharmacy staff in Australia.AimThis research aimed to assess the research barriers, enablers, and motivators of a statewide public hospital pharmacy service at the organizational, team, and individual levels in Queensland, Australia.MethodThis study forms part of a statewide, prospective, cross-sectional survey conducted across 16 public hospitals and health services in Queensland, Australia. Using the validated Research Capacity in Context Tool, data were collected via a survey that included open-ended questions to explore research barriers, enablers, and motivators. Reflexive, inductive thematic analysis was conducted to identify key themes and subthemes in free-text responses, with subthemes mapped against the Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour model and the Theoretical Domains Framework.ResultsOf the 481 survey participants, 401 provided open-ended responses. Six overarching themes and 14 subthemes were developed that influence research engagement. Themes are time and workload constraints, building research capacity, support from management, career progression and personal satisfaction, altruistic motivators, and navigating research processes. Lack of protected research time, limited access to resources, and variation in leadership support were prominent barriers to research uptake. Enablers of research uptake included availability of mentorship, interdisciplinary collaboration, intrinsic interest, and desire to contribute to patient-centered care.ConclusionThis study underscores the multifaceted barriers and enablers that act as motivators to cultivate research capacity within hospital pharmacy departments. Addressing concerns such as protected research time, mentorship, and streamlined governance processes is essential to nurturing a sustainable research culture.Keywords: Healthcare workforce, Hospital pharmacy, Mentorship, Protected time, Qualitative data, Research capacity

Introduction

Research-infused health services deliver better patient outcomes with studies showing they improve mortality, patient outcomes, and patient satisfaction [1–3]. There is evidence of improved health system performance when services have a positive research culture [4]. Global literature supports the notion that high academic output is associated with the ability to provide superior quality of care [1, 2, 4–6]. Additionally, research-active health services have greater attraction, satisfaction, and retention of skilled and diverse workforces [7, 8].

The Strategic Review of Health and Medical Research in Australia in 2013 suggested incorporating research into health systems and involving workforces in research for continuous improvement [9]. The development of the research capacity of allied health professionals has also been recognized as a priority to cultivate positive changes for patients, healthcare professionals, healthcare organizations, and society [10]. Evidence suggests that, due to various interdependent factors, research capacity building efforts must be targeted at all levels and supported by management [10, 11].

Studies have shown the value of research conducted by pharmacy staff in improving health services [12, 13]. There is a need to enhance pharmacy practice research to inform health service planning, especially considering the expanding scope of pharmacy services [14]. However, despite the crucial nature of clinical research, hospital pharmacy research output varies considerably [12, 15], and there is limited evidence on the current research culture and capacity [16].

International research showed that hospital pharmacists had high levels of confidence in research, but a lack of dedicated time [17–21]. Conversely, a cross-sectional survey in Qatar found that while hospital pharmacists’ general attitudes towards research were positive, there were barriers to support (e.g., administration) and cultivate research culture [17]. Another UK study done in the UK regarding hospital pharmacists echoed similar sentiments, whereby their current research involvement rate is low, as is the confidence in applying research to practice [22]. One study in the 2021 systematic review was conducted that identified nine studies worldwide concerning the barriers and enablers of research for hospital pharmacists and identified challenges across four areas: pharmacist capacity and capability, workplace environment, access to research resources, and research culture [23]. In Australia, one study was found during our literature search that used an online survey methodology conducted to characterize the research capacity and culture of a state-wide public-hospital pharmacy service. However, the study was specific to the pharmacy services in South Australia [16]. Furthermore, few studies exist specifically to explore research culture concerning pharmacy technicians and assistants [16].

Aim

This research aimed to assess the research barriers, enablers, and motivators of a statewide public hospital pharmacy service at the organizational, team, and individual levels in Queensland, Australia.

Method

A prospective, cross-sectional state-wide survey was conducted to explore research barriers, enablers, motivators, research activity and perceptions concerning research capacity and culture across all public hospital pharmacy departments of sixteen hospital and health services (HHS) in Queensland, Australia. This paper reports on the open-ended responses (Part 2) of the survey, while Part 1 is in the process of being reported elsewhere. The separation of quantitative and qualitative findings was to ensure fair representation and analysis of the large amount of data. The study was conducted and reported following the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Survey tool

The Research Capacity in Context (RCC) Tool was used and has been validated to report on research capacity and culture within healthcare settings [25]. It utilizes statements that can be rated on a numerical scale to evaluate perceptions of research skills, support structures, and research culture across individual, team, and organizational levels. Open-ended questions were added at the end of each section to explore research barriers, enablers, and motivators.

Participants and recruitment

Individuals employed by the public hospital pharmacy departments of sixteen Hospital and Health Services (HHS) in Queensland, Australia, were eligible to participate: pharmacists, internship or practicum pharmacists, and pharmacy technicians/assistants. Directors of Pharmacy were approached to provide mailing lists for survey distribution and tracking of response rates over five weeks between April and May 2024. Two HHSs chose to use an email from the Director of Pharmacy to advertise the survey. After the initial invitation email to 1818 individuals, reminder emails to complete the survey were sent weekly for three weeks. Local site survey champions were approached to promote survey completion, including research portfolio holders, clinical educators, and Directors of Pharmacy.

Data analysis

Reflexive, inductive thematic analysis was conducted on free‑text survey responses using NVivo 2020 to identify prominent themes. Thematic analysis began with three researchers (LH, NF, and NZ) independently reading the free-text comment data. One researcher (NZ) initially coded approximately 10% of the free-text, and two researchers (LH and NF) reviewed the coding. After agreeing on a coding framework, NZ completed the coding, with LH and NF independently checking. At each stage, discrepancies were discussed with other researchers (NZ, LH, NF, and EM). Analysis to refine the themes continued throughout the report-writing process. Subsequent to the thematic analysis, the subthemes were mapped against the COM-B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, Behaviour) Behaviour Change Wheel, and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDM) [24, 25]. COM-B was used as a first step for high-level mapping that was then followed by a mapping against the TDF for a more granular breakdown of the influences in behaviours that enable or act as barriers to research culture. Illustrative quotes were selected for presentation in the results.

Ethics approval

Ethics exemption was granted for the study by the local Research Ethics Committee (EX/2023/MNHA/102531).

Time and workload constraints

Protected research time

Participants consistently identified time constraints, particularly a lack of protected research time, as a major barrier to research engagement: “the pharmacy department wants research to be done but expects staff to do it in their own time (P39).” “Having protected time to complete workplace research (P261)” was seen as an enabler. Participants also noted that other competing responsibilities often take precedent:

Time [is a barrier] - I have a full clinical load... immediate acute patient care need[s] to be prioritized... I recently supervised a [research] student on top of my clinical load... staying back at work late most nights of the project.... research workload I’ve done has always been completed outside work hours (P73).

Staff are being asked to do more with less time...department expectation of research is unfair on staff who have other life priorities and there’s no offline support for research activities to be done (P365).

Some appreciated the flexibility of non-traditional clinical roles (such as working in outpatient clinics), which allowed for integration of research into clinical practice as “hours tend to be more flexible/family friendly than the rigid hours of a pharmacy department (P455).”

Difficulty in obtaining backfill due to being “the only pharmacist in a unique role (P186)” and thus “any audits/research done are on top of normal workload (P186)” was raised. There were also barriers in obtaining funding as “request for funding applications to my manager …[have] not being prioritized (P186).”

Where research was seen to be clinically relevant and important, this was more likely to be prioritized, thus promoting a research-centered culture and facilitating time for the research:

Working to integrate research into clinical activities and fostering an attitude where research is part of clinical service, I feel is a great approach to reduce fear and increase peoples involvement in research. Small achievable projects are key to facilitating and fostering this culture (P604).

Variations in clinical and personal responsibilities

Variations in workload and personal commitments influenced research capacity. Postgraduate studies, family responsibilities, and teaching loads were cited as competing priorities. Financial constraints also limited the ability to reduce clinical hours to pursue research or higher degrees, with one participant noting that “to be able to move into my PhD and study in a part-time capacity would greatly affect my family’s income (P551).” Another participant mirrored this sentiment, whereby it's difficult to prioritize research in their “own [personal time] hours due to personal reasons and postgraduate efforts (P520).”

Other participants mentioned their satisfaction with the diversity in their role as it is “an extension of the traditional clinical role that adds variety to day-to-day workload (P168).” Participants also noted how they found opportunities in research through other commitments such as post-graduate studies, residency programs, and conjoint supervision for honors projects.

Building research capacity

Collaboration opportunities

Opportunities for collaboration emerged as a key enabler of research engagement, for instance, regarding interdisciplinary partnerships:

Increased engagement with [other healthcare] professional, opportunity to collaborate across-HHs [Hospital and Health Services] and build professional networks interstate (P253).

Networking through “attendance at conferences (P359)” enables the chance to “present research” and “increases opportunities for networking.” However, reduced access to “research-trained colleagues limits collaboration.” In particular, a respondent cited that “not being a’Metro’ Hospital and Health Service (HHS), [it]feels like [there is] limited support from universities from a research perspective (P126).” Inconsistent engagement from other healthcare professionals was also mentioned as a barrier:

Inability to access staff involved in the project - in particular accessing medical staff in a timely manner to help facilitate the project/complete milestones (P727).

Whilst there are opportunities for collaboration with interprofessional research teams, there appear to be barriers that prevent these leading to successful completion of projects:

CA4’s [senior clinical assistants-pharmacy technicians/assistants] across the board are under-utilized ... There have been many times I offered to be involved in many different multidisciplinary teams research as a CA4, even simple data collection, but [they have] not come to fruition for various reasons (P490).

Access to research resources, expertise and funding

Access to resources, including funding, fellowships, mentorship, and statistical support, varied across sites. Several participants expressed a desire for structured guidance, such as a “guideline on how to conduct research and mentorship (P553).” Insufficient technical infrastructure and research expertise, especially in rural and regional settings, further hindered capacity building. Others identified that they were unaware of available local resources.

Conversely, ease of access to data, statisticians, computers, and funding were seen as enablers:

... to have a mentor that could assist in the design and creation of research [would motivate me to undertake research] ... (P64).

Pathway to securing additional funding and thus improving the quality of clinical services and staffing levels (P282).

Opportunities for research

There were perceptions that there were inequities in the distribution of research resources, with some reporting minimal access to projects or supported research experiences. This was exceptionally evident among pharmacy technicians and staff in non-metropolitan health services:

It is heavily stacked towards larger facilities. BUT I have met some inspirational researchers in rural/remote... (P589).

I find that most of the research opportunities go to interns instead of pharmacists on the floor (P80).

Pharmacy techs don’t do research like pharmacists do (P539).

Enablers of research were involvement in “TRIP [Translating Research into Practice Program] projects (directly applicable to practice) (P556),” the ability to “be involved in assisting pharmacy students with projects (P186),” and the “opportunity for fellowships and fundings (P341).”

Barriers mentioned were uncertainty or limitations in available opportunities. One mentioned geographical-related barrier potentially diluting their research capacity as they “feel research is not supported in rural and remote areas (P653),” while others challenged with finding an interested “[research] student to be here to complete project especially as hospital is located further away from [major city] (P496).” Another participant cited tensions between research priorities as they “may wish to conduct research or audit one topic or area but having to prioritize another topic due to safety incidents or due to management priorities (P538).”

Support from management

Perceived leadership support impacted research engagement, such as working in a department that values research, with support from senior staff. Motivators to undertake research involved “support and encouragement from seniors within my team/organization (P673)” or “support from the ADOP (Acting Director of Pharmacy) and pharmacy team leader from [the] ward you are assigned (P344).” However, many participants described “organizational support for research is lacking (P580),” with research often deprioritized in favor of clinical priorities. One shared, “I have been actively discouraged from conducting research due to 100% contract time being required for clinical patient care (P574)” despite “requesting to be involved in research (P574).”

One participant cited a lack of direction as there are “no department wide research plan highlighting areas of need very individual driven (P530).” Others noted a disunity concerning research ideas and the distribution of research opportunities by management, which contributed to a fragmented research culture:

Feeling unsupported by education team/management ...all resources are aimed purely at resident pharmacists (P69).

Career progression and personal satisfaction

Research interest

Personal interest in research was a strong motivator, with many participants expressing enthusiasm for quality improvement and evidence-based practice:

I enjoy research, identifying problems and figuring out ways to solve them. Knowledge is power and evidence-based practice in healthcare is essential to achieving optimal patient outcomes (P339).

One participant had noted that as they “have a research degree (P126),” it would “be a waste not to use those skills (P126).” However, others reported limited interest or perceived research as irrelevant to their roles:

It not the culture for CA’s [clinical assistants] to participate in research generally... no process or framework to support [research]...lack of [research] culture is mainly due to CA’s historically not being interested in research... (490).

Overall perception of research not being overly important as unable to cover normal pharmacy business (P435).

A few noted a lack of motivation, with one participant mentioning that they are “at the end of … career (P515),” thus had “no motivation to start now (P515).” Another participant cited their frustration with the lack of changes:

Research doesn’t seem to translate into practice in pharmacy which makes you wonder why bother sometimes (P697).

Promotion and recognition

Professional recognition was cited by a few participants as strong motivators:

Knowing that we are making a difference. Research into pharmacy practice driving our service improvement, making evidence-based decisions on strategies and planning. Seeing staff achieve their goals and developing skills in research. Seeing culture of enquiry and well-designed research into practice slowly improving our team. (P91).

Some viewed research as a pathway to career advancement and professional differentiation, while others noted inadequate incentives or recognition for research contributions:

Get paid more... be appreciated by senior management/leadership. Opportunities for promotion/advancement ...(P69).

I would like to organize with other pharmacy CA’s [clinical assistants] in pharmacy on increasing our classification levels and having our roles and responsibilities changed... (P138).

Concerns about the absence of clear post-PhD career pathways and the perception that research did not translate into tangible benefits were also raised as research is “not really incorporated into the role, even when it is in job description… not an expectation for the senior HP [Health Professional], especially if the managers don’t have research background (P85).” Another participant echoed a similar sentiment as they noted that “research does not lead to career progression the same way as managerial studies / pathway, and it may be detrimental to career progression… (P85).”

Desire to contribute within the research community

Some participants noted intrinsic factors to be motivators for research such as having “peer reviewed publications (P198),” the “ability to present at conferences to disseminate findings (P198)” and to be a part of the research community.

Altruistic motivators

Patient-centered care

Improving patient care was a central motivator for engaging in research, as many mentioned incentives such as improving care, and multidisciplinary collaboration:

The positive difference it can make to patient outcomes and experiences; and also, the benefit and efficiency that may result for the multidisciplinary team (P296).

Looking to implement the translational research findings into actual work practice is one of the biggest motivators (P56).

Participants highlighted the potential of research to optimize clinical outcomes, enhance service delivery, and contribute to the evidence base, particularly in rural and remote settings. One participant stated they “love doing research (P381)” as they "believe in providing evidence-based care to consumers (P381).”

Enhancing pharmacy reputation

Increasing departmental reputation seemed to be a common motivator for some participants as they wish to “highlight the important work pharmacists do (P207)” or “capture the amazing work our hospital pharmacy team does… celebrate it at events such as Medicines Management SHPA [national hospital pharmacy conference] and other forums (P335)” which for others may be linked to obtaining “more funding … to our department to allow for staffing positions to be created (P207).”

One participant expressed pride in showcasing departmental achievements and supporting junior colleagues in their research journeys:

Also, I have every publication from our department printed and laminated and hung around the top walls of our department. So, when people publish it, they are excited to bring me the publication and hang it on the wall for show. Need to celebrate the wins (P397).

One noted it may assist with “staff retention (P77)” if they become “a site that conducts research (P77),” while another noted the wish to create “an academic base of excellence (P100).”

Mentoring others

Being role models to guide other members, especially juniors, was also cited as another altruistic motivator as it allowed them to “reflect on my personal contributions (P466),” “share knowledge (P129),” or simply as it is rewarding. Some participants noted wanting to assist with junior pharmacists as they:

Enjoy supporting Foundation Residents with their research projects - fantastic to see them collaborating in MDTs [multidisciplinary teams] and having posters accepted at conferences (P350).

Another participant noted wishing to mentor junior pharmacists to supplement for the lack of programs at their site:

Helping junior pharmacist with portfolio journey especially because we do not have a residency program (P496).

One participant mentioned that they enjoyed having a “buddy system … motivation to success comes from working alongside other people with a positive mindset (P225).”

Navigating research processes

Understanding research processes

Understanding and navigating research processes posed significant challenges, as some were “unsure of the processes (P732).” Ethics approvals, governance requirements, and publication processes were described as complex and burdensome. Participants called for streamlined pathways, particularly for low-risk projects:

It would... be great if the ethics pathways were simplified - especially for exemptions which we regularly organize for students. It sometimes becomes a significant task for a simple [short-term student] project (P155).

Many of the processes often led to “delays when submitting research to journals (P125).” Additionally, the “constant preparation then submitting to a journal only having to then resubmit and reprepare to another journal (P609)” may be “debilitating (P609).”

Educational needs

Participants desired structured training in research methods, ethics applications, and manuscript writing:

Would love some general research pharmacy-specific resources on ’how to’ write ethics application, how to design surveys, how to write a manuscript etc. (P452).

I would also be interested in courses on stats if available to Queensland Health staff (P155).

One participant noted the advantages of developing research skills in new staff:

It would be great to provide early career pharmacists or new employees with optional research opportunities. I think it would help develop their skillsets, exposure, and create better engagement. It also gives them encouragement and a steppingstone to develop themselves (P388).

Many reported acquiring research skills informally and expressed uncertainty about how to initiate projects as they have trouble “getting started with an idea…have tried to engage with University to assist… mentors advise to start small, but … lack research know how (P77).”

Some noted the insufficient experience or guidance in the department:

No previous staff member has completed higher-level research whilst working as a member of this pharmacy team. [I’m] Unsure where to start / who to contact. [I’m] Unsure if [research is] financially viable (P394).

COM-B and TDF analysis

Mapping of the subthemes against the COM_B and domains of the TDF (Table 3) showed that the behaviour change components of the COM-B model were integrated and played a role in the domains of the TDF, which could inform future interventions to grow pharmacy research. Four themes were mapped against the capability and opportunity domains, representing barriers and enablers linked to resources or conditions that impact behaviour. These were: knowledge, skills, social influences, environmental context, and resources. Two themes were mapped against the motivation domain; representing the reasons or incentives driving individuals’ behaviour: beliefs about consequences and social or professional role and identity.

Discussion

The study explored hospital pharmacy staff's views on research participation, identifying six themes and 14 subthemes of barriers, enablers, and motivators. Mapping of the themes and subthemes against the COM-B and TDF showed the distinct constructs in behaviour change that impact research capacity and how to address these in future implementation strategies. The results highlight how institutional support, access to resources, and individual motivation collectively shape a sustainable research culture within pharmacy departments.

Participants frequently reported that research is expected to be conducted outside of work. These findings echoed studies involving pharmacists and medical practitioners who found inadequate time and other work roles being prioritized [14–16]. Due to the lack of protected research time, additional research work leads to disruption of work-life balance and burnout [27].

Participants in rural and remote areas reported limited access to funding, mentors, and technical infrastructure compared to metropolitan areas. This is consistent with a study conducted in France on hospital pharmacists, which showed that the availability of funding assisted with the promotion of research [28]. A study exploring clinician-researchers in Victoria, Australia, showed the importance of mentors as role models to facilitate the development of research, networks, and translation capacity [29]. This study also noted that mentoring is strengthened in a collaborative environment. A study involving pharmacists in Canada similarly identified mentorship programs as enablers of research [18]. Research has also highlighted that the paucity of research activity in rural and remote areas may contribute to limited rural‐specific scientific evidence, thus contributing to health disparities [19], and enhancing partnerships between smaller health services and academic institutions, as well as investing in digital infrastructure, may help bridge this divide.

Leadership support was reported as a critical enabler of research engagement, which was consistent with current literature [30]. Departments with a strong research culture and visible support from senior staff are more likely to foster participation than found in other studies [31–33]. Conversely, inconsistent messaging and lack of strategic direction were reported to act as a barrier to research activity. These findings reinforce the role of leadership in shaping research culture and suggest that departmental research strategies and mentorship programs enhance engagement.

While many participants expressed intrinsic motivation to engage in research which reflects similar findings done in Qatar [17], others cited a limited interest or perceived misalignment with roles. The absence of clear career pathways and recognition for research contributions was a recurring concern. Research shows allied health clinician researchers similarly report a lack of opportunities for clinician researcher roles and career pathways, and not being valued or incentivized by health services [34],whereas academic roles facilitated research [28]. This highlights the need for structured career development frameworks that integrate research as a valued component of professional advancement.

Altruistic motivations, including improving patient care and mentoring others, were powerful enablers of research engagement. These findings suggest that framing research to enhance clinical practice and departmental reputation may resonate with staff and increase participation. Personal satisfaction and the opportunity to learn about disease states were also driving factors for conducting research [18]. Celebrating research achievements and fostering a sense of collective purpose may further strengthen engagement [35].

Participants reported significant challenges in navigating research processes, especially ethics and governance approvals, as research barriers, which echoes other international literature such as the cross-sectional survey done in Qatar [17]. Lack of experience and uncertainty in starting research projects are also barriers to research capacity building. There is also evidence that pharmacists with research experience are more confident in designing a research study and more likely to participate in future research opportunities [36]. Hence, streamlining ethics processes for low-risk projects and providing targeted training in research methods could mitigate these barriers.

A key strength of this study is that it is the first statewide investigation in Queensland involving public hospital pharmacy staff. It captures a comprehensive view of research capacity and culture, as well as perspectives across diverse roles from pharmacy assistants to management, including metropolitan, regional, rural, and remote sites. The use of a validated survey combined with qualitative free-text responses, analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis and mapped to the COM-B and TDF frameworks, enhances the study’s analytical rigor. However, limitations include potential response and participant bias due to voluntary participation, as those more interested in research may have been more likely to respond. Additionally, as the data represents a single time point, findings may not reflect changes in research culture over time, particularly amidst organizational change.

Future research should focus on the evaluation of research capacity-building initiatives to assess the impact of targeted interventions in relation to site-specific needs and resources. Further exploration of underrepresented groups, such as pharmacy assistants and interns, may identify unique barriers and motivators. Aligning research priorities with contemporary healthcare challenges may strengthen future research efforts.

Conclusion

This statewide study identified the diverse factors shaping research engagement among Queensland Health hospital pharmacy staff. To strengthen research culture, key enablers include protected research time, improved mentorship and collaboration, clearer career pathways, streamlined ethics processes, and embedding research training into professional development and/or higher-level roles. Changes in organizational, national, and international policies and/or frameworks (e.g., frameworks under the International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP)) could potentially promote research capability-building efforts provided that organizational support is present. Sustained funding is essential to support these initiatives and build long‑term research capacity.
