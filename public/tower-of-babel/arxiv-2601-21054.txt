Setting and main result

We consider

1.2.
Related work

Particle systems with selection were proposed in
[
8
,
9
]
as models for natural selection in population dynamics, where the position of a particle on the real line represents the degree of fitness of an individual to its environment. A closely related model,
referred to as the
N
-particle branching Brownian motion (
N
-BBM), was introduced in
[
20
]
. Here,
N
Brownian particles on the line are subject to branching and selection, where upon each branching event, the leftmost particle is removed.
At the hydrodynamic limit, it was shown to give rise to a free boundary problem (FBP)
[
13
,
6
]
.
Various other particle systems with selection, with and without branching, were characterized by FBP at the hydrodynamic limit, including Brownian systems in dimension one
[
11
,
1
]
and higher
[
4
,
5
]
, and non-local branching models
[
15
,
14
,
2
]
.
Apart from models involving selection, work on particle systems that are macroscopically described by FBP include
[
12
]
which studies a variant of the simple exclusion process, and
[
10
,
3
]
which study the Atlas model.

A population model with selection was studied in
[
17
]
, where individuals die at rate that depends on the local population density, establishing macroscopic approximations. Our model, like
[
17
]
, captures density dependent selection. It also exhibits clear similarities to the
N
-BBM, where removals occur at the extremes: specifically, the leftmost particle in the
N
-BBM and the most populated site in our model.

We now draw an analogy between the macroscopic model that arises from the
N
-BBM
[
13
,
6
]
, and the one obtained in our work. In the former case, this is a FBP that takes the following form. Given an initial density
u_{0}
,
|u_{0}|_{1}=1
, find
(u,\sigma)
solving

(8)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}=\partial_{xx}u+u&x>\sigma(t),\\
u=0&x\leq\sigma(t),\\
|u(\cdot,t)|_{1}=1&t>0,\\
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

Here,
u
represents the mass density and
\sigma
the left edge of its support.
Consider now equation (
6
) as a free obstacle problem. Given
u_{0}
,
|u_{0}|_{1}=1
, find
(u,h)
such that

(9)

\begin{cases}0\leq u(x,t)\leq h(t)&x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},t>0,\\
\partial_{t}u(x,t)={\mathcal{L}}^{*}u(x,t)+u(x,t)&\text{\rm if }u(x,t)<h(t),\\
|u(\cdot,t)|_{1}=1&t>0,\\
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

The role of
|u|_{\infty}
in (
6
) is played here by
h
, and the condition that
\beta_{t}
is a probability measure for a.e.
t
is recast by the mass conservation condition.
(For a treatment of an obstacle problem of parabolic type via viscosity solutions, with a given obstacle
h
and without a global constraint such as
|u(\cdot,t)|_{1}=1
, see
[
16
, Theorem 8.6]
).

Although there are similarities, the tools required are quite different. The proofs in
[
13
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
use the barrier method technique, that is similar to a Trotter scheme which allows one to separate the motion and branching from the removal mechanism. This technique relies crucially on monotonicity properties with respect to mass transport inequalities, which does not seem to have an analogue in the model we study here. We were unable to implement a Trotter scheme for proving uniqueness of solutions to (
6
). The proofs in
[
6
,
4
]
are based on the existence of classical solutions to (
8
).

1.3.
Sketch of the proof

The proof is based on the compactness–uniqueness approach.
Showing uniqueness of solutions to (
6
) crucially uses
the continuity of the component
u
of the solution (specifically, that
u\in{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
). Therefore, relative compactness of the laws of
(\xi^{N},\beta^{N})
is insufficient on its own, and must be supplemented with estimates that guarantee that limits of
\xi^{N}
have continuous densities w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

The first step of the proof, carried out in Section
2
, is to take the
N
limit, with
\varepsilon
fixed, and show that it can be described by an ODE on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, equation (
12
). This equation can be seen as a discrete analogue of equation (
6
) along with the constraint
\beta\in\mathbf{B}^{u}
expressed by (
5
). In Lemma
2.2
, it is shown that the ODE has at most one solution. Then, in the series of Lemmas
2.3
,
2.4
,
2.5
it is shown that the sequence
(\xi^{N},\beta^{N})
is tight. Finally, in Proposition
2.6
, it is shown that all limits are supported on solutions to the ODE (
12
). Hence, denoting in what follows the unique solution to (
12
) by
(\mathbf{u}^{(\varepsilon)},\mathbf{\Lambda}^{(\varepsilon)})
, the above line achieves the convergence in probability
(\xi^{\varepsilon,N},\beta^{\varepsilon,N})\to(\xi^{(\varepsilon)},\beta^{(\varepsilon)})
as
N\to\infty
, where

\xi^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}(dx)=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{u}^{(\varepsilon)}(y,t)\delta_{y}(dx),\qquad\beta^{(\varepsilon)}(dx,dt)=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{(\varepsilon)}(y,t)\delta_{y}(dx)dt.

The functions
U^{(\varepsilon)}:=\varepsilon^{-d}\mathbf{u}^{(\varepsilon)}
defined on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
are then considered as prelimit versions for the density of limits of
\xi^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The goal of Section
3
is to provide estimates on the modulus of continuity of
U^{(\varepsilon)}
that are uniform in
\varepsilon
(Proposition
3.2
). The main idea here is to use a coupling of two Markov processes (constructed in equation (
24
)), for which the generator of each is given by
\bar{\mathcal{L}}=\mathnormal{\Delta}-b\cdot\nabla\cdot
. It is shown in Lemma
3.3
that differences
U^{(\varepsilon)}(x,t)-U^{(\varepsilon)}(y,t)
can be controlled in terms of this coupling.

The limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
is taken in Section
4
, where it is shown, in Proposition
4.1
, that the family
(\xi^{(\varepsilon)},\beta^{(\varepsilon)})
is relatively compact and limits of its first component have densities that form solutions to (
6
). Here, the aforementioned estimates on
U^{(\varepsilon)}
are used to show that these are continuous.

Finally, in Section
5
, it is shown that (
6
) has at most one solution. The continuity of the component
u
of the solution makes it possible to mollify and get approximations in the uniform topology.
We noted above that monotonicity played a role in earlier work in establishing uniqueness for FBP that arise from selection models. The same is true in our approach, although here a different form of monotonicity is essential, namely, the monotonicity of the set-valued operator
u\rightarrow\mathbf{B}^{u}
expressed as follows:
\langle\beta_{t}-\gamma_{t},u(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,t)\rangle\geq 0
for a.e.
t
provided that
\beta\in\mathbf{B}^{u}
and
\gamma\in\mathbf{B}^{v}
.

1.4.
Examples and open problems

In the discussion that follows, we will refer to the sets
\{x:u(x,t)=|u(\cdot,t)|_{\infty}\}
as the
argmax sets
. First we show that in some cases explicit solutions can be found.

Example 1.6

(Flat top solution)
.

Here we give an explicit stationary solution in dimension 1, in the case where the drift is given by
b(x)=-2\tanh(x)
. Let

w=\log(1+\sqrt{2}),\qquad h=\frac{1}{2(w+\sqrt{2})},

u(x,t)=u(x)=\begin{cases}h&|x|\leq w\\
\displaystyle 2h\frac{\sinh|x|}{\cosh^{2}|x|}&|x|>w,\end{cases}

\beta_{t}(dx)=h(1+{\rm sech}^{2}x)1_{[-w,w]}(x)dx,\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

Then
(u,\beta)
is a stationary solution of (
6
), as can be checked by direct calculation. Note that
b
is bounded Lipschitz, and so our results apply; in particular, the convergence stated in Theorem
1.5
holds provided the initial density is
u_{0}=u
.

Example 1.7

(Sharp and flat top solutions)
.

We next consider the drift
b(x)=-a\,{\rm sign}(x)
,

and show that there are multiple stationary solutions in this case. Some of them have argmax sets in the form of an interval (“flat top”), as in Example
1.6
, while in others the argmax is a singleton (“sharp top”).
Although the discontinuous drift coefficient precludes applying our results, the solutions to (
6
) we construct are well-defined in the sense of Definition
1.4
and provide motivation for some new questions.

First, consider
a>2
. Then for any
w\geq 0
, the following is a stationary solution of (
6

1.4
:

(10)

u(x,t)=u(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2(w+a)}&|x|\leq w\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(w+a)r}\left[\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{1}(|x|-w)}-\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{2}(|x|-w)}\right]&|x|>w\end{cases}

\beta_{t}(dx)=\frac{a\delta_{0}(dx)}{w+a}+\frac{1_{[-w,w]}(x)}{2(w+a)}dx

where

and the decay constants are

\lambda_{1}=\frac{a-r}{2}
,
\lambda_{2}=\frac{a+r}{2}
.

Next, one can show that for any
u(0)\in[\frac{1}{2a},\frac{1}{a-r}]
there exists a stationary solution
(u,\beta)
where the argmax set is the singleton

and

.
Developing a proof of Theorem

using this approach would necessitate estimates to ensure that limit densities lie in

2.
From particle system to ODE

In this section it is shown that, for fixed
\varepsilon

and
N\to\infty
, the empirical measure of the particle system converges to deterministic dynamics given by a system of ODE indexed by
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
.
Throughout the section,
\varepsilon
is fixed.
Moreover, Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
apply throughout the remainder of this article. Recall that by Assumption
1.2
,
we have
\xi^{\varepsilon,N}_{0}\to\xi^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}
in probability in
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
as
N\to\infty
.

First we define the formal adjoint of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
and introduce some related notation.
For
1\leq i\leq 2d
, let
i^{*}=i+d
if
i\leq d

and
i^{*}=i-d
if
i\geq d+1
. With this,
k_{i^{*}}=-k_{i}
.
Let

\rho_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=r_{\varepsilon,i^{*}}(x+\varepsilon k_{i}).

Denote
\bar{r}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sum_{i\leq 2d}r_{\varepsilon,i}(x)

and
\bar{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sum_{i\leq 2d}\rho_{\varepsilon,i}(x)
.
Let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq 2d}[r_{\varepsilon,i}(x-\varepsilon k_{i})f(x-\varepsilon k_{i})-r_{\varepsilon,i}(x)f(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq 2d}\rho_{\varepsilon,i}(x)f(x+\varepsilon k_{i})-\bar{r}_{\varepsilon}(x)f(x),\qquad\quad x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}.

The duality relation holds,

(11)

\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}f,g\rangle=\langle f,{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{*}g\rangle,

for
f
and
g
defined on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, and, say, one of them is compactly supported, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denoting the usual inner product on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
.

The ODE on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
is an equation for
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{\Lambda})
, which, given an initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}({\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, reads

(12)

\begin{split}&\mathbf{u}(x,t)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}({\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{\Lambda})(x,s)ds,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\\
&\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,t)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{x}\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,t)=1,\hskip 72.00008ptx\in{\mathcal{S}},\ \text{a.e. }t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\\
&\sum_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}(|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)|_{\infty}-\mathbf{u}(x,t))\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,t)dt=0.\end{split}

Definition 2.1

(Solution to (
12
))
.

A solution to (
12
) is a pair
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{\Lambda})
where
\mathbf{u}
is a mapping
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
\mathbf{u}(x,\cdot)\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
for all
x
,
|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)|_{1}=1
for all
t
,
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is a measurable mapping
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and (
12
) holds.

Lemma 2.2
.

i.
If
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{\Lambda})
and
(\bar{\mathbf{u}},\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}})
are two solutions to (
12
) for an initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}({\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
then
\mathbf{u}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}
and, for every
x
and a.e.
t
,
\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,t)=\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(x,t)
.

ii. Suppose there exists a solution
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{\Lambda})
to (
12
). Then for
0\leq s<t
,

(13)

\mathbf{u}(y,t)=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{u}(x,s)\mathfrak{s}_{t-s}(x,y)-\int_{s}^{t}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,\tau)\mathfrak{s}_{t-\tau}(x,y)d\tau,

where
\mathfrak{s}_{t}(x,y)=\mathfrak{s}^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}(x,y)=e^{t}\mathfrak{p}_{t}(x,t)
, and
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}^{(\varepsilon)}
is the fundamental solution of
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}={\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.
i.
Denote
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}
. Clearly,
|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)|_{2}\vee|\bar{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,t)|_{2}\leq 1
. Moreover,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{w}(\cdot,t)|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{w}(\cdot,0)|^{2}_{2}+2\sum_{x}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{w}({\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{\Lambda}+\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}})(x,s)ds.

By Assumption
1.1
,
r_{\varepsilon}
is bounded (recall
\varepsilon
is fixed). Hence

|{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}f(x)|\leq c\sum_{i\leq 2d}|f(x+\varepsilon k_{i})|+c|f(x)|
, and

\sum_{x}|f(x){\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}f(x)|\leq c|f|^{2}_{2}.

This shows that

|\mathbf{w}(\cdot,t)|^{2}_{2}\leq c\int_{0}^{t}|\mathbf{w}(\cdot,s)|^{2}_{2}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x}(\mathbf{u}-\bar{\mathbf{u}})(-\mathbf{\Lambda}+\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}})(x,s)ds.

The last integral is
\leq 0
because for all
s
,

\sum_{x}\mathbf{u}(x,s)\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(x,s)\leq\sum_{x}|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,s)|_{\infty}\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(x,s)=|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,s)|_{\infty}

whereas for a.e.
s
,

\sum_{x}\mathbf{u}(x,s)\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,s)=\sum_{x}|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,s)|_{\infty}\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,s)=|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,s)|_{\infty},

and a similar statement holds for
\bar{\mathbf{u}}
. Hence by Gronwall’s lemma,
\mathbf{w}=0
.

The a.e. equality
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\bar{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
follows because, by (
12
), for a.e.
t
,
\mathbf{\Lambda}(x,t)=(\dot{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{*}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u})(x,t)
.

ii. It is easy to see that
\mathfrak{s}^{(\varepsilon)}
is the fundamental solution of
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}={\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
. Thus (
13
) follows from the first line in (
12
) by Duhamel’s principle.
∎

In the remainder of this section we show that
(\xi^{N},\beta^{N})
is a tight sequence and its limit can be characterized in terms of the unique solution to (
12
).
A useful tool here will be a coupling of
\xi^{N}
of (

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{N}_{t}=\xi^{N}_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times[2d]\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,t]}1_{[0,Nr_{i}(x)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s-}(x)]}(\theta)(\delta_{x+\varepsilon k_{i}}-\delta_{x})\Pi(dx,di,d\theta,ds)

(14)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,t]}1_{[0,N\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s-}(x)]}(\theta)\delta_{x}\,\Gamma(dx,d\theta,ds).

This is the normalized configuration measure for mutually independent branching random walks. It is a standard fact that
\bar{\xi}^{N}
dominates
\xi^{N}
, but for completeness, we provide a proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

One has

\xi^{N}_{t}(x)\leq\bar{\xi}^{N}_{t}(x)
for all
t
and
x
a.s.

Proof.
Let

\tau=\inf\{t:\bar{\xi}^{N}_{t}(x)<\xi^{N}_{t}(x)\text{ for some }x\}.

On an event of full measure,
\Pi
and
\Gamma
are locally finite, and charge at most one point mass to each set of the form
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times[2d]\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\{t\}
or
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\{t\}
, respectively.
Consider the intersection of this event with
\{\tau<\infty\}
. On this event,
\xi^{N}_{t}(x)\leq\bar{\xi}^{N}_{t}(x)
for all
t<\tau
and all
x
, and moreover, there exists
y
such that
\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau}(y)<\xi^{N}_{\tau}(y)
. Note that
\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(y)=\xi^{N}_{\tau-}(y)
must hold.
This can only occur if (the cases below correspond to the first three terms in (
7
); the fourth term does not have a counterpart of in (
14
)):

-
\Pi
has, for some
i
and some
x
, an atom in
\{x\}\times\{i\}\times[0,Nr_{i}(x)\xi^{N}_{\tau-}(x)]\times\{\tau\}
, but it does not have one in
\{x\}\times\{i\}\times[0,Nr_{i}(x)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(x)]\times\{\tau\}
. This is impossible because
\xi^{N}_{\tau-}(x)\leq\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(x)
.

-
\Pi
has, for some
i
, an atom in
\{y\}\times\{i\}\times[0,Nr_{i}(y)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(y)]\times\{\tau\}
, but it does not have one in
\{y\}\times\{i\}\times[0,Nr_{i}(y)\xi^{N}_{\tau-}(y)]\times\{\tau\}
. This is impossible because
\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(y)=\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(y)
.

-
\Gamma
has an atom in
\{y\}\times[0,Nr_{i}(y)\xi^{N}_{\tau-}(y)]\times\{\tau\}
, but it does not have one in
\{y\}\times[0,Nr_{i}(y)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(y)]\times\{\tau\}
. This is impossible because
\xi^{N}_{\tau-}(y)\leq\bar{\xi}^{N}_{\tau-}(y)
.

Thus
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau<\infty)=0
and the result follows.
∎

Using this we can provide an upper bound on

as follows.
Let

Lemma 2.4
.

Given

,

\sup_{N}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}[\xi^{N}_{t}({\mathcal{B}}_{r}^{c})]\leq\delta.

Proof.
In view of Lemma
2.3
, it suffices to prove the estimate for
\bar{\xi}^{N}
. Now,
\bar{\xi}^{N}
is nothing but the normalized configuration measure of independent branching RWs starting
\xi^{N}_{0}
.
To be more precise, let
\bar{X}^{N,i}_{t}
,

be a collection of Branching random walks on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
that are mutually independent conditioned on the initial configuration
\sigma\{\bar{X}^{N,i}_{0}\}
, with branching at rate

and generator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
. Let the root particle of
\bar{X}^{N,i}
be labeled as
(i,0)
and the descendants as
(i,j)

. Let
\tau^{N,i,j}

\bar{X}^{N,i,j}
. Let

\tilde{\xi}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j}1_{\{t\geq\tau^{N,i,j}\}}\delta_{\bar{X}^{N,i,j}_{t}}

. Then the process

is equal in law to
\bar{\xi}^{N}
. Hence by the many-to-one lemma
[
18
]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\xi}^{N}_{t}({\mathcal{B}}_{r}^{c})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sum_{j}1_{\{t\geq\tau^{N,i,j}\}}1_{{\mathcal{B}}_{r}^{c}}(\bar{X}^{N,i,j}_{t})\Big]=\frac{e^{t}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}[1_{{\mathcal{B}}_{r}^{c}}(\bar{X}^{N,i,0}_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq e^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\xi^{N}_{0}({\mathcal{B}}_{r/2}^{c})]+\frac{e^{t}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(|\bar{X}^{N,i,0}_{t}-\bar{X}^{N,i,0}_{0}|\geq\frac{r}{2}\Big).

The above expression is bounded, uniformly in

\delta
, provided that

is sufficiently large. Indeed, the first term is bounded by

(for each

. For the second term, the same is true because the number of jumps

∎

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}}g(\theta,t)1_{\{y=x\}}\Pi(dy,d\theta,dt),\hskip 64.00003ptx\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}.

Then by (

\displaystyle u^{N}(x,t)

\displaystyle=u^{N}_{0}(x)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,t]}1_{[0,Nr_{i}(x)u^{N}(x,s-)]}(\theta)\Pi_{x,i}(d\theta,ds)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,t]}1_{[0,Nr_{i}(x-\varepsilon k_{i})u^{N}(x-\varepsilon k_{i},s-)]}(\theta)\Pi_{x-\varepsilon k_{i},i}(d\theta,ds)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,t]}1_{[0,Nu^{N}(x,s-)]}(\theta)\Gamma_{x}(d\theta,ds)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,t]}1_{[0,Nu^{N}(y,s-)]}(\theta)1_{\{x=\argmax^{*}u^{N}(\cdot,s-)\}}\Gamma_{y}(d\theta,ds).

\displaystyle u^{N}(x,t)

\displaystyle=u^{N}_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i}[r_{i}(x-\varepsilon k_{i})u^{N}(x-\varepsilon k_{i},s)-r_{i}(x)u^{N}(x,s)]ds

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\int_{0}^{t}u^{N}(x,s)ds-\sum_{y}\int_{0}^{t}u^{N}(y,s)1_{\{x=\argmax^{*}u^{N}(\cdot,s)\}}ds+M^{N}_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=u^{N}_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}[({\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}u^{N}+u^{N})(x,s)]ds-\beta^{N}(\{x\}\times[0,t])+M^{N}_{x}(t),

Lemma 2.5
.

\xi^{N}
is

-tight in

Proposition 2.6

There exists

In view of the tightness stated in Lemma

Next, recalling the estimate (

) and invoking Skorohod’s representation, we assume without loss of generality that, along the subsequence,

Estimates on modulus of continuity

For

For the most part, we will suppress the dependence on

.
This gives

|\langle w^{\delta},\nabla\cdot(bw^{\delta})\rangle|\leq c|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}.

Also,

\langle w^{\delta},\nabla\cdot\theta_{\delta}\rangle=-\int\nabla w^{\delta}\cdot\theta_{\delta}.

For any
a>0
,

\Big|\int\nabla w^{\delta}\cdot\theta_{\delta}\Big|\leq|\nabla w^{\delta}|_{2}|\theta_{\delta}|_{2}\leq a|\nabla w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}+a^{-1}|\theta_{\delta}|^{2}_{2}.

Combining the above estimates,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-(1-a)\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(s)ds+c\int_{0}^{t}|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}a^{-1}|\theta_{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(s)ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\langle w^{\delta},\gamma^{\delta}_{s}-\beta^{\delta}_{s}\rangle ds.

Because
b
and
w
are uniformly continuous in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T]
, resp., we have that
|\theta_{\delta}|_{\infty}=|(bw)^{\delta}-bw^{\delta}|_{\infty}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
.
Since
u,v\in{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and
b
is bounded, it follows that
|\theta_{\delta}|_{2}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
.
We can therefore find
a=a_{\delta}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
such that one has
\kappa_{\delta}:=a_{\delta}^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}|\theta_{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(s)ds\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
.
This gives, for
t\in[0,T]
,

\frac{1}{2}|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(t)\leq c\int_{0}^{t}|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(s)ds+\kappa_{\delta}+\int_{0}^{t}\langle w^{\delta},\gamma^{\delta}_{s}-\beta^{\delta}_{s}\rangle ds.

Now

\int_{0}^{t}\langle w^{\delta},\gamma^{\delta}_{s}-\beta^{\delta}_{s}\rangle ds=\int_{0}^{t}\langle w^{\delta\delta},\gamma_{s}-\beta_{s}\rangle ds,

where
w^{\delta\delta}
is the second convolution

w^{\delta\delta}:=\eta_{\delta}*\eta_{\delta}*w
.
Since, by the definition of
{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
,
x\mapsto w(x,t)
is continuous in
x
uniformly in
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T]
, there exists
\alpha_{\delta}
such that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|w-w^{\delta\delta}|_{\infty}(t)<\alpha_{\delta}
and
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\alpha_{\delta}=0
. It follows that

\int_{0}^{t}\langle w^{\delta\delta},\gamma_{s}-\beta_{s}\rangle ds\leq t\alpha_{\delta}+\int_{0}^{t}\langle w,\gamma_{s}-\beta_{s}\rangle ds\leq t\alpha_{\delta}\ .

The last inequality follows by the monotonicity of the set valued operator
u\rightarrow\mathbf{B}^{u}
, namely
\langle\beta_{s}-\gamma_{s},u(\cdot,s)-v(\cdot,s)\rangle\geq 0
for a.e.
s
. Indeed,

\langle\beta_{s},u(\cdot,s)\rangle=|u(\cdot,s)|_{\infty}
,

\langle\beta_{s},v(\cdot,s)\rangle\leq|v(\cdot,s)|_{\infty}

for any
\beta\in\mathbf{B}^{u}
and a.e.
s
, by definition (
5
).
Thus for all small
\delta
,

\frac{1}{2}|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(t)\leq c\int_{0}^{t}|w^{\delta}|^{2}_{2}(s)ds+\kappa_{\delta}+t\alpha_{\delta},\qquad t\in[0,T].

Using Gronwall’s lemma, sending
\delta\to 0
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|w^{\delta}(\cdot,t)|_{2}=0.

By the continuity of
w
, this shows that
w=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T]
, and the proof is complete.
∎
