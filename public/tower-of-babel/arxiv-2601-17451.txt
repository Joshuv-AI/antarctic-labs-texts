Isotropic submanifolds of T ​ 𝕊 n T\mathbb{S}^{n} and their focal sets
Nikos Georgiou, Brendan Guilfoyle, Morgan Robson

Abstract
. Families of oriented lines in ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} are studied via their identification with submanifolds of T ​ 𝕊 n T\mathbb{S}^{n} . In particular, families of oriented lines which are orthogonal to submanifolds in ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} are shown to characterise those which are isotropic with respect to the canonical symplectic structure on T ​ 𝕊 n T\mathbb{S}^{n} . Families of lines that are tangent to a k k -dimensional submanifold of ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} are then studied. For such families, isotropy is shown to be equivalent to the generating vector field being geodesic and hypersurface-orthogonal on the submanifold. The focal set in ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} of a family of lines is introduced, extending the classical definition for families normal to hypersurfaces, to general families of lines of arbitrary codimension. A formula is derived that expresses certain sectional curvatures of the focal set in terms of the signed distances between corresponding focal points. We then solve an inverse problem for the focal sets of hypersurfaces and show certain sectional and Ricci curvatures of the focal set are determined by the differences between the hypersurface’s radii of curvature. This generalises a Theorem of Bianchi from 1874 - namely that surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} of constant astigmatism have pseudo-spherical focal sets.

\mathrm{d}\pi(X),\mathcal{K}(X)\perp\xi
), implying the
\subseteq
direction. To show the
\supseteq
direction, suppose
X\in T_{(\xi,\eta)}T\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\langle c,\xi\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(X)+\mathcal{K}(X)=0
. Define the curve
\sigma
in
\mathcal{L}(c)
by

\sigma(t)=\big(\xi(t),c-\langle c,\xi(t)\rangle\xi(t)\big),

such that
\sigma(0)=(\xi,\eta)
and
\xi^{\prime}(0)=\mathrm{d}\pi(X)

\mathrm{d}\pi(\sigma^{\prime}(0))=\mathrm{d}\pi(X)
also. Furthermore since
\sigma(t)

\mathcal{L}(c)

\subseteq

\mathcal{K}(\sigma^{\prime}(0))=-\langle c,\xi\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(\sigma^{\prime}(0))=-\langle c,\xi\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(X)=\mathcal{K}(X).

Hence as their horizontal and vertical components agree,

Corollary 1
.

Let
((\xi,\eta),r)\in\Gamma\times\mathbb{R}
. Then
p=\Psi((\xi,\eta),r)
is a focal point of
\Gamma
if and only if
T_{(\xi,\eta)}\Gamma~\cap~T_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathcal{L}(p)\neq\{0\}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
(\xi,\eta)\in\Gamma
. If
\dim\Gamma>n
, every point along the oriented line
(\xi,\eta)
is focal, or if
\dim\Gamma\leq n
then there are at most
n
focal points along
(\xi,\eta)
.

Proof.

Fix
(\xi,\eta)\in\Gamma
and let
c_{r}=\Psi((\xi,\eta),r)
, with
r
a real variable. By Lemma
2
and since
\mathrm{d}\pi|_{H\mathbb{S}^{n}}:H\mathbb{S}^{n}\to T\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\mathcal{K}|_{V\mathbb{S}^{n}}:V\mathbb{S}^{n}\to T\mathbb{S}^{n}
are linear isomorphisms, bases of
H_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathbb{S}^{n},V_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
T_{\xi}\mathbb{S}^{n}
can be chosen as to make the identifications

\displaystyle H_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathbb{S}^{n}\oplus V_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathbb{S}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle T_{(\xi,\eta)}\Gamma=\ker A,

\displaystyle T_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathcal{L}(c_{r})=\mbox{Im }G(r),

where
A
and
G(r)=[I_{n}|-rI_{n}]^{T}
are respectively
(2n-\dim\Gamma)\times 2n
and
2n\times n
matrices with
I_{n}
the identity matrix.
AG(r)
is thus a
(2n-\dim\Gamma)\times n
matrix and

T_{(\xi,\eta)}\Gamma\cap T_{(\xi,\eta)}\mathcal{L}(c_{r})\neq\{0\}\iff\ker AG(r)\neq\{0\}\iff\mbox{rank }AG(r)<n.

If
\dim\Gamma>n
, then
\mbox{rank }AG(r)<n
due to the size of
AG(r)
, so the line
{(\xi,\eta)}
possesses a focal point (namely
c_{r}
) for any value of
r
. Conversely if
\dim\Gamma\leq n
then

\mbox{rank }AG(r)<n\iff\text{all }n\times n\text{ minors of }AG(r)\text{ vanish.}

Each minor is a polynomial in
r
of at most degree
n
so has at most
n
real roots.
∎

Remark 1
.

A focal point exists on a given line iff

\binom{2n-{\dim\Gamma}}{n}

polynomials (the number of minors in the above proof) have a common solution. This number is decreasing in
\dim\Gamma
and for
\dim\Gamma=n
results in a single polynomial equation for
r
. Hence focal points become less common the smaller
\dim\Gamma
is relative to
n
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq T\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be immersed submanifolds.
\Sigma
is called an
orthogonal submanifold of

\Gamma
if
\dim\Sigma>0
and there exists a smooth unit normal section
\nu\in\Gamma(N\Sigma)
such that
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)\subseteq\Gamma
, where
\Phi_{\nu}
is given in (

).

The next proposition shows that when
\Gamma
is isotropic, dimension
\dim\Gamma
orthogonal submanifolds of
\Gamma
exist and pass through every point of
\Psi(\Gamma\times\mathbb{R})
which is not focal.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq T\mathbb{S}^{n}
be isotropic and
\dim\Gamma>0
. Suppose that
p\in\Psi(\Gamma\times\mathbb{R})
is not a focal point. Then
p
is contained in an orthogonal submanifold of
\Gamma
of dimension
\dim\Gamma
which takes the form

\Sigma_{0}:=\Psi\Big(\Big\{((\xi,\eta),F(\xi,\eta)):(\xi,\eta)\in W\Big\}\Big),

with
W\subset\Gamma
open and
F:W\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function such that
\theta=\mathrm{d}F
and
F(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})=r_{0}
where
p=\Psi((\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),r_{0})
and
\theta
is the Liouville 1-form (
2.1
).

Proof.

Let
p=\Psi((\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),r_{0})
. Since
\Gamma
is isotropic,
\theta|_{\Gamma}
is locally exact, so there exists a neighbourhood
W\subseteq\Gamma
of
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
and a smooth function
F:W\to\mathbb{R}
with
\theta=\mathrm{d}F
. Without loss of generality assume
F(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})=r_{0}
. Define
\Sigma_{0}=\Psi(\text{graph}(F))
where

\displaystyle\mbox{graph}(F)=\Big\{\big((\xi,\eta),F(\xi,\eta)\big):(\xi,\eta)\in W\Big\}.

As
p
is not focal,
\Psi
is a local diffeomorphism near
((\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),F(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))
hence
\Psi|_{\text{graph}(F)}
is a diffeomorphism onto
S_{0}
(after possibly shrinking
W
). Thus
\Sigma_{0}
is immersed and
\dim\Sigma_{0}=\dim\Gamma
. To show
\Sigma_{0}
satisfies Definition
2
, if
q\in\Sigma_{0}
, there exists a unique
(\xi_{q},\eta_{q})
such that
q=\Psi(\xi_{q},\eta_{q},F(\xi_{q},\eta_{q}))
, and the map
q\mapsto\xi_{q}
is smooth. Define the unit section
\nu:q\mapsto\xi_{q}
. It follows that
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma_{0})=W\subseteq\Gamma
. Also
\xi_{q}
is normal to
T_{q}\Sigma_{0}
;

\displaystyle T_{q}\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\Psi(T_{(\xi_{q},\eta_{q},F(\xi_{q},\eta_{q}))}\text{graph}(F))

\displaystyle=\{F(\xi_{q},\eta_{q})\mathrm{d}\pi(X)+\mathcal{K}(X)+(\mathrm{d}F(X)-\theta(X))\xi_{q}:X\in T_{(\xi_{q},\eta_{q})}\Gamma\}

\displaystyle=\{F(\xi_{q},\eta_{q})\mathrm{d}\pi(X)+\mathcal{K}(X):X\in T_{(\xi_{q},\eta_{q})}\Gamma\},

the second line following by Lemma

. Hence
\xi_{q}\in N_{q}\Sigma_{0}
and thus
\nu\in\Gamma(N\Sigma_{0})
.
∎

Corollary 2

If
\Gamma
is assumed simply connected, we may take
W=\Gamma
so that a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
exists to which
\Gamma
is globally orthogonal.

Among orthogonal submanifolds of
\Gamma

\Sigma_{0}
are ‘maximal’ since other orthogonal submanifolds are contained in them - at least locally.

Theorem 1

(Structure Theorem for Orthogonal Submanifolds)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset T\mathbb{S}^{n}
be isotropic and
\mathcal{U}
an orthogonal submanifold of
\Gamma
without focal points. For all

there exists
V_{p}
, an open neighbourhood of
p
in
\mathcal{U}
such that
V_{p}\subset\Sigma_{p}
, where

\Sigma_{p}:=\Psi\Big(\Big\{((\xi,\eta),F(\xi,\eta)):(\xi,\eta)\in W\Big\}\Big),

with
W\subseteq\Gamma
open in
\Gamma
and
F:W\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function such that
\theta|_{W}=\mathrm{d}F
.

Before proving this we first prove a lemma concerning the map (
2.4
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be immersed and
v
a unit section over
S
. For all
X\in T_{p}S
the map
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}:TS\to TT\mathbb{S}^{n}
satisfies

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))=\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))=X-\langle X,v(p)\rangle v(p)-\langle p,v(p)\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),

and
\ker\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}|_{p}\subseteq\mbox{Span}\{v(p)\}
. Furthermore if
v(p)\notin T_{p}S
, then
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}|_{p}
is injective.

Proof.

Let
\alpha(t)
be a curve in
S
such that
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=X
. Then

(\Phi_{v}\circ\alpha)(t)=\big((v\circ\alpha)(t),\alpha(t)-\langle\alpha(t),(v\circ\alpha)(t)\rangle(v\circ\alpha)(t)\big),

is a curve in
T\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that
(\pi\circ\Phi_{v}\circ\alpha)(t)=(v\circ\alpha)(t)
, and
\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))=\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p)
follows after differentiating. The second expression follows from taking the covariant derivative of
\alpha(t)-\langle\alpha(t),(v\circ\alpha)(t)\rangle(v\circ\alpha)(t)
with respect to
D
along the curve
(v\circ\alpha)(t)
in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. If ‘
\top

T_{v(p)}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
onto
T_{v(p)}\mathbb{S}^{n}
;

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{D}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\alpha(t)-\langle\alpha(t),(v\circ\alpha)(t)\rangle(v\circ\alpha)(t)\right)\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\left(\derivative{t}\Big[\alpha(t)-\langle\alpha(t),(v\circ\alpha)(t)\rangle(v\circ\alpha)(t)\Big]\right|_{t=0}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(X-\langle X,v(p)\rangle-\langle p,\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p)\rangle v(p)-\langle p,v(p)\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p)\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=X-\langle X,v(p)\rangle v(p)-\langle p,v(p)\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p).

To prove the last claims, let
X\in\ker\mathrm{d}\Phi|_{p}
. Then
\mathrm{d}\Phi(X)=0
implying
\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))=\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))=0
. This implies
X=\langle X,v(p)\rangle v(p)
from the first part of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\Phi_{v}(S)\subset T\mathbb{S}^{n}
be immersed with
v
a unit section over
S
. For
X,Y\in T_{p}S

(\Phi_{v}^{\ast}\Omega)_{p}(X,Y)=\langle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),Y\rangle-\langle\overline{\nabla}_{Y}v(p),X\rangle.

Proof.

If

and

then equation (

) gives

\displaystyle(\Phi_{v}^{\ast}\Omega)(X,Y)=g\Big(\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X)),\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(Y))\Big)-g\Big(\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(Y)),\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X))\big).

Using Lemma

gives

\displaystyle g\Big(\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(X)),\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(Y))\Big)

\displaystyle=g\Big(\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),Y-\langle v(p),Y\rangle v(p)-\langle v(p),p\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{Y}v(p)\Big)

\displaystyle=\langle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),Y\rangle-\langle v(p),p\rangle\langle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),\overline{\nabla}_{Y}v(p)\rangle.

A similar calculation with
X
and

\displaystyle(\Phi_{v}^{\ast}\Omega)_{p}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\langle\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p),Y\rangle-\langle\overline{\nabla}_{Y}v(p),X\rangle.

For the rest of this section we use the notation
(\Sigma,\nu)
to denote a pair such that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is immersed and
\nu\in\Gamma(N\Sigma)
is unit.

Proposition 4
.

Assume
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)\in T\mathbb{S}^{n}
is immersed and
\dim\Sigma>0
. Then
\Sigma
is an orthogonal submanifold of
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)
and
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)
is isotropic.

Proof.

The first claim holds by definition. For the second, let

. Then

\displaystyle(\Phi_{\nu}^{\ast}\Omega)_{p}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\langle\overline{\nabla}_{X}\nu(p),Y\rangle-\langle\overline{\nabla}_{Y}\nu(p),X\rangle

(By Lemma

)

\displaystyle=\langle[X,Y],\nu(p)\rangle

(Since
\overline{\nabla}
is torsion free and
\nu(p)\perp T_{p}\Sigma

\displaystyle=0.

(Since
\nu(p)\perp T_{p}\Sigma

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)\subset T\mathbb{S}^{n}
is immersed. Let

and let

lie on the line

. Then,

is a focal point of

satisfying the eigenvalue equation

In particular, the eigenvalue is the distance along

from

to the focal set of

. If

is a hypersurface, then

5.

Now we consider lines generated by the pair
(S,v)
when

is a unit tangent vector field over
S
, and again

is immersed. In this case we prove that

is a part of the focal set of
\Phi_{v}(S)
and provide some characterisations of
\Phi_{v}(S)
being isotropic. First a characterisation of when
\Phi_{v}
is an immersion is given.

Lemma 5
.

The map
\Phi_{v}
is an immersion if and only if
v\in\Gamma(TS)
is nowhere geodesic with respect to the Euclidean connection
\overline{\nabla}
(i.e.
\overline{\nabla}_{v}v(p)\neq 0
for all

).

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive, namely for each
p\in S

\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}|_{p}\text{ has non trivial kernel }\iff\overline{\nabla}_{v}v(p)=0.

The backwards implication follows by letting
X=v(p)
in Lemma
3
. To prove the forward implication let
X\in\mbox{Ker }\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}|_{p}
, with
X\neq 0
. Lemma
3
implies
\overline{\nabla}_{X}v(p)=0
and
X=\lambda v(p)
with
\lambda\neq 0
. Since
\overline{\nabla}
is tensorial we have
\overline{\nabla}_{v}v(p)=0
.
∎

Hence
\Phi_{v}
fails to be an immersion precisely when the integral curves of

, thought of as curves in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, have zero curvature.

Proposition 6
.

Suppose
\Phi_{v}
is an immersion and
\Phi_{v}(S)\subset T\mathbb{S}^{n}
is immersed. The focal set of
\Phi_{v}(S)
contains

.

Proof.

Given any
p\in S
, note that
p=\Psi(\Phi_{v}(p),\langle p,v(p)\rangle)\in\Psi(\Phi_{v}(S)\times\mathbb{R})
. Let
X=\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v}(v(p))\in T_{\Phi_{v}(p)}\Phi_{v}(S)
, which is non zero by Lemma

. One quickly shows

\displaystyle\langle p,v(p)\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi(X)+\mathcal{K}(X)

\displaystyle=0,

by an application of Lemma

. Applying Proposition

completes the proof.
∎

Main Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\Phi_{v}
is an immersion and
\Phi_{v}(S)\subset T\mathbb{S}^{n}
is immersed. The following are equivalent.

(a)

\Phi_{v}(S)
is isotropic.

(b)

Each
p\in S
belongs to an open set
U\subseteq S
on which
v|_{U}=\mbox{grad~}f
for some smooth function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
. If
S
is simply connected we may take
U=S
.

(c)

The distribution
v^{\bot}
is integrable and
v
is a geodesic vector field on
S

Proof.

(a)\!\implies\!(b)
: Define the
1
-form
A:p\mapsto\langle~\cdot~,v(p)\rangle\Big|_{T_{p}S}~
on
S
. Since
\overline{\nabla}
is the Levi-Civita connection one quickly shows that for
X,Y\in\Gamma(TS)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}A(X,Y)

\displaystyle=X(A(Y))-Y(A(X))-A([X,Y])

\displaystyle=\langle X,\overline{\nabla}_{Y}v\rangle-\langle Y,\overline{\nabla}_{X}v\rangle

(Since
\overline{\nabla}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
’s Levi-Civita connection.)

\displaystyle=\langle Y,\nabla_{X}v\rangle-\langle X,\nabla_{Y}v\rangle

(Since
\nabla
is
\overline{\nabla}
restricted to
S
)

\displaystyle=-\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(X,Y).

(By Lemma
4
)

Thus, since
\Phi
is an immersion

\Omega|_{\Phi_{v}(S)}=0\implies\Phi^{\ast}\Omega=0\implies\mathrm{d}A=0.

The Poincaré Lemma now implies for each
p\in S
there is an open neighbourhood
U
and a smooth function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
such that with
A|_{U}=\mathrm{d}f
, i.e.
v|_{U}=\mbox{grad~}f
.

(b)\!\implies\!(c)
: First we show
v
is a geodesic vector field on
S
. Let
p\in S
and
v|_{U}=\mbox{grad~}f
with
U
and
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
as above. Then for any
X\in T_{p}S
,

\langle\nabla_{v}v(p),X\rangle=\mbox{Hess}(f)_{p}(v(p),X)=\mbox{Hess}(f)_{p}(X,v(p))=\langle\nabla_{X}v(p),v(p)\rangle=0,

as
v
is unit. Since
p
and
X
were arbitrary,
\nabla_{v}v=0
. To show

v^{\bot}:p\mapsto\{X\in T_{p}S~:~\langle X,v(p)\rangle=0\},

is integrable, let
c:=f(p)
. Since
||\mbox{grad~}f||=||v||=1
, it follows that
\mbox{rank~}\mathrm{d}f=1
and
c
is a regular value of
f
. Hence
f^{-1}(c)
is an embedded submanifold of
U
of dimension
\dim S-1
containing
p
. It is then easily checked that
T_{p}f^{-1}(c)=v^{\bot}(p)
.

(c)\!\implies\!(a)
: Due to the properties of
v
, both
v^{\perp}
and
\mbox{Span~}v
are regular, smooth subbundles of
TS
and we have the bundle splitting

TS=v^{\perp}\oplus\mbox{Span~}v.

Now take any
X,Y\in\Gamma(TS)
and decompose them via the above:

\displaystyle X=\widetilde{X}+\alpha v

and

\displaystyle Y=\widetilde{Y}+\beta v,

with
\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}\in\Gamma(v^{\perp})
and
\alpha,\beta
smooth functions on
S
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(X,Y)=\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})+\alpha\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(v,\widetilde{Y})+\beta\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(\widetilde{X},v).

Applying Lemma
4
and the conditions that
v
is geodesic and unit gives

\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(v,\widetilde{Y})=\langle\nabla_{v}v,\widetilde{Y}\rangle+\langle v,\nabla_{\widetilde{Y}}v\rangle=0,

and similarly
\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(\widetilde{X},v)=0
. Also since
v^{\perp}
is involutive,
[\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}]\in v^{\perp}
, hence

\displaystyle\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\widetilde{X}}v,\widetilde{Y}\rangle-\langle\nabla_{\widetilde{Y}}v,\widetilde{X}\rangle

(Lemma
4
)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{X}(\langle v,\widetilde{Y}\rangle)-\widetilde{Y}(\langle v,\widetilde{X}\rangle)-\langle v,[\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}]\rangle

(Since
\nabla
is Levi-Civita)

\displaystyle=0.

(Since
\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y},[\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}]\in v^{\perp}
)

It follows that
\Phi^{\ast}\Omega(X,Y)=0
. Since
X
and
Y
were arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

Remark 3
.

The function

, pp.178).

.

.

6.
The Curvatures of Focal Sheets

We now explore the geometry of focal sets when they possess a
C^{2}
-smooth manifold structure and their associated family of lines is isotropic. To this end, we consider two immersed submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
equipped with unit tangent vector fields, namely the pairs
(S_{i},v_{i})
for
i=1,2
, which satisfy the conditions

(1)

\Phi_{v_{1}}(S_{1})=\Phi_{v_{2}}(S_{2})
,

(2)

\Phi_{v_{i}}:S_{i}\to\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
is a diffeomorphism,

(3)

\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
is isotropic.

Condition (
1
) ensures that both pairs
(S_{i},v_{i})
generate the same family of oriented lines, namely
\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
, for either
i=1
or
2
. Since the vector field
v_{i}
is tangent to
S_{i}
, Proposition
6
shows both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are contained in the focal set of
\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
. Condition (
2
) ensures the submanifolds
S_{i}
can be related to one another through their common image under
\Phi_{v_{i}}
. Indeed
S_{1}
is diffeomorphic to
S_{2}
under the map
\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}}:S_{1}\to S_{2}
, and if
p_{i}\in S_{i}
are related by
\Phi_{v_{1}}(p_{1})=\Phi_{v_{2}}(p_{2})
then

(6.1)

\displaystyle v_{1}(p_{1})=v_{2}(p_{2}),

and

\displaystyle p_{1}-\langle p_{1},v_{1}(p_{1})\rangle=p_{2}-\langle p_{2},v_{2}(p_{2})\rangle,

by definition of
\Phi_{v_{i}}
. Therefore we may write

p_{1}-p_{2}=\langle p_{1}-p_{2},v_{1}(p_{1})\rangle v_{1}(p_{1}),

with
\langle p_{1}-p_{2},v_{1}(p_{1})\rangle
being the signed distance in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
between
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
along the line
v_{1}(p_{1})
. Motivated by this we define the signed distance function

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{12}:

\displaystyle S_{1}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle p\longmapsto\left\langle p-(\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}})(p),v_{1}(p)\right\rangle.

from
S_{1}
to
S_{2}
. It follows that
(\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}})(p)=p-\mu_{12}(p)v(p)
, so
\mu_{12}(p)\neq 0
iff
p
and
(\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}})(p)
are distinct focal points on the line
\Phi_{v_{1}}(p)
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\nabla
be the connection on
S_{1}
induced by
\overline{\nabla}
and define

\displaystyle E_{2}:=V_{2}-\langle V_{2},v_{1}\rangle v_{1}

where

\displaystyle V_{2}:=\mathrm{d}(\Phi^{-1}_{v_{1}}\circ\Phi_{v_{2}})(v_{2}).

Suppose
\mu_{12}
is nowhere
0
. Then
E_{2}
is non-zero and satisfies

(6.3)

\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1}=\mu_{12}^{-1}E_{2}.

Furthermore, if
p\in\mathcal{M}
with
\mathcal{M}
an integrable manifold of
v_{1}^{\perp}
, then
E_{2}(p)\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
is a principal direction of
\mathcal{M}
with principal curvature
1/\mu_{12}(p)
.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}\in S_{i}
satisfy
\Phi_{v_{1}}(p_{1})=\Phi_{v_{2}}(p_{2})
. Also
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v_{1}}|_{p_{1}}(V_{2})=\mathrm{d}\Phi_{v_{2}}|_{p_{2}}(v_{2})
holds by definition of
V_{2}
. After projecting to
T\mathbb{S}^{n}
via
\mathrm{d}\pi
and
\mathcal{K}
, Lemma
3
gives

(6.4)

\hskip-15.00002pt\left\{\begin{aligned} &\overline{\nabla}_{V_{2}}v_{1}(p_{1})=\overline{\nabla}_{v_{2}}v_{2}(p_{2})\\
&V_{2}(p_{1}\!)\!-\!\langle V_{2}(p_{1}\!),\!v_{1}\!(p_{1}\!)\rangle v_{1}\!(p_{1}\!)\!-\!\langle p_{1},\!v_{1}\!(p_{1}\!)\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{V_{2}}v_{1}\!(p_{1}\!)\!=\!-\langle p_{2},\!v_{2}(p_{2}\!)\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{v_{2}}v_{2}(p_{2}\!).\end{aligned}\right.

Furthermore by combining the these equations with equations (
6.1
), one derives that

V_{2}(p_{1})=\langle V_{2}(p_{1}),v_{1}(p_{1})\rangle v_{1}(p_{1})+\langle p_{1}-p_{2},v_{1}(p_{1})\rangle\overline{\nabla}_{V_{2}}v_{1}(p_{1}),

which implies that
\overline{\nabla}_{V_{2}}v_{1}(p_{1})\in T_{p_{1}}S_{1}
and thus
\overline{\nabla}_{V_{2}}v_{1}(p_{1})=\nabla_{V_{2}}v_{1}(p_{1})
. The above decomposes
V_{2}
into its components orthogonal and parallel to
v_{1}
;

(6.5)

V_{2}=\langle V_{2},v_{1}\rangle v_{1}+\mu_{12}\nabla_{V_{2}}v_{1}.

Then,

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=V_{2}-\langle V_{2},v_{1}\rangle v_{1}

(By definition of
E_{2}
)

\displaystyle=\mu_{12}\nabla_{V_{2}}v_{1}

(Identity (
6.5
))

\displaystyle=\mu_{12}(\langle V_{2},v_{1}\rangle\nabla_{v_{1}}v_{1}+\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1})

(By definition of
E_{2}
)

\displaystyle=\mu_{12}\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1}.

(Condition (
3
) and Main Theorem
1
)

As
\mu_{12}\neq 0
, equation (
6.3
) follows. Also, by the above calculation and equations (
6.4
)

E_{2}=\mu_{12}\nabla_{V_{2}}v_{1}=\mu_{12}\overline{\nabla}_{v_{2}}v_{2}.

Since
\Phi_{v_{2}}
is a diffeomorphism,
\overline{\nabla}_{v_{2}}v_{2}\neq 0
by Lemma
5
, thus
E_{2}\neq 0
. Finally, if
\mathcal{M}
is an integral manifold of
v_{1}^{\bot}
(which exists by Main Theorem
1
), then
\mathcal{M}
is a hypersurface in
S_{1}
with unit normal
v_{1}|_{\mathcal{M}}
and shape operator
X\mapsto\nabla_{X}v_{1}
. If
p\in\mathcal{M}
, then
E_{2}(p)\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
as
E_{2}\perp v_{1}
. Evaluating equation (
6.3
) at
p
completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\mu_{12}
is nowhere
0
. The sectional curvature
K
of
S_{1}
satisfies

K(V_{2},v_{1})=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{12}(v_{1})-1}{\mu_{12}^{2}}.

Proof.

Note that since
v_{1}\wedge V_{2}
and
v_{1}\wedge E_{2}
define the same plane, we have

K(V_{2},v_{1})=K(E_{2},v_{1}).

Furthermore, after rescaling if necessary we may assume
E_{2}
is unit - in which case equation (
6.3
) continues to hold. It follows that
\{E_{2},v_{1}\}
are orthonormal and

\displaystyle K(E_{2},v_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle R(E_{2},v_{1})v_{1},E_{2}\rangle

(Where
R(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot))
is the Riemann tensor)

\displaystyle=-\Big(\langle\nabla_{v_{1}}\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1},E_{2}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{[E_{2},v_{1}]}v_{1},E_{2}\rangle\Big).

(Since
\nabla_{v_{1}}v_{1}=0
by Main Theorem
1
)

Calculating these two terms gives

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{v_{1}}\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1},E_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{v_{1}}(\mu_{12}^{-1}E_{2}),E_{2}\rangle

(By Equation
6.3
)

\displaystyle=\langle-\mu_{12}^{-2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{12}(v_{1})E_{2}+\mu_{12}^{-1}\nabla_{v_{1}}E_{2},E_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\mu_{12}^{-2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{12}(v_{1})+\mu_{12}^{-1}\langle\nabla_{v_{1}}E_{2},E_{2}\rangle.

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{[E_{2},v_{1}]}v_{1},E_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1},[E_{2},v_{1}]\rangle

(By Lemma
4
, since
\Omega|_{\Phi_{v_{1}}(S_{1})}=0
)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1},\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1}\rangle-\langle\nabla_{E_{2}}v_{1},\nabla_{v_{1}}E_{2}\rangle

(
\nabla
is torsion free)

\displaystyle=\mu_{12}^{-2}-\mu_{12}^{-1}\langle E_{2},\nabla_{v_{1}}E_{2}\rangle.

(By Equation (
6.3
))

Insertion of the above into the formula for
K(E_{2},v_{1})
yields

\displaystyle K(E_{2},v_{1})=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{12}(v_{1})-1}{\mu_{12}^{2}}.

∎

If more of the focal set is known, further control of the geometry of
S_{1}
is uncovered.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(S_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(S_{k},v_{k})
satisfy pairwise conditions
(\ref{cond: Phi1=Phi2})
-
(\ref{cond: Phi isotropic})
, with
\dim S_{i}=k
. Define
\mu_{ij}:S_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
, the signed distance from
S_{i}
to
S_{j}
by

(6.6)

\mu_{ij}(q)=\left\langle q-(\Phi_{v_{j}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{i}})(q),v_{i}(q)\right\rangle,\qquad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.

Assume
\mu_{ij}
is nowhere
0
for
i\neq j
and define
E_{ij},V_{ij}\in\Gamma(TS_{i})
by

\displaystyle E_{ij}:=V_{ij}-\langle V_{ij},v_{i}\rangle v_{i}

where

\displaystyle V_{ij}:=\mathrm{d}(\Phi^{-1}_{v_{i}}\circ\Phi_{v_{j}})(v_{j}).

a)

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset S_{1}
be an integrable manifold of
v_{1}^{\perp}
and
p\in\mathcal{M}
. Then
\{E_{1j}(p)\}^{k}_{j=2}
are principal directions of
\mathcal{M}
at
p
with distinct principal values
\{1/\mu_{1j}(p)\}_{j=2}^{k}
.

b)

The set of vector fields
\{E_{1j}\}^{k}_{j=2}
are an orthogonal frame of
v_{1}^{\perp}
and the Ricci curvature tensor of
S_{1}
satisfies

\mbox{Ric}(v_{1},v_{1})=\sum_{j=2}^{k}K(v_{1},E_{1j})=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1j}(v_{1})-1}{(\mu_{1j})^{2}}.

Proof.

(a): Let
q\in S_{1}
, first we show that
\mu_{1j}(q)\neq\mu_{1i}(q)
when
i\neq j
Define
q_{j}=\Phi_{v_{j}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}}(q)\in S_{j}
and
q_{i}=\Phi_{v_{i}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}}(q)\in S_{i}
so that
\Phi_{v_{i}}(q_{i})=\Phi_{v_{j}}(q_{j})=\Phi_{v}(q)
. Then
v_{1}(q)=v_{j}(q_{j})=v_{i}(q_{i})
and from the definitions of
\mu_{1j},\mu_{1i}
and
\mu_{ij}
one finds

\mu_{1j}(q)-\mu_{1i}(q)=\mu_{ij}(q_{i}).

Hence
\mu_{1j}(q)\neq\mu_{1i}(q)
, otherwise

, contradicting

. Let
\mathcal{M}
and

be as above. By definition

. Applying Proposition
7
to the pair
(S_{1},v_{1}),(S_{j},v_{j})
in place of

proves (a).

(b) The orthogonality follows since

are (pointwise) the principal directions of

.
∎

7.
The Hypersurface case

Given a hypersurface
\Sigma
, the results from the previous section are now applied to investigate the curvature of its focal sheets. A classical result in this direction is

Theorem A

(Bianchi, 1874)
.

[
9
]

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
C^{3}
-smooth, oriented, ridge free and strictly convex surface. Suppose the radii of curvature of
\Sigma
satisfy
r_{2}-r_{1}\equiv s
for
s\neq 0
a constant. Then the two focal sheets of
\Sigma
have Gaussian curvature
-1/s^{2}
.

Some discussion of the above terminology is warranted. In general focal sheets are singular, see
[
11
]
for recent work on this. At points where the radii of curvature
r_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
have multiplicity
\geq 1
, the functions
r_{i}
are not in general differentiable and the focal sheets are singular.

Definition 3
.

Given a hypersurface
\Sigma
the functions

\displaystyle s_{ij}:~

\displaystyle\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle p\to r_{i}(p)-r_{j}(p),

with
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
are called the
astigmatisms
of
\Sigma
.

The multiplicities of all radii of curvature at a point
p\in\Sigma
are
1
iff
s_{ij}(p)\neq 0
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
i\neq j
. Hence the requirement
s\neq 0
in Bianchi’s theorem.

Definition 4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a hypersurface with a unit normal field
\nu
and
r_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
a radii of curvature of
\Sigma
. A point
q\in\Sigma
is called a
ridge point associated to

r_{i}

[
28
]
if

(7.1)

\displaystyle r_{i}(q)\overline{\nabla}_{X}\nu=X

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{i}(X)=0,

for some non-zero
X\in T_{q}\Sigma
.
\Sigma
is called
ridge free
if it has no ridge points.

The ridge free condition in Bianchi’s thoerem ensures the maps
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
are immersions, or in other words, that the surface is locally diffeomorphic to its focal sheets.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a
C^{3}
-smooth hypersurface. Let
\nu
be a unit normal vector field and
r_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
a radii of curvature with multiplicity
1
. If
X\in T_{q}\Sigma
then

(7.2)

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}(X)=X-\mathrm{d}r_{i}(X)\nu(q)-r_{i}(q)\overline{\nabla}_{X}\nu(q).

In particular,
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
is an immersion at
q
iff
q
is not a ridge point.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is
C^{3}
-smooth and has no umbilic points, the radii of curvature function
r_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable. The expression for
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}
now easily follows from equation (
1.2
). It is also easy to show, due to the orthogonality of
\nu(q)
with
X
and
\overline{\nabla}_{X}\nu(q)
, that
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}|_{q}
has non-zero kernel iff
q
is a ridge point.
∎

Even with the ridge free condition, a focal sheet may self intersect. Therefore injectivity conditions on the maps
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
are natural to ensure a manifold structure. Since the curvature properties of focal sets are discussed, both conditions are assumed - i.e. that the maps
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
are diffeomorphisms onto their images.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a
C^{3}
-smooth, strictly convex hypersurface with unit normal
\nu
whose radii of curvature
(r_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
have multiplicity
1
. Let
S_{i}:=\mathcal{Y}_{i}(\Sigma)
be a focal sheet with
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
a diffeomorphism. Define
X_{i}\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
by

\displaystyle r_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{X_{i}}\nu=X_{i}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{i}(X_{i})=1,

so
X_{i}
is a principal vector field. Then
v_{i}:=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}(X_{i})
is a unit and geodesic vector field over
S_{i}
and
v_{i}^{\perp}
is integrable. Also
\Phi_{v_{i}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\Phi_{\nu}
,
v_{i}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\nu
and
\Phi_{v_{i}}
is an immersion.

Proof.

We first note
X_{i}
is well defined since the radii of curvature have multiplicity
1
and
\Sigma
is ridge free. Also
v_{i}
is well defined.
The above claim is simply part
(c)
of Theorem
1
applied to
(S_{i},v_{i})
. We thus only need to show that part
(a)
holds (i.e.
\Phi_{v_{i}}(S)
is isotropic), and that
v_{i}
satisfies Theorem
1
’s prerequisites, that is,
v_{i}
is a unit vector field and
\Phi_{v_{i}}
is an immersion. One checks using Lemma
6
that if
q\in\Sigma
,

(v_{i}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i})(q)=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}{\big|_{q}}(X_{i})=-X_{i}(q)+\mathrm{d}r_{i}|_{q}(X_{i})\nu(q)+r_{i}(q)\overline{\nabla}_{X_{i}}\nu(q)=\nu(q),

by definition of
X_{i}
. Hence
v_{i}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\nu
and so
v_{i}
is unit. If
p\in S_{i}
and
q\in\Sigma
satisfy
p=\mathcal{Y}_{i}(q)
, then

\displaystyle\Phi_{v_{i}}(p)

\displaystyle=(v_{i}(p),p-\langle p,v_{i}(p)\rangle v_{i}(p)),

(By definition of
\Phi_{v_{i}}
)

\displaystyle=(\nu(q),\mathcal{Y}_{i}(q)-\langle\mathcal{Y}_{i}(q),\nu(q)\rangle\nu(q)),

(By definition of
p
and
v_{i}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\nu
)

\displaystyle=(\nu(q),q-\langle q,\nu(q)\rangle),

(By definition of
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\nu}(q).

Hence
\Phi_{v_{i}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\Phi_{\nu}
and so
\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})=\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)
. However,
\Sigma
is an orthogonal submanifold of
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)
, thus
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)
, and hence
\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
, is isotropic. Also by the above
\Phi_{v_{i}}=\Phi_{\nu}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}^{-1}
. Thus
\Phi_{v_{i}}
is an immersion as
\Phi_{\nu}
is (by Lemma
3
).
∎

Together, Theorem
2
and Proposition
8
allow us to relate together the geometry of different focal sheets of a fixed hypersurface as follows.

Main Theorem 2
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a
C^{3}
-smooth, strictly convex hypersurface with unit normal field
\nu
whose radii of curvature
(r_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
have multiplicity
1
. For
i=1,2
let
S_{i}:=\mathcal{Y}_{i}(\Sigma)
be focal sheets with
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
a diffeomorphism. If
X_{i}\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
satisfy

\displaystyle r_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{X_{i}}\nu=X_{i}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{i}(X_{i})=1,

The sectional curvature of
S_{1}
, denoted
K_{1}
, satisfies

(7.3)

(\mathcal{Y}_{1}^{\ast}K_{1})\big(X_{1},X_{2})=\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{12}(X_{1})-1}{(s_{12})^{2}}.

Proof.

Applying Proposition
8
to each focal set
S_{i}:=\mathcal{Y}(\Sigma)
for
i=1,2
gives a pair
(S_{i},v_{i})
where
v_{i}\in\Gamma(TS_{i})
is a unit vector field defined by
v_{i}:=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}(X_{i})
and satisfying
\Phi_{v_{i}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\Phi_{\nu}
,
v_{i}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\nu
and with each
\Phi_{v_{i}}
an immersion. We first show that this pair satisfy the conditions introduced at the start of Section
6
;

(1)

\Phi_{v_{1}}(S_{1})=\Phi_{v_{2}}(S_{2})
.

(2)

\Phi_{v_{i}}:S_{i}\to\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
is a diffeomorphism.

(3)

\Phi_{v_{i}}(S_{i})
is isotropic.

Begin by noting that since
\Phi_{v_{i}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\Phi_{\nu}
we have
\Phi_{v_{1}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1}=\Phi_{v_{2}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{2}
. Hence
\Phi_{v_{1}}(S_{1})=\Phi_{v_{2}}(S_{2})
, which is (
1
). For (
2
) we remark that as curvature is a local notion, to derive equation (
7.3
) it suffices to derive

K_{1}\big(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{1}(X_{1}),\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{1}(X_{2}))=\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{12}(X_{1})-1}{(s_{12})^{2}},

on some open neighbourhood about any point of
S_{1}
. Hence by working locally we may assume that
\Phi_{v_{1}}
is a diffeomorphism once its domain is suitably restricted. Since
\Phi_{v_{2}}=\Phi_{v_{1}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{-1}
, the same domain can be pulled back under
\mathcal{Y}_{1}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{-1}
on which
\Phi_{v_{2}}
is a diffeomorphism, thus (
2
). Condition (
3
) follows from (
1
) since
\Phi_{\nu}(\Sigma)
is isotropic. To calculate the signed distance between
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
(Cf. equation (
6.2
)) let
p\in S_{1}
and
q\in\Sigma
be related by
p=\mathcal{Y}_{1}(q)
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{12}(p)

\displaystyle:=\langle p-(\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}})(p),v_{1}(p)\rangle,

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{Y}_{1}(q)-(\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1})(q),\nu(q)\rangle,

(Using
v_{1}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1}=\nu
)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{Y}_{1}(q)-\mathcal{Y}_{2}(q),\nu(q)\rangle,

(Using
\Phi_{v_{2}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{1}}=\mathcal{Y}_{2}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1}^{-1}
)

\displaystyle=r_{1}(q)-r_{2}(q)

(Using the definition of
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
)

\displaystyle=-s_{12}(q).

Hence
\mu_{12}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1}=-s_{12}
. Since
\Sigma
has radii of curvatures which have multiplicity
1
,
s_{12}
, and thus
\mu_{12}
, are nowhere
0
. Hence we apply Theorem
2
to find

K(V_{2},v_{1})=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{12}(v_{1})-1}{\mu^{2}},

where
V_{2}=\mathrm{d}(\Phi^{-1}_{v_{1}}\circ\Phi_{v_{2}})(v_{2})
. However,

v_{2}=\mathrm{d}(\Phi^{-1}_{v_{1}}\circ\Phi_{v_{2}})(v_{2})=\mathrm{d}(\mathcal{Y}_{1}\circ\mathcal{Y}^{-1}_{2})(v_{2})=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{1}(X_{2}),

by using the definition of
v_{2}
and the derived relationships between
\Phi_{v_{i}}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
. Using the above together with
v_{1}=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{1}(X_{1})
and
\mu_{12}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{1}=-s_{12}
, lets one write the above sectional curvature formula as equation (
7.3
).
∎

Main Theorem 3
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a
C^{3}
-smooth, strictly convex hypersurface with unit normal field
\nu
whose radii of curvature
(r_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
have multiplicity
1
. Let
S_{j}:=\mathcal{Y}_{j}(\Sigma)
be focal sheets with
\mathcal{Y}_{j}
a diffeomorphism for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
X_{j}\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
satisfy

\displaystyle r_{j}\overline{\nabla}_{X_{j}}\nu=X_{j}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{j}(X_{j})=1,

the Ricci curvature of
S_{i}
(
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
) satisfies

(\mathcal{Y}_{i}^{\ast}\mathrm{Ric})(X_{i},X_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{X_{i}(s_{ij})-1}{(s_{ij})^{2}},

Proof.

Fix
i
as in the theorem statement. We proceed along similar lines as those in Theorem
2
, now applying Theorem
3
, instead of Theorem
2
. For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, set
v_{j}:=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{j}(X_{j})
. Then
(S_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(S_{n},v_{n})
satisfy the prerequisites of Theorem
3
, that is
v_{j}\in\Gamma(TS_{j})
are unit and conditions (
1
)-(
3
) are satisfied. Furthermore
\mu_{ij}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=-s_{ij}
and so
\mu_{ij}
is nowhere
0
. Hence by Theorem
3
(with
(S_{i},v_{i})
in place of
(S_{1},v_{1})
) gives

\mbox{Ric}(v_{i},v_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{ij}(v_{i})-1}{(\mu_{ij})^{2}}.

Inserting
v_{i}=-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Y}_{i}(X_{i})
and
\mu_{ij}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{i}=-s_{ij}
in the above completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4
.

We remark that a similar results holds when
\Sigma
has codimension greater than
1
, with minor tweaking of the above proofs. In particular there is no preferred normal unit vector field
\nu\in\Gamma(N\Sigma)
and the vector fields
X_{i}\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
and functions
r_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
are now just solutions of the following eigenproblem on
\Sigma

\displaystyle r_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{X_{i}}\nu=X_{i}

and

\displaystyle X_{i}(r_{i})=1.

We note that solutions exist, if
\Sigma
is assumed to satisfy the ridge free condition (which is trivially generalised to higher codimension) and
s_{ij}:=r_{i}-r_{j}
is assumed non vanishing, so that the multiplicities of the above eigenvalue problem are
1
.
