Suprasellar Desmoplastic Infantile Ganglioglioma in a 7‐Month‐Old Infant: A Rare Diagnostic Consideration in Infant Suprasellar Masses
Authors: Tawfiq Zuhair Abdullah Allaylah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDesmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma should be considered when an infant presents with a suprasellar mass, nystagmus, and developmental delay. Deep midline location limits resection, so BRAF V600E testing matters: it can open a targeted treatment option when surgery cannot control the disease.Keywords: brain neoplasms, ganglioglioma, immunohistochemistry, infant, proto‐oncogene proteins B‐raf, sella turcica

Introduction

Desmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma (DIG) and desmoplastic infantile astrocytoma (DIA) are rare neuroepithelial neoplasms that together account for roughly 0.4% of primary brain tumors and between 1.3% and 15.8% of tumors arising in infancy [1, 2, 3, 4]. Both are grouped as a single entity in the fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, designated CNS WHO Grade 1, and share a dominant desmoplastic leptomeningeal component; the presence of a neoplastic neuronal population distinguishes DIG from DIA [1, 2]. Most patients present before 24 months of age with macrocephaly, a bulging fontanelle, seizures, or raised intracranial pressure, and the classic lesion is a bulky superficial hemispheric mass with an enhancing nodule, one or more large cysts, and frequent dural attachment [4, 5]. Despite an alarming radiological appearance, prognosis after gross total resection is excellent [6].

Lesions arising outside the cerebral hemispheres are distinctly uncommon, and the suprasellar region is among the rarest sites. Only 18 suprasellar cases have been assembled in the largest pooled analysis, and the fifth‐edition WHO criteria note that extra‐hemispheric examples have generally not been confirmed genetically [2, 7]. This matters clinically because the imaging phenotype differs—suprasellar tumors are predominantly solid and rarely suggest DIG preoperatively—and because reported outcomes are considerably worse, with adherence to the optic apparatus, hypothalamus, and perforating vessels precluding complete excision [5, 7, 8].

A further consideration is that desmoplastic infantile tumors are driven by MAPK pathway activation, and a substantial minority carry the BRAF p.V600E substitution [2, 9]. Since dabrafenib combined with trametinib was shown to outperform carboplatin and vincristine as first‐line treatment of pediatric low‐grade glioma with BRAF V600 mutations, identifying that mutation in a child whose tumor cannot be resected has become a therapeutic decision [10]. We report a suprasellar DIG in a 7‐month‐old girl in whom mutation‐specific immunohistochemistry demonstrated BRAF V600E‐mutant protein, and in whom a positive result subsequently guided management when leptomeningeal dissemination was suspected.

Case Presentation

Clinical History and Examination

A 7‐month‐old girl was referred to the neurosurgical service with a 2‐month history of persistent nystagmus and parental concern about delayed motor milestones. There had been no vomiting, seizures, irritability, or febrile illness, and no abnormality of visual behavior had been noticed before the nystagmus. She was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy and delivery, and there was no family history of neurological disease, neurocutaneous syndrome, or childhood malignancy.

On examination, the occipitofrontal head circumference was 46 cm, corresponding to approximately the 90th percentile for age; serial measurements did not demonstrate the rapidly enlarging head circumference that typifies hemispheric disease. The anterior fontanelle was soft and not bulging. She was unable to sit unsupported, indicating gross motor delay for age. Ocular examination showed left‐beating gaze‐evoked nystagmus without ptosis, strabismus, or other cranial nerve deficit. Tone, power, and deep tendon reflexes were symmetrical and there was no hemiparesis. The remainder of the general and systemic examination was unremarkable, and there were no clinical features of diabetes insipidus, precocious puberty, diencephalic syndrome, or other hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction at presentation.

Radiological Findings

Non‐contrast cranial CT demonstrated a heterogeneous 2.9 cm mass centered on the suprasellar region, with focal hyperattenuating areas consistent with dystrophic calcification and a large well‐defined cystic component of near‐CSF attenuation posterolaterally on the right (Figure 1). The lesion extended posteriorly along the optic tracts toward the right basal ganglia and thalamus. The lateral ventricles were not dilated.

Axial CT scan showing a heterogeneous suprasellar mass extending posteriorly. The mass measures 2.9 cm in maximum diameter and involves the optic tracts, midbrain, right basal ganglia, and right thalamus. The lesion demonstrates mixed density with areas of hypodensity and hyperdensity.

MRI confirmed a complex solid‐cystic lesion (Figure 2). The solid component was isointense to gray matter on T1‐weighted sequences and hyperintense on T2/FLAIR, with avid heterogeneous gadolinium enhancement. The cystic component was of near‐CSF signal without enhancement. The tumor was intimately related to the optic chiasm anteriorly, the pituitary stalk and hypothalamus superiorly, and the floor of the third ventricle, which was effaced and distorted; posterior extension involved the right thalamus and midbrain. There was no hydrocephalus, hemorrhage, diffusion restriction, or leptomeningeal enhancement at diagnosis. The combination of a deep midline epicenter, mixed solid‐cystic architecture, avid enhancement, and extension along the optic tracts placed craniopharyngioma, pilocytic astrocytoma, hypothalamic‐chiasmatic glioma, and germinoma at the top of the preoperative differential; DIG was not considered until histology was available.

Axial FLAIR MRI sequence demonstrating the mixed solid and cystic components of the tumor. The image shows hyperintense signal in the suprasellar region with extension to adjacent structures. The lesion causes mass effect on surrounding brain parenchyma.

Surgical Procedure

A right pterional craniotomy with a trans‐sylvian approach was performed. The tumor was firm, grayish‐white and rubbery, was densely adherent to the optic chiasm and the floor of the third ventricle, and derived a rich blood supply from surrounding perforating vessels. Because of this adherence to the optic apparatus, hypothalamus, and adjacent vasculature, only partial resection was achieved: the cyst was decompressed and the accessible solid component debulked, and dissection was deliberately abandoned where the plane with the chiasm and hypothalamus could not be developed safely. There was no dural attachment and no leptomeningeal nodularity at any point in the exposure.

Pathological Findings

Intraoperative assessment showed a cellular, biphasic‐appearing neoplasm, and raised the possibility of a spindle‐cell lesion; definitive interpretation was appropriately deferred to permanent sections. This experience mirrors the recognized tendency of desmoplastic infantile tumors to be overgraded on squash preparations and mistaken for an infiltrating glioma because the desmoplasia yields scant, spindled, and worrying‐looking material [4, 5].

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) showed the biphasic architecture that defines the entity (Figures 3 and 4). A dominant desmoplastic component composed of dense eosinophilic collagen bundles was arranged in interlacing fascicles and short storiform streams. Embedded within this matrix were spindled to ovoid neoplastic cells with elongated, mildly hyperchromatic nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli, together with a minor population of smaller, darker cells. Scattered larger cells with vesicular nuclei, visible nucleoli, and modest eosinophilic cytoplasm were identified and interpreted as ganglioid or immature ganglion cells (Figure 5); these were sparse and focal rather than conspicuous, and unequivocal fully mature dysmorphic ganglion cells with abundant Nissl substance were not seen. Mitotic figures were not identified. There was no necrosis, no glomeruloid microvascular proliferation, no perivascular chronic inflammatory infiltrate, and no xanthomatous change. A poorly differentiated, embryonal‐like small cell component of the type described in a proportion of desmoplastic infantile tumors was not represented in the submitted material.

A reticulin impregnation demonstrated an abundant argyrophilic meshwork investing individual cells and small cell groups in a pericellular pattern, confirming the extensive basal‐lamina‐rich desmoplastic stroma (Figure 6).

The glial component showed strong, diffuse cytoplasmic, and process labeling for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Figure 7) and strong nuclear and cytoplasmic labeling for S100 protein (Figure 8), and OLIG2 was expressed in the nuclei of the spindled population. Synaptophysin labeling was weak to moderate, patchy, and confined to scattered cells and a delicate neuropil‐like background rather than being strong and diffuse (Figure 9); we regard this as supportive of, rather than conclusive proof of, a neoplastic neuronal element. Neuronal nuclear antigen (NeuN), neurofilament protein, and chromogranin A were negative in the lesional cells. The Ki‐67 labeling index was very low, with only isolated positive nuclei and an estimated index below 2% (Figure 10).

Immunohistochemistry with the mutation‐specific antibody to BRAF p.V600E (clone VE1) showed convincing granular cytoplasmic expression of mutant protein (Figure 11). The distribution was informative in itself: labeling followed the neoplastic fascicles and the ganglioid cells within them, while the intervening bands of desmoplastic collagen remained entirely unstained. The unstained stroma therefore functioned as an internal negative control within the same field, and the pattern reproduces the compartmental distribution of mutant protein previously described in this entity, in which expression is confined to the tumor cell population and the leptomeningeal desmoplastic component is predominantly negative [11].

Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

The essential fifth‐edition WHO criterion for DIG or DIA is a desmoplastic neuroepithelial tumor arising in infancy with a leptomeningeal component, and this was met: the patient was 7 months old, the tumor had a dominant desmoplastic stroma with reticulin‐rich pericellular matrix, and GFAP‐positive spindled cells were the predominant population.

Assignment as DIG rather than DIA requires a convincing neoplastic neuronal component. The evidence in this case is focal synaptophysin labeling in scattered cells with ganglioid morphology. NeuN, neurofilament protein, and chromogranin A were negative, and fully mature dysmorphic ganglion cells were not identified. We therefore acknowledge that DIA with ganglion‐like elements cannot be excluded from this partial specimen, but note that the distinction carries no current management consequence since both are CNS WHO Grade 1 and treated identically.

The principal differential diagnoses were excluded as follows. Craniopharyngioma was excluded by the absence of squamous epithelium, wet keratin, and CTNNB1 nuclear expression. Pilocytic astrocytoma was excluded by the absence of biphasic architecture, Rosenthal fibers, and eosinophilic granular bodies, and by the prominent desmoplasia. Germinoma was excluded by the absence of large vesicular cells, lymphocytic infiltrate, and PLAP positivity. Infant‐type hemispheric glioma with NTRK or ALK fusion was excluded by the absence of the characteristic nodular architecture and by the BRAF V600E result, which is not a feature of that entity [12]. Diffuse midline glioma was excluded by the absence of H3 K27M mutant protein expression and by the Grade 1 morphology.

Molecular Interpretation and Its Limitations

Desmoplastic infantile tumors are driven by MAPK pathway activation, most often through BRAF p.V600E [2, 9]. Immunohistochemistry with clone VE1 showed convincing granular cytoplasmic expression of mutant protein in the neoplastic fascicles and ganglioid cells, while the intervening desmoplastic collagen was unstained (Figure 11). This compartmental pattern, with expression restricted to the tumor cell population and sparing of the stroma, reproduces the distribution described by Koelsche et al. in the only dedicated VE1 study of this entity, where the unstained stroma provided an internal negative control [11]. A positive result at this site is uncommon: VE1 was positive in only 3/18 tumors in that series, and all three Mayo Clinic suprasellar cases were VE1‐negative [8, 11].

VE1 is a surrogate for genotype and cannot detect non‐V600E substitutions or BRAF fusions; sequence‐level confirmation was not available. H3K27me3 expression was lost in the neoplastic cells, a finding reported in a subset of desmoplastic infantile tumors and distinct from H3 K27M mutant protein, which was negative, excluding diffuse midline glioma.

Postoperative Course, Adjuvant Therapy, and Follow‐Up

The immediate postoperative course was uncomplicated, without new visual deterioration, diabetes insipidus, electrolyte disturbance, seizures, or need for CSF diversion. Baseline pituitary and ophthalmological assessments were normal. Following partial resection, the case was reviewed at a multidisciplinary pediatric neuro‐oncology meeting, and neuraxis MRI at 6 weeks provided a surveillance baseline.

Serial gadolinium‐enhanced MRI showed stable residual suprasellar tumor without interval growth. New leptomeningeal enhancement, including in the pre‐pontine cistern, was radiologically suspicious for cerebrospinal fluid dissemination. As no leptomeningeal enhancement was present preoperatively or on early postoperative MRI, this was an interval change. Cerebrospinal fluid cytology was not obtained; dissemination therefore remains suspected rather than proven.

The combination of an unresectable residual tumor and new leptomeningeal disease was interpreted as disease activity beyond the original tumor bed and taken as an indication for systemic therapy, and the demonstration of BRAF V600E‐mutant protein on the resection specimen directly determined its choice. After multidisciplinary discussion and with written informed parental consent, combined MAPK pathway inhibition was commenced using dabrafenib 5.25 mg/kg/day, given as an oral suspension in two divided doses, together with trametinib 0.032 mg/kg once daily, the weight‐based pediatric doses established for children under 12 and under 6 years of age respectively [10, 13]. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy with carboplatin and vincristine was given concurrently, reflecting both the extent of leptomeningeal involvement and the absence of published experience with targeted therapy alone in disseminated desmoplastic infantile tumors. The approved pediatric indication for dabrafenib with trametinib in low‐grade glioma begins at 1 year of age; treatment in this patient was therefore initiated off‐label, on the basis of the molecular result and the absence of an effective alternative, after formal multidisciplinary review and with explicit parental consent. Monitoring for the recognized toxicities of this combination, including pyrexia, rash, diarrhea, and cardiac and ophthalmological adverse effects, was undertaken according to institutional protocol, and growth and development were assessed at each visit.

At the most recent review, 4 months after surgery, the child was alive and continuing on combined targeted and cytotoxic therapy, which had been tolerated without dose‐limiting toxicity. The suprasellar residuum showed no interval growth, and the leptomeningeal and pre‐pontine enhancement was unchanged in extent, indicating radiological stability of the disseminated component on treatment rather than demonstrable regression. Nystagmus persisted but had not progressed; visual behavior was preserved to the extent assessable at this age, anterior and posterior pituitary function remained intact with normal growth velocity and no diabetes insipidus, and gross motor development continued to lag behind age expectation, for which she remains in a structured early intervention program. Clinical, ophthalmological, endocrine, and radiological review continues at three‐monthly intervals, with whole‐neuraxis imaging incorporated into surveillance because of the suspicion of cerebrospinal fluid dissemination. The follow‐up interval remains short, and no conclusion can yet be drawn about the durability of disease control.

Discussion

Three features make this case instructive: the location, the pathological argument achievable from a small deep specimen, and the way a single immunohistochemical result changed management once surgery had reached its limit.

The clinical presentation reflected the anatomy. Hemispheric DIG/DIA characteristically declares itself through macrocephaly, a bulging fontanelle, or seizures [4, 5]. Our patient had neither hydrocephalus nor raised intracranial pressure; her presenting features—nystagmus and motor delay—reflected compression of the visual pathways and deep midline structures. This distinction matters because atypical symptoms may delay consideration of DIG in the differential.

Radiologically, the present case is a partial exception to the reported suprasellar phenotype. Most published suprasellar cases have been predominantly solid and homogeneously enhancing, whereas our tumor retained the mixed solid‐cystic architecture more typical of hemispheric disease [5, 7]. That overlap did not resolve the preoperative differential, and DIG was not considered until histology was available, consistent with every previously reported suprasellar case.

Surgically, the constraint we encountered is the rule. Radical removal has never been achieved in any published suprasellar case, and incomplete resection is the expected outcome [5, 7, 8]. This is the most important practical difference between suprasellar and hemispheric disease, and it is the reason BRAF testing has become therapeutically consequential at this site.

The subsequent course illustrates why. Surveillance imaging showed stable residual tumor but new leptomeningeal and pre‐pontine enhancement, consistent with CSF dissemination, a pattern reported in several prior suprasellar cases [7]. A positive BRAF V600E result allowed dabrafenib and trametinib to be combined with carboplatin and vincristine. Bouffet et al. demonstrated in a randomized trial that this combination achieved a response rate of 47% versus 11% for chemotherapy alone, with a progression‐free survival of 20.1 versus 7.4 months, in pediatric low‐grade glioma with BRAF V600 mutations [10]. That trial enrolled DIA and DIG patients, making it directly relevant.

The proposition that suprasellar disease behaves differently from hemispheric disease is supported by the available data but should be treated as a hypothesis. In the largest pooled series (Table 2), the suprasellar lesion progressed in 9 of 18 patients and 5 died over a median follow‐up of only 25 months [7]. However, location is confounded with extent of resection, which is the most consistently identified prognostic variable, so worse outcomes may reflect unresectability rather than intrinsic biology. A formal individual participant data meta‐analysis identified high Ki‐67 and multifocality, not location, as the variables associated with recurrence [6, 22]. The defensible statement is that suprasellar location is associated with worse reported outcomes, that this is plausibly explained by surgical inaccessibility, and that whether a genuine biological difference exists remains unresolved.

Limitations

Several limitations apply. BRAF V600E was demonstrated by immunohistochemistry alone; sequence‐level confirmation and DNA methylation profiling were not available, so the fifth‐edition WHO pathway for genetically unresolved lesions could not be completed. Only part of the tumor was examined, so a poorly differentiated component or a more convincing neuronal population elsewhere cannot be excluded, and the DIG versus DIA distinction is provisional. The suspicion of CSF dissemination rests on imaging rather than cytological proof, and reactive postoperative leptomeningeal enhancement cannot be entirely dismissed. Follow‐up is limited to 4 months, which is insufficient to establish the durability of disease control on MAPK pathway inhibition, and the radiological stability observed to date should not be interpreted as evidence of sustained efficacy.

Conclusion

A 7‐month‐old girl with nystagmus and developmental delay proved to have a suprasellar DIG, a location documented in only 18 previous patients. Four practical messages emerge. First, DIG/DIA belongs in the differential diagnosis of an infant suprasellar mass, and ocular signs in this setting should increase rather than decrease suspicion. Second, integrated morphological and immunohistochemical assessment can establish a desmoplastic glioneuronal tumor confidently on a deep partial specimen, though the DIG versus DIA distinction may remain provisional. Third, BRAF V600E testing should be regarded as a therapeutic investigation whenever gross total resection has not been achieved. Fourth, the apparently worse outcomes of suprasellar disease should be treated as a hypothesis confounded by unresectability, not as an established biological property, and argue for prolonged neuraxis surveillance rather than therapeutic pessimism.
