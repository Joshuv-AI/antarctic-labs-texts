Adaptable Manufacturing and Biofabrication of Milliscale Organ Chips With Perfusable Vascular Beds
Authors: Charles Ethan Byrne, Ashley T Martier, Gideon Wills Kpeli, Kevin Michael Conrad, William Bralower, Elisabet Olsen, Gabrielle Fortes, Caroline C Culp, Max Wendell, Keefer A Boone, Matthew R Burow, Mark J Mondrinos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrophysiological systems (MPS) containing perfusable vascular beds unlock the ability to model tissue‐scale elements of vascular physiology and disease in vitro. Access to inexpensive stereolithography (SLA) 3D printers now enables benchtop fabrication of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) organ chips, eliminating the need for cleanroom access and microfabrication expertise, and can facilitate broader adoption of MPS approaches in preclinical research. Rapid prototyping of organ chip mold designs accelerates the processes of design, testing, and iteration, but geometric distortion and surface roughness of SLA resin prints can impede the development of standardizable manufacturing workflows. This study reports postprocessing procedures for manufacturing SLA‐printed molds that produce fully cured, flat, patently bonded, and optically clear polydimethyl siloxane (PDMS) organ chips. Injection loading tests were conducted to identify milliscale membrane‐free organ chip (MFOC) designs that allowed reproducible device loading by target end‐users, a key requirement for broad nonexpert adoption in preclinical research. The optimized milliscale MFOC design was used to develop tissue engineering protocols for (i) driving bulk tissue vasculogenesis in MFOC, and (ii) seeding the bulk tissue interfaces with a confluent endothelium to stimulate self‐assembly of perfusable anastomoses with the internal vasculature. Comparison of rocker‐ and pump‐based protocols for flow‐conditioning of anastomosed vascular beds revealed that continuous pump‐driven flow is required for reproducible barrier maturation throughout the 3D tissue bulk. Demonstrated applications include nanoparticle perfusion and engineering perfusable tumor vasculature. These easily adaptable methods for designing and fabricating vascularized microphysiological systems can accelerate their adoption in a diverse range of preclinical laboratory settings.Keywords: 3D printing, endothelial barrier, organ chips, tissue engineering, tumor vasculature, vascularization

Introduction

Early organ chips were manufactured using photolithographic methods to create silicon master molds used for downstream polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) soft lithography and microfluidic device assembly, thereby requiring clean room access and narrow technical expertise [1, 2]. Advances in digital manufacturing now enable low‐cost, rapid prototyping capabilities that eliminate the need for cleanroom access and cut the mold fabrication time from days to hours [3, 4]. Depending on the geometric intricacy and size scale of the design, inexpensive commercial stereolithography (SLA) based 3D printers can deliver the resolution and accuracy necessary to fabricate master molds for PDMS soft lithography, even in low resource settings [5]. Lower cost and faster manufacturing of SLA printed parts comes with the tradeoffs of material property limitations. SLA resins are prone to warping upon drying and curing and have been shown to leach chemicals that can inhibit PDMS curing and injure cultured cells [6, 7]. Furthermore, the surface roughness of SLA‐printed molds negates the accurate feature replication, ease of release, and transparency of molded PDMS that are hallmarks of silicon master molds used to fabricate microfluidic devices [8, 9]. Various methods for mitigating surface roughness of 3D printed molds have been reported [10, 11], but not within the context of a comprehensive postprocessing workflow that addresses a range of material property limitations. Widely adaptable procedures were developed to correct these limitations and enable efficient application‐specific organ chip manufacturing using low‐cost SLA printers as a fabrication centerpiece.

Common organ chip design paradigms include devices with vertically stacked layers of channels and chambers separated by porous membranes [12], and membrane‐free organ chip (MFOC) designs with horizontally arranged chambers separated by features, such as regularly spaced vertical pillars or continuous horizontal guide rails, that create surface tension to pattern injected hydrogels [13, 14, 15]. Accessible tissue interfaces unimpeded by the placement of a membrane are a critical feature of the various MFOC designs that enables anastomotic connection between an internal vascular network and external fluidic channels. Notably, pillar‐based designs pattern the location of anastomoses in the gaps between pillars [16, 17, 18], whereas anastomoses form de novo along the length of the tissue boundary in rail‐based designs [14, 19, 20]. This study focused on breaching of injected liquids beyond patterned regions as a primary failure mode of MFOC featuring the guide rail design [21]. Reduction to the practice of target milliscale MFOC designs was achieved via systematic injection loading tests to quantify the effects of guide structure dimensions, bulk tissue volume, and PDMS surface properties on MFOC loading success rates. Loading success is dictated by both the designed guide dimensions and the PDMS surface hydrophobicity that influences liquid cohesion.

The ability to create bulk tissues with an anastomosed and perfusable internal vasculature makes MFOC ideal venues for modeling aspects of vascular physiology such as barrier function, vascular inflammation, and intravascular delivery of compounds and materials [22, 23]. The optimal MFOC design identified by user testing was used to develop protocols for creating milliscale bulk tissues with a perfusable internal vasculature. A two‐step MFOC seeding protocol was developed in which bulk tissues are first allowed to undergo the initial stages of vasculogenesis before seeding the surfaces of open tissue interfaces with endothelial cells. Rapid formation of a confluent endothelium on the surface of the tissue boundary facilitates the autonomous formation of fluidically accessible vascular anastomoses at points of contact with the internal vasculature. The recruitment of perfusable vessels throughout the 3D bulk with the formation of a stable endothelial barrier occurred by 9 days of static culture. Unidirectional pump‐driven flow and pump‐free oscillatory rocking were investigated as methods of flow conditioning to accelerate endothelial barrier function in the perfusable vascular beds. The base configuration of flow‐conditioned vascular beds was then adapted to construct a microphysiological model of a bulk tumor continuum for investigating cancer‐associated changes in the tumor vasculature.

Materials and Methods

SLA Printing

Device molds were drafted in SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes) or Fusion 360 (Autodesk) and printed using a Form 3B SLA Printer (Formlabs) via Formlab's PreForm software. All molds were printed using Formlab's proprietary “Grey” or “Clear” SLA resins. CAD files for all organ chip mold designs used in these studies are available in downloadable form along with a list of dimensions for all designs in the Supporting Information.

Mold Postprocessing

Completed prints were washed in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 20 min in the FormWash (Formlabs). Postwash, molds were dried until IPA was completely evaporated. Molds were then dried and cured under UV light (FormCure, Formlabs) at 60°C. Clear resin parts were cured for 15 min. Grey resin parts were cured for 30 min. Warping caused by distortion of the printing process and the curing process was corrected by baking molds at 130°C for 3 h. For the last 30 min of bake time, molds were sandwiched between two stainless steel preheated jeweler's blocks. Parts were then removed from oven and clamped between jeweler's blocks at room temperature until cool. We used a commercial painting airbrush (Model 105 Patriot Fine Gravity Airbrush, Badger Airbrush Co.) to coat molds with automotive clear coat (Finish 1 FC720/FH612, Sherman Williams) mixed at a 1:4 ratio of hardener to clear coat. Four layers were applied with continuous back and forth spraying from approximately 8 inches away with the mold rotated 90° between applications. Coated molds were dried for 6 h before silanization according to standard protocols for PDMS soft lithography.

PDMS Soft Lithography

PDMS (Sylgard 184, Ellsworth Chemical) was mixed at a 1:10 ratio of PDMS curing agent to PDMS elastomer by weight and degassed. PDMS soft lithography was carried out according to standard protocols used for silicon master molds [1]. To produce PDMS molds with two flat surfaces, we applied a cleaned 2 × 3 inch glass slide to the top of the mold to sandwich the uncured PDMS, carefully avoiding bubble formation. A jeweler's block was placed on top of the slide to ensure a tight seal, and filled molds were baked at 60°C for at least 4 h.

Device Assembly

Cured PDMS device layers were removed from molds and detached from the glass slides using a razor blade. Clear packing tape was used to remove any dust from the PDMS parts. Through features were removed using biopsy punches and an X‐acto knife as needed. Device bonding was achieved by PDMS stamping as reported previously [24, 25]. Briefly, 3 g of PDMS was spin coated on a Petri dish to create a thin stamping layer, layers were stamped into this PDMS layer and stacked together. Reproducibility of layer alignment was ensured by the inclusion of Lego‐like press fitting features (Video S2). Assembled devices were cured at room temperature for at least 24 h. Many PDMS microfluidic device fabrication protocols utilize plasma bonding, but plasma treatment produces irreversible bonding which prohibits collection of tissues for downstream analyses including PCR and RNA sequencing [26, 27]. PDMS stamping, conversely, allows for reversible bonding and enables tissue collection while eliminating the need for access to expensive plasma coating machinery.

Fabrication of Double Guide MFOC

The upper guide structure in the double guide MFOC design required the generation of a second mold. Due to the negative feature resolution limitations of our SLA printer, we bisected the symmetrical design and printed two molds that were mirror images of each other. PDMS parts cured in these molds were PDMS stamped and sandwiched together with the guide structures facing inward. Cap layers containing media reservoirs were fabricated and PDMS stamped to the top of double guide MFOC to complete the final assembly before use in culture.

Injection Testing

To test the usability of each design iteration, the devices underwent a series of injection testing. At least six users with varying levels of familiarity with injecting microfluidic devices were recruited. Three replicates of each design were fabricated. Users injected devices with a food dye and water solution. After each test, water was removed, and devices were dried using compressed air. Success was defined as injected water reaching the outlet without breaching the middle channel. Each user completed 10 tests per design, and loading success rates were recorded. To visualize the tissue interfaces in the device, a 100 µg/mL solution of 40 kDa FITC‐dextran (Sigma–Aldrich) was mixed into a collagen hydrogel, injected, and imaged via laser scanning confocal microscopy.

Cell Culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (ATCC) were maintained in a Vascular Cell Basal Medium supplemented with Endothelial Cell Growth Kit‐VEGF (ATCC). Human lung fibroblasts (HLFs) (ATCC and Lonza) were maintained in Fibroblast Basal Medium supplemented with fibroblast Growth Kit‐Low Serum (ATCC). A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells were cultured in F12 medium with 10% FBS. All culture media contained 1% antibiotic‐antimycotic (Corning). Cells were maintained in a humidified tissue culture incubator maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 and used at Passages 3–7.

Toxicity Testing of Polyurethane Coating

PDMS slabs cast in clear coated and uncoated molds were soaked in media for 48 h. HUVEC and HLF were grown in 24 well dishes at 10,000 cells per well in control media for 24 h before incubation with the conditioned media for 48 h. Select wells were incubated with 4 µM calcein‐AM and 10 µM ethidium homodimer‐1 to assess for viability (Live/Dead, Invitrogen), and additional wells were fixed in 4% PFA, blocked and permeabilized in 0.1% Triton‐X and 1% BSA for 15 min, and then stained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) and phalloidin conjugated to AlexaFluor 488 (Invitrogen) to visualize cell nuclei and cytoskeleton to assess morphology.

Organ Chip Seeding

Organ chips were sterilized via UV light for 1 h. The surfaces of chambers of multilayer devices and lanes of MFOC that house bulk 3D tissues were functionalized for ECM hydrogel anchorage using the polydopamine (PDA) coating method as previously described [28]. Briefly, PDA solution was prepared by mixing 10 mg of dopamine hydrochloride (Sigma–Aldrich) with 10 mM Tris‐hydrochloride (Sigma–Aldrich). PDA solution was then sterile filtered, injected into device tissue chambers, and incubated in UV light for 2 h. PDA was then aspirated, and devices were washed with sterile water. This method has been demonstrated to produce stable functionalization of PDMS culture surfaces for up to 4 months at room temperature that can withstand up to 100,000 cycles of repetitive loading [29, 30]. Vascular tissue constructs are stable for at least 10 days in PDA‐treated devices [28]. Multilayer membrane‐bound organ chips were loaded with collagen Type I hydrogel (2.5 mg/mL) containing primary normal HLFs (5 × 105 cells/mL) in the 3D tissue chamber, and the upper tissue layer channel was seeded with A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells (1 × 106 cells/mL). MFOC was seeded with HLF and HUVEC (2 × 106 cells/mL each) admixed in 2.5 mg/mL collagen I (Corning), 5 mg/mL fibrinogen (Sigma–Aldrich), and 1 U/mL thrombin (Sigma–Aldrich). This cell inoculated hydrogel precursor was injected into central tissue lane of MFOC devices and incubated for 15 min before filling the side channels and media reservoirs with endothelial cell growth medium (VEGF kit) containing 25 µg/mL aprotinin (EMD Millipore) to inhibit fibrin degradation.

Establishing Anastomosed and Perfusable Bulk Vasculature

Two methods of initiating anastomoses were explored. In the first method, after 2 days of bulk tissue vasculogenesis initiated as described in section 2.10, HUVECs (10 × 106 cells/mL) were injected into the MFOC side channels. Devices were maintained in static culture for another 7 days with media changes ever 24 h to allow for lumen formation and vessel maturation. We define static culture as no pumping or rocking, but slight differences in liquid head height of medium reservoirs lead to trickling flows in the anastomosing vasculature. In the second method, side channels were seeded at the time of seeding the bulk tissues. After allowing cell adhesion and integration overnight, flow was applied across the bulk tissue using a laboratory rocker or syringe pump for 4 days. A laboratory rocker was placed in an incubator and set for a 15° incline at 1 cycle/min. Devices were oriented with a central channel in line with the axis of rotation. For pump‐driven conditioning, a syringe pump was set to pull 20 µL/h from one side of a device. The opposite channel of the device was connected to a liquid media reservoir via stainless‐steel fittings and Tygon tubing.

Perfusion and Permeability Quantification

Perfusion throughout vascular networks was assessed using an FITC‐dextran solution and confocal microscopy. One device was mounted on the stage of a confocal microscope. Liquid media channels and reservoirs were emptied, then one channel was filled with 40 kDa FITC‐dextran diluted in media at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL; the reservoir was filled completely to maintain consistent hydrostatic pressure across each trial. DIC‐confocal microscopy was used to capture a 10‐min time series at 10x magnification and 1 frame/5 s. Large composite images were taken after each time series. To quantify perfusion and diffusion throughout the vascular network, FITC fluorescence was measured in the intravascular and extravascular space. A DIC image at t = 0 was used to create a binary mask of the vascular area. All computation was performed in MATLAB (R2021b). The alkaline lignin‐g‐PLGA nanoparticles (100–200 nm size range) entrapped with Cy5 used for perfusion testing were provided as a gift by Dr. Cristina Sabliov [33]. Briefly, particles were washed, resuspended in media at 1 mg/mL, and sonicated for dispersion. Nanoparticle solution was then injected into the anastomosed vasculature using the same method for FITC‐dextran perfusions described above, and LCSM stacks were acquired for visualization.

Patient‐Derived Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer Model

A triple‐negative patient‐derived tumor, designated as TU‐BcX‐2‐K1, was acquired in collaboration with the Louisiana Cancer Research Consortium Biospecimen Core. Tumor fragments were propagated in immunocompromised SCID/beige mice and harvested at an approximate volume of 1 cm3 [31, 32]. After harvest, the remaining connective tissue was removed, and explants were minced to maximize surface area. Extracellular matrix of harvested explants was digested with an enzyme solution containing Collagenase III, DNase I, and Dispase II. Tumor explants were incubated in enzyme solution for 30–60 min, triturated, and subsequently washed. The tumor homogenate was then strained to select tumor pieces between 40 and 250 µm. Red blood cell lysis was performed by resuspending in distilled water for 30 s before restoring normal osmolarity by adding a 10% volume of 10X PBS. One part of the resultant disaggregated slurry was added to four parts of the baseline formulation of HUVEC and HLF in blended collagen and fibrin hydrogel precursor to form vascularized triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) tissues in MFOC. MFOC were loaded, and the culture protocol for establishing vascular anastomosis was executed with no change to the culture medium.

Whole Mount Staining in Organ Chips

Tissue layers and bulk tissues in organ chips were stained using adaptations of previously reported protocols [28, 34]. Live tissues were incubated with 4 µM calcein‐AM for 30 min (Live/Dead, Invitrogen) to visualize viable cells. For other stains, tissues were fixed by loading 4% paraformaldehyde in the organ chip liquid channels, incubated for 1 h at room temperature, then overnight at 4°C. Devices were washed with PBS and stored at 4°C before staining. To visualize the vasculature, tissues were stained with 4 µL/mL DAPI to label nuclei, 4 µL/mL Alexa488‐conjugated phalloidins to label F‐actin, and 20 µL/mL DyLight594‐conjugated Ulex Europeas agglutinin I (UEA‐1, Vector Laboratories) to label endothelial cells [35]. The staining cocktail was prepared in 1X PBS with 0.2% Triton‐X 100 and 1% BSA. Devices were loaded with the staining cocktail, rocked gently for 1 h at room temperature, refrigerated overnight, and rocked again for 1 h at room temperature before washing. For whole‐mount immunohistochemistry for ICAM‐1, laminin, and Ki67, devices were blocked and permeabilized in 3% BSA and 0.2% Triton‐X 100 in PBS for 30 min followed by incubation with rabbit anti‐Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580), mouse anti‐ICAM‐1 (Abcam, ab171123), or rabbit anti‐laminin (Abcam, ab11575) antibody diluted 1:100 in 1% BSA with 0.1% Triton‐X 100 on a gentle rock for 4 h at room temperature and then overnight at 4°C. Devices underwent multiple PBS washes over 3 h and then were incubated with a 1:250 dilution of anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse secondary antibodies (Abcam) with optional phalloidin and DAPI counterstaining as described above. Samples were washed five times in PBS and stored loaded with PBS at 4°C until imaging.

Imaging and Image Analysis

Stained tissues were fixed in position on a slide and imaged on an inverted Nikon C2 LCSM equipped with a Nikon DS‐FI3 camera. Fluorescence intensity quantification was collected at fixed exposure times and laser intensities. Max intensity projection Z‐stacks were exported as TIFF files without LUTs. All image analysis, including morphometric analysis of vascular networks and perfused vasculature, was completed in MATLAB (R2021b). We used a pretrained deep neural network for denoising and then applied adaptive histogram equalization to standardize contrast across the image set [36]. Rounded cells were identified via a Hugh transform, counted to assess network completeness, and removed before segmentation and morphometric analysis. Images were smoothed with an edge‐preserving filter with a Gaussian kernel, and a threshold was applied to remove the remaining low‐intensity noise. Preprocessed images were segmented, and morphometric parameters were quantified using REAVER, an open‐source automated segmentation tool [37]. All devices stained with ICAM‐1 were imaged at a fixed laser intensity, and mean pixel intensity was computed for each image. ICAM‐1 localization to endothelial cells was discriminated by colocalization with UEA‐1 lectin.

Statistical Analysis

All vasculogenesis experiments were repeated n = 3 times. A large, composite 1 × 6 stitched image was acquired from each sample to ensure convergence of morphological variables. Images were preprocessed and segmented as described in section 2.14. All statistical analyses were completed in GraphPad Prism V 9.2. Comparison of MFOC injection loading success rates, metrics of vascular morphometry, and ICAM‐1 fluorescence intensity in the TNBC model were made using unpaired t tests with a 95% confidence limit for statistical significance, that is, p < 0.05. Sample sizes and the number of independent experiments performed are indicated in the respective figure captions.

Results and Discussion

Milliscale Organ Chip Manufacturing Using Low‐Cost SLA Printers

The resolution of the Formlabs F3 SLA printer for our applications was determined by measuring the percent deviation from the designed dimensions of common organ chip mold features such as rectangular channels and cylindrical posts (Figure S1). Positive features protruding from the primary mold surface were accurate down to 200 µm, whereas negative features sinking below the surface plane were only accurate down to the 400–600‐µm. This resolution range was suitable for the fabrication of accurate molds in the milliscale size range for fluidic channels and 3D tissue chambers, but the material characteristics of the as‐printed molds presented challenges for standardizable organ chip fabrication. The first goal of this study was to develop easily adaptable postprocessing procedures to mitigate these manufacturing complications (Figure S2).

Molds stored at ambient temperature before PDMS soft lithography retained volatiles present in the SLA resin that impeded PDMS curing (Figure 1A). Issues with PDMS curing in SLA‐printed molds have been reported by multiple groups using different commercial SLA printers and different resin compositions [38, 39, 40]. Although the formulation of proprietary SLA resins is often not published, many commercial SLA resins rely on phosphine‐oxide‐based photo‐initiators [41, 42]. Unreacted remnants of these photo‐initiators may remain on the surface and within the bulk of SLA prints and interfere with platinum‐based silicon‐curing catalysts [38]. We found that heating the printed molds removed the chemicals that interfered with PDMS curing, but this heating step exacerbates mold warping that occurs during the UV curing process, leading to significant mold curvature that precludes the fabrication of flat PDMS device layers (Figure 1B). Flat PDMS layers are required for consistent bonding during organ chip fabrication and in‐plane imaging of cells growing on horizontal surfaces such as membranes [1]. Clamping cured molds with heated jeweler blocks removes volatiles and leverages the malleability of SLA resins to produce flat molds (Figure 1C). Although higher cost printers and commercial SLA printing services can deliver flat and geometrically accurate molds, inexpensive and thus more widely accessible SLA printers are more likely to produce distorted prints, due to a combination of heating during ultraviolet curing and internal mechanical forces generated during resin polymerization [43, 44].

PDMS layers produced using these molds are opaque due to the surface roughness of the unfinished, matte resin material (Figure 1C). Rough‐surfaced, opaque PDMS layers are suitable for certain organ chip configurations that do not require imaging through PDMS layers or control of surface tension during liquid loading such as traditional multilayered organ chips. For example, a 5‐layer organ chip design consisting of three vertically stacked PDMS layers with two intervening polyester membranes that requires PDMS layers with evenly flat upper and lower surfaces for effective bonding was used as a framework to confirm standardizable organ chip fabrication (Figure 1D). Multilayer organ chips fabricated using stamped polyester membranes for layer partitioning did not show evidence of leakage between layers (Figure 1E). The 5‐layer device was used to create a prototype model of the interface between lung carcinoma and an adjacent lung interstitium by seeding A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells in the upper channel and normal HLFs embedded in Type I collagen hydrogel in the 3D tissue chamber (Figure 1F). Robust adhesion and growth of both cell types were observed throughout the more than 1 mm vertical height of the patterned tissue interface (Figure 1G, Video S1).The multilayer organ chip configuration is a highly versatile tool for modeling tissue interfaces and processes driven by the exchange of soluble factors [45, 46, 47, 48], but the presence of physical membranes precludes the integration of a perfusable 3D vasculature. Optimization of MFOC manufacturing was emphasized to establish designs and workflows for routine creation of perfusable vascular beds.

Many organ chip applications rely on the optical clarity of PDMS devices to enable repeat imaging and data collection. Undesirable PDMS opacity resulting from the rough surfaces of as‐printed SLA resin molds was mitigated by clear coating the molds with polyurethane (PU) used for automotive detailing to produce a smooth surface finish (Figure 1H). This manufacturing workaround qualitatively yields optically clear PDMS parts (Figure S3) as confirmed quantitatively by spectrophotometry (Figure 1I). To ensure that this PU coating did not affect cell viability, HUVEC and HLF were cultured in media conditioned by PDMS cast in molds that were clear coated and molds that were not coated. No difference in viability or morphology was noted between these groups (Figure S4). The durability of master molds manufactured with this baking, flattening, and clear coating workflow was confirmed for at least 40 cycles of molding without any decrement in the quality of molded PDMS layers. In addition, the PDMS stamping‐based organ chip fabrication protocol produces grossly patent bonded devices with a 100% success rate when using flat PDMS layers produced with optimally postprocessed molds.

PDMS surface roughness also influences hydrophobicity and therefore liquid interactions that influence surface tension‐based patterning of injected liquids without the presence of partitioning membranes. Although liquid patterning in MFOCs is accomplished via surface tension created by the guide structures [14], the PDMS surface hydrophobicity influences cohesion and therefore stability of liquid pinning along the guide structures during injection. We hypothesized that loading efficiency would be decreased in MFOC from uncoated molds, based on the notion that the increased hydrophobicity derived from surface roughness will tend to repel injected liquids and counteract the cohesion needed for stable pinning along the guide structure [49]. Contact angle measurements confirmed increased hydrophobicity of uncoated surfaces, which can be explained by theory that predicts increased liquid repellence which could promote cavitation and liquid spilling over the guide (Figure S5) [50, 51]. Stronger cohesion of the liquid with the PDMS guide structure facilitated by the hydrophilicity of a smoother surface would then be expected to promote the pinning of injected liquids along the guide. Increased surface roughness of PDMS from uncoated molds was visually confirmed by DIC imaging, and increased hydrophobicity was quantified with contact angle measurements (Figure S5). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a markedly smoother surface texture of PU‐coated molds compared with uncoated molds (Figure S5). Injection of rough‐surfaced devices from uncoated molds resulted in more frequent loading failures defined by the breaching of liquid over internal guide structures (Figure 1J). The loading success rate of smooth‐surfaced MFOC from PU‐coated molds was 98% ± 4% compared to 65% ± 11% for rough‐surfaced MFOC (Figure 1K). Thus, tuning SLA resin mold surface roughness provides engineering control of optical clarity and liquid patterning in the derivative PDMS devices.

Membrane‐Free Organ Chip Reduction to Practice

Reproducibly successful injection loading of organ chips is a critical requirement for broad adoption by nonexperts. Previously reported microscale MFOC using continuous horizontal guide structures termed “phase guides” were designed with the guide structure height at 25% of the bulk tissue chamber height (H), creating an open tissue interface height (h) that is 75% of the bulk tissue height (h/H = 0.75) [15, 20]. Pilot injection tests revealed that h/H = 0.75 is not tractable on the millimeter scale based on the complete failure to pattern injected liquids along guide structures. We used h/H = 0.5 as a starting point for milliscale design iteration and compared single‐ and double‐guide designs (Figure 2B, C). Single‐guide devices only have guide structures along the bottom surface of the chamber, while double‐guide devices divide the same total guide height (h) between two symmetrical guide structures along the top and bottom surfaces. Loading success rates of 85%–90% were achieved in both designs by nonexpert users (Figure 2D). The double‐guide configuration was chosen for further design iteration and testing due to the advantage of a symmetrically positioned central interface. We also found that tissues cast in double guide devices were more stable, that is, less prone to detachment, upon the introduction of pressure‐driven flow. Loading efficiency of double guide MFOC with h/H values of 0.6, 0.65, and 0.7 were tested to determine if the area of open tissue boundary could be increased, thereby maximizing the surface area for mass transport. Loading efficiency dropped below 40% with h/H = 0.6 or 0.65 and dropped below 20% with h/H = 0.7 (Figure 2E). Thus, h/H = 0.5 was found to be a necessary design specification to achieve reliably successful pipette loading of milliscale MFOC.

Although miniaturization is often pursued in MPS design to achieve high throughput capabilities, many biochemical methods of nucleic acid and protein analysis benefit from increased amounts of sampling materials. Furthermore, boundary effects are amplified with miniaturization that increases the surface area to volume ratio, thereby reducing the contribution of bulk effects that dominate in macroscale organs [52]. Miniaturization of lab‐on‐a‐chip devices for analytical chemistry has been shown to decrease the signal‐to‐noise ratio compared to counterpart conventional techniques, thereby degrading the quality and depth of acquired data [53]. This study investigated the effect of increasing tissue volume housed in MFOC on loading efficiency by increasing the values of H and chamber width, W, while maintaining h/H = 0.5. Loading efficiency decreased from nearly 100% with the base 8 mm3 design to approximately 90% with a 16 mm3 volume in this cohort of injection tests, but the difference was not significant and further increases up to 48 mm3 yielded similar results (Figure 2F). Loading FITC‐dextran collagen hydrogel in the base 8 mm3 design to visualize the tissue boundary shape in MFOC consistently revealed a downward slope of approximately 45°–60° with the formation of a foot along the bottom guide (Figure 2G). These collective observations from MFOC loading tests suggest that stable liquid patterning requires a balance between the inertial force generated by the momentum of the injected liquid tending to drive breaching over the guide and the combined effects of the liquid surface tension force and the cohesion force between the liquid and PDMS surface (F m, F st, and F c in Figure 2H).

Engineering Milliscale Tissues With Perfusable Bulk Vasculature in MFOC

The formation of an interconnected vascular network distributed uniformly throughout the 3D tissue volume in MFOC is a necessary first step to establishing a perfusable bulk vasculature. HUVECs and HLFs were admixed in a collagen Type I and fibrin hydrogel blend at a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL for each cell type to facilitate bulk tissue vasculogenesis in MFOC (Figures 3A, B). 3D vascular network assembly was evident by 3 days in culture using this formulation (Figure 3C). Interconnected networks spanning the entire milliscale tissue bulk assemble by 7 days in culture (Figure 3D). Fully enclosed tissues cast in MFOC are surface anchored via a PDA coating [28, 30, 54]. Importantly, this anchorage allowed stable tissue generation; tissues that achieve vascular network formation and maturation without detachment from the microfluidic device can continue in culture without detachment for up to 14 days. This configuration maintains a constant tissue volume and produces vascular network morphologies that more accurately mimic the architecture of capillary networks in vivo [28, 55]. Although true pericytes are not present in the model, closely associated and enrobing fibroblasts contribute to the formation of structures reminiscent of perivascular niches (Figure 3F) [56]. Stabilization of established vasculature was indicated by the formation of continuous laminin‐rich basal lamina enrobing interconnected tubules of the emergent vascular network (Figure 3G). Morphometric evidence of maturation included decreasing nonparticipating endothelial cell numbers, increasing vessel diameters, and increasing diffusion distances (network spacing) (Figure 3E, H). Thus, organ chip models of vasculogenesis can capture key aspects of capillary network morphogenesis and stabilization in a highly configurable and volume‐constrained format.

Achieving perfusion of the internal bulk vasculature requires anastomosis with inlets and outlets to a liquid medium in the MFOC side channels. Surfaces of the bulk tissue interfaces must be endothelialized to establish a complete vascular barrier that prevents free diffusive transport between the liquid medium and the bulk tissue volume. Side channel seeding with a high density of endothelial cells (5–8 × 106 cells/mL depending on yields, see Section 2) after 2 days of bulk tissue vasculogenesis results in complete coverage of the tissue interfaces (Figure 4B). The lack of gross leakage at tissue boundaries confirms the formation of a confluent and patent endothelium. Numerous anastomoses with the internal vasculature form de novo by 9 days in culture (Figure 4C). 3D reconstructions revealed that anastomoses appear as open pipe faces on a manifold along the length of the tissue boundary upon 3D visualization (Figure 4C inset). Internal vessels are inaccessible in devices without the side channels seeded due to the formation of dense cell layers aligned parallel to the tissue boundary (Figure 4D, E). Interestingly, more network branches were oriented toward and docked at the tissue boundary when a confluent endothelium was present, suggesting a directive chemotactic or haptotactic effect driving anastomosis.

The anastomosed vasculature was perfusable and patent after 9 days of static culture (7 days after side channel seeding) as demonstrated by uniform perfusion of FITC‐conjugated dextran (Figure 4F). Although establishment of perfusable vasculature without gross leakage was attainable using this protocol, it is well‐established that the fluid shear stress from flow positively regulates endothelial barrier function [57, 58]. We compared unidirectional syringe pump‐driven flow with the more convenient rocking method that produces oscillatory bidirectional flow but does not require external connections with the device (Figure 5). Syringe pump‐driven flow pulling at a rate of 20 µL/h across the bulk tissue for 96 h generated a patent vasculature as verified by perfusion testing (Figure 5A). Although the position of anastomoses is not patterned in guide rail‐based MFOC, anastomoses form in a regular pattern along the 10‐mm length of the device (Figure 5C). Perfusion of finer vessels is visible at higher magnification with longer exposure times (Figure 5C inset). Rocking for 96 h yielded inconsistent results, ranging from a lack of perfusion to incomplete perfusion with gross evidence of leakage into the interstitial spaces (Figure 5B). Perfusion in pumping conditioned devices demonstrated subjectively less leakage into the extravascular space.

Analysis of network permeability from time course imaging revealed rapid recruitment and establishment of maximum intravascular fluorescence in perfused vessels of pumped devices upon introduction of FITC‐dextran, whereas the extravascular fluorescence in pumped devices increased only slightly during the same 15‐min time increment (Figure 5G, Video S4). In contrast, vessel recruitment was delayed in rocked devices, and extracellular fluorescence gradually increased during the time course of perfusion, indicating vascular leakage due to incomplete endothelial barrier formation (Figure 5G). There were subjective differences in the morphology of pumped and rocked vasculature, with measured trends toward increased vessel diameter, increased diffusion distances, and shorter vessel length in the vasculature of pumped devices (Figure 5F). The flow‐conditioned vasculature in pumped MFOC was then tested as a conduit for delivering nanoparticles to the 3D bulk tissue. Nanoparticles were distributed throughout the bulk vasculature, with larger aggregates wedged near branch points within the vasculature and smaller particle aggregates present in the extravascular spaces (Figure 5D). A layer of adherent particles persisted on large vessels near anastomosis points following the perfusion (Figure 5E).

Microfluidic models with perfusable vasculature have been reported in numerous formats for more than a decade [18, 22, 23, 59, 60]. Notably, we successfully achieved patent anastomosis in our system with static culture using standard vascular cell media formulations (Figure 4). There are natural fluctuations of pressure that can drive flow in the anastomosed vessels in static culture conditions, due to slight differences in media reservoir volumes and device tilting during handling for feeding and imaging. The impact of shear stress alterations on endothelial barrier function is a tenet of vascular physiology, and mechanical conditioning of engineered microvessels via flow‐induced shear stress has been shown to improve endothelial barrier function [61, 62]. Our study exploring flow conditioning (Figure 5) corroborates previous reports of improved barrier formation with syringe pump‐driven flow when compared to the more convenient method of device rocking that produces oscillatory bidirectional flow. Importantly, those studies were performed using engineered single microvessels, which allow for precise determination of applied shear stresses. Although the correlation of applied shear stresses with permeability in the 3D vasculature of our milliscale MFOC models is more challenging, our results suggest that continuous unidirectional pump‐driven flow is the optimal method to accelerate and enhance barrier maturation in engineered perfusable vasculature.

Anastomosis patterning and formation differs considerably in rail‐based MFOC with fully open tissue boundaries versus pillar‐based MFOC with intermittent regions open tissue boundary determined by pillar spacing. Pillar‐based designs have the advantage of standardizing the number of inlet and outlet vessels in the perfusable vascular bed. Other designs utilize a diamond‐shaped chamber with a single inlet and outlet, although these vascular bed models are prone to shunting through a central vessel upon the introduction of flow under pressure [63, 64]. More research is needed to determine the advantages and disadvantages of the various MFOC configurations for application‐specific vascular bed engineering. Key features for optimization may include but are not limited to, rapid anastomosis formation, uniformity of perfusion in the 3D tissue bulk, and completeness of endothelial barrier maturation. The impact of adding cellular components of the microvascular niche, including pericytes and vascular smooth muscle cells, should be explored depending on the necessity of lineage‐specific signals or investigation of direct pharmacological effects in these cell types. More broadly, the complexity of engineered vascular beds required to address a specific research question must be considered to find a balance of physiological mimicry, ease of fabrication, and usability in a wide range of research settings.

Microphysiological Model of Perfusable Bulk Tumor Vasculature

Tumor‐on‐a‐chip models typically utilize cancer cell line spheroids or patient‐derived tumor organoids dispersed in a 3D hydrogel, with or without the addition of exogenous cell types [65, 66, 67]. From a bioengineering perspective, cellular elements of the tumor microenvironment (TME) absent in cancer spheroids and patient‐derived organoids can be modeled with the inclusion of nonautologous cells. This approach is a powerful integration of organoids, tissue engineering, and organ‐on‐a‐chip technology but fails to model the continuum of a solid tumor bulk, rather approximating a field of micrometastases or the leading edge of an invasive tumor characterized by individual and collective cell migration away from the bulk [68]. The concept of engineering a bulk tumor continuum has received relatively little attention, yet the effects of tumor cell packing, and the dense microstructure of bulk tumors can significantly impact drug delivery and pharmacological effects [69]. We developed a simple method for generating a bulk tumor continuum by homogenizing TNBC derivatives maintained as patient‐derived xenografts and admixing the homogenate with the mixture of endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and collagen/fibrin hydrogel precursor used to establish the baseline model of perfusable vascular beds (Figures 4, 5). The injectable homogenate was then loaded into MFOC, and the same procedures of side channel seeding for anastomosis and pump‐driven flow conditioning were followed to generate a model of perfusable bulk tumor vasculature (Figure 6A, B).

Viable TNBC cells were seen throughout the densely cellular milliscale 3D bulk, indicating adequate nutrient transport in the model (Figure 6B, C). The average Ki67 index (percentage of cells with current or recent cell cycle participation) in the TNBC bulk was approximately 70% (Figure 6D, E), which is consistent with reported values from histopathological analysis of aggressive TNBC biopsies [70]. Comparing the gross morphology of bulk vasculature in the presence and absence of TNBC suggested a qualitatively more dysmorphic architecture in the TNBC model, but these differences were not statistically significant using the current battery of metrics (Figure 6F, G). Expression of the inflammatory cell adhesion molecule ICAM‐1 was measured in the baseline and TNBC vasculature using immunofluorescence approaches to explore modeling tumor inflammation associated with more aggressive forms of TNBC [71]. The presence of TNBC increased the endothelial‐specific ICAM‐1 signal by more than an order of magnitude, indicating acute inflammatory activation of the vasculature (Figure 6H–J). This proof‐of‐concept study demonstrates the ability to model cancer‐associated pathological changes in vascular beds and sets the stage for further development of vascularized bulk tumor continuum models using MPS.

Conclusion

The overarching goal of this study was to provide a methodological framework to help nonexpert laboratories overcome the technical barriers associated with fabricating fluidic culture devices and facilitate the broader adoption of organ chip technologies in microvascular research. The procedures for using low‐cost benchtop SLA resin printers to manufacture master molds suitable for PDMS soft lithography should be adaptable in a wide range of laboratory settings. The described postprocessing workflows enable application‐specific organ chip design and fabrication in labs without prior microfabrication experience and in settings without cleanroom facilities. All organ chip mold designs can be downloaded and used in the designed form or configured according to user specifications (see Supporting Information). Regardless of mold design, the reported postprocessing workflow addresses the impact of resin leachates on PDMS curing in resin molds, warping of resin molds and molded PDMS layers, and resin mold surface roughness that dictates optical clarity and hydrophobicity of molded PDMS layers. Researchers can customize the designs, following the guide heights (h/H ratio) determined to enable reproducibly successful loading of milliscale MFOCs. The impact of externally applied flow conditions on barrier function requires more systematic investigation to establish precise engineering control, although it is a challenging modeling problem from an experimental and theoretical viewpoint due to the heterogeneous 3D architecture of bulk vasculature and the resultant fluctuations of local flowrates and shear stresses within the tissue. Although oscillatory flow from rocking was inferior to continuous pump‐driven flow in the current study, the relative convenience and scalability of organ chip culture on rockers warrants more investigation of approaches to improve the uniformity of perfusion and endothelial barrier maturation. Regarding model construction, the results attained using HUVEC and HLF as the stromal‐vascular cell source should be replicated using organ‐specific combinations of fibroblasts and endothelial cells to create organotypic models. The TNBC vasculature model introduces a method for tissue engineering a bulk tumor continuum and sets the stage for future experiments comparing features of tumor vasculature in model iterations constructed with multiple cancer subtypes, multiple donors of the same subtype, or before and after exposure to chemotherapeutics. Lastly, the easily adaptable approach to engineer complex models of perfusable tumor vasculature will enable the investigation of pharmacologic interventions in a more pathophysiologically relevant context, including the delivery of nanoparticle therapeutics and cytotoxic T cell infusions.
