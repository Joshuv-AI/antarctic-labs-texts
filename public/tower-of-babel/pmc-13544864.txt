“Cingulate Island” Sign in a Patient with Reversible Parkinsonism due to a Portosystemic Shunt
Authors: Arnab Adhya, Divyani Garg, Ayush Agarwal, Madhavi Tripathi, Ajay Garg, Achal K Srivastava
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Atypical parkinsonism comprises a group of neurodegenerative disorders marked by red flags such as poor levodopa responsiveness and more rapid progression compared to idiopathic Parkinson’s disease.[1] Rarely, secondary causes can mimic atypical parkinsonian syndromes; identifying these is crucial, as they may be reversible. Acquired hepatocerebral degeneration (AHD)—a form of chronic liver disease—can present with parkinsonism, ataxia, and chorea, along with cognitive and behavioral symptoms, and is one such etiology.[2] We report a patient who presented with fluctuating alertness, cognitive impairment, and parkinsonism, along with the “cingulate island” (CIS) sign on positron emission tomography (PET) imaging due to AHD. This initially prompted consideration of dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)[3]; however, identification and treatment of portosystemic shunting due to underlying cryptogenic cirrhosis resulted in a striking recovery.

A 65-year-old man presented with a one-year history of progressive generalized slowness in activities, associated with limb rigidity and postural instability. He had also developed cognitive impairment, predominantly apathy and inattentiveness. Over the preceding six months, he experienced three discrete episodes of reduced sensorium, with deficits involving visuospatial, executive, and working memory domains. These episodes occurred at intervals of 2–3 months, had no identifiable precipitating factors, lasted 2–3 days, and were followed by gradual spontaneous recovery to baseline. Notably, during the last two episodes, he developed formed visual hallucinations and a history suggestive of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder. He presented to our center a few days after the most recent episode. He had no medical comorbidities or addictions and no relevant family history.

Examination revealed symmetrical parkinsonism, with rest and postural tremors in the upper limbs. Detailed cognitive examination revealed significant impairment in attention, fluency, motor Luria sequencing, and visuospatial domains. The remainder of the neurological and systemic examination was unremarkable. A trial of levodopa had been administered at an outside center (total daily levodopa equivalent dose, 600 mg) for four months, without response.

Routine blood tests showed a normal hemogram; hepatic, renal, and thyroid function; vitamin B12 level; serum ceruloplasmin; and 24-hour urinary copper, with negative human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and venereal disease research laboratory serology. Serum autoimmune and paraneoplastic panels were negative, and cerebrospinal fluid was normal, with no evidence of inflammation or infection. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed enlarged Virchow–Robin spaces and Fazekas grade 2 white matter changes [Figure 1]. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) of the brain demonstrated bilaterally symmetrical hypometabolism in the parieto-occipital and frontal lobes, with sparing of the cingulate cortex, consistent with the CIS sign in DLB, with a CIS rating score of 3 [Figure 2]. Although he had no history of liver disease or alcohol use, serum ammonia—checked in view of fluctuating sensorium—was elevated (97 µmol/L; normal, 15–40 µmol/L). Abdominal ultrasound showed increased liver echogenicity, and FibroScan findings were consistent with cirrhosis. Triple-phase computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen revealed a 13 mm spontaneous portosystemic shunt (SPSS) connecting the left splenic vein to the left renal vein, resulting in decompression of the portal system. A diagnosis of episodic hepatic encephalopathy secondary to a spontaneous portosystemic shunt due to cirrhosis (type C hepatic encephalopathy) was made. The patient underwent shunt closure, following which there was significant clinical improvement. However, the CIS sign persisted on follow-up PET imaging at three months. At one-year follow-up, he remained symptom-free. Motor examination was normal, and cognitive assessment showed a Montreal Cognitive Assessment score of 30/30 with normal performance across lobar domains.

Our case illustrates a classic diagnostic dilemma in the geriatric population, in whom concomitant illnesses are common. As the patient initially presented with progressive cognitive decline impairing activities of daily living over one year, we considered differentials for rapidly progressive dementia.[4] The main possibilities included immune-mediated disorders (e.g., anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), IgLON5 antibodies) and metabolic derangements. In addition, given the presence of parkinsonism with clear fluctuations, a history of REM sleep behavior disorder, and formed visual hallucinations, DLB was also considered,[3] particularly in the context of characteristic cingulate sparing on PET imaging. The elevated serum ammonia level was the key clue that led to the final diagnosis of AHD. The term AHD, coined by Victor et al.,[5] refers to a chronic neurological disorder characterized by basal ganglia dysfunction and cognitive decline due to cirrhosis, clinically distinct from the more acute presentation of hepatic encephalopathy and associated with normal copper metabolism. In one of the largest series of AHD (11 patients) from a liver transplant unit, all patients had symmetric atypical parkinsonism, with a rapid mean time to peak of 7.2 months.[6] Approximately 20% of patients with AHD develop parkinsonism. Excess manganese deposition in the basal ganglia and substantia nigra in the setting of hepatic dysfunction may underlie the development of parkinsonism. SPSS can cause reduced sensorium by diverting intestinal ammonia into the systemic circulation, causing hepatic encephalopathy.[7] A high index of suspicion is required, particularly in patients without a known history of liver disease or risk factors such as alcohol use. SPSS-related parkinsonism can improve substantially after shunt closure; however, outcomes depend on the duration of the shunt and whether structural changes (e.g., manganese deposition or neurodegeneration) have already occurred. Accordingly, the absence of hepatolenticular degeneration or manganese deposition is a favorable prognostic sign.

Interestingly, the CIS was observed in our patient. The CIS sign is classically described in DLB and denotes relative preservation of the mid- to posterior cingulate cortex compared with the surrounding parietal and occipital cortices, particularly when the CIS rating score is above 1.[8] In a study differentiating DLB from Alzheimer’s disease, CIS demonstrated 62–86% sensitivity and 100% specificity.[9] The occurrence of CIS in the setting of AHD has not been reported to date. Long-term follow-up will be informative to determine whether AHD in this patient eventually “unmasks” underlying DLB.

Our case highlights the importance of considering reversible metabolic disorders in patients with rapid cognitive decline, especially in elderly individuals in whom multiple conditions may overlap.
