Wild refitting for black box prediction


Abstract
We describe and analyze a computionally efficient refitting procedure for computing high-probability upper bounds on the instance-wise mean-squared prediction error of penalized nonparametric estimates based on least-squares minimization. Requiring only a single dataset and black box access to the prediction method, it consists of three steps: computing suitable residuals, symmetrizing and scaling them with a pre-factor ρ \rho , and using them to define and solve a modified prediction problem recentered at the current estimate. We refer to it as wild refitting, since it uses Rademacher residual symmetrization as in a wild bootstrap variant. Under relatively mild conditions allowing for noise heterogeneity, we establish a high probability guarantee on its performance, showing that the wild refit with a suitably chosen wild noise scale ρ \rho gives an upper bound on prediction error. This theoretical analysis provides guidance into the design of such procedures, including how the residuals should be formed, the amount of noise rescaling in the wild sub-problem needed for upper bounds, and the local stability properties of the block-box procedure. We illustrate the applicability of this procedure to various problems, including non-rigid structure-from-motion recovery with structured matrix penalties; plug-and-play image restoration with deep neural network priors; and randomized sketching with kernel methods.

Introduction

Prediction problems are ubiquitous throughout science and engineering.
They are defined by a feature vector or covariate
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
a scalar response
Y\in\real
, and the goal is to determine a
function
x\mapsto\widehat{f}(x)
that is a “good approximation” to
y

in a certain sense. When the quality of an estimate is measured by
its mean-squared error, then it is a classical fact that the best
predictor is given by the regression function
f^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]
. The quality of a given estimate
\widehat{f}
can then be
measured by its mean-squared error or risk
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.04553pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{f}):\,=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\big[(Y-\widehat{f}(X))^{2}
, or its excess risk

\displaystyle\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.51776pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle:\,=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\big[(Y-\widehat{f}(X))^{2}\Big]}_{\bar{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{f})}-\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\big[(Y-f^{*}(X))^{2}\big].

(1)

Here the expectations are taken over a future sample
(X,Y)
,
independent of any training data used to produce
\widehat{f}
. Since

f^{*}
is the optimal predictor in the mean-squared sense, the
excess risk is always non-negative.

1.1
Methods for risk estimation

Given a predictor
\widehat{f}
that has been fit on some training data, an
important problem in practice is to estimate the excess risk

\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.51776pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f})
, or simply the risk
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.04553pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{f})
itself. Given its
importance, this problem has been intensively studied, with a variety
of approaches available. The simplest and most classical approach is
the
hold-out method
; we split the original dataset into two
separate pieces, and use the first piece to fit the estimate
\widehat{f}
,
and the second piece to obtain an unbiased estimate of the risk

\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.04553pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{f})
. However, the hold-out approach suffers from a number
of deficiencies, notably its wastefulness in data use. A more
sophisticated approach, dating back to the classic
papers
[
23
,
10
]
, is
cross-validation; see the paper
[
2
]
for a modern
overview. Cross-validation involves repeated splitting of the dataset
into parts used for training and testing; in this way, it avoids the
wasteful use of data associated with the hold-out method. On the flip
side, it is significantly more computationally expensive; for example,
roughly speaking, a CV approach based on
k
-splits will be require

k
-times more computation than hold-out. Note that hold-out and
cross-validation apply to i.i.d. datasets

There are
relaxations of the i.i.d. condition, but the essential requirement is
that the risks across different splits of the dataset are
equivalent.
, so that risks are preserved in expectation across
splits. It is also possible to perform risk estimation via various
types of resampling methods, including both parametric and
non-parametric forms of the
bootstrap
[
8
,
9
]
. Of
particular relevance to this paper is the
wild bootstrap

(e.g.,
[
29
,
17
,
16
,
18
]
),
a bootstrapping procedure that is well-suited to heteroskedastic data;
we discuss it at more length below. For problems with particular

one is willing to assume that the effective noise

follows a Gaussian distribution, then Stein’s unbiased estimate of
risk

, along with the associated family of

[

, be used for

[

.

It should be noted that many methods—among them cross-validation and
various types of covariance-based penalties—do
not
provide
estimates of the risk

; this risk is itself is a random
variable, due to the data used to train
\widehat{f}
. Instead, such methods
are targeting the deterministic quantity obtained by averaging

the corresponding excess risk
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.51776pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f})

1.2
Contributions

This paper is motivated by the goal of obtaining a method for risk
estimation with the following properties:

(a)

For a given predictor
\widehat{f}
, it provides an estimate of
the instance-wise risk
\widehat{R}_{n}(\widehat{f})
(or the instance-wise excess
risk
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.51776pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f})
), and this estimate is equipped with an
explicit and non-asymptotic guarantee.

(b)

It allows for heteroskedasticity, and other forms of
heterogeneity in the distribution of pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
used to fit
the predictor
\widehat{f}
.

(c)

It requires only black-box access to the statistical method

\mathcal{M}
for producing predictors—that is, we can re-fit the method
for a new dataset, but we have no visibility into its inner
structure.

(d)

It makes a limited number of queries to the method
\mathcal{M}
.

Let us provide some motivation for these desiderata. Beginning with
requirement (a), it is desirable to target the instance-wise risk

\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.04553pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{f})
, since it correctly reflects the mean-squared error
that will achieved by applying the given predictor
\widehat{f}
—with the
underlying training data held fixed—on future data. Non-asymptotic
bounds are desirable in that they are explicit and hold for all sample
sizes (as opposed to an asymptotic statement). Re requirement (b),
modern datasets often exhibit various forms of heterogeneity, so it is
desirable to weaken independence or exchangeability assumptions as
much as possible. As for the black-box requirement in item (c), many
methods in modern statistics and machine learning are relatively
complex, and it is desirable to have methods for risk estimation that
do not require explicit knowledge about their structure. Deep neural
networks, which are widely used for prediction, are a canonical
instance of such an opaque model. Lastly, the limited query condition
is item (d) is also important in modern statistical practice; the
training process required to fit a method
\mathcal{M}
can be quite expensive,
so it is undesirable to have a procedure (e.g., such as a typical
bootstrap) that requires repeated re-fitting of the model a large number
of times.

In this paper, we describe a procedure for risk estimation that is
equipped with properties (a) through (d). It is inspired by a classic
line of work on the wild bootstrap
(e.g.,
[
29
,
17
,
16
,
19
]
), and for this reason, we refer to it as the

wild refitting procedure
. At its core is a form of Rademacher
symmetrization applied to a set of estimated residuals;
see
Section

2.3
for a detailed description. This type of
Rademacher symmetrization is standard in the analysis of uniform laws
of large numbers and related empirical
processes
[
26
,
25
,
28
]
. In the context of the
wild bootstrap, it has also been proposed and
studied
[
17
,
19
,
18
]
,
with theoretical results given on its asymptotic behavior. In
contrast, we analyze this form of Rademacher in a non-asymptotic
setting, making esssential use of the sharp concentration properties
of Lipschitz functions of Rademacher variables
[
15
]
.

2
Problem set-up

In this section, we begin by setting up the problem more precisely,
before describing the wild refitting procedure that we study in this paper.

2.1
M
-estimation and fixed design prediction

Given a covariate-response pair
(X,Y)
, the optimal function

f^{*}
for generating predictions
x\mapsto\widehat{y}
is given by the

regression function

f^{*}(x):\,=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]
.
Thus, given a collection of
n
covariate-response
pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
, they can viewed as generated from the model

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=f^{*}(x_{i})+w_{i}\qquad\mbox{for $i=1,\ldots,n$}

where each
w_{i}
is a realization of a conditionally zero-mean random
variable
W_{i}
(i.e., such that
\mathbb{E}[W_{i}\mid X_{i}=x_{i}]=0
.) The
focus of this paper is the
fixed design
case, where we
condition upon the given realization
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of the
covariates, and compute prediction errors using the empirical
distribution
\mathbb{P}_{n}
that places mass
1/n
at each
x_{i}
.

On one hand, this type of conditional analysis can be performed for
any prediction problem; moreover, there are many prediction problems
where it is most natural to view the covariates as fixed. Examples
include prediction of a time series, where covariates correspond to
particular points in time; and prediction in images or video, where
covariates correspond to particular two-dimensional spatial positions
(for an image), or 2D-space-time positions (for a video).

Prediction via
M
-estimation:

In this paper, we study prediction methods that are specified by a
real-valued class
\mathscr{F}
of functions on the covariate space

\mathcal{X}
, and a penalty function
\mathcal{P}:\mathscr{F}\rightarrow\real
.
Given a data set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we obtain the fitted
predictor
\widehat{f}
by solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\widehat{f}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathscr{F}}\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_{i}-f(x_{i})\big)^{2}+\mathcal{P}(f)\Big\}.

(2)

In general, procedures of this type are referred to as non-parametric

M
-estimators; in this paper, we are analyzing the case of
non-parametric and penalized least-squares.

Let us summarize some shorthand notation that we use throughout the
remainder of this paper. For a pair of real-valued functions
f
and

g
defined on the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
, we define the squared
norm

\displaystyle\|f-g\|^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle:\,=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(x_{i})-g(x_{i})\big)^{2}

(3a)

along with the associated inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,\,g\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle:\,=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})g(x_{i}).

(3b)

2.2
Excess risk and optimism

Given a predictor
\widehat{f}
, recall the
definition (
1
) of its population excess risk.
Given the fixed design set-up of this paper, this population excess
risk takes the form

\displaystyle\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.51776pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\big)^{2}}_{\equiv\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}},

(4a)

corresponding to the
mean-squared error
or MSE between
\widehat{f}

and
f^{*}
evaluated on the fixed covariates

\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

In analogy to the population excess risk (
1
),
given a data set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we can also define
the
empirical excess risk
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle:\,=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\widehat{f}(x_{i}))^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f^{*}(x_{i}))^{2}.

(4b)

In contrast to the population case, the empirical excess risk need not
be non-negative.

The difference between the population and empirical excess risks
defines the
optimism
of an estimate
\widehat{f}
. For the
least-squares objective considered here, some simple algebra yields

\displaystyle\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.51776pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\widehat{f})+2\,\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f}):\,=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\big(\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\big)}_{\equiv\langle w,\,\widehat{f}-f^{*}\rangle_{n}},

(5)

where
w_{i}:\,=y_{i}-f^{*}(x_{i})
is the
effective noise in the regression model. Thus, in order to obtain an
upper bound on the population excess risk, it suffices to obtain an
upper bound on the optimism
\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})
.

2.3
Wild refitting

The main contribution of this paper is to demonstrate the
effectiveness—both empirically and theoretically—of a simple
refitting procedure for computing upper bounds on the optimism, and
hence the population excess risk. As noted earlier, we refer to this
method as
wild refitting
, since like one variant of the wild
bootstrap (e.g.,
[
29
,
17
,
16
,
19
]
), it makes use of random signs (Rademacher
variables) to symmetrize an appropriate set of residuals, and also
allows for general heteroskedasticity. Note that
\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})
is a
random variable, since it depends on both the noise terms
w_{i}

and estimate
\widehat{f}
itself. Thus, the wild refitting procedure also
returns a random variable.

While our theoretical analysis focuses on
M
-estimators of the
form (
2
), the procedure itself applies to any statistical
method
\mathcal{M}
that uses an
n
-vector
y
of responses to
compute a predictor
\widehat{f}:\,=\mathcal{M}(y)
belonging to some function
space
\mathscr{F}
. Any instantiation of the wild refitting procedure
depends on a recentering predictor
\widetilde{f}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\real

and a scalar noise level
\rho>0
. It requires only black-box
access to the estimator
\mathcal{M}
, that we can compute the function

\mathcal{M}(u)\in\mathscr{F}
for any possible response vector
u\in\real^{n}
.

, along with the Rademacher
sequence

introduced as part of the

establish conditions under which the quantities defining this wild

excess risk.

that hold based on a
single realization
of the Rademacher
vector
\varepsilon\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
. Thus, we do not provide
distributional guarantees, but instead a non-asymptotic guarantee.
Moreover, the wild refitting procedure only requires a single call to
the prediction method
\mathcal{M}
, as laid out in item (d) of our
motivating desiderata (see
Section

1.2
).

2.4
A simple illustration: Tikhonov versus TV regularization

So as to provide intuition, let us begin by considering a simple form
of model selection in the context of non-parametric regression. Let

f^{*}:[0,1]\rightarrow\real

n

\displaystyle y_{i}=f^{*}(x_{i})+w_{i}\qquad\mbox{where $x_{i}=i/n$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$,}

and
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is an i.i.d. sequence of zero-mean noise
variables. Various classical approaches are based on

of the form (

3
Analysis and non-asymptotic guarantees

We now turn to some analysis of the wild refitting procedure, in
particular in the form of non-asymptotic guarantees on the wild
optimism. In this section, we prove two main results:

•

Theorem

1
gives an upper bound on the true optimism
in terms of the wild optimism
\widetilde{\mathrm{Opt}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}(f^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho})
evaluated at an
appropriate wild noise scale
\rho
. However, this choice depends
on the estimation error
\widehat{r}_{n}:\,=\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}
,
which is unknown.

•

Theorem

2
closes this gap by showing how wild
refitting can be used to generate a high-probability upper bound on

\widehat{r}_{n}
. This bound provides guidance on the setting of wild
noise scale for which the guarantee from
Theorem

1

holds.

3.1
Problem set-up and wild complexity

Our analysis applies to an estimate
\widehat{f}
generated by solving an
optimization problem of the form (
2
). Given this
structure, it is natural to define

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{f\in\mathscr{F}}\Big\{\|f-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\mathcal{P}(f)\Big\},

(9)

corresponding to the best penalized approximation to
f^{*}
in the
absence of any noise. Note that
f^{\dagger}
is a deterministic function,
since we are viewing the covariates as fixed.

3.1.1
Assumptions

We now describe the assumptions that underlie our analysis.

Firm non-expansiveness:

For a given function
f\in\mathscr{F}
, introduce the convenient
shorthand
f(x_{1}^{n}):\,=\big(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n})\big)\in\real^{n}
.
In the bulk of our analysis, we focus on penalty
functions
\mathcal{P}:\mathscr{F}\rightarrow\real^{+}
of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(f)

\displaystyle:\,=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $f(x_{1}^{n})\in\mathcal{C}$,
and}\\
+\infty&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(10)

where
\mathcal{C}\subset\real^{n}
is a given compact subset of

n
. In future work, we will explore theoretical
extensions to regularized estimators in the Lagrangian case.

Our analysis requires that the resulting method
\mathcal{M}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathscr{F}
, as defined in by equation (
2
), be

firmly non-expansive
around
f^{*}
. More precisely, noting
that
f^{\dagger}=\mathcal{M}(f^{*}(x_{1}^{n}))
by assumption, we require that
the estimate
\widetilde{f}=\mathcal{M}(f^{*}(x_{1}^{n})+u)
obtained from via any
perturbation vector
u\in\real^{n}
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\underbrace{\|\mathcal{M}\big(f^{*}(x_{1}^{n})+u\big)-\mathcal{M}\big(f^{*}(x_{1}^{n})\big)\|_{n}^{2}}_{\equiv\|\widetilde{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\leq\;\;\langle u,\,\widetilde{f}-f^{\dagger}\rangle_{n}.

(11a)

When the set
\mathcal{C}
is convex, then this firm-expansiveness follows
from standard optimization-theoretic properties of projections onto
convex sets (e.g.,
[
12
]
). It is worth noting, however,
that a local property of this type can hold more generally for certain
non-convex sets (e.g., prox-regular sets
[
21
]
).

Noise conditions:

Recall that our observations
take the form
y_{i}=f^{*}(x_{i})+w_{i}
, where each
w_{i}
is an
additive noise variable. We
require that

Conditioned on
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
:

(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
are independent, each with symmetric
distribution.

(11b)

The conditional symmetry assumption implies that
\mathbb{E}[w_{i}\mid x_{i}]=0
, a fact that we exploit in our analysis. When the symmetry
condition fails to hold, the bounds that we give here all hold with an
increased constant factor. (Essentially, we lose a factor of two in
order to handle non-symmetric noise, since we need to introduce two
separate copies of
w
and
w^{\prime}
, and take their difference in order to
form the symmetrized analog.)

Remark:
Other than the
conditions (
11b
), we impose no other assumptions on the
noise variables. In particular, they are permitted to be
heteroskedastic and/or exhibit highly non-Gaussian tail behavior.

3.1.2
From wild optimism to wild complexity

For future reference, it is convenient to highlight an important
connection between the wild optimism, and a function
r\mapsto W_{n}(r)
that we refer to as the wild complexity. Recall that the

at noise scale
\rho

is

Here

3.2
Bounding the true optimism

With these ingredients in place, we are now ready to state a
high-probability bound on the true optimism
\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})
in terms of
the wild optimism
\widetilde{\mathrm{Opt}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}(f^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho})
.

Theorem 1

(Wild optimism bound)
.

Under the noise condition (
11b
), consider any radius

r
such that
\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}\leq r
,
and let
\rho>0
be the noise scale for which
\|f^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho}-\widehat{f}\|_{n}=2r
.
Then for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{Opt}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}(f^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho})\;+\;\big\{H_{n}(t)+A_{n}(\widetilde{f})\big\}\quad\mbox{with
prob. at least $1-4e^{-t^{2}}$,}

(14)

where the probability deviation term is given by

\displaystyle H_{n}(t):\,=\big\{3r+\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}\big\}\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}

(15a)

and the pilot error is given by

\displaystyle A_{n}(\widetilde{f}):\,=\sup\limits_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widetilde{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i}))\>(f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})).

(15b)

See
Section

5.1
for the proof of
this theorem.

To interpret this result, note that inequality (
14
)
shows that the true optimism
\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})
can be upper bounded by the
wild optimism for an
appropriately chosen

\rho
, along with

15a
) and the pilot error

). Let us comment on these two terms.

Probability deviation term:

The guarantee holds with exponentially high probability (i.e.,

) in the deviation term

; this sharp
tail behavior arises due to the highly favorable concentration
behavior of Rademacher variables used the wild refitting procedure.
Our proof exploits concentration inequalities for convex and Lipschitz
functions of Rademacher variables so as to preserve this behavior. The
function
H_{n}(t)
defined in
equation (
15a
) scales as

, so that the distributional properties of
the additive noise vector

enter purely via the
sup-norm
\|w\|_{\infty}
. This fact means that it is possible to
handle noise with tail behavior much heavier than Gaussian. For
example, consider a noise distribution that has only a finite
polynomial moment—say
\mathbb{E}|w|^{k}\leq C
for some
k>2
. An easy
calculation shows that
\mathbb{E}\|w\|_{\infty}\leq(Cn)^{1/k}
, with
similar bounds for the event
\{\|w\|_{\infty}\geq s\}
, so
that
Theorem

1
can still yield non-trivial guarantees.
We illustrate the behavior of the wild-refitting procedure for
heavy-tailed Student

-noise in
Section

4.2
; in particular,
see
Figure

7
.

Now let us consider the pilot error
term (
15b

optimism. From the equivalence (
13

14

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{Opt}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}(f^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho})

\displaystyle=W_{n}(2r)\;=\;\sup\limits_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{w}_{i}(f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})).

By comparison with the definition (

), we see that
the pilot error has the same form, with the residual

Consequently, as long as the pilot function

is a reasonable

—so that the difference

is typically

—then we can expect that the pilot error
is dominated by
\widetilde{\mathrm{Opt}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}(f^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho})
.

3.3
Bounding the estimation error

The key assumption in
Theorem

1
is the existence of some
known
r
such that
r\geq\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|_{n}
. Thus, it
becomes useful only in so far as we can obtain an upper bound on the

statistical estimation error

\widehat{r}_{n}:\,=\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}
. This quantity corresponds to the error associated with
estimating the best approximation
f^{\dagger}
to
f^{*}
from the chosen
function class
\mathscr{F}
(cf. equation (
9
)). In this
section, we describe an explicit procedure that can be used to obtain
upper bounds on
\widehat{r}_{n}
. It involves an inequality defined in the
terms of the wild complexity
W_{n}(r)
from
equation (
12b
).

Theorem 2

(Estimation error bounds via wild complexity)
.

Under the non-expansive condition (
11a
) and the noise
condition (
11b
), for any
t\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{W_{n}\big(\big(2+\tfrac{1}{t}\big)\widehat{r}_{n}\big),\;\>\tfrac{t^{4}}{n}\Big\}+\tfrac{6\|w\|_{\infty}}{t}\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}+A_{n}(\widetilde{f})

(16a)

with probability at least
1-2e^{-t^{2}}
. Moreover, if
\mathscr{F}
is
convex, then with the same probability, for any noise scale
\rho>0

4.2.1
Set-up

Let us begin by setting up the denoising problem using the notation of
this paper. In abstract terms, we can represent an 2D-image as a
function
f^{*}:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\real
. The digitized version
of the image—one that we would view on a computer—can be thought
of as a finite set of function evaluations: fixing some set of
covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
contained within the unit square,
the digitized image is defined by the function evaluations

\{f^{*}(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In a typical case, the covariates
would form a regular grid pattern on the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
.

In a denoising problem, we observe
n
samples of the form
y_{i}=f^{*}(x_{i})+w_{i}
for some additive noise variables
w_{i}

satisfying the noise conditions (
11b
), and our goal is
to recover an accurate estimate
\widehat{f}
of the original image

f^{*}
. A broad class of methods are based on solving an
optimization problem of the form (
2
), for a suitably
penalty function
\mathcal{P}:\mathscr{F}\rightarrow\real
. Often, the
exponentiated quantity
e^{-\mathcal{P}(f)}
is interpreted (modulo
normalization) as a prior over the space of images. In these
approaches to image denoising—now known as plug-and-play
methods
[
27
,
6
,
11
,
13
]
—the
function
\mathcal{P}
(or its gradient
\nabla\mathcal{P}
) is learned from a
large image database, typically using deep neural networks. This
penalty function is then combined with iterative optimization methods
(e.g., proximal gradient, ADMM etc.) so as to solve the underlying
optimization problem (
2
). We refer the reader to Kamilov
et al.
[
14
]
for a survey overview.

In order to explore the use of wild refitting for plug-and-play
denoising, we used a Python implementation of a proximal plug-and-play
method due to Hurault et al.
[
13
]
. It provides a
sequence of penalty functions
\{\mathcal{P}_{\gamma},\gamma\geq 0\}
,
indexed by the
smoothness parameter

\gamma
(corresponding to
noise level used in training the deep net prior). In our experiments,
we explore the use of wild refitting to automatically select this
tuning parameter so as to minimize the MSE of the resulting
reconstruction
\widehat{f}\equiv\widehat{f}_{\gamma}
.

Using wild refitting for smoothing parameter selection:

In practice, given a noisy image
y
, we would like an automated way
of choosing the bandwidth parameter so that the estimate
\widehat{f}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(y)
has smallest MSE. The
oracle choice
is to
choose

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}:\,=\arg\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\|\widehat{f}_{\gamma}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}.

(21a)

However, it is not implementable, since the true image
f^{*}
is
unknown. However, for a suitable choice of
\rho
, wild refitting
gives us an upper bound
B_{\rho}(\gamma)
on the MSE
\|\widehat{f}_{\gamma}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
, and so we can attempt to mimic the oracle rule by
instead choosing the value

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho}:\,=\arg\min_{\gamma\geq 0}B_{\rho}(\gamma).

(21b)

We can then compare the oracle MSE
\|\widehat{f}_{\gamma^{*}}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
to the MSE
\|\widehat{f}_{\gamma^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho}}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
obtained from the wild refitting choice

\gamma^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}_{\rho}
.

5.1
Proof of
Theorem

1

Our proof involves relating the optimism and its wild variant
via the intermediate quantities

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}^{\dagger}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\big(\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big),\quad\mbox{and}

(22a)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle:\,=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(f^{\dagger})}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}\big(f(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big)\Big].

(22b)

The following lemma shows how they can be used to bound the
optimism:

Lemma 2
.

For any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Opt}^{\dagger}(\widehat{f})+\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}\>\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}\qquad\mbox{with probability at least $1-e^{-t^{2}}$.}

(23a)

Moreover, for any
r\geq\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}
and any
t>0
,
we have

\displaystyle\max\big\{\mathrm{Opt}^{\dagger}(\widehat{f}),Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]+r\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}.

(23b)

with probability at least
1-2e^{-t^{2}}
.

See
Section

5.2.1
for the proof of this claim.

Based on
Lemma

2
, we see that for any
r\geq\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}
, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]+\big\{r+\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}\big\}\;\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}

(24)

with probability at least
1-3e^{-t^{2}}
.
Our next step is
to relate the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]
to the wild
optimism
\widetilde{\mathrm{Opt}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.4pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245759}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.98pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.99998pt}{\resizebox{245760}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.7pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{$\blacklozenge$}$}}}}}}(\widehat{f})
. In order to do so, we make use of the
intermediate family of random variables

\displaystyle W^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{777917}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{777917}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{678955}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{678955}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}}_{n}(r)

\displaystyle:\,=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}\big(f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\big)\Big].

and

.

We have the deterministic bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[W^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{782769}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{782769}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{661497}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{660811}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}}_{n}(r+\widehat{r}_{n})]

along with the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[W^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{782769}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{782769}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{661497}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{660811}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}}_{n}(r)]

\displaystyle\leq W^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{782769}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{782769}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{661497}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{660811}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}}_{n}(r)+r\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}\qquad\mbox{with probability at least $1-e^{-t^{2}}$.}

(26b)

See
Section

5.2.2
for the proof of this claim.

imply that

\displaystyle\leq W^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{777917}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{777917}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{678955}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{678955}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}}_{n}(2r)+(2r)\;\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}

.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle\leq W^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{777917}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{777917}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{678955}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{678955}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{ideal}$}}}}}}_{n}(2r)+\big\{3r+\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}\big\}\;\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}

with probability at least

. Our final step is to
relate

5.2.1
Proof of

We split our analysis into two parts, corresponding to the

Proof of the bound (
23a
):

Beginning with the definition of the optimism (
5
),
we can add and subtract terms involving
f^{\dagger}
, thereby obtaining

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}^{\star}(\widehat{f})=\underbrace{\langle w,\,\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\rangle_{n}}_{\mathrm{Opt}^{\dagger}(\widehat{f})}+\underbrace{\langle w,\,f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\rangle_{n}}_{T}.

It remains to bound the term

. Since variable
w_{i}
has
a distribution symmetric around zero, we can write
w_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}|w_{i}|
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
is a random
sign (Rademacher variable) independent of
|w_{i}|
. Conditioning
on the absolute values, we can represent

as a realization of
the linear (and hence convex) function

\displaystyle\big(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto G(\varepsilon):\,=\langle\varepsilon|w|,\,f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\rangle_{n}\;=\;\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}|w_{i}|\big(f^{\dagger}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\big)\Big\}.

Letting
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
be two Rademacher sequences,
applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\big|G(\varepsilon)-G(\varepsilon^{\prime})\big|\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime})\,|w_{i}|\big(f^{\dagger}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}\big(f^{\dagger}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\big)^{1/2}\;\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\|w\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}\,\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}

showing that the function
G
is
L
-Lipschitz with
L:\,=\tfrac{\|w\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}
.
Since
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[G(\varepsilon)]=0
, standard concentration
results for convex/Lipschitz functions of Rademacher variables
(cf. Theorem 3.24 in the book
[
28
]
) guarantee that

\displaystyle G(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{2\|w\|_{\infty}t}{\sqrt{n}}\;\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}\qquad\mbox{with probability at least $1-e^{-t^{2}}$.}

Proof of
the bound (
23b
):

We condition on the vector

|w|
of absolute values throughout this proof. Letting

\varepsilon\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
be an i.i.d. vector of Rademacher
variables, we can view the random variable

as a
function

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mapsto H(\varepsilon):\,=Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r):\,=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(f^{\dagger})}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}|w_{i}|\big(f(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big)\Big].

As in our previous proof, we can write

, where
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
is a Rademacher
variable independent of
|w_{i}|
. Thus, we recognize the random
variable

as a particular realization of the random variable
defined by

. Consequently, bounding the difference

is equivalent to establishing an upper tail

in terms of its expectation

. In particular, the claim is equivalent to

with probability at least

.

In order to establish this claim, we again make use of concentration
bounds for convex and Lipschitz functions of independent bounded
random variables (cf. Theorem 3.24 in the book
[
28
]
). As a
supremum of linear functions, the function

is convex. The claimed
bound follows as long as we can establish the Lipschitz bound

\displaystyle|H(\varepsilon)-H(\varepsilon^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}\qquad\mbox{for $L=\tfrac{\|w\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}r$,}

valid for any pair of Rademacher vectors
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
.

Let

by any function that achieves the supremum defining

\displaystyle H(\varepsilon)-H(\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}|w_{i}|\big(g(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big)\Big]-\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}|w_{i}|\big(g(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\big)|w_{i}|\big(g(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big).

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle H(\varepsilon)-H(\varepsilon^{\prime})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{n}\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}\big(g(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\big\}^{1/2}\;\big(\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|w\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\|g-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\|w\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\,r\,\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}.

where the final bound

follows since

. The same argument applies with the roles of
\varepsilon
and

\varepsilon^{\prime}
reversed, from which the claimed Lipschitz

) follows.
