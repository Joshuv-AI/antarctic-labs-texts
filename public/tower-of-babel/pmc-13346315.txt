Integrating Network Pharmacology and Experimental Validation to Elucidate the Role of Huaier in Attenuating Liver Fibrosis via the AKT Signalling Pathway
Authors: Jiachun Ding, Jiaqiang Ren, Fan Chen, Ye Lu, Yifei Ma, Ting Zhang, Zehua Shao, Huijing Tian, Siyu Wang, Zhenchao Gao, Yiqun Song, Jiahui Zeng, Jiaoxing Wu, Zhengyuan Feng, Cancan Zhou, Zheng Wang, Weikun Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver fibrosis, a critical pathological precursor of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, represents a significant unmet global health challenge because of the lack of effective targeted therapies that can reverse established fibrotic scarring. An integrated strategy was employed. Network pharmacology was used to identify potential targets, followed by molecular docking and dynamics simulations. In vivo validation utilized two established murine models: carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and bile duct ligation (BDL)-induced fibrosis. In vitro studies employed human LX-2 hepatic stellate cells to assess the antifibrotic effects on myofibroblasts. Huaier administration significantly attenuated liver fibrosis, improved liver function and reduced collagen deposition in both animal models. Histopathological analysis confirmed diminished inflammatory infiltration and fibrotic scarring. In vitro, Huaier suppressed LX-2 cell proliferation and migration. Bioinformatics and simulation analyses suggested AKT1 as a central target, with high-affinity binding with the bioactive steroidal components of Huaier. Mechanistically, Huaier specifically inhibited the phosphorylation and activation of the AKT signalling pathway in hepatic myofibroblasts. This study provides the first compelling evidence that Huaier granules alleviate liver fibrosis by targeting the AKT pathway to inhibit myofibroblast activation. Introduction

Liver fibrosis, a common pathological outcome of chronic liver injury, is characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, which can progress to cirrhosis, liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma, posing a significant threat to global health [1, 2]. Despite advances in understanding its molecular mechanisms, which primarily involve the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and complex cytokine networks, effective antifibrotic therapies remain a major unmet medical need [3, 4]. Current strategies predominantly focus on removing the underlying aetiology, such as antiviral therapy for viral hepatitis [5]. However, this approach often has limited efficacy in reversing established fibrosis, and for many patients, the causative agent cannot be eliminated [6]. Consequently, the development of targeted and effective pharmacological agents to halt or reverse the fibrogenic process is an urgent priority and a significant challenge in hepatology [7, 8].

Huaier (scientific name: Trametes robiniophila Murr.), a traditional medicinal fungus, has been used in China for centuries [9]. Its extract, the active component of the commercial preparation Huaier granule, is approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China as an adjuvant therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma [10]. Modern pharmacological studies have revealed that Huaier is rich in various bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides, proteoglycans and amino acids, endowing it with a wide spectrum of biological activities, such as immunomodulatory, antitumour, anti-inflammatory and antiviral effects [11]. Notably, in the context of liver diseases, clinical and experimental studies have demonstrated that Huaier granules can improve liver function and inhibit tumour cell proliferation, revealing their therapeutic potential and safety profile [12].

Emerging evidence suggests that Huaier holds promise for the treatment of liver fibrosis [13]. A pioneering multicentre trial explored the potential of Huaier granules, a traditional Chinese medicine, as an adjuvant therapy following curative resection of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Conducting across 39 centres with 1,044 patients, a randomized phase IV study demonstrated a significant improvement in recurrence-free survival and overall survival rates in the treatment group compared with those in the control group [10]. These findings provide compelling evidence that Huaier granules effectively reduce both intrahepatic and extrahepatic tumour recurrence, offering a promising postoperative strategy for HCC management. Its multifaceted actions, particularly its potent anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory properties, make it a compelling candidate for targeting the complex pathogenesis of fibrosis. However, the existing body of research is still in its early stages. The precise molecular targets, the key effective components responsible for its antifibrotic effects, and the comprehensive mechanisms of action remain largely elusive [14]. This lack of a systematic understanding hinders its potential application as a targeted antifibrotic therapy.

Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically investigate the therapeutic effects of Huaier on liver fibrosis and elucidate its underlying mechanisms. To achieve this goal, we employed an innovative integrated strategy combining network pharmacology with in vivo validation in a mouse model. Network pharmacology was used to predict the potential active compounds, key targets and signalling pathways involved in the antifibrotic activity of Huaier [15]. These predictions were subsequently experimentally validated in a CCl4- and BDL-induced mouse model of liver fibrosis through histopathological and molecular biological techniques. This research provides not only a solid scientific basis for the clinical application of Huaier in antifibrosis therapy but also a novel paradigm for exploring the mechanisms of traditional medicines using a multidisciplinary approach.

Materials and methods

Mice and liver fibrosis model

Eight-week-old male and female C57BL/6 wild-type mice were obtained from Xi’an Jiaotong University and housed under conventional pathogen-free conditions, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, ambient temperature maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity at 50 ± 10%. Food and water were provided ad libitum. To establish chronic liver fibrosis, the mice received intraperitoneal injections of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) at 0.6 mL/kg body weight three times per week for six weeks. Forty-eight hours after the final injection, at 14 weeks of age, the mice were humanely euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation in a euthanasia chamber. Blood and liver tissues were subsequently collected for analysis. In a parallel set of experiments, liver fibrosis was induced via bile duct ligation (BDL). The mice were fasted for 12 h prior to surgery and sacrificed two weeks post-surgery for tissue and serum collection. All the mice were randomly divided into the following groups: the control group (100 µL/day saline, gavage) and the Huaier group [16] (2 g/kg/day, gavage). Huaier extract was obtained from Gaitianli Medicine Co., Ltd. (Qidong, Jiangsu, China). To validate the mechanistic role of the AKT signalling pathway in Huaier-mediated protection against liver fibrosis, additional cohorts of mice subjected to the same CCl4 administration protocol were treated with either the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 or the AKT agonist SC79 in combination with Huaier. MK-2206 was administered at 40 mg/kg via oral gavage every 72 h [16], starting from the second week in parallel with Huaier treatment, while SC79 was administered at 10 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection every 48 h for the same duration [17]; all treatments were maintained for five consecutive weeks. Sample size was determined on the basis of a pilot study using the expected effect size of Huaier on fibrotic area (Sirius Red staining). A sample size of 6 mice per group was calculated to achieve adequate statistical power (80%) with a two‑sided significance level of α = 0.05, and this number was chosen to ensure sufficient power while minimizing animal use. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated random number table (Excel Rand function). The allocation sequence was concealed in opaque sealed envelopes, and group assignment was performed by a researcher who was not involved in the animal procedures. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and were approved by the Animal Experiment Administration Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University to ensure ethical and humane treatment of the animals.

Histology

Harvested liver tissues were gently perfused and fixed in fresh 4% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by washing in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For histological analysis, the samples were dehydrated, cleared and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sect. (5 μm-thick) were prepared, mounted on glass slides, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Cat# ST047; Solarbio), Masson’s Trichrome (Cat# abs9348; Absin) and Sirius Red (Cat# DC0041; Leagene Biotech). Bright-field imaging was performed using an Olympus microscope. Digitized images were analysed with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics) to evaluate the histological architecture and quantify fibrosis. All histological analyses (H&E, Sirius Red and Masson’s trichrome) were performed by investigators blinded to the treatment groups. Liver samples were coded with random numbers before sectioning and staining, and the code was not broken until after image acquisition and quantitative analysis had been completed.

Immunofluorescence staining

For cryosectioning, liver tissues were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound (Cat# 4583; Tissue-Tek), and 5 μm-thick sections were prepared using a cryostat maintained at -20 °C. The resulting sections were mounted on adhesive-coated glass slides. The sections were subsequently blocked and permeabilized for 1 h at room temperature with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Cat# V900933; Sigma‒Aldrich) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Cat# T8200; Solarbio).

Primary antibodies against the following proteins were applied overnight: α-SMA-Cy3 (1:100; Cat# C6198; Sigma‒Aldrich), Ki-67 (1:100; Cat# ab16667; Abcam), AKT (1:100; Cat# ab8805; Abcam), P-AKT (1:100; Cat# ab192623; Abcam) and phalloidin-594 (1:100; Cat# 8953 S; CST). The following day, the slides were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat# A11008 and A11012; Invitrogen). Nuclei were visualized by counterstaining with DAPI (1 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min. After three washes with PBS, the sections were coverslipped using antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Images were captured with a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000, Core Facilities Sharing Platform at the School of Basic Medical Sciences). All immunofluorescence analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the treatment groups. Liver samples and cell samples were coded with random numbers before sectioning and staining, and the code was not broken until after image acquisition and quantitative analysis had been completed.

Assays for assessing hydroxyproline levels in liver tissue

We used a hydroxyproline content assay kit (Cat# BC0250; Solarbio) to measure the hydroxyproline content in mouse liver, beginning by homogenizing approximately 0.2 g of liver tissue and hydrolysing it with 2 ml of 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 4 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was neutralized to a pH of 6–8 using NaOH and then diluted to 4 ml with distilled water. The mixture was centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 20 min, after which the supernatant was collected. Using a multifunction microplate reader (POLARstar OPTIMA; BMG, Offenburg, Germany) set at 560 nm, 200 µl of the supernatant was mixed with 200 µl of Reagent 1, incubated for 20 min, 200 µl of Reagent 2 was added, and the mixture was heated at 60 °C for another 20 min. After cooling, the absorbance was measured. A standard curve constructed with hydroxyproline standards enables precise quantification, reflecting collagen metabolism in the liver with accuracy and elegance.

Western blot analysis

Isolated livers or myofibroblasts were lysed using RIPA buffer (Cat# 9806; Cell Signalling) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cat# 04906837001; Roche). Following lysis, the homogenates were centrifuged at 11,752 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-collagen I (1:1000; Cat# ab279711; Abcam), anti-α-SMA (1:1000; Cat# AF1032; Affinity), anti-Ki-67 (1:1000; Cat# ab16667; Abcam), anti-AKT (1:100; Cat# ab8805; Abcam and Cat# ab179463; Abcam), anti-P-AKT (1:100; Cat# ab192623; Abcam), anti-β-actin (1:1000; Cat# ab6276; Abcam) and anti-α-tubulin (1:1000; Cat# ab81299; Abcam). The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Bio-Rad).

Quantitative RT‒PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from myofibroblasts or liver tissues using TRIzol reagent (Cat# 15596018; Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (Cat# 170–8891; Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) was performed using a StepOne Plus System (Applied Biosystems) with universal SYBR Green mix (Cat# 172–5122; Bio-Rad). The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference for normalization. The quantitative PCR sequences of primers used were as follows: Acta2: 5’-gaggcaccactgaaccctaa-3’/5’-tacatggcggggacattgaa-3’; Col1a1, 5’-ttcagggaatgcctggtgaa-3’/5’-acctttgggaccagcatca-3’; Col1a2, 5’- aagggtgctactggactccc-3’/5’-ttgttaccggattctcctttgg-3’; Col2a1, 5’- agcaggtccttggaaacctt-3’/5’-aaggagtttcatctggccct-3’; Ki-67, 5’-atttgcttctggccttcccc-3’/5’-ccaaacaagcaggtgctgag-3’; and GAPDH, 5’- gtaacccgttgaaccccatt-3’/5’-ccatccaatcggtagtagcg-3’.

Serum ALT and AST Assays

Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in mice were determined using commercial assay kits (ALT: Cat# C009-2-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute; AST: Cat# C010-2-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum aliquots were directly subjected to analysis without further dilution. For the assay, the samples were incubated with the respective substrate solutions at 37 °C for 30 min. The enzymatic reactions were then terminated by the addition of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine. Following a 20-minute incubation at 37 °C, the resulting solutions were alkalized, and the coloured products were allowed to develop at room temperature for 15 min. The absorbance was measured at 505 nm for ALT and 510 nm for AST using a multifunction microplate reader (POLARstar OPTIMA; BMG, Offenburg, Germany). The enzyme activities, expressed in Karmen units, were calculated on the basis of standard curves and subsequently converted to international units per litre (U/L).

Cell culture and treatment

The human hepatic stellate cell line LX-2 (Cat# CL-0560; Procell, Inc.) was cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin at 37 °C under a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Prior to experimental treatment, the cells were serum-starved overnight in serum-free DMEM. Differentiation into myofibroblasts was induced by stimulation with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 (Cat# HY-P70648, MCE) for 72 h. The differentiated myofibroblasts were subsequently treated with or without Huaier (8 mg/mL) for 48 h.

Cell viability assay

Myofibroblasts were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Afterwards, the cells were treated with (2, 4, 8 and 16 mg/mL) or without Huaier for 24 h, 48 h, 72–96 h. After treatment, the culture medium was carefully removed, and each well was supplemented with 100 µL of DMEM and 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Cat# 40203ES60, YEASEN). The plates were then incubated for an additional 3 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multifunctional microplate reader (POLARstar OPTIMA, BMG, Offenburg, Germany).

Cell migration assays

Myofibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates premarked with grid lines on the underside. After the cells reached 100% confluence, a straight wound was created in each well using a 200 µl pipette tip. The cells were then treated with or without Huaier (8 mg/mL). Wound closure was monitored at 12 and 24 h post-treatment by imaging the same predefined locations under an inverted microscope. The extent of wound healing was quantitatively analysed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, USA).

siRNA-mediated knockdown

Cells were transfected with siRNAs using Opti‑MEM (Cat# 31985070, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Cat# 13778100, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For transfection, 20 pmol of siRNA was gently mixed with RNAiMAX in Opti‑MEM and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. The mixture was then added to the cell culture medium. After 6 hours, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Experiments were performed 48 hours after transfection. siRNAs targeting AKT were purchased from Genepharma. The target sequences of the human AKT siRNAs were as follows: AKT sense, 5’-AGCGACGUGGCUAUUGUGATT-3’; AKT antisense, 5’-UCACAAUAGCCACGUCGCUTT-3’.

Preparation of Huaier extract

Commercial Huaier granules were ground into fine powder using a mortar and pestle. The powder (10 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of 80% ethanol (Cat# 459836, Sigma-Aldrich) and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The extraction was repeated twice more with fresh 80% ethanol. The combined supernatants were concentrated under reduced pressure at 45 °C and then lyophilized to obtain the crude Huaier extract. The extract was stored at − 20 °C until further use.

Biotinylation of Huaier extract

Since the steroidal components in the Huaier extract lack primary amine groups for direct NHS‑biotin labeling, an amine‑containing linker was introduced onto the steroidal scaffolds via a mild chemical modification strategy directly on the extract. Briefly, 50 mg of Huaier extract was dissolved in 5 mL of anhydrous DMF (Cat# 227056, Sigma-Aldrich), then 2‑(2‑(2‑aminoethoxy)ethoxy)acetic acid (Cat# Y18974, Baiao Leibo) was added together with EDC·HCl (Cat# QN0669, Baiao Leibo) and NHS (Cat# 24500, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 12 h. The resulting amine‑derivatized extract was dialyzed against water (Cat# 15230162, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove excess reagents and then lyophilized. Subsequently, the amine‑modified extract was incubated with NHS‑biotin (Cat# 21217, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in PBS (pH 7.4) containing 10% DMSO (Cat# D2650, Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C overnight. The biotinylated Huaier extract was desalted using a Zeba™ spin column (Cat# 89889, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at − 80 °C.

Pull‑down assay

Recombinant human AKT1 protein (full‑length, active; 2 µg) (Cat# NBP2-52121, R&D Systems) was incubated with 50 µg/mL of biotinylated Huaier extract in binding buffer (50 mM Tris‑HCl, pH 7.5 (Cat# 20–160, Sigma-Aldrich), 150 mM NaCl (Cat# 20–159, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1% NP‑40 (Cat# 492016, Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM DTT (Cat# 10197777001, Sigma-Aldrich), and protease inhibitors (Cat# HY-K0010, MCE) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. For competition control, a 50‑fold mass excess (2.5 mg/mL) of unmodified Huaier extract was added to the reaction mixture 30 min before the addition of the biotinylated extract. After incubation, 30 µL of streptavidin‑coated magnetic beads (Cat# 65001, Invitrogen) were added and incubated for another 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were then washed five times with binding buffer containing 0.5% NP‑40, followed by two washes with PBS. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 2× Laemmli buffer (Cat# 1610737, Bio-Rad) for 10 min. Eluates were resolved by SDS‑PAGE (10% gel) and subjected to Western blot analysis using an anti‑AKT1 antibody at 1:1000 dilution, followed by HRP‑conjugated secondary antibody. Signal was developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

Network pharmacology

Twenty-six bioactive components of Huaier were predicted through a combination of database mining using HERB (http://herb.ac.cn/) and a review of previously published literature. On the basis of the Swiss Target Prediction platform (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), potential target proteins of the active ingredients from Huaier were predicted. The search parameter was set to “Homo sapiens”, and targets were selected under the criterion of “probability > 0”. Potential targets associated with liver cirrhosis were retrieved from multiple public databases, including OMIM (https://omim.org/), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), TTD (http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), PharmGkb (https://www.pharmgkb.org/) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), using the keyword “liver cirrhosis”. To identify potential targets of Huaier in liver fibrosis, we first retrieved disease-associated genes from public databases. Given that major disease databases—including OMIM, DrugBank, TTD, PharmGKB, and GeneCards—do not annotate “liver fibrosis” as an independent disease term because it is classified as a histopathological feature rather than a discrete clinical entity, we used “liver cirrhosis” (ICD-11: DB93) as the primary search keyword. This approach is widely accepted in network pharmacology studies of fibrosis, as liver fibrosis and cirrhosis represent a continuous disease spectrum sharing core molecular drivers such as hepatic stellate cell activation, extracellular matrix remodelling, and profibrotic signalling pathways. To further enrich the target set with fibrosis-specific genes, we supplemented the cirrhosis-derived genes with results from GeneCards using the keyword “liver fibrosis” and cross-referenced them with previously published fibrosis-related gene sets. The combined target list was subsequently used for network pharmacology analysis. Common targets between Huaier and liver cirrhosis were identified using the Venny 2.1.0 platform (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html), and their overlap was visualized in a Venn diagram. On the basis of a Venn diagram, a compound‒target interaction network was constructed using Cytoscape software (v3.10.3). A protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network was subsequently constructed with the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org) and visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.10.3). Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, were performed for the key target clusters via the Metascape database (https://metascape.org). The results of these enrichment analyses were visualized using an online bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking is widely employed in early-stage drug discovery and target prioritization to rapidly screen compound libraries and predict ligand binding modes. This in silico approach provides a rational basis for selecting potential inhibitors prior to experimental validation, as demonstrated by previous studies that used docking to identify ellagic acid as a promising EZH2 inhibitor and to screen FDA-approved compounds against PTPN3 for breast cancer therapy [18]. Following these strategies, we performed molecular docking to evaluate the binding interactions of our candidate molecules with the target protein. The three-dimensional structures of the bioactive compounds derived from Huaier were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The crystal structure of the target protein was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock software (version 4.2.6), and the corresponding binding energies were calculated. For each compound–target pair, five independent docking runs were conducted using different random seeds to ensure reproducibility. The lowest-energy conformation from each run was selected, and the binding free energies (ΔG, kcal/mol) are expressed as the means ± standards deviation derived from five replicates.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To further assess the stability and dynamic behavior of protein–ligand complexes under near-physiological conditions, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation serves as a powerful complementary tool in computational drug discovery. As illustrated in previous studies, MD simulations not only validate docking results but also provide critical insights into conformational stability, hydrogen bonding patterns, and binding free energies, thereby bridging in silico predictions with subsequent experimental efforts [19]. Accordingly, we performed MD simulations to examine the dynamic stability of the predicted complexes and to reinforce the translational relevance of our computational findings. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.6 to model ligand–protein interactions over a 100-ns period. The topology of the small-molecule ligand was generated with Sobtop 1.0 using the GAFF force field, while the protein topology was constructed with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. To neutralize the system, sodium and chloride ions were introduced, and the complex was solvated in a truncated octahedral box of TIP3P water molecules with a 1.0-nm buffer distance. Energy minimization was carried out in two stages: First, the steepest descent algorithm was used for 2500 steps, followed by the conjugate gradient method for another 2500 steps. The system was subsequently equilibrated under an NVT ensemble for 100 ps at 298.15 K and then under an NPT ensemble for another 100 ps at the same temperature. Using the PME approach, long-range electrostatic interactions were estimated in an NPT ensemble simulation at 100 nm with a periodic boundary condition. A collision frequency of 2 ps and a nonbond cut-off distance of 1 nm were determined. The trajectories were recorded at 10-ps intervals, the integration step was set to 2 s, and the system pressure was kept at 101.325 kPa.

Single-cell RNA-seq dataset processing

Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE145086 [20]. Data processing and analysis were performed using the Seurat R package (v4.3.0.1). A Seurat object was created from the expression matrix and accompanying metadata using the Create Seurat Object function. Low-quality cells were filtered out on the basis of the following criteria: number of detected genes between 300 and 6,000, total Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counts between 1,000 and 30,000, and mitochondrial gene percentage less than 20%. After filtration, the data were log-normalized with a scale factor of 10,000. Variable features were identified using the “vst” method, followed by scaling and principal component analysis (PCA). The first 50 principal components were used for subsequent clustering and visualization. Subsequent dimensionality reduction and visualization were carried out via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) using the Run UMAP function with 15 principal components (dims = 1:15). Violin plots were generated with ggplot2 (v3.5.1), and feature expression overlays on UMAP projections were rendered using Seurat’s visualization utilities.

Statistics

All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of preliminary or prior experiments to ensure sufficient statistical power. Samples with inadequate RNA/cDNA quality or suboptimal tissue integrity after processing (below commonly accepted thresholds) were excluded from analysis. The data are expressed as the means ± SD. Differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test, and a P value ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Bioactive composition and molecular structures of Huaier

On the basis of previously published literature and the HERB database [11], a total of 30 bioactive substances have been identified in processed Huaier (Trametes robiniophila Murr.). These active components are broadly classified into four major categories: proteoglycans, bioactive small molecules, steroids and alkaloids.

Proteoglycans include polysaccharide (SP1), water-soluble neutral polysaccharide (W-NTRP), T. robiniophila polysaccharide (TRP) and proteoglycans (TPG-1). According to existing research, the bioactive small molecules include uracil, methyl 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate, 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, cyclo(Leu-Pro), cyclo(Phe-Ala), cyclo(Phe-Gly), methylparaben, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, p-hydroxybenzaldehyde, ethyl 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate, genistein, 4,4’-biphenyldithiol, dehydrofalcarinone, diphenyldisulfide, glyodin, O-acetylserine, pirbuterol, semustine, sulfometuron-methyl, tyrosol 4-sulfate and valinopine.

In addition, the steroid constituents include ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol, ergosterol, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5,22-dien-7-one and sitogluside. The alkaloids identified consisted of adenosine (Fig. 1A). The chemical structures of the bioactive molecules identified in Huaier were obtained from the PubChem database (Fig. 1B).

Eight-week-old C57 mice were subjected to intraperitoneal injections of 0.6 mL/kg body weight CCl4 three times per week for six weeks. Beginning from the second week, control mice received normal saline (100 µL/day) by oral gavage, while the treatment group was administered Huaier (2 g/kg/day) by oral gavage for five weeks. Forty-eight hours after the final CCl4 injection, 14-week-old mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation, and blood and liver samples were collected (Fig. 2A). Body weight monitoring during the modelling period suggested that compared with control mice, Huaier-treated mice experienced less weight loss, with a statistically significant difference observed by the sixth week (Fig. 2B). The liver-to-body weight ratio was significantly lower in the Huaier group (Fig. 2C). Histological analysis suggested reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in H&E-stained sections, along with markedly diminished fibrotic scarring by Sirius Red and Masson staining (Fig. 2D), which was quantitatively confirmed (Fig. 2E), indicating a significant antifibrotic effect of Huaier. Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA suggested a significant reduction in myofibroblasts in the treatment group (Fig. 2F), as supported by the results of the quantitative analysis (Fig. 2G). Further analysis revealed a significant decrease in hepatic hydroxyproline content in the Huaier-treated mice (Fig. 2H). Western blot analysis results suggested decreased protein expression of collagen I and α-SMA in the Huaier-treated mice (Fig. 2I–K), and qPCR suggested reduced mRNA levels of fibrogenic genes such as Acta2, Col1a1, Col1a2 and Col2a1 in the Huaier-treated mice (Fig. 2L). Finally, the serum ALT and AST levels were significantly lower in the Huaier group, which is consistent with improved liver function (Fig. 2M). Collectively, these findings substantiate the protective role of Huaier against CCl4-induced liver fibrosis in mice.

Huaier attenuated the degree of liver fibrosis induced by BDL in mice

Eight-week-old C57 mice underwent bile duct ligation (BDL). Starting on the third day after surgery, control mice received normal saline (100 µL/day) by oral gavage, while the treatment group was administered Huaier (2 g/kg/day) for 12 days. Liver and blood samples were collected two weeks post-modelling (Fig. 3A). Body weight changes throughout the experimental period suggested that compared with control mice, Huaier-treated mice exhibited less pronounced weight loss, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups by the second week (Fig. 3B). The liver-to-body weight ratio was significantly lower in the Huaier-treated group (Fig. 3C). Histological evaluation demonstrated a marked reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration in H&E-stained liver sections from the treatment group. Additionally, Sirius Red and Masson staining suggested substantially attenuated fibrotic scarring, which was quantitatively confirmed, indicating that Huaier significantly alleviated BDL-induced liver fibrosis (Fig. 3D–E). Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA suggested a significant decrease in the area positive for myofibroblasts in the Huaier-treated mice, as quantified, suggesting the suppression of myofibroblast activation (Fig. 3F–G). Further biochemical analysis suggested that the hepatic hydroxyproline concentration significantly decreased in the treatment group (Fig. 3H). Western blot analysis suggested decreased protein expression of collagen I and α-SMA in the livers of the Huaier-treated mice (Fig. 3I–K). Consistent with these findings, the mRNA expression of fibrosis-related genes, including Acta2, Col1a1, Col1a2 and Col2a1, decreased (Fig. 3L). Finally, serum levels of ALT and AST were significantly lower in the Huaier group, reflecting improved liver function (Fig. 3M). Together, these results substantiate the antifibrotic efficacy of Huaier in a BDL-induced liver fibrosis model.

Huaier attenuated the degree of liver fibrosis by inhibiting the proliferation of myofibroblasts in vivo and in vitro.

We employed immunofluorescence staining to assess the proliferation of myofibroblasts in liver tissues from mice with CCl4-induced liver fibrosis. Myofibroblasts were labelled with α-SMA, and proliferating cells were identified using Ki-67. The results suggested a significant reduction in the number of proliferating myofibroblasts in the Huaier-treated group compared with that in the control group (Fig. 4A–B). Similarly, in the BDL-induced liver fibrosis model, Huaier treatment also suppressed the proliferation of myofibroblasts in fibrotic livers (Fig. 4C–D). To determine the appropriate concentration for mechanistic studies, we performed CCK‑8 assays to assess the anti‑proliferative activity of Huaier in LX‑2 cells. Treatment with 2 or 4 mg/mL had no significant effect on cell proliferation, whereas treatment with 8 mg/mL significantly suppressed proliferation without inducing excessive cytotoxicity. In contrast, 16 mg/mL exhibited both antiproliferative and cytotoxic effects. On the basis of these results, 8 mg/mL was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments (Supplementary Figure S1A). In vitro, using an LX-2-derived myofibroblast model, a wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the effect of Huaier on cell migration. We observed that Huaier significantly inhibited the migratory capacity of myofibroblasts (Fig. 4E–G). Furthermore, a CCK-8 assay suggested that Huaier suppressed the proliferative activity of LX-2 myofibroblasts in culture (Fig. 4H). Additional immunofluorescence staining for Ki-67 confirmed that Huaier treatment led to a notable decrease in the proportion of proliferating myofibroblasts (Fig. 4I–J). At the molecular level, western blot analysis demonstrated that Huaier downregulated Ki-67 protein expression in myofibroblasts (Fig. 4K–L). Consistent with these findings, the results of the qPCR analysis indicated a reduction in Ki-67 mRNA levels following Huaier treatment (Fig. 4M). Collectively, these findings illustrate that Huaier effectively curbs both the migration and proliferation of myofibroblasts in vitro, supporting its antifibrotic role.

Network pharmacology analysis suggested potential mechanisms by which Huaier attenuates the degree of liver fibrosis

Twenty-six bioactive components of Huaier were identified through an integrated approach involving database mining via HERB (http://herb.ac.cn/) and a comprehensive review of previously published literature. Using the Swiss Target Prediction platform (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), potential target proteins of these twenty-six bioactive components from Huaier were systematically predicted. A total of 604 protein targets associated with the bioactive components of Huaier were predicted. Given the absence of a specific disease category for liver fibrosis in the database, we selected liver cirrhosis, clinically characterized by features of liver fibrosis, as a proxy to identify relevant disease targets. Concurrently, 10,872 therapeutic targets for liver cirrhosis were retrieved from multiple databases, including OMIM (https://omim.org/), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), TTD (http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), PharmGkb (https://www.pharmgkb.org/) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). Among these, 486 overlapping targets were found between the protein targets of the bioactive components of Huaier and the therapeutic targets for liver cirrhosis. A Venn diagram was constructed to illustrate these intersecting therapeutic targets (Fig. 5A). On the basis of the intersection results, a compound–target interaction network was constructed, delineating the relationships between each of the 26 active components of Huaier and the corresponding therapeutic targets for liver cirrhosis (Fig. 5B).

For the 486 liver cirrhosis targets potentially modulated by the active constituents of Huaier, a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network was subsequently constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org) and visualized with Cytoscape software (v3.10.3). The PPI network was screened via a topological analysis method, with nodes coloured in red indicating higher Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) values (MCC ≥ 0.6) [21]. Nodes were ranked by the MCC value, with higher MCC values indicating greater centrality within the network. Among these genes, AKT1 exhibited the highest MCC value, strongest and most prominent expression, followed by GAPDH, IL6, TNF and IL1β (Fig. 5C–F). These predictions serve to generate hypotheses regarding potential targets and pathways, rather than to establish definitive mechanisms. The identified overlapping targets and PPI network centrality metrics serve solely to prioritize candidates for experimental validation; they do not constitute in silico proof of functional relevance.

The results suggested the top 13 signalling pathways, among which the top 3 enriched pathways for the target genes were neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, lipid and atherosclerosis, and hormone signalling (Fig. 6B). The three-dimensional structures of 26 bioactive compounds derived from Huaier were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The crystal structures of AKT1, GAPDH, IL6, TNF and IL1β were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Collectively, these findings suggest that Huaier may elicit antifibrotic effects in the liver via multitarget and multipathway mechanisms, in which the AKT1 signalling pathway appears to play a central role.

Molecular dynamics simulation for identifying the binding between the bioactive components of Huaier and AKT1

To assess the stability of the binding interactions between AKT1 and several key bioactive compounds, namely, Ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol, ergosterol, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5,22-dien-7-one and sitogluside, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on the corresponding docked complexes using GROMACS software. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) was employed as a key metric to evaluate the conformational stability and reliability of each complex. An RMSD fluctuation within 0.2 nm is generally indicative of a structurally stable system [22]. The RMSD values of the ligands across all the systems remained consistent around or below 0.2 nm throughout the simulation trajectory, confirming the overall structural integrity of the complexes (Fig. 7A–D).

(A–D) Root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), distance between molecules and radius of gyration (Rg) between ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol, ergosterol, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5,22-dien-7-one, sitogluside and AKT1, respectively. Binding energies are presented as mean ± SD from five independent docking runs, and that RMSD/RMSF values represent trajectory averages without group comparisons

Furthermore, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was utilized to characterize atomic-level flexibility, providing insight into the local mobility of residues within the protein structure [23]. The RMSF profiles suggested that the peptide residues in all the complexes, Ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol-AKT1, Ergosterol-AKT1, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5,22-dien-7-one-AKT1 and Sitogluside-AKT1, fluctuated largely within approximately 0.2 nm during the entire simulation (Fig. 7A–D). These observations suggest that AKT1 maintains considerable structural rigidity upon binding to these bioactive constituents of Huaier, with no marked conformational rearrangements, thereby underscoring the stability of the interactions.

The distance between molecules is crucial for characterizing binding affinity and interaction stability. Tracking its fluctuations helps identify stable bound states, transient encounters and dissociation events throughout a simulation. The intermolecular distance profiles demonstrate that throughout the simulation, the binding interfaces in all the complexes, Ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol-AKT1, Ergosterol-AKT1, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5,22-dien-7-one-AKT1 and Sitogluside-AKT1, fluctuated predominantly within approximately 1.0 nm (Fig. 7A–D). As a fundamental metric in molecular simulations, the distance between molecules provides critical insights into interaction stability and the dynamic behaviour of complexes. The observed narrow fluctuation range indicates that sustained proximity is essential for molecular recognition and effective binding. These results suggest that upon binding with these bioactive constituents of Huaier, AKT1 is involved in well-defined molecular interactions characterized by persistent interactions, with only minor transient variations, reflecting a stable and specific binding mode.

The radius of gyration (Rg) was employed to assess the structural compactness of the protein, where a lower value corresponds to a more tightly folded conformation [24]. Within the 100 ns simulation period, the Rg values of all the complexes, Ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol-AKT1, Ergosterol-AKT1, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5,22-dien-7-one-AKT1 and Sitogluside-AKT1, rapidly converged to an equilibrium state and stabilized within the range of 2.15–2.20 nm (Fig. 7A–D). These results indicate that AKT1 is capable of forming compact and stable complexes with the bioactive constituents of Huaier. Such structural consolidation is conducive to the establishment of stable interactions between active compounds and AKT1, which may underly the pharmacological role of Huaier in mitigating liver fibrosis through the modulation of myofibroblast activity.

Huaier exerted antifibrotic effects in CCl4‑treated mice via the AKT signalling pathway

Eight-week-old C57 mice were subjected to intraperitoneal injections of 0.6 mL/kg body weight CCl4 three times per week for six weeks. Beginning from the second week, control mice received normal saline (100 µL/day) by oral gavage, while the treatment group was administered Huaier (2 g/kg/day) by oral gavage for five weeks. To validate the mechanistic role of the AKT signalling pathway in Huaier-mediated protection against liver fibrosis, additional cohorts of mice were treated with either the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 or the AKT agonist SC79 in combination with Huaier. Specifically, mice subjected to the same CCl4 administration protocol were divided into four groups: (1) the control group (CCl4 only), (2) the Huaier group (CCl4 + Huaier), (3) the Huaier + MK-2206 group (CCl4 + Huaier + MK-2206), and (4) the Huaier + SC79 group (CCl4 + Huaier + SC79). MK-2206 was administered at 40 mg/kg via oral gavage every 72 h, starting from the second week in parallel with Huaier treatment, while SC79 was administered at 10 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection every 48 h for the same duration; all treatments were maintained for five consecutive weeks. Forty-eight hours after the final CCl4 injection, 14-week-old mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation, and blood and liver samples were collected (Fig. 10A–B). Body weight monitoring during the modelling period suggested that compared with control mice, Huaier‑treated mice experienced less weight loss, with a statistically significant difference observed by the sixth week. No significant difference in body weight was detected between the Huaier group and the Huaier plus MK‑2206 group, suggesting that AKT inhibition did not further alter the weight-sparing effect of Huaier. In contrast, mice treated with Huaier alone exhibited less weight loss than those receiving Huaier in combination with SC79, an AKT activator, with no statistically significant difference (Fig. 10C). Compared with that in the control group, the liver‑to‑body weight ratio in the Huaier group was significantly lower (Fig. 10D). No significant difference was detected between the Huaier group and the Huaier plus MK‑2206 group. However, compared with Huaier alone, the combination of Huaier with SC79, an AKT activator, resulted in a liver‑to‑body weight ratio comparable to that observed in the Huaier group alone, with no statistically significant difference (Fig. 10D). Histological analysis suggested reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in H&E-stained sections, along with markedly diminished fibrotic scarring, as determined by Sirius Red and Masson staining, which was quantitatively confirmed (Fig. 10E), indicating a significant antifibrotic effect of Huaier. Comparable reductions in inflammation and fibrosis were observed in the Huaier plus MK-2206 group, with no significant difference from those in the Huaier group. However, compared with Huaier alone, the addition of SC79, an AKT activator, attenuated these effects, resulting in greater inflammatory infiltration and more severe fibrosis (Fig. 10E–F). To validate whether Huaier components directly interact with AKT protein, we performed pull-down assays using biotinylated Huaier extract. LX-2 cell lysates or recombinant AKT protein were incubated with biotinylated Huaier extract, followed by capture with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. Western blot analysis suggested that Huaier extract successfully pulled down endogenous AKT from LX-2 cell lysates, whereas no AKT signal was detected in the beads-only control group. The recombinant AKT protein group confirmed the specificity of the antibody and the assay system (Fig. 10G). These results provide direct biochemical evidence that Huaier components bind to AKT protein under biologically relevant conditions. To further explore the functional role of AKT in LX-2 cells, we performed siRNA-mediated AKT knockdown (siAKT) and verified the intervention efficacy via western blot analysis. The WB results confirmed successful AKT knockdown in LX-2 cells, accompanied by a marked reduction in the level of phosphorylated AKT (P-AKT). Compared with those in the control group, the expression levels of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen I were significantly lower in both the Huaier group and the siAKT monotherapy group. Notably, the siAKT + Huaier combination group displayed similarly low expression levels of α-SMA and collagen I, with no statistically significant differences observed relative to either the Huaier monotherapy group or the siAKT monotherapy group (Fig. 10H–I).

Discussion

The results of the present study reveal the therapeutic potential of Huaier extract against liver injury and fibrosis, suggesting that Huaier warrants further investigation in preclinical and, if justified, early clinical studies. It is important to note that all findings presented in this study are derived from mouse models of liver fibrosis (CCl4‑ and BDL‑induced) and the human hepatic stellate cell line LX‑2; therefore, clinical translation of these observations will require further validation in human subjects and more clinically relevant settings. Our findings demonstrate that Huaier administration significantly mitigates the pathological progression of liver fibrosis by inhibiting the activity of the AKT signalling pathway, a critical axis that regulates fibrogenic and inflammatory responses in hepatic stellate cells. This mechanism is consistent with the multicomponent regulatory properties of traditional herbal medicines and provides a preliminary molecular basis for its antifibrotic activity [26].

A previous clinical study reported that Huaier granules may improve the hepatic microenvironment in patients with HBV-related HCC [10]. The observed attenuation of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis in our study implies that Huaier may modify the protumorigenic hepatic microenvironment. Our data show that Huaier treatment leads to measurable improvements in established fibrosis, a pathological process that is widely considered difficult to reverse, supporting the potential value of Huaier as an adjuvant intervention. However, these clinical findings remain preliminary because of the retrospective study design, and further validation in large-scale prospective randomized controlled trials is needed.

A key finding of this study is that Huaier markedly suppressed AKT signalling, which is known to drive hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and myofibroblast differentiation [27]. By modulating this signalling node, Huaier attenuates core processes associated with fibrogenesis. Notably, the biological actions of Huaier are not limited to this single pathway.

AKT signalling functions as a major regulator of myofibroblast function during liver fibrosis [28], coordinating multiple cellular processes that contribute to disease progression. Our results show that Huaier reduces the levels of phosphorylated AKT (P-AKT) without changing total AKT expression, indicating that selective inhibition of AKT activation is sufficient to reduce its pro-fibrotic effects. These observations suggest that targeting AKT phosphorylation may represent a potential strategy to suppress fibrogenesis by inducing myofibroblast quiescence, reducing myofibroblast abundance, and reducing scar tissue formation.

The antifibrotic effects of Huaier observed in this study highlight its multicomponent pharmacological activity in the context of liver fibrosis. The inhibition of the AKT pathway indicates that Huaier acts as a multicomponent signalling modulator rather than a single-target cytokine inhibitor. This characteristic is clinically relevant given the complex pathogenesis and redundant signalling networks involved in liver fibrosis [29]. Single-target therapies often have limited efficacy because of pathway compensation in the fibrotic liver microenvironment. Huaier inhibits HSC activation, myofibroblast proliferation, and inflammatory responses, which may contribute to its favourable antifibrotic profile. This multicomponent action corresponds to the complex nature of liver fibrosis and may offer advantages over single-target therapies. This finding supports a conceptual shift from selective single-pathway blockade towards broader restoration of hepatic homeostasis.

While our data demonstrate elevated AKT1 expression in myofibroblasts during liver fibrosis, descriptive expression patterns alone do not establish functional relevance. To address this, we performed cell‑type‑specific AKT knockdown in LX‑2 cells, which significantly reduced myofibroblast activation markers (α‑SMA and Collagen I), confirming a causal role of AKT in fibrotic phenotypes. Beyond direct regulation, AKT activity can be modulated by diverse upstream molecular inputs. Recent evidence has linked ubiquitination to PI3K/AKT activation in both cancer and fibrotic signalling. For example, a study demonstrated that the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM29 promotes K48‑linked polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NEFL, thereby activating the PI3K/AKT pathway in glioblastoma [30]. Such mechanisms illustrate how multiple upstream regulators converge on AKT, providing a conceptual framework that aligns with the multi‑component nature of Huaier. Given that Huaier is a complex extract containing numerous bioactive molecules, it is plausible that it modulates AKT activity through both direct binding (as supported by our pull‑down assay) and indirect effects on upstream regulatory proteins, such as ubiquitin ligases or deubiquitinases. Future studies employing tissue‑specific AKT knockout models and unbiased proteomic screens for Huaier‑interacting proteins will be required to fully dissect the causal hierarchy.

A similar integrative strategy has been successfully applied to identify ion channel‑related cancer biomarkers and validate them experimentally. For instance, a recent series of studies first performed a pan‑cancer analysis of voltage‑gated sodium channels (VGSCs), systematically profiling the expression and clinical associations of all subunits across multiple cancer types [31]. The same group then focused on SCN3B in glioma, demonstrating its prognostic value and association with immune infiltration using TCGA and CGGA cohorts. Finally, functional experiments in C6 glioma cells suggested that the extracellular Ig domain of the β3 subunit modulates cytoskeletal organization and cell motility independently of channel activity [32]. Together, this series of studies represents a paradigm that moves from computational discovery through clinical correlation to mechanistic validation – closely mirroring the design of our current work and reinforcing the value of a generalizable bioinformatic‑to‑experimental framework.

While our pull‑down assay provides direct evidence of binding between Huaier components and AKT protein under cell‑lysate conditions, we acknowledge that no single assay can fully recapitulate the complex intracellular environment. Future studies employing live‑cell approaches—such as cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), NanoBRET, or CRISPR‑based endogenous tagging—will be valuable to further validate this interaction at the physiological level.

A methodological consideration in this study is the use of liver cirrhosis-associated genes as surrogates for liver fibrosis in network pharmacology analysis. In major public databases, diseases are classified according to clinical ontologies (e.g., ICD-11, MeSH), and liver fibrosis is not annotated as an independent disease entity but as a pathological stage of chronic liver disease. Therefore, related targets and gene annotations are primarily catalogued under liver cirrhosis or specific liver diseases. Fibrosis and cirrhosis represent a continuous spectrum of chronic liver injury, which involves core mechanisms such as HSC activation, ECM accumulation, and signalling through the TGF-β/Smad and PI3K/AKT pathways. We therefore considered cirrhosis-related targets a reasonable starting point for predicting the antifibrotic targets of Huaier. These computational predictions were further verified by in vitro and in vivo experiments, ensuring that our conclusions were supported by functional data rather than bioinformatics analysis alone. However, this approach may enrich for targets associated with advanced liver disease, and future studies using stage-specific fibrosis datasets will help refine these analyses.

It is important to consider whether the antifibrotic effects of Huaier observed in this study are mediated by fibrosis‑specific mechanisms or may partly result from general cytotoxicity, given that AKT is a ubiquitous signalling pathway. Several observations argue against non‑specific cytotoxicity as the primary explanation. First, the concentration of Huaier used in vitro (8 mg/mL) suppressed myofibroblast proliferation and migration without causing significant cell death, as shown by CCK‑8 assays. Second, in vivo, Huaier treatment did not exacerbate liver injury; instead, it reduced serum ALT/AST levels and improved histological parameters, indicating an absence of overt hepatotoxicity. Third, Huaier selectively inhibited AKT phosphorylation without affecting total AKT expression, a pattern that is more consistent with pathway‑specific modulation than with general cytotoxic stress. Nevertheless, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that some off‑target or low‑level cytotoxic effects may contribute to the overall phenotype. Future studies using conditional, cell‑type‑specific AKT knockout models and detailed toxicity profiling in primary human hepatocytes will help further dissect the fibrosis‑specific versus cytotoxic actions of Huaier.

Several limitations of this study should be clearly acknowledged. First, the antifibrotic effects of Huaier were evaluated only in CCl4-induced and BDL-induced mouse models, which mimic chemical and cholestatic liver injury, respectively, but do not fully replicate the clinical aetiology of liver fibrosis, including chronic viral hepatitis, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and alcoholic liver disease. Second, in vitro experiments were limited to the LX-2 human HSC line; primary HSCs or organotypic liver models that better recapitulate the hepatic microenvironment were not used. Third, although we found that the steroid components of Huaier bind to AKT1, the specific active monomers that mediate the antifibrotic effects and their synergistic interactions with other components remain uncharacterized, and the holistic pharmacodynamic basis of Huaier requires further investigation. Fourth, this study focused exclusively on the AKT pathway and did not explore crosstalk with other core fibrogenic pathways, such as the TGF-β/Smad and PDGF signalling pathways, which limits the mechanistic scope of our analysis. In addition, all the data were obtained from preclinical models, and human liver tissues or patient cohorts were not included to confirm translational relevance. Furthermore, the relatively short intervention period and limited dose range in animal experiments restrict conclusions regarding long-term efficacy, safety, and dose-dependent effects in vivo. Future studies will address these limitations by using more clinically relevant animal models, isolating and identifying active compounds, validating targets in human samples, and investigating the crosstalk between fibrotic signalling pathways. Fifth, it is important to emphasize that network pharmacology results are computational predictions that require experimental verification. Our study therefore treated these in silico findings as hypothesis‑generating, and subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments were designed to test the predicted involvement of the AKT pathway. Sixth, all data presented in this study were obtained from preclinical models (CCl4- and BDL-induced liver fibrosis in mice, and the LX-2 human hepatic stellate cell line). Human liver tissues or patient cohorts were not included to confirm the translational relevance of our findings. Therefore, the therapeutic potential of Huaier in human liver fibrosis remains to be validated in future clinical studies. Seventh, Although our molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated stable binding between Huaier steroid components and AKT1, these analyses did not directly assess whether compound binding impairs the functional dynamics of AKT1, such as its interactions with downstream partner proteins. Future computational studies simulating AKT1-substrate complexes in the presence and absence of Huaier compounds, combined with experimental validation (e.g., co-immunoprecipitation of AKT with its effectors), will be needed to determine whether the observed reduction in AKT phosphorylation translates into impaired functional dynamics. Eighth, we acknowledge that the fibrotic microenvironment is complex, involving multiple cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular matrix components beyond TGF‑β1. In our in vitro experiments, we used TGF‑β1 (5 ng/mL, 72 h) to differentiate LX‑2 cells into myofibroblasts prior to Huaier treatment. TGF‑β1 is widely recognized as a key inducer of the fibrotic phenotype in hepatic stellate cells [33]. While our TGF‑β1‑based model represents a standard and well‑accepted approach for studying myofibroblast activation in vitro, we recognize that additional factors (e.g., PDGF, inflammatory cytokines, or fibrotic serum) contribute to the full fibrotic milieu. Future studies using more complex conditioning, such as fibrotic mouse serum or cytokine cocktails, will help further validate the antifibrotic effects of Huaier under more physiologically relevant conditions.

It is important to note that the gene regulatory dynamics linking liver fibrosis to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are non‑linear and change drastically across discrete phases of fibrosis progression [34]. Our experiments were conducted in CCl4‑ and BDL‑induced mouse models representing relatively early‑to‑mid stages of fibrosis, not in advanced cirrhosis or HCC models. Therefore, the potential application of Huaier against HCC by targeting liver fibrosis should be interpreted with caution. Whether Huaier remains effective at later fibrotic stages, when the regulatory network may become refractory to intervention, remains unknown and warrants further investigation using stage‑specific fibrosis models.

While our findings are encouraging, the translation of Huaier from preclinical research to clinical application faces multiple challenges. The multicomponent nature of herbal medicines, while potentially beneficial for treating complex diseases, complicates quality control, standardization, and mechanistic research. Future studies should move beyond efficacy validation to identify the core active ingredients and their synergistic interactions, which is essential for ensuring batch-to-batch consistency and reliable clinical outcomes. Our data also suggest that the effects of Huaier may vary across experimental models, supporting the need to identify predictive biomarkers. Defining patient subgroups (e.g., those stratified by dominant fibrogenic pathways such as TGF-β or PDGF) that may benefit most from Huaier treatment is important. The long-term goal is to develop a precision medicine approach instead of empirical administration, allowing for Huaier to be used in appropriate patients at appropriate disease stages, possibly in combination with other agents, to develop rational therapeutic regimens for preventing or reversing liver fibrosis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Huaier attenuates liver fibrosis by inhibiting AKT phosphorylation and suppressing hepatic myofibroblast activation. Using network pharmacology combined with experimental validation, we show that Huaier reduces myofibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition and that its steroid components display favourable binding affinity to AKT1. Mechanistically, Huaier selectively inhibited AKT phosphorylation without affecting total AKT expression. These findings provide a preliminary molecular mechanism for the antifibrotic effects of Huaier and support further investigation of this traditional herbal medicine as a potential intervention for liver fibrosis.
