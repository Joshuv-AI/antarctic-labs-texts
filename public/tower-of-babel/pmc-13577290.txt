Trends in HPV vaccination among adults aged 27–45 years in the United States
Authors: Marcie Fisher-Borne, Nicolae Done, Amanda Eiden, Salini Mohanty, Travis Wang, Anyu Zhu, Ellen Sears, Oscar Patterson-Lomba, Yan Song, Erika L Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite expanded human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination recommendations via shared clinical decision-making (SCDM) for adults aged 27–45 years (i.e., mid-adults), uptake remains low in the United States. Examining trends in HPV vaccination under SCDM across sociodemographic groups, vaccination sites, provider types, and geographical regions can offer insights into SCDM guideline implementation for HPV vaccination in eligible mid-adults. A retrospective cohort study was conducted using the Merative MarketScan® commercial claims database (2006–2023) to evaluate HPV vaccination uptake among mid-adults (aged 27–45 years). Annual and cumulative HPV vaccination rates (2019–2023) were analyzed overall and stratified by sociodemographic characteristics, provider type, and vaccination setting. Among the 3 to 4 million annually unvaccinated eligible adults aged 27–45 years identified in the database, the annual rate of HPV vaccination initiation rose modestly from 0.21% in 2019 to 0.54% in 2023 (5-year cumulative vaccination uptake: 0.84%). Most HPV vaccinations occurred in provider offices, though the proportion administered in pharmacies increased over time while the proportion in provider offices declined. Geographic variability in HPV vaccine uptake was substantial, with state-level rates ranging from 0.23% to 1.56%. These findings indicate that HPV vaccination uptake among mid-adults in the US remains low. Further research is needed to characterize geographic and sex-based variation in adult HPV vaccination and to further explore the growing role of pharmacies as HPV vaccination sites.KEYWORDS: HPV vaccine, shared clinical decision-making, vaccination rates, United States

Introduction

An estimated 85% of people will be infected with human papillomavirus (HPV) in their lifetime.1 High-risk types of HPV cause cancers of the cervix, oropharynx, anus, penis, vagina, and vulva.2 Although over 90% of HPV-related cancers are preventable through vaccination,3 approximately 39,000 new cases of HPV-related cancers are diagnosed annually in the United States (US).4,5

HPV vaccination recommendations in the US have expanded since the first HPV vaccine was approved in 2006. That year, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Gardasil®, which targeted 4 HPV types (6, 11, 16, and 18),6 and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommended routine vaccination with Gardasil for females aged 11–12 y, with catch-up vaccination up to age 26. In 2009, the ACIP expanded its recommendation to include males aged 9–26 y and added it as part of a routine recommendation in 2011.7 Subsequent FDA approvals included Cervarix® in 2009, which targeted 2 high-risk HPV types (16 and 18),8,9 and Gardasil 9 in 2014, which provided coverage against 9 HPV types (6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58).10 In 2015, the ACIP recommended Gardasil 9 for routine use in both females and males aged 11–12, with catch-up vaccination up to age 26.11

In 2018, the FDA approved Gardasil 9 for use in adults aged ≤ 45 years,12 and in 2019, the ACIP expanded its recommendation for HPV vaccination with Gardasil 9 to include adults aged 27–45 years (mid-adults) who are not adequately vaccinated (i.e., those who have not received 2 or 3 doses if they initiated vaccination before or at age 15 or older, respectively), to be guided by shared clinical decision-making (SCDM) between patients and healthcare providers.13

Despite expanded recommendations, HPV vaccination uptake among mid-adults remains low. Self-reported data from the 2019 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) indicate that among the mid-adult respondents aged 27–45 years, the HPV vaccination prevalence rate (≥1 dose) was 15–16%,14,15 while the 2022 NHIS reported a prevalence rate of 21.8% in this age group.16 However, these overall estimates include adults who may have been vaccinated before becoming age-eligible for SCDM. Among adults aged 27–45 years who were not vaccinated before age 27 y, HPV vaccination coverage in the 2022 NHIS was only 2.7%,16 highlighting persistently low uptake in the population most aligned with SCDM eligibility. Similarly, a claims-based study of eligible adults aged 27 to 45 years found that uptake remained low as of 2020 (282 and 128 per 100,000 persons for women and men, respectively), despite increases in HPV vaccine administration following the 2019 ACIP recommendations.17

Although prior studies have described HPV vaccination prevalence and administration trends among adults aged 27–45 years, less is known about SCDM-based HPV vaccination delivery patterns following the 2019 ACIP recommendation. In particular, limited evidence is available on where mid-adult HPV vaccination is delivered, which provider types administer it, and how uptake varies across sociodemographic groups, sex, and geographic regions. This evidence can help identify patterns in, and potential barriers to, SCDM-based HPV vaccination delivery. Such insights can inform strategies to enhance implementation of HPV vaccination through SCDM among eligible adult populations.

This study provides contemporary, real-world evidence characterizing trends in HPV vaccination uptake among US commercially insured adults aged 27–45 years. The study further examines variations in vaccination rates according to vaccination settings and healthcare provider type/specialty to characterize patterns associated with HPV vaccine uptake in mid-adults.

This analysis is descriptive in design and is intended to characterize patterns of HPV vaccination uptake following the 2019 recommendation.

Patients and methods

Data source

This retrospective, observational cohort study used data from the Merative MarketScan® Commercial Database from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2023. The database comprises inpatient and outpatient medical utilization, expenditure and pharmacy claims data for millions of individuals annually (i.e., employees, their spouses, and dependents) covered by employer-sponsored private health insurance, altogether representing more than 350 large employers and 40 unique health plans across the US. The data include demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, and region), health plan information, medical care received across settings (e.g., ambulatory, inpatient, emergency, and post-acute care), diagnoses, procedures, and medications. Geographic region is derived from enrollee residence and categorized into four regions (Northeast, North Central, South, and West) corresponding to US Census Bureau classifications.

This study did not require review or approval by an institutional review board or ethics committee because it involved only secondary analysis of preexisting, de-identified data. The data were de-identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), specifically 45 CFR § 164.514 (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.514), and the study met the criteria for exempt research under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4) (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-46/subpart-A/section-46.104). This research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/).

We focused on a commercially insured population because HPV vaccination is generally a covered benefit in this group, allowing implementation of the 2019 SCDM recommendation to be examined with reduced confounding by direct cost barriers. Consistent with prior claims-based work, our measures capture vaccination initiation, defined as receipt of at least one dose, rather than series completion; characterizing initiation is a necessary first step for evaluating SCDM-based delivery.

Study population

The study population consisted of commercially insured adults in the US who were eligible to receive the HPV vaccine through SCDM based on the 2019 ACIP recommendations (i.e., adults aged 27–45 years who were not adequately vaccinated). Eligibility was determined by age, health plan enrollment history, and prior vaccination status, as outlined below.

Because claims data do not permit reliable assessment of HPV vaccine series completion before age 27, SCDM eligibility was operationalized as having no documented prior HPV vaccine dose in the observable claims history before the calendar year of analysis. Thus, the cohort should be interpreted as adults with no documented prior HPV vaccination in the observable data, rather than adults known to have initiated but not completed the HPV vaccine series.

The HPV cohort (2019–2023) comprised adults who met eligibility criteria for SCDM-based HPV vaccination according to ACIP guidelines between 2019 and 2023, including those who received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine during this period. Individuals were required to be aged 27–45 years old in at least one calendar year between 2019 and 2023, and to have continuous health plan enrollment during the calendar year of analysis. Additionally, at least one year of continuous enrollment prior to the year in which they became age-eligible for vaccination (defined as the year they turned 27) was required. For individuals who became age-eligible between 2019 and 2023, this prior enrollment requirement applied to the year before they reached the age of 27. For individuals who became age-eligible prior to 2019, eligibility required at least one year of continuous enrollment prior to the first year of observation; for example, individuals who turned 27 before 2019 were required to have continuous enrollment in 2018 for inclusion in the 2019 analysis.

To ensure accurate identification of unvaccinated adults, available historical claims were used to identify and exclude individuals with any documented receipt of an HPV vaccine before the age of 27 since the relevant product’s approval date. Specifically, individuals were excluded if they had claims for Gardasil 9 (9vHPV) between December 10, 2014, and the end of the calendar year prior to the year of analysis; Gardasil (4vHPV) between June 8, 2006, and the end of the prior year; or Cervarix (2vHPV) between October 16, 2009, and the end of the prior year. Continuous eligibility requirements were not imposed for years prior to 2018 to preserve sample size and generalizability of the study population. A 1-year continuous-enrollment requirement was selected as a pragmatic baseline lookback period to improve ascertainment of prior HPV vaccination history and establish observable eligibility before cohort entry while preserving the size of a broadly representative commercially insured population. See sample selection steps in Supplemental Materials A4.

To assess the robustness of findings to potential misclassification of prior vaccination status, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which HPV vaccination rates were recalculated under a range of assumed eligibility misclassification levels (see Supplemental Materials A7 for details).

Among individuals who met all eligibility criteria, those who received at least one dose of HPV vaccination, including Gardasil 9 (9vHPV) or Gardasil (4vHPV) between 2019 and 2023, were identified (see Supplemental Materials A1 for coding specifications). Each annual cohort thus consisted of an eligible population defined by demographic and enrollment criteria, within which receipt of HPV vaccination was ascertained.

Because eligibility was assessed separately for each calendar year, individuals could contribute to multiple annual eligible cohorts while they remained unvaccinated and otherwise met eligibility criteria; once an individual had a documented HPV vaccination, they were excluded from eligible denominators in subsequent years.

Study measures

Sociodemographic characteristics of vaccinated individuals were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and proportions for categorical variables. Variables assessed included age, sex, health plan type, region, urbanicity, and employment status. Vaccination delivery variables, including vaccination setting and provider type, were also evaluated.

Analysis

HPV vaccine administration rates among eligible mid-adults were calculated annually from 2019 to 2023, corresponding to the period following the SCDM recommendations. For each year, the annual HPV vaccination rate was defined as the number of age-eligible individuals who received at least one HPV vaccine dose in that calendar year divided by the total number of adults eligible for vaccination under SCDM guidelines in the same year. This measure captures vaccination initiation and is consistent with the formulation used to estimate vaccination initiation in national surveys (e.g., NHIS), facilitating comparison of the resulting estimates; the underlying data capture differs, however, in that the present analysis relies on objective claims records rather than self-report. This measure does not reflect completion of the recommended HPV vaccine series. A 5-year cumulative vaccination uptake was also estimated as the total number of HPV-vaccinated individuals from 2019 to 2023 divided by the total number of eligible adults aged 27–45 during this period (Supplemental Materials A2). Vaccination rates were stratified by key demographic characteristics and vaccination delivery variables (i.e., setting and provider type), and geographic patterns were visualized using a choropleth map. All analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and visualizations were generated with Microsoft Excel.

HPV vaccination shares by site and provider type

Most HPV vaccinations over the study period were administered in provider offices (75.0%) followed by pharmacies (17.0%) (Table 1). Provider types varied: family medicine/general practice, obstetrics and gynecology (OB/GYN), and pharmacy accounted for the specialty of approximately 62% of the HPV vaccinators, followed by internal medicine (10.5%), while nurse practitioners, acute care hospital staff, and physician assistants each accounted for smaller shares (<10%).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that HPV vaccination uptake among mid-adults in the US remains low despite SCDM recommendations. Between 2019 and 2023, only 0.84% of eligible commercially insured adults aged 27–45 years received at least one dose of an HPV vaccine, with annual vaccination rates (defined as receipt of at least one dose) increasing modestly from 0.21% to 0.54% over this period. These estimates are generally consistent with findings from another claims-based study using Optum data, which reported HPV vaccine administration rates of 282 and 128 per 100,000 person-years among eligible women and men, respectively, as of 2020.17 However, our estimates are notably lower than the 2.7% vaccination coverage reported among adults aged 27–45 years who were not vaccinated before age 27 in the 2022 NHIS.16 Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in data sources and study design, particularly in how eligible populations are defined and how vaccination events are captured.14–17 Claims-based estimates may underestimate vaccination uptake due to incomplete capture of vaccination events (including vaccinations delivered in nontraditional, non-billed, or otherwise out-of-network settings) and potential overestimation of the eligible-population denominator through misclassification of prior vaccination status. In contrast, survey estimates derived from self-reported vaccination may be influenced by recall or reporting bias, which, through social desirability, may bias self-reported coverage upward. More broadly, these discrepancies reflect differences in both numerator (how vaccination events are ascertained) and denominator construction (how the eligible population is defined) across data sources, not measurement error alone.

Beyond confirming low uptake of HPV vaccination among vaccine-eligible mid-adults, this study extends prior research by providing a comprehensive, multi-year assessment of vaccination patterns following implementation of the SCDM recommendation. Using a large, national commercial claims database, we characterized variation by demographic characteristics, state, vaccination site, and provider type – offering a detailed and policy-relevant understanding of how HPV vaccination has been adopted in real-world clinical practice among mid-adults.

Vaccination uptake varied meaningfully across demographic, health insurance plan, and geographic characteristics. Vaccinated individuals were disproportionately female, urban-residing, and enrolled in preferred provider or consumer-driven health plans. In particular, the predominance of women among vaccinated mid-adults may reflect the historical framing of HPV vaccination as a cervical cancer prevention strategy, as well as greater opportunities for HPV-related counseling through gynecologic care. However, the 2019 ACIP SCDM recommendation applies to both women and men aged 27–45 years who are not adequately vaccinated. Persistently low uptake among mid-adult men is therefore an important public health concern, given the role of HPV in diseases and cancers affecting men, including oropharyngeal and anal cancers. Given the rising incidence rates of oropharyngeal cancer among men in the US,18 these findings highlight the need for future research to better characterize sex-specific barriers to SCDM-based HPV vaccination and to identify strategies for improving vaccination discussions and uptake among men.

Substantial geographic heterogeneity was also observed: cumulative HPV vaccination rates from 2019 to 2023 in states with the highest uptake (e.g., Massachusetts, South Dakota, and Maryland) were approximately 5 times those in states with the lowest uptake (e.g., Idaho, Delaware, and Mississippi). These differences may reflect variations in health system performance, local public health infrastructure, and population-level attitudes, underscoring opportunities for targeted, state- or region-specific interventions that were not directly examined in this study. This pattern is consistent with a national report documenting marked state-level heterogeneity in adolescent HPV vaccination, where pharmacy vaccination policies, standing orders, and other implementation environments differ across states.19 Prior analyses of adult vaccination, including HPV, have shown that changes in state-level rates have generally been modest over time, suggesting that cross-state differences may persist absent targeted efforts.20

Variation in vaccination site and provider type over time highlights evolving delivery dynamics in adult immunization. Although provider offices remained the predominant site of HPV vaccine administration throughout the study period, notable year-over-year growth in pharmacy-based vaccination was observed between 2019 and 2023. This shift also may reflect factors that were not directly measured in this study, such as patient preference for convenient community-based access and broader trends in healthcare delivery, as well as disrupted access to healthcare providers during the COVID-19 pandemic. Existing literature supports these findings, highlighting the growing involvement of pharmacies in adult vaccination delivery and their potential role as accessible vaccination sites, including for adult HPV vaccination.21–24 Nevertheless, overall HPV vaccine uptake remains low across care settings, underscoring persistent implementation challenges despite diversification of vaccination venues.

Structural and informational barriers may contribute to the low uptake among mid-adults. Ambiguities in ACIP’s SCDM guidance, particularly regarding how to assess individual-level risk of HPV infection based on factors such as sexual partnerships and relationship status,13,25 may discourage healthcare providers from initiating vaccination discussions.

Prior studies indicate that providers have limited knowledge and awareness of the latest SCDM recommendations, and are uncertiain about how to effectively communicate the benefits of mid-adult HPV vaccination within this framework. One study found that 57% of physicians were uncertain about the key points to emphasize during SCDM conversations regarding HPV vaccination with mid-adults,26 whereas other studies revealed gaps between providers’ perceived awareness and actual knowledge of SCDM recommendations.27 These provider-level challenges, compounded by patient hesitancy, varying perceptions of vaccine benefits, safety concerns, and social stigma surrounding HPV as a sexually transmitted infection, likely contribute to the persistently low uptake rates observed among inadequately vaccinated mid-adults.28–30

Extending HPV vaccination to adults aged 27-45 years remains clinically relevant as the risk of HPV infection persists throughout life, regardless of prior exposure, treatment history, or population subgroup. Multiple studies have demonstrated that a substantial proportion of causal HPV infections – defined as infections responsible for lesions detected at screening – occur well into mid-adulthood. Two modeling analyses consistently estimated the median age of causal infection to be in the mid-20s (23.9 and 24.7 y), with approximately 43%-47% of causal infections occurring at age 26–27 y or older;31,32 another one reported a median causal infection age of 20.6 years, with an additional 25% of infections acquired by age 30.6 years.33 Other modeling studies have estimated that up to 75% of causal infections may be acquired by ages ranging from 35 to 52, depending on modeling assumptions.34 Collectively, these findings underscore the ongoing risk of oncogenic HPV acquisition beyond early adulthood and support the relevance of SCDM-based vaccination in mid-adult populations.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, identification of vaccination events relied on administrative codes and may be subject to misclassification. Second, we analyzed only services billed through commercial insurance and did not capture vaccinations administered in nontraditional settings or among individuals covered by Medicaid or Medicare, limiting generalizability and likely omitting disparities in vaccination initiation associated with these types of medical coverage. Third, key socioeconomic determinants (e.g., income, education) of vaccination uptake were not captured in the claims data, limiting our ability to assess socioeconomic patterns in vaccination uptake or identify potential factors associated with uptake. Fourth, claims data do not permit reliable assessment of HPV vaccine series completion in this population; accordingly, our measures reflect vaccination initiation (receipt of at least one dose) rather than completion of the recommended series. Fifth, individuals with intermittent or no insurance coverage were excluded because continuous enrollment was required to improve the capture of HPV vaccination events and prior vaccination history. This criterion may favor the inclusion of individuals with more stable insurance coverage and potentially greater healthcare engagement, thereby limiting generalizability to the broader population of adults aged 27–45 years. Even among included individuals, however, the observable enrollment period prior to age 27 may have been insufficient to fully capture HPV vaccination histories. As a result, some individuals classified as eligible for SCDM HPV vaccination may have been previously vaccinated but misclassified as unvaccinated, potentially leading to an overestimation of the eligible population and underestimation of vaccination rates. To mitigate this, individuals with any documented HPV vaccination before the age of 27 since the respective vaccines’ approval date were excluded. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis recalculating vaccination rates under varying assumed misclassification levels confirmed that the study’s main conclusions are robust to uncertainty in the classification of prior vaccination status (Supplemental Materials A7). A meaningful share of vaccinations was attributed to a heterogeneous “Other” provider category, which limits the granularity of provider-specific inferences. Finally, the proportion of vaccinated individuals with missing urbanicity status increased over the study period (approaching 20% by 2023), which may affect the robustness of rural/urban comparisons in later years.

In conclusion, HPV vaccination uptake through SCDM remains low among adults aged 27–45 years, with pronounced sex-based and geographic variation. These findings highlight persistent gaps in the uptake of a vaccine indicated for the prevention of HPV-related diseases and cancers and point to the need for targeted interventions. Future research should also examine HPV vaccine series completion rates among mid-adults who initiate vaccination. Expanding access through pharmacies and other community-based settings may help reduce logistical barriers, while clearer SCDM guidance and enhanced provider education could improve vaccination discussions and recommendations. Together, these efforts may help close persistent gaps in HPV vaccination coverage and strengthen protection against HPV-related cancers in mid-adult populations.
