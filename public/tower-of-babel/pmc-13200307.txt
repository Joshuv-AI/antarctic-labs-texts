Neonatal sevoflurane exposure disrupts the lung–brain axis and drives microglial neuroinflammation and cognitive deficits
Authors: Lirong Liang, Shuhui Cao, Youyi Zhao, Bing Liu, Jiachen Wang, Peiqin Gong, Yifei Wang, Guanghui Hao, Xingchen ZhangMu, Naining Lu, Haopeng Zhang, Shengxi Wu, Fang Kuang, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal sevoflurane exposure in mice induces microglial activation and long-term cognitive deficits, a finding that raises significant concerns for pediatric anesthesia. The lung-brain axis, a critical pathway mediating pulmonary-central nervous system communication, is indispensable for maintaining organismal homeostasis. However, existing research on anesthetic neurotoxicity has focused predominantly on central mechanisms, with insufficient attention to the lung—a major immune organ with extensive bidirectional crosstalk with the brain. Herein, we aim to explore the lung-brain interactions underlying long-term cognitive sequelae of neonatal sevoflurane exposure.MethodsC57BL/6J mice were selected and exposed to 3% sevoflurane for 2 h daily on postnatal days 6–8. Upon reaching adulthood, cognitive function and microglial activation status were evaluated. At 4 weeks post-exposure, 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metabolomic analysis were performed respectively to characterize the structure of the pulmonary microbiota and the metabolite profile. Proximity ligation assay (PLA), fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy-fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FLIM-FRET), and co-immunoprecipitation (COIP) were employed to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which lung-derived metabolites mediate brain effects. Additionally, rescue experiments were conducted by administering the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulator FTY720 and Moce to validate the aforementioned effects.ResultsRepeated neonatal sevoflurane exposure impaired adult cognitive function, induced microglial activation, and was concurrent with pulmonary microbiome dysbiosis and metabolic alterations. Notably, sphingosine—a key membrane lipid—was significantly decreased. Intratracheal administration of FTY720, a sphingosine analog, alleviated neuroinflammation and ameliorated cognitive deficits. Mechanistically, sevoflurane exposure upregulated HDAC1 and downregulated KLF4, whereas FTY720 significantly rescued these sevoflurane-induced expression aberrations, implicating the HDAC1/KLF4 axis in the regulation of neuroinflammation. Additionally, MOCE significantly alleviated neuroinflammation and ameliorated cognitive deficits.ConclusionsDevelopmental sevoflurane exposure induces microglial activation and cognitive decline via a pulmonary dysbiosis-sphingosine reduction cascade. The sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulator FTY720 mitigates this impairment by regulating microglial activation and neuroinflammation. These findings reveal novel mechanisms of anesthetic neurotoxicity and identify potential neuroprotective targets for pediatric anesthesia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03808-0.Keywords: Sevoflurane, Lung-brain axis, Microglia, Development brain, Sphingosine

Introduction

The use of anesthetic agents during critical developmental periods, particularly in neonates, has raised significant concerns regarding their long-term neurodevelopmental impacts. Among these agents, inhaled anesthetic drug sevoflurane is commonly utilized in pediatric anesthesia, Yet, its impacts on brain development continue to be an important issue. Recent studies have highlighted the potential impacts of anesthetics, which include neuroinflammation, neural cell death, synaptic plasticity and so on [1]. Neuroinflammation, which has been linked to neurodegenerative diseases, is the mechanism most frequently mentioned. There exist numerous hypotheses on sevoflurane-induced neuroinflammation, such as microglial function alterations [2], breakdown of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [3], and autophagy [4]. Nevertheless, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying sevoflurane-induced neuroinflammation remain an enigma awaiting thorough elucidation.

Microglia serve as the primary immune cells in the central nervous system (CNS) and their activation state is closely linked to neuroinflammatory responses that may contribute to cognitive impairment [5]. Specifically, the activation of microglia in response to sevoflurane exposure may trigger a cascade of inflammatory processes that can lead to sustained cognitive deficits in neonates exposed to these anesthetics [6]. Previous studies have shown that the periphery-central axis has a crucial function in the inflammatory reaction of microglia [7]. Sevoflurane is absorbed into the lungs through respiratory action. It then acts on the brain via the bloodstream and thus exerts an anesthetic effect [8]. The lung is one of the organs having the highest frequency of connections to the external environment. Through the act of respiration, it ceaselessly exchanges materials with the outside environment. Being constantly exposed to microorganisms and contaminants, it is furthermore recognized as the first line of immune defense for the human body [9].

Previous research is predominantly focused on the direct neurotoxic effects of anesthetics [10]. Nevertheless, it frequently disregards the intricate interplay between anesthetic agents and the host’s microbiome as well as metabolic pathways. Recent studies have suggested that the brain and lung can communicate with each other through lung microbiota and microbiome, disruptions in the lung microbiome can have a remarkably significant impact on the CNS and have been strongly associated with various neurodevelopmental disorders [11]. Notwithstanding these discoveries, the issue of whether sevoflurane exposure impacts the pulmonary microbiota remains unaddressed. Additionally, it is currently inconclusive whether sevoflurane also influences the central nervous system via the periphery. Therefore, we postulate that the lung-brain axis, mediated by microbiota and metabolism, might serve as a potential mechanism for cognitive impairments induced by sevoflurane exposure at early life period.

Our study on mouse model further expanded the metabolic profiling approach, aiming to identify key metabolites associated with anesthetic-induced neurotoxicity. Intriguingly, we discovered a significant decrease in sphingosine levels following sevoflurane exposure, highlighting its potential involvement in the pathophysiological processes. Sphingosine, a naturally occurring lipid integral to cell signaling pathways, serves as a precursor for the synthesis of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), a bioactive molecule that modulates diverse cellular functions. To explore the causal relationship between sphingosine dysregulation and anesthetic-induced neurotoxicity, we leveraged fingolimod (FTY720), a synthetic analog of sphingosine with modified chemical properties. As a selective modulator of S1P receptors, FTY720 offers a unique opportunity to dissect the role of the sphingolipid signaling pathway in sevoflurane-induced cognitive impairment and microglial activation.

We have discovered that disruptions in the pulmonary microbiota caused by sevoflurane exposure might contribute to neuroinflammatory processes and cognitive impairments. We meticulously observed the ameliorative effect of FTY720 on cognitive behavior subsequent to sevoflurane exposure through the HDAC1/KLF4 signaling pathway.

In this study, our objective is to explore the mechanism linking lung microbiota dysbiosis to microglial neuroinflammation and cognitive impairments induced by sevoflurane exposure. We have, for the first time, observed the salvaging effect of pulmonary metabolites on advanced brain functions following sevoflurane exposure. This significant discovery possesses the potential to offer valuable insights specifically for mitigating the risks associated with pediatric anesthesia in clinic.

Materials and methods

Animals

All the C57BL/6J mice were bought from the animal center of Air Force Medical University (Fourth Military Medical University). The mice were maintained at 24 ± 2 °C, with a 12 h dark/12 h light cycle, and free access to water and food. All animal experiments were carried out under protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fourth Military Medical University and according to “Policies on the Use of Animals and Humans in Neuroscience Research” revised and approved by the Society for Neuroscience in 1995. Postnatal day 6 (PND6) Mice were randomly assigned to different groups. No animal was excluded from the study. The researchers independently carried out the experiments and evaluated the results objectively.

Experimental design and mice treatments

Neonatal C57BL/6J mice at P6 were assigned to the following groups and treated. (1) Control (Con) group, the mice were treated with 60% oxygen (balanced with nitrogen) for 2 h (h)/day for 3 consecutive days as described [12, 13]. (2) Sevoflurane (Sev) group, mice were treated with 3% Sev (Cat #: H20110142) + 60% oxygen (balanced with nitrogen) for 2 h/day for 3 consecutive days. (3) Sev + FTY720 (Fingolimod hydrochloride, MedChemExpress, HY-12005) group. Mice were treated with FTY720 (0.3 mg/kg) at P35 by intratracheal administration continuously for 7 days. (4) Sev + Mocetinostat (moce) group. Mice were treated with moce (5 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, HY-12164). All treatments were conducted from 8:00 to 10:00 AM. A warming blanket was used during anesthesia to prevent hypothermia.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry, mice were perfused intracardially with 4% paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer (PFA). Once perfusion was completed, the mouse brains were gathered and post-fixed in PFA for 3 h at 4 °C. Serial coronal sections were prepared and blocked by PBS containing 3% bull serum albumin (BSA) and 0.3% Triton-X100, and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at room temperature as the following: rabbit anti-Iba1 (Wako, Cat: #019–19741, 1:800), mouse anti-CD68 (biolegend, Cat: #137001, 1:500), mouse anti-HDAC1 (proteintech, Cat:66085-1-Ig, 1:200). After washing with PBS, corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluro 488 (donkey anti-rabbit, donkey anti-mouse, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:500, AB 2313584, AB 2338845) or Alexa Fluro 594 (donkey anti-mouse, donkey anti-rabbit, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:500, AB 2338871, AB 2338059) were incubated with the sections for 3 h at room temperature protected from light. After washing with PBS, sections were counterstained with Hoechst33324 (1:1000, Sigma) for 30 min.

Western- blotting

Hippocampal tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing proteinase inhibitors cocktail. Protein concentration was measured by BCA assay. Protein samples were separated by 8–12% gel. After SDS-PAGE, protein was transferred to PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked with TBS containing 5% non-fat milk and 0.1% Tween-20 and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C as the following: mouse anti-β-actin (proteintech, Cat: 66009-1-Ig, 1:4000), rabbit anti-iNOS (proteintech, Cat:18985-1-AP, 1:1000), mouse anti-Arg1 (proteintech, Cat:66129-1-Ig, 1:1000), rabbit anti-CD68 (ABclonal, Cat: A13286, 1:1000), rabbit anti-TGF β (proteintech, Cat:21898-1-AP, 1:1000), mouse anti-HDAC1 (proteintech, Cat:66085-1-Ig, 1:1000) and rabbit anti-KLF4 (abclonal, Cat: Al3673, 1:1000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit (Proteintech, Cat: #SA00001-2, 1:5000), HRP-conjugated anti-mouse (Proteintech, Cat: #SA00001-1) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized with an ECL kit (Thermo, Cat#:32106). Images were analyzed by Image J.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampal tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The purity of RNA was detected using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). qPCR was carried out using a SYBR Green PCR Mix Kit (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’ s manual. The results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. GAPDH was used as an internal control.

Protein interaction between HDAC1 and KLF4

Protein interaction between HDAC1 and KLF4 was evaluated in hippocampal tissue. Hippocampal tissue homogenate was prepared using mild NP-40 (Nonidet P-40) buffer (PL004, Fei Yang Bio, China) with PMSF and subjected to immunoprecipitation after removing the insoluble material by centrifugation. Protein A/G agarose beads was added to each antibody conjugated sample and samples were incubated at 4 °C for 4 h for immunoprecipitation. Subsequently, beads were washed five times with cold lysis buffer. Immunoprecipitates were finally eluted from the beads by adding 50 µl of SDS-PAGE sample buffer and verified by Western-blotting of HDAC1 (proteintech, Cat:10197-1-AP, 1:100). Subsequently, Western-blotting of KLF4 (abclonal, Cat: Al3673, 1:500) was performed to test protein interaction. To eliminate non-specific interaction, a normal mouse IgG antibody (CST, #3420) was used as a negative control.

Cell culture and treatment

The BV-2 murine microglia cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2, containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA).

For Sev exposure, Sev was delivered from an anesthesia machine to a sealed plastic box in a 37 °C incubator. An anesthetic gas monitor (Drager, bloomfield, USA) was used to continuously monitor the concentrations of carbon dioxide, oxygen and sevoflurane. BV-2 murine microglia cells were treated with Sev (60% O2, 5% CO2 and 4.1% Sev) for six hours.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

A DuoLink™ Proximity Ligation Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) was used to detect HDAC1-KLF4 protein interactions following the manufacturer’s protocols. BV-2 murine microglial cells were blocked with blocking buffer for 60 min at 37 °C, then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with primary antibodies diluted in DuoLink™ dilution buffer, including: mouse anti-HDAC1 (proteintech, Cat:66085-1-Ig, 1:200) and rabbit anti-KLF4 (ABclonal, Cat: Al3673, 1:200). After washing, the cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with PLA probes (anti-rabbit PLUS and anti-mouse MINUS). Ligation and amplification were executed at 37 °C using a DuoLink™ In Situ Detection Reagent kit (Sigma-Aldrich, DUO92101, Germany). The PLA signals were detected using FV3000 fluorescence confocal microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Collection of Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

Mice were under anesthesia while their thoraxes were opened. Then, a cannula was inserted into the trachea, and with the help of the tracheal cannula, the lung lobe was washed 3 times in total, with 1 ml pre-warmed (37 °C) PBS for each wash. BALF was collected into 1.5 ml tubes and centrifuged at 4 °C at 2500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was removed and stored at − 80 °C until further processing.

16S rRNA sequencing

16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiseqTM 2500/4000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). In brief, Microbial DNA was extracted using the HiPure bacterial DNA Kits (Magen, China) according to manufacturer’s protocols. The 16S rDNA V3-V4 region of the RNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers as follow: 341 F, 5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’; 806R, 5’- GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT-3’. Purified amplicons were combined in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end sequencing (PE250) on the Illumina platform following the standard procedures. The raw reads were submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analyses were performed on BALF using an Agilent Technologies 1290 UHPLC system, equipped with a Waters UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm), and coupled with a Thermo Q Exactive mass spectrometer. For positive mode, mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water; for negative mode, 5 mmol/l ammonium acetate in water. Mobile phase B was acetonitrile. Elution gradient: 0 min, 1% B; 1 min, 1% B; 8 min, 99% B; 10 min, 99% B; 10.1 min, 1% B; 12 min, 1% B. Flow rate: 0.5 ml/min. Injection volume: 2 µl. The QE mass spectrometer was used for its ability to acquire MS/MS spectra on an information-dependent basis (IDA) during LC/MS experiments. Acquisition software (Xcalibur 4.0.27, Thermo) continuously evaluates full scan survey MS data and triggers MS/MS spectra acquisition based on preselected criteria. ESI source conditions: sheath gas flow rate 45 Arb, aux gas flow rate 15 Arb, capillary temperature 320 °C, full MS resolution 70,000, MS/MS resolution 17,500, collision energy 20/40/60 eV (NCE model), spray voltage 3.8 kV (positive) or −3.1 kV (negative).

Raw data from UHPLC-MS/MS was processed with Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher) for metabolite peak alignment, picking, and quantitation. Differentially expressed metabolites: P < 0.05, VIP > 1. Applied PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA using R package models. Metabolomic profiling (LC-MS/MS and bioinformatics) done in collaboration with Guangzhou Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Lung microbiota depletion and transplantation

Mice in the antibiotic‑treated (ABX) group received daily intratracheal instillation of 50 µL of an antibiotic cocktail (ampicillin 1 mg/mL, vancomycin 0.5 mg/mL, metronidazole 1 mg/mL, neomycin 1 mg/mL; all from Absin) for 7 consecutive days, commencing 1 week after sevoflurane exposure. This protocol was slightly modified based on a previously established method [14].

Total DNA was purified from BALF samples using the Microbiome DNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, cat# DC502).Amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was carried out with primers cited from reference [14]: forward 5’-GGTGAATACGTTCCCGG-3’ and reverse 5’-TACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′. qPCR was performed with SYBR GreenER™ qPCR SuperMix Universal (ThermoFisher).The relative level of 16S rRNA was analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method.

For microbiota transplantation (MT), lung microbiota isolated from control mice was intratracheally administered to ABX‑treated, sevoflurane‑exposed mice for 7 consecutive days to reconstitute pulmonary microbial communities.

Novel object recognition test

On the first day, the mice were placed in an open field (measuring 40 × 40 × 35 cm) for a 10 - minute free exploration session. On the following day, initially, the mice were introduced into the very same open field that now contained two identical objects for a 10 - minute exploration period. After an hour, one of the objects was substituted with a new object of a different shape, and the mice were then returned to the open field for another 10 - minute exploration. Subsequently, the ratio of the exploration time spent on the novel object to that on the old object was computed.

Fear conditioning

All experimental mice were pre - acclimated to the startle chambers (manufactured by SanDiego Instruments) three days before the commencement of training. During the cued fear conditioning training session, they underwent five trials in which a paired conditioned stimulus (CS) in the form of tones (with a duration of 30 s, frequencies of either 6–12 kHz, and an intensity of 90 dB) was presented concurrently with an unconditioned stimulus (US) in the form of an electric shock (lasting 500 milliseconds and with an amplitude of 1.0 mA) at intervals of five minutes. The startle responses elicited by the shocks and the proportion of time during which the mice exhibited freezing behavior in response to the tones were quantified using Xmaze software (developed by XinRuan informatics Co., Shanghai).

Morris water maze test

Water maze was made by a round container with the diameter of 120 cm and the wall of 40 cm. The water maze was divided into 4 quadrants. The objective platform was placed 1.5 cm under water. For training, mice were put into water randomly in one quadrant facing the wall. The time when mice found the platform was recorded as escaping time. The mice which could not find the platform within 60 s were guided to the platform for learning. Mice were trained 4 times per day for 4 days. In the fifth day, mice were let swim freely in the maze. The total time mice stayed in the platform quadrant were recorded. The swimming traces and the escaping behavior were recorded using the software SMART 3.0.

Statistical analysis

All behavioral analyses and statistical procedures were carried out by an investigator blinded to the experimental design. Sample sizes were determined based on pilot effect sizes and ethical guidelines. Post hoc power analysis confirmed ≥ 80% power for key outcomes. Separate cohorts of animals were utilized for each behavioral test. The data are reported as mean ± standard error. The Shapiro - Wilk test was implemented to assess the normality of the data distribution. Statistical comparisons were performed using either Student’s t – test, one - way or two – way ANOVA followed by Student Newman - Keuls post - hoc analysis. A P - value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Repeated exposure of neonates to sevoflurane elicits microglia activation and subsequently leads to long-term cognitive impairment

It has been documented that repeated exposures to sevoflurane during the neonatal stage can induce cognitive dysfunction, anxiety - like behaviors and social abnormalities in adult mice [2, 15, 16]. To explore the long-term impact of repeated sevoflurane exposures on cognitive function, neonatal mice were exposed to 3% sevoflurane for 2 h on postnatal day 6, 7, and 8. Long-term cognitive deficits and brain microglial activation were evaluated in adult mice (Fig. 1A). In the novel object recognition test, the administration of sevoflurane led to a significant reduction in the discrimination index (Fig. 1B and C) and percentage of time dedicated to the exploration of the novel object (Fig. 1D). In the fear conditioning experiment, mice subjected to sevoflurane treatment exhibited a significantly shorter freezing duration during conditioned stimulation in comparison with the control group (Fig. 1E and F). Mice subjected to sevoflurane treatment manifested a notably prolonged escape time subsequent to training and resided for a substantially diminished duration in the target quadrant in contrast to the control group (Fig. 1G-I). Collectively, these behavioral manifestations imply that repeated exposures to sevoflurane possess the capacity to instigate cognitive impairment within the context of brain development.

Microglia, serving as the resident phagocytes in the CNS, are the initial responders to stressors compared to others. They quickly adjust their phenotype and experience functional changes [6]. Research has established that microglia play a crucial mediating role in cognitive impairment resulting from sevoflurane exposure [17, 18]. Sholl analysis was employed to detect morphological alterations of microglia in the hippocampal region, serving as confirmation of its activation and associated functional changes. We discovered that the morphology of microglia in the hippocampus underwent remarkable changes (Fig. 1J). These changes included a significantly increased soma size (Fig. 1K), a decreased total number of branch points (Fig. 1L) and total branch length (Fig. 1M), as well as a declined complexity of branches after repeated exposure to sevoflurane (Fig. 1N). Following sevoflurane exposure, proinflammatory phenotype markers such as iNOS mRNA, CD68 mRNA, and IL 1β were all upregulated. In contrast, anti-inflammatory phenotype markers including Arg1 mRNA, CD206 mRNA, and TGF-β mRNA were downregulated when compared with the control group (Fig. 1O). Similarly, in the Sev-treated group, the protein level of the pro-inflammatory factors (iNOS, CD68) was increased, whereas the protein level of the anti-inflammatory mediators (Arg1, TGF-β) was decreased (Fig. 1P and Q). Double immunostaining revealed that in mice treated with sevoflurane, significantly more Iba1/CD68 positive cells were observed compared with that in control mice (Fig. 1R and S).

Employing the aforementioned behavioral and actived microglia datasets, we performed a Pearson’s correlation-based analysis to delineate functional profiles of microglia linked to neurobehavioral phenotypes in sevoflurane-exposed murine models. Results showed that behavioral alterations were negatively correlated with iNOS protein levels and positively correlated with Arg1 levels (Supplementary Fig. 1A-F). Collectively, these data indicate that microglia in the hippocampus are activated and enter a pro-inflammatory state following repeated exposure to sevoflurane.

Repeated neonatal exposure to sevoflurane altered the composition of the lung microbiota

Sevoflurane, the most prevalently utilized inhaled anesthetic in pediatric anesthesia, is assimilated by the lungs during the respiratory process. Subsequently, it exerts an influence on the brain through the bloodstream circulation, thereby inducing an anesthetic effect. Recent research findings indicate the existence of a bidirectional communication route that encompasses pulmonary microbiota, their metabolites within the lungs, and the central and autonomic nervous systems [19]. Firstly, we assessed the impact of developmental sevoflurane exposure on murine lung histopathology via Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining. Results showed no significant differences in lung structure between groups, with no obvious alveolar luminal exudate or alveolar septal thickening (Fig. 2A and B). These data indicate that developmental sevoflurane exposure does not induce significant pathological changes in murine lung tissue.

To determine whether repeated exposure to sevoflurane would have an impact on the lung microbiota, we collected bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and conducted 16S rRNA microbial sequencing four weeks after exposure to sevoflurane. To evaluate how the compositions of microbial communities alter after exposure to sevoflurane, we investigated the specific variations in microorganisms in two groups, and the changes of predominant bacteria between the two groups are vividly demonstrated at the phylum and family levels (Fig. 2C and D).

At the phylum level, the lung microbiota of the two groups mainly consists of Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Firmicutes. These phyla are crucial for maintaining the balance and functionality of the lung microbial ecosystem [20]. Proteobacteria are known for having diverse metabolic capabilities [21]. Actinobacteria make contributions to various biological processes [22]. Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes also have their own unique functions in the lung environment [23]. In comparison with the Con group, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes was reduced, whereas that of Actinobacteria was increased in Sev group (Fig. 2C). At the family level, the predominant bacteria, Rhodobacteraceae, Burkholderiaceae, Sporichthyaceae, and Ilumatobacteraceae, showed changes. Compared with the Con group, the relative abundance of Rhodobacteraceae and Burkholderiaceae decreased, while that of Sporichthyaceae and Ilumatobacteraceae increased. (Fig. 2D).

Subsequently, we conducted an investigation into the α and β diversities within the BALF of mice treated with sevoflurane and those in the control group. The α diversity was assessed by calculating chao1, ace, observed species, Shannon index, and Simpson index. Since the indices here are related to OTU richness, evenness, and rare species, there was no statistical difference between the groups (Fig. 2E-I). The results evidently show that the two groups exhibited similar phenotypes with regard to OTU richness, evenness, and rare species. The β diversity was further evaluated through Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). The lung microbiota in the two groups showed a changed distribution and an obvious clustering pattern, suggesting that the structure of lung microbiota was altered after sevoflurane exposure (Fig. 2J and K).

Moreover, the Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method was utilized to identify the taxa at different taxonomic levels between the Sev group and the Con group. The LEfSe analysis results showed that Acidimicrobiia was underrepresented in the control group (LDA > 4.0). Additionally, several phylotypes were overrepresented in the Con group. Rhodobacteraceae, Rhodobacterales, and Micrococcales were the top three enriched phylotypes with LDA < −4.0 (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). The result of indicator species analysis at the family level showed that Rhodobacteraceae, Burkholderiaceae, and Microbacteriaceae were Sev-related indicators (Fig. 2L). The prediction of the function of putative pathogenic bacteria by Bugbase showed that sevoflurane exposure led to an increase in the proportional abundance of Euryarchaeota and a decrease in those of Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria (Fig. 2M). In addition, we also conducted functional prediction using Tax4fun. The heatmap suggested that these changes in bacterial diversity might be connected with different metabolic processes and signal transduction as the dominant function (Fig. 2N).

Eventually, we tested the content of several strains in BALF through qPCR and observed that Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Firmicutes were reduced after repeated exposure to Sev. This was entirely in line with the sequencing results (Fig. 2O). Collectively, these results indicate that repeated exposure to Sev during development causes dysbiosis of the lung microbiota.

Disruption of the pulmonary microbiota triggers microglial activation and subsequent cognitive impairment

Next, we analyzed correlations between the top 10 most abundant bacterial families (at the family level) and cognitive behavioral indices in mice. Results showed that two families Rhodobacteraceae and Burkholderiaceae with significantly reduced relative abundances following sevoflurane exposure, exhibited a significant positive correlation with target quadrant residence time in the Morris water maze, cognitive index in the novel object recognition test, and freezing percentage in contextual fear conditioning (Fig. 3B). Using the aforementioned microbiota, behavioral, and activated microglia datasets, we conducted a Pearson’s correlation-based analysis in Sev-exposed murine models. Results demonstrated that Proteobacteria were positively correlated with behavioral alterations and Arg1 levels, while negatively associated with iNOS protein expression (Fig. 3C-G).

To further define the role of pulmonary microbiota in long-term cognitive impairment induced by developmental sevoflurane exposure, ampicillin (Amp) was administered to mice via intratracheal instillation at 100 mg/kg body weight for 7 consecutive days, starting from 4 w post-exposure. Subsequently, the influence on cognitive behavior was observed during adulthood. From our study, it has been observed that Amp alone can trigger microglia activation, accompanied by an increase in iNOS and a decrease in Arg1. Sev + Amp leads to a further activation of microglia in comparison with either the Amp group or the Sev group (Fig. 3H-M). We then assessed the behavioral performance of subjects treated with Amp or Sev + Amp. Novel object recognition test results demonstrated that, compared with Con group, Sev group exhibited a significant decrease in the percentage of time spent exploring the novel object. In the fear conditioning test, the freezing percentage within Sev + Amp group was comparable to that within the groups subjected to either Sev - only or Amp - only treatment regimens (Fig. 3O). Repeated neonatal exposure to sevoflurane exerts an impact on the pulmonary metabolome

Metabolites having a close relationship with the phenotype of the organism can reflect the organism’s state in a more complete and dynamic way [24]. For this reason, we utilized non-targeted metabolomics techniques to detect metabolites in BALF of two groups of mice. First, we analyzed the difference in global metabolites between the sevoflurane-treated group and the control group. The principal component analysis (PCA) score plots demonstrated that the samples from the sevoflurane-treated group and the control group indicated consistent classification and significant separation in the positive ion mode negative ion mode. The principal components PC1 and PC2 accounted for 20% and 12% of the variation respectively in positive ion mode (Fig. 4A). And the principal components PC1 and PC2 accounted for 19% and 13.3% of the variation respectively in negative ion mode (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Next, the Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) model was employed to conduct further analysis on the compounds that are responsible for the differences among different treatment groups [25]. The OPLS-DA scores indicated that Con group and Sev group were dispersed into two distinct regions, both in positive ion mode and in negative ion mode. The goodness of fit values and predictive ability values in positive ion mode were R2X = 0.282, R2Y = 0.995, and Q2 = 0.26 (Fig. 4B), and in negative ion mode, the values were R2X = 0.259, R2Y = 0.996, and Q2 = 0.41 (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Model validation carried out using permutation test ensures that this OPLS-DA model is not random nor overfitting and is reliable in explaining and predicting the variations in X and Y matrices (Fig. 4C, in positive ion mode, intercps: R2 0.0, 0.99; Q2 0.0, 0.28) (Supplementary Fig. 3 C, in negative ion mode, intercps: R2 0.0, 1; Q2 0.0, 0.31).

The Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) model was employed to identify the components or latent variables that can differentiate as much as possible between two or more different groups of samples, in line with their maximum covariance with a target class specified in the Y data block [26]. The PLS-DA scores indicated that the goodness of fit values and predictive ability values were R2X = 0.282, R2Y = 0.995, and Q2 = 0.519 in positive ion mode (Fig. 4D), and in negative ion mode, the values were R2X = 0.259, R2Y = 0.996, and Q2 = 0.464 (Supplementary Fig. 3D), indicating that the PLS-DA model had a satisfactory fit and good predictive power. Permutation test results in positive ion mode were as follows: R2 0.0, 0.99; Q2 0.0, 0.36 (Fig. 4E). In negative ion mode, the results were R2 0.0, 1; Q2 0.0, 0.41 (Supplementary Fig. 3E). These results indicate that exposure to Sev changed the composition of lung metabolites in mice during development.

Subsequently, in this study, the metabolites were further screened by using variable importance in projection (VIP), which is an important load evaluation parameter, as a threshold. A total of 19 differential metabolites were identified between the Con group and the Sev exposed group. In positive mode, compared with those in the Con group, the levels of 8 metabolites increased and those of 11 metabolites decreased in the Sev group (Fig. 4F). Besides, in negative ion mode, after exposure to Sev, the levels of 2 metabolites increased and those of 2 metabolites decreased (Supplementary Fig. 3 F). Hierarchical clustering was conducted, and the results were depicted as a heatmap, demonstrating the 30 metabolites along with their detailed relative expression levels. Notably, sphingosine levels were significantly downregulated in the BALF of Sev-exposed mice (Fig. 4G).

To determine the relevant metabolic pathways related to Sev exposure and understand the functions of metabolites, differential metabolites were placed in the KEGG database for pathway construction and analysis. The results demonstrated that these metabolites represent key metabolic pathways in positive mode, including sphingolipid signaling pathway, glycerophospholipid metabolic network, and phospholipase D signaling cascade in the sevoflurane-exposed group and so on (Fig. 4H). KEGG enrichment analysis results demonstrated that the changes in metabolites might have an impact on the functions of the nervous system, thereby influencing its normal operation. In the negative ion mode, these are the metabolic pathways as follows: steroid biosynthesis, degradation of aromatic compounds, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3G,3 H).

The lung - brain axis represents a crucial causal factor in the sevoflurane - induced neurotoxicity

Utilizing the above-stated lung microbiome and metabolomics data, we initiated Pearson’s correlation-based clustering analysis in order to recognize microbe-associated metabolites in mice subjected to sevoflurane. Correlation heatmaps clustered together the microbiota that manifested similar correlative patterns with altered metabolites at family level (Fig. 5A). The results unveiled that there is a strikingly significant correlation between the disrupted lung microbiome and the altered metabolites. This finding indicates that variations in the lung microbiome are closely intertwined with alterations in metabolite levels, suggesting a potential interaction between these two elements following exposure to Sev. Among these metabolites, previous studies have demonstrated that Sphingosine, a canonical sphingoid long-chain base, exemplifies a natural bioactive lipid endowed with robust antimicrobial activities. Preclinical in vivo investigations have unequivocally demonstrated that prophylactic inhalation of sphingosine confers significant survival advantages to infection-susceptible murine models [27].

We hypothesize that the neurotoxicity induced by sevoflurane inhalation is unlikely to arise directly from sevoflurane itself. Relevant to this, the small-molecule therapeutic FTY720 is a synthetic sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulator approved by regulatory agencies for the treatment of relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) in both adult and pediatric patient cohorts, may be a candidate for targeting such indirect pathways [28]. To further verify whether pulmonary microbiota-derived metabolites mediate lung-to-brain communication, we performed antibiotic-induced microbiota depletion (ABX) and subsequent microbiota transplantation (MT) treatments (Fig. 5B and C). Briefly, ABX treatment was used to eliminate endogenous pulmonary microbiota, while MT was performed to reconstitute the pulmonary microbiome, allowing us to evaluate the causal role of the lung microbiota in regulating lung-brain crosstalk. As shown in Fig. 5B and C, sevoflurane exposure significantly reduced the levels of sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) and sphingosine (Sph) in the lung, and these reductions were reversed by microbiota transplantation, confirming that the pulmonary microbiota directly modulates sphingolipid metabolism. To precisely determine by which mechanisms the lung metabolite exerts an influence on the central nervous system process, we traced the fluorescent dye cy5-fingolimod that was directly introduced into the lung by means of endotracheal administration (Fig. 5D and E). The mass spectrum of Cy5‑fingolimod is presented (Supplementary Fig. 4). CY5-labeled fluorescent staining revealed that specific red fluorescent signals were detected in the brain, which colocalized with microglia (Fig. 5F). These findings imply that the fingolimod may serve as a crucial mediator in relation to the lung-brain axis.

Previous comprehensive studies have meticulously demonstrated that FTY720 assumes a highly significant role by exerting essential neuroprotective effects in a wide variety of central nervous system injuries [29]. This clearly and unequivocally indicates that substances that are intimately related to fingolimod may potentially exert a profoundly extensive and significant impact on the treatment and in-depth understanding of central nervous system disorders, particularly with regard to microglia.

To explore the role of FTY720 in microglia activation induced by sevoflurane exposure during development, we subsequently administered FTY720 at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg/day via nasal instillation for 7 consecutive days commencing from postnatal day 35 [30] (Fig. 6A). The effects were then evaluated in adult mice. The results of immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that in comparison with Con group, the complexity of microglial branches in the Sev group was diminished by FTY720. Nonetheless, following FTY720 treatment, the total number of branches, branch length, and the number of branch intersections of microglia markedly increased (Fig. 6B-F). The Western Blot results manifested that exposure to sevoflurane resulted in an elevation of iNOS expression and a reduction of Arg1 expression. Concurrently, FTY720 markedly reversed the changes in iNOS and Arg1 protein expression levels brought about by sevoflurane exposure (Fig. 6G and H). In BV2 microglia, FTY720 markedly reversed the Sev-induced changes in iNOS and Arg1 protein expression levels, which was consistent with the in vivo results (Supplementary Fig. 5A-D). These results imply that intratrackal FTY720 can counteract the activation of microglia induced by Sev exposure and promote the transformation of microglia to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. To further comprehensively explore the activation status of microglia, we conducted staining for Iba1 and CD68 to label the activated microglial cells. The result showed a significant increase in CD68 levels in the Sev group, while FTY720 prominently reversed these alterations (Fig. 6I and J). In summary, these data indicate that FTY720 attenuates microglia activation resulting from repeated Sev exposure in neonatal mice.

Discussion

The results of this current study have enabled us to put forward the model that is schematically represented in Fig. 11. During the neonatal stage, repeated exposure to Sev induces the disruption of the pulmonary microbiome and variations in pulmonary metabolites, consequently leading to the down - regulation of Arg1 expression and the subsequent elevation of iNOS and CD68 levels, indicating proinflammatory activation of microglia. This phenotype of microglia activation thereby further contributs to cognitive impairment in the later life stage. The most remarkable finding in this study was the identification of sphingosine, an endogenous modulator, as a key mediator along the lung–brain axis that influences cognitive impairment through sphingolipid signaling networks. Despite mounting evidence for the lung-brain axis’s role in neurological disorders, critical gaps remain in understanding its specific regulatory mechanisms in anesthetic-induced neurotoxicity, especially during pediatric neurodevelopment [34, 35]. Nevertheless, for Sev, a commonly- used inhaled anesthetic, the effects on the pulmonary microbiota and metabolites are frequently neglected. A recent study has demonstrated that dysbiosis of the lung microbiota exerts a significant impact on the autoimmune responses within the CNS [11]. The alteration of the composition within the pulmonary flora induces a decline in cognitive function [36]. However, it remains ambiguous whether the pulmonary microbiota undergoes changes, which specific microbiota are involved in this alteration, and how they participate in the modulation of cognitive impairment caused by repeated Sev exposure.

Schematic Model of the Lung–Brain Axis Mediating Cognitive Impairment Induced by Repeated Neonatal Sevoflurane Exposure. Under homeostatic conditions, the pulmonary microbiome maintains a balanced metabolic profile, including normal levels of sphingosine (Sph) and sphingosine‑1‑phosphate (S1P). In hippocampal microglia, HDAC1 and KLF4 expression are tightly coordinated to sustain a quiescent, anti‑inflammatory state. Repeated neonatal sevoflurane exposure disrupts pulmonary microbiome homeostasis, triggering a significant reduction in both Sph and S1P levels. This metabolic perturbation propagates centrally to the hippocampus, where it drives HDAC1 upregulation and enhanced HDAC1–KLF4 binding, ultimately leading to KLF4 downregulation, pro‑inflammatory microglial activation, and subsequent cognitive impairment. Targeted interventions effectively interrupt this pathological cascade: microbiota transplantation (MT) restores pulmonary microbiome balance, normalizing Sph/S1P levels; the sphingosine‑1‑phosphate receptor modulator FTY720 restores S1P signaling to dampen HDAC1 activation; and the HDAC1 inhibitor mocetinostat (Moce) directly blocks HDAC1 activity, restoring KLF4 function and mitigating microglial overactivation. Each intervention ultimately alleviates sevoflurane‑induced cognitive deficits

To date, no reports have emerged regarding the effect of Sev exposure during the developmental period on pulmonary microbiota. This finding strongly implies that pulmonary metabolites mediate the lung-brain axis by activating microglia implicated in Sev-induced cognitive dysfunction. Confirmed by 16S rRNA microbial sequencing, the alterations in the lung microbiome, particularly the reduction of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes along with the increase in Actinobacteria, suggest that anesthetic exposure may induce dysbiosis, which can have profound implications for CNS. The disruption of the microbiome could potentially lead to impaired immune responses, as the microbiota play a crucial role in modulating host immunity and inflammation. Previous research has indicated that gut and lung microbiota can influence neuroinflammatory pathways [37, 38] which are often activated following anesthesia exposure, potentially contributing to long-term cognitive deficits observed post-operatively [39, 40]. This highlights the need for further exploration into how anesthetics may disrupt microbiome homeostasis and the subsequent effects on neuroinflammation and cognitive function. Studies have firmly demonstrated that ampicillin may expedite the dysbiosis of the pulmonary microbiota. To further clarify the role of pulmonary microbiota in long-term cognitive impairment induced by developmental Sev exposure, the present study demonstrates that Amp treatment promotes microglial polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, which exacerbates neuroinflammation and further aggravates such cognitive impairment in mice. This finding aligns with previous findings [41].

Moreover, the metabolic profiling revealed significant alterations in key metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, which are critical for maintaining neural health [42]. The identified changes in metabolites, such as increased glycine and decreased serine levels, may indicate a shift in metabolic homeostasis that could exacerbate neuroinflammatory responses [43]. The association between altered metabolic states and cognitive impairment suggests that Sev may not only affect microbial communities but also disrupt essential metabolic pathways that support cognitive function. Understanding these relationships is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of anesthetics on cognitive outcomes, particularly in vulnerable populations such as pediatric patients and the elderly.

Previous studies have demonstrated that short term or single exposure to Sev has a mild impact on cognitive function, whereas long term or repeated exposure to general anesthetics during critical developmental periods, particularly in neonatal stages, may induce cognitive dysfunction [44]. Volatile anesthetics, such as Sev, have been implicated in neurodevelopmental deficits, potentially due to their ability to disrupt normal synaptic development and induce neuroinflammation [45, 46]. Such disturbances may arise from altered immune responses and inflammatory processes, both of which can have lasting effects on cognitive function and behavior in later life stages [18, 47].

It has been shown that fingolimod is efficacious in attenuating the cognitive impairment induced by Sev [48]. Further investigations have demonstrated that FTY720 treatment significantly alleviates the activation level of microglia in the hippocampus within the animal model of chronic epilepsy [49]. Of note, FTY720 was administered intratracheally from postnatal day 35 to day 41 in the present study. This intervention window was selected based on our pulmonary microbiota and metabolomic analyses, which demonstrated that sevoflurane-induced reductions in Sph in BALF reached the most significant level at P35. We therefore initiated FTY720 treatment at this time point to specifically target the established metabolic disorder in the lung. Herein, our results have firmly established that FTY720 treatment not only alleviates cognitive impairment but also mitigates the activation of microglia following Sev exposure.

The significant upregulation of HDAC1 and downregulation of KLF4 following exposure suggests that Sev may activate neuroinflammatory pathways that contribute to cognitive deficits. Former researches have firmly validated the engagement of the HDAC1/KLF4 axis within diverse neuroinflammatory conditions [32], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for mitigating cognitive impairment associated with anesthetic exposure. The role of the HDAC1/KLF4 signaling pathway in mediating the effects of Sev on neuroinflammation presents a compelling avenue for further exploration. Numerous studies have illustrated that FTY720 potently inhibits HDAC1, thereby repressing histone deacetylation and rectifying memory deficits [32]. The findings from our research align with this body of work, suggesting that pharmacological modulation of this pathway may offer protective benefits against anesthetic-induced neuroinflammation and cognitive decline.

In our study, 16S rRNA sequencing technology and untargeted metabolomics analysis were employed to revealed the disturbance of the pulmonary flora caused by repeated inhalation of Sev, and found decreased proportion of sphingosine in the pulmonary metabolome of the mice exposed to sevofluorane. Fingolimod is structurally similar to sphingosine—a naturally occurring lipid involved in cell signaling—and it was chemically modified to act as a modulator of sphingosine − 1 - phosphate receptors. The KEGG pathway analysis also indicated that the sphingolipid signaling pathway was significantly altered after Sev exposure. This suggests a potential link between Sev - induced effects and the sphingolipid - related molecular mechanisms. Through the intratracheal administration of cy5 - Fingolimod, cy5 fluorescence was observed in microglial cells within the hippocampal region. This finding provides direct evidence that fingolimod can reach the brain via the respiratory route and interact with microglia, which play crucial roles in neuroinflammation and neuronal function. Considering the importance of microglial activity in cognitive processes, the observed presence of fingolimod in these cells may be associated with the modulation of neuroinflammatory responses and subsequent impacts on cognitive function. Further investigations are warranted to clarify the exact molecular events and functional consequences of fingolimod’s interaction with microglia in the context of Sev - related cognitive impairment. Subsequently, we delved into the potential mechanism through which FTY720 exerts its protective effect against cognitive impairment induced by Sev exposure. The HDAC1/KLF4 signaling pathway is tightly associated with microglial activation and cognitive dysfunction. Our study has revealed that FTY720 harbors the capacity to ameliorate microglial activation and cognitive impairment instigated by Sev exposure and to suppress the HDAC1/KLF4 signaling pathway. Thereby, this signaling pathway has been identified as a cardinal underlying mechanism underlying cognitive dysfunction following Sev exposure in mice within the ambit of our investigation. These findings enhance the understanding of the interplay among anesthetics, microbiome and neurodevelopment and pave the way for future therapies for Sev - induced cognitive impairments. Targeted interventions, especially those for microglial modulation, have great potential to improve clinical outcomes in relevant populations. Overall, this study may provide new insights for the prophylaxis and treatment of the neurotoxicity of general anesthetic agents.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while our work identifies a role for the HDAC1/KLF4 axis in this process, we did not fully dissect its detailed regulatory networks up- and downstream, nor the precise synergistic mechanisms by which this pathway functions. A more complete characterization of these regulatory cascades will require further exploration. Second, all our experiments were performed in a mouse model, but not include human samples or clinical data to verify our findings. This limits the direct translational relevance of our results to humans, and additional clinical studies will be necessary to confirm our observations.

Conclusions

Repeated Sev exposure in the newborns disrupts the pulmonary microbiome and alters pulmonary metabolites. Our findings suggest that the lung-brain axis, which encompasses complex neuroimmune interactions and microbiome-mediated signaling pathways, underlies the cognitive impairment induced by repeated neonatal exposure to Sev. Furthermore, the sphingosine‑1‑phosphate receptor modulator FTY720 may represent a promising intervention to alleviate such cognitive impairment by suppressing microglial activation and neuroinflammation.
