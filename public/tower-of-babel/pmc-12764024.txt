What cancers are increasing in young adults: a population-based study of incidence trends in adults 20–49 years old in the South of Spain
Authors: Dafina Petrova, Alejandro Sánchez-Martín, Daniel Redondo-Sánchez, Blanca Madrid Pérez-Esparza, Lucia Suárez Betancourt, Encarnación González-Flores, Oscar Mendoza-García, Jose-Juan Jiménez-Moleón, Maria-José Sánchez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have documented sharp increases in the incidence of several types of cancer in young adults, highlighting the need to monitor and understand epidemiological trends in this population. The aim of this study was to analyze cancer incidence trends among young adults (20–49 years) in the province of Granada, Spain, from 1985 to 2019 and to compare the trends in young adults to those observed in adults over 50 years old.MethodsWe analyzed all incident cancer cases (ICD-10 codes C00-C96), diagnosed between 1985 and 2019, in individuals aged ≥ 20 years old, from the population-based Cancer Registry of Granada, Spain. Age-adjusted incidence rates (truncated age-standardized rates) were calculated and analyzed using Joinpoint Regression to estimate annual percentage changes (APC), stratified by age group (20–49 vs. older 50+), sex, and cancer site.Results18,601 cases were identified in young adults, 58.0% in women. Overall cancer incidence increased to a larger extent in young women (APC of + 2.4%, 95% CI [2.1, 2.8]) than in young men (+ 0.8%, [0.5, 1.1]). In young men, cancer sites with significant increasing trends included thyroid (+ 9.6%, [3.9, 15.5]), testicle (+ 7.6% [6.0, 9.3]), skin melanoma (+ 4.5% [2.8, 6.1]), non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) (+ 3% [2.3, 3.8]), and brain and central nervous system (CNS) tumors (+ 2% [0.9, 3.2]). In young women, cancer sites with significant increases included CNS (+ 5.6% [3.1, 8.1]), NMSC (+ 4.3% [3.5, 5.1]), thyroid (+ 3.5% [2.3, 4.7]), skin melanoma (+ 3.2 [1.8, 4.5]), breast (+ 1.8% [1.4, 2.3]), and corpus uteri (+ 3.1% [1.2, 4.9]). Cancer sites where incidence increased to a larger extent among young vs. older persons included: rectum, thyroid, and testicular cancer for men and NMSC, rectum, CNS, lung, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, bladder, and corpus uteri for women.ConclusionThe incidence of several types of cancer has increased significantly among young adults in southern Spain, with more pronounced increases among women. There is an urgent need to investigate the underlying causes of increasing early-onset cancer trends and explore changes in early detection strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25707-4.Keywords: Cancer, Early-onset, Young adults, Incidence trends, Population-based

Introduction

Cancer can affect persons of any age but the majority of cases are diagnosed in people older than 65 years [1]. As a result, it is predominantly regarded as a disease of older age and represents a growing public health concern in light of the ageing populations in many countries. However, recent evidence shows several epidemiological shifts that have drawn attention to a growing cancer burden among younger people [2, 3].

In particular, many cancers, traditionally diagnosed in older adults, are increasingly being diagnosed in younger patients under the age of 50, a phenomenon referred to as “early-onset” cancer [3]. Multiple reports from different countries have documented rising incidence of early-onset breast and digestive system cancers, including cancers of the colorectum, extrahepatic bile duct, gallbladder, liver, stomach, and pancreas [3–8]. Increases have been reported also for tumors of the endometrium, head and neck, kidney, prostate, thyroid, and multiple myeloma (MM) [3]. The reasons behind these trends are the subject of ongoing research, with hypotheses centered on the role of exposome in early life, during pregnancy, infancy, adolescence, and early adulthood. These hypotheses focus on the impact of factors such as diet, lifestyle, obesity, antibiotics, environmental exposures, and the microbiome during these early life stages [3, 9, 10].

The growing use of screening and early detection before age 50 has likely contributed to the increasing incidence of some tumors such as breast, prostate, or thyroid cancers. However, it does not fully explain the documented trends. In addition, when cancer is diagnosed in individuals aged 20 to 49, it often presents distinct biological features from cancers diagnosed in older age [9]. Due to its young age of onset, it also presents unique personal, social, and economic consequences for patients and their families [11]. These circumstances have made incidence trends of early-onset cancer a topic of high interest, due to its implications for early detection strategies and broader cancer prevention policies [2].

Importantly, not all types of cancer are increasing among young adults and there are heterogeneous trends. To illustrate, other cancer types than those mentioned above—such as cervical, lung, laryngeal, and ovarian cancers—have shown declining trends among young adults [4]. These decreases have been attributed to successful prevention and screening programs, as well as reductions in tobacco use and infections, such as those with the Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) through vaccination.

There have also been large variations between countries and regions [5, 12]. For instance, documented increases in early-onset breast cancer have been observed fairly consistently across North America and Europe, with modest annual increases of about 1% [4]. However, in the case of early-onset colorectal cancer, data from population-based cancer registries indicate large between-country variation [13]. Incidence rates increased by up to 4% annually in New Zealand, Chile, Puerto Rico, and England, but remained stable or decreased in the Baltic countries, Italy, Spain, or Poland [12, 14]. In the case of Spain, more detailed age-group analysis revealed that colorectal cancer incidence increased but only among adults aged 20–29 [15]. Such variation likely reflects multiple factors including differential exposure to risk factors and access to early diagnosis [16].

This variation between cancers and countries illustrates the need to investigate incidence trends also regionally, taking into account the role of the specific context. In addition, with a few exceptions [9], most studies considering different types of cancer in young adults have been conducted in the United States, Asia, and Northern/Central Europe. One previous publication reported on early-onset cancer incidence in Spain but it was conducted on non-population based data drawn from a single hospital registry [17]. Such data, although useful for resource planning, are susceptible to bias due to ill-defined or incomplete population coverage, selective patient inclusion, and incomplete or inconsistent data capture. Recent population-based data from the Mediterranean regions and Spain, in particular, remain scarce, highlighting a critical gap in cancer surveillance and research that needs to be addressed to inform effective public health responses.

The main objective of this study was to investigate cancer incidence trends from 1985 to 2019 in young adults (aged 20–49 years) in the province of Granada in the south of Spain. A secondary objective was to compare the trends in young adults to those observed in adults over 50 years old, in order to provide context and identify cancers with unique early-onset increases.

Methods

A retrospective observational study was conducted to analyze cancer incidence trends in young adults (20–49 years) in Granada, Spain, from 1985 to 2019. Data were obtained from the Granada Cancer Registry, a population-based cancer registry in southern Spain that covers a reference population of approximately 950,000 inhabitants. The Granada Cancer Registry was established in 1985, has been included in successive volumes of Cancer Incidence in Five Continents, and is an active member of the European Network of Cancer Registries (ENCR).

All incident cases of invasive cancer (ICD-10 codes C00-C96), diagnosed from January 1 st, 1985 to December 31 st, 2019 in individuals aged ≥ 20 years old were included. The population was divided into two groups: young adults (20–49 years old) and older adults (≥ 50 years old).

Non-melanoma skin cancers (ICD-10: C44) were included, as were uncertain behavior and in situ tumors from the urinary tract (ICD-10: D09.0-D09.1 and D41.1-D41.4), and benign and uncertain behavior of the brain and central nervous system (CNS) (ICD-10: D32, D33, D35.2-D35.4, D42, D43, and D44.3-D44.5), according to the recommendations of the European Network of Cancer Registries (ENCR) [18, 19]. Cancer data were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3); the most recent release of the check/conversion tool was applied to recode topography into the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases [20] (see Table S1 in the Supplement). The completeness and accuracy of the data complied with national and international standards [21].

Population data were extracted from the Spanish National Institute of Statistics (http://www.ine.es).

Statistical analysis

The variables analyzed included sex, age at diagnosis, primary cancer site, and year of diagnosis. We computed annual age-standardized incidence rates (truncated age-standardized rates, ASR-E) for adults aged 20–49 overall and by sex, cancer site, and calendar year. The 2013 European standard population was used for standardization, because it currently offers the best representation of the contemporary European age distribution and is recommended for cancer trend analyses across Europe [22]. Tumor-specific trends were calculated for the 10 most frequent cancer sites for young men and women during the study period. Corpus uteri was also included as an anatomical site of interest in females. All rates were expressed per 100,000 population. For the comparison with trends observed in older adults, the same standardization methods were applied to the population aged 50 and older.

We used the Joinpoint Regression Program from the US National Cancer Institute [23] to analyze trends in the incidence rate. The estimated annual percent change (APC), with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), was calculated. Analyses were stratified by anatomical site, sex, age group, and calendar year. Trends are described as increasing or decreasing if the APC is statistically significantly different from zero (p- value < 0.05).

Results

During the study period (1985–2019), 18,601 new cases of cancer were identified in young adults in the province of Granada, 58% in women. The most common cancers in young men were non-melanoma skin (1,824 cases), lung (523 cases), and colorectal (504 cases), whereas in young women the most frequent tumors were breast (3,387 cases), non-melanoma skin (2,071 cases), and thyroid (682 cases) (see Table 1).

Discussion

In line with the hypothesized “emerging global epidemic of early onset cancer” [3], the incidence of cancer among young adults aged 20 to 49 in Granada, Spain, has increased over the past three decades [3, 4]. The overall increase has been modest and significantly more pronounced among women than among men.

Whereas such overall trends are epidemiologically informative, they mask substantial heterogeneity across cancer sites [14]. To illustrate, the modest increase in total cancer observed among young men (an APC of + 0.8%, [0.5, 1.1]) - and the flat trend after excluding NMSC (APC of + 0.1%, [−0.2, 0.5]) - may suggest a stable epidemiological situation. However, the site-specific analyses revealed a more complex picture. Among young men, there was rising incidence of thyroid, testicle, skin melanoma, NMSC, and brain and CNS tumors, in combination with recent decreasing trends in lung cancer. This heterogeneity aligns with the results of a recent study form Italy [14], a country with geographic, economic, health system, and cultural contexts similar to Spain.

Among young women, cancer incidence has increased for a larger number of cancer sites than in young men, including brain and CNS, NMSC, thyroid, skin melanoma, breast, and corpus uteri cancer, among others. These results are in line with recent evidence pointing to a growing sex disparity in early-onset cancer [24]. In particular, a study using data from the US Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) cancer registry databases reported significantly greater increases in incidence among young women for cancers such as pancreatic, gastric, myeloma, and colorectal, between 2000 and 2020 [24].

Our study detected a 4.1% annual decrease in the incidence of lung cancer in young men since the 1990s. This most likely reflects a decrease in smoking rates among young men driven by comprehensive anti-tobacco policies implemented in recent decades [25]. In contrast, the reasons behind the observed increases in many other cancer sites remain unclear and are of particular scientific interest, given their implications for prevention and early detection [9]. The factors hypothesized to play a role include overweight and obesity, gut microbiome alterations, dietary changes - increased consumption of ultraprocessed foods, the westernization of the diet, and alcohol consumption - and some environmental exposures (smoke, air pollutions, gasoline….) [9, 26, 27]. Much of the current discussion has focused on factors linked to rising rates of breast and digestive cancers, the cancers with most pronounced or consistent increases observed in other countries [3–5].

However, for some cancer sites with increasing trends in the current study, such as breast and thyroid, greater detection through opportunistic screening may also be responsible for at least part of the observed changes in trends [3]. In the case of breast cancer, there is no reliable information available regarding the extent of opportunistic screening outside of screening programs in Granada or elsewhere in Spain. In addition, the observed annual increase of 1.8% among young women aligns with the trends documented in a recent literature review across multiple countries [4]. Observing similar increases in young women across countries with different healthcare systems suggests that, although greater detection through opportunistic screening could play a role, it is unlikely the only factor explaining the increase.

In the case of thyroid cancer, a previous population-based study in Granada considering people of all ages showed increasing incidence of papillary thyroid cancer, with increasing proportion of microcarcinomas until 2013 [28]. This suggests an increase in the overdiagnosis of very small tumors (< 1 cm) is likely contributing to the rise in incidence, as a result of changes in the management of thyroid nodular disease and more availability of neck ultrasound [28, 29]. These results are further supported by a more recent study from the Balearic islands, showing a similar increase in micropapillary carcinoma and a reduction in tumor size over time [29].

In addition, a notable sex disparity was observed in thyroid cancer, such that incidence increased more sharply among young men (9.6%) than among young women (3.5%). Thyroid cancer is more often diagnosed in women and such a large sex disparity was not observed among older individuals (6.9% vs. 5.9% for men vs. women, respectively). Our results stand in contrast to findings from Canada, where thyroid cancer has been found to increase more sharply among young women than men [30]. Instead, they are more in line with results from the Nordic countries, where thyroid cancer incidence increased at similar rates for men and women [31]. A global analysis of thyroid cancer incidence showed that the proportion of thyroid cancer cases attributable to overdiagnosis was overall higher among women than among men [32]. Potential risk factors that could play a role in the increase among young people include increasing obesity rates, exposure to ionizing radiation, environmental exposures, or – as recently hypothesized – the use of mobile phones [31, 33, 34].

In the case of colorectal cancer, colon cancer trends remained relatively stable in both sexes, whereas non-significant increases were observed for rectal cancer both men (4% [−1.2, 9.4]) and women (3.6% [−2.3, 9.8]). In several countries, including US, UK, and New Zealand [12, 35], colorectal cancer incidence has risen sharply among young adults, driven largely by increases in rectal cancer [27] and neuroendocrine tumors [36]. The trends observed in our study (i.e., non-significant increases in rectal cancers) may indicate the early stages of a similar trend.

Internationally, the colorectal cancer increases in young individuals are often accompanied by a declining incidence in those over 50 (e.g. Shiels et al. [35]). However, our study found increasing rates even in the older age group. This may reflect the delayed implementation and relatively low adherence to the population-based colorectal cancer screening program in Andalucía, which did not start until 2014 [37]. To illustrate, the most recent data show that in 2024, participation in the screening program remained at below 40% of the target population, and increasing participation continues to be a key priority of the Andalusian Cancer Strategy [38]. As more recent data become available and the screening coverage improves, it is likely that Granada—and Spain more broadly—will begin to mirror international colorectal cancer trends.

In the case of skin cancer, incidence has increased among young adults for both melanoma and NMSC in both sexes. These trends may be partly explained by increased sun exposure and the use of tanning devices. Despite Spain’s high levels of natural sunlight, it has one of the highest prevalences of sunbed use in Europe [39]. A recent study examined trends in the incidence, mortality, and survival from melanoma in Granada [40]. It reported marked improvements in survival among younger age groups, highlighting the possible impact of prevention strategies and advances in treatment [40].

For the remaining cancer sites, brain and CNS tumors increased in both sexes, with a sharper rise among women and similar trends across young and older age groups. These incidence patterns are at odds with results from the US where a decreasing incidence trend was found among young adults [41]. Several factors could be contributing to the observed discrepancy. On one hand, during the early 2000 s, technological enhancement were introduced in imaging diagnostic methods (CT and, especially, MRI), improving the ability to differentiate between brain tumor types [42]. On the other hand, following ENCR guidelines distributed at the time, in 2003 the Granada Cancer Registry started to register CNS tumors with benign or uncertain behavior, due to their potentially severe clinical effects, among other reasons [18]. A visual inspection of the raw data (not shown) is indeed consistent with a sharp increase in incidence in the early 2000s. In addition, an extensive epidemiological analysis by the Spanish Network of Cancer Registries (REDECAN), excluding tumors of benign and uncertain behavior, found no significant increases in CNS tumors after this period and until 2015 [42]. This suggests that much of the increase observed in our study may be due to tumors classified as non-malignant.

At the same time, there has been an increase in mortality from CNS tumors in young women 20–49 years old in Spain (2.4% for the period 2018–2022 vs. 1999–2003) and a decrease for young men (−2.4% comparing the same periods). This suggests that, at least among women, there may be a relevant increase in life-threatening CNS tumors, warranting more detailed examination in future studies. This could be due to exposure to ionizing radiation (e.g., for previous cancer treatment) or other environmental exposures [43].

The observed increases in sex-specific cancers such as testicular and corpus uteri cancer are also in line with evidence from other countries [6].

Limitations of the current study include the relatively small population covered by the Granada Cancer Registry (about 1 million residents). This results in a low number of cases diagnosed among young people for some tumors, which limits the possibility to model their trends reliably. As a consequence, there was not enough data to study trends for some cancer sites of high interest such as pancreatic, stomach, kidney, prostate, or multiple myeloma [3, 4]. A nation-wide study of incidence trends in young adults is underway in the Spanish Network of Cancer Registries (REDECAN), which will allow to investigate trends in these and other cancers, also permitting analysis of tumor subtypes of interest, to help elucidate potential contributors to the trends observed.

Another limitation is that the most recent year of analysis was 2019, which fails to capture changes that may have occurred in the past 5 years. Such delays in data collection are, unfortunately, the rule and not the exception among cancer registries in Spain. This is the case for many countries in Europe as well, resulting in the most recent Europe-wide trends analysis rarely surpassing 2018 [12–14] or even 2012 [44]. The European Commission is taking steps to improve the timeliness of data produced by cancer registries by launching the Joint Action CancerWatch in September 2025. It will propose strategies and solutions to improve the quality and timeliness of data across different European contexts, including Spain.

A major strength of the study is the population-based nature of the data, which was obtained from a cancer registry complying with the highest current international and European standards for data collection. Population-based cancer registries cover all cases that occur among the residents of a certain area, thereby minimizing selection and information biases, and maximizing external validity. Another strength is the inclusion of NMSC, for which only a small number of cancer registries collect data. Finally, the comparison of trends in young vs. older adults helps provide context and identify cancer sites with different or more pronounced changes in younger populations, that may warrant further investigation.

The current study has implications for early detection and prevention policies. Its results support the idea that traditional assumptions about cancer as primarily a disease of older adults may be slowly losing validity [2]. Taken together, recent epidemiological results suggest that there may be a need to revise the clinical guidelines regarding the detection of certain tumors, because the threshold for suspicion and investigation may need to be lowered in young adults to minimize late-stage diagnosis. For instance, in the US the starting age for colorectal cancer screening was lowered to 45 reflecting the shifting incidence patterns of the disease in the population [45].

In order to be able to design and deliver pertinent prevention interventions, more research is needed into the causes of early onset tumors [7]. Experts recommend that this should include understanding the early-onset cancer integrome by disentangling the effects and interactions of simultaneous exposures and underlying predispositions, along with understanding the tumorigenic processes and evolutionary trajectories specific to early-onset cancers [9]. Finally, if the observed increases are maintained, the health services should prepare for a greater demand in cancer diagnosis, treatment, and further support among this age group, which, due to the early age of cancer onset, presents unique personal, social, and economic consequences for patients and their families [11].

Conclusions

Over the past 30 + years, in the province of Granada in southern Spain, there has been an increase in early-onset cancer incidence in individuals aged 20–49. Cancer incidence has increased to a larger extent in young women (APC of + 2.4%, [2.1, 2.8]) than in young men (+ 0.8%, [0.5, 1.1]). Cancer sites with unique early-onset increases (i.e., where incidence increased to a larger extent among young vs. older persons) included: rectum, thyroid, and testicular cancer for men and NMSC, rectum, brain and CNS, lung, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, bladder, and corpus uteri for women. There is an urgent need to investigate the underlying environmental and molecular causes of increasing early-onset cancer trends and explore changes in early detection strategies for some cancers.
