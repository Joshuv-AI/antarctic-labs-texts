Lurbinectedin plus irinotecan in pretreated gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms: results of a phase II expansion cohort
Authors: R Garcia-Carbonero, GM Cote, A Le Cesne, B Antón-Pascual, T Alonso-Gordoa, A Falcon, A Gil-Torralvo, S Martínez, C Kahatt, V Alfaro, P Zubiaur, J Jimenez, M Siguero, J Molina-Cerrillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA phase I/II trial (NCT02611024) evaluated the lurbinectedin 2.0 mg/m2 Day (D)1 plus irinotecan 75 mg/m2 D1 and D8 every three weeks combination in multiple solid tumors. Data from the phase II cohort of gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are reported in this manuscript.Patients and methodsThirty-four patients with NENs (second line or greater) were included, 20 with poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), and 14 with grade 2/3 well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of digestive origin. The primary efficacy endpoint was objective response rate (ORR) according to RECIST v.1.1. Secondary endpoints included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), safety, and pharmacokinetics.ResultsIn patients with NECs, the ORR with lurbinectedin plus irinotecan was 15.0% [95% confidence interval (CI) 3.2%-37.9%], the median PFS was 3.1 months (95% CI 1.4-5.6 months), and the median OS was 7.2 months (95% CI 2.9-17.8 months). In patients with NETs, the ORR with lurbinectedin plus irinotecan was 7.1% (95% CI 0.2%-33.9%), the median PFS was 3.4 months (95% CI 1.4-8.9 months), and the median OS was 16.6 months (95% CI 3.9 months-not reached). The most common treatment-related adverse events were gastrointestinal disorders (nausea, 61.8% of patients; diarrhea, 44.1%; and vomiting, 35.3%), fatigue (58.8%), and decreased appetite (20.6%); these events were mostly grades 1 and 2. Grade ≥3 neutropenia was observed in 47.1% of patients and febrile neutropenia in 5.9%. Grade ≥3 transaminase increases were the most common severe laboratory biochemical abnormalities reported during treatment regardless of relationship to study drugs.ConclusionsThe combination of lurbinectedin plus irinotecan with primary granulocyte colony-stimulating factor prophylaxis showed antitumor activity in GEP-NECs but limited activity in GEP-NETs. This combination had a predictable and manageable safety profile, with myelosuppression, gastrointestinal disorders, and fatigue as main toxicities. The lurbinectedin plus irinotecan combination deserves to be further explored in GEP-NECs after failure of platinum therapy.Key words: irinotecan, lurbinectedin, neuroendocrine carcinomas, phase II clinical trial

Introduction

Digestive neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are an uncommon and heterogeneous family of tumors. Prognosis widely varies based on primary tumor site, histological differentiation, and proliferative index. High-grade [grade 3 (G3), Ki67 proliferative index >20%] NENs have a particularly aggressive behavior and limited treatment options.1, 2, 3 One of the largest European prospective registries (NORDIC NEC2) has reported objective response rates (ORRs) with first-line chemotherapy of 27% and 33% for well-differentiated G3 neuroendocrine tumors (G3 NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), and overall survival (OS) of 21.8 and 7.4 months, respectively.4 The most used regimens are temozolomide-based chemotherapy for G3 NETs and platinum-based chemotherapy for NECs. Radioligand therapy is an alternative treatment option for somatostatin–receptor-positive G3 NETs with Ki-67 index <55%, achieving ORR of 48% and median progression-free survival (PFS) of 22 months in the first-line setting, although it is not currently approved in Europe for this indication. Second-line chemotherapy yields ORR of 10%-15% for both G3 NETs and NECs, PFS of ∼6 and 2-3 months, and OS of ∼14 and 4-6 months.5,6 Alternative treatment options are therefore urgently needed.

Lurbinectedin is a synthetic marine-derived antitumor agent that acts as a selective inhibitor of oncogenic transcription.7 Lurbinectedin–DNA adducts in guanine-rich DNA sequences stall RNA polymerase II elongation, promoting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, and inducing DNA breaks that ultimately trigger apoptosis. Lurbinectedin in combination with irinotecan showed synergistic antitumor activity in preclinical studies.8 The combination of lurbinectedin and irinotecan combines distinct DNA-damaging mechanisms that inhibit both transcription and DNA replication, leading to enhanced apoptosis. Lurbinectedin binds DNA minor grooves, inhibiting RNA polymerase II,9 whereas irinotecan inhibits topoisomerase I,10 preventing repair of double-strand breaks. A phase I/II trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02611024) evaluated this combination in patients with different advanced solid tumors.11 The phase II stage evaluated the antitumor activity of the combination in tumor-specific cohorts selected based on the efficacy results of the first stage. Preliminary results showed antitumor activity and manageable toxicity in small-cell lung cancer (SCLC),12,13 soft tissue sarcoma (STS),14 and gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) NECs.15

Lurbinectedin showed encouraging efficacy in patients with pretreated small-cell lung NECs, both as a single agent16 and in combination with other chemotherapeutic drugs such as irinotecan or doxorubicin.17 In 2020, accelerated approval was granted in the U.S. and later in 22 other countries to lurbinectedin for metastatic SCLC upon progression to platinum-based chemotherapy,18 and in 2025, lurbinectedin was also approved in the U.S. and Switzerland in combination with atezolizumab as maintenance therapy in the first-line setting for patients with extensive-stage SCLC who have not progressed during first-line induction therapy.19 A recent retrospective analysis evaluated 19 patients with high-grade extrapulmonary NENs treated with lurbinectedin: ORR was 52.6% [95% confidence interval (CI) 36.0% to 78.4%], and median PFS was 3.4 months (95% CI 2.5-8.4 months).20 Hence, lurbinectedin combined with irinotecan was also explored in NENs of other primary tumor sites, such as GEP-NENs, as a potential new therapeutic option in this ultraorphan disease setting. This report describes the efficacy and safety results of lurbinectedin plus irinotecan in the GEP-NEN expansion cohort of this phase I/II study.

Materials and methods

The trial was conducted at 11 hospitals from Europe (France and Spain) and the U.S. in compliance with the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The protocol was approved by the centers’ Clinical Research Ethics Committees. Signed written informed consent was obtained for each patient before any study-specific procedures.

Patient selection

Eligible patients in this cohort were GEP-NENs aged ≥18 years, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score ≤1, a life expectancy ≥3 months, measurable disease according to RECIST v.1.1,21 documented disease progression during or immediately after last therapy, no residual toxicity from previous therapies > grade 1, and adequate bone marrow, hepatic, renal, and metabolic function. Patients were excluded if they had been pretreated with lurbinectedin or trabectedin; had received bone marrow or stem cell transplantation or radiotherapy in >35% of bone marrow; were lactating women or fertile man or woman not using effective contraceptives; and had symptomatic brain metastases or leptomeningeal disease, relevant cardiac disease, active infection, any disease interfering with study outcome, or hypersensitivity to any of the study drugs or their excipients.

Classification of NENs was based on tumor differentiation and proliferation rate, done according to the 2019 World Health Organization classification of tumors of the digestive system.22 Two cohorts of patients were included:

•Cohort 1: Patients with poorly differentiated G3 NECs (Ki-67 >20%) of GEP origin or unknown primary site (lung primary tumors were excluded) pretreated with one prior line of platinum-based chemotherapy.

Cohort 1: Patients with poorly differentiated G3 NECs (Ki-67 >20%) of GEP origin or unknown primary site (lung primary tumors were excluded) pretreated with one prior line of platinum-based chemotherapy.

•Cohort 2: Well-differentiated grade 2 high (Ki-67 11%-20%) or grade 3 low (Ki-67 21%-55%) GEP-NETs pretreated with no more than three prior lines of systemic therapy [that could include somatostatin analogs (SSAs), chemotherapy, everolimus, and/or sunitinib].

Cohort 2: Well-differentiated grade 2 high (Ki-67 11%-20%) or grade 3 low (Ki-67 21%-55%) GEP-NETs pretreated with no more than three prior lines of systemic therapy [that could include somatostatin analogs (SSAs), chemotherapy, everolimus, and/or sunitinib].

Study treatment

Patients received treatment at the recommended phase I dose11: lurbinectedin 2.0 mg/m2 administered on day (D)1 as a 1-h i.v. infusion combined with irinotecan 75 mg/m2 administered on D1 and D8 as a 90-min i.v. infusion every 3 weeks (q3wk), with primary granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) prophylaxis. In case of irinotecan discontinuation, patients could continue treatment with lurbinectedin as a single agent at the monotherapy approved dose (3.2 mg/m2).18 Patients could not continue treatment with irinotecan alone.

Treatment was administered until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, intercurrent illness precluding study continuation, patient refusal and/or noncompliance with study requirements, treatment delay >15 days (except if clear clinical benefit), or >2 dose reductions required per drug (irinotecan or lurbinectedin). Standard antiemetic prophylaxis (dexamethasone 8 mg and ondansetron 8 mg, or equivalent) was given before each infusion. No primary antidiarrheal prophylaxis was administered.

Efficacy assessment

The primary efficacy endpoint was ORR according to RECIST v.1.1. Secondary efficacy endpoints comprised duration of response (DoR), clinical benefit rate [ORR or stable disease (SD) for ≥4 months], disease control rate (ORR or SD of any duration), PFS, and OS. Patients were assessable for efficacy if they received at least one complete dose of lurbinectedin and irinotecan. Radiological assessment (computed tomography scan or magnetic resonance imaging) was done by the investigators every 6 weeks.

Safety assessment

Patients were assessable for safety if they received at least one partial or complete infusion of the study treatment. Safety was monitored throughout treatment and up to 30 days after the last treatment infusion or the start of a new antitumor therapy, whichever occurred first. Any treatment-related adverse event (AE) was followed until recovery to grade ≤1, or until the start of a new antitumor therapy, whichever occurred first. Hematology and biochemistry tests were done at baseline, weekly during cycle 1, and on D1 and D8 during subsequent cycles. AEs and laboratory abnormalities were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities v.25.0 (McLean, Virginia, USA) and graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) v.4 (Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Pharmacokinetic assessment

The plasma pharmacokinetics (PK) of lurbinectedin and irinotecan (and its metabolite SN-38) were evaluated during cycle 1 with a schedule of 12 samples (from preinfusion until 7 days after the start of the first infusion). Standard noncompartmental analysis was done using Phoenix WinNonLin v.8.2.2 (Certara USA Inc, Princeton, USA). Lurbinectedin, irinotecan, and SN-38 concentrations were measured in plasma by means of a validated liquid extraction method followed by ultraperformance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry detection.

Statistical analysis

The phase II expansion stage aimed to include 20 patients in each cohort (NECs and NETs). The NET cohort (n = 14), however, was prematurely closed due to the low accrual rate. In each of these cohorts, the maximum half-width of the estimated ORR with exact CI at 95% for 20 patients would be between 8% (if all treated patients showed response to treatment) and 23% (if half of the treated patients showed response to treatment).

Continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics and categorical variables were presented in frequency tables. Binomial exact distribution was used to calculate 95% CIs for categorical variables. Time-to-event variables were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. PFS was defined as the time from initiation of study therapy to disease progression or death or last follow-up in alive patients without progression. OS was defined as the time from study treatment initiation to death from any cause or last follow-up in alive patients. Statistical Analysis System v.9.4 (Cary, North Carolina, USA) was used for all analyses.

Cohort 1: patients with GEP-NECs (n = 20)

The majority of patients were male (n = 13, 65.0%), Caucasian (n = 19, 95.0%), had an ECOG PS of 1 (n = 14, 70.0%), and metastatic disease at diagnosis (n = 18, 90.0%). The median age was 57 years (range, 41-75 years) and the most common primary sites were pancreas (n = 5, 25.0%) and gastric and unknown (n = 4, 20.0% each). Median number of metastatic sites at baseline was 3 (range, 1-7). Most NECs had a Ki-67 >55% (n = 12, 60.0%) and were large cell (n = 10, 50.0%). All patients had received prior platinum-based chemotherapy. Patients had received a median of one prior line of systemic therapy (range, 1-6), with 35.0% of patients having received ≥2 lines.

Cohort 2: patients with GEP-NETs (n = 14)

Half of the patients were male (n = 7, 50.0%) and the majority were Caucasian (n = 13, 92.9%), had an ECOG PS of 1 (n = 9, 64.3%), and presented metastatic disease at diagnosis (n = 10, 71.4%). The median age was 61.5 years (range, 38-74 years) and the most common primary site was the pancreas (n = 8, 57.1%). Median number of metastatic sites at baseline was 3 (range, 1-5). NETs were grade 2 (n = 8, 57.1%) or grade 3 (n = 6, 42.9%). Ki-67 ≤55% was reported in all 14 patients (100.0%). Patients had received a median of two prior lines of systemic therapy (range, 1-3), with 42.9% of patients having received ≥2 lines. Most common prior therapy included chemotherapy [capecitabine and temozolomide (n = 6, 42.9%) and platinum compounds (n = 5, 35.7%)], targeted therapy (n = 6, 42.9%), and SSAs (n = 5, 35.7%).

Treatment exposure

Patients were on treatment for a median of 3.1 months (range, 0.7-34.5 months). No patients received lurbinectedin alone.

Cohort 1: patients with GEP-NECs (n = 20)

The median number of cycles per patient was 3.0 (range, 1-47 cycles). The median relative dose intensities for lurbinectedin and irinotecan were 94.8% and 67.4%, respectively.

Cohort 2: patients with GEP-NETs (n = 14)

The median number of cycles per patient was 4.5 (range, 2-27 cycles). The median relative dose intensities for lurbinectedin and irinotecan were 87.9% and 75.5%, respectively.

Cohort 1: patients with GEP-NECs (n = 20)

ORR was 15.0% (95% CI 3.2% to 37.9%), including three partial responses (PRs). All patients had progressed to platinum-based chemotherapy. One patient had a metastatic large cell gastric NEC (Ki-67 = 20%) pretreated with one line of therapy (PD as best previous response); this patient achieved best response after cycle 5 and completed a total of 13 cycles until disease progression. The second patient had a metastatic large cell jejunal NEC (Ki-67 = 50%) pretreated with two lines of therapy (SD as best previous response); this patient achieved best response after cycle 7 and completed a total of 47 cycles. Treatment was ultimately discontinued without evidence of disease progression to allow a treatment break. The third patient had a metastatic small-cell NEC of unknown primary origin (Ki-67 = 70%-80%) pretreated with one line of therapy (PR as best previous response); this patient achieved best response after cycle 7 and completed a total of 18 cycles before disease progression. Clinical benefit rate and disease control rate were 40.0% and 60.0%, respectively. Median DoR was 8.1 months [95% CI 5.7 months—upper limit not reached (NR)], median PFS was 3.1 months (95% CI 1.4-5.6 months), and median OS was 7.2 months (95% CI 2.9-17.8 months). Safety results

Safety is provided for all GEP-NENs. The most frequent AEs of any grade that were related to treatment (or with unknown relationship) in all 34 patients were gastrointestinal disorders [nausea (61.8% of patients), diarrhea (44.1%), and vomiting (35.3%)], fatigue (58.8%), and decreased appetite (20.6%) (Table 3). The most common grade ≥3 AEs were fatigue (8.8%), diarrhea (8.8%), febrile neutropenia (5.9%), nausea (5.9%), and vomiting (5.9%).

The most common grade ≥3 laboratory abnormalities regardless of relationship reported during treatment were hematological (Table 3). Grade ≥3 neutropenia (47.1% of patients, grade 4 in 17.6%) lasted a median of 5 days (range, 1-46 days) and was mostly afebrile, and febrile neutropenia was observed in 5.9% of patients. Grade ≥3 anemia (23.5%, grade 4 in 0%) occurred mostly among patients who already had anemia at baseline (grade 1-2 anemia was present initially in 52.9% of patients). Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia (11.8%, grade 4 in 5.9%) lasted a median of 6 days (range, 3-9 days) and was not associated with bleeding. Overall, seven patients (20.6%) required additional G-CSF therapeutic support, five patients (14.7%) received red blood cell transfusions, one patient (2.9%) received platelet transfusions, and four patients (11.8%) received erythropoiesis-stimulating agents during treatment. Grade ≥3 transaminase increases were the most common severe laboratory biochemical abnormalities reported regardless of relationship during treatment (Table 3). Grade ≥3 aspartate aminotransferase (AST) increases (17.6%) lasted a median of 5 days (range, 2-8 days). Grade ≥3 alanine aminotransferase (ALT) increases (14.7% of patients) lasted a median of 3 days (range, 2-15 days).

Most treatment discontinuations (n = 24, 70.6%) were due to disease progression. The rate of treatment discontinuation due to any grade treatment-related AEs was 8.8% (n = 3 patients); these AEs consisted of grade 3 pseudomembranous colitis, grade 2 asthenia, and grade 4 hyperbilirubinemia.

Death related to treatment occurred in one patient (2.9%); this was a 63-year-old man with a pancreatic NEC with liver metastasis, a baseline ECOG PS of 1, who was pretreated with one line of platinum and etoposide and received one study treatment dose with prophylactic G-CSF. On day 8, he was hospitalized with grade 4 febrile neutropenia and grade 5 Staphylococcal bacteremia. Despite antibiotic therapy and supportive care, the patient died on day 24.

PK results

PK parameters obtained during cycle 1 are shown in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108534). In agreement with previous phase I findings,11 wide variability was observed in total clearance (CL) for all three analytes: 28.65% for lurbinectedin, 46.26% for irinotecan, and 75.88% for SN-38. An assessment of the relationships between the CL and area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) showed a negative relationship between lurbinectedin AUC and irinotecan CL (R2 = 0.039, P < 0.001), and between irinotecan AUC and lurbinectedin CL (R2 = 0.051, P < 0.001) in patients with GEP-NENs (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108534). Therefore, a drug–drug interaction (DDI) cannot be ruled out.

Discussion

This phase I/II trial assessed lurbinectedin and irinotecan in two different cohorts of GEP-NENs, a cohort of 20 patients with poorly differentiated NECs and a cohort of 14 patients with well-differentiated NETs. The results show encouraging activity and an acceptable safety profile, particularly in the most aggressive subgroup of NECs, who have very limited treatment options and a dismal prognosis.

In patients with pretreated NECs, ORR with lurbinectedin plus irinotecan was 15.0%, median PFS was 3.1 months, and median OS was 7.2 months. Particularly remarkable were outcomes for patients with NECs and Ki-67 proliferation index ≤55% (ORR of 28.6%, median PFS of 8.7 months, and median OS of 15.1 months), although this subgroup analysis should be interpreted with caution due to the low patient numbers. These results are encouraging, considering that the largest prospective study in this setting, the NORDIC NEC2, reported for second-line chemotherapy a response rate of 14%, a median PFS of 2.1 months, and a median OS of 4.4 months.4 The global results of our study are also well aligned with the efficacy of current second-line standard of care regimens, such as 5-FU/irinotecanand nal-IRI/5-FU/folinic acid,5,6,23 that have reported ORR of 11%-18%, median PFS of 3-3.5 months, and median OS of 6.0-8.9 months. It should also be noted that our study population, as opposed to patients included in the NET02 or BEVANEC trials,2,24 was more heavily pretreated, as it was not a pure second-line treatment setting (patients received up to five prior lines of treatment and 25% had received two or more prior treatment lines).

The NET cohort (n = 14) was prematurely closed due to low accrual rate. Only one objective response (ORR 7.1%) was observed in a patient with a grade 2 (Ki-67 = 20%) metastatic pancreatic NET pretreated with three prior lines of therapy. The median PFS in this cohort was 3.4 months and the median OS was 16.6 months. Treatment options for advanced progressive and pretreated well-differentiated NETs vary by primary tumor site and are rather limited for lung (everolimus and cabozantinib) or small bowel primaries (Lu177-Dotatate, everolimus, and cabozantinib), with pancreatic NETs having a broader spectrum of approved active therapies (Lu177-Dotatate, everolimus, sunitinib, cabozantinib, and chemotherapy) and being the most sensitive to chemotherapy. Targeted therapies (everolimus, sunitinib, and cabozantinib), mostly tested in grade 1-2 NETs, are generally associated with low response rates (generally <10%), with median PFS ranging from 8 to 14 months.25, 26, 27, 28, 29 Other options associated with higher response rates, although widely variable depending on proliferation rates (lower in grade 1-2 versus grade 3 tumors) and primary tumor sites (lower in gastrointestinal versus pancreatic primaries), include 177Lu-DOTATATE (ORR ∼15%-40% and PFS ∼23-28 months)30,31 and chemotherapy (CAPTEM or STZ-5FU) (ORR ∼10%-30% and PFS ∼9-23 months).32, 33, 34 Although cross-trial comparisons are particularly challenging in this very heterogeneous patient population and the size of the NET population in our study was very small, overall efficacy of the lurbinectedin and irinotecan combination seems limited.

The combination of lurbinectedin 2.0 mg/m2 D1 plus irinotecan 75 mg/m2 D1 and D8 q3wk with primary G-CSF prophylaxis was well tolerated and showed a predictable and manageable safety profile in patients with NENs with no new safety concerns. The most common treatment-related AEs were gastrointestinal disorders (nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting), fatigue, and decreased appetite; these events were mostly grades 1 and 2 and rarely had any effects on treatment. Grade 3/4 neutropenia was not cumulative and was managed with delays, irinotecan dose omissions, and dose reductions. It should be noted that the incidence of grade 3/4 neutropenia per current definition (NCI-CTCAE v.6) would be much lower (17.6%). Few biochemical abnormalities reached grade 3/4, with the most common being transaminase increases. The safety profile of this combination was similar to that observed in patients with SCLC12 or STS.14 The lurbinectedin plus irinotecan combination is currently being evaluated in an ongoing randomized phase III study (LAGOON trial; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05153239) in second-line SCLC.35

The safety profile of the combination of lurbinectedin plus irinotecan at the recommended dose in this phase II cohort was compared with the safety profile of single-agent lurbinectedin from pooled data in 554 patients treated with lurbinectedin 3.2 mg/m2 q3wk in one phase II trial and one phase III trial.36 Compared with single-agent lurbinectedin, patients treated with lurbinectedin plus irinotecan showed higher incidences of treatment-related gastrointestinal toxicities (Table 4). These findings are consistent with the results of phase II and III studies conducted with single-agent irinotecan in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer.37,38 In these studies, gastrointestinal disorders (mainly diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting) were the most clinically relevant AEs related to single-agent irinotecan administered every 2 weeks to these patients. The incidence of treatment-related AEs (both all grades and grade ≥3) was higher with lurbinectedin plus irinotecan than with single-agent lurbinectedin (Table 4). As a result, more patients treated with lurbinectedin plus irinotecan required dose reduction due to treatment-related AEs (35% versus 21%) and discontinued treatment (9% versus 3%) than patients treated with lurbinectedin alone.

A potential DDI between lurbinectedin and irinotecan was observed, whereas no apparent interaction was detected for SN-38. Both lurbinectedin and irinotecan are metabolized by CYP3A4, whereas SN-38 is primarily metabolized by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase isoforms to glucuronide conjugates and eliminated either unchanged or as glucuronides via biliary excretion, mediated by ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters. Irinotecan can also be excreted unchanged into bile.39,40 Although the precise mechanism underlying this DDI is unknown, the selective effect on irinotecan but not on SN-38 suggests that modulation of CYP3A4, rather than interference with ABC transporters, is the more likely mechanism. Because neither drug is considered an inhibitor of CYP3A4 and the AUC of either drug is inversely related to the clearance of the other, the competitive inhibition of CYP3A4 seems the most likely explanation. Overall, the magnitude of the DDI appears small, accounting for only ∼5% of the PK variability for both drugs (R2 = 0.039 and 0.051; Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108534) and is therefore unlikely to be clinically meaningful.

The study nevertheless has some limitations, including the heterogeneity of the study population, the typically limited number of patients in phase I-II clinical trials, the early closure of the NET cohort, the lack of a control arm, and the lack of a central pathology review to confirm an adequate classification of these tumors.

Conclusions

The combination of lurbinectedin 2.0 mg/m2 as a 1-h i.v. infusion on D1 plus irinotecan 75 mg/m2 as a 90-min i.v. infusion on D1 and D8 q3wk with primary G-CSF prophylaxis showed encouraging antitumor activity in GEP-NECs but limited activity in GEP-NETs. This combination had a predictable and manageable safety profile, with myelosuppression, gastrointestinal disorders, and fatigue as the main reported toxicities. Poorly differentiated GEP-NECs are rapidly lethal neoplasms of challenging clinical management and limited treatment options.1 In this context, the lurbinectedin plus irinotecan combination deserves to be further explored in patients who have failed previous platinum therapy.
