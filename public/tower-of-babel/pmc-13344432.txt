Qualitative perceptions of child and youth mental health services by caregivers in the WHO European Region: a systematic review of qualitative literature
Authors: Tania Cardona, Katie H Atmore, Raffaella Sibilio, Jennifer Hall, Anastasia Giannaki, Penny Kalpaxi, Joao Breda, Ledia Lazeri, Tatiana Taylor Salisbury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rising prevalence of mental health conditions in children and young people requires responsive, high-quality care from child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) that consider the needs of service-users and their caregivers. This systematic review seeks to contribute evidence-based recommendations from caregivers’ perspectives to inform the development of quality standards for CAMHS in the World Health Organization (WHO) European Region. A systematic review of qualitative literature on caregiver perspectives on CAMHS quality published in the WHO European Region between 1 January 2010 and 9 October 2023 in Embase, OVID MEDLINE, APA PsycINFO, Global Health, and CINAHL databases was conducted. Quantitative studies, studies focusing on neurodevelopmental disorders, inpatient, general practitioner, joint working, early-intervention, or transitional care services were excluded. The search strategy was registered on PROSPERO (ref: CRD42023467689). Data from 18 papers including the perspectives of 754 caregivers, mostly from Western Europe and particularly the UK, were thematically analysed into three main themes and sub-themes covering help-seeking, service provision, and caregiving. The study outcomes highlight the need to consider the wellbeing of caregivers as well as service-users, address the mental health literacy of caregivers, the wider community, and frontline professionals, ensure sufficient resources for a competent and appropriate workforce to address stigma and support the delivery of appropriate care, engage caregivers in care provision, design responsive and holistic services that enhance smooth care transitions, and support the active participation of young people and their caregivers in care delivery.

Introduction

Mental health conditions are common among children and adolescents, and their prevalence has increased in recent years [1]. Globally, they affect an estimated 293 million, or 11.63%, of persons aged 5–24 years [2], with suicide being the fourth leading cause of death in adolescents [3]. Figures for Europe indicate an estimated 15.5% pooled prevalence of mental health conditions among 5- to 18-year-olds [4]. Many young people needing mental healthcare do not receive a diagnosis or access appropriate services [5] and if left untreated, young-onset mental health conditions can have significant health, social, and academic impacts and lead to greater psychological burden later in life [1–3, 6].

Caregivers play a major role in the health and wellbeing of young people and are often responsible for their healthcare needs [1, 7]. This role is acknowledged within Article 5 of the UN’s Convention of the Rights of the Child, which emphasizes the need to balance young peoples’ rights with the rights and responsibilities of those legally responsible for their wellbeing [8]. Therefore, activities focused on improving the quality of child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) should incorporate caregivers’ perspectives and needs. Evidence suggests caregivers may encounter challenges when supporting service use due to obstacles such as waiting lists, unaffordable costs, confidentiality issues, and personal circumstances including the strength of their support network [9].

Improving CAMHS quality is an integral objective of the WHO Regional Office for Europe [10]. As part of this work, the Office has sought to develop new quality standards for outpatient and community-based child and youth mental health services that incorporate the perspectives of service-users, caregivers, and staff [3]. Caregiver perspectives of CAMHS quality within the WHO European Region have not been reviewed previously. This systematic review of qualitative literature aims to synthesize caregivers’ qualitative perspectives of service quality to generate recommendations to inform the content of these standards.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic search for research published in English between 1 January 2010 and 9 October 2023 was conducted across Embase, OVID MEDLINE, APA PsycINFO, Global Health, and CINAHL databases. Search terms included MeSH and expanded terms adapted to each database to capture concepts relating to children and adolescents, caregivers, mental health, qualitative research, and the WHO European Region. Included studies were published in peer-reviewed journals and used qualitative methods such as interviews, focus groups, or free-text feedback to review existing CAMHS from the perspectives of adult caregivers. Services reviewed had to be specialized outpatient or community mental health services for persons up to age 25 years with a mental health condition as defined by ICD-11.

Studies were excluded if they analysed qualitative data not collected for the study objectives, such as case notes, audits, and session recordings. To improve comparability and maintain a focus on CAMHS, reviews of inpatient services, general practitioner mental health services, early intervention services, and services not providing treatment for mental health conditions (such as promotion or prevention services) were excluded, as were reviews of multiple services, including transitions from child to adult services or joint services, and specialized services for neurodevelopmental disorders as defined by ICD-11. In case of insufficient evidence in the text, authors were contacted to ascertain details. The search strategy was registered on PROSPERO (ref: CRD42023467689). Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11].

Researchers used the Rayyan platform for deduplication and screening of titles and abstracts. K.A. screened all titles and abstracts, T.C., R.S., P.K., A.G., and J.H. double-screened 20% of the list for accuracy and agreement in line with methodological guidance for rapid reviews [12]. K.A., T.C., and R.S. conducted full-text screening, and 20% were double-screened by a second team member. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussion to reach consensus.

Data analysis

T.C. extracted study characteristics and qualitative outcomes describing service quality from caregiver perspectives, with the results presented in tabular form and reviewed by K.A., R.S., and T.T.S.. T.C. used the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist [13] to assess the quality of included publications, with the outcomes reviewed by K.A. and R.S. Studies scoring ‘No’ on more than one CASP criterion were excluded before thematic synthesis to ensure methodological rigour. This threshold was applied pragmatically by the review team to ensure that included studies reached minimum methodological quality standards while retaining sufficient breadth for analysis. Thematic synthesis [14] was then undertaken to identify themes aligned with the scope of the research aim and objectives, and patterns across studies were analysed to inform actionable recommendations. Sensitivity analysis was not conducted due to heterogeneity in the design, context, and reporting methods of included studies.

Role of the funding source

The funder had no role in study design, data collection, analysis or interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Database searches yielded 22 926 results, of which 378 full-text articles were screened for eligibility, with 18 articles [15–32], representing 18 studies, included in the thematic synthesis (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). All studies were conducted in Western European countries, with the majority (61.1%; n = 11) from the UK. Half of the studies (50%; n = 9) related to CAMHS for multiple mental health conditions, while the rest were conducted in services for specific conditions. One study included services for children aged seven years or older (5.5%), five studies included services for adolescents aged 11 years or older (27.8%), six studies (33.3%) included services for 0–18 years, one study included services for service-users of 0–25 years, and one study included services for children under six. The rest (22.2%; n = 4) did not specify an age-range but reported providing CAMHS services (Table 1). The included studies report data from the perspectives of 754 caregivers, most of whom were mothers (77.6%; n = 541 from 697 where details were included) (Supplementary Table S2).

IPA: Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis.

Three main themes relating to quality of care were identified: caregiver perspectives of help-seeking, service provision, and caregiving (Table 2). Overall, the findings show how poor mental health literacy and stigma often delay initial help-seeking, increasing the likelihood that caregivers approach services during crisis points. Once help was sought, gatekeeping processes, fragmented care pathways, and service inefficiencies intensified distrust in the services and caregiver burden. In turn, caregivers’ resilience and capacity to continue navigating complex systems was impacted, heightening distress.

Theme 1: perspectives of help-seeking

Caregivers reported that poor mental health literacy, both among themselves and also within their community, often contributed to delayed symptom recognition, affected their trust in services, and postponed timely engagement with care [15–17, 24, 31]. They stated that the issue was made worse by young peoples’ lack of self-awareness [17] and stigma and discrimination surrounding mental health [16–18, 24], with minority groups experiencing additional difficulties [24].

‘I was disappointed with most of my family because how they treated my daughter. She is a very close family member, and still they were making fun of her.’ (Pakistani Mother, Specialist Paediatric Psychiatry Clinic, UK) [24]

Caregivers highlighted how these issues often led to them waiting until crisis point before seeking help, when they no longer felt able to cope on their own [17, 24, 26, 31].

‘You put up with it for a long time and then there’s a point when you break… when you refer yourself to CAMHS or to anybody you waited till the last minute, till you can’t cope.’ (Parent, CAMHS, UK) [17]

Theme 2: perspectives of service provision

Caregivers described service provision as a fragmented process, where staff mental health literacy, stigma, lack of resources, gatekeeping processes, communication, continuity of care, and support provision were closely interconnected.

Caregivers were concerned about frontline professionals’ mental health literacy and stigma towards mental health, linking these with lack of willingness to refer to specialized care and provide support, delayed or unsuccessful referrals, and caregivers feeling misunderstood and distrustful of the system [17, 18, 29, 31].

‘There was nothing. I just walked into the walls. The GP didn’t know what to do and did not trust himself to give the child any medication.’ (Parent, National Mental Health Service, Iceland) [31]

Caregivers’ trust was also impacted by their previous experiences dealing with services, particularly by their perception of healthcare professionals’ competency to manage the young person’s mental health condition. Caregivers expected specialized staff to provide empathic, non-judgemental, and privacy-sensitive care, reflecting their concerns on stigma and its potential impact on young people’s future and social standing [15, 16, 18, 19, 22–24, 26, 27, 30]. Experiences of discrimination, such as for example racial stereotyping, impacted particularly minoritized ethnic groups [24], while culturally-informed services and interpreter access improved caregiver experiences and trust in care [15, 19, 24].

‘They just do not see or recognize that person beyond their color, and it takes a while, even with my daughter it took a while, for them to see past her culture, and see her as a person.’ (Mother, Specialist Paediatric Psychiatry Clinic, UK) [24]

Caregivers sought professionals that used age-appropriate support for recovery, engaged regularly with caregivers, and practiced conflict mediation [15, 18, 19, 26–28, 30, 32]. Apart from this, caregivers looked to healthcare services to alleviate their caregiving struggles [15–17, 26, 31]. They valued services that provided useful resources and flexibility [16, 17, 22–24, 26–29], empowered caregivers through active engagement [16, 22, 23, 25–31], facilitated advocacy [16, 17, 21, 26], and addressed caregivers’ physical and mental health needs [24].

‘Whilst I’m going through this and nobody has said ‘are you okay? How is this playing out on you?’ (Parent, CAMHS, UK) [26]

Caregivers highlighted lack of resources as another important factor impacting quality care. Lack of human, infrastructural, and financial resources led to gatekeeping of specialized care services [17, 24, 31, 32], continuity of care issues across sectors [23, 26–28, 30], and threatened the delivery of integrated and holistic services providing personalized care [17, 22, 24, 31]. These challenges led to service access delays, staff shortages, high staff turnovers, lack of caregiver engagement, lack of support for caregivers to provide care at home, and caregivers resorting to private care [15, 17, 19, 20, 22, 24–28, 30–32].

‘One always has the sense that the service is not as well-resourced as it might be (…). So probably we didn’t see the most expert people as quickly as we should have.’ (Father, Specialist Paediatric Psychiatry Clinic, UK) [24]

Caregivers expressed the need for professionals to liaise with support services on their behalf, especially when these may be inadequately trained or have different priorities not aligned with the young peoples’ needs [21, 26].

‘You might have a fantastic CAMHS worker and have great sessions there and then you’ve got to go to school and nobody understands you, there’s no point. So it has to be everyone.’ (Parent, CAMHS, UK) [26]

Positive experiences were reported when caregivers’ concerns were acknowledged [17], they were provided with support and resources while awaiting specialized care [32] and during care transitions [26, 30], and were provided with clear communication and resources to discuss ongoing concerns [28, 30]. Single-point-of-access services reduced gatekeeping issues as caregivers could self-refer the young person and had direct access to specialist staff [29].

‘Because […] the bridge of talking to someone, the step of going and doing it online, it’s not face-to-face. It’s just one less hurdle really, isn’t it?’ (Parent, CAMHS, UK) [29]

Theme 3: perspectives of caregiving

Caregivers described their own struggles and costs of caregiving, together with their priorities to alleviate caregiver burden.

Caregivers described balancing multiple roles and responsibilities while supporting young people with mental health conditions, often alongside other family, personal, and professional commitments [17, 19, 22, 23, 25, 27, 30]. They also faced difficulties navigating complex, fragmented, and inefficient services [16–18, 24, 26, 32], uncertainty about their role in care and apprehension about making decisions that could potentially cause harm [22, 25], and balancing their engagement with therapeutic goals, the wishes of service-users [22, 23, 30], and their own competences and capacities [19, 25].

‘All my life…I’ve been the interpreter! It’s bothered me to do the two things at the same time! And when we got here in fact, what helped me it’s that there was someone else who took over that role [of interpreter]. Finally each of us had our own role. Everyone had their own place!’ (Parent, CAMHS, France) [19]

Caregiving was associated with significant emotional, physical, and financial strain [16–18, 25, 26], with some caregivers experiencing overwhelm and physical and mental ill-health [16, 17, 26, 27]. Many reported limited access to tailored support or lack of awareness of available services [16, 26, 27].

‘I couldn’t love him more, he’s just incredible […] he’s also very challenging and tiring, living with a child with attachment difficulties is knackering.’ (Parent, CAMHS, UK) [26]

Caregivers suggested improved communication, defined roles and responsibilities, protection of the rights and needs of young people and caregivers [17, 20, 22, 30], empowerment through practical support and resources [16, 17, 20, 23, 26, 30], assistance navigating care systems, and flexible services [24, 26–29, 32].

‘I’ve got this huge fear of thinking but if you stop seeing us and we need you again, I can’t face, I mean I would do it if I had to do it, but the idea of having to face, having to go back through any of that to try and access it again at a later date.’ (Parent, CAMHS, UK) [26]

They highlighted opportunities for personal growth, reconnections, and increased confidence through caregiving [26, 27].

Discussion

Caregivers often accompany young people along their therapeutic journey, playing an important role in accessing resources and facilitating recovery. This systematic review identified several barriers to high-quality CAMHS, including limited mental health literacy, stigma, fragmented services, poor continuity of care, and inadequate caregiver support. These factors influenced help-seeking, service engagement, and caregivers’ ability to provide care, often resulting in significant financial, emotional, and health burdens.

The findings of this review align with other studies on caregiver experience. Research focusing on caregivers’ role in help-seeking on behalf of young people [5, 6, 9, 33, 34] links inadequate mental health literacy with observed discrepancies between recognition of mental health conditions and perceived need for specialized care. A Dutch cross-sectional study that included parents of 5–6 year-olds reported that while 63.1% of parents recognized emotional or behavioural problems in children, only 22.9% sought care [6]. Stigma also influences caregivers’ perception and response to mental health conditions in their children, while fear of discrimination or social judgement can deter them from seeking timely care [6, 33, 34].

Our findings on caregiver priorities for service provision align with WHO’s Quality of Care Framework, which also reflects the domains of safety, effectiveness, person-centredness, integrated care, equity, timeliness, and efficiency [35]. Research into caregivers’ priorities for outpatient CAMHS in the UK [36, 37] and for inpatient services for physical illnesses [38, 39] identified similar themes concerning caregivers’ priorities for quality services, finding that caregivers prioritize holistic, person-centred services that address young people’s psychological, social, educational, and functional needs [40]. Caregivers also value competent professionals, confidentiality, personalized care, caregiver engagement, and multidisciplinary support [38–41]. Challenges arise when service-users do not meet the service inclusion criteria, leading to frontline staff dismissing caregivers’ concerns and delaying referrals to specialized care [42]. Moreover, conflicting recommendations by different professionals within the multidisciplinary team impact trust in the system [43] and contribute to caregivers feeling overwhelmed by fragmented systems and siloed services that leave them solely responsible for advocating for young peoples’ needs [9, 41].

The outcomes relating to the challenges of caregiving align with existing global evidence on caregiver burden [7, 44, 45]. An Indian cross-sectional study reported a 70% prevalence of subjective stress, 22.5% prevalence of subjective demand burden, and 37.5% prevalence of objective burden among caregivers of patients with depression [45]. Other studies highlighted the disproportionate impact on caregivers’ mental and physical health when services do not reliably address young peoples’ mental and physical health needs [7, 46–48], particularly when the mental health conditions increase the young person’s dependency on their caregivers to function [7, 44, 48]. Caregivers also deal with external factors impacting service efficiency and systems that do not recognize their efforts, leaving them feeling disempowered. Many are inadequately prepared to handle these challenges as few get training, leading to burnout. As caregivers’ lives are negatively impacted, the quality of care they provide is also affected with possible repercussions on those under their care [7, 44].

Evidence from the literature shows that education and training for caregivers and frontline professionals, which include educators, can address mental health literacy and related stigma to improve timely referrals to CAMHS [5, 33, 43, 49]. A Canadian qualitative study exploring the impact of caregivers’ mental health literacy on help-seeking additionally found that caregivers’ lived experience of mental health conditions improved help-seeking [43]. Often, educators are the first to recognize mental health conditions and refer to specialized care, with existing research linking educators’ professional training [5, 6, 33] and years of teaching experience [33] to improved mental health literacy.

Help-seeking is a critical first step for service access; however, structural barriers hinder timely care. Low-severity cases experience delays or rejection as high-severity cases are prioritized [42]. Several countries have implemented community- and school-based services to improve early intervention and support young people who do not meet specialist CAMHS criteria [1, 42, 50]. Examples from Australia and the UK suggest that alternative referral pathways and ‘no wrong door’ models may improve access, reduce pressure on specialist services, and support early intervention [45, 50].

Young people with mental health conditions often present with complex psychological, developmental, biological, and social needs requiring coordinated and personalized care [51]. Emerging causes of mental health issues among youth, such as digital addiction and social withdrawal, pose further challenges to the health workforce who may lack specialized training to address these cases effectively [1]. In addition to this, ethical issues concerning youth autonomy, confidentiality, and evolving capacity to participate in healthcare decision-making create challenges for services seeking to provide person-centred care [52, 53]. The resultant mismatch between service availability and user needs can lead to fragmented and ineffective care [1, 53].

Addressing this requires integrated and personalized services that take into consideration the needs of young persons and their caregivers. Evidence suggests that integrated care pathways, multidisciplinary support, and community-based services may improve service engagement, quality of life, and psychological outcomes among young people, although the paucity of literature in this area limited the generalizability of results [54, 55]. Improved outcomes for youth with mental health conditions were also observed when caregivers were formally integrated into care models; however, evidence highlights the importance of balancing caregiver involvement with respect for young people’s autonomy and rights to participate in decisions about their care [56].

Addressing caregiver burden requires comprehensive, multipronged, and evidence-based interventions. Systematic reviews [7, 48] identified several key strategies to enhance caregiver support. Service functionality and accessibility can be improved through out-of-hours emergency care, telemedicine, and community-based services. Caregiver support initiatives can include respite care services, linking caregivers to peer support groups, addressing caregivers’ physical and mental healthcare needs, and supporting service-users via vocational training and employment support. Financial assistance, such as subsidies, grants, social care programmes, and national health insurance schemes, can alleviate economic strain [7]. Furthermore, digital and innovative solutions can be harnessed to provide cost-effective solutions. Initiatives proposed include discussion forums, educational materials, structured training courses, self-help tools, and internet-based peer support networks for caregivers [48].

The review findings, derived predominantly from caregiver experiences in Western European countries, and particularly from the UK, were broadly consistent with existing evidence from other geographical and healthcare contexts regarding caregivers’ priorities for quality CAMHS, pointing towards common systemic challenges impacting child and youth mental health services. Together, these outcomes indicate that improving CAMHS quality within European public health systems necessitate a multipronged approach that consider not just CAMHS capacity, but also incorporate early identification and referral pathways, workforce competences and mental health literacy, continuity of care, coordinated cross-sectoral services and integration of CAMHS within school and community services, caregiver empowerment and engagement, and caregiver support as integral components of effective youth mental healthcare services. These findings support a public health approach to CAMHS aimed at reducing duplication and fragmentation of care and improve equitable access.

Our findings showed that caregivers consistently highlighted the importance of timely access, clear referral pathways, integrated and person-centred care, caregiver involvement, and supportive relationships with competent and non-judgemental professionals. These outcomes strongly support recommendations for a competent and trained workforce to reduce stigma and provide appropriate care, services with clear remits and robust referral mechanisms, timely access to care, strengthened communication and integration between CAMHS and community services, and education and inclusion of caregivers throughout provision of care.

Caregiver experiences of emotional, financial, and health burdens additionally support integrating caregiver support into CAMHS to strengthen coping strategies and wellbeing. Broader system-level challenges, such as fragmented services and limited evidence on cost-effectiveness and efficiency of different aspects of the care pathway, support prioritizing data collection and research to improve understanding of CAMHS quality and efficiency. Although studies indicated that digital technologies and innovative service models may improve accessibility, efficiency, and coverage, evidence in this area was limited and therefore recommendations regarding digital innovation should be interpreted more cautiously.

The results of this review hold important implications for the development of WHO Quality standards for child and youth mental health services. Specifically, the findings highlight that high-quality care for child and youth mental health services, from the perspectives of caregivers, includes care that considers caregivers’ wellbeing with:

support to strengthen the mental health literacy of caregivers,

support to strengthen the mental health literacy of caregivers,

sufficient resources for a competent and appropriate workforce to reduce stigma and provide age-appropriate care,

sufficient resources for a competent and appropriate workforce to reduce stigma and provide age-appropriate care,

caregiver empowerment and engagement,

caregiver empowerment and engagement,

smooth care transitions with holistic and responsive services,

smooth care transitions with holistic and responsive services,

the active participation of young people and their caregivers in care delivery.

the active participation of young people and their caregivers in care delivery.

Alongside consultations and other scientific reviews [57], these findings were used to support the development of WHO Quality standards for child and youth mental health services. Future research should prioritize qualitative evidence from underrepresented regions to ensure that recommendations for quality standards adequately reflect the diversity of health systems contexts across the WHO European Region.

This systematic review applied a reproducible search strategy from peer-reviewed literature sourced from five databases. Utilization of qualitative studies allowed for capture of a richer perspective of quality care that was not limited to researchers’ priorities but permitted a wider and more flexible exploration of caregivers’ priorities for CAMHS.

Several limitations need to be considered when interpreting the results. The geographical distribution of included studies indicates bias towards higher income countries with more resources for academic research, while the disproportionate number of UK studies likely reflect the inclusion of only English-language publications. Similar literature published before or after the date limits could have been missed, as were studies that did not fit the precise inclusion criteria, did not include the criteria in the title or abstract, or those that were excluded because of a low CASP scoring. Exclusion of unpublished studies and those that were not peer-reviewed, such as grey literature, could cause publication bias, while the subjective nature of the process and the authors’ beliefs and ideas could have contributed to reporting bias [58].

The thematic synthesis represents the perspectives of caregivers who opted to engage in research and who are more likely to be supportive of young people with mental health conditions than non-participants. Bias could result if less supportive caregivers were under-represented. Moreover, few studies included the views of caregivers of younger children, who are likely to have different interactions with services and may be more involved in decision-making than caregivers of adolescents. As caregivers and young people may have different priorities [36, 38], any clinical recommendations should consider the perspectives of all stakeholders, including young people, staff, and policymakers, to ensure buy-in and integration into practice. Additionally, the exclusion of settings other than CAMHS limit interpretation of findings and generalizability to broader, integrated care pathways.

Caregivers play an important role in the care journey of children and adolescents experiencing mental health conditions. Understanding and addressing systemic barriers to quality care and factors that exacerbate caregiver burden within different geographical and social contexts is essential to improve service quality and outcomes for young people. This literature review highlights the need for health services that are evidence-based and responsive to service-users’ needs, including caregivers. It also highlights the need to invest in caregivers by alleviating caregiver burden, supporting their engagement in care, and addressing their physical, psychological, and social care needs to enable them to provide high-quality care for young people.
