Epigenetic modifier m⁶A methylation: insights into the pathogenesis and therapeutic potential of autoimmune diseases
Authors: Xue Lv, Wenxin Zhang, Yuefang Liu, Yuelin Hu, Xin Wang, Hongsong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractN6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methylation is a predominant form of RNA modification in eukaryotic cells. It exerts profound influence over the processing of mRNA, rRNA and tRNA, thereby playing a critical role in various biological processes, including RNA transcription, splicing, degradation, and translation regulation. The m⁶A methylation significantly affects cell fate and signal transduction pathways by regulating gene expression. Notably, aberrant m⁶A modification is strongly associated with the onset and progression of autoimmune diseases. Emerging evidence indicates that these regulatory factors display disease-specific expression patterns and functional roles, influencing immune-cell differentiation and effector functions, inflammatory responses, and tissue injury. This article provides an overview of recent advances in m⁶A biology in autoimmunity and discusses their implications for diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Keywords: M⁶A methylation, Autoimmune diseases, Regulation, Diagnosis, Therapeutic intervention

Introduction

RNA modifications are widespread in both coding RNAs and various non-coding RNAs, where they play a critical role in regulating RNA stability and mRNA translation efficiency [1, 2]. Among these modifications, methylation is the most prominent, accounting for approximately two-thirds of all RNA modifications [1]. N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methylation is the most common form of methylation in eukaryotes. It is a dynamic and reversible RNA modification first identified in the 1970s [3], occurring when a methyl group replaces a hydrogen atom on the sixth nitrogen of adenine in RNA. The modulation of m⁶A involves three main types of enzymes: “Writers” (m⁶A methyltransferases), “Erasers” (m⁶A demethylases), and “Readers” (m⁶A-binding proteins) [4]. m⁶A methylation plays a crucial role in transcriptional control and RNA metabolism, influencing biological processes such as cell differentiation, embryonic development, and stress responses [5]. Aberrant m⁶A modification has been linked to multiple disease states, including autoimmune diseases, cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and urinary tract dysfunctions [6–9]. Although other RNA modifications—such as 5-methylcytosine (m⁵C), N1-methyladenosine (m¹A), and pseudouridine—are fundamental to RNA biology, the dynamic reversibility of m⁶A, together with its broad regulatory effects on RNA metabolism and its critical roles in immune cells, has made it the most intensively studied modification in the contexts of immune regulation and autoimmunity.

Autoimmune diseases occur when the immune system malfunctions, attacking the body’s own tissues [10]. The m⁶A RNA modification is crucial for regulating immune cell functions and maintaining the immune system’s balance and response. For example, Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) plays a significant role in T cell homeostasis and maintaining the suppressive function of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [11]. Han et al. [12] found that the absence of YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1) in classical dendritic cells (cDCs) enhances tumor antigen cross-presentation, inducing cross-reactivity in CD8+ T cells in vivo. Additionally, Zhou et al. [13] demonstrated that the m⁶A RNA demethylase AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) is vital for regulating CD4+ T cell function during experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE). ALKBH5 reduces m⁶A modification levels in C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CXCL2) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) mRNA, thereby increasing their stability and enhancing CD4+ T cell responses. Recently, m⁶A RNA modification has gained significant attention and become a prominent area of research in the life sciences. The mechanisms by which m⁶A modification influences autoimmune diseases are increasingly being understood, highlighting its role in the initiation and progression of these conditions through various pathways. This review focuses on recent advances in m⁶A methylation research related to autoimmune diseases, providing new insights for their diagnosis and treatment.

Dynamic regulation of RNA m⁶A modification

RNA m⁶A methylation is delicately regulated by three main components: methyltransferases, demethylases, and recognition proteins (Table 1; Fig. 1). The methyltransferase complex includes proteins such as METTL3, Methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14), Methyltransferase-like 16 (METTL16), Wilms’ tumor 1-associated protein (WTAP), and RNA-binding motif protein 15 (RBM15), which catalyze the methylation in RNA. Among these, METTL3 serves as the primary catalytic unit, with S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) binding sites and a Asp-Pro-Pro-Trp (DPPW) domain that facilitates the conversion of adenine to m⁶A [14]. METTL14, which also contains SAM binding sites and DPPW domains, forms a heterodimer with METTL3. This partnership likely serves two key roles: preserving the integrity of the complex and improving the binding affinity for RNA substrates [15, 16]. WTAP serves as a regulatory subunit by forming a complex with METTL3 and METTL14 in nuclear speckles, where it regulates the activity of the m⁶A methyltransferase involved in pre-mRNA processing [17].

The m⁶A methylation modification can be either passively or actively removed through mRNA degradation or demethylation mediated by the m⁶A demethylases FTO (fat mass and obesity associated protein) or ALKBH5 [18, 19]. FTO, the first identified m⁶A demethylase, is localized in nuclear speckles and sequentially converts m⁶A to hm⁶A, then to f6A, and finally to adenosine (A) [18]. In 2013, ALKBH5, another m⁶A RNA demethylase, was discovered in mammals. Unlike FTO, ALKBH5 can directly convert m⁶A to A without generating intermediates. Mutations in ALKBH5 are associated with increased m⁶A levels in total mRNA and specifically accumulate m⁶A in certain mRNAs. The demethylation activity of ALKBH5 is crucial for regulating mRNA export [19].

m⁶A-binding proteins are vital for recognizing m⁶A modification on RNA and influencing processes such as mRNA splicing, nuclear translocation, translation, degradation, and cytoplasmic storage. Various m⁶A-binding proteins, such as YTH domain proteins, HNRNP family members, and IGF2BPs, have been identified in mammals [20–22]. Among these, the YTH domain proteins, which include YTH domain-containing protein 1 (YTHDC1), YTH domain-containing protein 2 (YTHDC2), YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1), YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 2 (YTHDF2), and YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 3 (YTHDF3), stand out for their effectiveness in recognizing and binding to m⁶A modification sites [20]. Except for YTHDC1, which is localized in the nucleus, the other four YTH domain proteins are cytoplasmic. YTHDF2 facilitates the degradation of specific mRNAs and enhances the dynamic regulation of gene expression by recruiting the CCR4-NOT complex [20]. YTHDC1, active in the nucleus, regulates gene splicing, while YTHDC2 enhances mRNA translation efficiency by binding to m⁶A sites and plays a significant role in meiosis [20]. The IGF2BP family (insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1/2/3) preferentially binds to m⁶A-modified mRNA, enhancing its stability and translation efficiency, thereby broadly regulating gene expression [21]. Additionally, the members of HNRNP family are also involved in m⁶A recognition. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2/B1 (hnRNPA2B1) is an RNA-binding protein that specifically interacts with m⁶A-modified RNA, playing a significant role in facilitating the processing and maturation of precursor miRNAs, thereby influencing miRNA biogenesis and function [22]. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein C (hnRNPC) and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein G (hnRNPG) could recognize and bind to m⁶A-dependent structural switches, and mediate the splicing of m⁶A-containing transcripts [23, 24].

m⁶A methylation and autoimmune diseases

We searched the PubMed database for studies published through September 2025 to provide a comprehensive overview of m⁶A in autoimmune diseases. The search used combinations of the terms “m⁶A,” “autoimmune diseases,” and the names of specific autoimmune diseases. To ensure methodological rigor, studies were eligible if they addressed at least two of four evidence levels: human (patient), animal model, cellular, and molecular. Studies confined to a single level, as well as database-only analyses without experimental or clinical validation, were excluded. In total, 59 studies met the inclusion criteria: 10 spanned two evidence levels, 32 spanned three, and 17 covered all four. All 59 included molecular data; 53 included cellular data; 29 used animal models; and 43 reported human data. These criteria yielded a comprehensive and reliable evidence base for subsequent in-depth analyses of m⁶A in autoimmune diseases.

m⁶A methylation and rheumatoid arthritis

RA is a highly prevalent autoimmune disorder, characterized by multiple symmetrical joint lesions and typically emerging in early adulthood, with a higher incidence in females [25]. Clinically, RA manifests as synovitis and systemic issues impacting the vascular system, metabolic functions, joints, and skeletal structure [26]. Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of RA by secreting cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin 1 (IL-1), interleukin 6 (IL-6), and interleukin 18 (IL-18), as well as chemokines, such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10). These factors drive joint destruction and cartilage invasion, fueling chronic inflammation [27]. In addition, immune system abnormalities, including the dysregulated activation of lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages, are critical in the pathogenesis of RA [28].

Recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of m⁶A methylation in the onset, progression, treatment, and prognosis of RA [29–31]. Wan et al. identified 120 genes in the synovium of RA patients that displayed alterations in m⁶A modification and mRNA expression using m⁶A sequencing and RNA sequencing [31]. Multiple m6A regulators including the writers METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP; the readers IGF2BP2 and IGF2BP3; and the erasers FTO and ALKBH5 are dysregulated in synovial tissue, in FLSs from human and rat RA models, and in the MH7A cell line. These alterations increase the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, thereby promoting FLS proliferation and invasion (Table 2) [32–42]. FTO also facilitates resistance to apoptosis in FLSs by modulating the m6A status of the lncRNA ENST0000061928 [43]. Notably, reduced YTHDC1 expression in RA FLSs inhibits migration, invasion, and proliferation and induces apoptosis, accompanied by altered levels of PCNA, CDK4, cyclin D1, Bcl-2, Bax, caspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3 [44].

Aberrant m⁶A methylation in autoimmune diseases

Beyond FLSs, m6A regulation is dynamically altered in immune cells from patients with RA. METTL3 expression is increased in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and monocytes from patients with RA, and METTL3 restrains proliferation and inflammatory activation in macrophages under the stimulation of LPS [45]. METTL14 is likewise upregulated in peripheral blood and synovial tissue and exerts pro-inflammatory effects in macrophages [46]. Transcripts encoding YTHDF family proteins are reduced in PBMCs from patients with RA; lower YTHDF levels are associated with increased expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, consistent with an anti-inflammatory role for YTHDF proteins [47]. In contrast, the writer RBM15 is downregulated in synovial biopsies from patients with RA; mechanistically, RBM15 suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages, while knockdown of RBM15 reversed these effects [48]. Therefore, the dysregulation of m6A modification is mainly exhibited as pro-inflammatory effects, while under the influence of exogenous inflammatory stimulation, it can show certain anti-inflammatory responses to counteract excessive inflammatory reactions.

Interplay between non-coding RNA and m⁶A modification in RA

The interaction between non-coding RNA and m⁶A modification is also closely related to the occurrence and progression of RA. Luo et al. found increased m⁶A methylation of hsa_circ_0007259 in RA, alongside reduced levels of hsa_miR-21-5p and elevated STAT3 mRNA expression. This high m⁶A methylation of hsa_circ_0007259 may contribute to the development of RA through the hsa_circ_0007259/hsa_miR-21-5p/STAT3 axis [49]. Another study revealed that circFTO facilitates the formation of the WTAP/METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex, which inhibits the expression of SRY-related high mobility group box 9 (SOX9), affecting cell growth, migration, apoptosis, and metabolic balance. Inhibiting circFTO alleviates RA symptoms [50]. Furthermore, circ_0066715 affects the methylation of WTAP and the expression of ETS1, influencing macrophage polarization in RA. Overexpressing circ_0066715 significantly elevates ETS1 levels in RA FLSs, reduces cytokine secretion from M1 macrophages, increases M2 cytokine production, and inhibits FLSs proliferation [51].

In summary, m⁶A methylation plays a pivotal role in RA pathogenesis by modulating key gene expression and regulatory factors. Various m⁶A-modified regulatory factors, including ALKBH5, METTL3, METTL14, YTHDF2 et al. influence FLS proliferation, migration, cytokine release, and NF-κB pathway activation, contributing to RA progression and joint destruction. Additionally, the interactions between non-coding RNA and m⁶A modification are also implicated in RA pathogenesis. The dysregulation of m⁶A modification regulators and non-coding RNAs in RA mainly affects the functions of FLSs and immune cells, revealing their importance as potential therapeutic targets for restoring immune balance.

m⁶A methylation and systemic lupus erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disease characterized by fever and multi-system involvement, primarily targeting connective tissue. Although the exact cause of SLE remains unclear, it is generally attributed to autoimmune system dysregulation [52]. Key features of SLE include widespread systemic dysfunction, increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and the presence of LE cells in blood or bone marrow. Dysregulated immune activity may promote a variety of clinical symptoms, with lupus nephritis being the most common and severe complication, necessitating personalized treatment to minimize toxicity [53]. Due to the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of SLE, diagnosing, treating, and developing new therapies for this condition remains a significant challenge.

Recent research has unveiled a potential link between m⁶A modification and SLE [54, 55]. Compared to healthy individuals, SLE patients exhibit reducing expression of YTHDF2, METTL14, and ALKBH5 in their PBMCs, with lower YTHDF2 levels associated with higher SLE risk [56]. Further studies indicate that the mRNA levels of METTL3, FTO, WTAP, ALKBH5, and YTHDF2 are significantly decreased in the peripheral blood of SLE patients, and ALKBH5 mRNA levels correlate with clinical features like anti-dsDNA antibodies and symptoms such as rash [57].

The role of immune cell m⁶A methylation in SLE pathogenesis

m⁶A methylation plays a multifaceted role in SLE pathogenesis, affecting various immune cells and regulatory networks. It helps modulate the balance between Th17 cells and Tregs [58]. Recent study indicated that METTL3 deficiency reduced Th1 and Th17 cells in naive T cells while increasing Th2 cells via the IL-7/STAT5/SOCS pathway [59]. Moreover, the specific knockout of METTL3 in CD4+ T cells disrupts Tregs function, impairing immune tolerance through SOCS- IL-2/STAT5 pathway [60]. These findings suggest that m⁶A methylation affects T cell homeostasis by modulating the suppressive function of Tregs. Reduced METTL3 expression in CD4+ T cells also destabilizes Foxp3 mRNA, impairing Tregs differentiation and contributing to SLE [61]. Additionally, recent study has shown significantly reduced ALKBH5 expression in T cells from SLE patients [62].

Beyond T cells, METTL3-mediated m⁶A methylation also promotes dendritic cells (DCs) maturation and activation, by increasing the transcript levels of CD40, CD80, and a Toll-like receptor 4(TLR4) signal adaptor(Tirap) and enhancing TLR4/NF-κB signaling, which further increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines [63]. Abnormal activation of DCs is linked to pathological innate immunity and autoinflammation. This imbalance can disrupt the equilibrium between effector T cells and Tregs, and may also enhance the production of autoantibodies by B cells, thereby exacerbating SLE [64].The loss of B cell tolerance is a critical factor in SLE onset [65]. Interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4), an m⁶A-modified gene, is highly expressed in the kidneys of SLE patients, and is associated with the proportion of peripheral B cells [66]. In addition, knocking out the METTL14 gene in mouse B cells disrupts their normal development, which potentially affecting T cell function in SLE [67, 68]. Furthermore, YTHDF2 expression in neutrophils from SLE patients is decreased and negatively correlates with inflammatory markers such as L%, LMR, NLR, SII and TNF-α in neutrophils [69].

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), a group of immunosuppressive cells composed of different subsets, including M-MDSCs (monocytic MDSCs) and G-MDSCs (granulocytic/polymorphonuclear MDSCs), play an important role in the pathogenesis of SLE [70]. FoxO1 regulates the immunosuppressive activity of M-MDSC by inhibiting Met transcription. ALKBH5 stabilizes FoxO1 mRNA via m⁶A modification, and its low expression exacerbates lupus progression [71]. Therefore, targeting the ALKBH5/FoxO1/Met axis may provide a novel strategy for the treatment of SLE.

Non-coding RNAs and their interplay with m⁶A modification in SLE

Non-coding RNAs and their interaction with m⁶A modification are also crucial in SLE. LncRNAs can regulate inflammation and serve as valuable biomarkers in SLE [72]. LncRNAs regulated-mRNA m⁶A modification, such as Xist, linc02446, and PSMB8-AS1, are involved in SLE pathogenesis [73]. Additionally, circGARS levels are increased in SLE patients, interacting with miR-19a to reduce YTHDF2 expression and promote SLE via the NF-κB pathway [74].

In summary, m⁶A methylation and its regulation of non-coding RNAs are essential in the pathogenesis of SLE, involving various immune cells and regulatory networks. Key m⁶A regulators like METTL3, METTL14, YTHDF2, and ALKBH5 show significant expression changes in SLE patients. Notably, METTL3 deficiency can worsen the immune inflammatory response, disrupt the balance between Th17 cells and Tregs, and exacerbate SLE by affecting the IL-7/STAT5/SOCS pathway and T cell homeostasis. Additionally, aberrant expression of lncRNA and circRNA is also closely linked to SLE clinical features. Thus, the dysregulation of key m⁶A regulators in SLE mainly affects the homeostasis and function of immune cells. Studying m⁶A modifications in SLE not only enhances our understanding of the disease but also aids in identifying novel therapeutic targets. This underscores their potential as promising targets for therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring immune balance and tolerance.

m⁶A methylation and type 1 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is the most common form of diabetes diagnosed in children [75]. It is a chronic autoimmune disorder driven by a complex interaction of genetic and environmental factors, impacting various stages of disease progression. This interaction may promote an aggressive autoimmune attack on pancreatic beta cells, which is associated with insulitis and the gradual loss of beta cells [76]. Despite significant advances in understanding its clinical features, the precise mechanisms linking genetic variants and environmental factors to T1D remain poorly understood. Deeper insights into these mechanisms could help identify novel targets for immunotherapy.

Recent studies have revealed m⁶A regulator variations are linked to T1D risk. Specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the Proline rich coiled-coil 2 A (PRRC2A) and YTHDC2 genes, such as rs2260051 and rs3130623, are significantly associated with positive pancreatic autoantibodies in T1D patients. Notably, the rs2260051 variant is associated with the upregulation of PRRC2A expression, which affects immune homeostasis in T1D through the PI3K/AKT pathway, leading to an imbalance in immune regulation [77]. Fang et al. [78]demonstrated that extracellular vesicles overexpressing hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), can activate autophagy via YTHDF1. This process promotes β-cell survival and reverses hypoxia-induced apoptosis and aging, offering a novel strategy to enhance the success rate of pancreatic islet transplantation.

m⁶A methylation in T1D-associated cognitive dysfunction and podocyte damage

In terms of T1D-associated cognitive dysfunction and podocyte damage, Li et al. [79] observed significant cognitive dysfunction in the hippocampus of diabetic mice, with increased protein levels of YTHDC2 and ALKBH5, and decreased expression of YTHDF1, YTHDF3, and WTAP. Overexpression of YTHDF1 in the hippocampus via AAV-YTHDF1 injection significantly improved cognitive function in STZ-induced diabetes mice. Additionally, Wang et al. [80] found downregulation of METTL3 and IGF2BP2 expression in T1D patients, whereas YTHDC1 and HNRNPA2B1 expression were upregulated. METTL3 may catalyze the m⁶A modification of TIMP2 mRNA through IGF2BP2, thereby regulating the Notch signaling pathway. This regulation can potentially induce pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic effects in podocytes.

Furthermore, De Jesus et al. [81] found that interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interferon-α (IFN-α) significantly increase METTL3 expression in human pancreatic islets and EndoC-β H1 cells. Overexpression of METTL3 in β-cells was shown to delay disease progression in T1D mouse model. Interestingly, METTL3 levels initially rise sharply and then decline rapidly during T1D development. Further research revealed that ROS accumulation impairs METTL3’s response to cytokines, while nitric oxide initially promotes METTL3 expression in human pancreatic islets, underscoring the crucial role of m⁶A methylation in regulating β-cell immune responses during the early stages of T1D.

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a severe T1D complication. Liu et al. [82] found decreased circ-0000953 expression in the kidneys of mice with DN through circular RNA sequencing. METTL3 can regulate the methylation level and expression of circ-0000953 via YTHDF2. Silencing circ-0000953 exacerbates proteinuria and podocyte damage through the miR665-3p-Atg4b pathway, suggesting that circ-0000953 could serve as a potential biomarker for the prevention and treatment of DN.

Overall, m⁶A modification-related genes regulate the insulin and immune-related gene expression by modulating m⁶A methylation levels. These processes affect pancreatic cell function, insulin secretion, and autoimmune responses, all of which are crucial in T1D onset. Moreover, abnormal expression of non-coding RNAs, such as circ-0000953, regulated by m⁶A methylation, contributes to DN development and further influences T1D progression by regulating key signaling pathways.

m⁶A methylation and autoimmune uveitis

Autoimmune uveitis (AU) is a prevalent form of non-infectious uveitis, characterized by the immune system attacking autoantigens in specific ocular tissues [83].The vitreous and peripheral retina are the most commonly affected in AU [84]. Poorly controlled inflammation or prolonged use of topical corticosteroids can lead to ocular complications, ranging from minor and temporary issues to severe and permanent vision loss. Early detection and treatment are essential for preserving vision and preventing complications [85].

Tang et al. [86] found that knocking down FTO increases m⁶A levels of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), decreasing its protein expression, and triggers the overexpression of inflammatory factors, ultimately leading to uveitis. He et al. [87] showed that knockdown FTO significantly increased the m⁶A methylation level of total RNA of glypican-4 (GPC4) and stabilizing GPC4 protein. FTO activates the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway through GPC4, to boost inflammatory reaction, while YTHDF3 inhibits GPC4 expression. Moreover, the FTO inhibitor FB23-2 exacerbates the inflammatory response in experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU), whereas the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 alleviates it. Therefore, restoring or activating FTO function could be a promising therapeutic approach for uveitis. Additionally, METTL3 expression is significantly decreased in the eyes and T cells of EAU mice. Ash1l might be a downstream target of METTL3. Knockout of Ash1l increased IL-17 and IL-23R expression, indicating that Ash1l regulates the inhibition of the pathogenic Th17 response induced by METTL3. Furthermore, METTL3 and YTHDC2 stabilize Ash1l mRNA through m⁶A modification and recognition, promoting Ash1l expression. Higher ASH1L reduces IL-17 and IL-23R levels, curbing Th17 responses and EAU incidence [88]. Notably, in activated DCs, METTL3 boosts Th17-polarizing cytokine production via miR-338-3p- MAPK/P38 signaling pathway and exacerbating EAU development [89]. Thus, METTL3 plays a dual function in EAU by shaping immune responses through different pathways.

In summary, m⁶A methylation is essential for regulating AU. Abnormal m⁶A levels are linked to immune dysfunction and improper activation, which can influence the progression of AU. Furthermore, the interaction between miR-338-3p and m⁶A methylation could be a key target for future treatment strategies. The dysregulation of key m⁶A regulators in AU primarily affects immune responses and inflammatory signaling pathways, underscoring the potential of these factors as therapeutic targets to restore immune balance and reduce inflammation.

m⁶A methylation and primary Sjögren’s syndrome

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is an autoimmune connective tissue disorder that primarily targets the exocrine glands, associated with structural and functional impairments. It can also cause systems symptoms, with sensory polyneuropathy being the most common neurological complication [90]. As our understanding of pSS deepens, the discovery of new biomarkers not only aids in classifying the phenotypes based on clinical manifestations and intrinsic characteristics but also helps in identifying potential mechanisms associated with the risk of lymphoproliferative disorders. This progress paves the way for personalized medicine in pSS [91].

m⁶A methylation plays a crucial role in shaping the immune microenvironment in pSS [92]. Fang et al. [93] analyzed RNA modifications by 23 m⁶A regulators using samples from pSS patients and found these factors are associated with immune cell infiltration. Additionally, binary logistic regression analysis indicated that increased ALKBH5 mRNA levels in PBMCs may increase the risk of pSS, and the m⁶A regulators FTO, YTHDF2-3, YTHDC2 may contribute to pSS pathogenesis by modulating ISG15 and activating type I interferon (IFN) signaling pathway [94]. Another study revealed that lower levels of RBM15B, RBMX, ALKBH5, and YTHDF1 in peripheral blood (PB) samples from pSS patients were detected. Interestingly, the significant down regulation of YTHDC2 in this study contrasts with findings from the GEO dataset and previous reports [94]. These discrepancies may be attributed to clinical differences and detection methods. Larger sample sizes and more standardized testing platforms are needed to further explore the varied expression of m⁶A regulatory genes in pSS patients.

A panel of METTL3, YTHDC2, YTHDF1, and RBMX can effectively differentiate pSS patients from HC [95]. Notably, in pSS patients with dry eye syndrome, both mRNA and protein levels of METTL3 and YTHDF1 are significantly elevated. The upregulation of METTL3 is associated with serum markers and dry eye symptoms [96]. Enhancer of Zeste Homologue 2 (EZH2) plays a crucial role in promoting B cell activation and differentiation. METTL3-mediated upregulation of EZH2 promoting B cell activation and autoantibody production. Additionally, EZH2 inhibits B cell apoptosis and promotes its proliferation by catalyzing the H3K27me3 modification at the CDKN1A locus [97]. In addition, Truffinet et al. [98] found that the expression of METTL3 and METTL14 is elevated in salivary gland epithelial cells (SGECs) from Sjogren’s Syndrome (SjD) patients. Inhibition of METTL3 increases pro-inflammatory gene expression and immune cell attraction, whereas inhibition of FTO produces the opposite effect. METTL3 regulates interferon signaling and inhibits the formation of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), suggesting its important role in the pathogenesis of SjD.

In summary, m⁶A methylation is essential in shaping the immune microenvironment in pSS. By regulating immune cell functions and modulating the expression of inflammation-related genes and autoimmune-related genes, m⁶A methylation contributes to the progression of pSS.

m⁶A methylation and multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease that targets the central nervous system (CNS), marked by demyelination and varying levels of axonal damage. Although the exact cause is not yet clear, MS is believed to be triggered by a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors leading to immune dysregulation. The immune basis of MS involves an immune response against CNS antigens, primarily driven by CD4+ myelin-reactive T cells, along with possible contributions from B cells [99]. MS typically manifests in adults between the ages of 20 and 40 and is two to three times more common in women than in men, with this gender disparity being particularly pronounced in certain regions [100].

Altered expression of m⁶A-related proteins in the nervous system may be associated with the development of MS [101]. Progressive multiple sclerosis (PMS) is a rare and serious MS subtype, associated with early irreversible disability. In MS patients, the expression levels of 13 core m⁶A methylation regulators (KIAA1429, METTL3, METTL14, RBM15, WTAP, ZC3H13, ALKBH5, FTO, HNRNPC, YTHDC1, YTHDC2, YTHDF1, and YTHDF2) are upregulated. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination is crucial for diagnosing MS, as it can confirm PMS by detecting proteins, cells, and antibodies, exclude other conditions, and monitor disease progression. Further analysis revealed that relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) CSF samples exhibited higher m⁶A methylation levels and gene-expression levels than those from patients with PMS, indicating that m⁶A methylation may be a promising biomarker for early diagnosis and for distinguishing MS subtypes [102].

Additionally, a study identified m⁶A-related single nucleotide polymorphisms(m⁶A-SNPs) associated with MS by integrating GWAS and RMVar databases, confirming their relationship with target genes. It also identified 26 genes exhibiting expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) signals and distinct expression patterns in MS. These findings offer new insights into the role of these m⁶A-SNPs in MS pathogenesis [103]. Thus, abnormal m⁶A methylation levels can alter inflammatory factors levels, enhance the immune system’s overreaction, worsen neuroinflammation and damage myelin, while disrupting genes associated with neuroprotection and regeneration, thereby further promoting disease progression.

m⁶A methylation and autoimmune thyroid disease

Autoimmune thyroid diseases (AITDs), including Graves’ disease (GD) and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT), are characterized by the production of thyroid autoantibodies and lymphocytes infiltration. While GD typically presents as hyperthyroidism and HT as hypothyroidism [104], both conditions are influenced by a combination of genetic, epigenetic, non-genetic, and environmental factors [105]. Recent studies have begun to uncover the role of m⁶A methylation in AITDs.

Mo et al. [106] developed a comprehensive single-cell atlas of AITD lesions, providing a valuable foundation for studying the mechanisms of HT and GD. This atlas offers new insights into the cell biology and genetics underlying these conditions. Notably, HNRNPC enhances ATF4 expression via m⁶A modification, triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress signals that lead to the apoptosis and necrosis of thyroid follicular epithelial (ThyFoEp) cells. This process may accelerate the progression of HT. Targeting HNRNPC and ATF4 could significantly reduce ThyFoEp cell death and improve outcomes for HT patients.

In summary, m⁶A methylation enhances the autoimmune response by altering the expression of genes associated with the immune response, promotes thyroid damage and dysfunction, and may exacerbate AITDs progression by influencing thyroid-specific gene expressions.

m⁶A methylation and systemic sclerosis

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by the production of autoantibodies, vascular abnormalities, and fibrosis that affects both the skin and internal organs. Raynaud’s phenomenon, a common symptom, typically represents an early manifestation in SSc patients [107]. The prevalence of SSc is higher in North America, Europe, and South America compared to East Asia, with the highest rates observed among native Canadians. Phenotypic differences are also influenced by age of onset, with more severe visceral organ involvement in elderly patients [108].

Despite progress, our understanding of SSc pathogenesis remains limited. In a mouse model of scleroderma, 843 mRNAs with altered m⁶A methylation suggested that the m⁶A methylation profile play potential role in scleroderma [109]. Yu et al. [110] found that the overexpression of FTO reduced m⁶A modification and expression of Tenascin C (TNC), alleviatiing skin fibrosis in the scleroderma mouse model. These findings suggest that m⁶A methylation modulates the pathological process of SSc by modulating the levels of fibrosis-related genes, promoting fibrosis and collagen production.

Overall, m⁶A methylation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of SSc by modulating gene expression. However, our understanding of the specific roles and regulatory networks of m⁶A methylation in SSc is still limited, highlighting the need for further research.

Common patterns of m⁶A modification in autoimmune diseases

Recent research has significantly improved our understanding of the role of m⁶A methylation in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases (Fig. 2). Essentially, m⁶A modification serves as a crucial epitranscriptomic regulator with significant roles in these disorders (Table 2). While there are disease-specific regulatory patterns, current evidence highlights several recurring themes.

Widespread dysregulation of the m⁶A regulatory machinery

The most consistent observation across studied autoimmune diseases is the widespread dysregulation of m⁶A regulatory enzyme. In RA, METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, ALKBH5, and FTO are frequently upregulated; in SLE, multiple regulators are generally downregulated; and in pSS, several enzymes are increased (Table 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that disrupted m⁶A homeostasis is a common pathological feature. Notably, the expression levels of these regulators often correlate with disease activity indices such as the Disease Activity Scores using 28-joint counts (DAS28) in RA and the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) in SLE. This correlation implies that assessing selected m⁶A regulators in peripheral blood immune cells could serve as a noninvasive, sensitive biomarker for evaluating disease activity, prognostication, and potentially guiding treatment. However, it is important to note that most available data originate from single-center, small-sample exploratory studies, and no meta-analyses have been conducted to provide higher-level evidence.

The imbalance of immune cell homeostasis

m⁶A modification regulates the differentiation, function, and homeostasis of immune cells such as T cells, macrophages, and DCs, thereby affecting the maintenance and disruption of self-tolerance. For T cells, m⁶A modification, especially that mediated by METTL3, regulates the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells and the function of Tregs by targeting the IL-7/STAT5/SOCS signaling pathway, which is crucial for maintaining immune balance. Its dysregulation can lead to T cell homeostasis disorder and promote autoimmunity [59]. Additionally, m⁶A modification is a key switch determining the polarization of macrophages to the pro-inflammatory M1 type or the anti-inflammatory M2 type. For example, METTL3 promotes M1 polarization (pro-inflammatory) by methylating and stabilizing STAT1 mRNA [111]. More recently, m⁶A modification of CD40, CD80, and Tirap (a TLR4 signaling adapter) mRNA mediated by METTL3 enhances the activation and function of DCs, thereby increasing their ability to stimulate T cell responses [63].

Potential shared signaling pathways and secretion of inflammatory factors

Although cross-disease comparative studies remain limited, available evidence suggests that m⁶A converges on several core signaling pathways implicated in multiple autoimmune diseases. The NF-κB pathway is the most clearly supported example: in RA, compelling data indicate that METTL3 promotes inflammation by activating NF-κB signaling and promoting IL-6 secretion [32]. Given NF-κB’s central role in inflammatory responses across autoimmune diseases, including SLE, m⁶A-dependent modulation of this pathway is likely a shared mechanism [74]. Additionally, the Th17/IL-17 axis is a key pathogenic pathway in SLE, and AU, implying that m⁶A may affect disease by regulating Th17-cell differentiation and function [88, 112]. Furthermore, type I interferon signaling is commonly aberrantly activated in connective tissue diseases such as T1D and pSS [81, 94]. In light of the established role of m⁶A in antiviral innate immunity, its cross-talk with the interferon pathway warrants systematic investigation.

The potential of m⁶A RNA modification in the diagnosis and treatment of autoimmune diseases

m⁶A modification as a novel diagnostic biomarker for autoimmune diseases

Aberrant m6A RNA modification is increasingly recognized as a contributor to the pathogenesis of multiple autoimmune diseases, providing a rationale for the development of novel diagnostic biomarkers. Measuring the expression of m6A regulatory enzymes or global m6A levels in peripheral blood or other biofluids may enable early diagnosis, assessment of disease activity, and prediction of prognosis.

m⁶A modification in the diagnosis of SLE

Multiple studies have documented dysregulated expression of m⁶A regulatory enzymes in patients with SLE. For example, ALKBH5 expression is significantly reduced in the peripheral blood of patients with SLE and is negatively correlated with several markers of autoimmunity. In addition, the expression of the methyltransferases METTL3 and METTL14 and the reader protein YTHDF2 is altered in PBMCs from patients with SLE, implicating these molecules in SLE pathogenesis [54, 57]. Although these findings highlight the potential of m⁶A-related enzymes as SLE biomarkers, translation into clinical practice remains limited. Bioinformatic analyses have proposed m⁶A-related diagnostic gene signatures [113, 114]. Nonetheless, no large, prospective studies have validated, in peripheral blood, the diagnostic performance of individual m⁶A enzymes or multi marker panels, and detailed metrics—such as sensitivity, specificity, and ROC–AUC—as well as cohort sizes are rarely reported. Therefore, while the concept is compelling, rigorous prospective validation is needed to establish clinical diagnostic utility.

m⁶A modification in the diagnosis of MS

The initial evidence linking m⁶A modification to MS diagnosis came from a study published in 2021, which first revealed the potential of m⁶A as a biomarker for MS. The study found that the overall level of m⁶A modification in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of progressive MS (PMS) patients was significantly higher than that in RRMS patients and healthy controls [102]. Although research on m⁶A modification as a diagnostic biomarker for MS is still in an early exploratory stage, given that the core pathology of MS involves immune dysregulation and nerve damage, exploring the application of m⁶A regulaters in MS diagnosis has a solid theoretical basis and potential clinical value.

Diagnostic potential of m⁶A modification in RA

In RA patient samples, the mRNA or protein levels of m⁶A regulators such as METTL3, IGF2BP3, and YTHDC2 have been found to be potentially associated with the occurrence of RA [115]. The expression levels of the demethylases FTO and ALKBH5 and the reader protein YTHDF2 are correlated with RA disease activity [116]. Additionally, the total m⁶A RNA levels in peripheral blood cells of RA patients are positively correlated with disease activity, with significantly higher m⁶A levels in the high-activity group than in the low-activity group and healthy controls [46, 117]. These m⁶A regulators show great potential in diagnosing RA, but current research has obvious shortcomings. First, most current m⁶A studies on RA focus on cultured cells (such as FLSs and MH7A) or synovial tissues obtained surgically, while research on minimally invasive and easily accessible body fluid samples such as plasma, serum, or synovial fluid is very limited. Second, although some literature mentions that the expression of enzymes such as FTO is correlated with disease activity score 28 (DAS28), systematic searches have found that no large-scale human cohort studies have clearly reported the statistical significance (p-value) and effect size of these associations. In the future, m⁶A epitranscriptomic data can be integrated with genomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and clinical phenotype data to construct multidimensional diagnostic models, which are expected to significantly improve the accuracy of early diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and treatment response prediction in RA.

Inhibitors of m⁶A-modifying enzymes as emerging therapeutics for autoimmune diseases: Evidence from animal models

Experience from oncology may inform autoimmune applications. In cancer models, combining the METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 with anti–PD-1 enhanced CD8+ T‑cell activity [118, 119]. Similarly, inhibitors of m⁶A machinery have shown therapeutic benefit in multiple autoimmune disease models, including collagen-induced arthritis (CIA), TID, EAU, and SSc (Table 3). Genetic loss‑ and gain‑of‑function studies further support target validity (Table 4).

Summary of m6A-Related mouse models used to investigate therapies for autoimmune diseases

In RA animal models, inhibitors targeting m⁶A-related regulators have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential. Specifically, the methyltransferase METTL3 inhibitors STM2457 [40] and quercetin [120] have been shown to suppress the expression of inflammatory factors, thereby reducing synovial hyperplasia and bone destruction. Furthermore, the METTL14 inhibitor WKYMVM has been found to attenuate Th1 and Th17 differentiation, decrease paw thickness, and reduce the production of inflammatory cytokines [121]. Intra-articular injection of lentivirus-shMETTL3 in a CIA mouse model has yielded favorable outcomes in alleviating the severity of arthritis and inhibiting the aggressive phenotype of synovial fibroblasts (SFs) [122]. Additionally, Li et al. [123] reported that the FTO inhibitor FB23-2 significantly reduced arthritis scores, paw thickness, and histologic markers of synovial inflammation, cartilage damage, and bone destruction, thereby demonstrating its potential therapeutic value in the treatment of RA. Moreover, intra-articular injection of FTO shRNA has been shown to significantly mitigate the severity of arthritis [123]. Genetic ablation of ALKBH5 [36] or treatment with the ALKBH5 inhibitor curcumin [124] increased antioxidant enzyme activity, decreased pro-oxidants, and lowered RA disease activity. Likewise, IGF2BP3 knockout [125] and its inhibitor berberine [126] alleviated RA manifestations. The YTHDC1 inhibitor ebselen also improved RA symptoms [127].

Beyond RA, multiple m⁶A-directed interventions have shown efficacy in SLE. In MRL/lpr lupus mice, quercetin (a METTL3 inhibitor) [128] and curcumin (an ALKBH5 inhibitor) [129] mitigated renal injury and reduced mesenteric lymph node (mLN) swelling. B cell-specific YTHDF1 deficiency ameliorated lupus in a mouse model [130]. In patients with SLE and active cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE), the FTO inhibitor mupirocin decreased skin inflammation [131]. Berberine, an IGF2BP3 inhibitor, also exerted anti-inflammatory effects in monocytes isolated from patients with SLE [132].

Evidence extends to additional autoimmune conditions. Quercetin conferred benefit in T1D [133], pSS [134], and SSc [135]. Curcumin ameliorated disease in T1D [136], AU [137], and pSS [138]. Berberine promoted islet β-cell proliferation in a T1D mouse model [139] and suppressed inflammation in an EAU rat model [140]. Moreover, islet β-cell–specific overexpression of METTL3 partially reversed T1D abnormalities [141].

Collectively, animal models and in vivo inhibitor studies suggest that m⁶A-targeted therapies can ameliorate autoimmune pathology by modulating immune cell differentiation, activation, and effector functions. Given the pleiotropic, context-dependent actions of m⁶A regulators across cell types and disease states, clinical translation will require careful dose optimization and rigorous patient stratification. Systematic studies are needed to define efficacy, pharmacodynamics, and safety across diverse autoimmune indications to enable precision medicine.

Limitations of modulating the m⁶A machinery and rationale for gene-targeted strategies

As a ubiquitous RNA modification, m⁶A is integral to processes such as stem cell differentiation and organ development. Maintaining appropriate m⁶A levels is therefore essential for physiological homeostasis, and indiscriminate modulation can produce unintended, system-wide effects with unpredictable consequences [142]. Tissue, cell types and the progression stages of the diseases also differ in their sensitivity to m⁶A perturbation; for example, METTL14 selectively regulates transcriptional programs in brown and white adipose tissue, shaping metabolic phenotypes and insulin sensitivity [143]. This context dependence underscores the centrality of m⁶A in diverse cell states and complicates efforts to achieve safe, tissue-restricted m⁶A modulation. Moreover, chronic manipulation of m⁶A may elicit compensatory epigenetic responses that attenuate efficacy or introduce new liabilities.

To mitigate these challenges, priority should be given to gene-level targeting. This can include directing interventions toward m⁶A regulators that are aberrantly expressed in a given disease—such as YTHDF2 in pSS [94]—or toward downstream effectors confined to specific cell types. By focusing on disease-relevant nodes, this approach aims to preserve physiological m⁶A functions while increasing therapeutic specificity.

Several implementation routes are available for gene-targeted regulation, including conditional knockout models, tissue-specific promoter-driven expression systems, and precise genome-editing platforms (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9) for locus-directed modification [144]. These strategies enable fine-tuning of the m⁶A network, lowering off-target risk and improving the safety and efficacy profile of prospective therapies.

Summary and outlook

m⁶A modificationis a dynamic, reversible epitranscriptomic mechanism. In multiple autoimmune diseases, the expression of m⁶A writers, erasers, and readers is dysregulated. By altering mRNA stability, translation, splicing, and transcription, m⁶A reshapes the function of key immune cells (for example, T cells, DCs and macrophages), disrupts immune homeostasis, and amplifies inflammatory responses. It can also promote disease by regulating the proliferation and migration of tissue-resident effector cells at disease sites (for example, RA-FLSs). Despite recent advances, important gaps remain: (1) most studies are exploratory and lack high-level evidence from multicenter, large-sample cohorts and meta-analyses; and (2) the precise upstream and downstream nodes through which m⁶A modulates shared signaling pathways, as well as its heterogeneous roles across cell subsets, require deeper investigation. Emerging techniques—such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics—should be leveraged to resolve m⁶A regulatory networks and cellular heterogeneity at single-cell and in situ resolution.

In diagnostics and therapeutics, research on m⁶A in autoimmune disease remains at an early stage. Numerous studies have documented abnormal expression of m⁶A regulators in SLE, MS, RA, and other conditions, and linked these alterations to disease activity or key pathogenic processes, thereby establishing a biological rationale for biomarker development and targeted interventions. The major bottleneck, however, is the lack of rigorous validation in large, multicenter clinical cohorts. Priorities for future studies include: (1) selecting optimal specimen types (for example, whole blood, PBMCs, serum, or disease-relevant body fluids); (2) developing standardized, high-throughput assays; and (3) prospectively evaluating the diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, predictive value) of candidate biomarkers and using ROC analyses to define clinically meaningful thresholds. These steps are prerequisites for translating m⁶A-related biomarkers from bench to bedside.

On the therapeutic front, preclinical models of RA highlight the promise of targeting METTL3, METTL14, and other key enzymes to modulate pathogenic immune cell function and slow disease progression, with downstream mechanisms increasingly delineated. Yet these insights have not advanced to clinical trials. This field faces three principal challenges: (1) Early stage of drug discovery: although several m⁶A enzyme inhibitors have entered preclinical and even early clinical testing in oncology [145], progress in autoimmunity lags behind; discovery and optimization of highly selective, potent, and drug‑like molecules remain priority tasks. (2) Safety: m⁶A is a fundamental, ubiquitous regulatory layer; systemic inhibition risks unintended toxicities. Tissue‑ or cell‑selective delivery and/or targeting of downstream effectors may improve the therapeutic index. (3) Translational gap: robust animal efficacy has not yet translated to complex human disease. Future trials must incorporate rational patient selection, dosing strategies, and validated pharmacodynamic and response biomarkers. Breakthroughs will depend on the development of potent, selective small-molecule inhibitors and the launch of first-in-human trials to assess safety and efficacy in patients with autoimmune disease.

Notwithstanding these advances, the relationship between m⁶A methylation and autoimmune diseases is complex and not yet fully elucidated. Progress is constrained by small sample sizes, heterogeneous experimental designs, and variability among study populations. To address these limitations, future work should emphasize adequately powered, well-controlled clinical studies and the construction of robust, disease-relevant animal models to clarify the mechanistic contributions of m⁶A methylation in various autoimmune diseases. Such efforts could enable personalized therapeutic strategies and reveal new drug targets, ultimately improving outcomes for patients with autoimmune diseases.
