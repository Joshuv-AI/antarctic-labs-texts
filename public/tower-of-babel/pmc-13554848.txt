Cardiac catheterization remains a vital modality for both diagnostic assessment and therapeutic intervention in critically ill infants with congenital heart disease who require venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) support. Cervical cannulation is commonly preferred in neonates and small infants because of the limited caliber of femoral vessels, which often precludes safe or effective access. Given the small femoral vessel sizes, percutaneous arterial intervention options remain limited.1,2 We describe, to our knowledge, the smallest reported infant in the literature to undergo coarctation stenting via the VA-ECMO circuit for vascular access.

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

A 3-week-old premature male infant born at 35 weeks with a birth weight of 1.6 kg was diagnosed with dextro-transposition of the great arteries (d-TGA). He first underwent a balloon atrial septostomy at 2 weeks of age to address a restrictive atrial septum. After discontinuation of prostaglandin E1 (PGE1), a previously obscured coarctation of the aorta (CoA) became evident and showed limited response to PGE1 infusion, with persistent residual CoA. This led to cardiogenic shock and acute kidney injury progressing to anuria. Because of the patient's size and limited continuous renal replacement therapy options, ECMO support was initiated to facilitate hemodialysis, with notable hemodynamically significant CoA remaining.

Investigations and Management

A benchtop model (Figure 1) was used to plan the feasibility of approaching the CoA via the ECMO circuit, proving the 8-F venous cannula to be the superior option for intervention given the patient's underlying anatomy. After sterile preparation of the right internal jugular ECMO tubing, the circuit tubing was punctured with an access needle distal to the orifice of the venous cannula. A 0.035-inch access wire was introduced and used to advance a 7-F sheath into the circuit. Initial angiography via a Glide catheter confirmed aortic coarctation (Figure 2). A coaxial system consisting of a 4-F Glide catheter (Terumo), Progreat microcatheter (Terumo), and 0.014-inch BMW wire (Abbott) was then used to maneuver through the right heart and out the transposed aorta. A 6 × 12 mm Formula stent (Cook Medical) was selected and delivered in the aorta; however it was positioned slightly too distal, which was only recognized after wire removal. Because of this, a second stent was needed and on recrossing the first stent, the device migrated distally. A new 6 × 16 mm Cook Formula stent was subsequently placed with overlap at the juxtaductal aorta and telescoped within the initial stent, resulting in complete resolution of the CoA (Figure 3). Angiography for stent position were performed through the umbilical arterial catheter. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient tolerated the procedure well and eventually decannulated off ECMO with peritoneal dialysis for renal support. Unfortunately, in the setting of progressive complications from his chronic renal failure, including pneumonia and peritonitis, as well as persistent ventilator dependence given severe lung disease, he was not considered a candidate for cardiac surgery or kidney transplantation. After discussion regarding the prognosis and goals of care, the family chose compassionate withdrawal of life-sustaining support.

Discussion

Studies have demonstrated that cardiac catheterization in patients supported by ECMO can be performed safely. This procedure may serve a pivotal role in the timely diagnosis and management of underlying cardiac lesions, thereby informing therapeutic decisions and potentially improving clinical outcomes.3 Additionally, the use of the ECMO circuit to facilitate cardiac catheterization has been described in several case reports and series, further supporting its feasibility in select patient populations.

Endemann et al4 previously described a technique for cardiac catheterization via the arterial cannula of the ECMO circuit in 5 adult patients to access the coronary arteries for angiography.4 Thuys et al5 reported a case involving a 10-year-old patient who experienced cardiac arrest, in which a similar approach using the femoral arterial cannula for angiography was employed.5 Góreczny et al2 documented 2 cases using access through the right internal carotid artery cannula to perform balloon angioplasty and stent implantation in systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunts.2 In a more recent report, Kang et al1 in 2020 described 3 pediatric cases in which this technique was employed for different purposes, including coronary angiography, dilation of a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) stent, and PDA stenting performed via the right common carotid artery.

In our experience, venous cannulation provided adequate access for CoA stenting in the context of d-TGA. The procedure served as a temporary bridge to allow adequate growth before definitive surgery, as the infant weighed less than 2 kg and was not an optimal surgical candidate given ongoing renal issues. The Cook Formula stent was selected because of its capacity for overdilation and intentional fracture, with established use in smaller children.6 An initial episode of stent migration was encountered, however it was effectively addressed without further complication. Procedural efficiency and safety were optimized through effective multidisciplinary communication. Preprocedural planning enabled a streamlined workflow, while intraprocedural collaboration—particularly with the ECMO team during sheath placement and circuit clamping—was critical. Meticulous measures were taken to minimize the risk of air entrainment into the ECMO circuit throughout the procedure.

Conclusions

Using the VA-ECMO circuit as vascular access for hemodynamic assessment and interventional procedures, such as stenting of aortic coarctation, represents a viable alternative route for percutaneous intervention in critically ill neonates. This technique may offer a platform for other transcatheter therapies in similar clinical contexts, with the approach of cannula selection being driven by the patient's underlying anatomy and physiology and the intervention required. Previous reports in the literature describe various techniques for accessing the ECMO circuit as well as possible interventional options to be performed in this manner. To our knowledge, this is the smallest reported patient to undergo CoA stenting via ECMO circuit approach.
