Abstract
Spatially extended Ly α \alpha nebulae, known as Ly α \alpha blobs (LABs), are a rare population at z > 2 z>2 that are thought to trace proto-groups or the progenitors of massive galaxies in the present-day universe. However, their dark matter halo properties (e.g., halo mass) are still uncertain due to their rarity and strong field-to-field variation. The One-hundred-deg 2 DECam Imaging in Narrowbands (ODIN) survey has discovered 103 and 112 LABs in the extended ( ∼ \sim 9 deg 2 ) COSMOS field at z ∼ 2.4 z\sim 2.4 and 3.1, respectively, enabling estimation of their bias and host halo masses through clustering analysis. We measure the angular auto-correlation functions (ACFs) of LABs and derive galaxy bias factors of b b = 4.0 ± 0.8 4.0\pm 0.8 and 3.8 ± 0.7 3.8\pm 0.7 , corresponding to minimum halo masses of 2.8 − 1.8 + 3.0 2.8^{+3.0}_{-1.8} and 0.7 − 0.5 + 0.8 × 10 12 0.7^{+0.8}_{-0.5}\times 10^{12} M ⊙ M_{\odot} and median halo masses of 4.2 − 2.5 + 3.8 4.2^{+3.8}_{-2.5} and 1.1 − 0.7 + 1.1 × 10 12 1.1^{+1.1}_{-0.7}\times 10^{12} M ⊙ M_{\odot} at z ∼ 2.4 z\sim 2.4 and 3.1, respectively. LABs occupy ∼ \sim 11 − 8 + 39 {}^{+39}_{-8} % and ∼ \sim 3 − 2 + 9 {}^{+9}_{-2} % of all dark matter halos above these minimum halo masses. These findings suggest that LABs inhabit massive dark matter halos, likely tracing proto-group environments that evolve into present-day massive halos ( ∼ \sim 10 13 M ⊙ M_{\odot} ), where massive elliptical galaxies or galaxy groups reside, by z = 0 z=0 .

III.1
Angular Auto–Correlation Function of LABs

We calculate the two-point angular correlation function (ACF) of LABs using the
34
estimator:

\omega_{\rm obs}(\theta)=\frac{D\!D(\theta)-2D\!R(\theta)+R\!R(\theta)}{R\!R(\theta)},

(1)

where
D\!D(\theta)
,
D\!R(\theta)
, and
R\!R(\theta)
are the numbers of LAB–LAB pairs, LAB–random pairs, and random–random pairs within the angular annulus
\theta\pm\Delta\theta/2
in logarithmic space.
We set the angular separation bins from
\theta=40
″ to
\theta=3000
″ with a bin size of
\Delta\log{\theta}=0.098
. The first bin starts at 40″ (
\sim
1.2 Mpc at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1), corresponding to the minimum separation between LABs. Each pair count is normalized by the total number of pairs.
We construct a random galaxy catalog containing 100 times as many objects as the observed LABs.
The probability of placing a random galaxy at a given position is weighted by the inverse variance maps of the narrowband image to account for surface brightness fluctuations.
Random sources within the masked regions used for LAB selection—such as those around bright stars and Galactic cirrus—are excluded
(
44
)
.

The uncertainty of the ACF is estimated using the jackknife method. We divide the ODIN survey area into
3\times 3
squares (
1\times 1
deg
2
each covering the maximum angular separation,
\theta=3000
″) and compute the ACFs using eight subregions, leaving out one of the nine subregions in each iteration. We obtain the uncertainty from the covariance matrix from
N=9
jackknife resamplings
(
46
)
.

Because the finite survey area limits the sample size,
our correlation amplitude will be underestimated. To avoid this, we apply an integral constraint
(
58
, IC, )
:

\mathrm{IC}~=~\frac{\sum_{i}{[R\!R(\theta_{i})~\omega(\theta_{i})]}}{\sum_{i}{R\!R(\theta_{i})}}

(2)

where
i
refers
i
-th angular bin.
If the intrinsic ACF,
\omega(\theta)
, can be approximated by a power law index
\beta
:

\displaystyle\omega(\theta)

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle A_{\omega}\theta^{-\beta},

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{obs}}(\theta)

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle\frac{\omega(\theta)-\mathrm{IC}}{1+\mathrm{IC}}

(3)

the observed ACF,
\omega_{\mathrm{obs}}(\theta)
, can be fit with Equation
III.1
, using
A_{\omega}
and
\beta
as free parameters.

Figure
2
shows the observed ACFs of LABs at
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
. The red solid lines indicate the best-fit power-law function with the index

(
50

Because the first bins (
40\arcsec\leq\theta<70\arcsec
, corresponding to 1–2 Mpc at both redshifts) have large uncertainties and their elevated amplitudes suggest that we may be detecting one-halo clustering, we exclude them from the fitting. A more detailed HOD analysis, including the one-halo term, will be explored in future work.
The results are summarized in Table

\displaystyle A_{\omega}

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\gamma}H_{0}r_{0}^{\gamma}}{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}{(dN/dz)^{2}\chi(z)^{1-\gamma}E(z)dz}

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma[(\gamma-1)/2]}{\Gamma(\gamma/2)}

\displaystyle b_{\sigma_{8}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{J_{2}(\gamma)\biggl(\frac{r_{0}}{8~h^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}}\biggr)^{\gamma}},

Halo Occupation Fraction and Median Halo Mass

We examine the fraction of dark mater that host LABs and thereby constrain their rarity within the halo population using the HOD framework.
The framework defines the galaxy bias by the probability that a dark matter halo contain
N
galaxies
(
6
, e.g.,)
.

In the simplest case, dark matter halos above a certain minimum mass can host at most one LAB, with a constant halo occupation fraction (
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
). Under this assumption, the halo occupation fraction and the minimum dark matter halo mass of LABs (
M_{\rm h,min}
) can be inferred using the following equations:

\displaystyle\langle b\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{M_{\mathrm{h,min}}}^{\infty}{b(M)n(M)dM}}{\int_{M_{\mathrm{h,min}}}^{\infty}{n(M)dM}}

(9)

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{LAB}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{LAB}}\int_{M_{\mathrm{h,min}}}^{\infty}{n(M)dM},

(10)

where
n(M)\,dM
denotes the halo mass function
(
61
)
, and
\langle b\rangle
represents the mean bias, which is assumed to be equivalent to the bias factor estimated from Equation
6
.
Here,
b(M)
is the bias factor of dark matter halos as a function of their mass (Equation
7
).
Using this formulation, the minimum halo masses of LABs are estimated to be
M_{\mathrm{h,min}}
=
2.8^{+3.0}_{-1.8}

\times

10^{12}

M_{\odot}
and
7.4^{+8.2}_{-4.6}\times 10^{11}
M_{\odot}
at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1, respectively.
The corresponding halo occupation fractions (
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
) are
0.11^{+0.39}_{-0.08}
and
0.03^{+0.09}_{-0.02}
at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1, respectively.
The uncertainties in
M_{\mathrm{h,min}}
are estimated following the procedure described in Section
III.1.2
, while the uncertainties in
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
are propagated from those of
M_{\rm h,min}
and the observed LAB number densities (
n_{\rm LAB}
).

As a representative halo mass scale for LABs, we adopt the median halo mass (
M_{\rm h,med}
), determined from the halo mass distribution down to the minimum halo mass, since median values are more robust than mean values. We obtain
M_{\rm h,med}
=
4.2^{+3.8}_{-2.5}
and
1.1^{+1.1}_{-0.7}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1, respectively.
The median mass of halos will evolve into halos with a mass of
\sim 10^{13}

M_{\odot}
by
z=0
.

III.2
Cross-correlation Function with LAEs

III.2.1
CCFs of Entire LAB samples

The cross-correlation function (CCF) between different types of galaxies—common (low-mass) galaxies and rare (massive) galaxies—has been used to investigate the clustering properties of rare objects for which the auto-correlation function cannot be reliably measured
(
74
;
23
;
55
, e.g., SMGs, QSOs, proto-clusters;)
.
Although our LAB sample is the largest assembled to date (
\sim
100 objects), the intrinsic rarity of LABs motivates an independent assessment of the clustering measurements. We therefore cross-check our results using the cross-correlation function (CCF) between LABs and the more numerous LAEs.
We use the Landy-Szalay estimator for the CCF,

\omega_{\mathrm{CCF}}(\theta)=\frac{D_{1}\!D_{2}(\theta)-D_{1}\!R_{2}(\theta)-D_{2}\!R_{1}(\theta)+R_{1}\!R_{2}(\theta)}{R_{1}\!R_{2}(\theta)},

(11)

where the subscripts ‘1’ and ‘2’ represent LABs and LAEs, respectively.
D_{1}D_{2}
(
R_{1}R_{2}
) are the observed (random) LAB–LAE pair counts at an angular separation of
\theta\pm\Delta\theta/2
, i.e., the same as the ACF definition (Section
III.1
).
D_{1}R_{2}(\theta)
and
D_{2}R_{1}(\theta)
denote the numbers of observed-random cross pairs for LABs and LAEs, respectively.

We use the ODIN LAE sample from the same field
(
16
)
, and construct the catalog of random LAEs following
55
and
24
. We select 50,000 random sources from the ODIN narrowband photometric catalogs with S/N
>
5 to account for small depth variations that could affect LAE selection.
The random catalog for LABs is the same as that used for the ACF (Section
III.1
).

Figure
4
shows the observed CCF between LABs and LAEs, together with the best-fit models using Equations
III.1
and
6
. We estimate the clustering properties of LABs following the procedure described in Section
III.1
, adopting the LAE clustering measurements from
72
.
The cross-correlation function between the two galaxy populations is the geometric mean of their respective ACFs assuming that both populations share the same power-law slope (
\beta
). Therefore, the correlation length and galaxy bias of the LABs are given by

\displaystyle r_{0,\mathrm{LAB}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r_{0,\mathrm{CCF}}^{2}}{r_{0,\mathrm{LAE}}}

(12)

\displaystyle b_{\mathrm{LAB}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b_{\mathrm{CCF}}^{2}}{b_{\mathrm{LAE}}}.

(13)

where the measured correlation lengths (
r_{0,\mathrm{CCF}}
) are
2.7\pm 0.4
and
3.4\pm 0.2

h^{-1}
Mpc for
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
, respectively.
Thus, the correlation lengths of LABs inferred from the CCF are
2.5\pm 0.8
and
3.9\pm 0.6

h^{-1}
Mpc at
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
, respectively.
The
b_{\mathrm{CCF}}
values at
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
are measured to be
1.8\pm 0.3
and
2.3\pm 0.1
.
These values correspond to to LAB biases of
b_{\rm LAB}
=
1.9\pm 0.6
and
2.6\pm 0.3
, respectively. We note that our results remain consistent when adopting the LAE biases from
24
rather than those from
72
.

Halo masses of LABs and their uncertainties are estimated from
b_{\mathrm{LAB}}
following Section
III.1.2
. The characteristic halo masses derived from the CCF analysis are
1.7^{+5.3}_{-1.6}\times 10^{11}

M_{\odot}
and
2.5^{+1.7}_{-1.1}\times 10^{11}

M_{\odot}
at
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
, respectively.
The results of the CCF analysis are summarized in Table
3
.

In summary, we find that the biases and corresponding halo masses derived from the CCF analysis are systematically smaller than those obtained from the ACF analysis.
For the LABs at
z\sim 3.1

\sim
1.6
\sigma

z\sim 2.4
sample shows a slightly larger discrepancy of
\sim
2.1
\sigma

IV.1

III.2.2
CCFs of Bright/Large LABs

Previous studies of individual bright (
L_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}\sim 10^{44}
erg s
-1
) and large (
\sim
100 arcsec
2
) LABs have reported larger halo masses of
\sim 3.2\times 10^{13}

M_{\odot}
, based on the galaxy–halo mass scaling relation
(
70
;
9
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore,
67
suggested that the stellar mass of LABs correlates with their Ly
\alpha
luminosities and sizes.
To investigate this possible connection between the Ly
\alpha
properties (sizes and luminosities) and halo mass, we measure the CCF between bright/large LABs and LAEs.
We note that an auto-correlation measurement for the bright/large LAB subsample is not feasible due to the relatively small sample size.
In addition, the bright/large LAB sample size at
z\sim 2.4
is also insufficient for a reliable CCF analysis.
Therefore, we restrict the CCF analysis to the bright/large LAB subsample at
z\sim 3.1
.

We define the bright/large LAB subsample as those with isophotal sizes (
A_{\rm iso}
) greater than 30 arcsec
2
. This subsample consists of 42 LABs at
z\sim 3.1
with
L_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}>1.5\times 10^{43}
erg s
-1
.
For the bright/large sample, we measure the correlation length, bias, and halo mass using the same method described in Section
III.2.1
.
Figure
4
(right) shows the best-fit result of the CCF (red line), with a bias of
b_{\mathrm{CCF}}=2.7\pm 0.2
, yielding
b_{\mathrm{LAB}}=3.7\pm 0.6
and an inferred characteristic halo mass of
1.2^{+1.0}_{-0.7}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
.
The CCF correlation length is
r_{0,\mathrm{CCF}}=4.2\pm 0.3

h^{-1}
Mpc, which implies
r_{0,\mathrm{LAB}}=5.9\pm 0.8

h^{-1}
Mpc.
Although our measurements are still limited by small-number statistics, the results suggest that the bright/large LABs reside in halos that are
\sim
5 times more massive (
1.2^{+1.0}_{-0.7}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
) than those of the full sample (
2.5^{+1.7}_{-1.1}\times 10^{11}

M_{\odot}
). These findings are consistent with
82
, who divided their
N=39
sample into a bright/large subsample (
N=22
) and measured the ACFs separately.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Discrepancy Between Bias Estimates from Auto- and Cross-correlation function

In Section
III.2
, the biases of LABs derived from the CCF measurements are lower at both
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1 than those obtained from the ACF measurements, although the significance of this discrepancy is only 2.1
\sigma
and 1.6
\sigma
, respectively. Here, we discuss possible reasons for the difference.

First, the discrepancies could simply be attributed to the small sample sizes and resulting statistical fluctuations.
Figure
2
shows significant scatter in the ACFs at both
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
.
In the case of the CCFs, the
z\sim 3.1
LABs show good agreement between all data points and the model fits. However, the
z\sim 2.4
LABs still exhibit substantial scatter around the models.
Therefore, the
z\sim 3.1
measurements are likely reliable within the 1.6
\sigma
level, whereas the
z\sim 2.4
results require larger samples for confirmation.
We expect that the auto- and cross-correlation functions derived from the full ODIN LAB sample will reduce these scatters and mitigate the current discrepancies.

Second, the construction of the random catalog for LAB could also contribute to the discrepancy. Although we use the inverse variance maps of the narrowband images to account for surface-brightness fluctuations, the actual detection efficiency and number density of LABs may be affected by additional factors, such as the surface-brightness profiles and morphologies of the blobs. With larger ODIN samples in the future, reliable LAB spatial profile will enable more realistic recovery tests on the Ly
\alpha
images. This will allow us to construct a more robust random catalog that accounts for the actual detectability of LABs at a given detection threshold and, in turn, yield more reliable auto-correlation function measurements.

On the other hand, if the discrepancies are intrinsic, this would indicate that LABs cluster differently from the predictions of the simple linear bias model
(
27
;
5
,
\delta_{g}=b\,\delta_{m}
, where
\delta_{g}
and
\delta_{m}
are the galaxy and matter overdensities;)
. Instead, their clustering might be influenced by non-linear (
\delta_{g}=b\,\delta_{m}+\delta_{m}^{2}\,b^{2}/2
) and/or stochastic bias that reflects the influence of small-scale perturbations on the formation of galaxies
(
17
;
11
;
65
;
63
, e.g.,)
.
The non-linear and/or stochastic bias could reduce the cross-correlation bias below the geometric mean between the two ACFs as shown in the previous observation
(
71
;
81
, e.g.,)
.

We speculate that the complex galaxy bias may be linked to the spatial distribution of LABs.

56
found that most LABs (
\sim
70%) are strongly associated with cosmic filaments, typically residing at projected distances within
<
2 Mpc.
They further demonstrated that the apparent association between LABs and protoclusters is a by-product of the converging filamentary structures where protoclusters commonly form.

The robust auto- and cross-correlation analyses using the final ODIN LAB samples (
N

\sim
1,000) will clarify whether the observed discrepancies are genuine and whether non-linear clustering processes must be taken into account. In addition, examining the spatial distributions of LAEs, LABs, proto-clusters, and filaments will yield valuable insights into the nature of large-scale structure and aid in the interpretation of the cross-correlation function.

IV.2
Comparison with Halo Mass Estimates from Previous Studies

We compare the dark matter halo properties of LABs with estimates from previous studies.
Due to their rarity, prior to the clustering analyses (this work and
82
), the halo masses of LABs had been inferred either from simple clustering analyses based on field-to-field variations
(
77
)
or from the stellar-to-halo mass relation (SHMR)
(
70
)
.
Here, we focus on two well-studied LAB samples, each employing one of these methods:
the LABs discovered in the Chandra Deep Field South
(
77
, CDFS LABs;)
at
z\sim 2.3
and
the SSA22 LABs
(
40
)
at
z\sim 3.1
, both of which span a wide range of Ly
\alpha
luminosities and sizes.

First, we compare our results with the minimum halo mass and halo occupation fraction of LABs derived by
77
. Their estimation method is essentially the same as our HOD framework in that it compares the observed LAB number density and field-to-field variance with predictions from an
N
-body simulation
(
41
, ABACUS;)
. Note that their ‘detectability fraction’ corresponds to the halo occupation fraction (
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
) in our framework.
They counted halos within small cells in the simulation box (the counts-in-cells, or C-in-C method) and determined the minimum halo mass required to reproduce the observed LAB number density for a given
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
. The minimum halo mass and
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
were constrained by finding the values that best reproduced the observed field-to-field variance (
\sigma_{v}=1.5
), based on the observed distribution of
N_{\rm LAB}=(6,0,0,0)
across four survey fields. They concluded that
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}

>0.5
and
M_{\rm min}

>

1.2\times 10^{13}

M_{\odot}
are required to achieve a model variance of
\sigma_{v}(\mathrm{fit})=0.93
, comparable to the observed value. These estimates are somewhat larger than our inferred halo masses and
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
.

Thanks to the greatly improved survey statistics of ODIN, we are now able to revisit the counts-in-cells analysis. To enable a direct comparison with their estimates, we measure the field-to-field variance of LABs at
z\sim 2.4
, which closely matches the redshift of the
77
sample.
First, we conduct a recovery test following
77
and
44
to assess how many CDFS LABs would be recovered at the ODIN survey depth. We confirm that the ODIN survey depth at
z
= 2.4 is comparable to that of the six bright CDFS LABs.
Next, we divide the 9 deg
2
COSMOS field into 72 subregions, each having the same survey volume (
1.2\times 10^{5}
Mpc
3
) as that of
77
, corresponding to a
22.1\times 22.1
arcmin
2
region.
We compute the surface-density variation of LABs following
48
and
77
:

\sigma^{2}_{v}=\frac{\langle n^{2}\rangle-\langle n\rangle^{2}}{\langle n\rangle^{2}}-\frac{1}{\langle n\rangle},

(14)

where
n
is the effective surface number density within each subregion.
The measured field-to-field variation is
\sigma_{v,\mathrm{ODIN}}=0.68\pm 0.12
.
The uncertainty is estimated using jackknife resampling, as described in Section
III.1
.

The new field-to-field variance measurement corresponds well to the lower end of the estimates in
77
, where
\sigma_{v}
,
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
, and
M_{\rm min}
are 0.76, 0.125, and
5.3\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
, respectively.
These quantities are consistent with our results within the
1\sigma
range:
(
\sigma_{v,\mathrm{ODIN}}
,
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
,
M_{\rm h,min}
) =
(
0.68\pm 0.12
,
0.11^{+0.39}_{-0.08}
,
2.8^{+3.0}_{-1.8}\times 10^{12}\,M_{\odot}
).
Therefore, we conclude that with the improved LAB statistics, the original
77
estimates can be revised slightly downward, bringing them into better agreement with our clustering analysis.

Second, we compare our results with halo masses inferred from the stellar masses derived for the SSA22 LAB sample at
z\sim 3.1

(
40
)
. The SSA22 LAB sample serves as an ideal comparison dataset because the properties of its members have been extensively characterized
(
67
;
32
;
28
, e.g.,)
.
For instance,
70
estimated that the brightest source, SSA22–LAB1, resides within a dark matter halo of
M_{\mathrm{h}}

\sim

10^{13}

M_{\odot}
using SHMR.

For a statistical comparison, we estimate the halo masses of the SSA22 LABs following the same methodology as in
70
and references therein.

67
conducted a deep near-infrared (NIR) survey and confirmed that 15 out of 20 LABs host one or more
K
-band–selected galaxies using photometric and spectroscopic redshifts. The stellar masses for these galaxies were derived through SED fitting using multi-band photometry (
u^{*}\,B\,V\,R\,i^{\prime}\,z^{\prime}\,J\,H\,K
). These 15 LABs provide a dataset for the comparison, spanning a wide range in Ly
\alpha
luminosities (
L_{{\rm Ly}\alpha}>0.7\times 10^{43}
erg s
-1
) and spatial extent (
>17
arcsec
2
).
We estimate the halo masses of the 15 SSA22 LABs by adopting a SHMR of
1.32^{+0.98}_{-0.57}\times 10^{-2}
from
13
. The uncertainties in the SHMR and stellar mass estimates are propagated into the resulting halo mass estimates. The derived physical properties of the SSA22 LABs are summarized in Table
4
.

We find that the representative halo masses estimated from stellar mass measurements of the SSA22 LABs are comparable to those derived from our ACF analysis.
The median halo mass of SSA22 LABs,
M_{\rm h,med}
=
3.9^{+2.8}_{-1.7}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
, is in a good agreement with the ODIN LABs at
z\sim 3.1
(
2.1^{+2.0}_{-1.2}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
).
Except for SSA22-LAB8 (
M_{h}=3.2\times 10^{11}

M_{\odot}
), which may be part of SSA22-LAB1, the individual halo mass estimates are larger than the minimum halo mass at
z\sim 3.1
derived in this work (
M_{\mathrm{h,min}}=9.4\times 10^{11}

M_{\odot}
).
If we restrict the SSA22 sample to bright/large LABs detectable at the ODIN survey depth, five out of the 15
K
-band–detected LABs are recovered, yielding a higher median halo mass of
7.0^{+7.6}_{-4.4}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
, which is still consistent with our ACF measurements within 1.3
\sigma
.

IV.3
Evolution of halo occupation fraction

The results of HOD framework (Table
2
) suggest an evolution of halo mass and halo occupation fration (
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
) over cosmic time.
LABs at
z\sim 2.4
reside in halos that are approximately four times more massive and exhibit
\sim
4 times higher
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
than those at
z\sim 3.1
.
The higher
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
at
z\sim 2.4
arises from the similar LAB number density but a smaller halo number density (
n_{\mathrm{halo}}=\int n(M)dM
) compared to
z\sim 3.1
.
This implies that LABs are more frequently observable in massive halos at
z\sim 2.4
, despite the smaller halo number density.

We speculate that the evolution of halo mass and halo occupation fraction may be driven by changes in the dominant power sources of LABs and may reflect the cosmic star formation history.
For example, if the major energy sources proposed for LABs, such as AGNs and SMGs in massive halos, are more prevalent at
z\sim 2
, the observed increase in halo mass and
f_{\mathrm{LAB}}
at this redshift can be naturally explained.
However, drawing firm conclusions about the evolution of LAB powering sources remains challenging without comprehensive multi-wavelength data analysis.

V
Summary

Using the largest Ly
\alpha
blob sample to date, we investigate the clustering and dark matter halo properties of LABs through measurements of the angular two-point auto correlation function for 103 LABs at
z\sim 2.4
and 112 LABs at
z\sim 3.1
discovered in the extended COSMOS field.

•

We obtain bias factors of the LABs:
b=4.0\pm 0.8
and
3.8\pm 0.7
at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1, respectively. Assuming a power-law angular correlation function with a fixed slope of
\beta=0.8
, we derive correlation lengths of
r_{0}
=
6.5\pm 1.0
and
5.2\pm 1.4

h^{-1}
Mpc for LABs at
z\sim 2.4
and
z\sim 3.1
, respectively.

•

The inferred median halo masses of LABs are
5.1^{+4.6}_{-3.0}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
and
1.4^{+1.3}_{-0.8}\times 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1, respectively. If we adopt a mean halo mass growth rate from simulations, the halos hosting LABs are likely to evolve into massive halos by

M_{\odot}

\sim

•

We compare the inferred halo properties with those in the literature, finding good overall agreement.
In particular, LABs at
z\sim 2.4
exhibit field-to-field variations, halo occupation fractions, and minimum halo masses comparable to those reported by
77
based on the counts-in-cells method. At
z\sim 3.1
, the inferred dark matter halo masses of LABs are consistent with those of the SSA22 LABs, whose halo masses were derived using the stellar-to-halo mass relation. These results support that LABs reside in more massive halos (
M_{\rm h}\gtrsim 10^{12}

M_{\odot}
) than typical LAEs and LBGs.

•

Measurements from the cross-correlation function between LABs and LAEs yield smaller bias factors than those derived from the auto-correlation function, with
b=1.9\pm 0.6
and
2.6\pm 0.3
at
z\sim 2.4
and 3.1, respectively. The observed differences can be attributed to limited sample sizes, random catalogs, or the presence of non-linear and stochastic bias effects, possibly related to the tendency of LABs to reside preferentially near filaments of the large-scale structure
(
56
)
. A robust joint analysis of auto- and cross-correlation functions using the full ODIN samples will be essential to draw firm conclusions.
