Effects of Acute Low‐ and Moderate‐Dose Alcohol on Chronic Disease‐Related Biomarkers in Healthy Light and Heavy Drinkers
Authors: Mollie A Monnig, Samantha E Clark, Peter M Monti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlcohol consumption is a major contributor to global chronic disease, with growing evidence indicating health risks even at low levels of intake. However, mechanistic understanding of these risks relies heavily on preclinical models and observational data, leaving a critical gap in controlled experimental evidence regarding how alcohol perturbs human biological systems in vivo.MethodsThe present study utilized plasma samples from a randomized, placebo‐controlled trial to evaluate the effects of low‐dose (0.35 g/kg) and moderate‐dose (0.60 g/kg) alcohol on disease‐relevant biomarkers in 32 healthy adults (mean age = 25.0 ± 3.8 years; 21 female/11 male), characterized by light (n = 15) or heavy (n = 17) drinking. This design enabled evaluation of effects across dose, timescale, and drinking history, as well as assessment of their interactions. Plasma was collected at prebeverage baseline and hourly for 4 h afterward. Immunoassays quantified 10 disease‐related biomarkers: adiponectin, angiogenin, D‐dimer, high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hsCRP), Intercellular Adhesion Molecule‐1 (ICAM‐1), Lipocalin‐2 (LCN2), Matrix Metalloproteinase‐7 (MMP‐7), Matrix Metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9), soluble Receptor for Advanced Glycation End‐products (sRAGE), and Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells 2 (TREM2).ResultsMain effects of group indicated that even in this young healthy sample, heavy drinking status was associated with higher levels of adiponectin, angiogenin, ICAM‐1, LCN2, and sRAGE, a profile suggesting altered vascular and metabolic activity. Acute alcohol administration induced changes in sRAGE and hsCRP. Specifically, moderate‐dose alcohol triggered an increase in the immunoglobulin sRAGE, which may reflect an acute compensatory response to inflammation and/or oxidative stress. Compared to placebo, hsCRP was lower in the low‐dose alcohol condition; however, this finding should be interpreted in light of CRP biology. MMP‐7, MMP‐9, and LCN2 showed time‐dependent fluctuations that were independent of experimental condition, highlighting the critical importance of placebo‐controlled designs to account for diurnal/postprandial variation in immune biomarkers.ConclusionFindings provide translational evidence that alcohol is associated with multisystem biomarker changes relevant to chronic disease and that alcohol‐related biomarker perturbations vary by dose and chronicity.Keywords: alcohol administration, biomarkers, chronic disease, heavy drinking, inflammation

Introduction

The role of alcohol use in the development of chronic disease is a rapidly growing area of research. For several decades, observational studies fostered the view that “moderate” alcohol use might be health protective, as reflected in the widely cited J‐shaped curve (Di Castelnuovo et al. 2006; Ronksley et al. 2011). However, recent rigorous evidence has challenged this interpretation, with large‐scale longitudinal cohorts and Mendelian randomization studies demonstrating increased risk for several chronic conditions even at low levels of alcohol use (Bagnardi et al. 2013; Biddinger et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Topiwala et al. 2025). Consequently, the central challenge in the field has shifted from reconciling observational associations to delineating alcohol's pathogenic effects across doses, time scales, and drinking histories.

A growing body of evidence indicates that alcohol's long‐term health effects arise not from isolated pathway perturbations, but from coordinated disruptions across interdependent biological systems. Chronic low‐grade inflammation represents one central pathway linking alcohol use to chronic disease (Barr et al. 2016; Crotty et al. 2023; Furman et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2010); however, peripheral immune signaling does not operate in isolation. Alcohol also perturbs vascular, neuroimmune, metabolic, and tissue repair/remodeling pathways that intersect with immune activation to shape downstream disease risk (Lee et al. 2025). In the vasculature, alcohol promotes endothelial dysfunction, oxidative injury, and maladaptive remodeling, accelerating atherogenesis and long‐term cardiovascular risk (Gusti et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025). In the central nervous system, alcohol‐related immune activation and oxidative stress are associated with microglial activation and neurotoxic signaling that contribute to structural degradation and accelerated cognitive decline (Crews et al. 2015). Metabolically, alcohol disrupts hepatic lipid processing and insulin regulation, promoting steatosis and fibrosis and creating a metabolic environment that favors carcinogenesis (Osna et al. 2017; Singh et al. 2019). Finally, alcohol disrupts extracellular matrix remodeling and regenerative processes in tissue, promoting maladaptive repair, fibrosis, and impaired recovery following injury (Simon et al. 2022). Taken together, these interrelated pathways provide a mechanistic framework for alcohol's broad disease burden and motivate studies designed to capture coordinated changes across systems.

Despite conceptual clarity around alcohol's deleterious health effects, prior research investigating a systems‐level model under conditions that support causal inference in humans remains limited. Much of the existing literature utilizes preclinical models or observational approaches, reflecting the tradeoff between mechanistic precision and clinical application (Boyko 2013; Pistollato et al. 2026). Human alcohol administration studies are uniquely positioned to narrow this gap via standardized dosing and other experimental controls, yet biomarker‐focused studies remain relatively uncommon, particularly those incorporating repeated sampling in vivo alongside a credible placebo control. Lack of placebo control is consequential because many circulating biomarkers vary over short time scales due to diurnal rhythms, postprandial physiology, and acute stress or procedural effects (Altara et al. 2015; Zhou et al. 2010), creating substantial risk of misattribution. Further gaps constrain interpretation as well. Prior in vivo human studies have not compared multiple alcohol doses within a single experimental framework, limiting dose–response inferences. This gap is important because dose is likely to shape both the magnitude and temporal dynamics of biological effects in vivo. In addition, prior studies have not compared individuals who differ on drinking history, leaving unclear whether chronic exposure affects acute biomarker responses to alcohol. Finally, studies conducted in older or clinical populations risk confounding alcohol‐related biological effects with processes already underway due to disease progression, that is, reverse causation (Selbæk 2021).

The present study addressed these gaps in a randomized, multidose, placebo‐controlled, within‐subject alcohol administration trial in healthy adults with well‐characterized light or heavy drinking status. Linear mixed effect models were used to test main and interactive effects of dose, time, and drinker group across 10 biomarkers. Biomarkers were selected a priori to represent five complementary biological domains implicated in chronic disease pathways: metabolic regulation and strain (adiponectin; lipocalin‐2 [LCN2], also known as neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin [NGAL]), endothelial activation and vascular signaling (angiogenin; D‐dimer; intercellular adhesion molecule 1 [ICAM‐1]; soluble receptor for advanced glycation endproducts [sRAGE]), tissue remodeling (matrix metalloproteinase‐7 [MMP‐7]; matrix metalloproteinase‐9 [MMP‐9]), systemic inflammation (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein [hsCRP]), and neuroimmune signaling (triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 [TREM2]). See File S1 for expanded biomarker rationale. This approach enabled us to characterize acute biomarker dynamics, distinguish alcohol‐specific responses from diurnal variation observed under placebo, and evaluate whether results differ by drinking history in a sample of healthy young adults. This work extends a companion report from the same sample and experimental protocol (Monnig et al. 2025) that examined biomarkers of microbial translocation (LPS), acute phase proteins (LBP, sCD14, sCD163), and cytokines (IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, MCP‐1, TNF‐α).

Method

Overview

This study utilized plasma samples from the previously published companion randomized clinical trial (Monnig et al. 2025). Briefly, healthy participants who met all eligibility criteria for a laboratory alcohol administration study attended three laboratory sessions in which they received placebo, low‐dose alcohol (0.35 g/kg), and moderate‐dose alcohol (0.60 g/kg) in randomized order in a within‐subjects design. Participants were categorized as light drinkers or heavy drinkers based on lifetime drinking history and past 90‐day consumption. Participants were ages 21–55; were nontreatment‐seeking for alcohol/drug use; screened negative for drug use disorder as well as urine drug metabolites; were free of chronic disease requiring use of medication; screened negative for alcohol withdrawal, major depression, psychosis, and bipolar disorder; tested negative for pregnancy; and were regular alcohol consumers. Heavy drinkers met NIAAA criteria for current excessive alcohol use (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism. 2021). See File S2 for full inclusion/exclusion criteria and group‐specific criteria. Family history of AUD in biological parents and grandparents was assessed using a standardized informant method adapted from the “family history method using diagnostic criteria” (Andreasen et al. 1977). The trial was approved by the Brown University Institutional Review Board and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03483389; date of registration: 3/30/2018).

Beverage Administration Procedure

On experimental days, participants received each beverage condition on separate days in randomized order, with at least 3 days between visits. Participants were rescheduled if they showed or reported any signs of illness or infection. Participants were instructed not to consume alcohol or cannabis for 48 h and to abstain from food or drink except water for 3 h before visits. All sessions started between 9 and 11 am and concluded between 2 and 4 pm. A standardized meal bar was given 30 min before beverage administration. See File S2 for full details of beverage administration. Breath alcohol peaked 30 min after alcohol consumption at 0.034% ± 0.010 (mean ± SD) in the low‐dose condition and 60 min after consumption at 0.063% ± 0.010 in the moderate‐dose condition. Plasma samples were collected prior to beverage administration (baseline/Hour 0) and at hourly intervals thereafter for 4 h.

Biomarker Assays

Blood samples were collected into EDTA tubes and were processed to plasma and stored at −80°C until assayed. Plasma concentrations of all biomarkers were quantified in duplicate or triplicate using the Biotechne (Minneapolis, MN, USA) Ella automated immunoassay system according to manufacturer instructions. The exact number of samples available differed for each Ella cartridge due to samples being expended in the parent study. Biomarkers were grouped according to the cartridges developed for the analysis, and thus the sample numbers were as follows: 448 for sRAGE; 440 for D‐dimer, MMP‐7, and TREM2; 416 for ICAM‐1, LCN2, and MMP‐9; and 388 for adiponectin, angiogenin, and hsCRP. There was no specific selection process to determine the number of samples allocated to each cartridge type.

Statistical Analysis

Individual biomarker datapoints were coded as missing if the coefficient of variation (CV) exceeded 20% or if values were outside the assay's range of detection. In addition, one sample containing adiponectin, angiogenin, and hsCRP for one participant appeared to have been overdiluted during assay processing, as reflected in values two magnitudes of order lower than the participant's other values across time points; these data points were excluded as apparent lab error. Distributions were checked for skewness and kurtosis. Nonnormal distributions were addressed either by excluding outliers > 3 SDs from the mean (preferred to preserve native units) or, if excluding outliers was ineffective, by applying logarithmic transformation. The following biomarkers were log‐transformed: D‐dimer, hsCRP, MMP‐7, and TREM2. Descriptive statistics were tabulated and reported for all biomarkers in native units. Missing data percentages after exclusions for outliers, values out of range, lab error, or CV > 20% were as follows: adiponectin, 3/388 (0.008%); angiogenin, 6/388 (0.015%); hsCRP, 2/388 (0.005%); D‐dimer, 2/440 (0.005%); ICAM‐1, 0/416 (0%); LCN2, 2/416 (0.005%); MMP‐7, 2/440 (0.005%); MMP‐9, 13/416 (0.03%); sRAGE, 3/448 (0.007%); and TREM2, 2/440 (0.005%).

Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (New York, NY, USA). For all statistical tests, the significance level (alpha value) was set at 0.05. Family history of AUD was compared between groups using the Pearson chi‐squared test. For each individual biomarker, linear mixed models tested main effects of drinker group (heavy/light), dose condition (placebo, low dose, moderate dose), and hour [baseline (0), 1, 2, 3, 4], as well as their three‐way interaction (group × dose × hour). Additionally, two‐way interactions were tested where indicated by examination of data plots. Significant main effects and interactions were followed up by pairwise tests on marginal means. For pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means within individual models, the Sidak correction was applied to adjust the familywise error rate, thereby controlling for multiple comparisons. No additional correction was applied across the entire set of models. In addition, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for baseline (Hour 0) values across the three conditions to give an estimate of the stability of values within individuals over time. The ICC was calculated using the standard formula of the ratio of variance between clustered data (here, biomarker values within participants) to total variance.

Sensitivity Analyses: Because drinker groups differed qualitatively (i.e., without reaching statistical significance) on smoking status and race, we performed an additional set of analyses to test for differences according to these characteristics at baseline. Specifically, we compared baseline levels of analytes across all sessions (placebo, low dose, and moderate dose) in nonsmokers versus smokers; and in race groups as defined by the Census Bureau, specifically, Asian (n = 3), Black/African American (n = 6), White (n = 17), Multiracial (n = 5), and Unknown/Not Reported (n = 1). The category of unknown/not reported was combined with multiracial to avoid a cell of n = 1. Where differences were found at p < 0.10 for either smoking or race, the relevant factor was added to the original models in sensitivity analyses to examine whether smoking or race affected model results.

Results

Sample Characteristics

Thirty‐two participants completed at least one lab session and were included in analyses. The rate of completion for all three lab studies was 87.5%. See Table 1 for participant characteristics. No demographic characteristics differed significantly between the nontreatment‐seeking heavy drinker group and light drinker group. Light drinkers had no history of regular heavy drinking (defined as weekly or more frequent) of duration > 6 months, as required by eligibility criteria (see File S2). Heavy drinkers' histories were as follows: heavy drinking onset at age 20.2 ± 2.3 (M ± 1SD); duration of 1.7 ± 1.7 years; 5.8 ± 3.0 drinks per occasion; and binge drinking (5+/4+drinks in a day for men/women) at least once a week for 13/17 individuals, 2–3 times per month for 3/17 individuals, and 7–11 times per year for 1/17 individuals. Four of 17 participants in the heavy drinking group and no participants in the light drinking group met DSM‐5 criteria for mild AUD. Participants did not differ in probable family history of AUD, although heavy drinkers had a qualitatively but nonsignificantly higher count: 8/17 participants in the heavy drinking group reported positive family history, and 3/15 participants in the light drinking group reported positive family history, X2(1) = 2.586, p = 0.108. These data are presented for context and were not included in analyses or interpretation.

Angiogenin

A main effect of group was observed. Heavy drinkers had higher levels of angiogenin than light drinkers, F(1,53.100) = 15.717, p < 0.001 (Figure 1B).

hsCRP

There was a main effect of dose, with lower hsCRP in the low‐dose condition compared to the placebo condition, F(2,355.090) = 9.388, p < 0.001 (Figure 1C).

D‐dimer

D‐dimer exhibited no significant effects.

ICAM‐1

A main effect of group was observed. Heavy drinkers had higher levels of ICAM‐1 than light drinkers, F(1,63.638) = 9.565, p = 0.003 (Figure 1D).

LCN2

LCN2 showed main effects of group and time. Heavy drinkers had higher levels of LCN2 than light drinkers, F(1,50.463) = 5.190, p = 0.027 (Figure 1E). There was a main effect of time, F(4,338.234) = 3.073, p = 0.017. LCN2 levels were higher at baseline relative to Hour 1 (p = 0.028) and Hour 2 (p = 0.014) (Figure 1F).

MMP‐7

A main effect of time was observed, F(4,322.522) = 3.319, p = 0.011. MMP‐7 decreased from baseline to Hour 1 (p = 0.027; Figure 1G).

MMP‐9

A main effect of time was observed, F(4, 318.269) = 5.905, p < 0.001. Relative to baseline, MMP‐9 increased at Hour 1 (p = 0.002), Hour 3 (p = 0.025), and Hour 4 (p < 0.001; Figure 1H).

sRAGE

sRAGE showed main effects of group, F(1,25.111) = 4.358, p = 0.047 (Figure 1I), and time, F(4,28.854) = 7.375, p < 0.001, as well as a dose by hour interaction, F(8,27.863) = 2.689, p = 0.025 (Figure 1J). sRAGE levels were higher overall in heavy drinkers compared to light drinkers. The main effect of time is interpreted in the context of the dose by hour interaction. In the placebo condition, sRAGE decreased from baseline to Hour 1 (p = 0.009). In the low‐dose condition, relative to baseline, sRAGE decreased at Hour 1 (p = 0.021), Hour 2 (p = 0.019), Hour 3 (p = 0.022), and Hour 4 (p = 0.015). In the moderate‐dose condition, sRAGE decreased from baseline to Hour 1 (p = 0.021) but increased from Hour 1 to Hour 2 (p = 0.002) and Hour 3 (p = 0.014).

TREM2

TREM2 exhibited no significant effects.

Biomarker ICCs

ICCs were calculated at the baseline time point for each condition to give an estimate of the stability of biomarker values within individuals over time. ICCs are interpreted according to published guidelines as follows: < 0.50 = low, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, and > 0.90 = excellent (Koo and Li 2016). ICCs were low for angiogenin (ICC = 0.481), ICAM‐1 (ICC = 0.307), MMP‐7 (ICC = 0.417), MMP‐9 (ICC = 0.326), and TREM2 (ICC = 0.492); moderate for adiponectin (ICC = 0.605), D‐dimer (ICC = 0.656), and LCN2 (ICC = 0.507); good for hsCRP (ICC = 0.770); and excellent for sRAGE (ICC = 0.938). These values may inform future research studies as to the stability of these biomarkers over time in similar populations.

Sensitivity Analyses

These analyses first tested group differences in each biomarker at baseline according to race and smoking status, then added the factor to the main model if p < 0.10 for that factor. As shown in Table 3, there were no significant baseline differences according to race or smoking status. For sensitivity analyses where race or smoking status was added to the main model, no changes were observed regarding the results of the models reported above.

Discussion

This study utilized samples from a randomized controlled alcohol administration trial to analyze biomarkers related to development of chronic disease in light and heavy drinkers. Participants were young healthy individuals reporting no known chronic disease, reducing methodological risk of reverse causality. Several key findings emerged related both to drinking status and acute alcohol effects. First, heavy drinkers had higher circulating levels of adiponectin, angiogenin, ICAM‐1, LCN2, and sRAGE relative to light drinkers. Second, hsCRP was lower under low‐dose alcohol relative to placebo. Third, a dose‐by‐time interaction was observed for sRAGE, wherein this biomarker showed a transient increase under moderate‐dose alcohol, but not placebo or low‐dose alcohol. Finally, LCN2, MMP‐7, and MMP‐9 showed main effects of time that were independent of beverage condition, consistent with short‐term physiological variation across the session (e.g., diurnal patterns, postprandial response, etc.). D‐dimer and TREM2 showed no significant main or interactive effects in this study, and no significant three‐way interactions were observed for any biomarker.

Group Differences Between Light and Heavy Drinkers

Relative to light drinkers, heavy drinkers exhibited elevations in five biomarkers: adiponectin, angiogenin, ICAM‐1, LCN2, and sRAGE. Observed elevated adiponectin is consistent with prior experimental evidence showing that daily consumption of 40 g alcohol per day for several weeks increased circulating adiponectin levels in healthy men (Beulens et al. 2008; Sierksma et al. 2004). Although typically regarded as metabolically favorable due to its insulin‐sensitizing and anti‐inflammatory properties (Wang and Scherer 2016), elevated levels can have varying functional implications depending on the population and context. In this sample, elevated adiponectin may be best interpreted as evidence of altered adipokine signaling associated with heavier alcohol exposure, although the functional implications of this shift in our healthy young adults remains unclear. In contrast, the remaining markers more clearly implicate vascular, metabolic, and inflammatory signaling pathways relevant to chronic disease risk. Elevated angiogenin and ICAM‐1, both associated with endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and heightened risk for CVD and cerebrovascular disease (Ewers et al. 2010; Yu et al. 2018), may reflect early engagement of vascular signaling pathways linked to endothelial activation prior to clinically overt disease. Elevated LCN2, an adipokine linked to metabolic dysregulation and low‐grade inflammation (Abella et al. 2015), is consistent with emerging evidence that alcohol contributes to systemic metabolic and immune dysfunction. Finally, higher sRAGE in heavy drinkers may reflect a compensatory response to increased proinflammatory ligand burden (e.g., AGEs, amyloid‐β) (Erusalimsky 2021).

Viewed collectively, these group differences indicate that heavy drinking status is associated with differences in biomarkers relevant to vascular, metabolic, and inflammatory signaling. These alterations are particularly notable given the young age of this sample (M = 25 years) and, among heavy drinkers, the relatively modest intensity and limited duration of drinking. Lifetime drinking history and AUD diagnosis were not considered in interpretation of results, due to the mild classification of severity and small number of those meeting AUD criteria. Importantly, main effects for between‐group differences, that is, differences not induced by the experimental paradigm, are cross‐sectional in nature. Although groups had comparable demographic characteristics and the sensitivity analyses on race and smoking status yielded null findings, between‐group differences may have been influenced by unmeasured confounds and should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

Dose‐Dependent Effects

Relative to placebo, hsCRP was lower in the low‐dose alcohol condition. However, this finding should be interpreted in light of CRP biology and the constraints of a 4‐h observation window. Because CRP is hepatically synthesized and exhibits slower induction and clearance kinetics, differences observed over hours are unlikely to reflect de novo inflammatory induction or suppression. A more plausible interpretation is short‐term modulation of the measured circulating concentration through acute physiological processes that can affect concentration independent of upstream inflammatory signaling, for example, altered distribution.

Dose‐By‐Time Interaction

sRAGE showed a dose‐by‐time interaction characterized by an initial decline across all conditions, followed by an increase only in the moderate‐dose condition. The Hour 1 decrease present under all conditions logically can be interpreted as a normal diurnal variation rather than an alcohol effect. Interestingly, the decrease in sRAGE relative to baseline remained significant in the low‐dose condition, but not the placebo condition, throughout the observation window. In contrast, a subsequent rise in sRAGE after Hour 1 was observed only under moderate‐dose alcohol and may suggest a compensatory response to increased proinflammatory ligand burden. Because sRAGE acts as a circulating decoy receptor that binds AGEs, amyloid‐β, and other proinflammatory ligands (Erusalimsky 2021), this increase may reflect adaptive upregulation to buffer heightened ligand load and/or oxidative stress. This interpretation is consistent with observational evidence that chronic heavy drinking and AUD are associated with elevated circulating sRAGE and increased brain tissue RAGE expression (Fisher et al. 2025; Rodríguez de Fonseca et al. 2024; Vetreno et al. 2013). Altered sRAGE levels (i.e., either elevation or depletion) are observed in diabetes, atherosclerosis, and other chronic inflammatory conditions (Chen et al. 2024; Ho et al. 2018), supporting a broader role for sRAGE as a risk marker for chronic disease. Although sRAGE increase occurred in our study under only a single administration with average peak breath alcohol of 0.063%, repeated increases could, over time, promote disease risk via dysregulation of the AGE–RAGE signaling axis. This hypothesis is consistent with the observed main effect of group discussed above, wherein heavy drinkers exhibited higher sRAGE levels overall, and warrants further investigation in longitudinal studies.

Time‐Dependent Effects

Time‐related changes were observed for several biomarkers independent of experimental factors. MMP‐7 decreased from baseline to Hour 1, whereas MMP‐9 increased throughout the observation period. LCN2 declined from baseline throughout Hours 1–2. Because all sessions started and ended within a circumscribed time window (start: 9–11 am; end: 2–4 pm) and effects were unrelated to beverage condition or drinking group, the observed fluctuations presumably reflect normal circadian and/or postprandial variation.

Integration With Our Prior Findings

Integrating the present findings with the immune and microbial translocation results reported in Monnig et al. (2025) reveals a multi‐system response characterized by distinct temporal signatures and mechanistic coupling. In both studies, heavy drinking status was associated with an allostatic set‐point across interconnected immune axes, evidenced by higher levels of biomarkers of macrophage activation (sCD163; Monnig et al. 2025), endothelial signaling (angiogenin, ICAM‐1; present study), and proinflammatory activity (LCN2, sRAGE; present study). These findings, paired with the conditioned immune modulation previously observed under placebo in heavy drinkers (Monnig et al. 2025), suggest that chronic exposure is linked to a “co‐primed” biological environment that governs acute responsivity. Acute administration findings provide mechanistic insight, revealing both main effects of dose and dose × group interactions. In the current study, sRAGE increased under the moderate‐dose condition in both groups. By contrast, Monnig et al. (2025) found that heavy drinkers showed differential acute cytokine responses relative to light drinkers, including transient IL‐8 and TNF‐α spikes in the low‐dose condition and IL‐6 suppression in low‐ and moderate‐dose conditions. Together, these findings suggest that acute engagement of an anti‐inflammatory buffering mechanism is a common physiologic response to alcohol, whereas the heavy‐drinking phenotype is characterized by unique acute responses that are not uniform across different classes of immune signaling messengers.

Notably, the absence of acute alcohol‐induced changes in LPS in the companion report suggests that the systemic interplay observed in both studies is likely driven by alternative internal triggers, rather than a sudden influx of gut‐derived endotoxin at the selected low and moderate doses (0.35 and 0.60 g/kg, respectively). Although correlations between LPS and cytokines (Monnig et al. 2025) support a functional gut‐immune signaling axis, the lack of an acute LPS spike may reflect a dose–response threshold or the preserved hepatic clearance capacity of this young healthy sample. The 4‐h observation period here appears to be characterized by the mobilization of rapid‐response signaling and buffering systems rather than acute gut‐barrier disruption, at least in this specific population. Furthermore, the divergence between rapid cytokine fluctuations and the distinct kinetic constraints governing markers such as hsCRP highlights the importance of temporal resolution. Within a 4‐h timeframe under our experimental conditions, alcohol's impact appears to be primarily reflected in rapid‐response signaling systems, whereas downstream systemic markers may provide a snapshot of chronic baseline modulation and early‐stage pathways associated with heavy drinking (see review by Barr et al. 2016).

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, while the randomized, placebo‐controlled design provides a framework for evaluating alcohol‐specific effects, several factors constrain the strength of causal inference. Although the within‐subjects approach increases power to detect effects of alcohol dose, the modest sample size increases the risk of false negatives for subtler effects and inhibits the ability to probe interactions across demographic subgroups. Second, although plasma biomarkers provide a noninvasive and scalable index of systemic processes, they may not reflect tissue‐specific or central nervous system dynamics. For example, peripheral concentrations of TREM2 may not serve as a reliable proxy for central nervous system microglial signaling; such tissue compartmentalization could account for null findings despite robust preclinical evidence of alcohol‐induced TREM2 dysregulation in brain tissue (Lan et al. 2022; Ulland and Colonna 2018).

With regard to the study population, participants were young and free of known disease. Although this approach minimizes confounds and the risk of reverse causality, it also may restrict generalizability to older or clinical populations where alcohol's biological effects are likely more pronounced and complicated by comorbidities. In addition, although family history of AUD was assessed and did not differ significantly between groups, data on family history of chronic disease were not collected. Because ethical and practical constraints preclude the experimental manipulation of long‐term heavy alcohol consumption, drinker status was used as a proxy for chronic exposure. Consequently, whereas the acute modulations observed during the challenge are experimentally induced, group differences reflect associations with drinking history rather than causal effects. Finally, the number of samples across all analyses was not equal as samples were expended. However, the number of samples allotted to each assay was not prioritized in any way and was determined according to logistical lab factors, thereby minimizing the likelihood of bias.

These limitations point to clear directions for future research. Replication in larger and more diverse samples is necessary to confirm the present patterns and examine potential moderators such as sex, race/ethnicity, and psychosocial factors. Incorporating multimodal approaches, including neuroimaging and multiomics profiling, would help to more definitively link circulating biomarkers to cellular and tissue‐level alterations. Longitudinal designs that capture repeated drinking episodes also are essential to determine whether and how transient shifts consolidate into persistent dysregulation over time. Furthermore, future studies should utilize a more expansive biomarker panel to promote mechanistic understanding. Additional candidates include inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β, as well as endocrine and appetite‐regulating hormones (e.g., insulin, GLP‐1, ghrelin, and leptin) to better characterize acute response to alcohol as a function of drinking status. Finally, in vivo placebo‐controlled studies using a wider range of alcohol doses and longer timescales are needed to characterize the transition to key mechanisms such as gut‐barrier disruption in both light and heavy drinkers. Such extensions will build on the current study's strengths to more fully characterize dynamics by which alcohol disrupts immune homeostasis and contributes to chronic disease risk.

Conclusion

This study provides translational evidence that both dose‐dependent acute alcohol and drinking history are associated with perturbations across multiple biological systems. Even in healthy young adults without extensive drinking histories, heavy drinkers showed elevations in biomarkers of metabolic activity (adiponectin), endothelial signaling (angiogenin, ICAM‐1), and inflammatory activity (LCN2, sRAGE) relative to demographically matched light drinkers. Moreover, a dose‐dependent effect was observed in sRAGE, a decoy receptor that binds proinflammatory ligands: sRAGE decreased under low‐dose alcohol but increased under moderate‐dose alcohol. The decline observed in hsCRP under low‐dose alcohol likely reflects its unique temporal and hepatic functions rather than a rapid alcohol‐induced signaling effect. Time‐related effects for LCN2, MMP‐7, and MMP‐9 were independent of dose condition or drinker group, underscoring the necessity of including a credible placebo condition in alcohol administration studies to isolate experimental effects from diurnal/postprandial effects. Overall, findings suggest that alcohol intake promotes immediate, dose‐dependent shifts in select biomarkers, as well as longer‐term adaptations linked with heavy drinking status. When considered alongside prior evidence of alcohol‐induced immune modulation in this cohort (Monnig et al. 2025), a cohesive picture begins to emerge in which alcohol perturbs interconnected signaling systems—vascular, metabolic, and immune—that may collectively influence long‐term chronic disease risk.
