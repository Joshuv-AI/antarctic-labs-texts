Conserved genetic and antigenic profiles of Pf12 and Pf41 in asymptomatic Plasmodium falciparum carriers in Tanzania
Authors: Fadhila Fitriana, Wang-Jong Lee, Hyun-Young Sim, Hojong Jun, Jadidan Hada Syahada, Fauzi Muh, Se Jin Lee, Sunghun Na, Kyu Joon Lee, Joo-Won Nam, Eun-Taek Han, Jim Todd, Alphaxard Manjurano, Winifrida Kidima, Ernest Mazigo, Jin-Hee Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasmodium falciparum remains a major cause of mortality in malaria-endemic regions, and strain-specific immunity limits vaccine efficacy. The 6-cysteine proteins Pf12 and Pf41 are proposed blood-stage vaccine candidates, yet their diversity and antigenicity in asymptomatic carriers are poorly defined. Genetic variation and natural selection pressure were analyzed in 172 P. falciparum isolates from asymptomatic individuals in Tanzania. Genomic DNA extracted from dried blood spots was amplified by PCR and subsequently sequenced to evaluate genetic diversity. IgG responses to recombinant Pf12 and Pf41 were quantified using a protein microarray. Statistical analyses included Spearman’s correlation to assess antibody associations and unpaired t-tests to compare IgG levels between parasite-positive and parasite-negative individuals. Both genes exhibited low nucleotide diversity (pf12 π = 0.00042 ± 0.00007; pf41 π = 0.00061 ± 0.00004) and negative Tajima’s D values suggest that both genes are under selective constraints, consistent with purifying or directional selection. Neutrality test indicated an excess of rare variants consistent with recent population expansion. Haplotype network showed limited regional differentiation, supporting strong conservation. Pf12 and Pf41 elicited high IgG seropositivity (97.7% and 98.3%), with strongly correlated antibody levels (ρ = 0.721, p < 0.001). A predominant Pf41 substitution, S232R (47.8%), and rare Asn/His variants (0.6%) were detected. Overall, both proteins display limited polymorphism and robust IgG recognition, features consistent with exposure-driven immunity. These findings highlight conserved merozoite antigens that warrant further investigations for their biological relevance and vaccine potential, particularly to determine their functional role in protective immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55360-9.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, 6-cysteine proteins, Asymptomatic malaria, Genetic diversity, IgG responses, Vaccine developmentSubject terms: Diseases, Genetics, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Malaria is a life-threatening infectious disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium that is transmitted through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito. The most pathogenic human malaria parasite is P. falciparum, which is responsible for nearly all malaria-related fatalities. In 2024, approximately 282 million malaria cases and around 610,000 deaths were reported globally1. The majority of these cases occurred in the African region, with substantial increases observed in Sub-Saharan African1. One of the most affected countries is the United Republic of Tanzania, where around 9 million cases were reported in 2024, contributed to 3.3% of global cases and 4.3% of global deaths1,2. Within Tanzania, Geita and Kigoma are classified as high-transmission regions, where the symptomatic cases reported between December 2022 to July 2023 accounted for 12.7% and 8.2% respectively2. Moreover, recent community-based surveys reported asymptomatic malaria prevalences in Geita and Kigoma of approximately 35.6% and 23.1%, respectively, markedly higher than in low-transmission areas such as Arusha (around 2–3%)2. In these high endemic settings, asymptomatic P. falciparum infections are common and constitute an important reservoir for ongoing transmission, underscoring the relevance characterizing parasite genetic diversity and naturally acquired antibody responses in asymptomatic carriers from Geita and Kigoma2,3. Despite the implementation of control strategies, the emergence of parasite resistance to antimalarial treatments and vector resistance to insecticides poses major challenges to malaria control and elimination worldwide.

Populations living in high endemic areas are frequently exposed to genetically diverse malaria parasites. This repeated exposure leads to partially protective naturally acquired immunity, which reduces clinical symptoms but does not eliminate the parasite, resulting in asymptomatic infections4-7. Asymptomatic individuals serve as silent reservoirs for continued transmission, allowing malaria diversification to persist as a public health concern2,4. Such silent reservoirs may contribute to ongoing transmission and pose significant challenges to malaria elimination strategies. To overcome these undetectable parasite infections, vaccine-induced immunity may offer more effective protection than naturally acquired immunity8. This form of immunity aims to prevent both clinical disease and transmission by reducing parasite burden and inhibiting merozoite invasion of erythrocytes. Existing malaria vaccines, including RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M, target pre-erythrocytic stage and show modest efficacy against diverse circulating parasites, indicating the need for more effective blood-stage vaccine candidates9,10. Despite these advances, RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M provide only partial and time-limited protection, particularly in high transmission settings. Therefore, there remains a continued need to identify and evaluate promising blood-stage vaccine targets11,12. Such strategies align with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommendation to develop asexual blood-stage vaccines targeting erythrocyte invasion to reduce malaria incidence13,14. Accordingly, current malaria vaccine research is focusing on blood-stage targets, particularly highly conserved merozoite antigens15.

Among these, the 6-cysteine (6-cys) protein family, consisting of 14 members, stands out as one of the most abundant groups of surface antigens, attracting interest as malaria vaccine candidates16. Members of this family are conserved across Plasmodium species and are expressed throughout the parasite life cycle16-18. These proteins exhibit conserved 6-cystein residues and similar tertiary structures, which may reflect functional conservation across the family19. Each protein plays an individual role in parasite transmission, immune evasion, or host cell invasion at specific developmental stages of the life cycle17. About half of the characterized 6-cys proteins possess glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors that tether them to the plasma membrane, while others may remain associated with the parasite surface via interactions with other membrane proteins18. Due to their roles in gamete development and fertilization, many 6-cys proteins have been studied as candidates for transmission blocking vaccines (TBVs)20. Proteins such as Pfs48/45, Pfs230, and Pfs47 have shown promise in this context, as antibodies targeting them can disrupt parasite development in the mosquito vector21. Additionally, PfP36 and PfP52 play important roles in sporozoite invasion into hepatocytes22. These essential functions across the Plasmodium life cycle reflect the importance of the 6-cys protein family in parasite survival and support their potential as vaccine candidates.

During the asexual blood-stage, additional members of the 6-cys protein family are expressed. Among them, Pf12 is one of the most abundant GPI-anchored 6-cys proteins on the surface of P. falciparum merozoites23. It forms a heterodimer with Pf41, another 6-cys protein that lacks a GPI anchor17,18,24. Although the essentiality of these genes remains debated depending on the experimental system, recent piggyBac mutagenesis analysis suggests that Pf12 is essential for parasite viability, whereas Pf41 contributes only moderately to parasite fitness25. Both proteins are exposed to the host immune system during merozoite invasion and are recognized by naturally acquired antibodies in individuals living in malaria-endemic areas18,26. This immunoreactivity suggests that they are targets of naturally acquired humoral responses, although their protective efficacy has not been clearly established. Given their strong antigenicity and surface localization, both proteins represent potential candidates for blood-stage vaccine development, although their protective efficacy remains to be established.

To better evaluate their potential as vaccine candidates, it is important to assess whether genetic variation or antigenic diversity could limit their effectiveness in natural infections. Since extensive polymorphism in vaccine targets can reduce protective efficacy, understanding the extent and geographic patterns of genetic diversity is critical27,28. An effective malaria vaccine should confer broad protection against the circulating variants across endemic regions29,30. Although Pf12 and Pf41 are known to be immunogenic and structurally conserved, their genetic and antigenic variability remain poorly understood, particularly in field isolates from asymptomatic infections. Previous studies in other malaria-endemic regions have shown limited polymorphism in Pf4131 and in Pv1232, the orthologue of Pf12. However, data from asymptomatic infections remain limited, and data on Pf12 itself are lacking, representing an important but underexplored aspect of the population genetic diversity of these antigens. These are especially relevant in high-transmission areas, where asymptomatic carriers represent a substantial reservoir for ongoing transmission and may harbor diverse parasite populations2.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the genetic diversity and antigenicity of Pf12 and Pf41 in asymptomatic P. falciparum infections from eight Tanzanian villages within two high-malaria endemic regions, Geita and Kigoma, to assess their suitability as blood-stage vaccine targets in high-transmission settings. This study further integrates serological analysis to evaluate the antigenic relevance of polymorphisms, providing functional insight beyond previous genetic diversity studies.

Results

Sample collection and population characteristics

The samples analyzed in this study were obtained from asymptomatic individuals participating in a community-based cross-sectional survey conducted in Geita and Kigoma, Tanzania. These regions were deliberately selected because they represent persistently high malaria transmission settings with limited prior molecular and immunological surveillance2, making them particularly suitable for investigating naturally acquired antibody responses in asymptomatic infections. Within each region, two districts were randomly selected, and from each district, two villages were randomly chosen to minimize selection bias and to capture intra-regional heterogeneity. A total of 242 whole blood samples were collected, and plasma was separated from all samples for antigenicity screening. However, only samples consistently confirmed as malaria-positive by both malaria rapid diagnostic test (mRDT) and microscopy, with sufficient DBS and plasma quality for downstream analysis, were included in the final dataset. As a result, 172 asymptomatic individuals from the eight villages were retained for antigenicity screening and genetic diversity analysis (Fig. 1).

The study population included both males (44.8%) and females (55.2%) across a broad age range (1–90 years), with a median age of 9 years. Most participants were aged 6–10 years (39.5%) (Table S2). Overall, the median parasitemia was 880 parasites/µL (range 440–2,790 parasites/µL), with Kigoma significantly exhibited higher parasitemia levels (median 1,100 parasites/µL; range 560–3,290 parasites/µL) than Geita (median 740 parasites/µL; range 330–2,730 parasites/µL) (Table S2).

Gene structure and polymorphism of pf12 and pf41

The full-length pf12 gene (PF3D7_0612700) contains a 1,044 bp coding region encoding a 347 amino acids protein with a predicted molecular weight of 39.4 kDa (Fig. 2A). A total of 15 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified, including 4 synonymous mutations and 11 non-synonymous mutations (Tables 1, 2). Two distinct non-synonymous mutations, L55V and L256S, were each detected in 8/167 isolates (4.8%) and were distributed across different haplotypes, with L55V located in the 6-cys domain 1 and L256S in domain 2. Additional novel non-synonymous mutations (N34K, P43H, T56I, E185Q, A215E, G222R, R312I, D313Y, and D318N) were detected at low frequencies (1/167 isolates, 0.6% each) across different villages. These mutations were distributed across multiple haplotypes, although N34K and P43H co-occurred in haplotype 5, and A215E and G222R in haplotype 11 (Fig. 2C).

The pf41 gene (PF3D7_0404900) comprises a 1,137 bp coding region encoding a 378 amino acids protein with a predicted molecular weight of 43.1 kDa (Fig. 2B). Two synonymous and nine non-synonymous mutations were identified in pf41 (Table 2). The most prevalent mutation, S232R, was detected in 77/160 isolates (48.8%) and was distributed across multiple haplotypes within the 6-cys domain 1. Rare alternative substitutions at the same position, Asn and His, were each observed in 1/160 isolates (0.6%). Additional novel non-synonymous mutations were detected at low frequencies (1/160 isolates, 0.6% each), including L86I, P108Q, T119I, D132E, and S191T in domain 1, and K333N in domain 2 (Fig. 2C). The overall number and frequency of amino acid substitutions was limited in both genes among asymptomatic infections. Tertiary structure analysis revealed no non-synonymous mutations were detected among residues directly involved in the Pf12-Pf41 interaction interface. Although substitutions at Pf12 position 256 and Pf41 position 232 were positioned toward the interface, they did not form direct contacts (Fig. 2C).

Genetic diversity and natural selection pressure of pf12 and pf41

The pf12 and pf41 genes were successfully genotyped from 167 (97.1%) and 160 (93.0%) of the 172 asymptomatic P. falciparum isolates, respectively. Analysis of nucleotide diversity (π) revealed regional variation in both genes. At the region level, although nucleotide diversity for pf12 appeared higher in Kigoma region (π ± SD., 0.00046 ± 0.00011) than in Geita (0.00039 ± 0.00009), the difference was not statistically significant (Table 1). Within Kigoma, Kibondo district exhibited slightly higher diversity (0.00048 ± 0.00015) than Kasulu (0.00045 ± 0.00014), while in Geita, Nyang’hwale (0.00042 ± 0.00013) showed greater diversity than Chato (0.00036 ± 0.00011) (Table S3). The overall nucleotide diversity of pf12 across all regions was 0.00042 ± 0.00007 (Table 1).

Neutrality tests for pf12 across all study villages yielded significantly negative values for Tajima’s D (− 2.22862, p < 0.01), Fu & Li’s D* (− 4.96005, p < 0.01), and F* (− 4.70725, p < 0.01), as well as Fu’s Fs (− 17.575) (Table 1). These results suggest an excess of rare variants, consistent with either recent population expansion or purifying selection. The strongly negative Fu’s Fs value is also consistent with a scenario of demographic expansion. The ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN-dS = 0.12, p = 0.45) was not statistically significant, suggesting limited or inconclusive evidence of purifying selection at the protein level (Table 2). Taken together, these results suggest that the observed genetic patterns in pf12 are more consistent with recent population expansion, although a contribution from purifying selection cannot be fully excluded.

At the regional level, nucleotide diversity of pf41 was slightly higher than that of pf12. The nucleotide diversity of pf41 was 0.00067 ± 0.00006 in the Kigoma region and 0.00057 ± 0.00004 in Gita (Table 1). Among the Kigoma districts, Mugombe (KKM, 0.00083 ± 0.00013) showed the highest diversity, followed by Kumuhasha (KKK, 0.00071 ± 0.00013), Bunyambo (KKB, 0.00063 ± 0.00012), and Nyamnyusi (KKN, 0.00055 ± 0.00008). In Geita, diversity was highest in Rwantaba (GCR, 0.00068 ± 0.00011), followed by Ihanga (GCI, 0.00058 ± 0.00010), Nyangalamila (GNN, 0.00056 ± 0.00007), and Kayenze (GNK, 0.00049 ± 0.00004) (Table S4). The overall nucleotide diversity for pf41 was 0.00061 ± 0.00004, which was significantly higher than that of pf12 (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Tajima’s D for pf41 was negative (− 1.51468) but not statistically significant. However, Fu and Li’s D* (− 3.94050, p < 0.01) and F* (− 3.66188, p < 0.01), as well as Fu’s Fs (− 8.537), were significantly negative, suggesting an excess of low-frequency polymorphisms (Table 1). This pattern may reflect population expansion or purifying selection. The dN-dS ratio was 0.64 (p = 0.26), suggesting a relatively higher proportion of non-synonymous substitutions, although the difference was not statistically significant, indicating no strong evidence for positive or purifying selection (Table 2). Collectively, the evidence suggests that demographic expansion likely contributes substantially to genetic variation in pf41, consistent with the pattern identified for pf12.

To evaluate inter-species selection pressure, a McDonald-Kreitman (MK) test was conducted using P. reichenowi as an outgroup. The neutrality index (NI) was 1.833 (p = 0.51) for pf12 and 2.207 (p = 0.47) for pf41 (Table 2). Although both values suggest a trend toward purifying selection at the inter-species level, the results were not statistically significant.

Haplotype diversity and network analysis of pf12 and pf41

A total of 14 and 12 haplotypes were identified for pf12 and pf41, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). In pf12, most sequences were either identical or differed by only one or two amino acid substitutions (Fig. 3C). The haplotype structure is dominated by a single high-frequency haplotype and closely related low-frequency variants. Despite limited haplotype divergence, the presence of geographically dispersed variants is consistent with recent population expansion (Fig. 3C). In contrast, pf41 exhibited broader amino acid variation, with several haplotypes present at moderate to high frequency (Fig. 3D). Compared to pf12, the pf41 haplotype network exhibited greater divergence and more mutational steps, reflecting a more extensive accumulation of variants likely driven by population expansion rather than strong selective pressure (Fig. 3D).

Antigenicity of Pf12 and Pf41 in asymptomatic infections

The recombinant proteins representing the extracellular domains of Pf12 (26–323 aa.) and Pf41 (21–378 aa.), containing all identified non-synonymous mutation sites, were expressed in soluble form as rPf12 (40.1 kDa) and rPf41 (47.0 kDa) (Fig. 4A). These recombinant proteins were used for antigenicity screening.

Total IgG reactivity in plasma samples from asymptomatic P. falciparum infections demonstrated significant antibody responses to both Pf12 and Pf41 across all surveyed villages (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B and C). Seropositivity for Pf12 ranged from 91.7% in Kayenze to 100% in most villages (Table S5), whereas Pf41 seropositivity ranged from 95.5% in Rwantaba to 100% in most villages (Table S6) with an overall specificity of 98.4%. MFI analysis revealed that individuals from Bunyambo, which showed the lowest MFI among the study villages, had a 9.1-fold higher IgG response to Pf12 and a 10.5-fold higher response to Pf41 compared to healthy controls.

To explore associations among host- and parasite-related variables, pairwise comparisons of parasitemia, age and IgG responses to Pf12 and Pf41 were visualized using pair plots with linear regression lines and KDEs stratified by village (Fig. 4D). The KDE plots illustrated the distribution patterns of IgG responses, parasitemia, and age across the study population, with no notable shifts in central tendency across villages. The overall shape and spread of the distributions were largely comparable across villages. A strong positive correlation was observed between Pf12 and Pf41 IgG levels (ρ = 0.721, p < 0.001). Age showed a weak but statistically significant positive correlation with IgG responses to Pf12 (ρ = 0.212, p = 0.005) and Pf41 (ρ = 0.303, p < 0.001), although the strength and direction of these associations varied by village, as reflected in the village-specific regression trends (Fig. 4D and Fig. S2). These village-specific analyses were performed to explore heterogeneity across locations and should be interpreted with caution due to multiple comparisons. Consistent with these age correlations, age-stratified analysis showed comparable Pf12 MFI between children (<15 years) and adults (≥15 years), whereas Pf41 MFI was significantly higher in adults than in children (p = 0.012) (Table S7). No significant correlation was found between parasitemia and antibody levels across the full study population for either Pf12 (ρ = − 0.004, p = 0.954) or Pf41 (ρ = 0.038, p = 0.624), although positive associations were observed in specific villages, including Bunyambo and Rwantaba for both Pf12 and Pf41, and Mugombe for Pf41 alone (Figs. 4D and S2). Similarly, parasitemia and age showed no significant correlation (ρ = 0.093, p = 0.226).

Discussion

An effective vaccine against P. falciparum malaria remains a research priority despite the recent WHO approval of two malaria vaccines, RTS,S and R21. Despite progress with pre-erythrocytic vaccines, the lack of effective blood-stage antigens remains a critical gap in malaria control. Here, we characterized the genetic diversity and antigenicity of Pf12 and its binding partner, Pf41, in asymptomatic P. falciparum isolates from high-transmission sites in Tanzania. We found both Pf12 and Pf41 exhibited low nucleotide diversity and limited haplotype variation, indicating substantial sequence conservation compared to highly polymorphic invasion ligands such as PfEBA-175 and PfAMA1, as well as the merozoite surface antigen PfMSP128,33,34. For pf12, 15 SNPs were identified, mostly non-synonymous, yielding low nucleotide diversity (π = 0.0004 ± 0.0001). This high level of conservation aligns with global patterns, which reported purifying selection (ω < 0.5) and predominantly neutral non-synonymous changes across diverse P. falciparum isolates, reinforcing Pf12 as a conserved vaccine candidate35. Similarly, pf41 displayed slightly higher nucleotide diversity (π = 0.0006 ± 0.0000) but still exhibited limited variation compared with West African field isolates (π = 0.0014 ± 0.0002 in Senegal)31. Consistent with our findings, analysis of the MalariaGen Pf8 dataset from Kigoma, Tanzania indicates lower genetic diversity in pf12 compared to pf41, supporting stronger functional constraint on Pf12, while overall heterozygosity remains low for both antigens in this high-transmission setting36.

Most non-synonymous substitutions clustered within domain 1 of pf41, including S232R/N/H, each of which have been reported globally as sites of mild balancing selection31. Importantly, no amino acid substitutions were detected at the Pf12-Pf41 interface, a region critical for heterodimer formation, indicating intense evolutionary conservation at functionally essential residues17,37. This observation aligns with crystallographic studies showing that residues Y264 (Pf12) and F337 (Pf41) are indispensable for maintaining the stability of the complex17. Alterations at these residues disrupt the Pf12-Pf41 interaction, indicating their role in parasite survival and invasion. Overall, the limited sequence diversity likely reflects selective maintenance of structural function, with demographic factors influencing rare-allele frequencies17,35,37.

Building on previous studies that have primarily described genetic diversity, our findings integrate serological profiling to assess the antigenic relevance of Pf12 and Pf41 polymorphisms and consider their interaction, particularly in asymptomatic populations. The observed sequence conservation aligns with the strong antibody response detected in this study. Both Pf12 and Pf41 elicited high seroprevalence (97.7% and 98.3%, respectively), and antibody levels to Pf12 and Pf41 were strongly positively correlated (ρ = 0.721, p < 0.001). These findings agree with previous observations that antibodies against 6-cys proteins are co-acquired through cumulative exposure, reflecting shared immune recognition and possibly cross-reactivity among related family members24,38. Importantly, while Pf12- and Pf41-specific antibodies were detectable in the study population, antibody presence alone does not necessarily imply functional or protective immunity39. The strongly negative Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs values argue against long-term immune-driven balancing and instead indicate an excess of rare variants consistent with recent population growth rather than long-term balancing selection. Together with the low dN−dS ratio (0.12), these findings suggest strong functional conservation of pf12 and support the interpretation that the observed antibody responses may primarily reflect cumulative exposure rather than protective efficacy. Functional assays will be required to determine whether antibodies targeting the Pf12-Pf41 complex directly contribute to parasite inhibition. In addition, interpretations of genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships in this study were made within the conventional framework of Neutral Theory, which has historically served as a useful baseline for molecular evolutionary analysis40-43. However, recent studies suggest that adaptive and non-neutral processes may also contribute substantially to sequence variation in pathogen populations44-46. Therefore, the observed polymorphism patterns in Pf12 and Pf41 should be interpreted with caution, as alternative evolutionary models may further refine our understanding of antigen diversity in P. falciparum.

Age-associated increases in antibody levels further suggest gradual acquisition of humoral immunity following repeated low-density infections, a hallmark of partial protection in endemic settings47,48. Age-stratified analysis showed children had adult-like MFI responses to Pf12, whereas Pf41 responses were more age-dependent. Taken together, these patterns indicate exposure-driven acquisition dynamics for both antigens, albeit with varying rapidity, without implying any direct functional interdependence between the two antigens.

While antibody recognition was strong, previous studies demonstrated that antibodies targeting Pf12 or Pf41, or their peptide fragments, alone have limited invasion-blocking capacity (10-20%)17,49. Thus, their immunological contribution may be indirect, facilitating opsonization or complement activation rather than direct invasion inhibition. Such functional roles are compatible with the partial immunity observed in asymptomatic carriers, where infection is controlled but not cleared. This nuanced immune response highlights that Pf12 and Pf41, although not sterilizing targets, may exert key supportive roles within multi-antigen vaccine formulations24. Given their structural stability and complementary functions, Pf12 and Pf41 could serve as stable immunogenic scaffolds that, when combined with invasion ligands such as PfEBA-175 or PfRh5, may broaden and prolong protective immune responses.

Some studies have questioned the protective significance of Pf12 and Pf41, whereas others emphasize their immunogenic stability and conserved epitopes as promising features for vaccine development17,24,49. Pf12 and Pf41 are localized primarily on the merozoite surface, making them readily accessible to host antibodies24. Their stability and surface accessibility, combined with more immunodominant but polymorphic targets such as PfMSP1, PfMSP3, or PfGLURP, make them attractive components for next-generation vaccine formulations50.

Recent epitope-mapping studies identified surface-exposed B-cell epitopes in Pf12 (residues 120–140 and 255–275) and Pf41 (residues 210–250), overlapping with regions important for Pf12-Pf41 complex formation17,24,51. Although our sequence analysis revealed non-synonymous substitutions within or near these regions (L256S in Pf12 and S232R/N/H in Pf41), the overall epitope architecture appears largely conserved, suggesting that broad antibody recognition may still be maintained. However, the relatively high frequency of S232R variant in Pf41 and its occurrence within predicted epitope regions raise the possibility that this variant could contribute to immune evasion. Alternatively, these polymorphisms may represent neutral variation that does not substantially alter antibody binding, particularly given the high seropositivity observed. Further functional studies would be required to distinguish between these possibilities.

Taken together, these findings indicate that pf12 is relatively conserved and immunogenic, whereas pf41 gene shows evidence of polymorphism, despite retaining key structural features. No amino acid substitutions found at interaction interface position. These characteristics suggest their potential utility as reliable components in multi-stage or multi-antigen malaria vaccines. Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, it relied exclusively on asymptomatic field samples, without evaluating functional antibody response such as growth inhibition, complement activation, or T-cell reactivity. Moreover, the cross-sectional design prevents the assessment of temporal dynamics and antibody persistence across successive transmission seasons. Future longitudinal investigations integrating genomic and immunological, and structural approaches will be essential to clarify whether antibodies to Pf12 and Pf41 can effectively block invasion or transmission in vivo. Although the sample size was sufficient to characterize dominant antibody response patterns, larger cohorts would enable validation of these findings and more granular analysis, particularly for rare haplotypes or subgroups. In addition, as samples were collected from asymptomatic individuals in high-transmission settings in Tanzania, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to symptomatic malaria or to lower-transmission or pre-elimination settings.

High seropositivity of Pf12 is likely driven by its high sequence conservation, which may preserve key conformational epitopes recognized by naturally acquired antibodies. This observation is consistent with the limited polymorphism observed in field isolates and with previous reports describing Pf12 as a relatively conserved member of the 6-cys protein family. In contrast, although the most prevalent Pf41 haplotype differed due to synonymous substitutions at position S137 in haplotypes 2 and 3, as well as an additional non-synonymous substitution at position S232R in haplotype 2, seropositivity within the community remained high. These results highlight the need for future studies to evaluate haplotype-specific antibody responses using recombinant antigens derived from locally prevalent sequences to confirm their functional conservation.

Furthermore, the use of Sanger sequencing primarily captures dominant alleles and may underestimate within-host diversity, particularly in the presence of mixed infections. In addition, the exclusion of samples with unresolved sequence ambiguity, although limited in number, may have biased diversity estimates toward dominant haplotypes. Although haplotypes were inferred through careful chromatogram inspection and quality control procedures, low-frequency variants may not have been detected. Future applications of long-read amplicon sequencing platforms, such as Oxford Nanopore Technologies, may enable full-length haplotype resolution and improving population genetic inference. Continuous monitoring of sequence variation and immune responses across diverse transmission settings, including endemic regions in Africa and Asia, will be critical to determine whether the genetic stability of pf12 and pf41 is globally maintained and to inform the design of broadly protective malaria vaccines.

Methods

Ethical clearance

Clinical samples were collected in accordance with approved ethical protocols from the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR), a division of the Ministry of Health, Tanzania (NIMR/HQ/R.81/Vol.IX/4114). The handling of clinical samples was also approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Board of Kangwon National University (KWNUIRB-2022-06-008). Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants and from a parent or legal guardian for children. All procedures adhered to national ethical guidelines and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and sample collection

The study sites and sample collection procedures were previously described2,27. Briefly, eight villages located in two high malaria-endemic regions of Tanzania were selected (Fig. 1). The overall malaria prevalence in these regions was approximately 13%, with P. falciparum as the predominant species. A community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted between December 2022 and July 2023 to collect samples from asymptomatic individuals in each village. Asymptomatic individuals were defined as those who tested positive by microscopic examination, had no fever or malaria-related symptoms within the preceding 4–5 days, and had not taken antimalarial drugs in the past 7 days. Clinical samples were collected in heparinized tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) during home visits conducted by a trained team consisting of a medical doctor and a laboratory technician. Malaria infection was confirmed using the Bioline™ Malaria Ag P.f/Pan rapid diagnostic test (RDT) (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA) and thin blood smears examined independently by two experienced microscopists. Plasma was separated, and whole blood samples were spotted onto Whatman 3MM filter paper (Cytiva, Seoul, Republic of Korea) to prepare dried blood spots (DBS). DBS samples were air-dried, individually sealed with desiccant, and stored at ambient temperature until transported to Kangwon National University for molecular diagnosis, antigenicity screening, and genetic diversity analysis.

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from 50 μL (one spot) of DBS samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 172 isolates were subjected to PCR amplification targeting the ectodomains of pf12 (nt. 79-969; 891 bp) and pf41 (nt. 61-1,122; 1,062 bp) genes (Fig. 2A and B, black bars). Primers were designed to cover the entire coding regions, with two sets of sequencing primers per gene (Table S1). PCR products were validated using 3D7 genomic DNA as a positive control and further confirmed in representative field samples, providing validation of primer specificity and amplification fidelity. Amplicons showed single bands of the expected size on agarose gels and were verified by Sanger sequencing followed by alignment to the reference genome. PCR amplification was performed in a 20 μL reaction mixture containing KOD One PCR Master mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), using gDNA templates of pf12 and pf41. For pf12, the reaction contained 0.5 μL of gDNA template, 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and 8 μL of distilled deionized water (DDW). For Pf41, the reaction contained 2 μL of gDNA template, 1 μL of each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and 6 μL of DDW. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 98℃ for 7 minutes, followed by 35 cycles (pf12) or 45 cycles (pf41) of 98℃ for 10 seconds, 58℃ for 5 seconds, and 68℃ for 10 seconds, with a final extension at 68℃ for 10 minutes. DDW and P. falciparum 3D7 gDNA were included in each PCR run as negative and positive controls, respectively, to monitor contamination and verify amplification efficiency52. PCR amplicons were visualized on a 1.2% agarose gel and purified using the PureLink™ PCR Purification Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), and bidirectionally sequenced on an ABI3730xl DNA analyzer performed by a commercial provider (Nbit, Chuncheon, Republic of Korea). Negative controls were included in all PCR reactions and showed no amplification. Therefore, they were not subjected to sequencing. Forward and reverse reads were assembled into consensus sequences using SnapGene (version 2.3.2; https://www.snapgene.com/) (GSL Biotech LLC, USA) and the Lasergene software package (version 11; https://www.dnastar.com/) (DNASTAR Inc., USA). Consensus sequences were aligned to the 3D7 reference sequence using the ClustalW multiple sequence alignment algorithm implemented in MEGA11 software (version 11.0.8; https://www.megasoftware.net/)53. Chromatograms were manually inspected to ensure base call quality and complete coverage of the target regions. High-quality sequences were obtained from 167 (97.1%) samples for pf12 and 160 (93.0%) samples for pf41. Samples with low DNA yield or poor DBS quality were excluded from genetic diversity analysis. For gene variation analysis, pf12 sequences were aligned to the 3D7 reference gene PF3D7_0612700, and pf41 sequences were aligned to PF3D7_0404900. Rare variants or overlapping chromatogram peaks were verified through three independent PCR amplifications and sequencing runs. Samples in which overlapping peaks persisted (2 samples for pf12 and 1 sample for pf41) were excluded due to unresolved sequence ambiguity. In addition, samples that yielded no PCR amplicons were not subjected to sequencing and were excluded from genetic diversity analysis. All high-quality pf12 and pf41 sequences generated in this study have been deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PV683568–PV683734 for pf12 and PV683735–PV683894 for pf41.

Tertiary structure modeling and refinement

The tertiary structures of Pf12 and Pf41 were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) using IDs C6KSX0 and Q8I1Y0, respectively, to obtain full-length structural models. The per-residue confidence scores (predicted Local Distance Difference Test; pLDDT) were examined to assess model reliability, with average pLDDT values of 83.1 for Pf12 and 84.6 for Pf41, indicative of high-confidence predictions. Model quality was further validated using Ramachandran plot analysis and ERRAT scoring. Initial assessments showed that 81.2% of residues in the Pf12 model were in favored regions with no residues in disallowed regions. For Pf41, 93.9% of residues were in favored regions, with 0.6% in disallowed regions. ERRAT quality scores were 88.9% for Pf12 and 93.9% for Pf41.

To enhance structural accuracy, the models were refined using the Galaxy Refine server54,55. Following refinement, the Pf12 structure showed 93.1% of residues in favored regions and no disallowed residues while the Pf41 model displayed 94.2% in favored regions with 0.9% in disallowed regions. The ERRAT scores also improved, reaching 95.8% for Pf12 and 95.1% for Pf41. The final refined structures were imported into PyMOL software (version 3.1.6.1; https://www.pymol.org/)56 and aligned to the crystal structure of the Pf12-Pf41 heterodimeric complex (PDB ID: 7S7Q) for visualization. Structural mapping of synonymous and non-synonymous mutations was performed, with particular focus on residues located at the interaction interface between Pf12 and Pf41.

Population genetics and natural selection analysis

To evaluate sequence diversity, nucleotide diversity (π), the number of polymorphic sites, haplotypes, and haplotype diversity (Hd) were calculated using DnaSP software (version 5.10.1; https://www.ub.edu/dnasp/)57. Signatures of natural selection were assessed using Tajima’s D, Fu and Li’s D* and F*, and Fu’s Fs tests, which detect deviations from neutral evolution. Under neutrality, Tajima’s D is expected to be zero. A significantly positive value may indicate balancing selection or population structure, whereas significantly negative values suggest recent population expansion or a selective sweep. Similarly, positive values in Fu and Li’s D* and F* suggest balancing selection or structure within the population, while negative values imply an excess of rare variants, often associated with population expansion or purifying selection. Fu’s Fs test evaluates haplotype distributions, where significantly negative values suggest an excess of alleles, typically reflecting recent population growth or genetic hitchhiking.

The proportion of synonymous (dS) and non-synonymous (dN) substitutions were estimated using the Nei-Gojobori method with 1,000 bootstrap replications in MEGA1153. A dN/dS ratio greater than one indicates positive selection, whereas a value less than one suggests purifying selection. To further assess evolutionary neutrality, the McDonald-Kreitman (MK) test was performed by comparing polymorphism within P. falciparum to divergence from P. reichenowi. For this analysis, P. reichenowi p12 (PRCDC_0611400) and p41 (PRCDC_0402500), which belong to the Laverania clade and are closely related to P. falciparum, were used as out-group sequences. MK test calculations were conducted in DnaSP. Haplotype diversity of pf12 and pf41 was further analyzed using DnaSP, and haplotype networks were constructed and visualized using the median-joining method implemented in Network software (version 10.2; https://www.fluxus-engineering.com/)58.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Recombinant constructs of Pf12 (PF3D7_0612700) and Pf41 (PF3D7_0404900) were designed based on the P. falciparum 3D7 reference sequence using illustrator of biological sequences (IBS) software (version 1.0.2; https://ibs.biocuckoo.org/)59. The predicted molecular weights with the 8 × His tag was about 40.1 kDa for Pf12 and 47.0 kDa for Pf41 (Fig. 2A and B; black bars). The constructs included all non-synonymous mutation sites identified from field isolates, enabling the screening of antigenic variation driven by sequence polymorphisms. Both proteins were expressed in a HEK293-6E cell-based expression system. Codon-optimized gene fragments encoding Pf12 and Pf41 were amplified using specific primer sets (Table S1) and subcloned into the pTT5 vector containing an 8 × His tag using the In-Fusion HD Cloning kit (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). The resulting plasmids were transfected into HEK293-6E cells using linear polyethyleneimine (PEI). Five days post-transfection, culture supernatant was collected, and secreted proteins were purified using Ni-NTA agarose (QIAGEN). Elution was performed with a buffer containing 300 mM imidazole, 50 mM HEPES, 5% glycerol, and 163 mM NaCl. Purified proteins (1 μg each) were mixed with 4× reducing buffer and resolved by SDS-PAGE on a 4-12% gradient Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gel (Invitrogen). Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Sigma-Aldrich) staining was used to confirm the integrity and overall quality of the purified proteins.

Antigenicity screening using protein microarrays

A total of 172 plasma samples from P. falciparum-positive asymptomatic individuals in Tanzania and 64 plasma samples from healthy controls under the age of 10 living in malaria non-endemic areas of the Republic of Korea during general health check-ups at Kangwon National University Hospital were tested for reactivity against Pf12 and Pf41 using protein microarray analysis. Aminopropyl-coated slides were prepared as previously described60. Recombinant Pf12 and Pf41 proteins were spotted separately onto individual wells of the amine-coated slides at optimized concentrations of 12.5 ng/µL for Pf12 and 25.0 ng/µL for Pf41, followed by incubation at 37℃ for 2 hours. Slides were then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS-T (0.1% Tween-20) for 1 hour at 37℃. Plasma samples were diluted 1:50 in PBS-T and applied in duplicate to each well, followed by incubation at 37℃ for 1 hour. Bound IgG antibodies were detected using Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (10 ng/µL; Invitrogen), and fluorescence signals were acquired using the InnoScan 300 microarray scanner (INNOPSYS, Carbonne, France). Cut-off values for positive reactivity were determined by adding three standard deviations to the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the healthy control group.

Pairwise relationship visualization

To explore the pairwise relationships among parasitological, immunological, and demographic variables, a pairwise scatter plot matrix was constructed using the pair plot function from the Seaborn library in Python 361. Variables included in the analysis were log10-transformed parasitemia (parasites/μL), age (year), and antigen-specific antibody responses measured as MFI against Pf12 and Pf41 antigens. Data points were stratified by village and color-coded accordingly to facilitate visualization of potential geographic clustering. Kernel density estimates (KDEs) were plotted along the diagonal axes to represent the marginal distributions of each variable. Scatter plots in the lower triangular matrix illustrated bivariate relationships, while the upper triangular matrix was either left blank or used for supplemental visualization, depending on the analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (version 8; https://www.graphpad.com/) (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). MFI values were log10-transformed prior to analysis. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Unpaired Student’s t-tests were used for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for data that did not follow a normal distribution. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was calculated to assess the association between antigenicity and clinical parameters, including age and parasitemia (parasites/µL). All statistical tests were two-tailed. For multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, including in correlation analyses where multiple tests were performed. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI).
