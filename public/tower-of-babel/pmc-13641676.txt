Precision pathophysiology in steatotic liver disease
Authors: Wonseok Lee, Da Kyung Hwang, Hyun Young Kim, David A. Brenner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Steatotic liver disease (SLD) comprises metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD), and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), which represent subclasses of liver disorders with overlapping etiologies. MASLD is defined as SLD with cardiometabolic dysfunction, whereas MetALD refers to MASLD with moderate alcohol consumption (140–350 g/week in females and 210–420 g/week in males). Despite being classified as distinct entities, MASLD and MetALD exhibit substantial phenotypic overlap, underscoring the need to delineate their pathological and molecular features and to develop models that capture the synergistic effects of alcohol and metabolic stress. Recent advances in multi-omic technologies have enabled integrated single-cell profiling of genetic, epigenetic, spatial, and proteomic features, providing high-resolution insights into cellular heterogeneity and disease mechanisms. In this review, we examine the pathophysiological landscape of SLD, highlight key distinctions between MASLD and MetALD, and discuss experimental models, including human liver spheroids. These approaches provide deeper insights into disease classification and accelerate the development of targeted therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Steatotic liver disease (SLD) constitutes a broad spectrum of disease characterized by excessive lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. SLD is diagnosed through histological examination or imaging techniques and has multiple potential etiologies. It is sub-classified into metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD), alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), and cryptogenic SLD [1]. MASLD, formerly named nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, is the most common chronic liver disease around the world, affecting over 30% of the global population. MASLD is defined as SLD in the presence of one or more cardiometabolic risk factors in the absence of excessive alcohol intake. Alcohol consumption is a major cause of hepatic steatosis, as prolonged heavy drinking leads to chronic liver damage known as ALD. MetALD represents a distinct SLD subtype with substantial phenotypic overlap between MASLD and ALD, highlighting the clinical reality that metabolic dysfunction and alcohol consumption often coexist, and their synergistic impact on disease progression [2].

Recent advances in multi-omics approaches have enabled the identification of genetic and epigenetic variations associated with MASLD risk, while providing a comprehensive view of the cellular landscape through transcriptomics and functional insights from proteomic and metabolomic analyses. Single nucleus multi-omic technologies provide simultaneous access to multiple molecular layers within individual cells. In combination, these approaches offer a powerful means to interrogate complex tissues composed of heterogeneous cell types and phenotypic states with distinct regulatory landscapes. These approaches reveal novel signaling pathways, transcription factors (TFs), and gene regulatory networks that drive SLD progression, providing a high-resolution molecular understanding of the SLD spectrum.

MASLD ranges from simple steatosis (5% or more fat accumulation in hepatocytes) to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and ultimately fibrosis. This progression is driven by a complex interplay among hepatocytes, which serve as the primary site of lipid accumulation, myeloid cells that drive inflammation, and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), the main source of collagen-producing myofibroblasts.

In this review, “precision pathophysiology” refers to a mechanism-based framework for understanding the heterogeneity of SLD across genetic, molecular, cellular, and metabolic layers. Rather than simply summarizing shared pathogenic processes such as steatosis, lipotoxicity, inflammation, and fibrosis, this approach emphasizes disease- driving features that distinguish SLD subtypes. Accordingly, the SLD spectrum is classified into MASLD and MetALD, and MASLD is further subdivided into simple steatosis (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver, MASL) and MASH with significant fibrosis. We discuss genetic variants, multi-omics signatures, and cellular pathways that contribute to disease progression and subtype differentiation. Although MASLD and MetALD share overlapping pathogenic mechanisms, integration of multi-omics and cellular-level information may provide a basis for more precise disease stratification and therapeutic targeting.

MULTI-OMIC STUDIES ON HUMAN SLD LIVERS

Genomics

Genome-wide association studies in patient cohorts with MASLD and its advanced subtype, MASH, have identified common genetic loci associated with susceptibility to SLD [3]. However, the majority of these risk variants are located in non-coding regions, limiting direct interpretation of their functional consequences [4]. To bridge this gap, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based analyses integrated with expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) mapping have emerged as critical approaches for linking disease-associated variants to downstream gene expression [5]. eQTL analyses have identified target genes whose expression is regulated by specific genetic variants in a tissue-, cell type-, and disease-dependent manner, thereby providing mechanistic insight into how risk loci influence lipid metabolism and disease progression [6,7]. In particular, SNP variants in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and MBOAT7 display the strongest and most consistent associations with disease risk [8,9], with extensive in vitro and in vivo studies providing functional validation of their pathogenic roles (Fig. 1).

Patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 3 (PNPLA3) is a robust genetic determinant of both SLD and ALD [8,10]. The highly prevalent PNPLA3 rs738409 missense variant p.I148M causes an isoleucine-to-methionine substitution at residue 148 and promotes hepatic lipid accumulation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, thus increasing the risk of liver disease progression [11,12]. In hepatocytes, PNPLA3 (I148M) located on the surface of lipid droplets inhibits ATGL-mediated triglyceride hydrolysis, predisposing cells to lipotoxic stress and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, and ultimately promoting macrophage recruitment and fibrogenesis [13]. In fact, liver samples from patients with the PNPLA3 I148M genotype show increased periportal infiltration and upregulated immune cell–associated gene expression [14].

Transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2) variants are associated with an increased risk of advanced fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in SLD. TM6SF2 is predominantly expressed in the endoplasmic reticulum and the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment. Its protein-altering variant, TM6SF2 p.E167K (c.449C>T; rs58542926), impairs TM6SF2 function, notably by reducing hepatic very low-density lipoprotein secretion from hepatocytes, thereby exacerbating hepatic steatosis [15,16]. The severity of hepatic steatosis associated with TM6SF2 variants correlates with fibrogenesis. For example, circulating levels of the N-terminal propeptide of type III procollagen (PRO-C), a biomarker of fibrosis, are significantly elevated in carriers of the TM6SF2 rs58542926 mutant allele [17].

Membrane-bound O-acyltransferase domain–containing protein 7 (MBOAT7) variants in patients with MASLD are associated with an increased risk of liver cancer, particularly in non-cirrhotic individuals [18]. MBOAT7 mediates arachidonic acid incorporation into phosphatidylinositol (PI), and its loss in hepatocytes alters PI composition while increasing triglyceride synthesis through non-canonical pathways [19]. Unlike PNPLA3 I148M and TM6SF2 E167K, the MBOAT7 rs641738 variant is a non-coding polymorphism located in the 3´-UTR that influences disease risk through reduced gene expression rather than protein structure alteration [20].

Variants in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and MBOAT7 represent shared genetic risk factors for MASH and MetALD. Although variants in alcohol-metabolizing enzymes, such as ADH1B and ALDH2, influence ethanol metabolism [21], genetic determinants specifically driving alcohol-induced liver injury remain largely undefined. Furthermore, only limited numbers of SLD-associated variants have been rigorously validated, and substantial numbers of loci remain functionally unexplored. Current eQTL analyses are hepatocyte-biased due to both the central role of hepatocytes in disease pathogenesis and the reliance on bulk liver tissue, leading to underrepresentation of variant regulatory effects in non-parenchymal cell types such as HSCs. Cell-state–interacting eQTL (ieQTL) analysis is a recently developed approach that integrates cell-state information derived from single-cell profiling to detect interactions between genetic variants and transcriptional regulation. By capturing state-specific gene regulatory programs, ieQTL analysis helps overcome the limited resolution of conventional eQTL approaches [7].

Transcriptomics & epigenomics

Single-cell studies of human livers have largely focused on gene expression profiling in relatively few patients [22-25]. An integrated single-cell and spatial atlas of normal and MASLD livers was generated from a cohort of 86 donor livers (manuscript under review). Using multiple single-cell assays, the authors profiled gene expression, chromatin accessibility, two histone modifications (H3K27ac and H3K27me3), and 3D genome architecture in human livers classified as normal, simple steatosis, MASH, and MASH with a history of alcohol consumption (MetALD) [26]. This multiplexed single-nucleus epigenomic approach enabled direct comparisons across disease states.

Importantly, this atlas demonstrates that although HSCs are central to fibrosis, the progression of SLD is driven by a coordinated multicellular reprogramming across hepatocytes, immune cells, and endothelial cells. The most prominent changes in the liver cell types across MASLD states were found in HSCs, which increase in abundance in high-fibrosis MASH, reflecting their activation in fibrosis as a major cause of MASH-associated changes. In MASH livers, HSCs show increased activity of proliferating signals—such as Hedgehog and Ras signaling—along with upregulation of fibrosis-related processes, including fiber formation and matrix metalloproteinase activity [26]. HSCs act as a hub of cell-cell interaction in liver disease, and recent analysis highlights that intercellular communication networks composed of HSCs, endothelial cells, and immune cells, together with their autocrine interaction, are critical for disease progression [27]. Within this network, the capillarization of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells [28] and the emergence of TREM2+ lipid-associated macrophages create a specialized pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrogenic niche that primes HSCs for activation [22]. In addition to these intercellular interactions, HSC-intrinsic autocrine signaling also contributes to disease progression. For example, an HSC-specific receptor-ligand autocrine loop mediated by neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 3 (NTRK3)–neurotrophin 3 contributes to MASH progression, whereas antagonism of NTRK3 inhibits HSC fibrogenicity and migration [27].

HSCs exhibit unique plasticity, transitioning from a quiescent state to an activated phenotype in response to liver injury [29]. These phenotypic transitions are governed by epigenetic mechanisms that reshape DNA modifications and chromatin architecture, thereby regulating transcription factor binding and gene expression (Fig. 2). HSC gene expression is regulated through the interplay between lineage-specific, cluster-specific and signal-specific TFs [30]. Transcription factor–mediated gene regulatory interactions were assessed by motif enrichment analysis using Find Individual Motif Occurrences [26,30]. Lineage-determining TFs are enriched in specific cell types, bind enhancers in a cell type–specific manner, and their deletion can lead to the acquisition of alternative cellular fate [31]. ETS1/2, IRF1/IRF2, and GATA4/6 are putative HSC lineage-specific TFs that maintain the quiescent phenotype, while JUNB, AP-1, TEAD, and SMAD have been described as lineage-specific TFs associated with the fibrogenic HSC phenotype [32,33]. Cluster-specific TFs are a group of TFs that are selectively upregulated in functionally distinct HSC subpopulations [30]. RUNX1/2 binding sites are most enriched in candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) associated with activated HSCs, whereas LHX1/2 and FXR are linked to quiescent HSCs. Some TFs are involved in transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)-mediated fibrogenic responses and are classified as signal-dependent TFs [34-38]. These TFs co-regulate multiple MASH-specific genes and drive HSC activation [34-36].

It is proposed that activation of HSCs is governed by small epigenetic/transcriptional changes in the activity of lineage-determining TFs that are tightly controlled by cluster-specific TFs, and locally regulated by signal-specific TFs.32 For example, expression of activated HSC genes specifically upregulated in MASH conditions, such as SERPINE1, is driven by subtle epigenetic and transcriptional shifts in lineage-determining TF activity, particularly increased JUNB/AP-1 activity relative to ETS activity. This process is tightly controlled by cluster-specific TFs, including activation-associated RUNX1/2 over quiescence-linked regulators such as LHX1/2 and FXR, and is locally modulated by signal-specific TFs [30].

In a cohort-scale single-cell multi-omic analysis in which MASL, MASH, and MetALD were each compared with normal livers, MetALD livers exhibited a greater number of differentially regulated genes and cCREs than MASH livers [26]. Given that both MASH and MetALD arise from metabolic injury, with MetALD exhibiting increased hepatic inflammation and fibrosis due to chronic alcohol consumption, their molecular similarities and differences were assessed by single-cell analysis. MetALD HSCs were enriched for cell–extracellular matrix interaction pathways, whereas MASH HSCs exhibited enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation, RAS processing, translation, and metabolism [26]. Consistent with these results, a recent AI-based analysis revealed increased collagen deposition in periportal and zone 2 regions in MetALD and ALD compared with MASLD [39].

Joint single-nucleus profiling of gene expression and histone modifications (H3K27ac and H3K27me3) using droplet-based Paired-Tag in pooled samples from 86 donors identified four major chromatin states: active (ATAC-seq+/H3K27ac), open (ATAC-seq), repressive (H3K27me3), and weakly active [26]. Notably, hepatocytes from fibrotic MASH livers exhibited a greater number of differentially active H3K27ac and H3K27me3 features compared to the other cell types. These hepatocyte-specific alterations often involve the loss of H3K27ac at enhancers for metabolic master regulators such as HNF4A and PPARα, leading to impaired lipid handling and a collapse of metabolic zonation [40]. In hepatocytes from nonfibrotic livers, genes overlapping regions with altered H3K27me3 activity were enriched in distinct pathways, showing increased repression of lipid metabolism, transport, and Wnt signaling, with reduced repression of cell structure, adhesion, extracellular matrix interaction, and cytokine signaling pathways, consistent with their activation [26]. HSC activation has also been associated with epigenomic alterations, such as changes in DNA methylation and histone acetylation (Fig. 2) [41]. DNA methylation (5-mC/5-hmC) in HSCs promotes cellular activation and fibrogenesis [42]. Histone lactylation may also facilitate HSC activation by promoting fibrogenic gene expression under high-lactate conditions, linking metabolic reprogramming to epigenetic control of fibrogenesis [43].

Metabolomics and proteomics

By profiling metabolic intermediates and end products, metabolomics provides a direct functional readout of cellular phenotype. MAT1A expression is commonly reduced in MASH and impairs the synthesis of S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe), the primary methyl donor in the liver. Genetic deletion of MAT1A in mice increased hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. Metabolomic analyses have indicated that its loss disrupts both SAMe-dependent pathways—including DNA and phospholipid methylation, polyamine synthesis, and glutathione production—and one-carbon metabolism by altering the flux of methionine, threonine, serine, and glycine into folate-dependent pathways [44]. In MASLD, a disease driven by multi-cellular and multi-organ interactions, this positions metabolomics as a powerful tool for risk prediction. Urinary metabolomic analysis of 30 healthy controls, 33 MASLD patients (steatosis with normal transaminase levels), and 45 MASH patients (steatosis with elevated transaminases) identified a panel of 31 metabolites that distinguished MASLD from MASH, including 3-indoleacetic acid, L-carnitine, pyroglutamic acid, and indolelactic acid [45]. Untargeted metabolomic analysis of stool samples (103 metabolites) from 19 healthy controls and 16 MASLD, 26 MASH, and 19 cirrhosis patients revealed that short-chain fatty acid levels were significantly reduced in MASLD, whereas cholic acid levels were increased. In contrast, palmitoylcarnitine and L-carnitine levels were significantly elevated in MASH and cirrhosis [46]. Serum metabolomic analysis of patients with MASLD (n=161) and healthy controls (n=149) identified 24 differentially abundant metabolites: 16 were upregulated in MASLD, including arachidonic acid, DHEA, and indoxyl sulfate, whereas 8 were significantly downregulated [47].

MetALD is defined by the presence of MASLD in association with increased alcohol consumption, resulting in both cardiometabolic and alcohol-related pathological features [48]. In a recent cohort study, proteomic profiling enabled stratification of MetALD patients based on molecular resemblance to either ALD or MASLD, with corresponding differences in clinical outcomes. A 10-protein model (MAMDC4, SSC4D, CEACAM16, CHI3L1, GGT1, C4BPB, CDHR5, OXT, FCAMR, and ADAM22) exhibited distinct patterns across disease groups and was significantly associated with alcohol consumption. This protein panel captured key biological pathways that distinguished ALD from MASLD. Notably, alcohol-predominant MetALD was associated with worse clinical outcomes than those of cardiometabolic-predominant MetALD [49].

DISTINCT PATHOGENIC PATHWAYS OF MASLD AND METALD

Despite distinct clinical definitions, MASLD and MetALD share a common pathogenic framework due to the overlap in underlying metabolic dysfunction and hepatic stress responses. Currently, no established pathological marker reliably distinguishes MASH from MetALD. However, some of their molecular mechanisms diverge in their upstream drivers and injury patterns. Recent findings demonstrate that MetALD patients present a higher risk of liver disease progression and mortality. In a large cohort of 4,683 MASLD patients (4,287 with pure MASLD and 396 with MetALD), both moderate and increased alcohol consumption were significantly associated with an increased risk of advanced fibrosis in males with MASLD [50]. In contrast to MASLD, MetALD incorporates ethanol-induced toxicity into this metabolic context, resulting in a combined metabolic–alcohol phenotype with distinct disease dynamics.

Chronic alcohol consumption induces CYP2E1 expression, which promotes lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and the generation of reactive intermediates, including 1-hydroxyethyl and lipid radicals [51]. CYP2E1 is predominantly expressed in hepatocytes within the pericentral zone. In a multicellular human liver spheroid system, the addition of ethanol to MASH-conditioned media further increased CYP2E1 protein expression [52]. Given the interspecies differences in CYP2E1, a humanized CYP2E1 mouse model was generated by expressing the human CYP2E1 gene under a promoter in a CYP2E1 knockout background to assess its role in alcohol-induced liver injury. In this model, intragastric alcohol feeding led to increased hepatic fibrosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress, while hepatic steatosis was attenuated, accompanied by reduced triacylglycerol levels [53].

Although the mechanisms underlying the higher mortality observed in MetALD require further investigation, several lines of evidence may help explain its distinct phenotype. CYP2E1 upregulation is not specific to ALD, as it can also occur in obesity, insulin resistance, MASLD/MASH, fasting, and other metabolic stress conditions. Therefore, more detailed approaches, such as isotope tracing, may be required to clarify the contribution of alcohol exposure to metabolic disease progression. In addition, ethanol-derived metabolites, such as acetaldehyde, and systemic consequences of alcohol exposure, including gut dysbiosis, may provide further clues to the biological distinction between MASH and MetALD [54].

Ethanol-fed mice exhibit more pronounced steatosis than pair-fed controls with matched caloric intake, indicating that alcohol-induced metabolic alterations drive steatosis independently of caloric load [55]. Stable isotope tracing demonstrated that ethanol significantly contributed to acetate and acetyl-CoA levels, accounting for up to 27%, 24%, and 18% of lipid carbon in newly synthesized fatty acids in plasma, liver, and cecum, respectively [56]. In contrast, isotope-labeled ethanol showed minimal incorporation into hepatic tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, indicating that orally administered ethanol was primarily converted to the acetate and acetyl-CoA pools, and was mainly used for de novo lipogenesis [56].

The gut–liver axis provides important mechanistic insights into the differences between MASLD and MetALD. Alcohol intake frequency is a strong driver of microbiota variance. In MASH, dysbiosis, increased intestinal permeability, and altered microbial metabolites are mainly driven by obesity, diet, and chronic metabolic inflammation [57]. In MetALD, ethanol disrupts tight junction integrity, promotes small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and increases translocation of endotoxin and other microbial products into the portal circulation, which enhances Kupffer cell activation and toll-like receptor 4-mediated inflammatory signaling [58]. As a result, alcohol-related disturbances of the gut–liver axis may potentiate the metabolic dysbiosis already present in MASH and may contribute to a stronger inflammatory and fibrogenic response in MetALD. Moreover, ethanol exposure promotes acetate dissimilation, leading to shifts in gut microbial composition driven by elevated acetate levels [56].

In MetALD-associated fibrosis, alcohol-related factors can provide additional profibrogenic stimuli [59]. Acetaldehyde can directly promote collagen production, enhance TGF-β signaling, and stabilize extracellular matrix [60]. In parallel, alcohol-induced upregulation of cytochrome P450 enzymes, particularly CYP2E1, increases oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, further stimulating HSCs through inflammatory and damage-associated signals [61]. Therefore, MetALD may be quantitatively more intense or progress more rapidly than MASH [39], reflecting the combined impact of metabolic stress and the alcohol-derived profibrogenic response.

REPRESENTATIVE MODELS FOR SLD SUBTYPES; MASL, MASH, AND METALD

Mouse models for MASL and MASH

Diet-induced rodent models remain the most extensively used experimental platforms for studying MASL and MASH [62]. Conventional high-fat diet (HFD) feeding induces hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and low-grade inflammation, therefore serving as a primary model of MASL [63]. Among diet-induced models, standard HFD regimens (typically 45–60% kcal from fat) are widely used because of their simplicity, reproducibility, and compatibility with genetic manipulation [64]. 12 weeks of HFD induces significant obesity, simple steatosis, and insulin resistance [65]. However, progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis and fibrosis is limited in HFD-fed mice and requires extended feeding durations [66].

Western diet-based models—broadly defined as dietary regimens enriched in fat, cholesterol, and refined carbohydrates—induce steatosis, hepatocellular injury, inflammation, and fibrosis [67]. Although the precise composition of Western diets varies across studies, these models recapitulate key histologic and molecular features of human metabolic disease more closely than do HFD models [68,69]. Western diet includes 40–60% kcal from saturated fat and 20–40% kcal from carbohydrate, together with high-fructose supplemented in drinking water [30]. Hepatic metabolism of fructose potently promotes de novo lipogenesis, intrahepatic lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, and hyperglycemia. In parallel, fructose suppresses mitochondrial β-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids and enhances triglyceride synthesis, promoting hepatic steatosis [66]. Administration of a Western diet for 24–32 weeks typically induces mild fibrosis (stages 1–2) with significant inflammation [70]. In Western diet models, ~0.2% (w/w) dietary cholesterol, which exceeds the physiological human intake—is used to accelerate MASH development, and an increase in dietary cholesterol of up to 2% further enhances disease progression [70,71].

To further accelerate disease progression, western diet feeding can be combined with low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) administration to induce pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and hyperglycemia [72]. Although this combination rapidly promotes steatohepatitis and fibrosis and is frequently used for preclinical efficacy testing, the non-physiological β-cell toxicity induced by STZ limits its relevance to the metabolic pathogenesis of human MASLD and MASH [73]. To accelerate fibrosis development, low-dose carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) can be combined with feeding a Western diet. For example, CCl₄ may be administered for 8 weeks following 8 weeks of Western diet-induced steatosis. This strategy enhances MASH progression, shortens induction time, and leads to more advanced fibrosis, including bridging fibrosis [74].

The amylin liver non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (AMLN) diet was developed as a high-fat, high-fructose, high-cholesterol diet to model MASH with robust and reproducible fibrosis. In comparison with the Western diet, the AMLN diet incorporates high fructose (~20% kcal) directly into the diet and contains ~2% (w/w) cholesterol [75]. The rationale for the higher cholesterol content is that mice absorb cholesterol less efficiently than humans [76]. This enrichment enhances inflammatory lipotoxicity and compensates for species-specific cholesterol handling to promote fibrogenesis in rodents [77]. AMLN diet models recapitulate the natural course of human MASH development, in which the mice become overweight before developing steatosis, followed by inflammation and fibrosis [78]. The Gubra–Amylin non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (GAN) diet is a widely adopted experimental model that reproducibly induces steatohepatitis and consistent fibrosis without the need for toxic or genetic manipulation [79]. Due to its robustness and reproducibility, GAN-based models are increasingly used for preclinical efficacy testing, although disease progression requires intermediate to long feeding durations [80].

Amino acid-deficient dietary regimens have been developed to induce steatohepatitis and fibrosis. Choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined and methionine- and choline-deficient diets robustly promote hepatic inflammation and fibrosis over short feeding periods, as deficiencies in choline and methionine impair very low density lipoprotein secretion and lead to hepatic lipotoxicity [81]. However, these models fail to induce obesity or insulin resistance and therefore do not recapitulate the metabolic context of human MASLD and MASH. Genetic models of obesity, including ob/ob and db/db mice, exhibit severe hepatic steatosis driven by leptin deficiency or resistance, but display limited inflammatory and fibrotic responses and, therefore, require additional dietary or injurious stimuli to model advanced MASH [72,82]. Foz/Foz mice with a mutation in the Alms1 gene are hyperphagic and develop hepatic steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma on a Western diet [69]. CCl₄ administration induces rapid and reproducible liver fibrosis through direct hepatotoxic injury and remains a widely used experimental model of hepatic fibrogenesis [83]. However, the absence of metabolic dysfunction restricts its utility for studying the pathogenesis of MASLD and MASH.

Consistent with these observations, a recent comprehensive analysis integrating histologic, metabolic, and transcriptomic features across multiple diet-induced models highlighted Western-style dietary paradigms, including AMLN- and GAN-type formulations, as the experimental systems most closely aligned with human MASH [70]. In contrast, nutrient-deficient and toxin-based models, while effective at inducing rapid fibrosis, diverged substantially from the metabolic and molecular signatures observed in patients (Table 1). These findings highlight the importance of using Western diet–based models optimized for metabolic context and inflammatory lipotoxicity in studies of MASH pathogenesis and in preclinical evaluations of therapeutic strategies targeting metabolically driven steatohepatitis and fibrosis [84]. To model fibrosis that develops in response to dietary intake, the GAN diet can be used, although it typically produces milder fibrosis, around stage 0.5–1.5. By contrast, simple fibrosis progression can be assessed using the CCl₄ administration model, which induces stage 2–3 fibrosis after approximately 6 weeks of intragastric administration [30,70].

In addition to dietary composition, environmental and biological factors can also affect the MASLD development in animal models. In research laboratories, mice are typically housed at 20–23°C, which is below their thermoneutral zone of 30–32°C, thereby inducing chronic cold stress, with an overall increase in energy expenditure and stress-driven production of corticosterone [85]. In contrast, housing at a thermoneutral temperature (30°C) was found to reduce serum corticosterone and exacerbate proinflammatory immune responses and HFD-induced MASLD pathogenesis [86]. Thus, thermoneutral conditions promote progression of steatohepatitis and fibrosis, and can improve the translational relevance of diet-induced MASH models [85].

Sex differences are another important factor to consider in diet induced MASLD mouse models. Premenopausal females are protected from metabolic liver disease in humans due to estrogen-driven pathways [87], but recent studies have reported that under certain dietary conditions, particularly in Western diet–based models, female mice can develop hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis that is comparable to —or even more severe than— that of males [88]. These discrepancies likely reflect species-specific differences in sex hormone signaling, adipose tissue distribution, systemic metabolism, and context-dependent interactions between diet and sex [89].

Mouse models for MetALD

MetALD results from the combined effects of metabolic stress and alcohol consumption, leading to liver injury that is more severe than that observed in MASLD or ALD alone [59,90]. To capture the synergistic interactions between metabolic and alcohol-related insults, experimental models of MetALD have been developed by integrating alcohol exposure and dietary regimens that induce metabolic dysfunction [91].

The Lieber–DeCarli (LDC) diet remains the most widely used experimental model of ALD and has provided fundamental insights into alcohol-induced steatosis, inflammation, and hepatocellular injury. This diet consists of 15% of total energy from protein and 36% from fat; the remaining energy is derived from carbohydrates or iso-calorically replaced by ethanol (~35.5–36%) [92,93]. The diet is further supplemented with essential vitamins, minerals, and fiber. In mice fed the LDC diet, the alcohol consumption leads to blood alcohol levels of 100–160 mg/dL, which are higher than those of mice given alcohol in drinking water [93]. However, this model lacks the metabolic context characteristic of MetALD [94]. In fact, the majority of hepatic fat accumulation in LDC-fed mice originates from dietary lipids contained in the diet [93]. Ad libitum feeding with the LDC diet for 4 weeks has been widely used, but induces only mild steatosis and slight elevations in serum alanine aminotransferase, with little or no liver inflammation. The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism model, which combines chronic ethanol feeding (10-day ad libitum LDC diet) with a single binge ethanol administration, synergistically induces liver injury, inflammation, and steatosis, but does not result in significant fibrosis [55].

An HFD diet is frequently combined with chronic ethanol administration to model metabolic–alcohol interactions in MetALD. These combined models induce hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and moderate fibrosis, reflecting both the additive and synergistic contributions of lipid overload and alcohol metabolism [95]. Variants incorporating binge ethanol exposure on a background of chronic HFD feeding further exacerbate liver injury and inflammatory responses, thereby modeling acute worsening in the context of chronic metabolic and alcohol exposure [96]. More recently, Western diet–based models combined with ethanol exposure have increasingly been adopted as experimental platforms with high translational relevance. By integrating excess fat, refined carbohydrates, and cholesterol with alcohol exposure, these models can induce severe steatohepatitis and advanced fibrosis, thereby closely recapitulating the histologic and metabolic features of human MetALD [97]. Importantly, these combined models demonstrate that metabolic stress and alcohol exposure interact synergistically rather than additively, resulting in more severe liver injury and fibrosis (Table 1).

Human liver spheroids to mimic MASH and MetALD

While animal models have been useful in defining key pathogenic mechanisms across the SLD spectrum, their translational relevance is constrained by species-specific differences in metabolism, immune responses, and fibrogenic pathways [98]. In fact, mouse models do not fully reproduce human MASH, as drugs that have improved MASH in mice (e.g., obeticholic acid, ASK1 inhibitor, LOXL2 inhibitor, and vitamin E) have failed in human trials [99]. To address these limitations, human-relevant in vitro systems, particularly multicellular liver spheroids, have emerged as complementary experimental platforms that enable more precise modeling of human MASL, MASH, and MetALD [100].

Three-dimensional spheroid cultures preserve essential features of human liver biology, including cell-type–specific metabolic programs, intercellular communication, and clinically relevant genetic backgrounds. Unlike conventional two-dimensional cultures, these systems support prolonged exposure to disease-relevant stressors and allow dynamic assessment of liver fibrosis progression and regression [52]. During MASH development, hepatocyte proportions decrease, whereas macrophage infiltration and HSC proliferation increase. Human liver spheroid systems can be designed to incorporate multiple liver cell types in disease-relevant compositions [52,101].

The human liver spheroid system has been successfully adapted to recapitulate distinct SLD subtypes through defined combinations of metabolic, inflammatory, and alcohol-related stressors. MASL-like states are typically induced by exposing organoids to lipid-rich and insulin-resistant conditions, such as excess free fatty acids (palmitic acid or oleic acid) and hyperinsulinemic or high-glucose environments, leading primarily to hepatocellular lipid accumulation with limited inflammatory and fibrogenic features [102]. Progression to MASH-like phenotypes is achieved by adding inflammatory and fibrotic stimuli onto a steatotic background. Human liver spheroids are incubated with fatty acids to generate hepatic steatosis and challenged with mediators, including TGF-β and lipopolysaccharide, resulting in hepatocellular injury, inflammatory signaling, and activation of fibrogenic pathways [52,103]. Importantly, these systems are not limited by species differences and thus provide a more accurate representation of human disease (Fig. 3).

Modeling MetALD in vitro requires incorporation of alcohol- related stress in addition to metabolic overload. In spheroid systems, MetALD phenotypes are induced by exposing the spheroids to MASH conditions supplemented with ethanol (100 mM) [52,104]. This combinatorial approach can recapitulate key features of metabolic–alcohol synergy, such as enhanced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory signaling, and enhanced activation of HSCs, thereby distinguishing MetALD from metabolically driven MASH within the same human experimental platform [105].

Human liver spheroid platforms offer substantial advantages for translational research. Patient-derived hepatic cells maintain donor-specific genetic and metabolic characteristics, which allow classification based on disease subtype, genetic variants, or therapeutic responsiveness [106]. As such, these systems provide valuable tools for preclinical drug screening, identification of responder and non-responder populations, and evaluation of therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, or fibrosis [107].

However, the human liver spheroid platform still has several limitations. These include the absence of vascular perfusion and hepatic zonation, limited immune complexity, and challenges in modeling chronic alcohol exposure [52]. MetALD models should reflect the combined effects of metabolic dysfunction and sustained alcohol exposure. Alcohol-associated liver injury typically arises in the centrilobular zone 3 region, where high CYP2E1 activity and relatively low oxygen levels increase vulnerability to ethanol-induced oxidative stress [108]. Because spheroids lack vascular perfusion and lobular architecture, they cannot fully reproduce hepatic zonation. In addition, as closed systems, they do not model the recruitment of bone marrow-derived immune cells [109], limiting their ability to reflect the immune complexity of chronic liver injury. Although 3D culture can extend spheroid maintenance to approximately 2–3 weeks, this duration may still be insufficient to fully recapitulate chronic liver disease progression [52]. Thus, models that recapitulate hepatic zonation and vascular perfusion are needed. Liver-on-a-chip systems may provide continuous media flow and tissue-like cellular architecture, and may, therefore, better reflect the physiological liver microenvironment [110].

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Recent advances in single-cell analysis have coincided with the development of integrated approaches for profiling genetic, epigenetic, spatial, and proteomic features at single-cell resolution, creating new opportunities to study distinct and minor cell populations. Within the SLD spectrum, molecular pathways and cellular interactomes that distinguish disease stages—including simple steatosis, MASH, and MetALD—will provide deeper insights into disease trajectories and therapeutic target development. HSCs showed the most prominent changes across MASLD states, with increased abundance in high-fibrosis MASH, highlighting their potential as targets for anti-fibrotic therapies.

Currently, no established pathological marker can reliably distinguish MASH from MetALD. Although the mechanisms underlying the higher mortality observed in MetALD remain to be fully elucidated, several lines of evidence may help explain its distinct phenotype. In a cohort-scale single-cell multi-omic study comparing MASL, MASH, and MetALD livers with normal livers, MetALD livers showed a greater number of differentially regulated genes and cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) than MASH livers [26]. However, because liver disease classification in this cohort was based on self-reported alcohol consumption history, there remains a critical need for multi-omic datasets with more accurate alcohol exposure assessment [111]. Identifying underreported alcohol consumption in patients presumed to have MASLD using alcohol biomarkers is essential [112]. Novel biomarkers such as phosphatidylethanol help distinguish MetALD from MASLD and facilitate the establishment of more reliable research cohorts.

The progress in anti-fibrotic target discovery and drug development relies on translational studies, which are limited by the availability of all human liver cells. Human liver spheroids have wide applications, and have been successfully used to study activity, intercellular interaction, signaling pathways, and disease-associated crosstalk between a variety of human liver cells. Thus, they may serve as a useful tool to link experimental and translational research. In particular, they preserve human-specific metabolic features, such as CYP2E1 activity, as well as donor-dependent genetic backgrounds. Mouse-derived spheroid systems are more readily scalable because of greater cell availability. Although they have limitations in translational relevance, they remain valuable for high-throughput target identification and drug screening because they allow shorter experimental timelines and easier genetic manipulation compared with conventional animal models.

Although changes in the metabolism of hepatocytes have been extensively studied in MASLD, our understanding of HSC metabolism in MASH is just beginning. Metabolic changes in HSCs include a switch to aerobic glycolysis, β-oxidation of lipids, and induction of glutaminolysis [32]. A greater understanding of HSC metabolism should result in new pathophysiological insights. As our understanding of HSC heterogeneity expands, the next step is to locate the different HSC phenotypes within the diseased liver. To that end, spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics will identify novel intercellular communications between different HSC phenotypes and other liver cells.

CONCLUSION

Precision pathophysiology offers a conceptual framework for understanding SLD heterogeneity by integrating genetic, molecular, cellular, and metabolic information. As MASLD and MetALD share overlapping but distinct pathogenic features, future studies should combine human multiomic datasets with physiologically relevant models to improve disease stratification and therapeutic targeting.
