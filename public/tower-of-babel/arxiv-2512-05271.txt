Robust forecast aggregation via additional queries
Rafael Frongillo, Mary Monroe, Eric Neyman, Bo Waggoner

Abstract
We study the problem of robust forecast aggregation: combining expert forecasts with provable accuracy guarantees compared to the best possible aggregation of the underlying information. Prior work shows strong impossibility results, e.g. that even under natural assumptions, no aggregation of the experts’ individual forecasts can outperform simply following a random expert ( 18 ) . In this paper, we introduce a more general framework that allows the principal to elicit richer information from experts through structured queries. Our framework ensures that experts will truthfully report their underlying beliefs, and also enables us to define notions of complexity over the difficulty of asking these queries. Under a general model of independent but overlapping expert signals, we show that optimal aggregation is achievable in the worst case with each complexity measure bounded above by the number of agents n n . We further establish tight tradeoffs between accuracy and query complexity: aggregation error decreases linearly with the number of queries, and vanishes when the “order of reasoning” and number of agents relevant to a query is ω ⁡ ( n ) \omega(\sqrt{n}) . These results demonstrate that modest extensions to the space of expert queries dramatically strengthen the power of robust forecast aggregation. We therefore expect that our new query framework will open up a fruitful line of research in this area.

Introduction

Aggregating distinct sources of information is a fundamental problem in statistics, machine learning, forecasting, and decision making.
We consider the problem of a principal aggregating forecasts from a set of experts into a single, provably-accurate forecast.
Traditional theoretical approaches often adopt specific parametric models of how agents acquire information, such as Gaussian signals with known covariance structures
(
24
;
15
)
.
For example, works have concluded that common aggregation techniques like linear or logarithmic pooling are optimal for certain information structures; intuitively, linear pooling is better for aggregating redundant information, whereas logarithmic pooling is better for independent sources
(
13
;
9
;
10
;
17
;
21
)
.
Unfortunately, these works all depend on narrow assumptions about the information structure. These assumptions may fail in practice, or it may not be possible to know which of these assumptions holds.

In the theoretical computer science literature, recent works of
1
and
18
offer an alternative:
robust aggregation
.
They instead develop worst-case bounds on the performance of a given aggregation rule, under some weak conditions on the information structure, such as informational substitutes.
These results are exciting as they do not rely on narrow assumptions about how information is obtained.
Unfortunately, the results are also quite pessimistic: in the worst case, aggregating forecasts from
n
agents is only a constant factor better than randomly picking one of their forecasts.
Moreover, these lower bounds are not driven by pathological examples, but appear to be an innate limitation of aggregation itself.
It may therefore seem impossible to design a general-purpose aggregation rule with favorable guarantees.

The key idea of this paper is a way around this impossibility:
change the types of forecasts
we collect from agents.
Instead of simply asking agents to predict the (expected value of the) quantity of interest, what if we ask them to predict its higher moments, or even predict what other agents will say?
We illustrate with the following striking example.

Example 1
.

There are
n
agents and
n+1
signals
X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1}
.
Each
X_{j}\sim\text{Normal}(0,1)
independently, and the principal aims to estimate
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}X_{j}
.
Signal
X_{n+1}
is a “common signal” observed by all agents.
Formally, each agent
i
observes the pair
\mathcal{S}_{i}=(X_{i},X_{n+1})
, and submits their posterior expectation
Y_{i}=X_{i}+X_{n+1}
.
According to a lower bound by
19
, there is no aggregation function of the forecasts
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
guaranteeing a nonvanishing improvement over the prior estimate of
0
.
In summary, this “shared common signal” setting is quite difficult under the standard approach.

But it turns out that by asking one additional question, we can optimally aggregate all information.
In addition to collecting
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
, we can simply ask any agent, say agent
1
, to predict the report of another agent, say agent
2
.
Then agent
1
’s answer is

\displaystyle Q\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{2}\mid S_{1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{2}+X_{n+1}\mid X_{1},X_{n+1}\right]=X_{n+1};

that is, agent
1
’s answer to this question reveals the common signal.
So given one extra higher-order question, we can calculate all of the agent’s private signals.
Namely,
X_{n+1}=Q
and
X_{i}=Y_{i}-X_{n+1}=Y_{i}-Q
.
Then we can compute the optimal aggregation,
A^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}X_{j}=Y
, with zero error.

The example above illustrates that, even with a single additional question, optimal aggregation is possible.
As we demonstrate in our results more generally, these additional forecasts, or “queries”, can drastically improve the quality of the aggregation.
Motivated by this example, our broad goal is to study the following question.

Central question:

What is the optimal worst-case aggregation error given complexity constraints on the queries?

To define complexity constraints, we introduce a framework of
DAG-elicitable query sets
.
Aside from constraining queries to “reasonable” ones, this framework has the nice benefit of guaranteeing that even strategic agents will answer their queries truthfully in equilibrium.
We introduce three notions of complexity for a DAG-elicitable query set to measure its usefulness in practice:

1.

query complexity
, the size of the query set;

2.

order complexity
, the “longest chain of reasoning” an agent must follow in order to answer the query; and

3.

agent complexity
, the maximum number of agents whose beliefs are relevant to any query.

Intuitively, while higher query complexity places more of a burden on the principal in collecting information from a large set of agents, higher order or agent complexity places a higher cognitive burden on experts in calculating their responses.

1.1
Results

In this paper, we study the central question above in the highly flexible yet simple “partial information” model due to
22
where agents each observe a large subset of independent signals.
Specifically, each nonempty subset of agents
T\subseteq[n]
observes a distinct random variable
X_{T}
, and the principal aims to estimate the variable
Y=\sum_{T}X_{T}
.

Our first result in §
3
is that one can achieve optimal error with DAG-elicitable queries.
Perhaps surprisingly, we find that optimal error is upper bounded by query, agent, and order complexity
n
; that is, we only need one query per expert to fully aggregate information.
A natural next question we address in §
4
is whether good performance is achievable under further restrictions on complexity measures.
First we study settings where query complexity is capped by
d\leq n
.
We completely characterize achievable error in this class, where the error is a worst-case ratio that measures the amount of randomness the aggregation scheme fails to account for in
Y
.

Theorem

(Informal)
.

If the principal is restricted to query complexity
d\leq n
, the best achievable error is
1-\frac{d}{n}
.

If we instead restrict agent and order complexity by
d<n
(e.g. in settings where agents are not able to conduct high-order reasoning about many others), the same linear upper bound is inherited.
However, with additional queries, we can characterize even tighter bounds on error.
Here we restrict to a class of “linear” aggregation rules, which are natural for our setting given the principal’s goal is to estimate the sum
\sum_{T}X_{T}
.

Theorem

(Informal)
.

If the principal is restricted to agent and order complexity
d<n
, the best achievable error under linear aggregation rules is:

1.

1-\Theta\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\right)
if
d=o(\sqrt{n})
;

2.

O\left(e^{\frac{-4d}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)
if
d=\omega(\sqrt{n})
.

That is, as long as agents are able to reason about
\omega(\sqrt{n})
other agents and conduct
\omega(\sqrt{n})
-order reasoning when answering queries, high-quality aggregation is possible in the worst case.

Our complexity bounds paint a more optimistic picture of worst-case aggregation capabilities than previous work.
Armed with a framework of additional queries, we find that low error rates can be achieved even when the principal is constrained to asking relatively simple queries.
Our results can moreover be interpreted from the perspectives of both elicitation and information theory.
We define a reasonable query scheme that is both (1) incentive compatible, meaning the principal is able to capture the correct information from experts; and
(2) expressive enough for the principal to recover most of the experts’ information within a single aggregated value.
By introducing a more general model of the questions that the principal may ask experts, our paper opens the floor for exciting future research to better understand the power and limitations of robust aggregation.

1.2
Related work

1
first introduced the problem of robust forecast aggregation, where two conditionally independent (or Blackwell-ordered) experts submit their posterior beliefs about a binary state space.
The authors characterize upper and lower bounds on an additive measure of error.

14
study a similar model when the principal has perfect knowledge of the marginal distribution over expert information, under restrictions on the correlation structure.

5

We note that each of these papers consider
probabilistic
forecasts over an event space, while we study aggregation of forecasts over real-valued quantities.

18
study robust forecast aggregation with multiple experts, and give tight bounds on a multiplicative measure of performance when expert signals satisfy a condition called
projective substitutes.

Their model restricts forecasts to posterior expectations.

11
construct an FPTAS for optimal robust aggregation (as measured by additive error) when the class of information structures is finite.
When the information structure is continuous, the authors also derive an approximation algorithm for the two-agent, binary state setting of
1
, where agents report their Bayesian posteriors.

The areas of peer prediction
6
and elicitation complexity
3
;
8
also explore how to elicit information by asking agents additional types of questions.
Other aggregation works have proposed asking additional/different questions, such as multiple guesses
24
, an implicit game
15
, or “confidence”
7
, but these works do not give robust aggregation guarantees and instead require narrow assumptions on the information structure.
The one exception to our knowledge is
20
, which applies peer prediction to robust aggregation, but in the simple setting of two experts and a binary outcome.

Preliminaries

2.1
Problem setting

There are
n
agents, called experts, with information relevant to a random variable
Y
of interest.
Throughout, we let
[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
There is a set
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m})
of
m
available signals, with the tuple
(Y,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
jointly distributed from a prior distribution
\mathcal{D}
that is known to all the experts.
Throughout this paper, we do not assume that the principal has any knowledge of
\mathcal{D}
.
Each expert
i
observes a subset
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.
For example, if each expert observes a distinct, single signal,
n=m

\mathcal{S}_{i}=(X_{i})
; if all information is shared,
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{X}
.
The posterior expectation of expert
i
, conditioned on their information, is
Y_{i}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]
.

A principal aims to output an
approximator

A\coloneqq A(\mathcal{D},X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
of
Y
.
Traditionally, the literature focuses on how a principal is able to aggregate the set of reports
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
.
For instance,
A=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
is an example of an approximator.
We consider a more general framework of queries.
Let
\mathcal{Q}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
denote a finite list of queries, where each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}
is a function
Q:(\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{D})\to\mathbb{R}
that maps expert
i
’s information to some real output.

We overload notation and let
Q=Q(\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{D})
also denote the random variable output of a query when the inputs are clear.
We write
\mathsf{agent}(Q)=i
to denote that the query
Q
was answered by expert
i
.

Definition 1
.

A (deterministic) aggregation rule
R=(\mathcal{Q},f)
is a tuple with
\mathcal{Q}
a list of queries and
f:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Q}}\to\mathbb{R}
a function from the query outputs to a real number.
A randomized aggregation rule is a distribution
P
, where a random context
Z\sim P
is drawn first from some sample space
\Omega
, determining the resulting aggregation rule
(\mathcal{Q}_{Z},f_{Z})
.
We denote
P_{\mathcal{Q}}
as the marginal distribution over query sets, and
P_{\mathcal{Q},f}
as the marginal distribution over aggregation rules.
An approximator
A
is
implemented
by a randomized aggregation rule
P
if
A=f_{Z}(\mathcal{Q}_{Z})
almost surely.

Example 2

(Random expert)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n}\}
, i.e. the query set corresponds to each expert’s expectation of
Y
; and let
P
draw an expert uniformly at random, i.e.
P=U([n])
where
U(S)
denotes the uniform distribution over set
S
.
Then for
i\sim P
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\{Y_{i}\}
, and
f_{i}(\mathcal{Q}_{i})=\mathcal{Q}_{i}
.
That is,
P
is a randomized aggregation rule which outputs a uniformly random expert’s expectation of
Y
.
We call
P
the “random expert” aggregation rule.

Note that we only allow ex-ante randomization over aggregation rules, so that e.g. the principal cannot condition on the realization of a subset of queries.

Error.

Given an approximator
A
, let
L(A)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[(A-Y)^{2}\right]
be the expected squared error of
A
.
The optimal aggregation of all information is denoted
A^{*}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{m}]
.
Because
A^{*}
is the Bayesian posterior expectation conditioned on all available information, the lowest possible expected error is
L(A^{*})
.

X_{j}
is observed by at least one agent, so that the agents’ pooled information determines
A^{*}
. Otherwise,
L(A^{*})

A baseline to compare algorithms against is
L(\mu)

\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y]

Y

Y
.
Following
18
, we define the worst-case error

of an approximator

to be

\mathsf{error}(A)\coloneqq\max_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{L(A)-L(A^{*})}{L(\mu)-L(A^{*})}.

That is, the error expresses the worst-case difference in information expressed by

versus
A^{*}
, normalized by the relative information in
A^{*}
.
We have
\mathsf{error}(\mu)=1

2.2
DAG-elicitable queries

As defined, an aggregation rule allows the principal to ask any set of queries.
However, in reality experts may not be incentivized to answer these queries truthfully according to their private information.
We are thus motivated to restrict admissable query sets in a way that guarantees agents will answer queries truthfully and accurately.
Moreover, we aim to define a framework that allows us to articulate the
complexity
of an approximator, given there may be many different aggregation rules that implement it.

Following the elicitation literature, we define a query function as elicitable if there is a payment scheme that incentivizes the expert to report the output truthfully.

Definition 2
.

A query
Q
is
elicitable
with respect to a set
\mathcal{R}
of real-valued random variables if there exists a payment function
\pi(r,\mathcal{R})
such that
Q(\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{D})=\arg\max_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\pi(r,\mathcal{R})\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]
.

For example, it is well-known that the quadratic score
\pi(r,Y)\coloneqq 1-(r-Y)^{2}
elicits the mean, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]=\arg\max_{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[1-(r-Y)^{2}\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]
, so that
Y_{i}
is elicitable with respect to
Y
.
But one could take this scheme a step further and elicit a query such as
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid\mathcal{S}_{j}]
, or expert
j
’s expectation of
i
’s expectation of
Y
, with the payment
\pi(r,Y_{i})\coloneqq 1-(r-Y_{i})^{2}
.
Then, since expert
i
is strictly incentivized to report truthfully, so is expert
j
.
We can thus generalize elicitability of a query set beyond simply scoring reports against
Y
as follows.

Definition 3
.

(DAG-elicitable)

A set of queries
\mathcal{Q}
is
DAG-elicitable
if we can arrange the queries into a directed acyclic graph
G=(V,E)
with
V\coloneqq\mathcal{Q}\cup\{Y\}
such that

1.

each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is elicitable with respect to the set
N_{G}(Q)
, where
N_{G}(Q)
is the set of out-neighbors of
Q
(which may include
Y
), and

2.

Y
is the unique sink.

If such a graph
G
exists, we say it DAG-elicits the query set
\mathcal{Q}
.

We will refer to an aggregation rule
R=(\mathcal{Q},f)
as DAG-elicitable if
\mathcal{Q}
is DAG-elicitable, and a randomized aggregation rule
P
as DAG-elicitable if each
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(P_{\mathcal{Q}})
is DAG-elicitable for
P_{\mathcal{Q}}
the marginal over query sets.
While the structure of a DAG-elicitable aggregation rule implies each expert’s payment depends on the reports of others, we can treat the rule as a one-shot mechanism and calculate all payments at once.
The following is then a straightforward observation.

Observation 1
.

Consider the Bayesian game induced by a DAG-elicitable aggregation rule and any graph
G
that DAG-elicits it: each agent has private information
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, submits a report
r_{i}:(\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{D})\to\mathbb{R}
, and receives payment
\pi(r_{i},N_{G}(Q))
.
Then it is a unique Bayes-Nash equilibrium for experts to report answers to each query truthfully, i.e.
r_{i}=Q(\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{D})
.

Consider a DAG-elicitable query set
\mathcal{Q}

1.

2.

2.3
Partial information model

The
partial information model
is the special case of the general information model, where the signals are independent mean-zero variables and
Y
is their sum.
Without loss of generality, we suppose there are
2^{n}
independent signals, each one corresponding to a particular subset of the experts.
Specifically, let
X_{T}\in\mathbb{R}
be an independent random variable drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{T}
for each subset
T\subseteq[n]
, where
\mu_{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[X_{T}]=0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[X_{T}^{2}]<\infty
.
For mathematical convenience we define
X_{\emptyset}=0
with probability one.
We assume there exists some
T\subseteq[n]
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})>0
; otherwise the aggregation problem is trivial.
The prior distribution
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{T}:T\subseteq[n])
is known to the experts.

Each random variable
X_{T}
is revealed only to the set of experts
T
.
That is, each expert
i
sees signal
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{X_{T}:i\in T\}
.
Here,
X_{\{i\}}
represents information private only to expert
i
, and
X_{[n]}
represents information that is shared across all experts.
Then the aggregator aims to estimate
Y=\sum_{T\subseteq[n]}X_{T}
.
(Example
1
is thus a special case of the partial information model.)

Note a forecaster
i
’s posterior expectation of
Y
,
Y_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]
, is simply the sum of their observed signals:

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{T\subseteq[n]}X_{T}\mid\{X_{T}:i\in T\}]=\sum_{T\subseteq[n]:i\in T}X_{T}.

Our results in this paper focus solely on the partial information model, which captures a multitude of settings where experts have overlapping information about the variable of interest.
We leave extensions to correlated signal structures as an exciting avenue for future work.

Error.

In the partial information model, the optimal approximator
A^{*}
satisfies
A^{*}=\sum_{T\subseteq[n]}X_{T}
, so that
L(A^{*})=0
.
It follows that we can write the error of an approximator
A
under the partial information model as

\mathsf{error}(A)=\max_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{L(A)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(Y)}=\max_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(A-\sum_{T}X_{T})^{2}\right]}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}.

(2)

That is,
\mathsf{error}(A)
measures the amount of randomness that
A
fails to account for in
Y
(as measured by variance), in the worst case over the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

3
Optimal Aggregation

We begin by asking if optimal aggregation is even possible in the DAG-elicitable framework, and if so, whether there are reasonable upper bounds on the complexity of aggregation rules that implement
A^{*}
.
It will be useful in this quest to start by defining a specific class of aggregation rules.

3.1
Linear aggregation rules

Given the goal of the principal is to estimate
Y=\sum_{T}X_{T}
, a natural class of approximators to consider are of the linear form
\sum_{T}\beta_{T}X_{T}
.
It turns out one can implement such functions using an DAG-elicitable class of rules we call the
linear aggregation rules
.

Definition 5
.

A
linear aggregation rule

R=(\mathcal{Q},f)
is an DAG-elicitable aggregation rule where, for some graph
G
that DAG-elicits
\mathcal{Q}
, each query
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
can be written as

Q=\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}Q^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]

for some set of constants
\{\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}\}_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}
;
and
f(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\beta_{Q}Q
for some set of real-valued constants
\{\beta_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
.

We define
P
as a
randomized linear aggregation rule
if
(\mathcal{Q},f)
is a linear aggregation rule for each
(\mathcal{Q},f)\in\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(P_{Q,f})
.

We note that implicit in the definition, each query
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is elicitable using the payment rule
\pi(Q,N_{G}(Q))=1-(Q-\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}Q^{\prime})^{2}
.
Moreover, using induction, we can show that any linear aggregation rule outputs a weighted linear combination of the
X_{T}
’s.

Proposition 1
.

Any linear aggregation rule outputs an approximator
A=\sum_{T}c_{T}X_{T}
for some set of constants
\{c_{T}\}_{T\subseteq[n]}
.

Proof.

We start by proving by induction that any linear query
Q
of order
d
in a linear aggregation rule can be expressed as
\sum_{T}c_{Q,T}X_{T}
for some constants
\{c_{Q,T}\}_{T\subseteq[n]}
.
Let
d=1
.
Then a query of order
d
can only be scored against the sink,
Y
.
That is, if
\mathsf{agent}(Q)=i
,
Q=\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{Q,Y}Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}\right]=\sum_{T\ni i}\alpha_{Q,Y}X_{T}=\sum_{T}c_{Q,T}X_{T}
for
c_{Q,T}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{i\in T}\alpha_{Q,Y}
.
Now, for
1\leq l\leq d
, assume any query
Q
of order
d
in the linear aggregation rule can be expressed as
Q=\sum_{T}c_{Q,T}X_{T}
.
Take any query
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
of order
d+1
with
\mathsf{agent}(Q)=i
, and consider a DAG
G
that DAG-elicits the rule.
Then

\displaystyle Q=\mathbb{E}\left[\textstyle\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}Q^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\textstyle\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{T}c_{Q^{\prime},T}X_{T}\right)\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{T\ni i}c_{Q^{\prime},T}X_{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{T}c_{Q,T}X_{T},

where the first equality follows by the inductive hypothesis; and
c_{Q,T}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{i\in T}\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in N_{G}(Q)}\alpha_{Q,Q^{\prime}}c_{Q^{\prime},T}
.

It follows that each query can be expressed as
Q=\sum_{T}c_{Q,T}X_{T}
, and therefore
f(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{Q}\beta_{Q}Q=\sum_{Q}\beta_{Q}\sum_{T}c_{Q,T}X_{T}=\sum_{T}c_{T}X_{T}
for
c_{T}\coloneqq\sum_{Q}\beta_{Q}c_{Q,T}
.
∎

Example 3
.

Consider the setting with three experts and three queries, indexed by each expert:
Q_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{1}]
,
Q_{2}(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{2}]
, and
Q_{3}(\mathcal{S}_{3},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{1}Q_{1}+\alpha_{2}Q_{2}\mid\mathcal{S}_{3}]
for
\alpha_{1}=-1,\alpha_{2}=2
; and
f(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q
.
Then as shown in Appendix
A.1
,
f(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{T\subseteq[n]}\beta_{T}X_{T}
with

\displaystyle\beta_{T}=\begin{cases}0,&T\cap\{1,2,3\}=\emptyset,\{3\},\{1,3\}\\
1,&T\cap\{1,2,3\}=\{1\},\{2\}\\
2,&T\cap\{1,2,3\}=\{1,2\}\\
3,&T\cap\{1,2,3\}=\{2,3\},\{1,2,3\}\end{cases}~.

3.2
Intersection queries

We now consider a specific class of linear aggregation rules which will be useful for achieving low error.
Generalizing the first-order queries
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]
, we can consider queries asked to expert
i
about their expectation of another expert
j
’s report.
Formally, we can ask for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{T\subseteq[n]:j\in T}X_{T}\mid\{X_{T}:i\in T\}]=\displaystyle\sum_{T\subseteq[n]:i,j\in T}X_{T}.

Intuitively,
i
knows that
j
’s expectation is the sum of the signals that
j
observes.
Of those,
i
also observes some, but the rest are independent and mean-zero and drop out.

We can then generalize this approach to higher orders.
Inductively, we define
iterated expectation queries
as follows:
\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i)\coloneqq Y_{i}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k}}\right]
for ordered list
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.
In other words,
\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
is agent
i_{k}
’s expectation of agent
i_{k-1}
’s expectation of …of agent
i_{1}
’s posterior expectation of
Y
.
We can immediately observe that in the partial information model, only the unordered set of indices
S=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
matters.

Proposition 2
.

Let
L\coloneqq(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.
Then

\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(L)=\sum_{T:S\subseteq T}X_{T}
for
S=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction over the length
k
of
L
.
Let
k=1
.
Then, by definition,
\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i)=\sum_{T\subseteq[n]:\{i\}\subseteq T}X_{T}
.
Now assume the statement holds for all ordered lists
L
with
1\leq|L|\leq k
.
Take an ordered list
L^{\prime}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1})
.
Then
\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{iter}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{T:\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq T}X_{T}\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}\right]=\sum_{T:\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1}\}\subseteq T}X_{T}
.

∎

In the context of the partial information model, then, we can define
intersection queries
as
\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(\{i\})\coloneqq Y_{i}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)\coloneqq\sum_{T:S\subseteq T}X_{T}
.
We define an
intersection query set
of order
d
as
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d)\coloneqq\{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S):|S|\leq d\}
.

Proposition 3
.

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d)
is DAG-elicitable, with
\mathsf{query\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))=2^{d}
,
\mathsf{order\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))\leq d
, and
\mathsf{agent\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))\leq d
.

Proof.

By construction, there is a graph
G
that DAG-elicits the query set, where each query
\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)
for
|S|=k
shares an edge with a query
\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S^{\prime})
for
|S|=k-1
.
Formally, if
k=1
and
\mathsf{agent}(Q)=i
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)=Y_{i}
, so that we can elicit
Q
with payment rule
\pi(\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(\{i\}),\mathcal{Q}\cup\{Y\})=1-(Q-Y)^{2}
.
Then we add an edge
(Q,Y)
in
G
.
Now, for
k>1
, consider query
Q=\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)
with
|S|=k
and
\mathsf{agent}(Q)=i
.
By definition,
\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S\backslash\{i\})\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}\right]
, so we can elicit
Q
with payment rule

\pi(\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S),\mathcal{Q}\cup\{Y\})=1-(Q-\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S\backslash\{i\}))^{2}
.
Then we add an edge
(Q,Q^{\prime})
in
G

Q^{\prime}=\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S\backslash\{i\})
.
It follows by induction that a query
Q=\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)
with
|S|=k
has order and agent complexity
k
in
G
, so that
\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell_{G}(Q)=d

\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}|I_{G}^{*}(Q)|=d

Moreover,
\mathsf{query\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))=|\mathsf{query\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))|=2^{d}
.

We define an
intersection aggregation rule
as an aggregation rule with an intersection query set and a linear aggregation function.

.

A (deterministic) intersection aggregation rule
R=(\mathcal{Q},f)
is an DAG-elicitable rule with

3.3
Optimal aggregation with intersection queries

Armed with intersection queries, we can now show that optimal aggregation is possible under the DAG-elicitable framework.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a deterministic intersection aggregation rule

3.4
Difference queries

We define another class of linear aggregation rules called
difference aggregation rules
.
Again, we consider some ordered list
L=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})
of agents.
Then we inductively define
difference queries
as follows:
\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i)\coloneqq Y_{i}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[Y-\sum_{1\leq j<k}\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-j})\mid\mathcal{S}_{k}]
.
In the partial information model, we can show that
\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
exactly expresses the expected value of
Y
conditioned on the
set difference
between
\mathcal{S}_{i_{k}}
and
\{\mathcal{S}_{i_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{k-1}
:

Proposition 4
.

Let
L\coloneqq(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.
Then
\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(L)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k}}\backslash\cup_{\ell<k}\mathcal{S}_{i_{\ell}}]
.

Proof.

We prove by induction over
k
that for
|L|=k
,
\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(L)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k}}\backslash\cup_{\ell<k}\mathcal{S}_{i_{\ell}}]
.
Let
k=1
.
Then
\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(L)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{1}}]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{1}}\backslash\emptyset]
.
Now, assume the statement holds for all ordered lists
L
with
1\leq|L|\leq k
.
Then consider an ordered list
L^{\prime}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1})
with
|L|=k+1
.
We have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}]-\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq k}\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}\right]-\sum_{1\leq j\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{j}}\backslash\cup_{\ell<j}S_{\ell_{j}}\right]\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\ni i_{k+1}}X_{T}-\sum_{1\leq j\leq k}\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T:\{i_{k+1},i_{j}\}\subseteq T,\\
i_{\ell}\notin T\;\forall\ell<j\end{subarray}}X_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T:i_{k+1}\in T,\\
i_{\ell}\notin T\;\forall\ell<k+1\end{subarray}}X_{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i_{k+1}}\backslash\cup_{\ell<k+1}S_{i_{\ell}}].

The fourth equality holds because each subset
T
satisfying
i_{\ell},i_{k+1}\in T
for some
\ell<k+1
must be covered exactly once in the right-hand sum.
That is,
T
it uniquely identified by the earliest index
\ell
such that
i_{\ell}\in T
.
∎

We define a
difference query set
over an ordered list
L\coloneqq(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
of agents as
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)\coloneqq\{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1}),\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1},i_{2}),\dots,\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\}.

We can then show that
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)
is DAG-elicited by an outdegree-1 DAG
G
, so that each of its complexity measures is bounded by
|L|
(see Appendix
A.1
for formal details).

Proposition 5
.

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)
is DAG-elicitable, with
\mathsf{order\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L))=|L|
,
\mathsf{order\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L))\leq|L|
,
\mathsf{agent\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L))\leq|L|
.

Definition 7
.

A (determistic) difference aggregation rule
R=(\mathcal{Q},f)
is an DAG-elicitable rule with
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)
for some ordered list of agents
L
, and
f(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\beta_{Q}Q
for some set of real-valued constants
\{\beta_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
.
We define
P
as a
randomized difference aggregation rule
if
(\mathcal{Q},f)
is a difference aggregation rule for each
(\mathcal{Q},f)\in\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(P_{Q,f})

3.5
Optimal aggregation with difference queries

In Theorem
1
, we showed that optimal aggregation is achievable with query complexity
2^{n}
.
While it seems plausible that the principal would need all
2^{n}
queries to fully aggregate
Y
, given there are
2^{n}
random variables embedded in its sum, we find that one can actually decrease query complexity of optimal aggregation significantly.
Intuitively, difference queries allow us to ask each expert what information they would
add
to the current estimation.
By induction over difference queries up to expert
n
, this approach allows us to fully re-construct
Y
with only
n
queries.

Theorem 2
.

There exists a deterministic difference aggregation rule
R
that implements
A^{*}
with
\mathsf{agent\text{-}c}(R)\leq n
,
\mathsf{order\text{-}c}(R)\leq n
, and
\mathsf{query\text{-}c}(R)=n
.

Proof.

Consider the DAG-elicitable query set
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)
for
L=(1,2,\dots,n)
, which by Proposition
5
is of agent and order complexity at most
n
, and query complexity
n
.
Let
Q_{i}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}(1,2,\dots,i)
, so that
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)=(Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots,Q_{n})
.
By Proposition
4
,

Q_{i}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}\backslash\cup_{j<i}\mathcal{S}_{j}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T:i\in T,\\
j\notin T\;\forall j<i\end{subarray}}X_{T}.

Since each
Q_{i}
collects exactly those
X_{T}
whose smallest index in
T
is
i
, and every subset
T\subseteq[n]
corresponds to a some unique smallest element
i
, the family of queries
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
partition and cover the
X_{T}
’s, and we have

A=\sum_{i\in[n]}Q_{i}=Y
.
It follows immediately that
\mathsf{error}(A)=0
with probability one.

∎

4
Approximate aggregation

Since we have shown that optimal aggregation is possible with agent, order, and query complexity
n
, a logical next step is to ask if good error rates are still achievable with lower complexity values.
In this section, we restrict approximators to those achievable by aggregation rules of (1) limited query complexity or (2) limited agent and order complexity.
We then give upper and lower bounds on error in both regimes.

4.1
Bounded query complexity

One can imagine a principal is constrained to only sample a small number
d<n
of queries from the experts when attempting to pool information.

Formally, let
\mathcal{A}_{d}
denote the set of approximators
A
that are implented by DAG-elicitable, randomized aggregation rules restricted to query complexity at most
d
.
While it seems plausible that the principal has no hope in achieving good approximations under limited query complexity, we find in Theorem
3
a clean linear relationship between
d
and achievable error.

Theorem 3
.

Let
d<n
. Then

\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}\mathsf{error}(A)=1-\frac{d}{n}
.

That is, each additional query reduces the best achievable error by
1/n
, and thus carries the same informational value.
This result is therefore optimistic, in that the principal is guaranteed steady, predictable gains over additional queries.
On the other hand, if the principal is constrained to a small number of queries, there are no early exponential improvements in adding one or two more queries to the set.

We leave the proof to Appendix
A.2
and give a sketch here.
To prove Theorem
3
, we first show that
1-\frac{d}{n}
is a lower bound on error for approximators in
\mathcal{A}_{d}
.
Specifically, consider an adversarial distribution
\mathcal{D}
which puts all variance on the singleton variables
\{X_{\{i\}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Applying the minimax inequality, we allow the principal to see
\mathcal{D}
first and use a deterministic aggregation rule to implement an approximator.
Since these aggregation rules must be of query complexity at most
d
, they must also be of agent complexity at most
d
.
Even if the principal can see
\mathcal{D}
first, the best they can do is take the expected sum of singleton set variables
\{X_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in S}
for some set
S
of agents with size at most
d
.
It follows that the variance of the
n-d
remaining singleton variables under
\mathcal{D}
induce an error of
1-\frac{d}{n}
.

We then show there exists an approximator
A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
that achieves the lower bound.
This approximator is implemented by a randomized difference aggregation rule: specifically, we sample a subset
S
of agents with size
d
uniformly at random, and then sum up the corresponding difference queries over
S
(mapped to an arbitrary ordered list) to recover the estimator
\sum_{T:T\cap S\neq\emptyset}X_{T}
.
An adversary’s best response is then to concentrate all variance on singleton variables
\{X_{\{i\}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, since those variables are the most likely not covered by the set of queried experts
S
.
The approximator’s error then corresponds to
\Pr[\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\}\cap S=\emptyset}=1]={n-1\choose d}/{n\choose d}=1-\frac{d}{n}
.

4.2
Bounded agent and order complexity

Since agent and order complexity put more burden on the experts to calculate complicated query responses, we also aim to understand how limiting these complexity measures to realistic magnitudes affect worst-case error.
Because query complexity limits both agent and order complexity, an immediate corollary of Theorem
3
is that approximators of limited agent and order complexity inherit
1-\frac{d}{n}
as an upper bound on achievable error.
We can then ask whether the principal is able to do any better when they are not constrained by query numbers.

Throughout the section, we restrict attention to randomized
linear
aggregation rules.
We believe it is reasonable to study this class under the partial information model, since we previously showed in Proposition
1
that any approximator implemented by linear aggregation rules is a linear function
\sum_{T}\beta_{T}X_{T}
, as is the random variable
Y
the principal aims to estimate.
We denote
\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}
as the set of approximators implementable using randomized linear aggregation rules restricted to agent and order complexity at most
d
.
We also let

be the set of polynomials of degree at most

.

Our main result indeed improves on the error bounds of Theorem

in the limited agent and order complexity case.
We characterizes bounds on the best achievable error under approximators in

, where there are three distinct asymptotic regimes.

Theorem 4
.

We obtain the following bounds across different regimes of

:

•

If

\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)=1-\Theta\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\right)

•

d=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)\in(0,1)
.

•

If
d=\omega(\sqrt{n})
,
\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)=O\left(e^{\frac{-4d}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)
.

4.3.2
Minimax error and Chebyshev polynomials

Now that we have pared down the space of aggregation rules that implement approximators in
\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}
significantly, we can directly express the value of the lowest achievable error.
Surprisingly, in Lemma

, we show that the best achievable error corresponds exactly to a simple polynomial approximation problem.
Specifically, the error is the minimum achievable infinity norm on a finite set of integer points, taken over all polynomials of degree at most
d
that satisfy one linear constraint.

Lemma 4
.

\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}p(t)^{2}.

(4)

Proof.

By Lemmas
2
and
3
, we only need to consider
symmetric
, deterministic intersection aggregation rules of order
d
that implement approximators
A
, or equivalently vectors
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with each
\beta_{t}
corresponding to subsets of size
t\in[d]
.
That is,
f_{\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))=\sum_{S:|S|\leq d}\beta_{|S|}\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(S)=\sum_{S:|S|\leq d}\beta_{|S|}\sum_{T\supseteq S}X_{T}=\sum_{T}\left(\sum_{S\subseteq T,|S|\leq d}\beta_{|S|}\right)X_{T}.

(We note that adding a nonzero constant to
f_{\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))
only increases the worst-case error.)
Denote
p(T)=\sum_{S\subseteq T,|S|\leq d}\beta_{|S|}
as the new coefficient for
X_{T}

Then

p(T)=\sum_{S\subseteq T,|S|=1}\beta_{1}+\sum_{S\subseteq T,|S|=2}\beta_{2}+\ldots+\sum_{S\subseteq T,|S|=d}\beta_{d},

or, if we denote

instead as a function over the

p(|T|)=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d}\beta_{k}{|T|\choose k}.

In other words,
p
is a polynomial of degree
d
over the size of

(with no degree 0 term), so that

f_{\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))=\sum_{T}p(|T|)X_{T}.

It follows the error of an approximator
A=f_{\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d))
for any symmetric, deterministic intersection aggregation rule
(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d),f_{\beta})

\mathcal{D}
, is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y-A)^{2}}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(\sum_{T}X_{T}-\sum_{T}p(|T|)X_{T})^{2}}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})(1-p(|T|))^{2}}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}.

In the worst case over
\mathcal{D}

such that

Formally, note for any valid distribution

that

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{T}(1-p(|T|))^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{t\in[n]}(1-p(t))^{2}\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}=\max_{t\in[n]}(1-p(t))^{2},

with the inequality achieved when

only if

It follows that

\displaystyle\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)

\displaystyle=\min_{(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}}(d),f_{\beta})}\mathsf{error}(f_{\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{inter}}(d)}))

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=0\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}(1-p(t))^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}p(t)^{2}.

∎

To achieve tight bounds on
\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)

Luckily, there is a vast literature that studies approximating arbitrary functions with bounded-degree polynomials.
Specifically,

\min_{p\in\mathcal{M}_{d}}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|,

(5)

d

The proof makes use of an

Intuitively, the polynomial should wiggle above and below zero at the same magnitude as many times as possible under its constraints, to minimize absolute distance to zero.
In fact, the

5
Discussion

We have initiated the study of worst-case aggregation when the principal may ask queries beyond the typical expectation queries.
We believe this is a rich and fruitful avenue of inquiry, and can see many open directions of interest.

5.1
Beyond partial information

Perhaps the most pressing direction is to prove results about the complexity of optimal and approximate aggregation beyond the partial information model studied here.
In particular, our results for the partial information seem to rely heavily on independence of the signals.
What can we do if signals are correlated?
A first attempt could be to allow the signals in the partial information model to be correlated with each other rather than independent.
But in this case, we obtain a fully general model, i.e. the information structure
\mathcal{D}
could be any joint distribution.
As trivial impossibilities exist for generic settings, we must look for some restriction on the information structures.

One promising approach, following
18
, is to focus on signals that are
informational substitutes
.
Intuitively, substitutes (introduced by
2
;
4
) are cases where the marginal value of a signal
X_{i}
for predicting
Y
is decreased when other signals
X_{j}
are already available.

18
showed that under “weak substitutes”, there is an aggregation rule on
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
achieving error
1-\tfrac{1}{n}
, and this is tight; in other words, one might as well pick a random expert’s opinion.
Under a stronger notion of “projective substitutes”,
18
showed that better error is possible, but still in the
1-O(\tfrac{1}{n})
regime.

5.2
Knowledge of the prior

Throughout we have assumed that the prior joint distribution
\mathcal{D}
is unknown to the aggregator.
It is natural to ask if the guarantees we have established can be improved if
\mathcal{D}
is known.
It turns out the answer is no, at least in the specific questions we study here, as in each case the worst-case instance
\mathcal{D}
can be determined and known ahead of time.
In other words, evidently minimax duality holds in this setting:
\inf_{A}\sup_{\mathcal{D}}\mathsf{error}(A_{\mathcal{D}})=\sup_{\mathcal{D}}\inf_{A}\mathsf{error}(A_{\mathcal{D}})
.

This duality can break beyond the partial information model, however.
For example, if we extend the partial information model to allow non-zero means, i.e.
\mu_{T}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]\neq 0
, then the means become nontrivial to contend with.

(
18
, Theorem 3.3)
give an example with
X_{\{i\}}\sim N(\mu,1)
for all
i
, and
X_{T}=0
otherwise, where for agent/order complexity
1
the optimal aggregation error is
1-\tfrac{2}{n}+\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}
; with knowledge of the prior, one can achieve optimal aggregation (error 0).
Interestingly, all of our constructions for optimal aggregation appear to go through even in this extension, as both the inclusion-exclusion and sum of difference queries automatically cancel out the
\mu_{T}
terms.

5.3
Adapting to easy instances

We have studied
worst-case
performance guarantees of the form
\mathsf{error}(A)\leq c
, which hold for all instances
\mathcal{D}
within a model class, like the partial information model.
If this bound is tight, then
\mathsf{error}(A)=c
for some set of “hard” instances.
In some sense, therefore, the worst-case optimal aggregation rules above are designed in response to these hardest instances, and these alone.
With a nod to the highly successful body of work on online learning algorithms for easy data
(
26
;
12
;
25
)
, we would like aggregation rules that retain the same worst-case error, but can improve on easy instances.

To illustrate, consider a generalization of Example
1
where the private and common signals
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
are still independent and mean-zero but could be any distribution otherwise.
For the optimal aggregation rule (over posterior expectation queries) proposed in
19
,
A=f(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})=\frac{2}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
, its worst-case error
\mathsf{error}(A)=1-\tfrac{4n}{(n+1)^{2}}
occurs for a distribution with
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{1})=\cdots=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{n+1})
.
On this instance, therefore, it would be pointless to ask experts for other statistics like
Q_{i,2}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}[Y\mid\mathcal{S}_{i}]
.

But such variance information would help significantly
outside
the worst-case.
Specifically, as
Q_{i,2}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{i})
, we can solve for
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{1}),\ldots,\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{n})
and thus derive better weights for the
Y_{i}
.
In particular, the optimal weight
\alpha_{i}
for
Y_{i}
will now be

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=1-n\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)\frac{\tau_{i}}{\tau}~,

where
\tau_{i}=1/\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{i})
is the “precision” of
X_{i}
, and
\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}
.
These weights are still

\frac{2}{n+1}
when the variances are all equal, giving the worst-case performance bound.
Outside of that worst case, however, performance strictly improves.
Specifically, fixing the total variance, the performance is now strictly increasing in the total precision
\tau
.

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}(f,\mathcal{Q})\in\mathcal{R}_{\text{det}}\\
\mathsf{query\text{-}c}(\mathcal{Q})\leq d\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y-f(\mathcal{Q}))^{2}}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{\{i\}}-\sum_{i\in S}X_{\{i\}})^{2}]}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]\backslash S}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}[X_{\{i\}}]}{n}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{d}{n}.

∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
d<n
. Then there exists an approximator
A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
such that
\mathsf{error}(A)=1-\frac{d}{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{d}\coloneqq\{S\subseteq[n]:1\leq|S|\leq d\}
be the collection of expert subsets of size at most
d
.
For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, let
L(S)
map
S
to an arbitrarily ordered list
L(S)
.

We define the randomized aggregation rule
P\coloneqq\text{unif}(\mathcal{T}_{d})
.
In other words,
P
uniformly randomly samples a subset
S
of
d
agents.
Then we let
(\mathcal{Q}_{S},f_{S})\coloneqq(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L(S)),\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}}Q)
, i.e. once a random subset of experts
S
is chosen and mapped to an ordered list
L(S)
, the randomized aggregation rule outputs the linear combination of the corresponding difference queries.
Let
A_{P}
be the approximator that
P
implements.
By Proposition
5
, for any list
L
of length
d
,
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathop{\mathrm{diff}}}(L)
has agent and order complexity
d
; moreover, the query complexity is
d
.
It follows that
\mathsf{query\text{-}c}(P)=d
,
\mathsf{order\text{-}c}(P)\leq d
, and
\mathsf{agent\text{-}c}(P)\leq d
, so that
A_{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
.

For a fixed subset
S
, by Proposition
4
,

f_{S}(\mathcal{Q}_{L(S)})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T:i_{k}\in T,\\
i_{\ell}\notin T\;\forall\ell<k\end{subarray}}X_{T}=\sum_{T:T\cap S\neq\emptyset}X_{T}.

Let
I_{T}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{T\cap S=\emptyset}
.
Then the error of
A_{P}
under a fixed distribution
\mathcal{D}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathcal{D}}(\sum_{T}X_{T}-f_{S}(\mathcal{Q}_{L(S)}))^{2}}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathcal{D}}[(\sum_{T}X_{T}-\sum_{T:T\cap S\neq\emptyset}X_{T})^{2}]}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{T}_{d})}\mathbb{E}_{\{X_{T}\}\sim\mathcal{D}}[(\sum_{T:T\cap S=\emptyset}X_{T})^{2}]}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{T}\sum_{U}\mathbb{E}[X_{T}X_{U}]\Pr[I_{T}=1,I_{U}=1]}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{T}\beta_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X_{T})}

(by independence of
X_{T}
’s),

where
\beta_{T}\coloneqq\Pr[I_{T}=1]=\frac{{n-|T|\choose d}}{{n\choose d}}
.
In particular, if
|T|=1
,
\beta_{T}=\frac{{n-1\choose d}}{{n\choose d}}=1-\frac{d}{n}
.
We note that
\beta_{T}
is the largest for
T
of size
1
, so that in the worst case over
\mathcal{D}
an adversary will put all variance on the singleton sets.
Formally, note for any valid distribution
\mathcal{D}
that

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{T}\beta_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{1}\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}=\beta_{1},

with the inequality achieved when
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})>0
only if
|T|=1
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathsf{error}(A_{P})=\max_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\sum_{T}\beta_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}{\sum_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{T})}\leq\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{d}{n}.

∎

.

Lemma 7
.

p^{*}\in\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[1,n]}|p(t)|

if and only if
p^{*}
equioscillates at
d+1
points in
[1,n]
(i.e. there exist
d+1
values
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
1\leq x_{0}\leq\ldots\leq x_{d}\leq n
, where
|p^{*}(x_{i})|=\sigma(-1)^{i}\max_{x\in[1,n]}p^{*}(x)
with
\sigma
either
-1
or
+1
).
Moreover, the polynomial
p^{*}
is unique.

Proof.

Our proof tracks the same reasoning as the original equioscillation theorem; however, since our polynomial constraints and domain are different, we provide a proof with adjustments here.
We refer the reader to Theorem 8.4 in
[
23
]
, whose structure we largely follow.

Sufficiency.

Assume if a function
p^{*}
satisfies the equioscillation property, it does not achieve the minimax value, so that there is some other
p\in P_{d}
with
p(0)=1
such that
\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p(x)|<\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p^{*}(x)|
.
Consider the polynomial
h=p^{*}-p
, which is also of degree at most
d
.
First note that
p^{*}(0)=p(0)=1
, so that
h(0)=0
.
Moreover, for every
x_{i}
of the
d+1
points
x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}
, we know by assumption that
|p(x_{i})|<|p^{*}(x_{i})|
, meaning
h(x_{i})=p^{*}(x_{i})-p(x_{i})
must have the same sign as
p^{*}
.
Thus
h
will alternate in sign on these
d+1
points, meaning
h
has
d+2
roots.
This is a contradiction, since
h
is a polynomial of degree at most
d
.

Necessity.

Assume
p^{*}\in P_{d}
achieves the minimax objective, and let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of points in
[1,n]
such that
|p^{*}(x)|=\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p^{*}(x)|
.
We first show that, for all
q\in P_{d}
with
q(0)=0
,
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p^{*}(x)q(x)\leq 0
.
Suppose not, i.e. there exists some
\tilde{q}\in P_{d}
,
q(0)=0
such that
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p^{*}(x)q(x)>2\epsilon
.
By continuity of the function
p^{*}(x)q(x)
, we can find a
\delta>0
such that

\max_{x\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}}p^{*}(x)q(x)>\epsilon,

(7)

where
\tilde{X}\coloneqq\{t\in[a,b]:\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|t-x|<\delta\}
(i.e.
\tilde{X}
is the set of points in a small neighborhood around those in
\mathcal{X}
).
Let
E\coloneqq\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p^{*}(x)|
, and let
\tilde{E}\coloneqq\max_{x\in[1,n]}|\tilde{q}(x)|
.
We now design a function
\tilde{p}
that achieves a better minimax value, with
\tilde{p}(x)=p^{*}(x)-\lambda\tilde{q}(x)
and
\lambda\in(0,\min\{2\epsilon/M^{2},\eta/\tilde{E}\})
.
This leads to a contradiction.
We note that for all
x\in\tilde{X}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=p^{*}(x)^{2}-2\lambda\tilde{q}(x)p^{*}(x)+\lambda^{2}\tilde{q}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=E^{2}-2\lambda\tilde{q}(x)p^{*}(x)+\lambda^{2}\tilde{q}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle<E^{2}+2\lambda\epsilon+\lambda^{2}M^{2}

(by Equation
7
)

\displaystyle\leq E^{2}-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{M^{2}}+\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{M^{2}}

\displaystyle=E^{2}.

Meanwhile, consider the set
\hat{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq[1,n]\backslash\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
.
By continuity, there is a value
\eta>0
such that
|p^{*}(x)|\leq E-\eta
for all
x\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
.
Since
\lambda<\eta/\tilde{E}
, we have for all
x\in\hat{X}
that

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}(x)|

\displaystyle=|p^{*}(x)-\lambda\tilde{q}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|p^{*}(x)|+\lambda|\tilde{q}(x)|

\displaystyle<(E-\eta)+\eta

\displaystyle\leq E.

Now we need to show that the set
\mathcal{X}
contains
d+1
points, and the error oscillates in sign at these points.
Suppose this is not the case, so we can only identify
m
points with oscillating sign in
\mathcal{X}
with
1\leq m<d+1
, labeled
x_{0}<\dots<x_{m-1}
.
We will then construct a function
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
,
q(0)=0
that violates the previous statement., i.e.
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p^{*}(x)q(x)>0
.
Note first that
E\coloneqq\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p^{*}(x)|>0
, since
p^{*}
is a polynomial of degree at least
1
.
If
m=1
, then
p^{*}
does not change sign on
\mathcal{X}
.
If we then take
q(x)=x
, either
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}q(x)p^{*}(x)>0
or
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}-q(x)p^{*}(x)>0
.
If
m>1
, there exist
m-1
zeroes of
p^{*}
,
\tilde{x}_{0}<\dots<\tilde{x}_{m-2}
, with each
\tilde{x}_{i}\in(x_{i-1},x_{i})
(where we define
x_{-1}\coloneqq 1
and
x_{m}\coloneqq n
).
Now we construct the function
q(x)=x\prod_{j=0}^{m-2}(x-\tilde{x}_{j})
.
First note that
q(0)=0
, and the degree of
q
is
m\leq d
.
Since the sign of
q
does not change between its roots, it does not change in the intervals
(1,\tilde{x}_{0}),(\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{x}_{1}),\dots,(\tilde{x}_{m-1},n)
; and it flips between these intervals.
It follows that the sign of
q(x)p^{*}(x)
is the same for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and either
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}q(x)p^{*}(x)>0
or
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}-q(x)p^{*}(x)>0
.

Uniqueness.

Suppose by contradiction there are two polynomials
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
that achieve the minimax value with
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
,
p_{1}(0)=1
, and
p_{2}(0)=1
.
Then take
q(x)=\frac{p_{1}(x)+p_{2}(x)}{2}
, and note that
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
and
q(0)=1
.
Moreover, applying the triangle inequality, one can see that
q(x)
also achieves the minimax value on
[1,n]
.
By the necessary condition proven above,
q
has
d+1
points
(x_{0},\dots,x_{d+1})
in
[1,n]
that oscillate in sign and achieve the extreme value.
We can then show for each
x_{i}
that
p_{1}(x_{i})=p_{2}(x_{i})
.
In particular, note
2|q(x_{i})|=|p_{1}(x_{i})+p_{2}(x_{i})|=2E
,
|q(x_{i})|\leq E
, and
|p_{2}(x_{i})|\leq E
.
We therefore must have
(p_{1}(x_{i})-p_{2}(x_{i}))^{2}=2p_{1}(x_{i})^{2}+2p_{2}(x_{i})^{2}-(p_{1}(x_{i})+p_{2}(x_{i}))^{2}\leq 0
, so that
p_{1}(x_{i})=p_{2}(x_{i})
.
Since
p_{1}(0)=p_{2}(0)
as well,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
agree at
d+2
points; but since they are both polynomials of degree
d
, we must have
p_{1}=p_{2}
.
Thus we have reached a contradiction.

∎

Lemma 9
.

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\

\displaystyle|p(x)|-|p(t_{j})|

(by the mean value theorem)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{2}d^{2}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|\;

(by the Markov brothers’ inequality)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{n-1}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|.

Rearranging, we have for any
x\in[1,1]
that
|p(x)|\leq\frac{d^{2}}{n-1}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(t)|+|p(t_{j})|
, so

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n-1}\right)\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|+\max_{t\in J}|p(t)|

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{d^{2}}{n-1}\right)\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t\in J}|p(t)|.

The statement follows by applying the affine mapping
m^{-1}(x)
to the intervals and noting the inequality holds for the minimizing polynomial in the set
\{p\in P_{d}:p(0)=1\}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Note first that

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}|p(t)|\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p(x)|,

and by increasing monotonicity of the function
f(x)=x^{2}
on
[1,\infty)
,

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}p(t)^{2}=\left(\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}|p(t)|\right)^{2}.

Combining Lemmas
4
,
8
, then,

\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}p(t)^{2}\leq\left(\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p(x)|\right)^{2}=\frac{4}{\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{2}}

for
q(n)=\frac{\sqrt{n}+1}{\sqrt{n}-1}
, and similarly by Lemma
9
,

\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(A)\geq\frac{4\left(1-\frac{d^{2}}{n}\right)^{2}}{\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{2}}.

Now, note that
4\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{-2}\leq 4q(n)^{2d}=4\left(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}+1}\right)^{2d}\leq 4e^{\frac{-4d}{\sqrt{n}+1}}
.
The first statement immediately follows.

Meanwhile, let
d=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, so for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
n_{0}
, if
n\geq n_{0}
then
c_{1}\sqrt{n}\leq d\leq c_{2}\sqrt{n}
.
Using the standard logarithmic Taylor expansion, we have for
u\coloneqq 1/\sqrt{n}
that

\ln q(n)=\ln(1+u)-\ln(1-u)=2\left(u+\frac{u^{3}}{3}+O(u^{5})\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}+O(n^{-3/2}).

Then for any
d
in the interval
[c_{1}\sqrt{n},c_{2}\sqrt{n}]
, we have

q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}=e^{d\ln q(n)}+e^{-d\ln q(n)}\to e^{2c}+e^{-2c}+o(1)

for some
c\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
as
n\to\infty
. Thus

\lim_{n\to\infty}4\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{-2}=4(e^{2c}+e^{-2c})^{-2}\in(0,1).

Similarly,
1-\frac{d^{2}}{n-1}\in[1-c_{2}^{2},1-c_{1}^{2}]\subseteq(0,1)
for all sufficiently large
n
. Multiplying by the previous limit gives

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{4(1-\frac{d^{2}}{n-1})^{2}}{(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d})^{2}}=C\in(0,1),

where
C
depends on
c_{1},c_{2}
.

Finally, let
d=o(\sqrt{n})
.
We will show that
4\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{-2}=1-\frac{4d^{2}}{n}+o(\frac{d^{2}}{n})=1-\Theta(\frac{d^{2}}{n})
, which immediately gives us the upper bound on error.
It follows by Lemma
9
that
\min_{R\in\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\text{lin}}}\mathsf{error}(R)\geq 4(1-\frac{d^{2}}{n-1})^{2}\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{-2}=1-\Theta(\frac{d^{2}}{n})
.

Let
t\coloneqq d\ln q(n)
, and note that
4\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{-2}=\cosh^{-2}(t).

Using the Taylor expansion for the logarithm with
u\coloneqq 1/\sqrt{n}
, we have

\ln q(n)=\ln(1+u)-\ln(1-u)=2\left(u+\frac{u^{3}}{3}+O(u^{5})\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}+O(n^{-3/2}).

Thus

t=d\ln q(n)=\frac{2d}{\sqrt{n}}+o\Big(\frac{d}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)
since
d=o(\sqrt{n}).

Next, using the Taylor expansion of
\cosh t
, we have

\cosh t=1+\frac{t^{2}}{2}+R(t)
for
R(t)\geq 0
,
R(t)=O(t^{4}),
so that

4\left(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}\right)^{-2}=\cosh^{-2}(t)=1-t^{2}+o(t^{2}).

Substituting
t^{2}=(d\ln q(n))^{2}=\frac{4d^{2}}{n}+o\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\Big)
gives the asymptotic expression

\frac{4}{(q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d})^{2}}=1-\frac{4d^{2}}{n}+o\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\Big).

∎

Lemma 10
.

p^{*}\in\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[n]}|p(t)|

if and only if
p^{*}
equioscillates at
d+1
integers in

d+1
integers
1\leq x_{0}\leq\ldots\leq x_{d}\leq n
such that
|p^{*}(x_{i})|=\sigma(-1)^{i}\max_{x\in[1,n]}p^{*}(x)
, with

).

Proof.

The proof of this lemma exactly follows the equioscillation theorem shown in Lemma
8
, but with the maximum in the problem taken over a finite set of points.
∎

Lemma 11
.

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in\{1,\dots,n\}}|p(t)|=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P_{d}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{x\in[1,n]}|p(x)|=\frac{2}{q(n)^{d}+q(n)^{-d}}

if and only if one of the cases hold:
d=1
,
d=2
and
n
is odd, or
d=3
and
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

T_{d}
achieves its maxima
\max_{x\in[-1,1]}T_{d}(x)
at points
x_{k}=\cos(\frac{k\pi}{d})
for
0\leq k\leq d
, so that the extrema of
\hat{T}_{d}(x)
occur at
\hat{x}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left((n-1)\cos(\frac{k\pi}{d})+(n+1)\right)
.
Moreover,
\hat{T}_{d}
is the unique polynomial that achieves the optimal minimax value for the continuous minimax problem by Lemma
8
.
By Lemma
10
, then,
\hat{T}_{d}
achieves the exact minimax error for our discrete problem if and only if each
\hat{x}_{k}
is an integer (by construction of
\hat{T}_{d}
, equioscillation is already achieved).
By Niven’s theorem, the only values
0\leq\frac{k}{d}\leq 1
such that
\cos(\frac{k\pi}{d})
are rational are
\frac{k}{d}=0,\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{3},
or
1
.
We can then break into cases over
d
and
k
to determine when
\hat{x}_{k}
is an integer.

•

k=0
: then
\hat{x}_{0}=n
, so the statement is true.

•

d
is even, and
k=\tfrac{d}{2}
: then
\cos\left(\tfrac{k\pi}{d}\right)=0
, so
\hat{x}_{k}=\frac{n+1}{2},
which is an integer if and only if
n
is odd.

•

3\mid d,
and
k=\tfrac{d}{3}
:
\cos\left(\tfrac{k\pi}{d}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}
, so

\hat{x}_{k}=\frac{(n-1)(\tfrac{1}{2})+(n+1)}{2}=\frac{3n+1}{4},

which is an integer if and only if
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

•

3\mid d,
and
k=\tfrac{2d}{3}
:
\cos\left(\tfrac{k\pi}{d}\right)=-\tfrac{1}{2}
, so

\hat{x}_{k}=\frac{(n-1)(-\tfrac{1}{2})+(n+1)}{2}=\frac{n+3}{4},

which is an integer if and only if
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

•

k=d
: then
\hat{x}_{d}=1
, so the statement is true.

For all other
k
and
d
pairs,
\cos(k\pi/d)
is irrational, and thus
\hat{x}_{k}
cannot be an integer.
Now, if
d=1
, then
\hat{x}_{0}
and
\hat{x}_{1}
are both integers, so the statement holds.
If
d=2
,
\hat{x}_{1}
is an integer if and only if
n
is odd.
If
d=3
,
\hat{x}_{1}
and
\hat{x}_{2}
are integers if and only if
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.
For
d>3
,
\hat{x}_{d-1}
does not fall into any of the cases.
∎
