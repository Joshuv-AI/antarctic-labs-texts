On Optimal Testing of Linearity
Vipul Arora, Esty Kelman, Uri Meir

Abstract
Linearity testing has been a focal problem in property testing of functions. We combine different known techniques and observations about linearity testing in order to resolve two recent versions of this task. First, we focus on the online manipulations model introduced by Kalemaj, Raskhodnikova and Varma (ITCS 2022 & Theory of Computing 2023). In this model, up to t t data entries are adversarially manipulated after each query is answered. Ben-Eliezer, Kelman, Meir, and Raskhodnikova (ITCS 2024) showed an asymptotically optimal linearity tester that is resilient to t t manipulations per query, but their approach fails if t t is too large. We extend this result, showing an optimal tester for almost any possible value of t t . First, we simplify their result when t t is small, and for larger values of t t we instead use sample-based testers, as defined by Goldreich and Ron (ACM Transactions on Computation Theory 2016). A key observation is that sample-based testing is resilient to online manipulations, but still achieves optimal query complexity for linearity when t t is large. We complement our result by showing that when t t is very large, any reasonable property, and in particular linearity, cannot be tested at all. Second, we consider linearity over the reals with proximity parameter ε \varepsilon . Fleming and Yoshida (ITCS 2020) gave a tester using O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ⋅ l ​ o ​ g ​ ( 1 / ε ) ) O(1/\varepsilon\ \cdot log(1/\varepsilon)) queries. We simplify their algorithms and modify the analysis accordingly, showing an optimal tester that only uses O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) O(1/\varepsilon) queries. This modification works for the low-degree testers presented in Arora, Bhattacharyya, Fleming, Kelman, and Yoshida (SODA 2023) as well, resulting in optimal testers for degree- d d polynomials, for any constant degree d d .

Linearity testing.

In this manuscript, we focus on testing functions over a field
\mathbb{F}
(in particular
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}

That is, the tester is allowed oracle access to some function

The most studied property of functions is linearity, for which a tester was first given in

The test, dubbed “the

denotes addition over

denotes point-wise addition.
By definition, if

is linear, this equality must hold for any pair

.
It was shown in various settings that if

is
\varepsilon
-far from linear, there is an

for at least an

\Omega(\varepsilon)
-fraction
of the pairs

[

BCH+96
,

]
.
Thus, repeating the process
\theta(1/\varepsilon)

tester for linearity.

It is well known that
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)

[

]
), proving the 3 points test of

is optimal in terms of query complexity.

Linearity over

The

In this model, after each query is answered,

In particular, they study linearity testing over

First, they note the

To overcome this, they devise the

It was then pointed out in

How many manipulations can the tester handle?

All the above testers share a similar limitation: they all require
t\leq 2^{n/c}
for some
c\geq 2
(See
Table 1
for the precise values).
It is natural to ask what is the highest value of
t
for which linearity is still testable. Clearly,
t=2^{n}
is too much, but how close can we get?

In
Section 2
we fully resolve this question.
Our observation, roughly speaking, is that all previous testers include some queries that are “too predictable”, making them susceptible to adversarial manipulations.
However, this only problematic when the adversary has very large manipulation budget
t=2^{\Omega(n)}
, in which case we can simply apply the sample-based tester instead, killing two birds with one stone.
First, sample-based testers almost automatically overcome online manipulations. Second, the seemingly high sample complexity of the tester is actually optimal as in this regime we have
\Theta(\log(t)+1/\varepsilon)=\Theta(n+1/\varepsilon)
. Formally, we have the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Doubly-optimal tester)
.

There exists a constant
c>0

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]

t\leq c\cdot\min{\left\{\varepsilon^{2},1/n^{2}\right\}}\cdot 2^{n}
,
there exists an
\varepsilon

that is resilient to
t
-online manipulations budget-managing adversaries and makes
O\left(\max{\left\{1/\varepsilon,\log t\right\}}\right)
queries. Furthermore, in the case of erasure adversary, the tester has one-sided error.

While our tester achieves optimal query complexity, it also handles essentially the largest manipulation budget

1.2
Testing Over the Reals

We start by describing the model of testing functions over the reals.
It is similar to the case of finite domains, with the important distinction that a uniform measure over the domain no longer exists.
This is indeed an issue, as the uniform measure over finite domains is crucial to most standard notions of distance (e.g., hamming distance).
Instead, it is natural to choose the standard (multivariate) Gaussian, denoted by
{\cal N}(\bm{0},I)
.
More generally, the
distribution-free
testing framework
[
GGR98
,
HK07
]
, considers a distance measure with respect to an unknown but samplable distribution over the domain.
Formally, for a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, a proximity parameter
\varepsilon>0
, an unknown but samplable distribution
{\cal D}
supported over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a property
{\cal P}
,
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from
{\cal P}
with respect to
{\cal D}
if
3
3

3

We use bold face, e.g.
\bm{x}
, to denote vectors/points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\delta_{{\cal D}}(f,{\cal P}):=\inf_{g\in{\cal P}}\left\{\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim{\cal D}}[f(\bm{x})\neq g(\bm{x})]\right\}>\varepsilon.

An algorithm is a
distribution-free tester
for
{\cal P}
, if given query access to
f
, sample access to
{\cal D}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, it accepts all
f\in{\cal P}
with probability at least
2/3
, and rejects all
f
such that
\delta_{{\cal D}}(f,{\cal P})>\varepsilon
, with probability at least
2/3
.

Linearity vs. additivity.

Over the reals, unlike the finite case, we have a distinction between

•

additivity
: for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},f(\bm{x})+f(\bm{y})=f(\bm{x}+\bm{y})
, and

•

linearity
:
f(\bm{x})\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}
, for some
\{c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}
.

While the two are equivalent over finite fields, some pathological examples show that functions over the reals can be additive but not linear
[
Ham05
]
.
In fact, over finite domains this equivalence is key for testing results, as the
3
-point BLR test directly checks for additivity.
While the tester and its analysis are similar for both properties, linearity requires additional assumptions (see
[
FY20
]
). The discussion below is therefore restricted to additivity.

In order to describe previous and new testing results over the reals, we first review another useful concept from the testing literature.

Self-correction.

The notion of self-correction dates back to
[
BLR93
]
and
has deep ties with many testing results.
To demonstrate this viewpoint, we revisit the
3
-point linearity tester over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Define the local correction of
p
according to
x
as
g_{x}(p):=f(p+x)-f(x)
, That is, by querying
x
and
p+x
we get an indication what the value of
p
“should be” in order to satisfy linearity.
Define the global correction to be

g(p):=\maj_{x}g_{x}(p).

Think of
g
as a function that “corrects” each
p
by assigning it the value that satisfies linearity for more choices of
x
.
Formally the
3
-point tester asks whether
f(p)=g_{x}(p)
, but morally it asks whether
f\equiv g
, by comparing them on a random input
p
and replacing
g
with a “representative”
g_{x}
which can be computed by
2
queries to
f
.
Denoting the rejection probability of the test by
\mu
, the tester is analyzed by showing two claims: (1) if
\mu
is small then
g
and
f
are close; and (2) if
\mu
is small then
g\in{\cal P}
.
Combining the claims, a small rejection probability implies that
f
is close to linear (specifically to
g
), which guarantees the soundness of the tester (by counter positive).
To show (1) and (2), a crucial condition is that evaluation of
g_{x}(p)
uses queries to
f
that have the same marginal distribution (here
x
and
p+x
are both marginally uniform over the domain).

Self-correcting over the reals.

It turns out that self-correcting a function over the reals is tricky in particular due to the marginals condition.
To this end,
[
FY20
]
defined their
2
queries linearity self-corrector in two steps. First, in a straightforward manner inside a small ball
B
centered at the origin, where one can show the marginals condition (approximately) holds, and the analysis follows through.
The second step is extrapolating
g
to the entire domain using the fact that an additive function respects multiplication by a rational scalar, i.e., for all
a\in\mathbb{Q}
it holds that
g(ax)=a\cdot g(x)
.

We leave the hairy details and precise definitions for
Section

3
, and merely point out that due to this nuisance, evaluation of
g
is explicit in their algorithm (unlike the
3
-point test for
\mathbb{F}_{2}
where it is implicit).
Eventually, their test asks whether
f\equiv g
using
\Theta(1/\varepsilon)
comparison points, but each evaluation of
g
is done using
\Theta\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)
queries to
f
, resulting in query complexity
\Theta\left((1/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)
.

Our modifications and results.

The soundness analysis is made via the self-correction paradigm: if
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from
{\cal P}
, it is also
\varepsilon
-far from
g
(recall the distance is according to the samplable distribution
{\cal D}
). Each comparison point sampled from
{\cal D}
thus has probability
\Omega(\varepsilon)
to indicate that
f\not\equiv g
. Taking
O(1/\varepsilon)
points amplify the success probability to, say,
9/10
.
However, the reason
[
FY20
]
use
\Theta\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)
queries to
f
for each value
g(\bm{p})
is merely to reduce the error probability from a constant to
O(\varepsilon)
.
This way, one can apply a union bound over all
O(1/\varepsilon)
comparison point, adding only an additional small constant error to the tester.
Our observation is that the algorithm can tolerate erroneous evaluations, thus reducing the error for all evaluations of
g
is is unnecessary.
Indeed, when analyzing the soundness, we simply say that
\bm{p}
is a witness for
f\not\equiv g
if it satisfies both
f(\bm{p})\neq g(\bm{p})
and the evaluation of
g
succeeded. This still gives an
\Omega(\varepsilon)
probability for a witness, and
O(1/\varepsilon)
samples from
{\cal D}
suffice. We do not care that other evaluations of
g
(roughly half of them) contain errors, only the one important point matters.
We get the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
There exist distribution-free, one-sided error
O(1/\varepsilon)
-query testers for both additivity of functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and linearity of continuous functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Testing over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with online manipulations

The online manipulation model was introduced by Kalmaj et al.
[
KRV23
]
and formally defined by Ben-Eliezer et al.
[
BKMR24
]
.
For the many motivations of the model, we refer to both these papers.
We next define the model and then proceed to prove
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.2
.

In the online manipulation testing model, the input is accessed via a sequence
{\left\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\right\}}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of oracles, where
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is used to answer the
i
-th query. The oracle
\mathcal{O}_{1}
gives access to the original input (e.g., when the input is a function
f
, we have
\mathcal{O}_{1}\equiv f
), and subsequent oracles are objects of the same type as the input (e.g., functions with the same domain and range). Each such oracle is obtained by the adversary by modifying the previous oracle to include a growing number of erasures/corruptions as
i
increases.
We use
{\text{Dist}}(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}^{\prime})
for the Hamming distance between the two oracles (i.e., the number of queries for which they give different answers).
We let
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denote the number of
erasures
(or
corruptions
)
per query
.
More formally, we define:

Definition 2.1

(Types of adversaries)
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to be the manipulation parameter, and let
\mathcal{O}={\left\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\right\}}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of oracles
4
4

4

All our results regrading the model only consider a finite prefix of this sequence.
, where
\mathcal{O}_{1}
is an oracle accessing the original input function
f
.
We have the following attributes of adversaries, each imposing a condition on the oracle sequence:

•

A
fixed rate
adversary must choose entries to manipulate immediately after a query is answered. Formally, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

{\text{Dist}}(\mathcal{O}_{i},\mathcal{O}_{i+1})\leq\left\lfloor{(i+1)\cdot t}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor{i\cdot t}\right\rfloor.

•

A
budget-managing
adversary can reserve its “manipulation rights” for later rounds, but it never exceeds
t
manipulations per each query made. Formally, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

{\text{Dist}}(\mathcal{O}_{1},\mathcal{O}_{i+1})\leq i\cdot t.

•

An
erasure
adversary can only change original values of
f
to a new special symbol
\bot
. Formally, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\mathcal{O}_{i+1}(x)\in{\left\{\mathcal{O}_{i}(x),\perp\right\}}
.

•

A
corruption
adversary can change values freely, but we assume it never uses values that aren’t viable outputs, as these immediately give away that the entry was manipulated.

Each adversary uses either erasures or corruptions, and each adversary either has fixed rate or can manage its manipulation budget.
We note that budget-managing adversaries are at least as strong as ones with fixed rate, making it harder to test against them.

Definition 2.2

(Online
\varepsilon
-tester)
.

Fix

\mathcal{O}={\left\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\right\}}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}

For all adversarial strategies of the specified type,

1.

if
f\in\mathcal{P}
, then
{\cal T}
accepts with probability at least 2/3, and

2.

if
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from
{\cal P},

then
{\cal T}
rejects with probability at least 2/3,

where the probability is taken over the random coins of the tester.

If
{\cal T}
always accepts all functions
f\in{\cal P}
, then it has
one-sided error.
If
\mathcal{T}
chooses its queries in advance, before observing any outputs from the oracle, then it is
nonadaptive
.

To ease notation, we use
\mathcal{O}(x)
for the oracle’s answer to query

If

Remark
.

All our results consider both erasures and corruptions.
Our linearity tester is resilient even to the stronger budget-managing adversaries, while our impossibility results in
