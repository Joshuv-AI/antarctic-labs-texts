The impact of preoperative depressive symptoms on acute and chronic postoperative pain trajectories after orthopedic surgery for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Sijia Ma, Zhen Wang, Jiacheng Yu, Yu Zhang, Jinhua Bo, Yu’e Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) patients frequently experience depressive symptoms, which may influence postoperative pain outcomes. However, high-quality prospective evidence remains scarce. This study aimed to investigate the association between preoperative depressive symptoms and postoperative pain trajectories in AIS.MethodsThis was a prospective study involving AIS patients aged 10–17 years who underwent surgical management for scoliosis correction under general anesthesia. Depressive symptoms, pain, functional activity, sleep quality, and quality of life were pre-operatively assessed using scales. Dynamic trajectories of acute postsurgical pain (APSP) and chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) were evaluated during a 90-day follow-up. The primary outcome was the incidence of APSP. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of CPSP, oral morphine milligram equivalents (MME) during POD 1–5, early complications, postoperative length of stay, and long-term rehabilitation status.ResultsPreoperative depressive symptoms (odds ratio [OR] = 28.30), female sex (OR = 4.87), and a higher number of surgical segments (OR = 1.27) were identified as independent risk factors for CPSP. A risk prediction model (nomogram) constructed by incorporating the above variables effectively predicted CPSP in these patients, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.854 (95% confidence intervals [CI]: 0.774–0.934, P < 0.01). The incidence of CPSP was significantly higher in postoperative AIS patients with persistent depressive symptoms than those with relieved depression or persistently non-depressed symptoms (100% vs. 67.86% vs. 10.40%, P < 0.001).ConclusionPreoperative depressive symptoms are risk factors for both APSP and CPSP in postoperative AIS patients, demonstrating a gradient association with the incidence of CPSP. A nomogram that incorporates female sex, the number of surgical segments, and preoperative depressive symptoms can effectively predict CPSP in this population. Background

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is the most common three-dimensional structural deformity of the spine during the peak growth period [1]. The global prevalence of AIS ranges from 2 to 4%, and it is more frequent in children aged 10–16 years (1–3%) [2–4]. Large-scale screening data in China indicate a female preference for AIS, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1:1.5 [5]. For AIS patients exhibiting substantial cardiac dysfunction or progressive deterioration of the Cobb angle (frequ ently ≥ 45–50°), posterior spinal corrective fusion surgery is recommended [6–8]. Despite the recognized effectiveness of surgical techniques in correcting abnormalities and enhancing trunk balance and cardiopulmonary function, these interventions often result in postoperative pain, which can hinder rehabilitation. Postoperative pain is a common occurrence, with the majority of AIS patients (> 80%) experiencing moderate-to-severe acute pain. Moreover, nearly half of these patients ultimately develop chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) [9].

A survey of 34% of adolescents revealed depressive symptoms, characterized by persistent poor mood, anhedonia, decreased energy levels, and sleep disturbances [10, 11]. Nonetheless, research findings have indicated that between 14 and 22% of adolescents diagnosed with idiopathic scoliosis manifest depressive symptoms or depression that fulfill clinically relevant criteria [12, 13]. This percentage is nonetheless noteworthy. The principal causes of this condition have been identified as social avoidance, treatment-related stress, and body image issues. The extant literature suggests that preoperative depression symptoms are strongly associated with increased costs, longer hospital stays, and worsening acute postsurgical pain (APSP) in patients undergoing surgery [14–16]. However, there is a paucity of high-quality prospective data concerning the impact of preoperative depressive symptoms on the risk factors and postoperative pain trajectories in AIS patients.

In this work, we evaluated postoperative AIS patients for APSP and CPSP risk variables and demonstrated how preoperative depressive symptoms affect postoperative pain trajectories. Furthermore, a nomogram was developed to predict the probability of CPSP in AIS patients. The results of this study should highlight the significance of an integrative psychological and analgesic intervention for high-risk AIS patients, as well as the standardized depression screening (e.g., the Children’s Depression Inventory [CDI]) in the regular perioperative assessment of AIS. This is in accordance with the most recent iteration of the STROCSS (Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort, Cross-Sectional, and Case–Control Studies in Surgery) Guideline [17].

Study design

Recruitment

Consecutive eligible patients admitted to the orthopedics department of the hospital for the aforementioned surgical procedure during the study period were screened. A research coordinator approached eligible patients and their guardians to explain the details of the study. Written informed consent was obtained before any study procedures.

Sample size estimation and participant grouping

The sample size of the present study was estimated based on the primary outcome of the incidence of APSP. A previous article reported that moderate-to-severe pain during patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) affects 70% of people treated with scoliosis orthopedic surgeries [18]. A preliminary pre-experiment survey revealed that 94% and 20% of AIS patients exhibited moderate-to-severe postoperative pain and depressive symptoms, respectively.

Based on a test power of 80%, a two-sided significance level of 0.05, and a dropout rate of 10%, a sample size of 155 was required, comprising 31 participants in the depression group and 124 in the non-depression group. In our study, 200 AIS patients were initially allocated, and 157 who completed all follow-ups were included in the final analysis. They were divided into the depression group (n = 32, CDI ≥ 19 points) and the non-depression group (n = 125, CDI < 19 points) based on the preoperative CDI scores.

Additionally, AIS patients were assigned into a persistent depression group (preoperative CDI ≥ 19 and postoperative CDI ≥ 19), a relieved depression group (preoperative CDI ≥ 19 continuously and postoperative CDI < 19), and a persistently non-depressed group (preoperative CDI < 19 and postoperative CDI < 19) based on dynamic changes in CDI scores on preoperative day 1 (pre-op day 1) and postoperative day 90 (POD 90).

Data collection and scale assessments

Baseline data of AIS patients were collected upon admission, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), education level, surgical history, medication history, ASA physical status classification, the New York Heart Association Functional Classification (NYHA), laboratory test results (complete blood count, liver and kidney function, electrolytes, etc.), and auxiliary examinations (electrocardiography and echocardiography). Perioperative indicators, including operation time (min), anesthesia time (min), total fluid volume (mL), intraoperative urine volume (mL), blood loss (mL), and transfusion status, were also recorded.

A series of scales was used to assess pain, depressive symptoms, sleep quality, and quality of life. Briefly, pain assessment was conducted twice daily (8:00–10:00 and 18:00–20:00). It was carried out on the pre-op day 1 and from 1 to 5 days postoperatively (POD 1–5), and was recorded by pre-trained investigators face-to-face in the ward. Additional assessments were performed on POD 30 and POD 90. The intensity of pain at rest (lying quietly) and during movement (immediately after standardized actions like turning over or sitting up) was assessed using the numerical rating scale (NRS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the most severe pain imaginable) [19]. APSP was determined by an NRS score of 4 or higher, indicating clinically significant moderate-to-severe pain. According to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) [20], CPSP was defined as persistent pain or new-onset pain at the original surgical site for at least three months after tissue healing. In this study, CPSP was recorded as positive in AIS patients reporting persistent pain at the incision site with an NRS score ≥ 1 on POD 90. Follow-up data were obtained on POD 30 and POD 90 through structured telephonic interviews. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the CDI on pre-op day 1, POD 30, and POD 90. The CDI consists of 27 items covering dimensions of negative mood, loss of pleasure, feelings of worthlessness, interpersonal distress, and somatic symptoms, ranging from 0 (no depressive symptoms) to 54 (all depressive symptoms are present) [21]. Based on previous findings, clinically significant depressive symptoms were determined by a total CDI score of ≥ 19 [22].

The patient’s cognitive function was assessed using the mini-mental state examination (MMSE) scale on pre-op day 1. Sleep quality and delirium were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and the 3-Minute Diagnostic Confusion Assessment Method (3D-CAM) on pre-op day 1 and POD 1–5, once daily between 8:00 and 10:00. Impaired sleep quality was determined by a PSQI score exceeding 5 [23, 24].

The Scoliosis Research Society 22-Item (SRS-22) and the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) were used to assess spinal function and health-related quality of life on pre-op day 1, POD 30, and POD 90, respectively [25–27]. All questionnaires were completed independently by the patients in a quiet environment. If necessary, pretrained investigators provided neutral explanations of the item meanings without influencing the patients’ responses.

All data were independently imported into an electronic database by two pretrained investigators and cross-verified to ensure data integrity and accuracy.

Surgical and anesthetic management

Following an 8-h fast, patients were sent to the operating room, where electrocardiogram (ECG), oxygen saturation (SpO2), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), and bispectral index (BIS) were routinely monitored. Anesthesia was induced with intravenous injections of midazolam (0.1 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg), propofol (2 mg/kg), and vecuronium (0.1 mg/kg). Mechanical ventilation after intubation was applied with the following parameters: inspired fraction of oxygen (FiO2) 60–80%; tidal volume (VT) 8–10 ml/kg; respiratory rate (RR) 12–15 breaths/min; and inspiration-to-expiration ratio (I/E), 1:2.

During the procedure, arterial blood pressure (ABP), central venous pressure (CVP), and BIS were continuously monitored. propofol (4–10 mg·kg− 1·h− 1), remifentanil (0.5–1.0 μg·kg− 1·min− 1), cisatracurium (0.2 mg·kg− 1·h− 1), and dexmedetomidine (0.2 μg·kg⁻1·h− 1) were continuously infused for anesthesia maintenance. Medications were timely adjusted based on the BIS values (target 40–60) and hemodynamic responses. Muscle relaxants were discontinued 30 min before the end of the procedure. Weaning from mechanical ventilation was conducted until patients regained spontaneous breathing and consciousness, followed by the initiation of PCIA. An NRS score of 4 and above was a sign of rescue analgesia. All opioids used on POD 1–5, including those used for PCIA and rescue anesthesia, were converted into oral morphine milligram equivalents (MME) using internationally accepted conversion factors (Appendix Table 6 for details).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the incidences of APSP. Secondary outcomes included the incidences of CPSP, early postoperative MME, and early complications (e.g., delirium), postoperative length of stay, and long-term rehabilitation status.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.4.0, http://www.R-project.org). Firth penalized logistic regression analysis was conducted using the logistic package (version 1.26.0), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was completed using the glmnet package (version 4.1–8). Data visualization was carried out using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables within a normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Otherwise, they were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage (n [%]) and compared by the chi-square test. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to examine data normality.

Repeated Measures ANCOVA was used to assess the effect of preoperative depressive symptoms on the dynamic trajectories in APSP. In this model, "time" (POD 1–5) was treated as a within-subject factor, and "preoperative depressive symptoms" was the between-subject factor. The preoperative pain NRS score, PSQI, total score of SRS-22, and scores on the mental health dimension in SF-36 were covariates to be controlled as confounders.

To identify predictors of chronic postoperative pain, the study employed an advanced modeling process designed for small-sample, multivariable data. First, Firth penalized regression was used to conduct univariate analysis on all candidate variables (including demographic characteristics, clinical indicators, psychological variables, and perioperative variables) to correct for small-sample bias. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with adjusted 95% confidence intervals (a95% CI). Then, variables with a P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in LASSO regression. The optimal penalty parameter was selected through ten-fold cross-validation based on the "one standard error rule (1se)" to complete variable selection and dimensionality reduction. Finally, the core subset of variables identified by LASSO was incorporated into a multivariable Firth logistic regression model for the final fitting, thereby constructing a robust predictive model.

Construct the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and conduct the decision curve analysis (DCA) for the aforementioned prediction models to assess their performance, including discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability. Ultimately, construct a nomogram to facilitate clinical use.

The predictive model performance comparison employs DeLong’s non-parametric test to compare the differences in the area under the curve (AUC) between the combined predictive model and single preoperative depression indicator models.

The analysis of the association between the dynamic evolution of depressive symptoms and chronic pain is based on dividing patients into three groups according to their depressive status from the preoperative period to 90 days post-operation (persistent depression, relieved depression, and persistently non-depressed groups). The CPSP incidence between groups is compared using the Pearson chi-square test. If the overall difference is significant, pairwise post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction are conducted to control type I error inflation.

Outcome indicators

There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of the incidence of APSP, MME within 5 days postoperatively, complication rates (such as delirium, incision infection, etc.), postoperative hospital stay, all-cause mortality within 30 days after surgery, and pain NRS scores on POD 30 (P > 0.05). Compared with the non-depression group, the CPSP incidence and pain NRS scores on POD 90 were significantly higher in the depression group (P < 0.001, Table 2). Regarding the longitudinal change in pain intensity within each group, the pain NRS scores on POD 30 and 90 did not differ significantly in the depression group (P > 0.05), whereas a significant decrease was observed in the non-depression group (P < 0.001).

The impact of preoperative depressive symptoms on SF-36 scores showed a complex dynamic trajectory after surgery. The depression group had significantly lower scores in vitality, role-emotional, and mental health at all time points (P < 0.001). By POD 90, the depression group had significantly higher scores than the non-depression group in physical function, role-physical, general health, and social function (P < 0.05). However, they still had significantly lower scores in vitality, role-emotional, and mental health (P < 0.001). The total score was significantly different between the groups preoperatively and at POD 30 (P < 0.001), and the difference remained at POD 90 (P < 0.05), although it was reduced (Table 3).

On the SRS-22 scale, the depression group had lower scores in function (18.73 vs. 20.92, P < 0.001), pain (19.38 vs. 20.98, P < 0.01), self-image (18.52 vs. 21.03, P < 0.001), mental health (13.18 vs. 20.95, P < 0.001), and satisfaction (7.34 vs. 8.35, P < 0.001). Discussion

This prospective cohort study systematically revealed the impact of preoperative depressive symptoms on postoperative pain trajectories in AIS patients. Although preoperative depressive symptoms did not have a direct effect on APSP, they significantly exacerbated pain intensities at rest and during movement, presenting a unique pain trajectory characterized by a high baseline and steady relief. We further demonstrated that preoperative depressive symptoms were the strongest independent predictor of CPSP, with an effect size far greater than that of traditional clinical variables. Superior to the analgesia-centered approach for controlling postoperative pain, an integrative psychological and analgesic intervention centered on preoperative depressive symptoms brought great benefits to AIS patients.

While the incidence of APSP was comparable in AIS patients, whether with preoperative depressive symptoms or not, those in the depression group suffered more from pain at rest and during movement. Compared to the persistent postoperative pain at rest after the surgical procedures for AIS, the pain during movement was relieved over time, showing a similar pain trajectory between groups.

We considered that spinal orthopedic surgery triggered APSP, and depressive symptoms acted as a pain modulator to influence how the body perceived and managed pain, which is known as pain experience. At the macro level, depressive symptoms often relate to functional abnormalities in brain regions that dominate pain management, including the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and prefrontal cortex (PFC) [28–30]. They are also intertwined with cognitive factors such as catastrophization of pain and fear-avoidant beliefs, all of which amplify the subjective pain perception [31, 32]. At the micro-level of neurobiology, depressive symptoms exacerbate central sensitization and prolong pain signal transmission through multiple mechanisms, such as activating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA), promoting the release of inflammatory factors, impairing hippocampal neurons, and mediating synaptic plasticity changes by targeting CREB/BDNF [27, 33]. Current neurobiological evidence indicates that depressive symptoms affect endogenous pain modulation via the descending pain inhibitory pathways, manifesting as decreases in the endogenous analgesic capacity and pain threshold [34]. This consequently induces an intrinsic hyper-vigilant state or central sensitization even without exogenous stimuli [35, 36]. Our findings consistently supported this conclusion: preoperative depressive symptoms significantly increased the baseline pain level, rather than impeding the postoperative pain relief naturally associated with wound healing. Although recognized as a positive clinical sign of normal physiological recovery, an increased pain intensity during the postoperative rehabilitation of AIS demands more effective and individualized analgesic management.

A previous study demonstrated a correlation between preoperative depressive symptoms and increased postoperative opioid consumption [37]. In contrast, we did not observe a significant difference in the MME during the first five days post-operatively between groups. The heightened pain perception associated with depressive symptoms was likely attributed to alterations in pain processing, rather than being the result of insufficient analgesia. A monotherapy of pharmacological analgesics was inadequate to fully control postoperative pain in AIS patients with preoperative depressive symptoms, thus necessitating a shift towards a multidimensional, integrated perioperative pain management model.

CPSP is strongly associated with preoperative depressive symptoms in individuals undergoing colorectal cancer surgery, thoracic surgery, and hip arthroplasty [38–40]. In our cohort, the incidence of CPSP was significantly higher in AIS patients with preoperative depressive symptoms (CDI ≥ 19) than in those without preoperative depressive symptoms (71.88% vs. 10.4%, P < 0.001). A meta-analysis demonstrated that preoperative depression not only increases the risk of CPSP following various types of surgeries but is also linked to the duration and severity [41]. The present study only focused on a single surgical procedure for spinal deformity correction, effectively enhancing the internal validity of the causal inference by controlling for confounders arising from the heterogeneity of surgery types.

This study found that female sex is an independent risk factor for the development of CPSP in AIS patients after surgery. This finding is consistent with a large body of research on the epidemiology of postoperative chronic pain, which shows that female patients generally report a higher risk of chronic pain after many types of surgery compared to males [42, 43]. Its potential mechanisms may be multifaceted, involving complex interactions between biological and psychosocial factors. At the biological level, significant gender differences exist in the endogenous pain modulation system. Research shows that women’s diffuse noxious inhibitory control function may be weaker than that of men, which means that the central nervous system’s top-down inhibitory capacity against persistent noxious stimuli is relatively insufficient, potentially leading to an increased risk of pain sensitization and chronicity [44, 45]. In addition, fluctuations in sex hormones, particularly estrogen and progesterone, have been shown to affect neurotransmitter systems, immune responses, and peripheral and central pain signaling pathways [46], thereby potentially modulating pain perception thresholds and recovery processes during specific periods (such as puberty and the perimenstrual period). At the psychosocial level, female adolescents with AIS endure unique pressures. The physical deformities caused by scoliosis may lead female adolescents, who are in the critical period of body image formation, to experience more significant anxiety, feelings of shame, and social avoidance behaviors [47].

In addition, the number of surgical segments is another independent predictor of CPSP [48]. It is a quantitative measure of the surgical trauma load and the intensity of biomechanical changes in the spine, which is directly related to the level of nociceptive input. First, prolonged exposure to intense or repetitive harmful stimuli can lead to sustained sensitization of peripheral nociceptors, excessive release of inflammatory mediators, and exacerbation of central sensitization [49]. Moreover, an increase in the number of surgical segments directly leads to more postoperative structural complications [50], which in some patients can induce long-term mechanical discomfort or pain.

Preoperative depressive symptoms identify high-risk individuals with abnormalities in central pain processing and emotional regulation. Female sex indicates baseline pain vulnerability based on physiological and psychosocial traits. The number of surgical segments quantifies the intensity of noxious stimuli brought about by the surgical intervention itself. The combined effect of these three factors determines the patient’s ultimate risk. Based on this framework, for individuals with multiple risk factors (e.g., a female patient with depressive symptoms who requires long-segment fusion), the most intensive multidisciplinary intervention pathway should be initiated, including preoperative psychological screening and intervention, optimized intraoperative and postoperative analgesic regimens, and a structured long-term follow-up plan.

Beyond its significant effect on pain outcomes, our study revealed a highly specific impact of preoperative depressive symptoms on patients’ health-related quality of life. The primary long-term influence was not a generalized decline in physical function but was concentrated in intrinsic mental health domains (e.g., mood, vitality) and the subjective experience of pain. Impairment in these psychological dimensions persisted throughout the postoperative period, suggesting they may represent relatively stable traits not rapidly corrected by surgical intervention. This also provides a potential explanation for preoperative depression as a risk factor for chronic pain. Notably, patients with depressive symptoms exhibited a "catch-up" effect or even surpassed their non-depressed counterparts in physical function and other somatic domains postoperatively, indicating a favorable potential for physical recovery. This finding offers clear direction for clinical practice: the focus of perioperative management should be on screening for and supporting mental health, rather than assuming an unfavorable physical rehabilitation outcome.

The grouping based on longitudinal changes in CDI scores showed that the highest incidence of CPSP (100.00%) was detected in AIS patients with persistent depressive symptoms, followed by those with relieved depressive symptoms (67.90%) and those with persistent non-depressive symptoms (10.40%). This gradient association strongly indicates the critical value of early management of preoperative depressive symptoms in surgically treated individuals. Interestingly, this reversible effect has been rarely reported in adults, potentially due to weaker neural plasticity and less sensitive psychological intervention windows in adulthood than in the adolescent period.

Overall, a dynamic psychological monitoring system covering the preoperative, early postoperative, and rehabilitation periods is recommended to screen high-risk populations for postoperative pain. In addition, multimodal psychological interventions can be applied to AIS patients, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), and structured preoperative psychological education. These measures help soothe negative emotions and break the pain-depression vicious cycle [51, 52]. Currently, a multimodal analgesia program has been recommended by the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) [53, 54]. However, guidelines for psychological interventions against postoperative pain are scant. Our findings provided solid foundations for the importance of standardized depression screening as a key link involved in the risk stratification and individualized intervention for surgically treated patients.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths. We presented a comprehensive view of pain trajectories in postoperative AIS patients, demonstrating that preoperative depressive symptoms were an independent risk factor for CPSP. Moreover, dynamic changes in depressive symptoms were closely associated with the prognosis of postoperative pain in AIS patients.

This study has several limitations. First, depressive symptoms were assessed solely using the CDI scale, and self-reported results may be influenced by recall bias or emotional interference. Second, as a single-center study, the sample size was relatively restricted, and the subgroup sample size for key predictors (preoperative depression) was small. Although we employed Firth-penalized regression to correct for potential bias, this still restricts the ability to estimate effect sizes (such as odds ratios) more precisely, and its external validity requires further verification in future multi-center, large-sample cohorts. Third, although we have confirmed that the number of surgical segments is a key risk factor and conducted supplementary analyses on multiple specific surgical parameters, some surgical details (such as the type of osteotomy) and precise rehabilitation milestone data (such as the exact time of first ambulation) were not fully incorporated into the multivariate model due to inconsistent recording or extremely low incidence in the cohort. This may, to some extent, limit the precision of risk stratification. Fourth, since this study is observational in design, although known confounding factors were adjusted for through multivariable regression, some unmeasured residual confounders (such as social support and pain catastrophizing cognition) may still exist. Finally, propensity score matching was not used to achieve baseline balance because the sample loss after matching was too large (> 40%). Therefore, multivariable regression, which is widely recognized and robust in prospective cohort studies, was used for adjustment [55].

Future studies can, through large-scale, multicenter collaboration, systematically collect more detailed clinical and rehabilitation data and explore advanced algorithms such as machine learning to construct more generalizable and precise predictive models.

Conclusions

Preoperative depressive symptoms are risk factors for both APSP and CPSP in postoperative AIS patients, demonstrating a gradient association with the risk of CPSP. A nomogram that incorporates female sex, the number of surgical segments, and preoperative depressive symptoms can effectively predict CPSP in this population. An integrative psychological and analgesic intervention centered on preoperative depressive symptoms is expected to benefit high-risk AIS patients.
