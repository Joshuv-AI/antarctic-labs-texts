Distribution of metastases after radiotherapy in relation to radiation exposure fields: evaluation by 18F-FDG PET/CT
Authors: Jiucen Liang, Wei Li, Rusen Zhang, Wen Li, Peiyin Lin, Hao Nie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the distribution of metastases after radiotherapy using 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT). 67 patients with 501 new metastases revealed by 18F-FDG PET/CT after radiotherapy were included in this retrospective study. The metastases were divided into radiation area (RA) and non-radiation area (NRA), based on their locations inside or outside the radiation exposure field. Patients were divided into two groups based on the comparison of lesions number between RA and NRA: group A (NRA ≤ RA) and group B(NRA > RA). The distribution characteristics of metastases and their relationship with clinical data were analyzed. 67 patients were included in the study. A total of 501 lesions were detected: 122 in RA and 379 in NRA. 28 patients resulted in group A and 39 in group B. Patients with lesion number (LN) ≤ 5 were primarily assigned to Group A (56.1%, 23/41), whereas those with LN > 5 were primarily assigned to Group B (80.8%, 21/26). A statistically significant difference in lesion count was observed between the two groups (χ2 = 8.809, P = 0.003).Among 19 patients with RA = 0, 17 had LN ≤ 5 and 2 had LN > 5. In contrast, among 25 patients with NRA = 0, 10 had LN ≤ 5 and 15 had LN > 5. A significant difference was noted between patients with RA = 0 and those with NRA = 0 (χ2 = 4.728, P = 0.030). An notable characteristic in the distribution of metastases following radiotherapy is their tendency to occur outside the irradiation area. This indicated that the radiotherapy exposure area acquires a certain tumor growth inhibitory effect, potentially lasting over time. Based on these observations, there may be potential for developing novel preventive irradiation techniques.Keywords: 18F-FDG, PET/CT, Radiotherapy, Ionizing radiation, MetastasisSubject terms: Cancer imaging, Radiotherapy, Metastasis

Introduction

Radiation therapy is a routine effective treatment for cancer, which has been used in clinical practice for more than a century. Radiotherapy combined with surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy and hormonal therapy has become one of the important modalities for cancer treatment1. The recurrence and metastasis of tumors after radiotherapy continue to pose a significant challenge to patient prognosis, prompting research into their associated factors. Due et al.2 have revealed that, in the context of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, the majority of recurrences after radiotherapy occur in the 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positive uptake areas or the solid tumor. However, few previous studies have focused on the distribution characteristics of new metastases after radiotherapy, and the relevant mechanisms are not clear.

The alterations in the microenvironment induced by radiotherapy are intricate. Frequently, inflammation or fibrosis caused by ionizing radiation leads to modifications in tissue components, including exudation, edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrinosis3. In addition, ionizing radiation possesses the capacity to alter the pro-immunogenic components within the microenvironment, exemplified by an elevation in the expression of neoantigens, an augmentation of intracellular peptide repertories, and the modulation of specific T cells populations4. However, the influence of these microenvironment changes post-radiotherapy on the distribution of new formed metastatic lesions remains an unresolved question to date.

In the present study, tumor patients who underwent radiotherapy were followed up using 18F-FDG Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET/CT). The whole-body distribution of new metastatic lesions post-radiotherapy and its relationship with the irradiation areas were recorded in detail. Furthermore, the relevant influencing factors of the distribution of new lesions post-radiotherapy were further analyzed.

Results

Patient population

A total of 67 patients were enrolled in the present study, 24 males and 43 females (age range: 26–77 years, median: 54). PET/CT revealed 501 metastatic lesions, predominantly from nasopharyngeal cancer (30.9%, 155/501) and cervical cancer (27.3%, 137/501). Each patient had 1 ~ 61 lesions, with a median of 2. According to the comparison of the lesion number between RA and NRA, all 67 patients were divided into group A (28 patients, 41.8%) and group B (39 patients, 58.2%). Based on the primary tumor location, patients were classified into three subgroups: head and neck cancer (24 cases), chest cancer (23 cases), and abdominal cancer (20 cases). A significantly higher proportion of females were found in the abdominal cancers (P = 0.000). Chest cancers received significantly higher radiation dose and dose/fraction (P = 0.005, 0.009). In contrast, head and neck cancers had a higher incidence of squamous carcinoma (P = 0.012). However, there were no significant differences among these three subgroups in terms of age, TNM stage, fraction, and time to last radiation.

Distribution of metastatic lesions in radiation area (RA) and non-radiation area (NRA)

Among 501 metastatic lesions, 122 (24.4%) were located in RA (range 0 ~ 14, median 1), and 379 (75.6%) were in NRA (range 0 ~ 60, median 4). Notably, the highest number（14） of RA lesions was observed in a patient with endometrial cancer, while the highest number of NRA lesions (60) was found in a patient with cervical cancer. There were 28(41.8%) patents with more lesions in RA than in NRA (NRA ≤ RA), including 19 patients with 0 lesions in NRA, which were classified as group A (Figs. 1, 2). The remaining 39 (58.2%) patients had more lesions in NRA than in RA (NRA > RA), including 25 patients with 0 lesions in RA, which were classified as group B (Figs. 3, 4). The detailed clinical characteristics of these patients were shown in Table 5. There was only a significant difference in lesion number between them (χ2 = 4.728, P = 0.030). Discussion

In recent years, 18F-FDG PET/CT has been widely used in tumor staging, evaluation of treatment response, and prognosis prediction. This imaging modality detects tumor lesions high sensitively due to the distinct glucose metabolism of malignant tumors compared to surrounding tissues. It also accurately locates these lesions through fusion images combining anatomical and functional information. In addition, PET/CT has the advantages of systemic examination6,7. Therefore, PET/CT was selected in the present study to evaluate the systemic distribution of metastatic lesions post-radiotherapy.

In this study, 501 metastases detected by PET/CT exhibited a clear distribution pattern, predominantly located in NRA (75.6%, 379/501) versus RA (24.4%, 122/501). Among all patients, the majority (58.2%, 39/67) were attributed to group B, characterized by more lesions in NRA compared to RA. Some previous studies on postoperative radiotherapy for breast cancer have similarly observed that recurrent cancer foci tend to occur outside the radiotherapy area8–10. The purpose of radiotherapy is to eliminate the tumor focus and kill cancer cells in RA. When no new lesions are found in RA during the follow-up, radiation therapist typically conclude that the treatment has achieved the desired effect. However, this overlooks the possibility that potential cancer cells may persist in NRA, where new metastatic foci may develop, leading to the observed metastatic distribution patterns. Furthermore, this highlights the necessity to consider strategies for eradicating or controlling these NRA potential cancer cells during radiotherapy. Compared to the localized treatment limitations of external beam radiotherapy, internal radionuclide therapy in nuclear medicine provides whole-body ionizing radiation, offering great potential for eliminating latent cancer cells. Current researches are exploring the feasibility of utilizing radionuclide therapy as a supplementary treatment to external beam radiotherapy. The combination of radionuclide therapy and external beam radiotherapy has been reported in melanoma11 and glioblastoma12 models.

However, considering the time factor, the presence of potential cancer cells in NRA cannot fully explain this distribution pattern. While, it may be tentatively applied to short-term phenomenon, it is infeasible for long-term observations. During the extended follow-up period post-radiotherapy, potential cancer cells in NRA have sufficient time to recirculate to RA and proliferate into detectable masses. Furthermore, this study further indicated that regardless of whether the time to last radiation was more or less than 24 months, the metastases were mainly located in NRA, with comparable proportion (59.0% and 57.1%). Even on some patients’ PET/CT images, a district boundary between NRA and RA was evident, highlighting the significant difference in lesion number. These findings suggest a higher propensity for lesion formation in the NRA and a sustained inhibition of lesion growth in the RA. Nevertheless, this phenomenon of “tumor suppression in radiation area” has not been specially reported, and its underlying mechanism remains elusive.

The findings of this study further implied that radiotherapy not only directly kill tumor cells, but also endows RA with a sustained ability to inhibit cancer cells growth. This discovery holds immense significance for clinical radiotherapy practice, suggesting the potential development of a novel radiotherapy technology. By employing preventive irradiation, this technology could enhance the tumor suppressive capacity of certain crucial organs or regions, thereby mitigating the risk of tumor onset. It is different from the conventional prophylactic irradiation techniques currently used in clinic, which primarily target the eradication of potential cancer cells and have limited clinical applications. The relevant reports mainly focus on prophylactic brain irradiation13. Thus, this emerging preventive irradiation technology represents a significance advancement in radiotherapy technology and could expand the clinical utility of radiotherapy. Therefore, the phenomenon of “tumor suppression in radiation area” warrants extensive research, and its underlying mechanisms deserve thorough analysis and discussion.

The pivotal "seed and soil" theory of tumor metastasis posits that the growth of cancer cells (seeds) into tumor lesions is intimately tied to the nature of the microenvironment “soil”5,14. Consequently, the microenvironment’s evolution and immune activation induced by radiotherapy may be the key to the generation of tumor inhibition ability in RA. Some scholars15,16 believed that radiotherapy can trigger the inhibition effect of regulatory T cells, which down-regulate the anticancer immune response, while inducing CD8 + and CD4 + T cells to bolster the immune system’s surveillance to tumor cells. Eric A. Reits17 found that a dose-dependent increase in MHC I gene expression on the cell surface following radiation, enhancing tumor cells’ sensitivity to CTL lysis. Other researchers18 proposed that high-dose (therapeutic) radiation may prevent tumor recurrence or metastasis by altering the local tissues microenvironment. Numerous studies and evidence suggested that radiotherapy not only directly impacts the primary tumor but may also reverse immunosuppressive barriers in the tumor microenvironment, restoring the primary tumor’s role as an immune source center19,20. However, extensive literature also documents an immunosuppressive mechanism in the post-radiotherapy microenvironment. Tissue hypoxia after radiotherapy upregulates hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α expression, reducing radiotherapy sensitivity and facilitating cancer cell escape21,22. Additionally, the activation of cell survival signaling pathway and the accumulation of immunosuppressive factors IL-6 and IL-10 post-radiotherapy can induce local immune resistance3. Therefore, microenvironment-mediated immune activation and suppression coexist after radiotherapy. Prior studies have not clearly elucidated the key factors leading to sustained tumor suppression in RA, warranting further exploration.

In addition, an interesting phenomenon was also observed in this study. Patients with ≤ 5 lesion were primarily assigned to group A, whereas those with > 5 lesions were predominantly catergorized into group B. This finding hints at a potential correlation between the manifestation of “tumor suppression in radiation area” on PET/CT and the lesion number, which becomes more evident with an increasing number of lesion. However, validation through extensive prospective clinical data is still necessary.

There were several limitations in this study. First, the small patient cohort may undermine the statistical power of the present findings. Second, all metastases were solely identified by 18F-FDG PET/CT, without confirmation through stereotactic biopsy. Lastly, the sample was heterogeneous due to the inclusion of various tumor pathologies.

Conclusions

A notable characteristic in the distribution of metastases following radiotherapy is their tendency to occur outside the irradiation area. This indicated that the radiotherapy exposure area acquires a certain tumor growth inhibitory effect, potentially lasting over time. Based on these observations, there may be potential for developing novel preventive irradiation techniques. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further investigation.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of Guangzhou Institute of Cancer Research and the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the institutional review board of Guangzhou Institute of Cancer Research.

Patient population

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included in this study if they1had a pathological confirmation of malignant tumor2 had undergone radiotherapy, either alone or in combination with other treatments (surgery and/or chemotherapy), and achieved complete remission3, were followed up by 18F-FDG PET/CT three months after radiotherapy4, had been revealed new metastases by PET/CT between January 2019 and August 2021.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded from this study if they1had other new malignancies2, had received leukocyte promotion therapy within 4 weeks3, lacked of information about clinical data.

Finally, a total of 67 patients passed through the screening and were enrolled in this study.

PET/CT imaging

On the 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging, all patients underwent hybrid PET/CT scanner(General Electric Company,710) ,18F-FDG was provided by Guangzhou Atomic High-tech Co. LTD, and its radiochemical purity is > 90%. All patients were required to fast for at least 5 h before PET/CT scanning to maintain fasting whole blood glucose level of 3.9 ~ 6.1 mmol/L. 18F-FDG (0.1 mCi/kg) was injected intravenously, and PET/CT scan was performed after 45 min rest in the supine position. A non-contrast-enhanced low-dose CT was collected first. The parameters are set as tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 80 ~ 300 mA, matrix 512 × 512 and section thickness 3.75 mm. PET was then performed in a 3-D mode. The images were reconstructed with a voxel size of 3.15 × 3.15 × 1.87mm3, and an object space of 192 × 192 × 117. All PET data were measured photon attenuation, corrected for dead time and attenuation. Finally, the images were processed with the fusion of PET and CT on the GE Healthcare AW VolumeShare.

Image analysis

All images were transferred in the dedicated diagnostic software (Taxus Medivoly Viewer), and all PET/CT images were analyzed independently by two nuclear medicine physicians with more than 10 years of experiences. Any differences of opinion shall be settled by consensus through consultation.

According to the CT image scope of the ionizing radiation exposure field from the initial radiotherapy, the body was divided into two distinct areas, the radiation area (RA) and the non-radiation area (NRA). Reviewers were tasked with assessing the presence of metastatic lesions and meticulously documenting the numbers of lesions in both areas. Subsequently, patients were divided into two groups based on the comparison of lesions number between RA and NRA: group A (NRA ≤ RA) and group B(NRA > RA). The ratio of lesion numbers between NRA and RA were calculated for each patient.

Clinical data

All patients were collected the clinical data as follows: age, gender, radiation dose, fraction, dose/fraction, time to last radiation, radiation exposure field, previous treatment, and primary tumor (① Head and neck cancer, ② chest cancer, and ③ abdominal cancer).

Statistical analysis

The continuous data were described by mean and standard deviation, and the differences between groups were analyzed by T-test (normal distribution) or rank sum test (non normal distribution). Categorical data was analyzed by chi-square test. All analyses were performed using the SPSS.22.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). P-value less than 0.05 were considered as significant difference.
