ExPO: an exposure-conditioned neural operator for L1000 signature prediction
Authors: Austin Spadaro, Alok Sharma, Iman Dehzangi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Experimental profiling of drug–cell transcriptomic responses over dose and time is sparse and irregular, complicating discovery. We present ExPO, an exposure-conditioned neural operator that predicts full L1000 (978-gene) z-score signatures for a given compound–cell context as a continuous function of exposure. ExPO ifanstantiates a DeepONet, fusing ChemBERTa-2 molecular embeddings (with LoRA adaptation) and a trunk over sinusoidal Fourier features, so signatures can be evaluated at arbitrary dose–time pairs without regridding. Training is discovery-aligned, combining robust regression with a two-list listwise objective to optimize early-rank gene ordering, plus lightweight priors (Sobolev smoothness and an optional dose-monotonicity penalty) for pharmacologic plausibility. On a scaffold-held-out CMap/L1000 benchmark, ExPO improves over strong baselines in both accuracy and ranking (MAE 0.83 vs 0.89, Spearman 0.52 vs 0.48 vs DeepCE; NDCG@50↑/↓ 0.74/0.72 vs 0.71/0.69 for CIGER), tolerates withheld exposures (ΔMAE + 0.03 for interior interpolation; + 0.07 at edges, reduced by − 0.02 with the monotonicity prior), and transfers across cell lines (LCL-O MAE 0.90 vs 0.95; ρ 0.44 vs 0.40). Quantile heads with conformal calibration yield reliable uncertainty (test PICP 0.80/0.90), and filtering the least-confident 20% reduces MAE by ~ 18%. By modeling exposure as a field rather than discrete buckets, ExPO delivers accurate, rank-faithful, and calibrated signatures for in-silico exploration of dose–time surfaces. ExPO as a standalone predictor, along with its source code and benchmark dataset, is available at https://github.com/MLBC-lab/ExPO. Introduction

Small-molecule perturbation profiling has become an important tool for mechanism-of-action analysis, drug repurposing, and compound prioritization. Large resources such as the Connectivity Map and LINCS made this strategy practical at scale through the L1000 assay, which measures a standardized set of 978 landmark transcripts across diverse perturbational conditions [1, 2]. Newer high-throughput transcriptomic assays, including DRUG-seq, have further broadened the experimental landscape for compound screening [3]. However, L1000 remains a central resource for computational modeling for its breadth, standardization, and extensive use in perturbational benchmarking. In this work, we therefore focus on predicting L1000 signatures across dose and time for unmeasured drug–cell exposures.

Recent machine-learning studies have approached L1000 signature prediction from several complementary directions. Regression-oriented models such as DeepCE and MultiDCP predict perturbational gene-expression responses from molecular structure and cellular context, with MultiDCP further extending the setting to dose-dependent prediction [4, 5]. Ranking-oriented methods such as CIGER and DIGERA instead emphasize the ordering of up- and down-regulated genes, reflecting the fact that perturbational signatures are often consumed as ranked lists in enrichment-style and virtual-screening workflows [6, 7]. More recent generative and representation-learning approaches, including TranSiGen and PRnet, aim to reconstruct or infer chemical-induced transcriptional profiles for unseen perturbations and conditions [8, 9]. Together, these studies place perturbational signature prediction within a broader multimodal biological machine-learning setting in which chemical structure, cellular context, and functional transcriptomic readouts are integrated to support downstream biological inference.

Importantly, the practical value of predicted perturbational signatures is not captured by pointwise accuracy alone. In the L1000 setting, these signatures are used to support connectivity analysis, ranked-gene comparison, hypothesis generation, mechanism-of-action inference, and compound prioritization [1, 2, 10]. More broadly, transcriptomic perturbation readouts are increasingly used in downstream decision contexts, including adverse-event prediction, toxicity modeling, target and mechanism assessment, and early drug-discovery prioritization, where the usefulness of a model depends on whether its outputs preserve the biological structure needed for action rather than only minimizing average prediction error [11–14]. These considerations help explain why ranking-based and exposure-aware formulations are especially relevant in perturbation modeling: in many downstream settings, preserving the relative ordering of strongly up- and down-regulated genes, as well as the shape of the dose–response relationship, is at least as important as minimizing average gene-wise error [5–7, 9]. These considerations suggest that perturbation models should be motivated and evaluated by agreement with measured expression values as well as by whether they preserve the ranked and exposure-dependent structure needed for discovery-oriented use.

From this perspective, neural operators provide an appealing conceptual framework. Conventional neural networks typically learn mappings between finite-dimensional inputs and outputs, whereas neural operators are designed to learn mappings between functions defined over continuous domains [15, 16]. DeepONet offers a practical realization of this idea by learning how an underlying system representation gives rise to outputs at arbitrary queried coordinates [17]. In the perturbation setting, the object of interest is naturally exposure-dependent: for a given drug–cell context, one would ideally model a single expression signature as well as a response function over dose and time. An operator-learning view is therefore well aligned with the structure of the problem, since it supports reasoning over irregular exposure grids and interpolation to unseen coordinates without reducing dose and time to a small set of disconnected bins [15, 17].

Motivated by these gaps, we present ExPO, an exposure-conditioned framework for L1000 signature prediction. Our central objective is to model transcriptomic response as a continuous function of exposure while preserving the aspects of the signature that matter most for discovery use, particularly ranked-gene behavior across sparse and irregular dose–time landscapes. As a result, we design our model for generating exposure-aware perturbational signatures that are more usable in downstream biological and pharmacological workflows instead of simply as another predictor of L1000 values.

Materials and methods

Benchmark dataset

We assembled small-molecule perturbation profiles from the Connectivity Map (LINCS/CMap) measured with the L1000 assay. Each record corresponds to a unique tuple of compound, cell line, exposure time, and dose. For the landmark-gene panel (978 genes), we used the z-score outputs provided by the L1000 processing pipeline. When multiple biological or technical replicates were available for an identical exposure tuple, we aggregated measurements by the within-group median to reduce the influence of outliers and measurement noise [18]. All replicate profiles belonging to the same compound–cell–time–dose combination were kept in the same data split to preclude leakage.

The connectivity map and the L1000 assay

The L1000 platform quantifies a reduced set of landmark genes that are jointly informative of global transcriptomic changes. For each perturbation, normalized z-scores capture the deviation from matched controls; positive values indicate up-regulation and negative values indicate down-regulation. We retained these z-scores in their native scale to preserve interpretability and comparability across compounds and conditions. Metadata for compound identity (including SMILES), cell line, time, and dose were standardized and used as model covariates.

Data access and processing

Compound structures were represented using the canonical_smiles field provided by LINCS/CMap, corresponding to the canonical isomeric SMILES of the parent standardized compound; according to the LINCS/DCIC standardization workflow, this parent representation is generated after removal of predefined salts/addends, charge normalization, tautomer standardization [19, 20]. We removed exact duplicate profiles and harmonized compound synonyms to the canonical identifier before constructing splits. Bemis–Murcko scaffolds were computed from the standardized structures and used to control out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluations [21]. No additional per-gene normalization was applied beyond the L1000 pipeline’s standardization, and the original landmark-gene ordering and indexing were preserved throughout.

Molecular and cell representations

We used a SMILES Transformer (ChemBERTa-2 class) as the molecular encoder to obtain a dense embedding for each compound. This choice was made a priori for three reasons: (1) large-scale chemical pretraining provides strong priors in low-label regimes; (2) randomized-SMILES augmentation enhances robustness without additional graph preprocessing; and (3) the SMILES pipeline avoids RDKit graph failures observed for a small fraction of salts/tautomers. The encoder comprised 12 Transformer blocks with hidden size 512 and 8 attention heads. To stabilize fine-tuning, the lower six blocks were frozen and the upper six were adapted using LoRA adapters (rank 8, α = 16) applied to query/key/value projections. SMILES were canonicalized and, with probability 0.5 per batch, replaced by randomized enumerations to reduce overfitting.

Cell context was represented by a learned embedding of dimension 512, initialized from a normal distribution and trained end-to-end. When basal transcriptomic summaries were available, they were projected to 512 dimensions by a two-layer MLP (512 → 512 with GELU and dropout 0.1) and added to the cell embedding. Features were derived from exposure time and dose (log-transformed with a small offset) as 16-frequency sinusoidal Fourier features to provide smooth positional encodings over the continuous exposure domain [22].

Since ExPO produces continuous gene-level z-score predictions, we complemented the thresholded ternary summaries with one-vs-rest ROC analyses computed directly from the scaffold-held-out test predictions. For the up-regulated class, genes with observed z ≥ 1.0 were treated as positives and the raw predicted z-score was used as the decision score. For the down-regulated class, genes with observed z ≤ − 1.0 were treated as positives and the negated predicted z-score was used as the decision score, so that larger values consistently indicated stronger evidence for the positive class. AUC-ROC values and their 95% confidence intervals were estimated from the test-set predictions themselves using DeLong’s method, rather than from variation across random seeds. To assess whether ExPO improved discrimination relative to the principal continuous baseline, we performed paired DeLong tests comparing ExPO and DeepCE on the same held-out instances. ROC-based results are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Results and discussion

Unless noted otherwise, we report on the scaffold-held-out split. Paired comparisons use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-compound metrics with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across families of tests [32–35]. The underlying benchmark was partitioned at the unique-compound level into scaffold-held-out training (n = 3,960), validation (n = 510), and test (n = 640) splits. Since the compared methods were developed under different learning objectives, we interpret the baseline comparisons by method family rather than as a claim that every model was optimized for the identical ranked-output objective. In particular, ExPO includes an explicit two-list ListNet term, whereas several comparison methods were originally proposed for regression or generative reconstruction. We did not retrofit those baselines with additional ranking-aware losses, since it would materially modify the original methods and require architecture-specific redesign and retuning. Accordingly, comparison to ranking-oriented methods such as CIGER is the most direct test of whether ExPO’s advantage persists beyond the use of ranked supervision itself, whereas comparison to regression and generative baselines is intended to illustrate the practical benefit of discovery-aligned training when downstream use depends on ranked perturbational signatures.

To further characterize the scaffold-held-out evaluation setting, we examined the chemical space induced by the split, shown in Supplementary Figures S1–S3. The low-dimensional projection indicates that held-out compounds remain embedded within the broader chemical landscape represented in the training data, with local shifts consistent with scaffold-level separation rather than simple random partitioning. This pattern is supported by the similarity analyses: the nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity from test compounds to the training set is moderate overall, and the full test–train pairwise similarity distribution is shifted toward lower values relative to the train–train distribution. Together, these observations are consistent with the intended interpretation of the benchmark as a chemically nontrivial out-of-distribution setting, while remaining within a plausible domain for generalization.

List fidelity is the metric most aligned with discovery workflows (e.g., GSEA, MOA recovery) [10]. Improvements at early ranks (higher NDCG and RBO) matter disproportionately: the top-K lists often seed downstream validation. The fact that continuous predictions yield better classification after thresholding (Fig. 2c) suggests the regression head retains informative ordering even when binarized, a useful property for pipelines that still require discrete up/down calls. Gains relative to CIGER indicate that ExPO’s advantage is not explained solely by the presence of ranked supervision, since CIGER is also explicitly ranking-oriented. At the same time, removing the two-list ListNet term from ExPO reduces NDCG@50 and MCC without materially affecting MAE (Fig. 5A, Table 5), showing that ranked supervision contributes meaningfully within our model. We therefore interpret the improvement conservatively: the results support the value of combining continuous exposure modeling with discovery-aligned supervision. However, it does not imply that all between-model differences can be attributed to the listwise objective alone. This decoupling is expected: pointwise losses control calibration and error magnitude, while listwise losses shape the ordering. The ablation underscores why optimizing explicitly for ranked-list fidelity is necessary when downstream tasks consume ranked outputs.

Comparisons to recent generative models

As summarized in Fig. 1a, PRnet attains MAE = 0.87 [0.86–0.88] and ρ = 0.50 [0.49–0.51] on the scaffold split, with TranSiGen similar. NDCG behavior in Fig. 2a, b and set overlap in Fig. 2c remain lower than our operator for the generative comparators considered here. We emphasize, however, that these methods were not originally designed around the same ranked-output objective used by ExPO. For that reason, we interpret these comparisons primarily as showing that a model trained explicitly for discovery-oriented ranked signatures can provide better early-rank fidelity in this setting, rather than as a claim that the generative baselines were optimized under an equivalent learning objective. The comparison to CIGER therefore remains the more direct reference point for ranked-output fairness. Our results suggest that combining operator modeling (to leverage exposure geometry) with listwise supervision is a better match to the discovery objective than likelihood alone.

Conclusion

We introduce an exposure-conditioned neural operator for transcriptional response prediction. By modeling responses as continuous functions over dose and time, and training with a listwise objective that prioritizes early-rank genes, the model yields consistent gains over discrete, condition-specific baselines. On scaffold-held-out compounds, our approach achieved MAE 0.83 and Spearman’s ρ 0.52, improving on DeepCE (MAE 0.89, ρ 0.48). Discovery-relevant ranking improved as well, with NDCG@50↑/↓ 0.74/0.72 and Jaccard@50 0.36/0.34, exceeding CIGER and thresholded DeepCE. Under LCL-O evaluation, the operator retained an advantage (ρ 0.44 vs 0.40; MAE 0.90 vs 0.95). Treating exposure as a continuous geometry produced smoother error surfaces with modest penalties for withheld interior points (ΔMAE + 0.03) and manageable edge extrapolation (ΔMAE + 0.07), while a weak dose-monotonicity prior further reduced edge error (− 0.02). Uncertainty estimates were usable in practice: conformal calibration delivered 0.80/0.90 coverage, and discarding the least-confident 20% of coordinates reduced MAE by ~ 18%. Together, these results indicate that a neural operator formulation, augmented with light biological priors and a listwise objective, improves both pointwise accuracy and the discovery utility of ranked gene lists. Remaining challenges (near-neutral responses and genes with high cross-line variability) point to value in richer cell context and targeted prospective assays. An additional limitation is that biological coherence was assessed primarily through qualitative pathway and case-study analyses. For our future work, we aim at including formal cross-compound pathway-level metrics, such as NES correlation, to quantify this more directly. However, our proposed framework provides a scalable, calibrated, and decision-ready foundation for virtual profiling.
