Supplement to “RepLoRA: Reparameterizing Low-Rank Adaptation via the Perspective of Mixture of Experts”
Tuan Truong, Chau Nguyen, Huy Nguyen, Minh Le, Trung Le, Nhat Ho

Abstract
Low-rank Adaptation (LoRA) has emerged as a powerful method for fine-tuning large-scale foundation models. Despite its popularity, the theoretical understanding of LoRA has remained limited. This paper presents a theoretical analysis of LoRA by examining its connection to the Mixture of Experts models. Under this framework, we show that simple reparameterizations of the LoRA matrices can notably accelerate the low-rank matrix estimation process. In particular, we prove that reparameterization can reduce the data needed to achieve a desired estimation error from an exponential to a polynomial scale. Motivated by this insight, we propose Rep arameterized Lo w- R ank A daptation (RepLoRA), which incorporates lightweight MLPs to reparameterize the LoRA matrices. Extensive experiments across multiple domains demonstrate that RepLoRA consistently outperforms vanilla LoRA. Notably, with limited data, RepLoRA surpasses LoRA by a margin of up to 40.0% and achieves LoRA’s performance with only 30.0% of the training data, highlighting both the theoretical and empirical robustness of our PEFT method.

Introduction

With the rapid growth in data availability and computational resources, large-scale models trained on extensive datasets have demonstrated remarkable generalization capabilities, enabling successful applications across language, vision, and multi-modal tasks
(
Dosovitskiy, 2021
;
Radford et al., 2021
;
Touvron et al., 2023
)
. However, fully fine-tuning such models for specific downstream tasks can be prohibitively expensive. To address this challenge, several parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods
(
Houlsby et al., 2019
;
Lester et al., 2021
;
Jia et al., 2022
)
have emerged, facilitating effective adaptation of large pre-trained models by adjusting a minimal set of parameters while keeping most of the backbone frozen. Among these methods,
Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA)
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
stands out for its simplicity and effectiveness and has been successfully applied across diverse domains
(
Li et al., 2022
;
Qin et al., 2023
;
Taori et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2024a
)
. Despite its successes, theoretical understanding of LoRA has remained limited, hindering our ability to optimize its performance further.

Building on the recent finding
(
Le et al., 2024
)

(
Vaswani, 2017
)
and the mixture of experts (MoE)
(
Jacobs et al., 1991
;
Jordan & Jacobs, 1994
)

(
Li & Liang, 2021
;
Le et al., 2025
)
, which represents low-rank matrices as the output of an MLP, can theoretically enhance the performance of LoRA. Specifically, our analysis reveals that this reparameterization can reduce the data needed to achieve a desired estimation error from an exponential scale to a polynomial scale, thereby substantially improving sample efficiency. Based on these insights, we introduce
R
eparameterized
Lo
w-
R
ank
A
daptation
(RepLoRA) - a novel PEFT method reparameterizes low-rank matrices through a lightweight MLP.

We conducted extensive experiments across multiple domains, including image, video, language, and multi-modal tasks. Our results indicate that RepLoRA consistently demonstrates better performance than vanilla LoRA. When only a tiny fraction of the training data is subsampled, RepLoRA improves up to
40%
over LoRA. This highlights the robustness and effectiveness of our method, both theoretically and empirically. Moreover, the MLP used for reparameterization can be discarded after training, ensuring that our approach remains as efficient as the standard counterparts at inference time.

2
Related Works

Parameter-Efficient Fine-tuning (PEFT).

With the recent rise of large models, PEFT methods are growing in popularity for their ability to fine-tune large-scale models by training a relatively small number of parameters for adapting to specific downstream tasks. Existing PEFT methods can be divided into three categories. The first category is referred to as
adapter-based
methods, which introduce additional trainable parameters to the frozen backbone. For example,
Series Adapter

(
Houlsby et al., 2019
)
proposes adding linear modules in sequence to the existing layer, or
Parallel Adapter

(
He et al., 2022
)
proposes integrating these modules in parallel. The second category of PEFT methods is
Prompt-based
methods that add extra trainable soft tokens, referred to as prompts, to the input
(
Lester et al., 2021
;
Razdaibiedina et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023
)
. A weakness of these methods is that they increase inference latency compared to the original model.

Low-Rank Adaptation
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
.

LoRA and its variants are among the third category of the PEFT method, which is well-known for its simplicity and for not adding extra inference burden. To fine-tune the linear layers of a large model, LoRA applies low-rank matrices to approximate the weight changes and then merges them with the pre-trained weights for inference. A recent variant of LoRA is DoRA
(
Liu et al., 2024b
)
, which proposes to decompose the weight change into a learnable magnitude and directional component. Another example is AdaLoRA
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
, which parameterizes the incremental updates in the form of singular value decomposition and prunes less significant singular values for more efficient updates. Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT)
(
Qiu et al., 2023
)
exploits the orthogonal factorization to fine-tune diffusion models. Recently, VeRA
(
Kopiczko et al., 2024
)
significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to LoRA by using learnable scaling vectors to adjust a shared pair of frozen random matrices across layers. Notably, our method, which will be proposed in the following few sections, also falls within this category, and we validate its efficacy alongside LoRA and its variants through theoretical analysis and comprehensive experimentation.

Mixture of Experts.
Expanding upon the foundational principles of mixture models
(
Jacobs et al., 1991
;
Jordan & Jacobs, 1994
)
, earlier research
(
Eigen et al., 2014
;
Shazeer et al., 2017
)
established the Mixture of Experts (MoE) layer as a crucial mechanism for effectively scaling model capacity. Over time, MoE models have gained significant recognition due to their versatility across multiple domains, including large language models
(
Du et al., 2022
;
Zhou et al., 2023
)
, computer vision
(
Riquelme et al., 2021
;
Puigcerver et al., 2024
)
, multimodal learning
(
Han et al., 2024
)
, and multi-task learning
(
Ma et al., 2018
)
. Recent studies have focused on analyzing the convergence rates of expert estimation in MoE models, exploring different assumptions and configurations related to gating mechanisms and expert functions. For instance, in the context of softmax gating,
Nguyen et al. (2023)
;
Nguyen et al. (2024a)
;
Nguyen et al. (2024b)
revealed that the expert estimation rates are shaped by the solvability of polynomial systems arising from interactions between gating and expert parameters. More recently,
Nguyen et al. (2024d)
;
Nguyen et al. (2024c)
;
Nguyen et al. (2025)
employed least squares estimation to establish an identifiability condition for expert functions, particularly in feedforward networks with nonlinear activation functions. These findings indicate that under these conditions, estimation rates improve significantly compared to models relying on polynomial experts.

3
Preliminaries

This section briefly reviews the background for multi-head self-attention in transformers, low-rank adaptation, and a mixture of expert models.

Multi-head Self-attention.
We begin by revisiting the architecture of the multi-head self-attention (MSA) layer in Transformer
(
Vaswani, 2017
;
Dosovitskiy, 2021
)
. Let
{\bm{X}}=[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
denote an input sequence of embeddings, where
N
is the sequence length and
d
denotes the embedding dimension. The MSA layer processes this sequence as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSA}({\bm{X}}_{Q},{\bm{X}}_{K},{\bm{X}}_{V})=\mathrm{Concat}({\bm{h}}_{1},...,{\bm{h}}_{m})\bm{W}^{O},

(1)

where each attention head is defined by
{\bm{h}}_{i}=\mathrm{Attention}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{i}^{Q},{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{i}^{K},{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{i}^{V})
for
i\in[m]
.
Here,
{\bm{X}}_{Q}=({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{1}^{Q},\ldots,{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{m}^{Q})
,
\mathbb{X}_{K}=({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{1}^{K},\ldots,{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{m}^{K})
, and
\mathbb{X}_{V}=({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{1}^{V},\ldots,{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{m}^{V})
are the query, key, and value matrices, respectively. Furthermore,
m
is the number of heads, and
\bm{W}^{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{md_{v}\times d}
is the output projection matrix. Each attention head
{\bm{h}}_{i}
is parameterized by
\bm{W}_{i}^{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{k}},\bm{W}_{i}^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{k}},\text{ and }\bm{W}_{i}^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{v}}
, with
d_{k}=d_{v}=\frac{d}{m}
.

Low-Rank Adaptation.
LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
has emerged as an efficient method for adapting large pre-trained transformer models to downstream tasks. Building upon the hypothesis that the updates during fine-tuning exhibit a low “intrinsic rank”, LoRA proposes to fine-tune the transformer architectures’ linear layers by incrementally updating the pre-trained weights with the product of two low-rank matrices. For a given pre-trained weight matrix
\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, LoRA represents its update as
\Delta\bm{W}={\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}
, where
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
, and
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
with
r\ll\min\{m,n\}
. Consequently, the output of the fine-tuned model is:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}=\bm{W}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}=\bm{W}_{0}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}.

(2)

During training,
\bm{W}_{0}
remains fixed, while
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are updated. Typically, LoRA adjusts the linear layers that generate transformer models’ queries and values (or keys, queries, and values). In line with prior work
(
Hu et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Xin et al., 2024
)
, here we fine-tune the query and value projection matrices, leading to the following output expression:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{LoRA}}({\bm{X}};{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})=\mathrm{Concat}(\widetilde{{\bm{h}}}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{{\bm{h}}}_{m})\bm{W}^{O},

(3)

where for each
i\in[m]
,
\widetilde{{\bm{h}}}_{i}=\mathrm{Attention}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{i}^{Q}+{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}}_{Q,i}{\bm{A}}_{Q,i},{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{i}^{K},{\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{i}^{V}+{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}}_{V,i}{\bm{A}}_{V,i})
. Here, we denote
{\bm{A}}=[{\bm{A}}_{Q},{\bm{A}}_{V}]
, and
{\bm{B}}=[{\bm{B}}_{Q},{\bm{B}}_{V}]
, where
{\bm{A}}_{Q}=({\bm{A}}_{Q,1},\ldots,{\bm{A}}_{Q,m})
, and
{\bm{A}}_{V}=({\bm{A}}_{V,1},\ldots,{\bm{A}}_{V,m})
. Likewise,
{\bm{B}}_{Q}=({\bm{B}}_{Q,1},\ldots,{\bm{B}}_{Q,m})
, and
{\bm{B}}_{V}=({\bm{B}}_{V,1},\ldots,{\bm{B}}_{V,m})
. For each head
i\in[m]
, the dimensions of these matrices are
{\bm{A}}_{Q,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{k}}
,
{\bm{B}}_{Q,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
,
{\bm{A}}_{V,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{v}}
, and
{\bm{B}}_{V,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
.

Mixture of Experts.
A mixture of experts (MoE)
(
Jacobs et al., 1991
;
Jordan & Jacobs, 1994
)
model consists of
N
expert networks,
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
for
i\in[N]
, and a gating function
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
that allocates contributions of each expert based on the input
{\bm{x}}
to the model. The output of the MoE model is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}G({\bm{x}})_{i}\cdot f_{i}({\bm{x}}),

where
G({\bm{x}})=\mathrm{softmax}(s_{1}({\bm{x}}),\cdots,s_{N}({\bm{x}}))
, and
s_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a score function. In the subsequent sections, we discuss how MoE relates to LoRA and provide a theoretical analysis of the proposed method.

4
LoRA from the perspective of MoE

Prior work
(
Le et al., 2024
;
Le et al., 2025
)
has shown that each attention head in the MSA layer can be viewed as a specialized architecture of multiple MoE models. Specifically, from Eq. (
1
), consider the output of the
l
-th head
{\bm{h}}_{l}=[{\bm{h}}_{l,1},\dots,{\bm{h}}_{l,N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{v}}
. Let
\mathbb{X}=\left[{\bm{x}}_{1}^{\top},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{N}^{\top}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
denote the concatenated input embeddings. We then define
N
experts
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
encoded within the MSA layer as follows:

\displaystyle f_{j}(\mathbb{X})={\bm{W}_{l}^{V}}^{\top}{\bm{E}}_{j}\mathbb{X}={\bm{W}_{l}^{V}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{j},

(4)

for
j\in[N]
, where the matrix
{\bm{E}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times Nd}
is such that
{\bm{E}}_{j}\mathbb{X}={\bm{x}}_{j}
. Next, we introduce
N\times N
score functions
s_{i,j}:\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
associated with these experts:

\displaystyle s_{i,j}(\mathbb{X})=\frac{\mathbb{X}^{\top}{\bm{E}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{W}_{l}^{Q}{\bm{W}_{l}^{K}}^{\top}{\bm{E}}_{j}\mathbb{X}}{\sqrt{d_{v}}}=\frac{{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{W}_{l}^{Q}{\bm{W}_{l}^{K}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{j}}{\sqrt{d_{v}}},

(5)

for
i\in[N]
and
j\in[N]
. Consequently, each output vector
{\bm{h}}_{l,i}
can be formulated as the result of an MoE model, utilizing the experts and score functions defined above:

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{l,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\exp(s_{i,j}(\mathbb{X}))}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(s_{i,k}(\mathbb{X})))}\cdot f_{j}(\mathbb{X}).

(6)

Within a pre-trained MSA layer, all parameters of these experts and score functions
\bm{W}_{l}^{Q}
,
\bm{W}_{l}^{K}
, and
\bm{W}_{l}^{V}
remain fixed. When LoRA is applied, it refines these experts and score functions with low-rank updates:

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{j}(\mathbb{X})=(\bm{W}_{l}^{V}+{\bm{B}}_{V,l}{\bm{A}}_{V,l})^{\top}{\bm{E}}_{j}\mathbb{X},

(7)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{i,j}(\mathbb{X})=\frac{\mathbb{X}^{\top}{\bm{E}}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{W}_{l}^{Q}+{\bm{B}}_{Q,l}{\bm{A}}_{Q,l}){\bm{W}_{l}^{K}}^{\top}{\bm{E}}_{j}\mathbb{X}}{\sqrt{d_{v}}},

(8)

for
i\in[N]
and
j\in[N]
. From these definitions and Eq. (
3
), the modified output of the
l
-th head
\tilde{{\bm{h}}}_{l}=[\tilde{{\bm{h}}}_{l,1},\dots,\tilde{{\bm{h}}}_{l,N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{v}}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{h}}}_{l,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\exp(\tilde{s}_{i,j}(\mathbb{X}))}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(\tilde{s}_{i,k}(\mathbb{X})))}\cdot\tilde{f}_{j}(\mathbb{X}).

(9)

From this perspective, LoRA effectively fine-tunes the pre-trained MoE models contained within each MSA head by incorporating low-rank modifications to both the expert and the score functions. Next section will leverage this MoE viewpoint to analyze the theoretical properties of LoRA.

5
Theoretical Analysis of LoRA: With and Without Reparameterization

This section presents the theoretical benefits of applying the reparameterization technique in LoRA via its connection to MoE as formulated in Section
4
. For simplicity, we will take into account only the first row of the first attention head
\tilde{{\bm{h}}}_{1,1}
specified in Eq. (
9
). Under this simplified setting, we will investigate the convergence behavior of low-rank matrices within the following MoE-based regression framework:

Problem setup.

Let
(\mathbb{X}_{1},{\bm{Y}}_{1}),(\mathbb{X}_{2},{\bm{Y}}_{2}),\ldots,(\mathbb{X}_{n},{\bm{Y}}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
be i.i.d. samples of size
n
generated from the following regression model:

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}_{i}=f_{G_{*}}(\mathbb{X}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n.

(10)

Above, we assume that
\mathbb{X}_{1},\mathbb{X}_{2},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n}
are i.i.d. samples from some probability distribution
\mu
with bounded support. Meanwhile,
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are independent Gaussian noise variables such that
\mathbb{E}[{\varepsilon_{i}}|\mathbb{X}_{i}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{i}|\mathbb{X}_{i})=\nu^{2}I_{\bar{d}}
for all
i\in[n]
. Next, the regression function
f_{G_{*}}(\cdot)
takes the form of an MoE model with
L
unknown experts, that is,

\displaystyle f_{G_{*}}(\mathbb{X}):=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+{\bm{B}}_{Q,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{Q,j}^{*}){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{j})}{D_{f}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\cdot({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+{\bm{B}}_{V,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{V,j}^{*})\mathbb{X},

(11)

where we denote

D_{f}(\mathbb{X}):=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+{\bm{B}}_{Q,k}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{Q,k}^{*}){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{k}),

while
G_{*}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\exp(c^{*}_{j^{\prime}})\delta_{({\bm{B}}_{Q,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{Q,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{B}}_{V,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{V,j^{\prime}}^{*})}
represents for a
mixing measure
, that is, a combination of Dirac measures
\delta
, associated with unknown parameters
(c_{j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{B}}_{Q,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{Q,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{B}}_{V,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{V,j^{\prime}}^{*})_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}
in the compact parameter space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times\bar{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times\bar{d}}
. In addition, we assume that the matrices
{\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times\bar{d}}
,
{\bm{M}}_{K}^{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times\bar{d}}
, and
{\bm{M}}_{V}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times\bar{d}}
are given to align with the formulation in Eq. (
9
).

With versus Without Reparametrization.
Subsequently, we establish the convergence rates of estimating the unknown low-rank matrices
\{{\bm{B}}_{Q,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{Q,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{B}}_{V,j^{\prime}}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{V,j^{\prime}}^{*}\}_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}
under two scenarios, namely without shared structures among these low-rank matrices (equivalently, without reparametrization) in Section
5.1
and with shared structures among these low-rank matrices (equivalently, with reparametrization) in Section
5.2
. Our ultimate goal is to demonstrate that sample efficiency of estimating these low-rank matrices under the shared structures setting is much better than that under the non-shared structures setting with a given error
\epsilon>0
. The theory sheds light on our design of Reparameterized LoRA (RepLoRA) in Section
6
.

5.1
Without Reparametrization: Suboptimal Sample Complexity

We begin our convergence analysis for low-rank matrices with the scenario where the LoRA reparametrization is absent. It is worth noting that we can estimate those unknown matrices via estimating the ground-truth mixing measure
G_{*}
, For that sake, we utilize the least square method
(
van de Geer, 2000
)
to obtain the following estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}\in\textnormal{arg}\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bm{Y}}_{i}-f_{G}(\mathbb{X}_{i})\Big)^{2},

(12)

where we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):=\{G=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\exp(c_{j^{\prime}})\delta_{({\bm{B}}_{Q,j^{\prime}},{\bm{A}}_{Q,j^{\prime}},{\bm{B}}_{V,j^{\prime}},{\bm{A}}_{V,j^{\prime}})}:1\leq\ell\leq L^{\prime},\ (c_{j^{\prime}},{\bm{B}}_{Q,j^{\prime}},{\bm{A}}_{Q,j^{\prime}},{\bm{B}}_{V,j^{\prime}},{\bm{A}}_{V,j^{\prime}})\in\Theta\}
the set of all mixing measures with at most
L^{\prime}
atoms. As the number of ground-truth experts
L
is typically unknown in practice, we assume that the number of fitted experts
L^{\prime}
is large enough such that
L^{\prime}>L
.
Then, to determine the convergence rates of the estimator
\widehat{G}_{n}
, we use a loss function built upon the concept of Voronoi cells
(
Manole & Ho, 2022
)
.

Voronoi loss.
Given a mixing measure
G
with
L^{\prime}>L
atoms, its Voronoi cell set
\{\mathcal{V}_{j}\equiv\mathcal{V}_{j}(G):j\in[L]\}

is generated by the atoms of
G_{*}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\mathcal{V}_{j}:=\{i\in[L^{\prime}]:\|{\bm{Z}}_{i}-{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{j}\|\leq\|{\bm{Z}}_{i}-{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\ell}\|,\forall\ell\neq j\},

where
{\bm{H}}:=({\bm{B}}_{Q},{\bm{A}}_{Q},{\bm{B}}_{V},{\bm{A}}_{V})
. Then, the Voronoi loss function used for this section is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(G,G_{*}):=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\Big|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}}\exp(c_{i})-\exp(c^{*}_{j^{\prime}})\Big|

\displaystyle+\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}}\exp(c_{i})(\|\Delta{\bm{B}}_{Q,ij^{\prime}}\|^{r}+\|\Delta{\bm{A}}_{Q,ij^{\prime}}\|^{r}

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt+\|\Delta{\bm{B}}_{V,ij^{\prime}}\|^{r}+\|\Delta{\bm{A}}_{V,ij^{\prime}}\|^{r}),

for
r\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\Delta{\bm{H}}_{ij^{\prime}}:={\bm{H}}_{i}-{\bm{H}}^{*}_{j^{\prime}}
for any
i,j^{\prime}
. Now, we are ready to study the sample efficiency of LoRA without reparametrization in Theorem
5.1
whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The following bound holds for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)\setminus\mathcal{G}_{L-1}(\Theta)}\mathbb{E}_{f_{G}}[\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(\widehat{G}_{n},{G})]\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

where
\mathbb{E}_{f_{G}}
denotes the expectation taken with respect to the product measure
f^{n}_{G}
.

Let us denote
\widehat{G}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{L_{n}}\exp(\hat{c}^{n}_{i})\delta_{(\hat{{\bm{B}}}^{n}_{Q,i},\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{n}_{Q,i},\hat{{\bm{B}}}^{n}_{V,i},\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{n}_{V,i})}
. Then, it follows from the result of Theorem
5.1
and the formulation of the loss
\mathcal{D}_{1,r}
that the convergence rates of the low-rank matrix estimators
\hat{{\bm{B}}}^{n}_{Q,i},\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{n}_{Q,i},\hat{{\bm{B}}}^{n}_{V,i},\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{n}_{V,i}
are slower than any polynomial rates
\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-1/2r})
for
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, these rates could become as slow as
\mathcal{O}_{P}(1/\log^{\tau}(n))
for some constant
\tau>0
(due to the inequality
\log(n)<n
). As a consequence, we need an exponential number of data
\mathcal{O}(\exp(\epsilon^{-1/\tau}))
to obtain the approximations of the low-rank matrices with an error
\epsilon
. This observation reflects the suboptimality of the sample complexity of the LoRA without applying the reparametrization technique.

5.2
With Reparametrization: Optimal Sample Complexity

In this section, we consider the scenario where the low-rank matrices share their structures with each other. In particular, we consider the case where the low-rank matrices are reparameterized as:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}_{Q}={\bm{A}}_{V}=\varphi_{1}({{\bm{A}}})

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}_{Q}={\bm{B}}_{V}=\varphi_{2}({{\bm{B}}}),

where
\varphi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\to\mathbb{R}^{r\times\bar{d}}
,
\varphi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\to\mathbb{R}^{r\times\bar{d}}
are some functions,
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}
are learnable matrices with given dimensions
m,m^{\prime}\geq 1
. We specifically note that, for the simplicity of the theoretical development, this formulation is simplified compared to what was used in practice because we set the low-rank matrices of queries to be equal to that of values. As we will show in this section, even with this simplified formulation, reparameterization gives superior sample complexity compared to vanilla LoRA without reparameterization.

After training, the reparameterization can be discarded, and only the low-rank matrices
{\bm{A}}_{Q},{\bm{A}}_{V},{\bm{B}}_{Q},{\bm{B}}_{V}
need to be stored. We observe that the reparameterization strategy implicitly encodes a shared structure between the query and value low-rank matrices. The primary difference compared to the original LoRA is that instead of learning the low-rank matrices separately, we reparameterize those matrices as the output of the two shared structures
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
. To study the theoretical advantages of the reparametrization technique, we focus on the following two settings of the functions
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
:

(i) Simple linear reparametrization:

\varphi_{1}({{\bm{A}}})=\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}}
and
\varphi_{2}({{\bm{B}}})=\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}}
.

(ii) Non-linear reparametrization:

\varphi_{1}({{\bm{A}}})=\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}})
and
\varphi_{2}({{\bm{B}}})=\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}})
, where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are two non-linear activation functions applied element-wise to the matrices
\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}}
and
\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}}
.

It should be noted that the above reparametrization settings can totally be generalized to the scenarios where the matrices
A_{Q}
and
A_{V}
share only the learnable matrix
A
. In particular, we can reformulate those matrices as
A_{Q}=W_{Q,1}A
and
A_{V}=W_{V,1}A
for the simple linear reparametrization and as
A_{Q}=\sigma_{1}(W_{Q,1}A)
and
A_{V}=\sigma_{1}(W_{V,1}A)
for the non-linear reparametrization. In order to tailor to these settings, it is necessary to include several terms involving parameters
W_{Q,1}
and
W_{V,1}
rather than merely parameters
W_{1}
as in the above settings. However, we realize that these terms not only provide no additional information on our convergence analysis but also make it unnecessarily complicated. Therefore, we assume without loss of generalization that
A_{Q}=A_{V}=W_{1}A
or
A_{Q}=A_{V}=\sigma_{1}(W_{1}A)
to simplify the analysis, making it more accessible.

5.2.1
Simple Linear Reparameterization

We first take into account the simple linear reparametrization where
{\bm{A}}_{Q}={\bm{A}}_{V}=\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}_{Q}={\bm{B}}_{V}=\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}}
. Under this setting, the ground-truth regression function in Eq. (
11
), which we denote now as
f_{\bar{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})
to avoid confusion, takes the following form:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\bm{W}_{2,j}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{j}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{j})}{\bar{D}_{f}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\cdot({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\bm{W}_{2,j}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{j}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{j}^{*})\mathbb{X},

(13)

where we denote
\bar{D}_{f}(\mathbb{X})=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\bm{W}_{2,k}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{k}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,k}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{k}^{*}){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{k})
, while the mixing measure is of the form
\bar{G}_{*}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\exp(c^{*}_{j^{\prime}})\delta_{\bm{W}_{2,j^{\prime}}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,j^{\prime}}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*}}
. Similar to Section
5.1
, we estimate the unknown low-rank matrices via estimating the ground-truth mixing measure
\bar{G}_{*}
using the least square method:

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{n}\in\textnormal{arg}\min_{\bar{G}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bm{Y}}_{i}-f_{\bar{G}}(\mathbb{X}_{i})\Big)^{2},

(14)

where
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):=\{G=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\exp(c_{i})\delta_{\bm{W}_{2,i}{\bm{B}}_{i}\bm{W}_{1,i}{\bm{A}}_{i}}:1\leq\ell\leq L^{\prime},\ (c_{i},\bm{W}_{2,i},{\bm{B}}_{i},\bm{W}_{1,i},{\bm{A}}_{i})\in\Theta\}
stands for the mixing measure set. To capture the convergence behavior of the estimator, we use the following Voronoi loss tailored to the simple linear reparameterization setting given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}(\bar{G},\bar{G}_{*}):=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\Big|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}}\exp(c_{i})-\exp(c_{j^{\prime}}^{*})\Big|

\displaystyle+{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[L]:|\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}|=1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}}\exp(c_{i})\|{\bm{Z}}_{i}-{\bm{Z}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*}\|}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[L]:|\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}|>1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}}\exp(c_{i})\|{\bm{Z}}_{i}-{\bm{Z}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*}\|^{2},

where we denote
{\bm{Z}}:=\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}}\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}}
. Given the above loss function, we study the sample efficiency of the LoRA with simple linear reparametrization in Theorem
5.2
.

Theorem 5.2
.

The estimator
\bar{G}_{n}
converges to the true mixing measure
\bar{G}_{*}
at the following rate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}(\bar{G}_{n},\bar{G}_{*})=\mathcal{O}_{P}(\sqrt{\log(n)/n}).

Proof of Theorem
5.2
is in Appendix
A.2
. The above bound together with the construction of the loss
\mathcal{D}_{2}
indicates that the convergence rates of estimating low-rank matrices
\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j^{\prime}}{\bm{B}}^{*}_{j^{\prime}}\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j^{\prime}}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{j^{\prime}}
, for
j^{\prime}\in[L]
, range from order
\mathcal{O}_{P}([\log(n)/n]^{\frac{1}{2}})
to order
\mathcal{O}_{P}([\log(n)/n]^{\frac{1}{4}})
. Thus, it costs at most a polynomial number of data
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
to approximate those low-rank matrices with the error
\epsilon
. Compared to the exponential number of data required in the LoRA without reparametrization in Section
5.1
, we observe that the LoRA with linear reparametrization is much more sample efficient.

5.2.2
Non-Linear Reparameterization

Next, we draw our attention to the LoRA with non-linear reparametrization where the low-rank matrices are parametrized as
{\bm{A}}_{Q}={\bm{A}}_{V}=\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}})
and
{\bm{B}}_{Q}={\bm{B}}_{V}=\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}})
. Then, the ground-truth regression function in Eq.(
11
), denoted as
f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})
in this section, admits the following form:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j}{\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{j})}{\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\cdot({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V,j}+\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j}{\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{j}))\mathbb{X},

(15)

where we denote
\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mathbb{X}):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}^{*}_{2,k}{\bm{B}}^{*}_{k})\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}^{*}_{1,k}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{k})){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{k})
and the mixing measure
\widetilde{G}_{*}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\exp(c^{*}_{j^{\prime}})\delta_{(\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j^{\prime}}{\bm{B}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*},\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j^{\prime}}{\bm{A}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*})}
associated with unknown parameters
(c_{j^{\prime}}^{*},\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j^{\prime}}{\bm{B}}_{j^{\prime}}^{*},\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j^{\prime}}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}
in the parameter space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
. The least-square estimator of the ground-truth mixing measure
G_{*}
is now defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{n}\in\textnormal{arg}\min_{\widetilde{G}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bm{Y}}_{i}-f_{\widetilde{G}}(\mathbb{X}_{i})\Big)^{2}.

(16)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):=\{G=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\exp(c_{i})\delta_{(\bm{W}_{2,i}{\bm{B}}_{i},\bm{W}_{1,i}{\bm{A}}_{i})}:1\leq\ell\leq L^{\prime},\ (c_{i},\bm{W}_{2,i}{\bm{B}}_{i},\bm{W}_{1,i}{\bm{A}}_{i})\in\Theta\}
. For the sake of capturing the convergence rate of the estimator
\widetilde{G}_{n}
, the Voronoi loss function is tailored to the non-linear reparametrization setting as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*}):=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{L}\Big|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}}\exp(c_{i})-\exp(c^{*}_{j^{\prime}})\Big|

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}\in[L]:\\
|\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}|=1,\\
i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\exp(c_{i})(\|\Delta(\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}})_{ij^{\prime}}\|+\|\Delta(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}})_{ij^{\prime}}\|)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}\in[L]:\\
|\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}|>1,\\
i\in\mathcal{V}_{j^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\exp(c_{i})(\|\Delta(\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}})_{ij^{\prime}}\|^{2}+\|\Delta(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}})_{ij^{\prime}}\|^{2}),

where we denote
\Delta(\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{B}})_{ij^{\prime}}:=\bm{W}_{2,i}{\bm{B}}_{i}-\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j^{\prime}}{\bm{B}}^{*}_{j^{\prime}}
and
\Delta(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{A}})_{ij^{\prime}}:=\bm{W}_{1,i}{\bm{A}}_{i}-\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j^{\prime}}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{j^{\prime}}
for any
i,j^{\prime}
. Before presenting the main result of this section in Theorem
5.3
, it is necessary to impose some mild assumptions on the activations
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
. Due to the space limit, we defer those assumptions to the proof of Theorem
5.3
in Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume that the activation functions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
meet the assumptions specified in Appendix
A.3
. Then, the estimator
\widetilde{G}_{n}
converges to the true mixing measure
\widetilde{G}_{*}
at the following rate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{*})=\mathcal{O}_{P}(\sqrt{\log(n)/n}).

Theorem
5.3
suggests that the convergence rates of estimating low-rank matrices
\bm{W}^{*}_{2,j}{\bm{B}}^{*}_{j}
and
\bm{W}^{*}_{1,j}{\bm{A}}^{*}_{j}
are either
\mathcal{O}_{P}([\log(n)/n]^{\frac{1}{2}})
or
\mathcal{O}_{P}([\log(n)/n]^{\frac{1}{4}})
depending on the cardinalities of their associated Voronoi cells, or equivalently, the number of their fitted parameters. In other words, we need a polynomial number of data,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
or
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
, to achieve the approximations of the low-rank matrices with the error
\epsilon
when employing the LoRA with non-linear reparametrization. Compared with the LoRA without reparametrization, which requires up to an exponential amount of data for the same task, the LoRA with non-linear reparametrization is more sample efficient.

6
Reparameterized Low-rank Adaptation

In the previous section, we demonstrated that vanilla LoRA without reparameterization establishes a suboptimal rate for low-rank matrix estimation while introducing shared structural reparameterization to achieve the optimal rate. Building on this theoretical insight, we introduce our method:
Rep
arameterized
Lo
w-
R
ank
A
daptation (RepLoRA)
. This method is tailored explicitly for fine-tuning transformer architectures by refining the linear layers that generate queries and values (or keys, queries, and values). This paper focuses on fine-tuning the queries and values for simplicity and clarity. Recall in vanilla LoRA, the matrices that generate the queries and values are given as:

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\prime}_{Q}=\bm{W}_{Q}+{\bm{B}}_{Q}{\bm{A}}_{Q}

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\prime}_{V}=\bm{W}_{V}+{\bm{B}}_{V}{\bm{A}}_{V},

(17)

where
{\bm{B}}_{Q},{\bm{B}}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
{\bm{A}}_{Q},{\bm{A}}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
are learnable low-rank matrices. Inspired by our theoretical results, RepLoRA innovatively reparameterizes
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
, modeling them as outputs of two MLPs. With non-linear reparameterization, the low-rank matrices are given by:

\displaystyle[{\bm{A}}_{Q},{\bm{A}}_{V}]=g_{\theta_{\bm{A}}}({{\bm{A}}})

\displaystyle[{\bm{B}}_{Q},{\bm{B}}_{V}]=g_{\theta_{\bm{B}}}({{\bm{B}}}),

(18)

where
{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}}
are learnable matrices, and
g_{\theta_{\bm{A}}},g_{\theta_{\bm{B}}}
are two-layer MLPs with a shared part and distinct output heads. In this approach,
{\bm{A}}_{Q}
and
{\bm{A}}_{V}
are derived from a shared underlying input
{\bm{A}}
, with distinct outputs
{\bm{A}}_{Q}
and
{\bm{A}}_{V}
produced by the separated heads of
g_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{A}}}
. Similarly,
{\bm{B}}_{Q}
and
{\bm{B}}_{V}
follow the same structure, leveraging a shared
{\bm{B}}
input. While we focus on fine-tuning the queries and values to streamline the analysis, this formulation can naturally be extended to fine-tune the keys. We implement
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
as diagonal matrices to ensure parameter efficiency. After training, the reparameterization
g_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{A}}}
and
g_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{B}}}
can be discarded, and only the fine-tuned matrices
{\bm{A}}_{Q}
,
{\bm{A}}_{V}
,
{\bm{B}}_{Q}
, and
{\bm{B}}_{V}
need to be retained for inference. Hence, this approach does not incur any additional computational overhead for inference. An illustration of this method is provided in Figure
1
.

Evaluation Metrics.
In Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), evaluations typically focus on performance and the number of trainable parameters. The goal is to maximize performance while minimizing the parameters required. To assess this trade-off, in addition to reporting performance, we adopt the
Performance-Parameter Trade-off (PPT)
metric, proposed by
Xin et al. (2024)
. Specifically, for a PETL algorithm
M
, the PPT metric incorporates its task performance
M_{t}
, the number of trainable parameters
P_{M}
, and a normalization constant
C
. Formally, we have:

\mathrm{PPT}_{M}=M_{t}\times\exp(-\log_{10}(\frac{P_{M}}{C}+1)).\vskip-5.69054pt

To ensure a fair comparison, we fine-tuned the models with RepLoRA using the same configuration as LoRA, keeping the rank fixed. As presented in Table

Enhancing Sampling Efficiency.
Our theoretical analysis has demonstrated that reparameterization achieves superior rates of sample efficiency compared to vanilla LoRA. To validate this claim, we evaluate the sample efficiency of RepLoRA on
FGVC
datasets. Following the approach of
d’Ascoli et al. (2021)
, we subsample each class at fractions
f=\{1\%,10\%,30\%,50\%,100\%\}
and scale the number of training epochs by
1/f
, ensuring the total number of data seen by the model remains constant. Figure
2
shows that RepLoRA consistently outperforms LoRA across all sampling fractions. The improvements are significant at smaller fractions, with RepLoRA achieving a remarkable
40.4%
gap at
f=1\%
. More importantly, we emphasize that RepLoRA matches LoRA’s performance with only
30%
training fraction, therefore underscoring RepLoRA’s superior sample efficiency, as predicted by our theoretical analysis. We refer to Appendix
C.1
for a breakdown of these results.

Linear vs. Non-linear Reparameterization.
Another conclusion from the theoretical analysis is that even a simple linear reparameterization with a shared structure offers significant efficiency gains compared to vanilla LoRA. Furthermore, as shown in Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
, incorporating non-linear reparameterization further improves the rate of low-rank matrix estimation. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted empirical experiments, with the results presented in Figure
3
. These results demonstrate that non-linear reparameterization outperforms the linear setting by substantial margins, underscoring its effectiveness. For a more detailed comparison of linear versus non-linear reparameterization performance, please refer to Appendix
C.2
.

Conclusion

We introduced a theoretical framework that bridges LoRA with MoE, offering new insights into the benefits of reparameterizing LoRA for achieving optimal sampling efficiency. Building on this theoretical foundation, we proposed RepLoRA, an effective and efficient approach to PEFT. To evaluate RepLoRA, we conducted extensive experiments across four diverse domains: image, video, text, and multimodal tasks. RepLoRA substantially outperformed LoRA and other PEFT methods in all settings, demonstrating its adaptability and effectiveness. These results highlight the potential of reparameterized structures in enhancing efficiency and effectiveness for fine-tuning large-scale models.

Step 1.

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\inf_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(G,G_{*})\leq\varepsilon}\frac{\|f_{G}-f_{G_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}}{\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(G,G_{*})}=0.

(19)

Note that it is sufficient to construct a mixing measure sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that satisfies both

and
{\|f_{G_{n}}-f_{G_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}}/{\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(G_{n},G_{*})}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.

For that purpose, we take into account the sequence
G_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{L+1}\exp(c^{n}_{i})\delta_{({\bm{B}}^{n}_{Q,i},{\bm{A}}^{n}_{Q,i},{\bm{B}}^{n}_{V,i},{\bm{A}}^{n}_{V,i})}
, where

•

\exp(c^{n}_{1})=\exp(c^{n}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\exp(c^{*}_{1})+\frac{1}{2n^{r+1}}
and
\exp(c^{n}_{i})=\exp(c^{*}_{i-1})
for any
3\leq i\leq L+1
;

•

{\bm{B}}^{n}_{Q,1}={\bm{B}}^{n}_{Q,2}={\bm{B}}^{*}_{Q,1}
and
{\bm{B}}^{n}_{Q,i}={\bm{B}}^{*}_{Q,i-1}
for any
3\leq i\leq L+1
;

•

{\bm{A}}^{n}_{Q,1}={\bm{A}}^{n}_{Q,2}={\bm{A}}^{*}_{Q,1}
and
{\bm{A}}^{n}_{Q,i}={\bm{A}}^{*}_{Q,i-1}
for any
3\leq i\leq L+1
;

•

{\bm{B}}^{n}_{V,1}={\bm{B}}^{*}_{V,1}+\frac{1}{n({\bm{A}}^{*}_{V,1})^{(1)}}(1,0,\ldots,0)
,
{\bm{B}}^{n}_{V,2}={\bm{B}}^{*}_{V,1}-\frac{1}{n({\bm{A}}^{*}_{V,1})^{(1)}}(1,0,\ldots,0)
and
{\bm{B}}^{n}_{V,i}={\bm{B}}^{*}_{V,i-1}
for any
3\leq i\leq L+1

•

{\bm{A}}^{n}_{V,1}={\bm{A}}^{n}_{V,2}={\bm{A}}^{*}_{V,1}
and
{\bm{A}}^{n}_{V,i}={\bm{A}}^{*}_{V,i-1}
for any
3\leq i\leq L+1
;

in which we assume WLOG that
({\bm{A}}^{*}_{V,1})^{(1)}\neq 0
.

Then, we can compute the loss function
\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(G_{n},G_{*})

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1,r}(G_{n},G_{*})=\frac{1}{n^{r+1}}+\Big[\exp(c^{*}_{1})+\frac{1}{n^{r+1}}\Big]\cdot\frac{1}{n^{r}}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-r}).

(20)

It can be seen that

Subsequently, we illustrate that

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\mathbb{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j}}\exp(c^{n}_{i})\Big[\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+{\bm{B}}^{n}_{Q,i}{\bm{A}}^{n}_{Q,i})\mathbb{X})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+{\bm{B}}^{n}_{V,i}{\bm{A}}^{n}_{V,i})\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+{\bm{B}}_{Q,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{Q,j}^{*})\mathbb{X})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+{\bm{B}}_{V,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{V,j}^{*})\mathbb{X}\Big]

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{j}}\exp(c^{n}_{i})\Big[\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q,i}+{\bm{B}}^{n}_{Q,i}{\bm{A}}^{n}_{Q,i})\mathbb{X})-\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+{\bm{B}}_{Q,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{Q,j}^{*})\mathbb{X})\Big]f_{G_{n}}(\mathbb{X})

Given that

\displaystyle+\tilde{\lambda}_{4,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{5,j}\mathbb{X}(\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X})^{2}+\tilde{\lambda}_{6,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{7,j}\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{7,j}\mathbb{X}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}\big)

\displaystyle\times({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X}+\tilde{\lambda}_{1,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})+\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}\tilde{\lambda}_{2,j}+\tilde{\lambda}_{4,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})+\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}\tilde{\lambda}_{6,j}+\tilde{\lambda}_{7,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\Big]

\displaystyle-\sum_{j:|\mathcal{V}_{j}|=1}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X})\Big[\tilde{\lambda}_{1,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})+\tilde{\lambda}_{2,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}\Big]f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})

\displaystyle-\sum_{j:|\mathcal{V}_{j}|>1}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X})\Big[\tilde{\lambda}_{1,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})+\tilde{\lambda}_{2,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{3,j}\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j}))^{2}

\displaystyle+\tilde{\lambda}_{4,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{5,j}\mathbb{X}(\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X})^{2}+\tilde{\lambda}_{6,j}^{\top}\mathbb{X}\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{7,j}\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}^{\top}\tilde{\lambda}_{7,j}\mathbb{X}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}^{*}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}^{*}_{j})\mathbb{X}\Big]f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\tilde{\lambda}_{0,j}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X})\Big[({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+{\bm{B}}_{j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{j}^{*})\mathbb{X}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})\Big]=0

(41)

for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
.
However, that equation implies that all the coefficients
\{\tilde{\lambda}_{0,j},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau,j}:j\in[L],\tau\in[7]\}
are 0. It is a contradiction. As a consequence, we obtain that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\inf_{\widetilde{G}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*})\leq\varepsilon}\|f_{\widetilde{G}}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}/\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*})>0.

A.3.2
Global Part

The result of the local part implies that we can find a positive constant
\varepsilon^{\prime}
such that

\inf_{\widetilde{G}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|f_{\widetilde{G}}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}/\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*})>0.

Therefore to obtain the conclusion of the theorem, we only need to prove that

\inf_{\widetilde{G}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta):\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*})>\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|f_{\widetilde{G}}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}/\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{G}_{*})>0.

We assume by contradiction that the above claim does not hold. It indicates that there exists a sequence of
measures
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp(c_{n,j})\delta_{({\bm{B}}_{n,j},{\bm{A}}_{n,j})}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)
such that

\left\{\begin{matrix}\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{*})>\varepsilon^{\prime}\\
\|f_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}/\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{*})\to 0\end{matrix}\right.

as
n\to\infty
, which implies that
\|f_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Given that
\Theta
is a compact set, there exists a mixing measure
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)
such that one of the
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}
’s subsequences converges to
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{*})>\varepsilon^{\prime}
, we obtain that
\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}^{\prime},\widetilde{G}_{*})>\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
An application of the Fatou’s lemma leads to

0=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}}-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\geq\int\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\|f_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}}(\mathbb{X})-f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})\right\|^{2}d\mu(\mathbb{X}).

The above inequality indicates that
f_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}}=f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}
for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
. From the identifiability property, we deduce that
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}\equiv\widetilde{G}_{*}
. It follows that
\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}^{\prime},\widetilde{G}_{*})=0
, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{D}_{3}(\widetilde{G}^{\prime},\widetilde{G}_{*})>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
.
Hence, the proof is completed.

Proof for the identifiability property.

We will prove that if
f_{\widetilde{G}}(\mathbb{X})=f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})
for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
, then
G\equiv\widetilde{G}_{*}
.
To ease the presentation, for any mixing measure
\widetilde{G}=\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{L}}\exp(c_{j})\delta_{({\bm{B}}_{j},{\bm{A}}_{j})}\in\mathcal{G}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)
, we denote

\displaystyle\mathrm{softmax}_{G}(u)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\exp(u)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{L}}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j})},

where
u\in\{\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j}:j\in[\tilde{L}]\}
.
The equation
f_{\widetilde{G}}(\mathbb{X})=f_{\widetilde{G}_{*}}(\mathbb{X})
indicates that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{L}}\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j}^{*})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X}

(42)

That equation implies that
\tilde{L}=L
. As a consequence, we find that

\displaystyle\{\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j}):j\in[\tilde{L}]\}=\{\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j}^{*}):j\in[L]\}

for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
. By relabelling the indices, we can assume without loss of generality that for any
j\in[L]

\displaystyle\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j}^{*})=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}+c_{j}),

for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
. Given the invariance to translation of the softmax function, the equation (
42
) leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{L}}\exp{(c_{j})}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp{(c_{j}^{*})}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X},

(43)

for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
.

Now, the index set
[L]
can be partitioned into
\tilde{m}
subsets
\tilde{K}_{1},\tilde{K}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{K}_{\tilde{m}}
where
\tilde{m}\leq L
, such that
\exp{(c_{j})}=\exp{(c_{j^{\prime}}^{*{}})}
for any indices
j,j^{\prime}\in\tilde{K}_{i}
and
i\in[\tilde{m}]
. Thus, equation (
43
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{m}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{K}_{i}}\exp{(c_{j})}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{m}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{K}_{i}}\exp{(c_{j}^{*})}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X})({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X},

for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
. The above equation implies that

\displaystyle\{({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}))\mathbb{X}:j\in\tilde{K}_{i}\}=\{({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\sigma_{2}({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*})\sigma_{1}({\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}))\mathbb{X}:j\in\tilde{K}_{i}\},

for any
i\in[\tilde{m}]
and for almost surely
\mathbb{X}
. Since the activation functions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are algebraically independent, the above result indicates that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{m}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{K}_{i}}\exp{(c_{j})}\delta_{({\bm{B}}_{j},{\bm{A}}_{j})}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{m}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{K}_{i}}\exp{(c_{j}^{*})}\delta_{({\bm{B}}_{j}^{*},{\bm{A}}_{j}^{*})}.

As a consequence,
G\equiv G_{*}
and the proof is completed.

A.4
Proof of Proposition
A.1

Recall from the setting that
({\bm{X}}_{1},Y_{1}),({\bm{X}}_{2},Y_{2}),\ldots,({\bm{X}}_{n},Y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
are i.i.d. samples from the following regression model:

\displaystyle Y_{i}=f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n,

where the Gaussian noises
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are i.i.d. and satisfy that
\mathbb{E}[{\varepsilon_{i}}|{\bm{X}}_{i}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{i}|{\bm{X}}_{i})=\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}}
for all
i\in[n]
. Furthermore,
f_{\bar{G}_{*}}(.)
admits the following form:

\displaystyle f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\bm{W}_{2,j}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{j}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{j}^{*}){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{j})}{\bar{D}_{f}(\mathbb{X})}\cdot({\bm{M}}^{0}_{V}+\bm{W}_{2,j}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{j}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,j}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{j}^{*})\mathbb{X},

where we denote
\bar{D}_{f}(\mathbb{X})=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\exp(\mathbb{X}^{\top}({\bm{M}}^{0}_{Q}+\bm{W}_{2,k}^{*}{\bm{B}}_{k}^{*}\bm{W}_{1,k}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{k}^{*}){\bm{M}}^{0}_{K}\mathbb{X}+c^{*}_{k})
. Finally, the least-square estimator
\bar{G}_{n}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{n}:=\textnormal{arg}\min_{G\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}-f_{G}({\bm{X}}_{i})\|^{2},

From the Gaussianity assumption of
\varepsilon_{i}|{\bm{X}}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, we have
Y_{i}|{\bm{X}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}}_{i}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}})
for all
i\in[n]
. Therefore, the least square estimator
\bar{G}_{n}
is indeed a maximum likelihood estimator with respect to the data
Y_{1}|{\bm{X}}_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}|{\bm{X}}_{n}
, which takes the following form:

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{n}\in\argmax_{G\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{\prime}}(\Theta)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(p(Y_{i}|f_{G}({\bm{X}}_{i}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}})).

Here,
p(Y_{i}|f_{G}({\bm{X}}_{i}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}})
stands for multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
f_{G}({\bm{X}})
and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}}
. An application of Theorem 7.4 from
(
van de Geer, 2000
)
leads to

\displaystyle h(p(Y|f_{\bar{G}_{n}}({\bm{X}}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}}),p(Y|f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}}))=\mathcal{O}_{P}(\sqrt{\log(n)/n}),

where
h
stands for the Hellinger distance. As the Hellinger distance between two multivariate Gaussian distributions has closed-form expression, direct calculation yields that

\displaystyle h^{2}(p(Y|f_{\bar{G}_{n}}({\bm{X}}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}}),p(Y|f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}}),\sigma^{2}I_{\bar{d}}))=1-\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{8\sigma^{2}}\|f_{\bar{G}_{n}}({\bm{X}})-f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}})\|^{2}\Bigg\}.

Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
, for some universal constant
C
the above inequality leads to

\displaystyle\|f_{\bar{G}_{n}}({\bm{X}})-f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 8\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\dfrac{1}{1-C\log(n)/n}\Big)

\displaystyle=8\sigma^{2}\log\Big(1+\dfrac{C\log(n)/n}{1-C\log(n)/n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 8\sigma^{2}\cdot\dfrac{C\log(n)/n}{1-C\log(n)/n}

\displaystyle\leq 16\sigma^{2}C\log(n)/n.

That inequality is equivalent to

\displaystyle\|f_{\bar{G}_{n}}({\bm{X}})-f_{\bar{G}_{*}}({\bm{X}})\|=\mathcal{O}_{P}(\sqrt{\log(n)/n}).

As a consequence, we find that

\displaystyle\|f_{\bar{G}_{n}}-f_{\bar{G}_{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\mathcal{O}_{P}(\sqrt{\log(n)/n}).

The proof of the proposition is completed.
