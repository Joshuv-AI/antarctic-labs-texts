Investigation of phubbing behavior in attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and cognitive disengagement syndrome: a case-control study in adolescent
Authors: Erdal Görkem Gavcar, Serdar Avunduk, Sena Aksoy Avunduk, Ahmet Büber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a common childhood neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity beyond developmental expectations. Problematic technology engagement, including increased smartphone use, is frequently observed among adolescents with ADHD. Phubbing, a term combining ‘phone’ and ‘snubbing,’ refers to ignoring others in a social setting by focusing on one’s smartphone rather than engaging in face-to-face interaction and has been linked to impaired interpersonal communication. Cognitive Disengagement Syndrome (CDS), formerly referred to as sluggish cognitive tempo (SCT), is characterized by excessive daydreaming, mental confusion, low initiative, and slowed responding and is associated with social withdrawal. However, no prior studies have investigated ADHD-phubbing or CDS-phubbing relationships in adolescents. This study examined group differences in phubbing and explored associations among phubbing, ADHD symptoms, and CDS severity.MethodsIn this case-control study, 48 adolescents diagnosed with ADHD and 38 healthy controls aged 12–17 completed; Sociodemographic Data Form, General Phubbing Scale, and Conners-Wells Adolescent Self-Report Scale Short Form. Parents completed Child and Adolescent Behavior Inventory SCT Scale. ADHD was diagnosed via DSM-5 based clinical interviews. Group comparisons and correlation analyses were conducted using appropriate statistical tests.ResultsPhubbing scores were significantly higher in the ADHD group than in controls. Phubbing scores demonstrated significant positive correlations with self-reported ADHD symptom domains, including conduct problems, inattention, hyperactivity, and the ADHD Index. CDS severity did not reach significance within the ADHD group but was significantly associated with phubbing scores in the full sample. Adolescents whose CDS symptoms impaired social functioning reported higher phubbing scores. Daily smartphone use exceeding 3 hours and social media use were also associated with elevated phubbing scores.ConclusionThese findings indicate that phubbing scores is elevated among adolescents with ADHD and is positively associated with ADHD symptom severity. CDS—particularly when accompanied by social impairment—is also associated with higher phubbing scores. The results highlight the potential utility of assessing smartphone and other electronic technology use when evaluating adolescents with ADHD and CDS. However, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal or directional inferences cannot be drawn. Future research with larger and untreated samples is warranted.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, Phubbing, Cognitive disengagement syndrome, Smartphone, Adolescent

Introduction

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders affecting children. The condition is characterized by symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that are inconsistent with the individual’s developmental stage [1, 2]. A meta-analysis of 175 research studies revealed that the worldwide prevalence of ADHD in children is 7.2% [3]. A 2021 meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of persistent adult ADHD, characterized by the onset of symptoms in childhood, was 2.58%. The prevalence of symptomatic adult ADHD, irrespective of childhood onset, was determined to be 6.76% [4]. For this reason, ADHD should not be considered a condition limited to childhood and adolescence [5].

The use of smartphones modifies daily routines, habits, social behaviors, familial relationships, and social interactions [6]. Smartphones represent a crucial element in the lives of adolescents, who are especially susceptible to the influences of emerging technology and media consumption [7]. Today, almost all young people in Western countries own smartphones [8]. People communicate in many ways and continue doing so as inherently social beings. In parallel with technological advancements, modes of communication are also diversifying and developing [9]. The increasing use of technology over time—now an integral part of daily life—has begun to replace face-to-face communication [10]. The time allocated to digital media for recreational purposes, including video games, social media, and online content consumption, has significantly increased over the past decade [11]. Impaired social skills and communication difficulties are linked to a novel phenomenon known as “phubbing” [12, 13]. The Macquarie Dictionary coined the term “phubbing” in 2013 by combining “phone” and “snubbing” [14]. Phubbing refers to the act of disregarding another individual in a social setting by focusing on one’s phone rather than engaging in interpersonal communication [14–16]. Individuals prone to phubbing tend to prioritize their smartphones over face-to-face interactions, which has been shown to undermine the quality of in-person social engagement [17]. This behavior occurs between the person ignoring others, the “phubber,” and the person being ignored, the “phubbee.” One study indicated that 26.7% of participants engaged in phubbing others 2–3 times per day, while 31.9% experienced being phubbed at the same frequency [14].

Impulsivity, one of the primary signs of ADHD, correlates with smartphone addiction [18, 19]. Multiple studies investigating the problematic use of different media types have found a positive correlation between ADHD and this usage [20–22]. High scores on addiction-related measures in children have been found to be strongly associated with ADHD symptoms [23]. A study in Korea indicated that students with ADHD exhibited a higher propensity for smartphone addiction [24]. It was also reported that fear of being away from one’s smartphone was an important predictor of attention deficit [25], and 23% of students had attention problems for this reason [26]. In addition, problematic technology use is also observed in individuals who exhibit high levels of ADHD characteristics but have not been diagnosed with ADHD [27, 28]. On the other hand, ADHD is marked by interpersonal challenges, peer rejection, and social difficulties [29, 30]. Further deterioration in peer relationships [31], poor friendship quality, and difficulties in sustaining friendships have also been reported [32]. Children with ADHD have very few reciprocal friendships, are less preferred by their peers [31], and are more likely to be disliked by peers than typically developing children [33, 34]. Considering the influence of primary ADHD symptoms and the difficulties encountered by individuals with ADHD in social interactions, we hypothesized that phubbing scores would be higher in the ADHD group compared to the control group and that a positive association would be present between self-reported ADHD symptoms and phubbing scores.

Cognitive Disengagement Syndrome (CDS), formerly referred to as sluggish cognitive tempo, encompasses a specific array of symptoms, including a confused demeanor, pronounced fatigue, frequent daydreaming, blank staring, and reduced movement speed. It is considered to differ from the inattention-dominant type of ADHD [35, 36]. CDS was initially considered a potential marker for the inattention-dominant type of ADHD. However, empirical studies involving young children, school-aged children, and adolescents have demonstrated the differentiation of CDS from ADHD and its associations with psychosocial outcomes [37, 38]. Among the identified consequences of CDS on psychopathology and functioning, one of the most significant psychosocial correlates is social withdrawal [36]. Another study found that one of the areas in which CDS cases differed significantly was social introversion [39]. Currently, there is no research specifically investigating the relationship between CDS and smartphone usage among adolescents.

Importantly, the mechanisms through which CDS may contribute to phubbing are theoretically distinct from those proposed for ADHD. Whereas ADHD-related phubbing may primarily stem from impulsivity and attentional dysregulation (whereby individuals impulsively shift their attention to their smartphones), CDS may promote phubbing through a social withdrawal pathway. Fredrick and Becker [36] advanced a conceptual model linking CDS to social withdrawal, identifying mechanisms such as task-unrelated thought, poorer social skills, and social anxiety through which core CDS features may lead individuals to disengage from face-to-face interactions. In this context, phubbing may serve as a behavioral manifestation of this disengagement, whereby the smartphone provides a less cognitively demanding alternative to active social participation. Supporting this distinction, Carlson and Mann [40] demonstrated that children with ADHD-predominantly inattentive type and high SCT symptoms exhibited a distinct impairment pattern characterized by elevated withdrawal and social dysfunction compared to those with low SCT symptoms. Similarly, Marshall et al. [41] found that youth with ADHD-inattentive type- and high SCT showed uniquely elevated social withdrawal that was not accounted for by co-occurring disorders. More broadly, accumulating evidence indicates that CDS symptoms remain associated with social impairment above and beyond ADHD symptoms [37, 38].

Numerous studies have elucidated the correlation between ADHD and problematic smartphone use, as well as smartphone addiction. However, to the best of our knowledge, the association between phubbing behavior- which is intrinsically linked to smartphone use- and ADHD has not been documented in the existing literature. Similarly, no studies have examined the association between CDS and phubbing. Considering the features of ADHD, such as distractibility and impulsivity, and the resulting social difficulties, we hypothesize that individuals with ADHD may engage in phubbing scores more frequently than those without ADHD. Furthermore, we believe that individuals with CDS, which are thought to be a different condition from ADHD in recent studies, may also exhibit more phubbing scores, due to they show similar characteristics to ADHD problems in the field of attention. In addition, based on the literature, we also aimed to examine the relationship between CDS-related social withdrawal with phubbing.

Based on all these rationales, we formulated our hypotheses as follows:

H1

The ADHD group will have elevated phubbing scores in comparison to the control group.

H2

A positive association will exist between self-reported ADHD symptoms and phubbing scores.

H3

A positive association will exist between CDS severity and phubbing scores.

H4

Phubbing scores will be more prevalent among individuals whose CDS significantly impairs social communication.

Methods

Participants

This study was designed as a case–control study. The study population consisted of adolescents aged 12–17 years who either sought treatment for ADHD at the Balıkesir Atatürk City Hospital Child and Adolescent Mental Health and Diseases Outpatient Clinics or visited the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinics for other reasons, provided that they had no psychiatric or physical disorders. Written informed consent was obtained from both the adolescents and their parents or legal guardians before participation.

All participants were evaluated through a DSM-5–based clinical interview and subsequently assigned to either the ADHD or the control group. The case group consisted of adolescents diagnosed with ADHD without any comorbid psychiatric or physical disorders. The control group comprised adolescents who presented to the clinic for various reasons (sleep regulation, issues related to adolescence, and consulting requests) but did not receive any psychiatric diagnosis during the DSM-5–based clinical interview and had no physical illnesses. All diagnoses were established by child and adolescent psychiatrists through DSM-5–based clinical interviews.

The inclusion criteria for the ADHD group were a DSM-5–based clinical diagnosis of ADHD, absence of any additional psychiatric or medical disorder, age between 12 and 17 years, and provision of informed consent to participate. The exclusion criteria included having any psychiatric diagnosis other than ADHD, presence of a comorbid medical condition, being younger than 12 or older than 17 years, or declining participation.

For the control group, the inclusion criteria were absence of any psychiatric diagnosis as determined through a DSM-5–based clinical interview, absence of any medical condition, age between 12 and 17 years, and voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria included the presence of any psychiatric or medical diagnosis, being outside the specified age range, or refusal to participate.

All participants in the ADHD group were receiving ADHD treatment. Among the adolescents diagnosed with ADHD in the study group, 35 were taking methylphenidate, 13 were taking atomoxetine, and 3 were taking guanfacine. The mean dosage was 34.65 ± 13.49 (min: 10, max: 72) mg/day for methylphenidate, 42.30 ± 16.32 (min: 10, max: 70) mg/day for atomoxetine, and 1.66 ± 0.57 (min: 1, max: 2) mg/day for guanfacine. Adolescents in the case group have been receiving an effective dose of ADHD treatment within the last month.

Procedure

Participants were asked to complete a Sociodemographic Data Form, the General Phubbing Scale, and the Conners–Wells Adolescent Self-Report Scale Short Form. In addition, the parents of the participants were asked to complete the Child and Adolescent Behavior Inventory Sluggish Cognitive Tempo Scale. All forms were administered in a hospital setting under the supervision of the research team. The entire procedure took approximately 20–25 min to complete.

Ethical principles

Ethics approval was obtained from the Balıkesir Atatürk City Hospital Ethics Committee (Reference No: E-30041352-514.19.99-262251453; 13.12.2024 date). We performed all the study procedures following the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from both the adolescents and their parents or legal guardians before participation.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was established via a power analysis conducted with the G*Power program. In the absence of a comparable study, the calculation utilized data from prior research [24] that investigated the association between ADHD and smartphone addiction, which is contextually linked to phubbing. Considering the effect sizes reported in previous studies, it was calculated that a minimum sample size of 52 participants (at least 26 in each group) would be required to achieve a power of 80.0% at a 95.0% confidence level. The final sample included 48 participants in the case group and 38 participants in the control group.

The data from the study were analyzed using SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Test selection was based on the assessment of parametric assumptions (normality of distribution) for each specific comparison. When parametric assumptions were met, parametric tests (Independent Samples t-test, Pearson correlation) were used; when assumptions were violated, the corresponding nonparametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney U test, Spearman correlation) were applied. In the analyses conducted using the Independent Samples t-test, the homogeneity of variances was assessed by examining the results of Levene’s Test for Equality of Variances. In cases where variances were not equal, the results of the Welch t-test were used. Chi-square analysis was employed to evaluate differences among categorical variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance. To control for the potential inflation of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure was applied to the correlation analyses [42].

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic Data Form (SDF)

SDF was developed by the researchers. It included items assessing age, gender, family structure, number of siblings, parents’ ages and educational levels, family income level, academic performance, peer relationship, parental relationship quality, average daily time spent on a smartphone, and purposes of smartphone use.

In our study, smartphone usage time was categorized as 0–3 h and more than 3 h per day, based on findings from previous research. One study reported an average daily smartphone usage time of 3.6 h [43], while others found that the most preferred usage duration was between 1 and 3 h per day [44, 45].

General Phubbing Scale (GPS)

Developed in 2018 by Chotpitayasunondh and Douglas, the scale evaluates phubbing in adults through 15 items across four sub-dimensions, using a 7-point Likert scale. These sub-dimensions are nomophobia, interpersonal conflict, self-isolation, and problem acknowledgment. A low score signifies minimal phubbing, while a high score indicates significant phubbing [46]. In 2023, Gavcar et al. conducted a Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale in an adolescent population [47]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the GPS was 0.914, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Conners-Wells Adolescent Self-Report Scale Short Form (CWASRSSF)

Developed by Conners et al., this scale evaluates conduct disorder, impulse control, attention deficit, hyperactivity, and ADHD symptoms in adolescents. It is based on self-evaluations by individuals aged 12 to 17 regarding their behavior over the last month. The abbreviated version was developed by identifying items with the highest factor loadings from the extended version. The scale includes three subscales: conduct disorder, cognitive problems/inattention, and hyperactivity. Additionally, it features the Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Index-ADHD scale, an auxiliary subscale for identifying individuals with a potential ADHD diagnosis. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3. Higher scores indicate greater symptom prevalence [48]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale for adolescents were established by Kaner et al. [49]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.773 for conduct disorder, 0.662 for cognitive problems/inattention, 0.785 for hyperactivity, and 0.684 for the ADHD Index. The slightly lower alpha values observed for the cognitive problems/inattention and ADHD Index subscales are consistent with those reported in the original Turkish validation study (0.67 and 0.69, respectively) [49].

Child and Adolescent Behavior Inventory Sluggish Cognitive Tempo Scale (CABI-SCT)

The scale comprises 15 items assessing symptoms of CDS (formerly known as SCT). It is structured as a 6-point Likert scale. Total scores are derived by aggregating individual item scores, with higher values reflecting increased symptom severity. The scale’s validity and reliability have been supported by several studies [50–52]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the full CABI was conducted by Çiftçi et al. [53], while the SCT subscale was validated by Öğütlü et al. [54]. In our study, only the SCT subscale was used. In the present sample, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the CABI-SCT Scale was 0.950, indicating excellent internal consistency.

At the end of the first section of the CABI scale, which assesses symptoms of CDS, parents are asked whether the presence of CDS-related symptoms causes social difficulties for their children. The four response levels, “does not affect, slightly affects, moderately affects, and greatly affects”, were grouped into two categories as “does not affect/slightly affects” and “moderate/greatly affects”.

The mean age of the ADHD group was 14.00 ± 1.75 years, and the mean age of the control group was 14.65 ± 1.72 years. Phubbing scores were analyzed in relation to the CWASRSSF subscale scores within the ADHD group and the total sample. Statistically significant positive correlations were found between phubbing scores and the subscales of conduct disorder (p < .001; r = .544), cognitive problems-inattention (p = .019; r = .338), and hyperactivity (p = .016; r = .345). In the full sample of 86 participants, statistically significant positive correlations were found between phubbing scores and the subscales of conduct disorder (p < .001; r = .355), cognitive problems-attention deficit (p = .002; r = .329) and hyperactivity (p < .001; r = .360). Discussion

The initial hypothesis posited that phubbing scores would be elevated in the ADHD-diagnosed group relative to the healthy control group. We hypothesized a positive association between self-reported ADHD symptoms and phubbing scores. Our findings supported these hypotheses, establishing a positive association between the self-reported ADHD symptoms and phubbing scores in the case group. All adolescents in the case group were receiving pharmacological treatment for ADHD. Notably, despite the general effectiveness of medication in managing ADHD symptoms, the persistence of a significant association between ADHD and phubbing scores even under treatment conditions highlights the robustness of this relationship. The relationship between ADHD and various addictions has been examined in meta-analysis studies [55–58]. Since phubbing behavior is closely related to factors such as smartphone/social media addiction [59–62], it is plausible that individuals with ADHD may exhibit higher levels of phubbing scores. To our knowledge, no prior research has examined the association between ADHD and phubbing in adolescents. Future studies with larger sample sizes and including untreated ADHD individuals will further validate our findings. We also found that the time spent with the smartphones was higher in the case group. Prior research has established a correlation between smartphone usage and ADHD in adolescents [24, 63, 64]. The increased smartphone use observed in the ADHD group aligns with the existing literature.

Our third hypothesis proposed a positive association between CDS severity and phubbing scores. The results obtained from our study indicated that the association between CDS scores and phubbing scores did not reach statistical significance within the ADHD group; however, a significant positive association was found in the full sample. To our knowledge, this finding represents the first evidence in the literature examining the CDS–phubbing relationship. The lack of a significant association between CDS and phubbing scores in the ADHD-based case group may be related to the limited sample size and the fact that case selection was centered on ADHD. Additionally, while the CDS form was completed by parents and the phubbing form was completed by adolescents themselves, this cross-informant approach warrants careful consideration. Parent and adolescent ratings are often only modestly correlated in child and adolescent psychopathology research [65], and this discrepancy may have attenuated the strength of the CDS–phubbing scores association, particularly within the ADHD group. The use of the parent-report CABI-SCT was because this instrument was developed and validated as a parent-report measure, as CDS symptoms such as mental fogginess, excessive daydreaming, and slowed behavior are often more reliably observed by external informants. While the use of different informants may have weakened the observed associations, it also reduces the risk of common method variance. Nevertheless, future studies utilizing multi-informant assessments for both CDS and phubbing would help clarify the true strength of this relationship.

Our fourth and last hypothesis was that phubbing scores would be higher in the CDS group, particularly those with greater social impairment. As a result of the comparison between the group in which CDS affected social relations more and the group in which CDS affected social relations less or not at all, we found that the phubbing scores were statistically significantly higher in the group with more affected social relations. Studies conducted with individuals diagnosed with ADHD and having high CDS scores show that social withdrawal behavior is more reported by teachers [40] and parents [41, 66] compared to individuals diagnosed with ADHD and having low CDS scores. People who exhibit phubbing behavior prefer to focus their attention and interest on their phones rather than the people around them when using their mobile phones, and they do not make eye contact with other people [67, 68]. Although this finding seems conceptually consistent with the theoretical framework of CDS and phubbing proposed by the existing literature, it should be interpreted with caution. It is important to consider that the participants had a diagnosis of ADHD. Future studies utilizing larger and more diverse samples will elucidate the mechanisms that underlie the relationship between CDS and phubbing.

The study revealed that individuals utilizing smartphones for over 3 h per day exhibited statistically significant higher phubbing scores compared to those using smartphones for less than 3 h daily. Prior research has established a relationship between phubbing behavior and smartphone usage among adults [59–62]. The relationship between parental phubbing behaviors and adolescents’ smartphone use was generally examined in the literature [69–72]. The findings are consistent with the current literature. Given the scarcity of literature evaluating phubbing behavior by self-report among adolescents, we assert that this facet of our study enhances the existing body of research. Furthermore, no significant association was found between age and phubbing scores in either the ADHD group or the full sample, indicating that the observed associations were consistent across the 12–17 age range. Nevertheless, future studies with larger samples may benefit from examining age-related differences in phubbing behavior across narrower developmental stages. Among the various smartphone usage purposes assessed, only social media use was significantly associated with higher phubbing scores. This finding is consistent with the existing literature, as various studies have indicated that addiction tendencies, and most importantly, social media addiction, are among the leading determinants of phubbing behavior [73–76].

This study has certain limitations. The initial limitation identified was the restricted sample size concerning our research. While the sample size was determined through power analysis, corroborating our findings with studies that utilize larger sample sizes will enhance the value of our results. The second limitation of our study was that all the adolescents with ADHD in our study were undergoing treatment. Future studies should be planned with individuals with ADHD who are newly diagnosed/not treated, thus minimizing the effects of ADHD treatment on the outcomes. The third limitation of our study was that ADHD-related data were evaluated only through adolescent self-report, whereas CDS-related data were evaluated only from the parent’s perspective. Parent and adolescent ratings are known to show only modest agreement in child and adolescent psychiatry research [65], and this informant discrepancy may have influenced the observed associations-particularly the nonsignificant CDS–phubbing scores association in the ADHD group. Although the cross-informant design reduces common method variance, future studies should employ multi-informant approaches for both constructs to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. The fourth limitation of our study is that the control group was not community-based but consisted of individuals who presented to the clinic but did not receive a diagnosis. Although they did not have any psychiatric diagnoses, their reasons for seeking clinical evaluation may have influenced phubbing behavior, which could limit the generalizability of the results. Fifth, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, the observed associations cannot establish causal or directional relationships between ADHD symptoms, CDS severity, and phubbing behavior. Therefore, the associational findings should be interpreted with caution, and future longitudinal studies are needed to elucidate the directionality and potential causal mechanisms underlying these relationships. Sixth, adolescents with ADHD may demonstrate a positive illusory bias (PIB), characterized by the tendency to overestimate their competence and underestimate their difficulties, which can influence the accuracy of self-report measures [77, 78]. This bias may have led to an underreporting of both ADHD symptoms on the CWASRSSF and phubbing behavior on the GPS within the ADHD group, potentially attenuating the observed associations. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that PIB may be less prevalent in adolescents with ADHD than previously assumed; rather than being a global phenomenon, only a small subset (18.4%) exhibits cross-domain bias [79]. Future studies incorporating multi-informant and objective measures of phubbing behavior could help address this limitation. Seventh, the ADHD group in our study consisted of adolescents without any comorbid psychiatric or medical conditions. While this ‘pure’ ADHD sample was methodologically advantageous for minimizing potential confounders, it represents a select subset of the ADHD population. Epidemiological evidence indicates that approximately two-thirds of children and adolescents with ADHD have at least one comorbid psychiatric disorder [80]. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of adolescents with ADHD (particularly those with common comorbidities such as oppositional defiant disorder, anxiety disorders, or learning disorders) may be limited. Future studies should examine phubbing behavior in ADHD samples that include common comorbid conditions to enhance the ecological validity of these findings. Eighth, systematic data regarding the number of adolescents screened to obtain the final ADHD and control groups were not prospectively recorded. Future studies would benefit from reporting detailed participant flow information, including the numbers screened, excluded, and enrolled, in accordance with standard reporting guidelines. Ninth, smartphone usage time was dichotomized as 0–3 h versus more than 3 h per day based on previous literature. This binary categorization may not adequately capture the full range of usage patterns, particularly among heavy users. Future studies should consider using continuous measures or finer-grained categories of smartphone usage duration to allow a more nuanced analysis of the relationship between usage time and phubbing.

Conclusion

Adolescents with ADHD reported significantly higher phubbing scores than healthy controls, and phubbing scores was positively associated with self-reported ADHD symptomatology—including conduct problems, inattention, hyperactivity, and the ADHD Index—even though all cases were under pharmacological treatment. While CDS severity per se did not correlate with phubbing scores, youth whose CDS symptoms more strongly impaired social relationships exhibited higher phubbing scores, suggesting that social functioning may be a key contextual factor. Beyond diagnosis, behavioral correlates were evident: daily smartphone use exceeding three hours and use for social media were each associated with higher phubbing scores. Taken together, these associations indicate that phubbing scores is elevated in ADHD and is positively correlated with core symptom burden and specific usage patterns. These results highlight the potential utility of assessing smartphone and other electronic technology use when evaluating adolescents with ADHD and suggest potential targets for clinical assessment and intervention (e.g., device-use hygiene, social media routines, and social skills supports). However, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal or directional inferences cannot be drawn from these associations. Future studies should test these relationships in larger and more diverse samples, include untreated/newly diagnosed ADHD cases, and employ multi-informant and observational measures. Longitudinal designs that incorporate potential mediators (e.g., social media or smartphone addiction) and community-recruited control groups will help clarify directionality and generalizability. In particular, future research should explore the potentially self-reinforcing relationship between feelings of social isolation and phubbing behavior, whereby social withdrawal may increase the appeal of smartphone use, which in turn may further impair face-to-face interactions.

From a clinical perspective, phubbing is a relatively new concept that is not yet routinely assessed in the clinical follow-up of adolescents with ADHD. Although smartphone use is receiving increasing clinical attention, it is often insufficiently addressed in routine evaluations. Our findings suggest that incorporating systematic assessment of smartphone usage patterns and phubbing behavior into routine ADHD evaluations may provide clinically valuable information. Specifically, clinicians should consider screening for excessive smartphone use and phubbing as part of comprehensive ADHD assessments and provide targeted psychoeducation for both adolescents and their families regarding the impact of these behaviors on social functioning. Social skills interventions and screen-time management strategies (e.g., device-use hygiene, structured social media routines) may serve as useful components of multimodal ADHD treatment. Furthermore, our CDS-related findings carry distinct clinical implications. Adolescents whose CDS symptoms are accompanied by social impairment exhibited higher phubbing scores, suggesting that these individuals may be substituting face-to-face communication with smartphone-mediated interaction as a way of managing social difficulties. Clinicians evaluating adolescents with CDS symptoms should therefore assess not only the severity of CDS but also its impact on social functioning and consider integrating holistic support approaches that address both social withdrawal and technology-related behaviors. Raising awareness among clinicians, families, and educators about the interplay between ADHD, CDS, social difficulties, and phubbing may facilitate earlier identification and more targeted interventions.
