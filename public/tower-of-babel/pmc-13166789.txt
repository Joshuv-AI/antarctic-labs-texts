Older adults bear the burden: risk factors for severe influenza outcomes among hospitalized adults in a 9-season multicenter active surveillance study
Authors: Lale Ozisik, Mine Durusu Tanriover, Murat Ozdede, Ayse Tulay Bagci Bosi, Banu Cakir, Selim Badur, Joan Puig-Barberà, Meral Akcay, Serhat Unal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seasonal influenza causes substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide, yet local multi-season data from middle income settings remain limited. This study aimed to assess the clinical burden of influenza hospitalization and identify the independent predictors of severe outcomes in adults in Türkiye. A multicenter active surveillance study was conducted across Global Influenza Hospital Surveillance Network sites in Türkiye over 9 influenza seasons (2012–2024). Patients screened within 72 hours of hospital admission with symptoms of influenza-like illness were included. Only adults with laboratory-confirmed influenza infection using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction were included in the risk analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors for intensive care unit (ICU) admission, mechanical ventilation (MV), and in-hospital mortality. Among 2439 hospitalized adults; 351 had laboratory-confirmed influenza, of whom 85.2% had at least 2 chronic condition. Severe outcomes were frequent: 21.1% required intensive care, 21.1% required MV, and 11.1% died. Of those who died in the hospital, 69.2% were 65 years and older. Each additional year of age increased the odds of ICU admission by 3% and in-hospital death by 2%. Diabetes increased ICU admission risk (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.91, 1.09–3.33). Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was the only independent predictor of MV (aOR 3.13, 1.77–5.61). Patients with chronic kidney disease had a more than fourfold increased risk of in-hospital mortality (aOR 4.54, 2.05–9.88) and malignancy (aOR 3.75, 1.63–8.38) were another strongest determinant of mortality. Influenza vaccination reduced the risk of ICU admission by 66% (aOR 0.34, 0.12–0.80). These findings highlight the considerable burden of influenza among older adults and those with chronic conditions, reinforcing the need for enhanced vaccination strategies and timely antiviral treatment for this population.

1. Introduction

Influenza is now known to be a significant disease that can lead to severe complications and multisystem dysfunction. The World Health Organization has declared that seasonal influenza results in 3 to 5 million severe illnesses and between 2,90,000 and 6,50,000 respiratory-related deaths annually.[1] Influenza-associated excess respiratory mortality estimates from the global data from 2002 to 2011 revealed an average of 3,89,000 respiratory deaths associated with influenza each year, of which 67% were among people 65 years and older.[2] The Global Influenza Hospital Surveillance Network (GIHSN) data from 15,660 hospitalized, laboratory-confirmed cases across 22 countries (2012–2019) showed that 5.1% required intensive care unit (ICU) admission, 3.2% needed mechanical ventilation (MV), and 2.7% died. Adults ≥ 65 years had a 2.2-fold higher risk of death compared with those aged 5 to 64 years.[3]

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) classified the 2024 to 2025 influenza season as a “high-severity season’’ across all age groups. This is the first such classification since 2017 to 2018. There were an estimated 43 million symptomatic cases, 19 million medical visits, 5,60,000 hospitalizations, and 38,000 deaths in the United States. The cumulative influenza-associated hospitalization rate was the highest since 2010 to 11 and patients aged ≥75 years had the highest rate compared to other age groups. Of those hospitalized, 89.1% had 1 or more underlying medical conditions, 16.8% were admitted to an ICU, and 6.1% required MV.[4]

The extent and severity of influenza outbreaks are shaped by viral factors and the demographic and socioeconomic profile of populations in different geographical regions. In addition to its significant mortality and morbidity, influenza imposes a substantial economic burden – particularly among unvaccinated older adults – with cost estimates varying between countries.[5] Hence, understanding regional and national data is crucial to designing effective, locally tailored prevention and control strategies for seasonal epidemics and potential pandemics.[3]

Surveillance systems provide essential, up-to-date information on infectious disease burden and form the basis for pandemic preparedness and healthcare planning. Although Türkiye has an active sentinel influenza-like illness (ILI) and severe acute respiratory infection surveillance system, multi-season local data on the clinical burden and risk factors of influenza remain limited. Vaccination rates in priority groups are low, with uptake among older adults ranging from 5.9% to 41.3%, and even lower among healthcare workers and individuals with chronic diseases.[6-8]

GIHSN, is an international, hospital-based, prospective surveillance platform designed to monitor severe influenza outcomes using a standardized core protocol across participating countries. Since 2012, GIHSN has prospectively monitored laboratory-confirmed influenza cases in 32 countries, using standardized demographic, clinical, and virological data. This network enables direct comparison of influenza burden, risk factors, and clinical outcomes across age groups.[9] Türkiye has participated in the GIHSN since the 2012 to 2013 influenza season through designated tertiary-care hospitals coordinated at the national level. The surveillance is active, patient-based, and focused on laboratory-confirmed influenza among hospitalized adults. Using GIHSN data of the Turkish sites from 2012 through 2024, this study aimed to define the clinical burden and progress of disease among hospitalized adult patients with laboratory-confirmed influenza, assessing the risk of ICU admission, MV, in-hospital mortality and prolonged length of stay (LOS) as well as the influence of specific patient-level covariates to guide national policies for the prevention and management of influenza in adults.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Screening, patient enrollment

This was a multicenter observational active surveillance study conducted across participating GIHSNsites in Türkiye over 9 influenza seasons (2012–2024). The study included adult patients hospitalized at 8 tertiary-care hospitals participating in the GIHSN Türkiye network across major regions of the country. These sites consist predominantly of university-affiliated and training-and-research hospitals providing advanced internal medicine and intensive care services.

In accordance with the GIHSN core protocol, eligible adult patients (≥19 years) admitted to designated beds at participating hospitals were screened prospectively for influenza during each influenza season, starting from the 2012 to 2013 season through the end of the 2023 to 2024 season.[9,10] Those patients who had 1 or more of the predefined International Classification of Diseases-10 codes (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, which illustrates the International Classification of Diseases Codes for eligible patients) were approached and evaluated clinically after getting informed consent. Patients who were screened within 72 hours of admission to the hospital and hospitalized for at least 1 night and had signs and symptoms compatible with ILI were included in the study. ILI was defined as the presence of at least 1 systemic symptom (fever, malaise, headache, or myalgia) and at least 1 respiratory symptom (cough, sore throat, dyspnea or nasal congestion), within the previous 7-10 days (see Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, which illustrates The GIHSN study flow diagram). Case identification was based on daily screening of admissions by trained surveillance personnel using standardized GIHSN case definitions. Only patients with laboratory-confirmed influenza infection were included in the risk analysis. Respiratory specimens (nasopharyngeal and/or oropharyngeal swabs) were collected from all enrolled patients according to the GIHSN protocol. Influenza infection was confirmed using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays. Laboratory testing was performed at the coordinating sites’ reference laboratories using validated commercial or in-house RT-PCR assays approved for routine clinical use, in accordance with World Health Organization recommendations.[10] RT-PCR testing targeted influenza A and B viruses, including subtype analysis for influenza A(H1N1) and A(H3N2), and lineage determination for influenza B (Yamagata and Victoria). Samples for which subtype or lineage could not be determined were recorded as “A not subtyped” or “B not lineage-determined.

Exclusion criteria included inability to communicate or provide consent. Surveillance was conducted seasonally until 2021 and year-round thereafter. In the 2017 to 2018 and 2018 to 2019 seasons, Turkish sites were unable to participate in the surveillance.

2.2. Data processing and statistical analyses

Data were collected using a standardized GIHSN case report forms at admission and were supplemented with information from clinical records at discharge. Clinical data included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, history of influenza vaccination, clinical course, ICU admission, need for MV, and date of discharge or death. Anonymized data were entered into the centralized GIHSN database and underwent systematic quality control procedures, including range and consistency checks.

Adults ≥ 19 years were included after systematic data cleaning that preserved legitimate clinical measurements. Continuous variables were presented as median and compared using Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), compared with Fisher’s exact test (cell counts < 5) or chi-square test. Missing data patterns were assessed and those with >30% missing data were excluded.

Among influenza-positive patients, univariate logistic regression identified indicators of MV, ICU admission, and in-hospital death. Variables with P < .10 entered multivariate models via backward stepwise selection. Variance inflation factors assessed multicollinearity (VIF > 5 indicating concern). Model discrimination was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% confidence intervals (CIs; DeLong’s method). Bootstrap internal validation (500 resamples) corrected for optimism: models refit on each bootstrap sample were tested on the original data to estimate overfitting. For LOS analysis, initial ordinary least-squares regression revealed non-normal residuals and heteroscedasticity on residual-versus-fitted and Q–Q plots. To address these violations, log-transformation was applied: log(LOS + 1) was modeled using linear regression, and exponentiated coefficients were reported as percent change in LOS with 95% CIs. Forest plots visualized odds ratios for binary outcomes and regression coefficients for continuous outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 within RStudio. Type I error was set at 0.05.

2.3. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Istanbul University Clinical Research Ethics Committee for 2012 to 2015 and from the Hacettepe University Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee for 2015 to 2024. All procedures performed in this study were conducted with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with the approved study protocol, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their clinical assessment.

3. Results

3.1. Patient population and basic characteristics

A total of 2439 hospitalized adult patients were included in the surveillance cohort over 9 study seasons. The median age of the patients was 68 [54–78] years and 1145 (46.2%) of the patients were female (Table 1). More than half of the adult patients (n = 1390, 56.9%) were 65 years or older and 86.6% (n = 2111) had at least 1 chronic medical condition. Information on smoking status, alcohol consumption, and obesity was not systematically collected as part of the standard protocol and therefore was not included in the analysis. Cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and diabetes were the most frequent chronic conditions. Overall, 13.2% (n = 322) of the patients were vaccinated during the current season. Among this cohort of admitted patients, 351 (14.4%) of them were influenza positive.

3.2. Influenza epidemiology

Among the influenza-positive samples (n = 351), influenza A was the dominant type (n = 303, 86.4%), among which influenza A(H3N2) constituted 42.5% (n = 149) and A(H1N1) constituted 30.2% (n = 106) over the 9 study seasons. Forty-eight samples (13.7%) could not be subtyped for influenza A. Influenza B/Yamagata was detected in 9.4% (n = 33), B/Victoria in 2.0% (n = 7) samples, while in 1.7% (n = 6) the lineage could not be defined. Influenza A and B coinfection was detected in 2 cases.

The dominant subtype/lineage across the seasons was variable; no B/Yamagata detections were observed in 2019 to 2020 or thereafter. (Fig. 1).

3.3. Clinical progress and outcomes of the influenza-positive patients

Seventy-four (21.1%) (n = 74) of influenza-positive patients were admitted to the ICU, 21.1% (n = 74) required MV, and 11.1% (n = 39) died in the hospital. (Matching numbers are coincidental, not represent coexistence of ICU admission and MV in the same group). These rates were not statistically significant than those observed in influenza-negative patients (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content that shows the comparison of clinical outcomes between Influenza negative and positive patients).

3.4. Risk factors for severe influenza outcomes and length of hospital stay

In analyses assessing predictors of influenza-related ICU admission, univariate models for ICU admission among confirmed influenza-positive patients showed that older age, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and influenza subtype A positivity were statistically significantly associated with ICU admission (P < .05; Fig. 3), but only older age and diabetes remained significant in the multivariate analysis (Fig. 4). Each additional year of age increased the odds of ICU admission by 3% (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.03; 95% CI: 1.02–1.05; P < .001), and patients with diabetes had nearly twice the odds compared with nondiabetic patients (aOR 1.91; 95% CI: 1.09–3.33; P = .02).

Furthermore, influenza vaccination during the current season was significantly and negatively associated with ICU admission (aOR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.12–0.80; P = .02), indicating a 66% relative protection among vaccinated patients.

In the univariate analysis, several factors were independently associated with an increased likelihood of requiring MV (Fig. 3). Older age, chronic kidney disease, COPD, having 2 or more comorbidities or 3 or more comorbidities, infection with influenza A subtypes significantly increased the odds of need for MV. However, in the multivariate logistic regression, only COPD remained independently associated with the need for MV (aOR 3.13; 95% CI: 1.77–5.61; P < .001; Fig. 4).

Univariate analysis identified age, COPD, chronic kidney disease, neoplasm, influenza subtype A and having 3 or more comorbidities as significant predictors of in-hospital death (Fig. 3); whilst, older age, chronic kidney disease and neoplasm stayed significant in multivariate analysis (Fig. 4). Patients with chronic kidney disease had a more than fourfold increased risk of in-hospital mortality (aOR 4.54; 95% CI: 2.05–9.88; P < .001). Those with neoplasms similarly demonstrated a markedly elevated risk (aOR 3.75; 95% CI: 1.63–8.38; P < .001). Moreover, each additional year of age increased the odds by 2% (aOR 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.05; P = .03).

A log-linear regression model was used to identify risk factors for longer hospital stay in hospitalized influenza-positive patients. The modeling revealed positive associations with age, ICU admission, chronic kidney disease, and COPD (Fig. 5). The expected LOS of those who were admitted to the ICU was 46.7% longer (95% CI: 22.2–76.1%) than that of their counterparts with no ICU admission. Likewise, the expected LOS of those who were ≥65 years (27.1%, 95% CI: 7.7–49.9%), had chronic kidney disease (44.6%, 95% CI: 14.5 to 82.8%), and COPD (24.7%, 95% CI: 5.0 to 48.1%) were statistically significantly longer than those who did not have these risk factors.

4. Discussion

This study, based on pooled data from GIHSN-Türkiye, compared personal and health-related characteristics of hospitalized ILI patients by influenza case status across 9 seasons and evaluated the determinants of severe outcomes during hospitalization. On average, one fifth of the influenza-positive patients were admitted to the ICU, one of every 5 patients required MV, and 1 out of 10 died in the hospital. In patients aged 65 years and older, these ratios increased to 32.3% (n = 62), 24.0% (n = 46), and 14.1% (n = 27), respectively; the highest discrepancy by age was in ICU admission. A study using CDC national hospital surveillance data from 1,07,941 influenza hospitalizations found that 16.2% were admitted to the ICU, 6.2% required MV, and in-hospital death rate was 2.7%.[11] It has also been shown that hospitalized patients in the low-middle income countries have a 7-times higher risk of being admitted to the ICU due to influenza when compared to those in high-income countries.[3]

In this cohort, 54.7% of the influenza-positive patients were 65 years and older, consistent with international surveillance reports, which indicate that 55% of influenza-related adult hospitalizations occur in individuals aged 65 years and older.[12,13] Additionally, 69.2% (n = 27) of patients who died in the hospital were 65 years and older, similar to patterns observed in other studies. According to CDC estimates, 70% to 85% of all hospitalizations and 50% to 70% of all deaths related to seasonal influenza occur among adults aged 65 years and older each year.[14] A 5-season influenza burden study in Spain, covering 2015 to 2020, reported that more than 60% of hospitalized influenza patients and 82% of those who died in the hospital were 65 years and older.[15] A systematic review characterizing the global clinical burden of seasonal influenza among adults found that ICU admission rates for hospitalized patients aged 65 years and older ranged from 4% to 27.3% across studies, with most reported rates between 10% and 20%.[16] Additionally, the in-hospital mortality of hospitalized patients aged 85 years and older was reported as 13.9% in a multicenter study, which also indicated that among ICU-admitted adults, mortality could reach up to 24%.[17]

In multivariate analysis, each additional year of age increased the risk of ICU admission by 3%, the presence of diabetes approximately doubled the risk, whilst seasonal influenza vaccination reduced ICU admission by 66%, supporting the need for increasing seasonal influenza vaccination coverage regardless of an age threshold of 65 years. COPD was the only independent predictor of MV, increasing the risk 3.1-fold. The strongest determinants of in-hospital mortality were chronic kidney disease which increased mortality 4.5-fold and malignancy 3.7-fold; age also increased the risk of mortality by 2% per year. These findings provide strong evidence of vulnerability and amplified risk of severe influenza disease and complications in the older age group and adults with noncommunicable diseases.

It is also important to consider the potential interplay of age and chronic medical conditions, which may further contribute to the increased disease burden and severity. This study identified a substantial comorbidity burden: 85.2% of influenza-positive patients had at least 1 comorbidity, similar to the findings from the US FluSurv-NET data indicating that 89.1% of hospitalized patients during the 2024-25 season had at least 1 comorbidity.[4] In this study, cardiovascular disease 54.4% was the most prevalent comorbidity, as also reported in the literature, particularly among older adults.[4] However, the presence of cardiovascular diseases did not turn out be a significant determinant of severe influenza outcomes. On the other hand, diabetes, chronic kidney diseases, COPD and malignancy stood up as significant risk factors for a complicated clinical course among hospitalized adult patients. A recent systematic review found that people with diabetes have higher rates of influenza-related hospitalization and death than those without diabetes, even after adjusting for confounders.[18] Moreover, epidemiologic data suggest that influenza contributes substantially to diabetes-related excess mortality, especially in older adults.[19] Chronic kidney disease raises the risk of severe influenza outcomes, including death (e.g. hospitalized influenza patients with end-stage renal disease having worse outcomes).[20] Similarly, malignancy has been previously implicated in higher mortality rates among influenza-hospitalized patients, with cancer patients having increased risk of influenza-related death compared to non-cancer cohorts.[21,22] Individuals with COPD develop more severe disease during influenza.[17] For instance, a recent surveillance study reported that COPD increases the risk of developing acute respiratory failure by approximately 13.4-fold in influenza patients.[23] Similarly, influenza infection in older patients with COPD was found to be strongly associated with COPD exacerbations and the need for MV.[24] Influenza infection can rapidly worsen respiratory function in COPD patients, leading to rapid progression to respiratory failure and potentially requiring MV. From a public health perspective, these findings emphasize the need for simultaneous implementation of effective primary prevention strategies to decrease the prevalence of noncommunicable diseases as well as to increase the influenza vaccination coverage rates, to establish surveillance systems that will guide policies, and to timely diagnose and treat the high-risk adult patients for influenza.

Influenza infections are known not only to increase the risk of hospitalization but also to extend the length of stay.[25] These results show that, as an additional correlate of influenza disease severity, hospital LOS was prolonged by 46.7% with ICU admission, by 44.6% with the presence of chronic kidney disease, by 27.1% for adults 65 years and older, and by 24.7% with the presence of COPD. In a Spanish cohort, supporting our findings, the median hospital stay for influenza patients was 5 days overall, but for those requiring ICU treatment it rose to 13 days, and the duration increased with age.[26] In a 5-year active surveillance study, Miron et al demonstrated that older age was associated with influenza-related complications and prolonged hospital stay.[23] These findings support the hypothesis that a weakened immune response and a higher burden of comorbidities in older patients contribute to a more severe course of influenza and longer hospitalization. Additionally, research indicates that individuals over 65 years of age who are hospitalized with influenza frequently experience a decline in functional independence and a substantial rise in both in-hospital and post-discharge long-term care needs.[27] Chronic medical conditions, particularly renal disorders, have been identified as risk factors for severe influenza, longer hospital stays and more complicated clinical courses.[23,28] A study covering 5 influenza seasons demonstrated that patients with COPD had a significantly longer length of hospital stay compared to those without COPD.[23] Furthermore, a study based on data from the international GIHSN project showed that COPD was associated with an average of 3.22 additional days of LOS among influenza patients.[29] Influenza complications are more common in patients with COPD due to a weakened inflammatory response and fragile respiratory system, leading to respiratory failure, increased need for MV and prolonged care. At the same time influenza can lead to exacerbation of COPD and further extending the length of hospitalization. These findings show that older adults and adults with certain comorbidities carry the major burden of influenza-related hospitalizations and death and during the influenza season which exerts a significant pressure on the healthcare system.[16]

In this study, seasonal influenza vaccination was associated with a 66% reduction in the odds of ICU admission compared with unvaccinated patients, even in a low vaccination coverage setting. This result is consistent with previous literature indicating the protective effects of vaccination against severe outcomes.[30] A CDC-supported multicenter study of hospitalized adults between 2012 and 2015 reported that, compared with unvaccinated individuals, vaccination was associated with an 82% lower risk of ICU admission among patients hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza and a 37% lower risk of non-ICU hospitalization, suggesting substantial attenuation in severity among breakthrough cases.[31] Similarly, observational analyses in hospitalized cohorts have found that vaccination, relative to no vaccination, reduced in-hospital death by approximately 52% to 79% and ICU admissions by approximately 37% in adults, supporting a protective effect on disease severity rather than infection prevention alone.[32] Overall, these findings strengthen the evidence that influenza vaccination reduces clinical severity and the need for intensive care among adults with laboratory-confirmed influenza.[33] Differences in results across studies likely reflect variation in circulating subtypes, vaccine match, analytic design, population characteristics, and potential confounding factors.

In older adults, immunosenescence and inflammaging reduce the antibody response to standard-dose inactivated influenza vaccines (SD-IIV), highlighting the need for enhanced formulations. High-dose inactivated influenza vaccine consistently show superior immunogenicity and clinical protection in adults ≥65 years, with randomized trials and meta-analyses demonstrating ~24% higher relative vaccine efficacy and 13 to 24% reductions in influenza- or pneumonia-related hospitalizations compared with standard-dose vaccines (SD-IIV).[34-36] The most recent real-world pooled data further show that high-dose inactivated influenza vaccine reduces laboratory-confirmed influenza hospitalizations by 31.9% compared with the SD-IIV.[37] Adjuvanted and recombinant vaccines also enhance immune responses, with observational evidence indicating 12% to 25% and 18% to 30% higher effectiveness, respectively, compared to SD-IIV.[38,39] Overall, enhanced vaccines provide additional protection in older adults and can effectively reduce influenza-related hospitalizations. These findings demonstrate the life-saving importance of tailored vaccination strategies for older adults and those with chronic diseases.

This study has certain strengths. First of all, the study utilized the core active surveillance methodology of GISHN to prospectively identify and actively follow the participants. Multiple centers sent data which increased the generalizability of the results. Cases presenting with ILI were tested by molecular assays to confirm influenza diagnosis. Each severity outcome was assessed separately which help identify the determinants of different severity outcomes. Lastly, the study recruited participants over 12 successive years for 9 influenza seasons pre-and post-coronavirus disease-19 pandemic constituting a large clinical database of influenza positive hospitalized patients.

On the other hand, the study has some limitations. First, the observational design precluded causal inference regarding the associations between patient-level factors and severe outcomes. Two seasons, one of which was the 2017 to 18 influenza season- the first most severe season after the 2009 influenza pandemic- were not represented in this dataset. Information regarding lifestyle factors, such smoking status, alcohol use, and body mass index was not collected, which may have limited the assessment of additional risk factors for severe outcomes. Another limitation is reliance on self-declaration of influenza vaccination status of the patients’ as there is no centralized influenza vaccination record system for adults in Türkiye. Antiviral treatment data were not available for the majority of cases; hence we couldn’t enter antiviral treatment as a possible determinant in the univariate and multivariate models. Lastly, we do not claim that this study epidemiologically represents the whole country.

In conclusion, findings of this study, support that increasing age among adults and patients with chronic medical conditions, primarily chronic kidney diseases, diabetes mellitus, malignancy and COPD represent a vulnerable subgroup among hospitalized influenza patients with regards to severe outcomes. These findings can have substantial implications in the prevention of severe influenza disease among adults, which could decrease the public health burden and the pressure on the healthcare system, and can guide policies and implementation strategies in Türkiye. Prevention of influenza and its complications with tailored immunization strategies, policies guided by active surveillance, strategies to timely diagnose and treat influenza and management guidelines for higher risk populations to provide the best protection can be achieved through informed decisions.
