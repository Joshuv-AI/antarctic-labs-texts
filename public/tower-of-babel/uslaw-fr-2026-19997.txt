Notice of Intended Repatriation: Autry Museum of the American 
West, Los Angeles, CA

AGENCY: National Park Service, Interior.

ACTION: Notice.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

SUMMARY: In accordance with the Native American Graves Protection and 
Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), the Autry Museum of the American West 
(Southwest Museum of the American Indian Collection) intends to 
repatriate certain cultural items that meet the definition of 
unassociated funerary objects, sacred objects, and/or objects of 
cultural patrimony and that have a cultural affiliation with the Indian 
Tribes or Native Hawaiian organizations in this notice.

DATES: Repatriation of the cultural items in this notice may occur on 
or after October 30, 2026.

ADDRESSES: Send additional, written requests for repatriation of the 
cultural items in this notice to Karimah Richardson, M.Phil., RPA, 
Associate Curator of Anthropology and Repatriation Supervisor, Autry 
Museum of the American West, 4700 Western Heritage Way, Los Angeles, CA 
90027, email [email protected].

SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION: This notice is published as part of the 
National Park Service's administrative responsibilities under NAGPRA. 
The determinations in this notice are the sole responsibility of the 
Autry Museum of the American West, and additional information on the 
determinations in this notice, including the results of consultation, 
can be found in the summary or related records. The National Park 
Service is not responsible for the determinations in this notice.

Abstract of Information Available

    A total of one cultural item has been requested for repatriation. 
The one unassociated funerary object is a necklace. In 1924, the 
Southwest Museum of the American Indian (now part of the Autry Museum 
of the American West) was donated cultural material by Mr. F. A. 
Insley. The S'eit (necklace) (348.G.10) was collected in Southeast 
Alaska at an unknown date, most likely collected by the donor himself. 
Through consultation with the Central Council of the Tlingit and Haida 
Indian Tribes of Alaska, the cultural material has been identified as 
an unassociated funerary object. Cultural material like this one, are 
shaman's personal items and therefore would have only been collected 
from a burial.
    A total of two cultural items has been requested for repatriation. 
The two sacred objects/objects of cultural patrimony are one button 
shirt and one beaded shirt. In 1956, the Southwest Museum purchased the 
cultural material from Mr. Robert Stolper. Mr. Stolper purchased the 
cultural material on unknown dates from unknown locations. The 
Yooka.oot Kud[aacute]s' (button shirt) (491.P.3327) and Kud[aacute]s 
(beaded shirt) (491.P.3328), were identified as being both objects of 
cultural patrimony

essential to the ongoing practice of traditional ceremonial rites.
    A total of one cultural item has been requested for repatriation. 
The one sacred object/object of cultural patrimony is one Chilkat 
blanket. In 1958, the Southwest Museum purchased one Naaxein (Chilkat

    A total of four cultural items has been requested for repatriation. 
The three sacred objects/objects of cultural patrimony are one 
headdress frontlet, one dagger, and one ``Slave Killer'' club; and the 
one unassociated funerary object is an oyster catcher rattle. In 1983, 
the Southwest Museum purchased cultural materials from Mr. George 
Terasaki of Sotheby Parke Bernet Auction House in New York City. The 
cultural materials are one Sh[aacute]kee.[aacute]t (headdress frontlet) 
(1983.P.10.1) and one Gw[aacute]laa (dagger) (1983.P.10.3A). Both

for the ongoing practice of traditional ceremonial rites and 
activities. Additionally, one Sheishoox (oyster catcher rattle) 
(1983.P.16.3) was collected by an unknown person at an unknown date, 
probably Alaska or Pacific Northwest Coast. Furthermore, one Goox Du 
Een (``Slave Killer'' club) (1983.P.16.1A) was collected in Alaska by 
an unknown person at an unknown date. This cultural object has been 
purchased multiple times, with the earliest known recorded collector 
being Mr. Jones, an antique dealer in Bath. He bought the object circa 
1895 at the Philadelphia Exhibition. It was then found in his cellar in 
Bath by Alban Head Esq. at Easter in 1925, who bought the cultural 
object. It then somehow made its way to Mr. H.G. Beasley of England, 
and then to Ms. Katherine White of Seattle, Washington. Through 
consultation with the Central Council of the Tlingit and Haida Indian 
Tribes of Alaska, the Sh[aacute]kee.[aacute]t (headdress frontlet) 
(1983.P.10.1), Gw[aacute]laa (dagger) (1983.P.10.3A), and Goox Du Een 
(``Slave Killer'' club) (1983.P.16.1A) have been identified as being 
both objects of cultural patrimony and sacred objects, while the 
Sheishoox (oyster catcher rattle) (1983.P.16.3) has been identified as 
an unassociated funerary object. Cultural objects, such as the 
Sh[aacute]kee.[aacute]t (headdress frontlet) (1983.P.10.1), 
Gw[aacute]laa (dagger) (1983.P.10.3A), and Goox Du Een (``Slave 
Killer'' club) (1983.P.16.1A) are living objects and, therefore, 
sacred. The Tlingit and Haida Tribes of Alaska have stated that they

and activities. The Sheishoox (oyster catcher rattle) (1983.P.16.3) has 
been identified by Tlingit representatives as a shaman's object, which 
is a personal object that would have only been collected from a burial.

    There is a relationship of shared group identity that can be 
reasonably traced between the unassociated funerary objects and sacred 
objects/objects of cultural patrimony through oral tradition and 
historic evidence to the Tlingit and Haida people. Through 
consultation, objects were identified as both sacred objects and 
objects of cultural patrimony. The Central Council of the Tlingit and 
Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska have stated that ``Crest objects such as 
the objects claimed in this claim are recognized by Tlingit as sacred 
objects.'' These sacred objects are essential to the practice of 
ceremonial rites and have ongoing historical, traditional, and cultural 
importance central to the Tlingit society and culture. Objects were 
also identified as unassociated funerary objects, either through museum 
records or there were identified as personal items by the Central 
Council of the Tlingit and Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska. Objects are 
currently being tested for potentially hazardous substances. Through 
consultation, the Central Council of the Tlingit and Haida Indian 
Tribes of Alaska stated cultural affiliation to the cultural materials.

Determinations

    The Autry Museum of the American West has determined that:
     The 18 unassociated funerary objects described in this 
notice are reasonably believed to have been placed intentionally with 
or near human remains, and are connected, either at the time of death 
or later as part of the death rite or ceremony of a Native American 
culture according to the Native American traditional knowledge of a 
lineal descendant, Indian Tribe, or Native Hawaiian organization. The 
unassociated funerary objects have been identified by a preponderance 
of the evidence as related to human remains, specific individuals, or 
families, or removed from a specific burial site or burial area of an 
individual or individuals with cultural affiliation to an Indian Tribe 
or Native Hawaiian organization.
     The 13 sacred objects/objects of cultural patrimony 
described in this notice are, according to the Native American 
traditional knowledge of an Indian Tribe or Native Hawaiian 
organization, specific ceremonial objects needed by a traditional 
Native American religious leader for present-day adherents to practice 
traditional Native American religion, and have ongoing historical, 
traditional, or cultural importance central to the Native American 
group, including any constituent sub-group (such as a band, clan, 
lineage, ceremonial society, or other subdivision).
     There is a connection between the cultural items described 
in this notice and the Central Council of the Tlingit & Haida Indian 
Tribes; Ketchikan Indian Community; and the Sitka Tribe of Alaska.

Requests for Repatriation

    Additional, written requests for repatriation of the cultural items 
in this notice must be sent to the authorized representative identified 
in this notice under ADDRESSES. Requests for repatriation may be 
submitted by any lineal descendant, Indian Tribe, or Native Hawaiian 
organization not identified in this notice who shows, by a 
preponderance of the evidence, that the requestor is a lineal 
descendant or a culturally affiliated Indian Tribe or Native Hawaiian 
organization.

[[Page 61880]]

    Repatriation of the cultural items in this notice to a requestor 
may occur on or after October 30, 2026. If competing requests for 
repatriation are received, the Autry Museum of the American West must 
determine the most appropriate requestor prior to repatriation. 
Requests for joint repatriation of the cultural items are considered a 
single request and not competing requests. The Autry Museum of the 
American West is responsible for sending a copy of this notice to the 
Indian Tribes and Native Hawaiian organizations identified in this 
notice and to any other consulting parties.
