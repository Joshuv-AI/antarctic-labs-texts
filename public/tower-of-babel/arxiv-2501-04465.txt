Probability distributions of the order parameter of the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) model
A. Rançon, B. Delamotte, L. Šaravanja, I. Balog

Abstract
We study the probability distribution function (PDF) of the order parameter of the three-dimensional O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) model at criticality using the functional renormalisation group. For this purpose, we generalize the method introduced in [Balog et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 129 , 210602 (2022)] to the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) model. We study the large N N limit, as well as the cases N = 2 N=2 and N = 3 N=3 at the level of the Local Potential Approximation (LPA), and compare our results to Monte Carlo simulations. We compute the entire family of universal scaling functions, obtained in the limit where the system size L L and the correlation length of the infinite system ξ ∞ \xi_{\infty} diverge, with the ratio ζ = L / ξ ∞ \zeta=L/\xi_{\infty} constant. We also generalize our results to the approach of criticality from the low-temperature phase where another infinite family of universal PDF exists. We find that the LPA describes very well the functional form of the family of PDFs, once we correct for a global amplitude of the (logarithm of the) PDF and of ζ \zeta .



I
Introduction

The emergence of universal behavior from strong correlations has intrigued physicists for decades. Near a continuous phase transition, the divergence of the correlation length effectively erases most microscopic details, leaving the system characterized by universal behaviors at large scales. These manifest as scaling laws for most observables. Crucially, broad classes of systems, categorized by factors such as the symmetry of the order parameter, dimensionality, and other global properties, exhibit identical universal properties.

Furthermore, the diverging correlation length implies that the system’s microscopic degrees of freedom are strongly correlated. This implies that close to criticality, the probability distribution function (PDF) of the order parameter can be non-Gaussian. For a model involving a
N
-component microscopic field
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
and described by a Hamiltonian
{\cal H}
, this PDF of a
d
-dimensional system of size
L
is given by

P(\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{N}\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\,\delta\left(\mathbf{s}-\hat{\mathbf{s}}\right)\exp(-{\cal H}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]/T),

(1)

where
\hat{\mathbf{s}}=L^{-d}\int_{\textbf{x}}\hat{\bm{\phi}}(\textbf{x})
is the total spin per unit volume,
L
the system size and
\mathcal{N}
a normalization factor. We focus here on systems with short-range interactions in dimension
2<d<4
, for which the PDF is non-Gaussian at criticality. Noting that the choice of boundary conditions affects the shape of the PDF, we will consider here only periodic boundary conditions. Equivalently, one can study the rate function
I(\mathbf{s})
, also known as the constraint effective potential in high-energy physics
[
1
,
2
,
3
]

I(\mathbf{s})=-\lim_{L\to\infty}L^{-d}\ln P(\mathbf{s}).

(2)

Standard scaling arguments imply that close to the critical temperature
T_{c}

, as a function of
\mathbf{s}
,

and

should obey a scaling law
[
4

I(\mathbf{s},\xi_{\infty},L)=b^{-d}I(\mathbf{s}b^{(d-2+\eta)/2},\xi_{\infty}/b,L/b),

(3)

where

to parametrize the distance to the critical point,

\mathbf{s}L^{(d-2+\eta)/2}

5

By systematically coarse-graining the microscopic degrees of freedom, RG leads to an effective macroscopic description, where universality arises because microscopically different systems can exhibit the same large-scale behavior. II
Large
N
limit

In this section, we compute the rate function of the
O(N)
model in the large
N
limit. We start presenting the derivation of the effective potential (Gibbs free energy), which is standard, see for instance
[
48
]
. This is useful for two reasons. First, the calculation of the rate function is very similar to that of the effective potential. Second, it is necessary to have the expression for the effective potential to find the critical point and define the correlation length and thus control the parameter
L/\xi_{\infty}
, which parametrizes the rate functions. It will also allow us to discuss the differences between the rate function and the effective potential at finite size.

II.1
Effective potential

The
O(N)
model is defined by the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]=\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(\frac{(\nabla\hat{\bm{\phi}})^{2}}{2}+V\left(\hat{\bm{\phi}}^{2}/2\right)\right),

(4)

where
V
is the microscopic potential, which is typically of the form
V(z)=r_{0}z+\frac{u_{0}}{6N}z^{2}
. The scaling in
N
is chosen such that
V(z)
is of order
N
if
z
is of order
N
.

In dimension
d>2
, there is a second-order phase transition between a ferromagnetic (
\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}\rangle\neq 0
) and a paramagnetic (
\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}\rangle=0
) phase when tuning the potential (e.g.
r_{0}
at fixed
u_{0}
). Here, averages are performed using
\exp(-\mathcal{H})
as a Boltzmann weight.
The generating functional of correlation functions of the
O(N)
model is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{Z}[\mathbf{h}]=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\,e^{-\mathcal{H}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]+\int_{\textbf{x}}\mathbf{h}.\hat{\bm{\phi}}}.\end{split}

(5)

In the large
N
limit, it is convenient to introduce two auxiliary fields
\lambda(\textbf{x})
and
\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})
such that

1=\int\mathcal{D}\lambda\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}\,\exp\left\{-i\int_{\textbf{x}}\lambda\left(\frac{\hat{\bm{\phi}}^{2}}{2}-\hat{\rho}\right)\right\}

(6)

allowing us to reexpress the partition function of the
O(N)
model as

\begin{split}\mathcal{Z}[\mathbf{h}]=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\mathcal{D}\lambda\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}\,e^{-\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(\frac{(\nabla\hat{\bm{\phi}})^{2}}{2}+i\lambda\frac{\hat{\bm{\phi}}^{2}}{2}\right)-\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(V(\hat{\rho})-i\lambda\hat{\rho}\right)+\int_{\textbf{x}}\mathbf{h}.\hat{\bm{\phi}}}.\end{split}

(7)

Writing the field
\hat{\bm{\phi}}=(\hat{\sigma},\hat{\bm{\pi}})
, with the
\hat{\sigma}
direction along
\mathbf{h}
, and integrating out the
\hat{\bm{\pi}}
fields, we finally obtain

\mathcal{Z}[\mathbf{h}]=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\sigma}\mathcal{D}\lambda\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}\,e^{-\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}[\hat{\sigma},\lambda,\hat{\rho}]+\int_{\textbf{x}}h\hat{\sigma}},

(8)

with

\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}[\hat{\sigma},\lambda,\hat{\rho}]=\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(\frac{(\nabla\hat{\sigma})^{2}}{2}+i\lambda\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\right)+\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(V(\hat{\rho})-i\lambda\hat{\rho}\right)+\frac{N-1}{2}{\rm Tr}\log(g_{\pi}^{-1}),

(9)

and with the correlation function
g_{\pi}
of the
\hat{\bm{\pi}}
fields satisfying
(-\nabla^{2}+i\lambda(\textbf{x}))g_{\pi}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y})
.

We aim at computing the effective action
\Gamma[\bm{\phi}]
(or Gibbs free energy), the Legendre transform of
\ln\mathcal{Z}[\mathbf{h}]
with respect to
\mathbf{h}
, i.e.

\Gamma[\bm{\phi}]=-\ln\mathcal{Z}[\mathbf{h}]+\int_{\textbf{x}}\mathbf{h}.\bm{\phi},

(10)

with
\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}(\textbf{x})\rangle
the magnetization in presence of the magnetic field
\mathbf{h}
.
Assuming that
\hat{\sigma}\sim\sqrt{N}
,
\hat{\rho}\sim N,\lambda\sim 1
and
V(\hat{\rho})\sim N
for
N
large, all terms in Eq. (
9
) are of order
N
and the functional integral can be evaluated at the saddle-point as
N\to\infty

\begin{split}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}}{\delta\lambda(\textbf{x})}\bigg|_{\lambda=\lambda_{0}}=0,\\
\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}}{\delta\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})}\bigg|_{\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}_{0}}=0.\end{split}

(11)

Furthermore, the effective action reads in this approximation

\Gamma[\bm{\phi}]=\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}[\bm{\phi},\lambda_{0},\hat{\rho}_{0}],

(12)

where we have used the
O(N)
invariance to replace
\hat{\sigma}
by
\bm{\phi}
when performing the Legendre transform.

In uniform magnetization
\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\bm{\phi}
,
\Gamma[\bm{\phi}]=L^{d}U(\rho)
with
\rho=\bm{\phi}^{2}/2
, and the saddle-point solutions
\lambda_{0}
and
\hat{\rho}_{0}
are also uniform (and dependent on
\rho
). One shows that
i\lambda_{0}=U^{\prime}(\rho)
, primes meaning derivatives with respect to
\rho
, while the saddle-point equations read

\begin{split}i\lambda_{0}&=V^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}_{0}),\\
\rho&=\hat{\rho}_{0}-\frac{N}{2}\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+W(\rho)},\end{split}

(13)

with
W\equiv U^{\prime}
, and the sum over momenta is understood as being regularized in the ultra-violet (UV), that is, for large momenta
|\mathbf{q}|
.

Finally, it is convenient to introduce the inverse of the function
V^{\prime}
, which we call
f_{\Lambda}(W)
for later convenience (see Sec.
III.4
), i.e.
V^{\prime}(f_{\Lambda}(W))=W
.
The gap equation can be rewritten as an implicit equation for
\rho
seen as a function of
W
,

\rho=f_{\Lambda}(W)-\frac{N}{2}\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+W}.

(14)

In the following, we regularize the sum over momenta by introducing an exponential factor
e^{-\mathbf{q}^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}
, which allows for using Schwinger’s representation of the propagator and perform explicitly the sum over momenta
[
48
]

\begin{split}\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+W}\ \to\ \frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\frac{e^{-\mathbf{q}^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+W}&=\frac{1}{L^{d}}e^{W/\Lambda^{2}}\int_{\Lambda^{-2}}^{\infty}ds\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{-s(\mathbf{q}^{2}+W)},\\
&=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\int_{\Lambda^{-2}}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-sW}\upvartheta^{d}(s4\pi/L^{2})+\mathcal{O}(W/\Lambda^{2}),\\
&=\frac{1}{L^{d-2}}\int_{\frac{4\pi}{\Lambda^{2}L^{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{4\pi}e^{-s\frac{L^{2}W}{4\pi}}\upvartheta^{d}(s)+\mathcal{O}(W/\Lambda^{2}),\end{split}

(15)

where we have neglected non-universal terms of order
W/\Lambda^{2}
(which can be absorbed into
f_{\Lambda}
) and
\upvartheta(s)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-s\pi n^{2}}
is a Jacobi theta function, which obeys in particular
\upvartheta(s)=s^{-1/2}\upvartheta(1/s)
. The UV divergence of the momentum integral translates into the divergence
s^{-d/2}
at small
s
for
2<d<4
. Adding and subtracting such a term and taking the limit
\Lambda\to\infty
in the integral, we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\frac{e^{-\mathbf{q}^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+W}&=\frac{2}{d-2}\frac{\Lambda^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}+\frac{1}{L^{d-2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{4\pi}\left(e^{-u\frac{L^{2}W}{4\pi}}\upvartheta^{d}(u)-u^{-d/2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(W^{2}/\Lambda^{2}).\end{split}

(16)

It will be convenient to include the UV-dependent constant into the function
f_{\Lambda}
, defining
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}(W)=f_{\Lambda}(W)-\frac{N}{2(d-2)}\frac{\Lambda^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}
.

The function

F_{d}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{4\pi}\left(e^{-uz}\upvartheta^{d}(u)-u^{-d/2}\right)

(17)

is shown in Fig.
1
. It is monotonically increasing and interpolates between
-1/(8\pi z)
at small
z
(corresponding to the term
\mathbf{q}=0
of the sum over momenta in Eq. (
13
)) and
-\Gamma(1-d/2)\frac{z^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{8\pi}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-2\sqrt{\pi z}})
at large
z
(corresponding to the thermodynamic limit of the sum up to exponentially small correction terms).

The critical point is found by taking the thermodynamic limit,
L\to\infty
, for which the effective potential becomes independent of
L
,
W\to W_{\infty}
, and the gap equation becomes in this limit

\rho=\hat{f}_{\Lambda}(W_{\infty})+Nc_{d}W_{\infty}^{\frac{d-2}{2}},

(18)

with

c_{d}=-\frac{\Gamma(1-d/2)}{2(4\pi)^{d/2}}>0.

(19)

Note that
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}(W)
is a regular function of
W
at small
W
(the regime of interest, since the correlation length
\xi
is proportional to
W^{-1/2}
), and the term proportional to
W_{\infty}^{(d-2)/2}
is therefore dominant compared to a term of order
W_{\infty}
. We can therefore expand
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}(W_{\infty})=-N\delta+\mathcal{O}(W_{\infty})
and the gap equation in the thermodynamic limit becomes

\begin{split}\rho/N=-\delta+c_{d}W_{\infty}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}.\end{split}

(20)

Note that
\delta
depends on the Hamiltonian and is thus non-universal (e.g.
\delta=3r_{0}/u_{0}+\Lambda^{d-2}/((4\pi)^{d/2}(d-2))
for a
(\hat{\bm{\phi}}^{2})^{2}
theory with the regularization described above).

The critical point is found by solving
W_{\infty}(0)=0
(minimum of the free energy at zero magnetization and infinite correlation length
\xi_{\infty}^{2}=1/W_{\infty}(0)
) and thus corresponds to
\delta=0
.
The system is disordered for
\delta>0
(minimum at zero magnetization and finite correlation length), with correlation length diverging as
\xi_{\infty}(\delta)=(\delta/c_{d})^{-\nu}
with
\nu=1/(d-2)
as expected. For
\delta<0
, the minimum of the free energy is at
\rho_{0}=N\delta
, from which we obtain the critical exponent
\beta=1/2
. Note that the effective potential is not defined for
\rho<\rho_{0}
since by construction
|\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}\rangle_{h,\infty}|^{2}\geq 2\rho_{0}
for all possible magnetic fields, and the relationship between
h
and
\phi
cannot be inverted (when doing the Legendre transform) for smaller magnetization.

For finite-size systems, in the limit of small
W
(i.e.
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}(W)=-N\delta+\mathcal{O}(W)
), the gap equation reads

\begin{split}\rho=-N\delta+\frac{N}{L^{d-2}}F_{d}\left(\frac{L^{2}W}{4\pi}\right).\end{split}

(21)

The system starts showing critical-like behavior for
\delta
small enough and large enough size
L
. As noted in
[
49
]
, there are many inequivalent ways to take the limit
\delta\to 0
and
L\to\infty
, leading to different scaling functions. We find it convenient to parametrize these in terms of
\zeta={\rm sign}(\delta)L/\xi_{\infty}(|\delta|)
, which is negative (positive) when criticality is obtained coming from the ordered (disordered) phase. Note that while the relationship between
\delta
and
\xi_{\infty}
depends on a non-universal amplitude,
\zeta
is free of such ambiguity, which makes it a good parametrization.

At finite size
L
,
W
cannot vanish for any
\rho
(since we would have a divergence at
\mathbf{q}=0
in the sum in Eq. (
13
)), and thus
W(\rho)>0
and the effective potential is strictly convex. Its minimum is thus at
\rho=0
for all
\delta
, corresponding to the fact that the magnetization of a finite system vanishes, since spontaneous symmetry breaking can only happen in infinite systems. The gap equation reads

\begin{split}F_{d}\left(\frac{L^{2}W}{4\pi}\right)=\frac{L^{d-2}\rho}{N}+\Delta,\end{split}

(22)

where we have introduced
\Delta=L^{d-2}\delta
, related to
\zeta
by

\Delta={\rm sign}(\delta)c_{d}|\zeta|^{d-2}.

(23)

We find that
L^{2}W
is a universal function of
\frac{L^{d-2}\rho}{N}
, and
\Delta
plays the role of a shift in
\rho
.

Deep in the disordered phase for finite but large
L
(i.e.
\delta>0
and
\delta\propto O(1)
independent of
L
, and
L
large),
W
differs from
W_{\infty}
by an exponentially small correction, see Eq. (
17
) and below.
Deep in the ordered side (
\delta<0
and
|\delta|\propto O(1)
independent of
L
, and
L
large), we find that
W(0)\propto L^{-d}
. Furthermore, we find that for
\rho<\rho_{0}
,
W(\rho)\simeq\frac{N}{2L^{d}(\rho_{0}-\rho)}
.
Finally, for
|\rho_{0}-\rho|\propto L^{-d+2}
,
W(\rho)\propto L^{-2}
.
The shape of the effective potential in the ordered phase can thus be expressed as
U(\rho)=\frac{N}{L^{d}}G\left(L^{d-2}(\rho-\rho_{0})/N\right)
with

G(x)\propto\begin{cases}\log|x|&\text{if $x<0$ and }|x|\gg 1,\\
x&\text{if }|x|\ll 1,\\
x^{\frac{d}{d-2}}&\text{if $x>0$ and }x\gg 1.\\
\end{cases}

(24)

For all
\Delta
,
W(\rho)\propto\rho^{\frac{2}{d-2}}
at large field, corresponding to
U(\rho)\propto\rho^{\frac{d}{d-2+\eta}}
with
\eta=0
for large
N
.
1
1

1

This is valid as long as
\rho
is not too large, i.e. as long as the universal part dominates compared to
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}
. At very large field, we recover
U(\rho)=V(\rho)
, the microscopic (and thus non-universal) potential.

II.2
Rate function

The derivation for the rate function is similar to that for the effective potential explained in Sec.
II.1
, see also
[
3
]
. Starting from Eq. (
1
) and using the exponential representation of the delta-function,
\delta(\mathbf{z})\propto\int d\mathbf{h}\,e^{i\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{z}}
,
2
2

2

See
[
50
]
for an alternative calculation using a different exponentiation of the delta-function.
such that

P(\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\int d\mathbf{h}\,e^{-iL^{d}\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{s}}\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\,e^{-{\cal H}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]+i\int_{\textbf{x}}\mathbf{h}.\hat{\bm{\phi}}}

(25)

with
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
a new normalization factor. Introducing two auxiliary fields
\check{\lambda}(\textbf{x})
and
\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})
, Eq. (
25
) reduces to

\begin{split}P(\mathbf{s})=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\mathcal{D}\check{\lambda}\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}d\mathbf{h}\,e^{-\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(\frac{(\nabla\hat{\bm{\phi}})^{2}}{2}+i\check{\lambda}\frac{\hat{\bm{\phi}}^{2}}{2}\right)-\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(V(\hat{\rho})-i\check{\lambda}\hat{\rho}\right)+i\mathbf{h}.\int_{\textbf{x}}(\mathbf{s}-\hat{\bm{\phi}})}.\end{split}

(26)

We stress that here,
\check{\lambda}
,
\hat{\rho}
and
\bm{\phi}
depend on

, whereas
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{s}
do not (in particular, the integral over

\check{\sigma}
the direction of
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
along
\mathbf{s}
, and splitting
\mathbf{h}
into
h_{\sigma}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\pi}

\hat{\bm{\pi}}
fields followed by that over
\mathbf{h}_{\pi}
gives

\begin{split}P(\mathbf{s})&=\int\mathcal{D}\check{\sigma}\mathcal{D}\check{\lambda}\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}d\mathbf{h}\,e^{-\mathcal{H}_{eff}[\check{\sigma},\check{\lambda},\hat{\rho}]+ih_{\sigma}\int_{\textbf{x}}(s-\check{\sigma})-\frac{N-1}{2}{\rm Tr}\log(\check{g}_{\pi}^{-1})-\mathbf{h}_{\pi}^{2}\int_{\textbf{x},\textbf{y}}\check{g}_{\pi}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})},\\

(27)

where
(-\nabla^{2}+i\check{\lambda}(\textbf{x}))\check{g}_{\pi}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y})

The saddle-point equations read, assuming that the solution is in constant fields,

\begin{split}\check{\sigma}&=s,\\
i\check{\lambda}\check{\sigma}&=ih_{\sigma},\\
i\check{\lambda}&=V^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}),\\
\frac{\check{\sigma}^{2}}{2}&=\hat{\rho}-\frac{N}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+i\check{\lambda}}.\end{split}

(28)

In the last equation, the subtraction of the
\mathbf{q}=0
term comes from the derivative with respect to
i\check{\lambda}
of
\log(\int_{\textbf{x},\textbf{y}}g_{\pi}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}))=\log(L^{d}/i\check{\lambda})

Writing

with
\check{\rho}=\mathbf{s}^{2}/2
, one shows that at the saddle-point,

, and thus the gap equation of the rate function is

\check{\rho}=f_{\Lambda}(\check{W})-\frac{N}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+\check{W}},

(29)

where
f_{\Lambda}
is the same function as in Eq. (
14
). Equation (
29
) is very similar to the gap equation (
14
), the only difference being the absence of the
\mathbf{q}=0
term in the sum over momenta.
We note right away that in the limit
L\to\infty
, the absence of this term does not matter, and the gap equations of
W
and
\check{W}
become identical, hence we recover the standard result that in the thermodynamic limit

III
Renormalization group approach to the rate function calculation

There is no known method to solve the
O(N)
model at finite
N
. In this section, we generalize the FRG approach devised in
[
49
]
to compute PDFs for Ising to the case of the
O(N)
model.

III.1
Definition of the effective actions
\Gamma_{M,k}

The derivation of the FRG for the calculation of rate functions follows closely the original derivation
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
, see
[
46
,
47
]
for reviews of the method. In a nutshell, given the Hamiltonian of a theory
\mathcal{H}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]
, a family of models is defined by introducing an addition to the Hamiltonian,
\Delta\mathcal{H}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{\phi}}.R_{k}.\hat{\bm{\phi}}
with
R_{k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})
an infrared regulator, parametrized by a momentum RG scale
k
. It is chosen such that the integration over modes with momenta
|q|<k
is suppressed while it leaves the modes
|q|>k
unchanged. Furthermore, we impose that
R_{k=0}=0
while
\lim_{k\to\Lambda}R_{k}
is such that the modified model is exactly solvable in this limit.
Defining a family of scale-dependent partition functions
\mathcal{Z}_{k}[\mathbf{h}]
and a scale-dependent free energy functional
-\ln(\mathcal{Z}_{k})
, its (modified) Legendre transform
\Gamma_{k}[\bm{\phi}]
is the object of interest. This scale-dependent effective action
\Gamma_{k}[\bm{\phi}]
obeys an exact RG flow equation, which can be approximated non-perturbatively.

To compute the rate function, one can follow a similar route, where now the (scale-dependent) partition function is modified to include the delta function found in Eq. (
1
),

\mathcal{Z}_{M,k}[\mathbf{h}]=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\,e^{-\mathcal{H}_{M}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]+\mathbf{h}.\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{\phi}}.R_{k}.\hat{\bm{\phi}}}

(36)

where
{\cal H}_{M}[\hat{\bm{\phi}}]={\cal H}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})+\frac{M^{2}}{2}[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}(\textbf{x})-\mathbf{s})]^{2}
. In the limit
M\to\infty
, the second term in
{\cal H}_{M}
becomes a delta function, and we recover the constraint
L^{-d}\int_{\textbf{x}}\hat{\bm{\phi}}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}
.
This implementation of the constraint is different from that of Sec.
II.2
, but it is better suited for the present purpose.

Introducing
\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\frac{\delta\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M,k}}{\delta\mathbf{h}(\textbf{x})}
, we define the (modified) Legendre transform of
\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M,k}
by

\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]=-\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M,k}[\mathbf{h}]+\mathbf{h}.\bm{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\phi}.R_{k}.\bm{\phi}-\frac{M^{2}}{2}\left[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})-\mathbf{s})\right]^{2}.

(37)

Using

\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M,k}}{\delta\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})}=\mathbf{h}(\textbf{x})-\int_{\textbf{y}}R_{k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\bm{\phi}(\textbf{y})-M^{2}\int_{\textbf{y}}(\bm{\phi}(\textbf{y})-\mathbf{s}),

(38)

allows us to write

\begin{split}e^{-\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]}=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})+\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M,k}}{\delta\bm{\phi}}.(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi})-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi}).R_{k}.(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi})}e^{-\frac{M^{2}}{2}[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}(\textbf{x})-\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x}))]^{2}}.\end{split}

(39)

Note that although it is not explicit from its definition, Eq. (
37
),
\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]
is independent of
s
as can be checked from Eq. (
43
) or by deriving Eq. (
37
) with respect to
\mathbf{s}
.

When evaluated in a constant field
\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}
and at
k=0
,

\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M,k=0}}{\delta\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})}\bigg|_{\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}}=\mathbf{h},

(40)

is a constant, by translation invariance, such that
\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}(\textbf{x})\rangle=\mathbf{s}
. Thus,

e^{-\Gamma_{M,k=0}[\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}]}=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\,e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})-\frac{M^{2}}{2}[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\mathbf{s})]^{2}+\mathbf{h}.\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\mathbf{s})},

(41)

and therefore

\begin{split}\lim_{M\to\infty}e^{-\Gamma_{M,k=0}[\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}]}\propto&\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,\delta\left(\mathbf{s}-L^{-d}\int_{\textbf{x}}\hat{\bm{\phi}}\right)e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})},\\
\propto&\ P(\mathbf{s}).\end{split}

(42)

This directly proves that
\lim_{M\to\infty}\Gamma_{M,k=0}[\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}]=L^{d}I(\mathbf{s})
.
At finite
k
, it is convenient to define
\check{\Gamma}_{k}[\bm{\phi}]=\lim_{M\to\infty}\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]
, and we interpret
L^{-d}\check{\Gamma}_{k}[\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})=\mathbf{s}]=I_{k}(\mathbf{s})
as a scale-dependent rate function.

Defining
R_{M,k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=R_{k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})+M^{2}
, Eq. (
39
) can be rewritten as

e^{-\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]}=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})+\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M,k}}{\delta\bm{\phi}}.(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi})-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi}).R_{M,k}.(\hat{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi})},

(43)

and therefore
\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]
can be interpreted as the scale-dependent Gibbs free energy of a system with Hamiltonian
\cal H
regularized by
R_{M,k}
. Thus, the exact flow equation of
\Gamma_{M,k}
can be found similarly to that of the standard scale-dependent effective action
\Gamma_{k}=\Gamma_{M=0,k}

[
49
]

\partial_{k}\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\int_{\textbf{x},\textbf{y}}\partial_{k}R_{M,k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\left(\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}+R_{M,k}\right)^{-1}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}),

(44)

where the trace is over the
O(N)
indices, and the elements of the matrix
\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}
are defined as

\left(\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}\right)_{ij}=\Gamma_{ij,M,k}^{(2)}[\textbf{x},\textbf{y};\bm{\phi}]=\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{M,k}}{\delta\phi_{i}(\textbf{x})\delta\phi_{j}(\textbf{y}))}.

(45)

The initial condition of the flow corresponds to
k=\Lambda
, for which
\lim_{k\to\Lambda}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\to\infty
. A saddle-point analysis gives

\lim_{k\to\Lambda}\mathcal{Z}_{M,k}[\mathbf{h}]\simeq e^{-\mathcal{H}_{M}[\bm{\phi}]+\mathbf{h}.\bm{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\phi}.R_{\Lambda}.\bm{\phi}},

(46)

with
\bm{\phi}[\mathbf{h}]
such that

\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{M}}{\delta\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})}-\mathbf{h}(\textbf{x})+\int_{\textbf{y}}R_{\Lambda}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\bm{\phi}(\textbf{y})=0.

(47)

Performing the Legendre transform and using Eq. (
37
), we obtain

\lim_{k\to\Lambda}\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]=\mathcal{H}[\bm{\phi}].

(48)

Finally, note that the effective action
\Gamma[\bm{\phi}]
of the original model, defined as the (true) Legendre transform of
\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M=0,k=0}[\mathbf{h}]
, is recovered from its scale-dependent counterpart
\Gamma_{k}[\bm{\phi}]=\Gamma_{M=0,k}[\bm{\phi}]
by taking the limit
k\to 0
. On the contrary, when
M\to\infty
,
\check{\Gamma}[\bm{\phi}]=\check{\Gamma}_{k=0}[\bm{\phi}]
is not a true Legendre transform even in this limit, due to the
M
term in Eq. (
37
), and can therefore be non-convex.

III.2
Local Potential Approximation

The Local Potential Approximation (LPA) corresponds to approximating the modified scale-dependent effective average action by the ansatz

\Gamma_{M,k}[\bm{\phi}]=\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(\frac{(\nabla\bm{\phi})^{2}}{2}+U_{M,k}(\rho)\right),

(49)

with
\rho(\textbf{x})=\bm{\phi}(\textbf{x})^{2}/2
, analogous to the LPA for the standard scale-dependent effective action
[
47
]
. Note that
I_{k}=\lim_{M\to\infty}U_{M,k}
and that
U_{k}=\lim_{M\to 0}U_{M,k}
.

Within this ansatz, the inverse propagator in momentum space and in constant field
\bm{\phi}
reads

\Gamma_{ij,M,k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{q};\bm{\phi})=\delta_{ij}(\mathbf{q}^{2}+U_{M,k}^{\prime}(\rho))+\phi_{i}\phi_{j}U_{M,k}^{\prime\prime}(\rho),

(50)

allowing to write explicitly the flow equation for
\Gamma_{M,k}
.
The propagator reads

(\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{q};\bm{\phi})+R_{k}(\mathbf{q}))^{-1}_{ij}=\frac{\phi_{i}\phi_{j}}{2\rho}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+U_{M,k}^{\prime}(\rho)+2\rho U_{M,k}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)}+\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{\phi_{i}\phi_{j}}{2\rho}\right)\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+U_{M,k}^{\prime}(\rho)}.

(51)

In this approximation, the only flowing quantity is
U_{M,k}
, and its flow equation reads

\begin{split}\partial_{k}U_{M,k}=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\left(\frac{N-1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+M^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}+U^{\prime}_{M,k}}+\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+M^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}+U^{\prime}_{M,k}+2\rho U^{\prime\prime}_{M,k}}\right),\end{split}

(52)

where the trace over the
O(N)
indices has been performed.

In the limit
M=0
, we recover the flow equation of the effective potential
U_{k}(\rho)
(at finite size with periodic boundary conditions
[
55
]
)

\begin{split}\partial_{k}U_{k}(\rho)=&\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\Bigg(\frac{N-1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+U^{\prime}_{k}}+\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+U^{\prime}_{k}+2\rho U^{\prime\prime}_{k}}\Bigg),\end{split}

(53)

while in the limit
M\to\infty
the zero-momentum contribution is suppressed, and the equation for
I_{k}
reads (recall that
\rho
is half of the average magnetization squared, while the argument of the rate function is
\check{\rho}=\mathbf{s}^{2}/2
)

\begin{split}\partial_{k}I_{k}(\check{\rho})=&\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\Bigg(\frac{N-1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+I^{\prime}_{k}}+\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+I^{\prime}_{k}+2\check{\rho}I^{\prime\prime}_{k}}\Bigg).\end{split}

(54)

In the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
, the two sums converge to the same integral, and we recover the equivalence between the two,
I_{k}(\check{\rho})=U_{k}(\rho=\check{\rho})
.

To study the critical behavior, it is convenient to introduce the dimensionless momenta
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{q}/k
and fields

\tilde{\rho}=k^{-(d-2+\eta)}\rho
and
\breve{\rho}=k^{-(d-2+\eta)}\check{\rho}
, and
\tilde{U}_{k}(\tilde{\rho})=k^{-d}U_{k}(\tilde{\rho}k^{d-2+\eta})
and
\tilde{I}_{k}(\breve{\rho})=k^{-d}I_{k}(\breve{\rho}k^{d-2+\eta})
. Here
\eta
is the anomalous dimension, equal to zero at LPA.

Writing the regulator as
R_{k}(\mathbf{q})=k^{2}\tilde{R}((\mathbf{q}/k)^{2})
, the flows become

\begin{split}k\partial_{k}\tilde{U}_{k}=&-d\tilde{U}_{k}+(d-2)\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}_{k}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2(kL)^{d}}\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}(2\tilde{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime})\Bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{U}^{\prime}_{k}+2\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}_{k}^{\prime\prime}}+\frac{N-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})+\tilde{U}^{\prime}_{k}}\Bigg),\end{split}

(55)

and

\begin{split}k\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{k}=&-d\tilde{I}_{k}+(d-2)\breve{\rho}\tilde{I}_{k}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2(kL)^{d}}\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\neq 0}(2\tilde{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime})\Bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{I}^{\prime}_{k}+2\breve{\rho}\tilde{I}_{k}^{\prime\prime}}+\frac{N-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})+\tilde{I}^{\prime}_{k}}\Bigg),\end{split}

(56)

In the thermodynamic limit, both flow equations become the standard dimensionless LPA equation

k\partial_{k}\tilde{U}_{k}=-d\tilde{U}_{k}+(d-2)\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}_{k}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}(2\tilde{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime})\Bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{U}^{\prime}_{k}+2\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}^{\prime\prime}_{k}}+\frac{N-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{U}^{\prime}_{k}}\Bigg),

(57)

and

k\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{k}=-d\tilde{I}_{k}+(d-2)\tilde{\breve{\rho}}I_{k}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}(2\tilde{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime})\Bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{I}^{\prime}_{k}+2\breve{\rho}\tilde{I}^{\prime\prime}_{k}}+\frac{N-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{I}^{\prime}_{k}}\Bigg),

(58)

and at criticality, the dimensionless effective potential reaches a fixed point as
k\to 0
, that is,
\lim_{k\to 0}\lim_{L\to\infty}\tilde{U}_{k}=\lim_{k\to 0}\lim_{L\to\infty}\tilde{I}_{k}=\tilde{U}^{*}
, with
\tilde{U}^{*}
the solution of

0=-d\tilde{U}^{*}+(d-2)\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}^{*^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}(2\tilde{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime})\Bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{U}^{*^{\prime}}+2\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}^{*^{\prime\prime}}}+\frac{N-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}+\tilde{U}^{*^{\prime}}}\Bigg).

(59)

It is important for the following to note that
\tilde{U}^{*}
, considered as a function of the modulus of the field
\tilde{\phi}=\sqrt{2\tilde{\rho}}
, is a non-convex function at small field (for
d<4
) whereas at
k=0
, the dimensionful effective potential
U_{k=0}
is convex for all values of the field, as it should for a function obtained from a (true) Legendre transform.

Beyond LPA,
\eta
takes a finite value at the fixed point, which, in particular, has the effect of turning
(d-2)
factors in the second terms of all equations between Eqs. (
55
) and (
59
) into
(d-2+\eta)
. For
2<d<4
, one expects the potential to increase at large fields. Thus, the integrand in Eq. (
59
) becomes negligible in this regime.
This leads to a power law behavior of
\tilde{U}^{*}

\tilde{U}^{*}\propto\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{d}{d-2+\eta}}.

(60)

The same power law behavior in the universal large field regime will be inherited by the rate function, giving

\tilde{I}\propto\breve{\rho}^{\frac{d}{d-2+\eta}},

(61)

with
\eta=0
at LPA.

At finite size
L
, the flow equations (
55
) and (
56
) are indistinguishable from Eqs. (
57
) and (
58
) as long as
kL\gg 1
. Therefore, at criticality (
T=T_{c}
), and for
a^{-1}\gg k\gg L^{-1}
, both
\tilde{U}_{k}
and
\tilde{I}_{k}
flow towards the same fixed point solution
\tilde{U}^{*}
, i.e.
U_{k}(\rho)\simeq k^{d}\tilde{U}^{*}(k^{-(d-2)}\rho)
and
I_{k}(\check{\rho})\simeq U_{k}(\rho=\check{\rho})\simeq k^{d}\tilde{U}^{*}(k^{-(d-2)}\check{\rho})
, which corresponds to the correct scaling with exponent
d-2+\eta
(with
\eta=0
at LPA).

However, for
kL\lesssim 1
the flows of
U_{k}
and
I_{k}
differ significantly. Indeed, the flow of the effective potential can be rewritten as

\partial_{k}U_{k}\simeq\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\partial_{k}R_{k}(0)\Bigg(\frac{1}{R_{k}(0)+U^{\prime}_{k}+2\rho U^{\prime\prime}_{k}}+\frac{N-1}{R_{k}(0)+U^{\prime}_{k}}\Bigg),

(62)

since the contribution of the finite momenta is negligible thanks to
\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})
in this regime.
This corresponds to the flow of a 0-dimensional field theory. Because
R_{k}(0)\to 0
as
k\to 0
, both
U^{\prime}_{k}(\rho)
and
U^{\prime}_{k}(\rho)+2\rho U^{\prime\prime}_{k}(\rho)
must become strictly positive as
k\to 0
: This induces a return to convexity, with the minimum of the effective potential at
\rho=0
, which is expected for a (true) Legendre transform.
On the other hand, the flow of the rate function
I_{k}
stops very quickly for
k\lesssim 1/L
since the zero-momentum mode is absent from the sum and the other modes do not contribute.
Finally, at large field, the flows of
U_{k}
and
I_{k}
is barely modified by the finite size effects,

4

4

[

]
for a discussion of the log-correction that appears in the rate function at large field.
and we recover the powerlaw behavior of the effective potential and rate function,
U_{k=0},I_{k=0}\propto\rho^{\frac{d}{d-2+\eta}}
(with
\eta=0
at LPA).

III.3
Numerical integration of the LPA flow equation

We are interested in computing the rate function
I_{\zeta}
in the critical regime, which takes a universal form.
It is therefore not necessary to take into account the early stage of the flow (say for a given lattice geometry at scale
k=\Lambda
with a specific initial condition) since all these microscopic details will be washed out when flowing towards the fixed point. When the flow is initialized on the critical surface, the fixed point is almost reached at an intermediary (non-universal) scale
k_{*}
while it is only when
k
is of the order of
L^{-1}
that finite size effects enter the flow.
For this reason, to get rid of the early stage RG flow, we first integrate the flow from an initial condition on the critical surface, down to a sufficiently small scale
k_{*}
such that the running dimensionless potential
\tilde{U}_{k_{*}}
and rate function
\tilde{I}_{k_{*}}
are (almost) identical to the fixed point potential
\tilde{U}^{*}
. In a second step, we take as new initial condition for the RG flow what was obtained at
k_{*}
, that is
\tilde{U}^{*}
. In practice, we numerically solve the fixed point equation Eq. (
59
), and use this solution as the starting point of the flow. All quantities are then measured in units of this new “microscopic” length scale
k_{*}
, which is equivalent to setting
k_{*}=1
. This has the advantage that all irrelevant perturbations to the fixed point have been set to zero, ensuring there will not be corrections to scaling.

Thus, we initialize the flows of the effective potential and rate function by
U_{k_{*}}(\rho)=k_{*}^{d}\tilde{U}^{*}(k_{*}^{-d+2}\rho)
and
I_{k_{*}}(\check{\rho})=k_{*}^{d}\tilde{U}^{*}(k_{*}^{-d+2}\check{\rho})
. Such an initial condition corresponds to
\zeta=0
(since the system is critical for
L\to\infty
, as it never leaves the fixed point). To generate a finite
\zeta
, we slightly perturb the fixed point, e.g. by adding a small quadratic term to the fixed point potential
\tilde{U}^{*}
. As this perturbation is relevant, this induces a flow into the ordered or disordered phase (in the thermodynamic limit) and allows us to vary
\zeta
, see below.

In practice, the field dependence of functions
U
and
I
is discretized on a grid such that the position of the minimum of
\tilde{U}^{*}
is between
1/15
and
1/5
of the grid. We use between
200
and
400
points on the grid and discretize derivatives using central derivatives with seven points. The fixed point solution, used as an initial condition is obtained by solving Eq. (
59
) using a variant of the Newton-Raphson method.
For the numerical integration of the flow equations, Eqs. (
52
) and (
54
), we use the Euler method with RG time steps of
10^{-5}-10^{-4}
(integration with Runge-Kutta of order 4 gives almost identical results).
We run the flow in terms of dimensionless quantities, i.e. integrate Eq. (
55
) and Eq. (
56
), until
kL\simeq 2-10
. Then we switch to dimensionful quantities, i.e. integrate Eq. (
54
), and run the flow until termination. The sum over momenta is performed by summing over all momenta
\mathbf{q}
such that
|\mathbf{q}|<6k
, beyond which the summands are negligible as they are cut by
\partial_{k}R_{k}
, excluding (including)
\mathbf{q}=0
for the flow of
I_{k}
(
U_{k}
).
For
kL\gtrsim 40
, we replace the sums over momenta by integrals (i.e. we use Eqs. (
57
) and (
58
)) and we have checked that the transition from integrals to sums is smooth when
kL\simeq 40
. By varying all parameters of our numerical integration of the flows, we have checked that our results are numerically converged up to high precision.

In the following, we have used the following families of regulator functions

\begin{split}R_{k,\exp}(\mathbf{q})&=\alpha k^{2}e^{-\mathbf{q}^{2}/k^{2}}\\
R_{k,\theta}(\mathbf{q})&=\alpha(k^{2}-\mathbf{q}^{2})\theta(k^{2}-\mathbf{q}^{2})\\
R_{k,W}(\mathbf{q})&=\alpha\frac{\mathbf{q}^{2}}{(\exp(\mathbf{q}^{2}/k^{2})-1)},\end{split}

(63)

that we refer to as “exponential”, “
\theta
” and “Wetterich” regulators respectively. In principle, physical quantities, and in particular universal scaling functions, should not depend on the regulator chosen. However, approximations such as the LPA induce a spurious dependence on the regulator.
The derivative expansion, the LPA being the lowest order, has been argued to be a convergent series when the regulator is appropriately chosen, as observed for the critical exponents
[
56
]
. Note that the
\theta
regulator does not properly regularize the derivative expansion flow equations beyond order two, but works at LPA.
For a given regulator family, this convergence occurs when the optimal prefactor
\alpha
is found at each order of the expansion so that the critical exponents reach an extremum. Here we chose to optimize the critical exponent
\nu
, see also
[
57
]
for a discussion of the convergence of the optimized
\alpha
with the order of the derivative expansion.

Studying the variations of various quantities (e.g. critical exponents) as a function of the regulator shape at a given approximation level allows for evaluating the stability of the method at that order. However, as detailed in
[
58
]
, the error bar estimated from these variations is typically too small and not a good estimate of the true error bar. To estimate the global error accurately, one would need to compute results at least two consecutive orders of the derivative expansion, which we are currently undertaking
[
59
]
.

III.4
Rate function at large
N
from FRG

Let us consider the large
N
limit, where the LPA flow of the potential becomes exact, as a benchmark for the numerical resolution of the flow equation. In this limit, starting from Eq. (
58
), we can derive the gap equation (
29
) straightforwardly. Following the reasoning from
[
60
]
, we first take the limit
N\to\infty
in Eq. (
54
) to obtain the corresponding large
N
flow equation.

\partial_{k}I_{k}(\check{\rho})=\frac{N}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\frac{\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+I^{\prime}_{k}(\check{\rho})}.

(68)

Now, we define
\check{f}_{k}(\check{W})=\check{\rho}
as the inverse function of
\check{W}_{k}(\check{\rho})\equiv I_{k}^{\prime}(\check{\rho})
. Note that since
\check{\Gamma}_{k=\Lambda}[\bm{\phi}]=\mathcal{H}[\bm{\phi}]
, we have that
I_{k=\Lambda}(\check{\rho})=V(\check{\rho})
and thus
\check{f}_{k=\Lambda}(\check{W})=f_{\Lambda}(\check{W})
, with
f_{\Lambda}
defined in Sec.
II.1
.

One readily finds the flow equation of
\check{f}_{k}
,

\partial_{k}\check{f}_{k}(\check{W})=\frac{N}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\frac{\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})}{(\mathbf{q}^{2}+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+\check{W})^{2}},

(69)

the right-hand side of which is a total derivative with respect to
k
.
Integrating this equation in
k
from
0
to
\Lambda
and using that
\lim_{k\to 0}R_{k}=0
and
\lim_{k\to\Lambda}R_{k}=\infty
, we recover the gap equation (
29
) for the rate function,

\check{\rho}=f_{\Lambda}(\check{W})-\frac{N}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+\check{W}},

(70)

from which the derivation detailed in Sec.
II.2
follows. Clearly, any regulator dependence disappears from the end result.

The same is not true however for the fixed point potential. One can show that the fixed point solution depends on the regulator at
N\to\infty

[
61
]
, where the LPA is exact. The corresponding fixed-point potential equation reads

-2\tilde{U}_{*}^{\prime}+(d-2)\tilde{\rho}\tilde{U}_{*}^{\prime\prime}-\tilde{U}_{*}^{\prime\prime}NL_{1}(\tilde{U}_{*}^{\prime})=0,

(71)

where the threshold function
L_{1}(w)
depends on the regulator
R_{k}(\mathbf{q})=k^{2}\tilde{R}(\mathbf{q}^{2}/k^{2})
,

L_{1}(w)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}\frac{2\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})}{\left(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})+w\right)^{2}}.

(72)

Introducing
\tilde{W}(\tilde{\rho})=\tilde{U}_{*}^{\prime}(\tilde{\rho})
, it has been shown in
[
61
]
that the solution to Eq. (
71
) reads for an arbitrary regulator

\tilde{\rho}=\tilde{F}_{d}\left(\tilde{W}(\tilde{\rho});\tilde{R}\right)\equiv\frac{N}{2(d-2)}\int_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}\frac{2\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})-\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})}{\left(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})\right)^{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(2,1-\frac{d}{2};2-\frac{d}{2}\bigg|-\frac{\tilde{W}(\tilde{\rho})}{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2})}\right),

(73)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c|-z)
is a hypergeometric function.

Note that
\tilde{F}_{d}(w;\tilde{R})
has a shape qualitatively similar to that of
\check{F}_{d}(w)
, see Eq. (
31
).
Indeed, the singularity of the hypergeometric function at
z=-1
,
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(2,1-\frac{d}{2};2-\frac{d}{2}\bigg|-z\right)\simeq\frac{2-d}{2(z+1)}
corresponds to the singularity at
w=w_{-\infty}\equiv-\min_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}+\tilde{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{2}))
in Eq. (
72
), similar to the pole at
z=-\pi
of
\check{F}_{d}(z)
, reached formally for
\tilde{\rho}\to-\infty
.
On the other hand, in the limit
\tilde{\rho}\to\infty
, i.e.
W(\tilde{\rho})\to\infty
, Eq. (
71
) gives
W(\tilde{\rho})\sim\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{2}{d-2}}
, which is recovered from Eq. (
73
) using that
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(2,1-\frac{d}{2};2-\frac{d}{2}\bigg|-z\right)\sim z^{\frac{d-2}{2}}
for
z\to\infty
, as does
\check{F}_{d}(z)
. Finally,
\tilde{F}_{d}(w;\tilde{R})=0
for some
w_{0}<0
. This explains why the fixed point potential
\tilde{U}^{*}
and the rate function have similar shapes, although the former is regulator dependent while the latter is universal (but depends on the boundary conditions).

While the shape of
\tilde{U}^{*}
depends on the regulator, the critical exponents, relevant or irrelevant, are independent of it
[
61
]
. By analyzing the stability matrix of the LPA flow equation, we find numerically in
d=3
that
\nu=1/(d-2)
up to numerical uncertainty at the level of
5
-th digit, independently of the regulator shape or prefactor
\alpha
.

V
Conclusion

We have conducted a detailed study of the rate function in the
O(N)
models. In the
N\to\infty
limit, we derived an exact solution, similar to the well-known derivation of the effective potential in this limit
[
48
]
. This result provides valuable insights into the general
O(N)
case, as it qualitatively captures the key behaviors across the full range of
N
.

We then applied the nonperturbative approach to calculating the rate function, first introduced in
[
49
]
, to the
O(N)
models. This approach leverages the full power of the FRG, allowing us to handle cases where exact solutions are not available. We focused on testing the reliability of results obtained using the Local Potential Approximation (LPA), the lowest order of the derivative expansion, which is the most widely used approximation in FRG studies.

By benchmarking against the
N\to\infty
case, where the LPA becomes exact, we confirmed the robustness of our numerical implementation of the method. For finite
N
, the dependence on the infrared regulator introduces no more than a
0.5\%
variation. However, estimating the universal scales characterizing the rate functions proved more challenging, with discrepancies of up to
25\%
. Despite this, the nontrivial universal features of the rate functions are qualitatively well captured, even at the LPA level.

Looking forward, future work will focus on improving the precision of universal scale estimates by extending the approximation level beyond LPA. Additionally, we aim to generalize the method to other models, in particular disordered systems and out-of-equilibrium statistical physics.
