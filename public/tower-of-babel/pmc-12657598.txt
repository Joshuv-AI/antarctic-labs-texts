Robotic-Assisted Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty is Associated With Decreased 90-Day Opioid Prescribing Patterns Compared to Manual Instrumentation
Authors: Nathaniel T Ondeck, Colin C Neitzke, Yu-Fen Chiu, Sonia K Chandi, Pravjit Bhatti, Alejandro Gonzalez Della Valle, Geoffrey H Westrich, Brian P Chalmers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic assistance minimizes bony resection and surrounding soft tissue damage during total knee arthroplasty (TKA), potentially decreasing postoperative pain. The objective of this study was to evaluate in-hospital opioid consumption, 90-day opioid prescribing patterns, length of stay (LOS), and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following robotic-assisted vs manual primary TKA.MethodsUtilizing an institutional database, all patients undergoing primary unilateral TKA between 2019 and 2022 with 90-day minimum follow-up were retrospectively queried. Patients were excluded if they had another surgery within 90 days of the index TKA, were discharged to a rehabilitation center, or were prescribed opioid or benzodiazepine medications preoperatively. One-to-one propensity score matching identified 1476 patients undergoing robotic-assisted (n = 738) or manual (n = 738) TKA. Multivariable regression analysis assessed in-hospital morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs) consumption, LOS, 90-day opioid prescribing patterns (not necessarily consumption), and PROMs (preoperative, 6 weeks, and 3 months).ResultsThe robotic-assisted cohort consumed on average 12 fewer in-hospital MMEs (P = .026) and had an average LOS that was 8 hours shorter than the manual cohort (P < .001). There was no difference in MMEs prescribed at discharge (P = .12), but the robotic-assisted cohort had on average 113 fewer 90-day postdischarge MMEs prescribed (P = .001) and 145 fewer total 90-day MMEs (consumed in-hospital plus prescribed) (P < .001). There was no difference in PROMs at 6 weeks or 3 months.ConclusionsRobotic-assisted TKA may confer shorter LOS with decreased 90-day opioid use patterns. This information is important given the increased scrutiny on opioid usage and recent focus on rapid recovery and ambulatory TKA pathways.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Pain, Opioids, Length of stay, Robotic-assisted TKA

Introduction

Postoperative pain can contribute to increased length of stay (LOS), worse clinical outcomes, and decreased patient satisfaction after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [[1], [2], [3]]. As such, appropriate pain management following TKA remains an important goal for surgeons and patients. There is literature to support robotic-assisted TKA having several advantages compared to manual TKA including more accurate bone cuts, more precise implant placement, improved gap balancing, and increased patient satisfaction [4,5]. Theoretically, this minimizes bone resection and soft tissue damage, which in turn, may contribute to decreased postoperative pain [6]. However, there is limited literature evaluating the impact of robotic assistance on postoperative pain following TKA.

Bhimani et al. demonstrated lower visual analog scale (VAS) scores for pain with activity and at rest, as well as decreased opioid consumption with robotic-assisted TKA vs manual TKA [7]. Greiner et al. demonstrated decreased in-hospital opioid consumption and fewer opioids prescribed at discharge following robotic-assisted TKA vs manual TKA [8]. However, both studies were largely small, single surgeon investigations, potentially limiting data generalizability.

Thus, the purpose of this study was to compare in-hospital Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores, in-hospital opioid consumption, and discharge and postdischarge opioid prescriptions in patients undergoing robotic-assisted vs manual TKA. Additionally, we sought to compare LOS, and short-term patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) after robotic-assisted vs manual TKA. We hypothesized that patients undergoing robotic-assisted TKA would experience a shorter LOS, lower in-hospital NRS pain scores, and consume fewer postoperative opioids within the in-hospital setting. Additionally, we hypothesized that robotic-assisted cohort would receive fewer prescribed morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs) within the discharge and postdischarge (90-day) settings and would have superior improvement in 6-week and 3-month PROMs from the preoperative baseline.

Material and methods

Following institutional review board approval, we queried our institutional database to retrospectively identify all patients who underwent primary, unilateral TKA via robotic assistance with the Mako Total Knee SmartRobotics (Stryker, Kalamazoo, Michigan) system or with manual instrumentation at the Hospital for Special Surgery (New York, New York) from January 1, 2019, through January 1, 2023, with a minimum of 90 days of postoperative follow-up in the electronic medical record. Patients were excluded if they had another unrelated orthopaedic procedure within 90 days preceding or following their index TKA, were discharged to a rehabilitation center as postdischarge rehabilitation opioid prescriptions are not reliably captured in our electronic medical record, or were prescribed opioid or benzodiazepine medications preoperatively. Finally, all patients who underwent primary TKA with computer navigation were excluded.

Patient demographic information was collected including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), race, ethnicity, smoking status, marital status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), insurance type, preoperative pain scores, and history of anxiety or depression. Intraoperative variables included the use of regional anesthesia as well as an identifier that reflected whether the TKA procedure was completed with robotic assistance or manual instrumentation.

Outcome variables included LOS (hours), in-hospital NRS pain scores, in-hospital opioid consumption in MMEs, MMEs prescribed (not necessarily consumed) at discharge, MMEs prescribed (not necessarily consumed) postdischarge, and total 90-day in-hospital MME consumption + MMEs prescribed. Additional outcome variables included the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (KOOS JR), Veteran’s Rand-12 Mental and Physical Component Scores, Lower Extremity Activity Scale, and VAS, which were obtained preoperatively as well as at 6 weeks and 3 months postoperatively. These postoperative timepoints are standard institutional PROM collection timepoints for all joint arthroplasty patients at our institution. A total of 33 fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons contributed cases to the final patient cohorts. This included 6 surgeons who contributed both robotic-assisted and manual cases, 12 surgeons who contributed only robotic-assisted cases, and 15 surgeons who contributed both robotic-assisted and manual cases. The decision to utilize robotic assistance vs manual instrumentation was at the discretion of the operating surgeon.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used and reported as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables or frequency and percentages for categorical variables. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test and quantile–quantile plots. Comparisons between cohorts were based on independent sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Pearson Chi-square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. One-to-one propensity score matching was performed using age, sex, BMI, ASA score, CCI, smoking status, insurance type, preoperative pain scores, history of anxiety or depression, use of regional anesthesia, and year of surgery. While the cohorts were propensity score matched, there was potential for residual confounding variables. As a result, parsimonious multivariable linear regression models were built to adjust for residual confounding variables using model fit criteria. All collected covariates were eligible and only the robotic-assisted vs manual variable was forced into each model. A power analysis was performed, and it was determined that we would have more than 80% power to detect minimally clinically meaningful differences for all proposed hypotheses. All analysis was performed using R Studio: A Language and Environment of Statistical Computing (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at α < 0.05.

Patients

In total, 1476 patients undergoing robotic-assisted (n = 738) or manual (n = 738) primary TKA were included in the study (Fig. 1). The mean age was 64 years, the mean BMI was 31 kg/m2, and 49% of the cohort were women (Table 1). There was no difference between cohorts regarding ASA risk level (P = .21), CCI (P = .81), smoking status (P = .28), or history of anxiety and/or depression (P = .94). The robotic-assisted cohort had slightly higher utilization of regional anesthesia (99.7 vs 98.5%; P = .012). There was no difference in the mean preoperative NRS pain score between the robotic-assisted and manual cohorts (2.8 vs 2.6; P = .182).

Results

In-hospital opioid consumption, pain, and LOS

The manual cohort consumed on average 12 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1-22) more in-hospital MMEs (P = .026) than the robotic-assisted cohort (Fig. 2). Average in-hospital patient pain, as reported via the NRS, was clinically similar between the manual and robotic-assisted cohorts (0.07; 95% CI: −0.09 to 0.23; P = .41) (Fig. 3). However, the manual cohort was associated with a greater maximum NRS in-hospital score (0.22; 95% CI: 0.01-0.43; P = .04).

Graphical representation of the linear regression comparing the association between manual vs robotic-assisted TKA procedures and in-hospital opioid consumption and discharge MMEs. The manual TKA cohort was associated with a clinically modest increase in in-hospital opioid consumption (11.86 MMEs; P = .026).

Graphical representation of the linear regression comparing the association between manual vs robotic-assisted TKA procedures and inhospital NRS for pain scores. The manual TKA cohort was associated with a statistically higher but clinically insignificant maximum inhospital NRS pain score (0.22; P = .04).

The manual cohort was associated with an 8.0 hour (95% CI: 4.5-11.5) longer LOS compared to the robotic-assisted cohort (P < .001). When controlling for LOS, the manual cohort trended toward consuming an average of 2.17 (95% CI: −0.27 to 4.62) more MME/hour compared to the robotic-assisted cohort, but this was not statistically significant (P = .081).

Discharge and postdischarge opioid prescriptions

There was no difference in MMEs prescribed at the time of discharge (P = .12). In terms of postdischarge MMEs prescribed (Fig. 4), the manual cohort had on average 113 (95% CI: 46-179) more 90-day postdischarge MMEs prescribed (P = .001).

Graphical representation of the linear regression comparing the association between manual vs robotic-assisted TKA procedures and postoperative opioid prescribing patterns. The manual TKA cohort was associated with clinically significant increases in postdischarge MMEs (112.58; P = .001) and total 90-day MMEs (145.40; P < .001).

Total 90-day opioid consumption plus prescription patterns

The manual cohort was associated with 145 (95% CI: 65-226) more total 90-day MMEs (consumed in-hospital and prescribed at discharge plus postdischarge) compared to the robotic-assisted cohort (P < .001).

PROMs

In terms of PROMs, there were no significantly different changes in the KOOS JR, Veteran’s Rand-12 Mental or Physical Component Scores, Lower Extremity Activity Scale, or VAS at 6 weeks or 3 months postoperatively in comparison to preoperatively between the 2 cohorts (Table 2). At 6 weeks, the robotic-assisted cohort saw a mean KOOS JR improvement of 12.1 points vs 13.0 in the manual TKA cohort (P = .76). At 3 months, the mean KOOS JR improvement was 18.2 points in the robotic-assisted cohort vs 19.5 in the manual cohort (P = .52).

Discussion

Appropriate pain control after TKA has an impact on many aspects of the episode of care. From a patient perspective, well-controlled pain is associated with improved patient satisfaction and better functional outcomes [1,2]. From a systems perspective, limiting patient discomfort has been correlated with shorter LOS, which is an important factor in rapid recovery protocols [3].

Theoretically, robotic-assisted TKA leads to smaller bony resections and less soft tissue damage in comparison to manual TKA, diminishing the pain associated with the procedure. Although there is some evidence to suggest this may be the case, the existing literature is limited by small sample sizes, largely single surgeon cohorts, as well as a lack of functional outcomes [7,8]. In this setting, the present study sought to evaluate inhospital opioid consumption and pain, LOS, 90-day opioid prescribing patterns (combination of inhospital opioid consumption and discharge + postdischarge opioid prescriptions), and PROMs following robotic-assisted vs manual primary TKA.

The present study found the robotic-assisted TKA cohort to be associated with significantly decreased inhospital opioid consumption in comparison to the manual TKA cohort. More specifically, the robotic-assisted cohort was associated with 12 fewer inhospital MMEs consumed (P = .026), which is of modest clinical significance. When controlling for differences in LOS between the cohorts, the robotic-assisted cohort was associated with consuming an average of 2.17 less MME/hour, but this was not statistically significant (P = .081) which may be secondary to power limitations. Despite these differences in opioid usage, there were clinically similar first postoperative, highest, lowest, and average inhospital NRS pain scores between the 2 cohorts, indicating that there was a similar level of overall pain control. This would support the hypothesis that haptically bound saw blades during robotic-assisted TKA allow for more precise bony resections [9], and a decreased need for soft tissue retraction/releases [[10], [11], [12]], which may be associated with less pain.

The robotic-assisted cohort was associated with an 8-hour shorter LOS compared to the manual cohort (P < .001). While at first glance this difference may seem minimal, these 8 hours are large enough to support patients being discharged the same day as their surgery as opposed to staying overnight in the hospital or ambulatory surgery center. Same day discharge is not only preferred by many patients [13], but also has significant financial implications [14].

At the time of discharge, there was no significant difference in opioid prescriptions between the 2 cohorts. This is thought to be due to discharge opioids being prescribed via standard electronic medical record pathways that adhere to institutional opioid prescribing guidelines. This generally includes prescribing 1 (2 when necessary) short-acting opioid prescription (oxycodone-acetaminophen 5/325 mg, hydrocodone-acetaminophen 5/325 mg, or hydromorphone 2 mg), issued as a 1-week supply, with direction to take 1 to 2 tablets every 4 to 6 hours as needed. Caution is advised when prescribing ≥50 MMEs/day and ≥90 MMEs/day are avoided.

With regards to postdischarge pain, the robotic-assisted cohort was associated with significantly decreased postdischarge opioid prescriptions compared to the manual cohort. The robotic-assisted cohort was associated with 113 fewer 90-day postdischarge MMEs prescribed (P = .001) which is a clinically significant difference that further supports the hypothesis that postoperative pain is decreased with the use of robotic technology. Importantly, in the discharge and postdischarge settings, we evaluated MMEs prescribed not consumed.

With regards to PROMs, when comparing the 2 cohorts, there were no significant differences between the preoperative to 6-week or 3-month postoperative timepoints. This finding suggests that although opioid use was different between the 2 cohorts, it did not ultimately affect the patient’s longer-term ability to rehabilitate and progress functionally.

Strengths of this study include the use of a relatively large sample size and a rigorous statistical approach utilizing propensity score matching. Confirmation of the impact of robotic-assisted TKA on opioid usage and LOS is more important than ever given the increasing scrutiny placed on opioid prescribing patterns, the growing role of patient satisfaction on future referrals and physician reimbursement, as well as the expanding pressure from insurers to perform economically favorable outpatient arthroplasty procedures.

This study is not without potential limitations. Importantly, there is an inability to control the differing LOS expectations that patients may have secondary to the participating surgeon and their care team. These expectations may come from the surgeon’s experience and operative volume as there is literature to suggest that higher volume surgeons are associated with shorter patient LOS following TKA [15]. Additionally, while our institution has standardized discharge opioid guidelines, there may differences among care teams in terms of postdischarge prescribing patterns and threshold for opioid refills.

It is also challenging to control for each surgeon’s intraoperative techniques including but not limited to the incision size, magnitude of the bone cuts, soft tissue releases performed, the amount of tibial subluxation, and the surgeon’s decision to use robotic assistance. The decision to use robotics, in and of itself, may be influenced by factors not captured in this study including patient motivation, surgeon selection biases, or insurance mandates despite no difference in the distribution of insurance types between cohorts (P = .88). Selection bias could have been secondary to patient demographics, perceived clinical benefits, case complexity and the need for deformity correction, or surgeon-specific workflow and efficiency needs. While we did employ a robust propensity-score matching methodology, these are potential confounding variables that are unaccounted for. Additionally, while all implants used within the robotic-assisted TKA cohort were of the same manufacture given the use of the Mako Total Knee SmartRobotics system, the manual TKA cohort was not restricted to the same implant manufacturer, and this could have influenced study outcomes. With regards to PROMs, the completion percentages at each timepoint varied from 15 to 85% among cohorts and may have introduced nonresponse bias but there is literature to suggest that nonresponders have similar satisfaction rates compared to patients who do initially respond to PROMs [16]. Despite new Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services mandates to achieve 50% PROM completion rates as a part of their inpatient quality reporting measure [17], even academic institutions who are actively engaged in the development of programs to increase PROM completion are struggling to meet the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services compliance thresholds. Finally, although we can comment on postdischarge prescriptions, this paper does not evaluate actual opioid consumption postdischarge only opioid prescriptions. Further study utilizing prospective, postdischarge data with evaluation of postdischarge MME consumption vs prescription patterns would be of value.

Conclusions

This study highlights that robotic-assisted TKA procedures are associated with decreased opioid consumption and prescriptions as well as shorter hospital LOS, with no short-term association with function as measured through PROMs. These findings suggest that, in addition to the known more accurate bone cuts and more precise implant placement associated with robotic-assisted TKA, there may be pain-related and resource utilization benefits. Overall, this information is valuable not only for surgeons as they decide upon the methods in which they will perform their TKA procedures but also for insurers as they consider reimbursements for the use of technology in arthroplasty.
