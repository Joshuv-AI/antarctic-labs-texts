Bridging Equity Gaps in Maternal and NCD Services: Policy Priorities for Bangladesh's Evolving Health Landscape
Authors: Abdullaha Al Mamun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOver the past decade, Bangladesh has made significant gains in maternal and child health, driven by investments in community‐based services, education, and targeted public health interventions. However, disparities in service access and a growing burden of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) continue to undermine the promise of universal and equitable care.ObjectiveThis perspective provides a policy‐oriented, interceptive synthesis of progress and persistent gaps in five critical domains—maternal care, child nutrition, family planning, immunization, and hypertension—by analyzing population‐level data from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHS) 2017–2018 and 2022. It aims to identify the systemic drivers of health inequity and offer evidence‐informed policy recommendations to bridge the divide between national averages and marginalized subgroups.ResultsBetween 2017 and 2022, national gains in maternal care and stunting reduction were notable. However, these aggregate successes mask persistent disparities; access to skilled birth attendance and antenatal care remains heavily stratified by education and wealth, with gaps exceeding 40% in some cohorts. While immunization coverage has reached a stabilization phase, hypertension prevalence is rising, with control rates alarmingly low among rural, uneducated, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. This dual burden of stalled progress in traditional health domains and an increasing NCD burden highlights a critical misalignment between existing service delivery and the needs of an evolving population.ConclusionBangladesh's health system must pivot from fragmented vertical programs toward an integrated, equity‐first model. Closing persistent gaps requires prioritizing NCD integration into primary healthcare, scaling digital registries to target “zero‐dose” children, and institutionalizing equity monitoring. Without a bold, equity‐driven transformation, these disparities will jeopardize the sustainability of past gains and compromise the country's ability to achieve universal health coverage.

Introduction

Bangladesh has made significant progress in maternal and child health, nutrition, and family planning over the past two decades, despite its status as a low‐ and middle‐income country (LMIC) [1]. Key health system interventions—including nationwide immunization campaigns, expansion of community‐based health services, and increased female education—have contributed to gains in reducing under‐5 mortality, improving maternal care utilization, and controlling fertility [2, 3, 4]. Nonetheless, emerging challenges such as urban‐rural disparities, regional imbalances, and rising non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) pose a significant threat to the sustainability of these achievements [5].

The Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHS), conducted at regular intervals, represent a primary source of population‐based health data. The BDHS 2017–2018 involved 672 clusters and 20,250 households, while the 2022 round covered the same number of clusters but expanded the sample to 30,330 households, enabling a robust national comparison across years. These surveys offer insight into progress and challenges across a range of public health indicators, from maternal and child health to chronic disease outcomes such as hypertension [6, 7]. However, significant disparities remain across geographic, socioeconomic, and educational gradients, which may challenge the country's trajectory toward achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030 [8, 9].

Among the observed gains, skilled birth attendance increased from 53.3% in 2017–2018 to 70.9% in 2022, and the percentage of women receiving at least four antenatal care (ANC) visits rose from 47.0% to 59.2%. Child stunting declined from 31.0% to 24.0% during the same period (see Table 1 and Graph 1, 1), reflecting improved nutrition and early‐life care [5, 10]. However, childhood immunization coverage stagnated—only modestly increasing from 76.1% to 78.2%—and regional gaps remain striking, with Sylhet and Chattogram trailing behind the national average [11].

The rising burden of NCDs, particularly hypertension, has compounded Bangladesh's health challenges. Hypertension prevalence increased from 26.2% in 2017–2018 to 28.0% in 2022, while awareness and control remain suboptimal, especially among men, rural populations, and the less educated [12, 13]. These trends echo broader patterns across LMICs, where gains in maternal and child health have not yet translated into effective NCD control [14].

This article serves as a policy‐oriented interpretive synthesis of the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) findings rather than a purely descriptive report. By moving beyond raw metrics, this perspective critically analyzes why specific disparities persist and how the health system must pivot to address a dual burden of disease. The five domains analyzed—maternal care, immunization, nutrition, family planning, and hypertension—were selected as they represent the “litmus test” of Bangladesh's health transition. While maternal and child health indicators track the progress of long‐standing Millennium Development Goal (MDG) investments, hypertension serves as the primary proxy for the burgeoning Non‐Communicable Disease (NCD) crisis. Hypertension was prioritized because, unlike many other NCDs, it was measured via objective biomarkers (physical blood pressure readings) in both the 2017–2018 and 2022 BDHS, providing a robust, longitudinal look at the “silent surge” of chronic conditions alongside traditional reproductive health needs.

Methods

This perspective utilizes a descriptive secondary analysis of cross‐sectional data from the two most recent rounds of the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS): 2017–2018 and 2022. Both surveys employed a nationally representative, two‐stage stratified cluster sampling design. In the first stage, Enumeration Areas (EAs) were selected based on the most recent census frames; in the second stage, a systematic sampling of households was conducted within each EA. The 2017–2018 survey included 20,250 households, while the 2022 survey was expanded to 30,330 households to ensure robust divisional estimates.

Data were extracted directly from the official final reports published by the National Institute of Population Research and Training (NIPORT) and ICF International. The indicators selected are maternal health (ANC, skilled delivery), child immunization, nutrition (stunting), family planning (CPR), and NCDs (hypertension), which were chosen as benchmark metrics (see Figure 1, 1) for the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). All comparisons across years and subgroups utilize the weighted point estimates provided in the official reports, which account for the complex survey design and non‐response rates. Rather than performing new primary statistical modeling, this article synthesizes these official findings through an interpretive lens to bridge the gap between raw data collection and high‐level policy action.

The following sections present an interpretive synthesis where results are presented alongside their policy implications to provide a cohesive narrative of the health transition.

Maternal Health: Gains Tempered by Deep Inequalities

Bangladesh's progress in maternal health reflects a strong documented legacy of community‐based interventions, health policy reforms, and increased awareness. The increase in the proportion of women receiving at least four antenatal care (ANC) visits from 46.9% in 2017–2018 to 59.2% in 2022 is a measurable indicator of health system responsiveness. Similarly, institutional deliveries improved markedly from 50.0% to 65.0%, and skilled birth attendance rose from 53.1% to 70.9% (see Figure 2), reflecting infrastructural investments and behavioral shifts (Table 1).

Maternal health improvements from 2017 to 2022.

However, this aggregate data reveals significant disparities. According to BDHS 2022, urban women had far better outcomes than their rural counterparts across all maternal indicators. The divide is more pronounced across educational lines, where the gap in receiving skilled delivery care exceeds 45% between women with higher education and those without any formal schooling. Economic inequalities mirror this gap, with the wealthiest women nearly 2.3 times more likely to receive four or more antenatal visits than the poorest (Table 2 and Graph 2, 4). These findings indicate that maternal health progress is not yet inclusive, requiring policy responses that move beyond aggregate statistics to target the socioeconomically disadvantaged.

It underscores an urgent need to move beyond aggregate statistics and prioritize targeted outreach to underserved groups. Without deliberate equity‐oriented programming, the risk is that current successes become intermediate benchmarks rather than steps toward universal maternal wellbeing.

Child Immunization and Nutrition: Between Plateau and Promise

The marginal increase in full basic immunization marks a stabilization of coverage in an established public health priority (Table 1). The current full basic immunization coverage stands at 81.6% in 2025 [19]. Urban‐rural differentials are pronounced, with urban children significantly more likely to be fully immunized than those in rural areas (see Table 2). Similarly, maternal education remains a key factor; children of highly educated mothers see coverage rates nearly 26% higher than children of uneducated mothers (see Table 2). Sylhet and Chattogram reported the lowest regional coverage, lagging behind Khulna and Dhaka (Table 3).

Nutrition trends present a more improving trend. While stunting among children under 5 declined nationally, which reflects the success of nutrition‐focused interventions, it remains a socioeconomic‐dependent issue (see Table 1 and Graph 1, 1). Yet, disparities remain: rural children had a stunting prevalence of 27.9%, compared to 17.4% among their urban counterparts. This remains above levels seen in Southeast Asia's more nutrition‐advanced countries, such as Vietnam (14.3%) [20] and Thailand (13.4%) [21]. Children in the poorest wealth quintile and those born to uneducated mothers face more than double the prevalence of stunting compared to their more advantaged counterparts (Table 2). Regionally, Sylhet and Mymensingh continue to lag, signaling a need for geo‐targeted nutrition and vaccination interventions (see Table 3 and Graph 3, 5).

This disparity suggests systemic factors beyond resource constraints. Urban‐rural disparities persist, with urban children significantly more likely to be fully immunized. Maternal education strongly influences coverage, as children of uneducated mothers often miss complete vaccinations. The COVID‐19 pandemic further disrupted outreach, leading to missed and delayed immunizations. Bangladesh now risks falling behind regional peers like Bhutan (99.6%) [22] and Sri Lanka (97.5%) [23], which have succeeded through data‐driven and mobile‐enabled systems. While nutrition interventions demonstrate effectiveness, they have yet to reach the most underserved. A more targeted, multisectoral approach—linking food security, maternal care, WASH, and social protection—is essential. To sustain gains, Bangladesh must move from broad strategies to precision‐targeted nutrition policies.

Family Planning: Progress and Service Distribution

The modest rise in the contraceptive prevalence rate (CPR) from 62.0% to 64.0% reveals a widening gap in reproductive health equity (see Table 1). BDHS 2022 data reveal a significant “education‐action gap,” where contraceptive use among the wealthiest and most educated women remains substantially higher than among the poorest cohorts (see Table 2 and Graph 2, 4). This disparity is not merely a product of resource constraints but reflects an observed gap in service delivery to reach marginalized rural populations with rights‐based, high‐quality counseling. For the poorest women, barriers include not only physical distance to clinics but also the hidden opportunity costs of seeking care and a lack of consistent follow‐up, leading to higher rates of method discontinuation.

Paradoxically, urban and educated women, groups usually assumed to have the best access to health care, report a higher unmet need for family planning. This “Urban Paradox” suggests a critical mismatch between existing service delivery and evolving reproductive preferences. While the national program continues to prioritize short‐term methods like pills and injectables, data indicate that career‐oriented urban women increasingly demand Long‐Acting Reversible Contraceptives (LARCs) and permanent methods, which remain under‐promoted and under‐available in many primary care settings. Consequently, transitioning from a commodity‐focused model to a client‐centric approach is necessary to empower all segments of society. Without a deliberate shift toward method diversification and LARC integration, the health system will continue to fail this growing demographic.

Furthermore, male involvement remains an under‐addressed factor in Bangladesh's family planning narrative. The negligible rates of vasectomy and condom use reflect sociocultural factors and a programmatic focus that places the entire burden of contraception on women. Quality and reliability issues—such as frequent stock‐outs of preferred methods and overburdened field workers—further impact service utilization. Newly married, young, and migrant women remain underserved due to mobility and communication barriers. To sustain past gains, Bangladesh must transition from a commodity‐focused delivery model to a client‐centric, gender‐sensitive approach that empowers all segments of society, regardless of their socioeconomic or geographic standing. Lessons from Cambodia [24] and Indonesia [25]—where LARC integration and method diversification boosted uptake to meet modern reproductive health demands.

Hypertension and Emerging NCDs: Rising Prevalence

Hypertension prevalence is rising slowly but steadily, representing a growing NCD burden that the current system requires additional capacity to manage (see Table 1). Control rates are alarmingly low across all groups, but the inequity is most visible among the vulnerable; hypertension control among the poorest and least educated is nearly half the rate of the wealthiest (see Table 2 and Graph 2, 4). These trends highlight a significant treatment gap, where rural populations and men remain largely undiagnosed until complications arise. Bangladesh's acute‐care‐oriented system must transition to an integrated primary healthcare model to prevent these chronic conditions from undermining decades of public health gains.

Regionally, hypertension prevalence varies modestly: Dhaka recorded the highest, followed closely by Khulna and Barisal, while Sylhet and Mymensingh showed slightly lower figures (see Table 3 and Graph 3, 5). However, prevalence is likely underestimated due to low awareness and detection, especially in under‐resourced areas.

Hypertension control in Bangladesh remains alarmingly low, with fewer than 20% of diagnosed individuals achieving adequate management. Rural populations, men, and those without formal education face the greatest risks, often remaining undiagnosed until complications arise. The primary healthcare system—still largely focused on maternal and child health—is ill‐equipped to address chronic diseases systematically, unlike countries such as Thailand, where control is 60% [26] and Vietnam (51%) [27] that have adopted digital tracking and community‐based models, Bangladesh relies on fragmented, facility‐based care, lacking both infrastructure and trained personnel for effective NCD management.

While diabetes is also increasing (affecting ~10% of adults), hypertension remains the most critical entry point for NCD policy because it is a “gateway” condition for cardiovascular health. Its management requires the same community‐based follow‐up infrastructure that Bangladesh used for its family planning success, making it the most feasible NCD to integrate into the existing primary healthcare (PHC) framework as a proof‐of‐concept for chronic disease care. Beyond hypertension, diabetes is notably higher in urban areas associated with lifestyle changes, diets, inactivity, and rising obesity. Around 10% of adults now live with diabetes, much of it undiagnosed [28]. Early onset among working‐age adults is being more frequently documented, potentially impacting productivity and increasing care burdens. Other NCDs, including respiratory conditions and cancers, are emerging but remain poorly tracked. Low health literacy, stigma, and weak referral systems compound these challenges. Bangladesh's acute‐care‐oriented system must urgently shift to integrated, preventive NCD care through better financing, workforce training, and digital tools. Without such a shift, the increasing NCD burden may impact decades of public health progress.

Achievements and Gaps: Two Steps Forward, One Step Overlooked

Bangladesh's recent public health trajectory is marked by measurable gains and persistent disparities (see Figures 3 and 2, 4). Between 2017 and 2022, institutional deliveries rose by 15 percentage points (from 50.0% to 65.0%), and skilled birth attendance by nearly 18 percentage points (from 53.1% to 70.9%), indicating progress in maternal health service delivery (see Table 1 and Figure 3). Stunting declined significantly—from 31.0% to 24.0%—reflecting cumulative impacts of nutrition (see Figure 3), maternal education, and WASH improvements. Family planning services retained traction, with contraceptive use increasing from 62.0% to 64.0% and unmet need falling from 12.0% to 10.0% (see Figure 3).

Achievements in the healthcare system of Bangladesh since 2017–2022.

However, when disaggregated by subgroup, the data reveals notable gaps. Only 38.0% of the poorest women received ≥ 4 ANC visits compared to 87.0% of the wealthiest (see Table 2 and Graph 2, 4). Full immunization coverage stood at 61.5% among the poorest, well below the 89.4% among the wealthiest (see Figure 2, 4). Similarly, stunting among children of uneducated mothers was 39.1%, more than double the 16.3% observed in children of highly educated mothers (see Figure 2, 4). These gaps reflect systemic barriers—geographic, economic, and educational—that limit the reach and quality of health services.

Gaps and challenges in healthcare system of Bangladesh.

Regional variations further illustrate these disparities. While Khulna reported high immunization (87.3%) and low stunting (19.5%), Sylhet reported lower coverage across nearly all indicators: only 54.5% of women received ANC4+, 63.8% had skilled delivery, 70.4% of children were fully immunized, and stunting prevalence remained high at 29.8% (see Table 3 and Graph 3, 5). Mymensingh shows similarly poor maternal and child health outcomes despite being a relatively new administrative division with policy attention.

The NCD data remains a critical concern. Hypertension, though rising slowly (26.2%–28.0%), remains frequently undiagnosed and poorly controlled, especially among rural residents and those with no education, where control rates fall below 14% (Table 2). This dual burden—persisting maternal‐child health inequities and growing NCD threats—underscores the need for an integrated, equity‐first approach in Bangladesh's next health policy cycle.

Mechanisms of Inequity: Why Gaps Persist

While these trends are descriptive, the persistent disparities revealed by the BDHS data are driven by a triad of systemic barriers: 1.Demand‐Side Barriers: Evidence suggests that limited health literacy and entrenched gender norms continue to restrict women's agency in seeking timely care. Furthermore, high out‐of‐pocket (OOP) expenditure—which accounts for nearly 79.3% of total health spending in Bangladesh—creates a “poverty trap.” The poorest households often forgo essential services like antenatal care or hypertension medication due to the high opportunity costs of seeking treatment [29].2.Supply‐Side Constraints: The data suggests a significant “urban‐bias” in health facility readiness. While specialized care is concentrated in urban hubs, rural primary healthcare centers (such as Community Clinics and Union Health & Family Welfare Centers) frequently report shortages of trained mid‐level providers and essential NCD medications. This lack of “service readiness” at the grassroots level ensures that even when demand exists, the system cannot reliably meet it [30].3.Governance and Financing: Bangladesh's health budget remains one of the lowest in South Asia as a percentage of GDP [31, 32]. The current financing model remains heavily “vertical,” focusing on siloed maternal and child programs. This fragmentation limiting the “horizontal” integration required for life‐course NCD management, leaving the system ill‐equipped to handle the complex needs of an aging population while still struggling with basic primary care coverage [33].

Recommendations: Toward a Responsive and Equitable Health System

To bridge persistent gaps and consolidate health gains, Bangladesh must reimagine its health system as both equity‐driven and future‐ready. The BDHS findings underscore that progress is stalled not by a lack of data, but by the fragmentation of service delivery. To maximize policy traction within the upcoming 5th Health, Population, and Nutrition Sector Program (HPNSP), the following three high‐impact, near‐term strategic reforms are imperative. The conceptual alignment of these reforms—moving from fragmented vertical programs to an integrated, data‐driven system—is visualized in Figure 3, 5.

A strategic framework for an integrated, equity‐first health system in Bangladesh. The model illustrates the transition from fragmented vertical programs to a unified primary healthcare (PHC) landscape, addressing systemic demand‐ and supply‐side barriers to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and SDG 3 targets.

Integrate NCDs Within Primary Healthcare

Given the reported low control rates—only 13.5% among uneducated individuals and 11.8% among the poorest (Table 2)—Bangladesh must embed hypertension and diabetes services into its primary health infrastructure [34, 35, 36]. Transform Community Clinics (CCs) from maternal‐only centers into NCD hubs. This includes standardizing the “HEARTS” technical package for hypertension and ensuring a 30‐day supply of essential antihypertensives at the village level [37, 38, 39]. Leveraging digital tracking and decentralized follow‐up, as done in Thailand [40], could bridge the diagnosis‐treatment‐control gap.

Expand Digital Immunization Systems

With full immunization still below 80% nationally and particularly low in Sylhet (70.4%) and rural areas (72.2%) (Tables 2 and 3), implementing digital registries can identify individuals missing scheduled vaccinations and improve follow‐up (see Figure 3, 5). Scale up electronic immunization registries (EIR) to identify “zero‐dose” children in urban slums and hard‐to‐reach rural pockets, this can help in addressing the current stagnation in vaccination coverage [41]. Pakistan's “Zindagi Mehfooz” [42] offers a replicable model.

Prioritize Nutritional Targeting

Disaggregated stunting rates—27.9% in rural areas and 35.4% among the poorest (Table 2)—call for targeted interventions. Geo‐mapping tools and conditional cash transfers, modeled after Brazil's Bolsa Família [43], could address these high‐burden clusters.

Rethink Family Planning Delivery

Urban women (67.8%) and the highly educated (72.5%) report higher CPR, but unmet needs persist due to method mismatch (Table 2). Scaling up LARCs and digital counseling, as in Nepal [44], can realign services with evolving preferences.

Decentralized Health Planning

With striking divisional variation in indicators—from skilled delivery rates (78.2% in Dhaka vs. 63.8% in Sylhet) to immunization (87.3% in Khulna vs. 70.4% in Sylhet) (see Table 3 and Graph 3, 5)—block grants and locally tailored strategies are essential (see Figure 3, 5). India's decentralized model under NRHM offers operational administrative insights [45].

Institutionalize Equity Monitoring

The gaps in ANC, skilled delivery, and immunization between the richest and poorest exceed 40 percentage points in some cases (Table 2), making it imperative to establish a National Health Equity Observatory (see Figure 3, 5) [46]. Establish a National Health Equity Observatory that uses real‐time BDHS and routine DHIS2 data to provide “equity dashboards” for district managers. This enables “precision public health,” in which resources are diverted to low‐performing divisions such as Sylhet and Mymensingh. Annual district audits can systematically prioritize marginalized populations.

Limitations

This study is based on cross‐sectional, secondary data from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHS) 2017–2018 and 2022, which constrains the ability to draw causal inferences. Furthermore, as this synthesis relies on the descriptive comparison of official weighted estimates, observed changes between 2017 and 2022 represent reported trends rather than a formal longitudinal cohort analysis. Several health indicators, including ANC visits and hypertension history, rely on self‐reporting and may be affected by recall or reporting bias. Anthropometric and biomarker measurements were collected from subsamples, potentially leading to underrepresentation of marginalized populations. Furthermore, critical domains such as mental health, service quality, and health system responsiveness could not be assessed due to variables and data unavailability in BDHS datasets. Finally, while international comparisons were used to contextualize findings, variations in survey years, indicators, and methodologies should be considered when interpreting these comparisons.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while Bangladesh has achieved meaningful gains in maternal care and nutrition, the non‐uniform nature of these progress metrics necessitates a recalibrated, equity‐focused strategy. Institutional deliveries, ANC coverage, and stunting have all improved meaningfully over the past 5 years. However, the plateau in immunization and the rising burden of hypertension, particularly among rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, highlight a critical gap in the current service delivery model. Maternal and child health should no longer be viewed in isolation from NCDs and broader determinants such as education, geography, and income.

As Bangladesh prepares its next Health Sector Program, shifting from vertical interventions to integrated, inclusive, and digitally supported systems will be critical to achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC). Localized planning, community participation, and real‐time monitoring are identified as the core pillars of this evolution. The evidence underscores the necessity of moving away from fragmented service delivery toward a decentralized and digitally‐empowered infrastructure that prioritizes both primary healthcare and institutionalized equity monitoring. Lessons from regional peers—Thailand, Sri Lanka, and Indonesia—offer valuable roadmaps for this shift.

Ultimately, health system performance requires evaluation not only by national averages but by equity of access. The data indicates that a healthy Bangladesh is one where every mother delivers safely, every child is nourished and vaccinated, and every adult has access to lifelong care—regardless of where they live or what they earn. Achieving the targets set forth in the SDGs calls for these data‐driven policy reforms to be delivered with urgency.
