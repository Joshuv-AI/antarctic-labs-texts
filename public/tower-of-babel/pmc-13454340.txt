Transition of blue space in the urban area of khulna city corporation using geospatial and machine learning techniques
Authors: Md Refath Hossan, Mohammad Ismail Hossain, Md. Wahidul Islam, Zahid Hassan Shaon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban blue-space loss and grey-space expansion have significantly altered the surface water systems of the Khulna City Corporation, increasing the environmental degradation and hydrological vulnerability of this climate-sensitive coastal city. This study used multi-temporal Landsat imagery, including Landsat 5 TM data from 1994 to 2004 and Landsat 8 OLI data from 2014 to 2024. The analysis integrated the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index, Normalized Difference Built-up Index, and Normalized Difference Water Index with Random Forest classification and post-classification change detection to quantify long-term land-use/land-cover transitions and urban blue-space transformation. Post-classification comparison generated directional transition matrices documenting conversion pathways among land-cover categories. The results show that surface water bodies have decreased from 4.20 km2 in 1994 to 2.27 km2 in 2024, while the built-up area has increased from 25.9 to 53.3% of the area studied. The accuracy assessment resulted in an overall accuracy of 84.5 to 89.3%, with Kappa coefficients ranging from 0.78 to 0.86. Water body classifications have remained reliable throughout all periods, which has supported the use of classifiable maps for long-term migration analysis. Temporal analysis revealed two main phases: an early phase characterized by mixed land reclamation and later phases dominated by conversion of water bodies into built-up areas. Spatial analysis identified critical hotspots of canal infill, wetland loss, and riverbank modification, mainly in central and southern municipal zones. These findings highlight the need to treat urban blue spaces as critical infrastructure and to promote integrated management, natural drainage solutions and regular remote sensing monitoring. The study frames the loss of blue space as a process of urban transformation and advocates better monitoring of urban water systems in coastal secondary coastal cities.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization has emerged as one of the most transformative processes shaping contemporary cities, particularly in developing countries where accelerated population growth, economic transition, and infrastructure expansion are occurring simultaneously1,2. While urbanization contributes to economic development, it has also generated significant environmental and public health challenges by fundamentally altering urban water systems and disrupting natural hydrological processes3,4. Bangladesh is experiencing an annual urban population growth rate of approximately 3.07%, exemplifying these pressures, particularly in coastal cities that are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts5. This rapid urban expansion, characterized by unplanned development patterns and inadequate infrastructure provisioning, profoundly affects water resource management, environmental quality, and human wellbeing6,7. The transformation of natural landscapes into built environments creates significant pressures on urban water systems, necessitating comprehensive spatial and temporal assessment to inform evidence-based policy formulation8,9. Urban water bodies also designated as blue space are critical ecological and hydrological systems that are intrinsically linked to the sustainability of cities, environmental health and the provision of ecosystem services10. These blue areas include a variety of aquatic features, including rivers, canals, wetlands, ponds and lacustrine systems, which together maintain biodiversity, facilitate water infiltration and recharge, and regulate the dynamics of floods11,12. Metropolises worldwide are experiencing unprecedented transformations driven by rapid urbanization, with significant implications for surface water dynamics, ecosystem integrity, and the availability of critical ecosystem services13. The systematic loss of urban blue spaces represents a fundamental challenge to the resilience and sustainability of contemporary cities, particularly in developing regions where urbanization often proceeds without adequate environmental planning or regulatory frameworks14,15. Contemporary patterns of land-use change in broader South Asian trends wherein unplanned urbanization systematically encroaches upon natural drainage systems, wetlands, and water retention areas, fundamentally disrupting critical hydrological and ecological processes16,17. The progressive conversion of low-lying areas and water bodies into built environments not only diminishes urban green and blue spaces but also compromises groundwater recharge mechanisms, exacerbates surface waterlogging, and weakens ecosystem resilience18,19. Prior empirical investigations of Khulna demonstrate alarming trends of blue space contraction across the city corporation area, while built-up urban space has expanded rapidly over the past two decades20. These transformations underscore the urgency of integrated urban water management strategies that acknowledge surface water bodies as vital infrastructure rather than merely developable land.

Similar patterns of urban blue-space degradation have been widely documented across Asian cities. In Bangladesh, Dhaka has experienced substantial loss of wetlands and water bodies due to unregulated urban expansion, resulting in increased flood vulnerability and environmental degradation21. In India, major cities such as Delhi and Kolkata have undergone extensive wetland encroachment and canal degradation driven by infrastructure development and population pressure22,23. In China, rapid urbanization has contributed to significant reduction of natural water bodies across metropolitan regions, with studies reporting substantial decline in urban surface water extent over recent decades24. Southeast Asian cities such as Jakarta, Bangkok, and Ho Chi Minh City have similarly experienced severe loss of canals and wetlands, leading to increased urban flooding and hydrological instability25–27. These regional patterns highlight that blue-space transformation is not only a local phenomenon but a widespread challenge across rapidly urbanizing Asian coastal and delta cities.

Geospatial technologies have emerged as essential methodological frameworks for systematically characterizing urban environmental transformation28. Multispectral and multitemporal satellite data facilitate comprehensive quantification of spatiotemporal variations in land cover dynamics and aquatic resource patterns29. By integrating temporally sequential remote sensing imagery with advanced spectral analysis, researchers can rigorously assess the magnitude and ecological implications of urban landscape restructuring30. Geospatial techniques enable sophisticated integration of multi-source satellite data, spectral index calculations, and change detection analyses to characterize land-cover transitions at both pixel and landscape scales31. This makes it possible to fully evaluate changes in land use and cover, trends in urban sprawl, and the effects of urbanization on the environment32,33. Such approaches are particularly valuable in rapidly urbanizing regions where ground-based monitoring is limited, allowing for consistent and scalable assessment of environmental change across time and space.

Post-classification comparison (PCC) methodologies, wherein independently classified satellite images from different temporal periods are systematically compared to quantify land cover changes, have emerged as standardized and widely validated approaches for measuring directional landscape transformations34,35. PCC advantages include independence from atmospheric conditions, direct generation of “from-to” transition matrices revealing specific conversion pathways between land cover categories, and suitability for identifying localized spatial change hotspots36. The integration of specialized spectral indices including the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) for vegetation quantification, Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI) for urban infrastructure delineation, and Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) for surface water body identification substantially enhances classification accuracy and enables simultaneous monitoring of multiple environmental dimensions within unified analytical frameworks37–39. Advanced machine learning algorithms substantially enhance classification accuracy and feature delineation, enabling more precise identification of urban expansion impacts on hydrological systems in complex urban environments40. In particular, Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) classification has been significantly improved through the application of ensemble-based machine learning techniques such as the Random Forest (RF) algorithm41,42. RF constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their outputs, thereby increasing classification robustness and reducing overfitting in high-dimensional remote sensing datasets43. Its capacity to model nonlinear relationships and process heterogeneous input variables makes it especially effective for analyzing multispectral satellite imagery44,45. By incorporating spectral bands alongside derived indices such as NDVI, NDBI, and NDWI, RF enhances class separability across complex urban landscapes. Furthermore, RF provides measures of variable importance, allowing identification of the most influential features driving classification performance46,47. Empirical studies have demonstrated that RF-based LULC classification often achieves higher overall accuracy and kappa coefficients compared to traditional methods, particularly in rapidly urbanizing and spatially heterogeneous environments42. When applied to multi-temporal satellite datasets, these integrated machine learning approaches frequently achieve classification accuracies exceeding 86%, thereby providing a robust foundation for reliable change detection and temporal analysis49,50. Recent advances in machine learning, particularly Random Forest and Support Vector Machine classifiers, have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional parametric approaches in heterogeneous urban environments42. Integrating these techniques with spectral indices and post-classification analysis provides enhanced capability for detecting complex land-cover transitions, especially in urban-water interfaces.

Remote sensing-based studies have been widely applied across global contexts to quantify urban expansion and environmental change. In European cities such as Rome and Berlin, spectral index-based analyses have successfully detected significant increases in urbanized areas using multi-temporal satellite data52. In North America, Landsat-derived indices have enabled systematic monitoring of urban growth across metropolitan regions. In Asia, similar approaches have been extensively applied. In India and Bangladesh, multi-temporal Landsat data combined with NDVI, NDBI, and NDWI have been used to quantify rapid urban expansion and surface water loss in cities such as Dhaka and Delhi11,42,43. In China, machine learning and remote sensing integration has been widely used to assess large-scale urbanization impacts on water systems, while Southeast Asian cities have utilized these techniques to monitor canal loss and wetland transformation under rapid urban growth56–58. These studies demonstrate the effectiveness of remote sensing and machine learning approaches in capturing urban land transformation across diverse environmental and socio-economic contexts.

Despite extensive research on urban land use change, there are still important gaps. Most previous studies focus mainly on the quantification of surface water loss without examining the pathways of directional change and the time course of blue-space transformation19. In addition, limited attention was paid to integrating machine learning classification with transformation-based analysis frameworks, especially in data-intensive coastal cities. Moreover, few studies provide a comparative methodological assessment or assess the reliability of classification approaches in a complex urban setting. This study addresses these gaps by introducing a transformation-based analytical framework that captures the gradual transformation of urban blue spaces and integrates machine learning with spectral indices to enhance the reliability of the classification.

This study aims to address these knowledge gaps by examining urban sprawl and surface water transformation in Khulna City using a multi-temporal remote sensing and GIS framework integrated with machine learning classification. In particular, this study emphasizes the identification of directional transition pathways of blue-space conversion, which remains underexplored in existing literature. The objectives are to: (1) quantify multi-decadal LULC change in Khulna City using multi-temporal Landsat imagery and machine learning classification; (2) assess spatial and temporal patterns of surface water transformation; and (3) identify transition pathways linking water-body loss with urban expansion. These findings will improve understanding of urban hydrological transformation processes and support policy development aligned with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 6 and 11). Beyond local analysis, this study contributes to broader scientific understanding by identifying transition-based patterns of urban blue-space transformation and providing a transferable analytical and conceptual framework for understanding urban blue-space transformation in rapidly urbanizing coastal cities.

Methodology

Study area

This study focuses on Khulna City Corporation, located in southwestern Bangladesh between 22°48′0″ N to 22°52′0″ N latitude and 89°31′0″ E to 89°35′0″ E longitude (Fig. 1). The study area covers approximately 46.79 km² and represents a rapidly urbanizing coastal city within the environmentally sensitive Ganges-Brahmaputra delta. As Bangladesh’s third-largest metropolitan area and an important industrial and economic hub, Khulna has experienced substantial land-use transformation over the last three decades. The city is characterized by an extensive network of rivers, canals, ponds, and wetlands that historically supported natural drainage, water storage, and local hydrological regulation. Khulna is strategically situated near the Rupsha, Bhairav, and Mayur rivers and has traditionally depended on a network of canals and wetlands for drainage and flood mitigation60. However, rapid and often unplanned urban expansion has increasingly altered these blue spaces, reducing their capacity to provide hydrological and ecological functions. Khulna was selected for this 30-year assessment (1994–2024) because it represents a coastal urban environment experiencing rapid land-cover change, increasing pressure on surface water systems, and complex water-management challenges. The city currently faces interconnected risks, including saltwater intrusion, river-system degradation, inadequate drainage, persistent waterlogging, and coastal flooding vulnerability. Therefore, analysis of urban blue-space loss and urban expansion in Khulna can provide evidence-based insights for sustainable water planning in Bangladesh and other rapidly urbanizing coastal delta cities.

Landsat 5 TM imagery acquired during 1994 and 2004 provides a foundational dataset with 30-meter spatial resolution across seven spectral bands. Landsat 8 OLI data acquired in 2014 and 2024 represent the transition to enhanced sensor capabilities, offering 30-meter resolution with eleven spectral bands and improved radiometric resolution compared to previous generations. All satellite images were downloaded from the USGS Earth Explorer platform (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/). Cloud coverage in all acquired images was maintained below 10% to ensure optimal image quality and minimize atmospheric interference. The use of Landsat imagery across all time periods ensures methodological consistency, minimizes cross-sensor spectral variability, and enhances reliability of long-term land-cover change detection, which is critical for multi-decadal urban analysis.

Image processing and methods

Image preprocessing and atmospheric correction

Multi-temporal satellite images underwent comprehensive preprocessing to ensure radiometric and geometric consistency. All imagery was geometrically corrected and co-registered to UTM Zone 45 N (WGS 1984 datum).​​ Landsat 5 TM and Landsat 8 OLI data were accessed as Collection 2 Level-2 Surface Reflectance products from GEE. To ensure consistency between multi-temporal datasets acquired from different sensors (Landsat 5 TM and Landsat 8 OLI), all images were processed using surface reflectance products with standardized atmospheric correction. Radiometric consistency between Landsat TM and OLI was checked using Collection 2 Level-2 surface reflectance products and visually stable reference areas. This helped reduce the possibility that detected changes were caused by sensor-response differences rather than land-cover transformation.

Spectral index calculation and analysis

Three primary spectral indices were calculated to enhance discrimination among distinct land cover categories and support robust LULC classification. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) was calculated as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{NDVI}=\frac{\mathrm{NIR}-\mathrm{RED}}{\mathrm{NIR}+\mathrm{RED}}$$\end{document}

where NIR represents reflectance in the near-infrared spectral band and RED represents reflectance in the red spectral band. Higher positive values (> 0.4) indicate dense vegetation canopy, moderate values (0.2–0.4) indicate sparse or degraded vegetation, and negative values indicate non-vegetated surfaces64.

The Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI) was computed using:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{NDBI}=\frac{\mathrm{SWIR1}-\mathrm{NIR}}{\mathrm{SWIR1}+\mathrm{NIR}}$$\end{document}

where SWIR1 represents shortwave infrared reflectance (typically 1550–1750 nm) and NIR represents near-infrared reflectance. NDBI distinguishes urban areas from non-urban ones, with higher values showing built-up regions.

The Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) was computed as39:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{NDWI}=\frac{\mathrm{GREEN}-\mathrm{NIR}}{\mathrm{GREEN}+\mathrm{NIR}}$$\end{document}

where GREEN represents green-band reflectance and NIR represents near-infrared reflectance. NDWI was calculated to enhance the spectral separability of open surface water39. However, in dense urban environments, NDWI may confuse highly reflective surfaces with water. NDWI has therefore not been used as a separate threshold-based method for water extraction. Instead, it has been included as one predictor variable in the Random Forest classification along with the original NDVI and NDBI spectral bands. This reduced the risk of relying on a single water index and allowed the classifier to separate water from built-up and bare surfaces using multiple spectral features.

The Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI), proposed by Xu (2006), can further reduce built-up surface confusion by replacing the NIR band with the SWIR1 band. MNDWI calculate as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:MNDWI=\frac{Green-SWIR1}{Green+SWIR1}$$\end{document}

Although MNDWI was not used as a primary classification input in this study, its methodological relevance is acknowledged. The reliability of the final water class was evaluated through class-specific user’s and producer’s accuracies rather than relying only on index-based thresholding.

Spectral indices were calculated for each preprocessed satellite image independently, generating raster datasets with pixel-level continuous-value outputs. Index values were subsequently rescaled to 8-bit unsigned integer format (0–255) for operational compatibility with GIS and classification algorithms, with original continuous index values preserved in separate floating-point datasets for statistical analysis. Additionally, these spectral indices were incorporated as predictor variables in the machine learning classification to enhance class separability, particularly between water, built-up, and bare land surfaces Fig. 2.

Land use land cover classification

A random forest (RF) machine learning classifier was used to categorize each time series (1994, 2004, 2014 and 2024) into four classes of land use and land cover: water bodies, built-up areas, vegetation and bare land. To increase methodological rigor, the RF classifier has been chosen because of its superior ability to process remote sensing data in high-dimensional form and to model nonlinear relationships compared to traditional classification approaches. Previous studies have shown that RF outperforms conventional methods such as maximum likelihood and are comparable to or better than support vector machines (SVM), especially in a heterogeneous urban environment71. In this study, the performance of the RF model was evaluated using out-of-bag (OOB) error estimation, along with repeated model runs to ensure stability and robustness.

Training sample collection

Training samples were systematically delineated using visual interpretation of high-resolution Google Earth Pro imagery (0.5–2.0 m resolution), field knowledge and municipal records, and spectral index analysis (NDVI, NDBI, NDWI). A minimum of 60 training samples per LULC class were selected using stratified random sampling to ensure representative coverage of spectral variability, prioritizing areas with minimal spectral ambiguity.​

The RF classifier utilized spectral bands together with derived indices (NDVI, NDBI, NDWI) as input variables, improving discrimination of spectrally similar classes such as shallow water and built-up surfaces73. Consequently, surface water extent was derived from the final Random Forest classified water class, not from NDWI thresholding alone. The Random Forest model was implemented using 500 decision trees, with the number of variables per split optimized using the square root of total input features74. Model performance stability was ensured through repeated runs and cross-validation.

Post-classification change detection

Post-classification comparison (PCC) was used to quantify land-use/land-cover (LULC) transitions in Khulna City for three periods: 1994–2004, 2004–2014, and 2014–2024. Independently classified LULC maps were compared on a pixel-by-pixel basis to generate from-to transition matrices, enabling direct measurement of conversion pathways while minimizing sensor and atmospheric effects. All LULC maps were harmonized to a common projection, resolution, and classification scheme consisting of water, vegetation, built-up, and bare land classes. Change rasters were produced using raster algebra in ArcGIS:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{Change}}_{{t}_{1}\mathrm{\--}{t}_{2}}=({\mathrm{LULC}}_{{t}_{1}}\times\:10)+{\mathrm{LULC}}_{{t}_{2}}$$\end{document}

This coding generated unique values for each land-cover transition, allowing identification of Water→Built-up, Water→Vegetation, and Water→Bare land changes. Transition areas were calculated using the Tabulate Area tool, producing quantitative matrices for each period. The transition matrices quantified as total area converted per land cover category (square kilometers and percentage), class persistence, conversion rates (square kilometers per decade), and water body fate (conversion to built-up, vegetation, bare ground, or persistence). Landscape fragmentation metrics including patch number, mean patch size, patch density, and largest patch index characterized water body configuration and fragmentation resulting from urban development. To enhance reliability, NDWI, NDVI, and NDBI were used to verify spectral consistency of the detected transitions, ensuring that water loss, vegetation expansion, and urban growth were physically meaningful. These outputs provide a spatial and temporal assessment of urban blue space transformation and its relationship with urban expansion in Khulna City.

Post-classification refinement and accuracy

A 3 × 3 moving window median filter eliminated salt-and-pepper noise while preserving boundary integrity. Classification accuracy was evaluated using error matrices comparing classified output with 30% independent validation samples. Accuracy metrics included overall accuracy, user’s accuracy, producer’s accuracy, and kappa coefficient, with target accuracy ≥ 85% overall and minimum per-class accuracy ≥ 80%. Software and data processing environment

All image processing, spectral index calculations, and LULC classification procedures were performed using ESRI ArcGIS (version 10.8) with the Spatial Analyst extension, supplemented by the open-source GDAL (Geospatial Data Abstraction Library) toolset for format conversions and batch processing operations. Statistical analyses of transition matrices and accuracy metrics were conducted in R (version 4.2.0), employing the raster, sf (simple features), and caret (Classification and Regression Training) packages for geospatial data manipulation and accuracy assessment. All datasets were maintained in GeoTIFF format with UTM Zone 45 N (WGS 1984) projection, ensuring geometric consistency across temporal epochs and facilitating integration with existing municipal GIS databases for Khulna City Corporation Fig. 3.

Results

Land cover change assessment

The area covered by the land cover includes the overall spatial distribution of all land and water features, including residential, commercial and industrial development, transport infrastructure networks, river systems, wetlands, lacustrine bodies, pond complexes, canal networks, arable vegetation, shrubs, parks, open spaces, grasslands and farmland. The basic land use and land cover classification (LULC) widely used in Bangladesh covers agricultural land, urban and built-up areas, forest and vegetative land, surface water bodies and wetland ecosystems. The machine learning classifier produced spatially consistent LULC maps across all temporal epochs, improving separation between built-up, bare land, and water classes, particularly in heterogeneous urban fringe areas.

In this context, the Khulna City Corporation area shows similar soil cover characteristics typical of tropical areas in Bangladesh, with a complex spatial diversity and dynamic time variations. The systematic land cover classification methodology used for the KCC area analysis covers four separate time periods: 1994, 2004, 2014 and 2024, using advanced remote-sensing techniques and geo-information systems applications. The current urbanization processes have accelerated significant land-cover changes in the KCC, leading to systematic replacement of surface water bodies by newly built-up areas Figs. 4 and 5.

This analysis shows a gradual decrease in the coverage of surface water bodies by 2.5 percent from 1994 to 2004, by 0.9 percent from 2004 to 2014 and by 0.8 percent from 2014 to 2024. On the contrary, the rate of urbanization growth has been increasing from 3.7 to 8.3 and from 8 to 16.3 per year, respectively, for three different time periods between 2004 and 2014 and 2014 to 2024. The quantitative analysis in Figure 5 shows that the most significant reductions in surface area in 1994-2004 and the increase in built-up area in 2014-24 occurred in a period of accelerating urban encroachment on natural resources over the past four decades. The accuracy assessment confirmed acceptable classification performance, with overall accuracy ranging from 84.5% to 89.3% and Kappa coefficients ranging from 0.78 to 0.86 (Table 3).

Surface water body area assessment

Surface water bodies include a variety of hydrological features, including river systems, permanent and seasonal wetlands, pond complexes, lacustrine environments, canal networks, and artificial dams. The Rupsha and Bhairav rivers are the main body of surface water in the KCC and contribute significantly to the overall hydrological environment. In addition, several ponds, lakes, canals and wetland systems together constitute a significant surface water coverage in the whole of the KCC, but in recent decades the extent of surface water has been systematically reduced to meet the demands of rapid urbanization. A temporal analysis of changes in surface water bodies, directly linked to the accelerated urbanization process in the KCC, Fig. 6 shows significant spatial and quantitative changes during the 30-year period of the study: 1994, 2004, 2014 and 2024. Analysis of three decades of surface water body changes shows a significant decrease in the coverage of surface water bodies across the KCC. Quantitative analysis shows that in February 1994 and 2004 the total surface area of the bodies of water measured 4.2021 and 3.0456 square kilometers, respectively. However, by 2014 and 2024, the study area covered only 2.6208 and 2.2698 square kilometers of surface water bodies respectively (Fig. 6).

Surface water body area to built-up urban area conversion assessment

Contemporary patterns of land-use change are characterized by the rapid urbanization and expansion phenomena that occur worldwide, and the KCC area is experiencing similar transformation processes. As a result, a significant part of the existing land cover is systematically transferred to alternative land use categories. The conversion of surface water bodies into alternative types of land cover not only compromises the physical characteristics of the surrounding environment, but also has significant negative environmental impacts, including altered hydrological regimes, reduced biodiversity and increased flood vulnerability. Quantitative transition matrix analysis derived from post-classification comparison revealed distinct spatial and temporal patterns of water body conversion to built-up urban areas across the three temporal periods. The conversion of surface water bodies into built-up areas occurred at rates of 0.68 km² (1994–2004), 0.39 km² (2004–2014), and 0.31 km² (2014–2024) (Fig. 8), respectively, demonstrating a declining trend in absolute water-to-built-up area conversion magnitude over the three decades.

Built-up expansion became the dominant destination of converted water bodies. Its share increased from 59.1% in 1994–2004 to nearly 90% in the later periods, indicating a shift toward urban-driven water-body loss (Table 2). This temporal transition evidence evolving developmental priorities, wherein early-period wetland reclamation for agricultural expansion progressively yielded to urban infrastructure development as the city industrialized and population density intensified. The declining absolute area of water-to-built-up conversion in recent years may reflect either saturation of economically accessible water bodies available for urban development, implementation of enhanced regulatory constraints on wetland conversion practices, or spatial redistribution of urban expansion to alternative land cover categories. Figure 8 displays the precise spatial location of surface water bodies that were filled in and subsequently developed for urban purposes, illustrating the concentration of water body infill within central and southern municipal zones. The application of NDBI to multi-temporal satellite imagery successfully delineated areas where surface water bodies previously existed but were subsequently developed for urban infrastructure, enabling precise spatial identification of conversion locations and quantification of cumulative water body loss attributable to urban expansion. Water-body conversion was highest during 1994–2004, when conversion occurred into built-up land, vegetation, and bare land. In later periods, conversion became increasingly dominated by built-up development, indicating a shift from mixed reclamation to urban-driven transformation.

The total conversion of water bodies dropped notably to only 0.43 km², representing a decline of approximately 62.6% from the 1994–2004 level. More importantly, the main destination of water body transfers shifted towards urban development, with 89.7% of water transfers supporting built-up areas, compared to 3.2% converted to vegetation and 7.1% to bare land (Table 2). This shift implies either completion of major land-use reclamation projects, the introduction of policies limiting wetland conversion, or a strategic reorientation of development priorities towards urban rather than agricultural expansion. This trend continued through 2014–2024, when water body conversion remained minimal at 0.35 km² and 88.6% of the conversions supported the development of built-up areas. The conversion from water to bare land, which was the predominant pattern during 1994–2004 (31.3%), fell further to 9.9% by 2014–2024. This development shows how water body filling, which was initially a mechanism for agricultural land expansion, increasingly supports urban development needs, albeit on a much smaller scale than in the earlier reclamation period.

The acceleration in conversion rates observed over the 2004-24 period points to an increase in urban pressure on natural water resources, which requires immediate policy action and sustainable urban planning strategies to mitigate further environmental degradation.

Accuracy validation and assessment

Classification accuracy was evaluated through error matrices comparing machine learning classification results against high-resolution reference data (Google Earth Pro, 0.5–2.0 m resolution). Water body classification consistently exceeded 85% thresholds (UA/PA > 85%) across all epochs (Table 3), confirming reliable aquatic system detection for urban blue space assessment. Built-up areas met 80% of standards throughout. All metrics demonstrate consistent improvement over time, reflecting enhanced sensor capabilities. Kappa values indicate substantial (1994: κ = 0.78) to excellent (2024: κ = 0.86) agreement beyond chance expectation, meeting κ ≥ 0.75 target thresholds. The relatively lower accuracy in 1994 reflects the limited availability of independent historical data on the ground; however, the accuracy of water users and producers remained above 85%, which supports a reliable interpretation of the change in water bodies. Classifications demonstrate adequate reliability for LULC change detection and evidence-based urban planning applications. Progressive accuracy improvement across epochs, coupled with consistent achievement of quality thresholds for all LULC categories, substantiates methodological validity and provides robust statistical foundation for quantitative change detection analysis of urban blue space dynamics and urban expansion patterns. The exceptional reliability of water body classification (> 85% across all epochs) affirms scientific credibility of aquatic system transformation assessments central to this investigation. The improved classification performance demonstrates the effectiveness of machine learning approaches in capturing complex urban land-cover patterns compared to traditional parametric methods.

Although NDWI may misclassify highly reflective impervious surfaces as water in dense urban environments, NDWI was not used as a standalone threshold-based extraction method in this study. Discussion

Blue space contraction and multifaceted causal mechanisms

The surface waters of Khulna have been significantly contracted during the study period, indicating a significant hydrological change. This pronounced blue space loss results from multiple interconnected causal mechanisms operating across distinct developmental phases. Blue space loss in KCC is driven by multiple factors (Table 4). These include canal encroachment, pond filling, inadequate drainage systems, and extreme rainfall events. Project-level infrastructure interventions constitute explicit direct mechanisms accelerating blue space loss: the KCC 400 Roads Construction Project (2019–2023) and KDA 3-Link Road Development Project (2023–2025) have directly infilled canal networks and eliminated critical water bodies, reducing hydrological connectivity. Several infrastructure projects have changed the canals, ponds and riverbanks. These interventions have improved urban services in some places, but have also reduced the priority given to local storage, drainage connections and the protection of small water bodies (Table 4). Climate-related stressors including saltwater intrusion from adjacent coastal rivers and precipitation variability further exacerbate surface water scarcity and contamination, collectively undermining the city’s hydrological resilience and natural drainage capacity. Beyond the Khulna context, the observed time dynamics indicate a more general pattern of urban blue-space transitions. The early stages of urbanization are characterized by mixed land use, including agriculture and land clearing, while the later stages are increasingly dominated by built-up. This transition reflects structural shifts in development pressure and hydrological risks common to the rapid urbanization of peripheral coastal towns and villages.

The temporal dynamics of water body conversion reveal fundamental restructuring of development priorities and sectoral expansion strategies. During the period 1994–2004, the conversion of water bodies was spread between built-up land, bare land and vegetation. This suggests an early stage of mixed reclamation rather than purely urban sprawl. This diversified conversion strategy distinguishes early Khulna development from contemporary patterns whereas Dhaka experienced sustained metropolitan sprawl driving water body loss across decades13, Khulna’s initial phase reflected strategic policy choices favoring both agricultural and urban land creation. After 2004, water-body conversion became increasingly urban dominated. Most converted water areas shifted to built-up land, reflecting stronger pressure from infrastructure expansion and urban consolidation. This phase transition from diversified to urban-driven blue space loss represents a critical inflection point in developmental trajectory, reflecting how secondary coastal cities replicate metropolitan expansion patterns once agricultural reclamation reaches saturation. Comparative analysis with South Asian, Asian, and global precedents reveals Khulna’s transformation exhibits accelerated temporal compression characteristic of secondary coastal cities. Dhaka required four decades (1960–2008) to lose over 60% of water bodies13, whereas Khulna achieved 46% loss within three decades (Fig. 9). In India, Kolkata experienced 30–40% expansion of impervious surfaces from 2000 to 2020 while green and blue spaces contracted correspondingly, amplifying urban flood vulnerability23. Across Asia, Chinese cities demonstrated severe water body loss analysis of 159 major cities revealed 20.6% loss of natural water bodies to urban expansion between 1990 and 2018, with peak losses during 2005–2010 when economic development and real estate speculation accelerated blue space encroachment. Globally, Western Sydney experienced 25% decline in wetland density (2010–2017) through urbanization-driven conversion, reflecting convergent patterns wherein infrastructure investment, real estate speculation, and sectoral reorientation toward urban-industrial development now constitute primary drivers of blue space loss in developing-world cities81.

The transformation pattern observed in Khulna differs from megacities such as Dhaka and Delhi7,22. As a medium-sized coastal city, Khulna exhibits a more rapid and spatially concentrated transition from mixed land reclamation to urban-dominated expansion. In contrast, megacities experience prolonged transformation driven by large-scale infrastructure and stronger governance frameworks7. The coastal environment of Khulna also introduces hydrological constraints such as tidal effects and the sensitivity of drainage. These differences underline the importance of urban scale, administrative capacity and the environmental context in shaping the transformation of the Blue Belt. The blue-space conversion at Khulna should be interpreted as more than just a reduction of the mapped surface of the water. The loss and fragmentation of canals, ponds and wetlands undermine the hydrological interconnection between the urban areas. This reduces local water storage, reduces groundwater recharge, interferes with drainage channels and increases the exposure to flooding. These processes show that the loss of blue areas is not only a transformation of the soil cover, but also a change in the urban water cycle and resilience capacity.

Systemic governance dysfunction and infrastructure paradoxes

The progression of surface water loss in Khulna reflects a structurally embedded governance crisis rather than merely a technical shortfall in drainage or supply infrastructure. Multiple agencies including Khulna City Corporation (KCC), Khulna Water Supply and Sewerage Authority (KWASA), Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB), Khulna Development Authority (KDA), and line departments share responsibility for land development, drainage, water supply, and river management in Khulna. These overlapping mandates create fragmented governance and weaken accountability for protecting urban blue space. This fragmentation has enabled systematic canal encroachment, pond infill, and riverbank modification despite formal legal protections, as infrastructure and land development projects proceed without meaningful ecological safeguards. For example, the Khulna Water Supply Project and subsequent network extension programs shifted the city’s investment focus toward a capital-intensive surface-water treatment plant and trunk distribution network, while leaving neighborhood-scale ponds and canals effectively unprotected and progressively neglected, particularly in poorer and peri-urban areas. The coexistence of a 323-million-dollar engineered water scheme with continued loss of small but hydrologically critical water bodies82, documented in Table 4, epitomizes a supply-side paradigm in which infrastructure expansion is pursued without parallel commitments to preserve the ecological systems that underpin long-term water security.

The land-use change flow diagram for Khulna (1994–2024) reinforces this interpretation by visually depicting persistent, one-directional transitions from water, vegetation, and bare land into the built-up category across all epochs, particularly between 2004 and 2024 (Fig. 10). The thickening flows into built-up nodes and progressive thinning of water and vegetation nodes show that, once converted, land remains effectively locked in impervious urban use, with negligible reverse transitions back to ecologically functional classes. This structural irreversibility reflects institutional preferences for urban consolidation over restoration or protection of blue–green infrastructure. Recent work on Khulna’s water landscape similarly identifies weak regulatory enforcement, politically mediated land allocation, and unequal network expansion as key drivers of canal filling and loss of surface water retention areas, with slum and low-income neighborhoods bearing disproportionate exposure to waterlogging and service deficits. Similar governance problems are visible in other South Asian cities. Dhaka has lost many designated retention areas despite planning regulations, while Delhi’s wetland degradation reflects weak implementation of ecological buffers13,22. In contrast, emerging blue-green infrastructure initiatives in Chinese and Indian cities (e.g., Shenzhen’s sponge-city interventions and Chennai’s integrated blue–green projects) demonstrate that coordinated, multi-agency frameworks can reverse or slow blue space loss when ecological objectives are mainstreamed into urban planning and investment decisions85. Against this comparative backdrop, Khulna’s more fragmented institutional landscape and absence of an integrated urban water authority create particularly acute coordination deficits, allowing ecologically damaging land-use transitions clearly evident in the land-use flow diagram to proceed even in the presence of substantial investment and formally articulated climate and resilience goals.

Regulatory control alone may be insufficient in contexts where enforcement capacity is limited. Market-based instruments can complement planning controls by changing the economic incentives that drive water-body conversion. Possible measures include development impact fees for projects that reduce drainage capacity, tax rebates for retaining ponds or wetlands, compensation schemes for private landowners who conserve water bodies, and public–private partnerships for canal restoration. Such instruments may be especially relevant for Khulna, where fragmented governance limits the effectiveness of command-and-control regulation.

Integrating NDVI, NDBI, and NDWI effectiveness and applicability in urban contexts

The methodological framework combining multi-temporal satellite analysis, integrated NDVI, NDBI, and NDWI spectral indices, and post-classification comparison (PCC) demonstrates substantially greater analytical power than single-index or pre-classification approaches for documenting urban blue space transformations. NDWI was useful for enhancing open-water separability, but its limitations in dense urban areas must be recognized. Highly reflective impervious surfaces can sometimes produce spectral responses similar to water, which is why MNDWI has been recommended for built-up environments. In this study, this limitation was addressed by using NDWI as one predictor within a Random Forest classification rather than as a standalone water-extraction threshold. The classifier also included original spectral bands, NDVI, and NDBI, allowing water, built-up land, vegetation, and bare land to be separated using multiple spectral features. The consistently high water-class user’s and producer’s accuracies support the reliability of the final water maps. Nevertheless, MNDWI remains useful for future sensitivity testing, especially in highly urbanized zones. To further strengthen methodological evaluation, the performance of the Random Forest (RF) classifier used in this study was compared with alternative classification approaches reported in previous research, including Support Vector Machines (SVM) and traditional parametric classifiers such as Maximum Likelihood. Studies in similar urban environments have demonstrated that RF generally provides higher classification accuracy due to its ensemble learning structure, ability to handle nonlinear relationships, and reduced sensitivity to noise. The classification accuracy achieved in this study, with overall accuracy ranging from 84.5% to 89.3% and Kappa coefficients from 0.78 to 0.86 (Table 3), is consistent with comparable LULC studies using machine learning approaches. This comparison confirms the robustness and reliability of the adopted methodology for complex urban land-cover analysis.

The synergistic three-index approach enables simultaneous monitoring of water, vegetation, and built-up expansion within unified frameworks, capturing complex land cover dynamics where single-index methods constrain accuracy in deltaic cities with spectral ambiguity between aquatic features, riparian vegetation, and urban structures. Recent comparable studies in Delhi (NDVI-NDBI for urban expansion detection (87–89%) accuracy and Dhaka (multi-spectral indices with SVM classification (91–93%) accuracy achieved comparable or superior accuracy metrics, suggesting that PCC methodologies, while methodologically sound, may underperform contemporary machine learning ensemble approaches. The framework’s reliance on 30-meter Landsat resolution obscures fine-scale canal networks and small ponds, potentially underestimating total blue space loss and integration with higher-resolution satellite datasets and incorporation of MNDWI or higher-resolution water indices would further enhance urban water-body delineation. Future research may incorporate higher-resolution datasets such as Sentinel-2 or commercial imagery to improve detection of fine-scale urban water features. While PCC provides transparent transition quantification, contemporary research increasingly employs deep learning semantic segmentation (U-Net, FCN architectures) achieving > 92% accuracy with explicit spatial pattern recognition capabilities, representing potential methodological advancement beyond traditional PCC approaches. Nonetheless, the integrated three-index PCC framework provides reliable, replicable, and resource-efficient methodology suitable for data-scarce developing-country contexts where machine learning expertise and computational infrastructure remain constrained.

Strategic imperatives and differentiated policy responses

Based on the spatial pattern of water-body loss and land-cover transition, policy responses can be differentiated into high-, moderate-, and low-severity zones (Table 5). High-severity zones include areas where canal infill, pond loss, and conversion to built-up land are concentrated, particularly in central and southern parts of Khulna. These areas require strict protection, restoration of drainage corridors, and prohibition of further water-body filling. Moderate-severity zones show partial fragmentation and should be managed through development controls, retention requirements, and local drainage improvement. Low-severity peripheral zones should be protected through preventive zoning before major conversion occurs. The findings necessitate fundamental restructuring of urban water governance and planning paradigms for KCC. Blue space preservation must transition from peripheral planning consideration to core infrastructure status requiring explicit policy protection, zoning constraints with legal force, and rigorous enforcement mechanisms with dedicated institutional authority. Contemporary nature-based solutions multifunctional green-blue corridors, wetland restoration, sponge city approaches enhancing rainwater infiltration, and retention basins distributing precipitation across distributed urban storage offer methodologically proven pathways for simultaneously addressing water scarcity, flood vulnerability, and ecosystem service provision in urbanizing coastal regions. Khulna’s institutional fragmentation demands governance reform establishing integrated water management authorities with unified mandate over surface water bodies, prohibiting sectoral agencies from unilateral decisions affecting aquatic resources. Unlike supply-side infrastructure expansion, which characterizes current policy paradigms, sustainability requires demand-side conservation measures including conservation-oriented progressive tariffs, mandatory stormwater retention standards in development projects, and explicit hydrological impact assessments for all infrastructure initiatives. Equity should be central to water policy. Low-income households are more exposed to waterlogging and often have fewer alternatives for safe water access. Restoration and drainage investments should therefore prioritize vulnerable neighborhoods. The Khulna case shows that blue-space loss is not inevitable. It reflects policy choices, weak enforcement, and development priorities that undervalue urban water systems. Comparison with emerging best practices in Asian cities indicates integrated approaches combining green infrastructure, decentralized water management, and ecosystem preservation can simultaneously deliver development objectives while maintaining hydrological resilience, contradicting conventional narratives positioning development and environmental protection as irreconcilable96. The methodological framework integrating high-resolution satellite monitoring with participatory governance and adaptive management provides transferable approaches for evidence-based urban water policy across developing-country coastal cities experiencing convergent pressures from rapid urbanization, climate change impacts, and resource constraints.

Study limitations

This study acknowledges several methodological and spatial limitations. First, the 30-m spatial resolution of Landsat imagery restricts the detection of small water bodies, narrow channels, and fine riparian features, which may result in conservative estimates of blue space extent and change. However, the exclusive use of Landsat data ensures temporal consistency across all study periods, reducing cross-sensor variability and improving comparability of long-term change detection. Second, index-based water extraction remains sensitive to atmospheric conditions and seasonal hydrological variability. While the use of temporally comparable seasonal imagery reduces these effects, inter-annual variations in precipitation and runoff may still influence observed trends. Third, the availability of historical ground-truth data for early periods (1994–2004) is limited, which may introduce uncertainty in classification accuracy for initial epochs. Fourth, the random forest classification employed in this study represents a well-established approach, Although machine learning improved classification performance, limitations remain related to spatial resolution, historical validation data availability, and exclusion of broader watershed-scale hydrological processes98. Finally, the study is limited by the boundaries of its municipal scope. It does not explicitly model the dynamics of upstream river basins, the effects of tidal intrusion and the regional hydrological interconnection between the Rupsa, Bhairava and Mayur systems. Changes in upstream flows, sedimentation, tidal effects and droughts can affect local drainage, salinity and availability of water. Future research should integrate hydrological data at the basin level with analysis of urban land cover change. Although NDWI has not been used as a separate threshold-based extraction method, the methodological limitation remains the lack of a complete comparison of the quantitative sensitivity of NDWI and MNDWI. Future work should compare water masks derived from NDWI and MNDWI using higher resolution validation data.

Conclusion

This study evaluated three decades of land cover and urban blue-space loss in the Khulna City Corporation, using multiple time-lapse Landsat images, random forest classification and transition analysis. Results show a significant decline in surface water bodies, together with rapid built-up. The conversion of water bodies followed a sequential pattern: early changes involved mixed reclamation, while later changes were characterized by built-up development. These findings show that the loss of blue area in Khulna is not only a problem of soil cover, but also a problem of hydrology and management. Loss and fragmentation of canals, wetlands and wetlands reduce the connectivity of drainage systems, reduce water retention and increase the exposure to flooding. Sustainable planning should therefore treat urban blue areas as critical infrastructure rather than as land open to development. Future urban water management in Khulna should combine strict protection of high-risk areas, rehabilitation of drainage corridors, development control in low-risk areas and precautionary zoning in peripheral areas. Regular remote sensing, hydrological assessments and participatory management are also needed to promote climate resilient urban development in Khulna and similar coastal secondary towns.
