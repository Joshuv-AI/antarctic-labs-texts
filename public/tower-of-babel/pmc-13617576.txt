DeepIR-Pred: identification of insulin receptors using metaheuristic optimization of biologically informed multi-view features with deep recurrent learning
Authors: Matee Ullah, Zhen Li, Yixiao Zhai, Changwei Lv, Kashif Ahmad Khan, Quan Zou, Xu Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe insulin receptor (IR) proteins play a central role in cellular growth and metabolic regulation. Disruption of IRs is linked to several fatal neurodegenerative and cancer-like diseases. Wet-lab experiments are time-consuming, costly, and resource-intensive. Therefore, accurately predicting IR proteins using computational methods is critical for understanding the IR-associated biological mechanism and advancing therapeutic research. In this work, we propose a biologically informed deep learning method, DeepIR-Pred, which is the first rigorous model to accurately identify IR proteins. To ensure model robustness, two rigorously curated non-redundant sequence-based training and testing datasets (IR2476 and IR274) were first constructed. A newly proposed transformed image-based feature descriptor [Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM)–rotation-invariant co-occurrence among adjacent local binary patterns], derived from PSSMs, was integrated with biologically informed qualitative characteristics and protein language model embeddings (Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2 and ProtTrans-T5). Next, an enhanced metaheuristic-based spatial bound whale optimization algorithm was employed to reduce and select the most discriminative features. Finally, DeepIR-Pred was learned using a deep recurrent neural network (Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit) architecture. Extensive experimental results, using 10-fold cross-validation and independent testing, demonstrated the significant performance of the DeepIR-Pred in terms of both robustness and generalization capability on unseen samples. Furthermore, interpretability analysis provided insights into feature contributions and discriminative characteristics associated with IR proteins, while the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis highlighted improved class separability. Collectively, DeepIR-Pred paves the way for researchers to investigate computational-based methods for IR proteins in depth and unlock new potential in therapeutic discovery. All data and source codes for DeepIR-Pred are available at https://github.com/MateeullahKhan/DeepIR-Pred for academic use.

Introduction

Insulin signaling is an essential biological process that plays a critical role in regulating glucose utilization, cellular growth, and energy homeostasis in multicellular organisms. The insulin receptor (IR), a transmembrane tyrosine kinase, central to insulin signaling, mediates cellular response to insulin in various tissues including muscle, liver, adipose tissue, and the central nervous system underscoring its central role in cellular growth and metabolic regulation [1, 2]. Upon activation, the IR triggers a cascade of phosphorylation-driven signaling, mainly through the Ras–MAPK and IRS–PI3K–Akt pathways. This controls numerous anabolic processes including glucose uptake, suppression of gluconeogenesis, and glycogen production. Additionally, the IR also enhances lipid synthesis while suppressing lipolytic activity, thereby aiding energy storage during nutrient-rich conditions [3]. Moreover, ﻿in the central nervous system, the IR plays a pivotal role in cognitive functions particularly involving memory and learning. Disruption of the IR signaling pathway is associated with several neurodegenerative diseases including the fatal Alzheimer’s disease, highlighting its physiological importance [4, 5]. In oncology, IR is known as a critical therapeutic target. Aberrant signaling is linked to cancer ﻿progression and targeted therapies like tyrosine kinase inhibitors are under investigation to intervene in these oncogenic pathways.

Given the critical role of the IR in numerous human-related illnesses, scientists and researchers have been motivated to pursue further investigations. The identification and study of IR have employed various experimental approaches, including radioligand binding assays [6], western blotting [7], immunohistochemistry [8], immunofluorescence [9], flow cytometry [10], and affinity chromatography [11]. These experimental approaches have evolved over time and made it possible to learn about the IR shape, dynamics, and various important biological processes. However, these experimental techniques still have several important limitations. They require a significant amount of resources and expertise. Furthermore, the successful implementation of these techniques often requires high-quality biological specimens, a requirement that can be challenging to obtain for complex cellular environments, tissues, or experimental conditions. In addition, they are time-consuming and costly which further limits these experimental techniques for large-scale IR identification.

During the last decades, computational-based identification has provided an alternative way to these time-consuming, resource-intensive, and costly experimental techniques, which accurately analyze and identify a range of biological proteins more rapidly with low operational cost [12–14]. Therefore, there is also a growing demand for computer intelligence-based methods for efficiently predicting IRs. Various feature descriptors have been proposed to effectively extract meaningful information such as evolutionary information and latent statistical patterns [15], physicochemical information [16], and structure-based information [17] from protein sequences. They require minimal effort with low resources. Additionally, deep learning-based models have become popular in recent years, achieving high performance across various research fields [18–21]. Therefore, ﻿to accurately predict the IR, deep learning models are the best choice as they can easily accommodate the rapidly growing biological data, achieving high accuracy with unprecedented speed that wet-lab experimental techniques alone cannot achieve effectively. While deep learning models have revolutionized protein science, their application to predicting the IR remains underexplored. To fill this gap, there is an urgent need to develop a deep learning model specifically targeting IRs.

In this study, we propose the first deep recurrent-based computational model to predict IRs from non-IRs. To effectively predict IRs, following are the five main contributions of this study: (i) construction of new benchmark training and testing IRs datasets; (ii) we propose a new transformed image-based evolutionary feature extraction method, PSSM_RICLBP, integrated with qualitative characteristics (QLC) features, Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2 (ESM2), and ProtT5 embeddings; (iii) a newly developed metaheuristic-based feature selection (FS) algorithm was introduced to optimally select best features and reduce the dimension of large multi-view protein vectors; (iv) a new deep bidirectional recurrent neural network (RNN) was specifically designed to effectively predict IR proteins and; (v) the generalization ability of the model was tested using an independent test dataset. Based on the above-mentioned pipeline and contribution, a powerful deep learning model, DeepIR-Pred, was implemented. Experimental results on the training and testing datasets further verify the model’s efficiency.

Datasets and methods

Dataset

A more reliable and stringent predictive model can only be developed when it is trained upon vigilantly created and collated datasets that are rigorous, comprehensive, and reliable. Therefore, in this study, we constructed our own protein sequence-based dataset for the IR. The sequence samples were carefully derived from the widely known data source UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org) [22]. The dataset is specifically collected for the problem of binary classification and contains IR sequences as the positive class and non-IR sequences as the negative class. The IR samples were retrieved by querying the keyword “Insulin receptor.” The reviewed filter (Swiss-Prot) was enabled which ensures that only high-quality and manually curated proteins were collected. For the negative class, proteins were derived using the intentionally designed query “Kinase AND Cytoplasm NOT Transmembrane NOT Signal” with retrieval restricted to reviewed only. This ensures that the negative class is biologically meaningful and challenging for the model rather than randomly selected proteins and hence cytoplasmic kinases without signal peptides and transmembrane regions were collected as negatives to ensure functional similarity while avoiding overlap with IRs. A total of 3255 IR and 11 218 non-IR sequences were retrieved.

To ensure the data quality, several preprocessing steps were applied. First, the unknown or non-standard amino acids ﻿B, J, U, Z, X, O were removed to avoid inconsistencies and noise and then sequences with amino acids <50 were filtered out. Next, to mitigate data scarcity and ensure there were enough sequences for training the deep learning model while preserving sufficient functional diversity as well, we employed the CD-HIT [23] tool with 60% similarity threshold to reduce the sequence redundancy. Stricter similarity thresholds were employed as well, which resulted in substantial data reduction, leading to data scarcity, and reduced functional diversity; hence, this threshold was empirically selected to remove highly similar samples. Finally, the IR protein reached 1375 and the non-IR protein reached 3026. Next, we randomly selected 1375 non-IR from the 3026 samples and the final obtained sequences were carefully checked to ensure there were no duplicates selected. We then divided the dataset into training and independent sets. The final training dataset comprises 1238 IR proteins (positive samples) and 1238 non-IR proteins (negative samples), while the independent test dataset consists of 137 positive and 137 negative samples. The trained and independent datasets were named IR2476 and IR274, respectively. Feature representation approaches

Representation of insulin receptor sequence as PSSM

The Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM) is a widely used representation for capturing evolutionary information in protein sequences. PSSM has shown remarkable efficiency across many bioinformatics problems [24–27]. Collectively, these existing studies underscore the significant contribution of evolutionary information to improved predictive modeling.

In this work, protein sequences were represented using their corresponding PSSM profiles. For a given L-length IR sequence, an L × 20 PSSM matrix was obtained, where 20 represents the standard amino acids. Each element, at a given position in the matrix, denotes the residue’s evolutionary mutation probability. A standard mathematical representation of the PSSM matrix P is provided in Equation 1.

To address the limitation of PSSM’s variable length, this study employed a novel technique that transforms the PSSM matrix into a 2D image representation and encodes it using an image-based feature encoding strategy. The details are provided in the subsequent section.

Notably, this study employed the local NCBI-BLAST+ [28] program together with the Swiss-Prot database [29, 30] to extract the PSSM profile for each provided sequence.

Qualitative characteristics features

The QLC feature [32] is a global representation method that characterizes amino acid sequences based on their underlying physicochemical properties. It incorporates seven fundamental physicochemical properties of amino acids, including polarity, charge, polarizability, solvent accessibility, hydrophobicity, normalized van der Waals volume, and secondary structure. These properties are divided into three separate classes based on their shared properties. Complete details for each group can be found in Supplementary Table S1 under Supplementary Text S2 in the SI. QLC offers a biologically meaningful representation by transforming complex sequence information into patterns derived from physicochemical properties which makes it well-suited for machine learning approaches.

QLC adopts the composition–transition–distribution framework [33], which allows it to capture complementary information about residue behavior in the sequence. The composition (C) operator measures the proportion of residues belonging to each physicochemical property group, which provides global information about sequence content. The transition (T) operator quantifies the transition frequency between two adjacent residues, thereby encoding local sequence order and variability information. The distribution (D) operator measures the positional spread of residues within the sequence at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% intervals. Encoding global composition with local transitions and distribution allows the model to effectively detect structural signals and functional motifs embedded in protein sequences while maintaining computational efficiency and interpretability compared to high-dimensional descriptors. For each provided IR protein sequence, the QLC generates a 147D feature vector with the C, T, and D operators contributing 21D, 21D, and 105D features, respectively.

Sequence encoding using protein language models

Protein language models (pLMs) use deep learning architectures, mainly transformers, to encode amino acid sequences into numerical representations by learning contextual relationships between residues. These dense numerical representations are often called embeddings. They are trained on a large-scale amino acid sequence database that learns rich representations, including structural, functional, and evolutionary information. Notably, pLM-based embeddings eradicate the need for computationally expensive multiple sequence alignment (MSA) and manual feature engineering while preserving biologically meaningful information.

In this study, we employed two state-of-the-art pLMs models, including evolutionary scale modeling (ESM) [34] and ProtTrans [35]. Evolutionary scale modeling

Built upon the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers architecture, the ESM model incorporates self-attention mechanisms to learn complex residue–residue interactions and long-range dependencies within protein sequences. The model is trained on a large-scale protein database, allowing it to learn complex contextual patterns associated with structural, functional, and evolutionary information.

Several ESM variants have been developed and trained, differing in training data, architectural complexity, and model size. Newer variants, such as ESM2, have further improved the performance of pLMs through the expansion of training data and architectural complexity. As discussed above, ESM models do not require MSA or homologous sequences, which make them suitable for large-scale protein analysis due to their computational efficiency. In this work, we employed the ESM2 model (esm2_t33_650M_UR50D), which has 650 million parameters and was trained on UniRef50 [36]. Protein sequences are first converted into FASTA format and then provided as input to the pretrained ESM2 model to extract sequence embeddings. To ensure the model incorporates the full sequence length while maintaining computational efficiency, sequences exceeding the maximum input length were chunked into a maximum of 1022 residues using a sliding window strategy with an overlap of 256 residues between consecutive chunks. Each chunk was tokenized using the ESM2 tokenizer and input independently to the ESM2 pretrained model, and residue-level embeddings were obtained from the penultimate layer. To retain only sequence representation, tokens were then excluded from the obtained embeddings. For residues that appeared in multiple overlapping regions, their embeddings were averaged to mitigate boundary effects and guarantee consistency. Finally, a global average pooling strategy was employed to obtain fixed-length 1280D embeddings, which served as the final feature vector for the corresponding protein sequence.

ProtTrans-T5

ProtTrans-T5 (referred to as ProtT5 in this study) is another state-of-the-art pLM built on the architecture of the Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer (T5) [37], in which protein sequences are treated the same as natural language while amino acid residues are represented as tokens. This approach allows the model to analyze amino acid sequence globally rather than considering local patterns. Like the ESM2, ProtTrans-T5 also relies on a self-attention mechanism, which effectively captures long-range dependencies and complex residue-residue interactions within protein sequences.

In this study, the ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 model was employed to obtain sequence embeddings. Specifically, protein sequences were first preprocessed to map unambiguous characters (U, Z, O, B) to X. To handle and process the full-length sequences, sequences exceeding the maximum length were chunked into a length of 512 tokens using a sliding window strategy with an overlap of 128 residues. Each chunk was then processed independently by ProtT5 to obtain per-residue embeddings, and embeddings for those residues that appeared in overlapping regions were averaged. Finally, a global average pooling strategy was adopted to generate a fixed-length 1024D embedding (feature vector) for each protein sequence.

Feature selection using metaheuristic-based approach

FS plays a vital role in high-dimensional feature space by selecting the most informative and non-redundant features by removing redundant and irrelevant features. In bioinformatics, the search space expands exponentially with the number of features, making optimal search computationally infeasible. To address this issue, metaheuristic optimization algorithms are widely utilized due to their ability to effectively explore large and complex search spaces. Among them, an enhanced variant of the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) [38] called the Spatial Bound Whale Optimization Algorithm (SBWOA) [39] has gained popularity. The WOA emulates the bubble-net hunting approach of humpback whales. Implementation of deep BiGRU

The Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (BiGRU) is a type of RNN designed to handle sequential data efficiently. It is an extension of the standard gated recurrent unit (GRU) [40]. In contrast to conventional GRUs, which process sequence data in one direction, BiGRUs process the input sequences in both forward and backward directions, allowing the model to capture dependencies bidirectionally. This mechanism of the BiGRU is particularly useful for tasks including protein sequences, natural language processing, or temporal data, where information from prior and subsequent states provides valuable context.

A GRU employs the update and reset gates to control the information flow through the network. Specifically, the update gate controls how much information from the previous state is incorporated into the present state. A larger value of the update gate enables more past state information to be retained. Similarly, the reset gate of the GRU controls the amount of information to be disregarded from the previous state, allowing the model to forget irrelevant information. Architecture of DeepIR-Pred

The architecture of the proposed DeepIR-Pred is illustrated in Fig. 2. For each provided input IR sequence, DeepIR-Pred first captures raw feature sets by calling the PSSM-RICLBP, QLC, ESM2, and ProtT5 feature extraction schemes (Feature Extraction in Fig. 2). All four extracted feature sets are concatenated in a serial fashion and further sent as an input into the metaheuristic SBWOA FS algorithm to obtain a low-dimensional optimal feature set (Feature Integration and Selection in Fig. 2). Finally, the low-dimensional optimal feature set is obtained for all the sequences in the IR2476 training dataset and sent into the designed BiGRU network to train DeepIR-Pred for the Target IR proteins (Model Training and Performance evaluation in Fig. 2). For prediction and generalizability, each provided IR sequence that needs to be predicted, is transformed into the low-dimensional optimal feature set using the Feature extraction, Feature integration and selection stages developed during model training. Finally, the trained model is then employed to predict the corresponding IR protein. Model evaluation metrics

The predictive performance of DeepIR-Pred was quantified using several widely utilized metrics designed for classification problems. Precisely, Accuracy (Acc), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Sensitivity (Sen), Specificity (Spe), and F1-Score were selected to quantify the performance across multiple conditional and joint distributions of true and predicted outputs. The formal definitions along with the mathematical formulas of these metrics are provided in Supplementary Text S3 of the SI. In addition, the DeepIR-Pred was further assessed using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AU-ROC curve or shortly AUC) and Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUPR curve). Both AUC and AUPR provide a comprehensive and threshold-independent assessment of the model’s predictive performance across IR and non-IR samples.

To ensure that DeepIR-Pred performance is robust and to minimize the bias toward the training dataset, the model was validated on unseen data. In this regard, a stratified k-fold cross-validation (CV) strategy was adopted to maintain the class proportions and reduce the risk of overfitting. Besides, to assess the model’s generalization capability on new unseen data, an independent testing strategy, where the independent test set (IR274) was completely excluded and untouched during the training, was implemented as well.

To enable the reproducibility of the proposed model, the detailed software and hardware configurations, and implementation settings used in this study, are provided in Supplementary Text S4 of the SI.

Conclusion

In this work, a novel and efficient computational-based prediction model, DeepIR-Pred, was developed to effectively solve the problem of distinguishing IR proteins from non-IR proteins. To achieve this, first, a new handcrafted feature extraction method, PSSM-RICLBP, was proposed and employed together with the QLC, ESM2, and ProtT5 to effectively represent the protein samples in numerical representation. These feature extraction methods laid a strong foundation by capturing evolutionary, physicochemical, and deep contextual information in protein samples. Second, all extracted features were integrated to obtain a powerful hybrid feature set, containing all the information captured by individual feature sets. To reduce the dimensionality of the hybrid feature set and obtain a more robust optimal feature set, the SBWOA FS method was utilized. Third, various deep learning models were trained and tested, among which the designed BiGRU model achieved the best performance. Finally, the BiGRU model was used to train the proposed DeepIR-Pred. The robust and generalization capability using 10-fold CV on the IR2476 and independent testing on the IR274 proved that the proposed DeepIR-Pred can be adopted into real-world applications, such as research academia and drug discovery. The outstanding performance of the proposed DeepIR-Pred is due to the rigorous construction of training and independent datasets, the use of SBWOA to obtain a hybrid optimal feature set that includes all the evolutionary, physicochemical, and deep contextual information captured by individual features hidden in protein samples, and the selection of a deep recurrent learning model. The Explainable AI and UMAP visualizations further validated the model’s interpretability and the discriminative capabilities of the learned representations.

We believe that the current DeepIR-Pred model is robust and has robust generalization capability; however, in the future, we will investigate additional feature extraction and classification methods to further enhance the IR prediction. Advanced feature integration and FS methods will be developed to strengthen the optimal feature set. Evaluating the proposed model with more challenging datasets that explicitly include highly homologous receptor tyrosine kinase families and other demanding protein datasets is another key consideration for the future. Additionally, a user-friendly web server is also a consideration for future IR-based prediction models.
