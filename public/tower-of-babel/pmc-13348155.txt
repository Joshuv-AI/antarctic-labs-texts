Association between social isolation, loneliness and the incidence of irritable bowel syndrome: a prospective cohort study in the UK Biobank
Authors: Ming Yao, Xingyuan Zhang, Fang Lei, Tiange Yan, Beining Jin, Jian Wang, Dongsheng Xv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial isolation and loneliness represent significant public health issues in society. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) severely impacts quality of life and requires early prevention. However, the relationship between social isolation, loneliness, and IBS remains uncertain. We aim to investigate the association between social isolation, loneliness, and the incidence of IBS in a large-scale population cohort.MethodsThis prospective cohort study involved 369,653 participants without IBS from the UK Biobank. The genetic risk of IBS was evaluated using polygenic risk scores. The Cox proportional hazards model was employed to analyze the relationship between social isolation, loneliness, and the risk of IBS incidence. We examined whether depression and anxiety mediate the association between social isolation, loneliness, and IBS risk.ResultsAmong the 369,653 participants (mean age: 56.7 ± 8.1 years; 47.7% male), 7,663 individuals were diagnosed with IBS during a median 13.6 years of follow-up. After comprehensive adjustment for socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, health status, and genetic susceptibility, the HR for IBS incidence was 1.156 (1.073, 1.245) among socially isolated participants compared to non-socially isolated participants. The HR for IBS incidence was 1.361 (1.244, 1.490) among lonely participants compared to non-lonely participants. Subgroup analysis showed that, compared to participants without social isolation, the risk of developing IBS in participants without diabetes and those with diabetes under social isolation was 1.133 times (95% CI: 1.050–1.223) and 1.655 times (95% CI: 1.214–2.257) higher, respectively, with the risk being greater in the diabetic group (P for interaction = 0.022).ConclusionSocial isolation and loneliness were associated with an increased risk of developing IBS, although loneliness may have a more significant effect on risk. Participants with diabetes have a higher risk of developing IBS under conditions of social isolation. Depression and anxiety mediated the association between social isolation, loneliness, and the risk of developing IBS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00649-2.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent disorder of gut-brain interaction, defined by recurrent abdominal pain and disruptions in bowel habits, including changes in stool form or frequency, affecting approximately 5% to 10% of the global population [1–3]. As a chronic condition, it severely impairs patients’ quality of life, leading to considerable medical, economic, and social challenges. When both direct and indirect expenses are taken into account, the economic burden of IBS is substantial, estimated at approximately €8 billion in Europe, USD 10 billion in the United States, and about 123 billion yuan in China [4]. Therefore, identifying potential risk factors that contribute to the onset and progression of IBS is crucial for disease prevention.

Within a biopsychosocial framework, the risk of developing IBS is influenced by the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors [5]. Among these, psychological distress is considered a particularly critical factor, with chronic stress induced by social isolation and loneliness activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, raising cortisol levels, and potentially leading to dysregulation of the gut-brain axis, which contributes to the gastrointestinal symptoms characteristic of IBS [6–8]. Additionally, social isolation and loneliness are related to inflammatory responses and alterations in gut microbiota, both of which contribute significantly to the mechanisms of IBS [9, 10]. Thus, psychological factors play a significant role in the pathogenesis of IBS.

With the increasing global focus on mental health, social isolation and loneliness have emerged as major public health issues. The term “social isolation” is used to describe a state of objective solitude or infrequent social interaction. In contrast, the term “loneliness” is employed to describe an emotional state that is characterized by a discrepancy between an individual’s desire for social connections and their actual level of social engagement [11, 12]. Furthermore, loneliness is not necessarily determined by the size of an individual’s social network or the degree of social isolation. Although social isolation and loneliness often occur together, one may experience limited social interaction without feeling lonely, while another may feel lonely despite frequent social interactions. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that 25% of older adults living in the community experience social isolation [13]. Another meta-analysis estimated that loneliness affects 1.8% to 12.0% of young and middle-aged adults in Europe, with prevalence rates ranging from 4.2% to 24.2% among older adults [14]. Previous research has established that social isolation and loneliness are associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus, all-cause mortality, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), Parkinson’s disease, and infection [15–19]. However, no study has evaluated the relationship between social isolation, loneliness and IBS. Additionally, the influence of genetic susceptibility to IBS on the association between social isolation, loneliness, and IBS risk has not been studied.

To tackle the aforementioned gap in understanding, an investigation was undertaken to evaluate the association between social isolation, loneliness, and the incidence of IBS in a study involving more than 360,000 adults from the UK Biobank. Additionally, we examine whether genetic susceptibility to IBS influences the role of social isolation and loneliness in the risk of IBS incidence.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data utilized in this study was obtained from the UK Biobank, which has been extensively detailed in previous publication [20]. Initially, 502,369 participants were enrolled in the baseline surveys. We excluded 132,716 participants for the following reasons: 35,707 participants lacked baseline social isolation or loneliness data; 68,505 participants had inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), coeliac disease, cancer, and IBS at baseline; and 28,504 participants failed genetic data quality control. Ultimately, 369,653 individuals were included in our analysis. The criteria for inclusion and exclusion are outlined in detail in Fig. 1. Detailed descriptions of the study design and participants are provided in Supplementary Text 1.

Assessment of exposure

The assessment of social isolation in the UK Biobank involved responding to three questions: [1] “How often do you visit friends or family or have them visit you?”; [2] “Which of the following (leisure or social activities) do you engage in once a week or more often?”; and [3] “Including yourself, how many people live in your household?”. Responses to these three questions were summed to produce a scale of 0 to 3. Participants with scores of 0 or 1 were classified as not socially isolated, while scores of 2 or 3 identified them as socially isolated [15, 21, 22]. Two questions were used to measure loneliness: “Do you often feel lonely?” and “How often are you able to confide in someone close to you?”. A value of one point was attributed to each question. Responses to these two questions were summed to produce a scale of 0 to 2. Scores of 0 or 1 classify individuals as “not lonely”, while a score of 2 identifies individuals as “lonely” [22]. Comparable questions can be found in scales like the revised UCLA Loneliness Scale [23]. Comprehensive details are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Assessment of outcome

The assessment of exposures was conducted at baseline between 2006 and 2010. As of this analysis, the latest follow-up in the UK Biobank was conducted in October 2022, resulting in a follow-up duration ranging from 12 to 16 years, with a median of 13.6 years. The outcome of IBS was determined using the ICD-10 code K58 (Supplemental Table 2). The UK Biobank provides a summary of the dates of first occurrences for different health outcomes, aligned with ICD-10 codes, using regularly updated data from primary care records, hospital inpatient records, death registries, and self-reported medical conditions. The identical definition method for incident IBS has been utilized in prior studies conducted within the UK Biobank [24]. Participants were censored based on whichever occurred first: IBS onset, death, or the final follow-up date.

Genetic risk score for IBS

The UK Biobank’s quality control and genotyping methods have been detailed previously [25]. A polygenic risk score (PRS) for IBS was constructed by obtaining heterozygous genotype data and selecting six IBS-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [26]. The weight for IBS PRS was calculated with the formula: PRS = (β1*SNP1 + β2*SNP2 + + β6*SNP6). Supplemental Table 3 provides information on the six SNPs that were selected. Participants were assigned to “low-risk” and “high-risk” groups based on their PRS, which were sorted from high to low. The median score served as the threshold for this division.

Assessment of covariates

In selecting covariates, factors related to social isolation, loneliness, and IBS were considered, while also considering data availability at baseline. These covariates included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), enrollment center, education level, smoking status, Townsend deprivation index category, alcohol drinking, PRS for IBS, ethnicity, the first 10 principal components of ancestry, healthy diet, sleep, physical activity, regularly taking pain-relief medications and/or gastrointestinal agents, CVD history, diabetes history, depression history and anxiety history. Detailed information is available in Supplemental Tables 2, 4 and 5, as well as Supplemental Text 2.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized by levels of social isolation and loneliness, with continuous variables shown as mean ± SD and categorical variables reported as frequencies and percentages. Proportions of participants with missing covariates who were included in our analysis are presented in Supplemental Table 6. Multiple imputations were carried out to address potential biases from missing data, employing the “mice” package in R [27]. We conducted Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to evaluate the associations between social isolation, loneliness, and the incidence of IBS. The follow-up period began from baseline to the date of first diagnosis of IBS, or censored at the end of study (October 31, 2022), the date of death or the lost to-follow-up for participants. We constructed four models: model 1 adjusted for age sex, and ethnicity; model 2 additionally adjusted for BMI category, Townsend deprivation index category, education level, enrollment center, smoking status, alcohol drinking, sleep category, PA category, healthy diet; model 3 additionally adjusted for CVD history, diabetes history, pain relief medication, gastrointestinal agents; and model 4 additionally adjusted for genetic risk, first 10 principal components of ancestry. Furthermore, to estimate the fraction of occurrences that would have been prevented by eliminating the exposure, we calculated the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF). Additionally, we performed supplementary analyses using Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves to depict the survival probability distributions of IBS across groups and log-rank tests for comparison. We also compared the baseline characteristics of participants who developed IBS to those who did not. Finally, we analyzed the associations between social isolation, loneliness, and genetic susceptibility to IBS, and their combined impact on the risk of incident IBS. We utilized the “Regmedint” package to perform a regression-based causal mediation analysis [28], assessing both the direct effects of social isolation and loneliness on IBS risk and the indirect effects through depression and anxiety.

To investigate if the relationship between social isolation, loneliness, and IBS differed across subpopulations, we examined potential interactions with age (< 65/≥ 65 years), sex (male; female), ethnicity (White; non-White), smoking (non-smoking; smoking), BMI (< 30/≥ 30 kg/m2), drinking (non-drinking; drinking) diabetes (yes/no), and CVD (yes/no). We used the likelihood ratio test to assess the statistical significance of these interactions.

Five sensitivity analyses were performed to validate the robustness of our findings. First, to address concerns about reverse causation, we excluded events that occurred within 2 years of baseline. Second, to mitigate potential biases and improve the precision of the model, we further adjusted the depression history and anxiety history. Third, the analysis was confined to those participants who provided complete covariate data to address potential biases. Fourth, we employed the Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazards model to avoid bias resulting from competing risks, treating death as a competing risk for incident IBS. Fifth, social isolation and loneliness were included in the same model to allow for mutual adjustment between the two factors.

The proportional hazard assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and no major violations were identified. The statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level of P < 0.05, and conducted using R software (version 4.0.5).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 369,653 participants involved in this study, stratified by social isolation and loneliness. Among these, 33,434 (9.0%) participants were categorized as socially isolated, and 17,513 (4.7%) as lonely. The mean age was 56.7 (SD, 8.1) years, the mean BMI was 27.4 (SD, 4.8) kg/m2, and 47.7% were male. Participants who experienced social isolation or loneliness showed a greater likelihood of being men, non-white, obese, having a lower education level, being current smokers, being infrequent drinkers, experiencing higher levels of material deprivation, adhering to poor diets, engaging in less physical activity, regularly taking pain relief medications and gastrointestinal agents, and having a higher prevalence of comorbidities (CVD, diabetes, depression, and anxiety). During a median follow-up period of 13.6 years, 7,663 participants were diagnosed with IBS. We summarized the baseline characteristics of 369,653 participants based on whether they developed IBS during the follow-up period (Supplemental Table 7).

Associations of social isolation and loneliness with IBS incidence risk

Table 2 shows that, after adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity, socially isolated participants had a higher risk of developing IBS compared to their non-isolated counterparts (HR: 1.264; 95% CI: 1.176, 1.360). Following additional adjustments for socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, health conditions, and genetic risk, the association was reduced to an HR of 1.156 (95% CI: 1.073, 1.245). Similarly, loneliness showed an HR of 1.544 (95% CI: 1.412, 1.689) for incident IBS after adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity; however, this risk was reduced to an HR of 1.361 (95% CI: 1.244, 1.490) after further adjustments for socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, health conditions, and genetic risk. PAF analysis indicated that 1.39% and 1.68% of incident IBS cases during the follow-up could have been prevented if there had been no social isolation or loneliness, respectively. Compared to individuals who were neither socially isolated nor lonely, those experiencing both conditions had a 43.80% increased risk of IBS (HR:1.438; 95% CI: 1.207, 1.713). As levels of social isolation and loneliness increased, the risk of developing IBS also increased significantly (P for trend < 0.001). These findings were consistent with the KM plots (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and 3). In the mediation analysis, we identified depression and anxiety as significant mediators in the relationship between social isolation, loneliness, and the risk of IBS. Specifically, social isolation mediated the risk of IBS through depression (17.21%) and anxiety (5.35%); loneliness did so through depression (25.61%) and anxiety (8.85%) (Fig. 2).

Supplemental Table 8 presents HRs for the incidence of IBS related to specific items of social isolation and loneliness. Infrequent contact with family/friends/groups was associated with a 5.4% increased risk of IBS incidence (Model 4; HR: 1.054; 95% CI: 1.005, 1.105). Similarly, living alone was associated with an 8.8% increased risk of IBS incidence (Model 4; HR: 1.088; 95% CI: 1.027, 1.152), and feeling lonely was associated with a 42.8% increased risk of IBS incidence (Model 4; HR: 1.428; 95% CI: 1.356, 1.504). However, being able to confide did not maintain a significant association with the risk of IBS incidence (Model 4; HR: 1.065; 95% CI: 0.998, 1.135). Furthermore, the PAF analysis identified feeling lonely as the greatest contributor to IBS risk (7.14%), followed by infrequent contact (1.86%) and living alone (1.56%).

No interaction effect between social isolation and loneliness on the risk of developing IBS was observed (P interaction = 0.079, likelihood ratio test). Stratified by social isolation status, loneliness was significantly associated with increased IBS risk in both socially isolated and non-socially isolated groups compared to those without loneliness (non-socially isolated: HR, 1.365; 95% CI: 1.231,1.514; socially isolated: HR, 1.246; 95% CI: 1.030, 1.507) (Table 3). Stratified by loneliness status, social isolation significantly increased IBS risk in the non-loneliness group (HR 1.139, 95% CI: 1.051, 1.234), but no significant association was found in the loneliness group (Supplemental Table 9).

Genetic contributions to social isolation, loneliness, and IBS

Supplementary Table 10 shows that individuals at high genetic risk had a higher risk of developing IBS than those at low genetic risk (HR: 1.078, 95% CI: 1.030, 1.128). Notably, this risk remains significant after adjusting for social isolation and loneliness (HR: 1.078, 95% CI: 1.030, 1.128), indicating that genetic predisposition to IBS is independent of these factors. Supplemental Tables 11–12 reveal that neither social isolation (P interaction = 0.376) nor loneliness (P interaction = 0.385) significantly interacts with genetic risk in influencing IBS incidence. Both joint and stratified analyses showed that, in individuals with low genetic risk, social isolation did not significantly affect IBS risk, while loneliness remained significantly associated with IBS across all genetic risk categories.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted stratified subgroup analyses based on age, sex, ethnicity, smoking, drinking, BMI, CVD, and diabetes. Individuals aged under 65 years, male, White, obese, smokers, non-drinkers, with CVD, and those with diabetes, demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of IBS incidence if they were socially isolated compared to those who were not socially isolated. Furthermore, diabetes and social isolation showed a significant interaction concerning IBS incidence (P interaction = 0.022), while no significant interaction was detected between diabetes and loneliness (P interaction = 0.147). Individuals under 65 years, male, non-White, normal weight, smokers, non-drinkers, without CVD, and those with diabetes, demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of IBS incidence if they reported feelings of loneliness, in contrast to those who did not experience loneliness (Supplemental Figs. 4–5).

Sensitivity analysis

To ascertain the consistency of the primary results, we carried out five sensitivity analyses. All sensitivity analyses showed results similar to the main study (Supplemental Tables 13–17).

Discussion

This large-scale prospective cohort study, involving over 360,000 participants and with a median follow-up of 13.6 years, revealed a significant association between social isolation, loneliness, and the risk of incident IBS, even after adjusting for potential confounders. Notably, loneliness had a more pronounced effect on IBS risk. These associations were found to be independent of genetic predisposition to IBS. Furthermore, when we assessed the combined effect of social isolation and loneliness on the risk of incident IBS, we found that individuals experiencing both social isolation and loneliness had a 43.8% higher risk of IBS incidence compared to those who were neither socially isolated nor lonely. In addition, our comprehensive analysis of different components of social isolation and loneliness showed that infrequent contact with family/friends/social groups, living alone, and feeling lonely were associated with the occurrence of IBS.

This is the first study, to our knowledge, that has examined the associations between social isolation, loneliness, and the onset of IBS. However, the current evidence for this association is limited. In 2023, a study involving 302,839 British individuals from the UK Biobank demonstrated that combined sleep behaviors and mental health, encompassing psychological distress and neuroticism, are associated with the incidence of IBS. Specifically, lower levels of psychological distress and neuroticism were correlated with a reduced risk of IBS [29]. Previous research found that negative interactions and low support were associated with more severe IBS symptoms among 235 patients with IBS [30]. In a cross-sectional study conducted in 2013, involving 1,024 participants from Iran, it was observed that individuals diagnosed with IBS experienced a higher frequency of stressful life events, higher perception of the intensity of stressors, and less social support [31]. Given the known relationships between social isolation, loneliness, and psychological as well as psychiatric disorders [32], our findings are consistent with existing research.

In addition, several intriguing findings emerged from our study. Firstly, in our combined analysis of the impact of social isolation and loneliness on the risk of IBS incidence, we observed that individuals experiencing social isolation without loneliness had a 13.9% higher risk of developing IBS compared to those experiencing neither isolation nor loneliness. Likewise, individuals experiencing loneliness without social isolation had a 36.3% higher risk of developing IBS compared to those experiencing neither isolation nor loneliness. Secondly, stratified analysis results indicate that, compared to participants who experienced no loneliness, experiencing loneliness increased the risk of developing IBS in both non-socially isolated and socially isolated groups. In contrast, social isolation significantly increased the risk of developing IBS only among participants who did not feel lonely. Besides, subgroup analyses showed that individuals reporting loneliness had a significantly elevated risk of IBS relative to those who were free of loneliness. Consistently, prior research has reported that subjective loneliness exerts a greater impact on events such as heart failure, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and mental disorders compared to objective social isolation [15, 21, 33]. Regardless of one’s objective social connections and the duration of social isolation from others, loneliness may still exist [11, 33]. Due to its common occurrence in individuals who are hostile or have tense social relationships and its bridging of the relationship between social isolation and depression, loneliness may represent a stronger psychological stressor [33–35]. Furthermore, irrespective of social isolation itself, individuals experiencing loneliness may perceive the world as more threatening, experience daily events as more stressful, and feel more vulnerable [36]. Thus, these findings suggest that subjective loneliness may be a more prominent risk factor for IBS compared to objective social isolation.

In the subgroup analysis of diabetes, a significant interaction between diabetes and social isolation on the risk of IBS was observed, with the risk of IBS being higher in diabetic patients. This may be due to the fact that diabetic patients are typically in a prolonged state of stress, which could impair the regulatory function of the immune and endocrine systems. Additionally, changes in immune function and the gut microbiome may exacerbate the risk of IBS through mechanisms such as chronic inflammation and impaired gut barrier function [37–39]. Furthermore, social isolation is a risk factor for the development of type 2 diabetes [15]. Therefore, diabetic patients, when facing social isolation, may experience a compounded effect due to their chronic disease status and the associated impact of social isolation, making their gut health more vulnerable and thus increasing the risk of IBS. Future research should further explore the specific mechanisms through which social isolation affects gut health in diabetic patients, particularly how it influences IBS occurrence through immune inflammation, gut barrier dysfunction, and changes in the microbiome. This will provide new insights for comprehensive interventions and management strategies for diabetic patients.

The physiological mechanisms linking social isolation and loneliness with IBS remain poorly understood. We propose the following hypotheses: firstly, social isolation and loneliness may be associated with increased activity in the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system [7], which could stimulate pro-inflammatory immune responses and oxidative stress [9]. These physiological changes may contribute to the development of IBS [40, 41]. Secondly, the current research indicates that dysregulation of the brain-gut axis may be a potential pathophysiological basis for the comorbidity of IBS with psychosocial factors [42]. This axis regulation is bidirectional, involving multiple systems, including the nervous, immune, and circulatory systems. It is accompanied by the production and release of various hormones, bacterial products, immune factors, and neurotransmitters. Under normal circumstances, it maintains a dynamic balance; however, once disrupted, it may lead to gastrointestinal disorders [43]. However, further research is needed to validate these potential mechanisms underlying their association.

In terms of clinical management, prevailing consensus reports focus predominantly on the diagnosis and treatment of IBS, with relatively little emphasis on preventive measures [44–46]. While psychological therapy is recognized as an effective intervention for the management of IBS [47], its preventive application may be limited in scalability, given the high prevalence of loneliness and social isolation. A more realistic approach could involve prioritizing broader and more feasible strategies, such as encouraging participation in group-based activities, and improving access to social and recreational resources. Furthermore, studies highlight that many IBS patients lack adequate knowledge about their condition and hold many misconceptions about the disease [48, 49]. To effectively manage IBS, physicians should confidently diagnose and clearly explain the symptom triggers and treatment strategies to patients [50, 51]. Additionally, physicians, as primary points of contact within the healthcare system, should leverage this role to provide comprehensive health education and psychological support to patients [52].

From a public health prevention perspective, addressing the challenges posed by social isolation and loneliness is paramount [53]. When developing strategies for the prevention of IBS, it is essential to focus on the socio-psychological aspects. This can involve initiatives such as fostering community development, encouraging social interactions, and offering psychological support [54, 55]. In addition, it is crucial to strengthen the mental health service system, and improve its coverage and quality, to enhance the efficiency of healthcare resource utilization and satisfy the needs of IBS patients [56]. In conclusion, recognizing the impact of psychosocial factors is vital for managing IBS, providing a reference for future prevention and treatment strategies.

This study exhibits some strengths. Firstly, it features a substantial sample size, a population-based prospective cohort, extensive follow-up, and comprehensive consideration of covariates. Notably, it is the first study to examine the impact of both social isolation and loneliness on developing IBS, with findings further validated by sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Secondly, by examining data from two dimensions (social isolation as an objective indicator and loneliness as a subjective assessment), this study enhances our understanding of their association with the risk of IBS, providing a more comprehensive and scientifically grounded insight into health risk assessment. Thirdly, it explores both the separate and combined associations of social isolation, loneliness, and genetic risk with IBS events, facilitating the assessment of gene-environment interactions. However, this study also has several limitations. Firstly, in exploring their associations in this study, exposure factors were assessed only by single measurements, which may overlook potential changes in these characteristics over time. Nevertheless, given the considerable stability of these traits across an individual’s lifespan, as indicated by a meta-analysis, their impact on the final results is likely minimal [57]. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal assessments of these exposures. Secondly, the measurement tool for social isolation may not fully capture individuals’ perceived isolation, as it does not account for personal needs or preferences. For instance, some individuals may meet with friends but still feel isolated, while those living in shared accommodations may feel isolated despite daily interactions with others. Additionally, the UK Biobank’s assessment of social isolation primarily focuses on face-to-face interactions, overlooking the complexity of virtual connections and the multidimensional nature of social relationships. Moreover, individual differences in temperament and introversion/extroversion may moderate the impact of social isolation and loneliness, yet such information was not available in the UK Biobank data, and future studies are warranted to further explore this aspect. Thirdly, the identification of incident IBS solely using the ICD-10 code K58 without applying the Rome III diagnostic criteria based on symptoms might be considered a limitation. However, since most incident IBS cases were verified through dependable clinical diagnostic data from primary care, hospital inpatient records, or death records, the outcome ascertainment process remains reliable. Additionally, compared to the updated Rome IV IBS diagnostic criteria, the utility of the Rome III criteria is limited due to their excessive sensitivity, potentially inflating the prevalence of IBS [58]. Fourthly, the study was conducted among a general population in the UK with predominantly white ethnicity, which raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings to other ethnicities or populations. To validate and extend our results, future long-term follow-up studies involving diverse ethnic cohorts are required. Lastly, being an observational study, it cannot establish a direct causal association between social isolation, loneliness, and the risk of developing IBS.

Conclusions

Our findings align with the biopsychosocial model of IBS, suggesting that psychosocial factors such as social isolation and loneliness may play a plausible role in IBS development. These results underscore the importance of integrating psychosocial determinants into the understanding of IBS aetiology, and highlight the need for future studies to clarify the biological and psychological mechanisms underlying these associations.
