Algebraic power scaling in a slowly-quenched bosonic quantum battery
Donny Dwiputra, Ahmad R. T. Nugraha, Sasfan A. Wella, Freddy P. Zen

Abstract
Bosonic modes provide a promising platform for quantum batteries as a result of their unbounded energy spectrum. However, the energy that can be stored during a coherent charging process is limited due to coherent oscillations between the charger and battery. In this Letter, we show that by introducing a slow quench in the interaction between a coherently driven quadratic oscillator battery and a charger system, the maximum battery power ( P B , m P_{B,m} ) scales algebraically with the quench duration ( τ Q \tau_{Q} ), i.e., P B , m ∝ τ Q α P_{B,m}\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha} , where 0 < α ≤ 2 0<\alpha\leq 2 is a function of the quench ramp exponent. This finding implies that, counterintuitively, slower quenches lead to faster charging. Such a quench suppresses coherent energy oscillations between the battery and the charger, allowing an unbounded increase in power. Furthermore, we discuss the effect of charger dissipation, which imposes a finite limit on the maximum power. We also show that the temporal extensive scaling occurs in a broader context by mapping the system to a coherently driven Tavis-Cummings battery.

Model.
—We model the charging process between the resonant charger
A
and the battery
B
as a nonequilibrium interaction within a quench duration
\tau_{Q}
, as in Fig.
1
for
r=1
. We consider the interaction picture Hamiltonian
H(t)
(with a natural unit
\hbar=1
) as follows,

H(t)=g(t)\left(ab^{\dagger}+a^{\dagger}b\right)+F(a+a^{\dagger})

(1)

where
a,b
(
a^{\dagger},b^{\dagger})
are the bosonic annihilation (creation) operators of the A and B subsystems, respectively. Here,
F
is the external driving amplitude applied to the charger only, whereas the quenched coupling interaction
g(t)
with maximum strength
{g_{f}}
and exponent
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is defined as

g(t)=\begin{cases}{g_{f}}(t/\tau_{Q})^{r},&0\leq t\leq\tau_{Q},\\
{g_{f}},&t>\tau_{Q}.\end{cases}

(2)

To compare with the case where the coupling is turned “on/off” immediately, we can keep the coupling in its maximum value after
\tau_{Q}
. At time
t=0
, both charger and battery are in the ground state
|\psi(0)\rangle_{AB}=|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}
, where
|0\rangle_{A,B}
is the respective ground state of
H_{A,B}
with
H_{A}=\omega_{0}a^{\dagger}a
and
H_{B}=\omega_{0}b^{\dagger}b
. Note that since
[ab^{\dagger}+a^{\dagger}b,H_{A}+H_{B}]=0
, the only source of energy injected into the charger is external driving.

We also incorporate the environmental effect where the charger is subject to dissipation, e.g., a leaky cavity. The effective dynamics is described by the following Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
,

\dot{\rho}_{AB}=\mathcal{L}\rho_{AB}=-i[H(t),\rho_{AB}]+\gamma\mathcal{D}[a]\rho_{AB},

(3)

where
\mathcal{D}[A]\rho=A\rho A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}A,\rho\}
is the GKSL dissipator. To calculate the Heisenberg equation of motion of an operator
A(t)
, one considers the adjoint equation
\dot{A}=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}A
, by the cyclicity of trace in
\langle A(t)\rangle={\text{tr}{[}}A(0)e^{\mathcal{L}t}\rho(0)]={\text{tr}{[}}e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A(0)\rho(0)]
. Note that the quantum jump term in Eq. (
3
) is irrelevant for our quadratic problem. Evidently, the model can be described by a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
Ashida et al. 2020
,

H_{NH}(t)=H(t)-i\frac{\gamma}{2}a^{\dagger}a.

(4)

Compared to using the full Liouvillian in Eq. (
3
), the computational time will be reduced quadratically. Both formalisms result in the same equations of motion, Eq. (
6
).

Charging dynamics and temporal extensivity
—We start with the constant coupling case,
r=0
, and a dissipationless charger,
\gamma=0
. The energy stored in the battery is
E_{B}(t)={\text{tr}{[}}H_{B}\rho(t)]
and the average power is
P_{B}(t)=E_{B}(t)/t
. The energy stored over time
t
reads
Farina et al. 2019

E^{(r=0)}_{B}(t)=4\frac{\omega_{0}F^{2}}{{g_{f}}^{2}}\sin^{4}\left(\frac{{g_{f}}t}{2}\right)

(5)

and thus the maximum energy is
E^{(r=0)}_{B,m}=4\omega_{0}F^{2}/{g_{f}}^{2}
. We define
t_{\text{m}}
as the shortest time to reach the first maximum of
E_{B}
, which in this case is
t^{(r=0)}_{\text{m}}=\pi/{g_{f}}
. This leads to the maximum power of
P^{(r=0)}_{B,m}=4\omega_{0}F^{2}/({g_{f}}\pi)
, that is, independent of
\tau_{Q}
. The constant coupling model
r=0
therefore does not show any advantage over a longer quench duration
\tau_{Q}
.

Now, we focus on the continuous quench with
r>0
. For a fast quench,
\tau_{Q}\lessapprox\pi{g_{f}}^{-1}
, the quench is too fast so that the first maximum of Eq. (
8
) is not yet reached. In this case
E_{B,m}
has the same value as in Eq. (
5
). Meanwhile, for a slow enough quench,
\tau_{Q}\gg{g_{f}}^{-1}
, the equations of motion for the first momenta are

\langle\dot{a}\rangle=-i\left(g(t)\langle b\rangle+F\right)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\langle a\rangle,\quad\langle\dot{b}\rangle=-ig(t)\langle a\rangle,

(6)

which can be decoupled for the battery part, for
t\leq\tau_{Q}
,

t\langle\ddot{b}\rangle+\left(\frac{\gamma t}{2}-r\right)\langle\dot{b}\rangle+k^{2}t^{2r+1}\langle b\rangle=-kt^{r+1}F,

(7)

where
k={g_{f}}/\tau_{Q}^{r}
. For a closed system,
\gamma=0
, this is a linear nonhomogeneous Emden-Fowler equation
Wong 1975
. The homogeneous solution is proportional to
\exp\left(\pm i\frac{\theta(t)}{1+r}\right)
where
\theta(t)=kt^{1+r}
. By implying the initial condition
|\psi(0)\rangle_{AB}
, and using the fact that the ground state expectation value of (normal-ordered) higher moments of the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
4
) can be described semiclassically, i.e.
\langle b^{\dagger}b\rangle_{0}=\langle b^{\dagger}\rangle_{0}\langle b\rangle_{0}

Farina et al. 2019
, we can obtain
E_{B}
via the standard Green’s function method to solve Eq. (
7
). The stored energy is thus as follows,

E_{B}(t)=\omega_{0}\left(\frac{Ft}{1+r}\right)^{2}\text{Re}\left[e^{i\frac{\theta(t)}{(1+r)}}\text{E}_{\alpha}\left(i\frac{\theta(t)}{1+r}\right)\right]^{2},

(8)

where
\text{E}_{\alpha}(z)=\int_{1}^{\infty}u^{-\alpha}e^{zu}\,du
denotes the generalized exponential integral
\alpha=r/(r+1)
appearing in the analysis of Emden-Fowler equations
Wong 1975
. The maximum stored energy is achieved at a specific value of
\theta(t_{m})\equiv\theta_{\text{m}}
. Therefore, since
t_{m}=(\theta_{m}/k)^{1/(1+r)}
, we can write
E_{B,m}\propto(\theta_{m}k)^{2/(1+r)}
. This reveals the algebraic temporal extensivity of the stored energy and power,

E_{B,m}\propto\tau_{Q}^{2\alpha},\quad P_{B,m}=\frac{E_{B,m}}{t_{m}}\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha},

(9)

which is the main result of this Letter. For
r\in[0,\infty)
, the scaling exponent is bounded by
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

The maximum value of the stored energy can be determined by calculating the time
t_{m}
to reach it. As an example, in a linear quench (
r=1,\alpha=1/2
), we can find the specific
\theta_{m}=kt_{m}^{1+r}
by numerically solving
dE_{B}/dt=0
. That is, by solving

\frac{\text{C}\big(\sqrt{\theta_{m}/\pi}\big)^{2}-\text{S}\big(\sqrt{\theta_{m}/\pi}\big)^{2}}{2\text{C}\big(\sqrt{\theta_{m}/\pi}\big)\text{S}\big(\sqrt{\theta_{m}/\pi}\big)}=\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{m}^{2}}{2}\right),

(10)

where
\text{C}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\cos(\pi u^{2}/2)\,du
and
\text{S}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\sin(\pi u^{2}/2)\,du
are the Fresnel integrals. In this case, we get
\theta_{m}\approx 2.14
,
E_{B,m}^{(r=1)}\approx\frac{0.9\pi\omega_{0}F^{2}}{{g_{f}}}\tau_{Q}
, and
P_{B,m}^{(r=1)}\propto\tau_{Q}^{1/2}
. Figure 2 depicts the dynamics of the stored energy
E_{B}(t)
[Fig. (2)a)] and the scaling of the maximum power
P_{B,m}
[Fig. 2(b)]. In Fig. 2(a), we observe that while a fast quench (small

5

P_{B,m}\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha}
, evidenced by the linear slope in the log-log plot, where the exponent

approaches unity for large quench exponents

The reason of why slow interaction quench leads to algebraic energy scaling in a closed system can be understood from Eq. (
6
). In the decoupled case, where
g(t)=0
, the energy of the charger
E_{A}
grows as
\sim F^{2}t^{2}
. If the charger is immediately connected to the battery with constant coupling,
E_{B}
loses its quadratic growth due to coherent energy oscillations between the charger and the battery. However, under a slowly quenched coupling, the battery retains quadratic growth as in Eq. (
8
) up to a certain time;
E_{A}
also grows similarly. In this case, once the coherent oscillation starts to dominate the dynamics, i.e. the exponential integral dominates, and
E_{B}
reaches its maximum value at
t_{\text{m}}\sim\tau_{Q}^{\alpha}
. This results in a monotonic increase of
P_{B,m}
with exponent
\alpha
. The fact that the energy of the decoupled charger grows quadratically, resulting in linear power growth, is exactly why
\alpha
has an upper bound of
1
. Hence, we conclude that continuous quench may suppress coherent energy oscillations so that coherent driving pumps the charger and the battery more effectively.

Effect of charger dissipation.
—In the presence of dissipation
\gamma\neq 0
, the exact solution of Eq. (
7
) is unknown. However, analytical solution is available for
r\to\infty\,(\alpha=1)
. In this case, the coupling can be replaced by a step function
g(t)={g_{f}}\,\theta(t-\tau_{Q})
. Thus, for
t\in[0,\tau_{Q})
the charger is effectively decoupled from the battery and its stored energy reads

\displaystyle E_{A}^{(r\to\infty)}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\frac{4F^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma t/2}-1\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}F^{2}t^{2}+O(t^{3}),

(11)

which also grows quadratically at initial times, as in the disipationless case. The battery reaches its maximum at
t_{\text{m}}=\tau_{Q}

P_{B,m}^{(r\to\infty)}\sim\frac{1}{\tau_{Q}\gamma^{2}}\left(e^{-\gamma\tau_{Q}/2}-1\right)^{2},

(12)

Mapping to a driven Tavis-Cummings battery.
—Here we show that the model can be mapped into the finite Tavis-Cummings model with a coherent drive. This is achieved by applying the Holstein-Primakoff transformation exclusively to the battery operators,
S^{+}=b^{{\dagger}}\,\sqrt{2s-b^{\dagger}b}
and
S^{-}=\sqrt{2s-b^{\dagger}b}\,b
, which can be approximated as
S^{\mp}\approx\sqrt{2s}\,b^{({\dagger})}
in the large spin
s
limit. Here,
s
is the collective spin quantum number, such that the Hilbert space dimension is
2s+1
. Hence, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) becomes

H(t)=\frac{g(t)}{\sqrt{2s}}(S^{+}a+S^{-}a)+F(a+a^{\dagger}),

(14)

which is the coherently driven Tavis-Cummings quantum battery
Lu et al. 2021
;
Quach et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Canzio et al. 2025
;
Hymas et al. 2025
, or the Dicke model
Dicke 1954
with rotating-wave approximation and a coherent driving. The transformation shows that the temporal power law scaling due to slow quenching may also occur in a quantum battery model with super-extensive power scaling (over the number of battery degree of freedom
S
). Interestingly, as a result of this mapping, the quenched coupling
g(t)
is related to the nonequilibrium superradiance phase transition
Bastidas et al. 2012
;
Jara Jr and Cosme 2024
; crossing such quantum criticality in finite speed implies the excitation of topological defects according to the Kibble-Zurek mechanism
Kibble 1976
;
Kibble 1980
;
Zurek 1985
;
Zurek 1996
;
del Campo and Zurek 2014
;
Dziarmaga 2010
. Here, the formation of defects is intrinsically linked to the non-adiabatic crossing of a quantum critical point, a feature which does not exist in the bosonic battery-charger model. However, our results show no sensitivity over a specific critical values of the quenched coupling
{g_{f}}
. The physical meaning of the equivalence between both models needs further scrutiny, in the sense of superradiance phase transition.

Possible experimental realizations.
—Experimental implementations of our bosonic charger-battery model should appear naturally in cavity and circuit QED architectures, where linearly coupled harmonic modes and Tavis-Cummings interactions are routinely realized
Quach et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
. In particular, superconducting circuits offer an ideal platform, where fast tunable couplers
Bialczak et al. 2011
;
Elghaayda et al. 2024
enable the precise engineering of the continuous time-dependent interaction profile
g(t)
required to observe algebraic scaling. Moreover, recent experiments with organic microcavities have already demonstrated collective charging and superextensive power output
Quach et al. 2022
;
Hymas et al. 2025
, suggesting that the necessary control over light–matter coupling is within reach. By extending these slow-quench protocols to superconducting or cold-atom systems
Quach et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
, one could directly test our predictions for temporal extensivity and experimentally verify the dissipation-limited optimal quench time.

Conclusions.
—We have demonstrated that a slow-quench charging protocol in a coherently driven oscillator battery leads to an algebraic scaling of the charging power with the quench duration,
P_{B,m}\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha}
. This temporal extensivity allows the battery to overcome the energy limitations imposed by coherent Rabi oscillations in standard constant-coupling schemes. Our results suggest an optimal driving protocol where the interaction is turned off at
t_{m}
to store the maximum energy. Although higher quench exponents
r
yield better scaling and higher power, they induce larger energy fluctuations and require precise timing near
\tau_{Q}
due to the sharp peak. Furthermore, the inclusion of dissipation reveals a finite optimal quench duration, providing a practical bound for experimental implementations. Finally, the mapping to the Tavis-Cummings model suggests that this algebraic scaling is a universal feature shared with critical many-body systems dynamics and superradiant systems.
