ENCOURAGEing Start: A Quasi‐Experimental Trial on Pre‐Op Physical Activity Intervention for Bariatric Surgery Candidates
Authors: Alex Edye‐Mazowita, Wenjing He, Todd A. Duhamel, Krista Hardy, Ashley Vergis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery recommends pre‐ and post‐operative physical activity. However, there are no surgery‐specific physical activity guidelines or standardized programs tailored for individuals preparing for metabolic and bariatric surgery, including programs that integrate structured behavior change approaches. A novel pre‐operative physical activity intervention, ENCOURAGEing Start, was developed and piloted to assist patients in reducing barriers to physical activity and encouraging positive physical activity habits.MethodsThis single arm pilot study recruited patients (n = 27, BMI = 47.1 ± 6.2 kg/m2) from the Victoria General Hospital's Centre for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery in Winnipeg, Manitoba between September 2017 and May 2018. A time‐series quasi‐experimental design was used. The 16‐week intervention took place at the University of Manitoba's Active Living Centre. The primary outcome was a six‐minute walk test, a measure of functional ability and predictive of post‐surgical weight loss and maintenance. Secondary outcomes included fitness, cardiovascular disease risk, and mental health. Outcomes were evaluated at baseline, 8 weeks, and 16 weeks.ResultsTen patients completed the intervention. Statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in the six‐minute walk test (457.21 vs. 497.58 m, p = 0.002), indirect 1RM leg press (128.7 vs. 150.4 kg, p < 0.001), 30 second chair stand test (12.78 vs. 14.82 repetitions, p < 0.01), and Laval quality of life (181.89/308 vs. 195.96/308, p = 0.028) were identified for completers.ConclusionsThe ENCOURAGEing Start intervention enhanced markers linked to long‐term post‐surgery outcomes, including a 10.7% improvement in the six‐minute walk test. This feasible program showed a similar effect size to more resource intensive interventions, including those in Canada. A larger study with a minimum of 55 participants should be undertaken to assess the sustainability of these effects.

Introduction

Strong evidence suggests that preoperative physical activity (PA) improves short‐ and long‐term surgical outcomes for patients undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) [1, 2, 3, 4]. These include higher and sustained weight loss (1), improved quality of life (2), preservation of lean body mass (3), and reduced length of hospital stay (4). While the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgeons considers preoperative health behavioral intervention a standard of care [5], the specific parameters and structure of such a program have not been developed. Due to a lengthy wait time for MBS and the substantial interim healthcare costs of obesity [6], the development of an upstream program to develop patients' physical activity and self‐efficacy is worthwhile. The preparatory phase standard to any MBS program, including consultation with a multidisciplinary team with behavior change education, is an opportune time for teaching physical activity skills.

Most PA interventions for patients preparing to undergo MBS focus on direct supervision during highly structured and prescribed exercise [7, 8] with no integrated behavior change techniques or education beyond the usage of facility‐based equipment. Although many health‐related behavior change techniques are well established [9, 10, 11], it is evident that most PA interventions are not designed with these in mind. Instead, current PA interventions often focus on general weight loss rather than on evidence‐informed programs specifically designed to improve peri‐ and post‐operative outcomes. Notably, according to available literature, at the time of this study there were no pre‐ or post‐operative PA interventions for patients preparing for MBS that integrate behavior change techniques such as social support and comparison [12], barrier identification/problem solving, and environmental restructuring [10]. Furthermore, there is often no social dynamic or peer modeling incorporated into the PA intervention, and participants often work solely one‐on‐one with an exercise professional [13], which may not address all psychosocial factors needed for lasting behavior change, particularly those better supported in a group setting [14, 15]. In addition to these methodological shortcomings, many pre‐operative PA interventions necessitate significant logistical demands such as the cost of one‐on‐one supervision, usage of dedicated research space, or frequency of sessions [7, 8, 16].

As such, an efficient intervention was designed to provide structured bouts of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity PA while also integrating behavior change techniques regularly. It was hypothesized that this comprehensive format would cause significant improvement in measures of physical fitness, quality of life, and symptoms of depression. A pilot project for this intervention was developed in partnership between the University of Manitoba and the Winnipeg Regional Health Authority. The goal was to determine an effect size for the intervention and to calculate the sample size needed to demonstrate a statistically significant change for the primary outcome in a future randomized controlled trial.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Patients were recruited from the Victoria General Hospital's Centre for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (CMBS) which is based in Winnipeg and serves as Manitoba's provincial bariatric center. As such, like many Canadian Bariatric institutions, its catchment area is vast, with a patient community potentially being 1000 km away. The CMBS patient eligibility criteria include age (18–59), BMI (40–55 kg/m2 or 35–39.9 kg.m2 with obesity‐related comorbidities), being mobile and able to perform PA, and previous attempts to lose weight and improve health through diet and exercise. CMBS patients received information about the intervention from CMBS staff during standard preoperative appointments and had the opportunity to complete a Permission to Contact form to self‐select into the intervention. Patients were required to attend sessions in person, which limited participants to those living in Winnipeg or the surrounding area that were willing to travel. An exclusion criterion was body weight (> 158.8 kg) due to manufacturer specifications of exercise equipment. Informed consent was obtained from all the individual participants included in the study.

All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Ethics approval for this study was granted by the University of Manitoba Research Ethics Board (reference #HS19982; H2016:283) and the Victoria General Hospital Foundation Research and Evaluation Office. Clinicaltrials.gov registration number: NCT03049696. Registration date: February 2017.

Intervention

The intervention and data collection occurred at the University of Manitoba's Active Living Centre (ALC). An overview of the intervention is shown in Table 1, followed by an explanation of its components. No incentives were provided for attendance or participation in any component of the program. Guiding documents used in the delivery of each session enabled standardization of all aspects of the intervention. Most intervention activities were led by intervention staff which included the first author (MSc, CSEP‐CEP) as well as fourth year kinesiology students at the University of Manitoba. Zumba classes were led by an external certified Zumba instructor, and cycle fitness classes were led by an external fitness instructor certified through the Canadian Society of Exercise Physiology (CESP). The first author, who is a clinical exercise physiologist certified through CSEP, was present at all structured components of the ENCOURAGEing Start intervention for program oversight.

Walk & Talk sessions promoted social support and behavior modification while gaining added health benefits of moderate‐intensity exercise with short bouts of vigorous intensity exercise. During the 45‐min session, participants walked in pairs or trios on a track while discussing provided health‐related questions. Upon completing each question, the grouping would do a hot lap, where they walked at a pace that would leave them breathing harder by its conclusion. Twice throughout the session, groupings would step off the track to engage in a checkstop with the intervention staff, where they learned or demonstrated a skill, or discussed a question in more depth. Early checkstops taught skills such as measuring heart rate, and then seeing if the speed of a regular lap puts the participant in a moderate‐intensity heart rate zone. Later, checkstops had participants apply learnings, such as providing suggestions to their grouping to reduce their barriers to PA or demonstrating a calisthenic exercise. Chairs were available to rest on it if needed. Groupings who felt unable to walk for a majority of the time used upright stationary bikes or elliptical machines as an alternative. Education seminars took place in weeks 1, 3, 5, 7 in a classroom setting and were led by intervention staff. Topics included benefits of PA, PA trends, and information on PA opportunities from facility‐based (finding certified exercise physiologists or personal trainers, costs and contracts, services) to community‐based opportunities (finding equipment, active transport, leisure activities).

Instructor‐led structured exercise allowed participants to experience a variety of PA options, which may have otherwise presented barriers of self‐confidence and social support. These included machine‐based resistance training, yoga, TRX suspension training, recreational sports, cycle fitness, and Zumba dance fitness. Zumba classes were led by an external instructor whose sole certification was Zumba instructor, and cycle fitness sessions were led by an external instructor who was certified through the Canadian Society of Exercise Physiology. All other PA options were led by intervention staff.

Homework assignments were assigned and integrated into the following week's Walk & Talk session. Examples include participants creating a weekly PA logsheet or trying a PA opportunity near their home and discussing their experience with the group.

These components promoted behavior change techniques such as behavioral practice/rehearsal and social comparison [10]. Behavior change techniques were also targeted during specific program content. Checkstops and instructor‐led exercise focused more on physically‐based behavior change techniques such as instructions on how to perform a behavior or demonstration of the behavior [10].

Measures

The primary outcome was the 6 minute walk test (6MWT), a validated measure of functional capacity for several populations and a commonly used indicator in pre‐ and post‐MBS studies [17]. 6MWT is relatively easy to administer and correlates with aerobic fitness (VO2max) [18].

Secondary outcomes included (1) anthropometric measurements (height, weight, BMI), neck circumference, waist and hip circumference, and body composition; (2) strength measurements (sit to stand, 1RM leg press, grip strength); (3) physical activity level (Actigraph accelerometer); (4) cardiovascular disease risk measurement (resting and post‐exercise blood pressure, small and large artery elasticity) [19]; (5) frailty measurement (five‐m gait test and Modified Fried Questionnaire‐physical activity/nutrition/exhaustion) [20]; (6) patient quality of life/satisfaction/mental health and depression surveys; (7) self‐compassion, anxiety and physical activity questionnaires [21, 22, 23, 24].

Statistical Approach

The targeted sample size was 48 participants. This would provide sufficient power for the primary outcome (6MWT) based on a conservative sample size calculation (two‐tailed, 90% power) and dropout rate of 40%. Three cohorts were planned to support recruitment feasibility based on ALC resources and the number of patients attending the CMBS seminar. Patients were assigned to the next available cohort once their baseline appointment was completed. The study design did not require blinding of participants or researchers.

A Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test was used to determine the distribution of individual baseline and 16‐week data for participants who attended baseline, 8‐week and 16‐week data collection appointments. A Student's T‐test (parametric) was performed on these data using baseline and 16‐week time points. Only participants who completed the protocol were included in the pre‐post assessment. No imputation of data was performed as all participants included in the analysis completed the protocol as intended. Analysis was done using SPSS software v26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). The null hypothesis was rejected at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

The participant flow chart is presented in Figure 2 and baseline characteristics of participants in Table 2. There was no significant difference in the primary outcome at baseline between completers and non‐completers. The post‐intervention analysis did not include an intention‐to‐treat, as the 16‐week data for participants lost to follow‐up was unavailable. No adverse or unintended effects attributable to the intervention were observed. The characteristics and pre‐post changes of participants completing all baseline and 16‐week appointments are shown in Table 3. The primary outcome of 6MWT increased significantly (p = 0.006) by 54 ± 34 m (10.7%). Body fat (%) decreased significantly (0.89%, p = 0.030) at 16‐week appointments. The other fitness‐related secondary outcome to increase significantly was the 30 second chair stand (3.7 ± 1.9, p = 0.002). Participants increased leg press by a mean of 31.4% (p = 0.012). The Laval Questionnaire improved by 35 ± 37 (p = 0.045). PHQ‐9 scores decreased from a moderate (10–14) to a mild (5–9) range, indicating a reduction in depressive symptoms (p = 0.039).

Discussion

The primary outcome of 6MWT increased significantly by 10.7%, which is greater than the 27 ± 10 m mean seen in Hardy's 2022 study on a pre‐operative PA intervention for CMBS patients [25]. This study's post‐intervention 6MWT scores are similar to or greater than Baillot (Canada‐situated) or Simmons' (American‐situated) pre‐operative bariatric PA interventions [16, 26], which are themselves similar to 6 month post‐operative scores with no intervention [27, 28]. A minimal clinically important difference for the 6MWT in a pre‐surgical bariatric population is unknown, but a 30–80 m increase may be clinically important across multiple pathological patient groups [29].

The indirect 1RM leg press, a measure of muscular strength, may have a protective effect against the significant loss of fat‐free mass and skeletal muscle mass typically seen after metabolic and bariatric surgery [30]. Participants experienced an increase in leg presses by a mean of 31.4% (p = 0.012). The other fitness‐related secondary outcome to increase significantly was the 30 second chair stand. This test is an indicator of balance and muscular endurance. The effect size was similar to Baillot's more intensive thrice‐weekly intervention (2, no SD given) [16].

PHQ‐9 scores decreased from a moderate (10–14) to a mild (5–9) range, indicating a reduction in depressive symptoms (p = 0.039). The Laval Questionnaire, a validated measure of health‐related quality of life in patients with severe obesity, improved significantly. As symptoms of depression and sedentary lifestyles have a bidirectional relationship [31], these effects may support increased post‐operative PA.

Although past research has examined the use of PA interventions for improving outcomes in patients waiting for MBS, those interventions required direct supervision of participants during highly structured and prescribed exercise [7, 8]. This study's approach utilized an efficient group approach in which up to 12 participants were managed by one staff, whereas other studies reported higher staff or time usage. For example, the Bari‐Active study provided a structured environment focusing on a behavior change curriculum and allocated up to 4.5 hours of instructor time per participant [2]. The EFIBAR intervention protocol, focused on individualized PA prescription and progression, requires 48 hours of instructor time per participant [13]. The format of this study's intervention requires 1.2 hours of instructor time per participant assuming a cohort of 12. The PreSET intervention [16] did not list an instructor to participant ratio, but the total instructor time per cohort would be 42 hours compared to the 26 hours in this study. This intervention resulted in 6MWT scores statistically similar to these other formats, with significantly less funding required. The 16‐week cost for each participant in a cohort of 12 is $190, which includes certified fitness staff, 4‐month facility access, and 15 minutes of instructor transition time before and after sessions. Efficacy was also shown in secondary outcomes. Daniels et al. [32] provided approximately 18 times more structured resistance training than participants in this study for a slightly higher leg press improvement (36.2% vs. 31.4%).

During the course of the intervention, 42% of participants met their individualized goals set by CMBS and were given a surgery date in the near future. These participants were unable to complete the program as intended and were therefore lost to the intervention's 8‐week and/or 16‐week data collection appointments. It is reasonable to believe that had the data from these successful patients been able to be included, the effect sizes for several outcomes may have been larger. Appointment scheduling for this intervention contrasts other pre‐operative physical activity interventions such as PreSET, which scheduled data collection relative to surgery date rather than at a predetermined week of the intervention. As a result, the PreSET interventions had no participants lost to follow‐up [33, 34]. Scheduling appears more appropriate for the variable timelines inherent to a surgical program and is a suggested methodological change for future implementations.

A limitation of this study is the use of Zumba as a group exercise activity in the intervention program. Despite being popular at the time of the ENCOURAGEing Start program development, Zumba has decreased in popularity since data collection for the study. As a result, lack of participant interest in Zumba and limited availability of Zumba classes may decrease the generalizability and feasibility of the intervention. Findings from this study should therefore be interpreted as relating to a structured, instructor‐led group dance exercise program rather than specifically to Zumba.

Initial recruitment was slower than expected as many CMBS patients were rural and declined participation for various reasons. Only 24% of new CMBS patients completed the Consent to Contact form. Several logistical delays such as securing gym access cards, training research staff, collecting baseline data, and securing space bookings within a dynamic facility (e.g., gyms were used for student exam writing) meant that many participants entered the intervention later in their CMBS program than planned.

The setting of a community fitness facility for a pre‐surgical PA intervention is novel. Based on available literature, all other in‐person pre‐operative interventions have taken place in medical fitness facilities, hospitals, or university lab areas [2, 8, 34, 35, 36]. Though a robust set of emergency response procedures was in place through both facility and research staffs, there were no adverse incidents. The versatility to operate the intervention in such a facility would maximize the number of potential sites, reduce cost, and presumably make its geographic location more central and ideal for participants. The program could also be run in an outdoor space—this was considered but discarded in this pilot trial due to the layout of the University grounds. Becoming comfortable in an active community space, rather than an inaccessible lab, may promote greater adoption of health behaviors. This would be particularly advantageous for the delivery of bariatric surgical programming in a Canadian setting where patients may be located remotely from their center. Consideration should also be given to the integration of virtual/web‐based intervention components. This may be an enhancement of an existing intervention component (e.g., an online discussion group between participants) or modification of the site‐based format to support patients who were unable to participate due to geographic barriers.

These findings appear to be generalizable to the typical patient preparing for metabolic and bariatric surgery. Baseline BMI, 6MWT and 30 second chair stand scores are similar to other pre‐surgical interventions. For example, participants had a BMI of 47.3 ± 5.6 kg/m2, comparable to Hardy (46.9 ± 5.4 kg/m2) [25], Baillot (46.6 kg/m2, no SD given) [37], and Cassin (49.6 ± 8.5 kg/m2) [38].

Based on the positive physical and mental health effects of this single arm pilot study, it is recommended that a larger study, with a minimum recruitment target of 55 participants, be undertaken using this generalizable, economical, standardized and effective ENCOURAGEing Start framework.
