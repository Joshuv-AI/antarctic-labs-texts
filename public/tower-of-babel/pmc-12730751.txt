Leveraging technology and human factor principles to improve outpatient medication reconciliation
Authors: Haytham Taha, Anish K Mammen, Mohd Abu Jubbeh, Siny Krishnan, Marleine Moukarzel, Mohamed Najeeb Madathil Thattandavida, Asif Hamza, RIzwan Ali

Problem

The department of medicine outpatient medication reconciliation compliance objective was increased to 92% in accordance with SSMC senior leadership’s desire to enhance patient safety and move towards safer practices; nevertheless, meeting this higher goal proved difficult.

According to an analysis of the first two quarters of 2023 performance data, the department of medicine’s average outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rate was persistently below the new goal of 92%, at 80%.

Background

Medication reconciliation involves the systematic review of a patient’s medication regimen during transitions in healthcare to prevent medication errors like omissions, duplications, dosing errors and drug interactions that can significantly impact patient safety.1 It involves verifying the medications a patient is currently taking, comparing them with the patient’s prescriptions and resolving any discrepancies.

The Joint Commission has underscored the importance of medication reconciliation as a National Patient Safety Goal due to its impact on mitigating medication errors and enhancing patient safety.2 A significant proportion of adverse drug events arises from unrecognised medication discrepancies during transitions of care, highlighting the necessity for robust medication reconciliation protocols. This is consistent with findings in various healthcare settings, wherein structured medication reconciliation processes have been shown to significantly reduce medication-related errors and improve clinical outcomes.3

The utilisation of health informatics and electronic medical record (EMR) systems plays a critical role in facilitating effective medication reconciliation in the outpatient setting. While technology yields promising results, overcoming barriers, such as lack of time, insufficient access to complete medication information or communication gaps between providers and patients, is key to improving medication reconciliation processes and achieving better patient outcomes.4 Addressing these challenges necessitates understanding of both technological advancements and human factor principles as well as robust interdisciplinary collaboration and training.5 The use of technology to refine electronic medication lists and provide reminders for patients underscores the critical intersection of human factors and technology in improving medication reconciliation compliance rates.6

The role of education, both for healthcare providers and patients, cannot be understated. Adequate education and training for healthcare professionals are necessary to ensure that they understand their roles in medication reconciliation and are equipped to use relevant technologies effectively.7 Active patient participation in their own care is vital for effective medication reconciliation and significantly contributes to improved outcomes.8 Patients should be encouraged to bring their medications to appointments and familiarise themselves with their medication regimens. This concept of patient engagement is necessary to ensure continuity of care and minimise medication errors.9

Human factor engineering plays a crucial role in enhancing medication safety and effectiveness in outpatient settings. Research shows that focusing on human factors can foster better teamwork and communication among healthcare providers, which is integral during patient care transitions where the potential for errors is significantly increased.10 The integration of human factor principles in system designs can help anticipate user needs and improve the overall usability of technology in clinical settings,11 further supporting the argument that a systematic approach to medication reconciliation can significantly mitigate the risk of medication errors during patient transitions.12

Baseline measurement

According to an analysis of the first two quarters of 2023 performance data, the department of medicine’s average outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rate was persistently below the new goal of 92%, at 80%.

Design

The outpatient medication reconciliation procedures in the internal medicine, cardiology, pulmonology, gastroenterology, rheumatology, nephrology, neurology, haematology, oncology and infectious disease clinics of the SSMC department of medicine were examined using a prospective study methodology. Information was gathered from 133 328 patient visits over the 14-month trial period, which ran from August 2023 to September 2024. The pharmacy informatics team audits using the EMR systems, which are essential for monitoring medication reconciliation compliance metrics, made it easier to retrieve information. Before the quality improvement methods were put into place, the average department of medicine outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rate was 80%.

Strategy

Outpatient medication reconciliation improvement efforts were initiated across all department of medicine outpatient specialty clinics including internal medicine, cardiology, gastroenterology, pulmonology, nephrology, neurology, rheumatology, infectious disease, haematology and oncology clinics. Our aim was to improve the department of medicine outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rate to meet the new target of 92% by 14 months.

To accomplish this goal, a multidisciplinary department of medicine Outpatient Medication Reconciliation Improvement Taskforce was formed and led this quality improvement project. The team included members from physicians, quality, pharmacy, pharmacy informatics and the department of information technology.

We used usability testing to evaluate how easy and effective it is for the physicians to use the outpatient medication reconciliation tool. Taskforce members went to the clinics and observed in real time the department of medicine outpatient physicians completing the outpatient medication reconciliation. This helped us understand through direct real-time feedback the usability issues and challenges of using the medication reconciliation tool and how to improve the tool design for better user experience.

Physicians stated that the main challenge in using the medication reconciliation tool is the fact that there were multiple active duplicates of the same medication and that patients do not bring their home medications to the clinic visit.

Physicians explained that the medication reconciliation tool has a forcing function that will not allow them to sign the medication reconciliation until all active medications are reviewed and either continued, discontinued or new medications prescribed, and with the many duplicates of the same medication, this was time-consuming and challenging especially in a busy clinic with limited clinic time for each patient.

When patients were asked why they did not bring their home medications to the clinic visit, they responded that they either forgot or did not know they needed to bring their medications.

Brainstorming sessions were conducted, and we used a fishbone diagram to identify root causes for outpatient physician medication reconciliation tool non-compliance as well as coming up with ideas and solutions to address those areas for improvement.

By engaging in critical thinking and utilising technology and human factors, the team implemented several interventions targeting the root causes of outpatient medication reconciliation non-compliance. Among these interventions, enhancing the EMR system and using human factor principles to engage both healthcare providers and patients were key. The interventions were tested through a series of Plan, Do, Study and Act (PDSA) cycles and their impact on improving the outpatient medication reconciliation compliance was monitored.

The strategic interventions implemented included engaging patients actively in the medication reconciliation process, optimising the EMR system for clearer medication lists and conducting refresher training sessions for physicians. These initiatives were designed to overcome barriers such as inefficiencies and lack of comprehensive medication lists, making it easy for both healthcare providers and patients to do the right thing and minimise workarounds.

PDSA cycle

Plan

The department of medicine outpatient specialty clinic providers faced challenges with medication reconciliation due to incomplete medication histories and inefficiencies in the medication reconciliation process. The multidisciplinary taskforce team identified three key areas for intervention: improving patient awareness, optimising the EMR system and providing refresher training for physicians. The goal was to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of the outpatient medication reconciliation process and achieve the hospital’s set compliance target of 92%.

Do

In the implementation phase, various interventions were rolled out from October 2023 onwards. Initially, efforts were directed at improving patient awareness. A reminder to bring home medications was incorporated into automated text messages sent to patients prior to their clinic appointments. Additionally, a message that emphasised the importance of bringing home medications to the clinic visit was displayed on all overhead monitors in the department of medicine outpatient specialty clinics (figure 1).

Patient reminder to bring home medications to clinic visit. SSMC, Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City.

Following this in November 2023, an EMR cleanup initiative was undertaken, removing duplicate medication entries older than 1 year, which burdened the medication reconciliation process. This optimisation facilitated a more concise and accurate medication list that helped healthcare providers complete the medication reconciliation process more efficiently.

Subsequently, a web-based physician refresher training was conducted in December 2023 aimed at improving the healthcare providers' use of the medication reconciliation electronic tool. All physicians receive formal training on medication reconciliation as part of the onboarding process. Mandatory targeted department of medicine web-based physician refresher training with presurveys and postsurveys was conducted with 100% completion rate. The training focused on common challenges, efficient workflows and best practices for accurate outpatient medication reconciliation.

Study

Over the 14-month study period, from August 2023 to September 2024, data were compiled from 133 328 patient visits. The extraction of information was facilitated by the pharmacy informatics team audits utilising the EMR systems, which play a key role in tracking medication reconciliation compliance metrics.

Act

Successful interventions derived from the implementation phase were fully integrated into routine clinic operations. Enhanced patient reminders were established as standard operating procedures throughout all outpatient clinics. Additionally, a quarterly EMR review process was initiated to mitigate the reaccumulation of unnecessary duplicate entries. Ongoing web-based medication reconciliation training modules were integrated into annual education programmes for physicians to maintain competency and adherence to medication reconciliation best practices.

It became evident that the interventions implemented not only led to an immediate rise in outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rates but also established a promising framework for sustained improvement moving forward. The ability to replicate these successful strategies across other departments became inherent to the broader goals of patient safety and improved outcomes across the organisation.

Results

Data from the EMR audits revealed a significant improvement in the department of medicine outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rates, rising from 80% in August 2023 to successfully meeting the new target of 92% by January 2024 and further achieving 97% compliance rate in April through September 2024 (figure 2).

Lessons and limitations

SSMC senior leadership support and empowerment was one of the key success factors for this quality improvement project. However, fostering buy-in and engagement from healthcare providers posed challenges due to a perceived belief that the medication reconciliation electronic tool was challenging to use and time-consuming, especially in a busy clinic environment. Addressing these misconceptions through targeted physician refresher training helped overcome resistance and improve compliance. However, continuing to sustain these improvements will require ongoing leadership support and meaningful engagement with healthcare providers at all levels.

There is a lot of evidence to support that improving medication reconciliation reduces medication errors. The limitation of this study is that the reduction in prescription medication discrepancies associated with the improvement in medication reconciliation compliance was not studied to assess the impact of improving medication reconciliation on patient safety.

Conclusion

The department of medicine was able to not only reach but surpass the new outpatient medication reconciliation objective through a team effort and several effective interventions that drew on technology and human factor principles. The department of medicine’s outpatient medication reconciliation compliance rate increased from an average of 80% in August 2023 to 97% in April through September 2024 as a result of the performance improvement measures that were put into place.

This study highlights the need for quality improvement teams using information technology to understand the clinical context and human elements at play. For an outpatient medication reconciliation procedure to be effective, technology and human factor concepts must be integrated. Effectively involving patients and healthcare professionals in the outpatient medication reconciliation process facilitates the shift to more effective and long-lasting medication reconciliation systems and helps overcome opposition. Healthcare professionals can promote a safer transition of care, reduce prescription errors and improve the general standard of patient care by utilising technology in conjunction with human factors concepts.

The department of medicine’s outpatient medication reconciliation improvement initiative paved the way for possible replication in other departments and healthcare organisations by disseminating the lessons acquired from this quality improvement project throughout the hospital.
