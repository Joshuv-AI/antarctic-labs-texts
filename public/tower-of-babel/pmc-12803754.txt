Spontaneous Superior Ophthalmic Vein Thrombosis Resulted in Posterior Fossa Venous Congestion in a Case of Cavernous Sinus Dural Arteriovenous Fistula: Successful Treatment with Direct Interruption Surgery
Authors: Yoshiro YAMADA, Kota KURISU, Masaki ITO, Toshiya OSANAI, Taku SUGIYAMA, Miki FUJIMURA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistulas are generally considered to have a benign clinical course, as spontaneous thrombosis of the fistula or draining veins can result in regression of the lesion. However, we encountered a rare case in which this hemodynamic change led to rapid neurological deterioration, necessitating microsurgical intervention. A 72-year-old woman presenting with chemosis and exophthalmos was diagnosed with a cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistula. Cerebral angiography revealed 2 venous drainage routes: the superior ophthalmic vein anteriorly and the superior petrosal sinus towards the petrosal vein posteriorly. Approximately 2 weeks after diagnosis, while awaiting elective endovascular treatment, the patient developed spontaneous thrombosis of the superior ophthalmic vein. This redirected shunt flow exclusively into the petrosal vein, leading to venous congestion in the posterior fossa. Consequently, the patient experienced rapid neurological deterioration accompanied by brain edema in the cerebellum and brainstem. Furthermore, the loss of transvenous access rendered curative endovascular treatment unfeasible. The patient was therefore treated with urgent microsurgical interruption of the petrosal vein via a retrosigmoid approach. This resulted in complete neurological recovery and angiographic obliteration of the cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistulas. This case highlights the importance of close observation while waiting for the intervention for cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistulas with cortical venous reflux, given the risk of spontaneous hemodynamic change. It also underscores the role of microsurgical intervention as a salvage option when endovascular treatment becomes unfeasible.

Introduction

A cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistula (CS-DAVF) is a cerebrovascular disorder characterized by pathological arteriovenous shunting within the cavernous sinus.1,2) It is the most common type of dural arteriovenous fistula (AVF) in Japan and has a particularly high prevalence among older female populations.2) A hallmark of CS-DAVFs is their wide spectrum of clinical presentations,3) which primarily reflects the pattern of venous drainage and reflux through the venous networks tributary to the cavernous sinus (CS). When shunt flow is directed anteriorly into the superior ophthalmic vein (SOV), patients typically present with ocular symptoms such as chemosis, proptosis, or increased intraocular pressure due to impaired orbital venous outflow. In contrast, when reflux is directed toward the intracranial cortical veins, such as the superficial middle cerebral vein, it can result in venous congestion of the brain parenchyma, leading to cerebral edema or even intracranial hemorrhage. Conversely, lesions without significant reflux, or with only minimal involvement, may remain asymptomatic or cause only mild symptoms.

Interestingly, several studies3,4) have reported that hemodynamics, particularly the venous drainage pattern, may change dynamically in CS-DAVFs. In some cases, spontaneous thrombosis of the draining vein can occur and lead to AVF obliteration with resolution of symptoms, even without any intervention.3,4) This potential for spontaneous regression has contributed to the general perception of CS-DAVFs as relatively benign lesions.

In this report, we describe a patient who developed spontaneous SOV thrombosis during the waiting period for elective endovascular treatment (EVT) of CS-DAVF. Rather than following a benign clinical course, this hemodynamic change redirected shunt flow into alternative venous pathways in the posterior fossa. This resulted in venous congestion, neurological deterioration, and loss of access for EVT. This rare but serious course illustrates that the hemodynamics of CS-DAVF can change unexpectedly. Furthermore, the case highlights microsurgical intervention remains a critical salvage option in the management of CS-DAVF, even in the modern endovascular era.

Case Report

Present illness

A 72-year-old woman with a history of hypertension presented with progressive left-sided chemosis and exophthalmos. A neuroradiological examination at the previous clinic detected a left-sided CS-DAVF, which was considered the underlying cause of the patient's ocular symptoms. She was referred to our institution for further examination and treatment. Cerebral angiography (Fig. 1A-C) was performed to facilitate diagnosis and to clarify the angioarchitecture of the DVF. Left internal carotid arteriography (Fig. 1A) revealed early venous filling of the left CS (arrow), with anterior drainage into the SOV (arrowheads). Left external carotid artery angiography revealed an additional venous drainage route towards the posterior fossa (Fig. 1B, arrowheads). Cone-beam computed tomography during the right internal carotid arteriography (Fig. 1C) revealed a shunt pouch on the posteromedial aspect of the CS, with venous drainage into the SOV toward the facial vein and into the superior petrosal sinus (SPS) toward the petrosal vein (PV), indicating the presence of cortical venous reflux (CVR) in the posterior fossa. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed marked dilation of the left SOV with no abnormal findings in the parenchyma of the posterior fossa (Fig. 1D). EVT by transvenous embolization of the shunt pouch was planned approximately 2 weeks after diagnosis.

Clinical course

During the waiting period prior to the planned EVT, the patient experienced mild gait disturbance. Follow-up MRI (Fig. 2A) revealed resolution of the previously dilated SOV, along with the appearance of a T2 high-intensity area in the left cerebellar hemisphere, suggestive of developing venous congestion in the posterior fossa. The appearance of flow void accumulation in the cerebellopontine cistern was also observed, indicating increased reflux into alternative venous pathways within the posterior fossa. Cerebral angiography (Fig. 2B) at the time of EVT revealed spontaneous thrombosis of the SOV, which was one of the primary drainage routes, but the shunt flow was draining towards a persistent posterior fossa CVR. These findings indicated a conversion from a Borden type II to type III CS-DAVF,5) owing to spontaneous occlusion of the SOV. During this EVT session, we attempted to access the CS via the occluded inferior petrosal sinus (IPS), but catheterization was unsuccessful. Regarding the occluded SOV route, preoperative angiography had demonstrated highly tortuous and complex angular veins and a hypoplastic facial vein. Therefore, a transvenous approach through this route was also considered technically unfeasible. Due to the loss of an accessible transvenous route, palliative transarterial embolization (TAE) was performed. The feeding arteries from the external carotid artery system (mainly the middle meningeal artery and accessory meningeal artery) were embolized with platinum coils to reduce flow. Embolization of the internal carotid artery feeders, primarily the meningohypophyseal trunk, was not attempted because of their small caliber and marked tortuosity.

However, the day after EVT, the patient began experiencing cerebellar ataxia, which worsened over the next few days. Furthermore, she developed dysphagia and lower cranial nerve palsies during the same period. Repeat MRI (Fig. 2C, post-TAE day 1; Fig. 2D, post-TAE day 5) revealed rapid expansion of the T2 high-intensity area within the posterior fossa, involving the brainstem. This finding indicated an increase in venous congestion within the posterior fossa. To resolve venous congestion resulting from reflux of shunt flow toward the posterior fossa, salvage surgery was planned on post-TAE day 6 to directly interrupt the CVR (which was the PV in this patient).

Surgery

The patient underwent a left retrosigmoid craniotomy in the park bench position. Following subarachnoid dissection of the cerebellopontine cistern, a markedly engorged and arterialized PV was identified adjacent to cranial nerve V (Fig. 3A). Intraoperative indocyanine green video angiography confirmed early filling of the PV, indicating the draining route of the CS-DAVF. This was interrupted using an aneurysmal clip placed as close as possible to the SPS to minimize the risk of developing alternative venous outflow routes (Fig. 3B). Disappearance of early venous filling of the PV and CVR in the posterior fossa was subsequently confirmed by intraoperative indocyanine green video angiography.

(B) The PV was surgically interrupted using a titanium aneurysm clip as close as possible to the superior petrosal sinus, and disappearance of early venous filling and posterior fossa cortical venous reflux was confirmed with ICG angiography. (C) Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging showed complete resolution of high-intensity area in posterior fossa. (D) The follow-up angiography demonstrating complete obliteration of the cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistula.

Postoperative course

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful. She experienced no new deficits, and her preexisting neurological symptoms resolved within one week. MRI demonstrated complete resolution of the high-intensity area in the posterior fossa (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the postoperative angiogram confirmed complete obliteration of the CS-DAVF (Fig. 3D). The patient was discharged without neurological deficit and remains under follow-up in the outpatient clinic. Informed consent for publication of her clinical details and images was obtained during the follow-up period.

Discussion

This report describes a patient with a CS-DAVF who developed spontaneous thrombosis of the SOV while awaiting elective EVT. Initially, cerebral angiography demonstrated 2 venous drainage routes: an anterior via the SOV and a posterior drainage via the SPS into the PV. Two weeks later, spontaneous SOV occlusion redirected shunt flow into the SPS-PV pathway, resulting in posterior fossa venous congestion and rapid neurological deterioration. In addition, the loss of access rendered the planned EVT unfeasible, necessitating urgent microsurgical intervention. This case underscores the potential for dynamic changes in venous drainage patterns in CS-DAVFs and highlights the importance of close observation during the waiting period for elective endovascular intervention―particularly for Borden type II or III lesions,5) where draining veins serve as both outflow routes and critical endovascular access routes.

Several studies3,6) have described spontaneous occlusion occurring during the natural course of CS-DAVFs. However, the detailed mechanisms and clinical characteristics of this hemodynamic change have not been well studied. Tan et al.3) conducted a retrospective analysis of 27 patients with CS-DAVFs and reported that 14 patients (52%) managed conservatively exhibited spontaneous closure of the AVF. A post hoc analysis of these cases suggested that patients with smaller CS volumes were more likely to exhibit spontaneous occlusion.3) Although the relationship between CS size and spontaneous occlusion remains controversial, the authors concluded that patients with larger sinus volumes might be less likely to develop spontaneous thrombosis; therefore, earlier treatment should be considered in such cases. Moreover, Suh et al.7) analyzed 58 cases of CS-DAVF and reported the relationship between venous drainage patterns and clinical symptoms. Of these, only 1 (approximately 1.7%) demonstrated venous drainage solely into the PV. Similar to our case, this patient developed severe neurological symptoms due to venous congestion of the brainstem and cerebellum. Although the precise prevalence of such severe presentations remains uncertain owing to the rarity of the disease, a clear understanding of the angioarchitectural characteristics of CS-DAVF is crucial for accurate diagnosis and optimal management.

The mechanism of spontaneous occlusion in AVF is still under investigation, but previous reports3,8) have suggested that venous hypertension and flow stagnation play an important role in promoting progressive thrombosis in DAVF. This phenomenon appears to occur more frequently in high-flow fistulas with a single dominant draining vein. Furthermore, spontaneous occlusion has been particularly well documented in CS-DAVFs compared with other DAVFs.8) This difference in frequency suggests that structural or anatomical features unique to the CS may contribute to the development of thrombosis. Specifically, the CS contains numerous trabeculae and small venous compartments,9) which may locally increase venous pressure within a compartment and trigger thrombosis. In clinical practice, it would be helpful to predict when, where, and how spontaneous thrombosis might occur, as such information could serve as an important indicator of the necessity and timing of intervention. To clarify these mechanisms, larger prospective observational studies are required to define the natural history and hemodynamic changes underlying CS-DAVF.

EVT using transvenous embolization is the first-line treatment for CS-DAVFs, offering high durability with minimal invasiveness.10,11) In most cases, access is achieved through the ipsilateral IPS, with a previous study12) reporting feasibility in over 90% of patients. However, in clinical practice, anatomical variations such as IPS hypoplasia, aplasia, or thrombosis can render this route inaccessible.13,14) In such cases, alternative venous routes―including the SPS, basilar venous plexus, pterygoid venous plexus, or the SOV―may be utilized. The SOV, in particular, is often approached via the facial vein or percutaneously, and, in some cases, even surgically exposed.

In the present case, both the IPS and the SOV were spontaneously thrombosed, precluding transvenous access despite attempts at EVT. Faced with progressive neurological deterioration and endovascular difficulty, we proceeded with direct surgical intervention. A retrosigmoid craniotomy was performed, and the PV, identified as the cortical venous drainage in the posterior fossa, was successfully interrupted using a titanium aneurysm clip. While surgical treatment for CS-DAVF is rare, 2 prior reports13,14) have described curative microsurgery without endovascular embolization. Kim et al.13) reported a case associated with intracerebral hemorrhage that was treated with surgical disconnection of the AVF. Ozono et al.14) reported a case treated with interruption of the superficial middle cerebral vein, which was the cortical venous drainage. To our knowledge, this is the first report of direct surgical interruption of a posterior fossa draining vein in CS-DAVF. Previous studies10,15) have shown that in well-selected cases, simple interruption of the cortical venous drainage can provide durable outcomes with low complication rates. Our experience underscores that microsurgical interruption of cortical venous drainage remains a valuable salvage strategy for treating CS-DAVF, particularly when endovascular access is not feasible. However, to prevent possible complications related to the development of alternative venous drainage routes, it is important to interrupt the draining vein as possible to the AV shunt. Furthermore, assessment of hemodynamic changes following outflow-interruption surgery is essential, either intraoperatively or in the early postoperative period.

Conclusions

Spontaneous thrombosis of a draining vein can lead to critical hemodynamic changes in patients with CS-DAVF, potentially resulting in rapid neurological deterioration and loss of endovascular access. Close observation and early intervention, whenever possible, are essential in cases with CVR to prevent such situations. When EVT becomes unfeasible, microsurgical intervention should be considered a viable therapeutic option.
