Cross-task interpretability through unified modeling reveals a universal shortcut bias in neoantigen prediction
Authors: Ziting Zhang, Lei Wei, Wenxu Wu, Hai Qi, Xiaowo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAccurate neoantigen prediction is central to the design of personalized cancer immunotherapy. The immune recognition of neoantigens is a multi-step process involving peptide-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) binding, antigen presentation, and T cell activation. Despite extensive computational efforts, the overall accuracy of neoantigen identification remains unsatisfactory, limiting translational potential. Here, we present ImmUni, a unified transformer-based framework that models the three immunological stages within a consistent architecture. Through this unified modeling, we discovered that deep-learning-based immunogenicity predictors have inadvertently learned shortcut correlations driven by intra-human leukocyte antigen (intra-HLA) label imbalance, revealing a previously unrecognized model-agnostic source of bias. We quantified this bias using an information-theoretic metric and proposed a mutual-information-guided debiasing strategy that mitigates shortcut learning and improves mutation-level generalization. ImmUni not only identifies the shortcut bias issue in current neoantigen prediction but also defines a general methodological framework for diagnosing and correcting bias across data-limited, multi-step tasks in computational biology scenarios.

Introduction

Neoantigens are abnormal peptides generated by tumor-specific genetic mutations and presented on the surface of cancer cells by major histocompatibility complex (MHC; referred to as human leukocyte antigen [HLA] in humans) molecules.1 As neoantigens are absent from normal tissues, T cells can recognize them as foreign targets. Therefore, they are considered to be of great significance for tumor control and have become ideal candidate targets for personalized cancer vaccines and adoptive T cell therapies.2 The key practical challenge is to determine which mutated peptides will form stable complexes with HLA and trigger effective immune responses, a problem that remains difficult despite recent advances.3,4

The immune recognition of neoantigens involves three major stages: peptide binding to HLA molecules, presentation of peptide-HLA complexes on the cell surface, and recognition by T cell receptors (TCRs) to activate immune responses.5 Recently, numerous computational models have been developed to predict each of these steps, achieving task-specific improvements in binding,6,7,8,9,10,11,12,13 presentation,14,15,16,17,18,19 or immunogenicity20,21,22,23,24,25,26,27,28 prediction. However, the overall performance of neoantigen identification remains unsatisfactory.29,30,31 For instance, in the TESLA consortium benchmark, which provided the largest experimental evaluation of neoantigen predictors, only 6% of predicted epitopes were validated as immunogenic,30 revealing a clear gap between current model performance and biological outcomes.

A major limitation in this field is that the immune recognition process is highly complex, whereas available data are scarce and uneven.32 Because of the high cost and limited throughput of experimental assays, experimental data collection often depends on predefined assumptions and narrow experimental designs.4,33 This is naturally contrary to the unbiased data distribution that machine learning datasets hope for. As a result, machine learning algorithms trained and tested on these data may rely on indirect or coincidental correlations that appear predictive within the dataset but are not causally related to immune recognition. Although this kind of shortcut learning has been discussed in the artificial intelligence (AI)34 field and examined in some AI-driven biomedical studies,35,36,37,38,39 including TCR-epitope prediction,40,41 its relevance to multi-step biological prediction tasks such as neoantigen modeling has not been systematically explored.

We present ImmUni, a unified transformer-based framework that models HLA binding, antigen presentation, and immunogenicity of neoantigens within a single architecture. This design enables consistent comparison and interpretation across tasks and reveals a hidden bias arising from intra-HLA label imbalance, an uneven distribution of positive and negative peptides within individual HLA alleles. While HLA allele frequency imbalance across datasets has been acknowledged,42 this subtler form of intra-allelic bias has not been systematically examined. We demonstrate that this bias causes models to predict immunogenicity by relying on spurious correlations related to HLA identity rather than biologically relevant features. This abnormal behavior is observed consistently across not only our models but also all existing representative methods with distinct model architectures. We further quantify this bias using an information-based metric and introduce a mutual-information (MI)-guided debiasing strategy that reduces shortcut learning and improves immunogenicity predictions on mutation variants. ImmUni provides both a practical tool for more reliable neoantigen prediction and a general framework for identifying and correcting bias in multi-step biomedical modeling.

Results

Unified transformer modeling of binding, presentation, and immunogenicity

We focused on three pivotal immunological processes that determine neoantigen recognition: peptide-HLA binding, antigen presentation, and T cell immunogenicity (Figure 1A). Each process represents a distinct yet interconnected stage in the immune recognition cascade and was formulated as a binary classification problem defined by the input HLA molecule and peptide sequence. Our aim was to establish a unified architecture that could generalize across these related tasks while providing consistent analytical insights through model interpretation.Figure 1Overview of ImmUni(A) Three key immunological processes underlying neoantigen recognition: binding, presentation, and immunogenicity, with representative computational tools indicated for each task.(B) ImmUni unifies these processes within a single transformer-based architecture that integrates peptide and HLA sequences for cross-task modeling and interpretation.

To this end, we developed ImmUni, a transformer-encoder-based framework43 that models all three processes within the same architecture (Figure 1B; STAR Methods). Separate models were trained for binding (ImmUni_B), presentation (ImmUni_P), and immunogenicity (ImmUni_I), allowing both within-task optimization and cross-task comparison under a consistent representational scheme. We deliberately adopted separate task-specific training rather than multi-task learning to preserve task-resolved interpretability and avoid confounding signal fusion across immunological stages. To better capture the pairwise dependencies between peptide and HLA sequences, ImmUni adopts an interaction-based design rather than a representation-based one, as used in existing models such as TransPHLA.13 This design choice allows the attention mechanism to directly encode position-wise complementarity between binding partners, facilitating interpretability through attention weights. ImmUni incorporates an attention-pooling mechanism similar to that in BERT,44 introducing a learnable special classifcation token ([CLS]) whose contextual embedding serves as a holistic representation of the peptide-HLA pair. This embedding is then passed to a projection head for binary classification.

Though attention-based interpretability has been explored in previous studies,7,9,10,13 these approaches often suffer from feature fusion or dependency on auxiliary networks, where features from different positions are merged before or after the attention operation. Consequently, the resulting attention maps offer only partial or indirect interpretability. In this work, the design of the [CLS] token and interaction-based model architecture creates a more direct correspondence between attention patterns and model outputs, enabling the model to capture the unique importance of the raw input directly in the output. By examining the attention weights associated with the [CLS] token, we can identify residues within both the peptide and the HLA molecule that contributed most to prediction outcomes.

Although several previous frameworks6,22 have integrated more than one task within a shared model architecture, their primary objective has been to optimize predictive performance. In contrast, the central aim of our study is to systematically analyze model interpretability across binding, presentation, and immunogenicity within a unified framework. This is achieved through the combination of a consistent architecture and a tailored interaction-based attention mechanism. These design choices allow us to examine not only how the model integrates sequence information across different immunological stages but also how deep learning models represent and potentially recapitulate key determinants of antigen recognition.

Investigating how deep learning models operate across immunological prediction tasks using ImmUni

We used ImmUni, a unified transformer framework, to investigate how deep learning models learn and make decisions across key immunological prediction tasks, including binding, presentation, and immunogenicity. For each task, we independently trained ImmUni multiple times based on cross-validation splitting and used the mean performance for evaluation, followed by averaged attention maps for interpretation (STAR Methods). While benchmark datasets are well established for binding and presentation, those for immunogenicity remain limited and heterogeneous. Therefore, we first evaluated ImmUni on the two former tasks to confirm that it provides a reliable baseline for model interpretation and bias analysis, ensuring that any observed effects reflect data or task properties rather than model underperformance.

For binding prediction, we adopted two benchmarks from a recent state-of-the-art study13 and compared ImmUni_B with nine existing models,7,10,13,15,45,46,47,48 including the current state-of-the-art method TransPHLA (STAR Methods). ImmUni_B achieved strong and consistent performance across multiple metrics (Figure 2A). For presentation prediction, ImmUni_P was evaluated using an independent benchmark19 and compared with four methods,15,17,18,19 including the IEDB-recommended NetMHCpan_EL and the current best performer MixMHCpred2.2 (STAR Methods). ImmUni_P achieved the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and comparable positive predictive value (PPV) to MixMHCpred2.2 (Figure 2B). To probe what the model had learned, we examined attention maps linking each amino acid in the peptide and HLA sequences to the [CLS] token, derived from the validation set (Figures 2C, 2D, and S1A). Both attention maps of binding and presentation models prominently highlighted the first two and last positions, with an emphasis on the 9th position, consistent with the canonical 9-mer peptide length. These positions correspond to anchor residues and play important roles in HLA binding and presentation, as widely acknowledged in previous structural studies.49,50 Allele-wise visualization across ten representative HLA types (four HLA-A, four HLA-B, and two HLA-C)51 further revealed distinct family-specific preferences (Figure S1B), such as the A2 preference for large aliphatic side chains and the B44 preference for negatively charged residues, consistent with previous reports.13,52 These findings again indicate that ImmUni learns biologically meaningful interaction patterns and provides an interpretable view of model decision-making.

We next examined how ImmUni behaved in the immunogenicity prediction task. Based on ImmUni_I, we visualized the attention map learned during training. Unlike the patterns observed in binding and presentation, the immunogenicity model showed abnormally high attention weights assigned to HLA residues (Figure 2E), which contradicted the biological understanding that the non-anchor peptide regions, rather than HLA or anchor residues, provide the key signals for T cell recognition.53,54 This unexpected pattern suggested that the model might be responding to confounding statistical signals rather than true immunogenic features. To assess the robustness of this interpretability analysis, we examined attention maps across cross-validation models for all three tasks, which exhibited consistent patterns both qualitatively and quantitatively (Figure S2).

Data imbalance drives a universal shortcut bias in immunogenicity prediction models

The abnormally high attention given to HLA residues in the immunogenicity model suggests that the networks capture spurious statistical correlations tied to HLA identity instead of biologically meaningful peptide-level determinants of immune recognition. To investigate this shortcut behavior, we systematically examined the composition of existing immunogenicity datasets from four representative studies,22,23,24,25 quantifying both inter- and intra-HLA class imbalance. A small subset of high-frequency HLA alleles contributed disproportionately to the overall training data (Figure 3A), revealing strong inter-HLA imbalance. As observed in presentation prediction tasks, inter-HLA population imbalance is often associated with reduced performance on rare alleles in the test set.42 To assess whether a similar pattern holds for immunogenicity prediction, we examined the relationship between inter-HLA sample frequency and model performance. Specifically, for each HLA allele, we computed the correlation between its number in training samples and the corresponding AUROC in the benchmark datasets. We then assessed whether intra-HLA imbalance might better account for the shortcut behavior. We found that current training datasets exhibit pronounced intra-HLA imbalance. Specifically, within each HLA allele, the ratio of positive to negative peptides often differed by more than 10-fold, with over half of the HLA alleles exhibiting such a significant intra-HLA imbalance (Figure 3B). We further examined the four datasets to prove that this feature is not a biologically relevant feature, as the same HLA allele often exhibits contrasting positive-to-negative ratios among different datasets (Figure S3B). This pronounced data imbalance likely arises from heterogeneous experimental designs and limited sample sizes in individual studies, further compounded by biased HLA sampling, as immunological investigations tend to prioritize alleles that are clinically relevant or experimentally accessible. Such extreme skew makes standard balancing techniques, such as class weighting or resampling, ineffective. This led to an important hypothesis: a model could appear predictive simply by exploiting HLA-specific label frequencies rather than by learning true determinants of immune activation.

To test this hypothesis, we used two benchmark datasets from BigMHC25 (the neoepitope benchmark and the IEDB infectious disease epitope benchmark) and calculated, for each HLA allele, the correlation between the positive-to-negative peptide ratio in the training set and the mean prediction score of ImmUni_I for test peptides associated with that HLA. A strong positive correlation was observed across all HLAs (Figure 3C), indicating that the model tended to assign higher immunogenicity probabilities to HLAs that were overrepresented by positive examples during training.

We next examined whether this bias was specific to our framework or shared by other models. We repeated the analysis on three representative deep learning models (BigMHC IM,25 DeepHLApan,22 and DeepImmuno23), using their respective training data. Consistently high Spearman correlation coefficients were observed in all cases (Figures 3C and S3C), demonstrating that this bias, induced by the intra-HLA imbalance, is universal across different model architectures and training strategies.

To further validate these findings, we performed a mutation-based sensitivity analysis24 on ImmUni_B, ImmUni_P, and ImmUni_I (Figure S4; STAR Methods). For the binding and presentation tasks, mutation at the HLA or anchor positions led to the largest score perturbations, consistent with their biological roles. In contrast, for immunogenicity prediction, perturbing HLA residues caused the largest prediction changes in all models, whereas mutations in peptide residues, which are expected to encode the key TCR-recognition signals, had comparatively minor effects. We found similar results in all immunogenicity prediction models ImmUni_I, BigMHC IM, DeepHLApan, and DeepImmuno (Figure S4). Together, these results support the conclusion that the models predominantly rely on HLA identity rather than peptide features, reflecting that intra-HLA label imbalance is a pervasive, model-agnostic source of bias in current deep learning approaches to immunogenicity prediction.

HLA-only model performance exposes critical flaws in immunogenicity benchmarks

The observation raised concerns about whether current immunogenicity benchmarks can fairly evaluate model performance. Because these benchmarks are also constructed from heterogeneous retrospective studies, a similar distribution of intra-HLA imbalance between training and testing data can lead to artificially inflated performance or, conversely, unfair penalization. This problem is further compounded when each deep learning model uses its own distinct training and evaluation datasets.

To test this, we trained three variants of ImmUni_I with different input configurations: peptides only, HLA alleles only, and peptide-HLA combined. Strikingly, the HLA-only model achieved the best performance across both immunogenicity benchmarks, even outperforming the peptide-HLA-combined model. On the IEDB benchmark, the AUROC and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of the HLA-only configuration exceeded those of all 15 models previously evaluated25 by 3.18%–15.95% and 5.13%–14.01%, respectively (Figure 3D).

These results demonstrate that current benchmarks overlook critical distributional biases in training data, providing shortcuts for models to achieve seemingly high performance simply by memorizing HLA-specific label frequencies rather than learning true immunogenicity determinants. Consequently, benchmark accuracy alone fails to reflect genuine biological generalization. This is particularly concerning because, in practical applications such as vaccine or immunotherapy design, where predictions must accurately prioritize immunogenic peptides for a patient’s specific HLA types,4 this bias could substantially limit model generalizability and biological validity.

We therefore highlight the importance of these findings for future benchmark and evaluation design. Specifically, we advocate for more standardized training datasets across models and for benchmark curation that explicitly considers HLA balance. In addition, future evaluations may benefit from incorporating allele-specific performance metrics and bias-aware assessment protocols to enable fairer and more informative model comparisons.

MI-based debiasing strategy disrupts shortcut learning and enables sensitive immunogenicity prediction on mutation variants

The ideal solution to data-driven shortcut bias would be to construct a large-scale, evenly distributed immunogenicity dataset with minimal batch effects. However, this is currently infeasible due to the high experimental cost, heterogeneous assay settings, and lack of a uniform sampling strategy.35 To address this challenge, we developed an MI-based debiasing strategy and demonstrated its effectiveness through two case studies.

The core idea of the approach is to use MI as a quantitative measure of statistical dependency between input features and the output label. MI captures how much knowing a feature (for example, an HLA allele) reduces the uncertainty of the label, thereby indicating the extent to which a model could exploit that feature for prediction. In the context of biased datasets, features that exhibit high MI with the label but lack a direct biological causal link are likely sources of shortcut learning.

We first calculated the MI between HLA alleles and immunogenicity labels to test whether MI reflects the degree of data imbalance that contributes to model bias. In datasets dominated by certain HLAs with strongly unequal proportions of positive and negative peptides, the conditional entropy of the label given each HLA allele becomes low, yielding high MI values (STAR Methods). Across four immunogenicity datasets from different studies,22,23,24,25 the MI calculations confirmed this relationship (Figure 4A), consistent with the imbalance patterns observed qualitatively in Figure 3B. Interestingly, HLA masking alone did not fully eliminate the bias. We next examined the MI scores between each position of 9-mer peptides and the immunogenicity label in these immunogenicity datasets (Figure 4B). The second and last positions consistently exhibited the highest MI values across all datasets, even though these anchor sites play only a secondary role in TCR interaction.53,54 Because peptides bound to different HLA alleles share conserved yet distinct anchor patterns,13,52 these positions can inadvertently leak HLA information and reinforce the same bias. In such cases where strong interdependencies exist among features, interpreting model attention alone may not suffice to identify the root cause of bias. MI thus provides a quantitative and complementary measure for detecting spurious dependencies between features and labels.

We thus applied masks to both HLA alleles and the two anchor positions of the peptide before training the model (Figure 4C). It should be noticed that the anchor positions can vary across HLA alleles; however, for simplicity and general applicability, we masked the second and C-terminal positions of the peptide for all samples, a strategy that captures the dominant anchor pattern for most HLA alleles.54 We evaluated this debiased ImmUni_I using a case study of nonsynonymous single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in a colorectal cancer neoantigen.57 Nonsynonymous SNVs represent a well-characterized class of neoantigens that can trigger strong immune responses58,59 or, conversely, lead to immune escape when immunogenicity is lost.60 Accurately predicting mutation-induced gains or losses in immunogenicity is therefore critical for both basic immunology and therapeutic design.

We compared the debiased ImmUni_I with the original ImmUni_I and five recent immunogenicity prediction models.19,22,23,24,25 Only the debiased model showed clear discrimination between immunogenic and non-immunogenic mutation variants, with significantly higher positive prioritization ability and greater prediction variance among mutation variants (Figure 4D). Notably, at least six of the eight test variants were included in the training sets of PRIME219 and BigMHC IM,25 yet both models failed to distinguish positive from negative mutations, indicating that data bias can profoundly distort model decision boundaries. To further validate generalizability, we tested the model on a SARS-CoV-2 mutation dataset,61 which shares a similar immunogenicity mechanism with tumor neoantigen but differs in peptide source. A comparable pattern was observed, with the debiased model showing greater sensitivity to mutations that altered immunogenicity (Figure S5). Nevertheless, even after debiasing, the model still struggled on several mutation groups, highlighting the intrinsic difficulty of predicting immunogenicity.

While hard masking inevitably introduces some information loss since HLA also participates in TCR interactions, the results collectively demonstrate the necessity of bias control and the effectiveness of the MI-based strategy in guiding the model to focus on biologically relevant signals for peptide immunogenicity prediction.

It should be noted that some previous immunogenicity models also masked specific regions, such as HLA alleles or peptide anchor residues, when training conventional machine learning models.26,28,53 However, these efforts were largely heuristic, guided by prior biological knowledge to simplify the feature space rather than by an awareness of data imbalance or hidden bias. As shown in Figure 4D, such methods fail to handle SNV mutations effectively, even with testing data included in the training dataset, highlighting the limitations of classical machine learning strategies. In contrast, the prevalent state-of-the-art deep learning models22,23,24,25 no longer include this step, instead assuming that neural networks can automatically learn biologically relevant features from raw sequences. Our findings show that this assumption is unreliable. Deep learning models excel at uncovering statistical regularities within data, which can lead to biased decision boundaries.34 By introducing an MI-based debiasing framework, we provide a general and data-driven approach to identify and mitigate such biases. Unlike prior heuristic masking, this method quantitatively detects spurious dependencies between features and labels, offering a systematic way to diagnose and control bias in immunogenicity prediction and other biological modeling tasks.

Clustering immunogenic peptides using immunogenicity-encoded representations reveals distinct biophysical preferences

The improved performance of ImmUni_I on immunogenicity prediction suggests that the model effectively captures latent representations associated with immunogenic epitopes. To explore whether these learned embeddings reflect biologically meaningful patterns, we extracted the representations from the final projection layer for all positive 9-mer peptides in the training set. Without debiasing, the embedding space appeared fragmented and strongly aligned with HLA distribution, again indicating that the model relied on HLA identity as a dominant feature. After debiasing, however, the clusters became smoother and more evenly distributed, implying that the model had shifted toward learning intrinsic peptide-level immunogenicity features.

A central question in immunology is the biophysical signatures that distinguish immunogenic peptides. Previous studies have suggested that properties such as bulkiness, aromaticity, and hydrophobicity contribute to immunogenicity.53,62 Following the amino acid classification of Nelson et al.,63 we grouped amino acids into five categories according to structural and physicochemical characteristics. For each cluster derived from the debiased ImmUni_I model, we performed two-sided binomial tests comparing the frequency of each amino acid group to that in the entire peptide population (STAR Methods). Distinct compositional preferences emerged across clusters (Figure 5B). For instance, cluster 1 was enriched for nonpolar aliphatic residues, whereas cluster 3 showed a co-enrichment of positively and negatively charged residues, accompanied by depletion of polar uncharged ones. Interestingly, aromatic and positively charged groups often displayed opposing enrichment trends. These observations not only align with previous reports53,57,62 but also suggest that immunogenicity may be shaped by cluster-specific biophysical patterns rather than a single universal rule.

Discussion

Neoantigens play a central role in immunotherapy and have shown encouraging clinical results.64,65 Despite progress in predicting individual steps of the neoantigen immune response, identifying true immunogenic neoantigens with minimal false positives remains a major challenge.4,66 In this study, we established a unified machine learning framework, ImmUni, that integrates binding, presentation, and immunogenicity prediction to examine how models learn across immunological tasks. Consistent with previous studies, our results show that peptide binding and presentation can be predicted with high accuracy, but immunogenicity prediction remains the key bottleneck in neoantigen discovery, mainly due to limited and heterogeneous data. Through cross-task interpretability analysis, we uncovered a previously overlooked source of bias in immunogenicity datasets: models tend to exploit the ratio of positive to negative peptides within each HLA allele as a shortcut rather than learning biologically meaningful features that drive T cell recognition. This bias also undermines the fairness of current immunogenicity benchmarks. While inter-HLA allele frequency imbalance has been recognized previously,42 this work systematically identifies and addresses intra-HLA label imbalance as a major source of bias in immunogenicity prediction. Our analysis provides a comprehensive characterization of how this bias affects model behavior in a model-agnostic way, supported by explicit assumptions and experimental evidence across a wide range of existing representative deep learning models.

These observations provide practical guidance for future model development and benchmark design. Previous research has attempted to improve immunogenicity prediction by transferring knowledge from binding or presentation tasks through model-based25 or feature-based19,27,28 transfer strategies. However, our findings suggest that such transfer learning can inadvertently amplify pre-existing biases, further coupling model predictions with HLA identity rather than causal biological determinants. In this study, we introduced an MI-based debiasing strategy that effectively disrupted shortcut learning and restored the model’s sensitivity to immunogenic variants. Building on these insights, we recommend a sequential strategy for neoantigen identification. Binding and presentation models should first identify potential binders for specific HLA alleles, followed by immunogenicity models that rank likely immunogenic neoantigens. Rigorous bias control at this second stage is critical to ensure fair and generalizable predictions. With careful data curation and model design, it is possible to maximize the utility of existing datasets and enhance prediction performance. For benchmark design and evaluation, test sets should avoid extreme intra-HLA imbalance so that each evaluated HLA allele contains a reasonably balanced ratio of positive and negative peptides, thereby reducing the risk that models succeed primarily by memorizing HLA-specific label frequencies. In addition, constructing deliberately mismatched or distribution-shifted control datasets can provide a useful test to assess whether a model is overly dependent on HLA identity rather than true immunogenic features.

More broadly, the phenomenon uncovered here extends beyond immunogenicity prediction and reflects a broader challenge in data-driven biological modeling. Biased data distributions are pervasive in biological research.67,68,69,70,71,72 Although deep learning has achieved remarkable success in modeling complex biological systems, there is a prevailing assumption that it can autonomously uncover meaningful biological principles from raw data. Yet, deep neural networks are fundamentally trained to learn statistical regularities within the data distribution, not the underlying biological laws. When the data are biased, models inevitably internalize and even amplify these biases, yielding predictions that may appear accurate but lack mechanistic validity. This problem is particularly pronounced in multi-step biological processes, such as immune recognition, where hierarchical dependencies can obscure shortcut learning.

Our framework provides a general strategy for uncovering and analyzing such biases. By decomposing the immunological prediction process into binding, presentation, and immunogenicity and by examining the representations learned at each stage, ImmUni enables precise identification of where bias enters the learning pipeline. This modular design is readily extendable to other multi-step biological prediction tasks, offering a structured way to build models that are both transparent and mechanistically grounded. Importantly, it also shifts interpretability from a passive consistency check to an active diagnostic tool, providing a practical means to assess model behavior throughout complex biological workflows.

In summary, ImmUni not only provides a unified framework for understanding how deep learning models operate across immunological prediction tasks but also exemplifies a broader principle: understanding how AI learns from biological data is as crucial as improving what it predicts. By revealing and addressing data-driven biases, our study contributes to the development of interpretable, generalizable, and biologically grounded AI systems for immunotherapy and beyond.

Limitations of the study

Several directions can further extend this work. First, the validation of the debiasing strategy in this paper is restricted to limited retrospective case studies. Expanding evaluations to large, systematically curated datasets will enable more rigorous benchmarking under controlled bias conditions. Second, a more refined debiasing strategy could explicitly model allele-specific anchor preferences rather than relying on fixed masking of the second and C-terminal positions across all HLAs. Future extensions may incorporate adaptive or data-driven masking schemes that better capture the diversity of HLA binding patterns while preserving informative contextual features around anchor sites. Third, for future immunogenicity modeling, incorporating structural information as auxiliary input73,74,75 or leveraging pretraining models76 may further improve performance when data are limited. Together, these developments will help translate interpretable and bias-aware immunogenicity modeling into robust tools for next-generation immunotherapy design.

Method details

Problem formulation

The prediction of binding, presentation, and immunogenicity is formulated in a unified framework as follows. Let x denote a sample consisting of an HLA–peptide pair drawn from the pair space X, and let y∈Y={0,1} represent its corresponding binary label. Each of the three processes, binding (B), presentation (P), and immunogenicity (I) defines a distinct data distribution over X×Y. Our objective is to train three task-specific models fθ:X→[0,1], each optimized with cross-entropy loss to perform binary classification for the respective processes. Importantly, binding, presentation, and immunogenicity represent successive prerequisite stages of antigen recognition. Accordingly, the support set SI of p(x,y = 1) is a subset of SP, which in turn is a subset of SB.

Structure of ImmUni

ImmUni was implemented using an interaction-based transformer encoder architecture. The core component of this framework is the attention mechanism, which takes an input matrix X∈Rl×d and an optional attention mask M∈{0, –∞}l×l, and produces contextual embeddings Xo∈Rl×do through adaptive information aggregation:Q=XWq,K=XWk,V=XWv,A=softmax(QK⊤+Mdh)∈Rl×dXo=VA∈Rl×do

The attention mask controls which tokens are allowed to attend to each other. Specifically, Mi,j = −∞ prevents the i-th token from querying the j-th token, while Mi,j = 0 allows attention between them. In certain experiments, we applied residue-level masking by setting M1,i,M2,i, …,Ml,i = −∞, thereby blocking all tokens from querying the i-th token. This operation effectively removes contextual information from the masked residues and allows direct investigation of their influence on model representations and predictions.

Mutual information–based debiasing strategy

Let b denote an abstract bias variable. For example, in our experiments, b represents the HLA type or the anchor residues, y is the immunogenicity label, and x is the paired HLA–peptide sequence. In biased datasets, the bias feature b is often highly correlated with the target label y, resulting in an imbalanced conditional probability p(y|b). However, in real-world scenarios, the joint distribution of y and b may shift between training and testing: ptrain(y|b)≠ptest(y|b), which causes models that rely on such correlations to fail to generalize.

To quantitatively detect potential bias features, we employed mutual information (MI) between the label y and specific subsets of the input features x (denoted as x′). MI provides a general measure of statistical dependency that has been successfully used in various learning tasks.77,78,79 It can be expressed in its marginal–conditional form as:I(x′;y)=H(y)−H(y|x′)

Entropy H(·) quantifies the uncertainty or uniformity of a distribution. Lower conditional entropy H(y|x′)indicates that the label distribution becomes more predictable once x′ is known; that is, x′ provides more information about y. Therefore, a higher MI value implies that the feature x′ carries substantial information about the label and may act as either a biologically meaningful determinant or a potential source of bias. Given that SI⊂SP⊂SB, features that are highly informative in upstream processes (e.g., binding or presentation) are prone to become bias candidates in downstream tasks such as immunogenicity prediction.

Once features with high MI are identified, we assess whether they have a direct biological causal relationship with the true label. Features lacking such causality or showing counterfactual relationships are treated as bias variables and masked before model input. This masking constitutes the core of our MI-guided debiasing strategy.

Training datasets

In this study, we focused on HLA class I (HLA-I) alleles, which are presented on the surface of most nucleated human cells. They are responsible for presenting intracellular abnormal peptide epitopes to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. The training datasets used for different tasks in ImmUni are described below. Typically, peptides bound to HLA-I molecules range from 8 to 14 amino acids in length, with a strong preference for 9-mers. Peptides longer than 15 residues were excluded from training.

The binding dataset was adopted from Chu et al.,13 originally curated from Mei et al.12 The training dataset contained 718,332 peptide–HLA pairs. For each HLA allele and peptide length, negative peptides were randomly sampled from the IEDB immunopeptidome source proteins. This approach has been shown to have minimal impact on model performance.13 A 5-fold cross-validation scheme identical to the original study13 was used to ensure consistency.

The presentation dataset was obtained from Reynisson et al.15 and Gfeller et al.,19 comprising 4,063,475 peptide–HLA pairs. Negative samples were generated by random extraction from the UniProt database, as previously described by Alvarez et al.80 Only mono-allelic samples were retained to ensure reliable associations between peptides and HLA alleles. HLA alleles that contained only positive or only negative samples (representing approximately 1.3% of the total dataset) were excluded to maintain class balance. Similar to the binding task, a 5-fold cross-validation was applied for training and evaluation consistency.15

For immunogenicity prediction, we used the most recent and comprehensive public dataset curated by Kim et al.,24 which included 19,933 peptide–HLA pairs. Only human-derived data were retained. HLA alleles lacking amino acid sequence information were either removed or replaced with alleles sharing the same two-digit typing. A 10-fold cross-validation scheme consistent with Li et al.23 was applied. For investigating the influence of bias shown in Figures 2 and 3, we adopted the training dataset curated by Albert et al.,25 which proposed the two benchmarks, to ensure consistency and comparability.

Details of model training

The input HLA sequence was represented by its core region, consisting of 34 residues.6 Each peptide was represented by its complete sequence, with a maximum length of 15 amino acids. Every residue was encoded as a learnable embedding vector. Sinusoidal positional embeddings were added separately to the HLA and peptide embeddings. The two embeddings, together with a learnable [CLS] token, were concatenated and fed into the model.

To ensure a unified and consistent architecture across all three prediction tasks, we applied identical model structures and hyperparameters. The backbone network was implemented as a pre-layer normalization (pre-LN) transformer encoder,81 composed of four layers, four attention heads, and 128 hidden dimensions. The dropout rate between layers was set to 0.1.

The projection head consisted of a two-layer perceptron activated by the ReLU function, with dimensions progressively reduced from 128 to 64 and finally to 2. Model parameters were optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, and exponential decay rates β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999 for the first and second moments, respectively.

Training was conducted for up to 1,000 epochs, with early stopping applied when the validation AUROC failed to improve for 10 consecutive epochs. In practice, convergence was reached within approximately 310 epochs for binding, 80 epochs for presentation, and 204 epochs for immunogenicity tasks. For experiments using cross-validation partitions, model ensembling was applied by averaging the output probabilities across folds, and the attention maps were also calculated through ensembling all the cross-validation models.

Details of benchmarking on binding and presentation prediction

For binding prediction, we adopted two benchmark datasets from Chu et al.,13 following the same experimental settings to ensure comparability. Since not all methods are capable of handling peptides of variable lengths or diverse HLA alleles, performance comparison was conducted only on samples for which all models could generate valid predictions. This ensured fairness across all evaluated methods. The performance results of the comparative methods were directly obtained from the previously published work.13

For presentation prediction, we evaluated all methods using an independent benchmark,19 including HLA-I peptidomics data derived from ten meningioma samples.82 To account for cases where multiple peptide–HLA pairings could exist within a single sample, we implemented a post-processing step: for each peptide, the highest prediction score among all possible HLA pairings in that sample was assigned as its final presentation score.19 The prediction scores of each method were directly obtained from the benchmark.19

The evaluation metrics for binding and presentation prediction follow established practices in the respective fields. Specifically, for binding prediction, we report Accuracy, AUROC, MCC, and F1, consistent with TransPHLA.13 For presentation prediction, we report AUC and positive predictive value (PPV), in line with MixMHCpred 2.2.19 This difference reflects the distinct data characteristics of the two tasks: presentation datasets are typically constructed with a 4-fold excess of negative samples, resulting in substantial class imbalance, for which PPV is widely regarded as a more informative metric than overall accuracy.

Mutation-based sensitivity analysis

For all 9-mer peptide–HLA pairs in each benchmark, we performed a mutation-based sensitivity analysis to examine how model predictions respond to input perturbations. Specifically, each HLA allele was systematically mutated into every other HLA allele present in the corresponding training dataset, and each peptide residue was substituted with the remaining 19 amino acids.

For each mutation, the absolute change in the model’s prediction score was recorded. These values were visualized to illustrate the distribution of sensitivity across positions. In principle, a larger score change indicates that the corresponding residue or HLA component exerts a stronger influence on the model output.

Clustering and biophysical property analysis

To investigate whether the learned peptide representations captured biologically meaningful patterns, we extracted the embeddings from the final projection layer of the debiased ImmUni_I model for all positive 9-mer peptides in the training set. Dimensionality reduction was first performed using PCA to reduce the feature space to 20 components. The resulting vectors were then clustered using the K-means algorithm. The number of clusters was set to K = 7 based on the elbow criterion, which balances model complexity and explained variance.

To visualize the latent space, the embeddings were further projected into three dimensions using PCA. For each cluster, amino acid composition was analyzed to identify potential biophysical preferences. Specifically, amino acids were grouped into five categories according to their structural and physicochemical properties, following the classification by Nelson et al.63 Within each cluster, a two-sided binomial test was conducted to compare the frequency of each amino acid group against its frequency in the overall peptide population.
