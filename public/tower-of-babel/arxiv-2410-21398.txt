Splitting Algorithms for Distributionally Robust Optimization
Luis Briceño-Arias, Sergio López-Rivera, Emilio Vilches

Abstract
In this paper, we provide different splitting methods for solving distributionally robust optimization problems in cases where the uncertainties are described by discrete distributions. The first method involves computing the proximity operator of the supremum function that appears in the optimization problem. The second method solves an equivalent monotone inclusion formulation derived from the first-order optimality conditions, where the resolvents of the monotone operators involved in the inclusion are computable. The proposed methods are applied to solve the Couette inverse problem with uncertainty and the denoising problem with uncertainty. We present numerical results to compare the efficiency of the algorithms.



Keywords
Distributionally Robust Optimization, Supremum Function, Proximal Mapping, Splitting Algorithms.

Introduction

Stochastic optimization is a method for solving decision problems that involve uncertainty or randomness. The goal is to find solutions that work well under different conditions by optimizing an objective function that accounts for the uncertainty, often focusing on expected results or reducing risks (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). However, this approach usually assumes full knowledge of the probability distributions, which may not always be practical in real situations. To address this situation, the theoretical framework of Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) has been introduced, which assumes that the true probability distribution belongs to a set of distributions known as the
ambiguity set
. Optimization is then performed based on the worst-case scenario within this ambiguity set. By considering different ambiguity sets, DRO encompasses Robust Optimization when the ambiguity set includes all possible distributions, and Stochastic Optimization when the ambiguity set consists of a single distribution. Hence, the DRO framework is well-suited for addressing problems with partial information, promoting distributed robustness in decision-making. We refer to
[
13
,
14
,
16
]
for more details.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a nonempty, closed and convex subset of probability measures defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
, supported on
\Xi\subset\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathcal{G}
is a Hilbert space. In this paper, we aim to study splitting methods for solving the following distributionally robust optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{Q}}\left\{h(x)+\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[F(x,\xi)\right]\right\},

(1)

where
\mathcal{Q}
is a nonempty convex closed subset of a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
h\colon\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex and differentiable function with
\beta^{-1}
-Lipschitz gradient for some
\beta>0
. Besides,
\xi\colon\Omega\to\Xi
is a random vector, and
F\colon\mathcal{H}\times\Xi\to\mathbb{R}
is the random cost function, with
F(\cdot,\xi)
being proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex for every
\xi\in\Xi
.
The set
\mathcal{P}
is called the
ambiguity set
and accounts for the level of knowledge about the probability model of the problem. This set can be constructed based on empirical statistical information or beliefs about the moments of the distribution itself (see
[
14
]
). Moreover, when the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
is a singleton, the DRO model (
1
) reduces to a stochastic optimization problem. Conversely, if
\mathcal{P}
represents the set of all probability measures defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
and supported on
\Xi
, then problem (
1
) becomes a robust optimization problem. Thus, the DRO model generalizes both robust and stochastic optimization, offering a unified framework where robust and stochastic optimization are two extreme cases.

It is well-known that both robust and stochastic optimization have certain limitations. First, robust optimization can be very conservative, as it ignores valuable probabilistic information, while stochastic optimization may require too much information about the probability distribution, which may not be available to the modeler. Second, the solutions provided by robust or stochastic optimization models may perform poorly in out-of-sample tests or may have intrinsic bias that cannot be eliminated simply by increasing the size of the sampled data. Third, robust models can be computationally difficult to solve. Additionally, stochastic programs may involve high-dimensional integration, which is also generally intractable. In this context, the DRO problem provides a potent modeling framework and addresses some of the disadvantages of both stochastic and robust optimization. We refer to
[
13
]
for a thorough discussion.

One way to tackle the problem (
1
) is to use the techniques proposed in
[
14
, Section 4]
or in
[
13
, Section 7]
, that is, formulate the dual program of the inner maximization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\overline{F}(x,\xi)]:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(x)+F(x,\xi)].

(2)

Let us illustrate this technique considering
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the following ambiguity set
[
14
, eq. 4.2.1]

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\Xi,\bm{\mu}^{+},\bm{\mu}^{-})=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{A})\,:\,\mathbb{P}(\xi\in\Xi)=1,\,\,\bm{\mu}^{-}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\xi]\leq\bm{\mu}^{+}\},

(3)

where
\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{A})
is the set of all the measures defined on
\mathcal{A}
,
\bm{\mu}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\bm{\mu}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
the set of all the measures induced by the random vector
\xi
. That is,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
if
\mu(A)=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{-1}(A))
, for all
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
Borel measurable set, where
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{A})
. Then, the problem (
2
) is equivalent to the following maximization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\,\,

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi}\overline{F}(x,\xi)d\mu(\xi)

(4)

s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi}d\mu(\xi)=1,\quad\int_{\Xi}\xi d\mu(\xi)\leq\bm{\mu}^{+},\quad\int_{\Xi}-\xi d\mu(\xi)\leq-\bm{\mu}^{-},

whose dual problem (
4
) is given by

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\bm{\beta}\geq 0,\bm{\gamma}\geq 0}\lambda+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{+}-\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{-}

(5)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{s.t. }\overline{F}(x,\xi)-\lambda+(\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\beta})^{\top}\xi\leq 0,\quad\forall\xi\in\Xi.

If the strong duality holds between the primal problem (
4
) and its dual (
5
) (for example, if the Slater conditions hold for (
4
)), we can replace the inner maximization problem with its dual in the original DRO problem (
1
). Thus, the problem (
1
) is equivalent to the following static robust optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{H},\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\bm{\beta}\geq 0,\bm{\gamma}\geq 0}

\displaystyle\,\,\lambda+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{+}-\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{-}

(6)

s.t.

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x,\xi)-\lambda+(\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\beta})^{\top}\xi\leq 0,\quad\forall\xi\in\Xi,

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{Q}.

The difficulty of the program (
6
) depends on the structure of the function
\overline{F}(x,\xi)
as well as of the set
\mathcal{Q}
. We focus on the discrete case, that is, when the set
\Xi
associated to
\xi
is finite:
\Xi=\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{G}
with
N\geq 1
. For every
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we denote
p_{i}:=\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega\,:\,\xi(\omega)=\xi_{i}\})
. Then, the set of all the probability measures is given by the following set (the probability simplex)

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}:=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=1\right\}.

and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[F(x,\xi)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}F(x,\xi_{i})
. Hence, the problem (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{Q}}\left\{h(x)+\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}f_{i}(x)\right\},

(7)

where
f_{i}:=F(\cdot,\xi_{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. As far as we know, for the above problem, there are no available splitting algorithms. Hence, the aim of this paper is twofold. First, to propose a new algorithm for solving the following generalization of problem (
1
) in the discrete case:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{H}^{N}}\left\{H(\mathbf{x})+\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}f_{i}(x_{i})\right\}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{x}\in V\cap\bigtimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{i},

(8)

where
H\colon\mathcal{H}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex differentiable function with
\beta^{-1}
-Lipschitz gradient,
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\subset\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty closed convex set,
V\subset\mathcal{H}^{N}
is a closed vector subspace, and
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta_{N}
is a nonempty closed convex set. Second, to propose different methods to solve the problem (
7
).

On the hand, we observe that whenever
V=\mathcal{D}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{N}\,:\,x_{1}=\cdots=x_{N}\}
,
H(\mathbf{x})=h(x_{1})
, and
\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\mathcal{Q}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we have that
(x,\ldots,x)\in\mathcal{D}
is solution to problem (
8
) if and only if
x\in\mathcal{H}
is a solution to (
7
). On the other hand, when
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V
is the nonanticipativity set, and
H=0
, problem (
8
) reduces to the problem considered in
[
10
, Example 4]
. In order to solve problem (

), we consider the following functions

and ambiguity sets

. For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,

(I)

f_{i}(x)=\langle a_{i},x\rangle+\xi_{i}
, where

and

.

(i)

.

(ii)

, where

is such that
\text{int}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})\cap\text{int}(\mathcal{P}_{2})\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}\neq\emptyset

\mathcal{P}=\{p\in\Delta_{N}\,:\,\mu_{-}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\xi_{i}\leq\mu_{+}\}

, and

2
Proximity of a supremum function

Consider the following function

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{N}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}):=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}f_{i}(x_{i}).

(13)

In the following result, we compute the proximity operator of
f
in the cases
(I)
and
(II)
. We will see that in the case
(II)
, the proximity of
f
has a closed-form.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{H})
and let
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta_{N}
be a nonempty closed convex set. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{N}
and
\lambda>0
. Then the following hold.

(I)

If for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
f_{i}(x)=\langle a_{i},x\rangle+\xi_{i}
with
a_{i}\in\mathcal{H}\backslash\{0\}
and
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}f(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{i}-\lambda\overline{p}_{i}a_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N},

(14)

where
\overline{p}\in\mathcal{P}
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\dfrac{1}{2}p^{\top}Dp-p^{\top}\beta,

(15)

with
D:=\textnormal{diag}(\lambda\|a_{1}\|^{2},\ldots,\lambda\|a_{N}\|^{2})
and
\beta:=(\langle a_{i},x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
.

(II)

If
\mathcal{P}=\Delta_{N}
and for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
f_{i}(x)=\|x-\xi_{i}\|^{2}
with
\xi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
, then

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}f(\mathbf{x})=\left(\frac{x_{i}+2\lambda\overline{p}_{i}\xi_{i}}{2\lambda\overline{p}_{i}+1}\right)_{i=1}^{N},

(16)

where
\overline{p}\in\Delta_{N}
is a solution to

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\Delta_{N}}\ell(p):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}\right)\alpha_{i},

(17)

and, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
\alpha_{i}=\|x_{i}-\xi_{i}\|^{2}
.

Proof.

Let us define the function
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{N}\mapsto g_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}f_{i}(x_{i})
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Note that, since
f_{i}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{H})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, then
\{g_{p}\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\subset\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{H}^{N})
. Moreover, the function
p\mapsto g_{p}(\mathbf{x})
is concave and upper semicontinuous for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{N}
. In addition, since
f_{i}
is proper for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta_{N}
, then
f=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}g_{p}
is proper. Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}
is a nonempty compact and convex set.
Then, by
[
12
, Theorem 3.5]
, we have that

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}f(\mathbf{x})=P_{\lambda}g_{\overline{p}}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\text{ with }\overline{p}\in\displaystyle\argmax_{p\in\mathcal{P}}e_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})

(18)

Case
(I)
: By
[
2
, Proposition 24.8(i) & Proposition 24.11]
, we deduce that

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}g_{\overline{p}}(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{i}-\lambda\overline{p}_{i}a_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}.

(19)

Let us compute
e_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})
. By (
19
), we obtain that

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=g_{p}(P_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x}))+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\mathbf{x}-P_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\langle a_{i},x_{i}-\lambda p_{i}a_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x_{i}-x_{i}+\lambda p_{i}a_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\langle a_{i},x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i})-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{2}\|a_{i}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda^{2}p_{i}^{2}\|a_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\langle a_{i},x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{2}\|a_{i}\|^{2}.

Set
\alpha_{i}=\lambda\|a_{i}\|^{2}
and
\beta_{i}=\langle a_{i},x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Then, in order to find
\overline{p}
in (
18
), we need to solve the following problem

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\beta_{i}-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{2}\alpha_{i},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\psi(p):=\dfrac{1}{2}p^{\top}Dp-p^{\top}\beta,

(20)

where
D=\text{diag}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{N\times N}
and
\beta=(\beta_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Note that, since
\nabla^{2}\psi(p)=D
is positive definite,
\psi
is strictly convex and coercive. Thus, from
[

Case
(II)
: By
[
2
, Proposition 24.11 & Proposition 24.8(i)]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}g_{\overline{p}}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\frac{1}{2\lambda\overline{p}_{i}+1}(x_{i}+2\lambda\overline{p}_{i}\xi_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{N}.

(21)

Let us calculate
e_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})
. By (

), we have that

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=g_{p}(P_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x}))+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\mathbf{x}-P_{\lambda}g_{p}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\left\|\frac{x_{i}+2\lambda p_{i}\xi_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}-\xi_{i}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{x_{i}+2\lambda p_{i}\xi_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}-x_{i}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\left\|\frac{x_{i}-\xi_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{2\lambda p_{i}\xi_{i}-2\lambda p_{i}x_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}\|x_{i}-\xi_{i}\|^{2}}{(1+2\lambda p_{i})^{2}}+\frac{(2\lambda p_{i})^{2}\|\xi_{i}-x_{i}\|^{2}}{2\lambda(1+2\lambda p_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}(1+2\lambda p_{i})}{(1+2\lambda p_{i})^{2}}\|x_{i}-\xi_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}\right)\|x_{i}-\xi_{i}\|^{2}.

Set
\alpha_{i}=\|x_{i}-\xi_{i}\|^{2}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Then, in order to find
\overline{p}
in (
18
), we need to solve in this case the following problem

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\Delta_{N}}\ell(p):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{1+2\lambda p_{i}}\right)\alpha_{i}.

(22)

Note that
\ell
is continuous and
\Delta_{N}
is a nonempty compact set. Therefore, the problem (
22
) has solutions.

Proposition 2.4

(II)

\overline{p}^{k+1}=p^{k+1}+u^{k+1}-p^{k}.\end{array}\right.

Then

Proof.

For every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, let us define the function

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{H}\mapsto\widetilde{g}_{p}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\langle x,Qx\rangle+\langle b_{i},x\rangle+c_{i}).

Note that
\{\widetilde{g}_{p}\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\subset\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{H})
. Moreover, the function

is concave and upper semicontinuous for all

is proper. Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}
is a nonempty compact and convex set.
Then, by
[
12

, we have that

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}\widetilde{f}(x)=P_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{\overline{p}}(x),\quad\text{ with }\overline{p}\in\displaystyle\argmax_{p\in\mathcal{P}}e_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{p}(x).

(32)

Note that
\widetilde{g}_{p}
is differentiable. Then, given

, we have

\displaystyle y=P_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{\overline{p}}(x)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x=y+\lambda\nabla\widetilde{g}_{\overline{p}}(y)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x=y+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{p}_{i}(2Qy+b_{i})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x=(\operatorname{Id}+2\lambda Q)y+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{p}_{i}b_{i}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow y=(\operatorname{Id}+2\lambda Q)^{-1}\left(x-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{p}_{i}b_{i}\right)

where

and

is the linear operator defined by

. Let us compute

with

. By (

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{g}_{p}(P_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{p}(x))+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|x-P_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{p}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\langle C(x-\lambda Bp),QC(x-\lambda Bp)\rangle+\langle b_{i},C(x-\lambda Bp)\rangle+c_{i})+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\|x-C(x-\lambda Bp)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=:\ell_{1}(p,x)+\ell_{2}(p,x).

Let

. Then the first term in (

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(p,x)

\displaystyle=\langle C(x-\lambda Bp),QC(x-\lambda Bp)\rangle+\langle Bp,C(x-\lambda Bp)\rangle+p^{\top}c

\displaystyle+\langle Bp,Cx\rangle-\lambda\langle p,B^{*}CBp\rangle+p^{\top}c

\displaystyle=\langle Cx,QCx\rangle+\lambda^{2}\langle p,(CB)^{*}QCBp\rangle-\lambda\langle p,B^{*}CBp\rangle

On the other hand, the second term in (

\displaystyle\ell_{2}(p,x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\|(\operatorname{Id}-C)x+\lambda CBp\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|(\operatorname{Id}-C)x\|^{2}+\langle(\operatorname{Id}-C)x,CBp\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|CBp\|^{2}.

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\|(\operatorname{Id}-C)x\|^{2}+\langle Bp,C(\operatorname{Id}-C)x\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\langle p,(CB)^{*}CBp\rangle.

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}\widetilde{g}_{p}(x)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|(\operatorname{Id}-C)x\|^{2}+\langle Cx,QCx\rangle-p^{\top}\gamma-\langle p,Mp\rangle,

3.5
Dual method

In this section, we use the techniques proposed in
[
14
, Section 4.2]
for solving problem (
7
) for the ambiguity sets considered in the cases
(I)(i)
,
(I)(ii)
, and
(I)(iii)
. In each case, we reformulate the dual of the following inner maximization problem

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}h(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}f_{i}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)).

(59)

Observe that by assumption of each case, we have that the problem (
59
) is a convex problem that satisfy the Slater conditions. Therefore, the strong duality hold.

Case
(I)(i)
: In this case
\mathcal{P}=\Delta_{N}
and the Lagrangian function is

\displaystyle L(p,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x))+\lambda(1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i})

\displaystyle=\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda).

Then, (
59
) is equivalent to the following dual problem

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\max_{p\geq 0}\left\{\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda)\right\}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\lambda+\max_{p\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda)\right\}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t. }f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Thus, problem (
7
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{H},\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda

(60)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

\displaystyle\quad\,\,x\in Q

Case
(I)(ii)
: In this case
\mathcal{P}=\{p\in\Delta_{N}\,:\,p\leq q\}
and hence the Lagrangian function is

\displaystyle L(p,\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x))+\lambda(1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}(q_{i}-p_{i})

\displaystyle=\lambda+\mu^{\top}q+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\mu_{i}-\lambda).

Then, from strong duality, (
59
) is equivalent to the following dual problem

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\\
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\end{subarray}}\max_{p\geq 0}\left\{\lambda+\mu^{\top}q+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\mu_{i}-\lambda)\right\}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\\
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\end{subarray}}\lambda+\mu^{\top}q

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t. }f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\mu_{i}-\lambda\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Thus, problem (
7
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{H},\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\\
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\end{subarray}}\lambda+\mu^{\top}q

(61)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\mu_{i}-\lambda\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

\displaystyle\quad\,\,x\in Q

Case
(I)(iii)
: Here
\mathcal{P}=\{p\in\Delta_{N}\,:\,\mu_{-}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\xi_{i}\leq\mu_{+}\}
and hence the Lagrangian function is

\displaystyle L(p,\lambda,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x))+\lambda(1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i})+\beta(\mu_{+}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\xi_{i})+\gamma(-\mu_{-}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\xi_{i})

\displaystyle=\lambda+\beta\mu_{+}-\gamma\mu_{-}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda+(\gamma-\beta)\xi_{i}).

Therefore, from strong duality, (
59
) is equivalent to the following dual problem

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\\
\beta\geq 0,\gamma\geq 0\end{subarray}}\max_{p\geq 0}\left\{\lambda+\beta\mu_{+}-\gamma\mu_{-}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda+(\gamma-\beta)\xi_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\\
\beta\geq 0,\gamma\geq 0\end{subarray}}\lambda+\beta\mu_{+}-\gamma\mu_{-}

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t. }f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda+\gamma\xi_{i}-\beta\xi_{i}\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Thus, problem (
7
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{H},\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\\
\beta\geq 0,\gamma\geq 0\end{subarray}}\lambda+\beta\mu_{+}-\gamma\mu_{-}

(62)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }f_{i}(x)+h(x)-\lambda+\gamma\xi_{i}-\beta\xi_{i}\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

\displaystyle\quad\,\,x\in Q

4.1
Couette inverse problem

Consider a rheometer of coaxial cylinders with fluid. Let
\Omega\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
representing the angular velocity of the inner cylinder. The function
\Omega
has the following form
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\Omega(\tau)=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\beta\tau}^{\tau}\dfrac{f(t)}{t}dt,

where
\tau
is the tangential shear stress on the cylindrical surface,
f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the rheological curve of the fluid, and
\beta<1
. Given
\Omega\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the Couette inverse problem consists in to find the function
f
. Suppose that we have a sample of size
r
of values
(\Omega_{i},\tau_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
representing measurements obtained from the rheometer. Assume that the function
f
has the form
f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{j}e_{j}(t)
, where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
e_{j}
is a function such that
t\mapsto\frac{e_{j}(t)}{t}
is integrable for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
. Given
(\Omega_{i},\tau_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
, the problem is to find the constants
(a_{j})_{j=1}^{\ell}
such that

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{j}\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\beta\tau_{i}}^{\tau_{i}}\dfrac{e_{j}(t)}{t}dt,\quad\text{for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,r\},

which is equivalent to
\Omega=Ax
, where
\Omega=(\Omega_{i})_{i=1}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times\ell}
is the matrix defined by
A_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\beta\tau_{i}}^{\tau_{i}}\frac{e_{j}(t)}{t}dt
, and
x=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{\ell}
. It is possible that the system
Ax=\Omega
has no solutions or that it has several solutions. In these cases, a common approach to finding an approximate solution is to solve the following regularized least squares problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\|Ax-\Omega\|^{2}+\lambda R(x),

(63)

where
R
is the regularization function which represents a prior information on
x
and
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter. In the case when
R(x)=\|Dx\|^{2}
, where
D\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\ell}
and assuming that
A^{\top}A+\lambda D^{\top}D
is positive definite (for example, if
D=\operatorname{Id}
), the Fermat’s rule implies that the solution to (
63
) is

\displaystyle\overline{x}=(A^{\top}A+\lambda D^{\top}D)^{-1}A^{\top}\Omega.

Suppose now that we have
N
samples of size
r
of values
(\Omega^{1}_{i})_{i=1}^{r},\ldots,(\Omega^{N}_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
. Let
\xi\colon\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\Xi
the random vector defined on a measurable space
(\overline{\Omega},\mathcal{A})
whose values are in the finite set
\Xi=\{\Omega^{1},\ldots,\Omega^{N}\}
and let
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta_{N}
a nonempty closed convex subset of probability measures on the space
(\overline{\Omega},\mathcal{A})
supported on
\Xi
. For every
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we denote
p_{k}=\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\in\overline{\Omega}\,:\,\xi(\omega)=\Omega^{k}\})
. Thus, we consider the following DRO problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}\|Ax-\Omega^{k}\|^{2}+\lambda\|Dx\|^{2}.

(64)

Note that, since
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta_{N}
, problem (
64
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\|Ax\|^{2}+\lambda\|Dx\|^{2}+\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(-2x^{\top}A^{\top}\Omega^{k}+\|\Omega^{k}\|^{2}),

which is equivalent to problem (
12
) when
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
,
h(x)=\|Ax\|^{2}+\lambda\|Dx\|^{2}
(which has Lipschitz gradient),
a_{k}=-2A^{\top}\Omega^{k}
, and
\xi_{k}=\|\Omega^{k}\|^{2}
.

Note that if
D=\operatorname{Id}
, then the problem (
64
) reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\dfrac{1}{2\mu}\|x\|^{2}+\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(\langle x,Qx\rangle+\langle b_{k},x\rangle+c_{k}),

(65)

where
Q=A^{\top}A
,
b_{k}=-2A^{\top}\Omega^{k}
,
c_{k}=\|\Omega^{k}\|^{2}
, and
\mu=\frac{1}{2\lambda}
. The problem (
65
) is equivalent to find
P_{\mu}\widetilde{f}(0)
, where
\widetilde{f}
is defined in (
30
). Thus, (
65
) can be solved using the algorithm proposed in Proposition
2.4
.

4.2
Denoising

Suppose that we have a noisy measurement of a signal
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle b=x+w,

where
x
is an unknown signal,
w
is an unknown noise vector, and
b
is the known measurement
vector. The denoising problem is the following: Given
b
, find a good estimate of
x
. The regularized least squares problem associated with this problem is

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|x-b\|^{2}+\lambda R(x),

(66)

where
R(x)
is a regularization term which represents some a priori information on the signal and
\lambda>0
is a given regularization parameter. Typically, it is considered a regularization function of the form

\displaystyle R(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_{i}-x_{i+1})^{2}.

Note that
R(x)=\|Lx\|^{2}
, where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
is given by

\displaystyle L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&-1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&1&-1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

In this case, by the Fermat’s rule, we have that the solution to (
66
) is given by

\displaystyle\overline{x}=(\operatorname{Id}+\lambda L^{\top}L)^{-1}b.

Suppose that we have
N
noisy measurement
b^{1},\ldots,b^{N}
:

\displaystyle b^{i}=x^{i}+w^{i},

where, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an unknown signal and
w^{i}
is an unknown noise vector. The objective is to find a good estimate of
x^{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
in order to minimize the largest norm of the noise vectors. Thus, we consider the following robust optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}}\left\{\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|L_{j}x^{j}\|^{2}+\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|x^{i}-b^{i}\|^{2}\right\},

(67)

where
\|L_{j}x^{j}\|^{2}
represents the regularization term of the variable
x^{j}
. The problem (
67
) is particular case of problem (
11
) when
V=\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
H(\mathbf{x})=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|L_{j}x^{j}\|^{2}
, and
\xi_{i}=b^{i}
. Note that
\nabla H(\mathbf{x})=2\lambda(L_{1}^{\top}L_{1}x^{1},\ldots,L_{N}^{\top}L_{N}x^{N})
. Then, for every
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{nN}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla H(\mathbf{x})-\nabla H(\mathbf{y})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|2\lambda L_{j}^{\top}L_{j}(x^{j}-y^{j})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(2\lambda)^{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\|L_{j}^{\top}L_{j}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}.

Hence
\nabla H
is
\beta^{-1}
-Lipschitz with
\beta^{-1}=2\lambda\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\|L_{j}^{\top}L_{j}\|
.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we provide different splitting algorithms for solving the discrete version of the distributionally robust optimization problem. This problem includes a supremum function in the objective function. The first method is based on calculating the proximity operator of the supremum function. In some cases, we propose an algorithm that converges to the proximity operator, while in a particular case, we provide a closed form for the proximity operator. On the other hand, under qualifications conditions, we prove that the problem is equivalent to solving a monotone inclusion that involves the sum of finitely many monotone operators, and we compute the resolvent of the monotone operators involved in the inclusion. The second method uses an algorithm specialized for that type of inclusion, which is proposed in
[
1
]
. Additionally, we reformulate the inclusion as one that involves two monotone operators and the normal cone to a vector subspace. For solving this reformulation, we use the algorithm proposed in
[
6
]
(third method) and the algorithm proposed in
[
9
]
(fourth method).

The proposed algorithms can be applied to solve the Couette inverse problem with uncertainty and the denoising problem with uncertainty. In addition, we prove the efficiency of the algorithms in two particular problems. The first numerical experiment shows that the algorithm which computes the proximity of the supremum function (proximal algorithm) is the most efficient when the dimension of the problem and the size of the uncertainty set are small, whereas the third and fourth methods are more efficient when the dimension of the problem is larger. On the other hand, the second numerical experiment shows that the proximal algorithm is more efficient when the uncertainty set is small, while the method proposed in the literature performs better in other cases.
