Superlearner can predict in-hospital mortality risk in critically ill patients with ischemic stroke: development and international validation
Authors: Xiaolong Hu, Shifei Ye, Rongguo Hu, Suya Li, Peng Li, Yibin Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritically ill patients with ischemic stroke face substantial in-hospital mortality. Early and accurate prediction of mortality risk may facilitate timely risk stratification and improve intensive care management. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a machine learning–based predictive model using multicenter datasets.MethodsWe extracted data from MIMIC-IV (n = 3,568), eICU-CRD (n = 2,535), and Tongji University Hospital (TJUH, n = 144). Eight predictors identified through multivariable logistic regression were used for model construction. Five algorithms—Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, Logistic Regression, and an ensemble SuperLearner—were trained and evaluated. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and calibration. DeLong’s test compared the AUROC of the SuperLearner with those of the four base models. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate clinical utility.ResultsThe SuperLearner demonstrated the strongest discrimination, with AUROCs of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75–0.85) in MIMIC-IV, 0.82 (95% CI: 0.80–0.84) in eICU-CRD, and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.64–0.87) in TJUH. Sensitivity and specificity ranged from 0.70 to 0.83 and 0.68–0.82 across datasets, outperforming the four baseline models (all p < 0.05). Calibration curves showed reasonable agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, although deviations were noted at probability extremes. DCA indicated net clinical benefit across a wide range of thresholds, while slight fluctuations suggest potential overestimation at higher thresholds.ConclusionsThe SuperLearner model achieved robust and consistent predictive performance across three independent cohorts. While showing potential utility for early mortality risk estimation in critically ill ischemic stroke patients, its clinical application requires further prospective validation and real-world implementation studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03351-1.Keywords: Intensive care units, Machine learning, Mortality prediction, Ischemic stroke, External validation

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) accounts for 70% of all strokes and carries a high risk of long-term recurrence [1, 2]. Research indicates that from 1990 to 2019, global IS-related deaths increased from 2.04 million to 3.29 million, representing 50.3% of all stroke-related deaths and 17.7% of all cardiovascular-related deaths [3–5]. By 2030, IS-related deaths are projected to further increase to 4.9 million [6]. Critically ill ischemic stroke patients experience high mortality and disability rates. Therefore, accurately predicting in-hospital mortality risk is crucial for risk stratification, optimizing ICU resource allocation, guiding individualized monitoring strategies, and improving prognostic communication.

Currently, clinical practice commonly employs ICU scoring systems such as APACHE-II and SOFA scores to assess illness severity [7–9]. However, these are general ICU severity scores and not disease-specific, which limits their predictive value in ischemic stroke patients [10]. Although a recent study has developed and validated a 30-day mortality prediction model for ICU patients with ischemic stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage [11], such models remain scarce, relatively recent, and limited to specific cohorts. Most existing studies investigating stroke mortality have focused on general hospitalized or community-based populations, often evaluating short-term outcomes (e.g., 30-day mortality or functional recovery). However, few have specifically addressed critically ill ischemic stroke patients requiring intensive care, who represent a distinct high-risk subgroup with complex management needs.To date, there is still no widely validated, disease-specific prediction model for in-hospital mortality risk in critically ill ischemic stroke patients, underscoring the need for our study.

Additionally, while machine learning algorithms have demonstrated stronger predictive performance compared to traditional statistical methods in risk prediction research [12, 13], challenges remain regarding model generalizability, applicability, interpretability, and implementation [14, 15].

In this study, we aimed to develop an ensemble learning algorithm-based prediction model for in-hospital mortality risk in critically ill ischemic stroke patients using variables from electronic health records in the MIMIC-IV dataset. We then validated the model’s performance using international datasets from America and Asia. Finally, we constructed a web-based calculator based on the Superlearner algorithm model to facilitate mortality risk assessment and risk stratification in critically ill ischemic stroke patients.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Office of the Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Tongji University (TJUH) under the title “Superlearner can predict in-hospital mortality risk in critically ill patients: development and international validation” in March 2025 (approve number: 2025023-001). Informed consent was waived due to the minimal risk associated with this study. This research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975.

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV (version 3.1) is publicly available and has received Institutional Review Board approval from both the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA, USA (2011-P-001699/14) and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, MA, USA (0403000206) [16]. The eICU-Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) is also publicly available and has received Institutional Review Board approval from 208 hospitals across the United States [17].

Study population

This study incorporated data from three research centers: The TJIH cohort, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD.The TJUH cohort data was collected from patients admitted to the Neurological Intensive Care Unit (NICU) at TJUH between January 2024 and December 2024. MIMIC-IV includes data from over 65,000 patients admitted to an ICU and more than 200,000 patients admitted to the emergency department at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston. The eICU-CRD contains data from 139,367 patients admitted to 335 units across 208 hospitals in the United States between 2014 and 2015.The inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study were as follows:

Inclusion criteria: Patients aged ≥ 18 years with a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke (ICD-10 codes I63.0–I63.9) confirmed by CT or MRI imaging and admitted to the ICU were included.

Exclusion criteria: 1) Stroke mimics, ischemic stroke secondary to aortic dissection, and ischemic stroke with hemorrhagic transformation were excluded to ensure diagnostic specificity;3) Length of ICU stay < 3 h; 4) Non-first-time ICU admission; 5) Patients with active malignancy were excluded. 6)Patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), defined as chronic dialysis-dependent renal failure, were excluded due to their disproportionately high non–stroke-related in-hospital mortality. These conditions are associated with disproportionately high non–stroke-related in-hospital mortality, which could confound the identification of stroke-specific predictors (Fig. 1).

Data collection and preprocessing

A total of 36 candidate variables were initially selected by clinical experts, and 30 were included for further analysis (Supplementary Table S1). All predictor variables were collected at a standardized early time point. Demographic characteristics were recorded at ICU admission. Laboratory variables and physiological measurements were extracted from the first 24 h following ICU admission, using the earliest available values. Severity scores (SAPS II, OASIS, and GCS) were calculated based on data obtained during this same period. This standardized approach ensured temporal consistency across patients and minimized the risk of information leakage. Missing data were handled as follows: variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded, while those with less than 20% missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with the random forest algorithm in R.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of this study was in-hospital all-cause mortality during the index hospitalization, defined as death occurring at any time from ICU admission until hospital discharge. Patients who survived to discharge were considered alive at the end of hospitalization, and no fixed follow-up window (e.g., 30 or 90 days) was applied. To ensure consistency across datasets, uniform definitions were applied. In the MIMIC-IV dataset, in-hospital mortality was determined using the hospital_expire_flag. In the eICU-CRD dataset, mortality status was extracted from the hospitaldischargestatus field. In the TJUH dataset, mortality labels were confirmed from hospital records and death certificates. This approach ensured a consistent definition of in-hospital mortality across all three cohorts.

Model construction

We trained four different base models, including Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Logistic Regression (LR), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Hyperparameter optimization was performed using grid search methodology combined with cross-validation to determine the optimal hyperparameters (supplementary materials Table S2).

Based on ensemble learning algorithms, each base model was initially trained independently, allowing each model to generate prediction results on the training data. The model fusion method employed was Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS), a technique from Stacking Generalization. NNLS was used to combine the prediction results from each base model while assigning weights to each model, minimizing the prediction error of the ensemble model. The final prediction result of the ensemble model represents a weighted average of the base models’ predictions (Fig. 2). Several regularization techniques were implemented to prevent model overfitting, including L1/L2 regularization, early stopping, weight decay, feature sampling, and data sampling. The final ML models, including the SuperLearner, were developed based on eight predictor variables identified through multivariable logistic regression analysis: age, weight, SAPS II, OASIS, GCS, white blood cell count, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), and fasting blood glucose (FBG).

Performance evaluation and statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0) and R 3.4.3. Variables with less than 20% missing data were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). Cases with missing outcome information were excluded from analysis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted to assess the normality of quantitative data. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± SD if normally distributed, while median and interquartile range (IQR) was provided for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies or percentages, with differences analyzed using Bonferroni correction. For non-normally distributed quantitative data, comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The reported statistical significance levels were all two-sided, with statistical significance set at 0.05. Feature selection was performed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, with in-hospital mortality as the binary outcome. Variables with p < 0.05 in multivariate analysis were retained as significant predictors, and the corresponding odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Eight parameters were selected for inclusion in the final model construction.

This study incorporated three datasets: MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and TJUH. The MIMIC-IV data was divided into training and testing datasets at an 8:2 ratio. The training dataset was used for model training, while the testing dataset was used for model validation. The eICU-CRD and TJUH datasets served as two independent external validation sets to verify model performance. We used Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUROC) to measure model discrimination performance. Since the study endpoint was binary (in-hospital mortality), AUROC was used as the standard discrimination metric, which is equivalent to the concordance index (C-index) for binary outcomes. DeLong’s test was employed to examine whether there were statistically significant differences in the Area Under the Curve (AUC) between the SuperLearner model and the other four base models. We evaluated the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of each model within the dataset. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was utilized to assess the clinical effectiveness of the predictive models, while calibration curves were employed to evaluate their accuracy in predicting absolute risk.

Interpretable analysis

As the SuperLearner model is a black-box model with complex internal mechanisms, direct interpretation is challenging. To address this issue, we conducted a grouped analysis of prediction results and examined standardized mean differences of covariates to interpret the model’s predictive behavior. First, all continuous variables were standardized. The dataset was then stratified into quartiles based on the SuperLearner model’s predictive scores. For each quartile, we calculated the mean value of each variable to describe its distribution across different risk groups, thereby facilitating model interpretation.

Figure 6 illustrates the performance of the SuperLearner model variables across the MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and TJUH datasets. Variables including age, FBG, OASIS, RDW, SAPS II, and WBC showed a consistent monotonic increasing trend across datasets, indicating that higher values were associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality (Fig. 6). Discussion

In this study, we identified eight parameters, including age and weight, significantly associated with in-hospital mortality risk in severe cerebral infarction patients using traditional statistical analysis. We developed four base models and a super learner model using ensemble learning algorithms, which accurately predicted in-hospital mortality risk. The superior-performing super learner model was further analyzed for interpretability and deployed as an online web application via Shiny apps, enabling healthcare professionals to assess mortality risk through simple parameter input.

In this study, we initially employed univariate COX regression analysis to identify prognostic factors. However, recognizing the limitations of univariate analysis [18] in controlling confounding variables, we subsequently performed multivariate COX regression analysis. This revealed that age, weight, SAPS II, OASIS, GCS, WBC, RDW, and FBG were independently associated with in-hospital mortality.

Traditional statistical methods are inadequate for handling complex nonlinear relationships among variables in large datasets. To address this, we successfully developed machine learning models using artificial intelligence algorithms [19–21]. Notably, unlike existing risk assessment models [22–24], this study established the first disease-specific mortality prediction model for severe cerebral infarction patients.

In this study, we developed four base models (RF, LR, XGBoost, and LightGBM) and constructed a super learner model using ensemble learning algorithms [25]. The super learner architecture consists of two layers: the first layer includes the four base models, and the second layer employs Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS) [26] from Stacking Generalization. NNLS, a widely used meta-model training method, ensures the rationality and interpretability of model integration through non-negative constraints.

The super learner model integrates information from different base models through cross-validation (CV) [27, 28], leveraging the strengths of each model while mitigating biases inherent in single-model approaches, thereby enhancing overall predictive performance. We evaluated the model’s performance using multiple methods. Delong’s test was employed to compare the AUC values between the super learner model and the four base models, revealing that the super learner model’s AUC was significantly higher (P < 0.05).Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to compare the standardized net benefits of different models, demonstrating that the super learner model consistently outperformed the four base models.We also utilized calibration curves to assess the consistency between predicted and actual probabilities across different datasets. The super learner model exhibited higher consistency and superior overall performance compared to the base models.We validated the super learner model’s performance using multicenter datasets from both America and Asia. The model demonstrated stable and excellent performance, indicating strong generalization capabilities.

Machine learning algorithms are often considered “black-box” models [29], making it challenging to interpret their predictions. In this study, we innovatively conducted interpretability analysis on the super learner. Patients were stratified into four risk groups based on the quartiles of the super learner’s prediction scores, and the distribution of variables across these groups was analyzed.Across different datasets, we observed that age, FBG, OASIS, RDW, SAPS, and WBC exhibited a monotonically increasing relationship with in-hospital mortality risk, while GCS scores showed a monotonically decreasing relationship.Yao et al. [30] reported that elevated FBG is associated with poor prognosis in acute ischemic stroke patients. This may be attributed to high blood glucose levels impairing endothelial-derived nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, reducing reperfusion in the infarcted area, increasing infarct volume, and worsening prognosis.You et al. [31] reported that elevated admission WBC levels are associated with prognosis in acute ischemic stroke patients. This aligns with our findings, as higher FBG and WBC levels were linked to increased in-hospital mortality risk.

Additionally, we deployed the model as a web application via the Shiny platform. By inputting key parameters (e.g., age, weight), clinicians can easily assess in-hospital mortality risk for severe cerebral infarction patients. This tool provides a convenient and intuitive risk assessment method, facilitating early identification of high-risk patients and enabling personalized treatment strategies.

Importantly, the clinical utility of our model should be clearly defined. This model is not intended to guide acute-phase therapeutic decisions such as reperfusion therapy, which remain primarily determined by clinical status and imaging findings. Instead, its value lies in the critical care setting. First, the model enables early risk stratification of severe cerebral infarction patients, allowing clinicians to tailor the intensity of monitoring and supportive care according to individual risk profiles. Second, it facilitates more rational allocation of limited ICU resources, such as mechanical ventilation and nursing intensity, by identifying patients at highest risk of in-hospital mortality. Third, the model provides an objective tool for prognostic communication with patients and families, supporting shared decision-making in critical care. Finally, this model may serve as a risk-adjustment instrument in clinical research and multicenter trials.

Traditional stroke prognostic scores such as PLAN, iScore, GWTG–Stroke, and MISS have shown good performance in predicting short-term outcomes among general ischemic stroke populations. However, these tools were not designed for critically ill ICU patients and rely on clinical variables such as NIHSS and imaging findings that were unavailable in the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD datasets. Our model therefore complements these existing approaches by focusing specifically on ICU-based mortality prediction.

This study has several limitations. First, not all potentially relevant variables were included due to database constraints, raising the possibility of residual bias. Specifically, data on treatment interventions (e.g., intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy) and stroke severity measures such as the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) were unavailable in the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD databases. We have acknowledged the absence of NIHSS data in the Limitations section and clarified that future prospective studies will include this key neurological measure to enhance the clinical interpretability of the model. Furthermore, we plan to incorporate detailed therapeutic information and dynamic NIHSS changes in our ongoing prospective TJUH cohort to further refine and validate the model’s clinical applicability.Second, model performance was only reported at a single threshold without evaluation across multiple cut-offs, prevalence-adjusted positive/negative predictive values, or decision curve analysis under alternative event rates.Third, the exclusion of patients with active malignancy or end-stage renal disease, while clinically justified to focus on stroke-specific mortality drivers, limits the generalizability of our findings to the entire ischemic stroke population and may introduce selection bias.Finally, the static model used in this study cannot capture the dynamic evolution of patients’ physiological states during hospitalization, and thus cannot provide real-time prediction of mortality risk in critically ill stroke patients. Future research should aim to develop machine learning models based on temporal data to achieve real-time, dynamic risk assessment for critically ill patients.

Conclusion

We developed a SuperLearner model using the MIMIC-IV database and externally validated its performance across multicenter datasets from both the United States and China. The model achieved strong and stable predictive performance for in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with ischemic stroke. Furthermore, we implemented the model as a web-based application, which may serve as a practical decision-support tool for ICU clinicians, pending further prospective validation.
