Andreev crystals in hybrid Josephson junction arrays
Anders Enevold Dahl, Andrea Maiani, Max Geier, Javad Shabani, Karsten Flensberg

Abstract
Andreev bound states are superpositions of electrons and holes in a metal that form by coherent reflection from a superconductor. When the length of the superconductor is comparable to the superconducting coherence length, Andreev bound states at opposite edges hybridize by quasiparticle tunneling. In a periodic array, these hybridized bound states form a band at energies below the superconducting gap. In this paper, we derive a theoretical framework for the transport properties of these Andreev crystals. We demonstrate that at high interface transparency and constant phase bias between neighboring superconductors, these bands are directional : one band consists only of right- while the other only of left-moving electronic states. This property enables the application of this device as a flux- and bias-voltage tunable filter that permits signal transmission in only one direction.



I
Introduction

Andreev reflection is a fundamental process in hybrid heterostructures, occurring at the interface between a superconductor and a metal, where an incident electron from the metal is retro-reflected as a hole or vice versa
Blonder et al. 1982
. At the same time, a Cooper pair is added to the superconducting condensate. With multiple superconducting interfaces, consecutive Andreev reflections can combine constructively and generate Andreev bound states (ABS) which are localized subgap states whose energy depends on the phases of various superconducting leads
Beenakker 1991
;
Beenakker and van Houten 1992
;
Johannsen and Schrade 2025
. The ABSs mediate the transfer of Cooper pairs across the junctions and there is a direct relation between the ABS spectrum and the current-phase relation (CPR). This relationship makes it possible to engineer Josephson junctions with tailored CPRs by designing and controlling the ABS spectrum. Such control over the CPR opens new possibilities for creating customized superconducting devices for quantum technology
Larsen et al. 2015
;
Casparis et al. 2018
;
Aguado 2020
;
Banerjee et al. 2023a
;
Banerjee et al. 2023b
;
Strickland et al. 2024
;
Coraiola et al. 2023

In recent years, the development of hybrid superconductor-semiconductor structures has revolutionized the field of superconducting electronics
Ingla-Aynés et al. 2025
. These heterostructures incorporate semiconductor nanostructures in contact with superconductors, allowing the creation of proximitized low-dimensional electron gases with tunable density and spin-orbit coupling
Moehle et al. 2021
. One of the remarkable features of such heterostructures is the long coherence length due to the low Fermi velocity in the semiconductor. The long coherence length significantly increases the probability amplitude of crossed Andreev reflection
Leijnse and Flensberg 2013
, a process where an electron from one point of a superconductor is converted into a hole at a different point. This process is fundamental for applications like Cooper pair splitters
Hofstetter et al. 2009
;
Hofstetter et al. 2011
;
Schindele et al. 2012
;
Herrmann et al. 2010
or poor man’s Majorana bound states
Leijnse and Flensberg 2012
;
Sau et al. 2010
;
Fulga et al. 2013
;
Dvir et al. 2023
;
ten Haaf et al. 2024
;
Escribano et al. 2025
, to name a few.

Another consequence of crossed Andreev reflection in hybrid heterostructures is the possibility of ABS hybridizing in different but neighboring semiconductor junctions, leading to so-called Andreev molecules
Pillet et al. 2019
;
Pillet et al. 2020
;
Haxell et al. 2023
;
Matsuo et al. 2023a
;
Matsuo et al. 2023b
. Andreev molecules represent an interesting new regime where the ABSs become extended over multiple junctions, thus creating a coherent coupling between distant superconductors.

Building on this concept, we propose a further step: creating periodic nanostructures where superconducting segments are arranged in a regular lattice. In such a system, the hybridization of ABS can occur not just between adjacent junctions but over an entire array of superconducting segments. The result is the formation of Andreev bands, similar to Bloch bands in crystalline solids, hence leading to what we define as
Andreev crystals

Rouco et al. 2021a
;
Rouco et al. 2021b
. Note that such hybridization between ABSs in adjacent junctions is the defining feature of the Andreev crystal and what differentiates this structure from conventional Josephson junction arrays.

In Ref.
Lykkegaard et al. 2025
, short ABS arrays were studied experimentally in the linear response regime. Here we study the finite-bias properties which allow for direct observation of the tunability of left- and right-mover bands. We present a theoretical framework for describing the physics of Andreev crystals and explore key phenomena such as nonlocal transport, which arises due to the coherent coupling between distant superconducting segments and the nonlocal Josephson effects.

As a key application, we show that high-transparency ABSs can operate as a directional signal filter between voltage-biased metals. The fundamental principle is that, in high-transparency junctions, left- and right-moving electrons give rise to different branches of the ABS spectrum. When different left and right dc bias voltages are set, transmission of charge is possible along one direction but suppressed along the other.

II.1
Scattering matrix approach

We use a scattering matrix approach to model the hybrid Josephson junction array sketched in Fig.
1
(a). The model is constructed by consecutive concatenation of normal-superconductor-normal (NSN) segments. We start by considering the clean case where an electron can undergo only Andreev reflection or transmission through the superconductor and later include backscattering in the normal regions. The scattering matrix for the superconducting segment takes the form

\displaystyle S_{S}(\omega)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
0 & r_{eh}(\omega) & t_{ee}(\omega) & 0 \\
r_{he}(\omega) & 0 & 0 & t_{hh}(\omega) \\
t_{ee}(\omega) & 0 & 0 & r_{eh}(\omega) \\
0 & t_{hh}(\omega) & r_{he}(\omega) & 0\endlx@physics@matrix),

(1)

where
r_{he}
(
r_{eh}
) denotes the amplitude for an electron (hole) undergoing Andreev retroreflection as a hole (electron), while
t_{ee}
(
t_{hh}
) represents the amplitude for an electron (hole) being transmitted through the superconductor.

The scattering matrix satisfies particle-hole symmetry,
\mathcal{P}S(\omega)\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}=S(-\omega)
with the particle-hole symmetry operator being
\mathcal{P}=i\tau_{y}\mathcal{K}
. Here,
\mathcal{K}
is complex conjugation, and
\tau_{i}
is a Pauli operator acting in particle-hole space. This constraint reduces the number of free parameters because
r_{eh}(\omega)=-r^{*}_{he}(-\omega)
and
t_{hh}=t^{*}_{ee}(-\omega)

Maiani et al. 2022
.

By matching the Bogoliubov-de Gennes wave function at the normal-superconductor interfaces, see Appendix.
A.4
, we find

\displaystyle r_{he}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}(x)}{1-r_{\Delta}^{2}(x)t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}(x)}r_{\Delta}(x)e^{-i\phi},

(2a)

\displaystyle t_{ee}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r_{\Delta}^{2}(x)}{1-r_{\Delta}^{2}(x)t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}(x)}t_{\mathrm{S}}(x)e^{ik_{\mathrm{F}S}l_{S}},

(2b)

where
\phi
is the superconducting phase,
l_{S}
is the width of the superconductor,
k_{\mathrm{F}S}
is the Fermi wave number, and we defined
r_{\Delta}
as the bare Andreev reflection amplitude, and
t_{S}
as the bare electron tunneling amplitude. Both functions depend on the ratio,
x\equiv\frac{\omega}{\Delta}
. In deriving Eqs. (
2
), we have used the Andreev approximation, which amounts to linearizing the energy dispersion.

The bare Andreev reflection amplitude is given by

\displaystyle r_{\Delta}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-i\arccos(x)},

which is the phase acquired in the reflection for a particle in a normal metal to semi-infinite superconductor interface
Beenakker and van Houten 1991
. The bare electron co-tunneling amplitude is

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{S}}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{iq(x)l_{s}},\ \;\quad\quad\quad q^{-1}(x)\equiv\frac{\pi\xi_{0}}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}},

(3)

where
q^{-1}
is the penetration length, which is an energy-dependent renormalization of the coherence length of the BCS theory,
\xi_{0}={\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}}/{\pi\Delta_{0}}
.

II.2
Non-perfect transmission in semiconductor

We incorporate the charge-conserving backscattering into the normal semiconducting junction segment, which we describe by a scattering matrix of the form

\displaystyle S_{N}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \quad r & 0 & t & 0 \\
0 & \quad r & 0 & t \\
t & 0 & -r & 0 \\
0 & t & 0 & -r\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

In the short-junction limit, the coefficients are energy independent and can be parametrized as
t=\sqrt{T}
and
r=\sqrt{1-T}
where
T\in[0,1]
denotes the junction transparency. Combining the scattering matrices for the superconducting and normal regions, see details in Appendix
A.1
, we find the full scattering matrix for a given device geometry. This construction assumes the short-junction limit, neglecting the phase accumulated during propagation through the semiconductor. For simplicity, we also neglect backscattering within the superconducting segments, which allows disorder to be captured by a single phenomenological transparency parameter.

II.3
Proximity effect

In the above expressions, the superconducting segments are described by having constant pairing potentials
\Delta
. In the actual proposed hybrid devices, the superconducting correlations in the semiconductor are proximity-induced into the semiconductor by the contact to the superconductors, as shown in Fig.
1
(a). In this setup, the virtual tunneling in and out of the superconductor then gives rise to a self-energy term of the form
Stanescu et al. 2010
;
Sau et al. 2010
;
Danon and Flensberg 2015
;
Hansen et al. 2016

\displaystyle\Sigma(\omega,\Delta_{0})=\Gamma\frac{-\omega+\Delta_{0}\tau_{x}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2}}},

where
\Gamma
is the coupling strength between the superconductor and semiconductor (proportional to the square of the tunneling amplitude and the normal density of states in the superconductor shell),
\Delta_{0}
is the gap of the parent superconductor, and
\omega
is the quasiparticle energy. The coupling to the superconducting segments thus modifies the quasiparticle spectrum in the semiconductor by a renormalized excitation energy and an effective gap described by

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\omega\quantity( 1 + \frac{\Gamma}{\sqrt{\Delta_0^2 - \omega^2}} ),

(4)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{0}\Gamma}{\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2}}}.

(5)

The renormalization and the energy-dependent gap parameter in Eq. (
4
) and (
5
) alter the dimensionless ratio between energy and the gap,
\omega/\Delta
, which can be incorporated into the results in the previous section by the replacement

\displaystyle x\rightarrow x(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{\Delta_{0}}\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\Gamma}\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta_{0}^{2}}}\right).

(6)

The proximity-induced renormalization increases the effective gap at low energies, while the correction vanishes as
\omega\to\Delta_{0}
where
\Gamma
plays the role of the pairing.

An important consequence of this energy dependence is the renormalization of the quasiparticle penetration length
q^{-1}
into the superconducting region. Since the coherence length is tied to the effective induced gap, it also becomes energy dependent. One notes that the renormalized decay length,
q^{-1}
, is:

\displaystyle q^{-1}\rightarrow q^{-1}=\frac{-i\pi\hbar v_{F}}{\pi\Delta(\omega)\sqrt{1-x^{2}({\omega})}},

(7)

which simplifies in the low-energy limit to

\displaystyle q^{-1}\xrightarrow{\omega\rightarrow 0}q^{-1}=-i\pi\xi_{\Gamma},\quad\text{with}\quad\xi_{\Gamma}=\frac{\hbar v_{F}}{\pi\Gamma}.

(8)

The renormalized energy in Eq. (
6
) is larger than the bare energy
\omega/\Delta_{0}
and, in fact, becomes larger than one at a critical value at the edge of the induced minigap in the semiconductor. For energies above this value,
q^{-1}
is real, because the solutions are in continuum with plane wave solutions rather than evanescent waves. See section
III.2
for a discussion of this in the context of the Andreev crystal.

Fig.
2
displays the normal transmission and Andreev–reflection probability,
T_{ee}=|t_{ee}|^{2}
and
R_{he}=|r_{he}|^{2}
, respectively, obtained from Eq. (
2
) with the renormalized decay length in Eq. (
7
) as a function of the superconducting segment length
l_{S}
for three representative excitation energies. For ultrashort segments,
l_{S}\ll\xi_{0}
, the quasiparticle traverses the proximitized region almost unimpeded, yielding
T_{ee}\approx 1
and
R_{he}\approx 0
. As
l_{S}
becomes comparable to the coherence length
\xi_{0}
, the electron acquires an increasing hole component and the probability of Andreev retro-reflection grows at the expense of direct transmission. In the long segment limit,
l_{S}\gg\xi_{0}
, the quasiparticle is fully Andreev reflected, so
R_{he}\to 1
.

II.4
Differential Conductance

We calculate the differential conductance matrix
G^{\alpha\beta}=dI_{\alpha}/dV_{\beta}
using the Landauer-Büttiker approach, see Appendix
B
. The zero-temperature differential conductance is given as
Takane and Ebisawa 1992
;
Danon et al. 2020

\displaystyle G_{\alpha\alpha}^{(0)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\quantity( N_\alpha- \qty|r^{\alpha\alpha}_{ee}|^2 + \qty|r^{\alpha\alpha}_{he}|^2 ),

(9a)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\quantity( \qty|t_{ee}^{\alpha\beta}|^2 - \qty|t_{he}^{\alpha\beta}|^2 ),

(9b)

where
N_{\alpha}
is the number of channels in lead
\alpha
and the factor of two represents a spin-degenerate system. The non-zero temperature results then follow from

\displaystyle G_{\alpha\beta}(eV_{\beta})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\;G_{\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(\omega-eV_{\beta})f^{\prime}(\omega),

(10)

with

f^{\prime}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2k_{B}\theta}\frac{1}{1+\cosh\quantity(\omega/k_B\theta)},

(11)

being the derivative of the Fermi-Dirac distribution with
\theta
denoting the electron temperature and
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant.

III
Time-independent transport properties

III.1
NSNSN junction

We start with the simplest case, which is a single Josephson junction connected to two additional normal leads, so in our notation a NSNSN device in the case of perfect transparency,
T=1
.

For energies below the gap,
\omega<\Delta
, we can solve the scattering matrix analytically, and the matrix will have the same form as the superconducting scattering matrix in Eq. (
1
) with coefficients that read

\displaystyle r_{he,LL,1}(x,\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\Delta}(x)(t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}-1)}{r_{\Delta}(x)^{2}t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}-1}\left(1+\frac{t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}(r_{\Delta}^{2}(x)-1)(r_{\Delta}^{*2}(-x)-1)}{r_{\Delta}(x)r_{\Delta}^{*}(-x)(t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}-1)^{2}+e^{i\phi}(r_{\Delta}^{2}(x)t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}-1)(r_{\Delta}^{*2}(-x)t_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}-1)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle t_{ee,LR,1}(x,\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}_{\mathrm{S}}(r^{2}_{\Delta}(x)-1)^{2}}{(1-r^{2}_{\Delta}(x)t^{2}_{\mathrm{S}})^{2}}\left(1+\frac{e^{-i\phi}r_{\Delta}(x)r_{\Delta}^{*}(-x)(1-t^{2}_{\mathrm{S}})^{2}}{(1-r^{2}_{\Delta}(x)t^{2}_{\Delta})(1-r^{*2}_{\Delta}(-x)t^{2}_{\Delta})}\right)^{-1}e^{2ik_{\mathrm{F}S}l_{S}}\,,

(13)

III.2
Andreev Crystal

(c) shows how the amplitude of the band also decreases as the transparency decreases.

IV
Time-dependent Transport Properties

The nonreciprocal behavior of the differential conductance suggests that the device can function as a directional filter. As illustrated in Fig.
7
, we consider a configuration in which a dc voltage bias is applied independently to each terminal, with a superimposed sinusoidal ac component. By tuning to an appropriate phase bias, the signal will only be transmitted one way, while any noise in the right part of the circuit has no way to affect the isolated part.

Let us consider generic biases
v_{\beta}(t)=V_{\beta}^{\mathrm{dc}}+v_{\beta}^{\mathrm{ac}}\sin(\omega_D t)
. Assuming that the drive frequency
\omega_{D}
is small compared to the dc bias, we can focus on the first-order response, see Appendix
C
and Ref.
Pedersen and Büttiker 1998
. This reads as

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(|1|)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\cos(\omega_D t)\sum_{\gamma}G_{\alpha\gamma}(V_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{dc}})v_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{ac}}.

(18)

The first-order response is proportional to the conductance and ac bias. To quantify if the device leaks to higher harmonics, we calculate the second harmonic of the response:

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(|2|)}(t)\approx\frac{1}{2}\cos\quantity(2\omega_D t)\sum_{\gamma}\quantity(v_\gamma^\mathrm{ac})^{2}\frac{\partial G_{\alpha\gamma}(V_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{dc}})}{\partial V_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{dc}}},

(19)

this is limited by the slope of the band, but is easily handled by tuning phase and voltage bias appropriately.

V
Discussion and summary

We described the transport properties of an Andreev crystal formed in an array of constantly biased Josephson junctions. Our device proposal consists of a semiconducting channel with an array of superconducting contacts, each with individual phase bias. The phase bias can be controlled by either
i)
threading a magnetic flux through individual loops connecting neighboring superconductors or
ii)
by connecting each segment to a superconducting current-carrying line, which thus has a phase gradient
Lykkegaard et al. 2025
. When each unit cell has a phase bias, an array of Andreev bound states is created and the resulting band can be tuned by this phase. For near-perfect contacts between the normal and the superconducting segments, the transport is directional because in this case, the two branches of the spectrum are made up purely of right- or left-moving electronic states, respectively.
Backscattering, such as by a non-perfect interface transparency, leads to a hybridization between the states originating from left- and right-moving electronic modes and a corresponding gap opening in the spectrum. For intermediate transparency, the bands retain their directional features away from the gap opening. This design can easily be extended to two-dimensional crystals where the relative phase bias in the two directions may differ.

We point out that the Andreev crystal with constant phase bias can be regarded as a discretized version of a superconductor with finite-momentum Cooper pairing
Fulde and Ferrell 1964
;
Larkin and Ovchinnikov 1964
;
Gor’kov and Rashba 2001
;
Yuan and Fu 2021
;
Yuan and Fu 2022
;
Daido et al. 2022
;
He et al. 2022
;
Ilić and Bergeret 2022
;
Pal et al. 2022
;
Hou et al. 2023
;
Banerjee et al. 2023c
;
Davydova et al. 2022
;
Davydova et al. 2024
.
In a finite-momentum superconductor, the condensate has a net supercurrent flow and
the spectrum of quasiparticles moving in- and against the supercurrent is Doppler shifted
Davydova et al. 2022
.
Intuitively, electrons moving with and against the flowing condensate have different kinetic energy as seen from the condensate – and consequently all properties associated to the condensate (including Andreev reflection and quasiparticle spectrum) are Doppler shifted
Davydova et al. 2024
.
As a consequence, quasiparticle modes that are co- and counterpropagating with the supercurrent flow are transmissive at different energies.
This gives rise to the directional conductance for electrons tunneling through a superconductor with supercurrent flow – whether in a finite-momentum superconductor or in an Andreev crystal where a constant phase bias between superconducting segments induces a net Josephson current flow.

The directional behavior is a general feature of the phenomenology of Andreev bound states and Andreev crystals.
It may serve as a diagnostic tool for NS interfaces, because of the sensitivity of the interference to backscattering. Finally, we described how the directional behavior can be applied as a directional filter, allowing a signal to be transmitted one way while noise is prevented from traveling in the reverse direction.
This property enables a new application for controlled signal processing in superconducting circuits
Ingla-Aynés et al. 2025
;
Krantz et al. 2019
.

Appendix A
Scattering matrix description

In this section, we explain the details of the method of scattering matrices.

In general, a
S
-matrix for a scatterer is a unitary matrix defined as

\psi_{i}^{(o)}=S_{ij}\psi_{j}^{(i)}

(20)

where
\psi_{i}^{(o)/(i)}
is the outgoing or incoming amplitude in channel
i
.

A.1
Combination of scattering matrices

Consider two scatter with
S
-matrices
A
and
B
with, respectively,
N_{B}
and
N_{A}
modes. We want to write the
S
-matrix of the composed system
C
in which we join the last
N_{j}
modes of
A
with the first
N_{J}
modes of
B
. With simple linear algebra, it is easy to prove that we can express the resulting scattering matrix
C
with the so-called Redheffer star product defined as

C=A\star_{N_{j}}B=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}+A_{12}(I-B_{11}A_{22})^{-1}B_{11}A_{21}&A_{12}(I-B_{11}A_{22})^{-1}B_{12}\\
B_{21}(I-A_{22}B_{11})^{-1}A_{21}&B_{22}+B_{21}(I-A_{22}B_{11})^{-1}A_{22}B_{12}\end{pmatrix}

(21)

The resulting matrix
C
has a dimension
N_{a}+N_{b}-2N_{j}
while the subblock matrices
B_{1}1
and
A_{2}2
as well as the identity
I
are
N_{j}\times N_{j}
. The other dimensions are inferred. The product is well-defined as long as
(I-B_{11}A_{22})
and
(I-A_{22}B_{11})
are invertible, which physically means that no bound states are formed between the two scatterers.

A.2
Boundary conditions

Consider a system with scattering matrix
S
and we now want to impose some boundary condition, i.e. open boundary condition or periodic. We can separate the channels where the boundary condition apply from the ones that are open and rewrite the scattering problem as

\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{d}^{(o)}\\
\psi_{l}^{(o)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{dd}&S_{dl}\\
S_{ld}&S_{ll}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{d}^{(i)}\\
\psi_{l}^{(i)}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(22)

The boundary condition can be written as linear relation like

\psi^{(i)}_{d}=\Lambda\psi^{(o)}_{d}

(23)

where the matrix
\Lambda
describe the boundary conditions.

We can then find a closed equation for the open leads scattering matrix that is

\tilde{S}_{ll}=S_{ll}+S_{ld}(1-\Lambda S_{dd})^{-1}\Lambda S_{dl}

(24)

A.3
Normal region scattering matrix

For the calculation, we work in the so-called Andreev approximation and linearize the dispersion relation in the neighborhood of the Fermi surface. In this way, the normal state quasiparticle energy is
\xi_{k}=\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}q
with
v_{\mathrm{F}}
as the Fermi velocity and we can rewrite the invert the dispersion relation in the normal region as

k=k_{\mathrm{F}}+q\simeq k_{\mathrm{F}}+\tau\frac{\xi}{v_{\mathrm{F}}}

(25)

while the one in the superconducting region will read as

k=k_{\mathrm{F}}+\tau\frac{\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}{v_{\mathrm{F}}}=k_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{\tau}{\pi\xi_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}-1}

(26)

where we defined the coherence length as
\xi_{0}^{-1}=\frac{\pi\Delta}{v_{\mathrm{F}}}
. When
\omega<\Delta
, the wave vectors become complex causing evanescent waves.

We start by considering the normal region.
The scattering matrix for a perfectly clean metal reads as

S_{\mathrm{N,cln}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&e^{+ik_{e}\ell_{\mathrm{N}}}&0\\
0&0&0&e^{-ik_{h}\ell_{\mathrm{N}}}\\
e^{+ik_{e}\ell_{\mathrm{N}}}&0&0&0\\
0&e^{-ik_{h}\ell_{\mathrm{N}}}&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

(27)

In the case of a disordered medium, we parametrize the scattering matrix as

S_{\mathrm{N,dis}}=\begin{pmatrix}+r&t\\
t&-r\end{pmatrix}\otimes\tau_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}+r&0&t&0\\
0&+r&0&t\\
t&0&-r&0\\
0&t&0&-r\\
\end{pmatrix}

(28)

where
r=\sqrt{1-T}
and
T=\sqrt{t}
where
T
is the transparency of the junction (here taken to be energy-independent).
S_{\mathrm{N}}
is unitary and satisfies PHS. Note that we are neglecting the dependence of the scattering matrix on the energy of the particle in the same fashion as Ref.
Beenakker 1991
.

A.4
NSN scattering matrix

We consider a clean one-dimensional metal in which a section of length
\ell_{\mathrm{S}}
.

For an incoming electron on the left-hand side, the wavefunction in the three sections can be written as

\begin{cases}\psi_{I}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}e^{i\tilde{k}_{e}z}+a\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}e^{+i\tilde{k}_{h}z}\\
\psi_{II}=b\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}e^{ik_{e}z}+d\begin{pmatrix}v\\
u\end{pmatrix}e^{+ik_{h}z}\\
\psi_{III}=f\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}e^{i\tilde{k}_{e}(z-\ell_{\mathrm{S}})}\end{cases}

(29)

where
\tilde{k}_{e}
and
\tilde{k}_{h}
are the wave vectors in the normal metal while
k_{e}=k_{\mathrm{F}}+q
and
k_{h}=k_{\mathrm{F}}-q
the analogues in the superconducting segments.

By wave function matching, we get the following linear system of equations for the coefficients

\begin{cases}1=bu+dv\\
a=bv+du\\
bue^{ik_{e}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}+dve^{+ik_{h}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}=f\\
bve^{ik_{e}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}+due^{+ik_{h}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}=0\end{cases}

(30)

which gives the following solutions for
r_{he}=a
and
t_{ee}=f

\displaystyle r_{he}

\displaystyle=\frac{(e^{-iq\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}-e^{+iq\ell_{\mathrm{S}}})uv}{e^{-iq\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}u^{2}-e^{+iq\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}v^{2}}

(31)

\displaystyle t_{ee}

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{e^{-iq\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}u^{2}-e^{+iq\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}v^{2}}e^{+ik_{F}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}

(32)

With a bit of algebra and defining the infinite-length limit Andreev-reflection amplitude
r_{he,\Delta}=v/u=\exp(-i \arccos(E/\Delta))
and the high-energy limit normal transmission amplitude
t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}=\exp(i \frac{\ell_\mathrm{S}}{\xi_0} \sqrt{E^2/\Delta^2-1})
, we get to the final form

\displaystyle r_{he}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}^{2}}{1-t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}^{2}r_{he,\Delta}^{2}}r_{he,\Delta}

(33)

\displaystyle t_{ee}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r_{he,\Delta}^{2}}{1-t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}^{2}r_{he,\Delta}^{2}}t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}e^{+ik_{F}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}

(34)

In the long
\ell_{\mathrm{S}}
limit, we can approximate them by

\displaystyle r_{he}

\displaystyle\simeq(1-t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}^{2})r_{he,\Delta}

(35)

\displaystyle t_{ee}

\displaystyle\simeq t_{ee,\mathrm{HE}}e^{+ik_{F}\ell_{\mathrm{S}}}

(36)

such that the unitarity of the
S
-matrix is still preserved.

Even if we derived these expressions in the clean limit, we have related them to universal quantities like the gap parameter and the coherence length. Therefore we can use them as a guess for building the
S
-matrix of the composite system by attributing all the normal reflection to the N region
S
-matrix.

S_{\mathrm{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&r_{eh}&t_{ee}&0\\
r_{he}&0&0&t_{hh}\\
t_{ee}&0&0&r_{eh}\\
0&t_{hh}&r_{he}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

(37)

Where
t_{hh}(\omega)=t_{ee}^{*}(-\omega^{*})
and
r_{eh}(\omega)=-r_{he}^{*}(-\omega^{*})
because of particle-hole symmetry.

Appendix B
Landauer-Büttiker approach

More specifically, the relation between the average currents and biases of a multiterminal device depends only on the transmission probability defined as

T_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma\delta}(\varepsilon;P)\equiv\tr\absolutevalue{S_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma\delta}(\varepsilon; P) }^{2},

(38)

where
S_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma\delta}
is the subblock of the scattering matrix that connects the channel of the incoming particles of type
\delta
from lead
\beta
to the channel of outgoing particles of type
\gamma
in lead
\alpha
for scattering events at energy
\varepsilon
. Since the device we are describing is a superconductor-semiconductor hybrid, the transmission matrix features a Nambu structure with particle and hole sectors,
\delta,\gamma\in\{e,h\}
.

In the version of the Landauer-Büttiker theory for superconducting systems, quasiparticle conservation takes the place of electron conservation for conventional devices. This implies that the electric charge is not explicitly conserved. Indeed, we can distinguish between the charge-conserving normal processes
T^{ee}_{\alpha\beta}
, and
T^{hh}_{\alpha\beta}
, and the non-charge-conserving Andreev processes
T^{eh}_{\alpha\beta}
, and
T^{he}_{\alpha\beta}
. The last two imply the creation and the destruction of one Cooper pair in the superconductive leads, respectively. See e.g Danon et al.
Danon et al. 2020
.

Within the Landauer-Büttiker approach, we can write the current flowing into the system through the lead
\alpha
as

\begin{split}I=&\frac{e}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential{\omega}\,\left[f(\omega-eV_{\alpha},\theta_{\alpha})-f(\omega,\theta_{S})\right]\left[N_{\alpha}-R_{\alpha}^{ee}(\omega)+R_{\alpha}^{he}(\omega)\right]\\
-&\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{e}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential{\omega}\,\left[f(\omega-eV_{\beta},\theta_{\beta})-f(\omega,\theta_{S})\right]\left[T_{\alpha\beta}^{ee}(\omega)-T_{\alpha\beta}^{he}(\omega)\right]\,\end{split}

(39)

where
f(\omega,\theta)=[1+\exp(\omega/k_B \theta)]^{-1}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution,
V_{\alpha}
are the voltage biases with respect to the ground,
\theta_{\alpha}
is the temperature of the leads while
\theta_{S}
is the temperature of the superconducting leads. The differential conductance is then easily calculated with
G_{\alpha\beta}=\differential I_{\alpha}/\differential V_{\beta}
resulting in

\begin{split}G_{\alpha\alpha}&=G_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential{\omega}\,h(\omega-eV_{\alpha})[N_{\alpha}-R_{\alpha}^{ee}(\omega)+R_{\alpha}^{he}(\omega)],\end{split}

(40)

for local differential conductance, while for the nonlocal differential conductance, we have

\begin{split}G_{\alpha\beta}&=-G_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential{\omega}\,h(\omega-eV_{\beta})[T_{\alpha\beta}^{ee}(\omega)-T_{\alpha\beta}^{he}(\omega)],\end{split}

(41)

where
G_{0}=\frac{e^{2}}{h}
is the conductance quantum and the function

h(\omega,\theta)=-\partial_{\omega}f(\omega,\theta)=\frac{1}{2k_{B}\theta}\frac{1}{1+\cosh(\omega/k_B \theta)}

(42)

is a soft peak function that describes the effect of thermal broadening.

Appendix C
Harmonics of the current

C.1
First harmonic components of the current

We use the Pedersen-Büttiker formula
Pedersen and Büttiker 1998
for the time-dependent current in lead
\alpha
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{e}{h}\int dE\sum_{\gamma,l,k}\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E+ (k-l)\hbar\omega_D) ]J_{l}\quantity(\frac{eV_\gamma^{\text{ac}}}{\hbar\omega_D})J_{k}\quantity(\frac{eV_\gamma^{\text{ac}}}{\hbar\omega_D})e^{-i(k-l)\omega_{D}t}f(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}}-l\hbar\omega_{D}),

(43)

we want to be in the low frequency limit, so we can expand the Fermi function in a Taylor series,

\displaystyle f(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}}-l\hbar\omega_{D})\approx f(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}})+l\hbar\omega_{D}f^{\prime}(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}})+\frac{l^{2}\hbar^{2}\omega_{D}^{2}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}})+\ldots,

(44)

to begin with we only consider the first order term, but the second order term could be important later for second harmonics. We also only consider
k-l=\pm 1
. Let start by considering
k-l=1
, then we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(1)}(t)\approx\frac{e}{h}\int dE\sum_{\gamma,l}\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E+ \hbar\omega_D) ]J_{l}\quantity(\frac{eV_\gamma^{\text{ac}}}{\hbar\omega_D})J_{l+1}\quantity(\frac{eV_\gamma^{\text{ac}}}{\hbar\omega_D})e^{-i\omega_{D}t}

\displaystyle\times\quantity( f(E - eV_\gamma^{\text{dc}}) - l\hbar\omega_D f'(E - eV_\gamma^{\text{dc}}) ),

(45)

we use the identities for the Bessel functions,

\displaystyle\sum_{l}J_{l}(x)J_{l+n}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,0},

(46)

\displaystyle\sum_{l}lJ_{l}(x)J_{l+n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2}(\delta_{n,1}+\delta_{n,-1}),

(47)

the current for the first harmonic becomes

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{e}{h}e^{-i\omega_{D}t}\int dE\sum_{\gamma}\frac{eV_{\gamma}^{\text{ac}}}{2}\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E+ \hbar\omega_D) ]\frac{\partial}{\partial E}f(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}}),

(48)

we use that for small
\omega_{D}
that
\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E+ \hbar\omega_D) ]\approx\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E) ]
, and the dc diffential conductance is defined as
G_{\alpha\gamma}=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\int\mathrm{d}E\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E) ]\frac{\partial}{\partial E}f(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}})
. The current then becomes

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-e^{-i\omega_{D}t}\sum_{\gamma}G_{\alpha\gamma}\frac{V_{\gamma}^{\text{ac}}}{2}.

(49)

We can do a similiar calculation for
k-l=-1
, then we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(-1)}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-e^{i\omega_{D}t}\sum_{\gamma}G_{\alpha\gamma}\frac{V_{\gamma}^{\text{ac}}}{2},

(50)

combining the two currents we find the total first harmonic current,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(|1|)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\cos(\omega_D t)\sum_{\gamma}G_{\alpha\gamma}V_{\gamma}^{\text{ac}}.

(51)

C.2
Second harmonic components of the current

We can do the same calculation for the second harmonic, but now we have to consider
k-l=\pm 2
, we start with
k-l=2
, then we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(2)}(t)\approx\frac{e}{h}\int dE\sum_{\gamma,l}\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E+ 2\hbar\omega_D) ]J_{l}\quantity(\frac{eV_\gamma^{\text{ac}}}{\hbar\omega_D})J_{l+2}\quantity(\frac{eV_\gamma^{\text{ac}}}{\hbar\omega_D})e^{-i2\omega_{D}t}

(52)

\displaystyle\times\quantity( f(E - eV_\gamma^{\text{dc}}) - l\hbar\omega_D f'(E - eV_\gamma^{\text{dc}}) + \frac{l^2 \hbar^2 \omega_D^2}{2} f''(E - eV_\gamma^{\text{dc}}) ),

(53)

only the last term contributes to the second harmonic, so we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e}{h}e^{-i2\omega_{D}t}\int dE\sum_{\gamma}\frac{e^{2}\quantity(V_\gamma^{\text{ac}})^{2}}{4}\Tr\quantity[ A_{\gamma\gamma} (\alpha, E, E+ 2\hbar\omega_D) ]\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial E^{2}}f(E-eV_{\gamma}^{\text{dc}}),

(54)

where we have used the Bessel identity,

\displaystyle\sum_{l}l^{2}J_{l}(x)J_{l+n}(x)=\frac{x}{2}(\delta_{n,-1}-\delta_{n,1}+)\frac{x^{2}}{4}(\delta_{n,2}+\delta_{n,-2}),

(55)

again we concentrate on the case with small
\omega_{D}
. By simple algebra, we get

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-i2\omega_{D}t}\sum_{\gamma}\quantity(\frac{v_\gamma^\mathrm{ac}}{2})^{2}\frac{\partial G_{\alpha\gamma}}{\partial V_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{dc}}},

(56)

and similar for
k-l=-2
, we find

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(-2)}(t)

\displaystyle\approx e^{i2\omega_{D}t}\sum_{\gamma}\quantity(\frac{v_\gamma^\mathrm{ac}}{2})^{2}\frac{\partial G_{\alpha\gamma}}{\partial V_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{dc}}},

(57)

and the second harmonic becomes

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{(|2|)}(t)\approx\frac{1}{2}\cos\quantity(2\omega_D t)\sum_{\gamma}\quantity(v_\gamma^\mathrm{ac})^{2}\frac{\partial G_{\alpha\gamma}}{\partial V_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{dc}}}

(58)
