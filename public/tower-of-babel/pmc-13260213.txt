Comparative analysis of the fine molecular structures and pasting properties of zhiqu and bread wheat starch
Authors: Zhenbing Ma, Yuanyuan Tian, Rong Xiong, Haibo Lan, Jia Zheng, Shunchuan Sun, Jian Su, Chenyang Wang, Yi Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWheat serves as raw material for daqu and bread production, while starch fine structure and pasting properties differ poorly understood between zhiqu and bread wheat cultivars. This study determined the starch-related properties of the two wheat types. The results showed that the amylose content of zhiqu wheat ranged from 27.44% to 27.90%, which was lower than that of bread wheat (28.38–30.94%). Zhiqu wheat had larger medium-sized starch granules (46.61 μm) compared with bread wheat (43.68 μm). XRD analysis confirmed that zhiqu wheat exhibited higher relative crystallinity (24.6%–26.1%) than bread wheat (24%–24.9%). In terms of pasting properties, zhiqu wheat showed higher peak viscosity, trough viscosity and final viscosity, as well as a lower degree of starch branching. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that zhiqu wheat had relatively stable and consistent quality traits. These findings deepen understanding of zhiqu wheat starch functionality and guide zhiqu wheat variety selectionKeywords: Zhiqu wheat, Wheat starch, Fine structure, Thermal properties, Pasting properties

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most widely cultivated, consumed, and traded cereal crops globally, due to its significant source of dietary starch and protein (Peng et al., 2025). Moreover, its agronomic adaptability, ease of storage, nutritional value, and versatility in products such as bread, cakes, biscuits, and pasta make it indispensable (Czaja et al., 2016). Wheat varieties are classified into strong-gluten, medium-gluten, and weak-gluten types based on gluten strength. The gluten structure is a primary determinant of end-use quality and processing characteristics, because the protein content and composition influence dough rheology and baking properties (Cho et al., 2018). Strong-gluten wheat exhibits higher wet gluten content, greater water absorption, longer dough development time and stability time, and a denser gluten network (Zhang et al., 2021). Weak-gluten wheat is suitable for pastries, biscuits, and cakes; medium-gluten wheat is suitable for noodles, dumplings, and Chinese steamed buns; and strong-gluten wheat is ideal for pasta and bread (Zhang et al., 2021). Thus, selecting the appropriate gluten strength based on the intended food product makes it possible to achieve superior texture and flavor.

With thousands of cultivated varieties worldwide, wheat contributes substantially to the global diet and is used extensively for feed, seed, and industrial purposes (Shevkani et al., 2016). wheat also serves as a crucial raw material for producing fermentation starters known as Daqu in China. For instance, varieties such as MM902, YM15, and FM8 are used to produce medium-high or high-temperature Daqu for iconic Baijiu brands like Wuliangye, Jiannanchun, and Maotai, respectively. Daqu provides a diverse community of microorganisms and enzymes essential for saccharification, fermentation, and aroma formation during Baijiu production. This foundational role is captured in the traditional sayong, “The character of the liquor is determined by the Daqu.” Since fermented wheat is the basis of Daqu production, its physicochemical properties ultimately shape the microbial community and enzyme accumulation in the starter, thereby influencing the flavor profile of the final Baijiu product.

The role of gluten-strength in wheat-based food production is often closely linked to the starch properties (Shevkani et al., 2016). In China, long-term production practice has established specific quality requirements for wheat used in Daqumaking, leading to the selection of varietiesknown as zhiqu wheat. These zhiqu wheat varieties are characterized by a thin seed coat, soft texture, high hygroscopicity, high flour yield and soft grains. They typically have low protein, and wet gluten content, weak strength, good extensibility, and short dough stability times.

Starch, the primary storage carbohydrate in wheat, constitutes approximately 70% of the grain (Song et al., 2024). Its physicochemical and structural properties not only influence wheat flour quality but also affect the rate of microbial enrichment and the composition of the microbial community during Daqu fermentation. The composition, crystallinity, and granule size distribution determine the physicochemical properties of starch, thereby shaping the functional and nutritional characteristics of starch-based foods. However, the fine structural differences between zhiqu wheat starch and bread wheat starch remain poorly understood.

In this study, six wheat varieties were selected for analysis: three zhiqu wheat varieties (MM902, YM15, and FM8) and three bread wheat varieties (ZM7698, XN9198, and XN1366). A comprehensive understanding of their physicochemical and structural differences will guide the selection, breeding, and optimization of wheat varieties for starch extraction. Ultimately, this will benefit the food industry by providing superior starch options and enabling enhanced product development.

Materials and methods

Materials

Three zhiqu wheat varieties (MM902: 21.5% gluten, 10.3% protein; YM15: 20.3% gluten, 10.5% protein; FM8: 30.5% gluten, 13.5% protein) and three bread wheat varieties (XN9198: 35.2% gluten, 15.0% protein; ZM7698: 33.1% gluten, 14.1% protein; XN1366: 33.2% gluten, 15.2% protein) were provided by the Sichuan Province Engineering Technology Research Center of Liquor-Making Grains, located in Yibin, Sichuan, China. The wheat samples were grown during the winter of 2024 in an experimental field of the same center in Lizhuang, Yibin, under local field conditions. A single application of fertilizers was applied, comprising 300 kg/ha urea, 150 kg/ha diammonium phosphate (as P2O5), 100 kg/ha K2SO4, and 10 kg/ha ZnSO4. All chemicals and reagents used were of analytical grade.

Starch extraction

The starch extraction method was based on that of Tutu et al. (2023), with a few modifications. Approximately 50 g of wheat grains was accurately weighed and soaked overnight in 0.5% NaOH solution. The soaked grains were homogenized using a juicer and filtered through four layers of gauze. The residue was rinsed with distilled water and combined with the filtrate. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was mixed with anhydrous ethanol, shaken for 10 min, passed through a 200-mesh sieve, and centrifuged again to remove the supernatant. The precipitate was washed 2–3 times with distilled water by resuspension and centrifugation. Subsequently, it was treated with a papain aqueous solution (1.6 g/L, 200 U/mg), shaken for 1 h, and centrifuged to discard the supernatant. The treated precipitate was mixed with 0.25% NaOH solution, shaken for 10 min, sieved through a 200-mesh sieve, and centrifuged. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was again washed 2–3 times with distilled water. Finally, the starch pellet was collected after centrifugation, dried at 40 °C, ground if needed, and passed through a 200-mesh sieve to obtain the final starch product.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the starch granules

The starch powder samples were evenly dispersed and placed on the microscope stage. The apparent shape of the starch particles was analyzed using a scanning electron microscope at a magnification of 500–2000 times, under an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a working voltage of 100 V.

Wheat starch granule size

To measure the granule size of wheat starch, 100 mg of wheat starch was blended with 1 mL of 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate wash buffer. The mixture was centrifuged for 1 min at 1000 rpm, and the supernatant was removed. This procedure was repeated twice. Subsequently, the sample was redispersed in 1 mL of deionized water and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The supernatant was discarded, and this process was repeated five times. Finally, 1 mL of 50% alcohol was added, and the solution was thoroughly mixed. The granule size was determined using a Mastersizer 3000 laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK) within a range of 0.1–3500 μm.

Starch chain length distribution

The high-performance anion exchange chromatography-pulsed amperometric detection method (HPAEC-PAD, Thermo ICS5000+ system) was adopted. For sample preparation, 10.0 mg of purified starch samples was accurately weighed and suspended in ultrapure water. After gelatinization via boiling water bath, acetic acid salt (50 μL, 0.6 M, pH 4.4), sodium azide (10 μL, 2% w/v), and isomaltase (10 μL, 1400 U) were added successively. The samples were shaken at 37 °C for 24 h to induce debranching. Sodium borohydride (0.5% w/v) was added to terminate the reaction. The samples were left to stand for 20 h and then dried under a nitrogen stream. The samples were re-solubilized in NaOH (30 μL, 1 M) and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 5 min) to obtain the supernatant. The chromatographic system used was a Thermo ICS5000 ion chromatography system (ICS5000+, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The Dionex CarboPac PA200 column (250 mm × 4.0 mm) was used with mobile phases A (0.2 M NaOH) and B (0.2 M NaOH/0.2 M NaAc). The elution gradient was as follows: 0 min A/B (90:10 V/V), 10 min A/B (90:10 V/V), 30 min A/B (40:60 V/V), 50 min A/B (40,60 V/V), 50.1 min A/B (90:10 V/V), and 60 min A/B (90,10 V/V).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The starch crystal structure and relative crystallinity of starch granules were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using a SmartLab SE X-ray diffraction (XRD; D/Max2550VB+/PC, Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The test parameters were as follows: Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 A), tube voltage of 40 kV, tube current of 30 mA, scanning rate of 2°/min, step width of 0.02°, scanning angle range 5°–50° (2θ). The uniformly pressed sample pellet was fixed on the sample stage, and the crystal type (A, B, or C type) was determined by collecting diffraction spectra. The relative crystallinity was calculated by peak fitting in the PDXL 2.0 software (Rigaku), with the ratio of the integral area of the crystalline peak to that of the amorphous peak as the quantitative basis.

Rapid viscosity analysis

The Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA) was adopted for the determination of the gelatinization characteristics of starch. The starch sample (3.0 g) and ultrapure water (25 mL) were mixed in an aluminum crucible. The temperature program was as follows: 50 °C for 1 min, increase at a rate of 6 °C/min to 95 °C, maintain for 5 min, cool at the same rate to 50 °C, and maintain for 2 min. The viscosity curve was recorded in real time, and the peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), and final viscosity (FV) were extracted. The disintegration value (PV-TV) and the rehydration value (FV-TV) were calculated to characterize the thermal stability and rehydration tendency of the starch. The RVA4800 (Sweden) was used for this purpose.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal properties of starch were determined by differential scanning calorimetry (Netzsch 200 F3 Maia, Germany). The starch sample (5.0 mg) and ultrapure water (10 μL) were precisely weighed and sealed in an aluminum crucible for mixing. The mixture was left to stand at 4 °C for 12 h and then placed in the DSC sample cell. The test program was as follows: the temperature increased from 4 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, and the heat flow-temperature curve was recorded simultaneously. The initial temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔHgel) were determined to characterize the gelatinization phase transition behavior of starch granules.

1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy

As previously reported by Schmitz et al. (2009), the 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVIII-400 spectrometer equipped with a 4-mm CP/MAS detection probe (Bruker Co. Ltd., Germany). Ten milligrams of starch was dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO‑d6 and heated at 40 °C for 1.5 h to dissolve. The solution was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube and sealed. The test conditions implemented 16 scans at 25 °C, with the residual DMSO‑d6 signal (δ 2.549 ppm) used for chemical shift calibration. Delta software was used to analyze the characteristic peaks and determine the anomeric hydrogen configuration and intermolecular interactions of the starch. The following equation was used to calculate the degree of branching (DB):

DB (%) = Iα−1,6Iα−1,6+Iα−1,4 × 100.

where Iα-1,6 is the peak area of the α-1,6 bond and Iα-1,4 is the peak area of the α-1,4 bond.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in triplicate. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. In all cases, p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All significant differences were analyzed using SPSS22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) (Duncan test). The graphics were drawn using Origin Pro 21.0.

Results

Granular morphology and granule size distribution

Starch granule size is an important parameter that influences the physical and chemical properties and applications of starch. Starch was isolated from six wheat varieties, and the morphology of starch granules was observed using SEM. Consistent with previous reports (Lindeboom et al., 2004), all starch granules exhibited typical spherical to oval granules of heterogeneous sizes (Fig. S1A). The large granules (type A) are mostly disc, oblate, or oval in shape, with smooth surfaces and clear edges. The medium-sized granules (type B) are mostly oval or irregular in shape, and depressions or pores are observed. The small-sized starch (type C) granules are mostly angular or fragmentary and may adhere to the surfaces of large granules. All starch granules exhibited a bimodal particle size distribution, featuring peaks at approximately 5 and 20 μm. Notably, the large granule content was higher in FM8 (D90 = 29.97 ± 0.06) among zhiqu wheat varieties and in XN1366 (D90 = 28.70 ± 0.00) among bread wheat varieties, compared to their respective counterparts (Fig. S1B, Table 1). Thus, the surface structure of starch granules was similar across the different sample types.

For a detailed analysis of size distribution, starch granules were categorized by size into three groups: large (A-granules, >15 μm), medium (B-granules, 5–15 μm), and small (C-granules, <5 μm), as listed in Table 1. For zhiqu wheat, the large, medium, and small granules had proportions ranging from 40.65% to 49.26% (average 44.48%), 40.74% to 50.4% (average 46.61%), and 7.42% to 9.99% (average 8.91%), respectively. In bread wheat, the corresponding proportions ranged from 42.88% to 54.25% (average 46.91%), 38.61% to 46.31% (average 43.68%), and 7.14% to 10.99% (average 9.41%), respectively. These results reveal that, compared to bread wheat varieties with more differentiated starch granule structures, zhiqu wheat varieties exhibit a more consistent particle size distribution and lower intra-group variation, which provides a particle-size-based theoretical foundation for screening special zhiqu wheat cultivars.

Chain length distributions of wheat starch

Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was employed to determine the chain length distribution (CLD) of the whole starch molecule in different wheat varieties. As shown in Fig. 1, zhiqu and bread wheat starch shared similar bimodal distributions of their CLDs, including amylopectin (AP) branches (degree of polymerization, DP 〈100) and one peak representing an amylose (AM) branch (DP > 100). The peak 1 and peak 2 represent short- and long-chain fractions of AP, respectively. Similarly, amylose was categorized into AM1 (short-branch chains, 100 < DP 〈1000) and AM2 (long-branch chains, DP > 1000) (Song & Jane, 2000), and the amylose content (AC) was determined by calculating the area ratio of AM to AM + AP. Variations in amylose content were observed between varieties. Zhiqu wheat exhibited a lower total amylose content (AM/AM+AP, 27.44–27.9%) compared to bread wheat; yet, its short-chain amylose (AM1) content was higher (Table S1). Such a pattern aligns with our previous data, further demonstrating that glutinous sorghum for Baijiu brewing is rich in AM1 (Ma et al., 2025). Accordingly, different processing methods affect the quality characteristics.

Chain length distributions of amylopectin

CLDs of amylopectin, a highly branched polymer of glucose units linked by α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds (Qi et al., 2024), was also determined. CLD of amylopectin was determined by analyzing isoamylase-treated starch samples using HPAEC-PAD. All sample profiles were normalized to the highest peak (DP 12) and fitted to the Wu-Gilbert mathematical model (Wu & Gilbert, 2010). The amylopectin CLDs exhibited notable differences in three regions: the first at DP < 10, the second at 10 < DP < 13, and the third at 14 < DP < 30 (Fig. 2A, B). Comparatively, bread wheat varieties had higher short-chain content (DP < 10) than zhiqu wheat varieties (Fig. 2D). FM8 exhibited the highest content in both the 10 < DP < 13 and 14 < DP < 30 regions, whereas MM902 had the lowest content (Fig. 2E, F).

1H NMR spectroscopy analysis of starch structure

The complex structures of six wheat starches, including α-1,4 and α-1,6-glycosidic linkages, were analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Pérez & Bertoft, 2010). The chemical shifts for the glucose residues at 5.11, 4.75, 5.42, 5.52, and 4.61 ppm correspond to the protons of α-1,4-glycosidic linkages, α-1,6-glycosidic linkages, OH2, OH3, and OH6, respectively, while the H2-H6 ring protons resonate between 3.18 and 4.10 ppm (Yang et al., 2019). In Fig. 3A, the relatively weak intensity of the α-(1,6) bond signal indicates a low degree of branching in wheat starch, compared to that of corn starch and waxy rice starch (Gu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2021). The degree of branching (DB) of six wheat varieties is shown in Fig. 3B. Among the zhiqu wheat varieties, the DB ranged from 3.63% to 3.77%, with MM902 being at 3.63%, YM15 at 3.68%, and FM8 at 3.77%. For bread wheat, the DB ranged from 3.70% to 4.54%, with ZM7698 being at 4.15%, XN9198 at 4.54%, and XN1366 at 3.70%. These results indicated that the zhiqu wheat varieties had lower DB and variability than the bread wheat varieties.

Crystalline structure of wheat starch

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to analyze the crystal structure and crystallinity of the starch samples and to measure the long-range order of different wheat starch. In Fig. 4A, characteristic peaks at 2θ = 15°, 17°, 18°, and 23° are observed in all samples, representing the typical A-type crystalline pattern (Zhang et al., 2013). However, the YM15, FM8, and ZM7698 samples exhibited a peak at 2θ = 20°, indicative of a V-type crystalline pattern from starch-lipid complexes (Zhang et al., 2013). In terms of relative crystallinity (RC), the zhiqu wheat starches (MM902, YM15, and FM8) had higher crystallinity than bread wheat starches (ZM7698, XN9198, and XN1366), which is consistent with the amylose content data (Table 1). Thus, the presence of amylose can disrupt the crystalline packing of amylopectin (You et al., 2015).

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to analyze the short-range ordered structure of starches from different wheat varieties. As shown in Fig. 4B, all samples exhibited similar spectral profiles with characteristic absorption bands, including O—H stretching vibrations from hydrogen bonds (3363 cm−1), -CH₂ stretching (2929 cm−1), O—H bending of bound water in amorphous starch (1649 cm−1), C—H bending (1369 cm−1), and C—O stretching vibrations (1157, 1081, and 1018 cm−1) (Luo et al., 2022). Additionally, peaks at 1054 cm−1 (ordered crystalline region), 1022 cm−1 (amorphous structure), and 991 cm−1 (intermolecular hydrogen bonding) were observed. The intensity ratios R1054/1022 and R1022/991, representing the degree of order (DO) and degree of double helix (DD), respectively, exhibited no distinguishable differences among the six wheat starch samples (Fig. 4C) (Tian et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2019). These results demonstrate that both zhiqu and bread wheat starches possess similar functional groups, but zhiqu wheat starch possesses a higher degree of short-range ordered structure and crystallinity, characterized by a more regular molecular arrangement within granules. By contrast, bread wheat starch has lower internal crystallinity, a smaller proportion of short-range ordered structures, and exists mainly in an amorphous state.

Thermal properties of wheat starch

DSC was used to explore the thermal characteristics of different wheat starches. Onset temperature (To) was lowest (55.6 °C) for YM15 and highest (58.3 °C) for MM902, while peak temperature (Tp) ranged from 60.7 °C (ZM7698) to 62.7 °C (MM902). The final temperature (Tc) was between 67.3 °C (FM8) and 69.7 °C (XN1366). This resulted in a gelatinization range (Tc–To) of 9.1–13.0 °C, with FM8 and XN1366 showing the narrowest and widest ranges, respectively. Zhiqu wheat starches typically exhibited higher To and Tp, as well as a lower Tc and a narrower Tc–To, with the exception of YM15 (Table 2). Consistent with our results, the positive correlation between RC and both To and Tp aligns with existing research (Li et al., 2022). However, further investigation is needed to confirm the intrinsic relationship between starch gelatinization properties and its crystalline structure. The gelatinization enthalpy (ΔHgel) reflects the thermal energy required to melt microcrystals and the dissociation/unwinding of double helices via the disruption of inter- and intra-helical hydrogen bonds (Hu et al., 2023). In this study, ΔHgel was lowest (4.70 J/g) for XN9198 and highest (10.8 J/g) for XN1366, as listed in Table 2. This result demonstrated that XN1366 possessed the most stable crystalline structure, requiring more energy to break down double-helical arrangements during gelatinization. By comparison, the crystalline structure of XN9198 was relatively unstable. The thermal properties of starch are influenced by multiple factors, including amylose and amylopectin contents, starch granule size, granule ultrastructure, as well as the type and proportion of crystalling structures (Gayin et al., 2016; Osei Tutu et al., 2023). In this study, the different thermal characteristics might result in amylose contents (Table S1), starch granule size (Table 1) and relative crystallinity (Fig. 4A). However, the detail mechanism requires further research.

Pasting characteristics of different wheat starch

The gelatinization viscosity characteristics of wheat starch significantly influence its applications, and the gelatinization viscosity varied considerably among different varieties (Fig. 5H). Peak viscosity (PV) reflects the swelling capacity and water-binding ability of starch during cooking, and it ranged from 1083 cP (ZM7698) to 2861.7 cP (XN1366) (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the results reported by Akonor et al. (2023), amylose content exerted a greater influence on peak viscosity. The breakdown (BD) value, calculated as the difference between PV and trough viscosity (TV), indicates the heat resistance and shear-thinning properties of starch (Shi et al., 2024). A higher BD value suggests greater instability of starch granules during heating and gelatinization. In this work, BD values ranged from 190 cP (ZM7698) to 582 cP (YM15) (Fig. 5D). The negative correlation between amylose content and BD is consistent with previous studies (Zhu et al., 2016). The setback (SB) value is the difference between final viscosity (FV) and TV, and it serves as an important indicator of starch retrogradation (Wang et al., 2020). A higher setback (SB) value indicates a greater tendency for starch to retrograde. FV is the viscosity resulting from the cooling of gelatinized starch, reflecting the gelling properties of the starch paste. FV ranged from 1171 cP (ZM7698) to 3434.67 cP (XN1366) (Fig. 5C). With the exception of ZM7698 and XN1366, there were no significant differences in peak time (PT) among the wheat starch varieties (Fig. 5F); yet, the pasting temperature (PaT) ranged from 89.38 °C (ZM7698) to 94.70 °C (XN9198) (Fig. 5G). ZM7698 exhibited the lowest PV, TV, BD, FV, SB, and PT among the six varieties, which was attributed to its high amylose content, collectively influencing its gelatinization viscosity characteristics. These results indicate that zhiqu wheat pasting properties are highly homogeneous, with medium-to-high viscosity and high pasting temperature. By contrast, bread wheat varieties differ markedly: XN1366 (a high-gluten wheat) has high viscosity and high setback; ZM7696 exhibits very low viscosity and setback, strong thermal stability, but weak pasting capacity; and XN1998 is intermediate between the two varieties.

Correlation and principal component analysis

To clarify the intrinsic correlations among the physicochemical properties of wheat starch, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed on 20 parameters covering structural, thermal, and gelatinization characteristics. As illustrated in Fig. 6A and Table S2, the degree of brancing (DB) was positively correlated with amylose content (AM/AM+AP and AM/AP), while negatively correlated with SB, FV, BD, TV, PV and ΔHgel. Short-branch amylose content (AM1/AM2) was positively correlated with PaT and PT. Consistent with previous results (Ma et al., 2025), RC was positively correlated with To and Tp but negatively correlated with Tc and (Tc-To). SB showed a significant positive correlation with Tp, PV, TV, BD, FV and A-granuies, while it was negatively correlated with B-granuies, C-granuies, AM/AM+AP and AM/AP. These results revealed significant intrinsic correlations among wheat starch granular structure, amylose content, and gelatinization characteristics, thereby providing data support for elucidating the mechanisms of starch quality formation. Principal component analysis plots were used to visualize the differences and similarities among the six wheat starch samples. In Fig. 6B, 69.1% of the total variance was described by two principal components, with 45.4% in the first principal component (PC1) and 23.7% in the second principal component (PC2). All zhiqu wheat varieties were closely related and had positive scores on PC1 and PC2. However, bread wheat varieties exhibited a scattered distribution in PCA scores, which suggested a weak correlation among these accessions and revealed that genotypic differences markedly affected starch physicochemical properties (Li et al., 2021). Again, AM1/AM2, RC, PaT, B granules, A granules, Tc-To, Tc and ΔHgel loaded heavily on PC2, whereas PV, SB, TV, FV, AM/(AM+AP), AM/AP, DB and BD were found on PC1. These results showed that distinct distribution of bread wheat varieties in PCA scores demonstrated genotype differences exert substantial effects on starch physicochemical characteristics (Li et al., 2021). Notably, C granules, B granules, ΔHgel, DB, AM/AP and AM/(AM+AP) presented negative loadings on PC1, whereas PaT, PT, SB, FV, BD, TV, PV, RC, A granules, Tc-To, Tc, Tp and To displayed positive loadings (Fig. 6C, E). With regard to PC2, negative loadings were assigned to SB, FV, TV, PV, A granules, ΔHgel, Tc-To, Tc, AM/AP and AM/(AM+AP), and positive loadings to PaT, PT, BD, RC, C granules, B granules, Tp, To, DB and AM1/AM2 (Fig. 6D, F).

Conclusion

In this study, We systematically compared the physicochemical and structural properties of zhiqu and bread wheat starch, and identified differences in granule size, morphology, molecular structure, crystallinity, thermal properties, and pasting characteristics. Our findings indicate that zhiqu and bread wheat starches possess similar morphologies, and functional groups. Nevertheless, significant genotypic variations were observed in both DSC and RVA of wheat starches. XN1366 showed the highest ΔHgel, PV, and SB value, indicating a stable crystalline structure, strong swelling capacity, and a strong tendency for short-term retrogradation. Conversely, ZM7698 had the lowest viscosity parameters and setback value, correlating with superior hot-paste stability and anti-retrogradation. Zhiqu wheats MM902 and FM8 had narrower gelatinization ranges (more synchronous gelatinization), while YM15 showed a broader range (more heterogeneous behavior). These coordinated differences provide key parameters for targeted wheat selection in Baijiu brewing. Pearson correlation analysis revealed that high amylose content suppressed starch breakdown and reduced peak viscosity, whereas short-branched amylose chains positively correlated with pasting temperature. Principal component analysis effectively distinguished zhiqu wheat from bread wheat, zhiqu wheats, clustered on the positive PC1 axis, were positively correlated with viscosity parameters, pasting temperatures, setback, and A-type granules, reflecting high-viscosity and high-retrogradation traits. Bread wheats, located on the negative PC1 axis, were associated with higher amylose ratios, B/C-type granules, and gelatinization enthalpy. These results highlight amylose ratio, granule distribution, and viscosity as key indicators for wheat selection in Baijiu brewing. Overall, these results confirm that wheat starch functionality is closely tied to structural features. The findings facilitate tailored starch selection for food processing and guide breeding for cultivars with desired starch traits.
