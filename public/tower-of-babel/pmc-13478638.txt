Modeling anisotropic preference manifolds for robust graph-based fashion recommendation
Authors: Xiao Zhang, Tien-Ping Tan, Haiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graph Convolutional Networks (GNNs) have become a dominant paradigm for collaborative filtering; however, their efficacy in complex domains like fashion is often hindered by a fundamental geometric mismatch between model assumptions and data reality. Conventional methods typically rely on scalar aggregation and Euclidean metrics, which implicitly assume that user interest clusters are isotropic (spherical) and uniformly dense. This assumption fails to capture the complexity of fashion preferences, where style distributions exhibit significant anisotropic variance–ranging from sparse, broad categories to dense, niche trends. To bridge this gap, we propose the Multi-Interest Mahalanobis Denoising Graph Convolutional Network (MIMD-GCN), a framework that synergizes structural disentanglement with geometry-aware denoising. We introduce a Poly-Attention mechanism to disentangle user representations into multiple latent interest centers, thereby resolving the collapse of diverse preferences into single vectors. Furthermore, we construct an anisotropic denoising module based on a learnable Mahalanobis distance barrier. Unlike static Euclidean thresholds, this mechanism dynamically adapts to the covariance structure of specific interest manifolds, establishing elliptical boundaries that effectively isolate spurious interactions while preserving valid niche signals. Extensive experiments on Amazon-Clothing and Taobao datasets demonstrate that MIMD-GCN consistently improves recommendation performance over strong baselines and exhibits enhanced robustness under synthetic noise. These results suggest that anisotropic modeling provides a beneficial geometric perspective for capturing complex user preferences in fashion recommendation.

Introduction

Recommender systems serve as critical filters in large-scale e-commerce ecosystems, guiding users through vast catalogs to discover relevant products. Recently, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as the dominant paradigm for collaborative filtering, owing to their ability to capture high-order user-item connectivity and propagate collaborative signals effectively1–3. Models such as LightGCN4 have demonstrated that simplified linear propagation can achieve state-of-the-art performance by refining embeddings over the user-item interaction graph.

Despite these advancements, the application of GNNs to complex, visually-driven domains like fashion reveals a fundamental geometric mismatch between standard modeling assumptions and the structural reality of user preferences. We identify two critical limitations in current architectures. First, most graph-based recommenders employ scalar aggregation, which compresses a user’s heterogeneous historical behaviors into a single embedding vector. This may lead to collapse collapses distinct interest facets–such as a user’s simultaneous preference for “casual streetwear” and “formal business attire”–into a homogenized representation that lacks specificity.

Second, and perhaps more critically, existing denoising strategies predominantly rely on Euclidean distance metrics, which implicitly assume that user interest clusters are isotropic (spherical) and uniformly dense in the latent space. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this assumption is ill-posed for fashion data. In reality, user interests manifest as anisotropic manifolds: broad preferences often form sparse, high-variance clouds, whereas niche trends concentrate into dense, low-variance regions. Under such conditions, rigid spherical boundaries fail to distinguish between valid long-tail signals and random noise.

To bridge this gap, we propose the Multi-Interest Mahalanobis Denoising Graph Convolutional Network (MIMD-GCN). Our framework synergizes structural disentanglement with geometry-aware denoising to learn more robust user representations. Specifically, we introduce a Poly-Attention mechanism that extracts multiple latent interest centers directly from the interaction graph, preventing representation collapse. A pivotal component of our approach is the adaptive Mahalanobis confidence barrier. Unlike static Euclidean thresholds, this mechanism dynamically models the covariance structure of each interest cluster, constructing elliptical decision boundaries(Fig. 1b) that selectively suppress spurious interactions while preserving valid niche behaviors.

We evaluate the proposed method on two real-world fashion recommendation datasets. Experimental results show that MIMD-GCN consistently improves recommendation performance over strong baselines and demonstrates enhanced robustness under synthetic noise conditions. Related work

Graph-based collaborative filtering

Graph-based collaborative filtering (GCF)5,6 has become a dominant paradigm in recommender systems due to its ability to exploit high-order connectivity in user–item graphs. Early works such as GC-MC2 and PinSage5 demonstrated the effectiveness of graph convolution for collaborative filtering, while NGCF3 further enhanced message passing with nonlinear transformations. LightGCN4 later showed that simplified linear neighborhood aggregation can achieve strong performance with improved scalability. Building on this backbone, numerous variants have been proposed, including DGCF7, IMP-GCN8, NIA-GCN9, and HS-GCN10, aiming to improve representation capacity through disentanglement or interest-aware propagation. However, most existing GCF models still compress heterogeneous user behaviors into a single embedding via scalar aggregation, which may lead to representation collapse and insufficient modeling of diverse fashion preferences.

Sequential recommendation

Sequential recommendation explicitly models temporal dynamics to capture evolving user interests11. Representative Transformer-based approaches such as SASRec12 and BERT4Rec13 have achieved strong performance by emphasizing recent interaction patterns. Dynamic graph-based models, including DGSR14 and related temporal GNN frameworks15–19, attempt to unify sequential modeling with graph structure by allowing embeddings to evolve over time. Despite these advances, sequential methods often focus on local transition patterns and may underutilize the global collaborative signals provided by the full interaction graph. Moreover, their reliance on fine-grained behavioral trajectories can increase sensitivity to noisy or irregular implicit feedback, limiting robustness in complex domains such as fashion.

Multi-interest modeling

To capture the inherent diversity of human preferences, multi-interest recommendation represents each user with multiple latent intent vectors rather than a single embedding. Models such as MIND20 and ComiRec21 extract diverse interests from sequences using routing or attention mechanisms, while subsequent studies explore clustering-based methods22, self-supervised learning23, and attention-driven24 disentanglement to enhance diversity modeling. Several recent works, including SINE25, MGNN-SP26, and ICLRec27, begin integrating multi-interest modeling with graph propagation. Recent intent-aware models like ESIGCF28 efficiently implicitly capture user intents within a simplified graph framework. However, they rely on unified representations and isotropic metrics without geometry-aware denoising. Consequently, robustly disentangling multiple interests under severe anisotropic noise remains an open challenge.

Graph denoising and geometric limitations

Interaction noise remains a pervasive challenge in recommender systems, motivating a range of denoising strategies. Some works focus on pruning unreliable relations or edges in social and interaction graphs based on preference confidence (e.g., robust preference-guided denoising) to improve influence diffusion29. Other approaches integrate adaptive graph contrastive learning with customized noise generators to alleviate sparsity and noise effects30. Recent models further propose collaborative signal decoupling and targeted denoising modules to strengthen robustness against unstable interactions31. Additional studies explore adaptive noise pruning and structural similarity-based interaction enrichment to mitigate noise while preserving data integrity32. Preference-aware denoising mechanisms have also been applied in social recommendation to filter noisy neighbor influences33. Nevertheless, prevailing denoising techniques still predominantly rely on isotropic similarity measures (e.g., cosine/Euclidean distances), which may be suboptimal across anisotropic interest manifolds such as those seen in fashion recommendation.

Differences from existing approaches

MIMD-GCN unifies graph collaborative filtering, multi-interest modeling, and interaction denoising into a geometry-aware framework, differing from existing works in three key ways. First, unlike conventional GCF models that collapse interactions into a single embedding, our approach disentangles user representations into multiple latent vectors to capture heterogeneous preferences. Second, while recent intent-aware models (e.g., ESIGCF28) efficiently simplify multi-interest extraction, they largely ignore structural anisotropic noise. MIMD-GCN directly performs disentanglement on the graph, inherently coupling it with noise filtration. Finally, compared to prevailing denoising strategies relying on rigid isotropic metrics (e.g., Euclidean distance), we introduce an anisotropic Mahalanobis barrier that adapts to the unique variance of distinct interest clusters. Overall, this unified integration provides a complementary perspective that significantly enhances both representation capacity and robustness.

Methods

To address the geometric limitations inherent in standard collaborative filtering, we introduce the Multi-Interest Mahalanobis Denoising Graph Convolutional Network (MIMD-GCN). Illustrated in Fig. 2, our framework advances the LightGCN architecture by integrating two synergistic mechanisms designed to capture the complexity of fashion preferences. First, we implement a structural multi-interest representation that disentangles heterogeneous user behaviors, thereby preventing the collapse of distinct stylistic preferences into a single, averaged embedding. Second, we incorporate an anisotropic Mahalanobis denoising module. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on rigid isotropic (Euclidean) assumptions, this mechanism constructs geometry-aware confidence barriers that model user interests as adaptive elliptical manifolds. To resolve the representation bottleneck, we introduce a Poly-Attention structural disentanglement mechanism that models each user as a set of K latent interest centers:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{C}_u = \{ \textbf{c}_{u,1}, \dots , \textbf{c}_{u,K} \}, \quad \textbf{c}_{u,k} \in \mathbb {R}^d,$$\end{document}Instead of learning interests from sequences, our key novelty is to extract multi-interest representations directly on the interaction graph, enabling global collaborative propagation across multiple preference channels.

Anisotropic denoising via Mahalanobis barrier

Implicit feedback in real-world fashion platforms inevitably contains substantial noise, such as accidental clicks, casual browsing, or short-term exploratory behaviors. If treated equally during graph propagation, these unreliable edges may distort high-order collaborative signals and lead to degraded recommendation robustness. Most existing graph denoising strategies adopt Euclidean distance as the similarity criterion, implicitly assuming that user preferences form isotropic spherical neighborhoods in the embedding space. However, fashion interests are inherently heterogeneous: some styles cluster tightly, whereas others exhibit sparse and elongated distributions. This motivates the need for an anisotropic geometry-aware denoising mechanism.

To address this limitation, we propose an interest-specific Mahalanobis confidence barrier, which constructs adaptive elliptical regions around each latent interest center and selectively suppresses noisy interactions outside these regions.

For each channel k, we learn a diagonal variance-aware weight vector:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{w}_k \in \mathbb {R}^d,$$\end{document}approximating the inverse covariance matrix. While the standard Mahalanobis distance requires the full inverse covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Sigma ^{-1}$$\end{document}, computing it is computationally prohibitive (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(d^3)$$\end{document}) and prone to instability in sparse settings. We adopt a learnable diagonal matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_k$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$diag(w_k)$$\end{document}) based on the assumption that the latent dimensions in the learned embedding space are implicitly disentangled. This design allows us to model the variance (i.e., the “width”) of the interest cluster along each axis independently, reducing the complexity to O(d) while effectively capturing the anisotropic nature of user preferences.

The Mahalanobis distance between item i and interest center \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c}_{u,k}$$\end{document} is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_M^{(k)}(u,i) = \sqrt{ (\textbf{e}_i - \textbf{c}_{u,k})^\top \textrm{diag}(\textbf{w}_k) (\textbf{e}_i - \textbf{c}_{u,k}) }.$$\end{document}We then define the interaction confidence as the minimum distance across interest manifolds:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{u,i} = \exp \,\left( -\frac{ \min _k \left( D_M^{(k)}(u,i)\right) ^2 }{\tau } \right) ,$$\end{document}where τ controls the softness of the barrier. To ensure numerical stability, we add a small scalar \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon =1e^{-9}$$\end{document} inside the square root calculation of the Mahalanobis distance.

Therefore, noisy interactions lying outside all elliptical interest regions receive low confidence and are suppressed during message passing, yielding a geometry-consistent denoising mechanism.

Geometric Interpretation. From a geometric perspective, each interest channel can be viewed as defining an adaptive preference manifold with its own variance structure in the embedding space. Under this formulation, the Mahalanobis-based confidence mechanism induces anisotropic (elliptical) regions rather than conventional isotropic (spherical) neighborhoods. Such shape-adaptive boundaries allow the model to better accommodate heterogeneous preference distributions, where different styles may exhibit varying levels of dispersion. As a result, interactions that are consistent with the underlying preference structure are more likely to be preserved, while less relevant or noisy signals are gradually suppressed during propagation. This provides an intuitive geometric interpretation of how anisotropic modeling contributes to more robust representation learning in graph-based recommendation.

Probabilistic Interpretation. The Mahalanobis distance used in our denoising module can also be interpreted from a probabilistic perspective. Specifically, the squared Mahalanobis distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(D_M^{(k)}(u,i))^2$$\end{document} corresponds to the quadratic form in the exponent of a multivariate Gaussian distribution. By defining the confidence score as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{u,i} = \exp \left( -\frac{\min _k (D_M^{(k)}(u,i))^2}{\tau }\right)$$\end{document}, the model implicitly assigns higher confidence to interactions that lie closer to the center of an interest-specific distribution. Under this view, the items associated with each latent interest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{u,k}$$\end{document} can be approximately characterized by a Gaussian distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {N}(c_{u,k}, \Sigma _k)$$\end{document}, where the covariance matrix is represented using a diagonal approximation, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Sigma _k \approx \textrm{diag}(w_k)^{-1}$$\end{document}. The resulting anisotropic confidence regions can therefore be understood as level sets of this distribution, reflecting different variance scales across embedding dimensions. This interpretation provides a probabilistic intuition for how the proposed mechanism adapts to heterogeneous preference structures while maintaining computational efficiency. Notably, when the covariance matrix reduces to an identity matrix, the Mahalanobis distance degenerates to the Euclidean distance. Therefore, the proposed method can be viewed as a generalized form of conventional isotropic denoising.

Prediction and optimization

Given the learned interest set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{C}_u$$\end{document}, the preference score between user u and a target item j is defined as the maximum similarity across all interest channels:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{u,j} = \max _k \textbf{c}_{u,k}^\top \textbf{e}_j.$$\end{document}The model is optimized using a confidence-reweighted Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) loss:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{\text {bpr}} = \sum _{(u,i,j) \in \mathscr {D}} - g_{u,i} \, \ln \sigma (\hat{y}_{u,i} - \hat{y}_{u,j}),$$\end{document}where the confidence score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{u,i}$$\end{document} down-weights unreliable interactions identified by the denoising module.

To prevent multiple interest heads from collapsing into the same mode, we impose orthogonality regularization:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{\text {orth}} = \left\| \textbf{Q}\textbf{Q}^\top - \textbf{I} \right\| _F^2,$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{I}$$\end{document} is the identity matrix. This constraint serves two purposes: (1) Orthogonality: It forces different query prototypes to be nearly perpendicular, encouraging them to capture distinct and complementary preference subspaces; (2) Normality: It constrains the unit norm of each prototype, preventing any single interest channel from dominating the attention mechanism purely due to magnitude scale.

The final training objective is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L} = \mathscr {L}_{\text {bpr}} + \lambda _{\text {orth}} \, \mathscr {L}_{\text {orth}},$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{\text {orth}}$$\end{document} controls the strength of the orthogonality regularization.

Complexity analysis

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\mathscr {E}|$$\end{document} denote the number of edges, d the embedding dimension, L the number of graph convolution layers, and K the number of interest channels. LightGCN has a per-epoch complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(L |\mathscr {E}| d)$$\end{document}, which is comparable since K is small.

MIMD-GCN introduces two additional costs. First, Poly-Attention aggregation performs K attention computations per edge, resulting in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(L |\mathscr {E}| d)$$\end{document}. Second, the Mahalanobis distance uses a diagonal approximation, reducing distance computation to element-wise operations with complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(L |\mathscr {E}| d)$$\end{document}. Overall, the per-epoch complexity is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(L |\mathscr {E}| d)$$\end{document}. Since K is typically small, the proposed model maintains scalability comparable to standard graph-based collaborative filtering methods.

Experiments

This section presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of the proposed MIMD-GCN framework. The experiments are designed to systematically verify the following hypotheses:

RQ1: Whether MIMD-GCN achieves superior recommendation accuracy compared with state-of-the-art baselines in fashion recommendation.

RQ2: Whether each proposed component–structural multi-interest modeling and anisotropic denoising–contributes independently to performance improvements.

RQ3: Whether the proposed Mahalanobis barrier can effectively identify and suppress noisy implicit interactions, thereby improving robustness.

RQ4: Whether modeling user interests as anisotropic (elliptical) manifolds provides measurable advantages over conventional isotropic (Euclidean) assumptions.

Experimental settings

Datasets

We evaluate our model on two real-world fashion recommendation datasets with distinct scales and sparsity characteristics.

Amazon-Clothing is constructed from the Clothing, Shoes and Jewelry category of the Amazon Review dataset. Following standard preprocessing protocols, we apply a 5-core filtering strategy and retain users with at least 20 interactions to ensure sufficient behavioral evidence.

Taobao-Clothing is a large-scale industrial dataset containing implicit click interactions on clothing items collected from the Taobao platform. To reduce extreme sparsity, users and items with fewer than 20 interactions are removed.

Evaluation metrics

Performance is evaluated using Recall@20 and NDCG@20, two standard metrics for Top-K recommendation. Recall@20 measures whether the ground-truth item appears in the top-20 ranked list, while NDCG@20 further accounts for ranking positions, assigning higher importance to top-ranked recommendations.

Baselines

We include representative models from different paradigms, including graph-based, sequential, and multi-interest recommendation methods, to ensure a fair and comprehensive comparison.

NGCF3: A graph-based collaborative filtering model that incorporates nonlinear feature transformations during message passing.

LightGCN4: A simplified and highly effective GCN-based recommender that relies solely on linear neighborhood aggregation. This model also serves as the backbone of our approach.

SASRec12: A Transformer-based sequential recommendation model that captures long-term dependencies in user interaction sequences.

ComiRec34: A multi-interest sequential recommendation model that extracts multiple user interests via multi-head attention.

ESIGCF28: An extremely simplified graph collaborative filtering model that incorporates intent-aware mechanisms to enhance representation learning while maintaining efficiency.

DGSR14: A dynamic graph neural network that integrates temporal information into graph-based recommendation.

These baselines allow us to evaluate both the benefit of structural multi-interest modeling and the effectiveness of anisotropic denoising.

Robustness to noisy interactions (RQ3)

Noise Injection Setup. To rigorously evaluate the robustness of the proposed framework, we corrupt the original user-item bipartite graph with synthetic noise. Following standard evaluation protocols, we simulate false-positive interactions–such as accidental clicks or casual browsing–by randomly sampling unobserved items and injecting them as positive edges for each user. This noise is introduced at a fixed ratio of 10% relative to the user’s original interaction count. This controlled contamination provides a quantitative basis for assessing the Mahalanobis denoising module’s efficacy in isolating genuine stylistic preferences from spurious signals.

Genuine interactions cluster densely near g ≈ 1.0, confirming that the learned elliptical envelopes accurately enclose valid user interests. In contrast, synthetic noise is heavily suppressed towards g ≈ 0.0. Summary

In this section, we empirically evaluated the proposed MIMD-GCN framework from multiple perspectives, including overall performance, component-wise contributions, robustness to noisy interactions, and geometric interpretability. The results show that MIMD-GCN consistently outperforms competitive baselines across both datasets, indicating that integrating multi-interest modeling with geometry-aware denoising can improve recommendation quality. The ablation study further demonstrates that both structural disentanglement and the Mahalanobis-based denoising mechanism contribute positively to performance, suggesting that these components provide complementary benefits. In terms of robustness, the observed separation in confidence scores indicates that the proposed denoising mechanism is capable of distinguishing informative interactions from injected noise, which helps stabilize model performance under noisy conditions. Additionally, the visualization results provide qualitative evidence that the learned representations better reflect heterogeneous preference structures compared to conventional isotropic assumptions. Overall, these findings suggest that incorporating anisotropic modeling into graph-based recommendation offers a useful perspective for handling diverse and noisy user preferences. While the improvements are moderate, the proposed framework provides consistent gains and enhanced robustness, particularly in complex domains such as fashion recommendation.

Conclusion and future work

In this work, we investigated the geometric limitations of conventional graph-based recommendation models in the context of fashion recommendation. To address these challenges, we proposed MIMD-GCN, a framework that integrates multi-interest representation learning with an anisotropic, Mahalanobis-based denoising mechanism. By combining structural disentanglement with geometry-aware interaction filtering, the model aims to better capture diverse user preferences while reducing the influence of noisy interactions.

Experimental results on Amazon-Clothing and Taobao-Clothing datasets show that MIMD-GCN achieves consistent improvements over strong baselines and demonstrates enhanced robustness under synthetic noise settings. These findings suggest that incorporating anisotropic modeling into graph-based recommendation provides a useful and flexible perspective for handling heterogeneous preference distributions.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations remain. First, the Mahalanobis distance is approximated using a diagonal covariance matrix, which ignores potential correlations across embedding dimensions and may limit the full expressiveness of anisotropic modeling. Second, the performance gains over competitive baselines are relatively modest, indicating that the proposed approach provides incremental rather than transformative improvements. Third, the model introduces additional hyperparameters, such as the number of interest heads K and the temperature τ, which require careful tuning and may affect generalization across datasets. Finally, the robustness evaluation relies on synthetic noise injection, which may not fully reflect the complexity of real-world noisy interactions.

In future work, we plan to explore more expressive covariance modeling strategies to capture cross-dimensional dependencies, as well as extend the framework to incorporate multi-modal signals in fashion recommendation. Another promising direction is to investigate adaptive mechanisms for dynamically adjusting the number of user interests based on interaction patterns, further improving flexibility and personalization.
