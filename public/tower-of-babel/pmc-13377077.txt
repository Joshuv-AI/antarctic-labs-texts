Genetic polymorphism of Plasmodium falciparum MSP-2 (3D7/IC) and multiplicity of infection in N’Djamena, Chad
Authors: Yamssi Cedric, Noukeu diomède, Masra Mislengar Hervé, Tchuenkam Kom Pacome, Djobo Abel, Tordibaye Ngaryedji, Barka Saleh, Haibo Hu, Noumedem Anangmo Christelle Nadia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains one of the most widespread endemic diseases in Africa. Molecular epidemiological studies of Plasmodium falciparum genetic diversity are essential for understanding transmission dynamics and informing effective control strategies. This study aimed to assess the genetic polymorphism of the merozoite surface protein 2 (msp2) 3D7/IC allelic family and multiplicity of infection in malaria patients. A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted from July to September 2024. Blood samples were collected from 217 participants attending Our Lady of the Apostles Hospital in N’Djamena, Chad. Malaria infection and parasite density were determined using immunochromatographic tests and thick blood smears. Parasite DNA was extracted using the Chelex-100 method, and the msp2 3D7/IC allelic family was amplified by nested PCR. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the Lower Left matrix with R software, and statistical analyses were conducted using Excel 2016 and SPSS version 23. The prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum infection was 23.5%, while the molecular frequency of the msp2 3D7/IC allelic family was 94.11%. Genetic polymorphism within this allelic family was observed, with a predominance of one genotype (18.46%) and low-frequency alleles ranging between 560 and 570 base pairs (3.07% and 1.53%, respectively). Single-clone infections were most frequent (64.65%), followed by double (33.33%) and triple infections (2.08%), with no quadruple infections detected. Phylogenetic analysis revealed eight clades, with clades 1, 4, and 7 showing a higher concentration of genetically related isolates. This study demonstrates substantial genetic diversity and multiplicity offalciparum infections within the msp-2 3D7/IC allelic family, reflecting ongoing malaria transmission in the study area. Introduction

Malaria remains the world’s leading parasitic endemic disease. Although curable, it is potentially fatal and caused by Plasmodium species (Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium malariae, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium ovale, and Plasmodium knowlesi)1. It is a febrile hemolytic disease that occurs endemically in tropical and subtropical regions. Its presence in society, along with its high morbidity and mortality, makes it a major public health concern2.

Globally, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) report, there were 247 million malaria cases in 2021, compared to 245 million in 2020. The estimated number of malaria-related deaths reached 619,000 in 2021, compared to 625,000 in 20203. Sub-Saharan Africa is the most affected region, accounting for over 90% of cases annually, making malaria the leading cause of hospitalizations in Africa4.

In Chad, malaria is also the leading cause of consultations in maternal and child health services in all hospitals. The 2023 malaria report recorded 1,028,800 malaria cases, including 192,928 severe cases and 8,880 deaths5. Children under five years of age accounted for approximately 80% of all malaria-related deaths6. This parasitic disease is a vector-borne infection, transmitted to humans by the bite of the female Anopheles mosquito, belonging to the Culicidae family6.

Since the 1990 s, malaria incidence and malaria-related mortality have both increased. The first cases of chloroquine resistance appeared in Asia and South America, and later in Africa around the 1980s7. In response, Chad, like other countries, adopted in 2005 a national policy for the management of uncomplicated malaria based on Artesunate-Amodiaquine (ASAQ) and Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) as first- and second-line treatments, respectively, and Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine (SP) as intermittent preventive treatment (IPTp) in pregnant women8.

Malaria remains a major public health concern, particularly due to the emergence and spread of drug-resistant Plasmodium falciparum strains. While the development of an effective vaccine represents a critical strategy for malaria control, progress is hindered by the parasite’s high genetic polymorphism, which allows it to evade host immunity9. Among the parasite’s surface antigens, the merozoite surface protein 2 (MSP2) is highly polymorphic and plays a central role in eliciting the immune response. Its genetic diversity, characterized by multiple alleles grouped into the FC27 and 3D7/IC families, not only complicates vaccine development but also reflects the multiplicity of infection (MOI) in endemic populations, where individuals often harbor multiple genetically distinct parasite clones10.

The merozoite surface protein 2 (MSP2) is a major antigen that elicits an immune response in humans11. The msp2 gene is located on chromosome 2 of Plasmodium falciparum and is highly polymorphic, with 19 known alleles classified into two main allelic families: FC27 and 3D7/IC12. The FC27 family is characterized by multiple tandem repeat motifs in its central variable region, which tend to be longer and more complex, whereas the 3D7/IC family has shorter, more conserved repeat motifs with distinct sequence patterns13. This extensive allelic diversity contributes to the high multiplicity of infection (MOI) observed in endemic areas, where individuals often carry multiple genetically distinct parasite clones simultaneously. Because of this variability,falciparum genotypes are often unique to each individual, making MSP2 a valuable molecular marker for assessing genetic diversity, estimating MOI, and distinguishing between recrudescence and new infections14.

The main goal of this study was to investigate the genetic diversity and allelic distribution of the Plasmodium falciparum merozoite surface protein 2 (MSP-2) 3D7/IC allelic family, and to determine the multiplicity of infection among malaria-infected patients at Our Lady of the Apostles Hospital in N’Djamena, Chad, in order to generate baseline molecular epidemiological data to support malaria control and management strategies.

Materials and methods

Type of study and study site

The present research was designed as a cross-sectional and analytical study. It was conducted at Our Lady of the Apostles Hospital, a major healthcare institution located in the 7th arrondissement of N’Djamena South District, Chad. Established in 1948, the hospital provides comprehensive medical services to a large and diverse population within the capital city and its surrounding areas. Figure 1 illustrates the geographical location of the study site within the city of N’Djamena.

Map of the city of N’Djamena15.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients who presented to Our Lady of the Apostles Hospital in N’Djamena for malaria diagnosis and who voluntarily provided informed consent were enrolled in the study. In the case of minors, participation was authorized through parental or guardian consent. Patients known to have hemoglobinopathies such as hemophilia or sickle cell disease, as well as those who had received antimalarial treatment within the two weeks preceding sample collection, were excluded from participation.

Molecular analyses

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted according to the method described by Walsh et al17.,. Briefly, filter paper samples were cut into small pieces and placed in Eppendorf tubes. One milliliter (1 mL) of 0.5% Tween 20 solution was added, and the tubes were incubated at 4 °C for at least 16 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 15 min. Then, 150 µL of 20% Chelex-100 resin was added to each tube, and the mixture was heated in a water bath at 100 °C for 10 min, with vortexing for 1 min, repeated three times. The samples were then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was transferred into a new microtube and centrifuged again for 5 min. The final supernatant, containing the extracted genomic DNA, was collected and stored for downstream molecular analysis.

Amplification of the MSP-2 3D7/IC allelic family of Plasmodium falciparum by nested Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

For the second reaction (Nest 2), 9.6 µL of Master Mix was combined with 0.4 µL of the Nest 1 amplicon, and amplification was performed as described in Table I. Electrophoresis and revelation

The 3D7/IC allelic family of MSP-2 PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.3% agarose gel prepared with SafeView DNA stain. Electrophoretic migration was conducted in 0.5× TBE buffer at 150 V for 60 min. The resulting DNA bands were visualized under a UV transilluminator.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the National Bioethics Committee of Chad (registration number 041/MESRSFP/SE/SG/CNBT/SG/2025). All methods were performed in accordance with (Declaration of Helsinki) the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. For minors, consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. Participants who tested positive for malaria were referred to appropriate healthcare services for treatment in accordance with national guidelines.

Molecular frequency of the MSP-2 3D7/IC allelic family

Figure 3 shows the molecular frequency of the MSP-2 3D7/IC allelic family. Of the 51 PCR-positive samples, 48 (94.11%) revealed the presence of the MSP-2 3D7/IC allele. Electrophoresis revealed DNA fragments ranging in size from 485 to 745 base pairs (bp). Figure 4 presents the electrophoretic profile of MSP-2 3D7/IC amplification products obtained from samples collected at Our Lady of the Apostles Hospital. A total of 65 allelic bands were detected across all samples, corresponding to 10 distinct 3D7/IC genotypes, reflecting a high degree of polymorphism within this allelic family. This genetic diversity indicates the presence of multiple parasite clones within the study population, consistent with intense malaria transmission and with potential implications for malaria control and treatment strategies in the study area.

Allelic frequency of the MSP-2 3D7/IC allelic family

Figure 5 illustrates the allelic frequency distribution of the MSP-2 3D7/IC allelic family. A total of 106 DNA fragments were detected, corresponding to 65 distinct 3D7/IC genotypes. The predominant genotypes had fragment sizes of 555 bp, 530 bp, and 500 bp, each exhibiting an allele frequency of 18.46%. In contrast, the least frequent genotypes, measuring 560 bp and 570 bp, displayed frequencies of 3.07% and 1.53%, respectively. This distribution highlights a marked heterogeneity within the 3D7/IC allelic family, reflecting substantial genetic variability among the Plasmodium falciparum isolates analyzed. Such high allelic diversity indicates the presence of multiple concurrent parasite clones within the host population, consistent with intense malaria transmission in the study area.

Multiplicity of infection (MOI), determined by the number of allelic bands per sample, showed that single-clone (monoclonal) infections were the most frequent (64.65%). Double infections accounted for 33.33% of cases, while triple infections represented 2.08%. No quadruple infections were observed, as illustrated in Fig. 7. These results indicate that multiple parasite clones are present within the study population, reflecting high transmission intensity and the potential complexity of malaria infections in the study area.

Discussion

The overall malaria prevalence of 23.5% observed in this study is consistent with the endemic nature of malaria in Chad, although it is lower than the 30% reported in other regions of the country20. Similarly, Abel et al21. reported a Plasmodium falciparum prevalence of 40.72% in N’Djamena, Chad, in a study on the genetic polymorphism of the merozoite surface protein 2 (MSP2) and its implications for parasite resistance, a finding consistent with the high transmission intensity observed in the region. This variation may be explained by seasonal differences at the time of sampling, as well as geographical and climatic factors that influence mosquito abundance and transmission efficiency. Differences in health-seeking behavior, access to diagnostic services, and the implementation of malaria control interventions may also contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Comparable prevalence levels have been reported in the North-West Region of Cameroon (34.7%)22, the Central African Republic (40.6%)23, and Nigeria (35.7%)24, highlighting the continued public health burden of malaria across Central Africa despite sustained control efforts.

The significantly higher prevalence observed among children aged 0–5 years (60%) aligns with reports from Cameroon and Nigeria, where young children consistently bear the greatest malaria burden25,26. This vulnerability is largely attributable to their limited acquisition of protective immunity and increased biological susceptibility. The comparatively lower prevalence recorded in N’Djamena relative to rural settings may reflect improved access to healthcare facilities, enhanced urban vector control measures, and wider availability and use of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs).

The predominance of pupils and students (33.72%) among malaria-positive individuals is in agreement with findings by Fomba et al27. in Mali and Ebogo et al28. in Cameroon. This trend may be influenced by behavioral factors, including late-evening outdoor activities, inconsistent use of LLINs, and increased exposure to mosquito vectors in and around educational environments. Although most sociodemographic variables examined in this study were not significantly associated with malaria infection, the absence of protective clothing was identified as a relevant risk factor. While non-use of LLINs showed a weak and non-significant association with malaria infection (OR = 2.48; p = 0.58), LLIN utilization remains a well-established protective factor in numerous studies conducted in Cameroon, Nigeria, and Ghana29–31. The lack of a statistically significant association between proximity to water bodies and infection risk may reflect effective nighttime protection measures or seasonal variations in mosquito breeding patterns at the time of the study.

Molecular analysis revealed a high frequency of msp2 detection (94.11%), confirming the predominance of Plasmodium falciparum among malaria cases in N’Djamena. This finding is consistent with reports from north-west Ethiopia, wherefalciparum accounted for 88% of infections32. The identification of 10 distinct msp2 alleles ranging from 485 to 745 bp, with an overall genetic diversity of 15.38%, indicates a moderately diverse parasite population. This level of diversity is considerably lower than that reported in north-west Ethiopia (95%)32, suggesting differences in transmission intensity, population structure, or sampling context, particularly between urban and rural settings.

The predominance of monoclonal infections (64.65%), relative to double (33.33%) and triple (2.08%) infections, further supports the characterization of malaria transmission in N’Djamena as moderate rather than high. This contrasts with hyperendemic regions of Ethiopia, where multiclonal infections are more common and the mean multiplicity of infection (MOI) reaches 3.4632. Nevertheless, the detection of multiclonal infections in 35.41% of cases indicates that parasite transmission persists within the study area. While such infections provide opportunities for genetic recombination, the present data alone do not permit direct conclusions regarding transmission intensity or evolutionary selection pressures.

The observed msp2 polymorphism, particularly involving alleles belonging to the 3D7 family, is consistent with findings from north-east Ethiopia, where 38 different msp2 alleles were reported, including 20 IC/3D7 and 18 FC27 variants33. High antigenic variability at this locus is a common feature offalciparum populations and reflects immune-driven diversification. Such diversity represents a recognized challenge for malaria vaccine development, as it may limit immune recognition of shared antigenic determinants34.

Although genetic diversity and multiplicity of infection are often discussed in the context of antimalarial resistance, it is important to emphasize that this study did not investigate validated resistance-associated loci. Consequently, no direct conclusions regarding drug resistance selection or emergence can be drawn. The presence of multiclonal infections may theoretically provide conditions favorable for genetic recombination, but confirmation of any link with resistance requires targeted analysis of resistance markers.

Therefore, future molecular surveillance efforts in Chad should integrate neutral diversity markers such as msp2 with established resistance loci, including pfcrt, pfdhfr, pfdhps, and pfkelch13. Such integrated approaches are necessary to accurately assess transmission dynamics, monitor resistance trends, and support evidence-based adjustments to malaria treatment policies.

The moderate genetic diversity and multiplicity of Plasmodium falciparum infections observed in N’Djamena indicate ongoing but controlled malaria transmission in an urban context. While msp2 polymorphism provides valuable insight into parasite population structure, comprehensive molecular surveillance incorporating resistance markers is essential for a more complete understanding of malaria epidemiology in Chad. These data will be critical for optimizing control strategies and supporting long-term malaria management efforts in the region.

Limitation

One limitation of this study is that only the 3D7/IC allelic family of the msp-2 gene was analyzed. The FC27 allelic family was not included due to resource constraints. As a result, the genetic diversity of Plasmodium falciparum and the multiplicity of infection may have been underestimated. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating both msp-2 allelic families (3D7/IC and FC27) are recommended to provide a more comprehensive assessment of parasite population structure and transmission dynamics.

Conclusion

This study suggests that individuals are exposed to multiple strains offalciparum, which may have implications for immunity and treatment efficacy. These data are essential for understanding local transmission dynamics and guiding malaria control strategies, such as vaccine development or antimalarial drug surveillance.
