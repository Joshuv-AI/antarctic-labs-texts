Agreement and repeatability between manual anthropometry and mobile 3D digital anthropometry in young adults
Authors: Beatriz Nácher-Fernández, Raquel Vaquero-Cristóbal, Mario Albaladejo-Saura, Jorge Valero-Zorraquino, Alfredo Ballester, Nicolás Baglietto, Malek Mecherques, Francisco Esparza-Ros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMobile 3D digital anthropometry has emerged as a rapid and accessible alternative to manual anthropometry. However, evidence regarding its agreement with manual methods and its suitability for interchangeable use remains limited, particularly when raw anthropometric variables are considered. This study aimed (a) to assess differences between manual anthropometry —following ISAK protocol— and digital anthropometry —using 3Davatar/Body mobile phone application— and (b) to evaluate the agreement between both methods.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 264 young adults (160 males and 104 females). Participants underwent manual anthropometric assessment performed by ISAK level 3 accredited anthropometrists, and digitized in 3D using the phone-based digital anthropometry application by non-certified users. Test-retest repeatability was evaluated using the standard error of measurement (SEM) and coefficient of variation (CV), while agreement between methods was evaluated using mean bias, Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), and Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsIntra-rater manual anthropometry showed good repeatability, with CV values generally below 2% for most girths and lengths. Digital anthropometry demonstrated good repeatability for girths and selected heights, particularly waist, hip, and lower-limb girths (CV = 0.8–1.5%), but lower repeatability for lengths of the arms and some breadths (CV > 2%). Trunk depths showed the lowest repeatability for both techniques, (CV > 2%). Significant systematic differences were observed between methods for most variables (p ≤ 0.05), with digital measurements tending to overestimate girths and breadths and underestimate heights and lengths compared to manual values. CCC values exceeded 0.80 only for waist girth (0.94), hip girth (0.91) and iliospinale height (0.93–0.94).ConclusionsPhone-based application 3D digital anthropometry demonstrates good repeatability for selected variables but shows systematic bias and limited agreement compared with manual anthropometry. Furthermore, the level of training and expertise of the technicians performing anthropometric data collection or operating the phone-based application is crucial for ensuring data reliability. Nonetheless, 3D digital anthropometry offers a simpler and faster assessment process and enables remote data collection, making it particularly useful in time-constrained or non-critical settings. Digital measurements can be readily integrated with health-related metrics and easily repeated when necessary. Although these methods should not currently be considered interchangeable for precise anthropometric assessment in young adults, the application of digital anthropometry in clinical practice may still be feasible, provided that method-specific reference values and classification criteria are developed through further research, similarly to the standards developed for manual anthropometry. In addition, incorporating a certification for the use of the application and an adaptive repeatability approach —such as that proposed by ISAK protocols– into digital acquisition procedures may further enhance measurement reliability.

Introduction

Anthropometry is widely used in nutrition, sports science, and health assessment to characterise body size, shape, and proportionality and to derive indicators related to nutritional status, physical performance, and cardiometabolic health [1–4]. In clinical and public health settings, measurements such as waist girth, hip girth, waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to-height ratio, and other anthropometric health indicators are commonly used to screen central adiposity and estimate metabolic and cardiovascular risk [1–3,5]. In sport, anthropometric assessment also supports athlete profiling, training monitoring, and the evaluation of body changes across the season, providing useful information for nutritional and performance-related decision-making [1,2,6–9]. Although body composition assessment remains one of its most established applications, the practical value of anthropometry extends beyond the estimation of fat and lean tissue, since raw body measurements themselves are often used directly for monitoring and interpretation in applied settings.

One of the most pertinent debates in this field concerns the distinction between the direct analysis of raw anthropometric variables—such as body dimensions and skinfolds—and the indirect estimation of body components—such as fat and muscle mass and their proportions [1,10]. Numerous studies highlight the advantages of raw data analysis over equation-based estimates, which may introduce generalisation bias or domain-shift bias due to assumptions and derivation samples that do not adequately represent the characteristics of a given population [1,10–13]. Direct analysis of raw anthropometric data also minimises the accumulation of error arising from external and transient factors. Hydration status, recent food or fluid intake, glycogen levels, recent physical activity, and menstrual cycle phase may temporarily alter body mass and consequently affect the indirect estimation of adipose or muscle tissue [1,4,14–21]. By contrast, direct anthropometric measurements are generally less sensitive to these short-term variations and may therefore provide more robust information for applied assessment [1,4,10,14,17,20,21].

Two general approaches are currently available for obtaining anthropometric raw data: traditional manual anthropometry and 3D digital anthropometry [1,4,20,21]. Traditional anthropometry relies on the manual measurement of body dimensions such as skinfolds, girths, breadths, lengths, and heights using simple, portable instruments [4,12,21]. Its main strengths are accessibility, low cost, and applicability across clinical, sports, and field settings [4,13,21]. However, precision and reproducibility depend strongly on technical expertise, accurate landmark identification, and standardised measurement procedures [4,21]. In this regard, the ISAK (International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry) framework addresses these limitations through structured training and accreditation, strict postural control, duplicate measurements, and adaptive replication criteria when differences between repeated measurements exceed predefined thresholds [4,22].

Over the past decade, 3D digital anthropometry has emerged as a promising alternative [20]. This approach uses scanning devices or mobile applications to capture external body dimensions and shape characteristics and generate digital body representations from which multiple anthropometric variables can be extracted automatically [20,23–26]. Its main advantages include rapid data acquisition, reduced physical contact, digital data storage, and the possibility of lowering some sources of manual measurement error [20,24]. These characteristics make digital anthropometry especially attractive for large-scale screening, remote assessment, and time-constrained applied settings. Nevertheless, important limitations remain, including the cost of some technologies, sensitivity to acquisition conditions, variability across devices and operators, and potentially lower precision in mobile systems compared with high-end scanners [23,27,28].

Previous studies examining the repeatability and compatibility of manual and digital anthropometry have reported mixed findings. Some studies have shown promising repeatability and strong associations between digital and manual measurements for selected variables, particularly waist and hip girths, under controlled conditions [23,27,28]. For example, Smith et al. [27]. reported strong correlations between a smartphone application and manual anthropometry, whereas Bušić et al. [23]. found coefficients of determination above 90% for a portable digital system. In parallel, the systematic review by Mocini et al. [20]. highlighted the growing evidence supporting the precision, reliability, and accuracy of digital anthropometry, while also emphasising the heterogeneity of technologies and findings across studies. Overall, the literature suggests that repeatability and agreement may be acceptable for some measurements but remain variable depending on the 3D scanning technology, anatomical region, acquisition protocol, and statistical approach used [20,23,24,27–29].

Despite this progress, several methodological limitations still affect the available evidence. Many studies have been conducted with small samples, often including fewer than 60 participants, which reduces generalisability [23,27]. In addition, the expertise of the evaluators performing manual anthropometry is not always clearly reported, despite the fact that operator competence is crucial for achieving high-quality reference measurements [4,21,22]. There is also considerable heterogeneity in the technologies used, making direct comparisons across studies difficult [20]. Moreover, several investigations report correlation coefficients without adequately assessing agreement, even though interchangeability between methods cannot be established on the basis of correlation alone [27,30,31]. From a clinical and applied perspective, this distinction is particularly important when measurements are used to derive anthropometric health indicators related to metabolic and cardiovascular risk, since even small systematic errors in girths, breadths, or trunk dimensions may alter the interpretation of risk classification [3].

Within the spectrum of 3D body scanning technologies, mobile phone applications are especially relevant in the context of health, nutrition and sports applications because they offer clear advantages in terms of portability, accessibility, and cost compared with 3D scanners requiring dedicated hardware and sensors [20,27,28]. However, while smartphone-based anthropometric systems have shown good agreement with validated reference applications and accurate girth measurements under controlled conditions, their performance should not be extrapolated beyond the variables, settings, and populations in which they have been validated [27,28]. Although the 3DAvatar Body (3DAB) phone-based application, developed by the Institute of Biomechanics of Valencia (IBV; Valencia, Spain), has been validated in the context of product design [32,33], its agreement with manual anthropometry for health-related assessment has not yet been established. Therefore, further studies are needed to compare manual anthropometry and mobile digital anthropometry in larger samples, under rigorous measurement standards, and using appropriate repeatability and agreement analyses.

Therefore, the aims of the present study were: (a) to assess the repeatability of both methods —manual anthropometry performed by accredited ISAK 3 expert anthropometrists and digital anthropometry obtained with the 3DAB by non-expert users following the instructions of the application—, (b) to assess the differences between them; and (c) to evaluate the agreement between these methods in a sample of young adults.

Based on the existing literature and the methodological differences between the methods evaluated, we hypothesised that: (a) both methods will exhibit good repeatability for most measurements; (b) significant differences would be observed between methods; and (c) there will be limited agreement between both methods, suggesting that the established reference values and cut-offs derived from manual anthropometric measurements may not be directly applied to the digital methods evaluated.

Materials and methods

The present study focuses on the comparison of measurement values obtained in two experimental contexts. On the one hand, the manual anthropometry performed by accredited ISAK 3 expert anthropometrists, which is the reference method and most used in nutrition and sports science. On the other hand, the use of 3DAvatar/Body by non-expert users following the instructions provided by the application, which simulates a home measurement or a non-professional measurement context.

Experimental design

The present research followed a descriptive, cross-sectional design. The sample recruitment was non-probabilistic by convenience. The calculation used to establish the minimum sample size was performed with R 4.4.1 software. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. The standard deviation (SD) for the total sample was based on previous studies on waist girth measurement (11.2 cm) [34]. Thus, the minimum sample size was determined to be 264 subjects, assuming an error (d) of 1.35 cm for the waist girth and for a 99% confidence interval (CI). Considering that acceptable statistical power is greater than 0.80, the calculated statistical power was 0.96, which is considered high.

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Catholic University San Antonio of Murcia (Murcia, Spain) under protocol number CE062103. All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection began.

Participants

A total of 264 young adults were recruited for this study (mean age = 22.7 ± 5.86 years old; mean body mass = 71.14 ± 13.70 kg; mean stretch stature = 172.24 ± 9.36 cm; mean urine specific gravity (USG) = 1020.26 ± 8.52 specific gravity), including 160 males and 104 females. The participants were recruited from the student body of the Catholic University San Antonio of Murcia (Murcia, Spain) using announcements in virtual classrooms related to body composition subjects in the fields of the Bachelor’s Degree in Nutrition, the Bachelor’s Degree in Physiotherapy, and the Bachelor’s Degree in Physical Activity and Sport Sciences.

Inclusion criteria were: [1] being between 18 and 35 years of age, and [2] fasting for at least 8 hours prior to the measurements. Exclusion criteria were: [1] engaging in vigorous physical exercise within 24 hours of testing or any physical activity on the same day; [2] consuming diuretic products or large meals within 24 hours of testing; [3] presenting any injury or medical condition affecting body composition assessment; [4] using corticosteroid or hormonal treatments within three months before the evaluation, except for treatments regulating the menstrual cycle; [5] for females, not being between the second half of the follicular phase and the first half of the late phase; [6] took supplements affecting body composition (e.g., creatine or fat burners); [7] urinary specific gravity (USG) ≥ 1.025 at the time of evaluation, corresponding to significant dehydration [35] or if they [8] failed to complete the full set of measurements. Protocol

Once eligible participants were identified through a preliminary questionnaire, appointments were scheduled between 8:00 and 10:00 AM to standardise testing conditions. Females' appointments were arranged to avoid variations due to different stages of the menstrual cycle. For this, an online questionnaire asking the total length of the menstrual cycle and the date of the last menstrual period was previously completed by each of the females.

The day of the measurements all the participants, once arrived, completed an ad hoc questionnaire on sociodemographic data (e.g. date of birth, ethnicity), including health status and medical history (past or current illnesses, injuries, surgeries and medication intake), dietary intake (24-hour recall), and exercise habits (48-hour recall) [36]. Furthermore, information on regular sports supplement use was also collected using a questionnaire that had been previously used in research for similar purposes [37].

Urine Specific Gravity (USG) was measured using a MASTER-URC/Nα refractometer (Atago, Japan) to determine the hydration status of the participants. This assessment followed the protocol outlined in previous research, which involves a urine sample collected in the 30 minutes prior to the measurements in a sterile, unused container [38,39]. Hydration status was assessed immediately before measurements by urinary specific gravity (USG) using a MASTER-URC/Nα refractometer (Atago Co., Ltd., Japan) on a urine sample collected in a sterile, previously unused, clean and sealed container. Following the methodology of previous studies, hydration status was classified as well-hydrated (USG ≤ 1.020), moderately dehydrated (USG 1.021–1.024), or significantly dehydrated (USG ≥ 1.025) [35,40]. Participants classified as significantly dehydrated (USG ≥ 1.025) were excluded from the study.

A total of 40 common anthropometric measurements were obtained using both manual anthropometry, following ISAK protocols, and digital anthropometry, using the 3DAvatar/Body phone-based application. Each participant underwent these assessments in a randomised order in a single session, with room temperature standardised to 24 °C and humidity standardised to 60% to avoid environmental influence on the measurements.

Manual anthropometric measurements

Manual anthropometric measurements were performed by two accredited anthropometrists with current ISAK level 3 accreditation [22]: One measurer performed the central measurements (i.e. head and torso) and the left-side measurements, while the second measurer performed the right-side measurements. The intra-evaluator technical error of measurement (TEM) were, respectively, 0.01% and 0.01% for basic measurements, 0.58% and 0.62% for girths, and 0.33% and 0.27% for lengths and heights, and 0.41% and 0.39% for breadths and depths. Body reference positions were reused between repetitions during all measurements to reduce intra-rater TEM.

A comprehensive measurement profile was utilised excluding skinfolds and including: basic measurements —i.e., body mass and stretched stature—, girth measurements —i.e., head, neck, arm relaxed, arm flexed and tensed, forearm, wrist, chest, waist, hips, thigh (1 cm gluteal), mid-thigh, calf, and ankle; lengths and heights —i.e., acromiale-radiale, radiale-stylion, midstylion-dactylion, iliospinale height, tibiale laterale height, foot, and tibiale mediale-sphyrion tibiale—, and finally breadths and depths —i.e., biacromial, antero-posterior abdominal depth, biiliocristal, transverse chest, antero-posterior chest depth, humerus, bi-styloid, femur, and bimalleolar. Each measurement was taken twice, and if discrepancies exceeded 1%, a third measurement was conducted. The values used for bias and agreement analyses were the medians following ISAK methodology. Finally, and given the inherent susceptibility of manual data entry to typographical errors, values were systematically screened and corrected when a plausible and logically consistent adjustment could be identified; otherwise, implausible values were excluded from the dataset. Not all the measurements gathered in the survey were used in this study (cf., Section 2.5).

All anthropometric tools were calibrated and verified for precision before starting the measures. Calibration followed manufacturer instructions and was repeated periodically throughout the data collection phase to ensure measurement accuracy. Instruments used included a TANITA MC-780-MA scale for body mass, a SECA stadiometer for stretch stature, an inextensible tape (Lufkin, Apex, NC, USA) for girths, a Cescorf segmometer (Cescorf, Brazil) for lengths and heights, and Realmet sliding callipers (Realmet, Spain) for breadths and depths.

3D digital anthropometry

The 3D digital anthropometry was conducted using the 3DAB application for mobile phones, developed by IBV. This application uses two images of the person to obtain shape features of the body and four sociodemographic parameters: age, sex, stature and body mass to calibrate the images. This technology provides a comprehensive 3D reconstruction of the body shape [25,33] and a set of predefined anthropometric measurements according to ISO standards (8559 and 7250) through a cloud processing service via API [33]. In this study, the basic measurements from the ISAK protocol—that is, body mass and stretched stature—were used as inputs for 3DAB.

A description of the optimal conditions to take the two photographs is provided in the illustrated instructions of the application, such conditions referred to background, lighting conditions, body postures, hair and attire of the subject [25]. The users of the app followed the guidelines provided, namely, they ensured uniform wall colour and free of reflective surfaces, controlled ambient lighting, and minimal daylight sources. To reduce measurement errors, all participants were required to be barefoot, without shoes or socks —like in the ISAK protocol—, and to wear tight-fitting clothing, and any items that could modify the actual silhouettes of the body —such as watches, necklaces, bracelets, or any other accessories— were removed prior to taking the photographs. To further mitigate potential errors caused by hair, all participants wore a swimming cap during the procedure.

Participants were photographed while standing in two postures following the guidelines of the application. The first photograph required the subject to stand erect in A-Pose (i.e., head looking forward, arms open at 40−55° and legs comfortably apart at shoulder level) front facing the photographer. The second photograph required the user to stand erect but looking to the right side, in this case with the arms on the sides of the body (in order to reveal the lumbar shape of the back) and the legs comfortably apart at shoulder level. Front and side body outlines were provided in the user interface of the application while taking the photographs to facilitate the alignment of the body image in the frame of the camera [25,26]. The technician guided the participants to achieve a posture aligned with the optimal positioning specified by the application. All photographs were captured using the same smartphone (Samsung Galaxy S22, Suwon, South Korea) and acquired by the same technician, who had no prior professional experience using the application.

The process of 3D body reconstruction using 3DAvatar/Body has already been described in the literature [25,26,33]. This process uses the silhouettes of the person and several key points extracted from both images (Figure 2a and b). Then, the application uses the silhouettes and the key points of an initial estimate the 3D body of the participant projected into the planes of the images of the person using the parameters of the camera. The distance between paired silhouettes is minimised using an iterative process that modifies the shape parameters of the 3D body model until the virtual silhouettes match those extracted from the photographs (Figure 2c). The parametric 3D body model included in 3DBA was learned from international databases processed by IBV [41,42]. It should be noted that the head region covered by the scanning cap and the feet, although relevant in ISAK protocols for obtaining head girth and foot length, respectively, are not specifically modelled for high-precision measurement [41,42].

Steps of the 3DBA processing (a) input images, (b) segmentation and feature extraction, (c) input silhouette (white mask) and optimisation result (coloured outlines), (d) 3D body surface reconstruction and (e) Measurements and landmarks computed on the 3D body surface.

To improve robustness to (i) postural noise not captured in the optimisation targets (i.e., silhouettes and extracted key points), as well as to potential occlusions (e.g., the arm overlapping the torso in the side view, obscuring the lumbar curve), and (ii) variability in body features not represented in the body model (e.g., different hair arrangements such as ponytails, buns, or swimming caps), differential weighting was applied to body regions in the front and side silhouettes and key points, according to the reliability of the observable input data (Figure 2c). For example, lower weights were assigned to the arms in the front view—since variations in shoulder flexion–extension can alter the apparent arm lengths and girths—, to the top, back and lateral of the head in the side and front views—due to variability introduced by hairstyles—, to the legs in the side view—because hip abduction–adduction can affect apparent leg length—, and to the lumbar region in the side view, which may be partially occluded by the arm. Consequently, body regions assigned lower weights are less influenced by the captured data and rely more heavily on the underlying body model and on correlations with higher-weighted regions.

Subsequently, the digital anthropometric measurements were automatically extracted from each individual’s 3D body model using algorithms developed by IBV, designed to approximate ISAK protocol definitions (Figure 2e). To compute the measurements, we used the automatic landmark estimations provided by the body model learnt within the template-fitting processing [43] based on large international 3D scan databases in A-Pose with annotated anatomical landmark positions [41,42]. Besides, some anatomical landmarks required in the ISAK procedure, but not included in the aforementioned datasets, were approximated using different anatomical positions and leading to slightly different measurement definitions. The medial and lateral epicondyles of the tibia were used instead of the superior edges of the tibial plateau, and were used to calculate Tibiale Laterale Height and Tibiale Mediale-Sphyrion Tibiale Length. Foot length was calculated using the tip of the second toe instead of the most forward point of the foot. Mid-thigh point was approximated using the trochanter and the knee point in the A-pose instead of the patellare and inguinal points in the seating posture, as required in the ISAK protocol to locate the Mid-thigh Girth. The digital measurement Transverse Chest Breadth was calculated as the maximum torso width at the mesosternale level, whereas the ISAK protocol defines the measurement plane at a 30 ° downward inclination of the calliper from the mesosternale level. Another key simplification leading to methodological differences was the use of a single standardised posture (A-Pose) without respiratory phase control, whereas ISAK requires posture adjustments beyond the A-Pose, and controlled breathing for specific measurements. This made it inappropriate to calculate some ISAK protocol measurements, which were therefore not included in the analysis: flexed and tense arm girth, midstylion-dactylion length and humerus and femur breadths.

A total of 40 measurements were analysed in this study, including bilateral variables. The process was conducted twice with each subject in order to compare the repeatability of this method with the intra-measurer repeatability following ISAK protocols.

Data cleaning

Prior to conducting the data analyses, the data from both methods was transformed using a bespoke algorithm into a metric based on Kernel density estimation (KDE) between pairs of measurements. Outlier values were identified and removed from the database when their scaled two-dimensional Kernel Density Estimation was below 98% for four or more pairs.

The ideal conditions for acquiring the photographs were met in most of the cases; however, 2%of the photographs were not optimally taken in terms of body orientation, body posture or attire. They were not excluded from the study because such errors —and the variability derived from it— makes part of the home use or non-professional use and intends to reproduce where there is no supervision of the outcomes of the process by professionals or certified experts unlike the ISAK process.

Repeatability of the measurements

The standard error of measurement (SEM) and the coefficient of variation (%CV) obtained using manual (MaM) and digital 3D Avatar Body (3DAB) techniques are presented in Table 1.

Overall, girths and heights demonstrated higher repeatability than breadths and depths across both methods. In general, the manual measurer on the left side exhibited higher repeatability than the one measuring the right side. In contrast, digital measurements showed similar repeatability between sides across all variables, with the exception of foot length, which was notably higher on the right side (i.e., 3.2% CV for the right foot versus 1.2% for the left foot).

Girths

All girths exhibited good repeatability, (i.e., CV below 2%) for both MaM and 3DAB, with similar repeatability values between techniques. Hip girth showed the highest repeatability across methods (below 1%). Manual methods showed slightly higher repeatability in the arm, chest and waist girths while digital methods showed higher repeatability in calf and ankle girths.

Lengths and heights

All lengths and heights exhibited good repeatability (i.e., CV < 3%) for both MaM and 3DAB, with similar repeatability values between techniques for most measurements. Iliospinale heights showed the highest repeatability across methods, with very low %CV values (0.5–0.6%) and similar SEMs bilaterally. Acromiale–radiale and radiale–stylion lengths were more repeatable when measured manually.

Breadths and depths

All breadths and depths exhibited good repeatability (i.e., CV < 3%) for both MaM and 3DAB, with similar repeatability values between techniques for most measurements. Overall, breadths tended to be less repeatable than other measurement types. Antero-posterior abdominal depth in both methods and antero-posterior chest depth in manual measurements showed the poorest repeatability (approximately 3% CV).

Pairwise Compatibility Analysis

Comparison of 3DAB and MaM revealed significant bias for all measurements except for radiale–stylion length in both sides. Nevertheless, 15 out of the 40 variables showed acceptable agreement according to Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC > 0.80).

Overall, the signed bias indicated that 3DAB tends to overestimate girths, breadths, and depths (with the exception of biacromial breadth), and to underestimate heights and lengths (with the exception of bilateral iliospinale height, left radiale–stylion length, and right foot length).

Girths

Overall, the signed bias indicated that 3DAB tends to overestimate girths compared with MaM. Waist girth (CCC = 0.94), hip girth (CCC = 0.91), and thigh (1 cm gluteal) girth (CCC ≈ 0.88) showed the strongest agreement. Bias values were statistically significant but small relative to the mean (<8% in all cases). In contrast, head girth demonstrated the lowest agreement (CCC = 0.15).

Lengths and heights

Overall, the signed bias indicated that 3DAB tends to underestimate heights and lengths compared with MaM (with the exception of radiale–stylion length and iliospinale height). Only three variables reached CCC > 0.80: left foot length and both iliospinale heights. Measurements related to tibiale mediale and tibiale laterale exhibited the largest biases and low concordance (CCC < 0.40).

Breadths and depths

Overall, the signed bias indicated that 3DAB tends to overestimate breadths and depths compared with MaM (with the exception of biacromial breadth). The biacromial breadth was the only width demonstrating good agreement (CCC = 0.82), although it showed a significant but relatively small bias (−10.1 mm, 2.7%). Other widths and depths presented low-to-moderate concordance (CCC = 0.29–0.74) with substantial bias, particularly transverse chest width, which showed the largest bias in the study (63.7 mm, 21.5%).

Bland–altman analysis

Bland–Altman plots showed small regression slopes across most measurements, although statistical significance was observed in 22 of the 40 variables. The discrepancy coefficient was below 0.44 for all measurements.

Discussion

Repeatability of the measurements

The repeatability of a measurement method is typically assessed in the literature using estimators such as the standard error of measurement (SEM), expressed in the measurement units, and the coefficient of variation (CV), used as a relative estimator.

The 3DAB digital technique demonstrated overall good repeatability for girths and heights, consistent with ranges reported in the literature for other mobile applications and 3D body scanners [32,46–49], as shown in Table 2 and Table 3. However, repeatability values in the present study were slightly lower (i.e., higher SEM and CV) than those reported in previous studies in which image acquisition was performed by expert users under optimal conditions [32,33]. Although those studies included smaller samples (n = 77 and n = 73), they covered a broader range of age and BMI. These findings suggest that operator expertise plays a key role in ensuring reliable measurements.

Lower-limb girths and hip girth showed particularly high repeatability. In contrast, it was lower for small girths (e.g., wrist and ankle) and trunk depths (antero-posterior abdominal and thoracic), which is consistent with previous evidence indicating that regions with smaller volumes or higher curvature are more susceptible to reconstruction noise [47]. Foot length on the right side showed the lowest repeatability (CV > 3%), possibly influenced by the way feet are modelled and optimised in the 3DAB system.

Manual measurements also demonstrated good intra-rater repeatability, consistent with studies using the ISAK protocol and generally superior to those employing other protocols [32,33,48], as shown in Table 4. These findings suggest that the experience and proficiency associated with ISAK Level 3 accreditation contribute to more consistent results compared with other standard manual anthropometric procedures. Moreover, ISAK protocols incorporate an adaptive repeatability approach, requiring a third measurement when the difference between the first two exceeds a predefined threshold, which further enhances measurement precision.

Although manual and digital techniques showed repeatability of a similar order of magnitude, one of the manual anthropometrists (i.e., responsible for central and left-side measurements) achieved higher repeatability than 3DAB, whereas the other (right-side measurements) demonstrated comparable repeatability. These differences were relatively small and likely reflect inter-operator variability, even among highly skilled (ISAK Level 3) measurers. The slightly higher repeatability observed in manual measurements may also be partly explained by the inclusion of a third repetition in the ISAK protocol. In the present study, a third measurement was required in 38% of cases. Applying the same ISAK criterion to the digital method suggests that 56% of 3D captures would have required an additional acquisition, indicating that performing more than two scans could improve the repeatability of 3DAB measurements.

Pairwise compatibility between manual and digital methods

Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) estimates the degree of agreement between measurement methods, whereas signed bias is used to assess whether data obtained from different methods can be considered interchangeable. Agreement evaluates how closely two measurement methods are related and how close their measurements are to the line of equality. Interchangeability goes one step further by assessing whether the differences between methods are sufficiently small for one method to replace the other in a given context. Therefore, for two methods to be considered interchangeable, they should show both high agreement and an acceptable bias. Table 5 and Table 6 present, respectively, a comparison of the signed biases and Lin’s CCC between manual and digital anthropometric measurements across different studies, including the present one.

The significant biases observed in this study are comparable in magnitude to those reported in previous studies involving phone-based applications and high-resolution 3D body scanners [32,46,48,49,51–53]. Despite their statistical significance, most biases were relatively small (<7%).

Agreement between methods was high (CCC > 0.80) for 38% of the measurements and moderate (0.60 < CCC ≤ 0.80) for 28%, while 35% showed low agreement (CCC < 0.60). The excellent agreement observed for hip girth (CCC > 0.90) is consistent with previous studies and, in some cases, exceeds previously reported values (CCC > 0.70). However, there is limited literature evaluating agreement between these techniques using Lin’s coefficient. In several studies, ISAK anthropometry is used as the reference method [29,46], and only a few include the use of the 3DAB application by expert users [32].

In general, differences between standardised manual anthropometry and digital methods —that is, when both methods adhere to the same theoretical standard definition— arise from several methodological factors: (i) the physical pressure applied by instruments in manual measurements, which is absent in digital methods and may lead to overestimation of girths, breadths, and depths, particularly breadths, since the ISAK protocol commonly requires compression of the soft tissue to reach the underlying bone; (ii) the use of measurement-specific postures in manual protocols, whereas digital methods typically rely on one or two standardised postures despite the influence of posture on most anthropometric variables; (iii) the control of respiratory phase in manual torso measurements, which is generally not accounted for in digital methods; (iv) the identification of anatomical landmarks by palpation in manual methods, compared with automatic estimation approaches in digital systems; and (v) the approximation of alignment relative to anatomical axes or planes in manual measurements, whereas digital methods can define these orientations precisely [54]. Between digital methods there are also algorithmic differences in the interpretation and implementation of standard definitions as well as differences related to postural standardisations of the data prior to computing the measurements [32,33,55]. Finally, in the case of model-based digital methods such as phone apps like 3DAB that use sparse data as input (e.g., outlines and key points) instead of capturing dense 3D surfaces, there can also be model-derived errors in the reconstruction of the full body surface [26,32,33].

The differences observed in this study between 3DAB and ISAK protocol can be explained by the aforementioned factors. In the case of 3DAB, its good repeatability and the systematic biases observed relative to reference methods —ISAK protocol in the present study, and 3D body scanners in previous literature— suggest that model-derived errors for this set of measurements are generally low. However, head girth, which showed a high relative bias (3.8%) and the lowest agreement (CCC = 0.15), is likely related to the absence of direct input data and to limitations in the model representation for this region and to differences in the measurement definition (See Section 2.5). In this context, other body regions, such as the arms and abdomen might also be influenced by reduced weighting during the optimisation of silhouettes and key points.

On the other hand, girths, lengths, and breadths tended to be overestimated, which may be associated with the absence of tissue compression in digital measurements (factor i). In some cases, this effect may be partially offset by alignment-related factors (factor v). Larger biases (>7%) and lower agreement (CCC < 0.60) observed in this study are likely associated with differences in posture (factor ii), lack of respiratory control (factor iii), and differences in anatomical landmark identification methods (factor iv), depending on the measurement.

Finally, lower agreement in torso measurements—such as chest girth (CCC = 0.66), antero-posterior abdominal depth (CCC = 0.39), chest width (CCC = 0.29), and antero-posterior chest depth (CCC = 0.38)—may be influenced by the lack of respiratory phase control in the digital protocol. Similarly, differences in body length measurements—such as tibiale Mediale–Sphyrion tibiale (CCC = 0.35 and 0.36), acromiale–radiale (CCC = 0.44 and 0.64), tibiale laterale (CCC = 0.32 and 0.40), bi-Iliocristal (CCC = 0.50), and bi-styloid (CCC = 0.35 and 0.36)—may be explained by both differences in posture (e.g., flexed vs. extended limbs) and differences in anatomical landmark identification methods and definition. Overall, the measurements with the lowest agreement (CCC < 0.50) appear to be influenced by combinations of these factors.

Nevertheless, the manual measurements used as reference method in this study may include inherent sources of error that should be considered when interpreting the observed differences. Biological variability, time of day, hydration status, measurement location, and measurement technique can all influence measurement error [4]. Previous studies have reported accepted error thresholds for manual anthropometry, including <1% for basic measurements and lengths and 1.7% for girths [56]. To minimise these effects, the study followed a strict ISAK-based protocol for landmark location and measurement technique. In addition, several factors related to the participant’s physical status and the time of the day were controlled, as previously explained in Sections 2.3 and 2.4.

Implications of the interchangeability of digital and manual measurements on Anthropometric Health and Sports Assessment Indicators

Most Anthropometric Health Indicators (AHIs) together with their respective classification criteria and cut-off values were originally developed for traditional anthropometry [3]. Despite its high repeatability, 3DBA showed systematic biases relative to ISAK methods in most of the measurements used to compute AHIs. Waist girth showed a positive bias (i.e., 24.4mm, 3.3%) relative to manual measurements. This systematic overestimation directly influences indicators such as Waist–to-Hip Ratio (WHR), Waist-to-Height Ratio (WHtR), Conicity Index (CI) and Abdominal Volume Index (AVI) among others [3]. Hip girth, also biased positively (i.e., 10mm), further affects WHR (leading to underestimation) and AVI (leading to overestimation). Such overestimations may potentially result in false positives, due to overclassification of abdominal obesity and cardiometabolic risk [3]. Neck girth, identified as an independent predictor of insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk [3], exhibited meaningful bias (i.e., 10.3mm). Even small systematic overestimations may shift individuals across clinical cut-points designed for manual measurement. Trunk-geometry indicators, such as antero-posterior abdominal depth, chest width and antero-posterior abdominal depth had larger positive biases (respectively, 34.8, 63.7, and 30.9mm), rendering their digital acquisition unsuitable for interpretation following manual cut-offs and classification criteria.

Furthermore, muscle mass estimation, proportionality and somatotyping are commonly used in sports science to assess muscle development and to characterise the requirements of different sports disciplines [4,58]. The equations used to estimate these indicators were also developed based on manual anthropometry. They are derived from girths and bone breadths that are overestimated by the digital methods and bone lengths that are underestimated by digital methods. Consequently, systematic errors in the variables used to calculate proportionality indices could substantially affect the interpretation of the results.

Finally, equations predicting visceral or regional fat distribution, which commonly incorporate waist girth, hip girth and trunk breadths, would propagate these systematic errors. The absolute values of the biases found suggest that, despite concordance found in several key measurements, the interchangeability between digital and manual measurements is limited and that the direct reuse of cut-offs and classification criteria remains unclear or should be revisited.

Limitations

Despite the methodological rigour and the large sample size of the present study, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample consisted exclusively of young, predominantly healthy adults recruited from a university setting. Although this homogeneity allowed for strict control of potential confounding factors and strengthened internal validity, it limits the generalisation of results to other populations such as older adults, adolescents, individuals with obesity, clinical populations, or elite athletes [10,13,59]. Finally, the present investigation did not include repeated measurements from several manual measurers and does not evaluate inter-rater repeatability and bias. Similarly, it does not include several users of the 3DAB.

Conclusions

Manual anthropometry performed by ISAK Level 3–accredited anthropometrists is widely recognised as a reference standard for high-precision body assessment in sports and nutrition sciences, due in part to its structured multi-sampling methodology. In this context, the present study shows that the 3D Avatar Body (3DAB) mobile application, even when operated by non-expert users, can achieve a high level of repeatability, particularly for key health-related measures such as waist and hip girth.

The analysis of compatibility between methods showed moderate to high agreement between methods for 65% of the variables but biases of several centimeters between methods for most variables, indicating that the observed discrepancies are predominantly systematic rather than random. Specifically, the tendency of the digital system to overestimate certain girths and breadths and underestimate some lengths suggests that these differences may be related to the characteristics of the digital reconstruction process and landmark identification procedures. The presence of these systematic offsets indicates that interchangeability with manual anthropometry may be limited to a subset of variables and highlights the need for method-specific adjustments prior to clinical application. Accordingly, if such biases are appropriately accounted for, digital measurements could potentially be used in conjunction with existing cut-off points and classification criteria. This would allow indices such as WC, WHR, WtHR, CI, and AVI to retain their clinical relevance when derived from digital data.

Furthermore, the results suggest that the repeatability of digital method could be improved by incorporating methodological elements commonly used in ISAK protocols. In particular, the adoption of multi-sampling approaches and certified multi-level operator training may help enhance repeatability and reduce variability related to acquisition conditions.

In conclusion, although 3DAB and manual ISAK-based anthropometry are not currently interchangeable, the digital method represents a repeatable and scalable tool for longitudinal monitoring. Its potential use in health-related classification, provided that systematic biases are addressed, supports its applicability in large-scale kinanthropometric assessments in clinical and athletic populations of young adults.

Future directions

Future research should include more diverse populations across different age groups, body composition profiles, and health conditions in order to develop and validate new equations for estimating skeletal muscle mass and calculate mesomorphism based on digital anthropometry. Future studies should also focus on developing cut-off points for health- and sports-related variables obtained using digital devices. In addition, population reference datasets for digital anthropometric data should be established.

In addition, multi-device and multi-operator study designs are needed to evaluate the robustness of measuring techniques under applied conditions, such as pre-competition or remote assessment scenarios, where time is a critical factor. Finally, further studies should also examine the effect of increasing the number of digital acquisitions on repeatability and agreement, as well as the impact of systematic measurement bias on anthropometric health indicators, performance-related metrics, and longitudinal monitoring.
