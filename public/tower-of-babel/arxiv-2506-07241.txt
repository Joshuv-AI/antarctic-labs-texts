Shellings of tropical hypersurfaces
George Balla, Michael Joswig, Lena Weis

Abstract
. The shellability of the boundary complex of an unbounded polyhedron is investigated. To this end, it is necessary to pass to a suitable compactification, e.g., by one point. This observation can be exploited to prove that any tropical hypersurface is shellable. Under the hood there is a subtle interplay between the duality of polyhedral complexes and their shellability. Translated into discrete Morse theory, that interplay entails that the tight span of an arbitrary regular subdivision is collapsible, but not shellable in general.



1.
Introduction

In a landmark paper Bruggesser and Mani showed that the boundary complex of an arbitrary convex polytope is shellable
[
BM71
]
.
The key idea is quite simple.
A generic line intersects each facet defining hyperplane in a unique point, and the ordering of these intersection points on that line induces a shelling order.
Shellings of this kind are known as
line shellings
.
Here we will extend the range of this method to certain unbounded polyhedra which occur naturally in tropical geometry.
We will then apply these techniques to obtain shellability results concerning tropical hypersurfaces and tropical polytopes.
A shelling of a regular cell complex serves as a certificate that the topological space supported on that complex is
“simple enough”
to allow for a combinatorial treatment.
Crucially, the Stanley–Reisner ring of a shellable simplicial complex satisfies the Cohen–Macaulay property
[
Sta96
,
BH93
]
.
Cohen–Macaulayness is at the core of the Upper Bound Theorem
[
Sta96
, §II.3]
and the
g
-theorem for simplicial polytopes
[
Sta96
, §III.1]
.

While our overall strategy is quite natural, there are two technical obstacles to overcome in our work.
The first difficulty is that we want to construct shellings of polyhedra which are unbounded.
In order for such a statement to even make sense it is necessary to talk about compactifications.
After all, the notion of shellability applies (or not) to finite regular cell complexes, which are necessarily compact.
We study two scenarios.
The easier case is the one-point compactification
\partial^{\bullet}P
of the boundary complex of an unbounded but pointed polyhedron
P
.
In
Corollary

4
we show that
\partial^{\bullet}P
admits a line shelling provided that the recession cone of
P
is either one-dimensional or full-dimensional.
Luckily, these are the two cases which occur for unbounded polyhedra relevant in tropical geometry, namely the extended Newton polyhedron and the dome of a tropical polynomial
[
Jos21
, §1.1, §1.2]
.
As an immediate consequence the one-point compactification of any tropical hypersurface turns out to be shellable, too (
Corollary

20
).
The second class of compactifications that we investigate arises naturally in tropical convexity.

We also look into shellability properties of tight spans of regular subdivisions, which were introduced in
[
HJ08
]
; see also
[
DRS10
, Thm 9.5.10]
.
Tropical polytopes form one class of examples, via their covector decompositions.
However, tight spans and tropical polytopes, seen as regular cell complexes, are not necessarily pure.
This fact gives rise to the second technical difficulty that we face: for some of our results we need to discuss the generalization of shellability to nonpure complexes by Björner and Wachs
[
BW96
]
.
In view of that observation, one might ask if tight spans of regular subdivisions are always (nonpure) shellable.
But here the answer is no; we give an explicit example related to a tropical plane curve of genus two (
Example

13
).
Yet in
Theorem

15
we can show that tight spans of regular subdivisions are always collapsible, and thus contractible.
The latter property was known before; see
[
Hir06
, Lem 4.5]
and
[
Jos21
, Thm 10.59]
.
Our proof is based on work of Chari
[
Cha00
]
who showed that shellings and collapsing strategies correspond to especially well behaved discrete Morse functions in the sense of Forman
[
For98
]
.

Related work

The role of shellability in tropical geometry has been investigated before.
Trappmann and Ziegler
[
TZ98
]
showed that the tropical Grassmannians TGr
(2,n)
(which are moduli spaces of metric trees) are shellable for arbitrary
n\geq 5
.
Markwig and Yu
[
MY09
]
proved that the space of tropically collinear points is shellable.
More recently, Amini and Piquerez introduced a notion of
“tropical shellability”
for tropical fans
[
AP21
, Def 5.3]
.
Such a tropical fan arises naturally, e.g., as the star of a vertex of a tropical hypersurface.

It is known that the relationship between shellability and duality is subtle.
Notably, via the Folkman–Lawrence representation theorem
[
FL78
]
, each not necessarily realizable oriented matroid gives rise to a cellular sphere
S
, which is shellable
[
Bjö+99
, Thm 4.3.5]
.
The maximal cells of
S
correspond to the topes of the oriented matroid.
Picking a tope yields a subcomplex of
S
, the Edmonds–Mandel complex of that tope.
The Edmonds–Mandel complex is shellable.
Yet it is open if the Las Vergnas complex, which is dual to the Edmonds–Mandel complex, is shellable, too.
We are indebted to Paul Mücksch for this hint.

2.
Shellings of unbounded polyhedra

A cell complex is
regular
if the boundary of each cell is embedded.
Shellability is a fundamental concept in combinatorial topology.
Intuitively, a regular cell complex is shellable if it is built from its maximal cells in an inductive fashion such that the change in topology in each step is controlled precisely.
Consequently, the topology of a shellable complex is severely restricted: they are necessarily homotopy equivalent to wedges of spheres.
Here we will consider the nonpure shellability introduced by Björner and Wachs
[
BW96
]
:

Definition 1
.

A total ordering
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
of the maximal cells of a
d
-dimensional regular cell complex
\Sigma
is a
shelling
if either
d=0
or if it satisfies the following conditions:

(S1)

There is an ordering of the maximal cells of
\partial\sigma_{1}
which is a shelling.

(S2)

For
2\leq j\leq m
, the complex
\partial\sigma_{j}\cap(\bigcup_{k=1}^{j-1}\partial\sigma_{k})
is pure of dimension
(\dim\sigma_{j}-1)
.

(S3)

For
2\leq j\leq m
, there is an ordering of the maximal cells of
\partial\sigma_{j}
which is a shelling and further, the maximal cells of
\partial\sigma_{j}\cap(\bigcup_{k=1}^{j-1}\partial\sigma_{k})
appear first in this ordering.

Initially, shellability was only defined for
pure
complexes; these are the ones where all maximal cells share the same dimension.
The additional requirement in the nonpure setting is (S3); cf. Ziegler
[
Zie95
, Def 8.1]
.
In fact, we will begin by analyzing
pure
polyhedral complexes.
The need for the more general
Definition

1
will become clear only later.

Our objects of study come from polyhedral geometry; cf.
[
DRS10
, Chap 5]
and
[
Jos21
, Chap 1]
.
A
polyhedron
is the intersection of finitely many affine halfspaces in Euclidean space.
It is
pointed
if it does not contain any affine line; further, a
polytope
is a polyhedron which is bounded.
A
polyhedral complex
is a finite collection of convex polyhedra in, say,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
meeting face-to-face, and which is closed with respect to taking faces.
Such a complex is
polytopal
if each face is a bounded polyhedron, i.e., a polytope.
Polytopal complexes are standard examples of regular cell complexes.
A
proper face
of a polyhedron
P
is the intersection of
P
with a supporting hyperplane.
The proper faces form the boundary complex
\partial P
; this is an example of a polyhedral complex.
If
P
is a polytope, then
\partial P
is a polytopal complex.

Consider an unbounded polyhedron
P
.
We would like to talk about shellability properties of its boundary.
However, the boundary
\partial P
is not even a cell complex, for lack of compactness.
The following statement will help us to overcome this technical obstacle.
Recall that any locally compact and connected topological space has a one-point compactification which is unique up to homeomorphism.
For instance, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
P
be a pointed, full-dimensional and unbounded polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
Then the bounded facets plus the one-point compactifications of the unbounded facets form a regular cell complex
\partial^{\bullet}P
which covers the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

Proof.

When
P
is pointed, the boundary
\partial P
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
*\notin\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be a point for obtaining the compactification
\partial P\cup\{*\}\approx\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
In the following we regard
\partial P
as a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
Each face
F
of
P
is a bounded or unbounded polyhedron in
\partial P\approx\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If it is unbounded then its closure in the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is compact.
We conclude that the compactification of
F
in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
equals
F\cup\{*\}
because this is the only point in the complement
\mathbb{S}^{d}\setminus\partial P
.
Consequently, compactifying the unbounded faces yields a cell complex.

If
F
is bounded, then its boundary complex
\partial F
is a subcomplex of
\partial P
and thus of its compactification.
If
F
is unbounded, then the boundary of the compactification
F\cup\{*\}
, which is a sphere, is embedded in the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
We conclude that the compactified boundary
\partial^{\bullet}P
is a regular cell complex.
∎

Before we proceed we recall the precise construction of Bruggesser and Mani
[
BM71
]
.
Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be a full-dimensional polyhedron, bounded or not, with facets
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
,
\ldots
,
F_{m}
.
Now we can pick an affine line
L
through some interior point
o\in P
.
This defines points of intersection
p_{i}=L\cap{\operatorname*{aff}}(F_{i})
with the facet defining hyperplanes.
We assume that
L
is
generic
, i.e.,
p_{i}\neq p_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
Fixing an orientation of
L
, there exists some
n^{\prime}\leq n
such that starting from
o
, the points on
L
in the forward direction are sorted as

(1)

p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n^{\prime}}\kern 5.0pt,

up to relabeling.
Again, up to relabeling, we can assume that the remaining points in the opposite direction are sorted as
p_{n^{\prime}+1},p_{n^{\prime}+2},\dots,p_{n}
.

Theorem 3

(Bruggesser–Mani)
.

Assume that
P
is bounded, i.e.,
P
is a polytope.
Then the ordering
F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{n}
is a shelling order.
In particular,
\partial P
is a shellable
d
-dimensional sphere.
Moreover, by varying the point
o
and the line
L
, the first and the last facet of the shelling order can be picked arbitrarily.

Every pointed polyhedron
P
is projectively equivalent to a polytope
Q
.
In that case, we may identify
\partial P
with
\partial Q
minus a proper face
E_{\infty}

at infinity
via a projective transformation.
For more details on the construction of
Q
see
[
JT13
]
.
The cone over
E_{\infty}
agrees with the recession cone of
P
.
In particular, the face
E_{\infty}
may attain any dimension between
0
and
d
; at any rate, the dimension of the recession cone of
P
is
\dim E_{\infty}+1
.
As projective transformations map lines to lines,
Theorem

3
admits the following interpretation for unbounded polyhedra.

Corollary 4
.

Now let
P
be an unbounded but pointed polyhedron whose recession cone has either dimension
1
or
d+1
.
Let
G_{i}
be the closure of
F_{i}
in the compactification
\partial P\cup\{*\}
.
Then the ordering
G_{1},G_{2},\dots,G_{m}
is a shelling order.
In particular,
\partial^{\bullet}P
is a shellable
d
-dimensional sphere.
Moreover, by varying the point
o
and the line
L
, the first facet of the shelling order can be picked arbitrarily.

Proof.

We have
\dim E_{\infty}=0
or
\dim E_{\infty}=d
.
The compactified boundary
\partial^{\bullet}P
is isomorphic, as a cell complex, to the contraction of
E_{\infty}
in
\partial Q
.

By picking a generic line
L
,
Theorem

3
yields a shelling order of the facets of
Q
.
First, let
E_{\infty}
be a facet of
Q
.
We choose
L
such that
E_{\infty}
comes last in the shelling order of the facets of
Q
.
The remaining facets of
Q
are in bijection with the facets of
P
.
Moreover, the first facet of the shelling order can still be chosen arbitrarily.

Or
E_{\infty}
is a single point.
Mapping
E_{\infty}
to
\{*\}
extends the bijection between the boundary of
P
and
Q
without the face at infinity to one between
\partial^{\bullet}P
and
\partial Q
.
Ordering the cells of
\partial^{\bullet}P
according to a shelling order of
\partial Q
is thus a shelling order as well.
∎

The following example shows that some assumption concerning the recession cone is necessary to derive shellability.

Example 5
.

Let
P
be the product of a ray and a hexagon.
That polyhedron is unbounded, pointed, and its recession cone is one-dimensional.
So it satisfies the assumptions of
Corollary

4
.
The complex
\partial^{\bullet}P
is a shellable sphere with seven maximal cells, six of which correspond to the unbounded facets of
P
.

Now we rotate one of the six unbounded facets slightly around the edge of the hexagon that it contains.
If we rotate toward the outside, we obtain a new unbounded polyhedron,
P^{\prime}
, whose recession cone is two-dimensional; i.e., the face at infinity in the projectively equivalent polytope
Q^{\prime}
is a segment.
Pick a generic line
L
which intersects the facets incident to that segment first; the line
L
induces a shelling of the
\partial Q^{\prime}
.
However, in
\partial^{\bullet}P^{\prime}
the face at infinity is shrunk to a point, and so
L
does not induce a shelling of the one-point compactification.

The
k-skeleton
of a cell complex is the subcomplex formed by the cells of dimension at most
k
.
The following basic result will turn out to be useful.

Proposition 6

(Björner and Wachs
[
BW96
, Thm 2.9]
)
.

The
k
-skeleton of a not necessarily pure and shellable cell complex is shellable.

3.
Regular subdivisions

Consider a finite point configuration
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We will assume that
A
is affinely spanning.
A
subdivision
of
A
is a polytopal complex
\Sigma
such that the vertices of
\Sigma
form a subset of
A
, and
\bigcup_{F\in\Sigma}={\operatorname*{conv}}(A)

We pick an arbitrary height function
\omega:A\to\mathbb{R}
, which gives rise to the unbounded polyhedron

(2)

\mathcal{U}(A,\omega)\ =\ {\operatorname*{conv}}\left\{(u,\omega(u))\mid u\in A\right\}+\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot e_{d+1}

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

of
A
with respect to
\omega
.
A

of

has an outward pointing normal vector

\langle h,e_{d+1}\rangle<0
.
That is to say, the vector
h
is pointing downward.
We often employ notions of direction like
“up”
or
“down”

e_{d+1}
.
We call a polytope

an

is a lower face of

Occasionally, we use the term

\omega

for

The
\omega
-cells of

\Sigma(A,\omega)
, which is called the

of
A
induced by
\omega
.

Since
A

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the maximal cells of
\Sigma_{\omega}(A)
are
d
-dimensional.
It follows that the affine span of each maximal lifted
\omega

\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

The following result is known.
A proof in the case where
\Sigma_{\omega}(A)
is a regular triangulation has been given by De Loera, Rambau and Santos

, Thm 9.5.10]
, the generalization to nongeneric height functions

We can apply

to obtain a variation of their proof.

Proposition 7
.

Ordering the intersection points on a generically perturbed vertical line

\mathcal{U}(A,\omega)

(d{+}1)

\partial^{\bullet}\mathcal{U}(A,\omega)
.
That shelling induces a shelling of the regular subdivision
\Sigma_{\omega}(A)
.
Moreover, the first maximal cell in the shelling order can be picked arbitrarily.

Proof.

The polyhedron
\mathcal{U}(A,\omega)
is full-dimensional, unbounded and pointed.
We pick an interior point
o
.
Then the vertical line
L=o+\mathbb{R}e_{d+1}
is not generic, because it contains the point at infinity in the positive
e_{d+1}
-direction.
In this case the face
E_{\infty}
is zero-dimensional.
However, any sufficiently small perturbation of

yields a generic line, say,
L^{\prime}
.
We orient

by descending

By

\partial^{\bullet}\mathcal{U}(A,\omega)

The lower facets occur first in the shelling order induced by

∎

We call the set

(3)

\mathcal{D}(A,\omega)\ \coloneqq\ \left\{(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,s\in\mathbb{R},\,s\leq\min_{u\in A}(\omega(u)+\langle u,x\rangle)\right\}

This is a slight generalization of the dome of a tropical polynomial

Suppose that

\begin{split}F(x)\ &=\ \bigoplus_{u\in A}\omega(u)\odot x_{1}^{u_{1}}x_{2}^{u_{2}}\dots x_{d}^{u_{d}}\\

4.
Collapsibility of tight spans

In
Example

13
we saw that the tight span of a regular subdivision does not need to be shellable.
However, as we will demonstrate next, tight spans are always collapsible.
As a technical tool, we will employ Chari’s version
[
Cha00
]
of Forman’s discrete Morse theory
[
For98
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)
be the Hasse diagram of the cell poset of the regular cell complex
\Sigma
, which does not need to be pure.
We view
\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)
as a directed graph with one node per cell and directed edges
(\sigma,\tau)
whenever
\sigma
is a maximal cell in the boundary
\partial\tau
.
As
\Sigma
is regular, the dimensions of
\sigma
and
\tau
differ by one.
By construction the digraph
\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)
is acyclic.
An
acyclic matching
is a matching
\mu
in the graph
\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)
with the additional property that reverting the order of the arcs in
\mu
does not generate a directed cycle.
A cell is
critical
with respect to
\mu
if it is unmatched.
Recall that a

Recall that a pseudomanifold is a pure regular cell complex such that every cell of codimension one is contained in at most two maximal cells, and the dual graph is connected.

Remark 17

Theorem

Here is a sketch.

Remark 21

Courdurier showed that the star of any cell in a shellable polytopal complex is shellable

In the remainder of this section we will look into the special case where the homogeneous tropical polynomial
F
is the product of tropical linear forms.
Then
{\mathcal{T}}(F)
is an arrangement of tropical hyperplanes in the tropical projective torus.
Tropical hyperplane arrangements are studied in the context of tropical convexity, which we explain next; see
[

,

]
.

Let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be a matrix with real entries.
The column
v^{(j)}
of
V
, or rather its negative, may be read as the coefficient vector of the
\min
-tropical linear form

L_{j}(x)\ =\ \min(x_{1}-v_{1}^{(j)},\ldots,x_{d}-v_{n}^{(j)})\kern 5.0pt.

Its tropical vanishing locus is the min-tropical hyperplane
{\mathcal{T}}(L_{j})
.
Since the coefficients of
V
are real, the support of
L_{j}
are the standard basis vectors
e_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
So the Newton polytope of each tropical linear form
L_{j}
is the standard simplex
\Delta_{d-1}={\operatorname*{conv}}\{e_{i}\mid i\in[d]\}
.
In the sequel we also use the same symbol
\Delta_{d-1}
for the labeled point configuration
(e_{1},\dots,e_{d})
.
The simplex
\Delta_{d-1}
coincides with the image of the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{U}(\Delta_{d-1},-v^{(j)})
.
The dome
\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{d-1},-v^{(j)})
is a pointed polyhedron with exactly one vertex and a
d
-dimensional recession cone; recall that we factored out the one-dimensional lineality space.
In this way, the dome becomes projectively equivalent to a
d
-simplex.

Multiplying the
n
min-tropical linear forms yields

(8)

L_{V}\ =\ L_{1}\odot\cdots\odot L_{n}\kern 5.0pt,

which is a homogeneous
d
-variate min-tropical polynomial of degree

.
The corresponding tropical hypersurface
{\mathcal{T}}(L_{V})
is a tropical hyperplane arrangement, namely the union of the tropical hyperplanes
{\mathcal{T}}(L_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{T}}(L_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
, see
[

, Lemma 4.6]
.
The support
\supp(L_{V})
is the Minkowski sum of the support of each tropical linear form

.
That is,

is formed by the lattice points in the scaled standard simplex
n\Delta_{d-1}

The regular subdivision
\Sigma(L_{V})
is a coherent mixed subdivision of

; see
[

.
That is, it is the image of a subdivision of the product of simplices
\Delta_{n-1}\times\Delta_{d-1}

The subdivision of
\Delta_{n-1}\times\Delta_{d-1}
is a triangulation if and only if the subdivision of

is fine if and only if the matrix

is tropically generic
[
Jos21
, Thm 4.14]
.

The

of

with respect to

is the
d
-tuple
(G_{1}(x),\ldots,G_{d}(x))
of subsets

G_{i}(x)\ \coloneqq\ \left\{j\in[n]\mid x_{i}-v_{i}^{(j)}=\min\{x_{k}-v_{k}^{(j)}\}\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

For
S=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})
and

, we write
G\supseteq H
whenever
G_{i}\supseteq H_{i}
for all

.
The
covector cell

X_{H}\ \coloneqq\ \left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}\mid(G_{1}(x),\ldots,G_{d}(x))\supseteq H\right\}

is a closed convex polyhedron in the ordinary sense; cf.
[

, Chapter 6.3]

We have

, and the covector cell

is bounded (but possibly empty) if and only if

.

Theorem 24
.

Let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be a matrix with real entries.
Ordering the maximal cells of the covector decomposition
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
lexicographically by their coarse types induces a shelling of the one-point compactification of
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
.

Proof.

First, the maximal cells of
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the maximal cells of its one-point compactification as well as with the facets of
\mathcal{D}(V)
.
Second, the coarse type of a maximal covector cell
X_{H}
corresponds to a lattice point in the support
\supp(L_{V})=n\Delta_{d-1}
which corresponds to a vertex of
\mathcal{U}(V)
.
Via duality that lattice point agrees with the first
d
entries of the normal vector of the supporting hyperplane of the facet of
\mathcal{D}(V)
corresponding to
X_{H}
.

Our proof strategy is a variation of our proof of
Proposition

7

To this end, we pick a point
(w,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
in the interior of the dome
\mathcal{D}(V)
.
That is,
t\leq L_{V}(w)
.
Since
\mathcal{D}(V)
is unbounded in the negative

direction it is possible to choose

such that the
(d{+}1)

is arbitrarily small; this will be important later.
Let

be small enough such that the vector
\eta\coloneqq(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2},\ldots,\epsilon^{d+1})
induces a lexicographic order of the vertices of
\mathcal{U}(V)
.
That is, for vertices
(u,s)
and
(u^{\prime},s^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{U}(V)
, we have
\langle\eta,(u,s)\rangle<\langle\eta,(u^{\prime},s^{\prime})\rangle
if and only if
(u,s)
is lexicographically smaller than
(u^{\prime},s^{\prime})
.
Observe that the latter property holds if and only if
u
is lexicographically smaller than
u^{\prime}
.

We claim that the line
L\coloneqq(w,t)+\mathbb{R}\eta
intersects the supporting hyperplanes of the facets of the dome in lexicographic order of their respective normal vectors.
Since
\eta
can also be considered as a point on the moment curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
we can always choose
\epsilon
such that the line
L
is generic, i.e., each such hyperplane is met in a unique point.
In order to find the intersection of the line

with the hyperplane
x_{d+1}=r+\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}x_{i}
we need to solve the following equation for

:

(9)

\displaystyle t-\left(r+\langle u,w\rangle\right)\ =\ \lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}\epsilon^{i}-\epsilon^{d+1}\kern 5.0pt,

where the left hand side of

As

has nonnegative coefficients and

, the expression
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}\epsilon^{i}
is positive but arbitrarily small; the term

may be neglected due to
\epsilon\ll 1
.
Now we can still pick

as a negative number of arbitrarily large absolute value, so that
\lambda
must be strictly negative.
That is to say, the ray

is entirely contained in
\mathcal{D}(V)
.
Moreover, the smaller

is, the larger the absolute value of

becomes.
Consequently, intersection points of

\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
.
Next we pass to a more interesting compactification, which occurs naturally in tropical geometry.
The set

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}\coloneqq\left((\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\})^{d}\setminus\{-\infty\textbf{1}\}\right)\big/{\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}}

is called the
(d{-}1)
-dimensional
\max
-tropical projective space
.
It carries the natural structure of a semimodule with respect to the
\max
-tropical scalar multiplication.
The pair of spaces
(\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1},\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1})
is homeomorphic to the pair formed by the standard simplex
\Delta_{d-1}
and its interior; see
[
Jos21
, Prop 5.3]
.
Note that the tropical projective torus, as a set, is agnostic to
\max
or
\min
as the tropical addition.
However, the compactifying tropical projective space exists in two flavors; here we stick to the
\max
-version.
We will see, in
Corollary

32
, that the closure of a min-tropical hyperplane arrangement in the max-tropical projective space
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
with full support is also shellable.
To mix between the two types of tropical addition here is motivated by tropical convexity.
After all, a max-tropical polytope corresponds to a min-tropical hyperplane arrangement.

The tropical projective space is equipped with a natural stratification into tropical projective tori of various dimensions.
Each such torus corresponds to the relative interior of some face of the simplex
\Delta_{d-1}
.
To fix our notation, we explain the details.
Let
Z\subsetneq[d]
.
The set

(11)

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}(Z)\ \coloneqq\ \left\{v+\textbf{1}\mathbb{R}\mid v_{i}=-\infty\text{ if and only if }i\in Z\right\}

is called the
relatively open
Z
-stratum
of
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
.
That is, the
Z
-stratum collects the points of support
[d]\setminus Z
.
Its closure is the
closed
Z
-stratum

\overline{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{d-1}(Z)\ \coloneqq\ \left\{v+\textbf{1}\mathbb{R}\mid v_{i}=-\infty\text{ for }i\in Z\right\}\kern 5.0pt,

which is isomorphic, as a semimodule, to the tropical projective space of dimension
d-|Z|
.
The relative open
Z
-stratum is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d-|Z|}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
.
Letting
Z
range over all proper subsets of
[d]
partitions
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
, where
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}(\emptyset)=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
forms the open cell.

Again, we fix a matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, whose columns read as a configuration of
n
labeled points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
.
Like in the tropical torus there exists a covector decomposition of the tropical projective space induced by the points in
V
.
We begin with a cell in
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
from the tropical torus, now denoted by
X_{u}(V)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
where
u
is the covector of coarse type of the cell.
Its compactification
\overline{X}_{u}(V)
is described in the following way.

Lemma 29
.

Let
X_{u}(V)
be an unbounded maximal cell of
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
.
We set
Z\coloneqq\left\{i\in[d]\mid u_{i}\neq 0\right\}
.
Then the closure of
X_{u}(V)
in
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
coincides with the union of the cell with the closed
Z
-stratum
\overline{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{d-1}(Z)
, i.e.,

\overline{X}_{u}(V)\ =\ X_{u}(V)\ \cup\overline{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{d-1}(Z)\kern 5.0pt.

Proof.

A direct inspection shows that

\overline{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{d-1}(Z)\ =\ \bigcup_{Z\subseteq Z^{\prime}\subsetneq[d]}\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}(Z^{\prime})\kern 5.0pt.

First, consider the covector decomposition
\operatorname{CovDec}(\{v\})
induced by a single point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
.
The maximal cells of
\operatorname{CovDec}(\{v\})
are of the form

v+S_{i}\ \coloneqq\ \bigl\{x_{i}-v_{i}=\min_{j\in[d]}\{x_{j}-v_{j}\}\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}\bigr\},\quad i\in[d]\kern 5.0pt.

Note that the coarse type of
v+S_{i}
is
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
i
-th standard vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The closure of
v+S_{i}
agrees with
(v+S_{i})\cup\overline{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{d-1}(\{i\})
; see
[
Jos21
, p.180]
.

Second, we consider the covector decomposition induced by two distinct points,
v
and
w
.
A covector cell will be either of the form
v+S_{i}
,
w+S_{i}
or
(v+S_{i})\cap(w+S_{j})
with
i\neq j
.
In the first two cases the covector is of coarse type
2e_{i}
where
e_{i}
refers to the
i
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The rest of the statement is true as seen above.
In the last case, the closure contains
\bigcup_{\{i,j\}\subseteq Z^{\prime}\subsetneq[d]}\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}(Z^{\prime})
.
Moreover, the support of the rays read
\{\emptyset\}\cup\{\{i,j\}\subseteq Z^{\prime}\subseteq[d]\}
; see
[
Jos21
, p.179]
.
Hence we have

\overline{(v+S_{i})\cap(w+S_{j})}\ =\ (v+S_{i})\cap(w+S_{j})\cup\bigcup_{\{i,j\}\in Z^{\prime}\subsetneq[d]}\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}(Z^{\prime})\kern 5.0pt.

The claim now follows by induction on the size of the set
V
.
The general inductive step is similar to passing from a single point to a pair.
∎

Although the covector subdivision of

In order to show this we need to introduce the following notion.
The
Schlegel diagram
of a polytope
P
based at a facet
F
of
P
is the image of a central projection of
P
from a point
y
beyond
F
to the hyperplane
{\operatorname*{aff}}(F)
.
It is combinatorially equivalent to the cell complex formed by
\partial P
without the relative interior of
F
; see
[
Zie95
, Proposition 5.6]
.

Proposition 31
.

Recall that
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
has full support.
In this case the covector decomposition of
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
induced by
V
is combinatorially equivalent to the Schlegel diagram of the dome
\mathcal{D}(V)
based at the facet at infinity.

Proof.

We begin by showing that the face at infinity
E_{\infty}
is a simplex.
Recall that
E_{\infty}
is the image of the recession cone of
\mathcal{D}(V)
.
Its facets arise as images of recession cones of facets of
\mathcal{D}(V)
.
Let
F
be an unbounded facet of
\mathcal{D}(V)
.
Observe that a point
(x,s)
is contained in the recession cone of
F
if and only if
x
is contained in the recession cone of the corresponding covector cell
X_{u}(V)
and
s=\langle x,u\rangle
.

Again, let
Z\coloneqq\left\{i\in[d]\mid u_{i}\neq 0\right\}
.
In the proof of
Lemma

29
we have seen that the recession cone of
X_{u}(V)
is formed by the rays whose support is contained in
\emptyset\cup\{Z\subseteq Z^{\prime}\subseteq[d]\}
.
This implies that the recession cone of
F
is the conic hull of the rays
(x^{Z^{\prime}},-n)+\mathbb{R}(\textbf{1},n)
, where
x^{Z^{\prime}}=(x_{i})
with

\displaystyle x_{i}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }\ i\in Z^{\prime}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and
Z\subseteq Z^{\prime}\subset[d]
.
Since the lineality space of the dome is spanned by
(\textbf{1},n)
, it coincides with the conic hull of
(y^{Z^{\prime}},0)+\mathbb{R}(\textbf{1},n)
, where
y^{Z^{\prime}}=(y_{i})
with

\displaystyle y_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\ i\notin Z^{\prime}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and
Z\subseteq Z^{\prime}\subset[d]
.
So the recession cone of
F
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\textbf{1}
is the simplicial cone spanned by
(e_{i},0)
,
i\notin Z
.

To conclude that the facet at infinity
E_{\infty}
is indeed a simplex, we observe that the coarse types
u
correspond to lattice points of the Newton polytope
n\Delta_{d-1}
.
This implies that the support
Z
of
u
is either a singleton
\{i\}
,
i\in[d]
, or a superset.
In particular every singleton occurs.
Thus
E_{\infty}
is the simplex formed by the images of the rays
(e_{i},0)
,
i\in[d]
.

Next we construct the combinatorial isomorphism between the Schlegel diagram of
\mathcal{D}(V)
set at
E_{\infty}
and the closure of the covector decomposition
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
.
Orthogonal projection establishes a bijection between the boundary faces of
\mathcal{D}(V)
which are not contained in
E_{\infty}
and the cells of the covector decomposition
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
while preserving inclusion.
By
Lemma

29
it suffices to extend this map by sending a facet of
E_{\infty}
formed by the images of the rays spanned by
(e_{j},0)
,
j\neq i
, to
\overline{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{d-1}(\{i\})
.
∎

Corollary 32
.

Recall that
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
has full support.
In this case the
\max
-tropical compactification of the
\min
-tropical covector subdivision
\operatorname{CovDec}(V)
is shellable.
Moreover, the
\max
-tropical compactification of the
\min
-tropical hyperplane arrangement
{\mathcal{T}}(L_{V})
is shellable, too.

Proof.

A shelling order of
\mathcal{D}(V)
where the facet at infinity comes last induces a shelling of the covector decomposition of
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
induced by
V
.
The shellability of the compactified hyperplane arrangement follows from
Proposition

6
.
∎
