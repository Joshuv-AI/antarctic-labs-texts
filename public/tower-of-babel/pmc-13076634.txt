Anchor-induced localized stress evolution and deformation prediction in SOI MEMS structures
Authors: Datai Hui, Xiao Meng, Jiaqian Ding, Bowen Xia, Guohua Sun, Laixian Zhang, Binbin Wang, Yongqian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuspended structures in silicon-on-insulator (SOI) microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) are critical components for sensing and actuation. However, undesired deformation in these structures significantly degrades device performance. Although such deformation is commonly attributed to compressive stress, the underlying mechanisms governing stress evolution and deformation remain unclear, limiting predictive capability and effective suppression strategies. Here, we investigate the evolution of localized stress and reveal its role in driving structural deformation. We demonstrate that the localized stress is not intrinsic to the device layer but is mechanically transferred from the buried oxide (BOX) anchor. Specifically, the deformation is an extrinsic response governed by a three-stage mechanism: lateral anchor expansion, interfacial stress transfer, and the formation of a localized bending moment. To enable deformation prediction, a theoretical model is established by introducing an equivalent thickness and a characteristic length that defines the effective stress-transfer region. The resulting models reveal deformation behaviors fundamentally distinct from traditional theories, including linear deflection scaling in cantilevers and pre-buckling nonlinear deflection in double-clamped beams. To validate the proposed mechanism, micro-Raman stress mapping provides direct experimental evidence of a transition from tensile stress in the anchor region to compressive stress in the suspended region. The proposed theoretical models are further validated through FEM simulations and experiments, showing good agreement with average relative errors consistently below 10%. Furthermore, a mechanism-guided stress-isolation beam design is demonstrated, reducing the deflection of a double-clamped beam by ~93%. This work proposes a unified, mechanism-based framework for understanding, predicting, and suppressing anchor-induced deformation in MEMS devices. Subject terms: Physics, Engineering

Introduction

Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) integrate mechanical elements, actuators, and electronics to enable a broad range of functionalities1,2. Among these, silicon-on-insulator (SOI)-based MEMS devices3 benefit from a single-crystal silicon device layer that provides stable mechanical and thermal properties4, enabling robust and reliable operation over a wide range of environmental conditions. These systems, including accelerometers5, gyroscopes6, pressure sensors7, and optical modulators8,9, have found broad applications in automotive systems10, aerospace11, light detection and ranging (LiDAR)12, and optical communications13. Suspended structures in these devices are critical functional elements that enable multi-degree-of-freedom (DOF) mechanical motion and precise electromechanical modulation of the device response. In MEMS accelerometers5, external acceleration deflects a cantilever with a proof mass, altering the electrode capacitance to enable high-sensitivity inertial measurements. Applied pressure on a MEMS sensor induces deflection of a suspended diaphragm7, which is transduced into an electrical signal via changes in capacitance, piezoresistance, or resonant frequency. In addition, suspended structures in optical devices serve as dynamically reconfigurable optical elements8,14, where controlled mechanical actuation enables dynamic modulation of light via reflection, diffraction, or phase shifting, enabling tunable optical functions such as beam steering, variable focusing, and spectral filtering.

However, these structures are highly susceptible to stress-induced deformation, which can cause structural warping and dimensional instability. Such effects degrade mechanical performance, sensing accuracy, and long-term reliability. In optical MEMS, near-perfect surface flatness is critical, typically requiring deformation below one-fourth of the wavelength to minimize wavefront distortion. For diffractive devices, even slight deformation alters the modulation depth, thereby redistributing energy among diffraction orders. Additionally, such structural deformation may lead to device failure, including stiction15 and electrostatic pull-in deformation16, as well as increased actuation voltage and power consumption. Undesired deformation in MEMS structures is primarily attributed to packaging-induced stresses17,18 and intrinsic stress concentration within the SOI device layer19,20. Packaging processes typically introduce residual stress due to mismatches in the coefficients of thermal expansion (CTEs) between adjacent materials. This packaging-induced stress has been rigorously modeled using the finite element method (FEM) and experimentally validated through laser interferometry. Packaging-induced stress generally leads to global structural deformation, which is largely predictable and can be effectively mitigated by optimizing adhesive material properties and bonding layer thickness21,22.

In contrast, other studies have reported initial deformation due to intrinsic stress concentrations localized within suspended structures. For example, compressive stress in the device layer can induce buckling in suspended membranes of optical MEMS devices, resulting in out-of-plane deformation that degrades optical performance23. Our previous work also reported an initial upward deformation of ~170 nm in the released grating ribbons of a MEMS optical modulator8. This deformation was attributed to intrinsic stress in the SOI device layer. However, stress arising from lattice mismatch during fabrication is generally considered negligible in single-crystal silicon because of its highly ordered and uniform crystal structure, which minimizes structural defects and dislocation-driven strain24. More recent studies suggest that compressive stress in MEMS structures might originate from the buried oxide (BOX) layer at the anchor region25,26. However, the underlying mechanisms responsible for the generation, propagation, and resulting deformation caused by such anchor-induced stress are not yet fully understood. Although experimental observations have confirmed the presence of such stress23–26, the underlying processes governing its generation, temporal evolution during fabrication, and the directionality of the induced deformation remain unclear and require further investigation. As a result, establishing a theoretical model for deformation prediction in SOI-based MEMS structures remains highly challenging. To date, a comprehensive understanding of the deformation mechanism and a corresponding theoretical model have not yet been reported, and effective design strategies for suppressing anchor-induced deformation remain limited.

In this work, we investigate the evolution of anchor-induced stress and the associated deformation mechanism in SOI MEMS structures. We identify that the localized stress observed in suspended structures is not intrinsic but is mechanically transferred from the anchors. More importantly, we reveal that the deformation is an extrinsic response driven by a three-stage mechanism, consisting of anchor expansion, interfacial stress transfer, and the subsequent formation of a localized bending moment. Building upon this mechanism, theoretical models are established to predict deformation in cantilever and double-clamped beams. These models reveal deformation behaviors fundamentally different from those predicted by traditional theories. Specifically, the models predict linear deflection scaling in cantilevers and pre-buckling nonlinear deflection in double-clamped beams. To validate the proposed mechanism and models, three representative structures, including double-clamped plates, microbridges, and microcantilevers, are experimentally characterized. Micro-Raman stress mapping provides direct experimental verification by revealing a transition from tensile stress in the anchor region to compressive stress in the suspended region. The proposed theoretical models are further validated through FEM simulations and experimental characterization, showing good agreement with average relative errors consistently below 10%. Moreover, guided by the proposed mechanism, a stress-isolation beam design is demonstrated to effectively decouple anchor-induced stress, resulting in an ~93% reduction in the initial deflection.

Results

Anchor-induced stress transfer and deformation mechanism

We first analyze the generation mechanism of the initial residual stress within the SOI wafer. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, SOI wafers are fabricated using Smart Cut™ technology27. Since intrinsic stress from lattice mismatch is negligible in single-crystal silicon, residual stress primarily originates from thermal mismatch between the silicon device layer and the buried oxide (BOX) layer due to their distinct coefficients of thermal expansion (CTEs). To investigate stress evolution, FEM simulations were performed under standard fabrication conditions, with the temperatures for oxidation, implantation, and annealing set to 1050 °C, 550 °C, and 1100 °C, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1b, c, the BOX layer retains a high level of compressive stress (~380 MPa) annealing at 1100 °C, showing weak dependence on layer thickness. In contrast, the device layer develops tensile stress on the order of several tens of MPa, which increases with the BOX thickness (Fig. 1d). Figure 1e reveals a nearly uniform stress distribution across the thickness with variations below 1%. The simulation results show good agreement with previously reported experimental data28,29. These results indicate that the compressive stress observed in suspended structure does not originate from intrinsic material stress. Instead, we propose that it is externally induced and mechanically transferred from the anchors during the BOX etching process, serving as the primary driving force of the deformation. To illustrate the mechanism, a representative double-clamped plate in a MEMS grating modulator is employed8.

Figure 2a illustrates the evolution of upward deformation in the double-clamped structure during the BOX etching process. As shown in Fig. 2b, the three layers are strongly bonded prior to release, which effectively constrains in-plane strain. In this state, the BOX layer shares the strain distribution through mechanical coupling with the adjacent silicon layers. When the BOX is partially etched to define the MEMS structure, the remaining BOX anchors act as locally constrained regions. The accumulated compressive strain then drives a lateral expansion of the anchors (Fig. 2c), which is mechanically transferred to the bottom region of the device layer through the bonding interface. This stress transfer is highly localized near the anchor region and gives rise to a nonuniform stress field at the end of the MEMS structure.

To analytically quantify this effect, we apply Saint-Venant’s principle30, which states that the complex, localized stress distribution near the anchor can be replaced by a statically equivalent load acting on the MEMS structure. Consequently, the localized interface stress is modeled as an equivalent compressive layer of thickness te at the bottom of the structure. Here te serves as an effective, structure-dependent parameter governed by the material properties and anchor geometry and is assumed constant for a given configuration. Within this effective layer (0≤z≤te), the axial stress is approximated as a uniform value σBOX.

Based on this understanding, we identify a distinct three-stage mechanism as the primary origin of the upward deflection in MEMS structures. To validate the proposed mechanism, FEM analysis was performed on a representative double-clamped beam to characterize the stress distribution (Fig. 2d). The simulation results confirm that the central span maintains the predicted linear negative stress gradient, consistent with bending moment theory. In contrast, the near-anchor regions exhibit significant stress concentration and nonlinearity due to local boundary effects. Figure 2e further reveals that this anchor stress concentration is intensified by increasing beam thickness but is alleviated in longer structures, prompting a more detailed investigation into the stress evolution.

To further evaluate the stress gradient distribution, the double-clamped beam is divided into five layers along its thickness to examine stress variations in both the axial and thickness directions, as shown in Fig. 3a. Figure 3b–e presents the axial stress profiles for beams with varying length-to-thickness ratios. For short beams (20:1), the stress remains axially uniform with a consistent linear gradient through the thickness. As the ratio increases to 60:1 and beyond, significant axial stress redistribution occurs, where the compressive stress peaks at the beam center in the bottom layer, while localized stress reversal begins to emerge near the anchors. At higher ratios (90:1 and 130:1), these reversal regions become pronounced near both fixed ends. They progressively counteract the central negative gradient, leading to the substantial attenuation of the average through thickness gradient summarized in Fig. 3f. Notably, this stress state differs fundamentally from that of pure bending. The anchor-induced axial stress shifts the neutral axis beyond the device layer, such that no neutral axis exists within the structure, resulting in a net compressive stress dominated by the combined effects of axial compression and bending moments.

Theoretical models for anchor-induced beam deformation

By introducing an equivalent thickness te, the complex stress field at the anchor is represented by two equivalent cross-sectional loads: an axial force NBOX and a bending moment M0. While stress concentrations exist at the geometric discontinuity, the stress transfer is confined to a limited length. To quantify this spatial extent, we define a characteristic length Le based on classical shear-lag theory32. This theory describes the competition between axial and interfacial shear stiffness, predicting that stress transfer occurs over a characteristic decay length32. In the context of anchor-induced stress transfer, Le represents the effective spatial extent over which the localized bending moment generated at the anchor is transmitted into the suspended structure. From a mechanical perspective, the bending moment M0 generated by the step-up anchor geometry is not applied at a single point but is distributed over a finite region due to the shear-lag mechanism. The parameter Le therefore characterizes the distance over which this moment is effectively acts on the beam. Based on Eq. Under an idealized assumption, it may be approximated by the shear modulus of the BOX layer to obtain a first-order estimate of the magnitude of Le. However, in practical MEMS structures, stress transfer is not governed by a single material interface but by a multidimensional constraint region involving the BOX height, undercut profile, and local shear-transfer paths. As a result, the interfacial shear stiffness Gint that controls the actual behavior is geometry-dependent and differs from the intrinsic material modulus. When Le is extracted from FEM or experiments, it therefore reflects this effective stiffness under a given configuration rather than simply relying on the material constant. Consequently, Eq. (8) provides a first-order estimate before fabrication, whereas FEM-fitted values incorporate additional geometric effects and may not strictly follow the monotonic trend predicted by the idealized shear-lag theory. Based on the proposed stress transfer and deformation mechanism, the bending deformation associated with the bending moment M0 is assumed to be confined to the region 0≤x≤Le. Beyond this region, the beam no longer experiences a significant bending moment and instead undergoes a rigid body rotation, as illustrated in Fig. 4a.

Unlike a cantilever beam, which is constrained only at one end, a double-clamped beam is constrained at both ends in terms of displacement and rotation. Owing to these boundary constraints, the anchor not only introduces an equivalent bending moment M0, but also transfers an axial load NBOX into the beam. This axial load couples with the initial bending deformation and further amplifies the deflection, giving rise to a geometrically nonlinear response. In this case, the maximum deflection of a double-clamped beam is no longer determined solely by the initial deformation induced by M0. Owing to the additional axial compressive load transferred from the anchor, the initial bending deformation is amplified through axial–bending coupling. As the axial load increases, this amplification effect becomes progressively stronger and tends to diverge as the load approaches the critical buckling condition. Based on the above theoretical model, numerical calculations were conducted to quantitatively determine the characteristic parameters. Specifically, the parameters te, Le, and α were simultaneously determined using a nonlinear least-squares fitting approach. Since these parameters originate from the same anchor-induced stress field and fabrication process, deflection of both cantilever and double-clamped beams were jointly fitted to ensure consistency across boundary conditions. As a representative example, a series of single-crystal silicon beams with a thickness of 1 µm was analyzed, subjected to a residual stress of 380 MPa in a 3 µm thick BOX layer. The fitting results yielded effective parameters of te=48.1 nm, Le = 4.08 µm, and a net bending-moment coefficient of α=0.85. Figure 4b compares the cantilever deflections calculated using our proposed model against those from traditional models, with FEM results serving as a reference. As the beam length increases, the proposed model predicts a linear dependence of deflection on beam length, demonstrating excellent agreement with the FEM results across the entire investigated range. In contrast, traditional cantilever models based on the assumptions of an equivalent end moment, a point load, or a uniform load exhibit higher-order scaling behavior. This discrepancy is attributed to the anchor-induced localized bending moment near the beam root, which significantly reduces the sensitivity of the overall deflection to beam length. Such a deformation mechanism is fundamentally distinct from that of conventional cantilever beams subjected to external loads. Therefore, applying conventional models to this configuration may result in significant deviations between predicted and actual deflection. Figure 4c compares the deflection of a double-clamped beam calculated using the proposed model with the results from three traditional load models. In the short beam length regime, the deflections predicted by all models exhibit an approximately linear increase with length. This suggests that structural bending stiffness dominates in this range, where nonlinear effects are negligible. As the beam length increases, the proposed model deviates from the power-law growth predicted by traditional models. Instead, the deflection exhibits a sharp increase, indicating a structural pre-buckling effect that traditional models fail to capture. The theoretical critical buckling length is calculated to be 174 µm. This instability is further corroborated by the FEM simulations, which fail to converge for beam lengths exceeding 160 µm, confirming that the structure is approaching a critical buckling state. Structures for verification

To verify the proposed mechanism, model, and simulation results, three different MEMS structures, including the double-clamped plate, microbridge, and microcantilever, were designed and fabricated. Figure 5a shows the schematic of the double-clamped plate structure. A series of release holes are used to define the effective suspended length, while the extension length beyond the free ends remains arbitrary. The release-hole configuration in current design is a balance among several practical considerations, including the requirements of micro-Raman mapping and the need for reliable HF undercut (a detailed discussion of the rationale for the configuration is provided in the Supplementary Information). By controlling the wet-etching time, a typical ratio between the effective suspended length and the anchor width can be achieved. Consequently, the stress distributions in both the suspended regions and the areas bonded to the anchors can be characterized. Figure 6b, c illustrates the designed microbridge and microcantilever beam structures for deformation verification. Due to over-etching during the wet-etching process, additional etched regions form at both ends of the structure. The over-etching length was controlled and monitored through a stepwise wet-etching process on the same sample. During etching, the sample was periodically removed from the HF solution at fixed time intervals and inspected under an optical microscope to check the extent of BOX removal. The lateral undercut length after each etch cycle provided a practical indication of the etching progress. This procedure allowed the over-etching length to be maintained within a reasonable and repeatable range, ensuring complete release of the suspended structures. Although over-etching is present in both cantilevers and microbridges, its mechanical consequences are fundamentally different. For cantilevers, over-etching effectively increases the free length and introduces compliance at the anchor, resulting in amplified deflection. However, in microbridges, this boundary compliance facilitates the release of anchor-induced stress, thereby reducing the overall deflection and making the beam less susceptible to buckling. To mitigate the anchor-induced undesired deformation, a stress-isolation beam was designed. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, the stress-isolation beam is integrated orthogonally into the suspended structure. This beam effectively intercepts the anchor-induced stress, thereby decoupling the anchor effects from the global bending response. To evaluate the feasibility of the proposed design, FEM simulations were performed to analyze the resulting stress field. The simulated stress map in Fig. 5e reveals a compressive stress concentration of ~300 MPa in the double-clamped isolation beam, whereas the central region of the suspended structure remains virtually unstressed and exhibits negligible displacement, as evidenced by the displacement field shown in Fig. 5f. These results confirm that the anchor-induced stress is locally confined within the isolation beam and prevented from propagating into the suspended structure.

Fabricated MEMS structures

Figure 6 presents the fabricated MEMS structures used to characterize residual stress and deformation. Figure 6a shows a double-clamped plate with an effective length of ~85 μm, where the underlying BOX anchor remains visible through the thin device layer. The cross-section in Fig. 6b reveals a trapezoidal anchor undercut. This geometry is governed by the intrinsic isotropic nature of wet-etching and the resulting time-lag effect, rather than by specific HF concentration. Because the upper portion of the BOX layer is exposed to the HF solution earlier and accumulates the longest effective etch duration, it experiences the greatest lateral retreat, resulting in the narrowest remaining anchor width at the top surface. Consequently, the characteristic trapezoidal undercut profile is naturally formed. These geometric features were incorporated into the FEM model. Figure 6c, d displays arrays of microbridges and microcantilevers. Insets reveal a consistent over-etching of ~20 μm around the double-clamped beams and 15 μm at the microcantilever roots. These dimensions were accounted for in subsequent FEM simulations. Figure 6e, f shows structures incorporating stress-isolation beams. As detailed in the insets, the isolation beams are oriented perpendicular to the anchors to minimize compressive stress, while the active structures are aligned parallel to the BOX anchor.

Miro-Raman stress mapping

To experimentally validate the proposed mechanism of anchor-induced stress transfer and its spatial evolution, micro-Raman spectroscopy was employed to characterize the surface stress distribution of the double-clamped structure. As shown in Fig. 7a, the characteristic Raman peak of single-crystal silicon appears at 520.7 cm⁻¹ under an excitation wavelength of 532 nm. This distribution experimentally validates the proposed stress transfer mechanism, confirming that the BOX anchor induces a compressive load on the adjacent suspended beam via interfacial shear coupling. Furthermore, the FEM results shown in Fig. 7d demonstrate excellent agreement with the experimental data, verifying the reliability of the simulation method for predicting stress evolution. This reliability enables the simulations to be used to further assess the influence of anchor-undercut geometry on stress transfer. The trapezoidal anchor undercut does not significantly affect the key stress-transfer behavior. To verify this point, additional FEM simulations with two different anchor geometries were conducted (Supplementary Fig. S2 in the Supplementary Information). In all cases, a transition from tensile stress in the anchor region to compressive stress in the suspended region is consistently observed along the axial direction.

Structural deformation

To characterize the deformation behavior of the fabricated microbridges and cantilevers, a confocal laser scanning microscope was used to capture the three-dimensional morphology of the structures. Figure 8a presents the experimentally measured and FEM-simulated surface morphologies of a representative microbridge with a beam length of 120 μm. The experimental results indicate that the microbridge exhibits an initial upward deformation after release. Figure 8b shows the corresponding deformation profile of the microbridge, where both experimental measurements and FEM simulations reveal a maximum deflection of ~250 nm. Deformation is also observed in the over-etched regions adjacent to the microbridge. As a result, the measured deflection includes contributions from both the microbridge itself and the surrounding over-etched areas, which also exhibit upward deformation due to stress release. Similar initial upward deformation is observed for all microbridges with beam lengths ranging from 50 μm to 180 μm. Notably, the microbridges remain in a stable, non-buckled state even for lengths exceeding 170 μm, as illustrated in the inset of Fig. 8c. This behavior can be attributed to the boundary compliance introduced by anchor undercutting, which effectively relieves the axial compressive stress and consequently increases the critical buckling threshold. This observation is consistent with the theoretical analysis presented earlier. Figure 8c compares the simulated and experimentally measured deflections of microbridges with varying beam lengths, showing good agreement across the entire range. The experimental data represents averages obtained from five samples for each beam length, ensuring statistical reliability and repeatability (detailed data are provided in Supplementary Table S1 in the Supplementary Information). Both experimental and simulation results demonstrate an increase in deflection with increasing beam length. In contrast to cantilever structures, for which an effective length correction can be applied (as discussed later), the double-clamped configuration involves a more complex stress-release mechanism. Simply incorporating the over-etch length into the model is insufficient to account for the stress reduction induced by undercutting. The development of a comprehensive analytical model that incorporates these boundary compliance effects will be the subject of future work.

This observation confirms that the anchor undercut contributes to the structural compliance. Consequently, our theoretical model accounts for this boundary effect by incorporating an effective length, defined as the sum of the nominal beam length and the over-etching length. A maximum deflection of ~200 nm is observed in the experiment, showing excellent agreement with the simulated value. Figure 8f illustrates the maximum deflection as a function of beam length. Due to stiction during wet-etching, reliable deformation data were obtained only for cantilevers with lengths between 10 and 65 μm, as shown in the inset. The observed asymmetrical deformation of the suspended structure near the anchor region in the color map originates from a slight tilt of the sample during the surface profile measurement. Although planarization reduced the tilt, a small residual angle produced a gradual color shift. To verify symmetry and beam-length independence, deformation profiles were extracted from samples of different lengths. As representative cases, Supplementary Fig. S5 shows consistent near-anchor deformation for 30 µm and 65 µm beams, both with a maximum value of about 26 nm. Supplementary Table S1 further confirms uniform near-anchor deformation across all measured cantilevers. Figure 8f shows that, in contrast to traditional models, the proposed theoretical model predicts a linear dependence of deflection on beam length, which agrees well with both FEM simulations and experimental results. The average relative error remains consistently below 10% (detailed data are provided in Supplementary Table S2). Overall, the theoretical predictions align closely with the simulations and measurements over the entire investigated range. This quantitative consistency across theory, simulation, and experiment validates the proposed mechanism and physical models. To evaluate the effectiveness of the stress-isolation beam, the deformation behaviors of structures with and without the isolation design were compared. Figure 8g presents the surface morphologies of 180 μm microbridges. The results reveal a distinct contrast that the conventional microbridge exhibits significant upward deflection, whereas the structure with the stress-isolation design shows negligible deformation. As shown in Fig. 8h, the conventional design reaches a maximum deflection of 450 nm, whereas the optimized design exhibits a substantially reduced deflection of only 30 nm, corresponding to a reduction of nearly 93%. This suppression effect is further confirmed in microcantilevers. Figure 8i shows that the conventional 60 μm cantilever undergoes pronounced upward bending, while the stress-isolated counterpart remains nearly flat, with the peak deflection reduced from ~200 nm to less than 40 nm.

Discussion

For SOI-based devices in optical and sensing applications, understanding residual stress and the resulting deformation in suspended structures is essential for performance and design reliability. In this work, we investigate the role of anchor-induced stress in driving deformation in SOI MEMS structures. Through theoretical analysis, FEM simulations, and experimental characterization, we reveal that the localized stress responsible for upward deformation is not intrinsic to the silicon device layer but originates from compressive stress in the BOX anchors and is mechanically transferred into the suspended structures during release. This stress transfer process follows a three-stage mechanism involving anchor expansion, interfacial stress transfer across the BOX-silicon interface, and the formation of a localized bending moment near the anchor.

Based on the proposed stress transfer mechanism, an analytical deformation model is established by introducing an equivalent thickness to represent the anchor-induced load and a characteristic length to describe the effective stress transfer region. The key distinction between the proposed model and traditional formulations lies in the consideration of anchor-induced localized stress, bending moments, and their influence on beam deflection. Traditional models assume uniform loading conditions such as equivalent end moments or distributed forces and therefore overlook the complex stress transfer near the anchor. As a result, they predict higher-order deflection scaling that does not reflect the actual behavior of SOI MEMS structures. In contrast, the proposed model accounts for this localized stress and its effect on global deformation. The bending-moment-induced deformation is confined to a limited region near the anchor, which yields different scaling behaviors. For cantilevers, the model predicts a linear dependence of deflection on length, whereas traditional models predict higher-order scaling because they ignore localized bending moments. For double-clamped beams, the proposed model also deviates from the power-law trend of traditional models. Instead of a continuous increase, it predicts a sharp rise in deflection associated with pre-buckling effects. Traditional models cannot capture this behavior because they do not include the localized stress and bending moments that dominate near the anchors.

The proposed model is also applicable to different SOI fabrication routes and various device materials. Whether the SOI wafer is produced by Smart-Cut™, SIMOX, or other processes, the silicon device layer and the buried oxide exhibit similar elastic constants, and the dominant BOX residual stress primarily originates from thermal mismatch during high-temperature processing steps. Since this thermally induced stress drives anchor-induced deformation, the present model can be extended to other SOI platforms by substituting the corresponding layer thicknesses. The model also applies to alternative device-layer materials such as LPCVD SiN or poly-Si on oxide. These materials behave as linear-elastic solids, and their mechanical parameters can be measured or obtained from databases. Because the governing stress-transfer mechanism follows linear elasticity, the model can also predict deformation in these systems when the corresponding material constants are used. However, when the model is extended to extreme geometries or unconventional material systems, several limitations may arise. The proposed model is based on linear elasticity and small-deflection theory. For ultrathin films or membranes, transverse deflection may become comparable to the thickness, making membrane stiffening and geometric nonlinearity significant. In such cases, the linear model may underestimate stiffness and overestimates deformation. In addition, as beam length increases and the axial load approaches the Euler critical value, linear stability theory cannot capture the post-buckling response, which follows nonlinear equilibrium paths. Beams operating near or beyond the critical point therefore require nonlinear stability analysis rather than the linear formulation. In materials with nonlinear or anisotropic elasticity, the assumption of a linear isotropic stress-strain law no longer holds. Nonlinear materials exhibit modulus variation during deformation, and anisotropic materials require directional stiffness descriptions. Accurate prediction therefore requires the corresponding nonlinear constitutive relations or anisotropic stiffness tensors.

To validate the proposed mechanism and theoretical models, three representative structures, including double-clamped plates, microbridges, and microcantilevers, were experimentally characterized. Micro-Raman stress mapping provides direct experimental evidence by revealing a clear transition from tensile stress in the anchor region to compressive stress in the suspended region. The predictive capability of the proposed models is further confirmed through FEM simulations and experimental measurements, showing good quantitative agreement with average relative errors consistently below 10%. Importantly, to avoid uncertainty in the extracted model parameters, the over-etching length was explicitly included rather than treated as an uncontrolled variation. Although over-etching occurs in both cantilevers and microbridges, its mechanical effects differ. In cantilevers, over-etching increases the free length and introduces anchor compliance, and this geometric influence can be compensated by defining an effective suspended length. The results in Fig. 8f confirm that this treatment properly accounts for over-etch variability. In contrast, over-etching in double-clamped microbridges extends the structure and partially releases anchor-induced stress, reducing deflection and lowering the buckling tendency. This coupled mechanism cannot be corrected by an effective length approach. Controlled over-etching experiments and further analysis are therefore required to establish a correction model for microbridges, which will be investigated in future work.

Moreover, guided by the proposed mechanism, a stress-isolation beam design is developed and experimentally demonstrated to effectively decouple anchor-induced stress, resulting in an ~93% reduction in the initial deflection. The key principle of the isolation beam is to introduce a segment that locally intercepts anchor-induced stress and prevents its propagation into the functional suspended structure. This concept is not limited to a single geometry. MEMS structures in which deformation originates from the anchor interface, such as suspended plates in deformable mirrors or torsional elements in resonators, can all benefit from a localized stress-isolation beam. In different MEMS layouts, the isolation-beam geometry can be adjusted to achieve the required stiffness and isolation capability. The length and thickness can be tuned to balance deformation suppression with mechanical performance. These strategies are not restricted to the SOI platform and can be applied to other material systems and structural configurations. In addition, the isolation-beam concept is useful for residual-stress test structures such as pointer beams. Traditional pointer structures determine the sign and magnitude of film stress from their rotation, assuming that the deformation originates solely from the test film. In MEMS platforms where the oxide layer carries residual stress, localized stress transfer from the oxide can modify the effective moment at the anchor and even reverse the rotation direction, leading to incorrect evaluation. Incorporating a stress-isolation beam suppresses this effect and ensures that the rotation reflects only the intrinsic film stress, thereby improving measurement accuracy. In practice, the isolation beam should be sufficiently stiff so that the anchor-induced bending moment is dissipated within itself rather than transmitted into the functional structure. Its stiffness, governed by geometry, can be increased by using a relatively short length and adequate thickness. For example, to keep isolation-beam deformation below 50 nm under the Smart-Cut™ SOI regime, the length-to-thickness ratio should be kept about 30:1. Orientation is also critical. Maintaining the corner connection angle between the isolation beam and its fixed end below 45° effectively enhances the stress-isolation effect. The acute-angle geometry blocks a portion of the anchor-induced stress from entering the isolation beam, while the remaining stress is redistributed more uniformly within the beam. Consequently, the stress transmitted to the functional beam is substantially reduced, thereby improving the overall stress-isolation performance. Beyond these geometric guidelines, the stiffness of the isolation beam can be further improved through topology-optimization strategies. Under fixed overall footprint and in-plane geometric constraints (such as fixed width or length), topology optimization can redistribute material within the isolation beam to increase its effective rigidity and further suppress the stress transferred from the anchor and thereby reduce the undesired defamation. This approach provides an additional degree of design freedom and enhances the applicability of the isolation-beam concept to a broader range of MEMS devices.

Broadly, the mechanism established in this work enables anchor-induced stress to be treated as a predictable and design-controllable factor rather than an empirical uncertainty. First, the proposed mechanism and analytical model enable prediction of deformation in suspended MEMS structures during the design stage. Within this framework, it is possible to determine whether a given geometry satisfies flatness requirements and how to design parameters, such as beam length, influence deformation. This capability shifts deformation control from trial-and-error processing to a predictable and quantitative design process. Second, the mechanism provides guidance for reducing anchor-induced deformation through fabrication improvements. Since the oxide-layer stress largely generates the local stress and bending moment at the anchor, using low-stress dielectric layers, replacing thermal oxide with low-stress SiN, or optimizing bonding and annealing conditions can effectively reduce deformation. In addition, the mechanism suggests structural approaches beyond the demonstrated stress-isolation beam. Because the anchor-induced bending moment is concentrated near the root region, adding a local compensating film with patterned compressive stress can counteract the moment and reduce deformation. As SOI-based MEMS continue to advance toward higher performance, the insights presented in this study provide a physical basis for future structural design and optimization.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of microstructures

The fabrication of the designed microstructures began with a commercial Smart-cutTM SOI wafer (Suzhou Rdmicro Co., Ltd), consisting of a 1.0 μm device layer, a 3.0 μm buried oxide layer, and a 400 μm silicon substrate. A contact lithography system (SUSS MA6) was used to pattern a photoresist layer (S1813) spun on top of the device layer. Subsequently, dry etching was performed via ICP etching (SPTS Rapier) to form holes and other release channels deep in the buried oxide layer. Following this step, the whole wafer was laser-diced into several chips for a single wet-etching release. Finally, the buried oxide layer of the standalone chip was partially etched with 49% hydrofluoric acid. 49% HF was selected because it is the standard chemistry for releasing SiO₂ sacrificial layers and provides consistent, reproducible processing. More importantly, it ensures efficient and complete BOX removal. In this process, the BOX must be laterally removed by more than 40 μm to form suspended structures for Raman characterization. Based on calibrated parameters for the same oxide stack, 49% HF etches thermal oxide at about 3000 nm/min, enabling full release in ~15 min. In contrast, 6:1 BOE is typically an order of magnitude slower, which would greatly extend release time. Therefore, 49% HF was chosen to ensure process reliability and fabrication throughput.

Characterization

The fabricated microstructures were observed by a metallurgical microscope (Leica DM6 M Upright Materials Microscopes). A scanning electron microscope with backscattered electron imaging (Phenom XL G2) was utilized to obtain the detailed cross-section view of the double-clamped plate. A confocal micro-Raman spectrometer (HORIBA LabRAM Odyssey) featuring a 532 nm laser with a minimum focused beam of 1 μm was used to measure the stress distribution of the top surface in a double-clamped plate. In the selected measurement region, ~6000 sample points were imaged to obtain the peak shift mapping. The 3D-morphology and deformation profile of fabricated microstructures were characterized by a confocal laser scanning microscope (Senofar S neox).
