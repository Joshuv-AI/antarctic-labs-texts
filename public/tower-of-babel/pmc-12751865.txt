Indoor air pollution exposure and acute respiratory infection among under-five children in low- and middle-income countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis of epidemiological studies
Authors: Belay Desye, Abebe Kassa Geto, Chala Daba, Gete Berihun, Leykun Berhanu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndoor air pollution is a major public health issue and contributor to acute respiratory infections among under-five children in low- and middle-income countries. Although numerous studies have explored this association, existing evidence is fragmented, varies across settings and lacks comprehensive synthesis. This study addresses this gap by quantifying the pooled effect size of the association between indoor air pollution exposure and acute respiratory infection and identifying determinant factors among under-five children in low- and middle-income countries.MethodsThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guideline was used for this review. The study reviewed 36 research articles obtained from databases including PubMed, ScienceDirect, HINARI, and Google Scholar from January 1, 2015, to September 1, 2024. Data were extracted using Microsoft Excel and then analyzed in STATA-17 software. The Joanna Briggs Institute quality appraisal tool was utilized to evaluate the included studies. A random effects model was applied to calculate the pooled effect size, while publication bias was examined using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Additionally, subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis were performed.ResultsThe pooled effect size of acute respiratory infection among under-five children associated with exposure to indoor air pollution was 13.56% (95% CI: 11.88–15.25), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.9%, p-value < 0.001). Exposure-related factors, including absence of a kitchen window (OR = 4.47, 95% CI: 1.66–7.28), use of traditional stoves (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.02–4.03), secondhand smoke exposure (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.32), and use of unclean fuels (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.25) were significantly associated with acute respiratory infection among under-five children. In addition, child health and nutritional factors such as diarrhea (OR = 3.76, 95% CI: 3.51–4.01), and stunting (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.31) were also linked to increased risk. Furthermore, demographic and household characteristics, including child’s age, sex, wealth index, lack of separate kitchen, presence of children near in stove during cooking and birth order were identified as significant predictors.ConclusionsThis study highlights a significant association between indoor air pollution and acute respiratory infection in under-five children. Targeted interventions, including the promotion of clean cooking fuels, smoke-free households, and improved household ventilation are essential.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12148-y.Keywords: Indoor air pollution, Household air pollution, Acute respiratory infections, Respiratory health, Under-five children, Low and middle-income countries

Introduction

Indoor air pollution (IAP) is a major public health concern, strongly associated with Acute Respiratory Infections (ARIs), particularly among children under five [1, 2]. In 2020, IAP contributed to 3.2 million deaths globally, including over 237,000 deaths in under-five children [3]. Major sources of IAP include tobacco smoke, combustion appliances, Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs), mold, and household dust, which can impair lung function, cause respiratory inflammation, and increase susceptibility to infections [4–6].

Children are especially vulnerable due to their developing respiratory systems, higher metabolic rates, and the substantial time they spend indoors. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), poor ventilation and the use of polluting fuels further exacerbate these risks, contributing to high ARI morbidity and mortality [7–9]. Globally, approximately 2.3 billion people rely on solid fuels such as wood, charcoal, crop waste, coal, and animal dung, often using inefficient stoves, with limited access to cleaner alternatives in LMICs [10].

Evidence from epidemiological studies indicates a strong association between IAP and ARIs. Systematic reviews have shown that biomass fuel use, lack of a separate kitchen, and household smoking significantly increase ARI risk among under-five children [11–13]. Reported prevalence varies widely, from 2.7% in India to 40.3% in Uganda [14, 15]. This variation may reflect differences in study settings, environmental conditions, household fuel types, and methodological approaches. Factors such as biomass fuel use, lack of a separate kitchen, household smoking, use of cow dung fuel, proximity to stoves, and traditional cooking stoves have all been associated with increased ARI risk [2, 14, 16].

Although global public health measures have reduced IAP-related disease, the burden remains disproportionately high in resource-limited settings. Previous systematic reviews in this area have often been geographically restricted primarily focused on single-country contexts such as Ethiopia or have examined only selected sources of IAP, such as biomass fuel use alone [11, 12].

These reviews therefore do not capture the broader variation in household energy practices, housing conditions, and exposure contexts across LMICs more generally. More recent evidence from multiple LMIC settings has also emerged in the past decade, reflecting evolving household fuel use and clean energy transitions, yet this has not been comprehensively synthesized [17, 18].

There remains a clear need for a comprehensive, multi-country synthesis that examines the full spectrum of IAP exposures and their relationship with ARIs among under-five children across LMICs. Previous studies have produced fragmented and context-specific findings, limiting the ability to draw generalizable conclusions and to identify consistent determinants of risk. This review therefore aims to estimate the pooled effect size of IAP on ARIs and to identify key predictors of infection. Generating this evidence is essential for informing targeted public health strategies including the promotion of clean cooking fuels, improved household ventilation, and smoke-free home environments particularly in resource-limited settings where exposure is both high and preventable. Strengthening these interventions has the potential to substantially reduce the burden of respiratory infections and improve child health outcomes.

Methods and materials

Study setting and registration of protocol

This study was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [19] (Fig. 1). It focused on LMICs as defined by World Bank data for 2023–2024 [20]. The protocol for this review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the record ID CRD42024589101 with registered date 21 September 2024, available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42024589101.

Moreover, articles written in English, both published and unpublished, and studies reported from January 1, 2015, to September 1, 2024, were also the inclusion criteria for this study.

Exclusion Criteria: Studies that investigated other than the association of IAP and ARIs among under-five children in LMICs. Descriptive epidemiological studies (e.g., descriptive cross-sectional, case reports, and case series), studies without a full report after three personal email contacts with the primary and corresponding authors, systematic reviews, conference abstracts, letters to the editors, qualitative studies, short communications, and commentaries were not considered for this study.

Operational definition

We used the WHO definition of ARIs, which describes it as “an infection of the airways that may interfere with normal breathing, including both upper and lower respiratory tract infections.” ARIs are typically identified by symptoms such as cough and/or difficulty breathing in the absence of non-respiratory causes [21]. Subgroup analyses between upper and lower respiratory infections were not performed because the included studies generally did not report these outcomes separately.

Information sources

A systematic literature search was undertaken using databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar, and HINARI, and ScienceDirect. The search was conducted for studies published from January 1, 2015, to September 1, 2024. In addition to the electronic databases, grey literature was identified through direct Google searches and by screening the reference lists of eligible studies. For the Google searches, relevant search terms were applied, and the first few pages of results were screened by title and abstract to identify potentially eligible studies.

Search strategies

Comprehensive search terms were used to identify relevant articles. These include MeSH terms and keywords such as IAP, household air pollution, ARIs, respiratory health, under-five children, LMICs. Details of the full PubMed search strategy, including Boolean operators, MeSH terms, and filters, are provided in (Supplementary File 1). The search strategy was modified as appropriate for other databases and other sources.

Study screening and selection

All stages of the review were conducted independently by three researchers (BD, LB, and AKG), with any conflicts resolved through discussion and involvement of an additional researcher (GB). Titles and abstracts of all identified citations were uploaded into Zotero for management, and duplicates were removed. Screening of titles and abstracts was then performed based on the predefined eligibility criteria, followed by retrieval of the full texts of potentially eligible studies. Studies not meeting the inclusion criteria were excluded with reasons, which are presented in the PRISMA 2020 flowchart. The review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines, ensuring transparency and reproducibility of the study selection process [19].

Risk of bias (quality) assessment of the selected studies

The quality of selected eligible studies was evaluated using the Joana Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist [22]. The quality assessment was conducted independently by three reviewers (BD, LB, and ABK). In case of any discrepancies encountered during the quality assessment, they were managed through evidence-based discussions with the involvement of the other reviewer (GB). In our study, we used a > 50% threshold for inclusion to ensure that only studies meeting at least half of the quality criteria were included, aiming to balance inclusivity with methodological rigor.

Data extraction and management

During data extraction, the extraction team used common data extraction tools that include author information, study country/region, study design, statistical methods, study periods, sample size, cases, prevalence, and quality scores. The extracted data were organized in a table format (Table 1).

Statistical methods and data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). The metan command was used to estimate pooled effect sizes under a random-effects model to account for heterogeneity across studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values of 25–50%, 50–75%, and > 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [55]; Subgroup analyses were performed by study region and study period, and meta-regression was conducted using these variables to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. In addition, publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive of search process

This study used the updated PRISMA 2020 guideline to report the study selection process. A total of 13,404 records were identified from electronic databases and 65 additional records through direct Google searches and citation tracking. After removing 9,786 duplicates, 3,618 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 1,253 studies were selected for full-text retrieval, and 908 full-text articles were successfully obtained and assessed for eligibility. A total of 345 studies could not be retrieved due to inaccessibility or incomplete information. Following the eligibility assessment, 31 studies met the inclusion criteria. In addition, five studies were identified through manual searching, resulting in a total of 36 studies included in the final review (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the eligible studies

Although analytical cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies were eligible for inclusion, all studies that met the inclusion criteria and were included in this review employed an analytical cross-sectional design. The studies were conducted across multiple low- and middle-income countries, including Uganda, India, Ethiopia, Nigeria, Bangladesh, and Nepal, among others. Sample sizes varied substantially, ranging from fewer than 1,000 to over 300,000 under-five children, with most data derived from nationally representative surveys such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS). All included studies used logistic regression models for statistical analysis. In total, this meta-analysis encompassed 1,299,277 under-five children, with study periods spanning from 2000 to 2021. The highest prevalence of ARIs among under-five children was reported in Uganda (40.3%).

Meta-analysis

The estimated pooled effect of IAP exposure on ARIs among under-five children was found to be 13.56% (95% CI: 11.88–15.25), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.9%, p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 2). High heterogeneity indicates possible variation in study population, exposure assessment, and contextual factor across studies.

Forest plot showing the pooled effect of IAP exposure on ARIs among under-five children in LMICs, 2024

Subgroup analysis revealed that studies conducted in the Africa region reported a prevalence of 14.3% (95% CI: 10.86–17.74) with I2 = 99.9%, p-value < 0.001, which is slightly higher than the 12.68% (95% CI: 10.79–14.58) with I2 = 100%, p-value < 0.001 found in studies conducted in the Asia region (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis based on study country category for association of IAP exposure and ARIs among under-five children in LMICs, 2024

Further subgroup analysis indicated that studies conducted during the study period ≤ 2016 had a prevalence of 12.58% (95% CI: 10.65–14.51) with I2 = 99.9%, p-value < 0.001, while those conducted after 2016 showed a prevalence of 14.75% (95% CI: 9.36–20.13) with I2 = 99.9%, p-value < 0.001. Additionally, studies that did not report their study period showed a pooled prevalence of 17.48% (95% CI: 0.81–34.15%) with I2 = 99.2%, p-value < 0.001 (Fig. 4). Two of the included studies lacked information on the study period, which may have contributed to the variability observed in this subgroup analysis.

Subgroup analysis based on study period category for association of IAP exposure and ARIs among under-five children in LMICs, 2024

Following the subgroup analysis, a meta-regression analysis was conducted using the study region and study period as factors to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. The finding indicated that neither the study region nor the study period was a statistically significant source of heterogeneity (p-value = 0.35, 0.44, respectively). Additionally, the analysis of sensitivity analysis did not provide strong evidence for the presence of a single study effect (Supplementary File 2).

The funnel plot for the pooled analysis showed a visually asymmetrical distribution of the included studies, suggesting potential publication bias (Fig. 5). However, the Egger’s regression test indicated no statistically significant publication bias (p = 0.39). This non-significant result suggests that the observed asymmetry is unlikely due to publication bias and may instead reflect study heterogeneity.

Factors associated with ARIs among under-five children in LMICs

In this meta-analysis, several factors associated with ARIs among children under five were identified. These included child age, sex, household wealth status, birth order, use of traditional stoves, use of unclean cooking fuels, exposure to second-hand smoke, absence of a separate kitchen, presence of children near the stove during cooking, lack of a kitchen window, recent diarrhea, and stunting. The reported associations are derived from a pooled meta-analysis of adjusted odds ratios from the included primary studies. The risk of ARIs was found to increase by 2.53 times for children using traditional stoves compared to using improved stoves (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.02–4.03) (Fig. 6)..However, this subgroup analysis was based on only two studies, and therefore the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Exposure to secondhand smoking in the household increased the risk of ARIs by 1.19 times (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.32) (Fig. 7), while households using unclean fuel sources faced a 16% higher risk (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.25) than households using clean fuel sources (Fig. 8).

Children affected by diarrhea had a significantly higher risk of ARIs (OR = 3.76, 95% CI: 3.51–4.01) (Fig. 9), and stunted children also faced increased risk of ARIs (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.31) (Fig. 10).

The strongest predictor of ARIs was the absence of a kitchen window (OR = 4.47, 95% CI: 1.66–7.28), followed by being aged 6–11 months (OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.43–2.82) and 12–23 months (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.29–2.60). Discussion

Indoor air pollution continues to pose serious public health challenges in resource-limited settings, where dependence on biomass fuels and traditional cooking methods remains widespread. Acute respiratory infections are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among under-five children in LMICs [17, 56]. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled effect size of ARIs among under-five children associated with exposure to IAP was 13.56% (95% CI: 11.88–15.25). Although this estimate is lower than previous systematic reviews conducted in Ethiopia (17.7% [12] and 22% [11]), it is consistent with evidence showing that IAP contributes substantially to respiratory illness in young children globally [57]. Differences across studies likely reflect variations in study populations and contexts. Susceptibility to ARIs may also begin in utero, as maternal exposure to household air pollutants can impair fetal lung development, increasing vulnerability in early childhood. Overall, the findings highlight that ARIs remain a substantial disease burden among under-five children in low-resource settings [58], highlighting the need for targeted interventions such as transitioning to improved stoves and cleaner fuels.

In our metaanalysis the absence of a kitchen window (OR = 4.47, 95% CI: 1.66–7.28) emerged as the strongest predictor ARIs among underfive children. This finding aligns with broader evidence that poor household ventilation markedly increases indoor airpollution exposure and respiratory risk for children [59]. Improving kitchen ventilation through design changes (windows, separate cooking area) or behavior change (use of exhaust, opening windows) should be a priority for interventions aimed at reducing ARIs in young children, especially in settings where fuel use, stove emissions and other risk factors are prevalent.

Households using traditional stove types were 2.53 times more likely to have children affected by ARIs compared to those using improved stoves. Improved cookstoves have been shown to reduce IAP-related disease burden [60], and cleaner fuel use is generally associated with reduced ARI incidence, although effects may vary depending on housing conditions, ventilation, and urban–rural differences [61, 62]. However, it is important to note that this subgroup finding in our review was based on only two studies, which limits the strength and generalizability of the evidence. Moreover, the broader literature remains mixed, as some studies report no significant reduction in ARIs following adoption of improved biomass cookstoves [63, 64]. These inconsistencies likely reflect differences in stove design, fuel type, adoption and maintenance, and the extent to which households fully transition from traditional cooking practices. Notably, stove stacking where households continue using traditional stoves alongside improved ones is common in many LMIC settings, and most included studies did not account for this practice, which may dilute the observed benefits. The limited number of studies evaluating improved stove interventions, despite their widespread promotion, highlights the need for more rigorous, context-specific assessments to determine when and how these stoves effectively reduce IAP related ARI risk. Evidence-based, context-sensitive implementation strategies are therefore essential to ensure that improved cookstoves achieve sustained health benefits.

Children living in households using unclean fuels such as biomass were 1.16 times more likely to develop ARIs than those using clean fuels. This aligns with studies from LMICs demonstrating that reliance on solid biomass for cooking increases ARI risk (1.17 times) among under-five children [65], and other evidence showing a more pronounced effect of biomass fuel exposure (2.6 times higher ARI risk) [11]. These findings reflect socioeconomic disadvantage, where households with lower SES are more likely to use unclean fuels and reside in poorly ventilated homes, thereby increasing ARI vulnerability [66, 67]. Consistent with previous studies [12, 68], exposure to IAP from solid fuel use continues to contribute substantially to ARIs among children. In line with these findings, the WHO recommends reducing reliance on unclean fuels and promoting a shift toward cleaner household energy sources [69, 70].

This review also demonstrated that children exposed to household smoking were 1.19 times more likely to develop ARIs, which is consistent with evidence showing the harmful respiratory effects of secondhand smoke exposure [71, 72]. Tobacco smoke contains fine particulates and toxic chemicals that impair respiratory defenses and increase susceptibility to infection among young children.

Children who experienced diarrhea were 3.76 times more likely to develop ARIs, consistent with previous studies reporting co-occurrence between diarrheal illness and respiratory infections [73, 74]. Recurrent diarrheal episodes weaken immune defenses and contribute to micronutrient depletion, increasing vulnerability to respiratory pathogens in environments where poor sanitation and IAP exposure frequently coexist.

Similarly, stunting was positively associated with ARIs among under-five children, supporting findings from earlier research [75–77]. Stunting reflects chronic undernutrition, which compromises immune function and heightens susceptibility to infection. This underscores the interconnected biological pathways linking environmental exposures, nutrition, and child health outcomes.

Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of integrated strategies in LMICs that reduce exposure to IAP while simultaneously addressing child malnutrition and household lifestyle risks. Expanding access to clean energy technologies, improving home ventilation, discouraging indoor smoking, and strengthening nutrition and sanitation programs are essential for reducing ARI burden among vulnerable children.

Strength and limitation of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several strengths. The study followed PRISMA guidelines, and the methodological quality of included studies was rigorously assessed using the JBI criteria. It contributes novel and comprehensive evidence on the association between IAP and ARIs among under-five children in LMICs, synthesizing findings across multiple regions and contexts. The inclusion of subgroup and sensitivity analyses further strengthens the robustness and reliability of the pooled estimates.

However, this study also has limitations. Most of the included studies were cross-sectional, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships. There was heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of study settings, measurement of exposure, and outcome assessments. Variation in ARI definitions across studies may have contributed to differences in reported effect sizes. Additionally, restricting the search to English-language publications may have led to the exclusion of relevant evidence from non-English sources.

Conclusions

Indoor air pollution remains a major contributor to ARIs among under-five children in LMICs, largely due to continued reliance on solid fuels and traditional cooking methods. Addressing this challenge requires coordinated policy action and practical implementation at household and community levels. Promoting access to clean cooking technologies, improving ventilation and housing conditions, and discouraging indoor smoking are key strategies. Strengthening national adoption and enforcement of WHO household air quality guidelines and integrating IAP reduction measures into existing maternal, child health, and nutrition programs will be essential for sustained impact.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies and intervention trials to assess the effectiveness and uptake of clean cooking interventions in real-world settings. Studies that employ direct and standardized measurements of IAP exposure are also needed to improve accuracy, comparability, and evidence for policy and practice.
