The State of Neurovascular Surgery in Nigeria: Past, Present, and Future

ABSTRACT
This narrative review elaborates on the development of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria, highlighting the growth over the years. It also elucidates the severe shortage of specialists, the financial burden to patients who may require neurovascular services, and the limited diagnostic and therapeutic equipment. It highlights the critical need for specialized training programs, workforce expansion, infrastructural improvements, sustainable financing, and financial reforms. The authors reviewed the literature narratively and examined, in broad strokes, the historical background of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria, the current practices, outcomes, and training and gave recommendations. Search keywords used include neurovascular surgery in Nigeria, history of neurosurgery in Nigeria, endovascular facilities in Nigeria, and advances in neuroimaging in Nigeria in various combinations using Boolean operators. Searches were conducted in a nonsystematic manner through Public/Publisher MEDLINE, SCOPUS, and African Journal Online. The field of neurovascular surgery experienced significant growth, driven by the contributions of Dr E. L. Odeku in the 1960s and 1970s. However, this progress was hindered by economic and political instability between the 1980s and 2001s. Since the resurgence era began in 2002, substantial advancements have been made. However, the challenges being faced are numerous, and development is quite slow. A multidisciplinary approach is crucial to improving neurovascular care in Nigeria and other low- and middle-income countries, and addressing these deficiencies is essential for optimizing patient outcomes and advancing neurovascular care. This study highlights the state of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria, elucidates the challenges of this practice, and offers recommendations for improvement.

Neurovascular surgery in Nigeria has evolved significantly since its inception 6 decades ago, marked by notable milestones and challenges. Its inception can be traced to Dr E. L. Odeku, a Nigerian-born, board-certified neurosurgeon trained at the University of Michigan, USA,1 who established the first neurosurgical practice in West Africa and published 8 papers on neurovascular anomalies.1

Over the years, the discipline has experienced varying periods of growth, stagnation, and resurgence. The initial phase, termed the pioneering era (1962-1981), began after the establishment of the discipline in the country and involved the initiation of training programs geared toward cementing neurosurgery as a core surgical field in Nigeria. A period of stagnation followed, precipitated by economic and political challenges, aptly called the recession era (1982-2001). Subsequently, the progress picked up again after the military regime, resulting in growth characterized by an influx of specialists, increased training opportunities, and infrastructural development. This era is known as the resurgence era (2002-2022).2

Neurovascular surgery in Nigeria is on a steady rise; however, it is marred by critical workforce and infrastructural challenges. Current estimates show that there are about 138 neurosurgeons for over 200 million people living under Nigerian jurisdiction, which gives a neurosurgeon:patient ratio of under 1:1.4 million people.3 Patient care is further constrained by an insufficient neurosurgical workforce, a scarcity of neurovascular specialists, and the absence of adequate endovascular therapeutic facilities.4

This review seeks to discuss the evolution and present state of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria by tracing the historical development of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria (Table 1),4 exploring various aspects of neurovascular surgical practice available in Nigeria and the outcome, explore the various challenges to the development of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria, and propose recommendations on the future of the practice.

METHODS

This review was conducted using a narrative, nonsystematic approach. The authors explored the historical background of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria, current practices, outcomes, and training pathways and provided recommendations for future development.

A literature search was performed using the databases Public/Publisher MEDLINE, Scopus, and African Journals Online. Search terms included “neurovascular surgery in Nigeria,” “history of neurosurgery in Nigeria,” “endovascular facilities in Nigeria,” and “advances in neuroimaging in Nigeria.” These keywords were combined using Boolean operators in various permutations. The search strategy was nonsystematic and aimed to identify relevant publications that provide insight into the evolution and current state of neurovascular surgery in Nigeria.

Historical Background

Before 1960, the practice of neurosurgery in Nigeria was confined to the occasional treatment of head injury and the removal of superficial lesions of the craniospinal axis,7 an example of such was a reported case of surgical management of compound skull fracture in Kano, with an attempt at cranioplasty by Dr H. M. Soar8 who was a general practitioner, and the patient had a good outcome.

The opening of University College Ibadan marked a significant milestone in neurosurgery development in Nigeria as it ushered in the creation of the faculty of medicine and the influx of general surgeons who practiced some degree of neurosurgery, but difficult cases were flown out or invariably ended fatally.9 For instance, in 1952, a neurosurgeon had to be flown from the Middlesex Hospital in London to treat a senior member of staff with severe brain injury who could not travel to England for treatment.7

In 1955, a consultant trauma surgeon, Mr. R. L. Batten, treated neurotrauma cases and Peter Konstam, who came to light for the treatment of spinal tuberculosis with neurological deficits, both at University College Hospital, Ibadan.7

Pioneering (1962-1981)

Before the return of Dr E. Latunde Odeku from the United States to Nigeria in 1962, there was no specialist neurosurgical care in the western part of the continent. Dr Odeku returned to the University of Ibadan, and that ushered in the pioneering period of neurosurgery. Five years later, in 1967, Dr Adelola Adeloye joined him. In 1974, he was also joined by Dr Adebayo Ajayi Olumide. A second service was started in Lagos by Drs De Silva and Nosiru Ojikutu, and in 1968, a third service was started by Dr Samuel Ohaegbuam in Enugu.10

This period showed a steady increase in workforce capacity and service delivery and witnessed the publication of 11 papers in neurovascular pathology and hence the term “pioneering”.4 This era came to an end in the early to mid-1980s because of subsequent economic and social turmoil that resulted from the military rule and political instability.

Recession (1982-2001)

Before the end of 1980, the number of neurosurgeons in the country grew to 8 after they returned to the country after training in the diaspora.5 However, setting up a complex surgical field in a country that had just gained independence came with many socioeconomic challenges, which led to some leaving the country. By 1990, only 5 neurosurgeons remained.5

By the year 2000, there were 10 neurosurgeons in a country with a population of more than 120 million.5 Over these 20 years, only 3 scientific papers on neurovascular pathology were published in Nigeria.4

Resurgence (2002-2024)

In the early 2000s, the neurosurgical specialty workforce began to witness a significant increase.5 After the military regime, many foreign-trained neurosurgeons returned to the country and established neurosurgical facilities and training in the country. International partnerships also helped to provide advanced diagnostic and therapeutic infrastructure and opportunities for training.

To promote neurosurgery education and practice, the Nigerian Academy of Neurological Surgeons was established in 2006 and the Continental Association of African Neurosurgery Societies was established in 2012. These establishments unified the surgeons and have brought significant advancements in the field through collaborations in training, conferences, the establishment of neurovascular subcommittees, etc.11

Furthermore, in the year 2020, there were 108 neurosurgeons meant to serve 206 million people in Nigeria and 132 by 2023. However, by 2030, there will be 329 neurosurgeons if there is an annual exponential growth rate of 11.8%; this is still low when one considers the ideal ratio of 1:100 000, highlighting the urgent need for continued efforts to expand the neurosurgical workforce in Nigeria.11 These epochs, the notable developments, and their challenges are summarized in Table 1.

Current Status

Diagnostics

Neuroimaging is invaluable when it comes to the diagnosis of neurovascular pathologies. However, in low middle income countries settings, the availability of these diagnostic facilities presents an obstacle to most surgeons. A study reported x-ray/carotid angiography to be the most used diagnostic equipment.4 The advent of computed tomography (CT) in 1987 in the country has changed the narrative of the diagnosis of neurovascular pathologies. MRI is still not widely available, and even when available, it presents financial constraints because most of the population cannot afford it.4

The most prevalent primary radiological findings were those of ischemic cerebrovascular disease, intracranial hematoma, and subarachnoid hemorrhage. The findings of arterial aneurysms have increased from the pioneering period to the resurgence period.4 Ojo et al11 reported treating 45 patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage in Lagos for over 10 years after the diagnosis was made by CT angiography.

Treatment

The treatment landscape of neurovascular conditions in Nigeria is gradually evolving, with a wide range of treatment options now being made available to patients with various conditions. These include microvascular and endovascular procedures. Others include emergency procedures like external ventricular drain placement, clot evacuation, and decompressive craniectomies.

Microvascular Neurosurgery in Nigeria

Microvascular techniques are currently being used to manage various conditions such as aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), and cavernomas Surgical clipping remains a primary treatment modality for intracranial aneurysms. There are, however, limited data available on the specific prevalence of aneurysms in Nigeria. A few studies have demonstrated the utilization and outcomes of microvascular clipping in managing aneurysms in Nigeria. These services, however, remain mostly confined to the urban areas. Ojo et al11 over 10 years used microvascular clipping in the management of 45 patients with ruptured cerebral aneurysms, with an 84.4% survival rate. A similar study by Idowu et al12 on 29 patients with cerebral aneurysms also showed an 82.8% survival rate.

Endovascular Neurosurgery in Nigeria

Dokponou et al13 conducted a survey that revealed only 8 centers in Africa offered endovascular treatment for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Endovascular neurosurgery is a newly emerging field in Nigeria as only about 3 centers currently offer this service. In addition, only a few interventional radiologists like Dr Ayokunle Ogungbemi and Hammed Ninalowo are known to offer this service in the country.14 At the time of this publication, endovascular neurosurgery is not a component of the training syllabus for neurosurgeons in Nigeria.

Stroke Care in Nigeria

Cerebrovascular disease is particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, accounting for 80% of stroke-related deaths in this region.15-17 A national survey on stroke in Nigeria revealed a national prevalence of 14.6 per 1000 individuals.18 Osuntokun et al18 in Ibadan between 1973 and 1975 reported a crude incidence of stroke among Nigerians of 58/100 000, whereas in 2007, Danesi et al19 in Lagos reported an incidence of 114/100 000.

Surgical management is an important option for cerebrovascular accident care, treatment, and prevention of stroke or its reoccurrence. These procedures include craniotomy and clot evacuations, hemispheric decompression, carotid endarterectomy for embolic events, micro- and endovascular surgery for aneurysms, AVMs, and mechanical thrombectomy for ischemic stroke.20,21

Management of ischemic stroke in Nigeria, using mechanical thrombectomy and or thrombolysis, is quite difficult because of the short window of treatment, 6 to 24 hours,22 which is affected by delays in decision, transport, out-of-pocket funding, delays in imaging, and lack of access to mechanical thrombectomy facilities.20 Arabambi et al23 revealed that there were only 5 dedicated stroke units in only 4 of the 6 geopolitical zones in the country as of 2022: 2 in the southwest and one each in the south-south, the southeast, and the north-central.

Available surgical options for stroke care in Nigeria include external ventricular drain insertion for hydrocephalus complicating intraventricular stroke extension, clot evacuation, decompressive craniectomy, aneurysm clipping and coiling, and carotid endarterectomy. Uguwanyi et al24 conducted a retrospective study from January to December 2016 and found that of the 15 patients who had hemorrhagic stroke with intraventricular extension who met the inclusion criteria, 5 had aneurysms, 4 of whom underwent clipping, one AVM had endovascular embolization, and one with intracranial hemorrhage had surgical evacuation. Based on the above, the relative lack of expertise, diagnostic delays, and lack of adequate resources have limited the development of the surgical/endovascular aspect of stroke care in Nigeria.

Postoperative Care and Outcomes of Neurovascular Surgery in Nigeria

A study by Ogunbiyi et al25 revealed that there were only 30 intensive care units (ICUs) in Nigeria; of these, 60% was in the urban areas, with an average bed space of 4 to 5 per unit. There were 26 general ICUs and no specialized neurosurgical ICU.

Physiotherapy

Physiotherapy is of paramount importance and, in this context, involves the rehabilitation of individuals with neurovascular disease who have had deficits from stroke or surgery. Hence, the field of neurological physiotherapy started to evolve around 20 years ago.26

In Nigeria, there is a scarcity of robust epidemiological data, an absence of standardized clinical and policy guidelines, an insufficient neurological rehabilitation workforce, and limited training opportunities for existing rehabilitation professionals.27 Despite all this, rates of physiotherapy referrals after stroke care are high, at nearly 80%, as documented by Olaleye et al.28 Patients are also aware of the benefits of physiotherapy as nearly 65% followed up on their referrals. Vincent-Onabajo et al29 found that patients most often preferred outpatient facilities or home-based rehabilitation. Taking into account the need for standardization of clinical practice and strengthening the practice of physiotherapy, results in improved patient well being and, ultimately, their outcome post surgery/stroke. Physiotherapy in Nigeria has room to continue to evolve, but currently provides great benefits to the patient population.

Outcome

A pooled analysis of studies published on neurovascular pathology in Nigeria from 1962 to 2022 by Nischal et al4 revealed that the average duration of follow-up, reported in 17 patients, was 18 ± 27 months. Of the 3203 patients who were included in the study, 48% had full recovery, 42% had partial recovery, and 10% had no improvement or deterioration. The overall mortality rate was 6%, and 2% had complications postoperatively, notably rebleeds and chest infection. The study by Ugwuanyi et al24 revealed a mortality rate of 53.3% for patients who had hemorrhagic stroke with intraventricular extension.

Neurovascular Training in Nigeria

Although there are a total of 15 neurosurgery training centers with accreditation for the West African College of Surgeons or the National Postgraduate Medical College of Nigeria or both, and one center that offers postfellowship training in peripheral nerve surgery,30 there is no specific center that offers fellowship training specifically in neurovascular surgery. However, as part of the basic training in neurosurgery, Nigerian trainees are exposed to diagnosing and treating common neurovascular conditions. In a survey of the status of neurosurgical training in Nigeria, conducted in 2022, Ukachukwu et al30 revealed that only 34 respondents (22.8%) underwent additional training in neurosurgical subspecialties, 5 of whom were in neurovascular surgery.

Workforce and Facilities

The neurosurgeon-to-patient ratio in Nigeria is critically low, reflecting a significant disparity between the available neurosurgical workforce and the population's healthcare needs. As of 2023, Nigeria had approximately 108 neurosurgeons serving a population exceeding 200 million, resulting in a ratio of about one neurosurgeon per 1.85 million people.31 There are very few neurosurgical centers equipped with the necessary facilities, and therefore, a lot of cases are being handled conservatively, by internal medicine specialists without collaborating with a neurosurgeon.12

There remains a deficiency in the availability of highly specialized medical professionals in attending to the complexities of neurosurgical care in Nigeria. The need for subspecialized doctors, nurses, and physiotherapists, among others, cannot be overemphasized.

Diagnostic Challenges

Neurodiagnostic resources are still limited in availability and, where available, inaccessible, mostly because of financial constraints, thereby posing a significant hindrance to neurosurgical practice.4 Advanced radiodiagnostic equipment, like the CT and MRI scans, are expensive and widely unavailable. Although the installation of CT scanners in the country is on the rise, the total number of CT machines in Nigeria as of 2021 was 209, unequally distributed across the 6 geopolitical zones of the country, a ratio of 1 per 1 000 000 people.32

In 2023, an MRI machine cost about $225 000 to $500 000, whereas the cost per session of a scan was about 80 000 to 150 000 naira (approximately $100).33 Most private healthcare facilities and even government institutions are reluctant to make such purchases as the return on investment is considerably low. In addition, such machines require high maintenance and specialized technicians, which are broadly unavailable.34

Stroke Care Challenges

In 2012, the cost of treating stroke in Nigeria ranged from $600 to $4860, which was already considered unaffordable.22 When adjusted for current inflation rates, these costs have risen significantly. In addition, the lack of rehabilitative care services has led to an increased burden of diseases, as reflected in the growing disability-adjusted life years.22 As with the case of an aneurysmal rupture, less than 30% of individuals get surgical intervention after an initial rupture.13 This can be a result of a lack of finance for investigations, delayed referral, and access to the theater. In this category of patients, there is a great risk of a rebleed and other complications if appropriate medical treatment is not received.13

There is also a lack of proper ambulance service in many states throughout Nigeria. But even in the few that have, the service is costly for most of the population. Furthermore, because of poverty and poor facilities, many diagnoses are never made because of the challenges of investigation.12 Proposals to incorporate neurosurgical care into the national health insurance schemes and explore innovative funding options are yet to be proven within Nigeria's limited resource environment.35

Recommendations

Despite the favorable achievements in the discipline in Nigeria, there is still a long way to go. Advancing neurovascular surgery requires a multifaceted approach, which can be stratified into workforce development, infrastructure enhancement, policy integration, financial strategies, and public awareness.

Neurovascular Workforce Development

Expansion of specialist training programs will increase the number of residency positions to address the neurosurgeon-to-patient ratio disparity and creation of internship opportunities for medical students will onboard passionate aspiring doctors to the discipline.36 In addition, there is a need for the creation of specialized training centers in neurovascular surgery to equip neurosurgeons with the necessary skills.36 International collaborations need to be encouraged and foster partnerships with global neurosurgical centers to allow for mentorship, knowledge exchange, and capacity-building initiatives (Table 2).37

Infrastructure Enhancement

Upgrade of pre-existing facilities is necessary as the state of medical technology in the country is deplorable and most of the public primary health centers cannot comply with the minimum quality standards.38 To bolster the field in the country and ensure positive outcomes, there must be both improvement and modernization of the already existing healthcare infrastructure in Nigeria, particularly the acquisition of surgical equipment and imaging, like CT.

Establishment of specialized centers is necessary to resolve the uneven provision of neurovascular services across the country.38 This will increase training centers and improve the neurosurgeon-to-patient ratio.

Policy Integration

The incorporation of neurovascular care into national health policies, prioritization of resource allocation, infrastructural development, and workforce planning in the field by embedding the discipline within the nation's health agenda, along with the provision of subsidies for neurovascular surgeries, enabling patients to get the required care as at when due is essential.35

Development of national guidelines and establishment of standardized protocols for the management of neurovascular conditions are necessary to ensure uniformity and quality care.

Financial Strategies

Sustainable funding and health insurance models should be developed, including partnerships, government funding, and international grants, to help finance services and infrastructure, making neurovascular care more affordable and accessible to the general population.

Prevention of Neurovascular Pathology

Nationwide awareness initiatives and collaboration with local communities are needed to educate them on healthy lifestyles and provide free screening programs to reduce the incidence of neurovascular conditions. There is a gradual trend toward an increasing risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension in Nigeria compared with western populations, resulting in a rising incidence of cerebrovascular accidents.17,18,39 Hence, health promotion activities should also be focused on the prevention of modifiable risk factors like excessive alcohol intake, smoking, treatment of hypertension, and obesity.39,40

These recommendations require coordination between the government, private individuals, healthcare professionals, the international community, and the local community. Such collaborative effort is vital to place neurovascular surgery in Nigeria on a better trajectory, improving patient outcomes and reducing the burden of cerebrovascular diseases.

CONCLUSION

Neurovascular surgery in Nigeria has progressed through pioneering, recession, and resurgence phases over the past 6 decades. Nigeria faces challenges in neurovascular care, including a shortage of specialists, limited facilities, and financial barriers. While microvascular neurosurgery is gradually developing, endovascular services and stroke care remain constrained.

To improve outcomes, Nigeria must expand specialized training, invest in modern technology, establish dedicated neurovascular units, and develop sustainable healthcare financing.
