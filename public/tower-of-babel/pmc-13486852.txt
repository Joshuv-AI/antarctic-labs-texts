A triangulation and neural network approach reveals the microbe-metabolite-sensory associations of fermented milk
Authors: Youming Tan, Tong Wu, Haojie Ni, Baosong Wang, Zihao Liu, Hong Zeng, Yanbo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the causal associations among microorganisms, flavor metabolites, and sensory attributes is essential for the precise regulation of the flavor of fermented milk. In this study, 22 commercial fermented milks were characterized using sensory evaluation, microbial analysis, and volatile metabolite profiling. Five sensory phenotypes were developed, each exhibiting unique flavor profiles. However, microbial community structures differed only slightly among phenotypes, suggesting that the community structure itself could not fully explain sensory diversification. Therefore, generalized microbe-phenotype triangulation analysis was performed and identified multiple causal taxa associated with different sensory phenotypes, while the mmvec model further revealed strong associations between certain microorganisms and characteristic aroma compounds. Overall, this study established a novel data-driven framework for exploring potential causal associations among microorganisms, flavor metabolites, and sensory phenotypes in fermented milks, representing a preliminary effort to move beyond conventional correlation-based analyses.

Introduction

Flavor is a key determinant of consumer preference and market acceptance (Alam, Prete, Faieta, et al., 2025). The diversity and complexity of flavor in plain fermented milk are primarily shaped by microbial metabolic activities, among which volatile compounds constitute the main chemical basis of flavor (Cheng, 2010). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) convert substrates such as lactose, proteins, and milkfat into flavor compounds or their precursors through metabolic pathways including glycolysis, proteolysis, and lipolysis, generating typical aroma-active compounds such as diacetyl and acetoin (Chen et al., 2017). In practice, fermented milk is usually produced through the co-fermentation of multiple LAB, where complex interactions occur among different strains and their metabolic activities mutually influence each other, further increasing the complexity of flavor formation. Besides, different LAB can produce different metabolites due to variations in their metabolic pathways, thereby contributing to the diversity of volatile compound composition and ultimately resulting in diverse sensory phenotype of plain fermented milks (Bai et al., 2025).

Previous studies have explored the diversity of sensory classification in fermented milk, particularly in yogurt. For instance, plain yogurts from supermarkets in the United States have been reported to exhibit multiple sensory categories (Brown and Chambers, 2015). Products fermented with different strains of Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus (Lb. bulgaricus) or Streptococcus thermophilus show distinct sensory and aroma categories (Liu, Liu, et al., 2022; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2022). With the advancement of analytical techniques, the characterization of volatile flavor metabolites in fermented milk has become more comprehensive and of higher resolution. However, identifying the key microorganisms responsible for specific sensory phenotypes and tracing the microbial origins of flavor compounds within such complex systems remain major challenges. Addressing these challenges is essential for the precise regulation and personalized design of fermented milk flavor, yet related studies are still relatively limited. For example, Suh et al. investigated microbes associated with different sensory categories in commercial yogurts from Korea, although no quantitative association analysis was performed (Suh and Kim, 2021). Liu et al. employed multivariate statistical methods to identify differential volatile compounds across sensory types of fermented milk (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2022). These correlation-based or simple statistical methods may not be suitable for compositional datasets such as microbiome and mass spectrometry dataset (Morton et al., 2019; Weiss et al., 2016).

The application of artificial intelligence in food science has provided new opportunities to unravel complex relationships within food systems, which has been used for sensory prediction (Wang J. et al., 2026), identification of key flavor compounds (Zhou et al., 2026), geographical origin identification (Xian et al., 2026), and discovery of driving factors in food matrices (Tan et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). But most existing studies primarily focus on correlation mining or predictive performance, with limited attention to causal relationships among variables. Recently, a triangulation computational framework was proposed to pinpoint causality by comparing recurring differential microorganisms across multiple phenotypes (Ke et al., 2023; Surana and Kasper, 2017). Inspired by this method, we aimed to identify causal microorganisms in fermented milk with different phenotypes by evaluating pairwise differential abundance comparisons across multiple predefined sensory phenotypes. In parallel, a neural network-based approaches called microbe-metabolite vectors (mmvec) enable the modeling of microbe-metabolite relationships by learning co-occurrence probabilities between microbes and metabolites in compositional datasets (Morton et al., 2019). It has shown superior performance than traditional statistical methods (Morton et al., 2019). Therefore, integrating triangulation-based causal inference with neural network modeling is a powerful approach for systematically elucidating the microbe-metabolite-sensory relationships in fermented milk.

In this study, commercially available fermented milks were firstly categorized into different sensory phenotype through sensory evaluation and was analyzed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography‒time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC × GC-TOFMS). Triangulation analysis and mmvec model were subsequently integrated to identify putative causal microorganisms associated with sensory phenotypes and to construct microbe-volatile associations. This study provides new insights into the microbial mechanisms underlying flavor diversification in commercial plain fermented milks and may provide an analysis framework for other fermented food systems.

Materials and methods

Samples and reagents

Twenty-two commercial plain fermented milk samples were purchased on the same day via e-commerce platforms to ensure similar production dates. Due to the relatively short shelf life of refrigerated products, imported plain fermented milks are limited in the market; therefore, only one product from Australia was included in this study (Table S1). Upon receipt, all samples were stored at 4 °C and subjected to sensory evaluation within four days under identical storage conditions. The remaining yogurt samples were subsequently stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Sodium chloride (>99.5%, analytical grade) was purchased from Fuchen Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). 2-Methyl-3-heptanone (>95% GC purity) was purchased from FUSION (Beijing Jianqiang Weiye Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). A C7-C40 n-alkane standard solution (500 μg/mL in hexane) was obtained from Alta Scientific Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Dichloromethane (analytical grade) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sensory evaluation

The sensory profiles of 22 fermented milk samples were obtained through quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA). Eleven trained panelists (5 males and 6 females, aged 20-24 years) were recruited from the School of Food and Health, Beijing Technology and Business University. All panelists had at least two years of experience in sensory evaluation and were absence of known food allergies. Odor descriptors were established through panel discussion and further refined based on previous studies by Qiu et al. (Qiu et al., 2024), Suh et al. (Suh and Kim, 2021), Liu et al. (Liu, Liu, et al., 2022), and Brown et al. (Brown and Chambers, 2015). Prior to formal evaluation, panelists underwent a one-week training using reference standards of odor descriptor (Table S2). The recruitment, selection, and training of panelists strictly followed the guidelines of ISO 8586:2023. The sensory evaluation protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Technology and Business University (Approval No. 2025-237), and informed consent was obtained from each panelist prior to the study.

Sensory evaluation was conducted in a standardized sensory evaluation room. Each panelist performed the evaluation individually in booths illuminated with white light. Approximately 10 g of fermented milk sample was placed in transparent cups labeled with randomized three-digit codes and presented in a randomized order. Panelists were asked to rate the odor intensity of each sample. A 10-point scale was used and detailed sensory evaluation criteria were provided in Table S2. Purified water and unsalted crackers were provided for palate cleansing, and coffee powder was supplied to help alleviate olfactory fatigue. Samples were evaluated in groups of three, with a 2 min interval between groups to minimize sensory fatigue. Each fermented milk sample was evaluated in triplicate.

Determination of microbial community

Microbial DNA from fermented milk samples was extracted using the BayBiopure Magnetic Soil DNA Kit (Guangzhou BayBio Bio-tech Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using a PCR system (GeneAmp 9700, ABI, USA) with the forward primer 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAG-3′) and reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel, purified using a PCR Clean-up Kit (Shanghai Majorbio Yuhua Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., China). The purified amplicons were sequenced by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA) using a PE300 strategy. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.19.6) and merged using FLASH (v1.2.7). The merged sequences were then denoised using the DADA2 plugin within the QIIME2 pipeline (Bolyen et al., 2019) to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), and sequences annotated as chloroplasts or mitochondria were removed. To minimize the impact of sequencing depth variation, the number of sequences per sample was rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth across all samples. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using the RDP Classifier against the SILVA 16S rRNA database (v138.2) with a confidence threshold of 0.7. Analyses of α-diversity, β-diversity, and related metrics were conducted on the Majorbio Cloud platform (Han C. et al., 2024).

Volatile flavor metabolites analysis through GC × GC-TOFMS

Headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME)

Volatile flavor compounds in fermented milk samples were extracted via HS-SPME according to the method of Qiu et al. (2024) with slight modifications. Specifically, 5 g of yogurt sample, 0.5 g of sodium chloride, and 2 μL of 2-methyl-3-heptanone as an internal standard (0.0816 μg/μL in dichloromethane) were added into a 20 mL headspace vial sealed with a silicone septum. The vial was equilibrated at 45 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, a SPME fiber (50/30 μm, DVB/PDMS/Carbon WR, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) was inserted into the headspace for 30 min to extract volatile compounds. After extraction, the fiber was inserted into the injection port and thermally desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min.

GC × GC-TOFMS analysis

Extracted volatile flavor metabolites were separated and analyzed using a GC × GC-TOFMS system equipped with a two-stage four-jet thermal modulator (Pegasus BTX4D, LECO Corp., St. Joseph, MI, USA). High-purity helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Samples were introduced in split mode with a split ratio of 5:1. The GC × GC system used two capillary columns of differing polarity: Stabilwax polar capillary column (30 m ✕ 0.25 mm i.d. ✕ 0.25 μm; Restek, PA, USA) and Rxi-17Sil MS mid-polar capillary column (2 m ✕ 0.18 mm i.d. ✕ 0.18 μm; Restek, PA, USA). The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 40 °C held for 5 min, ramped at 5 °C/min to 240 °C, and held for 5 min. The modulation period was set to 4 s. The secondary oven and modulator temperatures were maintained at 5 °C and 15 °C higher than the primary oven temperature, respectively. The transfer line temperature was set at 250 °C. The TOFMS detector acquired data at a rate of 200 spectra/s over a mass range of 35-450 m/z. The ion source temperature was set to 250 °C, with an electron ionization energy of 70 eV. The solvent delay time was set to 1 min.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis

Raw data were processed using LECO ChromaTOF™ software for automated peak detection and deconvolution. Compound identification was achieved by comparison with the NIST 2023 database in combination with retention index (RI) matching against reference standards. Only compounds with a spectral similarity score higher than 700 and a RI deviation within ± 50 were considered positively identified. The RIs of the compounds were calculated based on the retention times of n-alkanes. Quantitative analysis of flavor metabolites was performed using an internal standard method.

Computational analysis of microbe-metabolite-sensory associations

Generalized microbe-phenotype triangulation (GMPT) analysis

This analysis was performed according to the method developed by Ke et al. (2023). Firstly, all samples were regrouped according to the sensory attribute scores of the examined phenotype, and differential abundance analysis (DAA) was performed for every pairwise group comparison. DAA was conducted using the R package ALDEx2 (v.1.4.3) (Fernandes et al., 2014). This method performs Monte Carlo sampling from the Dirichlet distribution and identifies differential features in compositional datasets based on centered log-ratio (CLR) transformed posterior distributions data. The number of Monte Carlo instances was set to 200, while the remaining parameters were kept at default settings. Differential features were evaluated using effect size, which has been reported to exhibit better reproducibility than p-values (Ke et al., 2023). Taxa with an absolute effect size greater than 0.5 were selected, and Spearman correlation coefficients between the relative abundance of taxa and phenotype scores were subsequently calculated to infer their potential roles. Phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA (v. 12.1) (Kumar et al., 2024) with maximum likelihood method and visualized in iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/). All GMPT-related analyses were performed in R (v. 4.5.2). Sample grouping strategy and phenotype scores can be found in supplementary material and Table S7, respectively.

Microbe-metabolite vectors (mmvec) neural network model

Microbial and metabolite tables were matched according to sample order and converted into BIOM format using the biomformat (v. 1.38.3) R package as input for mmvec. mmvec is a shallow neural network model that infers microbe-metabolite associations by learning the conditional probability distribution of metabolites given microbial features. Model parameters were optimized through cross-validation, with latent dimension, epochs, and batch size set to 2, 2000, and 50, respectively. Analyses were performed using the mmvec package (v. 1.0.5) (Morton et al., 2019). Model outputs included microbial and metabolite embeddings, dimensionality reduction coordinates of conditional probabilities, and a rank matrix representing CLR-transformed conditional probabilities distribution (https://github.com/beichuanheqi/MMS-of-fermented-milk).

Statistical analysis

The PCA score plot in Fig. 1 was generated using SIMCA software (v.14.1, Umetrics, Malmö, Sweden). Heatmap in Fig. 1 were constructed using Python. Lollipop plots and bar charts in Fig. 2 were generated using R. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was performed using the scipy package (v.1.16.3) based on Euclidean distance with Ward's linkage method. Sensory scores were standardized prior to clustering. K-means clustering was conducted using the KMeans algorithm implemented in the scikit-learn package (v. 1.7.2). Results

Sensory evaluation of different commercial fermented milks

Commercial fermented milks were selected from products available on e-commerce platforms, covering plain fermented milks from major brands in the Chinese market. The selection criteria considered both product sales volume and the diversity of starter cultures used in fermentation. Based on our previous studies of yogurt aroma type (Qiu et al., 2024) and related literature (Coggins et al., 2008; Liu, Liu, et al., 2022; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2022; Suh and Kim, 2021), eight odor descriptors for fermented milk were selected, including fermented, cheesy, milky, creamy, buttery, fruity, sweet, and sour.

According to the results, the samples were classified into five sensory phenotypes, namely fermented (FER), cheesy (CHE), milky (MIL), fruity (FRU), and miscellaneous (also referred to as balanced, BAN). The classification was based on the dominant sensory attribute of each sample, whereby a sample was assigned to the phenotype corresponding to its highest attribute score. Specifically, fermented and sour attributes were grouped into the FER phenotype, fruity and sweet into the FRU phenotype, and milky, creamy, and buttery into the MIL phenotype, while the BAN phenotype was defined for samples with comparable scores across multiple attributes. This classification scheme is supported by previous studies. Xu et al. reported that sour, fermented, and grassy notes clustered into the same group based on sensory data of fermented milk (Xu et al., 2025). Qiu et al. suggested that milky, creamy, and buttery attributes represent different intensity levels of milky aroma (Qiu et al., 2024), while Zhao et al. demonstrated that no significant differences were observed between creamy and milky attributes (Zhao et al., 2023). Consistently, in the present study, most samples did not show significant differences among these attributes (Table S3).

The sensory profiles of different sensory phenotypes were illustrated by the radar charts (Fig. 1A), showing that FER and MIL samples exhibited dominant fermented and milky attributes, respectively. CHE samples were characterized by cheesy attributes, although fermented attributes were also relatively high. No significant differences were observed between these two attributes within CHE samples (Table S3), suggesting that panelists had limited ability to clearly distinguish between fermented and cheesy notes, or that the subtle differences between these attributes affected sensory perception. The two BAN samples exhibited relatively similar scores across multiple sensory attributes (Fig. 1A–S19, S20), indicating that their sensory characteristics were intermediate among the other phenotypes. This is further supported by the distribution of BAN samples (yellow points) in the PCA biplot (Fig. 1B). The PCA biplot clearly illustrates the relationships between samples and sensory attributes, with samples from each phenotype clustering around their corresponding dominant attributes. For example, FER samples were distributed between sour and fermented attributes, MIL samples clustered with milky, creamy, and buttery descriptors, and CHE samples were located near the cheesy attribute. Furthermore, unsupervised clustering analyses provided additional support for the sensory-based classification. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) revealed that the fermented milk samples were mainly grouped into four clusters based on their sensory scores (Fig. S1A). MIL samples were assigned to the yellow cluster, whereas FRU samples formed a distinct green cluster. The red cluster mainly consisted of FER samples, while four CHE samples were clustered within the purple cluster. Notably, the two BAN samples (S19 and S20) were not separated into an independent cluster, which was also observed in K-means clustering (Fig. S1B). In the K-means analysis, BAN and FRU samples were assigned to the same cluster (Cluster 2). This clustering pattern may be attributed to the intermediate sensory characteristics of BAN samples, which showed relatively similar scores across multiple attributes, resulting in their proximity to other sensory phenotypes during distance-based clustering. K-means clustering showed good separation of most sensory phenotypes: Cluster 1 exclusively contained MIL samples, Cluster 3 consisted mainly of CHE samples, while FER samples were distributed into two clusters (Clusters 4 and 5). Together, these unsupervised clustering approaches supported the presence of distinct sensory patterns among fermented milk samples and provided further validation for the sensory phenotype classification.

The correlations among sensory attributes were further evaluated using a heatmap (Fig. 1C). The main attributes used for classification, including fermented, cheesy, and milky, exhibited low correlations with each other, indicating their relative independence and supporting their use as classification criteria. In contrast, creamy showed a significant positive correlation with milky, and both creamy and milky were positively correlated with buttery, supporting the rationale for grouping these attributes into the same phenotype. Additionally, significant negative correlations were observed between fruity and fermented attributes, as well as between sweet and cheesy attributes. Overall, these results demonstrate that the defined sensory phenotypes are well supported by both statistical distribution and attribute correlations.

GC × GC-TOFMS revealed comprehensive volatiles in fermented milks

GC × GC systems, due to their high resolution and powerful separation capabilities, have been widely applied in flavoromics analysis of various food, such as ham and Baijiu (Chen et al., 2024; Wang Y. et al., 2026). After data processing, nearly 1000 peaks with signal-to-noise ratios greater than 10 were detected in each fermented milk sample, highlighting the superior separation efficiency and data acquisition capability of the GC × GC-TOFMS system (Fig. 2A). In total, 179 volatile flavor compounds were identified across all fermented milk samples, including 26 aldehydes, 33 ketones, 24 esters, 36 alcohols, 19 acids, 12 aromatic compounds, 8 sulfur-containing compounds, 7 nitrogen-containing compounds, 4 terpenes, and 10 other compounds (Fig. 2B–Table S4). The odor activity value (OAV), defined as the ratio of concentration of a compound to its odor threshold, is widely used to assess its contribution to the overall aroma. Compounds with higher OAVs contribute more to the overall aroma of food (Song and Liu, 2018; Sun et al., 2020). A total of 27 compounds with OAV > 1 in at least one sample were identified as odor-active compounds (Table S5). The concentration distributions of volatile flavor and odor-active compounds exhibited distinct patterns among the five sensory phenotypes of fermented milks (Fig. 2C and S2). Ketones were the predominant volatile compounds across all classes, consistent with previous findings reported by Bai et al. (2025) (Fig. 2C and S2).

Ketones, particularly methyl ketones and diketones, are key contributors to the aroma of fermented milk and yogurt. They are primarily formed through the β-oxidation of saturated fatty acids and citrate metabolism, while some branched-chain ketones are produced from the thermal degradation of amino acids. Among them, 2,3-butanedione (diacetyl) and acetoin are recognized as the principal contributors to the milky and buttery notes of fermented milk (Lu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2022). Diacetyl is mainly produced from milk citrate via a series of reactions involving citrate lyase and decarboxylation, and can be further reduced to acetoin and 2,3-butanediol. In this study, both diacetyl and acetoin exhibited OAV > 1 in most samples, with diacetyl exceeding 100 in certain samples, indicating its substantial contribution to fermented milk aroma. In contrast, 2,3-butanediol showed limited aroma impact because of its relatively high odor threshold. A homologous series of methyl ketones, ranging from 2-butanone to 2-decanone, was also detected. These compounds are mainly formed via decarboxylation of β-keto acids generated during fatty acid β-oxidation. Among them, 2-heptanone was the most abundant ketone and is associated with sweet and fruity notes. Another odor-active methyl ketone, 2-nonanone, is characterized by cheesy, dairy, and buttery aromas. Both compounds are commonly reported as important odorants in fermented milk (Bai et al., 2025; Li X. et al., 2024).

The identified aldehydes exhibited an interesting pattern, comprising a homologous series of saturated aldehydes (butanal to decanal) and α, β-unsaturated aldehydes ((E)-2-hexenal to (E)-2-undecenal). These compounds are typical products of lipid oxidation, originating from the oxidative cleavage of naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids in milk fat (Zhao et al., 2025). Aldehydes can subsequently be oxidized to acids or reduced to alcohols. Several branched-chain aldehydes are derived from amino acid metabolism; for example, 2-methylpropanal originates from valine catabolism, while phenylacetaldehyde (classified here as an aromatic compound) is formed from phenylalanine. Due to their low odor thresholds, aldehydes constituted the largest group of odor-active compounds (Table S5), underscoring their importance in fermented milk aroma (Bai et al., 2025). Among the odor-active aldehydes, acetaldehyde is widely recognized as the characteristic aroma compound of yogurt, imparting refreshing, green apple-like notes (Yu et al., 2024). Another aldehyde with a high OAV in this study was nonanal, which is associated with citrus and grassy notes and is mainly derived from the oxidation of fatty acids, mainly from oleic acid (Su et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2024).

Alcohols generally occur at relatively low concentrations in fermented milk and typically possess higher odor thresholds, resulting in a less pronounced contribution to aroma than aldehydes and ketones. Alcohols are mainly formed through reduction reactions: methyl ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols, whereas aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols. Among them, 1-octen-3-ol exhibited a relatively high OAV, owing to its low odor threshold. It exhibits a distinctive mushroom-like aroma and is primarily generated via lipoxygenase-mediated oxidation of fatty acids, especially linoleic acid (Wang, Long et al., 2025). Other alcohols may also indirectly influence fermented milk aroma through esterification or interactions with other volatile compounds (Li D. et al., 2024).

A homologous series of straight-chain carboxylic acids, ranging from acetic acid to decanoic acid, was also identified. Acids are generally exhibiting vinegar-like, rancid, or cheesy notes, contributing uniquely to the overall aroma of fermented milk (Bai et al., 2025; Dan et al., 2019). Esters, which are generally associated with fruity aromas, are formed by reactions between acids and alcohols, while γ- and δ-lactones arise from the β-oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (H. Cheng, 2010). The four odor-active esters identified in this study all exhibited floral and fruity characteristics, enhancing the aromatic complexity of fermented milk.

In addition, trace levels of terpenes were detected (Table S4). These compounds are plant secondary metabolites that typically impart fruity, woody, and floral aromas (Fan et al., 2023). These compounds are mainly derived from forage, transferred to milk after ingestion by dairy cows, and ultimately retained in fermented dairy products (Noni and Battelli, 2008; Poulopoulou et al., 2012). Several terpene-derived terpenoids, including geranylacetone, prenol, linalool, α-terpineol, geraniol, and cedrol, were also identified. Although most originate from plants, prenol, which was present at relatively high levels and is characterized by lavender-like and fruity notes, can also be synthesized by microorganisms via the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) or mevalonate (MVA) pathways (Carruthers et al., 2025; Sommer et al., 2022). Styrene was the only aromatic compound with an OAV greater than 1, and it was likely derived from migration from packaging materials (Condurso et al., 2008). Sulfur-containing compounds, generated through the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids, generally have very low odor thresholds and are often associated with off-flavors or sulfurous notes in dairy products (Wang, Gao et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025).

Overall, GC × GC-TOFMS enabled a comprehensive characterization of the volatile profile of fermented milk, encompassing not only the characteristic aroma compounds of fermented milk but also trace constituents, including those potentially derived from forage.

Microbial diversity and structural differences of fermented milks

To investigate the relationship between microbial community and its sensory phenotype, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed. Rarefaction curves based on the Shannon index increased at the initial stage and gradually reached a plateau, indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the microbial diversity of the samples (Fig. S3A) (Li et al., 2022). After excluding taxa not typically associated with fermented milk, a total of 55 ASVs were retained (Table S6).

At the phylum level, only Bacillota and Actinomycetota were detected in the commercial fermented milks (Fig. S3B). Bacillota was the overwhelmingly dominant phylum, which was consistent with the lactic acid bacteria-dominated characteristics of fermented milk systems. Actinomycetota mainly originated from the addition of Bifidobacterium strains in certain products. At the genus level, Streptococcus was the dominant taxon in all samples, accounting for more than 80% of the relative abundance in each sample (Fig. S3C), which was consistent with previous observations in Korean commercial yogurt products (Suh and Kim, 2021). Further species-level analysis demonstrated that this dominance was primarily attributed to Streptococcus salivarius subsp. thermophilus (S. thermophilus) (Fig. S3D). As one of the most widely used starter bacteria in fermented milk, S. thermophilus rapidly converts lactose into lactic acid, resulting in pH reduction and the production of flavor-related metabolites such as acetoin and diacetyl (Delorme, 2008; Iyer et al., 2010). In addition, exopolysaccharides produced by S. thermophilus can further influence the rheological and sensory properties of fermented milk (Nie et al., 2025). Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus (Lb. bulgaricus) was another important starter microorganism and represented the second most abundant species in several samples, including S4, S6, and S13 (Fig. S3D). Correspondingly, Lactobacillus became the secondary dominant genus in these samples. In several samples, relatively high abundances of Bifidobacterium (S1, S10 and S14) or Lactococcus (S19 and S21) were also observed. Bifidobacterium species are commonly added to fermented milks as probiotics due to their potential intestinal health benefits (Li et al., 2025). In addition, probiotic strains can influence the composition of flavor compounds and sensory characteristics by affect fermentation metabolism (Wang et al., 2021).

α-diversity analysis revealed limited differences among fermented milk samples with different sensory phenotypes (Fig. 3A–E). Among the five α-diversity indices, richness-related indices including observed species richness (Sobs) and Chao1 indices showed significant differences among groups (adjusted p = 0.02275), with the FER samples exhibiting significantly higher richness than the BAN samples (p < 0.01). In contrast, no significant differences were observed for the Ace index. Although the Shannon index and Simpson index showed overall group effects, pairwise comparisons did not reveal significant differences between sensory groups after post hoc testing. These results suggested that different sensory phenotypes of commercial fermented milks exhibited only limited variation in microbial richness and evenness. In addition, β-diversity analysis further demonstrated the high similarity of microbial community structures among sensory groups. In both principal component analysis (PCA) and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), samples from different sensory phenotypes showed substantial overlap without clear separation, despite partial clustering tendencies (Fig. 3F and G). The above results suggested that conventional microbial community analyses based on overall community structure were insufficient to fully explain the sensory differences among commercial fermented milks with relatively simple microbial compositions. Therefore, a triangulation analysis was conducted to reveal the phenotype-associated putative causal taxa and their potential relationships with sensory characteristics.

Deciphering the microbial signature for sensory phenotypes via GMPT

Previous correlation-based studies investigating microbe-flavor associations may overlook directionality and state dependency (Ke et al., 2023) and are therefore limited to descriptive correlations without inferring causality. Recently developed GMPT methods provide a potential approach to address this limitation.

GMPT analysis was performed to identify putative causal microorganisms associated with different sensory phenotypes. The BAN phenotype was excluded because its sensory characteristics were relatively balanced across multiple attributes and were not dominated by a single defining attribute. The overall workflow of the analysis is shown in Fig. 4A. The above analytical workflow was subsequently applied to the MIL, CHE, and FRU phenotypes. The identified putative causal taxa for each phenotype were summarized in Fig. 4B. The highest occurrence frequency among differential taxa was four, observed for the promotive taxon ASV11 (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum) and the inhibitive taxon ASV12 (Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. lactis) in the MIL phenotype. In total, seven putative causal taxa were identified for the MIL phenotype, representing the highest number among all phenotypes. The larger number of putative causal taxa identified in the MIL phenotype suggested that its sensory characteristics were influenced by multiple microorganisms rather than a single dominant taxon. In contrast, only four candidate taxa were identified in the CHE phenotype, among which three taxa appeared in only two comparisons. For the FRU phenotype, both identified taxa, ASV5 and ASV9 (Streptococcus sp.), were classified as inhibitive taxa, reflecting that the positive microbial drivers associated with the FRU phenotype is relatively limited. Across the four GMPT analyses, a total of 12 taxa appeared in at least one pairwise comparison (Fig. S4). Several taxa were associated with multiple sensory phenotypes and exhibited opposite roles in different phenotypes. For example, ASV4 acted as a promotive taxon in both FER and CHE phenotypes but as an inhibitive taxon in the MIL phenotype, whereas ASV5 was identified as an inhibitive taxon in both FER and FRU phenotypes. Such multifunctionality indicates that the sensory contributions of microbes are context-dependent and influenced by microbial interactions within fermented milk community.

Unveiling microbe-metabolite interactions through mmvec neural networks

To investigate the interactions between these microbes and flavor metabolites, a probability-based neural network model, mmvec, was employed. It outperformed traditional correlation-based methods, such as Spearman and Pearson correlations, which are prone to false-positive and false-negative results when applied to compositional data (Morton et al., 2019). Considering the sparsity of the microbiome dataset, ASVs detected in only one sample were filtered, resulting in the retention of 20 taxa for subsequent analysis. For the metabolite dataset, compounds with OAV > 1 were included. Model hyperparameters were optimized by evaluating the cross-validation root mean square error (RMSE) and the model loss during training (Fig. S5).

The model successfully captured several co-occurrence patterns among microorganisms (Fig. 5A). Strong co-occurrences were observed between ASV1 and ASV4, as well as between ASV2 (Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus) and ASV17 (Bifidobacterium sp.). Consistent with the mmvec, the combination of S. thermophilus and Lb. bulgaricus was present in the starter cultures of all fermented milks except S19 and S21, while all samples containing ASV2 also contained Bifidobacterium (Table S1). In contrast, ASV5 (Lactococcus sp.), ASV10 (Lacticaseibacillus sp.), and ASV19 (Lactobacillus acidophilus) were detected only in a subset of samples and therefore did not exhibit strong co-occurrence patterns with other taxa. Notably, unlike co-occurrence patterns observed in natural niches, microbial co-occurrence in fermented milk primarily reflects the combinations of starter cultures selected by manufacturers. S. thermophilus and Lb. bulgaricus represent the most widely used starter culture combination in yogurt and fermented milk production. Most volatile compounds were located near the origin of the biplot (Fig. 5A). Ranks are the CRL-transformed conditional probabilities (Fig. 5B). The higher the conditional probability, the higher the rank value, and vice versa. Across most microbial taxa, several ketones and organic acids, including 2-heptanone, acetoin, 2-nonanone, hexanoic acid and butanoic acid, consistently exhibited positive rank values, indicating higher co-occurrence with these microbes. These compounds are usually recognized as characteristic contributors to the aroma and flavor of fermented milk (Bai et al., 2025). In contrast, compounds such as (E)-2-nonenal, ethyl hexanoate, linalool and dimethyl tetrasulfide generally displayed negative rank values across most taxa, suggesting lower occurrence probability compared with the average metabolite conditional probabilities. Among the analyzed taxa, ASV4 (Lb. bulgaricus) exhibited high co-occurrence with several aroma compounds, including acetaldehyde, 2,3-butanedione, acetoin, 2-nonanone and 2-heptanone. Similarly, ASV1, ASV19, ASV17 and ASV2 displayed high co-occurrence probabilities profiles characterized by elevated ranks for ketones. These observations suggest that the starter and probiotic microorganisms share common flavor-associated metabolite signatures.

Discussion

It is well acknowledged that the starter cultures, including primarily lab cultures, play a crucial role in shaping the characteristic flavor of fermented milks. However, despite the clear sensory separation among the five phenotypes, only limited differences in α-diversity and substantial overlap in β-diversity were observed among the sensory groups. A similar pattern has also been reported in fermented yak milk from three different region of Gannan (Li H. et al., 2024). This apparent discrepancy may reflect a decoupling between microbial taxonomic composition and metabolic function. Commercial fermented milks are generally dominated by a limited number of common starter and probiotic species, resulting in similar community structures. Nevertheless, strains belonging to the same species may differ in flavor-related functional genes, gene expression, and enzyme activities, thereby producing distinct metabolic profiles (Liu et al., 2016). Therefore, approaches focusing on identifying phenotype-associated microorganisms are more informative than conventional community-level analyses when investigating flavor diversification in fermented milk.

To address this issue, we applied a causal inference-based computational method to identify key microorganisms potentially associated with specific sensory phenotypes and flavor metabolites. Specifically, GMPT prioritized microorganisms with putative causal associations with specific sensory phenotypes, whereas mmvec model provided complementary information on the flavor metabolites that showed relatively high conditional co-occurrence probabilities with these microorganisms. For the FER and CHE phenotypes, Lb. bulgaricus represented a potential microbial contributor. ASV4, assigned to Lb. bulgaricus, was identified by GMPT as a promotive taxon for both FER and CHE, and the mmvec model further showed relatively high co-occurrence probabilities between ASV4 and several odor-active compounds, including acetaldehyde, 2,3-butanedione and organic acids. Acetaldehyde can be generated from pyruvate metabolism or through threonine cleavage and is widely recognized as a characteristic aroma compound of fermented milk (Gonzalez et al., 1994). Organic acids are mainly produced through the lipolysis of milk triglycerides (Dan et al., 2019) and commonly detected in Lb. bulgaricus solely fermented milk (Cheng et al., 2026; Dan et al., 2019). The generated short- and medium-chain fatty acids usually contributed to the FER and CHE phenotype of fermented milk. Han et al. reported heptanoic acid, pentanoic acid and acetic acid were key compounds for FER phenotype and pentanoic acid, hexanoic acid, heptanoic acid were key compounds for CHE phenotype (Han H. et al., 2024). Liu et al. reported that octanoic acid and hexanoic acid were crucial to fermented type and butanoic acid was crucial to cheesy type (Liu, Liu, et al., 2022; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2022).

By contrast, the MIL phenotype appeared to be more closely associated with pyruvate- and citrate-derived ketone metabolism. GMPT identified S. thermophilus (ASV1) as a promotive taxon for the MIL phenotype, while mmvec showed that ASV1 was characterized by high co-occurrence probabilities with several ketones. During milk fermentation, lactose-derived pyruvate and citrate can be converted into α-acetolactate, which is subsequently transformed into 2,3-butanedione or decarboxylated into acetoin; these metabolites may be further reduced to 2,3-butanediol (Tian et al., 2024). Study showed that the production of acetoin and 2,3-butanedione in fermented milk were mainly related to S. thermophilus (Alam, Prete, Rannou, et al., 2025). Acetoin and 2,3-butanedione contribute strong buttery, creamy, and milky notes and therefore provide a mechanistic basis for the positive association between S. thermophilus and the MIL phenotype, which was consistent with previous research (Han H. et al., 2024). Other ketones, such as 2-nonanone and 2-undecanone, also contribute to the milky aroma of fermented milk (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2022). However, owing to strain-specific differences in substrate uptake, metabolic conversion, and metabolite export, the production and accumulation of the same compound can vary substantially among strains. For example, fermented milks produced with different strains of Lb. bulgaricus exhibited distinct aroma type (Liu, Liu, et al., 2022). Thus, strain-level metabolic heterogeneity may explain why fermented milks with broadly similar taxonomic compositions nevertheless exhibited distinct sensory profiles.

The contrasting role of Lb. bulgaricus across sensory phenotypes further suggests that microbial effects on flavor depend on the balance among competing metabolic pathways rather than on the presence of a single taxon. ASV4 was positively associated with FER and CHE but negatively associated with MIL. Although Lb. bulgaricus may co-occur with diacetyl and acetoin, stronger acidification, acetaldehyde production, proteolysis, or liberation of free fatty acids could shift the overall aroma away from creamy and buttery notes toward fermented, sour, or cheesy characteristics. Thus, the sensory contribution of a microorganism may be determined by the relative flux through glycolytic, proteolytic, and lipolytic pathways, as well as by interactions with coexisting starter cultures.

It should be noted that the associations inferred by mmvec model represent conditional co-occurrence probabilities rather than direct biochemical transformations. Therefore, the predicted microbe-metabolite interaction should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that a specific microorganism directly produces a given metabolite. Instead, these associations provide hypotheses regarding potential microbial contributors to flavor formation and offer valuable guidance for future validation experiments.

Although GMPT was developed as a causal inference framework to prioritize candidate causal microorganisms based on their recurrent differential abundance across multiple phenotype comparisons, the present analysis was conducted using observational data. Therefore, the microorganisms identified in this study are referred to as putative causal taxa, and their causal roles require further validation through controlled fermentation, microbial perturbation, and functional experiments. Several additional limitations should also be considered. First, the relatively limited number of commercial fermented milks may restrict the statistical power of the analysis. This limitation is particularly relevant for phenotypes with small samples size, specifically, the FRU and BAN phenotypes. Although technical replicates of 16S sequencing were used in the GMPT analysis to ensure algorithmic robustness, the putative causal taxa identified for the FRU phenotype should be interpreted with caution or regarded merely as a reference. Future studies incorporating larger sample collections and independent validation cohorts are required to further evaluate the robustness and generalizability of these microbial-sensory relationships. Second, microbial characterization based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing could not resolve strain-level variation or directly characterize microbial functional activity. Integrating metagenomics with transcriptomics, proteomics, and controlled fermentation experiments are therefore required to determine how strain-specific functional genes, their expression, and interactions among starter cultures collectively shape the flavor characteristics of fermented milk.

Overall, by integrating sensory phenotyping, GMPT-based causal inference, and mmvec neural network modeling, this study established a data-driven framework linking microorganisms, volatile metabolites, and sensory phenotypes in fermented milks. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the first efforts to apply a causal inference-based approach (GMPT) for investigating microbial contributions underlying flavor diversification in fermented foods, extending computational approaches developed in microbiome research into food science field. More importantly, the analytical framework employed in this study provides a promising strategy for deciphering microbe-sensory associations in more complex fermented food systems, such as cheese, wine, and Baijiu. These systems exhibit higher microbial diversity and greater sensory heterogeneity, which may facilitate the identification of key microorganisms associated with specific flavor types.
