Living the good life: an evolutionary concept analysis of mino-pimâtisiwin and related teachings from Turtle Island
Authors: Keatton Tiernan, Genevieve Montemurro, Trudy Cardinal, Tara-Leigh McHugh, Leah J Ferguson, Kate Storey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Mino-pimâtisiwin (Cree) and mino-bimaadiziwin (Anishinaabe), often translated as ‘the good life’, are Indigenous teachings that describe relational and wholistic understandings of well-being. Although these concepts are increasingly used across scholarly and grey literature, their meanings are not always clearly defined. This study used evolutionary concept analysis to examine how mino-pimâtisiwin is defined and applied in scholarly and grey literature, while attending to mino-bimaadiziwin and related terms as conceptually connected teachings from Turtle Island. A systematic search of MEDLINE, Academic Search Complete, Scopus, and Google Advanced Search identified 30 sources for analysis. Qualitative content analysis was used to identify defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. Four defining attributes were identified: relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving. Antecedents included cultural teachings, awareness of relational interconnectedness, supportive environments of community, land, and culture, and opportunities to enact relational responsibilities. Consequences included individual well-being and purpose, cultural continuity, ecological stewardship, and community well-being. Rather than describing well-being as something held by an individual, the literature portrays mino-pimâtisiwin as a way of living well through reciprocal relationships with human and more-than-human relations. This analysis clarifies mino-pimâtisiwin as a relational framework of well-being and may support Indigenous health promotion approaches grounded in community, culture, land, responsibility, and collective thriving.Keywords: Indigenous health promotion, Indigenous wellness, Indigenous knowledge systems, relationality, wholistic health, miyo-pimâtisiwin

Introduction

Indigenous teachings have long articulated understandings of health and well-being that reach beyond biomedical or individualistic conceptions of health (Hart 1999, Ross 2022, Nelson et al. 2025b). In many Indigenous traditions, well-being arises through relationships among individuals, families, communities, cultures, and the natural world. These relational understandings of health have increasingly informed scholarship across fields, including public health, environmental governance, education, and community development (Sobierajski et al. 2022, Oni et al. 2023, Conaway and Leoso 2024, Panek et al. 2025). Such perspectives challenge dominant approaches that conceptualize well-being as primarily an individual issue, instead emphasizing the interconnected social, cultural, and ecological conditions through which life is sustained. For health promotion, this shift matters because how well-being is understood shapes the kinds of relationships, responsibilities, and conditions that are recognized as central to health.

Within Cree and Anishinaabe traditions, teachings describing the good life are often expressed through the concepts mino-pimâtisiwin (Cree) and mino-bimaadiziwin (Anishinaabe). Both terms are commonly translated as ‘the good life’ and discussed in scholarship on Indigenous health and well-being. Linguistically, they derive from closely related Algonquian languages: miyo- (Plains Cree) and mino- (Woodland Cree and Anishinaabe) translate to ‘good’, while pimâtisiwin (Cree) and bimaadiziwin (Anishinaabe) refer to life or living (Hart 1999, Hansen 2022). Because these languages are verb-based, they are better understood as an ongoing process, roughly ‘living the good life’. In this paper, Turtle Island is used to refer broadly to the lands commonly known as North America, while recognizing that this term is not used uniformly by all Indigenous Nations and does not replace specific Nation, language, or place-based names. In this analysis, mino-pimâtisiwin is used as the primary organizing term while attending to mino-bimaadiziwin and related variants where they appear. This choice reflects the lead author’s Métis and Cree positionality and provides analytic consistency, while recognizing that these terms should not be collapsed across Nations, dialects, languages, or knowledge systems.

Across academic and community literature, mino-pimâtisiwin and mino-bimaadiziwin are frequently used to describe Indigenous understandings of wholistic well-being grounded in relationships with family, community, land, and the broader living world (Landry et al. 2019, Kress 2022, Sobierajski et al. 2022). In this paper, the spelling ‘wholistic’ is used intentionally to emphasize the ‘whole-ism’ of well-being as relational and interconnected, rather than referring solely to the health of an individual body, as is often implied in conventional uses of ‘holistic’ health (Miles et al. 2023). Although the phrase ‘the good life’ provides a useful translation, it does not fully capture the depth of meaning embedded in these teachings. Within Indigenous languages and knowledge systems, these concepts encompass wider philosophical understandings of relational life, responsibility, and well-being that go beyond what can be conveyed through direct translation.

Mino-pimâtisiwin and mino-bimaadiziwin are increasingly cited across disciplines, but authors do not always explain what they mean in conceptual terms. As a result, the ways in which these teachings are defined, interpreted, and applied vary considerably across fields (Landry et al. 2019, Patterson et al. 2023, Conaway and Leoso 2024). When concepts such as mino-pimâtisiwin are used without conceptual clarity, they risk becoming shorthand for Indigenous well-being without sufficient attention to the relationships, responsibilities, lands, languages, and knowledge systems that give them meaning. Clearer conceptual attention is needed to show how these teachings are represented in scholarly and grey literature, and how they shape wider conversations about well-being in health promotion, public health, and social science.

This study addresses this gap by conducting an evolutionary concept analysis of mino-pimâtisiwin and related teachings within scholarly and grey literature. Guided by Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis framework (Rodgers 1989, Richmond et al. 2022), the study examines how the concept is defined and used, identifies its defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents, and develops a conceptual synthesis of its relational structure. Consistent with this analytic approach, the review asks: (i) How are mino-pimâtisiwin and related teachings of living well defined and used across scholarly and grey literature? and (ii) What defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents characterize mino-pimâtisiwin as represented in this literature? This analysis engages literature referencing mino-pimâtisiwin, miyo-pimâtisiwin, and mino-bimaadiziwin. These terms emerge from distinct Cree and Anishinaabe linguistic and cultural traditions, yet are often discussed together in scholarship because of their shared attention to living well through relationality, responsibility, balance, and collective well-being. While mino-pimâtisiwin serves as the organizing term, the analysis attends to how related teachings are represented across the literature without treating them as interchangeable or culturally equivalent. By clarifying how mino-pimâtisiwin and related teachings are represented in the literature, this analysis contributes to Indigenous health promotion scholarship by articulating a relational framework of well-being that may inform culturally grounded research, policy, and practice.

Methods

Conceptual approach

This study uses Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis to examine how authors define and apply mino-pimâtisiwin (Cree) and mino-bimaadiziwin (Anishinaabe), teachings commonly translated as ‘the good life’, in the literature. Concept analysis examines how a concept is defined, applied, and understood across contexts, recognizing that meanings evolve across disciplinary and social settings (Rodgers 1989, Richmond et al. 2022).

Guided by the researcher’s positionality as a Métis and Cree scholar, the study took an Indigenist and anticolonial orientation that centers Indigenous knowledge and relational accountability. Consistent with this positional grounding, the analysis engages literature referencing both mino-pimâtisiwin and mino-bimaadiziwin while using the Cree term mino-pimâtisiwin as the primary organizing term. Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis (Rodgers 1989), as adapted by Smith et al. (2020), provided the guiding analytic framework, conceptualizing meanings as dynamic and contextually situated. This approach is appropriate for mino-pimâtisiwin because its meanings are carried in relationships, practice, and cultural context.

The analysis followed eight iterative stages: identifying the concept, surrogate terms and relevant uses; selecting a sample; determining defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents; and developing relational vignettes corresponding to model, borderline, and contrary cases.

The study did not aim to produce a universal or pan-Indigenous definition, but to examine how the concept has been used in scholarly and grey literature and develop an interpretation grounded in the lead author’s Métis and Cree positionality while attending to related Cree and Anishinaabe teachings represented in the literature.

Literature search and study selection

A systematic search of the literature was conducted in MEDLINE, Academic Search Complete, and Scopus to identify publications referencing mino-pimâtisiwin and related terms. Search terms included ‘mino-pimatisiwin’, ‘mino-pimâtisiwin’, ‘miyo-pimatisiwin’, ‘miyo-pimâtisiwin’, and ‘mino-bimaadiziwin’. These terms were selected to capture orthographic variation across Cree and Anishinaabe spellings, as well as differences in diacritic usage within the literature. Database searches identified 44 records, including 5 from MEDLINE, 15 from Academic Search Complete, and 24 from Scopus. Searches were supplemented using Google Advanced Search to identify relevant grey literature, defined here as publicly accessible, non-peer-reviewed sources that substantively defined, contextualized, or applied the concept, including community reports, organizational webpages, educational materials, and other non-academic publications. The same search terms used in the database searches were applied in Google Advanced Search. Because Google searches can generate a large number of results, screening was limited to the first 50 results ranked by relevance, which identified 18 potentially relevant grey literature records. Together, the database and grey literature searches identified 62 records. After removal of duplicates (n = 12), 50 records remained for screening.

A structured search protocol guided search and screening procedures and documented methodological decisions (Supplementary File 1). The search was last conducted on 5 March 2026, with no date restrictions to capture historical and contemporary uses across disciplines.

Study identification and screening followed PRISMA reporting standards (Page et al. 2021), with the selection process illustrated in Fig. 1. Titles and abstracts, or available summaries for grey literature, were reviewed for relevance. Full-text sources were then assessed against inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample of 30 sources. Screening and eligibility decisions were conducted by the lead author according to the pre-defined search protocol (Supplementary File 1). The final sample included peer-reviewed journal articles, book chapters, and grey literature sources such as reports, webpages, and educational materials. The literature spanned multiple disciplinary contexts, including Indigenous health, education, environmental governance, and community well-being. Literature was included if it was published in Cree, English, or French; discussed mino-pimâtisiwin or mino-bimaadiziwin; and provided a contextual explanation and definition of the concept. Literature was excluded if it was written in languages other than Cree, English, or French; used the term without contextual explanation and definition of the concept; or used the term only as a simple translation (e.g. ‘the good life’). Both peer-reviewed and grey literature was eligible to capture the range of contexts in which the concept appears. Peer-reviewed and grey literature was not treated as equivalent forms of evidence; each source was interpreted in relation to its authorship, purpose, publication context, and conceptual contribution. Because this was a concept analysis rather than a review of intervention effectiveness, sources were not ranked by methodological quality or weighted by study design. Analytic attention focused on how each source defined, contextualized, and applied the concept. Because this study analyzed publicly available scholarly and grey literature and did not involve recruitment with human participants, institutional research ethics approval was not required.

The authorship team included both Indigenous and non-Indigenous scholars. All authors have extensive experience working with Indigenous communities, with collective expertise spanning health promotion, education, physical activity, youth well-being, qualitative inquiry, and community-engaged research. The first author, a Métis and Cree scholar, led the conceptualization, searching, screening, extraction, formal analysis, and initial manuscript preparation. Co-authors provided critical methodological, theoretical, and interpretive guidance throughout the study. Team discussion informed the review purpose, search strategy, eligibility criteria, analytic categories, conceptual boundaries, interpretation of findings, and development of the conceptual synthesis.

Data extraction

Data from included sources were extracted into a structured table including title, source database, publication date, author(s), material type, language, place of publication, document URL, term used (e.g. mino-pimâtisiwin, mino-bimaadiziwin, and miyo-pimâtisiwin), who defined the concept (e.g. the author, third party, Elder, and participants), and how the concept was used within the document. This structured extraction supported transparency and dependability in the analytic process.

Data analysis

Qualitative content analysis guided analysis of the extracted data and supported systematic identification of patterns in how mino-pimâtisiwin was defined and used (Rodgers 1989, Smith et al. 2020, Richmond et al. 2022, Mayan 2023). Analysis proceeded in two stages. First, inductive coding identified recurring patterns related to meanings and uses of the concept, which were grouped into broader categories that informed the defining attributes. Second, deductive coding identified antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. Consistent with Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis framework, defining attributes informed the development of relational vignettes corresponding to model, borderline, and contrary cases, which were compared against the coded dataset (Rodgers 1989). The inductive and deductive phases were revisited iteratively throughout the analytic process to ensure coherence between emerging categories and the evolving interpretation of the concept. The first author developed the initial codes, categories, and analytic memos. Co-authors critically discussed and helped refine the attributes, distinctions among attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents, and the interpretation of the relational synthesis. Queries or uncertainties were resolved through team discussion with attention to conceptual fit and cultural and contextual specificity.

Rigor and reflexivity

Rigor was supported through an audit trail documenting search strategies, inclusion decisions, coding development, and analytic interpretations. The study also drew on Lincoln and Guba’s criteria (Lincoln and Guba 1986) for qualitative rigor, including credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was supported through thick description, peer debriefing and critical discussion within the authorship team, and persistent observation across cultural and scholarly contexts. Persistent observation reflects the lead author’s longstanding engagement with the concept through lived experience and prior scholarship as a Métis and Cree scholar, including previous research on mino-pimâtisiwin (Tiernan et al. 2025). Transferability was supported through thick description and transparent reporting of the search approach. Dependability and confirmability were strengthened through PRISMA standards and a structured search protocol (Supplementary File 1).

Given the concept’s cultural significance, reflexivity was essential. The lead author maintained a reflexive journal throughout the study to document interpretive decisions and reflects on how his positionality as a Métis and Cree scholar shaped the analytic process. Team discussions also served a reflexive function by bringing Indigenous and non-Indigenous perspectives, disciplinary training, methodological expertise, and community-engaged research experience to the interpretation of the literature. These discussions supported attention to how the authors’ positions and assumptions shaped the analysis. Although this literature-based study did not involve direct engagement with Indigenous communities, the analysis was guided by relational accountability to the cultural and knowledge contexts from which these teachings emerge.

Results

Uses and definitions of mino-pimâtisiwin and related teachings

Across the literature reviewed, mino-pimâtisiwin, miyo-pimâtisiwin, and mino-bimaadiziwin are commonly translated as ‘the good life’ or ‘living the good life’ (Hart 1999, Hansen 2022, Nelson et al. 2025a). However, authors rarely use these concepts as a simple outcome or individual condition. Instead, they are frequently used as shorthand for Indigenous understandings of wholistic health and well-being that are relational, process-oriented, and grounded in responsibilities to family, community, land, culture and the wider living world (Landry et al. 2019, Ross 2022, Sobierajski et al. 2022).

Although these terms emerge from distinct Cree and Anishinaabe linguistic and cultural traditions, the literature often discusses them together because of their shared attention to living well through relationship, responsibility, balance, and connection. In fields such as Indigenous health, environmental governance, education, and community well-being, mino-pimâtisiwin and related teachings are presented as both a way of living and a condition of well-being sustained through relationships (Hart 2009, Oni et al. 2023, Conaway and Leoso 2024). In this sense, ‘the good life’ functions less as a simple translation than as shorthand for relational ways of living well.

Defining attributes of mino-pimâtisiwin

Analysis of the literature identified four defining attributes of mino-pimâtisiwin: relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving. These attributes describe a relational understanding of well-being grounded in human and more-than-human relationships, enacted through ethical responsibilities, and oriented toward collective thriving. Although discussed separately for analytic clarity, they operate as interconnected dimensions of a relational framework.

Relationality

Relationality forms the philosophical foundation of mino-pimâtisiwin. Within the teachings referenced by authors, relationality is not understood simply as interpersonal connection, but as a broader worldview that understands life, well-being, and identity as fundamentally constituted through relationships. Individuals are defined by their interconnected web of relationships, including family, community, ancestors, land, and other more-than-human relations. In this sense, well-being, like identity, is understood as emerging through relationships rather than residing solely within individuals.

Within this relational worldview, relationships extend beyond human communities to what many teachings describe as ‘all relations’. These relations include the land, animals, waters, plants, ancestors, and spiritual beings that together form the interconnected systems through which life is sustained. Authors consistently emphasize that well-being depends on recognizing and maintaining respectful relationships with both human and more-than-human beings (Kruse et al. 2019, Nelson et al. 2025a, 2025b).

Living mino-pimâtisiwin therefore involves maintaining respectful and reciprocal relationships with family, community, ancestors, land, and other more-than-human relations. Well-being arises from relationships that sustain individuals, communities, and their environments. For example, authors describe the good life as ‘seek[ing] balance and harmony with all of creation’ (Nelson et al. 2025b, p. 26) and ‘emphasizing balance and interconnectedness’ among human and non-human beings (Antwi et al. 2025, p. 10).

Relationality provides the worldview through which life, identity, and well-being are constituted through relationships; the remaining attributes describe how those relationships are recognized, enacted, and sustained.

Relational responsibilities

Recognizing these relationships also entails responsibility. Within teachings of mino-pimâtisiwin, relationships are understood not as passive connections, but as relationships that carry obligations. Living the good life involves recognizing obligations to care for and sustain the relationships that support life. Authors consistently describe mino-pimâtisiwin as requiring individuals to act with responsibility, reciprocity, and care toward others, including family members, communities, the land, and the broader living world.

These responsibilities extend beyond individual well-being to include responsibilities to the community and future generations. Hart describes growth toward the good life as ‘overcoming negative attitudes and behaviors and recognizing connections to something greater than the self, including ancestors, other life forms, nature, and the broader community’ (Hart 2009, p. 135). This framing highlights that relational life carries obligations for how individuals think, act, and relate to others. Sources also highlight that relationships with land and other living beings are grounded in reciprocity and responsibility, underscoring the ethical obligations embedded in relational life as essential components of mino-pimâtisiwin (Daigle 2019, Nightingale and Richmond 2022, Conaway and Leoso 2024).

Relational responsibility is therefore the ethical dimension of mino-pimâtisiwin: relationships are sustained through reciprocity, care, and obligations to the networks of life.

Balance and harmony

Balance and harmony are frequently described across the literature as central conditions of mino-pimâtisiwin. Within these teachings, well-being emerges when relationships within oneself, with others, and with the broader living world are maintained in balance. Rather than representing a static state, balance is described as an ongoing process of sustaining equilibrium across the interconnected dimensions of life.

This balance is often articulated through physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual dimensions of well-being. Authors describe these dimensions as interdependent aspects of life in which an imbalance in one can reverberate through others. For example, Kopp et al. (2021, p. 214) describe the good life as ‘achieving and maintaining balance between these four realms’. Across sources, authors emphasize that imbalance in one domain can reverberate through family, community, cultural practices, and land, underscoring the interconnected nature of well-being (Doerfler 2010, Landry et al. 2019, Kopp et al. 2021, Ross 2022, Antwi et al. 2025, Government of Manitoba n.d.). Thus, balance and harmony emerge when relationships and responsibilities sustain equilibrium across individuals, communities, and the broader living world.

Collective thriving

Mino-pimâtisiwin is described as extending beyond individual well-being to encompass the flourishing of families, communities, and the broader living world. The good life is understood not as a purely personal achievement but as a collective condition in which the well-being of individuals is inseparable from the well-being of the relationships and communities that sustain them.

Several authors describe mino-pimâtisiwin as involving the strengthening of community well-being and the collective conditions that support healthy and meaningful lives. For example, Nelson et al. (2025b), highlight that living the good life can foster stronger relational connections, reduced loneliness, and greater integration across relational domains. Similarly, Kress (2022) describes mino-pimâtisiwin as a state of comprehensive well-being rooted in relationships, spirituality, and connection to land, emphasizing the importance of belonging, self-determination, and the flourishing of individuals within community contexts. Other sources likewise highlight improved individual and community well-being as outcomes associated with living mino-pimâtisiwin (Ross 2022, Oni et al. 2023, Hayward 2024).

Collective thriving expresses the relational outcome of mino-pimâtisiwin: individual well-being is inseparable from the flourishing of communities and the broader networks of life.

These attributes illustrate that mino-pimâtisiwin is not a single state of well-being but a relational process through which life is lived in a good way. The concept begins with a relational worldview grounded in relationships with human and more-than-human relations, and is enacted through responsibilities that sustain those relationships. When these relationships are maintained with care and reciprocity, balance and harmony can be achieved, creating the conditions for the flourishing of individuals, communities, and the broader living world.

Antecedents

Within Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis, antecedents refer to the conditions that must be present before a concept can emerge. For mino-pimâtisiwin, these include the social, cultural, and relational contexts that enable living the good life. Because the concept is relational and process-oriented, some antecedents overlap with defining attributes. Analytically, the antecedents identified here refer to enabling conditions rather than attributes of the concept itself.

Within the literature reviewed, these antecedents most often relate to the presence of cultural teachings and knowledge systems, recognition of interconnectedness, grounding in community, land, and cultural life, and opportunities for participation and contribution. These conditions are described as interrelated processes that sustain the possibility of living mino-pimâtisiwin, rather than functioning as isolated prerequisites.

Cultural teachings and knowledge systems

Access to cultural teachings and knowledge systems is frequently identified across the literature as a foundational condition that enables the pursuit of mino-pimâtisiwin. These teachings provide ethical guidance and relational principles that shape how individuals come to understand and practice living well (Landry et al. 2019, Nightingale and Richmond 2022, Harms and Petrella 2025). Through the sharing of teachings by Elders, family members, and community knowledge holders, individuals learn the values and responsibilities that guide life within a relational worldview. As Hansen explains, ‘if we train ourselves to be in tune with our Creator, we will hear the teachings and instructions resulting in miyo-pimatisiwin’ (Hansen 2022, p. 91). This framing highlights how cultural teachings serve as the foundation for learning the principles and responsibilities of living mino-pimâtisiwin.

Recognition of interconnectedness

Across the literature, recognizing interconnectedness is described as an important condition that precedes living mino-pimâtisiwin. Authors describe well-being as inseparable from relationships with family, community, the land, and more-than-human relations (Madsen 2011, Simon and Martin 2024, Panek et al. 2025). These relations extend beyond human beings to include animals, plants, waters, rocks, and other elements of the natural world, all of which are understood to carry spirit and participate in the web of life. This recognition reflects a worldview in which humans are understood as part of, rather than separate from, the systems that sustain life.

This recognition is described as emerging through cultural teachings, lived experience, and participation in community and land-based practices. Through these processes, individuals come to understand their place within networks of human and more-than-human relations and develop understandings of how their actions affect the well-being of others. In this way, awareness of relational interconnectedness forms an important foundation for the ethical orientations and relational practices associated with mino-pimâtisiwin.

Grounding in community, land, and culture

The literature also highlights the importance of being grounded in community, land, and cultural life as a condition that supports the pursuit of mino-pimâtisiwin. These contexts provide opportunities for individuals to participate in cultural practices, engage with the land, and learn from family members, Elders, and community knowledge holders (Bridges et al. 2023, Nelson et al. 2025a, Government of Anishinaabe Nation in Treaty 2 Territory n.d.). Through these relationships and places, individuals are situated within the networks of care, guidance, and responsibility that sustain Indigenous ways of living.

Authors emphasize that access to community, land, and culture is essential for learning and practicing the teachings associated with the good life. Participation in community life, cultural activities, and land-based practices creates the conditions through which individuals experience connection, responsibility, and reciprocity in everyday life. In this way, relationships with community, land, and culture provide the relational context through which individuals can move toward mino-pimâtisiwin.

Opportunities for participation and contribution

Finally, the literature emphasizes that opportunities for participation and contribution are important conditions that support the pursuit of mino-pimâtisiwin. Within the teachings referenced by authors, living well is not understood solely as a set of beliefs or values but as something learned through participation in relationships and community life (Doerfler 2010, Wabie 2019). Opportunities to contribute to family, support community well-being, care for the land, and uphold cultural practices allow individuals to practice the responsibilities that sustain relational ways of living.

Several authors describe these opportunities as emerging through everyday acts of care, participation in cultural and ceremonial life, and engagement in community responsibilities. Through these experiences, individuals learn to embody the values of respect, reciprocity, and responsibility that guide relationships with others and with the more-than-human world. In this way, opportunities for participation and contribution create the conditions through which the teachings associated with mino-pimâtisiwin can be practiced and sustained.

Together, these antecedent conditions show the social, cultural, and relational contexts that make mino-pimâtisiwin possible. Their overlap with the concept’s defining attributes is not a contradiction, but a reflection of mino-pimâtisiwin as a circular and relational process. The conditions that support living well are also renewed through the ongoing practice of living well with others, with land, and with the broader living world.

Consequences

Within Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis, consequences refer to the outcomes or events that occur as a result of the presence of a concept. The literature describes living mino-pimâtisiwin as associated with a range of outcomes that extend beyond individual well-being to include the flourishing of cultures, the stewardship of the natural world, and the thriving of communities. These consequences reflect the fundamentally relational nature of the good life, where well-being is experienced not only at the level of the individual but across relationships with family, community, culture, and the land.

Individual well-being and purpose

Across the literature, living mino-pimâtisiwin is frequently associated with improved individual well-being and a strengthened sense of purpose in life. Authors describe the good life as supporting wholistic well-being across emotional, mental, physical, and spiritual dimensions, while fostering hope, resilience, and a sense of meaning (Daigle 2019, Kruse et al. 2019, Kress 2022, Nightingale and Richmond 2022). Through relationships with family, community, and cultural teachings, individuals develop a sense of identity and purpose that guides their actions and decision-making. In this way, individual well-being emerges not as an isolated state but as something grounded within relational ways of living.

Cultural continuity and resurgence

The literature also emphasizes that living mino-pimâtisiwin contributes to the continuity and renewal of Indigenous cultures (Daigle 2019, Hansen 2022, Bridges et al. 2023, Government of Anishinaabe Nation in Treaty 2 Territory n.d.). Participation in cultural teachings, ceremonies, language, and traditional practices strengthens cultural identity and supports the transmission of knowledge across generations. Several authors describe these processes as contributing to cultural resurgence and the revitalization of language, traditions, and community knowledge systems. Through these practices, cultural teachings associated with the good life are sustained and carried forward within families and communities.

Ecological stewardship and sustainability

Because relational responsibilities extend beyond human relationships to include the natural world, living mino-pimâtisiwin is also associated with environmental stewardship and sustainable relationships with the land and living systems. Authors describe how teachings about the good life encourage respectful relationships with animals, plants, waters, and other elements of the natural world (Landry et al. 2019, Conaway and Leoso 2024, Panek et al. 2025). These relationships emphasize reciprocity, care, and responsibility for maintaining the balance of living systems. In this way, mino-pimâtisiwin supports ecological sustainability by guiding practices that honor the interconnected well-being of people and the land.

Strengthened community life and intergenerational well-being

Finally, the literature describes the good life as contributing to strengthened family, community, and intergenerational well-being. This consequence overlaps with collective thriving as a defining attribute of the concept, reflecting the circular and relational nature of mino-pimâtisiwin. However, where collective thriving names a core quality of the good life, strengthened community life points to what is sustained when teachings associated with mino-pimâtisiwin are lived over time. When individuals and communities enact these teachings, relationships within families, communities, and the broader living world are strengthened, supporting shared responsibility for community life. Authors link these processes to broader forms of Indigenous resurgence and self-determination, where communities sustain cultural teachings, strengthen governance, and kinship systems and create conditions that support future generations (Hart 2009, Simon and Martin 2024, Nelson et al. 2025a). In this way, mino-pimâtisiwin is understood as both a collective condition and a generative process through which individuals, community, cultural, and land-based well-being are mutually renewed.

Empirical referents

Within Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis, empirical referents refer to observable indicators through which a concept can be recognized in practice. In the literature reviewed, mino-pimâtisiwin is most often identified through relational practices and behaviors that reflect the teachings associated with living the good life. Rather than being measured through single indicators, these referents are expressed through cultural participation, relationships with community and land, and ethical ways of relating to others and the natural world.

Examples include participation in cultural practices and ceremonies, engagement in community life and kinship networks, land-based activities such as harvesting or cultural land use, and relational ethics such as reciprocity, respect, and care for others and the land. These referents highlight that mino-pimâtisiwin is most often recognized through relational practices and lived experiences rather than through standardized individual-level measures of health or well-being. These referents should be understood as context-dependent expressions of the concept rather than universal indicators to be applied across Indigenous communities.

Conceptual synthesis of mino-pimâtisiwin

Mino-pimâtisiwin is best understood as a relational understanding of well-being grounded in living in a good way with all relations. Well-being emerges through balanced and reciprocal relationships with human and more-than-human relations, characterized by four defining attributes: relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving.

When these relational teachings and responsibilities are practiced, outcomes extend beyond individual well-being to include cultural continuity, ecological stewardship, and family and community thriving. Well-being within mino-pimâtisiwin is therefore not an individual state but a relational condition. In this way, mino-pimâtisiwin reflects a wholistic understanding of the good life in which the well-being of individuals, cultures, communities, and the natural world are understood as mutually sustaining. At the center are the antecedent conditions that support the possibility of living mino-pimâtisiwin, including cultural teachings and knowledge systems, recognition of interconnectedness, grounding in community, land, and cultural life, and opportunities for participation and contribution. These conditions create the foundation through which the defining attributes of mino-pimâtisiwin are realized: relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving. When these attributes are lived and sustained in practice, they contribute to consequences such as individual well-being and purpose, cultural continuity and resurgence, ecological stewardship and sustainability, and strengthened community life and intergenerational well-being. The outer ring identifies empirical referents, or observable expressions through which mino-pimâtisiwin can be recognized in practice, including cultural practices and ceremonies, land-based activities, community life and kinship networks, relational ethics, and practices of reciprocity, respect, and care. Although presented as concentric analytic layers, these components are relational and overlapping rather than discrete, reflecting the circular and mutually sustaining nature of mino-pimâtisiwin.

Relational vignettes: illustrating degrees of alignment with mino-pimâtisiwin

Concept analyses often use model, borderline, and contrary cases to clarify defining attributes. Here, these examples are presented as relational vignettes rather than prescriptive cases, reflecting the study’s Indigenist and anticolonial orientation. This avoids positioning mino-pimâtisiwin as a fixed standard against which individuals or communities are judged. As a living teaching and relational process, mino-pimâtisiwin is not a static achievement or universal checklist.

The following vignettes echo the methodological purpose of model, borderline, and contrary cases while presenting them as illustrative stories. They show different degrees of alignment with the defining attributes: relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving.

Living toward mino-pimâtisiwin

A person participates regularly in cultural and community life. Through teachings shared by Elders, family members, and community knowledge holders, they have learned that living well involves maintaining respectful relationships with family, community, land, ancestors, and the broader living world. They spend time on the land and with relatives, participating in seasonal activities that connect them with medicines, animals, plants, waters, and other elements of the natural world. In daily life, they seek to act with respect and reciprocity toward others by helping care for younger family members, supporting community events, and contributing to shared responsibilities within their community.

Through these relationships and practices, this person works to maintain balance across the emotional, physical, mental, and spiritual dimensions of life. Their well-being is grounded in relationships with family, community, culture, and land and these relationships also contribute to the well-being of those around them. This vignette illustrates one possible expression of mino-pimâtisiwin in which relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving are all present. It should be understood as aspirational and illustrative, rather than as a singular or ideal form of living the good life.

Partial or constrained expression

A person has developed a strong commitment to maintaining their physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual well-being. They spend time outdoors and feel grounded when walking near the river or being on the land. They value cultural teachings and attend cultural gatherings or community events when they are able. These practices support personal balance and reflect some recognition of relationships with land, culture, and community.

At the same time, their current circumstances limit their participation in the reciprocal responsibilities that sustain community life. Work demands, caregiving responsibilities, distance from community, limited access to cultural spaces, or other structural constraints may make it difficult to participate consistently in collective activities or land-based practices. As a result, this vignette reflects some attributes of mino-pimâtisiwin, particularly balance, connection, and recognition of relationality, while the fuller enactment of relational responsibilities and collective thriving is less visible. This partial expression does not represent a personal failure. It illustrates how some attributes of the concept may be present while others are constrained by context, access, and circumstance.

Disconnection from relational attributes

A person’s understanding of well-being is shaped primarily by individual achievement, personal security, and success within dominant social and economic systems. Their decisions about work, time, consumption, and lifestyle are guided mainly by individual benefit, efficiency, or advancement. Relationships with family, community, land, culture, and the broader living world are not central to how they understand or pursue well-being.

In this orientation, land may be approached primarily as property, resource, or recreational space rather than as a living relation requiring care, reciprocity, and responsibility. Engagement with cultural teachings, community responsibilities, and reciprocal relationships is minimal or absent. This vignette illustrates a situation in which the defining attributes of mino-pimâtisiwin are not evident. The purpose is not to judge the person, but to clarify conceptually that mino-pimâtisiwin cannot be reduced to individual success, personal wellness, or self-improvement when these are disconnected from relational responsibilities, balance, and collective thriving.

Discussion

Mino-pimâtisiwin as a relational framework for health promotion

This concept analysis examined mino-pimâtisiwin as a way of understanding well-being through responsibilities to human and more-than-human relations. Across the literature, the good life emerges through balanced and reciprocal relationships with family, community, culture, and the natural world (e.g. Landry et al. 2019, Kopp et al. 2021, Nelson et al. 2025b). For health promotion, this shifts attention from individual behavior or wellness toward the relational, cultural, ecological, and community conditions that support living well.

Balance within mino-pimâtisiwin is maintained through relational responsibilities. Teachings associated with the good life emphasize reciprocity, respect, and care for others, including family members, communities, and the broader living world (Hart 2009, Nelson et al. 2025a, Turtle Mountain Community College n.d.). Through everyday care, cultural participation, and land stewardship, people contribute to the relationships that sustain life (Landry 2019, Nightingale and Richmond 2022, Conaway and Leoso 2024). Well-being is thus continually enacted through relationships rather than possessed by individuals.

Many well-being frameworks conceptualize health primarily at the individual level, emphasizing psychological states, personal resilience, and individual health behaviors (Ryan and Deci 2001, Kauppi et al. 2023, Schramme 2023). Even models that adopt a ‘holistic’ orientation typically locate well-being within the individual, examining the individuals’ physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual dimensions of health. Mino-pimâtisiwin still attends to these dimensions of health but also understands them as emerging through reciprocal relationships with family, community, culture, land, and more-than-human relations. By articulating these relational dimensions, this analysis contributes to health promotion scholarship by showing how well-being may be conceptualized through the interconnected conditions that sustain life.

Collective thriving further distinguishes mino-pimâtisiwin from individual models of wellness. The good life is a relational condition in which the well-being of individuals is inseparable from that of families, communities, cultures, and the natural world (Hansen 2022, Ross 2022, Cheechoo 2023). Upholding responsibilities, maintaining balance, and participating in cultural and community life can therefore sustain both personal and collective well-being.

Implications for research, policy, and practice

Health promotion approaches grounded in mino-pimâtisiwin must attend to the relationships and conditions that make living well possible, moving beyond individual knowledge, attitudes, behaviors, or outcomes to support connection to family, community, culture, land, language, and more-than-human relations. This does not mean translating mino-pimâtisiwin into a standardized intervention model. It means recognizing that Indigenous well-being is shaped through relationships, responsibilities, and place-based knowledge systems that must be understood in context.

For policy and practice, this orientation supports Indigenous-led approaches that strengthen cultural continuity, land-based relationships, community participation, and intergenerational responsibilities. Illustratively, a community-led youth initiative might bring together land-based activity, time with Elders and family, language learning, and meaningful opportunities for youth to contribute, rather than defining success only through individual behavior change. Policy could likewise treat access to land, cultural programming, intergenerational knowledge sharing, and community-defined participation as conditions for health rather than optional cultural additions. These examples are illustrative rather than prescriptive; their relevance and form must be determined by particular communities, relationships, lands, and knowledge systems.

In research, mino-pimâtisiwin can support approaches that attend to the relationships, contexts, and knowledge systems through which Indigenous experiences of well-being are understood (Hyett et al. 2018, Andersen and Gabel 2023). For example, evaluation could examine belonging, reciprocal contribution, cultural continuity, connection to land, and collective capacity alongside individual health outcomes. Engaging the framework this way may support culturally grounded health promotion research while respecting the knowledge systems from which these teachings emerge.

Several sources emphasize that mino-pimâtisiwin is not restricted to particular abilities, social roles, or forms of participation, but may be pursued through relationships with others, land, and community (Kruse et al. 2019, Kress 2022, Cheechoo 2023). As Kress (2022) notes in discussions of disability and belonging, teachings associated with mino-pimâtisiwin emphasize that all members of the community have gifts and responsibilities that contribute to collective life. Within this perspective, disability is not viewed as a disqualification from living well but as one of many ways individuals participate in relational networks of care, knowledge, and community life. This framing is important for health promotion because it resists narrow understandings of well-being based on independence, productivity, individual achievement, or individual capacity alone. Within a relational understanding of the good life, well-being is grounded in belonging and participation.

Implications for measurement and evaluation

The empirical referents identified in this analysis may help researchers and practitioners recognize how mino-pimâtisiwin is expressed in practice, including through cultural participation, relationships with land and community, kinship networks, and ethical practices of reciprocity, respect, and care. However, these referents should not be treated as universal indicators or applied as a checklist across Indigenous communities. Their meaning depends on specific languages, lands, relationships, and cultural contexts.

This has important implications for health promotion research and evaluation. Measures of well-being that focus only on individual health status, behavior, or psychological outcomes may miss the relational conditions through which Indigenous well-being is sustained (Linton et al. 2016, Kauppi et al. 2023). At the same time, attempts to measure or evaluate mino-pimâtisiwin must avoid detaching the concept from the communities, teachings, and responsibilities that give it meaning. Future research may therefore benefit from engaging directly with Indigenous knowledge holders, scholars, and communities to determine how, whether, and in what contexts the concept should inform research, evaluation, policy, or practice (Hyett et al. 2018, Andersen and Gabel 2023).

Strengths and limitations

Several strengths and limitations should be considered. A key strength is the inclusion of both scholarly and grey literature, which allowed the analysis to engage community reports, educational materials, and other non-academic sources where teachings such as mino-pimâtisiwin are often discussed. The use of Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis also supported attention to how the concept is represented across disciplinary and contextual settings (Rodgers 1989, Richmond et al. 2022), while the lead author’s Métis and Cree positionality supported reflexive engagement with the cultural and relational significance of the teaching.

At the same time, as a literature-based concept analysis, this study examines how mino-pimâtisiwin has been represented in scholarly and grey literature rather than how it is understood and lived within specific Indigenous communities. Its meanings are grounded in Indigenous languages, cultural teachings, and community relationships, and cannot be fully captured through literature alone (Hyett et al. 2018, Andersen and Gabel 2023). Although the analysis engaged literature referencing both mino-pimâtisiwin and mino-bimaadiziwin, the interpretation presented here is shaped by the lead author’s positionality as a Métis and Cree scholar and should not be understood as a universal or pan-Indigenous definition of the concept.

Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis attends to how concepts shift across time and contexts, but the literature offered limited insight into the historical evolution of mino-pimâtisiwin. This likely reflects the fact that teachings associated with mino-pimâtisiwin are often transmitted through oral traditions, cultural practices, and community relationships rather than through written academic literature (Hart 1999, Hansen 2022, Andersen and Gabel 2023). Because references to mino-pimâtisiwin appear across diverse disciplines and publication types, sources reflected varying levels of conceptual depth and contextual explanation.

Finally, mino-pimâtisiwin remains a living teaching whose meanings continue to evolve as Indigenous peoples sustain cultural teachings, strengthen community relationships, and assert self-determination. The findings should therefore be understood as one stage in an ongoing conversation, not a definitive, static definition. Continued engagement with Indigenous knowledge systems, languages, and communities is essential to understanding how this relational philosophy guides living well with one another, the land, and the broader living world.

Conclusion

This concept analysis examined how mino-pimâtisiwin is understood and applied across scholarly and grey literature. The findings suggest that mino-pimâtisiwin is best understood not simply as ‘the good life’ or as an individual state of well-being, but as a relational process grounded in responsibilities to human and more-than-human relations. The analysis identified four defining attributes: relationality, relational responsibilities, balance and harmony, and collective thriving. Together, these findings clarify mino-pimâtisiwin as a wholistic and relational framework of well-being in which the well-being of individuals, families, communities, cultures, and the natural world are mutually sustaining. For health promotion, this analysis underscores the importance of approaches that support the relational, cultural, ecological, and community conditions through which Indigenous well-being is lived and sustained. While this analysis offers one interpretation grounded in the literature and the author’s positionality, mino-pimâtisiwin remains a living teaching that continues to be shaped within Indigenous communities. Ongoing engagement with Indigenous knowledge systems, languages, and communities remains essential to future use in research, policy, or practice.
