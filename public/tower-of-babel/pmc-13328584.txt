Physics-guided resampling and asymmetric fusion for ultra-high-frequency bolt-loosening detection
Authors: Zihao Luo, Xinrui Shi, Kai Xie, Chang Wen, Sixiang Liu, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable detection of bolt loosening in safety-critical infrastructure requires monitoring that captures microsecond-scale transients. However, processing 1 MHz vibration signals at the edge presents a fundamental dilemma: standard downsampling can smear sparse diagnostic impulses, whereas full-bandwidth processing is often computationally prohibitive. Here we propose PGRF-Net, a physics-guided deep learning framework that reconciles transient preservation with extreme compression. PGRF-Net integrates (1) a physics-guided resampling operator for high-rate vibration signals (e.g., 150 : 1 compression); (2) a heterogeneous gradient–spectral tensor representation that augments time–frequency information with morphology-aware channels; and (3) an asymmetric fusion module over two representations derived from the same signal (pseudo-image representation + waveform representation). On a six-class 1 MHz PVDF vibration dataset (109,668 segments; temporal-split test = 16,131), PGRF-Net reaches a best-run clean test accuracy of 95.12% under a block-wise temporal split. A strict file-disjoint split is also reported as a cross-scene hold-out protocol. On an independent Zenodo benchmark (3-fold file-disjoint CV, 9 runs per experiment), we evaluate the proposed pipeline together with three feature-engineering controls (Exp 501–503). These results support a practical compression–learning pipeline for industrial monitoring where both transient fidelity and computational efficiency are required.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Bolted connections are ubiquitous in engineering structures and mechanical assemblies because they are convenient, serviceable, and load-bearing. However, bolt loosening under dynamic loading and environmental influences remains a major cause of degradation and failure in bridges, wind-turbine towers, pipelines, and manufacturing equipment1. The mechanics of self-loosening—a coupled process involving preload loss, frictional slip, and dynamic excitation—have been studied extensively, showing a progressive reduction in clamping force and eventual loss of joint integrity1. Reliable and timely detection of bolt loosening is therefore essential for structural health monitoring (SHM) and condition-based maintenance.

A broad spectrum of detection techniques have been proposed. Reviews of bolted-connection monitoring commonly group approaches into three families2: (1) physics-based inspection using ultrasonic, guided-wave, acoustic, or electromechanical impedance methods3–5; (2) vibration-based modal and signal-processing techniques that examine shifts in natural frequency, damping, or energy distribution6,7; and (3) vision-based and data-driven methods that exploit images or learned features for automated assessment8–10. Each family offers distinct advantages: ultrasonic and guided-wave methods provide physically interpretable metrics for clamping force or contact state3,4, whereas vibration-based methods can be implemented with modest sensor infrastructure and are sensitive to contact nonlinearities11,12. In field deployments, however, performance can degrade due to coupling variability, environmental noise, and the subtlety of early-stage loosening signatures.

Vision and image-based solutions have attracted increasing interest because they can non-intrusively capture geometric and surface cues associated with loosening (e.g., head rotation, gap formation) and can be scaled using modern imaging hardware8,13. Several studies demonstrate effective bolt rotation detection and localization using classical image processing and machine learning pipelines8,13, and more recent work integrates deep convolutional neural networks and graphical models to improve robustness under viewpoint and illumination changes10,14,15. Despite these advances, purely visual methods are inherently limited in detecting micro-loosening or internal preload loss when no apparent surface change occurs; therefore, they are best suited as part of a multimodal inspection strategy.

Ultrasonic and piezoelectric techniques remain indispensable for quantitative assessment of bolt preload and contact state. Combined longitudinal/transverse ultrasonic probing, time-of-flight, and velocity estimation have been used to infer clamping force and detect loosening with high sensitivity under controlled conditions3,4. Piezo-impedance and actuator–sensor schemes enable local condition monitoring and residual-life estimation by tracking electromechanical coupling variations5,16,17. Vibro-acoustic modulation and time-reversal approaches further enhance the detection of weak nonlinear contact features associated with early looseness17,18. Nevertheless, these methods typically require careful sensor coupling and calibration, and their performance can degrade in complex, noisy operational environments.

Vibration-signal analysis and data-driven models address these limitations by leveraging temporal patterns and statistical signatures in measured responses. Time–frequency representations (e.g., STFT, wavelets) and energy-concentration measures provide compact descriptors for transient and cyclostationary phenomena associated with loosened joints11,12. Sparse factorization and tensor methods can emphasize impulsive or localized spectral content relevant to contact faults19. Recent tool-condition and milling-fault studies further strengthen this direction, including degradation-state modeling for tool wear and RUL prediction, ViT-based semi-supervised milling diagnosis, and burst-informed acoustic-emission diagnostics20–22. For industrial assemblies, impact-response monitoring in closed pipeline networks and wearable impedance plus multi-channel fusion in steel frames highlight progress toward deployable field diagnostics23,24. In bolt monitoring, however, signatures that separate fine-grained loosening states can be weak and easily masked by ambient noise or domain shift, which can limit generalization.

Two recurring challenges emerge from the literature. First, feature imbalance and dominance: when representations are fused naively, high-dimensional cues can dominate and suppress weaker but diagnostically important transient information3,10. Second, scale sensitivity and temporal sparsity: micro-loosening can manifest as sparse impulsive events at multiple temporal scales, and fixed-scale encoders or single-resolution spectrograms may under-sample these events11,19,25. Domain shifts (sensor drift, sampling differences, and environmental perturbations) further degrade the stability of learned representations.

To advance bolted-joint monitoring toward robust, fine-grained condition classification, recent studies emphasize stronger time–frequency feature engineering and fusion across representations or sensing channels rather than relying on a single handcrafted descriptor3,6,10,23,24,26–28. In practical settings, fusion must be designed to preserve weak but diagnostically crucial transient cues while leveraging more stable complementary cues (e.g., waveform morphology vs. time–frequency textures), and evaluation protocols must minimize leakage due to temporal autocorrelation and overlapping windows. On the feature front, higher-order and sparsity-aware decompositions (e.g., low-rank + sparse separation) and phase-sensitive descriptors have shown potential to disentangle intrinsic structural components from transient interference19. Additionally, multi-resolution training paradigms and hybrid encoder stacks (e.g., CNN Bi-LSTM attention/Transformer) have been proposed to capture both local impulsive events and long-range temporal dependencies29–32.

Despite these advancements, a critical gap remains in processing ultra-high-frequency (1 MHz) vibration signals for bolt loosening detection. While high-rate sampling is important for capturing micro-transients indicative of early-stage loosening, it substantially increases computational and memory costs. Conventional averaging-based downsampling can attenuate sparse impulses, whereas relying on lower-rate sensing can miss key micro-transient cues. This creates a practical dilemma: balancing computational efficiency with transient preservation—a challenge that existing methods11,12,19 do not directly address.

Motivated by these observations, we introduce PGRF-Net (Physics-Guided Resampling and Asymmetric Fusion Network), a deep learning framework designed for ultra-high-frequency bolt loosening detection. To avoid ambiguity with PDE-constrained physics-informed neural networks, we use the term physics-guided for this signal-processing-driven design. PGRF-Net builds on prior work in ultrasonic/vibration monitoring3–5,16–18, time–frequency analysis11,12,19, and modern fault diagnosis architectures29–33, while explicitly targeting the challenges of 1 MHz signals. The main contributions are:

1. Physics-guided adaptive resampling: a resampling strategy that compresses 1 MHz vibration signals by 150 : 1 while preserving impulse-like transients via a hybrid peak/energy operator.

2. Heterogeneous gradient–spectral representation: a 5-channel tensor that combines time–frequency magnitude, time/frequency gradients, a normalized energy map, and a time-domain embedding to retain complementary cues.

3. Asymmetric attention-based fusion: an attention mechanism that fuses pseudo-image and waveform representations derived from the same signal to mitigate representation imbalance and improve robustness.

In addition to the model, we provide an end-to-end pipeline and a C#-based desktop application for real-time inference, illustrating practical feasibility for edge deployments.

Methods

Experimental setup and data acquisition

We collect ultra-high-frequency vibration signals using a PVDF film sensor (LDT0-028K) attached to a bolted joint. Signals are sampled at 1 MHz. Six bolt states are created using controlled torque settings (Severe Loose, Loose, Critical, Transition, Secure, Over-Tight). Under the main setting (compression 150 : 1), this yields 109,668 segments in total, with a temporal-split test set of 16,131 segments. For each state, five independent acquisition files (run01–run05) are recorded under different scene conditions (environment and setup-state variations), so each file is treated as a scene-level unit in split design. Multi-representation learning and fusion

Although the architecture contains two branches, both are derived from the same vibration signal: (i) a pseudo-image branch built from time–frequency and gradient-enhanced representations; and (ii) a 1D signal branch that processes the resampled waveform. We therefore describe PGRF-Net as multi-representation learning rather than true multi-sensor multimodal fusion.

Evaluation protocol and data integrity

High-rate vibration data exhibit strong temporal autocorrelation; naive random shuffling or intra-file overlap can leak near-duplicate windows across train/val/test. We therefore use a block-wise temporal split within each file (temporal split) as the main protocol: each measurement file is split into contiguous train/val/test time blocks. We additionally report a file-disjoint split (no file appears in more than one split), which serves as a cross-scene hold-out test because each file corresponds to a distinct acquisition scene. Overlapping windows are used within each split only. For the main setting of Exp 222, we report repeated runs with three seeds (256, 2026, and 3407); mean ± standard deviation are reported where applicable. For scene-level validation, we adopt a Leave-One-Scene-Out (LOSO-5) protocol in which one scene (run01–run05) is used for testing, one distinct scene is used for validation, and the remaining three scenes are used for training in each fold; final performance is reported as mean ± standard deviation across folds. Accordingly, lower performance under the file-disjoint split is interpreted as scene-transfer difficulty (background, excitation path, and coupling shifts) rather than as evidence of data leakage.

Implementation and reproducibility

All models are implemented in PyTorch (2.8.0) with Python 3.12 and CUDA 12.8. Experiments are run on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. In addition to the in-house ablation suite (Exp 022–222), we report a Zenodo external suite on the public Lab Structure Dynamic Test dataset (record 15516419)34: classical reference models Exp 401–404 and feature-engineering controls Exp 501–503. The Zenodo suite follows strict file-disjoint 3-fold CV with three seeds per fold (9 runs per experiment). Scripts for generating all figures and tables are included in the released code repository.

Representation weighting analysis

Average weighting

Figure 20 shows that the average attention weights are strongly biased toward the waveform representation. This is expected because the waveform retains higher temporal resolution than the pseudo-image representation produced by time–frequency transforms and resizing. This average trend does not imply that the pseudo-image branch is dispensable; branch importance is sample-dependent and pseudo-image cues become critical on hard boundary samples.

Average attention weights under the temporal split. For many samples, the model assigns higher weight to the waveform representation than to the pseudo-image representation derived from STFT features.

Outlier cases

However, averages can obscure behavior on challenging boundary cases. The outlier analysis in Fig. 21 shows that attention can shift toward the pseudo-image branch for hard samples, especially near critical operating boundaries where misclassification risk is highest.

Outlier analysis of attention weights. While the waveform representation dominates on average, the pseudo-image branch can receive substantially higher weight for boundary cases (e.g., the highest-ranked Severe Loose outlier), indicating complementary cues from the time–frequency representation.

For hard samples—particularly near the boundaries of Severe Loose/Critical/Transition/Secure—the attention mechanism assigns substantially higher weight to the pseudo-image representation (up to in our analysis). This supports the role of the pseudo-image branch as a safety-relevant complementary channel in ambiguity-prone operating regions. We note that this analysis is descriptive; further controlled studies are required to quantify boundary-risk reduction under deployment shifts.

Discussion

PGRF-Net targets three practical challenges in ultra-high-frequency bolt loosening detection: (1) computational efficiency versus transient preservation, (2) representation imbalance between time–frequency textures and waveform cues, and (3) fine-grained state discrimination under domain shift and noise. The hybrid resampling and heterogeneous representation aim to retain sparse impact signatures in the compressed domain (Fig. 7), while the fusion module aggregates complementary cues from two representations derived from the same signal. Regarding data integrity, the file-disjoint split control (Table 10 and Fig. 10) shows that cross-scene generalization is substantially more difficult than intra-file temporal generalization, plausibly due to scene-dependent background and acquisition differences across scene-level files. Robustness under controlled AWGN injection is summarized in Table 11 and Fig. 13; Exp 222 is strongest across all reported SNR levels, but all models still degrade in low-SNR regimes. In the independent Zenodo benchmark (Table 13 and Figs. 15, 16, 17, 18, 19), all tested pipelines exhibit a common challenging fold (d15_cfg22), suggesting persistent condition-shift sensitivity beyond the primary platform. Finally, the remaining ambiguity between Secure and Over-Tight is consistent with smaller vibration changes at high preload, which can limit separability using vibration alone.

Study limitations

Two limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, although the dataset contains five scene-level files per state and file-disjoint evaluation therefore measures within-platform cross-scene generalization, all data are still collected on one bolt platform with one primary sensor configuration. Consequently, current evidence does not yet establish cross-sensor or cross-device generalization. A dedicated multi-sensor and multi-device dataset for this task is under construction.

Second, although we report two external suites (Exp 301–307 on our dataset and Exp 401–404/501–503 on the public Zenodo dataset), this is not yet an exhaustive protocol-matched benchmark against all recent methods. Specifically, the Zenodo experiments emphasize strict split control and a controlled A/B/C feature-engineering comparison, while broader cross-device retuning and domain adaptation remain future work. Therefore, these results are best interpreted as complementary evidence rather than a definitive universal ranking.

Real-world deployment feasibility

Practical monitoring systems often require on-device inference under limited compute budgets. To support deployment, we implemented a standalone desktop application in C# (.NET 10.0) that integrates an ONNX-exported model for real-time inference and USB-based data acquisition. On AMD Ryzen 5 9600X, the CPU-only benchmark reports a mean latency of 51.17 ms per sample (batch = 1, about 19.54 samples/s) and 27.48 samples/s at batch = 4 (Table 16), providing a reproducible software-side efficiency reference for this deployment setting.

Conclusion

PGRF-Net addresses a practical bottleneck in bolt loosening monitoring: learning from 1 MHz vibration signals under extreme compression while retaining transient signatures that correlate with contact dynamics. The proposed pipeline integrates a physics-guided hybrid resampling operator, a heterogeneous gradient–spectral representation, and an asymmetric fusion module over two complementary representations derived from the same signal. Under the main block-wise temporal split, PGRF-Net reaches a best-run clean test accuracy of 95.12% and a three-seed clean mean of 94.91% ± 0.27%. A strict file-disjoint split is additionally reported as a cross-scene hold-out control and highlights the difficulty of scene transfer even within the same hardware setup. On the Zenodo benchmark with 3-fold file-disjoint CV and three seeds per fold, clean macro-F1 spans 0.757–0.849 across Exp 401–404 and 0.809–0.825 across Exp 501–503, while the d15_cfg22 fold and low-SNR settings remain difficult. Overall, these findings support the proposed design within the evaluated settings, while broader cross-device validation and fully protocol-matched benchmarking remain important future work.
