Reconstruction of chronic tibialis anterior tendon rupture using a degradable synthetic matrix: A surgical technique
Authors: Maliha Arzumand, Ryan McBride
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tibialis anterior tendon (TAT) ruptures are uncommon and may occur acutely or present in a chronic, neglected form. Chronic ruptures, generally defined as those present for more than four weeks, are frequently associated with tendon retraction, degenerative stump tissue, and a segmental defect that precludes primary end-to-end repair. Established reconstructive options include tendon transfer, autograft interposition, and allograft interposition, each carrying trade-offs in donor site morbidity, tissue availability, and cost. We describe reconstruction of a chronic insertional TAT rupture in a 68-year-old male using a degradable synthetic polycaprolactone-based polyurethane urea matrix (FlexBand; Artelon, Inc., Marietta, GA, USA) secured to the medial cuneiform with two bone anchors and sutured to a debrided and lengthened proximal tendon stump. At final documented follow-up of 12 weeks, the patient ambulated without foot slap or compensatory gait deviation, with dorsiflexion and plantarflexion clinically symmetric to the contralateral limb and no restriction in activities of daily living. This report describes the technical feasibility of the approach.

Introduction

The tibialis anterior tendon (TAT) originates from the lateral condyle of the tibia and inserts on the base of the first metatarsal and the inferior aspect of the medial cuneiform. It is the primary dorsiflexor and an invertor of the foot, contributing to balance, clearance of the foot during swing phase, and to controlled lowering of the forefoot after heel strike [1]. The tendon also absorbs energy during walking [2]. Its blood supply arises proximally from muscular branches of the anterior tibial artery and distally from medial tarsal branches [1]. This supply is not uniformly distributed. The posterior half of the tendon is vascularized along its full length, whereas the anterior half contains an avascular segment of 45 to 67 mm, and this watershed zone is where degeneration and spontaneous rupture typically occur [3].

Ruptures of the TAT are rare, accounting for approximately 10 of 1014 foot and ankle tendon ruptures in one reported series [4], [5]. Traumatic ruptures caused by laceration or blunt injury to the dorsal foot are recognized early and treated promptly [6]. Atraumatic ruptures behave differently. They occur in older patients, often without a discrete precipitating event, and produce vague anterior ankle discomfort with a pseudotumorous soft tissue prominence at the site of the retracted stump. Because the extensor digitorum longus and extensor hallucis longus partially compensate for loss of the TAT, dorsiflexion is weakened rather than abolished, and the deficit is easily attributed to a neurologic cause or dismissed altogether [7], [8]. Diagnosis is therefore frequently delayed by weeks to months, by which time retraction and stump degeneration have already occurred. Untreated rupture leads to steppage gait, foot slap, progressive flatfoot deformity, and pain [4].

Once a rupture is chronic, reconstructive strategies divide broadly into four categories, and selection among them is driven largely by the size of the residual defect and the quality of the remaining stump tissue. Direct end-to-end repair is preferred when it can be achieved without excessive tension, but it is seldom possible beyond four to six weeks because of myotendinous retraction; Markarian et al. reported superior results with operative treatment overall, although the cases amenable to primary repair were predominantly acute [6]. Tendon transfer, most commonly of the extensor hallucis longus, restores active dorsiflexion using local tissue and avoids graft cost, but sacrifices the function of the transferred tendon and requires an intact and expendable donor [5], [9]. Autograft interposition using the semitendinosus, gracilis, or a hemitendon of the peroneus longus provides autologous tissue with predictable incorporation, and several reports document reliable restoration of dorsiflexion [10], [11], [12]. Its cost is donor site morbidity, a second surgical field, and additional operative time. Allograft interposition avoids donor site morbidity and is available in the lengths required for large defects. Anagnostakos et al. reconstructed a chronic rupture with semitendinosus allograft and reported only mild residual dorsiflexion and plantarflexion strength deficit at one year [7]. Aderinto and Gross described independent, painless ambulation eight years after Achilles tendon allograft reconstruction of a 4 cm defect [8]. Burton and Aydogan reported gracilis allograft for a major defect [13], and Huh et al. published a series of chronic TAT ruptures reconstructed with allograft [14]. Allograft nevertheless carries cost, variability in donor tissue quality, dependence on tissue bank availability, and a small but non-zero risk of disease transmission.

Synthetic augmentation is the least established of these strategies in this particular indication. Degradable polymer matrices are supplied off the shelf in consistent dimensions and mechanical properties, require neither donor tissue nor a second surgical field, and are not subject to tissue bank availability. Polycaprolactone-based polyurethane urea (PUUR) matrices are engineered to approximate the elastic modulus of ligament rather than to maximize rigidity, on the rationale that a compliant scaffold shares load with healing tissue instead of shielding it from load [15]. Kelly et al. reviewed 105 consecutive foot and ankle soft tissue reconstructions augmented with a PUUR matrix and reported an adverse event rate of 12.4% with a single graft removal at a mean follow-up beyond six months [16]. Published experience in the TAT specifically remains limited to isolated reports. A further consideration in chronic TAT rupture is the rehabilitation timeline. Salvage procedures generally require prolonged postoperative immobilization to protect the reconstruction [10], and prolonged immobilization is itself associated with measurable muscle atrophy and delayed functional recovery [17].

We describe reconstruction of a chronic insertional TAT rupture using a degradable synthetic PUUR matrix anchored to the medial cuneiform. In this patient the proximal stump remained viable and of adequate quality after debridement, so the reconstruction required a means of bridging to the insertion rather than wholesale replacement of the tendon. A synthetic matrix was selected because it provided that bridge without sacrificing the extensor hallucis longus, without creating a donor site, and without dependence on allograft availability or donor tissue variability. We report the technique and the early clinical course.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old male presented with right anterior ankle discomfort that had worsened over several months, with a marked change four to six weeks before presentation when he experienced an acute increase in pain accompanied by a tearing sensation over the dorsum of the foot. He denied any injury, fall, or precipitating event. Since that episode he had noticed difficulty dorsiflexing the foot and a change in his gait. Past medical history included prostate adenocarcinoma, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and a ruptured spinal disc. Past surgical history included spinal disc surgery. He took no medications and specifically denied corticosteroid and fluoroquinolone exposure. He had no history of diabetes mellitus or inflammatory arthropathy. The systemic and pharmacologic factors most often implicated in atraumatic tendon rupture were therefore absent, leaving the anterior watershed zone of the tendon as the presumed substrate for degenerative failure.

On examination there was a palpable defect in the TAT at its insertion, with thickening of the tendon just proximal to the inferior extensor retinaculum corresponding to the retracted stump. Dorsiflexion was weak but present, consistent with partial compensation by the extensor digitorum longus and extensor hallucis longus. Gait examination demonstrated a mild drop foot with audible slapping of the right foot at initial contact. He ambulated without an assistive device and had not been braced. Formal manual muscle testing grades, goniometric range of motion, and a validated pain score were not recorded at the initial encounter, which we acknowledge as a limitation of this retrospective report.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a tear of the TAT at its insertion with approximately 1.7 cm retraction of the proximal stump.

Non-operative management with an ankle-foot orthosis and operative reconstruction were both discussed. The patient declined bracing and elected surgical reconstruction, citing the wish to restore active dorsiflexion rather than accommodate the deficit. Approximately 8 to 10 weeks had elapsed between the presumed rupture and surgery, and the injury was therefore classified as chronic.

Surgical technique

Description of the augmentation device

The device used was a FlexBand degradable synthetic matrix (Artelon, Inc., Marietta, GA, USA). It is a knitted mesh wet-spun from polycaprolactone-based polyurethane urea fibers. The knit can be cut to length and width without unraveling, so the strip is sized to the defect intraoperatively. Its porosity is intended to permit host cellular ingrowth. The copolymer is also more compliant than the non-degradable synthetics used in earlier tendon and ligament reconstructions, such as polyester and polyethylene, with an elastic modulus closer to that of native ligament. The matrix undergoes hydrolytic degradation over an estimated four to six years, during which load is progressively transferred from the implant to the ingrown host tissue [15].

Two points regarding fixation principles warrant emphasis. First, the matrix is a reinforcement scaffold rather than a load-bearing tendon substitute. Initial mechanical strength of the construct is supplied by the suture and anchor fixation, and the matrix contributes progressively as host tissue incorporates into it. Second, in the present case the matrix was used to bridge a segmental defect rather than to reinforce an intact repair. This application is consistent with the general indication for soft tissue reinforcement but extends beyond the augmentation configurations in which the device has been most extensively reported, and we discuss the implications of this below.

Operative technique

The patient was placed supine under ankle-block anesthesia with a tourniquet applied to the right thigh. The operative leg was prepped and draped in standard sterile fashion. A longitudinal incision was made directly over the course of the tendon, centered on the palpable defect. Blunt dissection was carried through the subcutaneous tissue with care to identify and protect neurovascular structures, and the extensor retinaculum was incised longitudinally to expose the tibialis anterior tendon.

The ruptured ends of the TAT were identified. Preoperative MRI had described an insertional tear with 1.7 cm retraction; intraoperatively, two discrete stumps were encountered with a segmental defect that was visibly larger than predicted by imaging and that precluded end-to-end repair even with the foot held in maximal dorsiflexion (Fig. 1). The defect was not measured intraoperatively, which we acknowledge as a limitation. Transfer of the extensor hallucis longus was considered but not pursued, as the proximal tibialis anterior stump remained viable and of adequate quality for reconstruction and transfer would have sacrificed a functioning tendon. The distal insertion site was identified over the medial aspect of the medial cuneiform. Under fluoroscopic guidance, two drill holes were created in a vertical orientation, one superior and one inferior, on the medial surface of the medial cuneiform for anchor placement (Fig. 2). Fluoroscopy confirmed satisfactory position and trajectory prior to drilling. Two anchors were then placed at these sites following the manufacturer's guidelines, securing the synthetic matrix to serve as the new distal fixation point (Fig. 3). The two free tails of the matrix were brought proximally and sutured side-to-side to the debrided and lengthened proximal tibialis anterior tendon stump using 2-0 FiberWire, with the foot held in dorsiflexion to restore physiologic tension (Fig. 4). A separate FiberWire suture was used to tubularize and reinforce the reconstructed segment along its length. The repair was taken through a range of motion and was stable, without gapping or undue tension (Fig. 5). The tendon sheath was coapted with 3-0 Vicryl. Subcuticular running skin tags were placed with 4-0 Vicryl, and the skin was approximated with 4-0 nylon in a running locking stitch. A non-adherent dressing was applied, followed by an incisional negative pressure wound therapy device. Results

The patient remained non-weightbearing in a posterior splint for 2 weeks, then progressed to protected weightbearing in a walking boot. At 4 weeks, dorsiflexion strength was recorded as 5/5 on manual muscle testing. At 6 weeks he was permitted to weightbear as tolerated and began physical therapy directed at range of motion and progressive strengthening. At 12 weeks, gait examination demonstrated intact dorsiflexion during swing phase with no residual foot drop, no foot slap, and no compensatory gait deviation.

This 12-week assessment is documented in the supplementary gait video, which shows the patient ambulating with a symmetric, unassisted gait without foot slap or compensatory deviation. Active and passive dorsiflexion and plantarflexion were clinically symmetric to the contralateral limb on side-to-side comparison, and he reported no restriction in activities of daily living and a return to his preoperative activity level (Fig. 6). No wound healing complications, infection, or re-rupture occurred during the follow-up period.

Assessment throughout was clinical. Discussion

Chronic TAT ruptures present late, and by the time of surgery, retraction and stump degeneration usually make end-to-end repair impossible [6]. The central technical question is therefore how to restore continuity to the insertion, and the available answers differ chiefly in what they cost the patient.

Tendon transfer, most often of the extensor hallucis longus, has been reported with favorable outcomes and uses local tissue at no graft cost [5], [9]. It requires accepting the loss of hallux extension, and it presupposes that the proximal TAT is not usable. In the present case the proximal stump was viable after debridement, so transfer would have sacrificed a functioning tendon to solve a problem that a bridging construct could solve without it. Autograft interposition, using semitendinosus, gracilis, or a peroneus longus hemitendon, offers autologous tissue with predictable biological incorporation and is well supported in the literature [10], [11], [12]. It requires a second surgical field, adds operative time, and carries donor site morbidity, considerations that weigh more heavily in an older patient with a degenerative issue rather than an athletic injury. Allograft avoids donor site morbidity and supplies whatever length the defect demands [7], [8], [13], [14]. Andronic et al. found comparable outcomes between autograft and allograft reconstruction in 21 patients at a mean of 82 months, though the autograft subgroup had significantly longer follow-up and more untreated contralateral disease [18]. Allograft depends on tissue bank supply, varies in donor quality, carries a small residual risk of disease transmission, and is expensive.

A degradable synthetic matrix balances these considerations differently. It is available off the shelf in consistent dimensions and mechanical properties, eliminating both donor site morbidity and donor tissue variability. It requires no second incision and no graft harvest, shortening operative time. Its stated four to six year hydrolytic degradation profile is intended to allow progressive load transfer to ingrown host tissue rather than permanent stress shielding, which distinguishes it from earlier non-degradable synthetics whose failure modes included fatigue rupture and particulate synovitis. Against this, three limitations of the material deserve explicit statement. First, a synthetic matrix has no intrinsic biological healing capacity; incorporation depends entirely on host tissue ingrowth into the scaffold, and unlike autograft it contributes no cells or matrix of its own. Second, the published evidence base in the TAT is thin. Kelly et al. reviewed 105 foot and ankle reconstructions using a PUUR matrix and reported a 12.4% adverse event rate with one graft removal. That cohort consisted largely of spring ligament, Achilles, and Broström procedures rather than TAT reconstruction, and mean follow-up was just over six months [16]. Third, the device is indicated for reinforcement of soft tissue, and its use here to bridge a segmental defect, while consistent with that general indication, extends beyond the augmentation configurations in which it has been most extensively described. Surgeons considering this approach should weigh that distinction. A direct cost comparison against allograft is difficult to state reliably. Institutional pricing for both synthetic devices and allograft tissue varies substantially by contract and region, and we did not have access to procurement data for this case.

The discrepancy between preoperative MRI and intraoperative findings in this case merits attention, because it has practical implications for surgical planning. Imaging described an insertional tear with 1.7 cm retraction, a pattern that might reasonably prompt a plan for primary insertional repair. What was encountered was a segmental defect between two discrete stumps that could not be closed end-to-end. MRI is known to underestimate the functional gap in chronic ruptures, where the retracted proximal stump forms a fibrotic mass that can be difficult to distinguish from intact tendon, and where interposed scar may appear as continuity. The practical consequence is that patients with chronic TAT rupture should be consented for graft reconstruction and the necessary implants should be available in the operating room, irrespective of whether imaging suggests a simple insertional repair will suffice. We did not measure the defect intraoperatively, and future reports should do so.

Coaptation of the tendon sheath was performed as part of the reconstruction. Experimental work on tendon healing indicates that an intact sheath influences the vascular response and adhesion formation during healing, with restoration of a vascular pattern approaching normal by 16 weeks in sheathed tendons [19]. No wound healing complications occurred in this case, which may relate in part to the use of incisional negative pressure wound therapy over an anterior ankle incision with limited soft tissue coverage. Reported complications of TAT reconstruction include wound healing problems and infection, stiffness, peritendinous adhesion formation, weakness, superficial peroneal neuritis, donor site complications where transfer or autograft is used, and re-rupture. Given the small number of published cases and the heterogeneity of techniques, true complication rates remain unknown [20].

Two broader contextual points bear on reports of this kind. Tendon and its osseous insertion remodel in response to their mechanical environment, and the histopathology of congenital and developmental limb conditions has contributed to understanding how that remodeling occurs [21]. These principles are relevant when a reconstruction relies on host tissue ingrowth into an implanted scaffold at a tendon-to-bone interface, as here. Separately, the accumulation of meaningful evidence for rare injuries such as TAT rupture depends on the ability to aggregate small numbers of cases across institutions and to trace implanted devices reliably over the years during which they degrade. Rovere et al. have described how distributed ledger technologies might support implant traceability, registry integrity, and data provenance in orthopedic practice [22]. For a degradable device with a four to six year resorption profile implanted in a rare indication, the capacity to track outcomes at that horizon is not incidental; it is the mechanism by which techniques such as the one described here would eventually be validated or abandoned.

Limitations

This report describes a single case and its limitations should be read accordingly. Outcome assessment was clinical and retrospective. Validated patient-reported outcome measures, goniometric range of motion in degrees, and instrumented dorsiflexion strength testing were not obtained, and the symmetry of motion reported here is a qualitative side-to-side examination finding. The intraoperative defect was not measured. Follow-up of 12 weeks is short for a tendon reconstruction and is well below the interval at which graft attenuation, re-rupture, or late failure would be expected to manifest; it is also far shorter than the four to six year degradation profile of the implanted matrix, so nothing in this report speaks to the behavior of the construct once the scaffold has resorbed. Comparative claims cannot be made from a single case, and the favorable early course described here should not be read as evidence that this technique is superior to tendon transfer, autograft, or allograft reconstruction.

Conclusion

Reconstruction of a chronic insertional tibialis anterior tendon rupture using a degradable synthetic polyurethane urea matrix anchored to the medial cuneiform was technically feasible in this patient and was followed by return of symmetric ankle motion and unrestricted ambulation without foot slap at 12 weeks. The technique avoids donor site morbidity, a second surgical field, and dependence on allograft availability. These observations establish feasibility only. Comparative studies with larger cohorts, validated outcome measures, and follow-up extending beyond the degradation interval of the implant are required before the role of synthetic matrix reconstruction in this injury can be defined.
