Efficacy and safety of transcutaneous supraorbital nerve stimulation versus topiramate for migraine prevention: a non-inferiority randomized trial
Authors: Yuchen Ye, Xinxin Xie, Hao Huang, Han Zhang, Ying Yu, Wensheng Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine remains a predominant neurological disorder that requires effective preventive therapies. Although topiramate is a well - recognized first - line pharmacotherapeutic approach, its use is often restricted by adverse reactions. Transcutaneous supraorbital nerve stimulation (tSNS) has emerged as a non - pharmacological alternative; however, its comparative efficacy remains unclear. This research aimed to directly compare the efficacy and safety of tSNS and topiramate in migraine prevention.MethodsIn this randomized, open - label, active - controlled trial, 232 adult migraineurs were randomly allocated to either tSNS or oral 25 mg topiramate for a 4 - week duration, followed by an 8 - week follow - up. The primary outcome was the proportion of responders. Secondary outcomes included changes such as monthly migraine days, severe headache days, days of acute medication use, and scores on the Headache Impact Test − 6. Non - inferiority testing with a pre - specified margin of 10% was also performed.ResultsThe tSNS group exhibited significantly lower responder rates than the low - dose topiramate group (20.6% vs. 33.9%), surpassing the 10% non - inferiority margin (p = 0.712, 95% CI: − 0.260 to − 0.007). Logistic regression affirmed the superior efficacy of 25 mg daily topiramate compared to the tSNS (adjusted OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.22–4.42, p = 0.011). When analyzing the secondary outcomes, the tSNS seemed to display efficacy in reducing severe headache days, while topiramate had a more pronounced effect on reducing moderate - severe headache days. In comparison with the tSNS, topiramate showed superior ability in reducing acute medication usage. The Headache Impact Test − 6 scores were comparable between the two groups. The tSNS presented significantly superior safety, with an adverse event rate of 4.3% as opposed to 22.4% in the topiramate group.ConclusionsThe tSNS seems to be less effective than even low - dose topiramate in achieving overall migraine prevention. Background

Migraine is a neurological disorder with a high prevalence, globally affecting approximately 1.158 billion individuals, corresponding to a prevalence rate of 15.4% of the population [1]. It is typified by recurrent bouts of moderate-to-severe headache, commonly accompanied by nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia, which result in substantial disability and a diminished quality of life [2].

For patients experiencing frequent migraine attacks, preventive therapy is of utmost importance to reduce the frequency and severity of attacks and the reliance on acute medications [3]. Current clinical guidelines recommend pharmacological agents, including topiramate, valproic acid, and propranolol, as first - line prophylactic treatments [3]. Nevertheless, patient adherence to these therapies is often compromised by adverse effects, with 43% reported for valproate and 7.7% for topiramate [4]. This limitation presents a particular challenge for individuals with drug hypersensitivity, pregnancy - related concerns, or medication - overuse headache, thus necessitating the exploration of non - pharmacological alternatives [5].

Neuromodulation has emerged as a promising strategy for migraine prevention [6], specifically transcutaneous supraorbital nerve stimulation (tSNS) [7]. It has attracted attention due to its non - invasive characteristic and favorable safety performance. In 2014, the first tSNS device obtained approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of episodic migraine, based on evidence indicating its ability to modulate the activity of the trigeminal ganglion and thalamus [8]. Clinical investigations have verified its efficacy. One study reported a 75% response rate among chronic migraine patients [8]. Moreover, a multicenter prospective trial revealed that tSNS led to a significant reduction in migraines over a three - month period, especially in patients who were intolerant to or had suboptimal responses to topiramate [9]. Real - world data further corroborate its effectiveness, demonstrating a ≥ 50% reduction in monthly headache attacks and a decreased use of analgesics in a considerable proportion of patients [7].

Despite these promising findings, previous research has predominantly evaluated tSNS in isolation or within refractory populations, and there have been no randomized controlled trials directly comparing its efficacy with that of standard pharmacological prophylaxis. Both reports and our clinical practice have shown that topiramate, a widely used first-line therapy, is effective in migraine prophylaxis at doses as low as 25 mg in Asian populations [10, 11]. Therefore, it was selected as an active comparator to ensure favorable tolerability. We hypothesized that tSNS would be non-inferior to 25 mg topiramate with respect to the 50% responder rate, using a prespecified non-inferiority margin of 10%. Through this head-to-head comparison, this study endeavors to elucidate the role of tSNS in migraine management, filling a crucial gap in the literature and guiding evidence-based treatment decisions for patients who may benefit from non-pharmacological interventions.

Methods

Study design and implementation

This randomized, open-label, assessor-blinded, non-inferiority trial was carried out at the Department of Neurology, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China. The study protocol obtained ethical approval from the Tongji Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2021 - S203) and was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT02590939). All eligible participants provided written informed consent after receiving comprehensive explanations regarding study procedures.

Participant recruitment and randomization

Potential participants were systematically screened and recruited from the headache specialty outpatient clinic by trained research coordinators. After a 4 - week baseline screening period during which participants maintained structured headache diaries, eligible candidates were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either intervention group using a computer - generated block randomization sequence implemented via a secure electronic data capture system. To ensure strict allocation concealment, randomization records were solely managed by an independent data manager who had no involvement in patient care, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis.

Study phases and procedures

The trial consisted of three distinct phases: (1) Baseline phase (Weeks 1–4): Screening and assessment of baseline characteristics; (2) Active treatment phase (Weeks 5–8): Intervention implementation; and, (3) Follow-up phase (Weeks 9–16): Post - treatment monitoring.

To mitigate potential bias stemming from participant interaction, staggered clinic visit schedules were implemented, and separate waiting areas were designated for different treatment groups. All participants underwent standardized in - clinic evaluations at four - week intervals throughout the study period. Comprehensive documentation of study discontinuations was maintained via structured telephone follow-ups and electronic case report forms. Participant selection criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Individuals aged between 18 and 60 years, regardless of gender; (2) A documented migraine diagnosis that adheres to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd Edition (ICHD − 3) criteria (code 1.1 for migraine without aura or 1.2 for migraine with aura); (3) An average of two or more monthly migraine attacks over the preceding three months; (4) Willingness and capacity to provide written informed consent; (5) Absence of known contraindications to either tSNS or topiramate therapy.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Use of any migraine preventive medication within three months prior to screening; (2) Concurrent headache disorders such as tension type headache, medication overuse headache, and secondary headache disorders; (3) Active severe neurological or psychiatric conditions (e.g., epilepsy, major depressive disorder); (4) Significant systemic diseases affecting the cardiovascular, central nervous, hematopoietic, hepatic, or renal systems; (5) Current pregnancy or lactation.

Treatment protocols

The study employed a two - arm parallel design, with participants randomly assigned to either neuromodulation therapy or pharmacological intervention. In the neurostimulation group, subjects received tSNS using the DJTT - Ib device (Hengyang Dajing Medical Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.). The treatment protocol involved precise bilateral electrode placement over the supratrochlear and supraorbital nerve distributions on the forehead. Each 20 - minute daily session delivered electrical stimulation with the following parameters: a frequency of 60 Hz, a pulse width of 300 µsec, and a pulse duration of 500 µsec. The current intensity followed a ramped protocol, gradually increasing to a peak of 16 mA by the 14 - minute mark.

The active comparator group received standard pharmacological therapy with topiramate (Tuotai Pian, Xian Janssen Pharmaceutical Ltd.), administered as a once - daily 25 mg oral dose in the morning. This dosage regimen was selected based on its established efficacy in migraine prophylaxis and favorable tolerability profile at a lower dose [10].

To maintain clinical relevance and address acute symptom management, both treatment arms permitted the use of standardized rescue medications as needed. Study neurologists prescribed either ibuprofen (200 mg) or rizatriptan (5–10 mg) for breakthrough migraine attacks. Participants were required to maintain consistency in their acute medication choice throughout the trial duration.

All rescue medication use was meticulously recorded in daily headache diaries to monitor treatment adherence and assess potential confounding effects on outcome measures. Protocol adherence was defined as completing ≥ 24 sessions during the treatment month, with at least 5 sessions per week. Usage data were extracted from device timestamps and self - reports, then cross - verified with patient diaries during the follow - ups.

Data collection and outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary efficacy endpoint of the study was the 50% responder rate, which is defined as the proportion of participants who achieved at least a 50% reduction in monthly migraine days (MMD) during the 4 - week active treatment period compared to the 4 - week baseline phase. This dichotomous outcome was chosen as it represents a clinically significant threshold for the success of preventive treatment, in accordance with the guidelines of the International Headache Society.

Secondary outcomes

Comprehensive secondary outcomes were analyzed to evaluate the multi - dimensional effects of treatment: (1) Headache frequency: Monthly headache days, MMDs, and moderate - to - severe headache days (defined as episodes with pain intensity ≥ 4 on a 0–10 visual analog scale); (2) Acute medication utilization: Reduction in the monthly days requiring rescue medications; (3) Functional impact: Hamilton depression and anxiety rating scale, Pittsburgh sleep quality index, and headache impact test − 6 (HIT − 6) scores.

Safety monitor

All participants maintained structured headache diaries to prospectively document the following: treatment adherence and response patterns; occurrence of predefined adverse events (AEs), including nausea, dizziness, paresthesia (both systemic and local), fatigue, dyspepsia, somnolence, and bleeding; unanticipated adverse device effects or drug reactions, and delayed - onset AEs, etc. AE data were subjected to dual verification through diary entries that were reviewed weekly by research coordinators.

Statistical analysis

Study design and sample size calculation

Based on the above parameters, we recalculated the required sample size using PASS 2021 software and updated all relevant descriptions throughout the entire text. The sample size estimation was based on the following assumptions: the expected response rate of topiramate was set at 45.33%, compared to 22.81% for the placebo [12]. Evidence from studies in Asian populations indicated that low - dose (25 mg) topiramate had comparable efficacy to other dosage regimens [10]. Accordingly, the non - inferiority margin (δ) was set at 10%, with a one - sided α of 0.025 and a statistical power (1 - β) of 80%. Calculation using the fixed margin method showed that a minimum of 114 subjects was required per group. Considering the anticipated low dropout rate, the final planned sample size was set at 232 subjects per group.

Data analysis methodology

All statistical analyses were conducted by experienced biostatisticians using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp.) in accordance with a predefined analysis protocol. Initial data screening involved the assessment of distributional properties through the Kolmogorov - Smirnov Z test to determine appropriate analytical methodologies. Comparative analyses among treatment groups included: Independent t - tests for continuously distributed variables that followed a normal distribution; Mann - Whitney U tests for continuously distributed or ordinal variables that did not conform to a normal distribution; and Chi - square tests for comparisons of categorical variables.

Outcome reporting and analytical approach

Results were presented using standardized descriptive statistical measures: the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuously distributed variables with a normal distribution; the median (interquartile range, IQR) for non - normally distributed or ordinal variables; and frequency counts and percentages for categorical variables.

The primary efficacy analysis adhered to the intention - to - treat (ITT) principle. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) via the `mice` R package. We created five imputed datasets, incorporating all analysis variables plus auxiliary variables to improve imputation accuracy. Models were fitted separately to each dataset, and results were pooled using Rubin’s rules to obtain final estimates and standard errors that account for imputation uncertainty.

After multiple imputation, pooled logistic regression results indicated a significant treatment effect after adjusting for baseline age, sex, migraine frequency, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality. These adjusted analyses yielded odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% Wald confidence intervals (CIs). was also evaluated by per - protocol analysis and through Chi - square tests. Exploratory data comparison was performed for the secondary outcomes without multiple correction. The post - intervention data were compared using ANCOVA after controlling for the respective pre - intervention data. Throughout all analyses, a two - tailed p - value < 0.05 was employed as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

This randomized controlled trial was conducted at the headache specialty clinic of Tongji Hospital from September 2021 to January 2025. Approximately 93.1% of the enrolled participants completed the initial four - week active treatment phase, and 88.4% completed the entire eight - week study protocol, including follow - up assessments.

During the treatment period, the patients in the tSNS arm completed an average of 26.1 sessions, with 92.2% meeting the adherence requirement; the topiramate group completed an average of 26.8 sessions, with 93.1% achieving the protocol - defined frequency.

Baseline demographic and migraine characteristics were generally comparable between the two treatment groups, except for age, which was slightly higher in the tSNS group compared with the topiramate group (Table 1, p < 0.05). The subsequent efficacy analysis for the primary outcome was adjusted for age to account for this imbalance.

Comparative efficacy and clinical implications

According to the per-protocol analysis, a statistically significant difference of the 50% responder rate was revealed between treatment arms, with the tSNS group demonstrating a lower response rate (20.6%, 22/107) when comparing to the 25 mg topiramate (33.9%, 37/109). The between-group difference of -13.3% points exceeded the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 10%, thereby failing to demonstrate non-inferiority (p = 0.712, 95% CI: -0.260 to -0.007).

Using ITT analysis, after adjusting for baseline age, sex, migraine frequency, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality, the odds of achieving a ≥ 50% response were significantly lower in the tSNS group compared with the topiramate group (adjusted OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.22–4.42, p = 0.011). This also didn’t meet the non-inferiority criterion (p = 0.580, 95% CI: -0.236 to 0.012) The unadjusted chi-square test between groups gave a similar result (χ² = 5.114, p = 0.024; suppl-Table 1).

However, the tSNS also presents distinct therapeutic advantages that merit clinical consideration, as indicated by the reduction of MMDs and headache days relative to its baseline (Fig. 2 and suppl-Table 1). When comparing the reduced days between groups, the tSNS appears to demonstrate efficacy in reducing severe headache days (p = 0.043), whereas topiramate exhibited greater potency in reducing moderate - severe headache days (p = 0.002). The ANCOVA results supported that after controlling for pre - treatment data, sex, and age, there was a significant difference in post - intervention between the two groups (severe headache days, F = 5.552, p = 0.019, partial η² = 0.026; moderate - severe headache days, F = 5.882, p = 0.016, partial η² = 0.027).

It was shown that topiramate had a superior capacity to reduce acute medication usage, with (− 1.24 ± 2.09) days for topiramate versus (− 0.45 ± 1.59) days for tSNS (p < 0.001). Despite the differential effects on headache parameters, the HIT − 6 scores were comparable between the two groups (p = 0.682) (Fig. 2 and suppl-Table 1).

Safety and tolerability considerations

The significantly superior safety profile of tSNS relative to topiramate was demonstrated during the treatment, with self - reported adverse event (AE) rates of 4.3% vs. 22.4% ((p < 0.001). Although the discontinuation rates were similar between groups (6% vs. 7%), the substantial difference in AE - driven discontinuations (33% for tSNS vs. 85% for topiramate) highlights tSNS as a particularly appropriate option for patients.

Discussion

This study offers direct comparative evidence of tSNS versus topiramate for migraine prophylaxis, uncovering distinct therapeutic profiles that can guide clinical decision-making. While confirming the superior efficacy of low-dose topiramate in achieving conventional responses, our findings also establish tSNS as a clinically valuable alternative with distinctive advantages that complement existing pharmacological options. This may be attributed to its superior tolerability and the reduction of severe headache days.

Comparative efficacy of tSNS with topiramate

The tSNS has emerged as a promising non - pharmacological intervention for migraine management. It is particularly advantageous for patients with medication - refractory symptoms or contraindications to conventional therapies [9, 13]. Clinical evidence indicates its effectiveness in reducing the frequency, intensity, and duration of migraines. Multiple trials have reported a 30–50% decrease in monthly migraine days compared to sham stimulation [14]. Longitudinal studies further confirm its sustained prophylactic benefits, while preliminary findings suggest potential acute therapeutic effects when administered during the early stages of migraine attacks [15].

The current investigation reveals that although tSNS exhibits significant clinical benefits, it fails to demonstrate non - inferiority when compared to the first - line pharmacological prophylaxis with topiramate. The observed responder rate was substantially lower in the tSNS cohort than that in the topiramate group, indicating that topiramate is a more efficacious option for migraine prevention, even at a low dose. This difference in efficacy likely stems from their distinct mechanisms of action: topiramate functions as a comprehensive neuromodulator, inhibiting cortical spreading depression and blocking voltage - gated sodium/calcium channels [16]. In contrast, the tSNS predominantly modulates trigeminal - thalamic pathways via peripheral nerve stimulation [8]. The sustained therapeutic effects of topiramate extending beyond the 4 - week treatment period might explain its superiority in achieving long - term reduction of MMDs compared to tSNS.

Notwithstanding its relatively lower responder rate, tSNS demonstrates several unique therapeutic advantages that enhance its clinical utility. In this study, it showed a superior reduction in severe headache days during active treatment and seemed to provide preventive benefits when compared with the baseline [8, 17]. However, we have to speculate that the superiority of tSNS in reducing severe headache days may be debatable because participants were prone to use the stimulation when a headache was attacking. The use of a fixed low - dose (25 mg) instead of titrating to a tolerated higher dose might have constrained the full preventive potential of topiramate, thereby resulting in diminished benefits and limiting the generalizability of our conclusions to comparisons between tSNS and other doses of topiramate.

Safety and tolerability profile

The tSNS demonstrates a significantly superior safety profile, with only 4.3% of participants reporting AEs compared to 22.4% in the topiramate group. Notably, neurological side effects (e.g., paresthesia, cognitive dysfunction) were the primary reason for topiramate discontinuation, while tSNS - related AEs were predominantly mild and localized.

Considering that approximately 30% of patients discontinue traditional migraine prophylaxis due to intolerance [18], the tSNS represents a valuable therapeutic alternative for specific patient populations, possibly including pregnant women, individuals with polypharmacy, patients with contraindications to topiramate, and those with other special conditions [9, 19]. The minimal systemic side effects associated with tSNS may also enhance long - term treatment adherence, making it particularly suitable for patients who have difficulties with pharmacological interventions.

Comprehensive benefit of treatments

Although topiramate demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing the frequency of headache days, both interventions resulted in comparable improvements in the patients’ overall headache-related disability, as suggested by HIT-6 scores. This may be attributed to the fact that the tSNS significantly reduced the number of severe headache days, which heavily influence the quality of life. Additionally, the side effect profile of topiramate might have offset some of the quality-of-life benefits gained from headache reduction, whereas the non-invasive nature of nerve stimulation might offer better tolerability.

Limitation of this research

Several methodological limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. Although the low - dose topiramate regimen (25 mg) has demonstrated clinical effectiveness in the Asian population and Chinese medical practice, its precise efficacy requires validation through rigorously designed trials.

We initially positioned this study as a preliminary exploration. The 4 - week treatment and follow - up duration might be insufficient to evaluate effect patterns or sustained therapeutic effects. This short duration may underestimate the full therapeutic effect, especially for topiramate. The use of fixed stimulation parameters potentially restricted the optimization of tSNS efficacy, which might be achieved through individualized titration protocols.

As a preliminary exploratory investigation rather than a typical placebo - based randomized controlled trial (RCT), the open - label design may introduce selective bias resulting from participants’ treatment preferences and measurement bias in patient - reported outcomes. The single - center design, homogeneous study population, and exclusion of individuals with comorbidities may restrict the broader generalizability of the study outcomes. The absence of a justified non - inferiority margin may introduce uncertainty into the validity of the comparative analysis. The exploratory findings from secondary outcomes should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Furthermore, the extended study timeline due to disruptions from the COVID − 19 pandemic may impact data consistency. These limitations highlight the need for future large - scale RCTs with extended follow - up periods, optimized stimulation protocols, and more diverse patient cohorts to validate and expand upon these preliminary observations.

Conclusions

Taken together, topiramate demonstrates superior efficacy to tSNS in migraine prophylaxis, reaffirming its role as the first - line intervention. However, tSNS serves as a valuable alternative for patients with contraindications or intolerance to topiramate. Its favorable safety profile and dual efficacy in both preventive and acute management make it a significant addition to the therapeutic armamentarium.
