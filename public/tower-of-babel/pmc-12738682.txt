Mechanical and thermal behaviour of waste spent coffee ground filler reinforced vinyl-ester composites for civil construction applications
Authors: A M Arun Mohan, V Vignesh, N Nagaprasad, Ramaswamy Krishnaraj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis current study examines the addition of Spent Coffee Grounds filler (SCGs) as a primary reinforcement with vinyl ester (VE) composites using the vacuum bagging process with varying filler contents ranging from 5 to 25 wt%. The mechanical properties, such as tensile strength, modulus, flexural strength, impact strength (Izod), and hardness, were evaluated. Vinyl ester resin with 15 weight percent used coffee grounds filler has superior mechanical qualities, including tensile strength, tensile modulus, hardness of shore-D, flexural strength, and impact strength, according to a thorough analysis of the data. The SCGs/VE composites outperformed than other types of SCG-VE bio-composites in terms of tensile strength (40 MPa), flexural strength (64 MPa), impact strength (15 kJ/m2), and shore-D hardness (82), and this trend persisted even when the filler loadings reached 15 wt%. Compared to other types of SCG- VE bio-composites, the ones with the lowest thermal conductivity (0.154 Wm−1 K−1) and the greatest heat deflection temperature (62 °C). The enhanced HDT is a result of the well-distributed and excellent adherence of the SCG filler to the vinyl ester matrix; these properties increase the material’s resistance to heat distortion. Using Scanning Electron Microscopy, the filler-matrix interaction was analyzed.Keywords: Spent coffee grounds filler, Vinyl-ester, Vacuum bagging method, Mechanical properties, Thermal properties, SEM analysis, Civil applicationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

There is an increasing demand for environmentally friendly materials that are sustainable and beneficial for society. Therefore, the utilization of waste fillers reinforced polymer composites is receiving significant attention and given their environmental use potential, these materials show a good replacement for traditional composites. Spent coffee grounds (SCGs), a by-product of the coffee industry, presents a novel and abundant source of reinforcement material. According to the International Coffee Organization, global coffee consumption has continued to grow, reaching 177.8 million bags, a 1.25% increase compared to 2023. This rise in consumption is estimated to generate around 6.2 million tonnes of spent coffee grounds waste, considering that one coffee bag (60 kg) typically produces approximately 35 kg of SCGs1. In the future, consumption is expected to rise even more. The yearly output of spent coffee grounds (SCGs) in 2023 was expected to reach 60 million tons, making it the most commonly generated waste during coffee brewing2. Disposing such a huge volume of spent coffee grounds (SCGs) creates extensive environmental concerns worldwide due to methane emissions when the SCG waste decomposes3,4. To avoid this, effective waste monitoring strategies need to be developed. Since the conventionally used techniques of SCG waste disposal, such as landfilling, are not sustainable and hugely impact ecological deterioration5. There are several other practised disposal methods concerning the handling of spent coffee grounds (SCGs) around the world apart from landfilling, which include composting, biofuel generation, and application as a soil amendment. However, these methodologies have drawbacks as they are not cost-effective and cannot be scaled too much. So, working with industry, these can be validated even more to prove the technical and economic viability on a larger scale6. Spent coffee grounds (SCGs) make biodiesel and ethanolic biodiesel. They are also used in the manufacture of biochar and activated carbon. For various materials, SCGs act as a supplier of biopolymers and bio-composites. Their phenolic compounds are extracted for use in cosmetics and pharmaceuticals. Additionally, SCGs can be repurposed as a direct fuel source or for energy generation7,8.

Spent coffee grounds revealed desirable characteristics such as high strength, stiffness, and thermal stability and have shown promise as a natural filler for polymer composites9,10. The mechanical characteristics and overall quality of composites can be greatly impacted by a variety of production procedures. Because it produces composites with a smaller void content and better matrix wetting than other techniques like compression moulding, resin transfer moulding (RTM) is thought to be the best choice. As a result of enhanced matrix–matrix contact and fewer defects, the final product has higher mechanical qualities. Controlled pressure and vacuum resin injection in RTM assist guarantee that the preform has been completely impregnated and minimise air entrapment. The RTM method’s closed design also reduces exposure to environmental threats, which enhances the quality and consistency of the matrix incorporated in the composite11,12. Surface treatment of SCGs showed improved dispersion in the PLA matrix, thereby increasing mechanical characteristics and enhancing the composite’s biodegradability compared to neat PLA13. However, strong SCG-polymer interaction is required, as low adhesion is the most predominant factor affecting composite strength.

Additionally, the irregular form and size distribution of SCG particles have been reported to adversely affect mechanical properties by causing stress concentration and matrix disruption14. Furthermore, recent research investigated the possibilities of biocomposites using sustainable reinforcing SCG fillers. Using Phanerochaete chrysosporium CK01 and Aspergillus Niger DWA8, solid-state fermentation was applied for biological treatment with moisture contents ranging from 40 to 55% w/w. Extensive mechanical performance bio-composites were generated from catalyst separation from the processed SCG slurry15. These advancements support green development strategies by maximising resource efficiency and minimising waste. In order to preserve equitable distribution and increased compatibility in polymer composites, the best surface treatment is thus required. It has been shown via research over the years that SCGs are stable, wear-resistant, and chemically inert, which makes them ideal for composite applications14–16. SCG-reinforced epoxy resin polymer composites, for example, had better tensile and flexural qualities than virgin epoxy17. Due to enhanced carbonisation, the inclusion of SCGs significantly altered the epoxy composite’s char residue production and breakdown properties18. The polyhydroxybutyrate-valerate (PHBV) composite filler made from spent coffee bean powder shown enhanced structural integrity and decomposition stability following many cycles of degradation, indicating potential for use in environmentally friendly packaging19. Additionally, new pectin-based bioplastics were made utilising wasted coffee grounds as fillers, with an emphasis on the potential for repurposing SCG waste. By using UV curing, the composites’ tensile strength and flexibility were improved. It was revealed that UV irradiation and SCG content could increase and control mechanical characteristics, emphasizing the potential of SCGs for manufacturing environmentally sustainable bioplastic food packaging materials20. Similarly, incorporating spent coffee grounds in varying ratios from 5 to 20% with polypropylene (PP) matrix revealed a considerable rise in the composite’s brittleness, whereas mechanical strength continued to be largely unaltered, making it a promising filler for PP composites21. Various researchers have reported on the properties of different polymer resins embedded with SCGs, but a few others still need to be discovered. Among the few vinyl ester resins still in use, it remains an intriguing possibility for various applications that demand ground-breaking solutions to certain issues.

Vinyl ester resin has excellent mechanical qualities, chemical resistance, and low workability. Regarding cost-effectiveness and resilience to impacts and vibrational loads, VE resins outperform epoxy and polyester resins22. The mechanical qualities that vinyl ester resins offer on natural fillers after being chemically treated improve adhesion and compatibility, resulting in composites with greater moisture resistance and thermal stability than had previously predicted. To be more sustainable, research on vinyl ester composites has been conducted to create plant-based fillers as alternatives to conventional petroleum-based materials23, owing to favourable results. This study examines the mechanical and hardness properties of vinyl ester composites reinforced with surface-treated SCGs at different compositions. The mechanical performance was characterized by tensile, flexural, impact and hardness testing. Also, thermal properties and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) examination of the fractured surfaces of the tests mentioned above was carried out to understand the interfacial bonding and failure mechanisms in these composite materials.

Experimentation and methodology

Materials

Procurement of spent coffee grounds was done at Kaapi shop, a local coffee shop in Seeragapadi, India. The resin and curing agents are bought from Covai Seenu & Company (Coimbatore, India). The resin employed in this study is bisphenol-A-epoxy vinyl ester with 45% styrene. The resin was formulated with an epoxy equivalent value of 50% and a viscosity at 25 °C ranging between 450 and 550 CP. At the same time, methyl ethyl ketone peroxide (MEKP), with a molecular weight of approximately 210 g/mol and specific gravity nearly equal to ~ 1.17 g/cm3 at room temperatures, has been used as a curing agent for this research.

N-dimethylaniline acted as an accelerator, and cobalt naphthenate as a promoter24.

Fabrication of composites

Figure 1 shows Filler preparation and fabrication process of SCGs Powder Filler Reinforced Vinyl Ester Composite. To obtain a neutral pH level, the SCGs are washed well with distilled water at room temperature. The washed SCGs are then oven-dried at 80 °C for 8 h. The dried material was ball-milled and sieved through a 300-mesh screen (< 50 µm) to obtain a uniform powder. Its mechanical strength is determined by the particle size and shape, its dispersion in the composite matrix, and the bonding between the particles and the polymer matrix25. Generally speaking, the particle size of spent coffee grounds influences the mechanical properties of polymer composites. Stiffness and tensile strength are two mechanical properties that are typically improved by particle size reduction. This is because smaller particles have a greater surface area to connect with the matrix, which improves stress transfer and fortifies the bonding in general. Nevertheless, aggregation from too-small particles might be detrimental to the composite’s properties26. Thus, SCGs are sieved to create particles smaller than 50 µm and then stored for subsequent use. A particle-size examination revealed that the average diameters, d₅₀ and d₉₀, were 38 and 48 µm, respectively. SCGs spent five hours at 50 °C submerged in a 1:2 solution of 0.5 N NaOH. The SCGs were soaked, washed again to get rid of any last bits of NaOH, and then dried at 80 °C for eight hours. The SCGs were soaked, washed well to get rid of any remaining NaOH, and then dried for eight hours at 80 °C.

Filler preparation and fabrication process of SCGs Powder Filler Reinforced Vinyl Ester Composite (a) Spent coffee waste, (b) NaOH treatment, (c) Hot air oven drying, (d) Ball milling, (e) Ultra sonicator Mechanical stirring, (f) Vinyl ester Matrix and SCGs filler, (g) Vacuum bagging fabrication, and (h) Composite plate.

Pre-treated SCGs were mixed with vinyl ester resin in mass ratios of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25%. A Metro Precision Sonicator was used to mechanically swirl this combination for 90 min at a speed of 1500 rpm and a temperature of 80 °C. In order to promote even filler dispersion and disintegrate particle agglomerates, the amplitude was kept at 40% power during ultrasonication, with a pulse sequence of 10 s ON / 5 s OFF for 15 min. After letting the mixture settle to room temperature, the curing agents N, N-dimethylaniline (0.5 phr) and cobalt naphthenate (6% Co, 1 phr) were added as accelerators and 1.5 parts per hundred resin (phr) of methyl ethyl ketone peroxide (MEKP) as the initiator. The mix was then post-cured at 80 °C for 4 h after being let to cure for around 24 h at room temperature. In manufacturing bio-composite materials, vacuum bagging eliminates voids, increases density, improves mechanical characteristics, and improves filler integration. The technique helps produce high-quality parts with intricate shapes cost-effectively27. Initially, the matrix was placed in a flexible bag for polymerization, and then the negative pressure was removed using a vacuum pump. Filler weight percentages in the order of 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 were poured into a 300 × 300 × 3 mm glass mold covered with release fabric and enclosed in a vacuum bag. A vacuum of roughly –0.85 bar gauge (15 kPa absolute pressure) was applied using a vacuum pump to evacuate air and compact the laminate. The composite was cured under this vacuum at room temperature for about 24 h. Subsequently, the laminate was post-cured in an oven at 80 °C for 4 h with a ramp-up of 2 °C/min to ensure complete curing. By employing the Archimedes method in accordance with ASTM D792 to measure the composite density and void content, the consolidation quality of the laminates was confirmed. All specimens measured void contents were less than 2%, indicating that the vacuum bagging procedure was successful and that the resin infiltration was consistent.

Mechanical testing

Tensile test

Tensile strength and tensile modulus were measured as per ASTM D638 Type I using an INSTRON 5582 universal testing machine. The specimens were 165 mm long, 13 mm wide, and 3 mm thick, tested at a crosshead speed of 2.5 mm/min. Each value represents the average of five specimens28.

Flexural test

Flexural strength was evaluated following ASTM D790 using the same testing machine with a span-to-depth ratio of 16:1 and a support span of 48 mm. The crosshead speed was 5 mm/min at 25 °C and 75% relative humidity. The mean of five specimens was reported28.

Impact test

Impact strength was determined using the Izod impact test per ASTM D256 with notched specimens (notch radius 0.25 mm) on a Tinius Olsen impact tester. The results represent the average of five samples26,29,30.

Hardness test

A shore-D hardness tester was used to determine the hardness of the SCGs particle-filled vinyl-ester polymer composite as per ASTM D2240 standard. The experiment was carried out at five distinct locations within the sample, and the hardness results were recorded30

Thermal properties

Thermal conductivity

The bio-composites’ transverse thermal conductivity was measured using a shielded heat flow meter (Unitherm Model No. 2022) in compliance with ASTM E153031. Test specimens of 50 mm × 3 mm were mounted between upper and lower plates at a pressure of 0.34 MPa to ensure uniform contact. The temperature setpoints were maintained at 25 °C (cold plate) and 75 °C (hot plate). Calibration was performed using a standard Pyrex reference sample before testing.

Heat deflection temperature(HDT) test

The HDT-VSP tester was used to measure HDT in compliance with ASTM D64831. The HDT test parameters used are the 3-point bending fixture, 0.25 mm deflection at 455 kPa. The measurement of temperature was subsequently documented as HDT.

Scanning electron microscope analysis (SEM)

Scanning electron microscope, performed fractography experiments at various magnifications on the composite specimen’s failure surface following various mechanical tests. A thin portion was cut and coated with gold to prepare the surface of the cracked specimen for morphological study. Secondary imaging settings were used to generate SEM micrographs of the shattered specimens, with a voltage ranging from 10 to 15 kV31.

Results and discussion

Tensile properties

The tensile characteristics of the different samples were obtained using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM). In Fig. 2, the tensile strength values that were produced from the SCGs/VE composites. According to Fig. 2, When the filler content was raised from 0 to 5% by weight, the strength went up from 20 to 28 MPa. After the filler was added, there was a 7.19% improvement. Additionally, when the filler quantity was raised from 5 to 15 weight percent, the tensile strength of the SCG filler-reinforced vinyl ester composite went from 28 to 40 MPa. Using these fillers together resulted in a 30% improvement. At a greater SCG weight content of 15 wt%, the ultimate tensile strength and modulus of the vinyl ester composite reinforced with SCG showed 40 MPa. This is due to the increased load transfer from the filler to the matrix32. Nevertheless, when the filler weight percentage was raised from 15 to 20 wt%, the tensile strength of the SCG filler-reinforced vinyl ester composite marginally declined all the way down to 36 MPa. The filler and matrix are not equally distributed, which is the cause of this. This pattern points to a filler proportion of around 15% as the sweet spot for optimum tensile strength. The tensile strength of composites reinforced with SCGs and VEs is higher than that of composites reinforced with the following materials: Rice husk/polyethylene (25%), Coconut shell/polyethylene (57%), Shorea robusta/polyester (58%), Boiled egg shell/vinyl ester (33.8%), Alumina/vinyl ester (25%), Sawdust/plastic (64%), and wood flour/pvc (55%).

Tensile strength variations of SCGs filler reinforced vinyl ester composite upon various filler loadings.

Figure 3 shows the variations in the Tensile modulus of SCG filler-reinforced vinyl ester composite upon various filler loadings. Similarly, the tensile modulus increases from 1.12 GPa for pure resin to 1.44 GPa at 10% filler content. Unlike tensile strength, the modulus instantly shows a slight decline after this point with higher filler percentages, with values of 1.41 GPa, 1.37 GPa, and 1.28 GPa for 15%, 20%, and 25% filler content, respectively.

Variations in Tensile modulus of SCGs filler reinforced vinyl ester composite upon various filler loadings.

The increase in tensile modulus in Fig. 3. indicates enhanced stiffness of the composites by adding fillers up to an optimal percentage. This observed trend can be attributed to the optimal dispersion of fillers at lower contents, which improves stress transfer and reinforcement, enhancing both tensile strength and modulus. However, at higher filler contents, agglomeration occurs, leading to poor dispersion and stress concentration points that reduce overall mechanical properties. Additionally, while initial filler-matrix interaction improves composite properties, excessive filler disrupts the matrix continuity, diminishing the effectiveness of stress transfer33. Flexural properties

The ultimate and widely measurable property of the composite material in structural applications is its flexural strength. The flexural strength of SCGs/VE composite is shown in Fig. 4 as a function of different SCG filler loadings. At 15 weight percent, the SCGs/VE composite shows a very high flexural strength of 64 MPa. The flexural strength is 34.3 percent higher when compared to the pure resin when combined with 15 weight percent SCGs/VE composite. According to this finding, when the matrix and the SCGs filler contact more, more stress may be transmitted from the matrix to the filler under external loading34. When compared to pure VE resin, the flexural strength of SCGs/VE composite at 5%wt is 54 MPa, a 22.2% increase. With a weight percentage of 5–10% SCGs/VE composite, the flexural strength improves from 54 to 60 MPa. The tensile strength of SCGs/VE composite drops from 64 to 56 MPa at 25% because the filler and matrix are not packed well. At 20 and 25 weight percent of SCGs/VE composite, the flexural strength is optimal as compared to pure VE. Composites may have low flexural strength due to plate ruptures, a drop in matrix content within the composite system caused by a rise in filler content, or both. The stress that is transferred from the matrix to the fillers is reduced when the matrix content is reduced.

Effect of various SCGs filler loadings on the flexural strength of SCGs/VE composite.

However, higher filler contents lead to agglomeration and stress concentration, which weaken the composite structure and reduce mechanical properties. Excessive fillers disrupt the matrix continuity, lowering stress transfer efficiency35–37. Flexural strength of the SCGs composites exhibits higher flexural strength than the rice husk/polyethylene composites (53%), Shorea robusta/polyester composites (58%), Boiled egg shell/vinyl ester composites (60%), Alumina/vinyl ester composites(50.38%), Sawdust/plastic composites (63%), wood flour/PVC composites (33%)31–34,38.

Impact strength

The impact strength of pure VE resin was 6 kJ/m2. However, it increased to 9 kJ/m2, 12 kJ/m2, and 15 kJ/m2 for the filler contents of 5%, 10%, and 15%, respectively. The graph in Fig. 5 shows the effect of various SCG filler loadings on the impact strength of SCGs/VE composite. An improvement in impact strength was recorded at up to 15% filler addition. However, a significant decrease was observed at 20% filler content addition and beyond. This trend is due to improved energy absorption and crack resistance at optimal filler levels. At the same time, higher contents lead to particle agglomeration, which reduces the material’s ability to absorb impact energy39–41.

Effect of various SCGs filler loadings on the impact strength of SCGs/VE composite.

Hardness property

The experimental results indicate that adding filler particles initially shows a significant increase in the hardness value of the composites. Figure 6 shows the Hardness values of SCG/VE composites at various filler loadings. This increase in hardness is likely due to the added filler particles resisting the deformation of the polymer matrix. Despite variable conditions, the strength of manufactured composites can rise with the addition of filler, hence improving the composites’ hardness. The hardness value of virgin vinyl ester was found to be 40. This is due to its rigid structure, high cross-linking, and balanced toughness, ensuring durability and environmental resistance properties. However, as filler content increases, the hardness values fluctuate. For instance, adding 5% filler increases the hardness to 48. The hardness value for 10% is further increased up to 60. With a 15% filler content, the hardness reaches a peak of 82, indicating an optimal enhancement in cross-linking density. These results suggest that while adding fillers initially boosts the hardness due to increased cross-linking density, beyond a certain point, the effect diminishes. However, further increases in filler content to 20%, and 25% result in hardness values of 74 and 64 respectively. The decreases of hardness value possibly due to the saturation of the matrix with filler particles and potential weak interactions caused by irregular particle distribution and the presence of voids in the composite. A similar trend was observed when adding almond shell bio-filler to develop a vinyl ester particulate composite38. Hardness value of the SCGs composites shows the better impact strength than the tamarind seed filler/vinyl ester composites (49.3%), calatropis/epoxy composites (58%)42,43.

Hardness values of SCG/VE composites at various filler loadings.

HDT

The Heat Deflection Temperature (HDT), often known as the softening point of a material, is the measured temperature at which a material deflects by 0.25 mm under a stress of 455 kPa44. Figure 7 shows the HDT values of SCG/VE composites at various filler loadings. Neat vinyl ester resin (containing no filler weight percentage) has an HDT value of 38 °C. From zero to five percent, the filler content is raised. At first, the SCGs with the matrix raise the temperature to 42 °C, but only marginally. The heat deflection temperature rises from 42 to 50 °C within the 5 to 10 wt% range as SCG content accumulates higher in SCG-VE composites (10% SCG). The current case filler improves heat deflection temperature by 16%. As a result of the homogeneous distribution of filler content and the thorough mixing of SCG-VE, the heat deflection temperature of SCG-VE composites in this study reaches 62 °C for a filler concentration of 15 wt%. When the filler content was raised above 15 wt%, there was a consistent decline in the HDT. The opposite was true when the filler wasn’t evenly distributed; this made the particles more likely to clump together, reducing their surface areas. Consequently, both the temperature and the fracture propagation were reduced35,44,45. This process explains why the impact strength of the composites decreases when subjected to 20% and 25% SCG weight loadings, respectively.

HDT values of SCG/VE composites at various filler loadings.

Thermal conductivity

Figure 8 displays the results of the thermal conductivity tests performed on the four different kinds of bio-composite. The percentages of heat transfer for the pure resin, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% bio-composites were determined to be 0.182, 0.178, 0.162, 0.156, 0.158 and 0.156 Wm−1 K−1, correspondingly. Due to the tightly packed matrix and filler during composite fabrication, the findings show that the thermal characteristics are reduced for 5% and 10% bio-filler composites. Increasing the filler from 10 to 15%wt decreases the thermal conductivity by 9.87%. One possible explanation is that the filler bio-composites were distributed uniformly throughout the matrix, which led to limited heat transmission and a reduced thermal conductivity compared to the 15%wt bio-composites. In addition, the thermal conductivity may be somewhat enhanced to 0.156 Wm−1 K−1 for composites with 20% bio-filler. Adding fillers up to 25% wt somewhat lowers the thermal conductivity. Lastly, when employed near hot surfaces, the reduced heat conductivity resulting from increased filler in the matrix was an added advantage45.

SEM analysis

Figure 9 displays SEM images of the fractured surfaces of SCGs/VE specimens following tensile testing. The morphology in Fig. 9a illustrates the 15% SCGs-VE composite specimen’s fractured surfaces, where uniform filler-matrix bonding and minimal cavity formation are observed. Consequently, micro-cracks are sparsely spread within the vinyl ester matrix, indicating effective stress transfer and optimal tensile strength. The superior filler-matrix interfacial strength at this filler percentage results in efficient stress distribution.

SEM images showing SCGs/VE specimen fractured micrographic surfaces after tensile test, (a) 15 wt.% filler loading, (b) 25 wt.% filler loading.

In contrast, Fig. 9b reveals the 25% SCGs-VE composite specimen, where significant filler agglomeration is evident. This poor dispersion leads to stress concentration points, reducing the tensile strength due to ineffective stress transfer within the composite structure.

Further SEM analysis of SCGs-VE specimens subjected to flexural strength tests is shown in Fig. 10. Figure 10a shows that at 15% filler content, the particles are well-distributed and bonded within the matrix, with few voids present. This results in enhanced flexural strength due to optimal stress transfer and reinforcement. Conversely, Fig. 10b depicts the 25% filler content composite, where filler agglomeration and voids are more prevalent. These defects hinder stress transfer and compromise the composite’s mechanical integrity, decreasing flexural strength35–37.

SEM images showing SCGs/VE specimen fractured micrographic surfaces after the flexural test. (a) 15 wt.% filler loading, (b) 25 wt.% filler loading.

Figure 11 presents SEM images of the cross-section surfaces of SCGs/VE impact-fractured composites. Specifically, Fig. 11a depicts the composite with 15% SCG filler loading, highlighting the strong interaction between the SCG filler and vinyl ester resin. This optimal interaction reduces the formation of voids within the composite, resulting in superior impact strength38,42–45. Figure 11b. illustrates the composite with 25% SCG filler loading. The increased filler content decreases matrix quantity, leading to debonding, void formation, and weak interfacial bonding. These defects cause filler pull-out, which reduces the composite’s structural capability and mechanical performance39–41. As filler content increases, the plastic region of the composite decreases, leading to brittle failure and reduced impact strength, as evidenced in Fig. 11b.

Civil construction applications

The use of spent coffee grounds (SCGs), a common agricultural byproduct, as a bio-filler in vinyl-ester composites designed for use in civil construction has shown significant potential. Scientific studies have shown that natural thermosets can benefit from adding SCGs at moderate loadings. This not only improves the curing kinetics but also increases the stiffness, toughness, and hardness of the final product compared to neat resins. Additionally, early research combining biochar generated from SCG with small amounts of vinyl-ester resin shows promise for enhanced mechanical and dielectric qualities, which might be useful in lightweight structural panels or insulating components. Incorporating SCGs into vinyl-ester composites provides an eco-friendly waste-to-resource method for building materials, resulting in bio-based composites that are stronger, more resilient, and better for the environment39–41.

Conclusions

The mechanical properties of the spent coffee grounds (SCGs) reinforced vinyl ester composite at various filler loadings (5–25 wt%) were thoroughly investigated. An in-depth examination of the data revealed that vinyl ester resin with Spent Coffee Grounds filler at 15 wt% exhibits better mechanical properties such as tensile strength, tensile modulus, hardness of shore-D, flexural strength and impact strength. Despite obtaining optimum results, mechanical properties and hardness were reduced when the filler weight per cent levels increased. The 15wt% SCG -VE composites exhibited lower thermal conductivity than the other forms of composites, according to the thermal study. This is due to the SCG filler structure is packed efficiently. A strong interfacial connection between the waste filler and vinyl ester matrix was seen in the SEM investigation of the cracked specimen. This bonding was crucial in enhancing the mechanical and thermal characteristics.
