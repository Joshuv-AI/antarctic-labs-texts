Exploring the knowledge, attitudes, and potential role of oral health practitioners in FASD screening in South Africa
Authors: Imaan Amina Roomaney, Clement Nyirenda, Manogari Chetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSouth Africa has the highest global burden of foetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD), yet awareness and prioritisation among healthcare professionals remain limited. Oral health practitioners (OHPs) are well-positioned to support FASD screening due to their community presence and craniofacial expertise. This study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and readiness of South African OHPs to engage in FASD screening.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was distributed to South African OHPs, including dentists, dental specialists, oral hygienists, and dental therapists. The survey explored participants’ knowledge of FASD, recognition of the cardinal features, willingness to screen for, and perceived barriers to screening. Responses were analysed using descriptive statistics and thematic analysis.ResultsOf the 147 respondents, 70% (n = 109) were dentists. While most indicated they were comfortable discussing alcohol-related health problems, only 20.4% (n = 30) routinely asked patients about alcohol use. Awareness of the national FASD burden was low, with just 39.5% (n = 58) aware that South Africa has the highest global prevalence. Most participants (79.6%, n = 117) considered knowledge about FASD relevant to patient management. Regarding screening, 85.7% (n = 126) agreed that OHPs should play a role. Interest in additional training on FASD and its screening was high (90.5%, n = 133), with oral hygienists showing the greatest interest (97%, n = 31). Key barriers identified included unclear referral pathways and concerns about the stigma associated with diagnosis. Although the response rate was low, the sample represented a geographically and professionally diverse group.ConclusionThe findings reveal substantial knowledge gaps among South African OHPs regarding FASD, but also indicate strong interest and readiness to advance their knowledge . To support the integration of FASD screening into oral healthcare practice, targeted training, clear referral systems, and curriculum inclusion are needed. Reducing stigma and promoting public acceptance will further enable OHPs to contribute meaningfully to early identification and interventions for FASD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07017-8.

Introduction

Despite well-established evidence that prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE) affects craniofacial and dental development, core areas of dental expertise, there has been little research on the role of oral health practitioners (OHPs) in screening for patients with Foetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder (FASD) [1–9]. Dentists have been described as the “overlooked primary healthcare resource” [10] and recognised for their “untapped potential as primary care providers” [11]. These titles have been proposed to acknowledge the expanding role and potential of dentists within health systems [12]. Numerous studies have highlighted dentists’ capacity to screen for, manage, advise on, counsel, and appropriately refer patients with various conditions [13–15], including cardiovascular disease [16], Diabetes [17], and even mental health challenges [18].

Dentists and other OHPs are uniquely positioned to engage with a generally “healthy” population that may not yet be integrated into the broader healthcare system. Population studies from the United States have shown that a substantial number of individuals visit the dentist annually without seeking care from medical providers [17, 19]. In addition, the population compositions differed significantly between those frequenting OHPs and those who did not [17]. While similar data is not available for South Africa, the country’s high prevalence of dental diseases, including early childhood caries [20, 21] and periodontal disease in adults [22], necessitates early and frequent interaction with OHPs, thereby revealing opportunities for early screening and management of health concerns, including FASD.

OHPs possess a strong foundational knowledge of craniofacial anatomy, with a specific focus on the face, where some of the dysmorphology associated with PAE is evident. Additionally, the tools available to OHPs are rapidly expanding and advancing to include technologies such as artificial intelligence applications, cephalometric radiography, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), facial photography, and intra-oral and extra-oral scanners. These advancements provide opportunities for dentists to screen for complex conditions, particularly those with high regional prevalence. This may be exemplified by FASD in South Africa, a disorder caused by alcohol, an established risk factor for numerous general and oral health conditions.

South Africa has the highest documented prevalence of FASD worldwide. In a meta-analysis, Popova et al. reported a prevalence of Foetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS)- the most severe form of FASD- at 58.5 per 1,000 population (95% CI: 43.1–76.2), compared to a global prevalence of 1.46 per 1,000 [23]. More recent research by May et al. [24] estimated a prevalence of 310 per 1,000 (95% CI: 283.4–336.7) in the Western Cape, while Louw et al. [25] documented rates ranging from 57.4 to 274 per 1,000 across eight sites in four South African provinces [26–30]. This staggering statistic is rooted in historical injustices and systemic failures, disproportionately affecting poorer communities [31–33].

Despite this burden, FASD remains under-recognised within South Africa’s overburdened health system, which is challenged by high unemployment, poverty, and inequality (Gini coefficient 0.63), as well as a chronic shortage of health professionals [34]. Furthermore, FASD has been insufficiently prioritised in policy and health professional training [35, 36] contributing to substantial rates of underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis [37, 38].

Despite the diagnostic challenges and the current limitations of the South African health system in supporting individuals with FASD, the transition to the National Health Insurance (NHI) presents an opportunity to strengthen care for this population. In alignment with the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals [39], the health system has a responsibility to ensure that individuals affected by FASD receive appropriate care and that ‘no one is left behind.’ One critical strategy for addressing this challenge is the implementation of effective screening methods to detect FASD within the general population, as identified in a situational and gap analysis on FASD in South Africa [40]. The authors recommended involving community-level service providers in FASD screening to improve access to diagnosis and intervention. In this study, we examined the potential role of OHPs in fulfilling this function and assessed their knowledge and attitudes towards screening for FASD in the South African context.

Methods

This study was embedded within a larger project investigating the role of oral health practitioners (OHPs) in FASD screening in South Africa. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to survey initiation. Following completion, participants were directed to educational resources on FASD available through the Foundation for Alcohol Related Research (FARR), and were provided with the principal investigator’s contact details for additional information.

Data collection tool

A cross-sectional design, utilising an online survey to collect data, was employed in this study. The online survey was chosen to increase reach and have a more geographically representative sample. The survey primarily consisted of structured, closed-ended questions to facilitate quantitative analysis, with a few open-ended questions included to capture additional qualitative insights.

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from Mukherjee et al. (2015), which evaluated the knowledge and opinions of professional groups concerning FASD in the United Kingdom [41]. To ensure contextual relevance for South African OHPs, the tool was adapted to include questions specific to their role. For example, participants were asked whether OHPs should be involved in screening patients for medical conditions, including FASD, and whether they believed the public would accept OHPs conducting such screenings. The final questionnaire (Supplementary Material) was designed as a Google Form and piloted prior to dissemination to refine its clarity and usability. Most questions were multiple-choice and categorical response questions to quantify participants’ experiences, knowledge, or perceptions. An open-ended question was incorporated to allow respondents to elaborate on a key aspect not covered in the closed-ended items.

The questionnaire was distributed online through institutional mailing lists and various social media platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn. Social media was chosen to maximise reach and ensure access to professionals working in diverse environments, rather than relying solely on university networks, which might overrepresent academic staff.

Study participants

The inclusion criteria included qualified dentists (including dental specialists), oral hygienists, and dental therapists registered with the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA). Current undergraduate students were excluded. Eligible participants were required to have practised in South Africa within the past five years, reflecting the study’s focus on the local context. These criteria were self-reported and acknowledged at consent, but were not independently verified as identifying data was not captured.

Study context

As of April 2020, the HPCSA reported 6,049 dentists, 1,257 oral hygienists, and approximately 500 dental specialists registered in South Africa. The practitioner-to-population ratio was 1:8,817 for dental practitioners [42], far below the World Health Organization’s recommended ratio of 1:1,000 [43].

Dentists in South Africa complete a five-year bachelor’s degree followed by one year of community service. Oral hygienists currently complete a three-year bachelor’s degree, though a two-year diploma was offered previously. Dental therapists, a midlevel OHP introduced in 1977 to address oral health inequalities [44], also complete a three-year bachelor’s degree.

Maldistribution of the dental workforce is a significant challenge. Approximately 20% of dentists serve the public sector, which caters to nearly 80% of South Africa’s population [42, 45]. With the proposed implementation of National Health Insurance (NHI), equitable distribution of healthcare professionals, including OHPs, is a critical consideration [46]. Thus, creating an opportune time to review the scopes of various professions.

In line with the United Nations’ FASD situational and gap analysis recommendations [40], this study expanded the focus beyond dentists to include other OHPs. Each professional group- dentists, oral hygienists, and dental therapists - has the potential to contribute to health screening at the community level.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected between September 2023 and June 2024, with an additional brief period from December 2024 to January 2025. Convenience sampling was used. Initial distribution via institutional mailing lists, Facebook groups, and Instagram generated limited engagement, while the later inclusion of LinkedIn proved more effective in reaching participants. Extending data collection allowed inclusion of those who had not engaged earlier, thereby enhancing sample diversity and representativeness.

The data collected via the Google Form questionnaire was downloaded and organised in Microsoft Excel. Responses that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded from the analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the data. Frequency distributions were calculated for all categorical variables, and results were expressed as proportional percentages. Unanswered items in the knowledge section were coded as incorrect to avoid artificially inflating scores. The open-ended responses were qualitatively analysed using thematic analysis.

Although the aim of this study is not to compare the professional groups (dentists, oral hygienists, and dental therapists), data are presented per group to identify variations in knowledge levels, opinions on FASD screening, and perceived barriers to implementation. Visual representations included 100% stacked bar charts to present key findings.

Results

A total of 150 responses were received, with 3 excluded as they were submitted by undergraduate students. This resulted in a final dataset of 147 responses.

Of the respondents, 70% (n = 103) were dental practitioners (dentists and dental specialists), 22% (n = 33) were oral hygienists, and 8% (n = 11) were dental therapists. Approximately one-third of the respondents graduated from the University of the Western Cape (UWC), South Africa’s largest dental school, which annually produces about one-third of the country’s dentists [47]. The graduation years ranged from 1989 to 2024, with most respondents graduating within the last five years (n = 52), reflecting a predominantly recent cohort of practitioners.

Of the respondents, 49% (n = 72) reported holding additional postgraduate qualifications. All nine provinces of South Africa were represented in the dataset, with the Western Cape (34.7%, n = 51) and Gauteng (27.2%, n = 40) provinces having the highest number of respondents. Most respondents (74%, n = 109) reported working in urban settings and were employed in private practice (69%, n = 75); however, the public sector (39%, n = 41) and academia (15%, n = 18) were also represented in the sample.

Attitudes and practices

Most respondents (95.9%, n = 147) believed OHPs should play a role in screening for medical conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, developmental delays, and autism spectrum disorder. This sentiment was consistent across all professional groups. Despite this, practices related to alcohol screening varied significantly. While 20.4% (n = 30) of respondents reported routinely asking patients about alcohol consumption, many respondents (49.0%, n = 72) indicated they only occasionally inquired, and 12.2% (n = 18) reported never asking about alcohol consumption.

Regarding comfort levels in discussing alcohol-related topics, 81% (n = 119) of respondents felt comfortable discussing alcohol consumption during pregnancy, with little variation across professional groups. Comfort levels were even higher (93.8%, n = 135) when discussing health outcomes related to alcohol consumption, with all dental specialists and dental therapists expressing confidence. Despite these positive attitudes, 64.6% (n = 95) of respondents expressed concern about stigmatisation associated with FASD diagnoses potentially impacting patients or parents.

Approximately 38.8% (n = 57) of respondents reported having suspected a patient of having undiagnosed FASD. However, referral practices varied, with 49.9% (n = 72) indicating they would refer such cases to a general medical practitioner, followed by medical specialists (13.6%, n = 20) and social workers (11.6%, n = 17). Notably, 8.8% (n = 13) of respondents reported uncertainty about where to refer suspected cases, and 6.1% (n = 9) indicated they would not refer at all. Nineteen practitioners answered an open-ended question on reasons for non-referral. The themes that emerged were that 42% thought they were not suitably qualified to refer, 31% indicated that they would not know who to refer to, and 15.8% indicated that they would refer only if the patient engaged with them on the topic.

Most participants (79.6%, n = 117) believed that knowledge about FASD was relevant to patient management, however, 15.0% (n = 22) were unsure, and 5.4% (n = 8) did not consider it relevant. A higher proportion of oral hygienists (90.9%, n = 30) and dental therapists (90.9%, n = 10) endorsed its relevance compared to dentists (74.5%, n = 73). When asked specifically about screening for FASD, 85.7% (n = 126) of respondents agreed that OHPs should play a role, with dental specialists (100%, n = 5) and dental therapists (90.9%, n = 10) showing the highest levels of support. However, 12.2% (n = 18) were unsure about the role of OHPs in FASD screening. Most respondents (90.5%, n = 133) expressed interest in receiving additional training on FASD and FASD screening, with oral hygienists (97%, n = 31) demonstrating the greatest interest.

Perceptions of public acceptance of OHPs’ involvement in FASD screening were mixed. While 59.9% (n = 88) believed the public would be accepting, 29.2% (n = 43) were unsure, and 10.9% (n = 16) did not believe the public would accept this role.

Most participants (89.0%, n = 130) correctly identified that no amount of alcohol is safe during pregnancy to prevent FASD. However, 8.2% (n = 12) incorrectly believed that consuming less than one glass of alcohol per day is safe.

Confidence in recognising the cardinal signs of FASD was low overall, with only 32.7% (n = 48) of participants reporting confidence. Oral hygienists were the most confident group (63.6%, n = 21), while dentists (25.5%, n = 25) and dental therapists (9.1%, n = 1) exhibited lower confidence. Nevertheless, participants demonstrated reasonably good knowledge of these specific features in the survey.

Of the 13 questions assessing knowledge of the features and signs of FASD, dental specialists achieved the highest average score (11.8 [range: 8–13]; 90.7%), followed by dentists (10.2 [range: 0–13], 78.5%), oral hygienists (9.2 [range: 0–13]; 70.8%), and dental therapists (7.9 [range: 0–13]; 60.8%). The overall group average was 9.9 of 13 (76.2%). The majority correctly identified neurodevelopmental delays (92.5%, n = 136), learning disabilities (93.2%, n = 137), and smaller head circumference (81.6%, n = 120) as features of FASD. High recognition was also noted for short stature (78.9%, n = 116), midface hypoplasia (79.6%, n = 117), and short palpebral fissures (74.8%, n = 110). The most distinctive facial features of FASD, the flat philtrum (76.2% (n = 112), thin upper lip (70.7%, n = 104) and short palpebral fissures (74.8%, n = 110) were frequently correctly identified. A minority of participants incorrectly identified thick upper lips (8.2%,n = 12), obesity (10.9%, n = 16), larger-than-average head circumference (19.1%, n = 28), and protrusive maxilla (20.4%, n = 30) as features of FASD.

Discussion

This study examined the knowledge and attitudes of OHPs in South Africa towards FASD and FASD screening. Despite a general lack of awareness about the scale of the problem in South Africa, OHPs across all evaluated categories expressed an interest in learning more about FASD and participating in screenings, not only for FASD but also for other health conditions.

Promisingly, many participants demonstrated awareness of the cardinal features of FASD and were able to correctly identify them, even when they initially doubted their ability to do so. A majority of participants (90.5%) expressed a willingness to advance their knowledge of FASD through training, further supporting the feasibility of integrating FASD-related education into oral health training programs. This finding is encouraging and suggests that with targeted education and training, OHPs could confidently incorporate FASD knowledge into their practice.

Although most participants were recent graduates, a substantial proportion (60.5%) were unaware that South Africa has the highest reported global burden of FASD [23]. This highlights a critical gap in oral health education. Based on a review of local BDS and BOH curricula, no formal instruction on the public health impact of FASD is included in oral health training programmes in South Africa, reflecting the condition’s low prioritisation in professional education. However, the BOH curriculum at UWC does include some content on FASD features and management of special needs groups. Notably, its stronger emphasis on prevention and health promotion may partially explain oral hygienists’ greater willingness to learn more and to participate in FASD screening.

This knowledge gap is not unique to oral health education. Both FASD and alcohol misuse represent substantial interrelated public health burdens in South Africa, yet national policy responses have historically been fragmented, under-resourced, and inadequately implemented [35, 36]. FASD is not explicitly recognised as a public health priority in national health policies, nor is it consistently addressed within maternal, child, or mental health frameworks [35, 36]. Recent research continues to highlight significant deficiencies in the development and execution of FASD-related policies in the South African context [35, 36]. Researchers have identified a complex interplay of systemic, structural, and social barriers, as well as a central challenge: the lack of political will and prioritisation [35, 48]. Adebiyi and Mukumbang also highlighted inadequate training and knowledge among health and social service providers, limiting their ability to recognise, diagnose, or appropriately refer FASD cases [35]. In earlier work, the authors recommended a comprehensive response spanning four domains: (1) prevention through public awareness, screening, and education; (2) early identification and diagnosis; (3) care and support for affected individuals and families; and (4) system-level coordination and monitoring [49].

Addressing these systemic and policy-level shortcomings requires innovative, multisectoral strategies that leverage multiple levels of health professionals. Findings from this study suggest that, with additional training, OHPs could play a supportive role in FASD prevention and early detection. While OHPs cannot resolve the FASD crisis alone, they remain a potentially valuable part of a broader, multisectoral response. However, this approach needs to be considered within the existing context. The oral health practitioner-to-population ratio is far below WHO recommendations [50] and unmet dental needs across the country are unacceptably high [51]. OHPs are overburdened within their current scope of practice, and projections suggest that by 2030, there may be a shortfall of up to 2,267 dentists and dental specialists [52]. The recently released National Oral Health Policy and Strategy (2024) identifies oral health human resources as a priority area for action. If integrated strategically into this agenda and aligned with the NHI’s vision for equitable, community-based care, FASD-related training and screening could strengthen the preventive role of OHPs, expand access in underserved areas, and contribute to a more responsive and inclusive health system [36, 49].

Achieving this potential, however, will depend on whether such an expanded role is accepted by the public and supported by effective, stigma-sensitive implementation strategies. Participants in this study expressed concerns about public perceptions regarding OHP involvement in FASD screening. While dentists’ participation in medical screenings has been explored for certain conditions, public attitudes toward this type of role expansion remain under-investigated [13, 15, 53]. Available evidence suggests that the public has generally been receptive to health screenings conducted within dental practices [54, 55]. In South Africa, where access to healthcare professionals is often limited, task-shifting is an established strategy and has been successfully implemented across multiple areas of the health system [56–59]. Expanding OHPs’ responsibilities to include FASD screening aligns with this approach, but requires careful, context-specific evaluation to ensure acceptability, feasibility, and effectiveness.

Building on concerns about public acceptance, substantial challenges remain regarding the stigma associated with an FASD diagnosis and its potential impact on both expanded OHP screening roles and the OHP–patient relationship, with 64.6% of respondents expressing concern. The stigma surrounding FASD is well-documented and contributes to mothers’ reluctance to disclose alcohol use during pregnancy and negatively impacts health-seeking behaviours [60, 61]. Notably, South Africa has made considerable strides in reducing HIV-related stigma through decades of advocacy, policy reforms, and public health initiatives aimed at “normalising” HIV [62]. Although challenges persist, anti-stigma campaigns and messaging have been central to these efforts [62, 63]. Applying similar, targeted strategies to FASD could help mitigate stigma, promote positive health-seeking behaviour, and support public acceptance.

A significant limitation of this study was the low survey response rate, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to all OHPs in South Africa. However, the sample diversity in terms of working and geographic contexts provides a valuable cross-section of perspectives within the profession. Additionally, the relatively young cohort of participants may reflect a group more open to adopting an expanded scope of practice, a sentiment that may differ among more established professionals.

The willingness of OHPs in this study to expand their scope of practice to include FASD screening is a promising finding. To translate this potential into impact, efforts should focus on developing targeted training programs and integrating FASD education into undergraduate oral health curricula. However, meaningful progress will also require addressing the broader systemic challenges, including workforce shortages, resource constraints, and the low prioritisation of FASD within national health policies.

Conclusion

This study highlights a critical gap in the knowledge and awareness of oral health practitioners (OHPs) in South Africa regarding the prevalence and impact of FASD, despite the country having the highest global burden of the condition. Encouragingly, OHPs demonstrated a willingness to enhance their understanding of FASD and to expand their scope of practice to include screening for FASD and related health conditions. However, systemic barriers remain, including limited health policy support, the absence of established management pathways, insufficient services for affected individuals, and persistent stigma. Addressing these challenges through targeted education, supportive implementation strategies, and broader health system reforms will be essential for enabling OHPs to contribute meaningfully to a coordinated, multisectoral response to FASD in South Africa.
