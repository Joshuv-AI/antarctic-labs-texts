Long-term lactate ingestion and exercise training differentially modulate body composition, resting substrate metabolism, and tissue-specific metabolic remodeling in mice
Authors: Inkwon Jang, Nayeon Kim, Deunsol Hwang, Sunghwan Kyun, Taeho Kim, Jungyong Lee, Hun-Young Park, Kiwon Lim, Jisu Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose] This study investigated whether long-term lactate ingestion induces systemic and tissue-specific metabolic adaptations under normal physiological conditions and whether these responses differ from those induced by exercise training.[Methods] Thirty-two male mice were randomly assigned to saline control, lactate ingestion, exercise training with saline, or exercise training with lactate groups (n = 8/group). Lactate (3 g/kg) or saline was administered by oral gavage five times per week for 16 weeks. Exercise-trained mice performed moderate-intensity treadmill exercise five times per week. Body composition, 24-h resting substrate utilization and energy expenditure, and metabolic protein abundance in the soleus muscle and epididymal white adipose tissue were assessed.[Results] Lactate ingestion reduced body fat percentage and absolute fat mass in untrained mice. Exercise increased resting carbohydrate oxidation, total energy expenditure, and skeletal muscle HK2, PDHA1, and GLUT4 abundance. Lactate ingestion increased skeletal muscle abundance of FAT/CD36, PDK4, MCT1, and MCT4 under untrained conditions. In epididymal white adipose tissue, molecular responses were limited, with the most robust changes observed for CIDEA, whereas the supplementation effect on GPR81 did not remain significant after false discovery rate correction. The combined intervention did not consistently produce additional adaptations beyond those observed with exercise training alone.[Conclusion] Long-term lactate ingestion and exercise training produced distinct but partially overlapping systemic and molecular response patterns. Lactate ingestion was associated with lower adiposity and increased abundance of skeletal muscle proteins related to fatty acid handling and lactate transport, whereas exercise training increased carbohydrate oxidation, energy expenditure, and the abundance of proteins associated with glucose metabolism.Keywords: lactate ingestion, exercise training, energy substrate metabolism, body composition, metabolic remodeling, skeletal muscle

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic flexibility refers to the capacity to adjust energy substrate utilization in response to changes in physiological demand and nutrient availability [1,2]. Efficient coordination between carbohydrate and lipid metabolism is fundamental to whole-body energy homeostasis and depends on integrated adaptations across metabolic tissues, particularly skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [2,3]. Exercise represents one of the most potent physiological stimuli for this regulation, because it acutely increases energy demand while chronically inducing adaptations that enhance substrate transport, oxidation, and energy expenditure [4-6].

Skeletal muscle plays a central role in exercise-induced metabolic adaptation by regulating the uptake and utilization of both glucose and fatty acids [7,8]. Repeated exercise promotes glucose transport and mitochondrial oxidative capacity and increases the abundance of proteins involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism [8,9]. Adipose tissue also contributes to systemic substrate regulation by storing and mobilizing lipids and undergoing metabolic remodeling in response to exercise-induced signals [10,11]. Thus, adaptations in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue together provide an important molecular basis for changes in whole-body substrate metabolism and body composition [12].

Among the metabolites whose turnover increases markedly during exercise, lactate has emerged as an important link between energy metabolism and cellular signaling [13,14]. Lactate is continuously produced under both resting and exercising conditions and can be transported between cells and tissues through monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), allowing it to serve as an oxidative energy substrate within the lactate shuttle system [15-17]. In addition to its role as a fuel, lactate functions as a signaling metabolite through mechanisms that include hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 1 (HCAR1/GPR81)-mediated signaling and regulation of metabolic pathways in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [14,18].

Experimental administration of exogenous lactate further supports this concept. Lactate exposure has been shown to activate AMPK-related metabolic signaling, whereas oral lactate administration has been reported to alter the expression of proteins and genes involved in glucose and fatty acid metabolism in skeletal muscle and other metabolic tissues [19,20]. In a previous study, lactate administration before exercise enhanced carbohydrate oxidation and altered the metabolic protein abundance in skeletal muscle [21].

Furthermore, five weeks of repeated lactate ingestion altered substrate utilization, including an increase in carbohydrate oxidation, suggesting that repeated exposure to exogenous lactate may influence metabolic regulation beyond its immediate use as an energy substrate [22]. However, it remains unclear whether these responses persist or develop into broader systemic and tissue-specific adaptations following long-term lactate ingestion. In addition, it remains unclear whether lactate-induced responses resemble or differ from those induced by exercise training and whether combining the two interventions produces additional adaptations. Addressing these gaps is important for distinguishing lactate-responsive effects from conventional exercise-induced metabolic adaptations. Thus, examining long-term lactate ingestion alongside exercise training provides a framework for determining whether repeated lactate exposure produces sustained tissue-specific responses that are distinct from or additive to those induced by exercise.

Accordingly, this study investigated the effects of 16 weeks of lactate intake, exercise training, and their combination under normal metabolic conditions. Whole-body adaptations were evaluated by assessing body composition, 24-h resting substrate utilization, and energy expenditure, whereas tissue-specific responses were examined through the abundance of proteins involved in carbohydrate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, lactate transport, and metabolic remodeling in the soleus muscle and epididymal white adipose tissue. Using this integrated approach, we aimed to characterize how long-term lactate ingestion and exercise training differentially affect systemic and tissue-specific energy metabolism.

METHODS

Ethics Approval

The animal study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Konkuk University (approval No. KU24122). All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. The study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Every effort was made to minimize animal discomfort and stress.

Animals and Experimental Design

Thirty-two male Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice, aged 7 weeks, were obtained from Orient Bio Inc. (Seongnam, Republic of Korea). After a 1-week acclimatization period, mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 8 per group): saline control (SAL), lactate ingestion (LAC), exercise training with saline (TRA+SAL), and exercise training with lactate (TRA+LAC). Four mice were housed per cage under controlled environmental conditions (23-25 °C, 40-50% relative humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle) and were provided with a standard chow diet (5L79; Orient Bio Inc., Seongnam, Republic of Korea; 65% carbohydrate, 21% protein, and 14% fat) and water ad libitum. The intervention was conducted for 16 weeks, and body weight was monitored throughout. At the end of the intervention, body composition was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), whereas whole-body substrate utilization and energy expenditure were measured over 24 h using indirect calorimetry. Forty-eight hours after the final treatment and exercise session, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and tissues were collected. Following euthanasia, adipose tissue depots and selected hindlimb skeletal muscles were dissected and weighed. Absolute and body weight-normalized tissue weights are provided in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4. Soleus muscle and epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analyses. A schematic overview of the 16-week experimental design is provided in Figure 1.

Schematic overview of the experimental design.

Lactate Administration

Sodium L-lactate (71718-50G; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered by oral gavage at a dose of 3 g/kg body weight, five times per week for 16 weeks, based on previously established protocols [22,23]. Sodium L-lactate was prepared in 0.9% saline, which served as the vehicle. The SAL and TRA+SAL groups received an equivalent volume of 0.9% saline using the same procedure. Saline or lactate was administered immediately after each exercise session in the trained groups. In the untrained groups, saline or lactate was administered at approximately the same time of day as the trained groups to minimize potential circadian influences.

Exercise Training

Exercise training was performed on a motorized treadmill for small animals (Daejong, Seoul, Republic of Korea) five times per week for 16 weeks. The exercise regimen consisted of moderate-intensity aerobic training at an 8° incline, corresponding to approximately 60-70% of maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂max), based on previously established protocols [24]. Running speed was progressively increased from 15 m/min during weeks 1-2 to 18 m/min during weeks 3-5, 20 m/min during weeks 6-9, 23 m/min during weeks 10-13, and 25 m/min during weeks 14-16, while exercise duration increased from 40 to 50 min per session. Mild electrical stimulation (0.4 mA constant current) at the rear of the treadmill was used when necessary to encourage running while minimizing stress.

Body Composition Assessment

Body composition was assessed using a small-animal DXA system (iNSiGHT VET; Osteosys, Seoul, Republic of Korea). The mice were fasted for 6 h before measurement and anesthetized with isoflurane during scanning. Each mouse was scanned twice, and the mean was used for analysis. Body fat and lean mass were determined from the DXA scans and analyzed as both absolute values (g) and percentages of body mass. Percentage data and absolute values are presented in Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure 2, respectively.

Resting whole-body substrate utilization and energy expenditure (REE)

Whole-body energy metabolism was assessed by indirect calorimetry using a metabolic gas analyzer (ARCO-2000; ARCO System, Chiba, Japan) connected to a gas-switching system (ARCO-2000-GS-8; ARCO System). Measurements were initiated after a 24-h recovery period to minimize the acute effects of the interventions. Airflow through each metabolic chamber was maintained at 1 L/min. Oxygen consumption (VO₂) and carbon dioxide production (VCO₂) were continuously measured over 24 h. Fat and carbohydrate oxidation were calculated from VO₂ and VCO₂ using the following equations: fat oxidation (mg/min) = 1.67 × (VO₂ − VCO₂) and carbohydrate oxidation (mg/min) = 4.51 × VCO₂ − 3.18 × VO₂. Energy expenditure was calculated using the equation 3.816 × VO₂ + 1.231 × VCO₂. The mice had free access to food and water throughout the measurement period, and no exercise was performed during the assessment. Twenty-four-hour measurements characterized resting whole-body substrate utilization and energy expenditure.

Western Blot Analysis

Soleus muscle and eWAT samples were homogenized in EzRIPA lysis buffer (WSE-7420; ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Whole soleus muscle was homogenized in 200 μL of lysis buffer, whereas 100 mg of eWAT was homogenized in 250 μL of lysis buffer. Samples were homogenized using a TissueRuptor (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected after removing the upper lipid layer. The total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Cat. No. 23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (10-20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE using 10-14% resolving gels selected according to target molecular weight and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in 0.1% PBS-T for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 90 min at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GERPN2232; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), and quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The proteins analyzed in the soleus were HK2, PFKM, PDHA1, FAT/CD36, PDK4, UCP3, MCT1, MCT4, and GLUT4, whereas GPR81, CIDEA, MCT1, MCT4, FAT/CD36, and CS were analyzed in eWAT. Protein abundance was normalized to total protein signal from Ponceau S staining (P9100-050; GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA) rather than to a housekeeping protein. Normalized values were expressed relative to the untrained SAL group, set to 100%. Detailed information on the antibodies used for Western blot analysis is provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Changes in body weight over the 16-week intervention period were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the effects of time, group, and their interaction. For post-intervention body weight, body composition, resting substrate utilization and energy expenditure, and protein abundance outcomes, a two-way ANOVA was used to assess the main effects of supplementation (saline vs. lactate), training (untrained vs. trained), and their interaction. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction when significant main effects or interactions were identified. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Because no across-outcome multiplicity adjustment was specified for the molecular endpoints, these analyses were considered exploratory. As a sensitivity analysis, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied separately within each tissue to the supplementation, training, and supplementation × training interaction effects across measured proteins. FDR-adjusted q values are provided in Supplementary Table 5. A q value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance in the FDR sensitivity analysis. For the FDR calculations, p values < 0.001 were conservatively entered as 0.001.

RESULTS

Body Weight and Body Composition

Body weight increased progressively throughout the 16-week intervention period, with no significant differences in the body weight trajectories among the groups (Figure 2A). Final body weight showed a significant main effect of training (p = 0.014), whereas supplementation (p = 0.235) and the supplementation × training interaction (p = 0.793) were not significant (Figure 2B).

Representative DXA images obtained after the intervention are shown in Figure 2C. Body fat percentage showed significant main effects of supplementation (p = 0.004) and training (p < 0.001), and a significant supplementation × training interaction (p < 0.001) (Figure 2D). Under untrained conditions, body fat percentage was significantly lower in LAC than in SAL (p < 0.001), whereas no difference was observed between TRA+SAL and TRA+LAC. Exercise training significantly reduced body fat percentage under both saline (p < 0.001) and lactate (p = 0.006) conditions.

Lean mass percentage showed a corresponding pattern, with significant main effects of supplementation (p = 0.004) and training (p < 0.001), as well as a significant supplementation × training interaction (p < 0.001) (Figure 2E). Under untrained conditions, lean mass percentage was significantly higher in LAC than in SAL (p < 0.001), whereas no difference was observed between TRA+SAL and TRA+LAC. Exercise training significantly increased lean mass percentage under both saline (p < 0.001) and lactate (p = 0.017) conditions. Analysis of the corresponding absolute DXA-derived values showed that absolute fat mass was also significantly lower in LAC than in SAL under untrained conditions, whereas absolute lean mass did not differ significantly between groups (Supplementary Figure 2). Direct tissue measurements further showed that eWAT weight was significantly lower in LAC than in SAL under untrained conditions, consistent with the reduction in DXA-derived fat mass (Supplementary Table 3). Exercise training also reduced the eWAT weight. Skeletal muscle weights showed significant main effects of training but no significant effects of lactate supplementation. Absolute muscle weights were generally higher in trained mice, whereas these differences were attenuated after normalization to body weight (Supplementary Table 4).

Together, these findings indicate that lactate ingestion reduced body fat percentage and absolute fat mass in untrained mice, whereas the higher lean mass was not accompanied by an increase in absolute lean mass.

Fat oxidation showed no significant main effects of supplementation or training and no significant supplementation × training interaction (Figure 3A).

Carbohydrate oxidation showed a significant main effect of training (p < 0.001) and a significant supplementation × training interaction (p = 0.010) (Figure 3B). Carbohydrate oxidation was significantly higher in TRA+SAL than in SAL (p < 0.001). Together, these results indicate that exercise training increased resting carbohydrate oxidation and total energy expenditure, whereas lactate ingestion alone did not significantly alter 24-h substrate oxidation or energy expenditure.

Metabolic Protein Abundance in Soleus Muscle

For carbohydrate metabolism-related proteins, HK2 showed a significant main effect of training (p < 0.001) and a significant supplementation × training interaction (p = 0.026) (Figure 4A). HK2 abundance was significantly higher in TRA+SAL than in SAL (p < 0.001), whereas the difference between LAC and SAL was not statistically significant (p = 0.054). PFKM abundance was not significantly affected by supplementation, training, or their interaction (Figure 4B). PDHA1 showed a significant main effect of training (p < 0.001), with significantly higher abundance in both trained groups than in their respective untrained groups, whereas neither supplementation effect nor supplementation × training interaction was significant (Figure 4C). Collectively, these results indicate that exercise training predominantly increased the abundance of selected proteins involved in carbohydrate metabolism in the soleus muscle, whereas lactate ingestion alone had limited effects on these proteins.

Among proteins related to lipid metabolism, FAT/CD36 showed significant main effects of supplementation (p < 0.001) and training (p = 0.004) (Figure 4E). FAT/CD36 abundance was significantly higher in LAC than in SAL under both untrained (p = 0.007) and trained (p = 0.003) conditions. PDK4 abundance was higher in LAC than in SAL under both untrained (p < 0.001) and trained (p = 0.027) conditions, whereas TRA+SAL also showed higher abundance than SAL (p = 0.001) (Figure 4F). UCP3 showed a nominal main effect of supplementation (p = 0.045), with higher abundance in LAC than in SAL under untrained conditions (p = 0.029); however, the supplementation effect did not remain significant after FDR correction (q = 0.081). No significant training or interaction effects were observed (Figure 4G). Thus, lactate ingestion was associated with increased abundance of several proteins related to fatty acid transport and substrate handling, particularly FAT/CD36 and PDK4.

Among the substrate transport-related proteins, MCT1 showed a significant main effect of supplementation (p < 0.001) and a significant supplementation × training interaction (p < 0.001) (Figure 4I). MCT1 abundance was significantly higher in LAC than in SAL under untrained conditions, whereas no supplementation-dependent difference was observed under trained conditions. MCT4 showed significant main effects of supplementation (p = 0.019) and training (p = 0.004), as well as a significant supplementation × training interaction (p = 0.042) (Figure 4J). Under untrained conditions, MCT4 abundance was significantly higher in LAC than in SAL (p = 0.003), whereas no significant difference was observed between the trained groups. In contrast, GLUT4 showed only a significant main effect of training (p < 0.001), with higher abundance in TRA+SAL than in SAL (p = 0.002) and in TRA+LAC than in LAC (p = 0.023), whereas neither the supplementation effect nor the supplementation × training interaction was significant (Figure 4K). Collectively, these findings indicate that lactate ingestion alone, even in the absence of exercise training, was associated with increased abundance of the skeletal muscle lactate transporters MCT1 and MCT4, whereas GLUT4 abundance was primarily responsive to exercise training. The FDR sensitivity analysis confirmed the supplementation effects on FAT/CD36, PDK4, MCT1, and MCT4, whereas the supplementation effect on UCP3 was not significant after correction for multiple testing (Supplementary Table 5).

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated whether long-term lactate ingestion induces systemic and tissue-specific metabolic adaptations and how these responses differ from those elicited by exercise training. Several findings emerged. First, lactate ingestion alone reduced body fat percentage in untrained mice and was associated with increased skeletal muscle abundance of FAT/CD36, PDK4, MCT1, and MCT4, all of which remained significant after FDR correction. UCP3 also showed a nominal supplementation effect, although this association was not significant after FDR correction.

Second, exercise training produced a distinct whole-body metabolic phenotype characterized by increased resting carbohydrate oxidation and energy expenditure and increased abundance of skeletal muscle proteins involved in carbohydrate utilization, particularly HK2, PDHA1, and GLUT4. Third, molecular responses in eWAT were comparatively limited. CIDEA showed the most robust response, with the main effects of supplementation and training and the supplementation × training interaction remaining significant after FDR correction. Training-related changes in GPR81 and MCT1 also remained significant after FDR correction, whereas the main effect of supplementation on GPR81 did not (Supplementary Table 5). Collectively, these findings indicate that long-term lactate ingestion and exercise training influence body composition and are associated with remodeling of proteins involved in energy substrate metabolism, but do so through largely distinct adaptive patterns rather than producing similar metabolic phenotypes. Thus, long-term lactate ingestion was associated with a distinct tissue-specific metabolic response that differed from the adaptations observed following exercise training. To our knowledge, this is the first study to concurrently characterize long-term lactate ingestion-induced metabolic protein remodeling in skeletal muscle and eWAT in direct comparison with exercise training.

Dietary intake did not differ significantly among groups over the 16-week experimental period (Supplementary Figure 1), suggesting that differences in food intake were unlikely to explain the observed changes in body composition. Under untrained conditions, the LAC group exhibited a lower body fat percentage and higher relative lean mass than the SAL group. Notably, the lower body fat percentage was accompanied by a significant reduction in absolute fat mass, whereas absolute lean mass was not significantly altered. Thus, the higher lean mass percentage in the LAC group primarily reflected lower fat mass rather than an increase in absolute lean mass. These findings indicate that long-term lactate ingestion was associated with differences in body composition that primarily reflected lower fat mass rather than an increase in lean tissue. However, evidence regarding the effects of repeated lactate administration on body composition remains limited. In our previous 5-week study using the same dose and route of sodium L-lactate administration (3 g/kg, oral gavage), lactate ingestion alone did not significantly reduce body fat, although a numerical difference toward lower body fat was observed [22]. This observation provided part of the rationale for extending the intervention period to 16 weeks and examining adipose tissue responses in the present study. Supporting the biological plausibility of such long-term effects, dietary lactate supplementation has been reported to promote adipose browning and thermogenesis and to protect against high-fat diet-induced obesity [25], whereas 12 weeks of L-lactate administration attenuated weight gain and fat accumulation in high-fat diet-fed mice [26]. Although these studies were conducted under obese or highfat diet conditions and differed in lactate dose and administration protocols, their findings suggest that repeated lactate exposure may influence adipose tissue regulation and body composition over longer intervention periods.

Despite the lower fat mass, 24-h resting metabolic measurements showed no significant differences in fat oxidation or total energy expenditure following lactate ingestion. Nevertheless, total energy expenditure was approximately 8-10% higher in the untrained LAC group than in the SAL group, although this difference was not statistically significant. This numerical difference should be interpreted cautiously. However, if relatively small changes in daily energy expenditure had been sustained over the 16-week intervention period, they could have contributed to cumulative differences in energy balance. Accordingly, the observed difference in fat mass may reflect the cumulative effects of repeated lactate exposure rather than a sustained increase in resting fat oxidation detectable during a single 24-h assessment. In contrast, exercise training was associated with significant increases in resting carbohydrate oxidation and total energy expenditure, consistent with established effects of repeated exercise on systemic energy turnover and substrate utilization [2,4].

The skeletal muscle protein profile further demonstrated that lactate ingestion and exercise training were associated with distinct changes in proteins involved in energy substrate metabolism. Exercise training was associated with increased abundance of HK2, PDHA1, and GLUT4, a pattern consistent with changes in proteins associated with glucose uptake and pyruvate metabolism in skeletal muscle. These changes in protein abundance were consistent with the higher resting carbohydrate oxidation observed in the trained mice and with previously described skeletal muscle responses to repeated increases in energy and substrate demand during exercise training [8,9]. In contrast, lactate ingestion alone had relatively limited effects on carbohydrate metabolism-related proteins. Although HK2 abundance was numerically higher in LAC than in SAL under untrained conditions, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.054; Figure 4A), whereas PDHA1 and GLUT4 were not significantly altered by lactate ingestion. Instead, a more pronounced response was observed for proteins related to fatty acid transport and substrate handling. FAT/CD36 and PDK4 abundance was significantly higher following lactate ingestion in untrained mice, whereas UCP3 showed a nominal increase that was not significant after FDR correction (Figure 4E-G). This pattern is consistent with previous acute studies showing that exogenous lactate administration can rapidly modify skeletal muscle metabolic regulation. Kyun et al. (2020) reported increased skeletal muscle FAT/CD36 abundance following a single oral lactate administration [20], whereas Yoo et al. (2022) demonstrated that acute oral lactate ingestion increased whole-body fat oxidation during the early resting period after administration [27]. Notably, both studies used acute oral lactate administration at 2 g/kg, a dose and route broadly comparable to the 3 g/kg oral sodium L-lactate administration used in the present study, although the experimental species and exposure duration differed. Furthermore, lactate administration has been shown to activate AMPK and other metabolism-related signaling pathways in skeletal muscle [19]. Taken together, these findings suggest that repeated lactate exposure may preferentially affect skeletal muscle proteins involved in fatty acid transport and substrate handling rather than reproducing the carbohydrate-oriented adaptations observed with exercise training.

A notable finding was the distinct pattern of MCT1 and MCT4 abundance (Figure 4I-J). Both transporters showed significantly higher protein abundance following lactate ingestion in untrained mice, suggesting that long-term repeated exposure to exogenous lactate was associated with increased MCT1 and MCT4 abundance in skeletal muscle even in the absence of exercise training. MCT1 is preferentially expressed in oxidative skeletal muscles and is associated with lactate uptake and its subsequent utilization as an oxidative substrate, whereas MCT4 is predominantly associated with the export of glycolytically produced lactate from skeletal muscle [28,29]. Although both MCT isoforms mediate proton-coupled bidirectional monocarboxylate transport, their differential distribution and transport characteristics are consistent with distinct physiological roles in skeletal muscle lactate handling. Therefore, the concurrent increase in MCT1 and MCT4 abundance indicates that repeated lactate exposure is associated with changes in proteins involved in skeletal muscle lactate transport.

In contrast, exercise training in the present study increased MCT1 abundance under saline conditions, without a corresponding increase in MCT4 abundance. This pattern is consistent with previous reports showing that chronic contractile activity preferentially increases MCT1 rather than MCT4 and that such an increase in MCT1 is accompanied by greater skeletal muscle lactate uptake [30]. Thus, the absence of an exercise-associated increase in MCT4 abundance should not necessarily be interpreted as an absence of adaptation in lactate handling. Instead, exercise training may preferentially alter the abundance of proteins associated with lactate uptake and oxidative metabolism. Consistent with this interpretation, endurance training has been shown to markedly increase skeletal muscle MCT1, whereas changes in MCT4 are more variable, and training-induced changes in MCT abundance have been associated with altered in vivo lactate kinetics [31]. Moreover, chronic post-exercise lactate administration has been shown to increase skeletal muscle MCT1 abundance, further supporting the responsiveness of the lactate transport machinery to repeated lactate exposure [32]. Taken together, these findings suggest that long-term lactate ingestion is associated with changes in proteins involved in energy substrate transport and handling, even in the absence of an exercise stimulus. Although such changes in protein abundance do not demonstrate improved metabolic flexibility or substrate transport, they indicate that repeated lactate exposure is associated with alterations in the proteins involved in substrate handling.

A distinct response pattern was observed in eWAT compared with skeletal muscle. Neither lactate ingestion nor exercise training consistently altered MCT1 or MCT4 abundance, and no significant changes were observed in FAT/CD36 or CS abundance. Instead, major responses were confined to GPR81 and CIDEA. This difference may be physiologically relevant because white adipose tissue primarily functions as a regulated reservoir for energy storage and as a metabolic buffer controlling the storage and release of lipid substrates, rather than as a major site of oxidative substrate disposal [33,34]. Efficient sequestration of triglycerides within adipocytes may help limit excessive lipid exposure to other metabolic tissues, and lipid-droplet-associated proteins play an important role in the regulation of adipose lipid storage and mobilization. In this context, CIDEA is particularly relevant because it localizes to lipid droplets and has been implicated in lipid droplet growth, fusion, and triglyceride storage [35,36]. Therefore, under the conditions in the present study, eWAT may have had relatively little requirement for broad increases in oxidative or lactate-transport capacity. One possible explanation is that the major changes related to energy substrate utilization occurred predominantly in skeletal muscle, whereas eWAT responded to lactate ingestion and exercise training mainly through regulatory processes associated with lactate sensing and lipid storage or mobilization. Although this interpretation remains speculative, it is consistent with the absence of substantial changes in MCT1, MCT4, FAT/CD36 abundance, and CS despite the observed changes in GPR81 and CIDEA abundance.

GPR81 provides a potential link between circulating lactate levels and adipose tissue regulation. GPR81, also known as hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 1 (HCAR1), is highly expressed in adipocytes and functions as a lactate receptor whose activation suppresses lipolysis through Gi-mediated signaling and inhibition of intracellular cAMP production [37].

However, this acute antilipolytic action does not necessarily predict net changes in adipose tissue mass during long-term intervention. The adipose tissue mass reflects the cumulative balance between lipid storage and mobilization, substrate utilization, thermogenesis, and whole-body energy balance over time. Dietary lactate supplementation has been reported to promote adipose browning and thermogenesis through a GPR81-dependent mechanism and protect against high-fat diet-induced obesity [25]. In the present study, the lower whole-body fat mass was accompanied by a significantly lower eWAT weight in lactate-treated untrained mice (Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, the nominal increase in GPR81 abundance following lactate ingestion may reflect altered lactate responsiveness in eWAT rather than providing direct evidence of sustained suppression of lipid mobilization. Importantly, because GPR81 activity and lipolytic flux were not directly measured, the present findings do not establish a causal relationship between increased GPR81 abundance and reduced adipose tissue mass.

CIDEA abundance was also higher following lactate ingestion and exercise training. However, this response should not be interpreted as evidence of direct triglyceride breakdown. CIDEA is a lipid droplet-associated protein that has been implicated in lipid droplet organization, triglyceride sequestration, and the regulation of lipid storage and mobilization [35,36]. Moreover, higher CIDEA abundance in human adipose tissue has been associated with greater insulin sensitivity, suggesting its potential relevance to adipose tissue metabolic phenotype. CIDEA may also participate in thermogenic remodeling under specific adipocyte conditions [38], although thermogenesis was not directly assessed in the present study. Therefore, changes in GPR81 and CIDEA may be consistent with an adipose response to lactate exposure involving pathways related to substrate storage and mobilization rather than a generalized enhancement of oxidative metabolism. Importantly, the present study did not establish a causal relationship between GPR81 and CIDEA abundance. Rather, their parallel changes in protein abundance suggest that repeated lactate exposure and exercise training may influence pathways associated with lactate sensing and lipid droplet remodeling in eWAT.

These tissue-specific findings indicate that lactate ingestion does not simply reproduce the metabolic adaptations observed following exercise training. Exercise training produced a broad increase in whole-body energy expenditure and carbohydrate oxidation together with increased HK2, PDHA1, GLUT4, and MCT1 protein abundance, consistent with responses to repeated increases in energy demand during exercise training. In contrast, lactate ingestion was associated primarily with changes in proteins involved in fatty acid handling and lactate transport in skeletal muscle and with changes in GPR81 and CIDEA abundance in eWAT. Therefore, the two interventions appear to overlap in some metabolic pathways while producing distinct patterns of protein abundance and whole-body metabolic processes. This distinction is important because the findings do not support considering exogenous lactate as a simple substitute for exercise. Rather, lactate may represent one component of the metabolic signaling environment associated with exercise and may independently influence selected pathways when repeatedly administered. Previous studies have demonstrated that exogenous lactate can influence AMPK-related signaling, substrate utilization, MCT abundance, and exercise metabolism [19,21,23], supporting the concept that lactate can act as a metabolic stimulus without reproducing the full physiological complexity of exercise.

Despite these findings, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, although the present study identified tissue-specific changes in protein abundance after 16 weeks of lactate ingestion, circulating and tissue lactate kinetics were not continuously characterized throughout the intervention. Therefore, the magnitude and duration of lactate exposure associated with the observed changes remain unclear. Second, the present study primarily assessed protein abundance rather than functional metabolic outcomes. Increased protein abundance of FAT/CD36, PDK4, UCP3, MCT1, and MCT4 does not necessarily indicate corresponding changes in substrate flux, transporter activity, enzyme activity, or mitochondrial oxidative function. Future studies integrating isotope tracer approaches, direct lactate transport measurements, enzyme activity assays, and mitochondrial functional analyses are required to determine whether the observed changes in protein abundance translate into altered metabolic flexibility. Third, the combined lactate and exercise intervention did not consistently produce additional changes beyond those observed with exercise training alone. One potential explanation relates to the timing of lactate administration relative to exercise. Lactate was administered immediately after each exercise session when endogenous lactate turnover and exercise-induced metabolic signaling were elevated. Under these conditions, exogenous lactate may have provided a stimulus that overlapped with exercise-associated recovery signaling, potentially limiting additional responses. Future experiments separating exercise and lactate exposure, for example by administering lactate several hours after exercise, may help determine whether temporally distinct exposures produce additional responses.

In conclusion, the present study showed that long-term lactate ingestion was associated with changes in body composition and tissue-specific metabolic protein abundance under the physiological conditions examined. Lactate ingestion alone was associated with lower adiposity and an increased abundance of skeletal muscle proteins associated with fatty acid handling and lactate transport, whereas exercise training was associated with increased resting carbohydrate oxidation, total energy expenditure, and abundance of proteins associated with glucose metabolism. In eWAT, changes in protein abundance were comparatively limited and were most consistently observed for CIDEA, whereas GPR81 abundance showed a more pronounced response to exercise training than to lactate ingestion. Thus, long-term lactate ingestion did not reproduce the pattern of metabolic responses observed with exercise training in the present study. Instead, the two interventions produced distinct but partially overlapping patterns of protein abundance. Because substrate flux, transporter activity, enzyme activity, and mitochondrial function were not directly measured, the functional consequences of these changes in protein abundance remain to be established.
