The role of the gut microbiota in radiation enteritis: from mechanistic insights to therapeutic applications
Authors: Mingyang Tao, Yang Liu, Huaijuan Guo, Siqi Gao, Ying Wang, Xuebing Yan, Yefei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiation enteritis (RE) is a severe adverse reaction after abdominal and pelvic radiotherapy, with a complicated pathogenesis and no effective treatment. Although gut microbiota dysbiosis was reported to play a critical role in RE, related molecular characteristics and intervention strategies have not been extensively summarized. In this review, the mechanisms of RE, including injury to intestinal stem cells, damage to the vascular endothelium, reshaped immune microenvironment, and mucosal barrier dysfunction, are discussed. In addition, radiation-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis is characterized by decreased abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium bifidum, and increased abundance of detrimental bacteria such as Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus. Beneficial and detrimental bacteria affect the development of RE by regulating inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB and JAK/STAT3, and through their metabolites such as bile acids and short-chain fatty acids. Accordingly, several microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies, including antibiotic administration, dietary interventions, fecal microbiota transplantation, probiotic administration and engineered bacteria, have been developed to mitigate RE. However, some challenges still remain for these therapeutic strategies. The goal of this review is to highlight the crucial role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of RE, thereby accelerating the development of individualized microbial therapies against this disease.

Introduction

According to global cancer statistics, there were approximately 20 million new cancer cases and nearly 10 million cancer-related deaths worldwide in 20221. From a demographic perspective, the number of new cancer cases worldwide is expected to increase to 35 million by 2050, a 77% increase compared with 20222. Tumor treatment is complex and multifaceted, and the main treatment methods include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy. With the rapid development of medical technology, novel therapeutic approaches such as immunotherapy and vaccines have increasingly revolutionized traditional anticancer methods and significantly extended the survival time of cancer patients3. Despite the development of these novel therapies, radiation therapy remains a critical tumor treatment4,5. Since the first patient with skin cancer was cured by radiotherapy in the late 19th century6,7, radiotherapy has continuously evolved and remains an essential modality with established therapeutic benefits across multiple malignancies, including lung8, oesophageal9, rectal10, and cervical cancers11. Radiotherapy is not only utilized as an adjuvant therapy for preventing postoperative recurrence but is also combined with chemotherapy or immunotherapy to control metastatic tumors12. However, radiation therapy can also cause various side effects, such as oral mucositis, radiation lung injury, radiation enteritis (RE), and other symptoms13, with the side effects depending on the radiation dose and exposure location. RE often occurs in patients with abdominopelvic tumors who receive abdominal radiotherapy and is characterized by acute or chronic inflammation of the small bowel wall accompanied by the apoptosis of proliferating epithelial cells14. While most acute gastrointestinal toxicity is transient and can be managed with supportive care, a subset of patients experience serious symptoms, leading to discontinuation of the treatment. Consequently, novel, effective and safe therapeutic strategies are needed15. The traditional therapies for RE include anti-inflammatory and antibiotic drugs, nutritional support, and endoscopic and operative treatments; however, their actual efficacy is limited16. Therefore, novel therapeutic strategies for this disease must be developed.

Recently, with the development of sequencing techniques and germ-free mouse models, increasing evidence has suggested that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in not only the initiation and development of cancer but also resistance to anticancer drugs17. Moreover, an imbalanced gut microbiota is associated with the toxicity of anticancer therapies. For instance, the abundance of Prevotella and Clostridia is correlated with increased chemotherapy toxicity in patients with lung cancer, while the abundance of Bacteroides and Klebsiella pneumoniae is correlated with that in patients with gastrointestinal cancer18. The abundance of intestinibacter bartlettii, Streptococcus spp., and Bacteroides intestinalis is correlated with an increased risk of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), whereas the abundance of Lachnospiraceae spp., Ligilactobacillus salivarius and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii is associated with the opposite effect19. In this review, we focused on the correlation of the gut microbiota with RE and aimed to assess recent microbiome-based therapies for this disease. This review not only provides novel insights into the role of the gut microbiota in radiation-induced tissue injury but also discusses the clinical translation of relevant fundamental research.

Pathogenesis of RE

The pathogenesis of RE is complicated and involves numerous inherent and environmental factors. In addition, the target region and irradiation dose may affect the occurrence and development of RE. Intestinal stem cells

The homeostasis of intestinal epithelial cells is dependent on the continuous self-renewal capacity of crypt intestinal stem cells (ISCs), particularly Lgr5+ stem cells located at the base of the crypt, which proliferate and differentiate into various epithelial lineages to maintain mucosal integrity and barrier function20. This specialized cell population maintains the stability of the stem cell pool through asymmetric division while providing an adequate source of cells for renewal and damage repair in the intestinal mucosa21. Ionizing radiation directly damages DNA in Lgr5+ stem cells at the base of the intestinal crypts, leading to cell cycle arrest or apoptosis22. In addition to actively cycling Lgr5+ crypt base columnar stem cells, the intestinal epithelium contains reserve stem cell populations that are preferentially engaged upon injury23. Among these, B lymphoma Mo-MLV insertion region 1 (BMI1), a polycomb-group chromatin regulator implicated in the epigenetic control of stemness and genome integrity, has been widely used to label an injury-responsive “reserve” intestinal stem-like state that is largely quiescent during homeostasis24. Historically, these reserve cells were discussed in relation to the so-called “+4 position”, which is defined as approximately four cell tiers above the crypt base; however, subsequent work supports a view in which the “reserve” function reflects a stress-activated cellular program rather than a strictly position-defined, marker-exclusive entity25. Consistent with this injury-activated framework, compared with rapidly cycling Lgr5+ CBC stem cells, BMI1-lineage cells can be preferentially engaged after genotoxic insult and display relative radioresilience, thereby contributing to crypt regeneration and epithelial repopulation26. Following irradiation, Bmi1+ cells can be activated to contribute to crypt regeneration and epithelial repopulation, partially compensating for the loss of Lgr5+ stem cells27. Emerging evidence indicates that injury-induced niche remodeling and stress-responsive signaling, including stromal-epithelial crosstalk, support the expansion and regenerative function of Bmi1-lineage cells28. The Wnt and Notch signaling pathways are essential for preserving the quiescent-proliferative equilibrium of intestinal epithelial stem cells29,30. The oxidation of key stem cell proteins (β-catenin) by radiation-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) destabilizes Wnt signaling, hinders the self-renewal function of stem cells and eventually destroys intestinal epithelial cells and induces apoptosis of crypt stem cells31. In contrast, Notch signaling regulates intestinal homeostasis by modulating intestinal stem cells32, and radiation induces neutrophil infiltration within tissues, accompanied by notable activation of Notch signaling, as evidenced by the increase in the expression of the Notch downstream DNA-binding protein RBPJ and its target gene Hes1. These findings suggest that Notch signaling is a critical component of the neutrophil-mediated response to radiation-induced tissue damage33. Recent studies have also highlighted the role of the Hippo-YAP signaling pathway in regulating the response of ISCs to injury, where activation of YAP not only promotes crypt regeneration but may also lead to aberrant epithelial cell proliferation34. In addition to intrinsic ISC damage, radiation impairs the stem cell niche, which provides critical support signals. Paneth cells, which are the main source of Wnt and Notch ligands in the crypt, are susceptible to radiation-induced apoptosis, which indirectly impairs the function of ISCs35. Radiation alters the surrounding mesenchymal and immune microenvironment, reducing the supply of factors essential for ISC survival and repair, such as R-spondin, epidermal growth factor (EGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF)36.

Vascular endothelium

Many studies have confirmed that radiation exposure causes irreversible damage to vascular endothelial cells37. First, ionizing radiation induces DNA double-strand breaks in vascular endothelial cells, activating the DNA damage response (DDR) machinery and p53-dependent checkpoint signaling, ultimately leading to arrest of the endothelial cell cycle and functional impairment38,39. In addition, ionizing radiation can trigger endothelial apoptosis through activation of the acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase)-ceramide pathway; radiation-induced TNF-related signaling promotes sphingomyelin hydrolysis to ceramide, which results in the formation of membrane signaling platforms that activate downstream stress cascades, including PKC-dependent pathways, culminating in apoptotic execution40. Second, radiation can indirectly induce endothelial cells to secrete TNF-α, interleukin (IL)-1β and other cytokines to activate the NF-κB pathway, leading to inflammatory infiltration, thus damaging the vascular endothelium and aggravating RE41. Third, irradiation of endothelial cells alters the expression of intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), and Platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1) also known as cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31), increases vascular permeability and leads to intestinal mucosal oedema42. Finally, irradiation leads to the upregulation of fibroblast and platelet-derived growth factor expression in endothelial cells, thereby facilitating neovascularization43. In turn, radiation-induced neovascularization is frequently associated with vascular hyperpermeability and incomplete formation of the basement membrane. These alterations in vascular integrity promote the extravasation of plasma proteins and the subsequent deposition of fibrin, which contributes to the development of intestinal mucosal fibrosis and impairs its normal physiological function44.

Immune microenvironment and inflammatory programming

The intestine is not only a digestive and absorptive organ but also the largest immune barrier in the human body, maintaining homeostasis through a combination of innate and adaptive immunity45. Radiation-induced dysregulation of the intestinal immune system often leads to a vicious cycle of infection, inflammation, and fibrosis in patients with RE. First, radiation activates the cGAS-cGAMP-STING pathway in the body, which results in the recruitment of macrophages to the site of radiation damage, leading to inflammation and fibrosis46. High-dose radiation can drive macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype; however, when combined with specific immunotherapies or stimulated by particular injury signals, macrophages may become overactivated and trigger a cytokine storm, which is typically associated with a proinflammatory M1 state47. Cellular pyroptosis plays a critical role in radiation injury and is triggered by gasdermin D cleavage, mediated by caspase-1, -4, -5, and -11, and recruited by immune cells48. Other members of the gasdermin family, such as gasdermin E (GSDME), have also been shown to be involved in radiation-induced tissue injury by promoting pyroptosis. GSDME is highly expressed in normal colon epithelium, and radiation induces GSDME-mediated pyroptotic cell death, resulting in the release of the proinflammatory cytokines IL1β, IL6, TNFα, and HMGB1, which recruit and activate NK cells, leading to radiotherapy-associated colitis49. Second, irradiation perturbs immune tolerance by altering the abundance and suppressive capacity of regulatory T cells (Tregs); consequently, reduced or temporally/spatially mismatched Treg-derived cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10, and IL-35) can weaken mucosal tolerance and reparative programs, whereas context-dependent Treg overexpansion may impair antimicrobial immunity, resulting in dysregulated intestinal homeostasis50,51. However, a clinical study demonstrated that the Treg level was negatively correlated with the severity of RE and that Treg function was impaired in RE patients, demonstrating the complex mechanism of Tregs in RE development52. Recent animal experiments based on single-cell sequencing revealed dynamic changes in T cells during early-stage RE. In particular, the numbers of CD8+ effector and CD4+ naïve T cells were increased on day 3 after irradiation, whereas the number of CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells was reduced on days 1–7 after irradiation53. The results of this pilot study suggest that different T-cell populations play complex roles in RE, but extensive investigations are needed in the future to elucidate these roles. In addition to T cells, radiation can directly kill other immune cells, such as B cells, thereby modulating the immune response54,55. Notably, B-lineage radiosensitivity appears to depend on the differentiation state, as activated B cells and plasma cells can display relative radioresistance, suggesting qualitative reshaping of humoral immunity rather than uniform depletion54. In addition to lymphocytes, irradiation remodels myeloid cell populations (e.g., monocytes/macrophages); consistent with radiation-triggered cGAS-cGAMP-STING activation and downstream inflammatory programs, these myeloid changes can amplify inflammatory signaling and promote profibrotic remodeling, providing a mechanistic bridge between acute immune perturbation and chronic RE progression56. Immune dysfunction is likely not only a driver of acute inflammation but also a key mechanism contributing to long-term RE.

Mucosal barrier integrity

The intestinal mucosal barrier is primarily composed of epithelial cells, tight junctions, and a mucus layer, which collectively contribute to the maintenance of intestinal integrity and protection against harmful agents57. Normally, this barrier is the first-line defence against intestinal microbiota and pathogens, and impaired barrier function can lead to inflammation58. The tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells and the integrity of the mucus layer are essential for intestinal barrier function59. Representative tight junction proteins include occludin and claudins60. Rather than acting directly on tight junction proteins, irradiation primarily perturbs their abundance and subcellular localization through oxidative stress and inflammatory reprogramming, including through NF-κB-dependent signaling cascades, as well as miRNA-mediated posttranscriptional regulation, ultimately leading to reduced ZO-1 and claudin-3 expression and compromised epithelial barrier integrity61. The expression of other important junctional components, including occludin, E-cadherin, and junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), is also reduced after radiation exposure, which contributes to radiation-induced intestinal mucosal injury62. Mechanistically, irradiation-associated NF-κB activation can transcriptionally upregulate miR-221/222, which suppresses Syndecan-1 (Sdc1) to weaken epithelial barrier integrity and aggravate mucosal injury63,64. In addition, radiation decreases the expression of MUC2, which is the major component of the mucus layer, thereby weakening the protective effect of the mucus layer and increasing the vulnerability of the intestinal epithelium to bacterial invasion63. Radiation-induced oxidative stress, characterized by excessive production of ROS, disrupts tight junction proteins and exacerbates mucosal damage65,66. Moreover, the apoptosis and depletion of Lgr5+ intestinal epithelial stem cells impairs epithelial regeneration, whereas the dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway hinders mucosal repair67. The barrier breakdown also allows the intestinal microbiota and its derivatives (e.g., lipopolysaccharides (LPS)) to penetrate the epithelial barrier, which triggers innate and adaptive immune signaling pathways68. Furthermore, this facilitates the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and attracts immune cells (e.g., macrophages, natural killer cells, and neutrophils), which accelerates stem cell injury and causes vascular endothelial dysfunction, resulting in a vicious cycle that worsens the progression of RE. In addition, bacteria-derived LPS increases Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) activity and activates downstream NF-κB signaling to promote inflammation and barrier dysfunction69. Therefore, the activation of TLR4 signaling by LPS released by intestinal bacteria may represent a therapeutic target for barrier protection during RE management.

Role of the gut microbiota in radiation enteritis

The gut microbiota includes all the microbial communities, such as bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses, distributed in the human gastrointestinal tract and plays important roles in nutrient absorption and immune regulation70. Radiation-induced damage disrupts the homeostasis of gut microorganisms, resulting in significant alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota and a concomitant decrease in microbial community diversity71. Changes in the intestinal microbiota are affected by factors such as radiation time, radiation level, radiation type, timing of sample collection, and the subjects studied. The general trend is an increase in the abundance of harmful bacteria (e.g., Enterococcus spp. and opportunistic fungi such as Aspergillus spp.) and a decrease in the abundance of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium bifidum and Lactobacilli)72. Ionizing radiation can significantly disrupt the microecology of the intestinal tract by decreasing the diversity of the microbiota and disrupting the community composition73. Wang et al. conducted a systematic review of eight studies encompassing 455 patients with pelvic malignancies and reported a consistent decline in the α diversity of the gut microbiota following abdominopelvic radiotherapy, particularly in those who developed radiation-induced diarrhoea74. Therefore, altered gut microbiota play a crucial role in RE development, suggesting the need to further identify key beneficial or pathogenic bacteria. Beneficial bacteria

Advanced sequencing techniques have demonstrated that the abundance of some bacteria is reduced in patients with RE, whereas experimental evidence has demonstrated the beneficial role of these bacteria in ameliorating inflammation and improving barrier function. Some of these bacteria are well known as probiotics, which are defined as live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host75. These beneficial bacteria have the potential to be utilized to address not only RE but also other intestinal diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Faecalibacterium prausnitzii

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (F. prausnitzii) is a commensal anaerobe belonging to the phylum Bacillota and is the dominant commensal organism in the human gut. Additionally, its abundance is reduced in patients with various diseases, such as IBD76, colorectal cancer77, and chronic kidney disease78. F. prausnitzii exerts a modulatory effect on the gut microbiota composition by increasing microbial diversity, increasing the levels of beneficial bacterial populations and reducing the levels of potentially pathogenic taxa79. Lapiere et al. reported that the prophylactic use of F. prausnitzii enhanced the intestinal mucosal barrier in rats receiving pelvic radiation and reduced side effects from radiotherapy80. The number of regulatory T cells was positively correlated with the abundance of F. prausnitzii in feces, suggesting its potential regulatory role in intestinal immunity81. In an experimental model of dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis, Mohebali and colleagues examined the protective role of F. prausnitzii and reported that its administration markedly mitigated intestinal inflammation, reinforced epithelial barrier function, sustained regulatory T-cell responses, limited Th17-driven immunity, and attenuated the release of inflammatory cytokines82. F. prausnitzii regulates immune cell activity by producing a fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) that converts oleoylethanolamide (OEA) into N-acyl amino acids (NAAAs) to activate immune-related receptors such as GPR132 and GPR31, thereby attenuating local immune overresponses, increasing immune homeostasis and protecting the intestinal microenvironment83. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) of F. prausnitzii attenuate intestinal fibrosis by inducing macrophage polarization, disrupting mitochondria to inhibit oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, and reducing PPARγ expression and cholesterol efflux84. By regulating the microbial composition and immune response, supplying energy to intestinal epithelial cells, and alleviating intestinal fibrosis, F. prausnitzii safeguards the homeostasis of the intestinal environment and promotes normal intestinal function. Thus, F. prausnitzii shows great potential for reshaping gut dysbiosis induced by ionizing radiation or other stress conditions.

Bifidobacterium

Bifidobacteria are a group of gram-positive anaerobic bacilli belonging to the phylum Actinobacteria and include Bifidobacterium longum subspecies, Bifidobacterium infantis, and Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum85. In the context of pelvic radiotherapy, the depletion of barrier-supportive commensals and the expansion of inflammatory pathobionts are recurring features of dysbiosis, positioning Bifidobacteria as mechanistically plausible candidates to mitigate radiation enteritis by restoring mucosal resilience86. Consistent with this rationale, a systematic review of gynaecologic cancer patients revealed that probiotic interventions significantly reduced the incidence and severity of radiation-induced diarrhoea and decreased anti-diarrhoeal use87. Notably, a phase II study in which Bifidobacterium longum BL21 was administered during pelvic radiotherapy revealed a favorable safety profile and an exploratory reduction in the incidence of ≥ grade 2 acute radiation enteritis compared with historical controls88. Bifidobacteria are important immunomodulators that modulate immune responses through multiple pathways, enhancing gut barrier function by upregulating suppressive Foxp3+ Tregs and inhibiting immune responses triggered by Th2 and Th17 cells89. At the epithelial interface, probiotic bacteria, including Bifidobacterium-containing formulations, can preserve tight junction organization (e.g., ZO-1/occludin/claudins) and counter increases in permeability, thereby limiting the translocation-driven inflammatory amplification that is central to radiation-induced mucosal injury90. In addition, bacterial supplementation has been proposed to attenuate radiation-associated epithelial injury by dampening NF-κB-centered inflammatory programs and reducing cytokine escalation, thereby providing a coherent mechanistic framework for Bifidobacteria-based strategies in patients with RE91. Taken together, the current evidence supports a mechanism-based model in which Bifidobacteria contributes to RE protection primarily through barrier reinforcement and anti-inflammatory immune recalibration, thereby breaking the feedforward loop of permeability, microbial translocation, and mucosal inflammation61.

Lactobacillaceae

Members of the family Lactobacillaceae are gram-positive bacteria within the order Lactobacillales, comprising multiple genera that commonly colonize in the human body. Species formerly classified within the genus Lactobacillus are gram-positive lactic acid bacteria belonging to the family Lactobacillaceae within the phylum Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes)92. Species formerly classified within the genus Lactobacillus are gram-positive lactic acid bacteria within the family Lactobacillaceae and are classified within the phylum Bacillota. Following the 2020 taxonomic revision by Zheng et al., the former broad Lactobacillus genus was reclassified into multiple distinct genera on the basis of genome-based taxonomy, with the name Lactobacillus now restricted to a narrowly defined clade93. Limosilactobacillus reuteri is a gram-positive, rod-shaped member of the genus Limosilactobacillus (family Lactobacillaceae) that is typically described as a facultative anaerobe, and it has been widely used as a probiotic commensal with the ability to support epithelial barrier function and modulate mucosal immune responses in a strain-dependent manner94. Intestinal epithelial renewal is governed by ISCs, and Limosilactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to promote ISC proliferation, facilitate the repair of radiation-induced intestinal mucosal damage, and enhance the intestinal anti-inflammatory response95. Membrane vesicles (MVs) secreted by L. reuteri reduce the permeability of macrophage mitochondria through the production of 3-hydroxypropionaldehyde (3-HPA), which reduces ROS production and alleviates oxidative stress to promote mucosal repair96. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a metabolite produced by L. reuteri, promotes mucin sulfation, reduces intestinal inflammation and protects the intestinal barrier via the AHR-Papss2-Slc35b3 pathway97. L. reuteri regulates the microbial composition by inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria such as Proteobacteria and Fusobacteriota and promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as Bacteroidota and Firmicutes98. An animal study revealed that proinflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6 and MCP-1 were inhibited, the structure of the intestinal microbiota was optimized, and metabolic functions improved after Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) pretreatment in a mouse model of RE99. Some LGG surface molecules also have beneficial functions. For example, sortase-dependent pili mediate mucosal adhesion via SpaC subunit binding; extracellular polysaccharides reduce inflammatory cytokine production by inhibiting the p38/MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways; lipophosphatidic acid attenuates radiation damage via D-alanyl esterification modification; and peptidoglycan increases the expression of the PGlyRP3 gene to exert an anti-inflammatory effect100. LGG-derived extracellular vesicles (LGG-EVs) activate the PI3K-AKT and HIF1α signaling pathways, increasing the levels of p-AKT and HIF1α and accelerating endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis101. Lactobacilli are essential for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, modulating immune responses, facilitating nutrient digestion and absorption, and protecting against pathogenic infections102.

Akkermansia muciniphila

Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) is a gram-negative, anaerobic bacterium classified within the genus Akkermansia that plays a significant role in intestinal homeostasis103. A. muciniphila can strengthen intestinal barrier function and modulate host immune signaling through defined microbial surface molecules104. Mechanistically, microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) regulate epithelial barrier function and mucosal immunity via evolutionarily conserved mechanisms involving G protein-coupled receptor signaling and histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity. Mucin-associated commensals such as A. muciniphila may shape this SCFA milieu, thereby coupling microbial metabolism to barrier-protective epithelial-immune programs that sustain mucosal homeostasis under inflammatory stress105. In addition, the A. muciniphila-secreted effector Amuc_1409 engages E-cadherin and activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling to increase intestinal stem cell activity and epithelial regeneration, a process that is highly relevant to crypt repair following irradiation106. Given that radiation enteritis is amplified by a feedforward loop involving barrier breakdown, microbial translocation, and inflammatory escalation, mucus-associated barrier-supportive commensals such as A. muciniphila are believed to mitigate injury by promoting the restoration of the epithelial architecture and accelerating regenerative restitution, thereby weakening this self-reinforcing cycle61. He et al. reported that the abundance of A. muciniphila was significantly decreased in the intestines of both mice and patients after radiotherapy, and A. muciniphila exerted a radioprotective effect by increasing propionic acid levels107. Propionic acid interacts with the G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43), thereby upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins (Occludin and ZO-1) and mucin (MUC2) in intestinal epithelial cells. This interaction reduces intestinal permeability and promotes the restoration of the intestinal epithelial barrier108. Propionic acid also exerts protective effects on epithelial cells by inhibiting histone deacetylase (HDAC), thereby promoting the acetylation of histones such as H3K9ac and H3K14ac and subsequently increasing the expression of the tight junction protein ZO-1 and mucin MUC2109. Analyses of serum and feces from mice and patients treated with radiotherapy revealed a significant reduction in the concentration of β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB)86,110. Notably, 3-hydroxybutyrate can modulate GPR43-linked inflammatory signaling, including IL6/STAT3 activation; in radiation proctopathy, BHB is metabolized by A. muciniphila and alleviates RE by downregulating the expression of IL6 and downstream STAT3 signaling110. Pretreatment with A. muciniphila reduces radiation-induced intestinal damage in mice by promoting ISC-mediated epithelial development111. More importantly, a novel isotype of the A. muciniphila strain was isolated from healthy human faeces, which subsequently was proven to enhance intestinal epithelial regeneration, strongly supporting its potential clinical utilization. Kang et al. demonstrated that A. muciniphila facilitates ISC regeneration and mitigates radiation-induced intestinal injury through the secretion of the protein Amuc_1409, which binds to E-cadherin and activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway106. In addition, A. muciniphila contributes to the enrichment of beneficial bacteria such as Rumenococcaceae and Trichosporonaceae, increasing the diversity of the intestinal microbiota and contributing to favorable intestinal homeostasis112. However, A. muciniphila plays a complex role in human health. For instance, A. muciniphila may exert context-dependent effects and, under certain conditions, has been associated with barrier dysfunction and inflammatory responses in neurological disease settings113. Therefore, future translational work may benefit from evaluating both whole-organism and component-based approaches, including defined effectors such as Amuc_1409, rather than the bacteria itself.

Pathogenic bacteria

Radiation-induced microbiome dysbiosis reflects not only a reduction in the abundance of beneficial bacteria but also an increase in the abundance of pathogenic bacteria that actively drive epithelial barrier dysfunction and amplify inflammatory responses. Escherichia-Shigella

Escherichia-Shigella represents a gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic Enterobacteriaceae “genus complex” that typically occurs at low abundance in the healthy gut; however, under dysbiosis and mucosal inflammation conditions, it can behave as a pathobiont, resulting in increased abundance114. In a previous retrospective study, 16S rDNA sequencing was used to analyse changes in the microbiome of patients with cervical cancer and RE, revealing a significant increase in the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella in the stool samples of these patients compared with that in healthy controls115. In an RE mouse model, compared with that in the unirradiated group, the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella was increased during the first week after radiation116. An increase in the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella in the intestine results in a decrease in the expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1, occludin and claudin-1 and a reduction in mucus secretion, which facilitates the translocation of luminal noxious factors, triggers inflammatory responses and exacerbates intestinal injury, thereby destabilizing the intestinal microenvironment117. In line with this barrier-disruptive phenotype, enrichment of Escherichia-Shigella has been linked to heightened IL-6/STAT3 inflammatory signaling, providing a plausible route through which this Enterobacteriaceae bloom amplifies epithelial stress responses and mucosal inflammation in patients with early RE118. As a gram-negative pathobiont, Escherichia-Shigella can increase the intestinal load of LPS, thereby engaging in TLR4-dependent innate sensing and downstream NF-κB programs; this cytokine-rich milieu can further converge to activate JAK/STAT3 signaling, increase permeability and amplify inflammation114,119. Collectively, these data suggest that enrichment of Escherichia-Shigella is not only a compositional hallmark of RE-associated dysbiosis but also a functionally relevant pathobiont shift that couples barrier collapse with IL-6-JAK/STAT3-driven and TLR4-NF-κB-driven increased inflammatory responses, thereby sustaining a feedforward injury loop120.

Enterotoxin-producing Bacteroides fragilis and enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli

Enterotoxin-producing Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) is a member of the phylum Bacteroidota and has been associated with intestinal disorders and persistent RE. ETBF induces an NF-κB/STAT3/IL-17 signaling cascade that recruits Th17 and CXCR2⁺ myeloid-derived suppressor cells and promotes the development of RE121. ETBF downregulates miR-149-3p in host cells, which results in the upregulation of PHF5A to modulate the alternative splicing of KAT2A, thereby increasing the expression of genes such as SOD2 and promoting intestinal inflammation122. Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) produces two enterotoxins: heat-stable enterotoxin (ST) and heat-labile enterotoxin (LT). ST activates guanylate cyclase, resulting in elevated intracellular cGMP levels, whereas LT activates adenylyl cyclase, leading to increased cAMP levels123. Both toxins drive intestinal hypersecretion, contributing to the development of diarrhoea124. In addition to fluid secretion, LT can reshape epithelial programs in the small intestine in vivo, supporting broader enteropathic effects that may aggravate inflammation when mucosal defences are weakened125. ETEC causes significant increases in the levels of IL-17A and IFN-γ, which results in many neutrophils accumulating in the intestinal tract, resulting in the host generating an immune response and disrupting the balance of the intestinal environment, which in turn leads to intestinal inflammation126. ETEC also changes the structure of the gut microbial community by decreasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Clostridium, Lactobacilli and Bifidobacterium127.

Enterococcus

Enterococcus spp. are gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic lactic acid bacteria that commonly reside in the human gut at low abundance; however, under ecological disturbance conditions, they can behave as pathobionts and shift towards opportunistic pathogenicity128. Barrier compromise and microbiota disruption can therefore relax colonization constraints and lead to Enterococcus outgrowth, an ecological pattern that is frequently discussed in radiation-associated intestinal injury and dysbiosis86. The genus Enterococcus comprises common opportunistic pathogens within the gut, including species such as E. faecalis and E. faecium129. These typically exist at low abundance in healthy individuals but are prone to abnormal proliferation when mucosal barriers are compromised or host immunity is weakened116. RE progression results in damage to intestinal epithelial cells, the perturbation of tight junction proteins and an imbalance of microbes, leading to conditions favorable for the enrichment of Enterococcus16. A recent study showed that in various models of intestinal inflammation, increased populations of Enterococcus are coupled with increased populations of Proteobacteria and decreased populations of more favorable bacteria such as Lactobacilli and Bifidobacterium130. A mechanistic study showed that reductions in the function of the intestinal barrier promote the adhesion and colonization of Enterococcus, which triggers inflammatory signaling through cell wall-associated components, such as lipoteichoic acid (LTA), which are recognized by pattern recognition receptors, including TLRs, and subsequently activates NF-κB-dependent inflammatory pathways131. Additionally, the transepithelial translocation ability of Enterococcus is strong under conditions of elevated intestinal mucosal permeability, facilitating the entry of bacteria into the blood flow and leading to bacteraemia or systemic infections, which can ultimately negatively affect patient outcomes132. Notably, some strains of E. faecium contain genes conferring multidrug resistance, which might present a challenge in the clinical management of patients with RE and infections133. The expansion of Enterococcaceae may partly reflect radiation-associated intestinal dysbiosis, and its detection during radiotherapy could help capture clinically relevant fluctuations in microbial burden and community structures71,134.

Clostridium sensu stricto 1

Clostridium sensu stricto 1 represents a gut-associated clade of predominantly gram-positive, spore-forming, obligately anaerobic rods that typically persists at low relative abundance but can respond rapidly to ecological disturbance135. Clostridium sensu stricto 1 belongs to the Firmicutes phylum. While present at low abundance in the normal gut, it often exhibits abnormal proliferation during inflammatory states and is considered a prototypical representative of the inflammation-associated microbiota136. Clostridium sensu stricto 1 has been shown to stimulate the development of enteritis through various mechanisms. First, it may secrete toxins or cell wall-associated molecules that directly damage epithelial cells and disrupt tight junctions, resulting in increased permeability of the intestinal barrier137. Second, it can stimulate the innate immune recognition mechanisms of the host, including the induction of the NF-κB signaling pathway via TLR2/TLR4, thus promoting the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, which initiates an inflammatory cascade response138. Wen et al. reported that the abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto 1 is positively correlated with the expression of genes linked to inflammation, such as REG3G and IDO1, suggesting its potential role in mediating inflammation139. A retrospective clinical study indicated that the abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto 1 is enriched in patients with ulcerative colitis and Clostridioides difficile infection, where its increased abundance may correlate with greater disease severity140. Moreover, meta-analyses have revealed that the expansion of this bacteria is frequently accompanied by a reduction in the levels of short-chain fatty acid-producing taxa and SCFA, which may aggravate intestinal inflammation and contribute to microbial dysbiosis141. In response to its potential hazards, several regulatory strategies have been proposed, including promoting the recovery of butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium, to competitively inhibit its proliferation and optimizing the gut microbiome structure through probiotic supplementation and dietary fiber interventions142.

Microbial metabolites

Bile acids (BAs) metabolized by the gut microbiota play a pivotal role in the regulation of gut health143. Conjugated primary bile acids, including cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), undergo deconjugation catalyzed by bile salt hydrolases (BSHs) expressed by probiotic bacteria, such as Lactobacillaceae and Bifidobacterium spp.leading to the production of free bile acids144. Clostridium spp. expresses 7α-dehydroxylase, which dehydroxylates free bile acids to form the secondary bile acids LCA and DCA145. Actinobacteria such as Eggerthella produce hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSDH), which mediates the redox reaction of bile acids, producing various oxidized or isomeric bile acids, promoting Treg cell differentiation, inhibiting Th17 expansion, and maintaining intestinal immune tolerance and homeostasis146. These secondary bile acids activate FXR receptors on the surface of the intestinal epithelium and immune cells, increase the expression of the intestinal epithelial tight junction and enhance intestinal barrier function. Furthermore, the activation of FXR receptors inhibits the NF-κB pathway, reduces the expression of the inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β, and reduces intestinal inflammation147. The activation of TGR5 receptors by secondary bile acids inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation via the cAMP-PKA pathway, promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype and attenuates the inflammatory response148. Moreover, TGR5 activation indirectly activates Wnt signaling through the cAMP-PKA cascade, thereby facilitating ISC regeneration149. Radiation damage leads to a significant reduction in the abundance of bile acid-metabolizing commensal taxa (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) in the gut, thereby blocking the secondary bile acid production pathway and causing bile acid metabolism disorders150. In addition, emerging work suggests that specific microbiota-derived secondary bile acids can directly suppress colonic inflammation through epithelial cGMP-PKG signaling (with downstream restraint of mTORC1), providing a mechanistic link between bile acid remodeling and mucosal protection151.

SCFAs are metabolic byproducts produced through the fermentation of dietary fiber by the gut microbiota and primarily consist of acetic, propionic, and butyric acids152. SCFAs play crucial roles in maintaining gut homeostasis; one representative example is butyrate, which protects barrier function, activates the immune response and suppresses proinflammatory processes153. Butyric acid activates the Nrf2 pathway to increase the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD2), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), reduce the oxidative damage caused by ROS, and maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier154. Key butyrate-producing taxa in the human gut include Faecalibacterium (notably F. prausnitzii), Roseburia, and several members of the Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families155–157. Beneficial gut microbiota, including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillaceae-associated taxa, Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, and other anaerobic fermenters, contribute directly or indirectly to SCFA production158. F. prausnitzii produces SCFAs through the acetyl-CoA pathway159. SCFAs are not only energy substrates but also act as signaling molecules that bind to G protein-coupled receptors (GPRs) to regulate immune responses. By binding to GPR41/43, SCFAs inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway; reduce the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α; and increase the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10. Collectively, these factors mitigate tissue damage160. SCFAs upregulate the expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and claudin-1 and mucin (MUC2), improve intestinal epithelial barrier function and reduce intestinal permeability161. The levels of SCFAs, such as propionic acid, are significantly increased in radiation-surviving mice. The supplementation of propionic acid significantly increased the survival rate of mice after radiotherapy, improved intestinal damage, and reduced oxidative stress and DNA damage162. Consistent with this concept, butyrate has been shown to hinder epithelial inflammatory programs via the HDAC-NF-κB axis while simultaneously reinforcing the integrity of tight junction proteins, thereby supporting a mechanistic framework in which SCFAs sufficiency couple anti-inflammatory signaling with barrier repair163.

Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, is metabolized by bacteria to produce indole and its derived metabolites, and in recent years, it has been regarded as an important signaling molecule that links the microbiota to host immunity164. Indole derivatives, including indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), indolepropionic acid (IPA), IAA, and indole-3-aldehyde (I3A), are products of microbial metabolism and serve as both intracellular signaling molecules and agonists of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)165. Microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites promote IL-22 secretion, antimicrobial peptide production and intestinal barrier repair by increasing AHR expression in immune cells and activating intestinal epithelial and endothelial cells166. AHR activation inhibits Th17 cell expansion, induces Treg cell differentiation, and attenuates intestinal inflammation167. AHR activation also attenuates NF-κB signaling, resulting in decreased expression of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α168. Indole downregulates the expression of LBP in macrophages by activating HTR2B receptors and blocks the IκB-α/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway, thus inhibiting the release of proinflammatory factors. Furthermore, it promotes the polarization of M2-type macrophages, thereby synergistically exerting anti-inflammatory effects and alleviating intestinal inflammatory responses169.

Microbiota-targeted interventions

The gut microbiota serves as a central regulatory hub in the pathogenesis of RE, playing multiple roles as a “victim” of radiation damage, a “mediator” of mucosal repair, and a “driver” or “suppressor” of inflammatory responses. Currently, several microbiota-targeted interventions, such as antibiotics, probiotics, dietary interventions and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), have been developed (Table 2). In addition, several novel microbial therapies, such as engineered bacteria and phages, show promise for RE treatment (Fig. 4). However, the composition and biological function of the gut microbiota are complicated under both physiological and irradiated conditions. For instance, Guo et al. reported that a limited number of mice could endure high doses of radiation energy and have a normal life span; these mice were considered elite survivors162. In this study, Enterococcaceae was significantly enriched in the intestines of these mice and was proven to promote haematopoiesis and attenuate gastrointestinal damage. However, the Enterococcaceae family is suggested to be opportunistic pathogens in some clinical settings86,170. Summary of evidence regarding microbiota-targeted interventions in radiation enteritis

Antibiotic-mediated remodeling of the gut microbiota

Antibiotics rapidly reduce microbial diversity and restructure community composition in the short term, resulting in substantial changes in microbial metabolic phenotypes and ultimately recalibrating mucosal homeostasis and inflammatory networks under radiation-induced stress. In terms of the management of chronic haemorrhagic radiation proctitis (HRP), a randomized controlled trial (RCT) was performed by Sahakitrungruang et al. to compare the efficacy of colonic irrigation combined with a short course of oral antibiotics against topical 4% formalin application171. This study revealed that the combined strategy was superior in ameliorating diarrhoea and tenesmus, suggesting the potential value of antibiotics in RE therapy. With respect to the management of chronic diarrhoea postradiotherapy, a prospective aetiological study by Danielsson et al. indicated that bile acid malabsorption and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) are prevalent in this patient population. Among patients with identifiable aetiological factors, the use of targeted interventions, including antibiotics, is associated with a significant reduction in the frequency of diarrhoea172,173. In addition to clinical observations, an animal experiment provided mechanistic insights into the potential of antibiotic interventions in RE. Zhao et al. utilized a murine abdominal irradiation model and reported that antibiotic pretreatment not only mitigated radiation-induced intestinal injury and improved survival outcomes but also significantly downregulated inflammation- and fibrosis-associated signaling pathways174. However, the microbiome-modulating effects of antibiotics are inherently nonspecific and carry substantial risks, including severe depletion of microbial diversity, expansion of opportunistic pathogens (e.g., Clostridioides difficile), and exacerbation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR)175. Therefore, despite supporting evidence, the use of antibiotics in RE should be cautiously recommended on the basis of disease severity, infection evidence and general conditions, including hepatic and renal function. In addition, short-term symptomatic benefits must be rigorously weighed against the risk of long-term dysbiosis caused by antibiotics175.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) involves the introduction of a complex microbial community from a healthy donor into a recipient’s gut, aiming to holistically restore the recipient’s microecological architecture and functional networks176. Considering that radiation exposure induces profound alterations in the gut microbiota of both animals and humans, FMT has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for RE. In this context, FMT holds promise for restoring functional deficits at the ecosystem level, restoring metabolic cross-talk, and establishing a permissive environment for mucosal barrier repair and the rebalancing of immune-inflammatory responses. Unlike single-strain supplementation, the main advantages of FMT are the use of a multispecies composition, inter-microbial synergy, and the use of diverse metabolite profiles, which more closely approximate the trajectory of natural ecological recovery. However, the clinical utility of FMT should not be evaluated solely using taxonomic shifts as a surrogate endpoint; instead, a robust evaluation framework centered on quantifiable clinical outcomes and longitudinal tracking of strain engraftment or functional recovery are needed to increase interpretability and reproducibility177. Case reports by Ding X et al. suggest that FMT can mitigate intestinal symptoms and ameliorate mucosal injury with a generally acceptable safety profile in a subset of RE patients, thereby providing direct evidence for its feasibility and potential benefits in RE178. Similarly, a case report by Wang L et al. demonstrated that a patient with chronic RE experienced sustained relief from symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and haematochezia following two courses of intestinal microecological transplantation, accompanied by a shift in microbial diversity and composition towards the donor’s profile179. Collectively, the results of existing studies indicate that FMT offers potential clinical benefits for certain chronic or refractory RE phenotypes; nevertheless, given that the current evidence stems primarily from small, nonrandomized cohort studies, the efficacy, optimal target populations, and long-term safety of this treatment must be further investigated through prospective, controlled studies based on standardized protocols177. Additionally, Bustamante et al. demonstrated that FMT can alter functions related to bacterial bile acid metabolism and remodel the faecal bile acid pool, suggesting that the effects of FMT extend beyond mere taxonomic replacement, encompassing the comprehensive reconfiguration of functional pathways and the restoration of metabolic outputs180. Specifically, Clostridium scindens (now known as Lachnoclostridium scindens) is a pivotal functional species that mediates the conversion of primary bile acids into secondary bile acids via 7α-dehydroxylation, thereby maintaining intestinal homeostasis and barrier integrity181. In patients with RE, the depletion of this bacterium exacerbates dysbiosis and inflammation, while its restoration, which is frequently observed following FMT, is crucial for suppressing pathogens and promoting mucosal repair182. Consequently, C. scindens serves as a key protective marker and therapeutic target for mitigating radiation-induced intestinal damage through bile acid-dependent mechanisms183. Furthermore, Fujimoto et al. showed that recipient bacteriomes and viromes can functionally recover towards healthy profiles post-FMT in clinical cohorts, supporting the clinical benefits of FMT through the maintenance of ecosystem stability184. He et al. and Chen et al. utilized distinct data frameworks and reported that intermicrobial interactions between donor and recipient microbiomes critically dictate strain transfer and are correlated with clinical outcomes185,186. Both of these studies suggest that donor-recipient matching should prioritize ecological compatibility and engraftment potential rather than relying on simplistic assessments of donor “quality”.

Despite encouraging evidence, two major limitations should be considered. First, a universally accepted and standardized protocol for FMT is lacking. Operational variables, such as delivery routes and transplantation modalities, can significantly impact success rates and reproducibility; thus, reproducible procedural frameworks and quality control systems must be established187. Second, even when clinical remission is achieved, FMT may carry latent long-term risks, including metabolic perturbations, immune dysregulation, and the inadvertent colonization of potentially pathogenic strains. For patients with chronic diseases such as RE, quantifying and delineating these risks through large-scale, long-term prospective cohort studies is particularly important.

Dietary intervention

Dietary factors are intricately linked to intestinal homeostasis, and maladaptive dietary stimuli can trigger barrier impairment, thereby inducing or exacerbating intestinal inflammation188. Emerging evidence suggests that distinct dietary patterns reshape the compositional and functional landscape of the gut microbiota, activating either proinflammatory or anti-inflammatory signatures189. Furthermore, animal models have demonstrated that specific dietary interventions can increase mucosal tolerance to ionizing radiation by modulating microbial metabolic outputs, potentially alleviating radiation-induced intestinal injury (RE) phenotypes190. A fiber-rich diet represents a low-risk microecological intervention, which is primarily valued for mitigating gastrointestinal toxicity and fortifying mucosal barrier resilience191. Malipatlolla et al. evaluated high-fiber and fiber-free diets in a fractionated distal colorectal irradiation mouse model and demonstrated that a fiber-rich diet significantly altered the temporal dynamics of histopathological progression following radiation, suggesting the protective profile of this dietary intervention in radiation enteritis through the prevention of late irradiation-induced bacterial infiltration and the reduction of proinflammatory systemic cytokine responses192. Recently, caloric restriction (CR) and fasting have been identified as representative interventions that modulate the gut microbiota and influence radiation outcomes193. Specifically, Li et al. demonstrated that short-term CR pretreatment alleviates radiation-induced intestinal injury; furthermore, its protective effects partially depend on sex-specific microbial remodeling194. Similarly, Zhang et al. confirmed that dietary restriction and short-term fasting promote recovery from radiation-induced damage, a process similar to structural shifts in the microbiota195. In addition to caloric reduction, recent preclinical studies have focused on chrono-nutritional regulation. For example, He et al. reported that daytime-restricted feeding (DTRF) reshaped the microbiota to increase intestinal creatine levels, thereby inhibiting radiation-induced ferroptosis and conferring radioprotective effects190. A ketogenic diet (KD), characterized by high fat, moderate protein, and minimal carbohydrate intake, mimics the metabolic state of fasting196. Several studies have suggested that KD modulates digestive, hormonal, and immune homeostasis via the gut microbiome197. In colitis models, KD has been shown to significantly attenuate inflammatory phenotypes, protect barrier function and alter microbial and metabolic profiles198. Despite its benefits, a long-term KD may lead to several adverse effects, such as impaired glycaemic control (including reduced glucose tolerance), an adverse atherogenic lipoprotein shift (e.g., elevated apolipoprotein B levels), increased systemic inflammatory burden (e.g., higher C-reactive protein levels), and persistent restructuring of the gut microbiome (altered β diversity)199.

A high-fat diet (HFD), characterized by an elevated proportion of fat-derived energy and saturated fatty acids, has been identified as a driver of colorectal tumorigenesis through the modulation of the gut microbiota and metabolites200. A recent animal study indicated that a HFD compromises intestinal ILC3 function and diminishes IL-22-mediated mucosal defence, leading to a breakdown in intestinal homeostasis201. Notably, we argue that the co-occurrence of RE and a HFD results in a unique metabolic and inflammatory niche. Under such extreme physiological stress, certain members of Ruminococcaceae may shift from homeostatic roles to contributing to intestinal dysbiosis, a phenomenon observed in several IBD and metabolic syndrome models202. Therefore, a HFD may exacerbate the inflammatory burden and undermine barrier homeostasis and should be avoided in patients with RE. Similarly, another detrimental dietary pattern known as a high-salt diet (HSD), defined by sustained, elevated salt intake, induces a significant dysbiotic shift towards proinflammatory taxa, thereby impairing homeostatic resilience203,204. Excessive salt intake may also exacerbate the development of RE.

Vitamins serve as essential micronutrients that maintain intestinal homeostasis by modulating the gut microecology and preserving epithelial barrier integrity205. Xian et al. demonstrated that vitamin‒lipid conjugate nanoassemblies significantly mitigated weight loss, diarrhoea, and haematochezia in colitis models while also increasing microbial diversity and the abundance of beneficial commensals at the expense of pathobionts206. While maintaining optimal vitamin levels is integral to supporting microbial balance and barrier function, caution is warranted against indiscriminate high-dose supplementation in the absence of documented deficiency. Furthermore, enteral nutrition (EN) is recommended as the primary nutritional support for patients with inadequate oral intake, as it may alleviate acute radiation-induced diarrhoea and modulate the gut microecology207. However, high-quality randomized controlled trials confirming whether EN can be used by patients with RE are lacking. Partial enteral nutrition (PEN) has been demonstrated to modulate the composition of the gut microbiota, particularly by promoting the proliferation of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria and enhancing mucosal barrier function208. However, while these therapeutic mechanisms show promise, robust clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of PEN specifically for RE remains limited; thus, further prospective studies are needed.

Despite the potential benefits of dietary interventions, their clinical translation remains hindered by several bottlenecks. A primary constraint is the significant methodological heterogeneity and lack of reproducibility of the existing studies. Variations in dietary composition, caloric targets, intervention intensity, and endpoint definitions across trials complicate the synthesis and extrapolation of evidence209. Furthermore, evidence from multicentre prospective cohort studies indicates that the efficacy of these interventions depends considerably on patient adherence to the behavioral intervention210. Critically, randomized evidence suggests that restrictive dietary regimens during radiotherapy may inadvertently increase the risk of weight loss, sarcopenia, and micronutrient insufficiencies211. Consequently, while dietary strategies show promise for mitigating radiation-induced gastrointestinal toxicity, the current evidence remains limited by uncertainties in terms of patient adherence and insufficient long-term safety monitoring.

In addition to macronutrient patterns, certain micronutrients, especially vitamins, are critical for sustaining intestinal homeostasis. Vitamins not only are crucial nutrients for the host but also play pivotal roles in maintaining the balance of the intestinal microbiota205. Deficiencies in vitamin A can lead to a reduction in the abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Lactobacilli and Bacteroides, while promoting the proliferation of potentially pathogenic species. Vitamin B supports the homeostasis of the intestinal microbiota and enhances intestinal barrier function and anti-inflammatory responses by regulating the growth, metabolism, and immune pathways of specific commensal bacteria. Vitamin C, which functions as an antioxidant, alleviates oxidative stress, thereby contributing to the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis212. Vitamin D modulates microbial diversity via the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and promotes the growth of beneficial microbiota, including Bifidobacteria and probiotic bacteria such as Lactobacillus spp213.. Vitamin E maintains intestinal barrier function by reducing oxidative stress, inflammation and microbiota imbalance214. Xian et al. developed vitamin C-lipid supramolecular nanoassemblies and demonstrated in DSS-induced colitis mice that this strategy significantly ameliorated disease severity, reduced inflammatory burden, and reshaped the gut microbial profile, thereby suggesting a potential role for vitamin-based interventions in modulating intestinal inflammation and microbiota homeostasis206. Therefore, appropriate vitamin supplementation may benefit RE therapy by maintaining healthy intestinal homeostasis.

Probiotics and synbiotics

Probiotics are defined as live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host75. As detailed in Sections 3.1.1–3.1.4, specific probiotic genera, such as Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus and Akkermansia muciniphila, exert protective effects against RE through defined mechanisms, including barrier reinforcement, immunomodulation, and the promotion of epithelial regeneration. A meta-analysis by Hamad et al. on radiation-induced bowel disease revealed that probiotic supplementation generally assisted in preventing radiation-related symptoms215. However, owing to substantial interstudy heterogeneity, the efficacy of this treatment and the reproducibility of the results must be carefully interpreted, accounting for the quality of the evidence and the patient stratification. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), Delia et al. reported promising outcomes for a high-potency multistrain formula of probiotics in terms of reducing the incidence and severity of radiation-induced diarrhoea216. Conversely, a multicentre RCT by Giralt et al. revealed that Lactobacillus casei DN-114 001 failed to significantly reduce diarrhoea incidence, although improvements in stool consistency were noted217. Most probiotics can also promote tryptophan metabolism to generate indoles, activate AhR to increase IL-22 secretion, promote epithelial repair and mucosal homeostasis, and have a protective effect against long-term radiation218. Synbiotics, defined as a synergistic mixture of “live microorganisms and a substrate selectively utilized by host microorganisms”, offer an integrated approach to enhancing host health219. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial, Nascimento et al. demonstrated that synbiotics significantly reduced the symptom burden of patients with acute radiation proctitis and improved patient quality of life, suggesting measurable clinical benefits for patients with localized rectal symptom profiles220. Furthermore, Stene et al. provided clinical evidence indicating that synbiotic intervention is correlated with attenuated tissue damage and favorable shifts in mucosa-associated microbiota, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of synbiotics in rectal cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy221. However, despite these promising clinical observations, the translation of these robust mechanistic insights into universally effective therapies faces a major challenge: marked interstudy heterogeneity. This variability stems from critical differences in probiotic strain composition, dosing schedules, radiation fields, cumulative dosages, and the use of concurrent chemotherapy. Therefore, to bridge the gap between mechanistic studies and clinical application, stratified randomized controlled trials that employ rigorously characterized microbial strains, standardized dosing protocols, and prespecified, patient-centered primary endpoints must be prioritized in future research.

Engineered probiotics and bacteriophages

Engineered probiotics are modified versions of commensal microbes designed with enhanced capabilities for target recognition, signal sensing, and the controlled release of therapeutic bioactive compounds. Recently, Chen W. et al. developed a pH-shielded E. coli Nissle 1917 platform engineered to secrete TGF-β, which successfully mitigated nanoplastic-induced gut barrier dysfunction in murine models222. In addition to genetic modification, delivery engineering has emerged as a critical factor in optimizing therapeutic outcomes. For example, Peng P. et al. demonstrated that E. coli Nissle 1917, modified via bioorthogonal conjugation and responsive nanocoatings, significantly improved both clinical manifestations and histopathological outcomes in experimental colitis models223. To overcome physiological barriers, Chang Y. et al. developed self-assembled DNA capsules that shield probiotics from gastric acidity while enabling inflammation-triggered release, thereby enhancing localized therapeutic gains224. Similarly, mucoadhesive microcarriers have been shown to prolong intestinal residence time, further augmenting the anti-inflammatory effects of oral probiotics225. Collectively, while the efficacy of engineered probiotics has consistently been demonstrated in preclinical models, their clinical translation for RE necessitates strict validation studies.

Bacteriophages, as integral constituents of the gut virome, play pivotal roles in modulating bacterial community dynamics and show potential as precision antimicrobial or gene-delivery vectors. In a preclinical study employing mucosal models, Wu J. et al. reported that the mucosal adherence of bacteriophages increases their in vivo protective efficacy, thereby shielding the murine gut from E. coli invasion and mitigating associated adverse sequelae226. Furthermore, Lam K.N. et al. demonstrated the utility of engineered M13 phages for the targeted delivery of DNA and CRISPR-Cas9 to E. coli within the murine gastrointestinal tract, enabling strain-specific depletion and precise genomic editing227. Additionally, clinical trials have been performed, with a metagenomic analysis of a randomized, double-blind trial for adolescent obesity revealing that donor phages can stably engraft FMT, driving the recipient’s phageome towards a composition similar to that of the donor while significantly increasing viral diversity228. In the context of intestinal inflammation, Islam M.S. et al. reported that phage therapy alleviates colitis-related outcomes by modulating both the gut microbiome and host immune responses229. While phage cocktails and broader therapeutic frameworks have been extensively explored for the eradication of enteric pathogens and multispecies biofilms, current evidence remains largely concentrated in the preclinical and proof-of-concept stages. Consequently, to apply phage-based interventions for RE in clinical settings, rigorous clinical validation and the establishment of standardized safety monitoring frameworks are needed230.

Future directions and challenges

RE is a complicated biological process in which ionizing radiation causes damage to intestinal stem cells and the vascular endothelium while also reducing the integrity of the epithelial barrier, thereby inducing immune and inflammatory responses. Increasing evidence suggests a close correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and RE pathogenesis. Both beneficial and detrimental bacteria affect the development of RE through various mechanisms, such as metabolite-mediated inflammatory signaling and alterations in immune cells. Therefore, microbiota-targeted strategies have great potential for use in RE management. Despite the significant novel findings in this field, several challenges should be mentioned. First, compared with investigations of other intestinal diseases, such as IBD and colorectal cancer, investigations of the related mechanism in RE are relatively limited. Core beneficial and detrimental bacteria and their key metabolites must be identified via metagenomics and metabolomics studies. In addition, advanced single-cell sequencing techniques are helpful for clarifying the impact of the gut microbiota on immune cells during RE development. Second, the current studies largely rely on cellular and animal models, whereas human colonic organoids may be more suitable since they are more physiologically relevant and better simulate the epithelial microenvironment. Finally, the isolation of beneficial and detrimental bacterial strains from different RE patients may contribute to understanding the complicated changes and functions of the gut microbiome during different stages of RE.

Regarding microbiota-targeted interventions, first, retrospective and prospective multicentre clinical studies with sufficient samples must be conducted to clarify the microbial profile of RE. Second, well-designed clinical trials are recommended to investigate the efficacy and safety of dietary interventions and probiotic administration in patients with RE, with a focus on patient selection, drug dosages and nutrient content. Third, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria should be applied to select donors before FMT. In addition, the storage and usage of bacterial solutions should be standardized according to guidelines. Finally, the evolution of artificial intelligence algorithms will benefit the analysis of sequencing data and the discovery of novel engineered bacteria and phages for RE therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, several studies have suggested that the gut microbiota and its metabolites play vital roles in the development of RE through the regulation of inflammatory signaling, which affects ISCs and immune cells. Microbiota-targeted interventions such as probiotic administration and FMT have shown promise in some clinical studies, but further validation is needed. With the increasing understanding of the role of the gut microbiota in RE, some related findings may benefit early diagnosis and individualized therapy for this disease.
