Assessing the causal effect of tobacco retail density on cardiovascular and pulmonary disease hospitalizations in the United States
Authors: Chris D. Baggett, Tzy-Mey Kuo, Paul N. Zivich, Jacqueline E. Rudolph, Kurt M. Ribisl, Shelley D. Golden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesHigh tobacco retail density is linked to greater tobacco-related disease, but most evidence is cross-sectional limiting causal inference. Here, we use longitudinal data (2002–2016) from 327 counties in five U.S. states to estimate the causal effect of reducing tobacco retail density on hospitalizations for cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases.MethodsCounty-level data for Kentucky, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, and North Carolina were included. Annual rates of hospitalizations for acute myocardial infarction, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), ischemic heart disease (IHD), and stroke were analyzed; annual tobacco retail density was dichotomized at ≥1.26 retailers per 1000 population. Marginal structural models with stabilized inverse probability weights were used to adjust for time-varying confounders.ResultsHigh tobacco retail density was associated with increased hospitalization incidence rate ratios (IRR) for asthma (IRR = 1.43 [1.21, 1.69]), COPD (IRR = 1.13 [95%CI: 1.01, 1.25]), IHD (IRR = 1.09 [95%CI: 1.00, 1.18]), and stroke (IRR = 1.08 [95%CI: 1.01, 1.15]). Incidence rate differences indicated that had all counties been below the cut point, thousands of hospitalizations would have been prevented over the 15-year study period.ConclusionThese findings provide further evidence supporting retail-focused tobacco control policies as effective strategies to reduce the burden of tobacco-related disease.

Introduction

Traditional tobacco control strategies such as raising excise taxes, adopting smoke free air laws, and launching media campaigns, have contributed substantially to declines in tobacco use (Akter et al., 2024). More recent policy efforts, however, have increasingly targeted the retail environment, recognizing its role in shaping access to and normalization of tobacco products. Several jurisdictions have implemented or considered strategies to reduce the number and concentration of tobacco retailers, including prohibitions on tobacco sales in pharmacies (Jin et al., 2016), restrictions on sales near schools (Ribisl et al., 2017), and limitations on the number or density of retail tobacco licenses (Glasser and Roberts, 2021).

These efforts have been stimulated in part by research indicating reducing tobacco retailer density may help lower smoking prevalence. Golden et al. (Golden et al., 2025) found that higher retailer density is associated with higher smoking prevalence, implying that density reductions may contribute to declines in smoking. This is consistent with conclusions from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026), which report that limiting retailer density can reduce smoking initiation and support cessation. A systematic review similarly show that greater retailer density is linked to higher smoking behavior across diverse populations (Baker et al., 2023). Together, these findings support the plausibility that policies reducing retailer density may help decrease smoking prevalence and improve downstream health outcomes.

Emerging evidence also suggests that elevated tobacco retail density is associated with increased incidence of tobacco-related chronic health conditions. Studies from California (Lipton and Banerjee, 2007) and Australia (Barnes et al., 2016) have documented positive associations between retail density and hospitalizations for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and heart disease among people who smoke. In Baltimore, Maryland, greater tobacco retail density was linked with lower life expectancy, higher all-cause mortality, and increased deaths attributable to chronic lower respiratory disease (Galiatsatos et al., 2018). A national sample including approximately half of U.S. counties (n = 1510) found that higher retail density was associated with a 19% increase in COPD-related hospital discharges, 22% more total inpatient days, and 30% higher hospitalization costs (Kong et al., 2021). Although prior studies in this area have documented the potential harmful impacts of greater tobacco retail density, they have largely utilized cross-sectional designs to investigate associations between tobacco retail density and disease. Therefore, prior evidence is limited by the lack of temporality between retailer density and health outcomes.

In this study, we aim to estimate the causal effect of tobacco retail density on cardiovascular and pulmonary disease hospitalizations. Using longitudinal data on retail density and hospital utilization across five U.S. states from 2002 to 2016, we apply a marginal structural model (MSM) and account for time-varying confounding using inverse probability weighting.

Methods

Study population

This study used county-level data for the years 2002–2016 from five states: Kentucky, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, and North Carolina, which include a total sample of 327 counties. These states were selected due to the availability of patient county of residence information for the full study time period and the diversity of demographic characteristics, tobacco retail density, and smoking behavior among these states.

Measures

Annual county rates of inpatient hospitalizations for acute myocardial infarction (AMI), asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), ischemic heart disease (IHD), and stroke were calculated based on inpatient discharge data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project, 2025) (HCUP) State Inpatient Databases (SID). The SID contain the entirety of hospital inpatient discharges from contributing states. We used the Chronic Conditions Warehouse definition of each condition (Chronic Conditions, 2025); the presence of a corresponding International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code in the principal diagnosis field, the condition primarily responsible for the hospitalization, indicated the occurrence of each outcome. The hospital discharge data were then aggregated to the county level and merged with county-level population data from the U.S. Census Bureau to measure the annual rate of each outcome in 2002–2016.

Our goal was to estimate the causal effect of reducing tobacco retail density across the follow-up period. Consistent with tobacco retail density cap policies which set a limit on the number of retailer licenses per capita that are issued (Lawman et al., 2020), we conceptualized a plan that reduced density below a specific threshold or cap. The time-varying exposure was county-level tobacco retail density measured each year. Tobacco retailers in each county were identified through the North American Industry Classification System codes in the National Establishment Time-Series (NETS) (Barnatchez et al., 2017). This method has been thoroughly described previously (Golden et al., 2022a). To calculate county retail density per 1000 population each year, we used the county of operation indicated in NETS for each retailer, summed the number of tobacco retailers in each county, then divided by the total population of the county and multiplied this by 1000.

Given the relatively small number of counties in the five states (N = 327) and the disproportionately high number of counties and high tobacco retail density of Kentucky (n = 120, Median density = 1.65 versus 1.20 per 1000 population in the other 4 states) we selected a cut point of greater than or equal to 1.26 to designate high tobacco retail density counties, which is at the 34th percentile for the entire sample, but provides a more even distribution of high versus low density counties within each state. Retail density exposure was defined as high in counties above or equal the cut point in a given year, and low in counties below the cut point. At baseline, 236 counties (73%) were above the cut point. While the cut point of 1.26 was fixed across years, the exposure status of a county was allowed to change each year.

Variables were selected based on the research team's expertise and previously published literature (Schultz et al., 2018; Kong et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2025; Danesh Yazdi et al., 2025; Hoe et al., 2022; Xing et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2025) of potential confounders— factors associated with both tobacco retail density and the health outcomes. Confounders included county-level annual average concentration of particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter < 2.5 μm (PM2.5) from the Environmental Protection Agency (Rudolph et al., 2022) and annual estimates of key sociodemographic characteristics. These estimates were constructed by integrating data from the 2000 and 2010 U.S. Census with the 2005–2016 American Community Survey and interpolating yearly values using natural cubic splines (Overview: EXPAND Procedure, 2026): Variables included the proportion of the population that was non-White, aged ≥65 years, living below the federal poverty line; median household income (inflation-adjusted to 2000 dollars); and an indicator for counties where at least half of residents lived in urban versus rural areas. Models also adjusted for state, year of hospitalization, and a binary indicator for prior-year tobacco retail density status (≥1.26 vs. <1.26).

Statistical analysis

To account for time-varying confounding, we used stabilized inverse probability weights (IPW) to estimate a marginal structural model for the effect of the tobacco retail cap on the selected health outcomes (Cole and Hernán, 2008; Hernán et al., 2000). The analysis was conducted in two stages: (1) constructing IPW by estimating the conditional exposure probabilities for each county within each year and (2) using the estimated IPW to fit a weighted regression model for the relationship between exposure and outcomes.

In the first stage, we estimated the IPW at each time point to adjust for time-varying confounding. For each year of the study period, the probability of each county being above the density cut point was estimated based on the covariate values for the given year. Stabilized weights were calculated as follows: we fit two pooled logistic models with exposure (density ≥ 1.26) as the response variable to estimate the denominator and numerator of IPW. Prior year density and year were the independent variables in the models to estimate the numerator, while prior year density, year, state and county-level covariates were independent variables in the denominator. We assessed several models using various functional forms of covariates (linear, categorical, restricted cubic spline). Stabilized IPW for each county were obtained by the cumulative product of the year-specific IPW up to 2016.

After construction of stabilized IPW, diagnostic checks of the estimated IPW were performed. First, within each year, a mean of all probability weights equal to one was used as assessment of appropriate model specification as the mean weight should be equal to one (Cole and Hernán, 2008). Second, standardized mean differences of covariates were compared between exposure groups to examine the balance of covariates.

In the second stage, the outcome analyses, we used Poisson models weighted by stabilized IPW to estimate the exposure effect (incidence rate ratio and incidence rate difference) of high tobacco retail density compared with that of non-exposure (low tobacco retail density) for each of the five outcomes. In addition to tobacco retail density as the main exposure, the year and a cumulative count of the number of years a county had tobacco retail density ≥ 1.26 prior to the year being analyzed were included as covariates in the outcome models. By including these additional variables along with the cumulative stabilized IPW—reflecting exposure history over time— the model allows density to affect outcomes across the entire study period while adjusting for time-varying confounding (i.e. retail exposure over all 15 years can affect outcomes in 2016).

To estimate the variance, the robust variance estimator was used (Robins et al., 2000). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to compare results at additional density cut points at the 20th percentile (1.13 retailers/1000) and at the 80th percentile (1.80 retailers/1000). To aid interpretation, we estimated the difference in the number of events between high- and low-density counties across the five states based on the incidence rate difference and population in each county. All analyses were conducted by using SAS software v9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, North Carolina). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of North Carolina-Chapel Hill (#17–1280).

Discussion

We found significant causal effects of tobacco retail density on rates of cardiovascular and pulmonary disease related hospitalizations over a 15-year time period. Counties with higher tobacco retail density had an increased incidence rate of 43%, 13%, 9%, and 8% compared to low tobacco retail density counties for hospitalizations related to asthma, COPD, IHD, and stroke. In the five included states, over the 15 years, reducing the density of tobacco retailers to less than 1.26 retailers per 1000 people would translate to a total of 22,286 fewer hospitalizations due to asthma, 11,549 fewer COPD hospitalizations, 18,906 fewer IHD hospitalizations, and 8324 fewer stroke-related hospitalizations. These findings add to the emerging literature suggesting high levels of tobacco availability in the retail environment are associated with higher risk of tobacco-related disease. Unlike previous research in this area, we utilized a longitudinal study design ensuring temporality and accounted for time-varying confounding, providing further evidence relevant to jurisdictions considering retail-focused tobacco control policies.

Given that tobacco retail density is a determinant of tobacco use, it may be expected that the association between tobacco retail density and health outcomes would follow the relative pattern of established smoking–disease relationships. Our observed effect estimates for COPD and asthma were greater than those of cardiovascular diseases. This follows the epidemiologic evidence hierarchy for smoking-related morbidity (National center for chronic disease prevention and health promotion (US) office on smoking and health, 2014); strongest for pulmonary disease, followed by CVD, providing rationale for our findings and suggesting greater health benefits from policies that reduce tobacco retail density to occur in pulmonary outcomes, while still contributing to meaningful reductions in cardiovascular morbidity.

We tested two additional cut points of retail density to examine the robustness of our findings. These analyses suggest that a lower density cut point (<1.13) would result in similar reductions in hospitalizations as the 1.26 cut point. A higher cut point of <1.80 may not have as much impact on reducing hospitalizations as the 1.26 cut point. In a previous analysis of birth outcomes (Baggett et al., 2024), we also found differing results at various cut points of tobacco retail density. Together, these findings indicate that there may not be a single ideal retailer cap for reducing adverse health outcomes, but rather any jurisdiction planning to implement a cap should take into consideration the goal of the cap and what size of a reduction may be feasible for the area. Related to this, we have only defined density on a per population basis. Other measures of density, such as retailers per land area, may differ in their association with the outcomes examined here. Further work should examine the use of other measures of retail density and their association with health outcomes.

Causal interpretation of our results depends on the assumptions of no unmeasured confounding (i.e., conditional exchangeability), positivity, causal consistency, and correct specification of the models. Regarding confounding, we identified several county-level variables likely to be important confounders of the relationship between tobacco retail density and health outcomes along with indicators of county retail density history. We believe that these variables are sufficient for exchangeability between high- and low-density counties. We considered the role of PM2.5 in the causal relationship between tobacco retail density and health outcomes, treating it as a confounder while acknowledging some uncertainty about its position in the pathway. Analyses excluding PM2.5 produced substantively similar results, indicating our findings are robust to this specification. Of note, we did not include contemporaneous smoking prevalence in our models as this is expected to be a mediator along the causal pathway between tobacco retail density and health outcomes; doing so would not allow us to estimate the total effect of density on health outcomes.

Although we did not include indicators of specific smoke-free air or retail density policy our inverse probability weights incorporated a time-varying county-level air quality measure, which may capture some of the downstream effects of smoke-free policies on hospitalization rates. The weights also included state and year, helping align counties with similar exposure histories and accounting for broad differences in state tobacco-control environments. For density-specific policies, our annual density calculations already reflect known policy changes such as state and local pharmacy tobacco sales bans (Golden et al., 2022b). Other policies, particularly retailer caps, are not implemented at the state level and are not tracked nationally at the local level. Only a small number of cities adopted such policies during our study period, so their impact on our analysis is likely minimal. Together, these features reduce, though cannot fully eliminate, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured local tobacco control policies.

Positivity necessitates that there be both high- and low-density counties at every combination of the included confounders. Given the chosen set of confounders included there is sufficient overlap between the high- and low-density counties across all levels of the confounders. The applied weights were shown to be well stabilized with resulting good balance of covariates between high- and low-density counties which should lead to minimal non-positivity bias. This also suggests correct specification of our models. We tested a number of models with differing covariates and various forms of the covariates (linear, categorical, splines), ultimately selecting a model the produced the required mean standardized weight of one and small standard deviation (Cole and Hernán, 2008).

The causal interpretation of our estimates relies on the assumption of consistency, meaning that different strategies to reduce retail density below a given threshold (e.g., regulation, licensing caps) would have equivalent effects on health outcomes. Evidence from Philadelphia shows that policies including density caps, school proximity restrictions, and stricter licensing enforcement led to a 20% decline in tobacco retail density within three years (Lawman et al., 2020). Similarly, New York City implemented a retail density cap associated with a 45% reduction in retailers (Giovenco et al., 2024). However, evidence directly linking density reductions to improved health outcomes remains limited. A recent review found that lower retailer density is associated with reduced smoking behaviors, though the magnitude and consistency of these associations vary across communities (Glasser and Roberts, 2021). These findings highlight the challenge of translating density reductions into measurable health improvements. Future research should further evaluate the consistency assumption across policy contexts.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that reducing tobacco retail density would have reduced hospitalizations over a 15-year period beginning in 2002. A reduction in tobacco retail density in counties with high density would have potentially prevented thousands of hospitalizations from asthma, COPD, IHD, and stroke. These findings strengthen evidence that reducing tobacco retail density can lower the burden of tobacco-related disease and underscore the importance of carefully designed policies tailored to community goals.
