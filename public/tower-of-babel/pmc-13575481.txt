Preparation and characterization of algae-based biopolymer nanocomposite hydrogels for the adsorptive-photocatalytic degradation of malachite green dye
Authors: Nada Sarhan, Fatma Mohamed, Omnia M. Salem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study combines experimental synthesis with molecular dynamic (MD) simulations to investigate the molecular level interactions governing the formation of the microalgae Ba/starch hydrogel. A molecular model representing the experimentally synthesized hydrogel was constructed, and Monte Carlo (MC) and MD simulations were performed to predict and validate the interfacial interactions and thermodynamic affinity between Ba and the starch matrix. Based on these computational insights, the purpose of this study is to prepare a highly absorbent hydrogel from microalgae and biopolymers for removing malachite green dye (MG) from wastewater via photodegradation. In this work, microalgae (Ba) + starch (Ba + St) hydrogel, microalgae Ba + chitosan (Ba + Cs) hydrogel, and Ba + St + Cs hydrogel are used as model substrates for the photocatalytic degradation of MG dye under solar radiation with a variety of reaction conditions. The surface and compositional changes of the produced photocatalyst were studied with XRD, FTIR, SEM, and UV-vis spectrophotometers in relation to the MG dye photodegradation, irradiation time, catalyst dose, dye concentration, and pH. The findings demonstrated that the decomposition progressed consistently from 5 to 360 minutes of irradiation time, reaching 11% to 99% for Ba + St hydrogel degradation and from 6% to 36% for Ba + Cs hydrogel and from 33% to 72% for Ba + St + Cs. This results from the MG dye molecule contact with the photocatalyst surface. The longer the irradiation period, the more the MG dye molecules interacted with the photocatalyst surface. As a result, the photocatalyst photodegradation efficiency increased. To reach a quicker rate of degradation, intervals of 360 minutes are employed.

Introduction

A widely used computational approach to modelling material behaviour at the atomic scale and predicting the properties of complex material systems is the Monte Carlo (MC) simulation. It provides useful information about the physical and chemical processes occurring in materials and helps to optimize the material design and processing conditions by relating the microstructure features to the macroscopic properties.1 MC simulations can be used in various applications to metals,2 alloys,3 ceramics,4 polymers,5 and advanced composite materials,6 also utilized in studying complex systems such as biochemical network,7 climate dynamics,8 and economic processes.1 This approach is built on the evaluation of a large number of stochastic scenarios, and it increases the prediction accuracy, helps the system optimization and improves the decision making in uncertain situations in various fields of science and engineering.1

Hydrogels are three dimensional (3D) biopolymeric networks with excellent water absorption and adsorption capacities due to the presence of hydrophilic functional groups.9 Their properties can be tuned by changing the synthesis parameters such as pH, temperature and agitation time.9 The crosslinking and reinforcing agents use increases their mechanical strength. The peculiar physicochemical properties of hydrogels make them highly functional, thus enabling their efficient use in various fields.10 The porosity and morphology are closely related to their wastewater treatment performance, which controls water and oxygen permeability and moisture absorption.11 This sustained release reduces excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), relieves oxidative stress, improves the microenvironment and stimulates the treatment process.11 Moreover, hydrogels can be divided into synthetic and natural hydrogels according to their raw materials, among which natural hydrogels are more in line with the principles of sustainable development.12 The synthetic polymers used for the preparation of hydrogels are generally non-biodegradable and may have some health hazards due to the presence of residual monomers, crosslinkers, and by-products formed during the polymerization.13 The most common natural raw materials for the synthesis of hydrogels are cellulose, chitosan, sodium alginate, starch, proteins, and their derivatives.14 In addition, hybrid hydrogels can be manufactured by mixing synthetic polymers with natural materials. In contrast, composite hydrogels are prepared by adding inorganic fillers like silica, zeolite, or calcium carbonate to the organic hydrogel matrices.14

Water is one of the worthiest supplies, playing a critical role in supporting life and keeping the environment safe, clean, and hygienic situations.15 However, when dangerous organic and inorganic pollutants are released into natural water bodies without being properly handled, human health and aquatic ecosystems are facing severe challenges. Specifically, the most harmful pollutants to aquatic and human life are synthetic organic colors,16 which the paper, textile, cosmetics, plastic, and leather industries can produce and release.17 Cause a widespread environmental catastrophe due to their unpleasant characteristics and their role as a carcinogen.18 The high solubility of textile dyes in water results in a rapid discharge of dark pigments, which blocks sunlight and causes serious harm to people and aquatic life in and near these industries' locations.19 There are over 10 000 of these dyes. When considering widespread use, the azo dye has enormous and crucial harm.20

MG dye (N-methylated diaminotriphenylmethane) is an organic compound with mainly a metallic sheen of green colored crystals and one of the most potent substances in the world.21t is extremely poisonous and must be cleaned before being discharged into the environment. It poisons the liver and damages the intestines, kidneys, gills and mammalian cells. If breathed in it causes cancer, if it touches the skin it irritates and hurts.22,23 Direct discharge of MG dye in hydrosphere causes environmental imbalance. This untreated water is used for irrigation which can affect the quality of crop output.24 These colors are still in use due to their cheapness, availability and strength, even though they are banned in many countries.25

The most important issue to be addressed is the treatment of this dye contamination before its release into water bodies. Researchers have tried out many methods to extract dyes from wastewater including reverse osmosis, precipitation, electrokinetic coagulation, microbial degradation, fungal decolorization, electrodialysis, nanofiltration, electrosorption, adsorption and filtration.26 Adsorption is the most successful technique for decolorization of wastewater because of its simplicity and low cost. For example, activated carbon and clay minerals as well as hydrogels were used as adsorbing materials for the removal of dyes.27

One of the more recent developments that aids in the elimination of organic contaminants from wastewater is the use of composite hydrogel algae based as a photocatalyst.28 These composite capacity for photocatalysis aid in ecosystem monitoring and enhancement. In fact, under various circumstances, they eliminate the harmful organic contaminants to the greatest degree possible. These materials are the most popular since they are inexpensive and produce nearly no hazardous byproducts.29

This study confirmed the interaction and fabrication between microalgae and starch hydrogel by MC and MD simulation and presents the first modification of microalgae Ba hydrogel based on starch (St), which have been synthesized and confirmed by MC and MD simulations. It also reports on the ability of the nanohydrogel composite to degrade a mixture of malachite green (MG) through photocatalysis.

Material and methods

Materials

The materials utilized in this study include extra pure starch derived from potatoes (St; Piochem, Egypt), chitosan (CS; Bio Basic, ON, Canada) characterized by a deacetylation degree of 96%, and Synechocystis sp. PAK 13 (Sy), which was isolated from both soil and water samples in Egyptian environments. The selected strain was procured from the Microbiology Department at the Faculty of Science, Beni-Suef University, Egypt, and subsequently cultivated without modification in 1 ml of Wuxal media (WM) mixed with 1 liter of distilled water and 5 g of sodium chloride in the microbiology laboratory at Beni-Suef University, Egypt, malachite green (MG) from Loba Chemie, and distilled water (DW).

Preparation of hydrogel composites

Two types of hydrogel composites were synthesized to investigate the role of chitosan in the structure matrix.

Chitosan + starch + microalgae hydrogel

Chitosan (CS) was initially solubilized in 25 ml of 1% (v/v) aqueous acetic acid. Concurrently, the starch (St) component was prepared by dissolving it in 25 ml of distilled water under mechanical stirring at 70 °C for 20 minutes. Separately, the microalgae (Sy) suspension was prepared in 25 ml of a 50 : 50 (v/v) distilled water/ethanol mixture and stirred for 1 hour at 70 °C. The three components were then combined and subjected to continuous mechanical stirring for 1.5 hours at room temperature. The resulting mixture was dried at 40 °C for 24 hours.30

Starch + microalgae hydrogel (control)

This formulation served as a control to isolate the effect of chitosan. The preparation followed the protocol described in Section 2.2.1, with the exclusion of the chitosan component. The starch and microalgae solutions were combined, stirred for 1.5 hours at room temperature, and subsequently dried at 40 °C for 24 hours under identical conditions.30

Preparation of dye stock solution

The first step is to accurately weigh 0.1 g MG dye on a digital balance. The weighed MG dye was transferred into a conical flask and dissolved in a small amount of DW. Finally, DW was added to prepare the final volume to 1000 ml, and the concentration of stock solution was 100 ppm. The solution stirred continuously for 5 min for complete dissolution and the prepared aqueous MG dye stock solution was stored for further use.

Photocatalytic activity

Efficient photodegradation of MG dye requires optimized operating conditions. The effect of various physicochemical parameters such as irradiation time, pH of MG dye solution, initial concentration of MG dye and catalyst dosage were systematically investigated. The photocatalytic degradation was carried out under sunlight using 25 ml aliquots of a 5 ppm MG dye solution and irradiation time ranging from 5 to 360 min. Based on these results, the effects of solution pH (3, 5, 8, and 10) and catalyst dosage (0.025–0.14 g) were further investigated using a 5 ppm MG dye solution. In addition, the effect of the initial MG dye concentration was evaluated over the concentration range of 5–40 ppm. The photocatalytic degradation of MG dye was monitored using UV-vis spectrophotometer by measuring the absorbance at the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax = 617 nm). Each photodegradation experiment was repeated three times under identical conditions, and the result are presented mean values with error bars representing the variation among repeated measurements.1Here, Co and Cf represents the initial and final concentration of MG. after the photocatalysis reaction. The experimental data obtained was processed using Origin 2024.

Computational study

The Monte Carlo dynamics (MC), and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in this study were performed using adsorption locator and the Forcite module to investigate the formation mechanisms of the Ba + St hydrogel system. The St surface was selected as the adsorption substrate for the microalgae Ba. Periodic boundary conditions were applied to construct the simulation box with dimensions 19.5 × 19.5 × 19.5 Å3. During the simulation process, the COMPASS III forcefield was employed to optimize the structures of the studied complexes. The main objective of these simulations was to identify the lowest energy most stable adsorption configurations and to elucidate the interaction mechanism of microalgae onto the St surface for the formation hydrogel.

Characterization

The crystalline structure of the prepared materials was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a PANalytical Empyrean X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Ka radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å) at 40 kV accelerating voltage and 35 mA current. The diffraction patterns were collected in the range of 20–70° 2θ with a scanning step of 0.02°. The surface morphology was studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Sigma 500, Zeiss, Germany) at 50 kV. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a Vertex 70 spectrometer (Burker, Germany) to determine the functional groups at a wave number of 400–4000 cm−1. The optical properties of the samples were studied using a Shimadzu UV-vis spectrophotometer (M 160 PC) and absorption spectra were recorded in the wavelength range of 250–1400 nm at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Computational study

This computational research has two central objectives: (1) to construct an atomistic model representing the proposed chemical structure of a novel starch (St) and microalgae (Ba) hydrogel based on its experimentally synthesized composition and to investigate its structural stability as well as (2) to utilize molecular dynamics (MD) simulation and energy calculations to evaluate the proposed formation of a reticulated 3D structure. Rather than simulating the experimentally prepared hydrogel, the computational study was performed on a representative atomistic model constructed from the experimentally proposed composition and cross linked structure. Adsorption energy calculations, bond length analysis, and water mobility evaluation were performed to assess the stability of the modelled hydrogel network, providing basic insights into the intermolecular interactions responsible for the formation of the rigid hydrogel network, thus complementing the experimental findings.

The inherent stability of the novelty synthesized microalgae Ba with St hydrogel was originally confirmed via an extensive thermodynamic evaluation. The calculated total adsorption energy presented a noticeably negative value of −484.67 kcal mol−1, supplying obvious evidence for the spontaneous and strongly exothermic interaction between microalgal Ba and the St polymer for formation hydrogel are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1a. The large magnitude of this energy proves that the adsorption process goes beyond simply intermolecular attraction, which implies the occurrence of chemisorption.31 This explanation is further strengthened by the Deformation energy −460.03 kcal mol−1, indicating that both the St chains and the microalgae Ba molecules exhibit major structural rearrangement to optimize interfacial contact, thus forming a highly stable and energy efficient hydrogel based approaches.

Photocatalytic studies for hydrogel

As shown in Fig. 6a, the adsorption efficiency of MG dye onto Ba + St hydrogel catalyst reached 62.4% under dark condition at an initial concentration of 5 ppm after 360 min, which indicates the significant adsorption capacity of hydrogel toward MG molecules. This result highlights the significant contribution of the adsorption process to the overall MG removal mechanism. In the photolysis experiment, under sunlight irradiation, the removal efficiency was only 3%, which showed the negligible contribution of direct photodegradation for the removal of MG dye.

Effect of (a) degradation time on MG dye using Ba + St hydrogel ( under dark conditions and sunlight irradiation) in comparison with Ba + Cs hydrogel, Ba + StCs and photolysis without catalyst under sunlight irradiation, (b) pH, (c) initial MG concentration, and (d) dosage of Ba + St hydrogel.

The photocatalytic degradation experiments were performed using aqueous solutions containing 5 ppm MG dye treated with Ba + St hydrogel, Ba + Cs hydrogel, and Ba + StCs composite hydrogel under sunlight irradiation for different exposure time. The gradual fading of the MG dye solution colour during irradiation, visually demonstrates the occurrence of the photocatalytic degradation process. The effect of irradiation time on photocatalytic removal efficiency of MG dye is shown in Fig. 6a. The degradation efficiency was increased gradually with the increase of the irradiation time from 5 to 360 min. The Ba + St hydrogel exhibited the highest photocatalytic performance, with MG degradation efficiency increasing from 11% after 5 min to 99% after 360 min of sunlight irradiation. In parallel, Ba + Cs hydrogel and Ba + StCs composite hydrogel increased from 6% to 36% and 33% to 72% respectively, over the same irradiation period. The improved photocatalytic efficiency can be attributed to the efficient interaction between the MG dye molecules within the hydrogel network and their subsequent interaction with the photocatalytically active sites, facilitating the degradation process.44 Therefore, an irradiation time of 360 min was selected as the optimum reaction period for achieving efficient MG dye removal.

In Fig. 6b illustrated the effect of pH on the time dependent photocatalytic degradation of MG dye using Ba + St hydrogel photocatalyst. The photocatalytic efficiency is increased from 23% to 25% when pH is increased from 1 to 5, then it is increased to 95% at pH 8 and remains constant at 99% at pH 10. The better photocatalytic performance under alkaline conditions than the neutral and acidic media as shown in Fig. 6b, indicating the favorable effect of alkaline environments in the photocatalytic process. This elucidated for the enhanced photocatalytic activity at pH 9 and 10. At alkaline pH, the Ba + St hydrogel photocatalyst surface is negatively charged because of surface functional group deprotonation and facilitates electrostatic attraction with cationic MG dye molecules, which improved photocatalytic decolorization efficiency.45,46 Also, the negative charge on the surface favour the formation of hydroxide ions.46 Thus, reactive hydroxyl radicals (OH°) are generated which decompose the MG dye, hence augmenting photocatalytic activity.46 Therefore, it can be concluded that the Ba + St hydrogel shows a higher photocatalytic efficiency for MG dye under alkaline conditions. The Ba + St hydrogel shows the best photo-decolorization of MG dye at pH 10.

A series of sample solutions with MG dye concentrations between 5 ppm and 40 ppm were generated to elucidate the correlation between initial dye concentration and the photodegradation process Fig. 6c. Each sample had a specified quantity of Ba + St hydrogel (0.025 g) in 25 ml of solution, and the photocatalytic procedure was executed at under sunlight irradiation for 6 hours. The experimental findings demonstrated that as the concentration of MG dye escalated, degradation diminished from 99% to 13% at concentrations of 5 ppm to 40 ppm, respectively. The observed decrease is attributed to the expected consumption of effective oxidizing radicals during the irradiation time of 360 min. In addition, the increase in MG concentration leads to turbidity of the reaction mixture, which blocks the light photons from reaching the active sites of the catalyst, thus reducing the efficiency of degradation.47 This is because of the initial condition at lower concentration of MG dye, where the number of active sites of hydrogel exceeds the number of dye molecules of MG dye. As the concentration of MG increases, the amount of dye molecules is higher than the active sites on the surface of Ba + St hydrogel,48 resulting in a decrease in degradation, as illustrated in Fig. 6c. Hydrogel is highly effective for dye degradation at low concentrations, commonly present in diverse wastewater sources.

To ascertain the correct dose of the catalyst, an MG dye solution with a specified neutral pH and concentration (5 ppm) will be utilized under sunlight. Various quantities of Ba + St hydrogel, from 0.025 g to 0.14 g, in a 25 ml solution during a duration of 6 h. The effect of Ba + St hydrogel dosage on the degrading process was evaluated with variable weigh of catalyst with range of 0.025 g to 0.14 g at 5 ppm MG dye at under sunlight irradiation are shown in Fig. 6d. The results showed that the effectiveness of degradation reduced with increasing the dose of catalyst which might be 0.025 g to 0.14 g from 99% to 70%. The maximum degradation efficiency was observed with a catalytic dosage of 0.025 gm for MG dye. The results demonstrated that a higher amount of Ba + St hydrogel affects the efficacy of the degrading process. It could be attributed to an increase in agglomeration and a decrease in active sites on the catalyst surface.49 Reduction of active sites impacts the availability for photon adsorption to form electrons–holes, limiting the transit of light through the solution due to the turbidity, increasing the opacity of suspension and the scattering of light.49 Additionally, when exposed to sunlight, the correct amount of catalyst can produce more reactive organic species, which will efficiently reach the MG dye molecules.50 Since the clouding effect, high catalyst loading was unable to boost efficiency since photons might not reach the dye molecules. At 0.025 gm, the greatest percentage of degradation was obtained.

Isothermal and kinetics studies

Isotherm models study

Photocatalytic refers to the process of photodegradation of dyes when exposed to light irradiation. The mechanism of photodegradation is contingent upon the characteristics of the catalyst.51 Consequently, at equilibrium under constant temperature, the relationship between the Ba + St hydrogel and MG dye is characterized by the isotherm of photodegradation. A photocatalytic isotherm is the method via which the interaction mechanism between the MG Dye molecule and Ba + St hydrogel surface may be analyzed and established when the MG dye concentration attains equilibrium.52 The isotherm models include Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin models. In the process of fitting experimental data to various models, numerous parameters are derived from the optimal fit, which can elucidate the mechanism, surface properties, and affinities of Ba + St hydrogel. This study aims to analyze and identify a viable model for the photocatalytic degradation of MG dye molecules utilizing Ba + St hydrogel, based on experimental data.

The Langmuir model isotherm is employed for dye degradation processes, wherein MG Dye molecules create a monomolecular layer on the uniform surface of Ba + St hydrogel. This model posits that photocatalysis transpires on a homogenous surface, devoid of interactions among the MG dye molecules.53 As per this model, Ba + St hydrogel surface comprises of identical active specific sites with equal energy and enthalpy. Thus, all the photodegradation sites have a comparable affinity for the MG dye. It signifies that a filled-up location cannot degrade further.54 Thus, a saturation point is attained where the maximum surface deterioration occurs. Catalyst reusability

The stability and recyclability of photocatalyst are indispensable for its potential large scale application. To evaluate the reusability of the Ba + St hydrogel, the catalyst was recovered after each photocatalytic reaction, separated and thoroughly washed with distilled water. The recovered catalyst was dried in a hot air oven at 60 °C for 1 h and then used for the next cycle. A new MG dye solution was prepared for each run. The test for reusability was performed up to five times for five consecutive cycles under the same experimental conditions. Complete photodegradation was obtained within 360 min of the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth cycles. The photodegradation efficiencies were 99%, 97.77%, 97.35%, 96.70%, and 96.44% for the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth run respectively. The recycling results are shown in Fig. 9. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 3) indicate very similar peaks for the fresh Ba + St hydrogel and the sample reused for five consecutive cycles, which suggests that the Ba + St hydrogel catalyst has excellent chemical stability in the degradation reactions under sunlight irradiation.

Moreover, the active species trapping experiments and previously reported photodegradation mechanisms of organic compounds, such as dyes,64–67 are used to present a schematic illustration of the photocatalytic mechanism under sunlight irradiation in Fig. 10. The introduced dopants in the Ba + St hydrogel catalyst result in the structural defects, especially the oxygen vacancies and lattice distortion, which enhance the light absorption and generate the localized defect states to favour the more efficient separation of the photogenerated charge carriers. Under sunlight irradiation, the electron–hole pairs (e−/h+) are generated, and these defected sites act as trapping centers which inhibit the fast recombination of the electron and holes by spatially separating them and prolonging their lifetime and enhancing the overall photocatalytic efficiency. Photogenerated holes (h+) oxidize adsorbed water molecules (H2O) or hydroxide ions to produce hydroxyl radicals (OH) and photogenerated electron (e−) reduce dissolved oxygen (O2) to produce superoxide radicals (O2−˙). Moreover, electrons can take part directly in the reduction and degradation of MG dye molecules. This efficient degradation of malachite green under sunlight irradiation is caused by such reactive oxygen species and direct redox reactions.64–67

Conclusion

MD and MC simulations successfully decoded that fundamental of microalgae BA with starch matrix mechanisms governing the hydrogel formation. The strong interfacial interactions and stable crosslinking patterns occur between the microalgae and starch matrix are proved. These theoretical results corroborate that the produced Ba + St hydrogel is a highly efficient superabsorbent material for removal of MG from aqueous solutions. The synthesized Ba + St hydrogel showed excellent photocatalytic degradation performance under solar radiation which provides a promising, eco-friendly, and sustainable solution for wastewater treatment. Degradation efficiency decreased with increasing catalyst dose, from 99% to 70% for 0.025 to 0.14 g respectively. The optimum degradation efficiency for MG dye was achieved at 0.025 gm catalytic dosage. It was observed that the efficiency of degrading process is affected by a higher concentration Ba + St hydrogel. It might be due to reduction of active sites of catalyst surface and increase of agglomeration. The reduction of active sites affects the availability of photon absorption for the generation of electron holes. The maximum dye photodegradation was achieved at pH with respect to other parameters investigated, indicating that Ba + St hydrogel has a better photocatalytic performance on MG dye in alkaline conditions. The Ba + St hydrogel is optimal on photo-decolorization. Considering all the above, the obtained Ba + St hydrogel is a hydrophilic functional polymeric network system that is a promising new superabsorbent substance for the treatment of waste and industrial water.
