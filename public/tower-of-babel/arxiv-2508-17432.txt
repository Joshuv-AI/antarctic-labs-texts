Probing In-Medium Effect via Giant Dipole Resonance in the Extended Quantum Molecular Dynamics Model
Chen-Zhong Shi(施晨钟), Xiang-Zhou Cai(蔡翔舟), Yu-Gang Ma(马余刚) ID

Abstract
This article uses a stochastic approach to analyze the collision term, rather than the geometric method used in the original EQMD model, to examine the width of the isovector giant dipole resonance (GDR) in 208 Pb. Based on the “soft” EQMD model, the response and strength functions are self-consistently determined for various symmetry energy coefficient and in-medium reduction factor values. The results confirm that the peak position and GDR width in 208 Pb are highly sensitive to the symmetry energy and the in-medium nucleon-nucleon ( NN ) cross section. This provides an opportunity to study the nuclear equation of state (EoS) and the medium effect. A significant reduction in free NN elastic cross sections within the medium is necessary to accurately reproduce the GDR width, as demonstrated by a comparison with evaluation data.

The EQMD model

II
Model and method

II.1
Equation of motion

In the framework of the EQMD model, the nucleons are assumed as Gaussian wave packets, and the total wave function is a direct production of nucleons as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\varphi_{i}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\left(\frac{v_{i}+v_{i}^{*}}{2\pi}\right)^{3/4}\exp\left[-\frac{v_{i}}{2}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{P}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{i}\right].

Here
\mathbf{R}_{i}
and
\mathbf{P}_{i}
are the position and moment of the center of wave packet belonging to the
i
-th nucleon in phase spaces.

v_{i}=1/\lambda_{i}+i\delta_{i}
is the complex width, where
\lambda_{i}
and
\delta_{i}
are the real and imaginary parts, respectively.
Its corresponding density distribution can be written as

\rho_{i}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(\pi\lambda_{i}\right)^{3/2}}\exp\left[-\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)^{2}/\lambda_{i}\right].

(2)

Under the time dependent variation principle (TDVP)
Feldmeier and Schnack 2000
, the propagation of particles can be described as 8-A dimensional classical equations of motion as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf{P}_{i}}+\mu_{\mathrm{R}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf{R}_{i}},~~\dot{\mathbf{P}}_{i}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf{R}_{i}}+\mu_{\mathrm{P}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf{P}_{i}},

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{3\hbar}{4}\dot{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\delta_{i}}+\mu_{\lambda}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda_{i}},~~\frac{3\hbar}{4}\dot{\delta}_{i}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda_{i}}+\mu_{\delta}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\delta_{i}},

where
\mu_{\mathbf{R}}
,
\mu_{\mathbf{P}}
,
\mu_{\lambda}
and
\mu_{\delta}
are the corresponding friction coefficients.
In the initial state, they are negative values that dissipate the energy of the total system down to its minimum point as the ground state
Maruyama et al. 1996
.
On the other side, they remain strictly zero to satisfy the conservation of total energy.
It is worth noting that the width of each wave packet is treated as a dynamical variable, which is considered as a semi-classical, semi-quantum mechanics involves more quantum effects than the fixed-width situation
Feldmeier and Schnack 2000
.

H
represents the Hamiltonian expressed as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Psi\left|\sum_{i}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla_{i}^{2}-\hat{T}_{\text{zero }}+\hat{H}_{\text{int }}\right|\Psi\right\rangle

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\mathbf{P}_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{3\hbar^{2}\left(1+\lambda_{i}^{2}\delta_{i}^{2}\right)}{4m\lambda_{i}}-\frac{t_{i}}{M_{i}}\right]+H_{\mathrm{int}}.

Here, the sum of the three terms in square brackets is the total kinetic energy belonging to the
i
-th particle.
The first term represents the kinetic energy of the center of the wave packet.
The second term arises from the momentum distribution.
And
t_{i}
is the zero-point kinetic energy, which is defined as follows:

t_{i}=\frac{\langle\phi_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{p}^{2}}|\phi_{i}\rangle}{2m}-\frac{\langle\phi_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{p}}|\phi_{i}\rangle^{2}}{2m}.

(5)

M_{i}
is the namely “mass number” of the fragment to which the
i
-th nucleon belongs.
It can be written as

\displaystyle M_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}F_{ij},

(6)

\displaystyle F_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\left|\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right|<a,\\
e^{-\left(\left|\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right|-a\right)^{2}/b},&\left|\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right|\geqslant a.\end{array}\right.

H_{\text{int}}
represents the total potential energy consisting of effective interaction, Coulomb interaction, and Pauli potential, i.e.,

H_{\text{int.}}=\int{u(\rho)\text{d}\mathbf{r}}+H_{\text{Coul.}}+H_{\text{Pauli}}.

(7)

In the most original EQMD model, a very simple form of effective interaction was adopted, leading to very hard incompressibilities that confuse with the current consensus.
Recently, we overcome this confusion by employing a typical template
Feng et al. 2008
of the Skyrme energy density function with “soft” interaction parameters
Shi et al. 2024a
;
Shi et al. 2024b
.
The energy density function can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle u(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\rho^{2}}{\rho_{0}}+\frac{\beta}{(\gamma+1)\rho_{0}^{\gamma}}\rho^{\gamma+1}

(8)

\displaystyle+\frac{g_{\text{Surf.}}}{2\rho_{0}}(\nabla\rho)^{2}+\frac{g_{\text{Surf.}}^{\text{iso }}}{2\rho_{0}}\left[\nabla\left(\rho_{\mathrm{n}}-\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{c_{s}}{2\rho_{0}^{\gamma_{s}}}\rho^{\gamma_{s}-1}\left(\rho_{\text{n}}-\rho_{\text{p}}\right)^{2}+g_{\tau}\frac{\rho^{8/3}}{\rho_{0}^{5/3}}.

The coefficients
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
are related to the bulk term.

g_{\text{Surf.}}
and
g_{\text{Surf.}}^{\text{iso}}
are the coefficients related to the surface interaction and its symmetry part, respectively.

c_{\text{s}}
is the symmetry energy coefficient.

\gamma_{s}
is taken as 0.5
Zhang et al. 2008
;
Famiano et al. 2006
, which represents a soft-asy EoS.
The momentum-dependent interaction is expressed in a density-dependent form derived from the local density approximation and
g_{\tau}
is the corresponding coefficient.
The Coulomb interaction utilized in this article consists of direct and exchange parts as follows:

H_{\text{Coul.}}(r)=\frac{a}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(r)\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\left(r^{\prime}\right)}{\left|r-r^{\prime}\right|}-\frac{3}{4}e^{2}\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{4/3}(r),

(9)

where
a
is the fine structure constant.
The Pauli potential
H_{\text{Pauli}}
is defined as

H_{\text{Pauli}}=\frac{c_{\text{P}}}{2}\sum_{i}\left(f_{i}-f_{0}\right)^{\mu}\theta\left(f_{i}-f_{0}\right),

(10)

where
c_{\text{P}}
and
\mu
are the strength and power parameters, respectively.
In Eq.
10
, it takes 12.0 MeV for
c_{\text{P}}
and 0.5 for
\mu
.

\theta
is the unit step function and
f_{0}
is the corresponding threshold parameter, which usually takes a value close to 1.
The
f_{i}
represents the overlap of the
i
-th nucleon with all identical particles defined as follows:

f_{i}\equiv\sum_{j}\delta\left(S_{i},S_{j}\right)\delta\left(T_{i},T_{j}\right)\left|\left\langle\phi_{i}\mid\phi_{j}\right\rangle\right|^{2}.

(11)

The Pauli potential mimics the fermion properties of nuclear matter in a low-energy region, which can be regarded as a repulsive force that prohibits the identical particles from being closed to each other in the phase space.

II.2
Two-body collision

Binary collision is another important aspect for transport models.
During every time step
\delta t
,
NN
scattering is tested for each pair of particles.
Before we introduce our method, a brief review of the collision term in the original EQMD model is given here.

Similarly to Bertsch’s prescription
Bertsch and Das
Gupta 1988
, there are two necessary conditions that must be satisfied, then the scattering can happen.
The first is that the relative distance between the colliding nucleons 1 and 2 must take the minimum value within a time step
[t,t+\delta t]
.
Generally speaking,
0.5~\text{fm}/c
is a typical value of
\delta t
for the low-intermediate energies reaction.
The second is that the minimum distance must be smaller than a threshold value, which takes
d_{\text{coll}}=2.0~\text{fm}
in the EQMD model.
If such two conditions are satisfied, a collision is attempted with probability
P_{\text{coll}}
as

P_{\text{coll}}=\frac{\sigma_{NN}}{\sigma_{\text{max}}}.

(12)

Here,
\sigma_{\text{max}}=\pi d^{2}_{\text{coll}}
.

\sigma_{NN}
is taken an energy-dependent cross section
Maruyama et al. 1996
;
Ono et al. 1992
, which is represented by
\sigma_{NN}^{\text{EQMD}}
in the text as

\sigma_{NN}^{\text{EQMD}}=\frac{100~\text{mb}}{1+\epsilon/200~\text{MeV}},

(13)

where
\textbf{p}_{\text{rel}}=\textbf{P}_{1}-\textbf{P}_{2}
is the relative momentum of the center of wave packets 1 and 2, and
\epsilon\equiv\frac{\textbf{p}^{2}_{\text{rel}}}{2m}
.
In the early AMD model
Ono et al. 1992
, such a cross-section design was used to ensure that the collision probability was less than 1.
Subsequent AMD updates
Ono et al. 1993
;
Ono and Horiuchi 2004
have used a more complex parameterization.
In the EQMD model, an isotropic angular distribution is assumed for the outgoing nucleons, and their relative momentum is adjusted iteratively until the final system satisfies the conservation of energy due to the Pauli potential, which also depends on the nucleon momenta.
If such a value for the relative momentum does not exist in the final system, the scattering is regarded as blocked.

After an attempted collision, the fermion properties are also checked.
The so-called occupation probability of the outgoing nucleons, which is defined as

(P_{\text{occ.}})_{i}=\text{min}\left(1,f_{i}^{\prime}-f_{0}\right).

(14)

Here,
i=1,~2
represents the scattered nucleons 1 and 2.

f_{i}^{\prime}
are their overlaps calculated with new centroid momenta
\textbf{P}_{i}^{\prime}
according to eq.
11
.
And
f_{0}
is the same parameter as in Eq.
10
.
Then the probability that Pauli-blocking occurred can be obtained as follows:

P_{\text{blocking}}=1-[1-(P_{\text{occ.}})_{1}][1-(P_{\text{occ.}})_{2}].

(15)

If a blocking occurred, the outgoing nucleons would restore their initial states.

Clearly, the two-body collision term used in the original EQMD is a typical geometric approach.
This approach is widely used in transport models for binary collisions.
However, recent studies
Xu 2019
have reported some shortcomings in this method.
For example, there is a 50% chance of spurious collisions arising for the same pair of collided particles in the subsequent time step.
One possible solution is to prevent the same pair from colliding again unless they have scattered with other particles.
Another issue is that the
\sigma_{\text{max}}
in Eq.
12
must be larger than the
NN
cross section to ensure the collision probability is less than 1; this is an arbitrary constant.
Similarly, the minimum distance
d_{\text{coll}}
is subject to the same issue.

II.3
Stochastic method

In view of the complexity in the detail of the geometrical approach utilized by different groups
Zhang et al. 2018
, we introduce a more reliable treatment, the so-called stochastic method, which can be considered as the mean free path method
Xu 2019
, into our “soft” EQMD model.
We assume that for colliding particles 1 and 2, the probability of scattering occurring is proportional to their density overlap
Ono and Horiuchi 2004
as follows:

\int\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=\left[\frac{1}{\left(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\right)\pi}\right]^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(\mathbf{R}_{1}-\mathbf{R}_{2}\right)^{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}\right],

(16)

and the collision vertex
\mathbf{r}_{1,2}
can be sampled according to the following distribution
Shi and Ma 2021
:

\mathbf{r}_{1,2}\sim\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{\int\rho_{1}\rho_{2}d\mathbf{r}}.

(17)

If the colliding nucleons move in a straight line within a time step
\left[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta t\right]
, the probability of a scatting occurring can be expressed as

\displaystyle P_{\text{coll}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{NN}}{\left(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\right)\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{d_{\perp}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{1}{\left(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\right)\pi}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\delta t}\exp\left[-\frac{d_{\parallel}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}\right]v_{\text{rel}}\mathrm{d}t.

Here,
d_{\parallel}
and
d_{\perp}
are the distances parallel and perpendicular to the relative velocity
v_{\text{rel}}
of the centers of their wave packets, respectively.

\sigma_{NN}
is taken the free
NN
elastic scattering cross section obtained from Ref.
Cugnon et al. 1996
, which is represented by
\sigma^{\text{free}}_{\text{NN}}
in the text.
For the pp/nn system, the elastic cross section can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{pp/nn}}

\displaystyle=628.367,

\displaystyle p_{\text{lab}}<0.1,

(19)

\displaystyle=34\left(\frac{p_{\text{lab}}}{0.4}\right)^{-2.104},

\displaystyle 0.1<p_{\text{lab}}<0.4,

\displaystyle=23.5+1000\left(p_{\mathrm{lab}}-0.7\right)^{4},

\displaystyle 0.4<p_{\mathrm{lab}}<0.8,

\displaystyle=\frac{1250}{p_{\mathrm{lab}}+50}-4\left(p_{\mathrm{lab}}-1.3\right)^{2},

\displaystyle 0.8<p_{\mathrm{lab}}<2.0,

\displaystyle=\frac{77}{p_{\mathrm{lab}}+1.5},

\displaystyle 2.0<p_{\mathrm{lab}}.

For the pn system, the elastic cross section can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{pn}}=

\displaystyle 3627.89,~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~p_{\text{lab}}<0.05,

(20)

\displaystyle 0.05<p_{\text{lab}}<0.4,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 33+196\left|p_{\mathrm{lab}}-0.95\right|^{2.5},~~~~~~~~~0.4<p_{\mathrm{lab}}<0.8,

\sigma_{\text{pp}}\left(0.1~\text{GeV}/c\right)
for pp/nn system at

and
\sigma_{\text{np}}\left(0.05~\text{GeV}/c\right)

same as Ref.

.

Note that the calculation of relative momentum between particles 1 and 2 in the scattering method differs from the most QMD-like approach.
In our previous works
Shi et al. 2020
;
Shi and Ma 2021
, it was found that the momenta of colliding particles should be extra considered since the specific definition of the total kinetic energy in the Hamiltonian, i.e., Eq.
4
.
In order to successfully extract the incident energy, we adopted the method suggested in our previous article.
The Wigner function of the particle
i
can be written as

\displaystyle w_{i}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{3}\exp\left[-\frac{1+\lambda_{i}^{2}\delta_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)^{2}\right.

(21)

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{2\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}}{\hbar}\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)\left(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{P}_{i}\right)-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\hbar^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{P}_{i}\right)^{2}\right].

In many ways, its behavior is the closest analogue to a classical phase-space density.
Equation
17
shows the collision vertex of particles 1 and 2.
When combined with their Wigner functions and the kinetic energy formula, their momenta can be obtained according to the following distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\hbar^{2}}\left[\mathbf{p}_{i}-\left(\mathbf{P}_{i}-\hbar\delta_{i}\mathbf{r}_{1,2}+\hbar\delta_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)\right]\right\},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{i}+\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{P}_{i}\right)\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M_{i}}}

where
i
= 1,2 represent particles 1 and 2.

M_{i}
is the same as in Eq.
6
.

\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}
is the momentum re-extracted that belongs to the
i
-th particle.
This treatment significantly impacts the calculation of bremsstrahlung.
For the EQMD model, the experimental data can only be reasonably reproduced by considering such a treatment.
Further details can be found elsewhere (see references
Shi et al. 2020
;
Shi and Ma 2021
.).

At the end of the subsection, we give the proof that our method is equivalent to the mean free path method when the real part of the wave packet width belonging to the incident particle approaches
0
.
If we assume nucleon 1 as the incident particle, its density distribution function (see Eq.
2
) becomes a delta function, i.e.,
\rho_{1}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)\rightarrow\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{1})
, when
\lambda_{1}\rightarrow 0
.
The value of the density overlap of nucleons 1 and 2 is
\rho_{2}\left(\mathbf{R}_{1}\right)
obtained by Eq.
16
.
According to the mean value theorem of integrals, the density overlap integrated over
d_{\parallel}
in the time step
\left[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta t\right]
can be written as

P_{\text{coll}}=\sigma_{\text{NN}}\bar{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}\delta t,

(23)

Here,
\bar{\rho}
is the average linear density on the trajectory of the incident particle.
From Ref.
Xu 2019
, the mean free path is known to be expressed as
1/\left(\sigma\rho\right)
, where
\rho
is the number density of the incident particle experienced in a small volume.
The relaxation time
\tau
for binary collision is
\lambda/\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}
.
The probability of scattering in the time interval
\delta t
is therefore
\delta t/\tau=\left(\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}/\lambda\right)\delta t=\sigma\rho\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}\delta t
.
The probability of two-body scattering is thus equivalent to Eq.
23
when the width of the incident particle approaches 0.

Besides, in actual calculations, to prevent the possibility of a collision probability greater than 1, we adopt the following treatment

P^{\prime}_{\text{coll}}=1-e^{-P_{\text{coll}}}.

(24)

Here,
P_{\text{coll}}
is obtained from Eq.
18
and
P^{\prime}_{\text{coll}}
is used to determine whether a collision has occurred.
A similar treatment has been employed by the CoMD model
Zhang et al. 2018
.
Until now, to our knowledge only the CoMD model utilizing the stochastic method has been reported for QMD-like models
Zhang et al. 2018
.

II.4
Isovector giant dipole resonance

The isovector giant dipole resonance can be regarded as a collective oscillation between centroids of protons and neutrons with opposite phases.
In order to excite a nucleus undergoing such a collective oscillation, a perturbation operator can be added to the Hamiltonian at
t_{0}
, which can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\text{exc.}}=

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{Q}\delta\left(t-t_{0}\right),

(25)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}=\sum^{A}_{i}{\hat{q}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{A}\sum_{i\in\text{p}}{\hat{z}_{i}}-\frac{Z}{A}\sum_{i\in\text{n}}{\hat{z}_{i}}.

Here
\hat{Q}
represents the dipole excitation operator and
\lambda
is a tiny variable, here it takes 25 MeV/
c
following Ref.
Urban 2012
, to ensure the perturbation condition.
Following linear response theory, the response of the excited nucleus can be expressed as a time-dependent function as

\displaystyle\Delta\langle\hat{Q}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{Q}\rangle\left(t\right)-\left\langle 0|\hat{Q}|0\right\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\lambda\theta(t)}{\hbar}\sum_{f}|\langle f|\hat{Q}|0\rangle|^{2}\sin\frac{\left(E_{f}-E_{0}\right)t}{\hbar}.

Here
|0>
,
|f>
are the nuclear state before and after perturbation, and
E_{0}
,
E_{f}
are the corresponding eig-energy.
Since
\langle\hat{Q}\rangle
only explicitly contains the z component, the new momentum of the wave packets is changed after perturbation
Urban 2012
as

(P_{i})_{z}\rightarrow\begin{cases}(P_{i})_{z}-\lambda\frac{N}{A},&i\in\text{proton}\\
(P_{i})_{z}+\lambda\frac{Z}{A},&i\in\text{neutron}\end{cases}

(27)

The GDR width is defined by the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the strength function, which can be obtained via a Fourier integral of
\Delta\langle\hat{Q}\rangle(t)
as follows:

S(E_{\gamma})=-\frac{1}{\pi\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}t\Delta\langle\hat{Q}\rangle(t)\sin\frac{E_{\gamma}t}{\hbar}.

(28)

Here
E_{\gamma}
is the photon emission energy.

In reality, an artificial damping factor of
e^{-\gamma t/\hbar}
is multiplied by
\Delta\langle\hat{Q}\rangle(t)
as usual for the calculation of the strength function.
This is a common practice for studying nuclear giant resonance using transport approaches, e.g., EQMD
He et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2017
, IBUU
Kong et al. 2017
, and LBUU
Wang et al. 2019
.
An purpose of this treatment is to widen the GDR width to avoid spurious oscillations due to the integration of finite-time span in Eq.
28
.
However, the collisional dissipation hidden in the GDR width has been ignored.

In the case of the free nucleon-nucleon cross section, the GDR width is significantly overestimated, as can be seen in Fig.

The primary reason for this lies in the lack of in-medium effects during binary collisions, when colliding nucleons are surrounded by a nuclear medium

Here, we adopt the most common phenomenological parameterization of the cross section to introduce in-medium effects, as follows:

\sigma_{\text{NN}}^{\ast}=\left(1-\eta\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}\right)\sigma_{\text{NN}}^{\text{free}},

(29)

\eta

\rho_{0}

\rho

The upper panel (a) of Fig.
6

\eta

As expected, the GDR damping decreases as the value of
\eta

At approximately
0.6

IV
Conclusion

In this article, we propose using a stochastic approach to model binary collisions, instead of the geometric approach employed in the original EQMD model.
Our results confirm that the peak position and width of the GDR are highly sensitive to the magnitude of the symmetry energy coefficient and the in-medium nucleon-nucleon cross section. This provides an opportunity to study the nuclear equation of state and the medium effect.
For the parameters
c_{\text{s}}=33.2~\text{MeV}
and
a=0.57
, the GDR calculation for
208
Pb is in good agreement with the evaluation data.
As with the LBUU model, our results also suggest a substantial decrease in
NN
scattering in the low-energy region.
