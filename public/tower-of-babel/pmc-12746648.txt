Evaluation of putrescine’s effects on the growth, visual quality, and some physio-biochemical parameters of Henna (Lawsonia inermis L.) under salinity stress conditions
Authors: Abolghasem Hamidi Moghaddam, Mohammad Khajeh Sorkhoeih
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHenna, a medicinal plant with a long history, is traditionally used as a dye for cosmetic purposes and also has the potential to be utilized as a hedge plant in tropical and subtropical landscapes. Putrescine (Put) is recognized as an important polyamine that has beneficial effects on plant growth and their responses to environmental stresses, including salinity, which is a key abiotic stress. An experiment was carried out to assess how Put (0 and 0.75 mM) and NaCl salinity (Control, 50, 100, 150 mM) affected the visual quality, growth, and physio-biochemical parameters of mature henna plants (two years old) over six weeks.ResultsThe findings indicated that, although henna’s visual quality, growth parameters, RWC% and lawsone content decreased when exposed to 150 mM salinity levels, the most significant decrease (-64.02%) was noted in the leaf area. Conversely, salinity stress led to increases in EL% (19.4%), Na+ content (3.9-fold), proline accumulation (2.63-fold), total phenolic compounds (43.5%) and antioxidant activity. Meanwhile, henna’s response to Put pretreatment was complex and varied. While there were no changes in visual quality, leaf area, or shoot and root biomass, Put caused a minor reduction in plant height, the length of axillary shoots, the number of axillary shoots, the length of internodes, root system architecture (RSA) and the growth index, along with an increase in EL%, SOD, CAT, and POD activity in henna leaves under normal salinity conditions. Moreover, the interaction between Put and salinity demonstrated that Put enhanced plant height, the length of axillary shoots, the growth index, RSA, the leaf greenness index, photosynthetic pigments, K+ content, RWC and CAT and APX activity, while reducing Na+ content in leaves by -41.3%, the Na+/K+ ratio by -49.2%, EL% by -14.7%, and SOD and POD activity by -15.4% and − 44.98%, respectively, under 150 mM salinity stress. Additionally, correlation analysis revealed that the Na+/K+ ratio had strong negative associations with other henna parameters.ConclusionsThe present research indicates that Put foliar spraying can ameliorate the adverse effects of salinity stress on mature henna plants by reducing the Na+/K+ ratio, improving RSA, modification in antioxidant system activity and preserving photosynthetic effectiveness. Nonetheless, its use under non-saline conditions slightly decreased growth, which may be a positive factor for hedge plants.Keywords: Antioxidant enzyme, Growth index, Lawsone, Salinity tolerance, Urban landscape

Background

Salinity stress poses a significant environmental challenge, especially in arid and semi-arid areas, adversely affecting plant growth, development, and yield by interfering with various morphological, physiological, and biochemical processes [1–3]. At elevated levels, it leads to osmotic and ionic stresses, such that plants under salinity conditions must deal with both osmotic stress and ion toxicity [4, 5]. Other significant outcomes for plants facing salinity include nutrient imbalances and the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Nonetheless, in order to reduce the harmful effects of ROS on cells, plants have established several non-enzymatic and enzymatic strategies to avert oxidative harm [5–7]. Salinity causes a decrease in the quality of ornamental plants through stunted growth, chlorosis, and necrosis [8, 9]. Conversely, it may positively impact the growth and visual quality of certain ornamental plants [10–12]. Since these plants don’t need to reach their highest potential growth, excessive shoot vigor is actually considered unwanted [10, 13]. Therefore, for these plants, visual quality is prioritized over the rate of growth, and exposure to salinity stress can actually improve their performance and visual appeal [9, 12, 14].

Putrescine (Put) and other polyamines (PAs), which are small aliphatic nitrogen bases, can regulate different biological processes such as flower development, embryogenesis, organogenesis, root growth, abscission, senescence, tissue lignification, programmed cell death, etc. They also participate in reactions to environmental stresses such as extreme temperatures, drought, salinity. ultraviolet radiation [15–18]. These reactions could be a result of their capacity to regulate osmosis and eliminate free radicals [19]. Put has a particularly significant role among PAs because it serves as the primary component and precursor for tertiary and quaternary PAs [16]. Previously, different levels of Put have been utilized to decrease the negative impacts salinity stress on the growth and development of plants such as Ginseng [17], Medicinal pumpkin [20], Tea [15], Tomato [7], Cucumber [21], Snap Bean [22], Zinnia [23], Cmelina [24] and Chicpeas [25]. Nonetheless, some reports indicated that Put resulted in a reduction of certain growth parameters [26–28]. Since compact growth is a beneficial factor for some ornamental plants (e.g., hedge plants), the inhibitory aspect of Put on growth may serve as a growth retardant, reducing pruning needs and enhancing aesthetic value.

Henna (Lawsonia inermis L.) is a single-species genus of the Lythraceae family, originating from the Middle East, Iran, India and Africa. It is regarded as both an ornamental and medicinal plant that possesses Lawson’s (2-hydroxy-1, 4-naphthoqunone) active substance and used for over 9000 years as a dye for cosmetic purposes. Henna is a branched shrub, reaching 2.4–5 m tall and grown as an ornamental or hedge plant in landscapes space of tropical and subtropical regions [29–34]. According to research, mature henna plants can tolerate salinity moderately [6, 35, 36]; however, it is relatively sensitive to salinity during the germination stage [37].

Studies have shown a significant decrease in various aspects of henna plants such as growth rate, shoot-to-root ratio, biomass, pigments involved in photosynthesis, protein levels, relative water content (RWC), quantum yield, and production of lawsone, a key substance, due to salinity. Conversely, it resulted in a rise in electrical leakage (EL), antioxidant activity, and proline accumulation [6, 35, 36]. Nevertheless, there is a lack of information regarding the effects of Put and its interaction with salinity stress on the growth and aesthetic appearance of henna. Thus, the aim of this study was to assess the impact of Put and different levels of salinity on the visual quality, as well as several morpho-physiological and biochemical traits of the mature henna plant, and while also determining whether Put treatment would enhance its tolerance to salinity stress.

Materials and methods

Growth conditions and plant resources

This research took place in the agricultural college’s experimental greenhouse at the University of Jiroft, Iran. Mature henna plants (two years old) were cultivated in 10-liter plastic pots filled with sand and irrigated daily using Hoagland’s diluted solution (pH 5.8). Four weeks after acclimating to the greenhouse environmental conditions (day/night temperatures of 28/23 ± 5ºC and relative humidity of 70 ± 5%), henna plants were sprayed twice with Put, followed by exposure to sodium chloride (NaCl) two weeks later.

Put and salinity treatments

Put treatments (1,4-diaminobutane, Sigma Aldrich, USA) at 0 mM (serving as a control) and 0.75 mM were formulated using distilled water containing 0.5% Tween 10 and were sprayed during the late afternoon with a one-week interval between each treatment using a handheld sprayer. While spraying, attention was given to make sure that both sides of the leaves were thoroughly dampened. Salinity stress was applied by adding 50, 100 and 150 mM NaCl (Merck, Germany) to the Hoagland’s diluted solution, while the control (0 mM, NaCl) consisted only of the Hoagland solution; which had electrical conductivities (EC) of 0.45, 4.92, 8.86 and 11.15 dS m− 1, respectively. Moreover, to prevent excessive salt accumulation, leaching was performed weekly.

Growth parameters

Sampling was conducted to assess the growth parameters after six weeks of salt stress application. Roots and shoots were promptly separated and washed. Afterwards, plant height and the number of leaves were measured. As both treatments had a simultaneous impact on the growth of new shoots, the measurement of the number of leaves and leaf area in the last 20 cm of the stem was conducted. The leaf area was assessed with a leaf area meter (Model LI-3100). To establish dry weight (DW), roots and shoots were dried in an oven at 50°C for 96 h before weighing.

To assess root system architecture (RSA), images of henna roots were examined with the GiA Roots software (General Image Analysis of Roots).

Visual quality

The visual quality of each plant was evaluated on the experiment’s final day using Niu et al.’s [38] scale. Plants were rated from 1 to 5: 1 indicating over 50% foliage damage or dead, 2 for moderate damage (25–50%), 3 for slight damage (< 25%), 4 for good quality with reasonable growth reduction and minimal leaf damage (suitable for landscaping), and 5 representing excellent quality.

Leaf greenness index

The leaf greenness index was assessed at consistent shoot midpoints on four leaves per plant using a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502 Plus – Konica Minolta) on the experiment’s final day.

Determination of potassium (K+) and sodium (Na+)

The concentration of K+ and Na+ in henna leaves was evaluated using the procedure outlined by Kalra [40]. Briefly, the leaves were rinsed extensively at least three times with deionized water and subsequently were dried in an oven at 70°C for 48 h. A total of 100 mg of dried sample was digested in 2 mL of 0.1 M nitric acid (HNO3). Thereafter, the levels of K+ and Na+ were quantified with a flame photometer (JENWAY PFP7, UK) and reported as mg g⁻¹ DW.

Relative water content

To evaluate RWC the procedure which was described by Ritchie et al. [41] was utilized. For this purpose, leaf disks with a 0.5 cm2 in diameter were excised from the youngest fully the expanded leaves, which had been previously washed with distilled water. They were weighed with a digital scale to determine fresh weight (FW). Subsequently, they were immersed in distilled water for 4 h to gain turgidity, after which the turgid weight (TW) was noted. The technique described by Bates et al. Total phenolic compounds

The Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method, based on Singleton et al. [44] with slight changes, was used to determine the total phenolic compounds (TPC). Briefly, Henna leaf samples (500 mg) were ground, homogenized with 10 mL of ethanol, and kept at 37 °C for 3 h. Following centrifugation, a 200 µL aliquot of diluted extract (10%) was added to 1000 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and allowed to incubate for 3 min at room temperature. Afterward, 800 µL of 75% sodium carbonate was added, shaken vigorously, and allowed to stand in the dark for 90 min at 25 °C. The absorbance was measured at 760 nm. The TPC was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry weight using a standard calibration curve of gallic acid.

Quantification of Lawsone

According to the quantification technique for lawsone, dried henna leaves were ground and extracted by immersing 100 mg of the powder in 10 ml of methanol and shaken for 3 h. After centrifugation at 6000×g for 15 min, the absorbance was recorded at 452 nm with a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Dynamica, HALO XB-10, UK). The concentration of lawsone was determined in mg g −1 DW, using a pure lawsone standard as reference [45].

Enzyme assay

Fresh and fully expanded leaves were sampled two weeks after salinity stress application and promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The method described by Zhang and Kirkham [47] was utilized for isolating enzymes with minor modifications. Exactly 200 mg of fresh material was weighed and crushed thoroughly by using liquid nitrogen in a mortar, followed by homogenization with chilled potassium phosphate (50mM, pH 7.0) consisting of EDTA (1mM) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (1%) to minimize interference from phenolic compounds. After being mixed together on ice, the samples were spun at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4ºC. The supernatant was utilized for enzyme activity measurement.

To measure activity of Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) the procedure outlined by Zhang & Kirkham [47] was followed. Its activity was assessed by monitoring the inhibition in photoreduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) in a reaction mixture comprising 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 63 µM NBT, 20 µL enzyme extract and 1.3 µM riboflavin which was added at the final. The experiment started by turning on the light and ran for 10 min until it was halted by switching off the light and wrapping the tubes in aluminum foil. The control was a reaction mixture that was not irradiated and did not change color. The initial rate of the process was calculated by measuring the rise in absorption at 560 nm with a Dynamica spectrophotometer (HALO XB-10, UK). One unit of SOD activity was characterized as the amount of enzyme required to achieve a 50% reduction in the rate of NBT photochemical reduction.

Activity of catalase (CAT) was assessed by monitoring the rate at which hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) disappeared at 240 nm. The 3 mL reaction mixture included potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7), along with H2O2 (15 mM) and the necessary enzyme extract. The CAT enzyme activity was determined by its extinction coefficient of 40 mM− 1 cm− 1 and quantified in units, with each unit decomposing one µmole of hydrogen peroxide per minute [48].

Peroxidase (POD) activity was assessed by analyzing the rise in absorbance at 470 nm using MacAdam et al. [49] methods with slight modifications. The reaction mixture included potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 6), along with H2O2 (0.37 mM) and guaiacol (3.36 mM) in a 3 mL volume. The process commenced by addition of enzyme extract. Activity of POD was calculated based on the extinction coefficient of tetraguaiacol (26.6 mM− 1cm− 1) and reported in units as the enzyme quantity needed for producing one µmole of tetraguaiacol per minute.

The activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) was evaluated by monitoring a reduction in absorbance at 290 nm, using the procedure outlined by Nakano and Asada [50]. The 3 mL reaction mixture included potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7), ascorbate (0.5 mM) and H2O2 (0.1 mM). The process began with the addition of the appropriate amount of enzyme extract, and the reduction in absorbance was recorded over a period of two minutes. The APX activity was determined by the ascorbate extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM− 1 cm− 1 and quantified in units as the enzyme quantity that oxidizes one µmole of ascorbate each minute.

Statistical analysis

The study involved a factorial experiment with three replications, employing a completely randomized design (CRD). The initial factor consisted of Put concentrations, while the subsequent factor involved of salinity stress. The data obtained were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) by JMP 17 software from SAS Institute in Cary, NC. In cases where the F statistic showed significance, the LSD test was employed to determine treatment differences (p ≤ 0.05).

Results

Growth parameters

Results showed that Put and salinity stress had a notable impact on henna height (P ≤ 0.01). Plants exposed to 150 mM salinity showed a shorter height of 101.67 cm than those in the control group at 126.67 cm. Additionally, the mean comparison revealed that Put pretreatment (0.75 mM) caused a 6.05% decrease in plant height at the control salinity level (Table 1). The interaction between Put and salinity stress showed that there was no significant variation in plant height at 0.75 mM Put level across control, 50, and 100 mM salinity stress levels. In fact, Put pretreatment mitigated the adverse impacts of 50 and 100 mM on the height of henna plants.

Comparison of means indicate that exposure to high salinity stress (150 mM) led to a 12.69% decrease in the quantity of henna branches compared to control group (Table 1). Nevertheless, there were no notable distinctions at 0mM Put concentrations across control, 50 and 100 mM salinity levels. The combination of Put and salinity demonstrated that application of 0.75 mM Put resulted in a notable decrease in henna lateral branches at salinity levels of 50, 100, and 150 mM (Table 1).

Result revealed that the henna axillary shoots remained unchanged with Put treatments under controlled salinity conditions. However, their length decreased when the salinity level was raised to 100 and 150 mM. A reduction of 19.14% in axillary shoot length was observed when exposed to highest salinity level of 150 mM, in comparison to the control. Also, the combination of Put and salinity revealed that there was no notable variation among the salinity levels when the Put level was 0.75 mM (Table 1).

As shown in Table 1, both Put and NaCl salinity treatments resulted in a notable decline in internode length (P ≤ 0.01). Exposure to Put (0.75 mM) led to a decrease of 19.48% in internode length under control salinity conditions. Although, pretreatment with Put helped to decease the adverse impacts of 50 and 100 mM NaCl salinity stress, a notable decline in internode’s length occurred when the salinity reached 150 mM.

Conversely, both Put (P ≤ 0.05) and NaCl salinity (P ≤ 0.01) treatments resulted in a notable rise in the number of leaves per 20 cm stem. Put pretreatment (0.75 mM) resulted in a 24.4% rise in the quantity of leaves in control salinity conditions (Table 1). Furthermore, Plants treated with 150 mM NaCl had more leaves than those in the control group. However, the interaction between Put and salinity stress indicated no notable variations between control, 50, and 100 mM salinity levels at the 0.75 mM Put level (Table 1).

Findings indicated that only salinity had a notable impact on the henna leaf area (P ≤ 0.01), causing a severe decrease (−64.02%) in leaf area of plants under 150 mM stress, whereas the plants in the control group displayed the largest leaf area. However, no notable discrepancy was noted in the salinity levels of 100 and 150 mM (Fig. 1a).

The growth index of henna significantly decreased with both Put and NaCl salinity treatments. The use of Put (0.75 mM) led to a reduction of 5.4% in the growth index at control salinity levels (Table 1). On the other hand, plants subjected to 150 mM salinity stress showed the lowest growth index (76.23) in comparison to control plants (93.67). Nonetheless, when Put was applied prior to salinity stress at a concentration of 0.75 mM, growth index was found to increase by 8.76% under 150 mM salinity conditions (Table 1).

Visual quality

Results showed that the visual quality of henna was not affected by Put treatment (P ≤ 0.05); however, it significantly decreased due to salinity stress. Despite there being no notable distinction between the control group and the plants subjected to 50 mM salinity stress, henna’s visual quality decreased slightly as the salinity stress increased to 100 and 150 mM (Fig. 1b), with a 23.4% decrease when exposed to the most elevated salinity level (150 mM) in comparison to the control group.

Leaf greenness index

Both Put and salinity stress had significant effects (P ≤ 0.01) on the greenness index (SPAD). Findings indicated that as Put levels was at zero, the greenness index notably decreased with an increase in salinity stress (Table 1). Henna plants subjected to 150 mM salinity stress had a greenness index of 33.70, which was lower than the control’s index of 43.77. Conversely, treating with Put increased the greenness index under 100 and 150 mM salinity levels.

Physio-biochemical parameters

Photosynthetic pigments

As shown in Table 3, the photosynthetic pigments of henna leaves were significantly impacted by Put and salinity treatments. The levels of all of photosynthetic pigments was remained statistically constant under 50 mM salinity. By increasing the salinity level to 100 mM, although the concentration of Chl a was not affected; but the levels of Chl b, and Chl a + b significantly decreased. However, plants grown under 150 mM salinity had the lowest levels of Chl a, Chl b, and Chl a + b compared to the control. Conversely, exposure to 100 and 150 mM salinity stress caused a rise in the amount of CAR in henna leaves. The interaction between Put and salinity stress showed that pre-treatment with Put improved all photosynthetic pigments at the 150 mM salinity level (Table 3). Nevertheless, the use of Put (0.75 mM) caused a reduction in Chl a concentration at both the 50 and 100 mM salinity levels, as well as in Chl a + b concentration at the 100 mM. Results showed that the concentration of CAR remained stable across all salinity levels when Put was applied; however, it caused the concentration of CAR to decrease at the moderate and high salinity levels compared to the control level of Put.

Findings indicated a notable rise in EL% in henna plants under stressful salinity conditions. Discussion

The present research investigates how Put treatment affects the RSA, visual quality and several physio-biochemical traits of mature henna plants when experiencing salinity stress. Results revealed that most of the traits assessed in the study were negatively affected by salinity. However, applying Put mitigated some adverse effects, improving the plants’ ability to tolerate salinity stress compared to untreated plants. Similar reports have also indicated that Put [15, 17, 23, 51], Spm [52–54] and Spd [55–58] could mitigate some adverse effects of salinity stress through different mechanisms, enhancing the plants’ ability to tolerate salinity compared to untreated plants. It has been suggested that Put plays a direct protective role in averting dehydration symptoms and may reflect suboptimal growth conditions, while Spm and Spd stabilize macromolecules and neutralize harmful ROS, appearing to play an important role in stress recovery [59, 60]. Although some researchers believe that Spd and Spm are more significantly related to plant tolerance to salinity stress than other PAs [18, 57], it has been observed that under saline conditions, Put act as a precursor for the production of Spm and Spd and cause an increased Spm + Spd/Put ratio, thereby indicating the protective role of polyamines during of salinity stress [60]. Hence, PAs, can play a complex role in stressed plants, and the varied responses of plants to them are probably due to factors including the type of PAs, the duration and nature of the stress, plant species, and treatment method in terms of quantity and duration [18, 22, 28, 61].

Our findings confirmed a previous report that salinity stress decreases henna growth parameters and biomass [35]. Comparable findings have also been documented in other plants like Atropa belladonna [61], Rosmarinus officinalis L [11]., Luffa acutangula [51], Viburnum taxa [12], Panax ginseng Meyer [17], Hypericum perforatum L [62]., Zinnia elegans [23]. Plants have developed intricate ways to detect, react to, and adjust to environmental stresses. To survive harsh conditions, they adjust membrane and cell wall structures, modulate cell division rates, and tune metabolism through activating tolerance mechanisms [63]. However, many adaptive mechanisms in which plants adapt to high salt levels involve changes in their morphological and anatomical traits [2]. Reduction in plant growth by salinity stress could be related to inhibiting the plant’s ability to absorb water from their roots [64]. Reduced osmotic potentials in salty environments may cause a water deficit, resulting in a drop in cell turgor pressure, such that Manchanda and Garg [65] have stated that this is the primary reason for hindering the elongation of plant cells in saline conditions. Additionally, the build-up of Cl- and Na+ in various parts of plant accelerates the process of cell death, leading to a reduction in photosynthesis and transportation of plant growth regulators and carbohydrates to the growing areas of the plant [2, 66]. In fact, a decrease in division and elongation of cells, along with salt ion accumulation, can inhibit root growth, shorten shoots, delay leaf emergence and reduce leaf area, ultimately causing a decrease in plant biomass [67]. Due to the significant negative correlation observed among the accumulation of Na+ and some growth parameters of henna (Fig. 4), it can be concluded that the decline in these parameters is likely a result of Na+ accumulation in henna leaves. Meanwhile, the clear correlations were noted among the shoot biomass and factors such as leaf area, length of inter node length of axillary shoots, and plant height, etc., indicating that differences in these parameters may lead to variations in biomass. (Fig. 4).

On the other hand, henna’s response to the exposure of Put was complex and different. According to the results, Put pretreatment improved the plant height, length of axillary shoots, number of leaves per 20 cm stem, leaf greenness index and root parameter of henna plants under high salinity stress (150 mM). Consistent with these findings, it has been noted that treating with Put improves growth parameters of Luffa acutangula [51], Panax ginseng Meyer [17] and Zinnia elegans [23] when exposed to high salinity conditions. Conversely, treating with Put decreased plant height, number of axillary shoots, length of axillary shoots, length of internode, growth index and RSA of henna under control salinity conditions. Comparable reports have also been documented by Youssef et al. [26] in Datura innoxia and D. stramonium; Ndayiragije and Lutts [27] in Oryza sativa L. and Suchak and Pandya [28] in Vigna radiate.

It has been noted that the production of ROS, particularly H2O2, may increase due to Put oxidation, leading to the activation of ROS-dependent hormone pathways such as the ABA signaling pathway, which results in stomatal closure, reduced water loss, and the activation of stress-related genes. Consequently, it helps plants mitigate the adverse effects of environmental stress [68–70]. On the other hand, the negative impact of Put on some growth parameters could be a reason for the dual effect of PAs. On one hand, the use of PAs, is linked to plants being more tolerant to environmental stresses, with factors including enhanced radical neutralization, regulation of enzyme activity, changes in certain phytohormone biosynthesis like abscisic acid, indole acetic acid, ethylene, gibberellins, etc., and modification of membrane permeability and ion transport mechanisms [7, 18, 51]. Conversely, PAs, could lower plants’ ability to handle stress by elevating hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels through their catabolism or inhibiting proteins synthesis [7, 71]. At elevated concentrations, it similar to other ROS, leads to oxidative stress [5, 7, 71]. This can explain the slight decreases on some morphological traits such as plant height, etc. (Table 1), and some parameters of RSA (Table 2) in henna plants under controlled salinity levels. Furthermore, a strong negative correlation was observed between proline content and certain growth parameters of henna (Fig. 4); it can be concluded that energy is likely shifted toward proline synthesis, ultimately leading to a decrease in some morphological traits and RSA [37].

In the present research, findings demonstrated how Put and salinity treatments affected the root system architecture (RSA) of mature henna plants. Exposure to low salinity (50 mM) led to a significant increase in Network Depth, indicating a possible adaptive mechanism that promotes vertical root growth during mild salinity stress. When salinity levels were elevated to 100 and 150 mM, only Specific Root Length (a parameter associated with efficient resource acquisition) increased. This also suggests a shift toward finer root elongation to enhance water and nutrient absorption under these salinity levels. Nonetheless, high salinity stress (150 mM) significantly decreased parameters associated with RSA such as Average Root Width, Network Width, Network Perimeter, Network Surface Area, Median Root Number, and Ellipse Axes dimensions. These results emphasize that severe stress places significant limitations on henna root growth, probably as a result of ionic toxicity and osmotic imbalance interfering with cell elongation and meristematic function [67].

Several investigations have previously shown that Put treatment affects RSA in various plants, which aligns with our results [72–74]. Our findings showed that Put spraying (0.75 mM) had contrasting effects on henna RSA, depending on the salinity levels. At the controlled salinity level, it significantly inhibited some lateral root expansion parameters (e.g., Average Root Width, Network Width, Minor Ellipse Axis and Ellipse Axes Ratio) but simultaneously improved the Specific Root Length. This indicates a shift toward prioritizing lengthening instead of growth in width. Conversely, under high salinity stress (150 mM), it improved various root parameters (Table 2), indicating that Put mitigates the negative effects of salinity stress on henna roots. This context-dependent effectiveness suggests that Put has a unique ability to alleviate ionic and osmotic stress through processes such as polyamine-facilitated ROS scavenging, ion balancing, and cellular osmotic regulation [18, 68, 73, 74].

Assessing the salt tolerance of perennial plants for ornamental landscapes relies heavily on their visual quality [75]. In the current research, no foliage damage was seen in the plants that were subjected to 50 mM salinity levels, and their visual quality was excellent. Furthermore, the visual quality of henna plants remained good under 100 mM salinity stress and showed a slight decline at 150 mM, which were suitable for landscaping purposes. Due to the notable negative correlation observed between Na+ accumulation and henna visual quality (Fig. 4), it is likely that the decline in visual quality is caused by an increase in Na+ accumulation in the leaves. Consistent with these findings, it has been noted that elevated salinity levels lead to buildup of Na+ ions inside cells; such that the plant leaves show chlorosis and necrosis due to toxicity, resulting in decreased visual appeal [2, 11, 38, 76, 77].

In line with previous findings by Farahbakhsh et al. [36], photosynthetic pigments of henna leaves also decreased in this research when exposed to high levels of salinity stress. Conversely, this finding was inconsistent with the results of Najar et al. [6]. However, Put spraying mitigated the negative impacts of salinity, enhancing the leaf greenness index. The acknowledgment that salinity stress amplifies the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is widely accepted [5]. Accumulation of these unstable oxygen molecules in plant cells results in the peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids in thylakoid membranes, leading to enhanced chlorophyll degradation [78]. Furthermore, it may be associated with the function of the chlorophyllase enzyme [79]. Based on Fig. 4, there was a significant link between the increase in Na+ and the reduction of photosynthetic pigments and leaf greenness in henna plants (P ≤ 0.01), suggesting that the decline in photosynthetic pigments and SPAD value may be caused by the build-up of Na+ in henna foliage. Additionally, a strong negative correlation was found between proline and the levels of photosynthetic pigments (Fig. 4); it is likely that an increase in proline synthesis and accumulation has resulted in a decrease in chlorophyll synthesis, since they both originate from the common substance glutamate. Several studies have shown that the detrimental effect of salinity on photosynthetic pigments can be alleviated by the use of PAs such as Put [20, 80–82], Spm [52] and Spd [57, 58, 83], which aligns with the findings of this research. Reports have shown that PAs are vital in stabilizing membranes and thylakoid structure, as well as enhancing leaf greenness and reducing chlorophyll breakdown under saline conditions [84–86]. They also positively influence chlorophyll levels by protecting chlorophyll-protein complexes on thylakoid membranes and inhibiting ethylene synthesis to prevent chlorophyll degradation [22, 87]. This results in a boost in photosynthetic pigments and overall plant health.

Results showed that Na+ levels significantly rose, while K+ levels dropped in henna leaves under salinity conditions, aligning with a previous study by Fernández-García et al. [35]. Excessive amounts of Na+ disrupt ion uptake from the rhizosphere, damaging roots’ membrane integrity, and ultimately leading to a higher accumulation of Na+, while a decline in K+ content in plants’ tissue. Moreover, the competition for absorption by plant root cells between Na+ and K+ may result in an increase in Na+/K+ ratios, negatively impacting the plant’s growth and development [88–90]. It has been mentioned that a system of proteins, consisting of H+-ATPase, K+ channels, Na+/K+ transporters, high-affinity K+ transporter (HKT) and Na+/H+ antiporter (NHX), regulates the movement of K+ and Na+ ions across the cell membrane. Na+ influx causes the root’s cell membrane to become depolarized, leading to disruption in K+ influx [67, 91–93].

Our findings revealed that treating with Put enhanced K+ uptake and reduced the concentration of Na+ in henna leaves under high salinity levels (150 mM); consequently, this led to a decrease in Na+/K+. Comparable findings have also been documented by Ndayiragije and Lutts [27]; Zhang et al. [55]; El-Beltagi et al. [22]; Mohammadi et al. [23]; Xu et al. [57]. Reports have shown that Put and Spd can help counteract the decrease in H+-ATPase activity caused by high salinity, leading to increased K+ and decreased Na+ accumulation in the plant’s leaves [81, 94]. Additionally, Xu et al. [57] reported that applying Spd elevated the expression of ion transport proteins such as the NHX and HKT, stabilizing cellular ion balance and enhancing salinity tolerance. Therefore, PAs have the ability to protect membrane integrity and regulate K+ and Na+ channels and transporters channels and on the cell membrane to inhibit Na+ influx and K+ efflux [57, 86].

In the present research, the RWC decreased slightly under high salinity conditions, confirming previous studies by Fernández-García et al. [35], Farahbakhsh et al. [36] and Najar et al. [6] which observed a reduction in RWC of henna leaves due to salinity stress. RWC is a measurement that specifically shows the water condition of a stressed plant, displaying how well the plant can hold onto water during challenging circumstances. Moreover, it also depends on the moisture levels in the rhizosphere [90, 95]. Reports suggest that salinity stress impairs the root systems’ ability to absorb water by reducing soil water potential, ultimately leading to lower RWC [96]. Conversely, Put spraying improved RWC of henna leaves at 150 mM salinity stress. Similar findings have also shown that the harmful impacts of salinity on RWC can be mitigated by the application of Put [22, 23, 51] and Spd [57], which aligns with the outcomes of this study. However, our results contrasted with the findings of Korbas et al. [56] who reported that the interaction between Spd and salinity caused a reduction in RWC in cucumber leaves. These distinctive responses may be related to factors including the type of PAs and plant species. The enhancement in RWC due to Put spraying may be attributed to its capability for osmotic adjustment [97] and its potential impact on increasing water levels in epidermal cells and underlying mesophyll cells when PAs come into direct contact with the leaf surface [98].

A significant rise in electrolyte leakage was observed in henna leaves exposed to salinity stress, which aligned with the findings of Farahbakhsh et al. [36]. This could be related to how salinity impacts disruptions in plant metabolism, leading to oxidative stress through the stimulation of ROS production. The buildup of ROS in distressed plant cells can oxidize fatty acids, thereby resulting in elevated EL [15, 80, 90]. Consistent with our findings, it has been observed that the use of Put reduced EL in Luffa acutangula [51], Zinnia elegans [23] as well as Spd in Vitis vinifera [57] when subjected to salinity. The positive role of Put and other PAs in maintaining cell membrane stability and reducing EL in plants under salinity conditions could be related to the increased ability to neutralize oxidative radicals (ROS), thereby lowering lipid peroxidation of the membrane [7, 18, 51, 80].

The current research also documented an increase in proline levels in henna-stressed plants, in alignment with the results of Najar et al. [6]. Proline not only serves as an osmoregulator but also plays a role in controlling redox potential, detoxifying ROS, and protecting various cellular components during stress [17, 99]. The rise in proline content under salinity stress could be related to the elevated activity of pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase and γ-glutamyl kinase, along with reduced activity of proline oxidase [100]. Our findings also indicated that the use of Put resulted in higher accumulation of proline in henna leaves under both control and 50 mM salinity conditions. Nevertheless, the levels of proline decreased as the salinity levels reached 100 and 150 mM. Similar findings have also been documented by Jiménez-Bremont et al. [101] and Lopez-Gomez et al. [102]. Reports indicate that PAs can serve as a substitute for proline under these conditions without the need for its accumulation [102]. Additionally, it might result from improvements in the plants’ antioxidant system and decreased protein breakdown [23, 55].

Several investigations have previously shown a rise in TPC in various plants as a result of salinity stress, which aligns with our results [15, 81, 90]. TPC could play a key role in the plant antioxidant system by halting the production and accumulation of reactive oxygen species, removing them during oxidative stress [81, 103], and possibly acting as a carbon source [19]. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that ROS serve as a key signal to activate phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), the main enzyme in phenylpropanoid metabolism, which in turn increases the production of phenolic compounds [90]. Our findings revealed that treating with Put also resulted in higher levels of TPC in henna leaves. In line with these results, it has been observed that application of PAs increased TPC in Rice [98], Jojoba [104], and Thyme [19]. This reaction could possibly be caused by the catalytic influence of Put on photosynthesis, which is recognized as the main source of phenolic compounds through the shikimic acid pathway [19, 98, 105]. Additionally, it has been suggested that the increase in TPC could be related to higher PAL activity [19]. These findings highlight the crucial role of phenolic compounds in plant defense mechanisms against environmental stressors and oxidative damage.

A significant decrease in lawsone content was observed in henna leaves exposed to high salinity levels, which aligned with the findings of Najar et al. [6]. This response could be associated with how salinity impacts photosynthesis, which supplies the primary precursors for lawsone synthesis through the shikimic acid pathway. Due to the notable negative correlation observed between Na+ accumulation and lawsone content (Fig. 4), as well as the significant link between the amount of lawsone and photosynthetic pigments, it is likely that a decrease in photosynthesis caused by Na+ accumulation in henna leaves contributed to the reduction of lawsone.

This study showed that the antioxidant capacity of henna extract was greatly enhanced by Put and salinity stress, leading to an increase in radical-scavenging activity. Najar et al. [6] have also reported similar results regarding the impact of salinity stress on the DPPH radical scavenging activity of henna extract. One of the primary consequences of plants being exposed to salt stress is the excessive production of ROS. In order to mitigate the harmful effects of ROS on cells, plants have evolved different enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms to prevent oxidative harm [la5–7]. It has been indicated that phenolic compounds could have a crucial function in scavenging ROS [5, 103]. Earlier research emphasizes the beneficial impact of Put on improving DPPH radical scavenging activity, which aligns with our results [7, 104, 106]. Due to the strong correlation (r=−0.64) between TPC amount and DPPH radical scavenging activity, it is likely that variations in TPC amounts contributed to changes in antioxidant activity (Fig. 4). This correlation is frequently documented in different species of plants, such as jojoba [104], rosemary [90] and tomato [7].

Our findings indicated that the activities of SOD, CAT, POD, and APX were affected in henna leaves as a result of being subjected to NaCl salinity stress, which may be related to the activation of defense mechanisms in plants. Consistent with these findings, numerous studies have reported that salt-tolerant plants exhibit higher levels of activity in antioxidant enzymes when exposed to salinity stress [17, 23, 36, 51, 57, 81, 107]. The assessment of relationships among antioxidant enzymes indicated that significant correlations existed between SOD and CAT, CAT and POD, as well as APX and POD, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Based on absolute enzyme activity, biological roles, and stability under severe stress, it appears that the contributions of SOD and CAT to henna’s salinity tolerance were greater than those of APX and POD. Nevertheless, a positive correlation was observed between APX and some photosynthetic pigments, such as Chl a, and Chl a + b (Fig. 4), indicating the role of APX in neutralizing H₂O₂ in chloroplasts. Although, our results contrasted with the findings of Islam et al. [17] and Xu et al. [57], who reported a decline in CAT activity due to salinity stress in Korean ginseng and grapevine, respectively; however, Farahbakhsh et al. [36] demonstrated increased CAT activity in henna plants under saline conditions, reflecting a distinct antioxidant mechanism in henna. Nonetheless, a minor decline in the activities APX was observed when salinity levels reached 150 mM (Table 4). This might occur due to surpassing the ability of the plant’s defense mechanisms or enzyme breakdown triggered by elevated stress [23]. Reports indicate that heightened superoxide radical (O2−) production in plants exposed to salt stress results in increased SOD activity, acting as a cue to enhance the induction of antioxidant enzymes [17]. SOD is a key component of the antioxidant system that functions as the primary defense mechanism by converting O₂⁻ into O₂ and H₂O₂. Subsequently, H₂O₂ is converted into H₂O and O₂ by various enzymes such as CAT, which serves as the main enzyme reducing H₂O₂ levels, along with APX, POD, and others, or through non-enzymatic antioxidants in different parts of the cell [5, 17, 51, 81, 107, 108]. Consistent with our findings, it has been noted that the use of Put led to a rise in the activation of antioxidant enzymes in some species of plants such as tea [15], luffa [51], ginseng [17], snap bean [22] and zinnia [23]. However, conflicting results have occasionally been reported regarding the effect of polyamines on antioxidant enzyme activity. For instance, the use of Spm in tomato plants caused a decrease in SOD activity, while CAT activity remained unaffected [54].

The interaction between Put and salinity showed that applying Put caused a reduction in POD activity when salinity levels were raised to 100 and 150 mM compared to the control. This aligns with the findings of Ghalati et al. [109] and Seleem et al. [80], which may result from enzymes breaking down due to heightened stress levels or related to Put ‘s ability to inhibit the formation of hydroxyl radicals, which could explain the decrease of POD activity indirectly [15, 80]. Additionally, as mentioned previously, the negative impact of Put on POD could be related to the dual effect of PAs on proteins synthesis [71]. Generally, the enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activity through Put application seems to be associated with the function of PAs as activators of antioxidant enzymes or their influence on gene expression related to antioxidant enzymes, along with their ability to eliminate ROS [55, 57, 106].

Conclusion

Visual quality is an aesthetic measure in landscape spaces and is crucial for assessing plants’ tolerance to salinity stress. In the current study, henna’s visual quality and growth parameters slightly decreased when exposed to high levels of salinity. Despite not impacting visual quality, Put improved the leaf greenness index and other aspects of henna by mitigating the negative impacts of salinity. Our results suggest that foliar spraying of Put can significantly alleviate the growth inhibition caused by salinity stress and improve the salinity tolerance of mature henna plants, making them suitable for landscaping in areas with high salinity levels in soil or water. Put’s ability to prioritize root elongation over width expansion, increase K+ and decrease Na+ accumulation, improve RWC, as well as modifications in antioxidant system activity, might be attributed to this. Since this study was conducted under greenhouse conditions, therefore, more research, particularly under field conditions, are required to confirm this finding and thoroughly understand the underlying mechanisms.
