Severe mitral regurgitation after aortic annular enlargement with Y-incision: a case report
Authors: Safa Özçelik, Farid Gojayev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Y-incision technique enables implantation of larger prosthetic valves by facilitating effective aortic annular enlargement. Although this approach improves hemodynamic outcomes, rare and clinically significant complications may occur and remain insufficiently described.Case presentationA patient undergoing surgical aortic valve replacement with concomitant Y-incision annular enlargement developed early postoperative severe central mitral regurgitation. Standard transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated abnormal aorto-mitral geometry and annular distortion. Reoperation revealed deformation of the mitral annulus adjacent to the patch implantation site. Based on intraoperative findings, imaging data, and schematic analysis, we hypothesized that turbulent flow within the pouch-like neo–aortomitral curtain combined with patch sutures placed near the mitral annulus contributed to annular distortion and subsequent mitral valve incompetence.ConclusionSevere mitral regurgitation represents a rare but clinically important complication of the Y-incision technique. Patient-specific aorto-mitral anatomy, including annular distance and aorto-mitral angle, may play a critical role in this mechanism. Careful preoperative anatomical assessment and meticulous intraoperative patch positioning are essential to minimize this risk and improve procedural safety.

Introduction

Lifelong management of patients with aortic valve disease requires meticulous prosthesis sizing to optimize postoperative hemodynamics and long-term outcomes [1]. The Y-incision aortic annular enlargement technique, described by Dr. Bo-Yang, offers a substantial advantage by enabling implantation of significantly larger prosthetic valves [2]. This approach relies on modification and superior extension of the aorto-mitral curtain. To date, no cases in the literature have reported associated functional mitral valve dysfunction following the Y-incision technique.

We report a rare case of severe postoperative mitral regurgitation that developed immediately after aortic annular enlargement using the Y-incision technique.

Case Presentation

A 58-year-old female patient presented with exertional dyspnea and angina and was diagnosed with severe aortic stenosis and an ascending aortic aneurysm. The patient’s height was 153 cm and weight was 63 kg, resulting in a calculated body surface area of 1.77 m².

Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%, a left ventricular end-systolic diameter of 24 mm, and an end-diastolic diameter of 44 mm. The aortic valve peak and mean gradients were 68 mmHg and 42 mmHg, respectively, with a calculated valve area of 0.6 cm². Trace mitral regurgitation was present, and a calcified appearance was observed at the posterior mitral annulus. Surgical treatment consisting of aortic valve replacement and ascending aortic replacement was planned using an upper J mini-sternotomy approach.

A mini-sternotomy was performed, and cardiopulmonary bypass was established via distal ascending aortic cannulation and percutaneous venous cannulation through the right femoral vein. Myocardial protection was achieved using antegrade del Nido cardioplegia.

After excision of the heavily calcified native aortic valve, annular sizing revealed that even a 19-mm prosthesis could not be accommodated. Therefore, aortic annular enlargement using the Y-incision technique was performed. The aortotomy was extended toward the commissure between the non-coronary and left coronary cusps. Subsequently, the incision was advanced subannularly on both sides until reaching the right and left fibrous trigones. A rectangular Dacron patch was tailored according to the measured intertrigonal distance.

The patch was sutured using a continuous 4 − 0 Prolene suture line and allowed to expand into the newly created space. Pledgeted sutures were placed in a subannular fashion around the native annulus. At the transition zone between the native annulus and the patch, the final pledgeted suture was placed, followed by non-pledgeted sutures along the patch (Fig. 1). A 23-mm On-X mechanical prosthesis (Artivion Inc.) was implanted (Fig. 2). The ascending aorta was replaced with a 30-mm InterGard Dacron® graft.

After deairing and aortic cross-clamp removal, the heart resumed sinus rhythm, and the patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without inotropic support (cross-clamp time: 147 min; cardiopulmonary bypass time: 187 min). The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit uneventfully and awakened at postoperative hour 6.

Due to difficulty in ventilator weaning, bedside transesophageal echocardiography was performed and revealed severe central mitral regurgitation. The postoperative examination also demonstrated a peak transvalvular gradient of 32 mmHg and a mean gradient of 18 mmHg across the implanted aortic valve. Immediate reintervention was deferred in order to avoid an early repeat cross-clamping and the associated cumulative myocardial ischemic burden. Aggressive diuretic therapy was initiated to achieve negative fluid balance. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 3 and transferred to the ward on day 4. Although symptoms partially improved with medical therapy, the patient declined reoperation during the same hospitalization and was discharged on postoperative day 10.

Despite continued medical management, the patient remained symptomatic with exertional dyspnea during follow-up. After renewed discussion, she consented to reoperation approximately 50 days after the initial surgery. A full median sternotomy was performed, and cardiopulmonary bypass was established using distal aortic and bicaval cannulation.

The mitral valve was exposed through a superior septal approach. Saline testing demonstrated a central regurgitant jet, consistent with anterior annular distortion. Given these findings, mitral valve replacement was performed using a 27-mm CarboMedics mechanical prosthesis. The superior septal incision was reconstructed with a pericardial patch. The patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without complications (cross-clamp time: 93 min; cardiopulmonary bypass time: 120 min) and was discharged on postoperative day 7 in stable condition.

Discussion

Appropriate prosthesis sizing is critical for preventing patient–prosthesis mismatch and ensuring optimal long-term outcomes in the surgical management of aortic valve disease. Aortic root enlargement procedures enable implantation of larger prosthetic valves and improve effective orifice area. While conventional enlargement techniques typically allow upsizing by one or two valve sizes, the Y-incision technique uniquely permits implantation of prostheses three to five sizes larger by selectively extending the aorto-mitral curtain.

Unlike the Manougian procedure, which directly involves the mitral annulus and is associated with a recognized risk of postoperative mitral regurgitation, the Y-incision technique has not previously been reported to cause mitral insufficiency [3]. In the present case, however, specific anatomical characteristics appear to have played a contributory role. Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a relatively short aorto-mitral curtain measuring approximately 1.7–1.8 cm and a narrow aorto-mitral angle, as illustrated in the echocardiographic image (Fig. 3).

As depicted in the schematic illustration, we hypothesize that the pouch-like configuration formed toward the left atrial roof following Yang root enlargement may create localized pressure transmission to the adjacent mitral annulus. In the setting of a short annular distance and narrow aorto-mitral angle, this pressure may preferentially affect the mid-anterior segment of the mitral annulus, resulting in annular distortion and subsequent central mitral regurgitation (Fig. 4). This proposed mechanism is supported by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography findings and direct surgical observations obtained during the reoperation.

These findings emphasize the delicate anatomical relationship between the reconstructed aorto-mitral continuity and the mitral valve apparatus. Careful preoperative assessment of aorto-mitral geometry, as well as meticulous intraoperative attention to patch positioning and annular proximity, may be essential to minimizing the risk of this rare but potentially serious complication.

Limitations

This report has several limitations inherent to its single-case design. First, ECG-gated cardiac computed tomography and three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography were not available preoperatively or postoperatively, which limited our ability to perform comprehensive three-dimensional assessment of the aorto-mitral curtain geometry and mitral annular configuration. Although standard transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography were used, advanced volumetric and spatial analyses could have provided more precise anatomical characterization.

Second, intraoperative photographic documentation of the annular suture line prior to patch implantation was not obtained, and a dedicated surgical video recording system was not available at our institution. As a result, direct visual correlation between the patch position and subsequent annular deformation could not be demonstrated.

Third, although intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography was performed, the imaging data could not be archived or exported for detailed postprocessing and inclusion in the manuscript due to a technical failure of the institutional imaging software. Consequently, the proposed mechanistic explanation is based on indirect imaging findings, intraoperative observations during reoperation, and schematic illustration rather than formal three-dimensional reconstruction. Therefore, causal inference should be interpreted with caution.

Despite these limitations, the present case provides valuable clinical insight into a previously unreported complication of the Y-incision technique and highlights the importance of individualized anatomical assessment and careful patch positioning during aortic annular enlargement procedures.

Conclusion

Severe mitral regurgitation may occur as a rare but clinically significant complication following aortic annular enlargement using the Y-incision technique. Patient-specific aorto-mitral anatomy, including annular distance and aorto-mitral angle, may play a critical role in this mechanism. Careful preoperative anatomical assessment and meticulous intraoperative patch positioning are essential to minimize this risk and improve procedural safety.
