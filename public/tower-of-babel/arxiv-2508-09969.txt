Regularity for hypergraphs with bounded VC 2 dimension
Lior Gishboliner, Asaf Shapira, Yuval Wigderson

Abstract
While Szemerédi’s graph regularity lemma is an indispensable tool for studying extremal problems in graph theory, using it comes with a hefty price, since in the worst case, a graph may only have regular partitions of tower-type order. It is thus sensible to ask if there is some natural restriction which forces graphs to have much smaller regular partitions. A celebrated result of this type, due to Alon–Fischer–Newman and Lovász–Szegedy, states that for graphs of bounded VC dimension, one can reduce the tower-type bounds to polynomial. The graph regularity lemma has been extended to the setting of k k -graphs by Gowers and by Rödl et al. Unfortunately, these lemmas come with even larger Ackermann-type bounds. Chernikov–Starchenko and Fox–Pach–Suk considered a strong notion of k k -graph VC dimension and proved that k k -graphs of bounded VC dimension have regular partitions of polynomial size. Shelah introduced a weaker and combinatorially more natural notion of dimension, called VC 2 dimension, which has been extensively studied in the past decade. In particular, Chernikov, Towsner, Terry, and Wolf asked if one can improve the worst case bounds for 3 3 -graph regularity when the 3 3 -graph has bounded VC 2 dimension. Our main result in this paper answers this question positively in the following strong sense: we show that in the setting of bounded VC 2 dimension, one can reduce the bounds for 3-graph regularity by one level in Ackermann hierarchy. Furthermore, our new improved bound is best possible. Our proof has two key steps. We first introduce a novel method for designing regularity lemmas for graphs of bounded VC dimension, based on the cylinder regularity lemma. We then prove a hypergraph version of the cylinder regularity lemma, which allows us to extend this new method to hypergraphs. We additionally highlight a few other applications of this cylinder regularity lemma, which we expect to find many other uses.

1.1.1
Graph regularity

One of the deepest and most powerful results in modern graph theory is Szemerédi’s regularity lemma

Roughly speaking, it states that any graph can be approximated as the union of a constant number of “random-like” graphs, where the number of such pieces depends only on the quality of the approximation and on how random-like we demand the pieces to be. 1.1.4
Hypergraph regularity and VC
2

After the introduction of Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, there was great interest in extending this technique to hypergraphs. There is a natural analogue of the notion of

This was accomplished independently in the mid-2000s, most notably by Gowers
[
35

and by Rödl et al.

, it is no longer sufficient to simply partition the vertices. Instead, in uniformity

Our main focus will be on uniformity
3

For a graph

1.3.3
Proof sketch of Theorem
1.4

In order to use the strategy we described in the previous subsection in uniformity
3

3

Theorem

2.13

Crucially, this cylinder regularity lemma yields a partition whose size is only of tower type, much better than the wowzer-type bounds needed to obtain a full regularity partition; this is analogous to the situation in graphs, where the full regularity lemma requires tower-type bounds, but the cylinder regularity lemma requires only exponential bounds. Unsurprisingly, even the notion of a cylinder partition becomes substantially more complicated when we move to hypergraphs. We defer a detailed discussion of these definitions to
Section

.

Let us now fix such a cylinder partition of
\mathcal{H}
, as given by the cylinder regularity lemma. The next thing we need is an induced counting lemma, in order to conclude that all the quasirandom cylinders are actually very sparse or very dense. As discussed earlier, the main issue in the development of the hypergraph regularity method is finding notions of quasirandomness that can yield such counting lemmas, while also supporting a corresponding regularity lemma. For our application, it is crucial that the counting lemma only requires polynomial bounds, so that we may set
\eta=\poly(\varepsilon)
as we did in the graph case (at the end of the penultimate paragraph of
Section

1.3.2
). While it is plausible that each of the notions of hypergraph regularity does indeed only require polynomial losses in the counting lemma, as far as we are aware, no one has proved this for the regularity notion of Rödl et al.
[
50
]
. On the other hand, Gowers’s
[
35
]
approach to the hypergraph regularity lemma does give a counting lemma with polynomial bounds, hence we use Gowers’s notion throughout. However, we remark that the final outcome is essentially agnostic
8
8

8

It is known that all the notions of hypergraph regularity are qualitatively equivalent (see e.g.
[
48
]
). However, the proofs of these equivalences require applications of the hypergraph regularity lemma itself, and hence are not quantitatively effective. Since the main thrust of
Theorem

1.4
is the quantitative improvement, we cannot freely pass between different regularity notions.
about which notion of regularity is used, because our final partition actually has the stronger
\varepsilon

\varepsilon

We now have a cylinder partition of
\mathcal{H}
in which almost all cylinders are very sparse or very dense. As before, we now pass to the Venn diagram partition, obtaining a genuine vertex partition and edge partition of
\mathcal{H}
. Unlike in the graph case, this step is essentially “for free”: although we exponentiate the number of parts, this loss is negligible given that the number of parts in the cylinder partition is already of tower type. As before, this step maintains the
\varepsilon
-homogeneity property, that is, almost all triples of vertices now lie in parts that are very sparse or very dense.

We are almost done, except that in a hypergraph regularity partition, we require the graphs arising from the edge partition to themselves be quasirandom. This was the case in the cylinder partition, but this property may have been destroyed when passing to the Venn diagram partition. Hence, we now need to apply Szemerédi’s regularity lemma in order to further refine the vertex partition, and to recover the quasirandomness of the graphs. This final step costs us another tower function, which is why the final bound is of double-tower type. However, we stress that we only apply the graph regularity lemma
once
, whereas it needs to be applied iteratively in the proof of the usual hypergraph regularity lemma
9
9

9

That said, the cylinder partition we use
is
required to have its underlying graph be quasirandom, in order to apply the induced counting lemma and conclude that almost all cylinders are very sparse or very dense. As such, in the proof of our cylinder regularity lemma for hypergraphs, we are required to regularize the graph at each step of the iteration. However, as we are working with cylinder partitions, we may apply the cylinder regularity lemma for graphs, rather than Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, at every step of the iteration, and thus only pay an exponential loss at every step; this is why our cylinder regularity lemma ends up incurring tower-type bounds.
. This difference is what ensures that we end up with a final bound of tower type, rather than wowzer type. To summarize, in the proof of the hypergraph regularity lemma, one iteratively applies Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, leading to wowzer-type bounds. In our proof, we iteratively apply the cylinder regularity lemma for graphs (incurring tower-type dependencies), and then apply Szemerédi’s regularity lemma only once (losing one more tower).

q(\mathcal{P})\coloneqq\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{i}\rvert}{\lvert X_{i}\rvert}\right)q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y)).

The basic observations we need about the mean-squared density are standard, namely that it is bounded and monotonic with respect to refinement. To formally state these properties, let us say that a vertex cylinder partition
\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime}

refines
another vertex cylinder partition
\mathcal{P}_{V}
if, for every
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime}
, there is a unique
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}
containing
Y^{\prime}
. That is, if
Y^{\prime}=Y_{1}^{\prime}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}Y_{t}^{\prime}
and
Y=Y_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}Y_{t}
, then
Y_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq Y_{i}
for all
i
. Similarly, an edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(G)

refines
another edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}(G)
of the same
t
-partite graph if for all pairs of parts
Y_{i},Y_{j}
, each part of
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(G;Y_{i}\times Y_{j})
lies in a unique part of
\mathcal{P}_{E}(G;Y_{i}\times Y_{j})
. For two edge partitions
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y),\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(Y)
of a vertex cylinder, we say that
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(Y)
refines
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y)
if it refines it in the sense above, where we view it as an edge partition of the complete
t
-partite graph. Finally, let us say that one cylinder chain partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime})

refines
another cylinder chain partition
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}_{V},\mathcal{P}_{E})
if
\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime}
refines
\mathcal{P}_{V}
in the sense above, and if
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}
refines
\mathcal{P}_{E}
in the following sense. If
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime}
, let
Y
be the part of
\mathcal{P}_{V}
containing
Y^{\prime}
. For all
i<j
, the partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{i}\times Y_{j})
naturally restricts to a partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{i}\times Y_{j})|_{Y_{i}^{\prime}\times Y_{j}^{\prime}}
of the complete bipartite graph
Y_{i}^{\prime}\times Y_{j}^{\prime}
. We then say that

\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}
refines
\mathcal{P}_{E}
if, for all
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime}
and for all
i<j
, the partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{\prime}\times Y_{j}^{\prime})
refines
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{i}\times Y_{j})|_{Y_{i}^{\prime}\times Y_{j}^{\prime}}
.

We may now state the properties we will use of the mean-squared density.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{H})
be a tripartite chain.

(a)

If
\mathcal{P}_{E}(G)
is an edge partition of
G
, then
0\leqslant q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(G))\leqslant 1
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(G)
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{E}(G)
, then
q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{\prime}(G))\geqslant q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(G))
.

Similarly, let
\mathcal{H}
be a
t
-partite
3
-graph.

(c)

If
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}_{V},\mathcal{P}_{E})
is a cylinder chain partition, then
0\leqslant q(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\binom{t}{3}
.

(d)

If
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}
, then
q(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\geqslant q(\mathcal{P})
.

Lemma

3.2
is proved by several elementary but tedious applications of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and we thus defer the proof to
Section

A.1
.

As in the majority of proofs of regularity lemmas, our proof of
Theorem

2.13
will proceed by an energy increment approach. Concretely, given a cylinder chain partition of
\mathcal{H}
, we will either find that it is sufficiently quasirandom, or else find a refinement of it that increases the mean-squared density by a constant amount (independent of
\mathcal{H}
or of the given partition). Each single step of this iteration will be obtained by the following lemma of Gowers
[
34
]
.
It states that if a tripartite chain is not
\eta
-quasirandom, then we can find an edge partition of it for which the mean-squared density increases appreciably, while the number of parts in the new edge partition is not too large.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
34
, Lemma 8.4]
)
.

Consider a tripartite graph
G
with vertex sets
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}
and edge sets
Z_{12},Z_{13},Z_{23}
. Let
\delta=\delta(G)
. Suppose that each of the three bipartite graphs is
\alpha
-quasirandom, where
\alpha\leqslant 2^{-100}\delta^{28}
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{H})
be a tripartite chain, and suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is not
\eta
-quasirandom relative to
G
. Let
d=d(\mathcal{H}\mid G)
be the relative density of
\mathcal{H}
. Then there is an edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}(G)
such that
q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(G))\geqslant d^{2}+2^{-10}\eta^{2}
. Moreover,
\lvert\mathcal{P}_{E}(G)\rvert\leqslant 3^{\delta^{-4}}
.

Using
Lemma

3.3
, we are able to take an irregular cylinder chain partition and further partition each of the irregular edge parts in order to increase the mean-squared density. However, we cannot then directly iterate
Lemma

3.3
, as the assumption of
Lemma

3.3
requires that each bipartite graph is
\alpha
-quasirandom, and this condition may be destroyed by the previous partitioning step. To get around this, we will apply the cylinder regularity lemma for graphs
[
22
]
at each step. We will actually use the following, slightly generalized, version of the cylinder regularity lemma for graphs, which allows us to simultaneously regularize a family of graphs on the same vertex set.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G_{1},\dots,G_{m}
be
t
-partite graphs on the same vertex set. For any
\alpha>0
, there exists a vertex cylinder partition
\mathcal{P}_{V}
such that the following holds. If
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
is chosen uniformly at random from
X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
, then with probability at least
1-\alpha/2
, we have that for all
i\in[m]
, the subgraph of
G_{i}
induced on the vertex set
\mathcal{P}_{V}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
is
\alpha
-quasirandom. Moreover,
\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert\leqslant 2^{mt^{2}\alpha^{-20}}
.

The only differences between
Lemma

3.4
and the cylinder regularity lemma proved by Duke, Lefmann, and Rödl
[
22
]
are that we are regularizing multiple graphs, and that we use a different notion of quasirandomness, leading to the different power of
\alpha
(since these two notions of graph quasirandomness are polynomially related). It is well known, and used frequently in hypergraph regularity theory, that one can simultaneously regularize multiple graphs on the same vertex set; see e.g.
[
34
, Theorem 7.8]
or
[
30
, Lemma 3.7]
. For a proof of the cylinder regularity lemma for multiple graphs, see
[
23
, Theorem 7.2]
.

3.2
Proof of Theorem
2.13

With these preliminaries, we are ready to prove
Theorem

2.13
. In fact, for future convenience (see
Lemma

4.2
), we prove the following slight strengthening of
Theorem

2.13
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Fix an integer
t
, a parameter
\eta>0
, and an increasing polynomial function
\psi:(0,1)\to(0,1)
satisfying
\psi(x)\leqslant 2^{-100}x^{28}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a
t
-partite
3
-graph. Then there exists a cylinder chain partition
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}_{V},\mathcal{P}_{E})
, such that
\mathcal{H}
is
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom relative to
\mathcal{P}
, and such that

\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert\leqslant\twr\left(\frac{10^{5}t^{6}}{\eta^{3}},\psi\left(\frac{\eta}{t^{2}}\right)^{-20}\right)

and

\lvert\mathcal{P}_{E}\rvert\leqslant\twr\left(\frac{10^{5}t^{6}}{\eta^{3}},\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\eta}\right)^{10}\right).

Furthermore, there is a constant
\delta>0
, depending only on
t,\eta
, and
\psi
, such that a
(1-\eta)
-fraction of tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
lie in a chain with product density at least
\delta
.

The only difference between
Theorems

2.13
and
3.5
is the final sentence, stating that most
t
-tuples lie in chains whose product density is not too small. This will be useful for us later, and arises naturally from the proof.

Proof of
Theorem

3.5
.

We begin by defining functions
A,B:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as follows. We set
A(0)=B(0)=1
, and recursively define

\displaystyle B(\tau+1)

\displaystyle=2^{2^{(t^{2}B(\tau)/\eta)^{2t^{2}}}},

\displaystyle A(\tau+1)

\displaystyle=2^{B(\tau+1)t^{4}\psi(\eta/(t^{2}B(\tau+1)))^{-20t^{2}}}A(\tau).

The fact that
\psi
is a polynomial function implies that

A(\tau)\leqslant\twr\left(3\tau,\psi\left(\frac{\eta}{t^{2}}\right)^{-20}\right)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B(\tau)\leqslant\twr\left(3\tau,\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\eta}\right)^{10}\right).

(4)

We remark that similar bounds would hold if
\psi
were any function such that
1/\psi
is of constant tower height; the only difference is that the tower height of
A(\tau)
would be at most
C\tau
, for some constant
C
depending on
\psi
. In case
\psi
is a polynomial, we may take
C=3
, as stated in (
4
).

We will define a sequence
\mathcal{P}^{(0)},\mathcal{P}^{(1)},\dots
of cylinder chain partitions, and inductively maintain the following properties, where
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
are the
t
vertex parts of
\mathcal{H}
.

(i)

\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}\rvert\leqslant A(\tau)
.

(ii)

\lvert\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}\rvert\leqslant B(\tau)
.

(iii)

q(\mathcal{P}^{(\tau)})\geqslant 2^{-10}\eta^{3}\tau/t^{3}
.

(iv)

Let
\delta=(\eta/(t^{2}B(\tau)))^{\binom{t}{2}}
and
\alpha=\psi(\delta)
. If we sample
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})\in X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
uniformly at random,
the following happens with probability at least
1-\eta/2
: the
t
-partite graph
Z\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
satisfies
\delta(Z)\geqslant\delta
and
Z
is
\alpha
-quasirandom.

(v)

Either
\mathcal{H}
is
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom with respect to
\mathcal{P}^{(\tau)}
, or else we can define
\mathcal{P}^{(\tau+1)}
satisfying the above properties.

To begin the induction, we let
\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(0)}
be the trivial cylinder partition into one part
Y^{0}=X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
, and let the corresponding edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y^{0}(X_{i})\times Y^{0}(X_{j}))
also be the trivial partition into one part
X_{i}\times X_{j}
, for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant t
. Then it is clear that the claimed properties
(i)
–
(iv)
hold.

Inductively, suppose we have defined
\mathcal{P}^{(\tau)}
satisfying properties
(i)
–
(iv)
. We are done if
\mathcal{H}
is
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom with respect to
\mathcal{P}^{(\tau)}
, so we may assume that this is not the case.

As in property
(iv)
, let
\delta=(\eta/(t^{2}B(\tau)))^{\binom{t}{2}}
and let
\alpha=\psi(\delta)
. The fact that
\mathcal{H}
is not
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom with respect to
\mathcal{P}^{(\tau)}
means that if we pick
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})\in X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
uniformly at random, then with probability at least
\eta
, the chain
\mathcal{H}[\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}];\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]]
is not
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom. That, in turn, means that with probability at least
\eta
, either the chain
\mathcal{H}[\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}];\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]]
is not
\eta
-quasirandom, or that the
t
-partite graph
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
is not
\psi(\delta(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]))
-quasirandom.

In this random experiment, we know by property
(iv)
that with probability at least
1-\eta/2
, the
t
-partite graph
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
satisfies
\delta(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}])\geqslant\delta
and is
\alpha
-quasirandom. Since
\psi
is increasing, this in turn implies that
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
is
\psi(\delta(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]))
-quasirandom. Therefore, with probability at least
\eta-\eta/2=\eta/2
, we have that
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
is
\psi(\delta(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]))
-quasirandom, whereas the chain
\mathcal{H}[\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}];\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]]
is not
\eta
-quasirandom. By the pigeonhole principle, there exist distinct indices
a,b,c\in[t]
such that for at least an
\eta/(2\binom{t}{3})
-fraction of the tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})\in X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
, the tripartite chain

\mathcal{H}[\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}];\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}];\{a,b,c\}]

(5)

is not
\eta
-quasirandom but does satisfy that
\delta(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}])\geqslant\delta
and that
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]
is
\psi(\delta(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}[x_{1},\dots,x_{t}]))

(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})

useful

Fix a useful

(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})

We may thus apply

. Doing this gives us, for each such useful chain, an edge partition of

We now do this process for all useful chains, and use this step to define a new edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}
(for the moment, we will not change
\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}
). To do so,

\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y)

\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})
be

. For each
1\leqslant\mu\leqslant\ell
, we consider the set of all tripartite graphs arising by adding to
Z_{ab}^{\mu}
some part of
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{a}\times Y_{c})
and
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{b}\times Y_{c})
. Some of these tripartite chains are useful and hence subpartitioned by

, and we then let

be partitioned as the common refinement of all of these partitions. By doing this for all

, we obtain a new edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})
. We do the same to obtain the edge partitions
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y_{a}\times Y_{c}),\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y_{b}\times Y_{c})
. For all

, we set

. Finally, doing this for all

We now claim that

q((\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)},\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}))\geqslant q((\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)},\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}))+2^{-10}\eta^{3}/t^{3}.

(6)

Indeed, we first note that as we did not change the vertex partition,

q((\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)},\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}))-q((\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)},\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}))=\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(\tau)}}\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}\frac{\lvert Y_{i}\rvert}{\lvert X_{i}\rvert}\right)\left(q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y))-q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y))\right).

(7)

To estimate the inner difference, let us fix some

. We then have by definition that

q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y))-q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y))=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}\left[q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(K(Y_{i},Y_{j},Y_{k})))-q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(K(Y_{i},Y_{j},Y_{k})))\right].

For each triple
(i,j,k)\neq(a,b,c)
, we recall that the edge partition
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(K(Y_{i},Y_{j},Y_{k}))

, hence

. Therefore,

q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y))-q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y))\geqslant q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(K(Y_{a},Y_{b},Y_{c})))-q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(K(Y_{a},Y_{b},Y_{c}))).

(8)

By definition, we have that

q(\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(K(Y_{a},Y_{b},Y_{c})))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z^{\prime}_{ab}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})\\

(9)

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})
refines
\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})
, and similarly for

and
Y_{b}\times Y_{c}
. This means that we may rearrange the sum in (

and thus find that

equals

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z_{ab}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})\\

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z_{ab}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})\\
Z_{ac}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{a}\times Y_{c})\\
Z_{bc}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau)}(Y_{b}\times Y_{c})\end{subarray}}\frac{\lvert\Delta(Z_{ab}\cup Z_{ac}\cup Z_{bc})\rvert}{\lvert Y_{a}\rvert\lvert Y_{b}\rvert\lvert Y_{c}\rvert}\hskip-7.6pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z^{\prime}_{ab}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}^{(\tau+\frac{1}{2})}(Y_{a}\times Y_{b})\\

For such

(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{t})\in Y\Longleftrightarrow(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{t})\in Y.

That is,
x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}
are in the same part of
\mathcal{Q}_{V}
if and only if they participate in exactly the same cylinders in
\mathcal{P}_{V}
.

Now, given parts
Y_{i}\subseteq X_{i},Y_{j}\subseteq X_{j}
of
\mathcal{Q}_{V}
, we define the edge partition
\mathcal{Q}_{E}(Y_{i}\times Y_{j})
as follows. Two edges
(x_{i},x_{j}),(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})\in Y_{i}\times Y_{j}
lie in the same part of
\mathcal{Q}_{E}(Y_{i}\times Y_{j})
if and only if they participate in all of the same edge parts of
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y)
, over all vertex cylinders
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}
which contain
Y_{i},Y_{j}
.

Note that, if
\mathcal{Q}
is the Venn diagram partition of
\mathcal{P}
, then
\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{V}\rvert\leqslant t2^{\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert}
, since, for each part

, there are at most
2^{\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert}
possible intersection patterns of cylinders in
\mathcal{P}_{V}
with
X_{i}
. Similarly,
\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{E}(Y_{i}\times Y_{j})\rvert\leqslant 2^{\lvert\mathcal{P}_{E}\rvert\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert}
for every
Y_{i},Y_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}_{V}
, since there are at most
\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert
options for a cylinder
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}
containing
Y_{i},Y_{j}
, and at most
\lvert\mathcal{P}_{E}\rvert
bipartite graphs in
\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y)
, and thus at most
2^{\lvert\mathcal{P}_{E}\rvert\lvert\mathcal{P}_{V}\rvert}
possible intersection patterns with
Y_{i}\times Y_{j}
.

Our next lemma shows that by taking the Venn diagram partition of the cylinder chain partition given by

, we can obtain a true chain partition such that almost all triples have relative density close to

Lemma 4.6

Let

Let

be a sufficiently large

such that the following holds for all

is sampled uniformly at random, then with probability at least

Moreover,

\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{V}\rvert\leqslant\twr\left(\left(\frac{t}{\zeta}\right)^{C}\right)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{E}(Y\times Y^{\prime})\rvert\leqslant\twr\left(\left(\frac{t}{\zeta}\right)^{C}\right)

for every

Proof.

Apply

4.3
with parameter

to obtain a cylinder chain partition

\mathcal{Q}
be the Venn diagram partition of
\mathcal{P}

For the rest of the proof, we fix some

, we define its

To justify this name, we first claim that a

Now, let

By the definition of typicality, a
(1-\gamma)

Putting this all together, we find that if

The final case, of a shattered

6
Concluding remarks and open problems

6.1
Other applications of cylinder regularity for hypergraphs

The major technical tool introduced in this paper is the cylinder regularity lemma for hypergraphs,
Theorem

2.13
, and we expect this result to have many other applications. We now briefly discuss two such applications, as well as some open problems related to them.

The first of these applications is a hypergraph extension of the quasirandom subset lemma of Conlon and Fox
[
18
]
. For a graph
G
and a set
U\subseteq V(G)
, we say that
U
is
\varepsilon
-quasirandom
if the pair
(U,U)
is
\varepsilon
-quasirandom. More formally,
U
is
\varepsilon
-quasirandom if the pair
(U,U)
is
\varepsilon
-quasirandom in the graph
G\times K_{2}
(the
bipartite double cover
of
G
), whose vertex set is two copies of
V(G)
, and whose edges are all pairs crossing the two parts that correspond to an edge of
G
. The quasirandom subset lemma then states that every graph contains a linear-sized quasirandom subset.

Lemma 6.1

(
[
18
, Lemma 5.2]
)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for every graph
G
, there is a set
U\subseteq V(G)
with
\lvert U\rvert\geqslant\delta\lvert V(G)\rvert
such that
U
is
\varepsilon
-quasirandom.

Lemma

6.1
is extremely useful (see e.g.
[
20
,
19
]
and
[
68
, Chapter 6]
for several applications). This statement can be deduced from Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, but the remarkable insight of Conlon and Fox is that one can find an alternative proof which yields much stronger quantitative bounds; Conlon and Fox proved that
1/\delta
can be taken to be merely double-exponential in
1/{\poly(\varepsilon)}
. The proof of
Lemma

6.1
with such a bound is quite short: it follows from an application of the cylinder regularity lemma and Ramsey’s theorem (although see
[
68
, Lemma 6.1.4]
for a direct energy-increment proof).

One of the many immediate consequences of
Lemma

6.1
is a famous result of Rödl
[
49
]
, which we now discuss.
Recall that a graph
G
is said to be
induced-
F
-free
if it does not have an induced subgraph isomorphic to
F
. Rödl proved that if
G
is induced-
F
-free, it has a linear-sized vertex subset whose edge density is close to
0
or
1
. More precisely, there is a set
U\subseteq V(G)
with
d_{G}(U)\in[0,\varepsilon]\cup[1-\varepsilon,1]
, where
\lvert U\rvert\geqslant\delta\lvert V(G)\rvert
and
\delta>0
depends only on
F
and
\varepsilon
. Rödl’s theorem turns out to be closely connected to other fundamental questions in graph theory such as the Erdős–Hajnal conjecture, see e.g.
[
10
]
for details.

There are a number of ways known for proving Rödl’s theorem, but one of the simplest is as a direct consequence of
Lemma

6.1
: by the induced counting lemma, a quasirandom set in an induced-
F
-free cannot have edge density bounded away from
0
or
1
. See e.g.
[
68
, Theorem 6.3.3]
for a detailed proof via this method.

As Rödl himself observed
[
49
]
, the obvious extension of this statement to hypergraphs is false. Indeed, the well-known
random tournament
construction—the
3
-graph whose hyperedges are the cyclic triangles in a random tournament—is induced-
K_{4}^{(3)}
-free, but all of its linear-sized subsets have edge density
\frac{1}{4}+o(1)
. In fact, the largest subsets of this
3
-graph with density close to
0
or
1
have logarithmic order, and a result of Amir, Shapira, and Tyomkyn
[
5
]
shows that this is the smallest possible in an induced-
F
-free
3
-graph.

The same random tournament construction is one of the classical examples demonstrating the difficulty in defining a successful notion of hypergraph regularity. Indeed, the same construction demonstrates that there is no direct hypergraph analogue of
Lemma

6.1
, in the sense that every linear-sized vertex subset of this random tournament hypergraph spans a
3
-graph which is not quasirandom. As such, one can learn the lessons of hypergraph regularity, and decide that in order to sensibly extend Rödl’s theorem to hypergraphs, one should not only restrict the vertex set of
\mathcal{H}
, but also the set of pairs. Indeed, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
F
be a
3
-graph, let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\psi:(0,1)\to(0,1)
be a polynomial function. There exists
\delta>0
such that the following holds. If
\mathcal{H}
is an induced-
F
-free
3
-graph, then there is a subset
U\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
and a graph
G
with vertex set
U
such that
\lvert U\rvert\geqslant\delta\lvert V(\mathcal{H})\rvert
,
e(G)\geqslant\delta\lvert U\rvert^{2}
,
G
is
\psi(\delta)
-quasirandom, and

d(\mathcal{H}[U,G]\mid G)\in[0,\varepsilon]\cup[1-\varepsilon,1].

Moreover, we may take
1/\delta\leqslant\twr(2^{2^{\poly(1/\varepsilon)}})
.

Note that it is natural to insist that
G
is highly quasirandom, as otherwise the result may be vacuously true (e.g. if
G
is a complete bipartite graph, which has positive density but no triangles). Moreover, this can be viewed as a direct extension of the condition in Rödl’s theorem, since every set of vertices is “highly quasirandom in uniformity
1
”.

We prove
Theorem

6.2
via the same proof technique discussed above, and as such, we begin by proving an analogue of
Lemma

6.1
for
3
-graphs.
Given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, a vertex subset
U\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
, and a graph
G
with vertex set
U
, let
\mathcal{H}[U,G]
denote the subhypergraph of
\mathcal{H}
obtained by only keeping the vertices in
U
, and only keeping the hyperedges supported on
\Delta(G)
. Let us say that
\mathcal{H}[U,G]
is
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom if the following tripartite chain is
(\eta,\psi)

\lvert E(\mathcal{H})\cap\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\lvert E(\mathcal{H})\cap\Delta(Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert,

as both sides count the number of
t
-tuples in
Y_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}Y_{t}
in which the first three vertices span a triangle in
Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23}
and form an edge of
\mathcal{H}
.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert E(\mathcal{H})\cap\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert}{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert E(\mathcal{H})\cap\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert E(\mathcal{H})\cap\Delta(Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert\Delta(Z^{r}_{12}\cup Z^{r}_{13}\cup Z^{r}_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r}).

Again by Cauchy–Schwarz, we conclude that

d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})^{2}\leqslant\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert\Delta(Z^{r}_{12}\cup Z^{r}_{13}\cup Z^{r}_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r})^{2}.

Plugging this in to the definition of
q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(K(Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3})))
, we find that

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(K(Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3})))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z_{12}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{1}\times Y_{2})\\
Z_{13}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{1}\times Y_{3})\\
Z_{23}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{2}\times Y_{3})\end{subarray}}\frac{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})\rvert}{\lvert Y_{1}\rvert\lvert Y_{2}\rvert\lvert Y_{3}\rvert}d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}\cup Z_{13}\cup Z_{23})^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z_{12}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{1}\times Y_{2})\\
Z_{13}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{1}\times Y_{3})\\
Z_{23}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{2}\times Y_{3})\end{subarray}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert\Delta(Z^{r}_{12}\cup Z^{r}_{13}\cup Z^{r}_{23})\rvert\cdot\prod_{\ell=4}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z_{12}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{1}\times Y_{2})\\
Z_{13}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{1}\times Y_{3})\\
Z_{23}\in\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y_{2}\times Y_{3})\end{subarray}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}\right)\frac{\lvert\Delta(Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r})\rvert}{\lvert Y_{1}^{r}\rvert\lvert Y_{2}^{r}\rvert\lvert Y_{3}^{r}\rvert}d(\mathcal{H}\mid Z_{12}^{r}\cup Z_{13}^{r}\cup Z_{23}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widehat{Z_{12}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(Y_{1}^{r}\times Y_{2}^{r})\\
\widehat{Z_{13}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(Y_{1}^{r}\times Y_{3}^{r})\\
\widehat{Z_{23}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(Y_{2}^{r}\times Y_{3}^{r})\end{subarray}}\frac{\lvert\Delta(\widehat{Z_{12}}\cup\widehat{Z_{13}}\cup\widehat{Z_{23}})\rvert}{\lvert Y_{1}^{r}\rvert\lvert Y_{2}^{r}\rvert\lvert Y_{3}^{r}\rvert}d(\mathcal{H}\mid\widehat{Z_{12}}\cup\widehat{Z_{13}}\cup\widehat{Z_{23}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}\right)q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(K(Y_{1}^{r},Y_{2}^{r},Y_{3}^{r}))),

where in the penultimate equality we use the definition of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}
, namely that the set of edge parts appearing in it is precisely the set of restrictions of edge parts of
\mathcal{P}_{E}
.

By the same argument, the above inequality holds for all indices
1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t
. Summing up over all choices for
i,j,k
, we conclude that for any
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}
, we have

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(K(Y_{i},Y_{j},Y_{k})))\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}\right)q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(K(Y_{i}^{r},Y_{j}^{r},Y_{k}^{r}))).

Therefore, by the definition of
q(\mathcal{P})
, we have

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert X_{\ell}\rvert}\right)q(\mathcal{P}_{E}(Y))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert X_{\ell}\rvert}\right)\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert Y_{\ell}\rvert}\right)q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(K(Y_{i}^{r},Y_{j}^{r},Y_{k}^{r})))

\displaystyle=\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}_{V}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{r}\rvert}{\lvert X_{\ell}\rvert}\right)\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(K(Y_{i}^{r},Y_{j}^{r},Y_{k}^{r})))

\displaystyle=\sum_{Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{V}^{\prime}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{\lvert Y_{\ell}^{\prime}\rvert}{\lvert X_{\ell}\rvert}\right)\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{E}}(K(Y_{i}^{\prime},Y_{j}^{\prime},Y_{k}^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}).

As we have already proved that
q(\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\leqslant q(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, this concludes the proof of
3.2(d)
.
∎

A.2
Proof of Lemma
4.1

We begin by recalling the statement of
[
35
, Corollary 5.3]
, specialized to the case of counting cliques in
3
-uniform hypergraphs.

Theorem A.1

(Special case of
[
35
, Corollary 5.3]
)
.

Let
Z
be a
t
-partite graph on parts
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
, and let
\mathcal{H}
be an
t
-partite
3
-graph supported on
\Delta(Z)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{100}]
, and define
\eta=\varepsilon^{8}\gamma^{10t^{3}}
and
\psi(x)=\varepsilon^{10}\gamma^{10t^{3}}x^{16t^{2}}
. Suppose that

d(\mathcal{H}[X;Z;\{i,j,k\}])\geqslant\gamma

and that
\mathcal{H}[X;Z;\{i,j,k\}]
is
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom for all
\{i,j,k\}\in\binom{[t]}{3}
.

If
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\in X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
are picked uniformly at random, then the probability that they span a
K_{t}^{(3)}
in
\mathcal{H}
—that is, the probability that
x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}\in E(\mathcal{H})
for all
\{i,j,k\}\in\binom{[t]}{3}
—is at least

(1-\varepsilon)\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant t}d(Z_{ij})\right)\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}d(\mathcal{H}[X;Z;\{i,j,k\}])\right).

As promised,
Lemma

4.1
follows as a straightforward corollary of
Theorem

A.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

Let the three vertex parts of
\mathcal{V}
be
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
, and let the vertices of
\mathcal{V}
be
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}
. Let
\iota:\{v_{1},\dots,v_{t}\}\to\{1,2,3\}
be the “index” function that associates to each
v_{i}
the unique index
\iota(i)
such that
v_{i}\in V_{\iota(i)}
.

We construct a
t
-partite graph
Z
and a
t
-partite
3
-graph
\mathcal{H}
as follows. For every
v_{i}
, we construct a set
X_{i}
, which is a copy of
U_{\iota(i)}
. Thus, the parts
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
consist of
\lvert V_{1}\rvert
copies of
U_{1}
,
\lvert V_{2}\rvert
copies of
U_{2}
, and
\lvert V_{3}\rvert
copies of
U_{3}
.

Next, we define the
\binom{t}{2}
bipartite graphs making up
Z
as follows. If
\iota(v_{i})\neq\iota(v_{j})
, then we define
Z_{ij}
to be a copy of
G[U_{\iota(i)}\times U_{\iota(j)}]
between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
. If
\iota(i)=\iota(j)
, we define
Z_{ij}
to be the complete bipartite graph between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
. Note that in this way, we ensure that each
Z_{ij}
is
\psi(\delta)
-quasirandom, and that each tripartite subgraph satisfies
\delta(Z_{ij}\cup Z_{ik}\cup Z_{jk})\geqslant\delta
.

Finally, we define each of the tripartite
3
-graphs making up
\mathcal{H}
. For indices
i,j,k\in\binom{[t]}{3}
, in case
\lvert\iota(i),\iota(j),\iota(k)\rvert\leqslant 2
, we include in
\mathcal{H}
all triangles in the tripartite graph
Z_{ij}\cup Z_{ik}\cup Z_{jk}
. In the remaining case, where
\iota(i),\iota(j),\iota(k)
are all distinct, we do the following. If
\{v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E(\mathcal{V})
, we put a copy of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
between
X_{i},X_{j},X_{k}
. However, if
\{v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}\}\notin E(\mathcal{V})
, we put between these three parts the
relative complement
of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
; that is, we include as a hyperedge every triangle in
Z_{ij}\cup Z_{ik}\cup Z_{jk}
which is not a hyperedge of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

We now claim that
\mathcal{H}[X;Z;\{i,j,k\}]
is
(\eta,\psi)
-quasirandom with
d(\mathcal{H}[X;Z;\{i,j,k\}])\geqslant\gamma
for all
i,j,k
. Indeed, for triples
i,j,k
with
\lvert\iota(i),\iota(j),\iota(k)\rvert\leqslant 2
, this is immediate since all triangles in
Z_{ij}\cup Z_{ik}\cup Z_{jk}
are present. For the remaining triples, this follows from the fact that we assumed
\gamma\leqslant d(\mathcal{H}_{0}[U;G])\leqslant 1-\gamma
, and that a tripartite hypergraph is
\eta
-quasirandom with respect to a tripartite graph if and only if its relative complement is
\eta
-quasirandom with respect to the same tripartite graph.

Now suppose that we pick
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})\in X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
uniformly at random. By
Theorem

A.1
, applied with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{100}
, the probability that they form a
K_{t}^{(3)}
in
\mathcal{H}
is at least

(1-\varepsilon)\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant t}d(Z_{ij})\right)\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant t}d(\mathcal{H}[X;Z;\{i,j,k\}])\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\binom{t}{2}}\gamma^{\binom{t}{3}}.

Additionally, for a fixed
i,j
, the probability that
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
correspond to the same vertex of
V(\mathcal{H}_{0})
is at most
1/{\min\{\lvert U_{1}\rvert,\lvert U_{2}\rvert,\lvert U_{3}\rvert\}}
, which is at most
\delta^{t^{2}}\gamma^{t^{3}}
by assumption. Hence, the probability that there is any collision among
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
is at most
t^{2}\delta^{t^{2}}\gamma^{t^{3}}
. Therefore, the probability that
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
correspond to distinct vertices in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and span a
K_{t}^{(3)}
in
\mathcal{H}
is at least

\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\binom{t}{2}}\gamma^{\binom{t}{3}}-t^{2}\delta^{t^{2}}\gamma^{t^{3}}>0.

We conclude that there is a choice of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})\in X_{1}\mathbin{\times\dotsb\times}X_{t}
satisfying both of these properties.

The final observation is that, by our construction of
\mathcal{H}
, these vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
correspond to an induced copy of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Indeed, since both
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}
are tripartite, all we need to check is that if
x_{i},x_{j},x_{k}
lie in distinct parts of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, then they form a hyperedge of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
if and only if
v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}
form a hyperedge of
\mathcal{V}
. But this is immediate from our choice of
\mathcal{H}
, since we included in
\mathcal{H}
all edges of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
when
\{v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E(\mathcal{V})
, and only non-edges of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
when
\{v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}\}\notin E(\mathcal{V})
. This concludes the proof.
∎
