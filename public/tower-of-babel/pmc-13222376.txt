Nanoarchitectonics of nitrogen/selenium functionalized Co-ZIF-9(III) nanorod/nanosheet for nanomolar-level detection of nitrofurantoin antibiotics in environmental and biomedical samples
Authors: Harshitha B. Tyagaraj, Sanjay Ballur Prasanna, Gagankumar Sakleshpur Kumar, Ali Mohammadi, Swapnil R. Patil, Shalmali R. Burse, Amal Al Ghaferi, Ebrahim Al Hajri, Suk Soon Choi, Nilesh R. Chodankar, Yun Suk Huh, Young-Kyu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing and developing innovative, cost-effective nanomaterials with outstanding activity and durability remains a significant challenge for next-generation electrochemical sensors. Herein, we developed an energy-efficient approach to synthesize N/Se-functionalized Co-ZIF-9(III) (CZ@N/Se) nanohybrids with a unique nanorod/nanosheet morphology, which serve as a high-performance platform for ultrasensitive nitrofurantoin (NFT) sensing. The unique nanorod/nanosheet morphology of N/Se-doped CZ provides an excellent platform for NFT sensing by enhancing electron-transfer kinetics and generating a high density of active sites through the synergistic interaction between the Co-framework and N/Se dopants. These features facilitate efficient NFT adsorption and catalytic reduction, thereby markedly improving the electrochemical sensitivity and selectivity. With optimized mass loading, the CZ@N/Se nanohybrid exhibits exceptional repeatability and reproducibility in NFT detection, achieving a low detection limit of 1.03 nM and a high sensitivity of 3.783 µA µM−1 cm−2 as confirmed by multiple electrochemical measurements. Selectivity tests against potential interferents confirmed the sensor's excellent specificity, with negligible interference observed. Besides, the analysis of spiked environmental and biological samples demonstrated accurate detection, validating the sensor reliability and practical applicability for NFT determination in real-world settings.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40580-026-00553-1.

Introduction

The ever-increasing use of antibiotics (ABs) in medicinal and agricultural activities, along with their improper disposal, has created an adverse effect on human health over the past decade [1]. Even minor trace-level contamination of these ABs will affect the animal food chain and the aquatic ecosystem [2–4]. Nevertheless, ABs remain widely used as therapeutic agents in humans and livestock to control diseases and enhance food production [5, 6]. Among them, nitrofurantoin (NFT), 1-((5-nitro-2-furfurylidene)-1-amino) hydantoin, is a commonly used nitrofuran antibacterial agent with broad-spectrum activity, employed as a therapeutic agent and food additive in livestock and aquatic products [7, 8]. NFT is the most frequently used drug for the prevention and treatment of urinary tract infections (UTIs) caused by Enterococcus spp., Citrobacter spp., Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella spp. [9, 10]. Moreover, NFT exhibits various biological activities, including skin sensitization, amebicide, fungicidal, and anti-inflammatory activities [11, 12]. For adults, the recommended oral dose is 50 to 200 mg per day, with a maximum treatment period of 1 week [13]. But it has been found that the over ingestion of NFTs primes several disorders like abdominal pain, flatulence, headache, drowsiness, depression, nausea, pulmonary toxicity, vomiting, neuropathy, dizziness, numbness, diarrhea, and hepatotoxicity [14]. Recent studies have reported that NFT induces oxidative stress in the liver and hepatocytes of rats. NFT is commonly used to treat UTIs in pregnant and breastfeeding women; however, its use should be avoided in late pregnancy due to the risk of red blood cell damage in newborn babies [15–17].

Most developed countries (including the United States of America, China, and several European countries) have banned nitrofuran-based ABs. Nevertheless, in many developing countries, these drugs continue to be used illegally in livestock through animal feed and drinking water because of their low cost and strong growth-promoting effects. Unfortunately, the adsorbed NFTs are rapidly released into the environment via animal body fluids, resulting in their presence in animal by-products, agricultural soils, and various water systems. As a result, NFTs have significantly impacted health concerns, environmental pollution, and food safety [18]. NFT detection has been carried out using a range of analytical methods, such as liquid chromatography, gas chromatography, mass spectrometry, electrochemical sensors, and surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy [19, 20]. In particular, electrochemical sensors offer significant advantages, including high sensitivity, lower cost, rapid detection, and portability, compared with alternative methods [21]. Although electrochemical sensors offer many advantages over other detection systems, their major limitation lies in the sensing material, as the overall performance is highly dependent on the properties of the nanomaterials used. Therefore, developing highly sensitive and efficient electrode materials remains a critical challenge for the accurate detection of NFT in environmental and biological samples.

Recently, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), also known as coordination polymers or coordination networks, have emerged as promising candidates for developing advanced sensors [22]. Pristine MOFs are particularly advantageous for electrochemical sensing owing to their high specific surface area (SSA), tunable pore sizes, open metal sites, and well-ordered crystalline structures [23, 24]. These characteristics enhance redox activity and facilitate the adsorption and reaction of target molecules, thereby improving overall sensing performance. MOFs are constructed by coordinating inorganic metal ions with organic ligand linkers, forming extended two- or three-dimensional networks. The metal sites in MOFs can serve as active sites, facilitating specific chemical interactions with the target analyte and thereby enhancing selectivity [25–30]. Among the various MOFs developed, zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), a subclass of MOFs, consist of tetrahedral metal centers (e.g., Zn, Co, or Cu) interconnected by imidazolate linkers. This arrangement forms porous structures composed of four, six, eight, or twelve tetrahedral clusters of ZnN4, CoN4, or CuN4. The specific coordination of these components controls the composition and properties of ZIFs [31–33]. In addition, ZIFs display exceptional thermal and chemical stability, substantially high SSA, and tunable porosity. These features position ZIF as an excellent candidate for electrochemical sensing applications. ZIF-8, ZIF-9, and ZIF-10 demonstrate superior chemical and thermal stability compared to other ZIFs [34]. ZIF-9 is known to exists in three related phases: ZIF-9(I) [Co(bIm)2, cubic system] and ZIF-9(II) [Co9(bIm)18, triclinic system], both of which obtained by heating ZIF-9(I), and ZIF-9(III), a permanently nonporous phase formed through slurry aging of ZIF-9(II) in water at room temperature for one week. Unlike ZIF-9(I) and ZIF-9(II), ZIF-9(III) exhibits higher structural stability in aqueous environments, making it more suitable for electrochemical applications [35–39]. ZIF-9(III) possesses hexagonal symmetry and a sodalite topology, consisting of corner-sharing tetrahedral CoN4 units. The coordination bonds formed with Co2+ cations and benzimidazole anions are recognized as some of the most stable for N-donor ligands [40, 41]. Despite the promising applications of MOFs and ZIF-derived materials, many reported sensors face challenges, including limited electrical conductivity and insufficient catalytic activity. Incorporating functional elements, such as nitrogen/selenium, into Co-ZIF-9(III) (CZ)-derived frameworks can enhance electron transfer, improve redox activity, and increase the density of catalytic sites through synergistic interactions with transition metals. Thus, this work presents an N/Se-engineered CZ (CZ@N/Se) nanohybrid with enhanced conductivity and electrocatalytic performance, providing a novel method for electrochemical detection of NFT.

Given the importance of rapid NFT detection for human safety, we have developed an innovative, highly sensitive electrochemical sensor based on single-metal zeolitic imidazolate framework-derived CZ@N/Se nanorod/nanosheet nanohybrids. The optimized CZ@N/Se nanohybrids exhibit a high SSA, enhanced chemical reactivity, and excellent stability, making them highly effective for sensing applications. When applied to NFT detection, the sensor demonstrates outstanding performance with a wide linear range (0.05–914.8 μM), an ultra-low detection limit (LOD) of 1.03 nM, high sensitivity (3.783 µA µM−1 cm−2), excellent selectivity, appreciable reproducibility, reusability, and long-term stability. Notably, this sensor was successfully applied to detect NFT in diverse real-world samples, including river water, pond water, drinking water, tap water, wastewater, and human saliva, serum, and urine, highlighting its strong potential for both environmental monitoring and clinical diagnostics.

Results and discussion

Structural and morphological analysis

The synthesis process for the CZ@N/Se nanohybrid, as illustrated in Fig. 1a, involves two main steps. Firstly, Co(NO3)2·6H2O, NaHCO3, and benzimidazole were mixed in 2 mL of ethanol to form a slurry, which was then ground in a mortar and pestle for 10 min, yielding a thick, purple paste. The resulting paste was centrifuged, washed three times with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water, and dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 60 °C to obtain CZ. Secondly, the obtained precursor CZ is mixed with Se powder at a 1:1 ratio and heat-treated for 1 h at different temperatures under an N2 atmosphere to synthesize CZ@N/Se, in which carbonization and selenization occur simultaneously. During this process, carbonization of the organic ligand generates a nitrogen-doped carbon skeleton that preserves the original framework porous architecture, while selenization introduces Se species that enhance the material electrical conductivity and provide additional electrocatalytically active sites [42]. The synergistic effect of these processes yields the CZ@N/Se nanohybrid, which exhibits a high SSA, improved electronic conductivity, and abundant electrochemically active sites derived from both Co and Se, making it highly suitable for advanced electrochemical sensing applications. The schematic in Fig. 1b illustrates the electrochemical reduction mechanism of NFT at the CZ@N/Se nanohybrid-modified glassy carbon electrode (GCE). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements indicate that CZ@N/Se800 produces the highest cathodic current, demonstrating its superior electrocatalytic performance. To enhance the overall electrochemical sensing performance, selenization was performed at temperatures ranging from 500 to 900 °C. The first step was to optimize the selenization temperature by conducting morphological and structural analyses. The surface morphology and microstructural properties of the CZ and CZ@N/Se nanohybrid selenized at different temperatures were analyzed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). As shown in Fig. S1a, the pristine CZ exhibited a rhombic dodecahedral form with clearly defined polyhedral crystallites, indicating the effective formation of a consistent ZIFs. CZ@N/Se500 exhibits partial collapse of the polyhedral structure and the formation of irregular agglomerates (Fig. S1b), indicating incomplete Se incorporation and framework deformation. As the temperature increased to 600 °C (Fig. S1c) and 700 °C (Fig. S1d), the materials exhibited a more porous structure while maintaining their original shape to some extent, indicating improved diffusion and dispersion of Se species within the CZ matrix. However, excessive thermal processing at 900 °C (Fig. S1e) resulted in significant structural degradation and particle agglomeration, accompanied by the formation of elongated, rod-like structures, which are likely to diminish electrochemical performance by reducing the surface area and limiting effective contact between active sites and the electrolyte [43, 44]. In contrast, the CZ@N/Se800 sample (Fig. 1c and d) retains a porous, interconnected structure with uniform particle distribution. The material exhibits highly interconnected features that benefit electron transport and enhance active-site exposure in electrochemical applications [45, 46]. Lower magnification image (Fig. 1e) reveals embedded nanorod-like structures well-anchored on the thin carbon nanosheet, likely due to the formation of cobalt selenium-based nanorods on the carbonaceous sheets, as confirmed by structural analysis. Typically, nanomaterials with higher surface areas are preferred to facilitate rapid, efficient electrochemical interactions. In this context, the sample selenized at 800 °C exhibits a well-defined nanoscale architecture, characterized by nanorods uniformly anchored to nanosheets. Such a hierarchical structure markedly increases the accessible surface area, thereby enhancing electrochemical activity and accelerating charge-transfer kinetics.

Detailed morphological and structural analyses of the prepared samples were further performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and the corresponding results are presented in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2a, the TEM image of the CZ sample reveals a typical planar carbon nanosheet morphology. Upon selenization at 800 °C, the CZ nanostructures become uniformly decorated with nanorod-like features, as evident in Fig. 2b–c, which can be attributed to the formation of CoSe nanorods. The successful formation of CoSe on the carbon nanosheets is further confirmed by the XRD analysis, as discussed in the following section. The darker, rod-shaped structures correspond to CoSe nanocrystals formed during selenization. In contrast, the brighter regions correspond to ultrathin carbon sheets that intimately enclose the nanorods (Fig. 2c). Their relatively uniform distribution indicates effective interaction and strong integration of the Se species within the CZ framework, providing a high SSA and abundant active sites that enhance electrochemical performance. The HRTEM image in Fig. 2d reveals lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.289 nm in the selected region, which can be indexed to the (101) plane of CoSe, respectively, consistent with the XRD results. The absence of lattice fringes in the adjacent area suggests that the material consists of amorphous carbon nanosheets, a common feature of carbon-based hybrid materials [47]. As shown in Fig. 2e, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern confirms the polycrystalline nature of CZ@N/Se800. The diffraction ring corresponding to the (101) plane is attributed to CoSe, while the (002) plane is associated with amorphous carbon, indicating the coexistence of both phases within the nanohybrid structure. The energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) elemental mappings of the CZ@N/Se800 composite are presented in Fig. 2f–l. The EDX pattern clearly shows a uniform distribution of Co, C, N, O, and Se across the nanostructures, demonstrating the incorporation of N and Se into the CZ framework. The atomic composition analysis revealed the presence of Co, C, N, O, and Se elements in approximate percentages of 3.22%, 72.34%, 3.16%, 14.17%, and 7.11%, respectively (Fig. 2f). The synergistic incorporation of N and Se significantly enhances the electrochemical performance of CZ@N/Se800. Se promotes the formation of highly conductive CoSe nanocrystals, thereby facilitating strong interfacial coupling with the carbon matrix, enhancing charge transfer, and improving structural stability. Meanwhile, N modulates the electronic structure of the carbon framework, creating additional active sites and enhancing electrical conductivity. These findings confirm that selenization at 800 °C enables optimal Se incorporation into the CZ framework, yielding a structurally integrated, compositionally uniform nanohybrid material. The crystalline nature and phase structure of the prepared CZ and CZ@N/Se800 nanohybrid were further characterized by XRD. As shown in Fig. 3a, CZ exhibited distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 7.25°, 10.28°, 12.70°, 14.67°, 16.48°, 17.99°, 22.08°, 24.35°, 26.62°, 29.64°, and 30.55°, which are characteristic of benzimidazole-based ZIFs and confirm the successful formation of the CZ phase [48]. The XRD spectra of the CZ@N/Se800 closely match the standard diffraction data of CoSe (JCPDS No. 70–2870), confirming the formation of CoSe within the composite. The diffraction peaks appearing at 2θ values of 33.2°, 45.4°, and 50.9° correspond to the (101), (102), and (110) planes of CoSe, respectively [31, 32]. The XRD patterns of CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, and CZ@N/Se900 are discussed in detail in the supporting information (Fig. S2a). To further investigate functional group variations and structural evolution induced by temperature-assisted Se integration, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed, and the results are presented in the supporting information (Fig. S2b). The FTIR spectrum of CZ exhibits characteristic peaks at 3075.44 and 3027.89 cm−1, corresponding to aromatic − CH and − C − N stretching vibrations, respectively [49]. The absorption peak at 1774.92 cm−1 is assigned to − C = O stretching, while the band at 1603.76 cm−1 corresponds to conjugated C = C stretching. The peak at 3027.89 cm−1 is also indicative of secondary amine − NH stretching [50]. These features confirm the successful formation of CZ. Upon Se incorporation and subsequent selenization at different temperatures, the intensity and sharpness of these characteristic vibrations gradually decrease. For CZ@N/Se500 and CZ@N/Se600, imidazolate-related bands remained visible, indicating partial structural retention. However, at higher temperatures (700–900 °C), the disappearance or weakening of these peaks, particularly the C = N and C–N bands, suggests progressive decomposition of the organic ligand and the formation of a carbonaceous matrix [51]. Additionally, new broad peaks observed at 1000–1200 cm−1 and 1600–1700 cm−1 in CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900 are attributed to C–O and C = O functionalities, indicating partial oxidation. The reduction of imidazolate-related signals, along with the emergence of Se-related features, confirms successful Se incorporation [52]. Raman spectroscopy was further employed to evaluate the graphitization behavior of the carbon components (Fig. S2c). All samples exhibit a distinct peak at 674.3 cm−1, corresponding to the A1g vibrational mode of CoSe [53]. The D band, associated with defective carbon, appears in the range of 1300–1400 cm−1, while the G band, corresponding to graphitic carbon, is observed between 1500 and 1600 cm−1. The intensity ratio of these bands (ID/IG) was used to assess the degree of structural disorder. The calculated ID/IG values for CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900 are 0.98, 0.99, 0.99, 1.03, and 1.00, respectively, indicating an increase in defect density following selenization. Furthermore, the broadening of the D and G band peaks suggests increased structural disorder within the graphite framework [54]. Among all samples, CZ@N/Se800 exhibits the broadest D and G bands, indicating the highest degree of graphitic disorder. Figure 3b demonstrates the N2 absorption/desorption isotherms of the prepared CZ and CZ@N/Se nanohybrids. All samples display a Type IV isotherm with H4 hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of both mesoporous and microporous structures [55]. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) SSA of CZ, CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900 was measured to be approximately 24.2, 38.6, 108.3, 113.1, 161.6, and 127.5 m2/g, respectively. Among these, CZ@N/Se800 has the highest surface area, indicating a strong potential for enhanced electrochemical sensing performance. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to analyze the elemental composition and chemical states of CZ@N/Se800. The survey spectrum (Fig. S2d) confirms the presence of Co, C, N, O, and Se. The deconvoluted Co 2p spectrum of CZ@N/Se800 (Fig. 3c) reveals two spin–orbit split doublets, Co 2p1/2 and Co 2p3/2, appearing at 796.5 and 780.4 eV, respectively, indicating the presence of Co2+ in CoSe. Two additional satellite peaks are also observed at 803.7 and 785.7 eV (labeled as Sat.) [46]. The peak at 778.3 eV is attributed to Co0, indicating the presence of metallic cobalt in CZ@N/Se800 [56]. The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 3d) is deconvoluted into four components: 284.7 eV, attributed to sp2-hybridized C–C; 285.9 eV, assigned to C–N bonds; 288.7 eV, associated with C = O groups; and 289.9 eV, also corresponding to C = O species. The presence of C–N bonds confirms successful nitrogen incorporation [57]. As displayed in Fig. 3e, the deconvoluted N 1 s spectrum reveals four distinct peaks located at 398.6 eV (pyridinic N), 400.7 eV (pyrrolic N), 403.1 eV (graphitic N), and 404.9 eV (oxidized N). Pyridinic- and pyrrolic-N introduce defect sites that serve as additional active centers, graphitic-N enhances charge transport through electron delocalization, and oxidized-N likely participates in surface redox reactions. The coexistence of these nitrogen species enhances both electrical conductivity and electrochemical activity, thereby improving the electrochemical performance of the composite [58]. The Se 3d spectrum (Fig. 3f) displays Se 3d5/2 and Se 3d3/2 peaks at 54.4 and 55.9 eV, respectively, confirming the presence of CoSe, while an additional peak at 59.1 eV is attributed to Se–O or Se–C species. The O 1 s spectrum (Fig. S3) can be resolved into three characteristic peaks. The peak at approximately 531.1 eV corresponds to metal–oxygen bonds, indicating that oxygen is bound to Co or Se species. The signal at 532.9 eV is assigned to oxygen in defect sites or oxygen-containing functional groups (C = O), while the peak at 536.7 eV is associated with surface-adsorbed oxygen species or hydroxyl groups (–OH) [59, 60]. Electroanalytical studies

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) has been used to examine the interfacial electron transport properties of the synthesized materials. EIS measurements were performed over a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 10 kHz with an applied amplitude of 50 mV in a 5 mM K3/K4[Fe(CN)6] solution containing 0.1 M KCl. The EIS curves of bare GCE, CZ, CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900, as shown in Fig. 4a, were analyzed using Nyquist plots. The inset of Fig. 4a presents a magnified view of the spectra, and the impedance data were fitted using the Randles circuit model, which includes the solution resistance (Rs), charge-transfer resistance (Rct), Warburg diffusion process (Zw), and double-layer capacitance (Cdl). The Nyquist plots were used to determine the Rct values of the modified electrodes. The semicircular region at high frequencies corresponds to the electron-transfer resistance at the electrode-analyte interface, whereas the linear region at low frequencies represents diffusion. The Rct values for bare GCE, CZ, CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900 were 143.02, 105.46, 18.67, 18.35, 7.35, 6.06, and 12.79 Ω, respectively. These results indicate that bare GCE exhibits the highest resistance, reflecting poor conductivity and slow electron-transfer kinetics. Compared with CZ, the CZ@N/Se nanohybrids exhibit improved conductivity, attributed to the synergistic interaction between Se and the CZ framework. Se provides high intrinsic electronic conductivity, while the porous CZ structure facilitates efficient electron transport and stable analyte-electrode interactions. In contrast, CZ@N/Se900 (12.79 Ω) showed a larger semicircle and a higher Rct than CZ@N/Se800. This behavior is likely due to structural degradation, reduced surface area, phase transformation, or weakened interfacial interactions resulting from excessive thermal treatment at 900 °C, which ultimately limit electron-transfer kinetics [60, 61]. Figure 4b presents a bar graph comparing the Rct values of the different electrocatalysts. Among them, CZ@N/Se800 exhibits the lowest Rct value, indicating superior electrical conductivity and enhanced electrochemical performance. The electrocatalytic sensing efficiencies of bare GCE, CZ, CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900 were examined using CV at a sweep rate of 50 mV s−1. As presented in Fig. 4c, the bare GCE, CZ, CZ@N/Se500, CZ@N/Se600, CZ@N/Se700, CZ@N/Se800, and CZ@N/Se900 display cathodic peak current (Ipa) values of 44.9, 42.44, 32.76, 40.83, 55.6, and 51.74 µA, respectively. The CZ@N/Se800 electrode exhibited the highest peak current among all samples. This enhanced response can be attributed to its larger electroactive surface area and superior electron-transfer capability, resulting in excellent electrochemical behavior. Figure 4d presents a bar graph comparing the electrocatalyst current response. CZ@N/Se800 exhibited the highest current response due to its enhanced catalytic activity, increased conductivity, and larger surface area. CV was further performed to assess the electrochemical behavior of CZ@N/Se800-modified electrodes towards NFT in 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution (PBS, pH 5) at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. Initially, it was essential to optimize the mass loading of CZ@N/Se800 on the GCE to achieve the highest sensing performance toward NFT detection. For this purpose, varying volumes of CZ@N/Se800 suspension (2–10 μL) were drop-cast onto the GCE surface for systematic evaluation. As shown in Fig. 5a, the maximum reduction current was observed at a loading of 6 µL. Further increases to 8 µL and 10 µL resulted in decreased current responses due to increased film thickness, which hinders electron transfer. Figure 5b presents the corresponding bar graph. From this analysis, 6 µL was selected as the optimal loading for subsequent experiments. The electrochemical performance of modified and unmodified electrodes was investigated using 100 µM NFT at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 in 0.1 M PBS. As presented in Fig. 5c, CZ@N/Se800 exhibits a significantly higher cathodic peak current compared to bare GCE and other modified electrodes. The weak response observed for bare GCE is attributed to slower charge-transfer kinetics at the electrode–electrolyte interface [62–65]. CZ@N/Se800 shows the highest peak current (− 55.6 μA), indicating an irreversible reduction process. Figure 5d presents the bar graph comparing peak currents for various modified electrodes. Selectivity is a crucial factor for detecting a wide range of harmful pollutants, biological molecules, and pharmaceutical compounds. Therefore, this study evaluates the anti-interference capabilities of NFT against different interfering metal ions and substances. The selectivity of NFT for CZ@N/Se800 was tested using DPV in the presence of various interferences, as shown in Fig. 7c. In this experiment, 100 μM NFT was combined with a fivefold excess of various interfering substances, including 4 − Nitrophenol, Nifedipine, Fluoroquinolone, Furazolidone, Hydrogen peroxide, Melamine, Dopamine, Ascorbic acid, and Indomethacin, at a pH of 5.0 with a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 (Fig. S4a). The NFT signal remained unaffected by these interferents, with a calculated relative error of 1.89% (Fig. S4b). The DPV peak potential for NFT was stable and was not influenced by the presence of interfering compounds; no additional peaks appeared within the applied potential range. These findings confirm that NFT exhibited negligible cross-reactivity with the tested interferents, highlighting the excellent selectivity of the CZ@N/Se800 sensor for real-time NFT detection. The resistance of the modified electrode to surface contamination was evaluated to assess long-term stability. The accumulation of oxidizing species on the electrode surface is a major factor that reduces sensor sensitivity [71–74]. Therefore, the stability of CZ@N/Se800 was examined using CV, as shown in Fig. 7d. The sensor response was monitored over 13 days at 2-day intervals. The electrode, stored at 4 °C, retained 98.00–98.18% of its initial current response after 13 days, demonstrating excellent storage stability. Reproducibility was assessed using five independently prepared CZ@N/Se800-modified GCEs in 0.1 M PBS (pH 5.0) containing 100 µM NFT. As shown in Fig. 7e, all electrodes produced nearly identical current responses, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 1.93%, indicating high reproducibility. In addition, the reusability of a single CZ@N/Se800 electrode was evaluated across five consecutive measurements (Fig. 7f), yielding an RSD of 1.86%. Overall, the CZ@N/Se800 sensor demonstrates excellent sensitivity, selectivity, stability, reproducibility, and reusability, highlighting its strong potential for practical NFT detection in real samples.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the CZ@N/Se nanohybrids were successfully synthesized via a synergistic approach that combines mechanochemical processing with Se-assisted thermal treatment. Comprehensive structural and morphological characterizations confirmed the formation of well-defined Se nanorods uniformly anchored to CZ nanosheets, thereby establishing a robust, high-surface-area architecture ideal for electrochemical applications. Electrochemical investigations using EIS, CV, and DPV demonstrated the outstanding sensing performance of the CZ@N/Se800 electrode for NFT detection, achieving an ultra-LOD of 1.03 nM and a high sensitivity of 3.783 μA μM−1 cm−2. The sensor also exhibited excellent selectivity, remaining unaffected by common interfering substances, while repeatability (RSD 1.86%) and reproducibility (RSD 1.93%) confirmed its reliability and stability. Additionally, the effective detection of NFT in environmental and biological samples confirms the sensor practical use in real-world settings. The developed electrocatalyst can be incorporated into portable and wearable electrochemical sensors for on-site, real-time NFT detection across various sample types. Furthermore, this sensor has potential applications in clinical diagnostics for early disease detection and ongoing health monitoring, extending its usefulness and influence. Overall, this study introduces a straightforward, durable, and affordable sensing platform capable of highly sensitive NFT detection, with promising prospects for environmental safety and public health monitoring protection.
