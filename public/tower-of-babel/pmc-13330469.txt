The expert learner: using the learning and study strategies inventory (LASSI) to investigate the characteristics of high-performing medical students
Authors: Killian J Bucci, Philip C Brewer, William S Wright, Mohammed K Khalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying the characteristics of high-performing medical students is essential for developing targeted interventions to support underperforming learners. This study examined the relationship between learning and study strategies, as measured by the Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI), and academic performance in medical school.MethodsThe LASSI was administered to 514 medical students (classes of 2019–2023) during the middle of the first year. Pearson product-moment correlation analyses assessed associations between LASSI scale scores and performance in biomedical sciences coursework, the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 1, and Step 2 Clinical Knowledge (CK). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc comparisons were used to evaluate differences in LASSI scores across student performance quartiles.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in Motivation, Self-Testing, Test Strategies, and Time Management scores between the 4th quartile (top) and the 1st quartile (bottom) across all performance measures. High performers demonstrated greater strategic ability, motivation, and proficiency in self-testing, test-taking, and time management. Motivation, Self-Testing, and Test Strategies were the most consistent predictors of academic success.ConclusionsMotivation, Self-Testing, and Test Strategies were the LASSI scales most strongly linked to academic success. These areas represent critical targets for instructional support and can inform the design of targeted interventions to enhance medical student learning and performance.

Introduction

The ‘expert’ learner has been described previously in the literature as someone who is goal-oriented and actively reflects on their own learning process, making thoughtful adjustments to their study regimens as needed [10]. The initial idea of the expert learner was primarily focused on knowledge acquisition in general, with many future studies confirming the links between self-regulation, self-efficacy, and academic performance [2, 10, 27, 32]. Continuous reflection and practice have even been shown extensively to be imperative to long-term retention of skills and information [9]. Within this framework, expert learners demonstrate characteristics associated with high levels of self-regulation, motivation, and metacognitive awareness. Artino et al. [2] emphasize that expert learners actively engage in self-regulated learning processes, including goal setting, strategic planning, self-monitoring, and reflective evaluation of learning outcomes. Similarly, Ertmer and Newby [10] highlight that expert learners possess well-organized domain knowledge, employ adaptive learning strategies, and exhibit a high degree of metacognitive control. In the context of medical education, Stegers-Jager et al. [27] found that expert learners are typically intrinsically motivated, perform at higher academic levels, and effectively navigate self-directed learning environments. West and Sadoski [32] further elaborate that such learners persist in the face of challenges, utilize deep learning strategies, and manage their learning through meaningful encoding and elaboration. collectively, these perspectives converge to present expert learners as individuals who integrate cognitive competence with affective resilience and strategic autonomy, thus enabling superior academic performance and lifelong learning capabilities.

Every individual, or group of individuals, also displays learner characteristics, which are highly variable sets of traits that influence how and what they learn [7, 12]. Learner characteristics can be further subdivided into personal, academic, social/emotional, and cognitive aspects, all of which are essential for designing effective, personalized learning environments [7]. Personal characteristics include demographics such as age, gender, and cultural background, but also other aspects that could impact an individual's learning process, such as a disability [7]. Academics includes an individual's level of education and educational goals [7]. Social/emotional pertains to an individual’s role within a group, or to the group as a whole, which includes aspects ranging from self-image to group structure [7]. Lastly, cognitive characteristics pertain to the learner’s capabilities to retain and understand information, such as attention span, memory, and problem-solving skills [7]. These characteristics are critical to academic instructional design, as they allow for creation of tailored instruction, which improves efficiency, effectiveness, and motivation [7]. Naturally, this raises a question, what learner characteristics are high-performing learner’s displaying in medical school? Although Drachsler & Kirschner’s framework draws from a broad educational literature, its domains (personal, academic, social/emotional, cognitive) align closely with medical education’s competency-based aims. Prior studies in medical student samples, including our group’s prior work correlating LASSI scales with anatomy and Step 1 performance, indicate that these constructs map to measurable differences in medical training and are therefore appropriate for studying high performers in this population [14, 15, 16].

An additional perspective for understanding expert learners can be drawn from the Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI), a standardized assessment tool that evaluates students' awareness and use of learning and study strategies across ten scales. The LASSI is a 10-scale, 60-item survey instrument that assesses students’ awareness and implementation of learning and study strategies [21, 31]. LASSI is designed to collect both cognitive and non-cognitive data for diagnostic purposes and to inform appropriate interventions in improving student’s academic outcomes, evaluation of learning strategies interventions, and predicting of students’ success. The 10 LASSI scales instrument includes three major components of strategic learning: skill, will, and self-regulation. Information Processing, Selecting Main Ideas, and Test Strategies are encompassed in the skill component of strategic learning, which assess cognitive abilities such as critical thinking, comprehension, and examination readiness. The will component of strategic learning includes Attitude, Motivation, and Anxiety reflecting social/emotional characteristics, including self-efficacy, resilience, and emotional control. The self-regulation component encompasses Concentration, Time Management, Self-Testing, and Using Academic Resources, which assess behavioral and metacognitive characteristics, such as self-regulation and strategic learning. Together, these ten scales offer a comprehensive framework for assessing the learning characteristics of medical students and informing strategies to support their academic development.

The ten Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI) scales have been shown to correlate with academic performance among medical students [14, 15, 32, 33]. Several studies support the association between LASSI scale scores and academic outcomes [23–25, 32]. Significant differences in the Anxiety, Motivation, and Test Strategies scales were observed between high- and low-performing medical students on both pre-clerkship assessments and United States Medical Licensing Examination® (USMLE) Step 1 [16]. Significant higher scores on the Anxiety, Attitude, Motivation, Concentration, Selection Main Ideas, and Test Strategies scales have also been documented among students with higher GPAs compared to those with lower GPAs [24]. Additionally, the Anxiety, Motivation, and Test strategies scales were correlated with performance in anatomical sciences, biomedical sciences, and USMLE Step 1 [15]. However, previous literature has not outlined the specific 10 LASSI scales and learner characteristics that are associated with high-performing medical students in pre-clerkship, USMLE Step 1, and USMLE Step 2 CK performance.

Continued investigation into these relationships across all four years of medical education may aid in the early recognition of low-performing students and support the development of targeted interventions to enhance learning and study strategies throughout the medical curriculum.

The primary aim of this study is to utilize the ten scales from the Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI) to identify characteristics associated with high-performing medical students. Specifically, the study explores correlations between each LASSI scale and performance in all preclinical coursework, as well as in USMLE Step 1 and Step 2 Clinical Knowledge (CK) examinations, comparing outcomes between low and high performing medical students. The findings aim to guide the development of targeted support programs and interventions tailored to the needs of underperforming students, emphasizing the importance of early identification and personalized academic support to enhance educational success and retention in medical training.

Methods

Participants

This is a cross-sectional study with a sample that included 514 medical students from the 2019–2023 graduating classes at the University of South Carolina School of Medicine Greenville, encompassing both pre-clerkship and clerkship phases. The Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI) was administered midway through the first year of medical school as part of the Academic Success Program’s diagnostic and prescriptive framework. Outcome measures, including the pre-clerkship weighted average, USMLE Step 1, and USMLE Step 2 CK scores, were obtained from institutional records in subsequent years. The sample size for each analysis varied based on data availability and is specified in the Results (e.g., pre-clerkship average: N = 491; Step 1: N = 484; Step 2 CK: N = 376). Participants were 56.6% female, 42.2% males, and 1.2% unspecified. Participants' average undergraduate Grade Point Average (GPA) was 3.70 on a four-point scale, and their overall Medical College Admission Test (MCAT) percentile was 70.2%. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the University of South Carolina (Ref. #: Pro00111001).

Educational context

The curriculum at the University of South Carolina School of Medicine Greenville (USCSOMG) follows an integrated model that combines basic and clinical sciences throughout the pre-clerkship years. The first year (M1) begins with Emergency Medical Technician (EMT) training, followed by foundational science modules: Molecular and Cellular Foundations of Medicine, Structure and Function of the Human Body I and II, Neuroscience, and Defenses and Responses. These modules cover core content in anatomical sciences, physiology, and biochemistry.

Concurrently, students participate in the Integrated Practice of Medicine (IPM) module, which emphasizes the development of clinical reasoning and physical examination skills. The second year (M2) builds on this foundation, with modules centered on the mechanisms and management of disease. These include Biomedical Principles of Disease and Therapy, Hematology/Oncology, Mind, Brain, and Behavior, Cardiovascular/Pulmonary/Renal, Gastrointestinal/Hepatic, Endocrine and Reproductive, and Musculoskeletal/Dermatology/Rheumatology. The IPM module continues throughout the M2 year in parallel with these courses.

The United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 1 is administered upon completion of the pre-clerkship curriculum. As the IPM module does not include assessments of basic science content, scores from this component are excluded from the current analysis.

The third (M3) and fourth (M4) years of the curriculum at USCSOMG focus primarily on integrating the knowledge and skills acquired during the previous two years and applying them in clinical clerkships of various specialties. The curriculum includes core clerkship rotations in General Surgery, Internal Medicine, Emergency Medicine, Psychiatry, Neurology, Pediatrics, Family Medicine, Obstetrics and Gynecology, and elective rotations in specialties of interest including, but not limited to, Cardiology, Gastroenterology, and Radiology. All core clerkship rotations, except Emergency Medicine, are followed by National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME) subject examinations in the respective discipline. M3 and M4 performance was conveyed by performance on the USMLE Step 2 CK examination only, which is taken during the M4 year upon completion of all core clerkships.

The LASSI has demonstrated strong psychometric properties. The manual reports internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) for the scales in the acceptable to excellent range, with median values between approximately 0.76 and 0.87 across diverse student samples [31], [1]. In the present study, we calculated Cronbach’s α for each subscale within our cohort,these values are reported in the Results section.

Data collection

The Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI) was administered in January of the first year (M1) to 514 medical students across five cohorts (graduating classes of 2019—2023).

To create a comprehensive pre-clerkship performance metric, we calculated a weighted average of module grades from the M1 and M2 years. This method accounts for variations in module length by weighting each module's grade according to its credit hours. The credit hours of a module are based on the duration of the module in weeks. Specifically, for each student, we multiplied each module score by its credit hours, summed these products across all modules, and then divided by the total number of credit hours. This yields a single weighted average in which longer modules contribute proportionally more to the final score than shorter ones.

Additionally, all cohorts included in the analysis (graduating classes 2019–2023) completed the three-digit (numerical) USMLE Step 1; Step 1 scores reported here reflect that scored era. This overall pre-clerkship metric, along with USMLE Step 1 and Step 2 CK scores, was compiled for analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using International Business Machines (IBM) Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was performed in Microsoft Excel 2016 using previously published methods [3].

The LASSI subscale scores, expressed as percentages, were treated as continuous data for analysis. This approach is supported by the composite nature of the scores, their approximately normal distributions, and the large sample size, which satisfies the Central Limit Theorem and ensures the robustness of parametric tests. Furthermore, the analyses required continuous input for meaningful interpretation of correlation coefficients and group mean differences.

To measure the strength of association between the LASSI 10-scale scores and performance on overall biomedical sciences, USMLE Step 1, and USMLE Step 2 CK, Pearson product-moment correlation analyses were performed. For the correlation analyses, interpretation emphasized the magnitude of associations in addition to statistical significance. The coefficient of determination (r2) was used to quantify the proportion of variance in academic performance explained by each LASSI scale. Because the primary objective of these analyses was to compare the relative strength and consistency of predictors across outcomes rather than to evaluate isolated null hypotheses, interpretation prioritized effect size magnitude and ranking of explanatory contribution.

Differences in LASSI scale scores among students grouped into performance quartiles (based on pre-clerkship weighted average, USMLE Step 1, and USMLE Step 2 CK scores) were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. When Levene’s test was not significant, a standard one-way ANOVA was conducted; when Levene’s test was significant, Welch’s ANOVA was used to account for unequal variances.

Within each outcome, the ten LASSI scale-level omnibus tests were treated as a single inferential family (m = 10), and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate procedure was applied to the omnibus p-values to control for multiple comparisons across scales. For scales that remained significant after BH adjustment, post hoc pairwise comparisons among quartiles were conducted (Bonferroni-adjusted for standard ANOVA and Games–Howell for Welch’s ANOVA). Because there are six possible pairwise contrasts among four quartiles (m = 6), the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was additionally applied to the set of pairwise comparisons within each significant scale to control for multiple testing at the contrast level.

A Cronbach’s α for each subscale within our cohort was performed for internal consistency. Because all cohorts in this dataset took Step 1 prior to the transition to pass/fail scoring, all Step 1 scores analyzed here were numeric and comparable across all included students.

To further assess the predictive value of the LASSI scales, R-squared values were calculated to determine the proportion of variance in academic performance explained by each scale. Discussion

The results of this study indicate that high-performing medical students' scores are significantly higher in certain LASSI scales across all performance measures (pre-clerkship courses, USMLE Step 1, USMLE Step 2). These LASSI scales include Motivation, Self-testing, Test Strategies, and Time Management (Table 7). Additionally, these scales had the highest percentages when attempting to explain the difference in scores among 1 st and 4th quartile students. These results align with Ertmer and Newby’s [10] framework of expert learners, who are described as possessing strong self-awareness, enhanced metacognitive knowledge, and effective self-regulation strategies. Together, these attributes exemplify the characteristics of expert learners, suggesting that high-performing medical students are not only knowledgeable but also strategic and self-directed in their learning behaviors. The LASSI scales of Motivation, Self-testing, Test Strategies, and Time Management have been consistently reported in the literature as being important for academic success [1, 5, 6, 17–19, 26, 28]. Among these, Motivation has been repeatedly linked to performance in medical school, particularly in relation to students’ studying habits [1, 17, 26]. Both Albaili [1] and Kusurkar, et al. [17] found that students with higher levels of motivation place more effort into studying, which consequently leads to improved academic performance. Sobral [26] further supported these findings, demonstrating that self-motivation is closely associated with the self-regulation characteristics of high performing medical students. These prior examples are in accord with our study’s findings that motivation is a key characteristic of high academic performers in medical education. Furthermore, the study by Kusurkar et al. [17] and Sobral [26] provide insight into how motivation influences score variability by highlighting its connection to LASSI scales categorized under self-regulation. Specifically, students with high motivation tend to recognize the value of studying and exhibit self-regulatory behaviors such as strategic learning, time management, and focused attention. As a result, they are more likely to engage in self-testing, effectively manage their time, and utilize academic resources. Given this relationship, discussions of the self-regulation component of strategic learning should always consider the role of student motivation.

Similar to Motivation, our findings underscore the significance role of the Self-testing scale. Previous studies have shown that Self-testing, particularly through practice examination, is associated with higher examination scores in higher education settings [15, 16, 18]. One possible explanation for this association is that practice examinations allow student to simulate the testing environment, thereby enhancing familiarity with the examination format and reducing uncertainty [18, 28]. Practice examinations may also help explain our findings that show a steep decrease in the role Anxiety plays in predicting performance on Step 2 CK. Third year medical students are able to practice for Step 2 CK throughout the entire year with clinical exposure which may result in lower test anxiety. Furthermore, practice examinations facilitate retrieval practice, a process in which actively recalling information enhances future recall. Research by Dunlosky et al. [8], shows that a student's academic performance improves when utilizing studying methods that involve retrieval practice. These include methods that force active recall of information, such as practice testing and spaced repetition. In contrast, passive study methods that do not force retrieval practice, such as highlighting, rereading, or summarizing, resulted in lower student performance [8].

Test-strategies, or test-taking skills, emerged as the most influential LASSI scale contributing to the performance variation among 1 st and 4th quartile students on both the USMLE Step 1 and USMLE Step 2 examinations. The significant impact of this scale across both medical licensing examinations may be attributed to its influence on several other LASSI scales [6]. In a study involving university-level mathematics students, Dodeen [6] demonstrated that test-taking skills (Test-strategies) positively affected students' motivation, attitude, and anxiety. This finding is similar to the role of motivation, which has also been shown to significantly impacts on multiple other LASSI scales [26]. Given that both Motivation and Test-strategies affect other LASSI scales, it is reasonable that these two scales impact academic performance across all four years of medical school despite the USMLE Step 2 CK being a clinically oriented examination while USMLE Step 1 is more associated with medical knowledge United States Medical Licensing Examination [30].

The skill of Time Management, as measured by the LASSI scale, is often recognized anecdotally as a characteristic of high performing students. While Time Management shows correlation with academic performance in the pre-clinical phase, its predictive value for score variation diminishes by the time medical students reach the Step 2 CK examination stage (Table 6). Similar trends were observed with Motivation, more so than with Self-testing or Test Strategies. One proposed explanation for this pattern is that, as students progress through medical school, they tend to improve in key areas such as time management, motivation, and test anxiety, regardless of their quartile rank [22]. Similar findings have been reported in the literature by Cho et al. [5] and Foong et al. [11] who found that although there is a shift in instructional design in the clinical setting of medical school, self-regulated learning remained at the forefront of importance. This theory is logical because as students are further along in their training they have likely improved from their baseline, owing to increased experience and maturity compared to their early years in medical school.

Because LASSI captures self-reported study strategies early in training (mid of M1 year), and because students commonly adapt strategies across clinical years (e.g., increased use of practice examinations and workplace learning during clerkships), predictive associations with outcomes occurring more than two years later (Step 2 CK) are expected to attenuate relative to closer measures (pre-clerkship and Step 1). We therefore interpret the Step 2 CK associations as conservative estimates of the persistent influence of early strategic learning tendencies. Future research should examine repeat LASSI administrations (pre- and post-clerkship) to measure within-student changes and their associations with later performance.

According to Ertmer and Newby [10], strategic learning comprises two major components: metacognitive knowledge and metacognitive control. Metacognitive knowledge involves the interaction between the learning tasks and the selection of effective strategies for accomplishing these tasks. These learning strategies could be cognitive, by selecting appropriate methods for information processing,motivational, by placing the amount of effort required for accomplishing the learning tasks; and/or environmental, by scheduling sufficient time and utilizing available resources. In this study, high performing medical students scored significantly higher in the scales of Information Processing, Selecting Main Ideas, Motivation, and Time Management when compared with low performing students. Metacognitive control or self-regulation involves the interactions between planning, monitoring and evaluating to achieve successful learning. Additionally, high performing students scored higher in Self-testing, which reflects the acquisition of superior monitoring skills.

The LASSI scale of Motivation, a key predictor of academic performance amongst medical students, aligns closely with the academic and social/emotional learner characteristics depicted by Drachsler and Kirschner [7]. Academic characteristics, such as setting the learning goals, are highly dependent on motivation, which varies among medical students. Setting goals, providing self-enforcement and using positive self-talk are also considered as part of motivation within strategic learning frameworks [10]. In contrast, Test-Strategies and Self-testing, which are recognized as cognitive learner characteristics, were also among the most predictive LASSI scales for academic performance. Notably, Motivation plays a foundational role in shaping the cognitive learner characteristics, as the development of effective test-taking skills and engagement in practice examinations require a baseline level of motivation. By comparing the LASSI scales with these learner characteristics, it becomes evident that academic and cognitive domains are the strongest predictors of high academic performance in medical students. The personal learner characteristics, such as age, race, and socioeconomic status were not assessed in this analysis,however, further research is warranted to explore their potential influence on academic performance.

The authors acknowledge the ongoing methodological debate regarding the treatment of ordinal Likert-type data as continuous. However, substantial evidence supports the use of parametric analyses when Likert items are aggregated into multi-item scales. Norman [20] demonstrated that parametric tests are robust to violations of strict interval assumptions and can be appropriately applied to Likert-scale data, particularly when sample sizes are moderate to large. Furthermore, when multiple Likert items are combined into a composite score, assessment of internal consistency is recommended to justify treating the summed scale as approximately interval-level data [29]. In the present study, each LASSI scale comprises six items [31], which were summed to form composite scale scores. These scales demonstrated acceptable to strong internal consistency (Table 2), supporting their treatment as continuous variables for parametric analyses [4, 13, 20].

Limitations of the study

The primary limitation of this study is the reliance on student-reported survey data. However, the inclusion of USMLE Step 1 and USMLE Step 2 examination scores enhances the validity of the data from pre-clerkship courses as many of the same LASSI scales are significantly correlated with performance on these standardized examinations. Another limitation is that all data were collected from a single medical school in the United States, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other institutions or educational settings.

All cohorts included in this study completed Step 1 during the period in which a three-digit numerical score was reported, and no post-transition Step 1 examinees were included. Therefore, our results reflect learning strategies and performance relationships in the numeric-score context. While this strengthens internal consistency by avoiding a mixed-scoring sample, it means that generalizability to cohorts who entered medical school after the transition to pass/fail scoring remains uncertain, as their learning-strategy emphasis may differ.

Conclusion

This study utilized the 10 LASSI scales to identify characteristics associated with high-performing medical students. Significant correlations existed between students' performance in pre-clerkship courses, USMLE Step 1, and USMLE Step 2 CK examinations and their scores on Motivation, Self-testing, and Test Strategies scales. These findings suggested that acquiring these skills is a key attribute of successful medical students throughout all four years of medical school. Consistent with Ertmer & Newby [10] concept of expert learners, high performers demonstrated greater self-awareness, enhanced metacognitive knowledge, and strong self-regulation skills.

Understanding the characteristics of medical students is essential for designing and developing instructional materials that align with their learning needs. This knowledge also supports designing targeted interventions to help students acquire the skills necessary for academic success. Engaging with students from the beginning of medical school to strengthen these characteristics can reduce attrition rate, enhance institution’s academic performance, and better prepare students for their future challenges in residency training. Practically, our findings suggest targeted interventions (e.g., structured retrieval practice and practice examinations for Self-Testing; instruction in evidence-based test preparation for Test Strategies; goal-setting and time-blocking workshops for Time Management; motivational interviewing or coaching for Motivation) may help learners develop attributes of high performers. Future work should test whether early screening with LASSI followed by targeted remediation improves outcomes and whether the instrument can help support widening-participation efforts by identifying students who would benefit from early support.
