The association between honey consumption amount and physical activity, sleep quality, and Nottingham health profile in young adults
Authors: Fatih Çakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHoney is a natural food with potential beneficial effects on health-related behaviors and well-being. However, evidence regarding the association between the amount of honey consumption and physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life in young adults remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the association between daily honey consumption and physical activity levels, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life among young adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional comparative study was conducted in January 2026 among university students attending a public university in eastern Türkiye and residing in state dormitories. The required sample size was estimated as 336 through power analysis, and the study was completed with 468 participants. Participants were classified into five groups according to daily honey consumption level: non-consumers, 10, 20, 30, and 40 g per day. Data were collected using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), the Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ), and the Nottingham Health Profile (NHP). Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, correlation, and regression analyses.ResultsMost participants had a normal body mass index (BMI), and the mean age was comparable across groups. Among honey consumers, IPAQ scores were positively correlated with RCSQ scores (r = 0.27, p < 0.01), while significant negative correlations were found between IPAQ and NHP, and between RCSQ and NHP (r=-0.26 and r=-0.58, respectively; p < 0.01). Among non-consumers, the correlation between IPAQ and RCSQ was negative and non-significant (r=-0.03, p > 0.05), the correlation between IPAQ and NHP was positive and non-significant (r = 0.02, p > 0.05), and the correlation between RCSQ and NHP was significantly negative (r=-0.29, p < 0.01). Regression analyses indicated that honey consumption level was significantly associated with physical activity, accounting for 6.8% of the variance in IPAQ scores (F = 16.02, p < 0.01), with sleep quality, accounting for 6.4% of the variance in RCSQ scores (F = 31.60, p < 0.01), and with health-related quality of life, accounting for 8.2% of the variance in NHP scores (F = 19.68, p < 0.01).ConclusionsIn young adults, daily honey consumption is associated with higher physical activity levels, better sleep quality, and a more favorable symptom burden as assessed by the NHP. Moreover, honey consumption level was significantly associated with variation in these health-related behaviors.

Introduction

Honey has historically played a central role not only as a dietary component but also in various cultural and traditional medicinal practices, and it remains a widely consumed natural sweetener worldwide [1]. Nevertheless, emerging scientific evidence indicates that honey cannot be viewed solely as a source of simple carbohydrates. Its biochemical profile is highly complex and includes a broad range of bioactive constituents such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, amino acids, biogenic amines, as well as serotonin and melatonin metabolites [2]. This multifaceted composition suggests that the potential biopharmacological effects of honey may extend beyond ener.

Recent clinical and epidemiological findings further indicate that the health effects of honey consumption are not uniform, but rather heterogeneous and context-dependent. Evidence suggests that honey and related products may demonstrate dose-dependent and condition-specific effect profiles on cardiometabolic outcomes [3–6]. These observations imply that honey may influence not only cardiometabolic biomarkers but also broader functional outcomes, including behavioral health indicators such as physical activity patterns and sleep-related parameters. This possibility is biologically plausible because honey contains naturally occurring carbohydrates that may support short-term energy availability and post-activity glycogen restoration, as well as bioactive compounds such as phenolics and flavonoids that may contribute to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In addition, recent analytical studies have reported the presence of serotonin-, melatonin-, and related metabolites in honey, indicating a potential link with sleep-wake regulation and psychophysiological functioning. However, despite growing interest in honey as a functional food, much of the existing literature has focused primarily on biochemical and clinical outcomes, whereas its possible associations with behavioral health indicators remain less well understood.

Physical inactivity and sedentary behavior represent major modifiable determinants of global disease burden and are associated with increased risks of non-communicable diseases, premature mortality, and reduced life expectancy [7]. Consequently, international public health guidelines, including those issued by the World Health Organization and the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, recommend increasing regular physical activity and minimizing sedentary behavior across the lifespan [8, 9]. Likewise, sleep quality has increasingly been recognized as a key behavioral determinant closely related to cardiometabolic, psychosocial, and cognitive health outcomes [10]. In parallel, a notable increase has been observed in research exploring diet-sleep interactions, reflecting growing recognition of the public health relevance of dietary behaviors for sleep outcomes [11–13].

Importantly, the WHO includes honey within the definition of free sugars; therefore, honey consumption, despite being a natural product-may increase total free sugar intake and should be evaluated from a public health perspective in terms of quantity [14, 15]. This framework highlights the balance between the potential benefits of honey and the possible risks associated with excessive intake, supporting the need for dose-based assessments in epidemiological research [15]. Yet, dose-response evidence regarding honey intake and key behavioral health outcomes remains scarce, and the relationship between honey consumption amount and physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life has not been adequately clarified in young adults.

Accordingly, examining the associations between varying levels of honey consumption and physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life/symptom burden may provide valuable clinical and population-level insights for behavioral health and nutritional epidemiology. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to investigate, using a dose-based and multidimensional approach, the association between daily honey consumption amount and physical activity levels, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life outcomes in young adults. To the best of our knowledge, evidence on the dose-based associations between daily honey consumption and key behavioral health outcomes-namely physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life/symptom burden-in young adults remains limited.

Research question

- What is the association between honey consumption and physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life in young adults?

Materials and methods

Study design, population, and sample

This descriptive, comparative, cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [16]. The target population comprised 2,642 university students residing in four state-operated Credit and Dormitories Institution (KYK) dormitories at a public university in eastern Türkiye during January 2026. Based on an a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 336 participants. The analysis was performed assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80 for one-way ANOVA comparisons across five groups. The choice of a medium effect size was based on Cohen’s conventional criteria for behavioral sciences and supported by previous studies investigating associations between dietary factors and behavioral health outcomes [17].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were included in the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) being enrolled at the university where the study was conducted; (2) residing in state dormitories and maintaining their nutritional routine within these dormitories; (3) being aged 18–25 years; (4) voluntarily agreeing to participate and providing written informed consent; (5) consuming their daily meals primarily in state dormitories and the university cafeteria; (6) having no diagnosed chronic disease; (7) not receiving psychological or psychiatric treatment; (8) reporting no substance use (including alcohol, tobacco, or psychoactive substances); and (9) not being an active athlete.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) participation in a regular physical activity or exercise program; (2) pregnancy; (3) regular use of pharmacological treatment, particularly sleep-related medications; (4) incomplete or incorrectly completed data collection forms; or (5) withdrawal from the study during the research period.

Data collection

Study data were collected using a Participant Information Form, the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), the Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) to assess sleep quality, and the Nottingham Health Profile (NHP) to evaluate health-related quality of life and symptom burden. Completion of the questionnaire package required approximately 15–20 min.

Participant information form

This form was developed by the researchers to obtain sociodemographic and anthropometric information. It consisted of five items assessing age, sex, height, body weight, and daily honey consumption amount.

International physical activity questionnaire (IPAQ)

The IPAQ was developed by Craig et al. to assess physical activity levels [18]. It has short and long versions, and the Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Öztürk [19]. In the present study, the short form, commonly used in public health research, was administered. The IPAQ-Short Form includes seven items regarding walking, moderate- and vigorous-intensity physical activity, and sitting time over the previous seven days. Weekly frequency (days) and duration (minutes) were collected for each activity type, and total physical activity was calculated as Metabolic Equivalent Task minutes per week (MET-min/week). Based on these scores, participants can be classified as inactive, minimally active, or highly active according to the standardized IPAQ scoring protocol [20], with higher scores indicating greater physical activity. The internal consistency of the IPAQ short form was reported as Cronbach’s α = 0.76 [18]; in this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.79.

Richards-campbell sleep questionnaire (RCSQ)

The RCSQ was developed by Richards [21] as a multidimensional measure of sleep quality, and its Turkish adaptation was performed by Özlü and Özer [22]. The instrument consists of six items assessing sleep depth, sleep latency, frequency and duration of awakenings, overall sleep satisfaction, and environmental noise level. Each item is rated on a 0-100 visual analog scale. The total RCSQ score is calculated as the mean of five items (excluding the noise item), yielding a score between 0 and 100, where higher scores indicate better sleep quality. The internal consistency coefficient was reported as Cronbach’s α = 0.91 [21]; in the current study, Cronbach’s α was 0.85.

Nottingham health profile (NHP)

The NHP is a generic instrument developed to assess perceived health status and health-related quality of life [23]. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Küçükdeveci [24]. The NHP is a self-administered questionnaire comprising two parts. The first part includes 38 dichotomous (yes/no) items across six dimensions: physical mobility, pain, emotional reactions, energy, social isolation, and sleep. The second part includes seven items assessing the impact of health on daily life domains (e.g., work, household activities, social life). Each dimension is scored on a scale from 0 (no problems) to 100 (maximum perceived problems) based on affirmative responses. Higher scores indicate greater health problems and consequently poorer perceived health status and quality of life [23]. In this study, the NHP was used to quantitatively assess health-related quality of life and symptom burden. The internal consistency coefficient of the NHP has been reported to range from Cronbach’s α = 0.70 to 0.92 [23]; in this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.77.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using an online survey method during the study period. Prior to data collection, participants were provided with clear information regarding the purpose and scope of the research, and the voluntary nature of participation was explicitly emphasized. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any stage without providing justification. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants through an online informed consent form, and individuals who did not provide consent were not granted access to the survey.

The study sample consisted of university students residing in KYK dormitories. Because all participants lived in four KYK dormitories and daily meals served both in the dormitories and the university cafeteria were planned and prepared by the same food service provider, a relative level of standardization in terms of main meal content and energy distribution was ensured across participants.

The Participant Information Form included items assessing the daily amount of honey consumption. Participants were asked to report their typical daily honey consumption based on habitual intake patterns within the dormitory setting. Based on self-reported responses, participants were classified into five groups according to daily honey intake: non-consumers and those consuming approximately 10 g, 20 g, 30 g, and 40 g per day. Prior to the study, participants’ honey consumption levels were assessed, and the distribution of intake was observed to cluster within specific ranges. Accordingly, daily honey consumption was evaluated based on the standardized 10 g single-use portions provided in state dormitories. Participants’ intake levels were categorized based on the number of portions consumed per day. Because the single-use portions were freely available in the dormitories, participants could obtain and consume more than one 10 g portion per day; accordingly, daily intake ranged from none (non-consumers) to four portions (40 g/day). This approach reflects real-life consumption patterns and provides a dose-based framework for comparing different levels of intake. Honey consumption level-the main independent variable-was therefore operationalized as an ordinal variable reflecting increasing intake levels. All instruments were completed by participants in an average duration of 15–20 min.

Honey provided in state dormitories

The honey served in state dormitories was blossom honey supplied from beekeepers in Bingöl, and it was provided in single-use portion pack of 10 g. Blossom honey is a natural bee product produced through enzymatic transformation of nectar collected from different flowering plant species; it is primarily composed of fructose and glucose, serving as an energy source, and it may exhibit antioxidant capacity due to its bioactive constituents such as phenolic compounds and flavonoids [25]. The composition of blossom honey varies depending on botanical origin, geographical region, season, and storage conditions; however, Bingöl honey is considered to have a distinctive profile due to the region’s high-altitude floristic diversity [25].

Previous analyses have reported that Bingöl honey is rich in bioactive components, with total phenolic content in phenolic extracts ranging from 476.08 to 865.22 mg GAE/100 g, total flavonoid content from 41.67 to 1249.74 mg QE/100 g, and total phenolic acid content from 0.74 to 58.35 mg SA/100 g. Moreover, β-carotene (1.70–3.60 mg/kg) and lycopene (0.89–3.41 mg/kg) levels have been reported as noteworthy. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analyses have demonstrated the presence of phenolic acids (e.g., gallic acid, caffeic acid, syringic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid) as well as flavonoids such as catechin, quercetin, and chrysin [25, 26]. This rich phenolic/flavonoid composition may contribute to the strong antioxidant capacity of Bingöl honey and highlights its potential antimicrobial and antiproliferative properties [25].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive characteristics of participants were summarized as mean±standard deviation or as numbers and percentages, as appropriate. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and by examining skewness and kurtosis coefficients. All continuous variables were found to be approximately normally distributed; therefore, parametric tests were deemed appropriate and used in subsequent analyses. Although physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life may be interrelated, these variables were considered conceptually distinct health outcomes. Physical activity reflects behavioral activity patterns, sleep quality reflects restorative physiological functioning, and NHP reflects perceived health status and symptom burden. Therefore, each outcome was analyzed separately to evaluate the association between honey consumption and different dimensions of health.

Between-group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. In addition, Pearson correlation analyses were performed separately among honey consumers and non-consumers to evaluate the relationships between physical activity, sleep quality, and health profile scores. To examine the associations between honey consumption and physical activity, sleep quality, and Nottingham Health Profile outcomes, linear regression analyses were conducted. In these models, the primary independent variable (honey consumption level) was included as an ordinal predictor reflecting increasing intake levels. In all statistical analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical statement

Prior to study initiation, ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of a public university (Approval No: 2026-01/05). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before data collection, participants were informed online about the purpose and scope of the research and were assured that participation was entirely voluntary. Online informed consent was obtained from all participants before granting access to the survey.

With respect to BMI, the majority of participants across all groups fell within the normal BMI range. Specifically, the prevalence of normal BMI was 62.2% among non-consumers and 69.6%, 71.2%, 56.1%, and 66.6% in the 10 g, 20 g, 30 g, and 40 g groups, respectively.

Mean age values were similar across honey consumption groups, with no statistically significant difference observed (p = 0.057). Collectively, these findings suggest that participants categorized according to honey consumption levels were broadly comparable with respect to key baseline sociodemographic characteristics.

Mean scores for physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life across honey consumption categories are presented in Table 2. Notably, IPAQ scores were markedly higher among participants consuming 30 g and 40 g of honey, suggesting a potential association between higher honey intake and increased physical activity levels.

RCSQ scores were higher among honey-consuming groups compared with non-consumers; however, the pattern was not strictly linear across intake levels. The mean RCSQ score was 44.78 ± 19.24 among non-consumers, rising to 48.35 ± 20.6 in the 10 g group, 52.17 ± 19.39 in the 20 g group, 59.09 ± 20.74 in the 30 g group, and 66.17 ± 22.51 in the 40 g group. These findings suggest that sleep quality tended to be higher among individuals with greater honey consumption; however, the observed pattern does not indicate a consistent stepwise or dose-dependent relationship.

With respect to symptom burden assessed by the NHP, mean scores were 33.96 ± 24.84 in non-consumers, 32.89 ± 19.58 in the 10 g group, 25.86 ± 22.44 in the 20 g group, 26.00 ± 25.24 in the 30 g group, and 12.34 ± 17.20 in the 40 g group. The substantially lower NHP scores observed particularly in the 40 g group suggest that higher levels of honey consumption may be associated with a reduced symptom burden and more favorable health-related quality of life outcomes.

When total physical activity scores (IPAQ; MET-min/week) were compared across honey consumption levels (Fig. 1), a statistically significant difference was observed between groups (F = 8.170, p < 0.01). The highest physical activity level was observed in the 30 g honey consumption group, and IPAQ scores in this group were significantly higher than those of the NH group (p < 0.01).

Among honey consumers, correlation analyses revealed a statistically significant positive association between IPAQ and RCSQ scores (p < 0.01), indicating that higher physical activity levels were related to better sleep quality. In addition, a significant negative correlation was observed between IPAQ and NHP scores (p < 0.01). Considering that higher NHP scores indicate greater symptom burden (i.e., poorer health-related quality of life), this finding suggests that increased physical activity among honey consumers is associated with lower NHP scores and thus a more favorable health-related quality of life. Likewise, a strong and statistically significant negative association was identified between RCSQ and NHP (p < 0.01), indicating that better sleep quality was related to lower symptom burden (Table 3).

In the unadjusted models (Model 1), honey consumption was significantly associated with all three outcomes. It was positively associated with physical activity (β = 0.26, F = 16.02, R²=0.068, p < 0.001) and sleep quality (β = 0.31, F = 31.60, R²=0.064, p < 0.001), and negatively associated with symptom burden as assessed by the NHP (β=-0.28, F = 19.68, R²=0.082, p < 0.001), indicating that higher honey consumption was related to a lower symptom burden and more favorable health-related quality of life.

Discussion

This study examined, using a multidimensional approach, the associations between daily honey consumption levels and physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life/symptom burden among young adults. Overall, the findings indicate that individuals with higher honey intake tended to exhibit higher physical activity levels, better sleep quality, and more favorable health-related quality of life outcomes. Moreover, among honey consumers, higher physical activity was associated with better sleep quality and quality of life, whereas no significant association was observed between these variables among non-consumers. The correlation analyses further demonstrated that these outcomes were significantly interrelated, particularly among honey consumers. However, the observed correlation coefficients were generally in the weak-to-moderate range, suggesting that physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life represent related but distinct dimensions of health rather than a single underlying construct. For this reason, the outcomes were evaluated separately while acknowledging their conceptual and statistical relationships.

In line with these observations, regression analyses demonstrated that honey consumption was significantly associated with physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life outcomes. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal or predictive relationships. Taken together, these findings suggest that honey consumption, when considered within the broader context of lifestyle behaviors, may represent a behavioral correlate of physical activity, sleep quality, and perceived health status.

In the present study, young adults consuming 30 g of honey per day exhibited higher IPAQ scores compared with other groups, with similarly elevated values also observed in the 40 g group. This pattern suggests that moderate levels of honey consumption may be associated with higher physical activity levels; however, the distribution of scores does not indicate a strictly linear or dose-dependent relationship across all intake levels. The observed association between honey consumption and physical activity was also supported by correlation analyses. From a biological perspective, the naturally occurring carbohydrates in honey-particularly glucose and fructose-may support short-term energy availability during physical activity and contribute to post-exercise glycogen resynthesis [27]. This rationale is consistent with previous studies investigating the effects of honey on exercise performance and recovery [27, 28]. Yusof et al. (2018) reported that honey may be associated with improvements in exercise performance and selected health indicators, potentially due to both its carbohydrate content and antioxidant properties [29]. Similarly, Ahmad et al. (2015) demonstrated that honey-containing beverages consumed after exercise in hot environments may help maintain glucose homeostasis and support subsequent performance [30]. More recently, a double-blind placebo-controlled trial in female athletes showed that a honey-sweetened beverage was associated with reduced post-exercise muscle soreness, lower perceived exertion, and improved certain performance parameters [31]. Taken together, these findings provide a plausible biological context for the observed association between honey intake and physical activity levels in the present study. Nevertheless, it should be noted that this pattern may not be attributable solely to biological mechanisms and may also reflect lifestyle-related factors, such as healthier dietary preferences and greater engagement in regular physical activity.

In addition, young adults consuming 30 g and 40 g of honey per day demonstrated higher RCSQ scores compared with other groups. Although higher sleep quality scores were observed among these groups, the pattern did not follow a strictly linear or stepwise increase across all levels of honey consumption. Therefore, the findings suggest a potential association between moderate-to-higher honey intake and improved sleep quality, rather than a consistent dose-dependent relationship.

Previous studies have also extended the use of the Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ), originally developed for ICU settings, to non-clinical populations, including healthy individuals. Evidence indicates that the RCSQ demonstrates acceptable construct and concurrent validity not only in ICU patients but also in cognitively intact home sleepers, suggesting its ability to capture subjective sleep quality across different populations [32]. In addition, recent studies conducted among young adults have reported meaningful associations between RCSQ scores and lifestyle-related factors such as physical activity and smoking behavior, supporting its applicability in non-clinical settings [33].

Beyond its role as a caloric source, honey has increasingly been characterized as a complex matrix that may contain neurobiologically relevant compounds. In this context, Kim et al. (2021) detected serotonin, melatonin, and related metabolites in honey samples using advanced analytical methods, suggesting that honey may include bioactive components involved in sleep-wake regulation [2]. This evidence provides a possible neuroendocrine framework that may help explain the observed association between honey consumption and sleep quality. Furthermore, potential biochemical pathways related to tryptophan metabolism have been proposed, including mechanisms involving kynurenic acid (KYNA), which has been linked to neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects [34]. Taken together, these mechanisms may contribute to understanding the observed relationship between honey consumption and sleep quality; however, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, these interpretations should be considered exploratory.

Regarding health-related quality of life, higher levels of honey consumption were associated with lower NHP scores. Given the inverse scoring direction of the NHP, these findings suggest that honey consumption may be associated with a more favorable perceived quality of life and reduced symptom burden. However, the distribution of NHP scores across groups did not follow a strictly linear pattern, with the most favorable outcomes observed particularly in the higher intake groups. This observation is consistent with literature indicating that honey may modulate antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways through its phenolic constituents [35, 36]. Evidence on the broader health profile of honey continues to expand, including its potential associations with psychological well-being, sleep quality, and physical activity. For instance, Zamri et al. (2023) highlighted the neuroprotective properties of honey and its potential to support cognitive function, while Zulkifli et al. (2024) reported possible sleep-regulatory roles via serotonin and melatonin metabolites [37, 38]. Hills et al. (2019) also emphasized that honey’s glucose and fructose content may support energy availability during exercise and contribute to recovery through glycogen resynthesis [27]. Taken together, these findings suggest that honey consumption may be associated with health-related quality of life outcomes not only as an energy source but also through its bioactive components. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously, as they may also reflect indirect pathways mediated by factors such as physical activity, sleep quality, and broader lifestyle behaviors.

A distinctive feature of this study is the differential pattern of inter-scale correlations observed between honey consumers and non-consumers. Among honey consumers, positive associations were observed between IPAQ and RCSQ, while negative associations were identified between IPAQ and NHP and between RCSQ and NHP. In contrast, among non-consumers, physical activity was not significantly associated with either sleep quality or health-related quality of life. This pattern may suggest that honey consumption co-occurs with broader lifestyle characteristics that are associated with more favorable behavioral and health-related outcomes. In this context, a potential “healthy user” profile may be considered, whereby individuals who consume honey may also be more likely to engage in other health-promoting behaviors. However, these interpretations should be approached with caution. The present study did not include interaction or moderation analyses; therefore, it cannot be concluded that honey consumption functions as an effect modifier in the relationships between physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life. Most participants in this study resided in the same state dormitories and consumed their daily meals primarily in the same dormitory and university cafeterias, which may have reduced variability in the dietary environment to some extent. However, overall dietary patterns, food preferences, and additional food consumption outside these settings were not assessed. Given that dietary habits are important determinants of physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life, some of the observed associations may reflect healthier dietary behaviors in addition to honey consumption. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within the context of broader lifestyle characteristics associated with honey consumption rather than as evidence of an independent effect of honey consumption alone.

Regression analyses further supported these observations by demonstrating that honey consumption was significantly associated with IPAQ, RCSQ, and NHP outcomes. Importantly, after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI in hierarchical models, honey consumption remained an independent and statistically significant predictor of all three outcomes, with no evidence of multicollinearity (all variance inflation factors ≤ 1.11), indicating that these associations were not driven by these demographic or anthropometric covariates. When considered together with the group comparisons and correlation analyses, these findings indicate a consistent pattern of associations across different analytical approaches. However, this consistency should not be interpreted as evidence of causality or strong explanatory power, particularly given the cross-sectional design of the study. The explained variance remained within a modest range (approximately 6–8%), which is consistent with the multifactorial nature of physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life [34]. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as indicating a strong or direct effect of honey consumption; rather, they suggest that honey consumption level may be one of several lifestyle-related factors associated with these behavioral and health-related outcomes.

Although honey is commonly perceived as a natural food, its classification within free sugars highlights the importance of considering intake quantity. Therefore, the findings of the present study should not be interpreted as supporting increased or high levels of honey consumption. Instead, honey intake should be evaluated cautiously within the context of overall dietary patterns and individual nutritional requirements. Given the observational nature of the study, the results should be interpreted as indicative of associations rather than causal relationships. Future research using longitudinal or experimental designs is warranted to further clarify the nature and direction of these relationships and to better understand the potential role of honey consumption within broader lifestyle and behavioral health frameworks.

Limitations of the study

The findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, although the sample size exceeded the minimum requirement identified through power analysis, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits the overall strength of evidence. Second, honey consumption was assessed through self-report, which may be subject to recall bias and misclassification. Third, physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life are multifactorial outcomes influenced by numerous biopsychosocial determinants; therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Although meals were partially standardized, detailed dietary intake, caffeine consumption, and other lifestyle factors were not assessed. Fourth, honey was provided free of charge in state dormitories, which may not reflect typical consumption patterns in the general population and may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, individuals who consumed honey regularly may have differed from non-consumers in unmeasured health-related behaviors, introducing potential healthy-user bias. Finally, the study was limited to healthy young adults residing in state dormitories; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the associations between daily honey consumption levels and physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life in a young adult sample. Young adults with higher honey intake tended to exhibit higher physical activity levels, better sleep quality, and more favorable health-related quality of life profiles. In addition, the relationships between physical activity and sleep quality/quality of life appeared to be more pronounced among honey consumers, whereas no significant associations were observed among non-consumers. Honey consumption was significantly associated with physical activity, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life outcomes; however, the magnitude of these associations was modest. These findings suggest that honey consumption may not act as a sole determinant but rather as a lifestyle-related component that co-occurs with other health-promoting behaviors. However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Furthermore, considering honey’s classification within free sugars, the results should not be interpreted as recommending high levels of honey intake. Honey consumption should be considered in moderation within the context of individual nutritional needs and overall dietary patterns. Future studies are warranted to further investigate these associations using longitudinal or experimental designs and more diverse populations.
