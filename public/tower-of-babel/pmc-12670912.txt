From design to Deployment: The innovative role of nanomicelles in targeted ophthalmic therapeutics
Authors: Mengli Sun, Jiale Wang, Wenhui Fan, Kexin Wang, Yiwen Wang, Jiehao Zhang, Shenao Ding, Chengzhi Zhang, Zhihui Feng, Yan-Ge Wang, Zongming Song, Ye Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Delivering medications to targeted lesions poses significant challenges due to the unique anatomical structure and physical barriers of the eyeball. Polymer nanomicelles are spontaneously assembled from surfactants and amphiphilic polymers, encapsulating insoluble drugs within their cores to augment their hydrosolubility. Nanomicelles have demonstrated potential to surmount these biological hurdles and convey encapsulated therapeutic agents to intended sites. Nanomicelle can prolong the drug half-life and promote molecule permeation through ocular epithelium. Notably, they can be formulated at concentrations marginally exceeding the critical micelle concentration while maintaining a stable conformation, thereby boosting therapeutic effectiveness. Moreover, the physicochemical attributes of polymeric micelles can be precisely adjusted by integrating diverse hydrophilic or hydrophobic moieties. Surface modifications can further impart specific charges or facilitate targeted organ delivery enhancing adhesion to ocular tissues and mitigating systemic toxicity. This review delves into the utilization of polymer nanomicelles in ophthalmological practice, encompassing block copolymer design, the pharmacokinetics of encapsulated drugs within nanomicelles, and the molecule pathways governing nanomedicines. This review also explores their applications in treating ocular disorders including infectious keratitis, DED, glaucoma, corneal graft rejection, neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), and DR, while introducing progress in clinical deployment and potentials in ocular diagnosis. We also discuss the key challenges in clinical translation and scalable manufacturing. With continued optimization, polymer nanomicelles are poised to become a versatile, non-invasive platform for personalized ophthalmologic therapies.

Introduction

Eye diseases represent a rapidly growing realm of disorders associated with aging, trauma, genetics and immune factors. Globally, the number of individuals affected by vision impairment exceeds 250 million, and the prognosis for morbidity remains discouraging [1]. The application of topical medications to the ocular surface is a commonly employed strategy for managing various ophthalmic conditions. Nevertheless, this delivery mode is not without defects, such as short retention time, limited drug accumulation and inadequate bioavailability. Poor drug retention can be attributed to several physiological barriers involving the corneal barrier, blood-retinal barrier (BRB) and blood-aqueous barrier (BAB) [2]. Furthermore, the physiological processes of blinking, reflex tear production and tear drainage would remove foreign substances (including medications) from the surface of the eye. The cumulative effects of these obstacles cause an approximate 95 % loss of medications after local administration [3]. It is widely known that hydrophobic nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as indomethacin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, have been used for inflammatory disorders. Although in-vitro studies have demonstrated their pharmacological effectiveness, clinical trials suggest that they fail to achieve sufficient therapeutic effects in patients [4]. Such failure should be ascribed to the low solubility of the drug and inadequate retention at the ocular lesion with inflammatory response, giving rise to a reduction in bioavailability. Therefore, these limitations underscore the necessity of prioritizing novel drug delivery strategies aimed at effectively enhancing the bioavailability of drugs.

Nanosystems have achieved marvelous advancements in biomedical applications such as ocular diseases, tissue engineering, immunotherapy and oncology therapy [5]. They possess several ideal characteristics, including a diminutive size, biodegradability and minimized irritation. The polymeric materials used for these nanocarriers are commonly synthesized through techniques such as emulsion polymerization, free radical polymerization and interfacial polymerization [6]. Generally, the interaction between nanomaterials and therapeutic agents is predominantly physical rather than chemical. Thus far, nanomaterials have been designed in diverse forms for ophthalmic drug delivery, including nanomicelles-loaded contact lenses, nanodecorated subconjunctival implants, polymeric hydrogel networks, single or mixed nanomicellar systems as well as nanostructured lipid carriers. Accumulating evidences suggest that nano-controlled release systems can enhance drug bioavailability while minimizing side effects, prolonging the drug retention and reducing dosage frequency [7]. Moreover, various novel nanomicelles are endowed with the noninvasive, nontoxic and sustainable capabilities to deliver drugs in a controlled manner. Despite significant triumphs in nanoscale drug delivery systems (DDS), most aforementioned systems remain at preliminary research stages, with only a small fraction progressing to clinical applications [8].

In recent years, Polymer nanomicelles have emerged as a promising approach for targeted hydrophobic drug delivery at desired concentration within ocular lesions. These micelles (10–200 nm) are constructed from amphiphilic molecules or block copolymers. Micelles of various sizes and shapes can be obtained dependent on the molecular weight of the polymers that constitute the core and crown-forming blocks. Nanomicelles routinely self-assemble into organized core-shell or supramolecular structures when their concentration surpasses the critical micelle concentration (CMC) in aqueous media [9]. The hydrophobic segments aggregate within the core of the micelles, packaging insoluble medications, while the hydrophilic segments extend outward to interact with the solvent, strengthening drug solubility. The amphipathic and diminutive size of nanomicelles facilitate enhanced penetration through lipophilic corneal epithelial cells, while their adhesiveness improves contact with the ocular surface without causing visual interference. Polymer nanomicelles also enhance the drug stability by dampening the elimination through mononuclear phagocyte systems and promoting prolonged blood circulation [10]. Targeting moieties such as specific surface receptors, transporter proteins or phage fusion proteins can be conjugated with nanomicelles to bestow them with desirable delivery capabilities [11]. By incorporating or conjugating special assembly units, polymer nanomicelles can release loaded drugs in response to various extra- and intra-cellular biological stimulus, thereby yielding higher selectivity and lower toxic effects. More importantly, nanomicelles may act as local depots for continuous drug release at targeted lesions and improve their treatment efficacy. Therefore, the ease of preparation coupled with a high capacity for drug encapsulation has rendered nanomicelles increasingly popular for ocular drug delivery. Currently, polymer nanomicelles represent an efficacious option for delivering hydrophobic molecules alongside gene vectors into ocular tissue.

Ideally, the polymers utilized to nanomicelles should be biodegradable and biocompatible. Biocompatible copolymers such as polyethylene oxide (PEO), polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polyacrylamide (PAM) constitute the hydrophilic segments of polymer nanomicelles [12]. However, within the context of polymer nanomicelles, the compatibility between the hydrophobic segment and pharmaceutical molecules primarily impedes drug encapsulation efficiency. The inherent chemical interaction between hydrophobic constituents and pharmaceutical compounds, along with the dimensions of their hydrophobic chains, further constricts the drug loading capacity. Thus far, a multitude of polymer nanomicelle-based medication delivery formulations have advanced to stages of preclinical or clinical trials. For instance, Cholkar et al. encapsulated Cyclosporin A (CsA) in nanomicelles and investigated its ocular tissue distribution through topical drop instillation in New Zealand White albino rabbits [13]. The findings revealed elevated CsA concentrations in anterior ocular tissue. Researchers also have endeavored to deliver drugs to the posterior segment using nanomicelles. Patel et al. formulated dexamethasone (DEX)-loaded nanomicelles using polyoxyl 40 stearate (P40S) and polysorbate 80 (P80) [14]. Therapeutic concentrations of DEX were detected in choroid and retinal tissue, providing a non-invasive method for treating posterior segment uveitis. In this review, we intend to introduce fundamental principles for preparing polymer nanomicelles suitable for ocular drug delivery, and outlining recent developments in encapsulate various drugs utilizing nanomicelles. Despite considerable potential of polymer nanomicelles, a discernible gap exists between their foundational research and clinical application. This review seeks to elucidate the polymer chemistry properties, fabrication methods, and the capacity of nanomicelles to overcome ocular barriers. We also assess recent advances in preclinical and clinical settings of nanomicelles encapsulated with various drugs to treat ocular diseases, such as dry eye, infectious keratitis, glaucoma, uveitis, and retinal degeneration. This review provides a critical appraisal of persistent challenges including stability, biocompatibility, scalability, and regulatory pathways. Furthermore, we discuss the developments in the biodistribution, targetability, therapeutic efficacy and tolerability associated with these micellar formulations. By synthesizing these facets, we establish a comprehensive reference for researchers and clinicians to devise innovative nanomicelle-based strategies. It is believable that further refinement of the knowledge would provide promising prospects for the clinical application of polymer nanomicelles.

Ocular barriers and constraints in anterior segment drug delivery

Ocular barriers to nanomicelle-based drug delivery

Tear film

The tear film consists of three layers: the innermost mucin film, the middle aqueous layer and the outer oily layer. These intricate structures can severely impede drug absorption. Mucin establishes a hydrophilic barrier on the glycocalyx of ocular surface, safeguarding the eye while creating an extra obstacle for drug permeation [15]. Following topical administration, drug concentrations decrease sharply due to the interactions with tear proteins and the accelerated clearance mechanisms of tear flow. Additionally, the instilled volume can stimulate reflex tearing, which dilutes the drug and accelerates its drainage via the nasolacrimal duct, resulting in low bioavailability [16]. When drop volumes exceed 30 μL (volume that can be retained in the cul-de-sac without overflow), nasolacrimal drainage acts as the principle pathway for drug loss [17]. Regular local ophthalmic formulations, like standard eye drops, tend to overflow or drain and this phenomenon combined with dilution by tears markedly diminishes therapeutic efficacy. In this context, the precorneal factors including tear drainage, dilution and reflex blinking reduce residence time for conventionally instilled dosage forms on ocular surface. Therefore, it is imperative to prolong the residence time on these surfaces and enhance corneal permeability. Viscosity enhancers like hyaluronic acid (HA) or chitosan are often incorporated into nanomicelles to prolong precorneal retention through mucoadhesion and reduced tear turnover. Eleonora Terreni et al. have demonstrated that ASMP-Nano mainly prolonged the CyA retention time in the anterior corneal region in the presence of HA (a mucosal adhesion polymer) in the pharmacokinetic study of rabbits [18]. Ligand conjugation with peptides or antibodies, enables active targeting to corneal or conjunctival epithelia, enhancing cellular uptake and local accumulation. Additionally, charge modulation for example cationic nanomicelles improves interaction with negatively charged ocular surfaces, further extending retention time and promoting transcorneal permeation. Lin et al. evaluated the ability of micelles to promote the penetration of hydrophobic macrolide immunosuppressant tacrolimus across the ocular surface. Results showed that the positively charged nanomicelles could significantly prolong the tacrolimus retention on ocular surface and enhance its corneal permeability under ex vivo and in vivo conditions. It yielded superior inhibitory effects on ocular surface inflammation and corneal epithelial apoptosis as assessed by slit lamp examination [19]. Hence, the positively charged nanomicelles might act as a good DDS for ocular surface medication.

Cornea

Cornea serves as a mechanical barrier that restricts the entry of exogenous substances into the eye and safeguards the ocular content. It is a transparent and avascular tissue composed of five distinct layers including the epithelium, Bowman's layer, stroma, Descemet's membrane and endothelium [20]. The corneal epithelium, being lipoidal in nature, presents a substantial resistance for the penetration of topically administered hydrophilic compounds. It possesses a physiologically negative charge, which hinders the binding and penetration of anionic compounds into the cornea. Furthermore, the superficial epithelial cells of the cornea are interconnected by desmosomes and encompassed by ribbon-like tight junctional complexes (zonula occludens) [21,22]. Notably, the presence of tight junctional complexes impedes paracellular diffusion of drugs from the tear film into intercellular spaces of epithelium and inner layer of the cornea. The stroma, accounting for 90 % of the corneal thickness, is composed of an extracellular matrix and features a lamellar arrangement of collagen fibrils. Its highly hydrated structure poses a significant hindrance to permeation of lipophilic drug molecules. Efflux transporters (e.g. P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and multidrug resistance proteins (MRPs)) in the plasma membrane of corneal cells represent another significant barrier that restricts drug permeation to targeted tissue. The expression of efflux pumps and their active role in drug efflux at the cell surface have been identified in both human and rabbit corneas [23]. These characteristics render the cornea a major obstacle for effective drug delivery to the anterior segment of the eye. Fortunately, permeation enhancers such as surfactants TPGS can temporarily disrupt the corneal epithelial tight junctions, facilitating drug penetration. Martina Ghezzi et al. demonstrated that micelles prepared with corneal enhancers tocopherol polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate (TPGS) and lysosol ® HS15 decompose upon contact with porcine corneas, damaging the corneal epithelium and thereby promoting the penetration and retention of cyclosporine within the cornea [24]. In addition, certain locally applied nanocapsules, such as L-valine (L-Val) and an HS15/DSPE-PEG2000-L-Val nanomicelle delivery system containing baicalin (BC) (BC@HS15/DSPE-PEG2000-L-Val), can effectively penetrate the cornea and enhance local drug delivery due to their high encapsulation rate, small particle size and nonirritating effect on the ocular surface. These findings indicate that nanomicelles not only extend the retention time but also enhance the efficiency to deliver drugs to the deeper layers of cornea [25].

Conjunctiva

The conjunctiva comprises two layers: epithelium and underlying stroma, which serves as a permeability barrier for topically administered medications [26]. It further impedes the passive diffusion of hydrophilic molecules due to trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) exceeding 1500 Ω cm2 in epithelial tight junctions [27]. Furthermore, it is highly vascularized and abundantly supplied with lymphatics. Consequently, topically instilled drugs into the cul-de-sac will be rapidly cleared via systemic circulation and lymphatic drainage, resulting in diminished bioavailability. It has been reported that at least 10 % of sodium fluorescein is eliminated through the lymphatics within 1 h following administration in the subconjunctival space [28]. At the cellular level, functional expression of P-gp and MRPs will further restrict drug transport across the conjunctiva [29]. Thus, delivering drug molecules to the posterior eye via this route is generally considered impractical. Compared with than free dexamethasone, Dexamethasone-loaded CSO-VV-SA nanomicelles achieve significantly greater ocular bioavailability and enhanced permeability across both corneal and conjunctival epithelial barriers, owing to their active targeting functionality [30]. It is reported that the polyhydroxyethyl asparagine containing polyethylene glycol and cetylamine (PHEA-PEG-C) micelles loaded DEX alcohol possess higher ocular bioavailability compared with water suspension administration of the same drug. It also showed greater in vitro permeability through primary cultured rabbit conjunctiva and corneal epithelial cells in all cases. These results suggest that nanomicelles enhance the penetration of drug molecules through conjunctival epithelial cells to deep ocular tissue [31].

The sclera and choroid

Sclera is primarily consisted of extracellular matrix involving collagen fibers, proteoglycans and glycoproteins, which work collectively to prevent foreign bodies from infiltrating the posterior tissue. Drug permeability diminishes progressively with increasing molecular radius and hydrophobicity, resulting in restricted passage through aqueous scleral pores [32,33]. In this context, drugs with high lipophilicity and large molecular weight will encounter substantial challenges in penetrating these pores [34]. Furthermore, transscleral permeability is markedly affected by the charge of the molecules. Positively charged molecules are less permeable across the sclera than negatively charged counterparts because the proteoglycan matrix of the sclera is negatively charged, which causing these positively charged solutes to bind [35]. The choroid is a pigmented middle layer situated between the sclera and retina. It consists of a complicated network of fenestrated capillaries that is responsible for delivering oxygenated blood and nutrients to the outer retina and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells. The rapid blood flow within choroidal capillaries facilitates the drug molecules entry into systemic circulation; thus it also acts as a dynamic barrier for effective drug delivery [36]. Additionally, RPE cells are supported by Bruch's membrane, a 2–4 μm thick elastic membrane serving as a basement membrane for RPE cells [37,38]. The Bruch's–choroid (BC) complex presents an even more formidable obstacle for transscleral drug delivery than does the sclera itself, especially for positively charged lipophilic drugs [39]. Collectively, topical administration is highly inefficient in terms of delivering therapeutic agents to the posterior segments of eyeball. It is noteworthy that a clear, aqueous mixed nanomicellar formulation (MNF) loaded with rapamycin can improve the efficiency of retinal drug delivery. Back-of-the-eye tissue distribution studies on white rabbits demonstrated very high rapamycin levels in retina-choroid (place of drug action) with a negligible pharmacokinetic partitioning into vitreous humor [40]. In addition, previous studies have shown that the hybrid nanomicelles composed of P40S and P80 (7:3) can efficiently load dexamethasone, significantly improve the drug solubility, and promote drug transmembrane penetration and delivery in the posterior segment of the eye. After topical eye drops, the system can achieve therapeutic concentration in the retina-choroid, effectively reduce drug exposure in the vitreous cavity, and provides a non-invasive treatment for posterior uveitis [41]. These findings indicate that nanomicelles can avoid low drug bioavailability resulted from rapid clearance of choroid circulation, thereby improving the drug distribution in the posterior eye tissue.

Blood ocular barrier (BOB)

BOB comprises the BAB and BRB which acts as major impediments to drug penetration into the retina (Fig. 1). BAB is formed by the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium of the ciliary body, the endothelial cells of the iris vessels and the inner lining endothelium of the Schlemm's canal [42]. It regulates solute movement between anterior and posterior segments through tight junctions, obstructing the entry of plasma-derived albumin and hydrophilic drugs into the aqueous humor. BRB functions as a physiological barrier that primarily modulates protein, ion and water transport from the systemic circulation to the retina. The inner BRB consists of tight junctions among retinal capillary endothelial cells while the outer BRB features tight junctions between RPE cells surrounded by choroidal capillaries [43]. The functionality of these tight junctions is supported by astrocytes and Müller cells. Astrocytes play a pivotal role in maintaining integrity while enhancing barrier properties within retinal endothelial capillaries [44]. These intercellular tight junctions enable the selective protection against foreign substances in circulation. However, they also effectively restrict the transport of molecules (particularly hydrophilic compounds and macromolecules) from choroidal blood flow to posterior ocular segments [45]. Moreover, the external BRB is rich of melanin with which drug molecules may interact, thus further obstructing their diffusion into the vitreous [46]. Additionally, expression levels alongside activity profiles for P-gp and MRP efflux pumps on both apical and basal surfaces of human retinal pigment epithelial cells (ARPE-19) have been documented [47]. Therefore, it is in urgent need to develop innovative delivery strategies which can ensure adequate bioavailability within posterior segment. The B-C12-CCDF-loaded nanomicelles self-assembled from polyoxyethylene hydrogenated castor oil 40 (HCO-40) and octoxynol 40 (OC-40) exhibit outstanding potential to overcome the ocular barrier. Their small particle size of ∼17.73 nm is adapted to the scleral pores of the eye. The encapsulated prodrug, by means of biotin, targets the highly expressed sodium-dependent vitamin transporter (SMVT) and C12 lipid chain in the retina to enhance lipid solubility and interaction with the cell membrane, facilitating the efficient cross-cell layer of corneal epithelium (HCE-T) and retinal pigment epithelium (D407) through the endocytosis pathway [48]. Recently, a study on polyethylene glycol-polycaprolactone block copolymer (PEG-b-PCL) nanomicelles showed that neither blank micelles nor PEG-b-PCL micelles with adaxatinib were found to have obvious ocular toxicity or structural abnormalities during the 28-day in vivo observation period after intravitreal injection in mice. The cornea is not damaged and the retina maintains its normal structure. In vitro histological examination further confirmed that there was only a very small amount of apoptosis and stress response in the retina, and at the same time, cell-level experiments also verified its excellent safety. This result indicates that PEG-b-PCL nanomicelles can not only maintain good biocompatibility in vivo, but also be effectively delivered to the deep ocular tissues and act on the retina through specific administration routes (such as vitreous injection), indirectly demonstrating its ability to break through the limitations of the blood-eye barrier on drug delivery. It also lays a foundation for the subsequent research and development and clinical transformation of nano-micellar drug delivery systems for ocular diseases such as proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) [49].Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the primary anatomical and physiological ocular barriers to drug delivery. Constraints of anterior segment administration

Topical administration

Topically administered medications symbolize the current standard of care predominantly for anterior segment diseases. These topical agents may be formulated as suspensions, liquids, gels, or ointments. Despite being the preferred local delivery route to the eye, traditional topical formulations encounter significant challenges in achieving effective ocular penetration [50]. Ophthalmic suspensions consist of water insoluble drugs dispersed within aqueous vehicles [51]. Typically, smaller drug particles (<10 μm) demonstrate higher solubility and dissolution rates but poor retention on the ocular surface. Conversely, larger particles (>10 μm) can cause corneal irritation which leads to excessive tearing and rapid drainage from the ocular surface [52]. Apart from rapid tear clearance due to blinking, the excess volume of liquid in the ocular tear film promotes increased flow into the nasolacrimal duct within seconds [53]. Consequently, it is estimated that only 1 %–5 % of the total dose administered can penetrate into intraocular tissue. Drug loss may also occur through the adsorption into systemic circulation via near-surface blood vessels [54]. Another significant deficiency of ophthalmic suspensions pertains to their stability. These suspensions should not be reserved in freezers as the particles will agglomerate and fail to redisperse upon shaking [55]. Alterations in their crystal structure during storage may also affect particle size, thereby influencing drug solubility and bioavailability. Ointments typically formulated with mineral oil present additional challenges for developing water-based therapies. Their oily base can impede drug dissolution and consequently diminish therapeutic efficacy [56]. Furthermore, the presence of oil can compromise tear film clarity, resulting in patient complaints such as eyelash adhesion or blurred vision (Fig. 2). Fortunately, various strategies such as mucoadhesives, polymer nanomicelles and permeability enhancers have been utilized to address these issues [57].Fig. 2The administration methods and limitations of the anterior segment. Cul de sac implants

Devices positioned between the palpebral and bulbar conjunctival surfaces, including topical ophthalmic drug delivery devices (TODDD), Lacrisert, and Ocusert, have been developed to enhance anterior segment drug delivery. These devices are inserted into an extrabulbar potential space, rendering them less invasive and consequently safer than traditional intraocular implants. TODDD is a little concave conjunctival shell worn or self-administered thin plastic insert placed beneath the upper eyelid on the bulbar conjunctival surface, enabling continuous drug release over several months [58]. However, they may provoke discomforts to patients and carry risks of extrusion, migration, and postoperative infection. Similarly, Ocusert is a non-bioerodible drug-eluting device designed for glaucoma management that delivers either 20 or 40 mg/h of pilocarpine over a 7-day period [59]. Nonetheless, it presents certain drawbacks including requirements for patient instruction, device movement or loss, transient blurring of vision, occasional cutting sensation, miosis and high costs [60]. Lacrisert is an ophthalmic insert made from hydroxypropyl cellulose. It can be implanted into the inferior cul-de-sac for treating dry eye disease (DED) [61]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that Lacrisert significantly alleviates symptoms associated with moderate-to-severe DED, such as corneal or conjunctival staining and conjunctival hyperemia. However, adverse events linked to hydroxypropyl cellulose inserts include the blurry vision, ocular irritation, foreign body sensation, hypersensitivity, hyperemia, eyelid edema, photophobia, and caking or drying of viscous material on the eyelashes [62]. Providentially, nanomicelles can circumvent the problems of postoperative extrusion such as dead-end implants, while also reducing the irritation along with minimizing infection risk due to their diminutive size.

Punctal plugs

Punctal plug insertion represents a pivotal therapeutic strategy for managing DED associated with severe aqueous deficiency or connective tissue disorders. This technique entails the punctum occlusion to retain tears, thereby enhancing both the quality and quantity of the tear film [63,64]. Similar to other delivery systems, punctal plugs possess inherent limitations and infrequent side effects that may vary according to the type of plug used [65]. One major constraint of punctal plugs is their capacity to accommodate only low doses, which are typically insufficient for potent medications such as corticosteroids and prostaglandins. This is exemplified by the fact that Dextenza™, currently the only approved plug system, delivers the DEX while efficacy with other drugs remains pending [66,67]. Regarding adverse effects, mild irritation or scratchiness in the tear duct area following initial insertion is among the most common complaints. Additionally, punctal plug insertion has also been associated with complications such as epiphora, plug extrusion, corneal or conjunctival abrasion, punctal stenosis and allergic reactions to the plug material [3,68]. It should be noted that biofilms forming on the punctal plugs may bring about bacterial infection in ocular tissue or adnexa, and have also been linked to acute conjunctivitis [69]. Prospectively, nanomicelles offer promise for loading high-dose medications while improving drug bioavailability in the targeted ocular tissue. These advantageous capabilities would substantially reduce the incidence of bacterial infection during application.

Subconjunctival and episcleral implants

Implants positioned in the subconjunctiva and episclera can be employed for delivering drugs to the anterior segment. Typically, they are inserted through a little incision in the conjunctiva and directly contact with the sclera. While the sclera exhibits permeability to molecules up to 120 kDa, physiological mechanisms including episcleral blood supply and lymphatic drainage may potentially facilitate clearance of drugs that traverse the conjunctiva, thereby diminishing drug absorption [70]. Quantitative studies have verified that the lymphatic elimination rate of subconjunctivally administered hydrophilic drugs (e.g., sodium fluorescein) reaches 105 ng/min within 30–60 min, and such active clearance is almost absent in postmortem eyes, confirming its dependence on physiological pathways [71]. In live rats, over 99 % of sodium fluorescein released from sub-Tenon's implants is cleared from episcleral tissues within 3 h, further supporting the limiting effect of clearance mechanisms on drug retention [72]. The drawbacks of ocular inserts encompass foreign body sensation, burst release preceding controlled discharge, undesired migration around the eye (e.g. causing visual disturbances or movement towards the upper fornix), inadvertent loss during sleep or following eye rubbing and difficulty in insertion or removal [73,74]. Recent reports on subconjunctival implants, such as the PreserFlo Microshunt, also highlight rare but serious long-term complications like device exposure or extrusion, which are associated with factors like conjunctival ischemia and thin Tenon's capsule, potentially necessitating surgical intervention [75]. Overall, the incidence of foreign body sensation, increased lacrimation, eye pain, conjunctival hemorrhage, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and blurred vision, are more prevalent in patients receiving implants compared with those using topical eye drops. Prominently, polymer nanomicelles optimized by physicochemical methods could be employed to achieve controlled drug release while reducing tissue irritation and prolonging drug retention within the conjunctiva.

Drug-eluting contact lenses (DECLs)

Drug-eluting bandage contact lenses (BCLs) have been extensively investigated as a potential substitute for eye drops owing to their capacity to prolong drug residence time and enhance bioavailability [76]. However, the clinical translation of DECLs is hindered by diverse factors including the safety considerations, toxicity issues, decreased drug loading capacity and altered release kinetics. The insufficient oxygen permeability of DECLs can cause corneal hypoxia, resulting in symptoms such as DED, blurred vision, and even ulcerative keratitis. In chronic ocular diseases, challenges related to lens application and removal may arise due to dysfunction of intraocular muscles. Additionally, tear film proteins like lysozyme are prone to deposit on the surface of contact lenses (CLs). It has been observed that CLs with higher water content exhibit substantial protein adhesion in sharp contrast to those with lower water content. The deposition of proteins on the CLs surface induces microbial attachment account for contact CLs-induced infection and inflammation [77]. Encouragingly, the distinctive surface properties of polymer nanomicelles significantly mitigate the risk of infection caused by protein deposition on CLs surface, offering novel insights into ophthalmic drug delivery.

Drug-eluting intraocular lenses

Drug-eluting intraocular lenses (IOLs) are impregnated with antibiotics, corticosteroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs to prevent infection and inflammation following cataract surgery [78]. Shukla et al. proposed a strategy that entailed the application of multilayer coatings to conventional IOL devices [79]. The dominance of this multilayer method is to seamlessly integrate side-by-side layers of antibiotic and anti-inflammatory agents into the same device. Although intraocular delivery of anti-inflammatory medications may eliminate the need for daily dosing, adverse events such as elevated IOP resulted from prolonged steroid exposure may pose a trade-off between safety and therapeutic benefits. Moreover, it remains uncertain whether these multilayer drug coatings possess sufficient flexibility and durability to withstand folding and friction during insertion through a small corneal incision. It is well established that posterior capsule opacification (PCO) and postoperative ocular inflammation are primary concerns associated with IOL implantation. Epithelial cell proliferation, growth and adhesion on the posterior surface of the lens capsule can engender vision loss months or years after IOL implantation (secondary cataract) [80]. There are inherent trade-offs among these diverse IOL-based drug delivery strategies, underscoring the necessity for further studies to evaluate their feasibility in human subjects. Fortunately, polymer nanomicelles do not cause epithelial cell proliferation and adhesion on their surface. They effectively minimize potential irritation to the cornea and surrounding ocular tissue. Additionally, these polymer nanomicelles can significantly lower infection risks, demonstrating remarkable potential for the treatment of cataract.

Iontophoresis

Iontophoresis represents an active drug delivery strategy that employs low levels of electrical current to facilitate the transport of drug molecules through body tissue. It has been utilized in numerous clinical trials aimed at treating ocular diseases. However, this procedure may trigger potential complications such as epithelial edema, reduction in endothelial cells, burns and inflammatory infiltration. The severity of these adverse effects depends on the site of application, current density and duration of iontophoresis [81]. For instance, corneal iontophoresis with varying current densities and compound types (e.g., tobramycin, DEX phosphate) has been shown to induce corneal epithelial cell edema and a decline in endothelial cell count within a brief period (1–10 min) [82]. Clinical trials have shown that higher electric currents (with densities of 100–Y0 mA/cm2) could result in retinal and choroidal burns, edema, hemorrhagic necrosis and infiltrations in subjects [83,84]. Lam et al. applied a current density of 531 mA/cm2 in scleral iontophoresis and observed that the region and severity retinochoroidopathy increased in parallel with the prolonged application time. Furthermore, reports demonstrate the occurrence of corneal opacification toxicity and interstitial edema following corneal iontophoresis [85,86]. Compared with iontophoresis, polymer nanomicelles do not provoke thermal injury or epithelial edema, which brings promising prospects for the treatment of ocular diseases.

Microelectromechanical systems

A microelectromechanical system (MEMS) designed for treating ocular diseases comprises a fluid reservoir connected to a cannula implanted in the subconjunctival space. The manually controlled pump includes a unidirectional valve to regulate drug delivery [87]. One of the challenges associated with pump is the potential risk of infection during or following surgical implantation procedures. Similar to the glaucoma aqueous drainage devices, precise positioning of the tube in the anterior chamber is crucial for minimizing traumatic damage to the corneal endothelium, pupillary margin, iris and lens. It is universally acknowledged that delicate placement of the tube into the posterior segment is required to prevent complications such as retinal detachment. Continued minute movements of the tube element relevant to the cornea and macroscopic contact of the cornea with an improperly positioned long tube are among the most prevalent causes of early postoperative damage [88]. It is evident that meticulous attention must be devoted during both fabrication and implantation processes to ensure a shorter beveled tube with a smoother tip. Polymer nanomicelles

Concept of polymer nanomicelles

Polymer nanomicelles are core-shell structures comprised by amphiphilic polymers, promoting the slow release and targeted delivery of hydrophobic medications to designated sites at optimal dosages. The polymeric chains compose of hydrophilic 'heads' and hydrophobic 'tails,' self-assembling into a hydrophilic corona and a hydrophobic core through reversible interactions [89]. A broad spectrum of water-insoluble therapeutics is encapsulated within permeable hydrophobic cores, thereby augmenting markedly their solubility and facilitating their passage through biological obstacles while reducing drug loss. The perpetuation of this trend is primarily attributed to the recent surge in biotechnological innovations and the escalating reliance for biocompatible materials. Micellization within aqueous milieus is governed by a complex interplay of intermolecular forces, with electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces being particularly influential [90]. The nanoscale dimensions and hydrophobic cores of polymer nanomicelles effectively surmount therapeutic challenges related to poor water solubility and the impediments posed by biological barriers. In aqueous solutions, amphiphilic polymers as well as block copolymers (such as diblock, triblock and pentablock copolymers) act as surfactants, diminishing the surface tension within the polymer-water interface. As polymeric chains continuously contribute to the system, their concentration reaches a threshold that induces aggregation precipitating micelle formation. This pivotal minimum concentration required for micelle formation, known as the CMC, acts as a pivotal parameter in gauging the thermodynamic stability of micelles [91,92]. More importantly, amphiphilic polymers possess lower CMC values compared with small-molecule surfactants, thereby endowing polymer nanomicelles with superior stability against dilution. Growing evidence indicates that nanomicelles can be utilized for addressing ocular surface inflammation, retinal degeneration and vascular proliferative disorders [93]. The amalgamation of polymeric materials with pharmaceutical agents promises long-term therapeutic drug delivery while opening new avenues for resolving the challenging complications encountered in clinical settings.

Polymer nanomicelles support materials

Ideally, the polymers employed to prepare nanomicelles should be biodegradable and biocompatible. The biodegradation of polymer ensures efficient clearance of inactive polymer from ocular tissue. It is imperative that the degradation products should be non-toxic and non-inflammatory, particularly to the delicate neural retina layers. Carrier materials for polymer nanomicelles can be categorized according to their origin into two distinct groups: natural and synthetic polyers (Fig. 3). Notably, the prevalence of high polymer materials, coupled with their exceptional biocompatibility and the benign nature of their degradation products, renders them highly attractive for chemical modifications. Natural materials

The spectrum of natural polymer nanomicelles carrier materials encompass hyaluronic acid (HA), albumin, and chitosan. Chitosan is a cationic polysaccharide with bioadhesive properties derived from D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine through deacetylation. Being a cationic molecule, it can facilitate the interactions of nanomicelles with negatively charged cell surfaces improving precorneal retention and ocular bioavailability. The mucoadhesiveness of chitosan is correlated not only with electrostatic interactions but also with hydrophobic effects and hydrogen bonds [94]. Moreover, the solubility of chitosan in aqueous solutions is restricted in acidic environments, where it undergoes a solution-gel transition when pH exceeds 6.2. Given the slightly acidic pH of tears, which ranges from 6.5 to 7.60, chitosan can function as a pH-responsive material. It has been widely harnessed for in-situ gel formation in ophthalmic drug delivery. In a recent study, chitosan/F127 micelles were fabricated for metipranolol at higher chitosan concentrations (0.3–0.8 %). At these concentrations, chitosan could enhance the metipranolol loading along with raised micelle zeta potential [31]. However, the first-hour drug release from chitosan/F127 micelles was 59 %, 69 %, and 88 % with 0.3 %, 0.5 % and 0.8 % of chitosan content, respectively. Despite an initial rapid drug release, 0.5 % chitosan/F127 micelles increased the area under the curve (AUC) of metipranolol by 1.67-fold compared with commercially available eye drops. In contrast, metipranolol-loaded F127 micelle exhibited similar AUC to that of commercially available eye drops. Therefore, the increment in AUC observed for chitosan/F127 micelles can be ascribed to the bioadhesive property imparted by chitosan. Alginate, a natural linear polymer composed of β-(1–4) mannuronic acid and β-(1–4) guluronic acid blocks, exhibits advantageous mucoadhesive properties through hydrogen bonding with mucin glycoproteins by a large number of hydroxyl groups, despite its negative charge [17]. A combination of chitosan and alginate in nanoparticle formulations, such as alginate-chitosan microspheres, has been shown to extend the retention time of drugs like azelastine in the conjunctival sac and on the corneal surface [62]. These microspheres demonstrated 73 % drug entrapment, 65 % mucin binding efficiency and controlled drug release over 8 h. Additionally, HA has been shown to raise corneal adhesion and drug utilization due to its mucosal adhesion potential [95]. The encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs within polymer micelles displaying high anti-inflammatory capacity, followed by complexation with HA, can augment the anti-inflammatory response and facilitate efficient drug delivery. Other common natural polymers utilized as drug carriers include albumin and gelatin. Albumin particles containing the anti-inflammatory piroxicam have been reported to increase bioavailability by 1.8-fold upon topical ocular administration in rabbits compared with standard commercial eyedrops [96]. Albumin has also been combined with chitosan to form microspheres loaded with anesthetics like tetracaine and atropine, which significantly prolonged the duration and peak effect of the drugs [67,97]. In summary, these natural polymeric materials are viable candidates for preparing polymeric nanosized micelles as drug delivery vehicles.

Synthetic materials

Biocompatible copolymers including PEG, PEO, PAM, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyethyleneimine (PEI), and PEO-based polyelectrolyte complexes formed by PEO and polyelectrolytes constitute the hydrophilic segments of polymer nanomicelles. Notably, PEG, particularly with a molecular weight range of 1000–12,000 Da, is the predominant hydrophilic component owing to its superior aqueous solubility, low toxicity, and non-immunogenicity. However, within the context of polymer nanomicelle drug delivery system, the degree of compatibility between the hydrophobic segment and the pharmaceutical molecule complemented by the dimensions of the hydrophobic chains, predominantly dictates the efficiency of drug encapsulation. Moreover, the characteristics of the hydrophobic segment inherently affect the release kinetics of the entrapped medications within the micellar structure. Commonly investigated hydrophobic constituents encompass biodegradable polyesters and amino acids, such as polylactide (PLLA), polyglycolide (PGA), polycaprolactone (PCL), polylactic glycolate (PLGA), polyaspartic acid (PAsp), polybenzylaspartic acid (PBLA), and polyglutamic acid (PGlu). Among these, PCL, PGA, and PLA have garnered the FDA approval owing to their exceptional biodegradability and biocompatibility. PLLA faces limitations in clinical application due to its hydrophobicity, slow degradation rate and acidic degradation products. Therefore, the combination of PLLA and PEG exhibits excellent hydrophilicity and biocompatibility, making it a promising medication carrier matrix. Furthermore, the incorporation of PEG can expedite the degradation of PLLA and mitigate the acidity of degradation products, thus enhancing the clinical utility of PLLA. Additionally, certain hydrophobic block polymers, like poloxamer (polyethylene oxide, polypropylene oxide, polyethylene oxide), can modulate ATP levels in cells, alter cell membrane fluidity, inhibit the efflux of P-gp associated with multi-drug resistance and resolve the challenge of multi-drug resistance. In short, these hydrophobic and hydrophilic copolymers are poised to become the fundamental materials in nanomicelles fabrication.

Preparation techniques of micelles

Thus far, the most prevalent methods for micelle formation mainly include direct dissolution, solvent evaporation, film hydration, dialysis method and emulsion process [98] (Fig. 4). The direct dissolution approach is frequently employed for preparing micelles from copolymers with high-water solubility. This technique involves dissolving the drug and block copolymer directly in an aqueous medium (distilled deionized water or buffer). The preparation may require stirring, heating or sonication to facilitate the loading of drugs into nanomicelles. This method is generally suitable for moderately hydrophobic polymers. For instance, poloxamer-poloxamine mixed micelles are successfully prepared to solubilize the antiretroviral drug efavirenz (EFV), resulting in a significant increase (8400-fold) in apparent solubility and achieving high encapsulation efficiency when adding the drug directly to the dispersed copolymers in aqueous medium [99]. This method has advantages such as simplicity of operation, relatively short processing times and the avoidance of organic solvents when the polymers along with drugs are dispersed together in an aqueous solvent. Another commonly used method is solvent evaporation, in which the drug and polymer are co-dissolved in an organic solvent (such as tetrahydrofuran, dimethyl sulfoxide, acetonitrile, methanol, acetone or N, N-dimethylformamide). The organic solvent can then be removed using a rotary evaporator. The selection of the solvent markedly influences the physical properties and drug encapsulation efficiency of the micelles. For instance, the use of DMF has been shown to increase drug loading efficiency compared with DMSO [100]. The mechanism of micelle formation is primarily dependent on the solvent removal modality. With water-miscible organic solvents, the solution can be dialyzed against deionized water, leading to the assembly of drug-loaded micelles when the organic phase is removed. This process is also known as the "dialysis method". Alternatively, the organic phase can be evaporated to produce a thin film that favors polymer-drug interactions and upon rehydration with an aqueous phase, the micelles are formed. This method is also referred to as the "thin film method". Generally, methods such as solvent evaporation and film hydration yield higher encapsulation efficiencies compared with direct dissolution and dialysis methods [101]. For example, the aqueous solubility of biphenyl dimethyl dicarboxylate in a mPEG2000-PLA1000 carrier system was 13.2 mg/ml using the film hydration method, compared with 2 mg/ml with the dialysis method [102]. For water-immiscible organic solvents (such as dichloromethane, chloroform or ethyl acetate), they can be added to a water-based medium and emulsified to form an oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion. The polymer rearranges into form micelles during the emulsification process, and the organic solvent is subsequently removed. This technique is also known as the "emulsification method". It is worth noting that all these techniques require sterilization and lyophilization processes; the freeze-dried mixture can be reconstituted to obtain drug-loaded micelles [103]. Co-solvents such as dimethylacetamide and tert-butanol are often used owing to their high vapor pressure, which facilitates rapid sublimation followed by lyophilization [104]. Micelles produced by this method also exhibit adequate shelf-life and high water dispersibility. Additionally, several alternative methodologies have been established for micelle preparation, including the blank micelle loading method, solid dispersion technology, supercritical fluid evaporation and microphase separation method (Fig. 4). The blank micelle loading method entails the formation of a blank micelle from a block copolymer, into which the drug is subsequently dissolved and diffuses to enter the micelle. Solid dispersion technology involves dissolving the drug and polymer in an organic solvent, followed by solvent removal to yield a drug-polymer mixed matrix. Upon the addition of a water-based solution to this preheated matrix, self-assembly occurs and results in micelle formation. This technique can enhance the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs and refine particle size distribution; however, it has low drug loading capacity and compromised micelle stability. Microphase separation method is to dissolve excessive drugs and polymers in organic solvents that are immiscible with water, and water-based media is gradually added during continuous stirring. As the water-based media increases, the polymer self-assembles to form micelles with a core-shell structure. Hence this method is also called precipitation method. The drug loading capacity of micelles prepared by microphase separation is influenced by the ratio of drug to polymer and the ratio of organic solvent to water-based medium. Lastly, the supercritical fluid evaporation method, a more recent and sophisticated approach, involves pressurizing a solution to dissolve the polymer and drug within a supercritical fluid, followed by rapid depressurization to induce micelle formation and solvent evaporation [94]. It can then be washed with water to yield stable aqueous solutions. Common supercritical fluids employed in this progress include trifluoromethane and carbon dioxide. Utilizing supercritical CO2 instead of organic solvents, the hydrophobic blocks of the polymer are solubilized in supercritical CO2, while the hydrophilic blocks remain dissolved in the aqueous medium. When CO2 transitions to a gas and diffuses out, the hydrophobic chains aggregate to form micellar cores. The use of supercritical fluids in the preparation of polymeric micelles (PMs) offers advantages such as expedited processing times, facile solvent removal and the elimination of prolonged freeze-drying or dialysis steps. Notably, the removal method of organic solvent used also impacts the drug-loading efficiency of the micelles, underscoring the necessity to develop various solvent removal systems to optimize micelles.

While the methodologies outlined above are robust for laboratory-scale preparation, they present formidable challenges for large-scale Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) production, a critical step for clinical translation. A primary concern is batch-to-batch variability in critical quality attributes such as particle size, polydispersity index, and drug loading efficiency. Subtle fluctuations in parameters like mixing shear force, temperature control, or solvent evaporation rates during scale-up can lead to inconsistent micelle characteristics, which in turn may alter ocular biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, the choice of organic solvent is not only a matter of drug solubilization and encapsulation efficiency but is also directly linked to ocular biocompatibility. Residual solvents with inherent cytotoxicity, such as dimethylformamide (DMF) or chloroform, must be scrupulously removed to meet stringent regulatory thresholds for ophthalmic products. The difficulty and cost of eliminating these solvents on a large scale make the selection of safer, volatile alternatives like ethanol or acetone a crucial consideration in early formulation design.

Classification of polymer nanomicelles

Polymer nanomicelles can be categorized from various perspectives according to the architecture of the constituent polymers (e.g., block, graft, or random copolymers). Notably, polymer nanomicelles used for ocular treatment can be classified according to their functional roles within the formulation. Typically, they can be classified into direct carrier for direct drug delivery to ocular tissue or as an auxiliary component integrated into other drug delivery platforms. This classification meets the actual needs of ophthalmic formulations as it distinguishes between independent micellar systems and synergistic micellar composites, each with different design principles and therapeutic applications.

Direct carrier

Polymer nanomicelles are primary delivery vehicles for direct ocular drug delivery encompassing both surface-modified and unmodified PMs. Unmodified micelles encompass random copolymers, block copolymers and graft copolymers, representing three prevalent polymer architectures. These architectures primarily differ in the arrangement of their polymer chains. Block copolymers are characterized by distinct monomer units arranged sequentially. This sequential arrangement creates clear interfaces between different blocks, which endow them with unique physicochemical properties. In vivo, block polymer nanomicelles are less likely to be recognized as foreign entities by immune cells, and their hydrophilic shell aids in evading the reticuloendothelial system, thus reducing the exclusion from bloodstream [105]. Zhang et al. successfully synthesized a triblock copolymer, specifically poly(methyl methacrylate-vinyl acetate)-polyethylene glycol-poly (methyl methacrylate-vinyl acetate), which significantly diminishes surface hydrophobicity and protein adsorption. This copolymer displays superior antifouling properties [106] (Fig. 5a). This material has been utilized for antifouling applications on ophthalmic device surfaces. Graft polymeric micelles, composed of amphiphilic graft polymers with hydrophobic backbones and hydrophilic branch chains, have been used as injectable polymer materials due to their superior biocompatibility and biodegradability. These polymers self-assemble into nanoparticles which bear a core-shell structure when dispersed in water, with the core formed by the hydrophobic backbone and the shell by the hydrophilic branches. A prominent example is Soluplus, a biodegradable and amphiphilic graft copolymer consisted of polyethylene-caprolactam-polyvinyl acetate-polyethylene glycol (PVC-PVA-PEG). The copolymer enhances the micellization or nanoparticle formation of insoluble drugs, largely due to its low CMC [107] (Fig. 5c). Soluplus micelles, demonstrating superior eye tolerance and corneal permeability, have been effectively used as topical ocular medication delivery systems for these poorly water-soluble drugs [108]. Random copolymers, with the random organization of monomer units within the polymer chain and devoid of discernible block structures, typically exhibit favorable solubility and fluidity. Surface-modified polymer nanomicelles, a prevalent class in advanced drug delivery systems, include polyelectrolyte micelles and intelligent polymer nanomicelles. These systems, comprised of charged block or graft copolymers, generate nanomicelles through electrostatic or hydrogen bonding with oppositely charged species, such as polymers, DNA, polyelectrolytes and enzymes. The aggregation process of polyelectrolyte micelles into a core structure is dictated by a suite of intermolecular forces, including hydrophobic interactions, metal chelation, electrostatics and hydrogen bonding. Conventional methods for preparing nanomicelles predominantly contain the synthesis of covalently bonded amphiphilic block or graft copolymers that self-assemble in aqueous environments. This preparation process requires significant amounts of organic solvents, and the primary drug loading methodologies (namely physical entrapment and adsorption) frequently yield a suboptimal loading rate for ionic drugs. Recently, there has been a pronounced shift towards utilizing high polymer materials with superior biological properties as drug carriers. Polyelectrolyte micelles, with their charged cores, are adept at carrying a variety of charged drugs including DNA and RNA. These micelles have advantageous stability and offering a distinct application in gene delivery [109]. Given the large molecular chains of polyelectrolytes and their ionizable characteristics akin to small molecules, they could be well-suited for sustained and controlled drug delivery. Presently, research on micellar delivery systems for ophthalmic diseases remains relatively nascent. Additionally, “smart” polymer nanomicelles or stimulus-responsive polymeric micelle delivery systems, are formed by stimulus-responsive polymers. These polymers exhibit changes in properties such as morphology, hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity and permeation rate in response to specific environmental stimuli, including pH, temperature, ionic strength, and enzymatic activity. Some of these polymers can respond to multiple stimuli, enabling the precision-controlled release of the required therapeutic dose at necessary timepoint and specific location [110]. Currently, two main synthesis strategies for pH-sensitive polymer nanomicelles have been identified: one based on the protonation or deprotonation of acid-sensitive bonds for controlled drug release, and the other utilizing acid-sensitive bonds as linkers between micelles and drugs [111]. In conclusion, the diverse array of nanomicelles is poised to play a pivotal role in the future of ophthalmic drug delivery.

The auxiliary components of the carrier

PMs can assume the function of auxiliary components within drug delivery systems by integrating into the carrier structure. This can be exemplified by the encapsulation of micelles within hydrogels or the incorporation into other materials, such as CLs [112]. In micelle-hydrogel systems, the encapsulation of micelles within hydrogels not only sequesters drugs within the micelles but also leverages the adhesive properties to reduce drug loss [113]. However, different polymers also exhibit various characteristics. Some polymers have the properties of gelling because of their special fragments, allow undergoing sol–gel transformation in response to changes in temperature, pH and other factors [96,97]. Hydrogel systems loaded with micelles are particularly promising, as they harness the combined advantages of both micelles and hydrogels. For instance, Wang et al. developed an FK 506-coated PEG-PBG polymeric micelle (292 nm in size) that was co-encapsulated with ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) in a temperature-sensitive hydrogel [114]. This chitosan-based hydrogel could gel at 37 °C under physiological conditions, thereby reducing the release rate of FK 506 compared with micelles alone. It has also demonstrated potent efficacy in protecting retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and repairing the optic nerve in a rabbit model of optic nerve injury (Fig. 5b). Micelle-embedded contact lenses (CLs-M) represent another promising auxiliary delivery platform. CLs, known for their portability and high adhesion, have been extensively utilized as ocular drug delivery devices. It is noteworthy that the softest contact lenses are composed of hydrophilic monomers, which may restrict the loading capacity of hydrophobic drugs [115]. The incorporation of polymer micelles not only enhances drug loading but also the sustained release profile of the drug. Nevertheless, the incorporation of certain insoluble drugs can diminish the optical transmittance and swelling characteristics of contact lenses [116]. The integration of micelles presents a solution to this challenge. This micellar loaded contact lens can significantly enhance the slow and controlled release of medications, which is attributed to a two-step diffusion process: the drug first permeates through the micellar structure into the CLs hydrogel, followed by its release through the hydrogel matrix. Researchers have crafted CLs-M capable of sustaining the release of timolol and latanoprost concurrently for glaucoma therapy. In vivo pharmacokinetic studies in rabbit eyes demonstrated the sustained release of timolol and latanoprost lasted for up to 120 h and 96 h in tear fluid, respectively. There was marked enhancement of the mean residence time and bioavailability for both timolol and latanoprost delivered via CLs-M as opposed to eye drops [117] (Fig. 5d). In this context, the utilization of PMs as indirect carriers significantly amplifies drug efficacy, positioning them as a promising delivery system for ophthalmic applications.

Binding and release of drugs in micelles

The primary mechanisms for incorporating drugs into polymer micelles are physical encapsulation, chemical conjugation and electrostatic interaction (Fig. 6a–b). Physical encapsulation and chemical conjugation are predominantly employed for the incorporation of small molecule drugs, whereas electrostatic interactions are primarily utilized for the inclusion of nucleic acid and protein drugs. Physical encapsulation involves the embedding of drugs within the micellar core through hydrophobic interactions, without the requirement for specific functional groups during chemical bonding. This method leverages the hydrophobic interactions within the micellar core and the hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding properties of insoluble drugs. These interactions enhance drug solubility in polymer micelles and render the approach applicable to the majority of hydrophobic drugs. The compatibility between the drug and the micellar core significantly influences the solubilization efficacy of the micelles, with greater compatibility and higher drug loading capacities. Moreover, the physical encapsulation of drugs within micelles is entirely contingent upon the meticulous control of the preparation process. Chemical conjugation for drug loading involves the covalent attachment of the hydrophobic ends in drug molecules to the amphiphilic polymer materials. As the materials self-assemble into micelles, the drugs conjugated at the hydrophobic ends are directly incorporated into the micellar core. The nature of the chemical bond not only determines the drug loading capacity of the micelles but also impacts their stability. The drug loading process for chemically conjugated medications is concurrent with the self-assembly of micelle formation. Electrostatic drug loading, on the other hand, involves the binding of charged drugs to polymers through electrostatic interactions, with drug loading occurring concomitantly with micelle formation. This method is particularly applicable for nucleic acid and protein drugs. Each unit structure of nucleic acid drugs is negatively charged, whereas protein drugs may be electrically neutralized with positive and negative charges at the same time, making nucleic acid drugs more amenable to electrostatic loading compared with protein drugs. Drug release of polymer-drug conjugates occurs predominantly via two mechanisms: one is that the polymer micelle degrades and then breaks the covalent bond between the micelle and the drug to achieve drug release; the other is that initial cleavage of the covalent bond between micelle and drugs, followed by the drug release through diffusion (Fig. 6c–f). Beyond these fundamental drug release mechanisms, the release profile from micelles is influenced by a multitude of factors. These include the physicochemical properties of the drug, the composition of the block copolymer, the rate of polymer degradation, the diffusion of drug molecules from the micellar core, environmental stimulations and micelle stability. If the drugs exist in the molecular state, it can accelerate the release process. Conversely, if the drugs exist in crystalline state, strong interactions between the crystal surface and hydrophobic fragments will decelerate drug release. An increase in micellar drug load, causing the drug to transition from a molecular to a crystalline form, can also result in a slower release rate. The molecular volume of the drug impacts release kinetics; larger volume is correlated with smaller diffusion coefficient and lower release rate. The location of the drug within the micelle, determined by its interaction parameters with hydrophobic and hydrophilic blocks, also affects release dynamics. Most hydrophobic drugs are situated in the micellar core, while amphiphilic drugs are found within the outer shell and at the interface between the shell and the core. If the drug is predominantly located in the micellar core, a longer hydrophobic block and larger core size can lead to slower drug release rate. Furthermore, modulating their reactivity to specific microenvironmental stimuli (such as temperature, pH, enzymes, REDOX reactions) can precisely control drug release kinetics, enabling immediate, pulsed, sustained or delayed drug release. For instance, benzaldehyde-based modifiers are frequently used to functionalize polymer carriers, aiming to introduce active carbonyl groups for the synthesis of acid-sensitive benzoate-Schiff base linkers. These micelles, containing benzoate-Schiff base links, exhibit robust stability under physiological conditions but are readily disintegrated in the intracellular acidic microenvironment, thereby promoting the accurate release of encapsulated drugs [13].

It is crucial to maintain a delicate balance between the desired drug release kinetics and micelle stability during therapeutic activities. The stability of PMs is influenced by both dynamic and thermodynamic factors. Excellent kinetic stability is characterized by slow dissociation into monomers, even under significant dilution below CMC. Thermodynamic stability is connected with the formation of micelles and the balance between monomers and organized structures. In particular, PMs exhibit higher thermodynamic stability than conventional surfactant micelles, making them less sensitive to decomposition at lower concentrations. The enhanced stability is directly associated with lower CMC values [14]. However, the micelle breakdown is necessary for drug release. Excluding the influence of copolymer biodegradation rate, a low CMC value may result in unsatisfactory drug release efficiency. Accordingly, when formulating micellar DDS, drug release kinetics and stability must be considered concurrently. In summary, the binding and release mechanisms of drugs in nanomicelles dictate their ultimate delivery efficiency and therapeutic outcome. It is noteworthy that, due to their vast differences in molecular size and physicochemical properties, the delivery strategies for small molecule drugs and large molecule biologics (e.g., proteins, nucleic acids) present distinct challenges and opportunities. General advantages of polymer nanomicelles for ocular drug delivery

Polymer nanomicelles have demonstrated multiple advantages in ophthalmic drug delivery and are more competitive than new carriers such as liposomes, polymer nanoparticles, implants, hydrogels, and DECLs (Table 5). It has broad application potential in the treatment of ophthalmic diseases (Fig. 7). Firstly, by using the hydrophobic nuclei formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic polymers, lipophilic drugs can be encapsulated within the nuclei, increasing their apparent solubility by tens to hundreds of times. This facilitates the preparation of transparent eye drops and enhances patient compliance. Secondly, by introducing adhesive polymers (such as chitosan and hyaluronic acid) into the hydrophilic shell, the interaction with the tear film mucus layer can be enhanced, the tear flushing and nasolacrimal duct drainage can be slowed down, thereby prolonging the drug's residence time on the ocular surface and improving the absorption efficiency of the cornea and conjunctiva. Again, the size of the nanomicelles is usually less than 100 nm, and the surface properties are adjustable, which is conducive to crossing the lipid barrier of the corneal epithelium; Surface modification can also reduce local irritation, avoid the effects of external pumps such as P-GP, and significantly improve intraocular bioavailability. Finally, the hydrophilic shell is often composed of polyethylene glycol or other biocompatible polymers, which facilitates further covalent connection to targeted ligands (such as peptides, antibodies) or responsive groups (pH, enzymes), enabling precise delivery to specific ocular tissues and controlled release under pathological stimulation, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and reducing non-targeted side effects. Dry eye disease (DED)

DED is a multifactorial malady of ocular surface characterized by the disruption in tear film homeostasis accompanied by ocular symptoms. Pivotal etiological factors comprise the tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, inflammation, impairment to the ocular surface as well as neurosensory abnormalities [118]. The standard remedy for DED typically involves non-medicated artificial tears, eye drops and lubricants aimed at preserving moisture on the ocular surface. However, the dilution of tears and drainage through the nasolacrimal ducts result in an exceedingly low concentration of topical medications, leading to inadequate corneal penetration and a brief duration of action on the ocular surface for these eye drops. To surmount the foregoing dilemmas, Fan et al. constructed a novel formulation of pimecrolimus nanomicelles (PNM) for managing Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca. The in vitro release profile revealed that PNM exhibited sustained release behavior compared with free pimecrolimus oil-based eye drops. The results verified that PNM had superior therapeutic efficacy among all formulations due to its enhanced drug encapsulation capacity, favorable permeability and durative release [119]. This study also underscores the potential of nanomicelles in transforming oily poorly bioavailable drugs into effective aqueous formulations for DED, potentially improving patient compliance and therapeutic outcomes. Myricetin (Myr), a naturally occurring flavonoid, has poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability during ocular administration. To enhance the therapeutic efficacy, it was encapsulated within polyvinyl caprolactam-polyvinyl acetate-polyethylene glycol graft copolymer (PVCL-PVA-PEG) micelles to augment its clinical applicability in ophthalmologic practice. The Myr micelle ophthalmic solution demonstrated distinguished enhancements in both cellular uptake and corneal permeation, alongside significant improvements in its antioxidant activity and anti-inflammatory efficacy [120]. Moreover, cholesterol-hyaluronate (C-HA) micelle-embedded CLs was developed for hydrophobic drug loading and controlled drug delivery. The C-HA micelle-loaded CLs showed statistically significant improvements in wettability and mechanical strength, thereby maintaining the satisfactory optical transmittance. Drug release tests revealed that the C-HA micelle-loaded CLs realized the controlled delivery of drug for more than 12 days, and demonstrated profound therapeutic effects on DED rabbit model [121]. In severe cases, immunosuppressive agents such as CsA have been employed for treating DED. CsA, a hydrophobic molecule, has poor ocular tolerance, low bioavailability and instability. Researchers also investigated methoxy poly (ethylene glycol)-poly (lactide) polymer (mPEG-PLA) micelles as alternative vehicles for solubilizing and delivering CsA to the eye. The in vitro stability assessments indicated that CsA-loaded mPEG-PLA remained stable for at least 3 months while realizing the sustained release of CsA from micelles. These formulations achieved a 4.5-fold increase in retention at eyes compared with 0.05 % CsA emulsion, thus prolongating therapeutic effects against DED [122,123]. To further actualize targeted delivery of CsA within DED-afflicted tissue (cornea and conjunctiva), researchers developed a novel nanomicellar solution (OTX-101). It has been found that concentrations of CsA in corneal tissue and superior bulbar conjunctiva accumulated in a dose-dependent manner [124,125]. In conclusion, nanomicelles escalate drug distribution within targeted ocular tissue while improving permeability, bringing new insights into remedies for DED.

DED can destroy the integrity of corneal epithelium and endothelium alongside disrupt tear film stability, resulting in reduced area of goblet cells within the conjunctiva. In order to alleviating these pathologies, novel dipotassium glycyrrhizinate micelles containing naringenin (DG-NAR) have tested in a benzalkonium chloride induced DED mouse model. This therapeutic strategy could augment tear volume and corneal sensitivity, ameliorate the histopathological symptoms and restore corneal epithelial integrity [126]. Zhao et al. developed m-PEG-PPG-PCL polymer micelles loaded with the N-acylethanolamine acid amidase (NAAA) inhibitor F96 (F96-MPPP). Studies have confirmed that compared with the aqueous solution of the drug, this micellar preparation can significantly prolong the ocular surface retention time of the drug. In DED model mice, F96-mPPP treatment can significantly increase tear secretion, restore the density of conjunctival goblet cells, reduce corneal fluorescein staining score, and inhibit corneal epithelial cell apoptosis [127]. Additionally, micelle-based eye drops (more specifically p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) inhibitor Losmapimod (Los)-loaded and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger Tempo (Tem)-conjugated cationic polypeptide micelles, designated as MTem/Los) were configurated for DED management. Cationic MTem/Los enhanced the ocular retention of Tem and Los through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged mucin on the cornea, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing dosing frequency. Mechanistically, MTem/Los efficaciously mitigated excessive ROS production, downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, inhibited macrophage proinflammatory phenotypic transformation and prevented cellular apoptosis. Therapeutically, the micelles effectively reversed corneal epithelial defects, restored goblet cell function and strengthened tear secretion, thus breaking the vicious cycle and relieving DED [128]. Melatonin can also mitigate ROS production and preserve mitochondrial function; however, the low solubility constrains its application. In this context, the Melatonin-loaded polyvinyl caprolactam-polyvinyl acetate-polyethylene glycol graft copolymer micelles (Mel-Mic) were fabricated to improve the solubility of Mel in aqueous solutions and decrease the apoptosis rate of human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs). In vivo studies revealed that mice treated with Mel-Mic solution exhibited markedly improved clinical parameters including enhanced tear production and reduced loss of goblet cells in a dose-dependent manner [129]. Keratitis

Bacterial keratitis (BK)

BK is a rapidly progressive infectious corneal disorder that can cause corneal perforation, intraocular infection, permanent corneal opacification and vision loss [130]. The local response to corneal bacterial infection involves an inflammatory process that is featured by the infiltration of inflammatory cells (e.g., macrophages), overproduction of ROS and accumulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines [131]. Consequently, ROS scavenging strategies aimed at mitigating inflammatory responses alongside pathogen elimination are necessary for the management of BK. However, the ocular bioavailability of potent antioxidants (e.g., ferulic acid) via topical administration is confined due to their poor solubility, low stability and short residence time. Investigators fabricated ferulic acid within a nanocomposite platform comprising nanogels and micelles to facilitate efficient drug delivery. These micelle-nanogel formulations augmented the solubility of ferulic acid-and realized the controlled drug release in vitro [132]. Dipotassium glycyrrhizinate (DG), the dipotassium salt of glycyrrhizin known for its high aqueous solubility, serves as a widely utilized anti-inflammatory agent. Song et al. built a DG-based nanomicelle ophthalmic solution encapsulating thymol (DG-THY) to improve the therapeutic outcomes for eye diseases. DG-THY has demonstrated remarkable enhancements regarding both in vitro antioxidant activity and membrane permeation properties for THY. DG-THY ophthalmic solution substantially alleviated inflammatory symptoms and ocular infections in rabbit models through decreasing colony-forming units [133].

Additionally, Levofloxacin (LEV) and chondroitin sulfate (CS) were co-encapsulated into poly (phenylboronic acid-(3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2(1H)-one))-co-(2-lactobionamidoethyl methacrylate) (p(PBA-DHPM-r-LAMA)) glycopolymeric micelles to address BK. The phenylboronic acid-(3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2(1H)-one) (PBA-DHPM) moieties not only serve as active targeting ligands that facilitating epithelial penetration and bacterial anchorage, but also function effectively as ROS-scavenging agents to mitigate inflammation. This antioxidant glycopolymeric micelle platform is endowed with multiple functionalities encompassing bacterial eradication, ROS scavenging and promotion of wound healing [134]. It has been reported that Chitosan-PLGA (C) conjugate was combined with poloxamers (F68/127) in the ratio of 1: 10 to formulate moxifloxacin (M)-encapsulated mixed micelles (MMs) designated as M@CF68(10)Ms and M@CF127(10) Ms. Both of them exhibited high cellular uptake rates, higher muco-adhesiveness and significant antibacterial effects. Notably, M@CF127(10)Ms harvested superior therapeutic outcomes in P. aeruginosa and S. aureus-infected BK mouse model by effectively reducing corneal bacterial load while preventing corneal damage [135] (Fig. 9a). Fungal keratitis (FK)

FK is an infectious corneal disease caused by pathogenic fungi. The fungus can invade and colonize the corneal stroma through defects in the epithelial layer, triggering a host inflammatory responses and corneal tissue necrosis [136]. Current antifungal strategies for treating FK still face challenges like low therapeutic efficacy and bioavailability in clinical applications, primarily owing to the inadequate corneal penetration and limited residence time at the targeted tissue. Hence, it is essential to investigate an amphiphilic block copolymer-based nanomicelles with preeminent corneal penetration capacity to combat fungal invasion [137]. Itraconazole, a water-insoluble triazole agent has been used for managing FK, and yielding pronounced antifungal activity against Aspergillus, Curvularia and Candida species. Sayed et al. prepared and optimized β-Cyclodextrin-consolidated micelle dispersions (CCMD) to reinforce the corneal permeability of itraconazole. The optimized CCMD demonstrated a rapid in vitro release rate along with significantly higher ex vivo corneal flux of itraconazole compared with conventional drug suspensions. Histological evaluations revealed substantial reductions in clinical symptoms (corneal opacity, anterior chamber white lesions with hypopyon) in rabbit eyes after treatment [138]. Sertaconazole nitrate (STZ), a novel broad-spectrum antifungal drug faces limitations regarding its poor solubility and short residence time at lesions. Notably, researchers have developed the STZ-loaded binary mixed micelles (STZ-MMs) enriched with various penetration enhancers. These STZ-MMs significantly strengthened aqueous solubility of STZ compared with traditional STZ suspensions. They also showed superior permeability, while delivering considerably increased levels of STZ into the corneal epithelium of rabbits. The obtained results suggest that the polymer nanomicelles could serve as the valid candidate for Keratomycosis-targeted therapy [139]. Voriconazole (VRC) is a second-generation triazole antifungal agent; however, its short contact time with the cornea limits effective delivery through the corneal epithelium into deeper corneal layers. An optimal formulation (OTMS) aimed at incorporating VRC into ternary micellar systems (TMSs) for the topical remedy of ocular mycosis. OTMS demonstrated acceptable stability over 3 months. Fungal susceptibility testing using Candida albicans revealed that OTMS outperformed VRC suspension in terms of greater and more sustained growth inhibition [140]. Additionally, researchers synthesized a phenylboronic acid-conjugated chitosan oligosaccharide-vitamin E copolymer (PBA-CS-VE) to establish the VRC-loaded nanomicelles. In vitro mucin binding and ex vivo eyeball adhesion assays indicated that this copolymer possessed strong mucoadhesive properties. The PBA-CS-VE-VRC nanomicelles yielded favorable therapeutic effects in a rabbit model of FK compared with free drug administration. These nanomicelles were able to improve the in vitro mucin binding and to prolong the residence time of the drug on the ocular surface (Fig. 9b). Moreover, the nanomicelles exhibited an enhanced drug permeability in cell monolayer models and 3D cell culture models [141]. Notably, beyond conventional polymer-based strategies, active targeting of corneal transporters offers a promising approach to enhance drug permeation. Exploiting the natural peptide transporters on corneal epithelium, a recent study developed nanomicelles by conjugating D-α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS) with Glycyl-Sarcosine (Gly-Sar), a dipeptide transporter ligand. When loaded with natamycin, the resulting Gly-Sar-TPGS nanomicelles (PeNatcel) significantly enhanced corneal permeation and exhibited potent antifungal efficacy against Candida albicans and Aspergillus flavus. Importantly, PeNatcel achieved comparable therapeutic activity with only 4 daily administrations, in contrast to the 12 doses required by a commercial 5 % natamycin suspension [142]. This transporter-targeted strategy provides a viable means to improve ocular delivery of poorly soluble and permeable drugs. Taken together, these findings indicate that both polymer-based and transporter-targeted nanomicelles may serve as innovative platforms for delivering a wide range of insoluble drugs (including most antifungals) efficiently, thus providing profound repercussions for treating FK. The advantage of CCMD lies in its "rapid onset" (fast in vitro release rate), making it more suitable for the emergency control of acute fungal keratitis. The long-lasting retention and high stability of OTMS (no significant degradation after 3 months of storage) are more suitable for the long-term treatment of chronic fungal infections. This difference highlights that the design of nanomicelles needs to be customized based on the clinical treatment requirements of the disease (such as acute/chronic, infection depth), rather than pursuing a single optimal performance.

Viral keratitis

Viral keratitis refers to the inflammation caused by viral infection of the cornea, often presenting with pronounced irritative symptoms, such as photophobia, tearing, soreness and discomfort. Its complications can include iridocyclitis, hyphema, or secondary glaucoma, making it one of the most prevalent causes of clinical blindness [143]. Acyclovir (ACV), a synthetic nucleoside analogue, has been extensively used as a safe and effective antiviral agent for ulcerative herpetic keratitis [144]. Due to its low aqueous solubility, strategies aimed at enhancing both its solubility and ocular penetration could favor the therapeutic efficacy. Angela et al. explored whether encapsulating acyclovir within Soluplus PMs would intensify its solubility, corneal permeability as well as scleral penetration. Soluplus micelles significantly consolidated the solubility of acyclovir while sustaining stability under dilution conditions. Although analogous permeability coefficients were observed when comparing free drug solutions to those formulated in Soluplus micelles, the latter formulation notably reduced the permeation lag time through the cornea. In contrast to free drug solutions, Soluplus micelles facilitated greater accumulation of acyclovir in both cornea and sclera, along with an increased steady-state flux [145]. Additionally, micellar formulation of Biotin-12Hydroxystearic acid-acyclovir (B-12HS-ACV) was developed with two nonionic surfactants, vitamin E TPGS and octoxynol-40. Results indicated that B-12HS-ACV was released slowly from nanomicelles without significant burst effects during the sustained release course. Ocular biocompatibility assessments revealed that these mixed nanomicelles exhibited non-toxicity and non-inflammatory properties towards corneal epithelial cells [146]. These findings suggest that nanomicellar technology represents a promising drug delivery option for ACV, which is expected to provide more possibilities for the clinical application of viral keratitis (Table 8).

Corneal graft rejection

Corneal graft rejection refers to the immunological response of the recipient after corneal transplantation, featured by classical signs such as a rejection line, anterior chamber (AC) reaction and graft edema. Antibiotics, such as levofloxacin hydrochloride (Lev), are frequently administered postoperatively following corneal graft procedures. However, they are hindered by limited ocular bioavailability and severe side effects. In a recent study, researchers engineered a Lev@RAPA micelle-loaded cationic peptide-based hydrogel (NapFFKK) as a dual-drug delivery system by incorporating RAPA micelles loaded with Lev into the cationic NapFFKK hydrogel to potentially downgrade the risk of corneal graft rejection. The Lev@RAPA micelle loaded NapFFKK hydrogel provided sustained in vitro drug release without undermining their inherent pharmacological activities. Topical instillation of this formulation resulted in excellent ocular tolerance and extended precorneal retention exceeding 60 min, which significantly improved the ocular bioavailability of both Lev and RAPA. Overall, this dual-drug delivery system might be a hopeful formulation for suppressing corneal graft failure [147]. CsA is a puissant immunosuppressant widely utilized to repress corneal graft rejection. However, it remains challenging to achieve therapeutic levels of CsA through topical administration, due to the consolidated corneal barriers that protect intraocular structures from foreign substances (including drugs). In this context, an aqueous micellar formulation was fabricated for ocular valid CsA delivery implementing TPGS (d-α-tocopheryl polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate) along with poloxamer 407 (Pluronic® F127). Compared with commercially available ophthalmic formulations, the poloxamer 407: TPGS at a molar ratio of 1:1 significantly could improve the CsA solubility, while maintaining stability upon dilution in simulated lachrymal fluid and freeze-drying. The MMs demonstrated effective retention capabilities for CsA and realized sustained drug delivery [148]. Additionally, methoxy poly (ethylene glycol)-hexylsubstituted poly(lactides) (MPEG-hexPLA) micelle formulations were also proposed for delivering CsA to prevent cornea graft rejection. Confocal analysis revealed that these nanosized carriers penetrated all layers of the cornea effectively. Rats treated by 0.5 % CsA micelles had significantly higher transparency scores alongside reduced edema post-surgery compared with physiological saline solution controls. At the study endpoint, the success rates for transplantations reached 73 % within treated cohorts versus 25 % within controls [149,150]. Rapamycin (RAPA) has been identified as an effective and novel immunosuppressive agent for patients received corneal transplantation; however, its high water insolubility and low bioavailability significantly restrain the clinical application [151]. In a recent study, rapamycin-loaded nano-micelle ophthalmic solutions (RAPA-NM) were utilized to prevent corneal allograft rejection. The stability tests indicated that RAPA-NM could be stored reliably for 12 weeks while manifesting satisfactory cytocompatibility and high membrane permeability [152]. Similarly, investigators demonstrated that corneal allograft survival in recipients treated with a RAPA nano-micelle ophthalmic solution was evidently prolongated for over 2 months, accompanied by decreased inflammatory infiltration and production of pro-inflammatory factors [153] (Fig. 8b). Cataract

Cataract, featured by opacity or light scattering in the eye lens, is typically caused by the accumulation of high-molecular-weight (HMW) protein aggregates or disruption of the lens microarchitecture. According to the reports of World Health Organization, approximately 18 million individuals worldwide are currently blind owing to cataracts, with an additional 100 million patients demanding surgery to regain vision [154]. The primary treatment for cataracts is surgical intervention, which involves removal of the cloudy lens and implantation of an intraocular lens. Pharmacological management is appropriate for patients exhibiting mild symptoms and these have not yet attained surgical criteria. Nifedipine (NFP), a classic vasodilator routinely used to alleviate angina symptoms, has been proposed as a therapeutic agent in cataract management. It is hypothesized that calcium channel blockers such as NFP may play a vital role in preventing oxidative cataract formation by confining extracellular calcium influx. Recently, the NFP-loaded PLA-PEG micelles have been fabricated as a novel eye drop formulation to preclude oxidative cataract formation and progression at the early stage. The NFP-loaded PLA-PEG micelles demonstrated satisfactory biocompatibility and bioavailability while availably enhancing anticataract efficacy through inhibiting the extracellular calcium ion influx. This research may provide neoteric insights into developing effectual remedies for cataracts [155]. Bromfenac sodium eye drops are topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs designed to inhibit production of certain cell-active substances responsible for pain and swelling, thereby fulfilling anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. However, their low permeability and unsatisfactory bioavailability delimit clinical application. Patel et al. contrived Bromfenac sodium-loaded Pluronic micelles characterized at nanoscale dimensions to reinforce corneal penetration. Ocular irritancy assessments evinced that this formulation was safe and non-irritating. In vitro corneal permeation studies of excised rabbit corneas revealed a 1.5 fold increase in ocular availability without provoking corneal injury compared with an aqueous suspension incorporating equivalent drug amounts formulated in nanomicelles [156]. Glaucoma

Glaucoma encompasses a spectrum of neurodegenerative disorders featured by the deformation of RGCs and irreversible damages to the optic nerve [115]. Contributing factors involve elevated IOP, optic nerve ischemia, and activation of oxidative stress-related pathways can lead to glaucoma. Local administration of therapeutic agent is generally regarded as the first-line remedy for glaucoma. One promising strategy involves employing mixed poloxamine PMs (Tetronic) to deliver the antiglaucoma agent ethoxzolamide (ETOX) for topical ocular application. These MMs conserved therapeutically relevant drug dissolution levels over 28 days post-administration. The co-micellization of poloxamines upgrades the stability of drug-loaded formulations and allow the modulation of drug loading and release process [157]. Nimodipine (NMD), a calcium channel blocker, has shown promise in glaucoma management. However, its ocular therapeutic application is constrained by inadequate aqueous solubility. Li et al. generated an NMD micelle ophthalmic solution utilizing nanocarriers consisted of rebaudioside A and D-α-tocopheryl polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate to intensify the NMD potential in topical ocular formulations. The NMD micelle ophthalmic solution corroborated favorable tolerance in rabbit eyes, with abatement in IOP and ameliorated miosis in vivo. Compared with free NMD, micellar formulations presented significantly enhanced membrane permeability and antioxidant activity in vitro [158]. Prostaglandin derivatives serve as selective F2a receptor agonists to lower IOP associated with open-angle glaucoma. Researchers synthesized a novel micellar formulation subsuming nonionic surfactant (Solutol HS 15), castor oil, water, and latanoprost to prolong drug retention within ocular tissue. Pharmacological studies have shown that latanoprost maintained efficacy for 24 months at 25 °C/75 % relative humidity (RH) and at 40 °C/25 % RH for 6 months, affirming its suitability via this formulation [159]. In addition, researchers developed a Latcel-CMC formulation that combines self-assembled Latanoprost nanomicelles (Latcel) with mucosal adhesion polymer N, O-carboxymethyl chitosan (N, O-CMC). The in vivo experiment in New Zealand white rabbits confirmed that the drug has a 24-h continuous release ability and good biocompatibility, and the eye retention time is extended to 8 h, which is much higher than 2 h of the ordinary solution, which provides a better strategy for the local treatment of glaucoma [160]. It is noteworthy that precise delivery of therapeutic agents to targeted sites is vital for effective IOP reduction in glaucoma (Fig. 10a). Increased stiffness of Schlemm's canal endothelial cells (SC cells) is a dominant contributing factor to the elevated pressure characteristic of primary open-angle glaucoma. Novel treatment strategies have employed actin depolymerizers and rho kinase inhibitors to alleviate this increased stiffness. To circumvent the undesirable off-target outcomes associated with these agents, Stack et al. engineered a targeted PEG-b-PPS micelle containing the actin depolymerizer latrunculin A (tLatA-MC). Notably, a single dose of tLatA-MC achieved a rapid and substantial reduction in IOP by 30–50 % in a mouse model, effectively normalizing the pressure within 4 h of administration and sustaining the effect for up to 24 h. The results validate its utilization as a cell-softening nanotherapy to selectively regulate SC cell stiffness for IOP reduction and glaucoma management [161](Fig. 11 a, b). This work is a paradigm shift in glaucoma therapy, moving beyond conventional IOP-lowering drugs to a novel, mechanism-based cellular targeting approach, which could minimize systemic side effects and enhance treatment specificity. Furthermore, microRNA (miRNA) has garnered extensive attention as a promising therapeutic agent for glaucoma. However, unmodified miR-21-5p cannot traverse the corneal barrier independently to reach target tissue. Thus, a biocompatible polymeric poly(L-lysine) (PLL) micelle coupled with collagenase and ABCA1 antibody has been meticulously fabricated to promote trans-corneal delivery of miR-21-5p specifically targeting trabecular meshwork (TM) and Schlemm's canal (SC). Topically administered PLL micelles successfully penetrated the cornea aided by collagenase and preferentially accumulated within TM/SC tissue under ABCA1 antibody guidance. Upon endocytosis by TM/SC cells, the PLL micelles could decompose within the reductive lysosomal environment to discharge miR-21-5p, which aimed at efficaciously lowering IOP through the activation of miR-21-5p/eNOS/MMP9 signaling axis. These biomaterials will open new avenues for glaucoma-specific gene therapy [162]. Additionally, the mPEG-PCL micelles CLs were formulated containing brinzolamide alongside timolol for glaucoma therapy. In vitro release studies revealed gradual liberation over durations reaching up to 72 h for timolol and 84 h for brinzolamide respectively. In vivo pharmacodynamic evaluations demonstrated markedly increased bioavailability and substantial reductions in IOP in the rabbit model [163]. Neovascularization

Neovascularization is the predominant pathology in various blinding disorders including wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD), macular edema (ME) and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (DR) [164]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays a crucial role in promoting the migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis of vascular endothelial cells. Current anti-VEGF therapies, while effective, require frequent intravitreal injections, which are inconvenient and may lead to potential complications such as endophthalmitis, retinal detachment and retinal hemorrhage [165]. To address these limitations, polymer nanomicelles offer a promising platform for sustained and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents. The dual delivery of an anti-inflammatory agent (i.e., diclofenac; DIC) alongside an anti-VEGF antibody (i.e., Avastin®; Ava) using thermosensitive hydrogel composed of poly (dl-lactide)-poly (ethylene glycol)-poly (dl-lactide) (PDLLA-PEG-PDLLA) (DIC@Ava-loaded hydrogel) was anticipated to effectively inhibit choroidal neovascularization (CNV) through their synergistic effects. In a rabbit model of CNV, treatment with the DIC@Ava-loaded hydrogel via a single subconjunctival injection resulted in significant regression of CNV over a 19-day study period. This may be attributed to reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and decreased VEGF expression [166]. Imatinib-loaded micelles, composed of HA, ethylenediamine (EDA), hexadecyl chains (C16), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and l-carnitine (CRN) were designed for treating neovascular ocular diseases. Compared with the administration of free drug, imatinib significantly inhibited endothelial cell tube formation while promoting disruption of cell tubes within the EHS matrix. Above all, these micelles were capable of interacting with the corneal barrier, thereby promoting transcorneal permeation of imatinib [167]. Additionally, researchers have developed mucoadhesive cationic polypeptide micelles termed Cabo-NPs to facilitate continuous release of cabozantinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor) to inhibit CNV. In vitro angiogenesis assays certified Cabo-NPs potently suppressed both cell migration and tube formation. Cabo-NPs showed remarkable reductions in neovascular area without apparent side effects [168]. Celastrol, a traditional Chinese medicine with anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic investigation but poor water solubility, has been encapsulated in polymer nanomicelles. The celastrol-loaded micelles dose-independently constricted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and strikingly restrained the length and area of CNV and downregulated the expression levels of VEGF, matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9), and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) in corneal tissue, thereby potently suppressing CNV [169,170]. The small interfering RNA (siRNA) holds great promise for treating ocular diseases due to its high efficacy and low toxicity in suppressing the expression of target genes. Recently, investigators have rationally devised PACD, an A-B-C type non-viral vector copolymer composed of a hydrophilic PEG block (A), a siRNA binding block (B), and a pH-responsive block (C). Encouragingly, the PACD/siRNA polyplexes showed remarkable intracellular endosomal escape efficiency, prominent gene silencing effects, and potent anti-angiogenesis impacts. This study proffers valuable design guidance for inventing non-viral ocular nucleic acid delivery systems [171]. Uveitis

Uveitis refers to the inflammation of the uvea, which includes the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. Its etiologies can be attributed to autoimmune diseases, trauma, infections, and systemic disorders. Treatment typically encompasses local eye drops, intraocular injections, or systemic corticosteroids in conjunction with local cycloplegic and mydriatic dilating agents. Typically, non-corticosteroid immunosuppressants are applied in severe and refractory cases [172]. DEX is the principal therapeutic agent employed to alleviate symptoms of uveitis. Researchers have developed the DEX-loaded polycaprolactone-polyethylene glycol-polycaprolactone (PCL-PEG-PCL) micelles to facilitate sustained release and improve ocular bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs. Meanwhile, data from ex vivo permeability tests indicated that these formulated micelles sustained the release of incorporated DEX for 5 days, demonstrating a significant improvement in corneal penetration [173]. Likewise, polylactide-polycaprolactone-polyethylene glycol-polylactide (PLA-PCL-PEG-PCL-PLA)-based micelles were also developed to deliver DEX. The micelles had suitable compatibility with L929 cells and were internalized by these cells in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. At 24 h and 36 h post-injection, PLA-PCL-PEG-PCL-PLA micelles exhibited superior inhibitory effects on uveitis compared with marketed eye drops. Taken together, these findings suggest that PCL-PEG-PCL and PLA-PCL-PEG-PCL-PLA micelles could serve as promising candidates for ocular delivery of DEX as well as potentially other steroid drugs [174]. Moreover, Difluprednate-loaded nanomicelles (Dicel), fabricated via the thin-film hydration method and optimized using Box-Behnken design, achieved 48 h sustained drug release, doubled corneal permeability compared with the commercially available difluprednate emulsion, and exhibited significantly superior anti-inflammatory activity at a reduced dosing frequency of once or twice daily versus the emulsion's four times daily regimen. With favorable stability and biocompatibility, these findings confirm that topical nanomicelles represent a promising novel delivery system for anterior uveitis treatment with reduced dosing frequency [175]. Topical steroid drops are generally well tolerated; however, the drug levels achieved in the intermediate and posterior ocular segments remain subtherapeutic. It is reported that the preparation of triamcinolone acetonide (TA)-loaded PEG-b-PCL and poly (ethylene glycol)-block-poly (lactic acid) (PEG-b-PLA) micelles served as a potential to treat the posterior uveitis. PEG-b-PLA micelles suspended in chitosan hydrogel demonstrated sustained drug release capabilities. In contrast to the plain drug hydrogel, a complete resolution of corneal inflammatory changes was observed with the micellar hydrogel [176]. In addition to corticosteroids and traditional immunosuppressant drug-loaded nanomicelles, in response to the side effect issues existing in current corticosteroid treatments, Kailasam et al. prepared the phosphodiesterase-4(PDE-4) inhibitor Apremilast-loaded nanomicelles (Aprecel). Comprehensive characterization has confirmed that it has an appropriate particle size, a high encapsulation rate and good stability, and can significantly improve the solubility and corneal permeability of Apremilast. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments have confirmed that it can effectively alleviate the inflammatory response in the endotoxin-induced anterior uveitis rat model, providing a safe and effective steroid replacement treatment option for acute anterior uveitis [177]. Besides, Mehra et al. developed a nanomicellar formulation of the immunosuppressant everolimus using Soluplus®, a grafted copolymer composed of PVCL-PVA-PEG, to enhance permeation through ocular epithelia with minimal irritation improving ocular bioavailability for posterior uveitis treatment. The nanomicelles possess high encapsulation efficiency and facilitate the sustained release of everolimus compared with its suspension form. The everolimus nanomicelles showed markedly greater ability to permeate across goat cornea than everolimus suspension [107]. These findings indicate that polymer nanomicelles significantly enhance drug bioavailability and may serve as promising topical drug delivery carriers for treating uveitis (Table 9).

Retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma is the most prevalent primary intraocular malignancy in children, typically manifesting as leukocoria or strabismus. At advanced stages of the disease, affected children may present with proptosis, buphthalmos, or hypopyon [178]. Celastrol, a traditional Chinese herbal medicine, has demonstrated antitumor activity against various tumor cell lines. However, its poor water solubility restricts further therapeutic applications. Li et al. created celastrol-loaded poly (ethylene glycol)-block-poly(ɛ-caprolactone) polymer nanomicelles to heighten the hydrophilicity of celastrol. These nanocarriers inhibit retinoblastoma growth in a mouse xenograft model by inducing apoptosis of SO-Rb 50 cells and suppressed hypoxia-induced HIF-1α and VEGF expression [179]. In another study, PMs were prepared for co-delivery of celastrol and siRNA to RB cells. The micelle vectors have exhibited sufficient cellular internalization and release of loaded-celastrol and HIF-1 siRNA. This co-delivery system synergistically inhibits HIF-1α and VEGF expression in RB cells while suppressing the HIF-1α/VEGF/VEGFR signaling pathway, consequently impeding proliferation, migration, and invasion of vascular endothelial cells [180]. These findings highlight the fact that polymeric nanomicelles loaded with anti-VEGF agents hold significant promise for treating retinoblastoma through their dual roles in combating angiogenesis and tumor growth. Doxorubicin (DOX) is an antitumor antibiotic that inhibits RNA and DNA synthesis, exhibiting cytotoxic effects on tumor cells across various growth cycles. Researchers developed a novel biodegradable doxorubicin-loaded poly(d,l-lactide-co-glycolide)-poly (ethylene glycol)-folate (PLGA-PEG-FOL) micelle formulation (DOXM), achieving targeted drug delivery to retinoblastoma (RB) cells. DOXM exhibited approximately a 4-fold higher uptake efficiency compared with free DOX in folate receptor-overexpressing Y-79 cells. Dispersion of DOXM in PLGA-PEG-PLGA gel facilitated the sustained drug release over a period of 2 weeks [181]. 3,4-Difluorobenzylidene curcumin (CDF), a synthetic derivative of curcumin, has demonstrated a 16-fold increase in half-life along with significant anticancer activity [182]. Recently, a novel folate receptor-targeted PMs loaded with CDF was developed for application in retinoblastoma treatment. Folic acid served as the targeting moiety to enhance both the specificity and bioavailability of CDF. Results indicated that the solubility of CDF increased significantly, achieving an entrapment efficiency exceeding 85 %. Importantly, this formulation exhibited no adverse cytotoxicity towards ARPE-19, verifying its safety profile. Conversely, it demonstrated substantial cell-killing activity against both Y-79 and WERI-RB retinoblastoma cell lines, underlining its potency against cancerous cells [183]. Melphalan (MEL) is a valid chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of RB. In a recent study, researchers designed MEL-loaded reduction-responsive N-acetylheparosan-CYS-Vitamin E succinate (AHV) micelles (MEL/AHV) for the improved delivery of MEL to Rb cells. In vitro release experiments demonstrated that MEL/AHV micelles exhibited obvious reduction sensitivity, resulting in enhanced drug release within tumor cells. These micelles were able to enter Rb cells more efficiently and disassemble rapidly in tumor cells to release MEL, contributing to the increased cytotoxicity. Moreover, MEL/AHV micelles were internalized into tumor cells through multiple pathways, with macropinocytosis playing a predominant role [184]. In addition to the aforementioned drugs, polymer nanomicelles loaded with sorafenib have been developed and optimized for posterior segment delivery in RB treatment, and comprehensive formulation and biological evaluations have been conducted. The optimized polymer nanomicelles exhibited excellent transscleral penetration and scleral deposition, showing significant anti-cancer activity against Y-79 cells at all test time points, while being non-toxic to ARPE-19 cells. Furthermore, it is non-irritating to the eyes and remains stable for six months, which indicates that it has the potential to be effectively administered to the posterior segment of the eye [185,186]. Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR)

PVR represents the most prevalent cause of failure in rhegmatogenous retinal detachment repair, characterized by the proliferation and contraction of cellular membranes within the vitreous cavity as well as on the surfaces of retina. The pathophysiological mechanisms encompass cell migration (RPE and glial cells), proliferation of migrating cells, and extracellular collagen synthesis, among others [187]. Current pharmaceutical strategies for PVR predominantly involve anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative, antigrowth factor, and antioxidant medications. Nevertheless, these interventions have failed to obtain satisfactory outcomes in clinical settings due to suboptimal administration time and inadequate drug concentrations within the retina [188]. Dasatinib, a poorly soluble multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, exhibits considerable promise for treating PVR. Li et al. successfully encapsulated dasatinib within nanoparticles derived from PEG-b-PCL micelles to enhance its solubility by 475-fold while ensuring sustained drug release. Micellar formulations of dasatinib significantly restrained cell proliferation, adhesion, and migration compared to free dasatinib. These findings underscore that dasatinib-encapsulated micelles hold substantial potential for managing PVR. On closer inspection, researchers evaluated the ocular safety/compatibility of both blank micelles and those loaded with dasatinib, which would set the stage for future in vivo efficacy assessments and clinical applications. Results indicated that both blank micelles and those containing dasatinib exhibited excellent safety profiles at cellular levels [49,189]. It is well known that one prevalent cause of PVR should be attributable to the formation of proliferative and contractile fibrocellular membranes. This dysregulated wound healing process is mediated by epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) alongside hyper-proliferation of RPE cells. In a recent study, researchers demonstrated that a bio-functional polymer alone could prevent retinal scarring in an experimental rabbit model with PVR. This process is primarily mediated through clathrin-dependent internalization of PMs, downstream suppression of canonical EMT transcription factors, reduction in hyper-proliferation and migration of RPE cells [190]. In this context, researches emphasize the potential of using synthetic bio-functional polymer to inhibit RPE cell proliferation and adhesion (Table 12).

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD)

nAMD is a prevalent, progressive retinal degenerative macular disease that is characterized by neovascularization of the choroid, accumulation of ROS along with subsequent RPE cell senescence [191]. Current therapeutic strategies for nAMD aim to reduce intraocular levels of VEGF. This can be accomplished through intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents (Fig. 11d). Nevertheless, these treatments necessitate repetitive administrations and pose the risk of consequences such as infection or retinal detachment [192]. To mitigate such complications, there has been considerable interest in developing polymer-based systems for topical drug delivery to the retina. A nanomicelle system composed of a copolymer known as EPC (nEPCs) has been established to deliver aflibercept to the posterior segment via corneal-scleral routes. Researchers demonstrated that aflibercept-loaded nEPCs were capable of penetrating the cornea in ex vivo porcine eye models, and delivered a clinically effective quantities of aflibercept to the retina in the laser-induced CNV murine models. Notably, this research also demonstrates that nEPCs possess intrinsic antiangiogenic properties, which may yield synergistic outcomes in conjunction with their dual capacity to deliver anti-VEGF therapeutics, thereby offering a promising remedy [193]. Tacrolimus, a potent immunosuppressive macrolide agent, has shown efficacy in preventing early retinal neovascularization in mice ocular tissue. Gote et al. developed a clear tacrolimus nanomicellar formulation (TAC-NMF) specifically targeting AMD treatment. Biocompatibility assay using macrophage RAW 264.7 cell line revealed reduced production of inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α after TAC-NMF treatment. Additionally, D407 cells pre-treated with sodium iodate (ROS inducing agent) exhibited reduced ROS, pro-inflammatory cytokines and VEGF-A levels after exposure to TAC-NMF. These findings indicate that TAC-NMF can effectively diminish pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS commonly seen in AMD [194]. Ranibizumab (Rabz), a Fab fragment of a human monoclonal antibody, can prevent the growth and leakage of abnormal blood vessels in the eyes. Xu et al. developed ranibizumab (Rabz)-loaded PLGA/PLA copolymeric nanomicelles (Rabz-CP-NMs) for long-acting nAMD therapy. Rabz-CP NMs achieves long-acting drug release for 48 h in PBS at pH7.4. In vitro experiments showed that the nanomicelles survived dose-dependent on the D407 and ARPE19 retinal pigment epithelial cell lines, and had the characteristics of low toxicity, high cellular uptake, low apoptosis rate, reduced ROS level and prolonging VEGF-A inhibition. It has been proved that Rabz-CP-NMs is safe and applicable for intraocular administration, providing a new option for improving the treatment of AMD [195]. Curcumin is a natural compound known for its numerous beneficial effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-VEGF activities. Its bioavailability remains negligible due to the poor aqueous solubility. Recently, an aqueous nanomicellar drop formulation of curcumin (CUR-NMF) was developed designed for back-of-the-eye delivery utilizing hydrogenated castor oil (HCO-40) and octoxynol-40 (OC-40) as carriers to treat AMD. In vitro drug release kinetics demonstrated a sustained drug release profile and a long-term protection ability of CUR-NMF against oxidative stress to retinal cells. In addition, ELISA results indicated that CUR-NMF significantly reduced VEGF release in D407 cell lines, thus diminishing the risk of angiogenesis [196]. Retinitis pigmentosa (RP)

RP is a neurodegenerative disorder that involves pigment epithelial dysfunction and heterogeneous inherited photoreceptors apoptosis [197]. At the early stages of RP, the vitality of rod photoreceptors is compromised, resulting in difficulties with dark adaptation, night blindness and loss of mid-peripheral visual field during adolescence or young adulthood. As the disease progresses and cone photoreceptors degenerate, central vision is ultimately lost [198]. Progesterone (PG; a C-21 steroid hormone) and its derivatives have been shown to promote cell survival and proliferation while providing protective effects for the RP. However, topical ocular delivery of PG is impeded by its poor aqueous solubility and low ocular bioavailability [199]. Adrián et al. developed micelles utilizing Pluronic F68 and Soluplus copolymers to enhance PG solubility and permeability. The results indicated that Pluronic micelles increased PG solubility by an order of magnitude in a buffered medium at pH 7.4, whereas Soluplus formulations enhanced apparent solubility by two orders of magnitude. Relevantly, the tested micelle dispersions prepared with 20 % Soluplus exhibited an encapsulation efficiency of approximately 100 %, indicating a strong affinity between PG and the micelle core. Soluplus micelles facilitate greater accumulation of PG within corneal and scleral tissue, while Pluronic F68 promote a faster penetration at lower PG doses [200](Fig. 11c). On the whole, the extensive range of PG that can be encapsulated within Pluronic and Soluplus micelles, offering an enormous versatility to formulate individualized dosages tailored for RP patients (Fig. 10 c) (Table 13).

Diabetic retinopathy (DR)

DR is a vascular and neurodegenerative disease originated from chronic progressive diabetes. It is characterized by the leakage and obstruction of retinal microvessels, ultimately leading to a series of fundus lesions, including microhemangioma, neovascularization, macular edema and even retinal detachment [201]. α-Lipoic acid (ALA) serves as a potent antioxidant valuable for the treatment of ophthalmic complications such as DR. Researchers have developed micelle-based formulations to enhance the solubility, stability, and corneal permeability of ALA. Soluplus nanomicelles demonstrated a tenfold enhancement in ALA solubility compared with conventional eye drops while withstanding strong dilution in the lachrymal fluid. In comparison to traditional surfactants like sodium dioctylsulfosuccinate, Soluplus® (a copolymer of polyvinyl caprolactam-polyvinyl acetate-polyethylene glycol) produced smaller micelles with improved solubilizing capacity for ALA while ensuring ocular compatibility [202]. It is well known that anti-Flt1 peptide of GNQWFI acts as an antagonistic peptide for VEGF receptor 1 (VEGFR1 or Flt1) inhibiting VEGFR1-mediated endothelial cell migration and tube formation. In a recent study, the anti-Flt1 peptide GGNQWFI was chemically conjugated to tetra-n-butyl ammonium modified hyaluronate (HA-TBA) through amide bond formation in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), utilizing benzotriazol-1-yloxy-tris(dimethylamino)phosphonium hexafluorophosphate (BOP). The resulting HA-GGNQWFI conjugate self-assembled into micelle-like nanoparticles in aqueous solution. According to in vitro biological activity assays, the HA-GGNQWFI conjugate exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of Flt1-Fc binding to VEGF165 coated on wells. Notably, treatment with the anti-Flt1 peptide-HA conjugate significantly reduced retinal vascular permeability and deformation of retinal vascular structures in DR model rats [203]. Collectively, these findings show that nanomicelles may serve as suitable carriers for vasoinhibitors that could outperform currently available eye drop solutions (Fig. 10d) (Table 13).

Advances of polymer nanomicelles

The ocular application of nanomicelles has gained conspicuous traction in the past decades, owing to their prominent and favorable intrinsic properties. Polymer micelles, comparable in size to lipoproteins and viruses, facilitate cellular uptake through endocytosis, thereby circumventing multidrug resistance associated with conventional drug release approaches [204]. A wide array of water-insoluble therapeutics, encompassing proteins and gene-based medications, can be enveloped within the hydrophobic core of polymer nanomicelles with a substantial enhancement in solubility ranging from 10 to 5000 fold. Meanwhile, this encapsulation minimizes the interaction between the drug and aqueous media, promoting prolonged retention and posterior infiltration into ocular tissue [205]. More importantly, amphiphilic polymers exhibit a lower CMC relative to small-molecule surfactants, which confers increased stability and dilution in polymeric nanomicelles [206]. Moreover, polymer nanomicelles could be easily functionally optimized due to their flexible polymer chains. The physical properties of them could be tailored using various hydrophilic or hydrophobic fragments, such as PEG-based micelles for ameliorative dispersity and chitosan (CS) micelles for enhanced uptake [207]. Furthermore, the structures of polymer nanomicelles have also achieved significant improvements. The organic–inorganic hybrid structure formed by integrating POSS with the PEG–PPG polymer enhances adhesion while effectively mitigating the scouring by eye tears [208]. The incorporation of PGMA into PEG–PGMA micelles endows them with the characteristics of controlled release since PGMA can be hydrolyzed to yield hydrophilic hydroxyl groups at neutral pH [209]. Notably, conjugating specific antibodies or ligands onto the surface of these micelles may introduce charge or targeting capabilities that enhance adhesion and specificity. These functions have positive significance for improving drug delivery efficiency. It reported that the modification of PEG-b-PPS with specific targeted peptides could effectively target SC cells and increase their uptake, which is a neoteric strategy for the management of glaucoma [161]. Recent reports have indicated that polymeric nanomicelles formulations withstand various sterilization methods and can be stored as freeze-dried solids that restore their original structure after reconstitution, facilitating the easy scale-up processes and reduced cost of production. Compared with the conventional local administration, polymer micelles have increased the loading and permeability of hydrophobic agents, prolonged the drug retention on targeted eye tissue, and led to more satisfactory therapeutic effects.

FDA-approved nanomicellar formulations and clinical translation

In the research on nanomicellar therapy for dry eye syndrome (DED), Cequa® (developed by Sun Pharma) is currently the only nano-micellar ophthalmic formulation approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (approved on June 28, 2018). Its successful market launches directly verified the clinical value of polymer nanomicelles in breaking through ocular drug delivery barriers such as insufficient corneal penetration and tear dilution and clearance [210]. It provided a key transformation example for the design principle of nanomicelles mentioned earlier. Cequa® employs the proprietary NCELL® nanomaterial platform, which is an unmodified amphiphilic block copolymer nanomaterial. The critical micelle concentration (CMC) of this system is approximately 0.01 % (w/v), significantly lower than that of small molecule surfactants, ensuring that it remains stable after being diluted by tears when dropped into the eyes. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) determined its particle size to be 20–50 nm. This size not only facilitates penetration of the corneal epithelium but also prevents rapid clearance by the conjunctival lymphatic system. Cequa® adopts a GMP-compliant membrane hydration dialysis scale-up and optimized process to ensure reproducibility, safety and consistency between batches. To meet the long-term medication needs of DED patients, it is packaged in 0.4 mL single-dose preservative-free low-density polyethylene (LDPE) bottles. LDPE material has high chemical inertness, no risk of drug adsorption, and complies with USP/EP Class VI standards. Equipped with an anti-open seal and a fine tip dropper, it ensures precise administration of approximately 0.05 mL per drop, and does not contain preservatives such as benzalkonium chloride, which can avoid ocular surface irritation caused by long-term use. The 2015–2016 Phase IIb trial confirmed the dose-dependent efficacy of 0.09 % CsA [211]. Two Phase III trials from 2016 to 2017demonstrated improvements in tear secretion, corneal staining, and tear film stability, without serious adverse events [212]. Post-marketing studies from 2019 to 2021 showed that the tolerance of the formulation reached 96 % and no new safety risks. In the global regulatory layout, Cequa® has been approved for the indication of "treating DED to increase tear secretion" in multiple markets: The United States (FDA, approved in 2018, with supplementary label data for non-inferiority to Restasis® in 2021), the European Union (EMA, approved in 2020, identified as an orphan drug for severe DED related to Sjogren's syndrome), and Canada (Health Canada, approved in 2021) Limited to patients who do not respond to artificial tears, Australia (TGA, approved in 2022, included in the drug welfare plan), China (NMPA, approved in 2023, localized production at the Shanghai factory, to be launched in the first quarter of 2024); The PMDA in Japan is currently in the Phase III trial stage (expected to be approved in 2026).

Future challenges of polymer nanomicelles

Despite remarkable preclinical advancements in polymer nanomicelle delivery systems, their clinical translation and widespread deployment remain hindered by multi-dimensional challenges, spanning from intrinsic material properties to industrial manufacturing and regulatory approval (Fig. 12).Fig. 12A summary chart of the future challenges of polymer nanomicelles.Fig. 12

A summary chart of the future challenges of polymer nanomicelles.

Fundamental drawbacks in physicochemical properties and functional performance restrict their therapeutic potential. Limited drug loading capacity, insufficient stability, irritant excipients and incomplete drug discharge are inherent bottlenecks. The chemical composition of micelles can induce alterations on the surface of cell membranes, exemplified by palmitic acid modifying the membrane interactions. Besides, the slow degradation of micelles can diminish their electrical properties, reducing the adhesion to the mucin layer. Hydrophilic micelles may also adhere to various substances. There are many other variable enigmas that could influence the release properties of polymer micelle [213]. Alterations in polymer block structures will also impact all properties of micelles such as CMC, encapsulation efficiency and degradation rate [214]. The size and charge of the micelles are crucial determinants for transmittance through anterior segments, the blood-eye barrier and BRB that impede access to posterior regions. Certain polymer nanomicelles incorporating polyethylene glycol demonstrate excellent solubility and biocompatibility. However, their limitations lie in the limited penetration into deeper ocular tissues such as the retina following topical administration, which necessitate injection administration and constrain topical application options.

With the expanding study on nanomedicines, there is an increasing focus on assessing the safety profiles of polymeric micelle formulations. A notable drawback of these systems necessitates thorough evaluation regarding potential toxicity associated with long-term ocular surface applications. Studies suggest that PEG-b-PLA micelles may exhibit potential cytotoxicity and instigate neuroendocrine disruption [215]. Consequently, exploring a biocompatible and safer micellar drug delivery system emerges as a promising research objective moving forward. Additionally, the choice of organic solvent (e.g., acetone, DMSO, chloroform) not only affects drug encapsulation efficiency but also raises concerns regarding residual solvent toxicity and ocular biocompatibility. For instance, residual dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) may cause ocular irritation and corneal damage, while chloroform is strictly regulated due to its potential toxicity. Therefore, solvent-free or green solvent-based methods (e.g., supercritical fluid technology) are increasingly explored to enhance biocompatibility and facilitate scalable production [101].

Clinical translation of polymer nanomicelles is hampered by multiple obstacles. At present, most current researches involving polymer nanomicelles rely heavily on animal models; however, interspecies variability among experimental data presents obstacles to clinical translation for these therapies [216]. Progress in clinical trials for ocular conditions is limited. Regulatory approval demands compelling evidence of safety (e.g., absence of corneal toxicity or irritation), superior efficacy over existing treatments, and favorable human pharmacokinetic profiles, all of which are challenged by unresolved issues like batch-to-batch variability in micelle size, drug loading efficiency, and stability.

Although laboratory-scale micelle preparation methods such as dialysis and solvent evaporation are suitable for proof-of-concept studies, they are often incompatible with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) production. These methods are time-consuming and difficult to scale, making it particularly difficult to maintain batch-to-batch consistency. Critical quality attributes, including particle size, polydispersity index, and drug loading efficiency, require stringent control, as minor variations can profoundly influence ocular biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy (Table 14). Continuous manufacturing approaches like microfluidic assembly offer promising pathways to enhance reproducibility and scalability, yet demand considerable investment and process optimization [217]. The removal of organic solvents to meet stringent regulatory limits for ophthalmic products represents another key challenge. Scaling up evaporation or dialysis processes to eliminate trace solvents without compromising micellar integrity remains a non-trivial engineering task. Stability must be from the micelle structure of physical stability and aseptic two aspects to consider. Nanomicelles in aqueous solutions are dynamic systems susceptible to Ostwald ripening, polymer hydrolysis, and drug leakage during long-term storage. These processes can lead to changes in particle size, reduced encapsulation efficiency, and aggregation, potentially causing ocular irritation. Lyophilized formulations reconstituted immediately before administration represent a common strategy to improve shelf-life, though they introduce complexities in cryoprotectant selection and reconstitution validation. Ophthalmic products must be manufactured as sterile formulations. However, terminal sterilization methods such as autoclaving or gamma irradiation often disrupt micelle integrity or induce polymer degradation. For larger particle size micelle systems, high-cost aseptic production processes need to be adopted, further increasing the difficulty of conversion.

Designing clinical trials for nanomicelle-based therapies entails considerations beyond conventional drug approval pathways. For anterior segment diseases, demonstrating statistically significant advantages over existing alternatives remains challenging. For posterior segment delivery via topical administration, clinical endpoints must convincingly verify retinal drug delivery in humans using advanced imaging or functional assessments [218]. When a nanomicelle formulation serves as a replacement for an existing drug, proof of bioequivalence is complicated by altered tissue distribution rather than systemic exposure. To claim superiority, trials must demonstrate not only enhanced bioavailability but also clinically meaningful improvements in patient outcomes such as delayed disease progression or reduced injection frequency. Additionally, specific protocols should monitor potential polymer-related adverse effects, including chronic local inflammation, changes in ocular tissue morphology, and systemic absorption of polymeric carriers.

The path to regulatory approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for an ocular nanomicellar product is multifaceted, requiring a comprehensive data package that extends beyond conventional formulations. The successful approval of OTX-101 (Cequa®), a nanomicellar formulation of cyclosporine A for dry eye disease, serves as an instructive case study and validates the clinical potential of this technology. The development of OTX-101 underscores several key regulatory requirements. Regulators demand exhaustive characterization of the nanomicelle itself, not just the active drug. This includes rigorous control over CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes) such as particle size distribution, CMC value, drug loading, and impurity profiles. Any change in the polymer source or manufacturing process must be demonstrated not to alter the product's critical performance and safety profiles. For chronic conditions like dry eye or glaucoma, regulators require compelling evidence of long-term ocular safety. This typically involves chronic toxicology studies in two animal species (e.g., rabbits and dogs) to assess local tolerance (cornea, conjunctiva, iris) and potential systemic exposure. For nanomicelles, these studies must specifically evaluate the cumulative effects of both the drug and the polymeric excipient on ocular tissues. The absence of such long-term safety data is a primary reason many promising nano-formulations fail to advance to clinical trials. OTX-101 succeeded by demonstrating significant improvement in both signs (corneal staining) and symptoms of dry eye disease in large-scale Phase 3 clinical trials. It provided a clear benefit over existing formulations (e.g., better tolerability and efficacy than oil-based emulsions) by solving the critical problem of delivering a highly insoluble drug in a comfortable, aqueous-based solution. The journey of OTX-101 from concept to clinic illustrates that overcoming the translational gap is possible. However, it also sets a high bar, emphasizing that meticulous attention to CMC, comprehensive long-term safety assessment, and well-designed clinical trials targeting clear endpoints are non-negotiable requirements for regulatory endorsement of polymer nanomicelles.

In summary, bridging this translational gap requires a multidisciplinary approach integrating materials science, pharmaceutical engineering, and regulatory science. Future efforts should prioritize scalable and cost-effective manufacturing, address stability and sterility challenges, and engage proactively with regulatory agencies to establish clear clinical evaluation pathways for these sophisticated nanoformulations.

Concluding remarks and prospects

Over the past few decades, polymeric micelle has emerged as an effective nanoscale vehicle for ocular drug delivery, offering potential benefits in the treatment of multifarious ophthalmic disorders. It can markedly enhance the solubility of insoluble drugs, protect therapeutic agents from degradation and extend their residence time within targeted ocular tissue, thus improving both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. Thus far, the preclinical exploitation of polymeric nanosized micelles has demonstrated their tolerance, relative safety and efficacy in animal models. However, the transition to clinical practice necessitates a substantial increase in drug trials. It is universally acknowledged that the potential irritation or toxicity generated by the amphiphilic polymer itself may lead to undesirable therapeutic outcomes and numerous complications. Looking ahead, next-generation micelles will be developed to address these existing challenges, and several concrete research avenues may hold particular promise. Firstly, the development of smart, stimulus-responsive micelles tailored to specific ocular pathologies should be prioritized. These systems can be engineered to release their payload in response to pathological cues such as the elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) levels in corneal ulceration, the acidic microenvironment of lysosomes post-cellular uptake, the high reactive oxygen species (ROS) burden in diabetic retinopathy, or the specific enzyme profiles in retinal degenerative zones. This targeted release can maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. Secondarily, the integration of nanomicelles with gene therapy and other biologic modalities represents a frontier for treating previously untreatable diseases. Polymeric micelles, particularly polyelectrolyte and cationic varieties, show great potential as non-viral vectors for delivering siRNA, miRNA, or CRISPR-Cas9 components. This approach could address the root causes of inherited retinal diseases (e.g., retinitis pigmentosa) or provide sustained suppression of pathogenic factors in refractory ocular diseases like neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) and glaucoma. Finally, the principles of precision medicine can be incorporated into nanomicelle design. Future efforts could focus on conjugating targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides, aptamers) selected based on individual patient's molecular profiling of tear fluid or ocular tissue. This "personalized targeting" strategy could ensure that micelles actively hone in on specific cell populations (e.g., overactive Schlemm's canal cells in glaucoma, specific neovascular endothelial cells, or activated microglia in neurodegeneration), thereby achieving unprecedented therapeutic specificity. In future work, challenges regarding long-term biocompatibility of novel polymers, scalable and reproducible manufacturing under GMP standards, and the development of clinically relevant animal models must be addressed. The unremitting exploration of nanomaterial science, coupled with cross-disciplinary collaboration, will undoubtedly enable these next-generation polymeric nanomicelles to transition from bench to
