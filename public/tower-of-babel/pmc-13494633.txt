Trigger failure, intraoperative recovery, and the cognitive architecture of clinical decision-making in in vitro fertilization: a case for integrative education in reproductive medicine
Authors: Lionel Reyftmann, Dean Morbeck
Published: 2026

We read with interest the exchange between Drs. Paulson and Shapiro published in these columns last year (1, 2), specifically in regards to the comment about cognitive biases in reproductive endocrinology and infertility practice and the need for future research. We would like to share a recent clinical case that we believe carries significant educational value for the reproductive medicine community, not only for what went wrong, but for the nature of the reasoning that ultimately rescued it. Institutional review board approval: the publication of this case was approved by the Genea scientific advisory committee on the 10/3/26 (CAS-NSW-030726).

The case involves a 32-year-old woman with an antimüllerian hormone of 42 pmol/L (5.7 ng/mL) and a peak estradiol of 12,000 pmol/L (3,270 pg/mL) undergoing her first in vitro fertilization antagonist cycle. She had received 10 days of oral contraceptive pill pretreatment for cycle scheduling and had required recombinant luteinizing hormone (LH) supplementation during stimulation due to a suboptimal estradiol rise. On the basis of her high-responder profile, a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist trigger was selected (a clinically defensible first-order decision) with a freeze-all strategy planned to mitigate ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome risk.

Posttrigger hormonal assessment, however, revealed a cause for concern. The LH response reached only 10.4 IU/L, below the widely accepted adequacy threshold of 15 IU/L which is known to be a potential marker of suboptimal gonadotropin-releasing hormone trigger (3). A concurrent progesterone rise of 41 nmol/L (12.9 ng/mL) provided partial reassurance, and the decision was made to proceed to ovum pick-up (OPU) without human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) rescue. This decision, in retrospect, was the pivotal error of the cycle. At OPU, only 2 oocytes were retrieved from 12 follicles on the first ovary, a yield of 16.7%, which was far below the expected 70%–80%, consistent with incomplete oocyte maturation secondary to an inadequate LH surge.

At this point, the case took a turn that is the primary subject of our letter. Rather than proceeding to puncture the contralateral ovary, the operating clinician elected to spare it entirely, administer hCG 5,000 IU immediately postprocedure, and return for a second OPU 36 hours later. The biological rationale was sound, and this strategy already has been published: intact, unpunctured follicles retain responsiveness to luteotropic stimulation, and hCG, acting directly on LH receptors independently of pituitary output, could drive the oocyte maturation cascade that the agonist trigger had failed to initiate. This strategy yielded 15 oocytes from the second OPU, 14 of which were mature, resulting in 10 frozen blastocysts in total, an outcome that any in vitro fertilization program would regard as excellent.

What makes this case educationally rich is the contrast between its two phases. The first phase, the trigger decision and its aftermath, is a forensic study in cognitive bias. We have identified at least seven discrete biases operating in sequence across the trigger decision cascade. The availability heuristic retrieved the familiar ‘high responder equals agonist trigger' template without interrogating whether this patient met its prerequisites. Anchoring on ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome risk suppressed the equally important inquiry into pituitary reserve. The framing of the absent functional hypothalamic amenorrhea diagnosis as evidence of normal pituitary function, rather than the absence of a specific pathology, obscured the functional suppression created by OCP pretreatment and the recombinant LH requirement. Search satisficing stopped the reasoning process once a sufficient justification for the agonist trigger was identified. Therapeutic momentum resisted course correction when the suboptimal LH was documented. Confirmation bias amplified the reassuring progesterone while minimizing the concerning LH and overconfidence closed the inquiry entirely. Each of these biases is well-described in the cognitive psychology literature; what this case illustrates is how they operate not in isolation but as a self-reinforcing cascade, each one narrowing the cognitive aperture through which the clinical evidence is perceived (4).

The second phase, the intraoperative recovery, is an equally instructive study in adaptive expertise. The decision to spare the contralateral ovary and administer rescue hCG was not produced by deliberate analytical reasoning; it emerged rapidly, as expert intuition characteristically does, from a deep and automated integration of physiological knowledge accumulated over years of clinical practice. Critically, it was preceded by a productive resolution of cognitive dissonance. Confronted with a yield that contradicted every expectation, the clinician did not defend the original plan (the path of least psychological resistance) but acknowledged the failure of the trigger strategy and sought a corrective action. This required intellectual humility, psychological resilience, and the professional authority to improvise without guideline support. These qualities are correlated with seniority and experience, which raises an important and underexamined question: how do we cultivate them in trainees who have not yet accumulated the experience from which they emerge? This commitment is made more urgent by the trajectory of contemporary training environments: as AI-assisted decision-support tools become increasingly integrated into ovarian stimulation protocols (5), future generations of reproductive endocrinology and infertility clinicians risk developing their clinical reasoning in an era of algorithmic guidance, potentially without the opportunity to build the independent, pattern-based intuition that proved decisive in this case, and that no algorithm, however sophisticated, can yet replicate in the unscripted theater of an unexpected intraoperative finding.

We believe this case, and the cognitive analysis it supports, merits the attention of the reproductive medicine community. Unexpected poor oocyte retrieval is a known but underappreciated complication. The cognitive mechanisms that allow it to occur despite adequate clinical knowledge are preventable. And the adaptive expertise that rescued this cycle is teachable, but only if we commit to teaching it.
