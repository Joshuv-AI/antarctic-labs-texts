The importance of epistasis in the evolution of viral pathogens
Authors: Swathi Nachiar Manivannan, Carolina Diaz Arenas, Nathan D Grubaugh, C Brandon Ogbunugafor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the genetic and genomic underpinnings of infectious disease outbreaks has emerged as a frontier of epidemiology. Here, we argue that epistasis—where the phenotypic effects of mutations or gene variants are dictated by the presence of other mutations or genes—should become a focus of genomic epidemiology. To demonstrate this, we present the results of a systematic review of the literature on epistasis in viruses, focusing on three major human viral systems: (i) influenza, (ii) SARS-CoV-2, and (iii) human immunodeficiency virus, as well as two other bodies of the literature mainly focusing on nonhuman viruses: (iv) tobacco etch virus and (v) experimental evolution of viruses. Our systematic review of these five bodies of the literature highlights that epistasis is prevalent in host–virus systems of various kinds, manifesting within and between different loci, with effects of different magnitudes and directions, and shaping various phenotypic traits of epidemiological interest. At the same time, our systematic review demonstrates that our ability to draw general conclusions about the direction and magnitude of epistasis in viral evolution is constrained by several factors: the idiosyncrasies of virus–host systems, biases in the underlying data collection exercises, and the limitations of existing methods. Moving forward, we encourage collaborations between genomic epidemiologists and evolutionary biologists to identify and measure epistasis in studying the evolution of viral pathogens.Keywords: epistasis, influenza, SARS-CoV-2, HIV, tobacco etch virus, genomic epidemiology, virus evolution

Introduction

Anticipating and controlling infectious disease outbreaks is a crucial public health challenge that will linger due to increasing anthropogenic activities that contribute to global environmental changes (Coker et al. 2011, Jones et al. 2013, Hassell et al. 2021). This threat exists in the form of spillover events (where pathogens successfully cross over from infecting one species into another), and in the emergence of novel strains of endemic pathogens (Morse 2009). In the face of this challenge, infectious disease epidemiologists and ecologists have called for more mechanistic and integrated approaches to understanding the genetic and ecological factors that shape the success of disease outbreak emergence and persistence (Geoghegan and Holmes 2017, Balasubramanian et al. 2022).

Advancements in the collection and sharing of sequencing data have significantly decreased barriers to obtaining global genomic and epidemiological information on infectious diseases (Hadfield et al. 2018, Grubaugh et al. 2019). However, understanding the proximate factors determining epidemics’ emergence and evolutionary potential remains challenging. Genomic technologies have been critical for describing characteristics of outbreaks, including the resolution of phylogenetic, geographical, and temporal dimensions, as well as transmission dynamics (Holmes et al. 1995, Koelle et al. 2006, Volz et al. 2013). Furthermore, these methods are often able to identify substitutions (i.e. mutations or genetic variants that become fixed in a pathogen lineage over evolutionary time scales; Holmes et al. 2016) associated with disease phenotypes, such as immune escape, cell tropism, and virulence factors (Urbanowicz et al. 2016, Grubaugh et al. 2019, Alpert et al. 2021, Kissler et al. 2021).

While classical models of population genetics may have prioritized the importance of single-gene variants in dictating organismal phenotypes, recent work has implemented more complex models of genetic architecture, some of which complicate the notion that individual mutations of large effect contribute to variation in traits (Boyle et al. 2017, Ball 2023). Similar questions have arisen in genomic epidemiology. Although microbial genomes can be relatively small, the traits they encode can nonetheless be complex, undermining simplistic framings of the relationship between genotype and phenotype: many actors and factors can craft how viral phenotypes manifest. In addition to the role of the ‘environment’ broadly considered in shaping genotype–phenotype space (via genotype × environment (G × E) interactions), epistasis is another key force to consider in crafting complex traits, even in viral genomes.

Epistasis has many definitions, but can be colloquially defined as ‘the surprise at the phenotype when mutations are combined, given the constituent mutations’ individual effects (Weinreich et al. 2013a). In other words, it is a phenomenon when the combined effect of two (or more) mutations or gene variants on a phenotypic trait deviates from the expected sum or product of individual mutational effects (Wagner 2015) (Fig. 1). Epistasis is the object of fascination in the population genetics community, as it can undermine simplistic and linear pictures of the relationship between genotype and phenotype, and therefore complicate our picture of molecular evolution (Moore and Williams 2005, de Visser et al. 2011, Weinreich et al. 2013a, Sackton and Hartl 2016, Bank 2022, Ogbunugafor and Scarpino 2022).

Although epistasis has been rigorously examined in microbial systems (Remold and Lenski 2004, Remold et al. 2008, Bank et al. 2015, Bank 2022), it has not been much discussed as a significant actor in epidemiological settings. Some of these reasons make sense: epidemic data can be noisy and is often driven by the short-term goals of epidemiology—to identify the sources of emergence and/or patterns of spread. These short-term needs can be incompatible with identifying the often subtle interactions between mutations that underlie epistasis. Furthermore, given the diverse ways epistasis manifests and can be measured, genomic epidemiologists also face the methodological challenge of determining the most appropriate ways to identify and measure epistasis from real-world data.

Here, we argue that epistasis should become a key feature in viral evolutionary and genomic epidemiology. To do this, we adopt a comparative approach, examining the literature on epistasis in several viral systems: influenza, SARS-CoV-2, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), tobacco etch virus (TEV), and experimental evolution of viruses. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to systematically review how epistasis has been studied in multiple viral systems, and it stands alongside other large reviews that have examined epistasis in influenza (Lyons and Lauring 2018), and other population genetics topics involving RNA viruses (Cuevas et al. 2009).

Our systematic review highlights how epistasis is widespread in host–virus systems of various kinds, manifesting between different genes, as effects of different magnitudes and directions, and consequently shaping phenotypic traits of epidemiological consequence (Table 1 and Fig. 2). However, we also show that our current understanding of epistasis in viral evolution is shaped by forces that can be described as sociological in origin. That is, what we know about epistasis in viral evolution is affected by biases in the underlying data collection and analysis of viral systems (Table 2). Summarizing, we argue that present and future efforts to study epistasis in viral evolution need to transcend biases to get a clearer picture of how epistasis plays a role in viral evolution, and propose frameworks and methodologies that epidemiologists, virologists, and evolutionary biologists can use moving forward (Table 3 and Fig. 3).

A conceptual map for ways to measure epistatic interactions in epidemiological contexts. For convenience, we use an example of an RNA virus infecting animals, like several of the subjects of this systematic review. (a) Starting from the RNA genome, isolate those mutations implicated in a given pathogenic phenotype such as increased infectivity, drug resistance and/or host shifts. (b) Rather than focusing on a single notable mutation, we should consider that notable mutation effects depend on others (different mutations represented by different shapes) that also appear to have arisen in the candidate virus. (c) Next, we should consider how these interactions could manifest differently in different genomic backgrounds (e.g. virus strains). Overview of the systematic review

Choice of study systems

We chose to study influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and HIV because they constitute three major (relatively) recent global threats to human health, having caused some of the major pandemics of the past century. These three pathogens have also been the subject of many studies, some of which have examined epistasis. To complement the three human viral pathogens, we also examined TEV, a plant virus. Lastly, we studied manuscripts featuring experimental evolution of viruses because they offer even more organismal breadth, which may offer a window into the generality of patterns across virus–host contexts. Furthermore, experimental evolution studies take place in relatively controlled settings, which can be ideal to resolve particular evolutionary and ecological questions in viral pathogens (Grubaugh and Andersen 2017).

In our review of experimental evolution studies, we focus on studies that adhere to the following definition of experimental evolution: the ‘study of evolutionary changes occurring in experimental populations as a consequence of conditions (environmental, demographic, genetic, social, etc.) imposed by the experimenter’ (Kawecki et al. 2012).

Methods for systematic review

The PRISMA pipeline (Page et al. 2021) was used to shortlist the studies used for the systematic review (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S1A–E). Records of research articles, published up to 16 September 2023, were initially obtained from three databases—PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus—based on the respective keywords for each system: (i) Influenza: ‘[epistatic interactions OR epistasis] AND influenza’, which gave 162 records. (ii) SARS-CoV-2: ‘[epistatic interactions OR epistasis] AND [SARS-CoV-2 OR COVID-19]’, which gave 130 records. (iii) HIV: ‘[epistatic interactions OR epistasis] AND HIV’, which gave 268 records. (iv) TEV: ‘[epistatic interactions OR epistasis] AND [tobacco etch virus]’, which gave 27 records. (v) Experimental evolution: ‘[epistatic interactions OR epistasis] AND virus AND experimental evolution’, which gave 180 records.

Following this, duplicate records were removed, and the remaining records’ titles, abstracts, and full texts were assessed for their relevance to our topic of interest. Of the relevant records, we then narrowed our list of records to studies that identified (potentially) mechanistic epistatic interactions between different loci. This meant that studies such as epidemiological or evolutionary modelling studies that characterized overall epistasis in the system as a parameter were excluded, but bioinformatics studies that identified putative or experimentally validated epistatic interactions between specific mutations/loci were included. We then performed a systematic review of the shortlisted records (41 studies on influenza, 28 studies on SARS-CoV-2, 35 studies on HIV-1, 11 studies on TEV, and 39 studies on viral experimental evolution), where we summarized information on the nature of the studies and reported epistatic interactions.

We also note that ‘entrenchment’ is used alongside or instead of ‘epistasis’, especially in the HIV literature (Biswas et al. 2019). As the term is only used sparingly and sometimes in concert with epistasis, we did not search for articles using ‘entrenchment’ as a search keyword.

Evidence for epistasis in viral systems and its significance for epidemiology

Our systematic review demonstrates that epistasis has been identified as a multifaceted mechanism contributing to the evolution of viral populations with various disease-related phenotypes, ranging from drug resistance, immune escape, and even host shifts (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2A–E). In this section, we first discuss how epistasis has been implicated in epidemiology of each of the viral systems we studied. Following which, we compare the nature of epistatic interactions reported across the viral systems, based on the (i) validity of interactions (putative and/or experimentally validated), (ii) level of organization at the genetic level (intragenic and/or intergenic), (iii) interaction order (pairwise and/or higher-order), and (iv) nature of interactions (positive, negative, and/or sign) (Fig. 2). We also refer readers to Supplementary Tables S2A–E and S3A–E, which provide a breakdown of how papers in each system were categorized (by viral strain, host species studied, phenotypic consequence, etc.), and the list of studies for each system, respectively. Finally, we contextualize these findings in the general study of viral evolution by discussing broader implications for understanding viral evolution.

Sign epistasis contributes to the evolution of various phenotypic traits in influenza

We note that influenza was the subject of a thorough 2018 review related to epistasis and evolution, and so those interested in a focus on influenza should consult that work as well (Lyons and Lauring 2018) as our study also included many of the studies referenced in that review.

Over half of the influenza studies in our systematic review reported sign epistatic interactions (Fig. 2d), which could be attributed to ‘compensatory’ or ‘permissive’ mutations. These mutations may be neutral in isolation but beneficial in the presence of another mutation, i.e. costly in fitness but confers some benefit (e.g. antiviral resistance (Bloom et al. 2010, Jiang et al. 2022), see Table 1). The resulting double mutation variant has higher fitness than both single mutants combined additively or multiplicatively, a signature of epistasis. In turn, this could allow pathogens to occupy new niches and adapt to fitness optima (such as evolving to infect new hosts (Bourret et al. 2017), acquiring greater host immune escape (Dankar et al. 2013) or antiviral resistance (Bloom et al. 2010)) by evolutionary pathways only made ‘accessible’ through the appropriate combination of variants, giving rise to more optimal trait values. The adaptive landscape of influenza is also constantly evolving, meaning that functional constraints and accessible trajectories are changing as epistatic interactions change over time, as demonstrated through a study on how the antigenic site B of haemagglutinin has evolved in H3N2 from 1968 to 2016 (Wu et al. 2020).

The majority of the studies also focused on HA and/or NA, the genes encoding for the viral surface proteins hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, respectively (Supplementary Table S2A). Consequently, at least 40% of the studies identified epistatic interactions that were implicated in the evasion of host immune responses (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2A). However, it must be noted that epistatic relationships implicating other viral genome segments formed ~40% of studies as well, which is reflected in the range of phenotypic consequences for which epistatic interactions were implicated in Supplementary Table S2A.

Positive epistasis is prevalent in computational studies of SARS-CoV-2 loci

In SARS-CoV-2, almost all studies in our systematic review overwhelmingly reported epistatic interactions within or between the S gene (encoding the Spike protein) and other genes (Supplementary Table S2B). As a result, our understanding of how positive and sign epistasis has shaped SARS-CoV-2 evolution (Fig. 2d) is mainly with respect to how epistasis has shaped the evolution of the S gene. While other proteins are also believed to contribute to viral traits, such as replication and pathogenesis (McGrath et al. 2022), they are understudied compared with the S gene, partly due to a lack of in vitro systems to examine non-Spike proteins (Carabelli et al. 2023).

One of the defining characteristics of VOCs is increased transmission and immune evasion compared with other ancestral strains. This can mainly be attributed to mutations in the S gene, particularly since it plays an essential role in host receptor recognition and viral fusion, and is also the prime target of antibodies, which causes it to be subject to immense selection pressure (Huang et al. 2020). Consequently, the phenotypic consequences described in these studies were mainly immune escape (15/28) and/or binding to the ACE2 host receptor (12/28) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2B). Particularly in the Omicron VOC (B.1.1.529), which was examined in half the studies in our review (Supplementary Table S2B), there are several mutations in the S gene that individually are likely to be deleterious, but beneficial in combination with each other, an example of positive epistasis at play (Martin et al. (2022), ref. 8 in Supplementary Table S3B).

Human immunodeficiency virus studies identify epistatic interactions shaping the evolution of drug resistance

Our systematic review of HIV studies highlights the role that epistasis has played in the evolution of drug resistance, with more than half of the studies identifying epistatic interactions involved in the evolution of drug resistance (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2C). However, some studies covered other phenotypic traits such as protein stability/function (6/35), escape/adaptation to host immunity (3/35), and other pathogenic properties such as progression to AIDS (Poon et al. 2009) (Supplementary Table S2C).

Almost all studies identified epistatic interactions involving the pol gene (Supplementary Table S2C), the major target of HIV antiretroviral treatment. The HIV pol gene encodes protease (PR), reverse transcriptase (RT), and integrase (IN)—all targets of HIV antiretroviral treatment (Biswas et al. 2019). An overwhelming majority of studies (27/35) examined epistasis in the pol gene, of which 22 focused on the pol segment that encodes viral PR. Positive, negative, and sign epistatic interactions were reported across studies on the pol gene, suggesting that the contribution of epistasis to the evolution of drug resistance in HIV is multidimensional, and not just simply a singular trajectory towards increased resistance by fixing more drug-resistance associated mutations.

All studies on HIV focused on HIV-1, with a majority of 25/35 studies focusing on Group M Subtype B (Supplementary Table S2C), the subtype most commonly found in Europe and the Americas (Siemieniuk et al. 2013) but accounting for only ~10% of cases worldwide (Hemelaar et al. 2011). On the other hand, three or few studies focus on each other subtype of Group M HIV-1. This includes subtype C, the predominant strain that circulates in southern Africa but represents the majority of global cases (Hemelaar et al. 2011, Serwin et al. 2021). Other HIV-1 groups were greatly underrepresented, reflecting underlying global inequalities in sequencing and resources for studying HIV, particularly in regions most affected by it.

Tobacco etch virus studies demonstrate epistasis across the genome

Studies of epistasis in TEV are limited in number (11) and were performed by a small number of research laboratories (see Supplementary Tables S2D and S3D). However, there are still many more studies on epistasis in TEV than, e.g. the tobacco mosaic virus, which is one of the first viruses to be discovered and has long been used as a model system in the study of plant virus evolution (Scholthof 2004).

A majority of TEV genomic loci were studied by the 11 studies, with the greatest proportion of studies that identified epistatic interactions involving VPg (viral protein genome-linked) (Supplementary Table S2D). The NIa gene encodes the NIa polyprotein that, when processed, produces NIa-Pro and VPg. VPg interacts with the 5′ end of viral RNA, several of the other potyviral proteins, as well as certain host factors, and is therefore thought to play an important role in viral translation and host infectivity (Léonard et al. 2000, Huang et al. 2010). NIa-Pro is the PR responsible for processing of the central and C-terminal of the viral polyprotein (Revers and García 2015). Other genes that were substantially covered include P1 and P3, which are both implicated in viral replication.

Almost all TEV studies also demonstrate positive, negative, and/or sign epistatic interactions across the TEV genome (Fig. 2d), generally confined to measures of fitness and adaptation to novel hosts such as Arabidopsis thaliana (Supplementary Tables S2D and S3D; Cervera et al. (2016b), Agudelo-Romero et al. 2008). In TEV, the magnitude of epistatic interaction detected could potentially be attributed to whether the implicated gene loci are also interacting with each other. For example, mutations in gene loci connected within a protein–protein interaction network were more likely to be involved in positive epistatic interactions than mutations in loci that were not connected (Lalíc and Elena (2012), ref. 8 in Supplementary Table S3D).

Experimental evolution studies mostly focus on Class IV viruses

Among the 39 studies of experimental evolution, a wide range of viruses were studied. Our systematic review of the literature showed that slightly over half of the studies were conducted in Class IV viruses (i.e. single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genomes). In addition to Class VII viruses (double-stranded DNA-RT), which were not studied in any of the studies, the other classes were represented in two to six studies. The virus featured in the largest number of studies was TEV (6/39 studies, Supplementary Tables S2E and S3E), all of which were also featured in the systematic review of TEV (Agudelo-Romero et al. (2008), Cervera et al. (2016b), Cuevas et al. (2015), Hillung et al. (2015), Lalíc and Elena (2015), Torres-Barceló et al. (2009), refs. 1, 9, 11, 18, 21, and 37 in Supplementary Table S3E). The studies also covered a wide range of hosts, with Escherichia coli featured in the greatest number of studies (12/39), followed by Homo sapiens (i.e. the viruses were human pathogens and the experimental evolution was also conducted in vitro in different human cell lines) (Supplementary Table S2E).

The experimental evolution studies covered a wide range of phenotypic consequences implicated by epistatic interactions, such as immune escape, tolerance of environmental stressors, and/or fitness recovery. More than a third of studies identified epistatic interactions that were implicated in the successful infection of a novel/ancestral host species (Supplementary Table S2E), highlighting the importance of epistasis in viruses adapting to a new ecological niche. For example, different sets of co-occurring mutations were fixed in different populations of vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), depending on whether they were evolved to either distinct host cell lines (HeLa cells or Madin-Darby Canine Kidney cells) or alternating between the two host cell types. These sets of co-occurring mutations were mutations found in multiple genes, suggesting that intergenic epistasis plays an important role in the ‘genetic architecture of host adaptation’, which could be attributed to differences in protein–protein interaction networks of VSV in response to different host environments (Remold et al. (2008), ref. 30 in Supplementary Table S3E).

Comparing epistatic interactions across viral systems

Figure 2 summarizes the nature of epistatic interactions reported across all the literature. For influenza, HIV, experimental evolution, and TEV, the interactions reported were primarily experimentally validated, i.e. the individual and combined mutation effects on trait values were experimentally assessed to validate epistatic interactions (Fig. 2a). By contrast, for SARS-CoV-2, a substantial majority of the studies (64%) reported putative interactions, which could be attributed to most SARS-CoV-2 studies being computational only (Supplementary Table S2B). This makes sense given the recency of the COVID-19 pandemic: there has not been enough time to develop the laboratory systems that would facilitate rigorous laboratory testing. Validating these putative epistatic interactions would be a natural next step for future studies conducted on epistasis in SARS-CoV-2. This has been shown to be feasible; e.g. in influenza, several exclusively computational studies (i.e. studies that identified epistatic interactions by employing statistical/computational tools on genomic sequences) tested their methods on experimental datasets (e.g. Kryazhimskiy et al. (2011)) and identified experimentally validated epistatic pairs. Furthermore, in the experimental evolution of bacteriophage ϕX174, some of the putative compensatory mutations identified in Poon and Chao (2005) were validated in Poon and Chao (2006).

In influenza, HIV, and SARS-CoV-2, intragenic epistatic interactions were reported more than intergenic epistatic interactions; particularly for SARS-CoV-2 and HIV, only 28% of SARS-CoV-2 studies and 20% of HIV studies, respectively, reported some form of intergenic epistatic interaction (with or without intragenic interactions) (Fig. 2b). This can be attributed to the majority of studies in each of these systems focusing on one/a few genes of interest (HA and/or NA in influenza, the S gene in SARS-CoV-2, and pol in HIV). In contrast, the proportion of studies reporting intragenic and/or intergenic epistatic interactions in the experimental evolution of virus systems and TEV was similar. This highlights the importance of future studies on pandemic viruses looking at how intergenic epistatic interactions manifest, particularly within and between understudied genes of these viruses.

Across all systems, pairwise interactions were reported in more studies than higher-order interactions (i.e. > 2 interacting mutations) (Fig. 2c), possibly due to limitations of current methods in identifying higher-order epistatic interactions (Weinreich et al. (2013b), Zhou et al. (2022); see also Table 3). This suggests that future studies could focus on developing methods for identifying higher-order interactions across all viral systems.

Finally, when discussing the type of epistasis reported in the five systems, sign epistasis was reported in the highest proportion of studies for influenza (63%). Positive epistasis was reported in the highest proportion of studies for SARS-CoV-2 (86%), HIV (80%), TEV (91%), and experimental evolution (68%) (Fig. 2d).

Compared to SARS-CoV-2, influenza and HIV, there were almost equal proportions of studies in the experimental evolution system that reported positive and negative (magnitude) epistasis, as well as sign epistasis. This suggests advantages to using experimental evolution methods to study epistasis. In particular, experimental evolution also allows one to observe every step of the evolution process. This means that negative/deleterious interactions could be identified more readily than in real-world systems, where we can only identify interactions based on the genomic variation of circulating viral populations.

Connections to the study of fitness landscapes

Here, we discuss the study of epistasis in viral pathogens with regard to the fitness landscape metaphor. The fitness landscape has been important in the study of disease evolution, including drug resistance (Weinreich et al. 2006, Palmer et al. 2015) and viral disease phenotypes (Elena et al. 2009, Lauring and Andino 2011). The key to its utility for understanding evolution is in how it allows us to measure the navigability of adaptive trajectories and, by extension, the evolvability of disease-related phenotypes.

Theoretical and experimental studies on fitness landscapes have suggested that the subset of mutations used to construct a fitness landscape have implications for our understanding of how evolution manifests in a system—e.g. the sign and magnitude of epistatic interactions experienced across different evolutionary trajectories can depend on the relative distance of individual genotypes from the fitness optimum, or whether mutations on a fitness landscape were either co-selected in an adaptive walk or selected independently of each other (Blanquart et al. 2014, Bank 2022). Nonetheless, the modern study of fitness landscapes has revealed how epistasis is a major force in crafting the topography of fitness landscapes (de Visser et al. 2011, Weinreich et al. 2013a, Blanquart and Bataillon 2016, Bank 2022).

Epistasis can make it challenging to predict the mutational effect of an individual mutation without having complete information about other loci in a genome. But recent work suggests that it may be possible to infer the distributions of mutational effects through a few parameters (Park et al. 2022, Johnson et al. 2023). For genomic epidemiologists who are tracking viral evolution in real time, this suggests that accounting for epistasis might be feasible. Moreover, because viral pathogens are characterized by large population sizes and high mutation rates, they are suitable systems to study rapid adaptation on rugged fitness landscapes (Elena and Sanjuán 2007, Cervera et al. 2016a), properties that also make them a constant threat to plant and animal health. In the future, the challenge of predicting and controlling the emergence of new variants could at least partially be resolved by identifying population genetic parameters and epidemiological conditions where fitness valley crossing is more likely (Draghi et al. 2024).

Challenges and gaps in current literature

There is an inherent limitation of our systematic review, i.e. worth exploring. While one can quantify the proportions of studies that have reported different types of epistatic interactions, it does not reflect the actual differences in epistatic interactions between different systems. Furthermore, we have biased our study towards human pathogens, as they are overrepresented in the viral evolution literature, although we tried to address this by studying TEV and looking at a breadth of viruses in the experimental evolution set.

However, what our systematic review highlights are biases and limitations in the current literature that can also explain why general conclusions have not been drawn about the predominant type of epistasis governing viral evolution. Understanding these biases and limitations will highlight the need for future approaches that can address these gaps. Below, we briefly discuss the gaps in the current literature. These gaps are also summarized in Table 2.

Biases in sampling of study systems

One of the challenges in obtaining a complete picture of the role of epistasis in viral evolution is the bias in sampling of study systems (Table 2). Although our systematic review is biased towards human RNA viruses, this bias is not only limited to our review but also a reflection of the body of literature on epistasis in viral evolution. In fact, when we attempted to remedy the bias in our systems by looking at experimental evolution of viruses, we found that more than half of the studies were on Class IV (single-stranded, positive-sense RNA) viruses and almost a third of the studies involved bacteriophages. By contrast, only 25% of the studies examined DNA viruses (Supplementary Table S2E).

These biases are not restricted to the study of epistasis across viruses. In fact, even within individual viral systems, we observed that there was an over-representation of certain strains, loci, or host species. In influenza, Homo sapiens was represented in all but three studies, while the next largest taxon group, birds (of various species), was represented in less than a third of the studies (Supplementary Table S2A). This could have resulted in certain phenotypic consequences and epistatic relationships being identified more frequently than others. For example, Lee and colleagues reported sign epistasis in H1N1 influenza between HA | N129D and other escape mutations in humans due to the compensatory nature of N129D. Notably, their study found that this sign epistasis was absent in ferret hosts (Lee et al. 2023). Across HIV studies, other patterns emerge: in addition to most studies that focus only on the pol gene, all the studies in the systematic review were conducted on HIV-1 Group M, the majority focusing on subtype B. As mentioned previously, while HIV-1 Group M subtype B has been the dominant HIV-1 strain in various parts of the world (Hemelaar et al. 2011), it only represents ~10% of cases worldwide; subtype C, which is the predominant strain that circulates in southern Africa, remains the most prevalent strain worldwide (Hemelaar et al. 2011, Serwin et al. 2021). Representation bias towards subtype B strains strongly points to how inequalities in resources, such as sequencing facilities and monetary investment in scientific research, can impact the ability of genomic epidemiologists to obtain a complete understanding of viral evolution in real time through outbreaks (Crane 2011), let alone the particular role of epistasis in viral evolution. Furthermore, as highlighted in previous sections, studies of epistasis in SARS-CoV-2 and HIV primarily focus on the S gene and the pol gene, respectively, meaning that much less is still understood about how epistasis manifests in the evolution of other genes in these viruses.

Therefore, more studies are needed on viruses that are underrepresented in the viral evolution and epistasis literature, such as fungal viruses and DNA viruses, as unique epistatic patterns can manifest in specific virus types. Important questions may include how the biochemical nature and structure of viral genomes influence the kinds of epistasis that manifest and how they affect evolutionary trajectories. And even within better characterized viral systems like influenza, HIV, and SARS-CoV-2, future studies should focus on understudied variants, genes, hosts, and/or phenotypes.

Limitations of methods to identify epistasis

Another challenge that hinders our complete understanding of epistasis in viral evolution is the limitations of existing methods (Table 2). In particular, we focus on the limitations of existing computational methods in identifying epistatic interactions and ongoing challenges in measuring higher-order interactions (see also Table 3 for a discussion of limitations specific to individual methods). Future studies will also need to consider these methodological challenges and develop approaches that can account for these limitations.

Limits of computational approaches

In our systematic review, we observed that across the three human viral pathogens (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and HIV), there were a number of studies that were computational-only, meaning that these studies primarily identified putative epistatic interactions using bioinformatics approaches. In the particular case of SARS-CoV-2, most studies are computational in nature (Supplementary Table S2B). In addition to not being able to validate these interactions without additional experimental work, another limitation of computational studies is that many of them identify epistatic interactions through associations and/or correlations between individual mutations (i.e. whether mutations are more likely to occur with each other than by chance), by looking at multiple genomic sequences (e.g. Rochman et al. (2021), Jankowiak et al. (2022), Alemrajabi et al. (2022), Neverov et al. (2023)). While not always the case (Alemrajabi et al. 2022, Neverov et al. 2023), this means that interactions identified by these methods are more likely to be of a positive nature (Rochman et al. 2021, Jankowiak et al. 2022) as identifying negative epistasis is more challenging with these methods (Lyons and Lauring 2018). In the case of SARS-CoV-2, where >75% of the studies reported positive epistatic interactions, and only 25% of the studies reported negative epistatic interactions (Fig. 2d), the limitations of the statistical methods used to identify epistatic interactions mean that we cannot confirm that positive epistasis is the most prevalent form of epistasis in the evolution of SARS-CoV-2.

Challenges in identifying higher-order interactions

Another challenge in current epistasis studies is identifying higher-order epistatic interactions. These interactions range from more than two mutations interacting with each other (e.g. third-order interactions between three mutations), to pairwise interactions changing with respect to genetic background (Weinreich et al. 2013a) or even environmental/host context (Lalíc and Elena 2013). Across the five viral systems in our systematic review, we also noticed that the proportion of studies that identified pairwise interactions was at least twice that of the proportion of studies identifying higher-order interactions (Fig. 2c).

While methods have been developed to study higher-order interactions, and higher-order epistasis has been shown to play a role in a number of fitness landscapes (Weinreich et al. 2013a), there are limitations to applying some of these existing methods. For example, the Walsh–Hadamard transform is typically applied to data, i.e. combinatorially complete with only two variants at a given locus of information (Weinreich et al. 2013a, Ogbunugafor and Scarpino 2022). Nevertheless, some of these methods have been applied to viral systems such as HIV (Crona et al. 2017), suggesting that existing methods can be applied to measure higher-order epistasis in viral evolution. Nonetheless, future methods should be developed to transcend the limitations of current methods, particularly as genomic epidemiological datasets are rarely combinatorially complete in structure.

Applications to genomic epidemiology

How could our findings influence genomic epidemiology? A richer understanding of more complex genomic signatures (including epistasis) of viral infection phenotypes can aid public health practices. For example, understanding mutation interactions can help experts more accurately determine when viral evolution yields variants of concern. This can be animated as a ‘watchlist’ of single mutations and suites of mutations that contribute to relevant virus phenotypes (Miller et al. 2016, Barnes et al. 2022, Innocenti et al. 2024).

An appreciation of the role of epistasis in genomic epidemiology must involve applying existing tools or developing new methods. In Table 3, we describe a few approaches used to measure epistasis. These constitute just a small sample of the many in existence, and we refer the reader to other works that highlight the methods used to measure epistasis in biological data sets (see, e.g. Johnson et al. (2023)). Therefore, in the future, we can perform retrospective analyses of existing genomic data on viruses, to ‘retell’ epidemiological stories of the past with a newer epistasis-inspired lens. This has already been shown in a recent study by Innocenti et al. (2024), where they demonstrated that epistatic interactions potentially implicated in the evolution of lineages with increased infectivity and immune escape could be identified in real time in a genomic database by conducting a retrospective analysis of the SARS-CoV-2 database (Innocenti et al. 2024). In addition, while our review has not focused on modelling studies, we note that many have incorporated epistasis into their epidemiological models to predict and/or explain viral evolution, such as evolving virulence and/or escape from vaccine-induced immune response (McLeod and Gandon 2022) or the punctuated evolutionary dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 (Nielsen et al. 2023).

In Fig. 3, we provide a conceptual picture for studying a hypothetical virus population that exploits animal hosts in an outbreak scenario. First, epidemiologists working with genomic data should consider how pairwise and higher-order interactions may influence the presumptive phenotypic consequences of outbreak-associated mutations, perhaps through interdisciplinary collaborations with evolutionary biologists. Furthermore, such mutations and mutation combinations should be tested in a broader number of contexts. These may include different genomic backgrounds (e.g. strains of a pathogenic virus) and in different ‘environments’ (e.g. different host types). This is supported by some studies in the systematic review, which have reported strain-specific mutational effects in the S gene of different variants of SARS-CoV-2 (Starr et al. 2022) and the NS1 gene of different influenza strains (Kim et al. 2022). Studies also reported host-specific epistatic interactions in influenza (human versus ferret hosts) (Lee et al. 2023) and experimental evolution of TEV (Arabidopsis thaliana versus Nicotiana tabacum) (Cervera et al. 2016b). From the perspective of evolutionary theory, these approaches highlight the relevance of cutting-edge topics such as ‘environmental epistasis’, referring to the notion that external environments can profoundly shape how epistatic interactions manifest between mutations in microbial disease systems (You and Yin 2002, Lindsey et al. 2013, Caudle et al. 2014, Ogbunugafor 2022).

Implications for media reporting and science communication

The problem of evaluating mutation effects in virus epidemics is also relevant to the intersection between epidemiology and science communication. This manifests in media reporting on the connection between mutations and outbreak-associated phenotypes (Carey and Glanz 2020, Cohen 2021, Rodriguez 2021, Tin 2024). Our results suggest that professional scientists and journalists should be mindful when reporting the phenotypic consequences of an epidemic-associated mutation. Specifically, we should emphasize (when possible) that genomic context can affect mutation effects (other mutations within a locus, or the genomic background of the pathogen). While explaining this level of detail might require detailed press conferences and media reports, it would have the longer-term benefit of accurately describing how viral evolution happens. We must also clarify that the charge to improve communication falls on both the basic scientists who study mutation effects in pathogens and on journalists and science communicators.

Conclusion

Here, we have performed one of the first systematic analyses of epistasis carried out across multiple types of viruses, three of which are the causes of the largest pandemics of the last century (influenza, HIV, and SARS-CoV-2). We find epistatic interactions of various sorts—intergenic and intragenic, pairwise and higher-order, positive, negative, and sign—to be widely prevalent across these settings, suggesting that epistasis is a central feature of how viral pathogen evolve. We have discussed the relevance of our findings with respect to theoretical concepts in evolutionary genetics such as the fitness landscape. And we have argued that epistasis should become part of the conceptual grammar and methodological toolkit of genomic epidemiology.

In sum, our systematic analysis of the literature reinforces an important message of modern population and evolutionary genetics: clearer links between genotype and phenotype will require more knowledge of how interactions between genetic parcels craft traits of interest. Genomic epidemiology can benefit from understanding how mutation effects depend on other mutations and genetic backgrounds, and this knowledge can aid our approach to the continuing threat of emerging infectious diseases.
