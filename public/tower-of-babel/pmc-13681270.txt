Meiotic recombination observed through preimplantation genetic testing for two X-linked conditions: first reported case in humans
Authors: Anna Iversen, Sara K Gerber, Solmaz Chamanara, Kajal Verma, Sophia M Adelson, Cindy Yang, Christina G Tise
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo report a case of human meiotic recombination illustrated by the use of preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) in a female patient heterozygous for pathogenic variants in cis on the X chromosome, highlighting the potential implications of meiotic recombination when performing PGT-M for ≥2 variants located on the same chromosome of a parental haplotype.DesignCase report.SubjectsA G3P3 female patient, heterozygous for two X-linked pathogenic variants in cis, involving the OTC and STS genes, sought to conceive an unaffected pregnancy through the use of in vitro fertilization (IVF) with PGT-M on the basis of her history of having a son with ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency and steroid sulfatase (STS) deficiency who died following a hyperammonemic crisis.ExposurePreimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders for maternal OTC and STS variants, previously determined to be in cis in the patient and her deceased son.Main Outcome MeasuresProportion of embryos that screened positive by PGT-M for both, neither, or only one of the maternal OTC and STS variants.ResultsPreimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders was performed on 13 embryos. Nine (69%) embryos screened positive for both or neither of the maternal OTC (c.788A>G) and STS (1.6Mb deletion of Xp22.31) variants, as expected given their configuration in cis. Four embryos (31%) screened positive for only one variant (either the OTC or STS variant), suggestive of X-linked meiotic recombination occurring at the frequency predicted on the basis of the genomic distance between STS and OTC. The patient transferred a female euploid embryo low-risk by PGT-M for the maternal OTC and STS variants, and declined prenatal diagnostic testing via chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis. The patient delivered a healthy term female infant via repeat Cesarean section.ConclusionMeiotic recombination may lead to unanticipated results when using PGT-M to screen embryos for multiple monogenic conditions involving variants on the same chromosome. Detection of linked variants when screening embryos for genetic conditions (i.e., instead of direct genotyping) could lead to a false-negative result. This case underscores the importance of involving medical geneticists and genetic counselors to facilitate comprehensive and accurate clinical genetic evaluations, variant interpretation, confirmatory testing, and risk assessment to promote informed reproductive decision-making.Key Words: Case report, linkage, meiotic recombination, PGT-M, X-linked

Reproductive partners at risk of having a child with an inherited genetic condition may wish to conceive using in vitro fertilization (IVF) with preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) of embryos to reduce the risk that a resulting fetus will be affected with the inherited genetic condition. Preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) involves biopsy of the trophectoderm of each embryo to obtain DNA for testing of the familial disease-causing variant(s) for subsequent selective transfer of embryos ostensibly unaffected by the genetic condition(s) in question (1). The PGT-M can be performed for ≥1 genetic conditions, provided the familial disease-causing variant(s) are known, and DNA from related positive controls is available. Depending on the clinical situation and laboratory capabilities, PGT-M using direct genotyping analysis and/or linkage-based analysis can be used to screen embryos for the presence or absence of the genetic variant(s).

When PGT-M is performed for two variants present in a single parent and located on the same chromosome (i.e., in cis configuration), it often is expected that embryos will either inherit both variants or neither variant because of chromosomal linkage during meiosis. This assumption is a foundational component of linkage-based PGT-M, which traditionally has been considered the gold standard for PGT-M, because of its ability to overcome the risk of allele dropout, favored amplification or failed amplification of one of two alleles, which disrupts the accuracy of PGT-M results (2, 3). Linkage-based PGT-M involves the evaluation of DNA markers located in close proximity (i.e., linkage disequilibrium) to the disease-causing variant of interest, and presumes both variants will be co-inherited by offspring (3). However, this may be confounded by meiotic recombination, the process by which homologous chromosomes exchange genetic material and create new combinations of alleles within offspring. The likelihood of a meiotic recombination event between two variants on the same chromosome of a parental haplotype increases with increased genomic distance between the two loci; this is the principle behind the concept of a centimorgan (cM), or a genomic distance that corresponds to an approximate 1% chance of meiotic recombination, or roughly equal to 1 million base pairs (Mb). In contrast to linkage-based PGT-M, which relies on linked markers for variant detection, PGT-M assays that use a direct genotyping approach can be used in combination with linkage-based PGT-M to improve variant detection (3).

We present a case of PGT-M used to detect the presence of two maternal disease-causing variants in cis on the X chromosome approximately 31 Mb apart (OTC and STS), demonstrating evidence of meiotic recombination at the estimated rate of approximately 31% (4 of 13) of screened embryos. This case demonstrates unanticipated PGT-M results, highlighting the clinical significance of considering this biological phenomenon in the interpretation of all PGT-M results, particularly those of linkage-based assays.

Case report

A 35-year-old then G2P2 female of South Asian (Indian) ancestry presented for preconception genetic counseling after recently discovering she was heterozygous for a disease-causing variant in OTC, an X-linked gene associated with the urea cycle disorder ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency. She was referred for preconception genetic counseling to discuss perinatal screening and testing options for future pregnancies because of a family history of OTC deficiency and steroid sulfatase (STS) deficiency in her recently deceased son. The patient provided explicit written consent for this publication.

The patient’s prior pregnancy with her son was notable for negative OTC carrier screening and low estriol on maternal serum screening. Maternal fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) studies identified the common pathogenic Xp22.3 deletion of the STS gene, explaining the low estriol level. The patient had genetic counseling to discuss this finding and was offered amniocentesis, which was declined. The patient’s son was delivered at 37 weeks via repeat Cesarean section. Physical examination at birth was notable for skin findings consistent with X-linked ichthyosis caused by steroid sulfatase deficiency (Fig. 1), but he otherwise was well-appearing (4).

At 45 hours of life, the newborn developed respiratory distress and subsequent cardiac arrest requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation. He was transferred to a quaternary neonatal intensive care unit, where he was started on dialysis. Comprehensive biochemical genetics laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyperammonemia (>900 mM; reference 55-107), marked elevation of urine orotic acid, and reduced plasma citrulline (5 mM; reference 1-45). Phenotype-informed rapid genome sequencing was ordered with the following clinical phenotype provided: pulmonary hemorrhage, prolonged prothrombin time, ichthyosis, abnormal pulmonary valve morphology (thickened), mitral regurgitation, abnormality of the adrenal glands (mass on right), hyperammonemia, lactic acidosis, organic aciduria, orotic aciduria, hydronephrosis (b/l), atrial septal defect, patent foramen ovale, anuria (resolved with catheter), apnea, erythema, scaling skin, hypernatremia, hyperkalemia, elevated circulating creatine kinase concentration, and encephalopathy (hypoxic-ischemic). Genome sequencing identified a maternally inherited, likely pathogenic variant in OTC (NM_000531.6(OTC):c.788A>G (p.Asp263Gly)), all consistent with a diagnosis of severe neonatal OTC deficiency (5). Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated extensive neurologic injury secondary to his hyperammonemic crisis, and the family decided to compassionately withdraw care. The newborn died at three days of life.

Retrospective inquiry into the patient’s prior negative carrier screening results confirmed the presence of the familial OTC variant, which previously had been classified by the carrier screening laboratory as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) on the basis of the American College of Medical Genetics variant classification guidelines at the time of testing, and therefore, was not reported initially. The VUS results are not typically included as part of carrier screening, per professional guidance from American College of Medical Genetics, which recommends that carrier screening results should include pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants, and that VUS reporting may be appropriate only in reproductive partners of identified carriers (6). Additional genetic testing via chromosomal microarray further characterized the patient’s pathogenic 1.6 Mb deletion of Xp22.31 as the recurrent 1.6 Mb pathogenic deletion encompassing the entirety of four coding genes, STS, PUDP, PNPLA4, and VCX, of which only STS is known to be associated with a genetic condition. Of note, the genome sequencing laboratory did not initially report the Xp22.31 deletion in the patient’s son; however, given the high clinical suspicion on the basis of his skin findings, reanalysis was performed using the patient’s DNA as a positive control. Ultimately, this genetic evaluation confirmed the presence of the heterozygous OTC variant and STS deletion in the patient, both of which were inherited by her son in a hemizygous state (i.e., presence of a variant on the single and only X chromosome in an XY individual) and confirmed the variants to be in cis configuration on the maternal haplotype.

After comprehensive genetic counseling, the patient and her partner elected to proceed with IVF with PGT-M. Whole-genome amplification of embryo samples was performed using the Ion SingleSeq kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hunt Valley, Maryland). Direct and indirect fluorescent polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based analysis of the maternal OTC variant, including pathogenic variant genotyping and linked polymorphic marker analysis, was performed using fragment analysis on an automated DNA sequencer. Indirect analysis for the Xp22.31 deletion was completed through PCR amplification of linked polymorphic markers. Thirteen embryos were created, each screened for aneuploidy (PGT-A) and PGT-M for the maternal OTC and STS variants (Table 1). Nine embryos (69%) screened positive for both or neither of the OTC and STS variants, consistent with and expected of two variants on the same chromosome and parental haplotype (i.e., in cis). Four embryos (31%) screened positive for only one of the two maternal X-linked variants, suggestive of a meiotic recombination event between the OTC and STS variants, vs. a less likely repeatedly inaccurate PGT-M result because of recurrent genotype error.

To assess this further, the distance between the OTC and STS genes was calculated using centimorgans (cM). The OTC gene is located at Xp11.4 (Genomic coordinates [GRCh38]: X:38,327,684-38,422,928). The STS gene is located at Xp22.31 (Genomic coordinates [GRCh38]: X:7,147,290-7,354,641). These two genes are located approximately 31 million base pairs away from each other, or approximately 31 centimorgans. This suggests a 31% chance of recombination between OTC and STS, which is consistent with the observed frequency of 31% (4 of 13 embryos).

After extensive genetic counseling with genetic counselors and a medical geneticist, the patient and her partner proceeded with transferring embryo #3, a female euploid embryo at low risk for the maternal OTC and STS variants by PGT-M. Prenatal diagnostic testing via chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis was offered to confirm the PGT-M results and was declined. The patient delivered a healthy female infant at 39 weeks via repeat Cesarean section, who had negative newborn screening and continues to grow and develop without concerns. No further confirmatory testing has been performed at this time.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of human meiotic recombination illustrated by the use of PGT-M for two disease-causing variants on the same chromosomal and parental haplotype (i.e., in cis). This case represents a highly specific clinical scenario, characterized by a few layers of unique clinical and decisional factors, including a female patient who is heterozygous for two X-linked monogenic conditions on the same chromosome pursues IVF, decides to proceed with PGT-M for both conditions, and has a sufficient number of embryos created and tested to appreciate the biological phenomenon of meiotic recombination. Although the assumption that variants demonstrated to be in cis will co-segregate in future offspring is generally reasonable for closely linked loci, it becomes increasingly unreliable as physical and genetic distance between variants increases. The maternal OTC variant at Xp11.4 and the maternal 1.6 Mb Xp22.31 deletion encompassing STS are separated by approximately 31 Mb (approximately 31 cM), which corresponds to an approximately 31% recombination probability. The frequency of observed embryo results (4/13, 31%) is consistent with the expected frequency of recombination, evidence to support meiotic recombination rather than laboratory artifact as the explanation for discordant segregation. This case represents an often-overlooked, but known, limitation of linkage-based PGT-M for monogenic disorders: the potential for meiotic recombination to disrupt assumed patterns of co-inheritance, even when familial pathogenic variants have been demonstrated clearly to be in linkage disequilibrium and/or in cis.

Preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders is used widely for monogenic disorders for which the disease-causing locus has been identified (1). Linkage-based PGT-M often is deemed the gold standard for PGT-M because of its ability to overcome the risk of allele dropout disrupting the accuracy of PGT-M results (2, 3). However, linkage-based PGT-M can misclassify embryos if meiotic recombination occurs between the disease-causing variant and its linked markers. In such cases, an embryo may inherit the pathogenic variant but not the expected marker pattern, potentially resulting in a false-negative classification, where an affected embryo is identified incorrectly as unaffected. Genetic recombination at or near the target locus can compromise accuracy of PGT-M screening results, as recombination events occur across most chromosomes (7), including the X chromosome (8). Linkage-based PGT-M relies on the assumption of stable co-segregation between markers and pathogenic variants (9), and therefore, recombination represents a known, although often underappreciated, source of false-negative PGT-M screening results (10).

The clinical implications of this phenomenon are significant. Linkage analysis alone may fail to detect recombination and, therefore, may misclassify affected embryos. Whenever possible, the use of direct variant analysis can reduce the risk of such misclassification. In this case, direct detection was possible only for the maternal OTC variant. The 1.6 Mb STS deletion remained incompatible with direct detection methods, so linkage-based analysis with polymorphic markers was used. This highlights the technical limitations of PGT-M for CNVs and the potential value of high-resolution approaches, which could directly detect small deletions, such as SNP-array or NGS methods on the basis of unique CNV breakpoints, to replace multiple PCR-based tests for the management of complex allele carriers (2, 11, 12, 13). For individuals for whom there is a concern for multiple disease-causing variants located on the same chromosome, testing for each variant in all embryos should be performed, and risk interpretation should be provided on the basis of results for variants, rather than a single variant. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine has recognized that meiotic recombination may pose technical limitations for PGT-M technology, specifically if a relative being used during linkage analysis is recombinant for the region(s) of interest, thus recommending the offering of confirmatory prenatal diagnosis during pregnancies following transfer of PGT-M-tested embryos (3). Additionally, positive and negative predictive value information are not available for individualized PGT-M probes, another important consideration for offering confirmatory prenatal diagnosis. An additional limitation of reproductive technology in this case is that neither the OTC nor STS variants were identified on preconception carrier screening, with the OTC variant classified as a VUS and STS not included on the initial carrier screening panel. This unique case illustrates the potential impact of carrier screening VUS results; carrier screening laboratories may wish to consider thoughtful reporting of VUS results in X-linked conditions in females and/or homozygous VUS results, in addition to the current guidelines that encourage reporting of VUS results in recessive genes for which the other reproductive partner has a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant (6).

Relevance of recombination on the X chromosome also is highlighted in this report. Recombination occurs more frequently during female meiosis than male meiosis (14). High-resolution, sex-specific recombination maps of the human genome show measurable rates of crossing over across broad chromosomal intervals in female meiosis, indicating that loci separated by tens of megabases (such as along the X chromosome short arm) experience detectable crossover events in typical gametogenesis (15). In this context, recombination is not a rare complication but rather a probable biological event. Genetic counselors and geneticists should consider informing families about the possibility of recombination events that could alter expected inheritance patterns, ensuring informed consent for reproductive decision-making.

The advantages of our report include the availability of a complete genetic evaluation of multiple members of this family using chromosomal microarray analysis, which determined the location and breakpoints of the STS deletion. The application of direct pathogenic variant detection instead of linkage analysis in PGT-M made it possible to demonstrate the occurrence of recombination. Limitations include the relatively small embryo sample size (n = 13), which limited frequency estimates. In addition, confirmatory prenatal testing was declined by the patient, restricting post-transfer outcome validation.

This phenomenon raises questions for future research, including further investigation into whether there is a threshold for the distance between two genetic loci and/or the type of genetic variants that can lead to meiotic recombination in the setting of PGT-M. Prospectively following the reproductive outcomes of PGT-M cycles for ≥2 linked conditions to confirm the reliability of the risk assessment for misdiagnosis is another future research direction.

In conclusion, this report demonstrates the foundational concept of meiotic recombination, unveiled by unanticipated PGT-M embryo screening results for two disease-causing genetic variants known to be in cis on the maternal X chromosome. X-linked conditions of reproductive significance, as the presence of a disease-causing variant in only one reproductive partner can lead to an increased reproductive risk. This case illustrates the possibility of false-negative PGT-M results when only linked variant technology is used, as opposed to direct genotyping. Health care providers and laboratories should consider estimates of meiotic recombination when using PGT-M to screen embryos for multiple monogenic conditions on the same chromosome, particularly those located on the X chromosome. This case highlights the importance of involving medical geneticists and genetic counselors in centers performing genetic screening of embryos to support informed reproductive decision-making.
