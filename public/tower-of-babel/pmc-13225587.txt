ENOX2 and the related mechanisms as potential genetic targets for varicose veins of lower extremities: An integrated bioinformatics and Mendelian analysis
Authors: Qiurui He, Xiaohong Zhang, Chen Tao, Chenming Chen, Weiming Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We employed an integrated bioinformatics screening approach along with Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore potential genetic targets for varicose veins of lower extremities (VVs) and identify potential treatment options for VVs. Differential expression analysis was conducted using R software to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of VVs from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify co-expression networks. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted for the identified genes. A protein–protein interaction network was constructed to analyze the interactions among the identified genes. Additionally, genome-wide association studies data for VVs were downloaded for MR analysis. Various methods, including inverse-variance weighted, were employed to assess potential causal associations with VVs risk, followed by sensitivity analysis. The DEGs identified from the VVs Gene Expression Omnibus dataset included 180 upregulated genes and 335 downregulated genes. Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis revealed that the downregulated DEGs were significantly associated with nuclear protein-containing complexes and nucleic acid binding (P < .05). WGCNA highlighted a highly significant “turquoise” module comprising 78 downregulated genes (P = 2e − 04). The protein–protein interaction network analysis of the significant DEGs and the WGCNA “turquoise” module identified 224 nodes and 491 edges, uncovering several hub genes such as BRCA1, NCBP2, GTPBP4, HDAC2, KHDRBS1, and HNRNPR. Detailed functional enrichment analysis indicated involvement in tumor-like cellular proliferation and differentiation processes, including protein acetylation, RNA splicing, and metabolic processes. MR analysis revealed a causal association between the tumor-related gene Ecto-NOX disulfide-thiol exchanger 2 (ENOX2) and the risk of VVs, with a statistical significance (odds ratio: 1.0016; 95% confidence interval: 1.0003–1.0029; P = .015) according to inverse-variance weighted analysis. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the absence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy in the observed associations (P > .05). “Leave-one-out” validation analysis did not indicate any changes. Our study unveils the involvement of ENOX2 and the related mechanisms in the pathogenesis of VVs, suggesting their potential as genetic targets for treatment.

1. Introduction

Varicose veins of lower extremities (VVs) is a prevalent chronic condition, affecting a significant portion of adults in the United States and placing a substantial economic strain on the healthcare system.[1] Those with varicose veins commonly experience skin changes and discomfort in the lower limbs, including symptoms like swelling, restlessness, and itching, and a notable percentage may ultimately develop severe ulceration.[2] The high incidence of varicose veins in the lower extremities imposes a significant strain on the healthcare system, particularly in cases where complications have arisen. Treatment options for VVs range from conservative measures like compression stockings and lifestyle adjustments to more invasive procedures such as sclerotherapy, endovenous laser therapy, and surgery.[3] Despite the availability of these treatments, it remains crucial to identify potential factors that may increase the risk of developing varicose veins in order to enable an ongoing need for more precise and effective therapies for individuals with VVs.[4]

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database serves as a publicly accessible repository for high-throughput gene expression data, chips, and microarrays, supporting functional genomics research.[5] It encompasses diverse raw data, encompassing disease gene sequencing expression profiles, making it a valuable resource for exploring disease-specific gene expression patterns and identifying potential targets. By elucidating disease-causing genes and their associated functions and cellular molecular pathways, the database offers valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms and potential treatment strategies for further investigation.[6] Mendelian randomization (MR) utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as genetic instruments, randomly assigned at conception and unaffected by environmental factors, which is ideal for causal inference.[7] Significantly associated SNPs are used as instrumental variables (IVs) in MR analysis. Algorithms like inverse-variance weighted (IVW) and Egger regression can synthesize effects estimated from multiple SNPs. MR overcomes confounding and reverse causality, providing more reliable estimates of causal effects than traditional observational studies. MR has successfully identified biomarkers and therapeutic targets for various diseases.[7,8] However, limited studies have established a causal relationship and mechanisms for VVs using the GEO and MR analysis.[9,10]

On the basis of the aforementioned, the aim of our study was to utilize the GEO database for transcriptome profiling analysis to identify differential gene expression data associated with VVs. We intend to search for potential candidate disease-causing genes, pathways, cellular functions, and molecular pathways from this analysis. Subsequently, through MR analysis of the potential functional angle identified in the above analysis, we aim to explore potential therapeutic targets for VVs, offering new insights into novel treatment approaches for this condition.

2. Methods

2.1. GEO analysis of VVs

2.1.1. Data source and gene expression profile

The GEO database, an open-access repository of high-throughput gene expression data, chips, and microarrays, was queried using the keywords “varicose veins of lower extremities” to obtain gene expression profile data for patients with VVs. The retrieved datasets were subjected to the following screening criteria. Firstly, the profiles should include both case and control groups. Secondly, the datasets should provide raw data for further analysis. Thirdly, all samples must originate from human tissues. The dataset identified for future studies was GEO series (GSE) number GSE51260, which consisted of raw transcriptomic information. Specifically, the dataset comprised 12 specimens, including 6 VVs samples and 6 adjacent normal segments of saphenous veins serving as control samples. The gene expression profiles were determined using GPL15314 Arraystar Human microarray V2.0 (Agilent-033010 Probe Name version) for Expression profiling by array (Table 1). Original data underwent background correction, normalization, and relative expression calculations. Subsequently, a genetic matrix was obtained with specimen names and gene symbols defining the rows and columns, respectively, for subsequent analysis. Ethical approval was not required as this study solely utilized publicly available data and did not involve any human experiments.

2.1.2. Data processing and the differential genes (DEGs) analysis

The raw data was analyzed using the “Limma” package in the R software, and the results were visualized through box plots. The matrix data underwent screening for differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A significance threshold of adjusted P values < .05 was used to determine statistical significance. Additionally, genes with a log FC (foldchange) value greater than or equal to 1.0 were selected as DEGs. The distribution of the GSE data was assessed using boxplots and principal component analysis cluster plots. Heatmaps and volcano plots were utilized to display the upregulated and downregulated DEGs.

2.1.3. Gene ontology (GO) function and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs

We further utilized the R software in conjunction with the “Bioconductor” and “GOplot” packages to perform GO function analysis of the DEGs. This analysis encompassed 3 categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was employed to gain insights into high-level functions and biological systems based on large-scale molecular datasets generated through high-throughput experimental technologies. A significance threshold of adjusted P value < .05 was applied as the cutoff criterion for both analyses.

2.1.4. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The DEGs satisfying the adjusted P value (<.05) were subjected to WGCNA using the R package. WGCNA was used to identify gene co-expression modules with great importance in biology and to discover the relevance between diseases and gene networks. The adjacency matrix was built using a soft threshold for topology calculation and a gene–gene correlation matrix to describe the degree of association between the nodes. According to the optimum soft threshold, the adjacency matrix was converted into a topological overlap matrix. Next, a gene hierarchical clustering dendrogram was performed to identify co-expression modules. Gene significance and module membership in each module were calculated to plot the scatter plots. The module eigengene was also calculated. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis was applied to estimate the relevance of disease emergence to the merged modules.

2.1.5. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction and module analysis

The online database Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (http://string-db.org/) version 11.0 was utilized to predict the network involving the downregulated DEGs or the most significant module identified through WGCNA. In this analysis, a confidence score threshold of > 0.7 and a species restriction to H sapiens were applied. The constructed PPI networks were visualized using Cytoscape software version 3.8.0. Subsequently, the Cytoscape plugin for molecular complex detection was employed to identify key modules within the PPI network. All interactions data were imported into Cytoscape for visualization purposes. Hub genes were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape, with the following parameters set: Hubba nodes ranked by degree; options = check the first-stage nodes, display the shortest path and the expanded subnetwork.

2.2. MR study

2.2.1. MR data sources and study design

Due to a connection between VVs and tumor-like cellular amplification pathways indentified in the GEO analysis of VVs, we conducted a screening with MR analysis on commonly studied tumor amplification-related proteins from the IEU database, including MYC, ERBB2, EGFR, MET, CCNB1, MDM2, and Ecto-NOX disulfide-thiol exchanger 2 (ENOX2). The results indicated a positive association between ENOX2 and VVs, while no positive relationships were found for the other proteins (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content). Thus, we focused the subsequent analyses on ENOX2, as the primary research subject. In details, we utilized publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from the IEU OpenGWAS Project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) to investigate the relationship between genetic susceptibility to ENOX2 and the risk of VVs. Summary-level data for ENOX2 involved 3301 individuals with 10,534,735 SNPs, while the VVs data were extracted from a GWAS comprising 10,044 cases and 452,966 controls, with a total of 9,851,867 SNPs. Detailed information for all summary data was provided in Table 2. In greater details, the ENOX2 dataset, referred to as “prot-a-946,” consisted of genetic data from 3301 individuals of European ancestry. This dataset contained information on 10,534,735 SNPs, which were used for genetic association studies in the context of MR. The data was particularly relevant for studying genetic variations linked to ENOX2 gene expression and its potential causal effects on various health outcomes. The VVs dataset, identified as “ukb-b-6720,” was derived from the UK Biobank and included data from a large sample size –10,044 cases and 452,966 controls, of European ancestry. With 9851,867 SNPs included in the analysis, this dataset was commonly used for large-scale genetic studies, including MR, to identify genetic risk factors for various traits and diseases. The extensive sample size and variety of genetic variants made it suitable for evaluating causal relationships in epidemiogical research.

To assess causality between genetic susceptibility to ENOX2 and VVs risk, a two-sample MR analysis was performed, controlling for horizontal pleiotropy. Genetic variants, the SNPs, were used as IVs to explore the causal relationship, following the 3 main assumptions in MR analysis: IVs are strongly related to exposure factors; IVs are not associated with confounding factors; and IVs only influence the risk of outcomes through exposure.

2.2.2. Two-sample MR analyses

The two-sample MR analysis using R Studio was applied to assess the causal relationship between genetic susceptibility to ENOX2 and the risk of VVs. SNPs were selected as IVs based on the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−5). To ensure the independence of the IVs, we applied a linkage disequilibrium threshold for grouping of r2 < 0.001 and a genetic distance of 10,000 kb. The PhenoScanner was utilized to investigate the associations of each included SNP with potential confounders, and those strongly associated with the confounders (P < 5 × 10−5) were excluded. In details, we used the Phenoscanner website to input the specific rsID of the SNPs identified in our study. Based on the traits associated with these SNPs provided by the website, we assessed whether any of these traits were confounders (i.e., related to factors such as prolonged standing, obesity, long periods of sitting, crossing legs, smoking, etc). If a confounding factor was identified, we considered the SNP to be associated with the confounder and excluded it from subsequent MR analyses.

In addition, we used F-statistics to address weak instrument bias. An F-statistic > 10 was indicative of a sufficiently strong correlation between the IVs and the exposure, thus avoiding weak instrument bias. For univariable analysis, we employed the IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median methods, and other approaches. The primary estimation method utilized was IVW, providing an asymptotic estimate of the standard error of the causal estimate from each variant. A two-sided P value < .05 was considered suggestive of statistical significance. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted, comprising 3 components: gene pleiotropy test; heterogeneity test; and leave-one-out method. To evaluate gene pleiotropy, the MR-Egger regression (MR-Egger) and MR-PRESSO method were employed. The MR-PRESSO method can identify outliers and provide a causal estimate after excluding relevant outliers to assess the horizontal pleiotropy. The Cochrane Q value was calculated for assessing heterogeneity. Additionally, a leave-one-out validation analysis was performed by conducting the MR analysis again while omitting each SNP in turn to determine whether any single SNP was driving the association.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing Austria, https://www.r-project.org/). A two-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall, the data consistently demonstrated a higher number of downregulated genes compared to upregulated genes.

3.5. MR analysis on the causality of VVs

On the basis of the above analysis investigating the mechanisms related to VVs, we further explored the causal relationship between genetic susceptibility of ENOX2 and the risk of VVs. A total of 20 SNPs were included as IVs in this analysis. None of the 20 selected SNPs were found to be associated with confounding factors, leading no exclusion of specific SNPs from our analysis. Besides, the F-statistics of these SNPs were all >10 (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content). Among all MR methods employed, the univariable MR analysis using the IVW method indicated a positive association between ENOX2 and VVs (odds ratio [OR]: 1.0016; 95% confidence interval: 1.0003–1.0029, P = .015). However, no significant associations were observed using other statistical models such as weighted median, MR-Egger, and MR-PRESSO (Fig. 6). Additionally, the forest plot illustrating the causal effect of ENOX2 on the risk of VVs, both for each SNP individually and overall, demonstrated a statistically significant relationship based on the IVW method (Fig. 7).

4. Discussion

Our study utilized the GEO database and MR analysis to jointly explore the pathogenic cellular mechanisms and potential disease targets associated with VVs. In details, our research was divided into 2 interconnected parts, both focusing on the relevant functional aspects and targets of VVs. We initially employed transcriptomic data of VVs to compare gene expression profiles between veins with varicosities and non-varicose veins, identifying DEGs. Through enrichment functional analysis, we preliminarily determined the functions and molecular pathways associated with these DEGs. Our results revealed statistically significant enrichment in cellular functions and molecular pathways for upregulated and downregulated genes, with particular significance observed in the enrichment analysis of downregulated genes. Subsequently, we utilized WGCNA to perform modular analysis on the DEGs identified through gene sequencing.[11] We focused on identifying the module directly correlated with the downregulation of gene expression, specifically the “turquoise” module. Furthermore, we conducted a PPI network analysis on the downregulated genes and the significantly downregulated modules identified by WGCNA. This analysis allowed us to identify key nodes in the network,[12] including BRCA1, NCBP2, GTPBP4, HDAC2, KHDRBS1, and HNRNPR.

The BRCA1 gene regulates cellular proliferation and apoptosis, ensuring the division and growth of normal cells. Mutations in this gene can result in loss of protein function or partial loss of function, leading to cellular abnormalities.[13] Under normal circumstances, the BRCA1 protein is involved in DNA repair, preventing mutations in cells. However, when the BRCA1 gene is mutated, the cell’s ability to repair DNA decreases, increasing the risk of tumor formation.[14] GTPBP4 is a GTP-binding protein that acts as a molecular switch in cellular activity in tumor.[15] HDAC2 belongs to the histone deacetylase family, which plays a role in deacetylating lysine residues in the N-terminal region of core histones. It functions by forming large multiprotein complexes and is involved in transcription regulation, cell cycle progression, and developmental events.[16] KHDRBS1 contains KH domains and is associated with RNA-binding signal transduction. It plays a role in various cellular processes, such as alternative splicing, cell cycle regulation, RNA 3’ end formation, tumor development, and modulation of the human immune system.[17] These key genes are mostly associated with cell expansion and proliferation activities in tumors. Further functional and pathway analysis of these genes in our study validated their involvement in functions related to cell and tumor proliferation and differentiation, including binding with nuclear proteins and protein methylation modifications related to proliferation. Literatures reported that VVs could also be caused by tumor-like dilation of venous wall cells. Venous varicosity expanded like a tumor and had a function similarity to tumors.[18] In the early stages of venous varicosity, the venous pressure increased and the lumen of the vessel mildly expanded, with submucosal tissue (mainly in the muscle layer) beginning to proliferate and thicken. Meanwhile, the circular channels that were easily compressed became more prominent during the middle stage as venous pressure continued to increase and blood reflux slowed down. The vessel walls exhibited atrophy and underwent regressive changes.[19] In the late stage, the degenerative changes in the venous wall became more pronounced, with loss of venous tension and further expansion of the lumen, resulting in severe tortuous or bead-like varicose veins, and even tumor-like varicose veins.[20] Based on the findings from the previous section, we focused on the characteristics of tumor-like cellular amplification and applied the MR analysis to explore potential pathogenic targets associated with VVs.

Due to the identification of a connection between VVs and tumorigenic amplification pathways in the first part of analysis, a screening using common tumor amplification-related proteins from the IEU database was then conducted. The results indicated a positive association between ENOX2 and VVs. Thus, we focused our subsequent analyses on ENOX2 as the primary research subject. Our later MR analysis confirmed a positive correlation between ENOX2 and the VVs SNPs, which could promote the incidence of venous varicosity. The target, ENOX2, is also a target related to tumor dilation oxidative stress.[21] It is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (reduced form) oxidase that can regulate cell growth and division. Studies found out that ENOX2 was highly expressed in malignant tumor cells during the process of proliferation and growth, and entered the blood.[22] Literature reported that the main function of ENOX2 protein was related to cell proliferation and expansion.[23] Our MR analysis results also confirmed the above viewpoint. During the cell enlargement, there is an production of exogenous nitrogen oxide, which is called ENOX1. ENOX1 is strictly regulated by the cell status. When cells need to enlarge and differentiate, ENOX1 can react with growth factors and hormones. However, in cancer, ENOX1 is replaced by an unregulated exogenous-NOX protein, which is called ENOX2. It does not react with growth factors and hormones, but could be structurally activated, allowing cells to grow uncontrollably. Both ENOX1 and ENOX2 are located in the outer membrane of the cytoplasm because that is where the process of controlling cell enlargement occurs.[24] The outer membrane cells shed, and their ENOX2 enters the bloodstream, allowing ENOX2 proteins to be detected in the serum. Cancer cells require ENOX2 for growth, and inhibiting ENOX2 with antibodies, drugs, or chemical substances could prevent cell proliferation, making it a promising target for intervention and treatment.[25]

Some previous studies employed MR analysis to investigate the genetic determinants of VVs and identify some potential pathogenic targets. Shadrina et al conducted a large-scale genetic study revealing 12 loci associated with VVs. Their work emphasized genes like CASZ1 and PIEZO1, and used MR to suggest causal effects of proteins like MICB and CD209 on VVs risk.[26] In a subsequent study, they confirmed that elevated CD209 levels were genetically linked to increased VVs risk.[9] Additionally, Lin et al utilized MR to identify 8 plasma proteins significant associated with increased riks of VVs, suggesting potential drug targets like IRF3 and LUM and advancing therapeutic strategies for VVs.[10] In our study, we integrated the GEO and IEU databases to comprehensively investigate the potential pathogenic mechanisms and targets of VVs. First, we performed differential gene expression analysis using clinical human samples from the GEO database, comparing venous segments with and without VVs. Based on the identified cellular and MFs, we further explored potential pathogenic targets for VVs through MR analysis using data from the IEU database. In our view, the innovation of this study lied in the differential gene analysis of VVs through genetic sequencing, followed by the corresponding MR analysis. The results suggested that VVs may be associated with cellular tumor-like amplification revealed by GEO analysis. Additionally, MR analysis was employed to validate and identify ENOX2 as a potential causal target related to cellular tumor-like amplification for VVs. This verification supported the cellular tumor-like amplification characteristics identified in the differential gene analysis, providing direction for potential therapeutic targets in VVs.

There were some limitations in our study. Firstly, we only screened the venous varicosity gene sequencing expression dataset, with 6 samples in each group and a total of 12 samples. However, these findings have not been validated in other studies on venous varicosity gene sequencing expression. Additionally, the gene sequencing data we used also included long non-coding RNA and microRNA, which might involve other biological scope. Secondly, in terms of MR analysis, our screening scope was relatively limited. We were unable to analyze all tumor-related target genes, and the degree of correlation between the identified targets and the pathogenesis of venous varicosity was relatively small. Additionally, although the OR in our study revealed a statistical significance (P = .015), the specific value was relatively low (1.0016; 95% confidence interval: 1.0003–1.0029). It may be related to the limited number of IVs or the restriction of the study population to a single ethnic group, specifically the European descent. Finally, our GEO differential gene expression analysis did not directly involve the gene expression of ENOX2, and the validation of ENOX2 target genes in the context of VVs or clinical research were not conducted. In conclusion, by the bioinformatics and MR analysis, our study unveiled the involvement of ENOX2 and tumor-like mechanisms in the pathogenesis of VVs. In future studies, based on the findings of our research, we would aim to conduct VVs-related intervention or modulation studies from the perspective of cellular tumor-like expansion and explore the effect of targeting ENOX2 on cell or animal models of VVs. This would help explore the underlying pathogenic mechanisms of VVs and identify potential therapeutic measurements.
