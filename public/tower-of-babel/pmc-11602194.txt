Different Behavior of Density Perturbations Between Dayside and Nightside in the Martian Thermosphere and the Ionosphere Associated With Atmospheric Gravity Waves
Authors: Hiromu Nakagawa, Scott L England, Aishwarya Kumar, Mehdi Benna, Yuki Harada, Shotaro Sakai, Naoki Terada, Kanako Seki, Nao Yoshida
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the excitation mechanism of ionospheric perturbations on Mars by the Neutral Gas and Ion Mass Spectrometer (NGIMS) onboard Mars Atmosphere and Volatile EvolutioN (MAVEN), we categorize ionospheric perturbations into three cases: (a) the ion‐neutral coupling cases where ion and neutral perturbations are well coupled, (b) the ion‐specific cases where ion perturbations move independently from neutrals, and (c) the coronal mass ejection cases associated with solar wind extreme events. A representative number of cases from total profiles are compared with a numerical model to determine the fraction that can be explained by an atmospheric gravity waves (GW). The neutral perturbations on the dayside at 170–190 km altitudes are in excellent agreement with the GW. Whereas, contrary to previous thoughts, neutral perturbations are not necessarily explained by the GW especially on the nightside at 190–210 km. Ion perturbations on the dayside at 170–190 km also show a good agreement with the GW. The agreement becomes extremely low on the nightside at 190–210 km, reaching the limit of strong ion‐neutral coupling around 190 km. Further investigation found that the behavior of the ion perturbations explicitly depends on the dayside and nightside. Its dominant driver potentially differs clearly between dayside and nightside. Statistics of relative perturbations demonstrate a clear effect associated with species scale height in neutrals. Whereas, the correlation between ions and neutrals breaks down at high solar zenith angle near southern dusk. We see currently unexplained behavior that cannot be fully interpreted by GW both at night and near southern dusk.Keywords: Mars, atmosphere, gravity waves, thermosphere, ionosphere, solar wind

Introduction

Large‐amplitude density perturbations ubiquitously exist in the Martian thermosphere (England et al., 2017; Siddle et al., 2019; Terada et al., 2017; Vals et al., 2019; Yîgit et al., 2015) and ionosphere (Collinson et al., 2019; Leelavathi et al., 2023; Mayyasi et al., 2019). Typical amplitudes of 10%–20% seen on Mars (Terada et al., 2017) are larger than those on Earth's thermosphere (∼1% at low latitude and 3%–8% at high latitude (Bruinsma & Forbes, 2008)).

One plausible excitation mechanism is the upward propagating atmospheric gravity waves (GW) from the lower atmosphere (e.g., Creasey et al., 2006; Fritts et al., 2006; Nakagawa et al., 2020; Tolson et al., 2007; Yiğit et al., 2021). Effective momentum/energy transports from the lower to upper atmosphere generate not only thermospheric perturbations but also ionospheric perturbations via ion‐neutral coupling on the dayside (Leelavathi et al., 2023; Mayyasi et al., 2019). In fact, Harada, Gurnett, Kopf, Halekas, and Ruhunusiri (2018) suggest the GW as a cause of the ionospheric diffuse echoes observed by Mars Advanced Radar for Subsurface and Ionosphere Sounding (MARSIS) onboard Mars Express. Collinson et al. (2019) show the periodic ionospheric waves driven by GW in the thermosphere which may have propagated upward from the lower atmosphere.

Another cause that drives the ionospheric perturbations is the external forcing by the solar wind (SW) and solar energetic particles. Dubinin et al. (2009) and Harada et al. (2017, 2018b) report the ionospheric disturbances and irregularities associated with solar extreme events via ionization processes and deposition of energy into the atmosphere. This is due to the lack of a strong intrinsic magnetic field on Mars, which causes a direct interaction of the upper atmosphere with space weather on Mars. The external forcing on the Martian ionosphere is pronounced and may sometimes create features observed by in situ observations that appear like neutral‐atmospheric wave fluctuations.

The ionosphere is important for Mars climate evolution in that it forms the reservoir from which the majority of oxygen escapes from Mars today and has over much of its history (Jakosky et al., 2018; Lillis et al., 2017). However, the excitation mechanism of ionospheric perturbations remains an open question. The relative importance of the impacts of GW and solar wind (SW) on the ionospheric perturbations is not clear.

In a region of rapid ion‐neutral collisions, it is expected that any GW patterns would show up in both the neutral and ion species. As these collisions reduce at higher altitudes with lower densities, this is expected to change, thus examining the correspondence between the perturbations seen in both ions and neutrals can provide information on the dynamic coupling of the neutral atmosphere and ionosphere, as well as the impact that external SW conditions may have.

Most previous studies (e.g., Collinson et al., 2019; Leelavathi et al., 2023) have focused on the apparent close coupling of ion and neutral density profiles (Hereafter we call as them ion‐neutral coupling cases). Meanwhile, very limited studies examined the cases in which ion perturbations evolve independently of neutral perturbations (Hereafter we call them as ion‐specific cases).

According to the recent observations of the nightside ionosphere on Mars by NASA's Mars Atmosphere and Volatile EvolutioN (MAVEN), ion densities on the nightside of the Martian ionosphere are 1–2 orders of magnitude smaller than on the dayside at altitude 180 km (Girazian et al., 2017a). For instance, the density of main component, O2 +, is in the order of ∼104 cm−3 on the dayside, and ∼102 cm−3 on the nightside at an altitude of 180 km. This is because the generation process of the ionospheric species is different between dayside and nightside. On the dayside, the ions are produced by the photoionization of the neutrals in the thermosphere; whereas the nightside ionosphere is mainly produced by precipitating particles from the solar wind and advection from the dayside ionosphere.

As a result, the collision frequencies between ions and neutrals are significantly different between dayside and nightside (for instance, ∼0.20 Hz on the dayside and ∼0.01 Hz on the nightside at an altitude of 180 km for O2 +). Assuming the typical gyrofrequency at an altitude of 180 km to be ∼0.1 Hz, the behavior of the ions on the dayside is dominated by collisions with neutrals, while on the nightside the magnetic field drives ion behavior. In other words, neutrals and ions tend to behave similarly on the dayside, only the ions are affected by the magnetic field and behave independently of the neutrals on the nightside. The boundary between these two regimes may be expected to vary with ionospheric density and magnetic field strength, so differences should be seen in the altitude direction as well as day and night. Naturally, the boundary also varies with solar wind conditions. Therefore, to investigate the excitation mechanism of ionospheric perturbations, we categorize ionospheric perturbations into three cases: (a) the ion‐neutral coupling cases where ion and neutral perturbations are well coupled, (b) the ion‐specific cases where ion perturbations move independently from neutrals, and (c) the coronal mass ejection (CME) cases associated with solar wind extreme events.

The purpose of this study is to identify the ionospheric perturbations associated with GW on Mars using neutral and ion density profiles obtained by the Neutral Gas and Ion Mass Spectrometer (NGIMS) onboard MAVEN. First, we explain observations and our method in Section 2. A representative number of cases from total profiles are compared with numerical model to determine the fraction that can be explained by a GW in Section 3. Next, we further investigate how the GW fraction varies along the solar zenith angle (SZA) and local‐solar time (LST) in Section 4. We provide statistics of relative density perturbations in neutrals and ions for all data set in Section 5. Finally, we draw discussions in Section 6 and conclusions in Section 7.

Observation and Methodology

MAVEN's Neutral Gas and Ion Mass Spectrometer (NGIMS) instrument is a quadrupole mass spectrometer that is capable of resolving ion and neutral density and composition in the mass range from 2 to 150 atomic mass units (Mahaffy et al., 2015). In the first couple of years of the mission, the instrument measured ions every other orbit (Benna et al., 2015). In subsequent years, ions were measured every orbit. We used Level 2 version 8 revision 1 of the NGIMS data set provided in the Planetary Data System (PDS). We apply over 5‐year data set of in‐situ density profiles acquired by NGIMS onboard MAVEN spacecraft, collected from 2014 to 2019, at altitudes between 170 and 190 km for lower altitudes and 190–210 km for higher altitudes as the periapsis of the MAVEN spacecraft. Observations were performed through a wide range of longitude, latitude, LST, and SZA, with a total number of 8,908 profiles. Here we refine our analysis data for CO2, N2, and CO2 + all observed in the altitude range 170–210 km. The data on which this article is based are available in Nakagawa (2024) and England (2024).

In the cases studied, the average altitude at which the gyro frequency for CO2 + equals the CO2 + to neutral collision frequency is close to 190 km. We have about 50% of cases with the altitude of gyro frequency = collision frequency being above 190 km, about half where it is below. The altitude range 170–210 km is set to ensure sufficient the signal‐to‐noise ratio. As the behavior at high and low altitude was different in some cases (as seen in Figure 4), we decided to divide the altitude range that could be analyzed into two.

The data set we apply in this study is restricted to the inbound segments for all species for more precise measurement accuracy. It is noted that the background level of reactive species such as CO2 increases in the outbound segments due to an interaction with the instrument's wall. Therefore, the data set we applied in this study is restricted in the inbound segments for all species for more precise measurement accuracy (Mahaffy et al., 2015). The data set of ion species are filtered according to the spacecraft potential, such that data taken when this is more negative than −3 V are excluded. Consequently, the total number of profiles applied in this study is 4579.

To identify the atmospheric gravity wave signatures, we utilized methods similar to those used by England et al. (2017) and Nakagawa et al. (2020), extracting the fractional density perturbations derived from observations. This involves estimating the background density from NGIMS data. A fourth polynomial fit to the logarithm of density observations in one satellite pass serves as a good estimate of the background density along the pass.

Between 170 and 210 km, ion density is generally very small, comparing with the neutrals (Benna et al., 2015; Mahaffy et al., 2015). Typically, ion number density varies from <101 to >103 cm−3 between day and night sides. The amplitudes of density perturbation are also highly variable between day and night sides. The uncertainties of individual ion density measurements follow a Poission counting statistics as function of ion density and is provided as part of the level 2 version 8 NGIMS data used here. A comparison of the uncertainty to the magnitude of wave perturbations is shown as the cumulative histogram in Figure 1. Typical uncertainty is well below 1% in most cases (>80% of the total) in Figures 1c and 1d. On the other hand, the amplitudes of CO2 + perturbations are mostly distributed above 1% up to 100% in Figures 1a and 1b. Furthermore, considering the number of measurements that go into characterizing a typical wave – that is, if it's wavelength is seen along track as 20–2,000 km (spacecraft motion, not altitude), then we get N measurements so the uncertainty in the wave is /√N. For typical observations considered here, this puts the observed wave amplitudes are well above their uncertainties.

We define three categories of observed profiles. The first cases are defined as the ion‐neutral perturbations coupling cases, implying the ion perturbations following the neutral perturbations, which is a sign of ion‐neutral coupling, which is defined by the correlation coefficient between ion‐neutral (CO2 and CO2 +) perturbations is more than 0.7 (839 cases in total). The second cases are defined as the ion‐specific cases, implying the ion perturbations moving independent of the neutral perturbations, which is defined by the correlation coefficient between ion‐neutral perturbations is less than 0.2 (823 cases in total). The third cases are defined by the CME cases, which are acquired during the passage of the CME through Mars. 31 profiles acquired during the CME events are listed based on Lee et al. (2017). Due to the limitation of the data set, all profiles of CME cases are acquired on the dayside.

Figures 2, 3, 4 show the examples of observed profiles of three cases. For comparison, profiles of other species such as Ar, O2 + are also shown in Figures 1, 2, 3. As reference, we show the gyrofrequency calculated by the observed magnetic field by the Magnetometer (MAG) onboard MAVEN (Connerney et al., 2015). In the ion‐neutral coupling case (Figure 2), the ion features follow the neutrals–a sign of ion‐neutral coupling. In the ion‐specific case (Figure 3), the ions move independent of the neutrals, and all ion species move together. On the other hand, ion perturbations show similar behavior to neutrals in Figure 2. In the CME case (Figure 4), ion‐specific perturbations are seen at higher altitudes above 210 km. In at least these cases, different behavior between ion‐neutral coupling and ion‐specific feature depends on the altitude range. This transition could be consistent with the increasing gyro/collision frequency ratio. While the number density, especially of ions, differs significantly between the day and night sides, the magnetic field strength is about twice as strong on the day side than on the night side (see Figure 5 in Crider et al., 2004). The balance between these determines whether the gyro frequency or the collision frequency is dominant.

The scatter plots of the relative density perturbation in each species are shown in Figures 2b, 2d and 3b, 3d, 4b, 4d. The dot lines represent the molecular mass ratio between two species. The relative perturbation in the neutrals is clearly associated with species scale height in all cases. Also, we can find the same effect associated with species scale height in ion perturbations as well in Figure 2. This behavior is expected from a simple consideration of the ways in which internal waves can modify the in‐situ density of a particular species. As noted by Dudis and Reber (1976), the density perturbations seen in‐situ for each species result from two effects: the first is divergence of the flow (which impacts all species equally) and the second is vertical advection of fluid parcels (the impact of which varies with species dependent scale height). This is the case for ion perturbations seen in ion‐neutral coupling case in Figure 2, in addition to all neutral perturbations. Whereas in the short‐wavelength limit the second term will be dominant, which is common to all species. This is the case for ion perturbations seen in ion‐specific case in Figure 4. The CME case in Figure 4 is a mixture of both cases between lower and higher altitudes. Such illustrating examples in an orbit imply the long‐wavelength waves in ion perturbations are well observed in the ion‐neutral coupling case but not in the ion‐specific case.

In order to determine how well the GW can describe the observed fluctuations in both the neutrals and ions, we make use of a two‐fluid model similar to that used by England et al. (2017). We evaluate the contribution of the GW on the observed perturbations depending on the goodness of the fit. Using a two‐fluid approximation, CO2 dominant, N2 (or CO2 +) momentum equations respond to collisions with CO2. We solve for the number density of CO2 and N2 (or CO2 +), and get a complex amplitude ratio that tells us the real amplitude and phase difference.

To run this model, we confine the number of observed profiles for the selection to reduce computational cost from each case. First, we select 292 profiles (170–190 km) and 290 profiles (190–210 km) from ion‐neutral coupling cases. These represent a large sample on which to test, and were selected based on those for which the background ion and neutral scale heights could be determined with confidence. Next, we select 554 profiles (170–190 km) and 551 profiles (190–210 km) from ion‐specific cases. Finally, all 31 profiles acquired during the CME events based on the list from Lee et al. (2017) are selected.

For each set of observations, we search through range of possible the GW frequencies, wavelengths and see if they can fit the observed amplitudes and phases of density perturbations. We look for GW with horizontal and vertical wavelengths in the range from 20 to 2,000 km – spanning from the smallest perturbations NGIMS can see (20 km equates to around 4 observations) through to the largest, given the distances covered during a periapsis pass. The detail descriptions of two‐fluid model can be found in England et al. (2017), with the modification here to account for using the method on an orbit‐by‐orbit basis, we do not utilize the decay rate in the vertical direction to constrain the goodness of fit, as the spacecraft does not typically see the same wave packet at a sufficient range of altitudes to determine this. We use the same the resonant collision frequency between CO2 and CO2 + based on Shunk and Nagy (1980). Here we apply this fit orbit by orbit to both the neutrals and ions. We focus on CO2 + instead of the main component O2 +, to investigate direct ion‐neutral coupling with CO2. For each case, we use the estimate of the goodness of fit to determine if an atmospheric GW can reasonably account for the fluctuations seen in both the neutrals and the ions present.

The Fraction of the Well‐Fit by GW in the Ion Perturbations

Our result shows that the neutral perturbations of the dayside at 170–190 km show an excellent agreement with the GW (99%–100%). The results agree with our intuition. On the other hand, on the nightside at 190–210 km altitudes, the GW fraction in the neutral perturbations decreased down to 66% in the ion‐specific cases. Contrary to previous thoughts, neutral perturbations on the nightside at high altitudes are not necessarily explained by GW.

The fraction of GW in the ion perturbations is high on the dayside at lower altitudes with a maximum of 95%. The fraction of GW in the ion perturbations gets extremely low value on the nightside at higher altitudes down to 23%, reaching the limit of strong ion‐neutral coupling at around 190 km. Thus, ion‐specific perturbations, those seen on the nightside, are difficult to explain in GW. This may imply that non‐GW excitation source, possibly SW, is the main driver to disturb the ionospheric species on the nightside.

During CME cases, the similar fraction of GW is obtained in the neutral perturbations as in the ion‐neutral coupling cases. Therefore, CME does not disturb the neutrals. The major difference in the CME cases is the decrease in ion perturbations; less than half of the ion perturbations observed during CME can be explained by GW. This suggests that CME has a strong impact on the ionospheric perturbations even on the dayside.

SZA and LST Dependences of the GW Fractions

We can further investigate the different behavior of density perturbations between dayside and nightside in Figure 10. Figure 10 shows the GW fractions that can explain the perturbations as a function of SZA at 170–190 km and 190–210 km altitudes for three categories.

GW fractions (percentage of GW successfully explained the fit) of neutrals (solid pink) and ions (solid light green), as a function of solar zenith angle. GW fractions in the ion‐neutral coupling cases are shown in the upper panels (a, b); those in the ion‐specific cases are shown in the bottom panels (c, d). GW fractions in the coronal mass ejection cases are also shown in the bottom panels as dotted lines.

Neutral perturbations are mostly explained by GW at SZA<100°, as seen in Figure 8. This is consistent with our intuition. Whereas, the GW fractions of neutral perturbations in the ion‐specific cases decrease across the terminator (SZA>100°) at 190–210 km in Figure 10d. This is in contradiction to previous thoughts and differs from the dayside. Additionally, CME cases suggest a significant decrease in GW fraction of neutral perturbations across the terminator in Figures 8c and 8d. This implies CME may potentially have an impact on the neutral perturbations around the terminator.

Regarding ion perturbations, the SZA dependence is more evident than that of neutrals. The GW fractions drastically decrease along a wide SZA range from 40° to 140° in the ion‐specific cases in Figures 10c and 10d. Interestingly, the GW fraction of ions clearly decreases along SZA from 40° to 90° at 190–210 km, down to ∼70% even in the ion‐neutral coupling cases in Figure 10b. Whereas, the coupling cases observed at 170–190 km show that a quite high percentage can be explained by GW in Figure 10a, as well as neutral.

The magnitude of decrease is most significant for the CME cases in ions. It is noteworthy that the significant decrease of GW fractions can be found even on the dayside at SZA<80° in ions, in contrast to neutrals in Figure 10d. The GW fractions drop below 50% on the nightside at the terminator SZA>90°. In the deep nightside at SZA>120°, the GW fractions are only below 20% at 190–210 km altitude.

The behavior of the ion perturbations explicitly depends on the dayside and nightside. Its dominant driver differs clearly between dayside and nightside. These results agree with our findings in Section 3.

Discussions

We conclude that the difference in the behavior of ion perturbations between day and night is even more pronounced. While most of the perturbations on the dayside at lower altitudes could be explained by GW, most of the perturbations on the nightside at higher altitudes are unlikely caused by GW, independent of the neutral perturbations. However, we note that this study cannot distinguish the difference of wave sources or generation mechanisms. The generation mechanism of the ionosphere is primarily photoionization with a generally uniform spatial structure on the day side (compared to the spatial scales of the NGIMS periapsis data used here), and precipitating electron impact ionization with spatial structure in the night side (Girazian et al., 2017b). Thus, the weak correlation with the neutrals in the night side is not surprising to see complex behavior on the nightside ionosphere.

Relative amplitudes between ion and neutral perturbations suggest pronounced ion‐specific features on the dust side in the southern hemisphere Although we have to note that the dawn data set see more northern hemisphere; meanwhile, the dusk data set see more southern hemisphere in this study, as seen in Figure 5, we may interpret the dawn‐dusk or the northern‐southern asymmetry as precipitating electrons in the tail lobe along the magnetic field. It may correspond an open magnetic topology as seen in Brain et al. (2006) in which aurora is observed most frequently in the dusk side in the southern hemisphere (Schneider et al., 2021). On other hand, the role of the crustal magnetic field on the ionospheric perturbations is not obvious in Figures 5a and 5d. The number of events with high amplitudes might not be dominated. To conclude our hypothesis, we need further data set during extreme SW events not only on the dayside but also on the nightside. In this paper, we started with extreme events in order to extract the most significant effects. A comprehensive investigation of the dependence on solar wind parameters, including dynamic pressure, is considered for future work. A more direct comparison with the energy flux of precipitating particles to the observed altitude may also be valuable.

Ionospheric perturbations on Mars can be interpreted by the similar analogy on Earth. Vadas et al. (2019) extracted the TADs on Earth from in‐situ observations and searched parameter space to determine if the observations of the TAD were consistent with the GW dispersion relation (in a manner that is similar, but not identical to the 2‐fluid approach used here). They found that some but not all of the waves they see can be explained as GWs. They concluded that the main source of them was in the mesosphere or thermosphere.

On the other hand, unlike on Earth where the influence of the lower atmosphere is uniformly dominant, the gyrofrequency is more dominant rather than collision frequency with neutrals on Mars at nightside and higher altitudes, indicating that ionospheric perturbations are strongly influenced by non‐GW sources (there may be a link to how the SW comes into play on the ionosphere on Mars). It is, however, noted that this study does not constrain its source. The perturbations that did not satisfy the GW parameters may have instead been from other sources including noise, acoustic waves, and local GW dissipation and momentum deposition (e.g., Vadas et al., 2014).

Open question raised by this study is what causes the neutral perturbations on the nightside that cannot be explained by GW? Since Mars does not have a strong intrinsic magnetic field, ion drag as seen on Earth is not expected. We do not have a complete explanation for this unexpected behavior of neutrals, but one possibility might be the direct disturbances by external forcing, such as SW, on the neutrals. Oyama et al. (2000) reported the field‐aligned ion‐neutral interaction in the terrestrial case. On Earth, ionospheric disturbances are associated with the electric field and electron precipitation in the auroral region, which enhance the thermal energy in the atmosphere through Joule/frictional heating and the heating due to the direct impact of incoming electrons with neutral particles. The ions affect the neutrals through frictional heating and momentum exchange. The ions are, in turn, affected by the neutral motions through collisions. The microscopic ion‐neutral coupling on the unmagnetized Mars, observed in this study, is not obvious.

We note that in this study, we are not able to strictly distinguish between temporal and spatial variability because of the in‐situ single point observations. Comprehensive study combined with global monitoring by aurora emission that is important signature of the input energy injected into the atmosphere. Study of ionospheric perturbations is for better understanding of the complex response of the magnetosphere‐ionosphere‐thermosphere coupling to space weather on a non‐magnetized planet.

Conclusions

Using neutral and ion species measurements by NGIMS onboard MAVEN spacecraft, we investigate density perturbations in the observed CO2, N2, and CO2 + profiles to identify the contribution of GW on the thermospheric and ionospheric perturbations. To investigate the excitation mechanism of ionospheric perturbations, we categorize ionospheric perturbations into three cases: (a) the ion‐neutral coupling cases where ion and neutral perturbations are well coupled, (b) the ion‐specific cases where ion perturbations move independently from neutrals, and (c) the CME cases associated with solar wind extreme events. A representative number of cases from total profiles are compared with numerical model to determine the fraction that can be explained by a GW. The main findings of our study are as follows.

Our results show that the neutral perturbations on the dayside at low altitudes are in excellent agreement with GW (99%–100% of cases). On the other hand, on the nightside at high altitudes, the fraction of GW in the neutral perturbations decreased down to 66%. Contrary to previous thoughts, neutral perturbations on the nightside at high altitudes are not necessarily explained by GW. We find a clear difference of the GW contribution in the neutral perturbations between dayside and nightside.

The fraction of GW in the ion perturbations is also excellent on the dayside at low altitudes with a maximum of 95%. The fraction of GW in the ion perturbations gets extremely low value on the nightside at high altitudes down to 23%, reaching the limit of strong ion‐neutral coupling at around 190 km. Thus, ion‐specific perturbations, those seen on the nightside, are difficult to explain in GW. We conclude that the SW can be the main driver to disturb the ionospheric species.

The similar value of fraction of GW on the dayside CME cases is obtained in the neutral perturbations as in the non‐CME daytime cases. This result indicates that CME does not disturb the neutrals. Whereas, almost only half of the ion perturbations observed during CME can be explained by GW. CME has an impact on the ionospheric perturbations even on the dayside.

Further investigations of GW fractions found a clear depletion of GW fractions across the terminator in the ion perturbations. The GW fractions of the ions significantly decrease down to 20% around SZA 120°. The behavior of the ion perturbations explicitly depends on the dayside and nightside. Its dominant driver differs clearly between dayside and nightside. We also found that CME may potentially have an impact on the neutral perturbations around the terminator.

Statistics of relative density perturbations demonstrate that a clear effect associated with species scale height in neutral perturbations at all SZA cases; whereas, the ion perturbations move independently of neutral perturbations and demonstrate how the correlation between ions and neutrals breaks down at high SZA especially on the dusk side in the southern hemisphere. Ion perturbations no longer correlate well with neutrals on the nightside.

We conclude that thermospheric and ionospheric perturbations on Mars can be interpreted by the similar analogy on Earth. We see, however, currently unexplained behavior that is strongly influenced by non‐GW sources both at night high‐altitudes, and especially near dusk in the southern hemisphere.
