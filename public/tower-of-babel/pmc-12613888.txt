Association of fructose-1,6-diphosphatase 1 with the progression of diabetic kidney disease
Authors: Ziqi Fu, Zitong Chen, Shuaibo Bian, Li Li, Suheng Geng, Jiaxin Dong, Simeng Liu, Lin Wu, Jingfeng Zhu, Honglei Guo, Ao Bian, Kang Liu, Jiafa Ren, Changying Xing, Zhimin Huang, Huijuan Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic Kidney Disease (DKD) is one of the most serious complications of diabetes mellitus, and a main cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). The identification of clinically applicable molecular biomarkers for monitoring DKD progression has become increasingly essential. In this study, we aim to investigate the relationship between the expression of fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1), a rate-limiting enzyme in gluconeogenesis, and DKD.MethodsA two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted using plasma protein quantitative trait loci data obtained from deCODE genetics and DKD genome-wide association study data acquired from the Finnish Biobank Alliance to assess the causal relationship between FBP1 and DKD. In parallel, a retrospective cohort study was performed involving 107 patients with biopsy-confirmed DKD, in which DKD progression was defined as a composite endpoint consisting of serum creatinine doubling or ESKD. For this endpoint, a predictive nomogram was subsequently developed.ResultsMR results suggested that a genetically predicted higher level of circulating FBP1 was associated with a low risk of DKD (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.33–0.98; P = 0.04) in diabetic patients. The expression level of renal FBP1 decreased with the progression of DKD pathological stage. Its expression level was positively correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (r = 0.717, P < 0.001) and expression of renal carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 A (a rate-limiting enzyme in fatty acid oxidation) (r = 0.745, P < 0.001). During a median follow-up of 26 months, 41 (38%) patients suffered from DKD progression. Multivariate Cox regression indicated that a reduced renal expression level of FBP1 was correlated with an increased risk of DKD progression (HR 0.325, 95% CI 0.107–0.986, P = 0.047). Finally, the integration of FBP1 with 24-h urine protein and eGFR into a nomogram significantly improved the prediction of 1-year and 3-year event-free survival in DKD (C-index: 0.871 vs. 0.794, P = 0.011), demonstrating its additive prognostic value.ConclusionsRenal FBP1 might be a potential biomarker reflecting the severity of renal function and kidney progression in biopsy-proven DKD stages II to IV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01038-4.Keywords: Diabetic kidney disease; Fructose-1,6-diphosphatase 1; Gluconeogenesis; Carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1A

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the most serious complications of diabetes-related microvascular diseases, and a leading cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [1]. The pathogenesis of DKD is complex, broadly involving glucose metabolism disorders, disrupted fatty acid metabolism, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, endothelial dysfunction, glomerular sclerosis, inflammation and fibrosis [2]. Generally, glucose and lipid metabolism disorders have a close link with DKD and its worsening [3]. In the progression of DKD, renal tubular epithelial cells undergo a series of pathological changes, such as cellular hyperplasia and fibrosis, which are closely linked to the advancement of the disease. Consequently, renal tubular epithelial cells in DKD are not merely passive victims but rather active participants that play a significant role in the onset and development of the disease [4, 5]. Moreover, the glucose and lipid metabolism of renal tubular epithelial cells is closely related to the progression of renal diseases [6].

The kidney and liver are the major organs producing glucose from non-hexose precursors through gluconeogenesis, which consists of a series of enzymatic reactions opposite to glycolysis [7]. Studies have shown that the glucose produced in the kidney accounts for 40% of the total during fasting [8], and gluconeogenesis mainly occurs in proximal renal tubular epithelial cells that express rate-limiting enzymes, like the fructose-1,6-diphosphatase (FBP1), phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1), and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC), with lactic acid as one of the main substrates [9]. While current research on key gluconeogenic enzymes predominantly focuses on their roles in oncology, these metabolic regulators are increasingly recognized for their involvement in cellular signaling, survival, proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and phenotypic modulation across various pathological conditions. Notably, enzymes such as PCK1 and G6PC demonstrate context-dependent, dual functionality in tumorigenesis, exhibiting both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive effects depending on tissue specificity. In contrast, FBP1 consistently manifests tumor-suppressive properties [10]. Given these differential profiles and considering the imperative for clinical translatability while minimizing potential oncogenic risks in other organ systems, our investigation specifically focuses on elucidating the role of FBP1 in DKD. In malignant tumors, FBP1 is usually down-regulated and linked with metabolic features of high-level glycolysis even in an aerobic environment [11, 12]. Overexpression of FBP1 is found to inhibit the growth, metastasis and glycolysis of breast cancer [13]. Beyond its canonical role in glucose metabolism, emerging evidence indicates that FBP1 participates in fatty acid metabolic reprogramming in hepatocellular carcinoma [13]. Particularly relevant to renal pathophysiology, improving fatty acid oxidation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for ameliorating DKD [14]. However, the precise alterations of FBP1 in DKD and its role in disease progression remain poorly understood.

To systematically investigate the FBP1 and DKD relationship, we employed a two-phase approach. First, we assessed the causal effect of FBP1 on DKD through two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis of large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) datasets. Subsequently, we evaluated its potential as a DKD progression biomarker by characterizing dynamic renal FBP1 expression patterns across different pathological stages (II-IV) and examining their correlation with carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 A (CPT1A), the rate-limiting enzyme in mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, in a well-characterized cohort of 107 biopsy-confirmed DKD patients.

Methods

MR study

Our MR study used pQTL data from deCODE Genetics, which included 35,559 individuals of European descent and provided FBP1 circulating plasma protein [15]. The GWAS data for DKD were sourced from the newly available Finnish Biobank Alliance (FinnGen) version 11 summary statistics, encompassing 5,042 cases and 51,410 controls [16]. The FinnGen genetic associations were adjusted for several factors, including gender, age, array-specific ancestry principal components, FinnGen chip version, and legacy genotyping batch, ensuring robust and accurate findings. The details of all QTL and GWAS datasets for this study are presented in Supplementary Methods. For carrying out MR, pQTL for FBP1 were selected based on the following criteria: cis-acting SNPs (single-nucleotide polymorphisms) within ± 1 megabase (Mb), genome-wide significance at P < 5 × 10−8, and linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2 ≤ 0.1 and distance ≤ 10,000 kb, reference to the 1000G European population). The explanatory power and robustness of genetic instruments were evaluated using the R2 statistic and F-statistic, respectively. Genetic instrumental variables were screened using three online databases (Ensembl, PhenoScanner, and GWAS Catalog) to identify and remove SNPs associated with potential confounders of DKD. Palindromic SNPs were excluded during harmonization to avoid ambiguity. Following this screening process, only one genetic instrument remained available for FBP1, and thus the Wald ratio method was employed as the primary analytical approach. Given the single-instrument design, assessments of heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q test) and horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger regression) were precluded, as they require a minimum of two and three variants, respectively. To assess the robustness of the primary causal estimate, a sensitivity analysis was performed by incorporating an additional SNP with potential pleiotropic effects.

Colocalization analysis

We performed colocalization analysis to assess whether FBP1 pQTL and DKD GWAS signals share common causal genetic variants. For the lead SNP, we analyzed all variants within ± 500 kb window, calculating posterior probabilities for five competing hypotheses: (H0) there are no causal variations in either trait; (H1) causal variations only affect gene expression; (H2) causal variations only affect the risk of disease; (H3) there are different causal variations in each trait; (H4) a single variation affects both traits. Using the default values (p1 = 1 × 10−4, p2 = 1 × 10−4, and p12 = 1 × 10−5), a posterior probability of H4 exceeding 0.70 indicated a common genetic effect between the traits.

Participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (No: 2024-SR-044), ensuring compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent.

From February 2018 to February 2023, 107 adult DKD patients with a clear pathological diagnosis by renal biopsy in the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University were included. Excluded were those younger than 18 years of age, lacking complete clinical or follow-up data, with a less precise renal biopsy (< 5 glomeruli), or complicated with severe systemic diseases (such as cirrhosis, heart failure, respiratory failure, malignancies, acute inflammation or infections). During the same period, paracancerous normal kidney tissues from 18 renal cell carcinoma patients were collected as controls.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, laboratory testing and follow-up data were collected from the electronic medical records, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI calculated by dividing the weight in kilograms by the height in meters squared), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), hemoglobin (Hb), serum creatinine (SCr), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), serum uric acid (UA), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), albumin, total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), 24-h urine protein and medication (Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI)/Angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor). All values were measured by standard methods in routine clinical laboratories, and eGFR was calculated using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration equation [17].

Renal pathology

Renal biopsy and pathology were performed to provide a diagnostic assessment of DKD. DKD was classified as follows [18]: Stage I, isolated glomerular basement membrane thickening with mild or nonspecific changes under an electronic microscope; Stage Ⅱ, mesangial dilation; Stage Ⅲ, one or more nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules) formed in the mesangial matrix; Stage Ⅳ, global glomerular sclerosis in more than 50% of glomeruli. Interstitial fibrosis and renal tubular atrophy (IFTA), interstitial inflammation, hyalinosis of blood vessels, and angiosclerosis were scored according to Tervaet’s classification [18].

Two pathologists blinded to grouping were independently responsible for renal pathology, and any disagreement was solved by a reassessment.

Definition of DKD progression

DKD progression was defined as a doubling of SCr or progression to ESKD (a condition of kidney failure that requires dialysis or kidney transplant). For patients who did not reach the endpoint of doubling of SCr or progression to ESKD, the end date was defined as the time of the most recent hospital admission or visit. Each patient was followed up every 3–6 months until April 2024. Event-free survival (EFS) refers to the time from participant enrollment to the occurrence of DKD progression or the last follow-up.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded renal tissue sections were sliced into 3 μm thickness, dewaxed, immersed in citrate buffer (pH = 6.0) for 20 min, and washed with phosphate buffer. After a 1-h incubation in the immunohistochemical blocking solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) at room temperature, renal tissues were incubated with the anti-FBP1 antibody (Abcam, UK) and anti-CPT1A antibody (Abcam, UK) at 4℃ overnight, and secondary antibodies at 37℃ for 1 h. Then, the sections were stained with DAB (MXB Biotechnology Co., LTD., China) for 1 min, and the nuclei were re-stained with hematoxylin. Positive staining was observed in 8–10 randomly selected fields per specimen using a microscope (Leica DM3000), and quantified by Image-Pro Plus.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing

The analysis in Figure S3 utilized processed single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from Wilson PC et al. [19], accessed via the Kidney Interactive Transcriptomics (KIT) database (http://humphreyslab.com/SingleCell/), a curated platform for exploring kidney cell-type-specific gene expression. Our secondary analyses focused on examining the expression patterns of gluconeogenesis-related genes across different cell types and their alterations between control and DKD groups.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 25.0, SPSS Inc.) and R software (version 4.4.1, statistical software) were used for statistical analysis. MR and colocalization analyses were performed using the “TwoSampleMR” (version 0.5.10) [20] and “coloc” (version 5.2.3) [21] R packages. Measurement data in a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD); otherwise, they were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as a number (percentage). Spearman correlation was performed to analyze the correlation of FBP1 with clinical indicators of DKD. Differences between groups were analyzed by the independent sample t-test, and those among three or more groups by the univariate analysis of variance, Kruskal-Wallis test, or Chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors for the prognosis of DKD. Factors with P < 0.05 in univariate Cox regression analysis were included in multivariate Cox regression analysis. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted to reflect the diagnostic and prognostic potential of FBP1 in DKD. Two predictive models were developed: a baseline model incorporating 24-h urine protein and eGFR, and an expanded model in which renal FBP1 levels were additionally included. The performance of the FBP1-integrated model in predicting 1-year and 3-year EFS of DKD was confirmed through likelihood ratio testing. Based on these comparative analysis results, a nomogram was subsequently constructed to facilitate clinical application. In all tests, a two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MR study reveals an inverse association between genetically predicted FBP1 levels and DKD risk

Three SNPs significantly associated with plasma FBP1 (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) and independent (r² ≤ 0.1) were initially selected as genetic instruments (Table S1). After screening for potential confounders, one SNP (rs2460137) was excluded due to its association with eGFR. During harmonization with the outcome dataset, an additional palindromic SNP was removed. The final analysis therefore utilized one SNP (rs4743962; F-statistic = 96) analyzed via the Wald ratio method, showing that genetically predicted higher FBP1 levels were associated with reduced DKD risk (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.33–0.98; P = 0.04) (Table S1). Sensitivity analyses evaluating individual SNP effect estimates yielded consistent directions of association (Figure S1). Notably, Bayesian colocalization analysis revealed a low posterior probability for a shared causal variant between plasma FBP1 and DKD (Figure S2; Table S1). The lead SNP identified by the locuscomparer R package (rs7035381) was defined as the variant with the smallest combined p-value from both the FBP1 pQTL and DKD GWAS studies for visualization purposes, although it was not the most significant SNP in either trait individually. As illustrated in Figure S2B, the actual lead SNPs for FBP1 (rs2460137) and DKD (rs184625543) are distinct and reside within separate linkage disequilibrium blocks, further supporting the absence of colocalization. The weak colocalization signal may reflect the presence of distinct causal variants influencing FBP1 levels and DKD risk, which could contribute to a higher false-negative rate in colocalization analysis.

Despite comprehensive searches in both the GTEx and NephQTL2 databases using a liberal significance threshold (P = 1 × 10⁻⁴), we failed to identify any SNPs significantly associated with renal FBP1 expression (Supplementary Material: Tissue-specific SNPs). This precludes definitive attribution of plasma FBP1 to renal sources, suggesting potential extrarenal origins. Given that renal tubular epithelial cells are known producers of FBP1, we specifically investigated whether renal FBP1 expression alterations correlate with DKD progression. Accordingly, subsequent experiments focused on characterizing the relationship between renal tissue FBP1 expression and DKD progression.

Comparative analysis of renal FBP1 expression between DKD patients and controls

A total of 107 eligible DKD patients were enrolled, including 76 (71%) males and 31 (29%) females, with a mean age of 52.26 ± 10.99 years (Fig. 1). Three participants suffered from type 1 diabetes (3%), while 104 participants had type 2 diabetes (97%). Their baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. In comparison to the controls from paracancerous normal kidney tissues, DKD patients showed significant differences in age, Hb, FBG, albumin, TG, serum UA, BUN, SCr, and eGFR (P < 0.05). No significant differences were detected in gender, BMI, HDL-C, TC, and LDL-C between the two groups (P > 0.05).

There were 37 DKD patients in stage Ⅱ, 43 in stage Ⅲ, and 27 in stage Ⅳ (Table 1). Significant differences were detected in Hb, albumin, HDL-C, TG, serum UA, BUN, SCr, eGFR, and 24-h urine protein among DKD patients at varied stages (P < 0.05). Pathological features, including renal tubulointerstitial injury, interstitial inflammation, and vasculopathy, as well as renal FBP1 expression, were also significantly different in varied stages (P < 0.05, Table S2). Consistently, single-cell transcriptomic sequencing [19] showed that PCK1, FBP1, and G6PC were significantly up-regulated in the proximal tubules of early-stage DKD patients (Figure S3). Stratified by renal tubulointerstitial injury, interstitial inflammation, vascular hyaline degeneration, and vascular sclerosis, it is found that the expression level of FBP1 significantly decreased with the increased pathological scores (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A-D).

Renal FBP1 expression positively correlates with eGFR in DKD patients

Expression level of FBP1 in DKD kidney tissues was positively correlated with Hb (r = 0.374, P < 0.001) and eGFR (r = 0.717, P < 0.001), and negatively correlated with BUN (r=−0.565, P < 0.001), SCr (r=−0.712, P < 0.001), and 24-h urine protein (r=−0.197, P = 0.042) (Table S3). In addition, the expression level of FBP1 was positively correlated with eGFR in DKD patients in all the pathological stages, most pronounced in stage Ⅳ (r = 0.816, P < 0.001), followed by stage Ⅲ (r = 0.790, P < 0.001) and stage Ⅱ (r = 0.375, P = 0.022) (Fig. 2E-H).

Positive correlation between renal FBP1 and CPT1A expression in DKD patients

Immunohistochemical results showed that FBP1 was highly expressed in the cytoplasm of renal tubular epithelial cells. A significantly higher expression level of FBP1 was detected in stage Ⅱ DKD kidney tissues, compared with paracancerous normal kidney tissues (Fig. 3A). Besides, the expression level of FBP1 in kidney tissues significantly decreased with the worsening of the pathological grade of DKD (Fig. 3B). Immunohistochemical staining showed that CPT1A was mainly expressed in the cytoplasm of renal tubular epithelial cells, and its expression level was significantly lower in DKD kidney tissues than in paracancerous normal kidney tissues (Fig. 3A). Semi-quantitative results showed that the expression level of CPT1A significantly decreased with the pathological worsening of DKD (Fig. 3C), and positively correlated with the expression level of FBP1 (r = 0.745, P < 0.001, Fig. 3D) in DKD patients.

Renal FBP1 serves as a kidney progression biomarker in biopsy-proven DKD (Stages II-IV)

During a median follow-up period of 26 months, 41 DKD patients progressed to doubling of SCr or ESKD. Stratified according to the median of FBP1 expression (13.98%), DKD patients were divided into the FBP1 high expression group (n = 50) and the FBP1 low expression group (n = 57). We then created a Cox proportional hazard model to assess the endpoint events during DKD progression. First, we established a hypothesis that the effect of FBP1 on DKD progression was consistent with the risk ratio. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that the pathological stage of DKD (HR 2.611, 95% CI 1.722–3.961, P < 0.001) and baseline Hb (HR 0.962, 95% CI 0.947–0.978, P < 0.001), albumin (HR 0.938, 95% CI 0.900- 0.978, P = 0.003), eGFR (HR 0.649, 95% CI 0.557–0.756, P < 0.001), 24-h urine protein (HR 4.442, 95% CI 2.209–8.931, P < 0.001), use of ACEI/ARB (HR 0.442, 95% CI 0.200- 0.974, P = 0.043), use of SGLT2 inhibitor (HR 0.451, 95% CI 0.213–0.956, P = 0.038), and FBP1 classification (HR 0.165, 95% CI (0.069–0.394, P < 0.001) were significantly correlated with DKD progression. After adjusting for pathological stage, Hb, albumin, 24-h urine protein, eGFR, use of ACEI/ARB, and use of SGLT2 inhibitor, the multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that FBP1 was a prognostic factor for preventing DKD progression (HR 0.325, 95% CI 0.107–0.986, P = 0.047, Table 2). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that the likelihood of DKD progression in the high FBP1 expression group was reduced by 67%, in comparison to the low FBP1 expression group (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A). The baseline model incorporating eGFR and 24-h urine protein showed moderate discriminative ability (C-index 0.794, 95% CI 0.728–0.860). Notably, integrating FBP1 significantly improved predictive performance (C-index 0.871, 95% CI 0.822–0.920; likelihood ratio test P = 0.011). We subsequently developed a clinical nomogram combining FBP1 expression, eGFR, and 24-h urine protein to predict 1-year and 3-year EFS in DKD (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). The nomogram demonstrated that lower eGFR, higher proteinuria, and reduced FBP1 expression collectively predicted worse renal outcomes.

Discussion

The present study investigated the correlation of FBP1 with DKD progression. MR first identified that a higher level of plasma FBP1 was associated with a lower risk of DKD in diabetic patients. FBP1 is mainly produced by the kidney and liver, but it is difficult to identify whether plasma FBP1 originates from the liver or the kidney in humans. It may impair the efficacy of circulating FBP1 in the detection of DKD. In addition, there are few reports on the activity of FBP1 in urine because of the relatively unstable activity of FBP1 in urine [22]. Moreover, it is possible that, due to the methodological limitations for accurate detection of FBP1 expression in human urine, no relevant report about the expression of FBP1 in urine was obtained. Therefore, we used immunohistochemical methods to determine the expression of FBP1 in kidney tissues. In kidney tissue from DKD patients, the expression level of FBP1 was positively correlated with eGFR but negatively correlated with 24-h urine protein, tubulointerstitial injury, interstitial inflammation, vascular hyalinosis, and vascular sclerosis. The expression level of renal FBP1 decreased with the worsening of DKD. In addition, CPT1A was down-regulated in DKD patients, and its expression was further reduced with the worsening of DKD. A positive correlation was identified between expression levels of FBP1 and CPT1A in the kidneys of DKD patients.

Moreover, renal FBP1 may be a prognostic factor for DKD progression. While eGFR and albuminuria remain gold-standard prognostic indicators, these parameters predominantly reflect glomerular dysfunction. However, accumulating evidence underscores the critical role of tubular injury in DKD progression [23]. The remarkable renoprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, which specifically target glucose reabsorption in proximal tubules, demonstrate that tubular glucose metabolism is not merely about glycemic control but plays a fundamental role in kidney function preservation [24]. FBP1 was selected as a candidate biomarker due to its tubule-specific expression pattern (primarily in proximal tubular epithelial cells) and its functional linkage to renal gluconeogenesis. Mechanistically, FBP1 downregulation correlates with DKD advancement. Therefore, we propose FBP1 as a potential molecular biomarker for prognostic assessment in patients with DKD. After multivariable adjustment, higher renal FBP1 expression was associated with reduced DKD progression risk, with Kaplan-Meier analysis showing a 67% lower progression likelihood in high-FBP1 versus low-FBP1 patients. Renal FBP1 levels significantly improved risk stratification beyond conventional metrics (C-index increase: 0.794 → 0.871; P = 0.011), suggesting its potential to reflect distinct pathways (e.g., metabolic dysregulation) in tubular cells not fully captured by clinical parameters alone. This aligns with oncology findings where FBP1 loss predicts poorer outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma [25] and leukemia [26], underscoring its broader role as a metabolic regulator in disease progression.

The present study revealed distinct patterns of FBP1 expression in DKD. Our MR analysis demonstrated that decreased plasma FBP1 levels are associated with a higher risk of DKD in diabetic patients. However, subsequent tissue-level investigations presented methodological challenges. Since renal biopsies are not routinely performed in diabetic patients without nephropathy complications, we compared FBP1 expression in DKD renal tissues against cancer-adjacent normal tissues as controls. Notably, no significant differences in renal FBP1 expression were observed between these groups. This finding may reflect the dynamic regulation of FBP1 during DKD progression - elevated FBP1 expression in early-stage DKD followed by a subsequent decline in advanced stages. This temporal variation suggests that renal FBP1 expression cannot distinguish between DKD and normal kidney tissue.

A large retrospective cohort analysis of 243,222 patients with CKD (chronic kidney disease, CKD is defined by the presence of kidney damage or a decreased GFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for a period of three months or longer) reported a higher incidence of hypoglycemia in CKD patients than in those without CKD [27]. Decreased renal glucose production is considered a marker of CKD [28]. In addition, CKD is associated with increased serum lactate, which is one of the gluconeogenic substrates. A previous study consistently reported that FBP1 was down-regulated in damaged tubular epithelial cells of a 14-day mouse model of unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) [29]. Given the role of FBP1 in gluconeogenesis, we speculated a correlation of FBP1 with DKD. Single-nucleus transcriptomic sequencing showed that PCK1, FBP1, and G6PC were significantly up-regulated in proximal tubules of early-stage DKD patients, indicating the activation and importance of gluconeogenesis in proximal tubules [19, 30] (Figure S3). While CKD typically demonstrates decreased renal FBP1 expression [27, 31, 32], our study reveals a paradoxical upregulation of FBP1 in early-stage DKD, mirroring the 40–200% increase in hepatic gluconeogenesis seen in diabetes due to insulin resistance [33]. This diabetic-specific pattern contrasts sharply with the FBP1 downregulation observed in UUO mice [27] and early human CKD, suggesting distinct metabolic reprogramming in DKD. Emerging evidence indicates these elevated gluconeogenic enzymes, particularly PCK1 [34], may exert protective effects, potentially explaining the renal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors, which appear to modulate gluconeogenic pathways [35] while suppressing fibrosis. Our findings position FBP1 as a stage-specific regulator in DKD pathogenesis, though its precise mechanistic role-whether adaptive or maladaptive-requires further investigation, given the complex interplay between renal gluconeogenesis, systemic glucose metabolism, and diabetic renal injury.

The kidneys are high-energy-demanding organs relying on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) [36]. CPT1A, a rate-limiting enzyme in FAO, produces acylcarnitine using carnitine and acetyl-CoA [37]. The activity of CPT1A is significantly inhibited in DKD, leading to cellular disorders and further aggravating renal fibrosis [38]. Effective approaches to enhance CPT1A activity contribute to the prevention of renal fibrosis [39]. Our study indicated a positive correlation between CPT1A and FBP1 in biopsy-proven DKD stages II to IV. Considering that both of these indicators have a close relationship with renal function, whether there is a direct link between them requires further confirmation. The underlying mechanism necessitates further exploration.

Notably, our cohort demonstrated a higher renal event rate (>30% over 2–3 years) compared to the 16% composite outcome reported in the DAPA-CKD trial’s diabetic subgroup during 2.4-year follow-up [40–42]. This discrepancy likely reflects three key methodological and clinical distinctions: (1) our rigorous enrollment criteria requiring 100% biopsy-confirmed DKD versus only 13% histological confirmation in DAPA-CKD [43]; (2) more advanced baseline disease severity in our cohort (21% vs. 14% with eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2) [40]; and (3) the known accelerated ESKD progression in histologically-validated DKD compared to mixed-etiology CKD populations [44]. These differences underscore the value of our histologically characterized cohort in defining true DKD progression patterns.

While our study provides novel insights into FBP1 dynamics in DKD progression, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the absence of stage I DKD specimens prevents characterization of initial metabolic alterations. Second, the retrospective design and single-center cohort may limit generalizability, necessitating validation through prospective, multi-center studies with larger samples. Third, currently available GWAS data lack DKD staging information, restricting our ability to perform stage-stratified genetic analyses. Additionally, the lack of paired blood and urinary FBP1 measurements precludes assessment of systemic versus renal-specific FBP1 contributions. Finally, while our FBP1-based nomogram showed promising predictive accuracy, its clinical utility requires verification in independent, ethnically diverse cohorts.

Conclusion

These findings suggest a novel and crucial link between downregulation of renal FBP1 expression and DKD progression. Renal FBP1 is a potential biomarker reflecting the severity of renal function in DKD, and a complementary prognostic factor for kidney progression in biopsy-proven DKD stages II to IV. It may open new avenues for targeting this enzyme for the mechanism and treatment of DKD.
