Immune‐Related Protein and Non‐Coding RNA Cargo of Extracellular Vesicles Participate in the Chronic Inflammation Induced by HIV Infection
Authors: Humberto Doriguêtto Gravina, Ricardo Cardoso Castro, Ana Margarida Gonçalves, Julia Oliveira Lima, Fabrícia Heloísa Cavicchioli Sugiyama, Brenda Cavalin Moreira, Mateus da Silva Matias Antunes, Caroline Fontanari, Valdes Roberto Bollela, Yann Yves Lamarre, Fausto Almeida, Simone Kashima, Margarida Saraiva, Nuno Osório, Fabiani Gai Frantz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) promote intercellular communication, playing a key role in the secondary immune‐related pathologies driven by chronic inflammation in people living with HIV (PLWH). To identify molecular components within large EVs (lEVs) from PLWH's plasma that may influence immune function and contribute to the pathological process. PLWH were classified using clinical data, cellular immunophenotyping, and plasma mediator profiling. lEVs were characterized using transcriptomic, proteomic, and interactome analysis. Their functional impact on immune cells was also assessed. PLWH showed signs of chronic basal inflammation. Compared to the control group, lEVs from PLWH carried the miR‐4433b‐3p, 31 long non‐coding RNAs and 45 proteins differentially expressed. Key proteins—FBXO7, C3, SUGT1 and DTX3L—were linked to the miR‐4433b‐3p regulatory network, suggesting their involvement in inflammation. Interactome and pathway enrichment analysis associated these molecules to critical pathways, including NF‐kappa B signalling and PI3K‐AKT signalling. Finally, lEVs from PLWH more effectively modulated the production of inflammatory mediators in bystander immune cells. This study underscores the role of lEVs in shaping immune response during chronic HIV infection. By identifying specific molecular components, it provides valuable insights into potential therapeutic targets and candidate biomarkers for disease progression monitoring.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, the cause of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), remains a major global public health concern. Despite advances in combined antiretroviral therapy (cART), which effectively controls viral load, the provirus is not eliminated, and HIV persists in latent reservoirs. While cART reduces AIDS‐related morbidity and mortality, it does not protect patients from chronic inflammation related to persistent infection. This dysregulated immune response in people living with HIV (PLWH) may be attributed to various factors, including the maintenance of viral reservoirs in latently infected peripheral and tissue cells (Espíndola et al. 2016), leakage of microbial products due to a damaged intestinal barrier (Brenchley et al. 2006; Ancuta et al. 2008), coinfections, and defective T cell proliferation (Deeks 2011). Reported pathological manifestations include neurocognitive disorders, lipodystrophy, metabolic and epigenetic alterations, early immunosenescence, and immune cell exhaustion (O'Meara et al. 2019; Srinivasa et al. 2021; Babu et al. 2019; Vestad et al. 2021).

Under physiological or pathological conditions, cells release portions of their contents into the extracellular environment in heterogeneous and spherical structures surrounded by lipid membranes but lacking a nucleus. These vesicles may originate from endosomal pathways, as in the case of small‐extracellular vesicles (sEVs; exosomes), or they may bud directly from the plasma membrane, as in the case of large‐EVs (lEVs; microvesicles) (Mathieu et al. 2019). The release of EVs facilitates intercellular communication by transporting membranous, cytosolic, and nuclear components, such as lipids, proteins, enzymes, messenger RNAs, and small RNAs, including microRNAs and long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) (Garcia‐Martin et al. 2022; Dixson et al. 2023). This cargo may contain inflammatory modulators and epigenetic modifiers (Wisler et al. 2022), interfering with host immune response under homeostasis (Lajqi et al. 2022), during infections (Moro et al. 2016), and in the context of tumour progression (Wang et al. 2022). EVs derived from gut microbiota, stem cells, tumour cells, or immune cells may modulate cell migration and differentiation, phagocytosis, and cytokine secretion by triggering diverse signalling pathways in recipient cells, primarily phagocytic populations such as neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs). Overall, EVs may exert a dual inflammatory effect on immune responses, regulating the development of both innate and adaptive immunity (Wisler et al. 2022; Lajqi et al. 2022; Moro et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2022).

In the inflammatory context of HIV, trans‐infection can occur within immunological synapses through the incorporation of viral genomic RNA into sEVs (Columba Cabezas and Federico 2013). Even though proper antiretroviral use reduces the packaging of genomic and other HIV RNAs, selective incorporation of TAR RNA copies within sEVs has been observed, suggesting the long‐term contribution of viral non‐coding RNA to HIV‐1 pathogenesis (Demarino et al. 2018). The presence of HIV non‐coding RNAs and proteins has been associated with various pathologies, including the neurocognitive outcomes of long‐term disease (Patters and Kumar 2018). HIV‐infected macrophages also release in vitro lEVs containing glutaminase, an enzyme responsible for glutamate synthesis. Dysregulation of this neurotransmitter has been linked to the development of neurocognitive disorders (Wu et al. 2015). Myeloid cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and DCs, efficiently uptake EVs (Mathieu et al. 2019; Robbins and Morelli 2014), further contributing to their biological relevance in HIV pathogenesis. For example, the uptake of lEVs released by infected T lymphocytes modulates the infection and maturation of DCs, enhancing T cell activation (Mercier et al. 2013).

Several studies have highlighted the importance of EV‐mediated intercellular communication. However, the specific ways in which EV constituents influence inflammatory patterns during disease progression remain to be fully elucidated. Here, we hypothesized that pivotal molecular components within lEVs may reflect the immune status of PLWH, contributing to the secondary pathologies. To test this hypothesis, lEVs were isolated from the plasma of PLWH and analyzed for their protein and non‐coding RNA cargo to uncover candidates associated with persistent inflammation. A set of lncRNAs was identified that may serve as a biosignature, along with one differentially expressed non‐coding microRNA, hsa‐miR‐4433b‐3p. When analyzed in conjunction with a defined set of proteins carried by lEVs, this miRNA may function as a key regulator of inflammation in PLWH, suggesting potential modulatory effects.

This work provides novel insights into the role of lEVs in modulating immune responses in PLWH. Importantly, we demonstrated that lEVs from PLWH exert a stronger influence on the inflammatory profile of bystander immune cells, further supporting their relevance in immune dysregulation. Through interactome analysis, we propose that the presence of miR‐4433b‐3p and proteins such as FBXO7, C3, SUGT1 and DTX3L may be linked to immune dysregulation by modulating key pathways such as NF‐kappa B (NF‐κB) signalling and phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase/protein kinase B‐PKB (PI3K‐AKT) signalling. These pathways play critical roles in immune cell activation, inflammation, and senescence, making them potential therapeutic targets for mitigating chronic inflammation in PLWH. Overall, this study underscores the importance of EV‐mediated intercellular communication in chronic HIV infection and highlights candidate biomarkers for disease monitoring and novel therapeutic interventions.

Results

Clinical Characterization of Participants

Thirty‐two volunteers were clinically assessed and divided into two groups, as described in Table 1. The groups included donors of both genders, with a mean age of 37 ± 7 years for the control group (CTR) and 34 ± 8 years for the PLWH group under cART. Clinical data were obtained exclusively from PLWH medical records, as individuals in the CTR group were not routinely monitored. Information regarding infection status and treatment included laboratory parameters such as diagnosis, therapy, last detectable viral load (LDLV), highest detected viral load (>VL), the lowest recorded CD4 count (NADIR) and the last CD4, CD8 and CD45 cell counts (Table S1).

Inflammatory Signatures of PLWH

To characterize the immunological profile of the PLWH cohort, blood cellularity and plasma mediator levels were assessed at the time of sample collection, together with patient medical records.

First, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the CTR and PLWH groups were evaluated by flow cytometry to assess monocytes and T lymphocytes proportions (Figure 1A–L). Figure S1A shows a schematic representation of CD3+CD4+ (CD4+ T) and CD3+CD8+ (CD8+ T) lymphocyte selection, while Figure S1B details the gating strategy for classical monocytes (cMo — CD14+CD16−) and intermediate monocytes (iMo — CD14+CD16+). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding the counts of total leukocytes (considering a summation of both CD3+ plus CD14+ cells), CD3+ lymphocytes or CD14+ monocytes (Figure 1A,B,G), nor in the frequencies of CD3+ lymphocytes and CD14+ monocytes (Figure 1C,H). However, PLWH exhibited reduced CD4+ T cell (Figure 1D) and increased CD8+ T cell frequencies (Figure 1E), resulting in a decreased CD4+/CD8+ ratio (Figure 1F). Additionally, the frequency of cMo was lower (Figure 1I), while iMo was increased (Figure 1K) in PLWH. Differences in the CD163 expression, a marker of differentiated macrophages, were not significant in both CD14+ cell populations (Figure 1J,L). Next, plasma cytokine levels were analyzed in all donors. The basal inflammatory profile of the PLWH group revealed elevated levels of CXCL‐10 (a chemokine that recruits leukocytes), TNF‐α (an acute‐phase cytokine inducing inflammation and apoptotic signalling) and IL‐6 (a multifactorial cytokine involved in haematopoiesis and immune responses), while IL‐1β (a pro‐inflammatory cytokine released during caspase‐1‐mediated pyroptosis) was reduced compared to the CTR group. Notably, donors with a lower CD4+/CD8+ ratio exhibited greater alterations, particularly for TNF‐α and less pronounced for IL‐6 (Figure 1M,P and Figure S2). Altogether, these findings indicate an inflamed pattern in PLWH.

To explore potential interactions between clinical data and blood cellularity, an integrated analysis was performed. Pearson correlations were used to assess parameter associations within each group, which were then extrapolated across groups (Figure S3). For the CTR group, only flow cytometry parameters were considered (Figure S3A), and the PLWH subjects were further subdivided based on their last detectable viral load (LDVL) in blood. Short‐time LDVL subgroup: patients with detectable viral load for less than 3 weeks before sample collection. Long‐time LDVL subgroup: patients with detectable viral load for more than 3 weeks (Figure S3B,C).

Long‐time LDVL patients exhibited strong positive correlations among time‐related clinical parameters (measured in weeks), including the time since: diagnosis, lowest CD4 T cell count (NADIR), highest detected viral load (>VL), and treatment initiation. Additionally, negative correlations were observed between temporal variables and immune cell counts (e.g., treatment duration [week] vs. leukocyte count, CD4+ T cell count, CD14+ monocyte count and CD4+/CD8+ ratio), suggesting a progressive decline in immune cell numbers due to persistent infection. These findings indicate that long‐time LDVL patients experience chronic immune system deterioration, likely involving early senescence and other HIV‐related pathologies (Figure S3B). Linear regression analysis confirmed associations between temporal parameters (e.g., treatment duration, time since LDVL, and time since NADIR) and immunological markers (e.g., CD4+ count and CD4+/CD8+ ratio) in chronically infected patients (black data points; Figure 1Q–T).

The effectiveness of cART emerged as a key determinant in defining the PLWH subgroup phenotypes. This was supported by the strong positive correlation between the time since NADIR and treatment duration (r = 0.89), indicating a lower probability of therapeutic failure in long‐time LDVL patients (Figure 1Q). Furthermore, the CD4+/CD8+ ratio showed a negative correlation with both treatment duration (r = −0.73) and time since LDVL (r = −0.73), indicating an initial CD4+/CD8+ ratio recovery followed by a gradual decline over time (Figure 1R,S). Although a positive correlation was observed between CD4+/CD8+ ratio and absolute CD4+ T cell count in chronically infected patients (r = 0.70), their values remained consistently below those of the CTR group (Figure 1T). Collectively, these findings align with current literature and reinforce the notion that prolonged HIV infection contributes to immune dysfunction, potentially aggravating secondary pathologies.

In contrast, short‐time LDVL patients did not exhibit significant correlations in time‐related parameters. However, an inverse relationship was observed between CD4+ T cell survival and inflammatory monocyte presence: patients with lower CD4+ T cell counts displayed higher frequencies of CD163+ iMo (Figure S3B). These donors (red data points), who initiated treatment around the time of sample collection, predominantly had low CD4+/CD8+ ratios. Unlike long‐time LDVL patients, the CD4+/CD8+ ratio in this subgroup did not correlate with treatment duration, time since LDVL or CD4+ count (Figure 1R–T). These findings suggest an inflamed profile characteristic of recently active HIV infection.

In parallel, inflammatory parameters—comprising blood cellularity and plasma mediator levels—were analyzed using principal component (PC) analysis to generate individual immunological scores for donors from both CTR and PLWH groups (immunological parameters (loadings) shown in Figure 1U; PC scores in Figure 1V). Loadings and individual identification of CTR and PLWH were plotted based on their contributions to the PC1/2 axes. Loadings were primarily concentrated in the right quadrants—particularly the upper right—co‐localizing with the PC scores of PLWH individuals (denoted as ‘H’). In contrast, the PC scores of CTR donors (‘C’) clustered in the left quadrants, exhibiting minimal association with the loadings. This spatial distribution indicates how each PLWH subject exhibited elevated inflammatory patterns compared to the CTR donors (Figure 1U,V).

Based on the preceding analysis, participants were selected for omics profiling according to their clinical and immunological divergence. Representative individuals from both CTR and PLWH groups were included, with the selected PLWH participants specifically representing the chronically infected ‘long‐time LDVL’ subgroup. Figure 1W illustrates loadings and PC scores for all donors enrolled in the omics study. All analyses were stratified by individual CD4+/CD8+ ratio (Figure 1U,W), which was used as an integrative marker of immune status.

Morphological and Immunophenotypic Characterization of lEVs From Plasma

lEVs were isolated from plasma of both CTR and PLHW groups through five sequential centrifugation steps and washes (Figure 2A). Then, vesicles were analyzed for morphological and phenotypic characteristics. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) demonstrated that lEVs displayed a typical EV‐associated morphology (Figure 2B). The vesicles exhibited a mean diameter size of 196.4 nm and a mode of 127.2 nm as determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis. Apoptotic bodies (>1000 nm vesicles) and platelets (approximately 2–4 µm) were not observed (Figure 2C). Sample quality and composition were assessed by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) followed by western blotting, which confirmed the presence of EV specific markers (Figure 2D,E). β‐actin and ALIX protein, a key component of the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) pathway, were identified in lEVs (Figure 2D). ALIX was enriched in both cell lysates and lEV preparations, appearing as two distinct bands, while its expression in whole plasma and lEV‐depleted plasma was minimal or undetectable (Figure 2D,E). In addition to ALIX, lEVs were strongly positive for the heat shock protein 70 (HSP‐70) and the tetraspanins CD9 and CD63, which are canonical markers of EVs (Figure 2E). Furthermore, western blotting analysis also demonstrated a substantial reduction in apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), a common plasma contaminant, in lEV samples, indicating effective vesicle isolation (Figure 2D). The presence of apolipoproteins in low levels was not differentially expressed between groups (data not shown). Also, platelet‐associated proteins—including human platelet glycoproteins CD41 (GPIIb, II‐β chain), CD61 (GPIIIa, III‐α chain), GPIX, GP1BA (GPIb‐α chain) and GP1BB (GPIb‐β chain)—as well as erythrocyte‐related proteins (haemoglobins), and soluble plasma proteins (e.g., fibrinogen and albumin) were detected in the lEV samples by proteomic analysis. However, because these components were not differentially expressed between groups, the reliability of the proteins that were truly differentially expressed between PLWH and control samples is further supported.

Differentially Expressed Non‐Coding RNAs in lEVs From PLWH

To determine whether lEV cargo relates to the chronic pathological conditions seen in PLWH group, we compared differentially expressed non‐coding RNA and protein cargo in lEVs isolated from plasma of PLWH and CTR groups. Then, we integrated proteomic and transcriptomic data.

Figure 3A presents the volcano plot of normalized mean and fold change expression of lncRNAs, comparing the lEV origins from CTR and PLWH groups. Figure 3B shows the miRNA content in lEVs, demonstrating that hsa‐miR‐4433b‐3p was upregulated in the PLWH group, with a Log2 fold change of 3.406. miRNAs, among non‐coding RNAs, are known to be potent regulators of a wide range of genes, playing a central role in diverse cellular processes. To assess whether hsa‐miR‐4433b‐3p is involved in different gene pathway regulations, we applied KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Li et al. 2023). Interestingly, the findings suggest the involvement of this miRNA in modulating immune‐related PI3K‐AKT signalling, as well as several pathways implicated in tumorigenesis, Cushing's syndrome, and the immune response to human papillomavirus infection (Figure 3C and Table S3).

A pathway enrichment analysis was also performed to provide mechanistic insights between miR‐4433b‐3p and all differentially expressed lncRNAs identified in lEVs from PLWH (Figure 3D). One of the main resulting pathways was ‘cellular senescence’, with 112 genes being modulated by both miRNA and lncRNAs simultaneously (p‐adjusted 1.29−14). Additionally, 86 other pathways were identified, with notable involvement in immunity and ageing interfaces, including, but not limited to—longevity‐regulating pathway, NF‐κB signalling pathway, PI3K‐AKT signalling pathway, mTOR signalling pathway, MAPK signalling pathway, HIF‐1 signalling pathway, and TNF signalling pathway (Table S4).

Analysis of Differentially Expressed Proteins in lEVs From PLWH Group

To better understand how lEV cargo may be related to the immune response in the PLWH group, we evaluated their protein content. Tables S5 and S6 describe a total of 57 differentially expressed proteins in lEVs from PLWH. Among these, structural proteins (such as histones, apolipoprotein F, and heavy variable immunoglobulins) were excluded. The remaining 45 proteins, both up‐ and downregulated, were grouped in a heatmap of relative expression between the CTR and PLWH groups or organized into an interaction network using the STRING platform (Snel 2000) (Table S7 and Figure 4). The heatmap shows the clustering of proteins between groups (Figure 4A).

Next, we constructed an interactome to map the predicted interactions between the identified proteins. This analysis revealed 25 proteins grouped into five clusters, while 20 proteins remained unclustered (represented in white) (Figure 4B). According to the Reactome pathway enrichment analysis, at least 16 proteins out of the 45 differentially modulated proteins in lEVs were associated with the ‘immune response pathway’ (Figure 4C, in purple), whose dysregulation may contribute to uncontrolled inflammation related to HIV. These proteins included MIF, ADGRE5, C3, FBXO7, PSMB4, DTX3L, TMEM179B, DNAJC5, VNN1, LAMTOR2, S100A9, SUGT1, DSP, CPPED1, DCD and ELANE. Using ‘Compartments’ and ‘GO Component’ analysis, we identified several proteins associated with secretory pathways—including extracellular exosomes, vesicles, and membrane‐bound organelles—with most proteins originating from the cytoplasm or cytosol (Table S7).

Protein‐protein interaction analysis further supported a close relationship between clustered proteins and two immune‐related pathways: NF‐κB and PI3K‐AKT signalling. These pathways play central roles in a network of pathways related to cytokine synthesis and cell survival during immune responses (Pasquereau and Herbein 2022). To explore potential biological connections, we incorporated the NF‐κB1 and AKT1 proteins into the STRING interaction network (nodes highlighted in yellow). These molecules were detected but were not differentially expressed in our dataset (Figure 4D,E). Notably, when NF‐κB1 was placed at the centre of the network, a large proportion of the identified proteins were linked to the NF‐κB signalling (Figure 4D). A similar pattern emerged with the addition of AKT1 (Figure 4E). Most of the clustered proteins were downregulated in lEVs from PLWH, correlating with both pathways (Figure 4D,E).

Integration of Proteomic and Transcriptomic Data

To integrate proteomic and transcriptomic data, we assessed whether the differential modulation of proteins was linked to the differential expression of miRNAs. We inserted the proteins detected in our samples and the predicted miR‐4433b‐3p target genes into a Venn diagram (Heberle et al. 2015). According to published data and in silico predictions, the miRWalk database identified 5977 target genes for miR‐4433b‐3p (http://mirwalk.umm.uni‐heidelberg.de). Among the 45 differentially expressed proteins, 20 were identified as potential miRNA targets—AHCY, ERG28, C3, SUGT1, KCNK6, ALDH18A1, FBXO7, PRKAR1A, TMEM179B, SDHC, GOT1, LPGAT1, FIS1, DDX6, TMPPE, SLC35A4, ADIPOQ, GRPEL1, YIPF6 and DTX3L (Figure 5A). Among these, four proteins (C3, SUGT1, FBXO7 and DTX3L) were closely associated with the ‘immune response pathway’, as identified in the Reactome pathway enrichment analysis (Figure 4C,B).

Through NcPath tools, fused input analysis revealed key enriched pathways—including cellular senescence pathway (involving E2F2, FBXW11 and MDM2), PI3K‐AKT signalling (COL1A1, EREG, LAMC1, MDM2, PHLPP2 and YWHAE), and longevity regulation pathway (ADIPOQ and BAX) (Figure 5C and Table S8).

Evaluation of the Immunomodulatory Capacity of lEVs on PBMCs

After establishing differences in the cargo of lEVs from PLWH and identifying their potential association with immune‐related pathways, particularly NF‐κB and PI3K‐AKT signalling, we investigated whether these vesicles could directly modulate the immune response. To address this question, we set up an in vitro experimental model evaluating the potential of lEVs isolated from CTR and PLWH plasma to induce the release of inflammatory mediators by PBMCs from an unrelated control individual (Figure 6A). Overall, lEVs from the PLWH group induced greater cytokine and chemokine production than lEVs from CTR group, as observed for TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐17 (a cytokine important for T cell activation and neutrophil recruitment) and IL‐4 (a regulator of adaptive immunity) (Figure 6B,C,F,G, respectively). Both lEV sources failed induce CXCL‐10 or IL‐1β (Figure 6D,E) but did stimulate IL‐2 (a T cell proliferating cytokine) and IL‐8 (a neutrophil‐attracting chemokine) (Figure 6H,I). Notably, lEVs, mainly from CTR group, antagonized the effects of the bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) when added simultaneously to the cell culture. In fact, these vesicles inhibited the release of IL‐1β, IFN‐γ and IL‐12 (strongly pro‐inflammatory cytokines), as well as IFN‐α (an antiviral cytokine that enhances cell‐mediated cytotoxicity) (Figure 6E,J,K,L). Remarkably, lEVs from PLWH induced higher levels of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10, while lEVs from both sources synergistically enhanced IL‐10 production when combined with LPS (Figure 6M). These findings suggest that lEVs play a role in sustaining basal inflammation during HIV infection by inducing proinflammatory mediators and regulating their synthesis when cells are challenged with LPS.

Discussion

Untreated HIV‐infected adults and the elderly share characteristics of immune aging (immunosenescence), including reduced lymphopoiesis, increased frequencies of low‐proliferative well‐differentiated memory CD28− T cells, decreased CD4/CD8 ratios, and increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α). These alterations are reflected in the chronic pathology (Deeks 2011), as persistent cellular damage may be driven by the continued presence of non‐genomic viral RNA species (Demarino et al. 2018) and the remodelled metabolism of immune cells (Guo et al. 2021). Although cART can partially reverse HIV‐associated changes in PLWH, residual inflammation often persist and contributes to the secondary immune‐related pathologies, even in patients under regular treatment (Deeks 2011). In this context, individuals who fail to exhibit robust CD4 T cell recovery during therapy remain more susceptible to latency‐associated pathologies (Molina‐Pinelo et al. 2009). Disease progression in these individuals is influenced by multiple factors, including the treatment initiation and the intensity and duration of the T CD4 NADIR and the LDVL. Remarkably, the patients included in this omics analysis had begun antiretroviral therapy during the AIDS stage, exhibiting a mean T CD4 NADIR of 91 cells/µL, and an average of 303 weeks from treatment initiation to blood collection. The mean time since LDVL was 225 weeks. At the time of blood collection, these patients exhibited undetectable viral loads, and the downregulation of viral proteins likely reflected suppressed viral transcription and replication due to cART.

The PLWH cohort in this work presented fewer CD4+ T lymphocytes and more inflammatory monocytes than the CTR group. Both cell types are targets for HIV infection and may act as latently infected reservoirs (Chen et al. 2022). During HIV pathogenesis, inflammatory monocytes are constantly stimulated, acquiring a state of exhaustion or senescence (Desai and Landay 2010). Additionally, active infection is usually accompanied by intense pyroptosis of CD4 T cells and a reduction in their renewal capacity, ultimately affecting the T cell repertoire (Doitsh et al. 2014). We observed the CD4+/CD8+ ratio as a reflection of CD4+ T cell count and serving, alongside the ‘treatment duration’ and ‘time since LDVL in blood’, as valuable parameters for PLWH's clinical characterization. Indeed, individuals with a lower CD4/CD8 ratio, shorter treatment duration, and recent viral load measurements were associated with a weakened adaptive immune response, imbalanced cytokine production, and dysregulated inflammatory response (Deeks 2011). Based on the timing of viral load detection, PLWH group was stratified into two subgroups with distinct clinical characterization. The short‐time LDVL subgroup, comprising individuals with recent active infection, showed a significantly reduced CD4+/CD8+ ratio. Following treatment, an initial recovery of this ratio was observed, which was subsequently followed by a gradual decline in patients with chronic infection (long‐time LDVL subgroup). Through Pearson correlations and PCA, we also generated individual clinical scores within groups of patients to select samples for omics analysis. By excluding the effects of active infection and avoiding the presence of viral components, we better demonstrated how HIV‐associated chronicity affects lEV composition.

Circulating levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (IL‐6, TNF‐α, CXCL‐10 and IL‐1β) typically rise during acute infections, contributing to tissue damage. However, cART‐treated individuals exhibit age‐related immune dysfunction, characterized by elevated baseline levels of inflammatory mediators (Nasi et al. 2017). This chronic immune activation reflects a proinflammatory, senescence‐associated secretory phenotype, known as inflammaging, driven by infections and other diseases (Liu et al. 2023). In this study, we found that the PLWH group displayed increased baseline plasma levels of TNF‐α, IL‐6 and CXCL‐10, but reduced levels of IL‐1β. Variations in cytokine levels depend on the duration of specific pathways activated in inflammatory cells, which involve epigenetic regulation. TNF‐α and CXCL‐10 were evident inflammatory biomarkers in plasma, while IL‐6, usually considered a convenient marker for peripheral inflammation in older adults with various comorbidities (Tylutka et al. 2024), was less prominent. As IL‐1β is a strictly inducible cytokine that requires protease‐mediated processing for activation (Pyrillou et al. 2020), its presence was probably inhibited during cART therapy. Persistent immune activation in PLWH under cART often features sustained elevations of CXCL10/IP‐10 and TNF‐α, while IL‐6 and IL‐1β responses are more variable across individuals and cohorts (Tenorio et al. 2014; Hunt 2012). Our findings are consistent with this pattern. Nevertheless, persistent inflammation is multifactorial and heterogeneous, influenced by clinical parameters (such as time since diagnosis and presence of comorbidities) (Nasi et al. 2017; Tylutka et al. 2024). Therefore, it is required to perform distinct assays (such as qPCR, ELISA, and ROC analysis) and epigenetic studies in independent cohorts to validate this finding.

Corroborating with the hypothesis of involvement of lEVs in chronic inflammation, the functional assay demonstrated that lEVs from both control and PLWH groups induced PBMCs to secrete TNF‐α and IL‐6 in vitro, but not CXCL‐10 or IL‐1β. Interestingly, lEVs also stimulated IL‐10 synthesis and attenuated the pro‐inflammatory effects of LPS on PBMCs. Specifically, lEVs from PLWH promoted the synthesis of TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐17, IL‐4 and IL‐10. These results support the hypothesis that lEVs may contribute to the persistence of a pathological inflammatory phenotype during chronic HIV infection. Notably, the anti‐inflammatory role of lEVs was also evident, as they suppressed several LPS‐induced pro‐inflammatory mediators while favouring IL‐10 production. This dual action may be attributed to the complexity of lEV cargo, which includes divergent stimulatory signals capable of modulating a wide network of immunological pathways. It is also important to note that the experiment was conducted using a single concentration of lEVs and of LPS. This toll‐like receptor‐4 (TLR4) ligand is a potent proinflammatory at different concentrations (typically between 0 and 1000 ng/mL) (Wisler et al. 2022; Ngkelo et al. 2012; Bastin et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2021), therefore, dose‐response experiments could be required to further explore lEV immunomodulatory effects.

Inflammatory environments modulate the types, proportions, and activation status of immune cells, thereby altering the composition of their EVs, their capacity for EV uptake, and their response to soluble inflammatory mediators. These changes dictate intercellular communication (Mathieu et al. 2019; Robbins and Morelli 2014). Once HIV alters the immunometabolism of host cells (Guo et al. 2021), we assessed the content of blood‐circulating lEVs from PLWH and evaluated their potential role in modulating the immune response in vitro. Previous omics‐based studies have identified markers for heterogeneous EV populations (Kowal et al. 2016), highlighted the miRNA roles in gene expression regulation (Roth et al. 2015; Vergauwen et al. 2021), and investigated the impact of HIV on PBMC transcriptomes (Vahey et al. 2002), as well as the effects of cART on HIV‐related tissue pathologies (Morse et al. 2012). In addition, alterations in the protein and RNA content of sEVs from HIV‐1‐infected cells have been shown to induce divergent responses in recipient cells (Barclay et al. 2019). By integrating next‐generation sequencing (NGS) and proteomics, we identified distinct molecular signatures in lEVs reflective of HIV chronicity under cART. A set of differentially expressed lncRNAs, the miR‐4433b‐3p and 45 proteins were identified in lEVs isolated from PLWH plasma, representing potential therapeutic targets or prognostic markers. Among these proteins, 20 were identified as putative gene targets of miR‐4433b‐3p. Reactome pathway enrichment analysis revealed that five of these proteins (FBXO7, C3, SUGT1, TMEM179B and DTX3L) are linked to immune response pathways and could be considered markers of immune activation.

Previous miRNA sequencing analysis has demonstrated downregulation of miR‐4433b‐3p in sEVs during immune thrombocytopenia (Sun et al. 2021) and identified this miRNA as a potential therapeutic target in Kaposi's Sarcoma, an opportunistic malignancy commonly associated with HIV infection (Tuncer et al. 2022). This present study provides novel insights into the interplay of miR‐4433b‐3p in immune pathways in the context of chronic HIV infection. In parallel, several proteins differentially expressed in lEVs from PLWH were associated with immune dysregulation or activation of inflammatory pathways. For instance, FBXO7—a negative regulator of NF‐κB signalling—was upregulated in lEVs from PLWH and may contribute to the uncontrolled inflammation state seen in the clinical profiles of these individuals (Kuiken et al. 2012). Complement component C3, also upregulated in PLWH‐derived‐lEVs, plays a key role in opsonization and enhances HIV‐1 uptake by Langerhans cells, thereby facilitating viral transmission via trans‐infection mechanisms (Nijmeijer et al. 2021). Conversely, SUGT1, a protein involved in regulating HIV‐1 nuclear import and cellular susceptibility to infection, was found to be downregulated in the lEVs of PLWH. Previous studies have shown that SUGT1 depletion confers resistance to HIV‐1 infection, highlighting its relevance as a potential modulator of viral entry and integration (Allouch et al. 2020). Additionally, the E3 ubiquitin ligase DTX3L, which forms a functional complex with PARP9, was detected in extracellular vesicles of PLWH. This complex enhances interferon‐mediated responses by binding to STAT1, histones and viral 3C proteases, thus modulating both viral replication and immunopathology. Therefore, this complex may serve as a controller of viral infection and immunopathology. Its presence in lEVs may suggest a compensatory mechanism aimed at promoting viral control (Zhang et al. 2015). Finally, TMEM179B—a transmembrane protein detected in PLWH‐derived lEVs—currently lacks established functional associations with mechanisms related to our hypothesis. As our compositional approach remains associative, these candidate biomarkers require further validation to determine their functional roles in EV‐mediated modulation of immune responses. Mechanistic confirmation could be made through targeted inhibition (e.g., using antagomirs, blocking antibodies, and small‐molecule inhibitors) as well as assays assessing the effects of biomarker overexpression. Thus, signalling pathways may be investigated as well as protein–protein interactions by co‐immunoprecipitation studies.

KEGG integrative enrichment analyses highlighted the relevance of lEV components in regulating NF‐κB and PI3K‐AKT signalling, which are involved in gene expression control. These pathways perpetuate immune stimulation and contribute to the activation of secondary pathological mechanisms. The presence of proteins related to inflammatory signalling, such as NF‐κB and AKT, reinforces their potential as targets for pharmacological modulation. On the host side, NF‐κB functions as the principal transcription factor used by immune cells to drive inflammatory mediator expression (Pasquereau et al. 2017), while AKT protein is a critical kinase that supports cell survival during infection, promoting viral latency and reactivation (Pasquereau and Herbein 2022). Activation of canonical and non‐canonical NF‐κB signalling in latently infected cells stimulates viral RNA transcription (Wong and Jiang 2021). Additionally, the HIV protein TAT has been shown to directly activate NF‐κB signalling (Li et al. 2024), and TAR RNA, enriched in lEVs from PLWH under cART, enhances infection susceptibility via TLR‐mediated NF‐κB activation, suggesting a pathogenic role for EV‐associated viral RNAs (Sampey et al. 2016). Given that NF‐κB signalling is primarily associated with the synthesis of pro‐inflammatory mediators, and the PI3K‐AKT signalling plays a key role in IL‐10 production and function (Saraiva and O'Garra 2010; Sharma et al. 2011), the protein‐protein interaction analysis performed using STRING suggests that of lEV components may act as modulators of immune responses. This finding supports the dual effects (synergistic or antagonistic, depending on cytokines) of lEVs and LPS co‐stimulation observed in the functional assay. LPS also induces NF‐κB and AKT phosphorylation in human PBMCs, particularly during acute bacterial infection and sepsis (Ngkelo et al. 2012). Several other immune pathways were identified in this work as potential targets of lEV components—including mTOR, MAPK, HIF‐1, TNF signalling pathways, cellular senescence pathway, and longevity regulation pathway. The mTOR pathway is closely linked to PI3K‐AKT signalling (Glaviano et al. 2023), while the MAPK signalling is functionally related to NF‐κB activation (Huth et al. 2017). Aberrant activation of these and other networks—such as JAK‐STAT, cGAS‐STING, AMPK, melatonin and sirtuin pathways—has been associated with immunosenescence, playing a key role in regulating immune function during aging (Fu et al. 2025). Accordingly, our pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the involvement of signalling cascades related to cellular immunosenescence and longevity regulation, both implicated in the premature immune aging characteristic of chronic HIV infection. These findings warrant further mechanistic investigation using distinct agonists and signalling inhibitors.

Our findings suggest that lEV cargo may serve not only as biomarkers but also as potential therapeutic targets, offering new insights into vesicle‐mediated intercellular communication during HIV infection. Nevertheless, further research is essential to unravel the precise mechanisms by which individual lEV components contribute to HIV pathogenesis, including epigenetic reprogramming, early immunosenescence, cellular exhaustion and the development of HIV‐associated metabolic, neurological and inflammatory disorders.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

The experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical procedures approved by the Research Ethics Committee (CEP) of the FCFRP/USP, under protocol number CAEE 30736120.7.0000.5403, approved on 10/23/2020. Volunteers invited to participate in the research signed a free and informed consent form. The control (CTR) group comprised 16 individuals with a negative HIV diagnosis, confirmed by the Regional Blood Centre of Ribeirão Preto—Hemocentro‐FMRP/USP. For the people living with HIV (PLWH) group, 16 patients diagnosed with the virus and undergoing combined antiretroviral treatment (cART) were invited. Their healthcare was provided by the Special Unit for Therapy of Infectious Diseases (UETDI) at the Clinical Hospital (HC) of the FMRP/USP. Volunteers of both genders were included, regardless of ethnicity, aged between 18 and 50 years old. Donors with symptoms of secondary infection, chronic diseases or pregnant women were excluded.

Patient Clinical Profiling

Data from medical records were collected at the HC‐FMRP/USP for personal identification (including registration number and collection date), demographic categorization (age, gender and ethnicity) and clinical characterization (diagnosis (DIAG) time, treatment (TREAT) schedule and duration, last viral load (LVL), last detectable viral load (LDVL), highest viral load (>VL), last CD4, CD8 and CD45 (L_CD) count, CD4/CD8 (count/count) ratio and NADIR).

Plasma Harvesting and Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell (PBMC) Isolation

Blood from non‐fasting donors was collected by venipuncture using Vacutainer needle tubes. The first 3 mL were discarded, and the subsequent fractions were collected first in citrate tubes and then in heparin tubes. Samples were maintained at room temperature until lEV isolation, following the recommendations of the Minimal Information for Blood EV research (MIBlood‐EV) tool (see attached) (Lucien et al. 2023). Blood‐containing tubes were centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 20°C to separate plasma from crude blood cell extract. No haemolysis was detected by visual inspection. Fresh plasma from citrate tubes was reserved for lEV isolation, while plasma from heparin tubes was frozen at −80°C for inflammatory mediator measurements. To isolate PBMCs, the cell extract from heparin tubes was suspended with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 637 × g for 30 min at 20°C through a Ficoll Paque Plus gradient (GE Healthcare Biosciences, Marlborough, Massachusetts, United States; Cat# Cytiva 17‐1440‐02).

Flow Cytometry

PBMCs freshly isolated from heparin tubes were immunostained ex vivo with fluorophore‐linked antibodies that had been previously titrated. Antibodies recognized cell surface proteins according to the panel: anti‐CD3, anti‐CD4, anti‐CD8, anti‐CD14, anti‐CD16, anti‐CD163 and the cell viability dye (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA; Cat# 565388) (Table 1). Polystyrene microparticles (CompBeads Anti‐Mouse Ig, κ/Negative Control Compensation Particles Set [BD Biosciences; Cat# 552843, RRID: AB_10051478]; CompBeads Anti‐Rat and Anti‐Hamster Ig κ/Negative Control Compensation Particles Set [BD Biosciences; Cat# 552845, RRID: AB_10058522]) were included in all assays to optimize fluorescence compensation settings. Flow cytometry data were acquired using the LSR Fortessa (EMU‐ 13/50618‐0) and FACS Canto I (EMU‐04/09448‐5) cytometers (Becton Dickinson & Company) at the Flow Cytometry Centre of the FCFRP/USP. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo 10.10, and cellularity was assessed by counting target‐positive (+) events and determining frequency of total cells, CD14+ monocytes (CD16+/−C163+/−) and CD3+ T cells (CD4+ or CD8+), as well as the CD4+/CD8+ ratio.

Detection of Cytokines and Chemokines

Inflammatory mediators (TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐8, CXCL‐10, IL‐10, IL‐1β, IFN‐γ, IL‐4, IL‐17, IL‐12p70 and IFN‐α) were simultaneously measured in plasma obtained from heparin tubes (CTR and PLWH groups). The specifications of the Human Magnetic Luminex Discovery Assay kit manufacturer (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; Cat# LXSAHM‐12) were followed.

Isolation of Large Extracellular Vesicles

lEVs were obtained from plasma samples by using a sequential centrifugation protocol adapted from Théry et al. 2006 (Théry et al. 2006) (Figure S4). Following MIS‐EV (Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles 2023) guidelines (Welsh et al. 2024), lEV isolation was initiated no later than 2 h after blood collection. Plasma from citrate tubes was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 20°C to remove platelets, apoptotic vesicles, and other cellular debris. Then, the plasma supernatant was transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes (280 µL per tube) and centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 27 min to isolate lEVs. To wash and stabilize the vesicular structure, 150 µL saline buffer containing EDTA was added to the precipitate (Buffer 1 ‐ 1 M HEPES + 1 M KCl + 1 M MgCl2 + 1 M NaCl + 0.5 M EDTA), followed by a second centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 27 min at 20°C. The supernatant was discarded, and 100 µL of the same buffer without EDTA was added to the precipitate (Buffer 2 ‐ 1 M HEPES + 1 M KCl + 1 M MgCl2 + 1 M NaCl). The sample was centrifuged again at 18,000 × g for 27 min at 20°C, and the supernatant was discarded. lEVs were resuspended in PBS, spun, homogenized, aliquoted and frozen at −80°C. lEV aliquots were used in bioassays, omics studies, and prepared for characterization through nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and SDS‐PAGE.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

Morphological characterization and quantification of lEVs were performed in accordance with the MISEV 2023 guidelines (Welsh et al. 2024). lEV aliquots were resuspended in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and the size distribution profile was determined using the NanoSight NS300 system (NanoSight Ltd., Malvern, United Kingdom) at the Department of Biochemistry and Immunology, FMRP/USP.

Transmission Electron Microscopy of EVs

lEVs were adsorbed onto carbon‐coated copper grids, air‐dried, rinsed and negatively stained with uranyl acetate. Imaging was performed using a Hitachi 7600 transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV, equipped with an AMT XR80 CCD camera.

Protein Extraction and Quantification

lEVs aliquots were treated with RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat# 89901) supplemented with Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA‐Free (Thermo; Cat# 78425) and Halt Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo; Cat# 78420). Samples were incubated at 95°C for 5 min under reducing conditions, followed by three cycles of sonication (5 min), vortexing (10 s), and incubation on ice (5 min). Protein concentration in the supernatant was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo; Cat# 23225), and absorbance was measured at 562 nm.

SDS‐PAGE

lEV samples were mixed with NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo; Cat# NP0008) containing 2‐mercaptoethanol (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA; Cat# 161–0710) at a 9:1 (v/v) ratio. The mixture was heated at 95°C for 7 min and loaded onto polyacrylamide gels (Mini‐Protean TGX Precast Gels, Bio‐Rad; Cat# 4561086 and 4561033). Precision Plus Protein Dual Colour Standards (Bio‐Rad; Cat# 1610374) were used as molecular weight markers. Following electrophoresis, EV protein content was analyzed by western blotting or stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R‐250 for subsequent proteomic analysis.

Western Blotting Assay

To assess purity, the presence of the soluble plasma contaminant apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) was evaluated. Phenotypic characterization of lEVs was based on the detection of EV‐associated proteins, including ALIX, CD9, CD63, HSP‐70 and β‐actin. Membranes were incubated overnight in TRIS‐buffered saline (TBS, 1X) + 1% Tween 20 (1X TBS + 1% Tween‐20) supplemented with 2% blotting‐grade blocker (Bio‐Rad; Cat# 1706404) and the following primary antibodies: anti‐ALIX (Abcam, Cambridge, U.K.; Cat# ab76608; RRID: AB_2042595), anti‐HSP‐70 (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat# 4872; RRID: AB_2279841), anti‐CD63 (Abcam; Cat# ab216130; RRID: AB_3076642; BD Biosciences; Cat# 556019; RRID: AB_396297), anti‐CD9 (Abcam; Cat# ab2215; RRID: AB_302894; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat# 312102; RRID: AB_31490) and anti‐β‐actin (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat# 4967; RRID: AB_330288). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies: anti‐mouse IgGk BP‐HRP (Abcam; Cat# ab6789; RRID: AB_955439; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; Cat# sc‐516102; RRID: AB_2687626) or anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat# 7074; RRID: AB_2099233; Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Cat# sc‐2357; RRID: AB_628497). Detection was performed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo; Cat# 34580) and visualized with the Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) at the Department of Cellular and Molecular Biology and Pathogenic Bioagents, FMRP/USP.

Sample Preparation and LC‐MS/MS

Proteomic analyses were performed on lEV aliquots from five donors per group, with at least 25 µg of protein per donor. Samples were electrophoresed on polyacrylamide gels and stained with 0.1% Coomassie Blue R‐250 following the Fiocruz‐PR SOP (Code: ICC‐POP.120). SDS‐PAGE was conducted at the Molecular Virology Laboratory, Carlos Chagas Institute/Fiocruz‐PR. Gel bands were excised and analyzed at the RPT02H mass spectrometry facility (http://plataformas.fiocruz.br/). Tryptic peptides were separated using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Elution was performed over a 60‐min linear gradient (5%–40% acetonitrile, 250 nL/min) using a 15 cm analytical C18 column (75 µm ID, 3 µm particle size). The instrument operated in positive ion mode (2300 V), acquiring precursor ions (300–1500 m/z) with a resolution of 120,000 in the Orbitrap. Dynamic exclusion was set to one repeat with a 60‐s exclusion and 10 ppm tolerance. MS2 acquisition (ddMS2 OT HCD) was performed in the quadrupole with a 1.6 m/z isolation window and 15,000 resolutions.

Proteome Data Analysis

Initial data processing, including protein identification, quantification, and validation, was conducted at the RPT02H/Fiocruz‐PR facility. Raw files were analyzed in a single run using MaxQuant (v1.5.2.8), employing the Andromeda search engine for database queries. Label‐free quantification (LFQ intensity) was applied for inter‐sample normalization. Differential protein expression clustering and interaction network analyses were conducted using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/) (Snel 2000).

RNA Extraction for RNA‐Seq

miRNA‐enriched RNA was extracted from lEV aliquots using the Quick‐RNA MiniPrep Plus Kit (Zymo Research; Cat# R1058), following the manufacturer's protocol. lEVs were mixed with DNA/RNA Shield (2X), followed by RNA Lysis Buffer (1:1), homogenization, and centrifugation (14,000 × g, 30 s, 18°C). The supernatant was mixed with 1.5× volume of ethanol and loaded onto a Zymo‐Spin IIICG column. DNase I treatment (Zymo; Cat# E1009‐A) was applied, followed by sequential washes. RNA was eluted with 100 µL DNase/RNase‐free water, freeze‐dried, and stored at −80°C. Lyophilized RNA was resuspended in reduced volume for quality control and next‐generation sequencing (NGS) (four donors per group). Zymo Research performed RNA QC, normalization, library preparation and sequencing.

Transcriptome Data Analysis

Bioinformatic processing of small RNA sequencing data (miRNA and non‐coding RNAs, excluding miRNAs and rRNAs) was conducted by Zymo Research using a custom Nextflow pipeline adapted from nf‐core/smrnaseq v2.2.0 (https://github.com/nf‐core/smrnaseq). Key steps included: (1) quality control using FastQC (using FastQC software), (2) 3′ adapter TGGAATTCTCGGGTGCCAAGG trimming (Trim Galore!), (3) miRNA quality control (mirtrace), (4) duplicate reads collapse via seqcsluter, alignment and quantification of miRNA/isomiRs using Bowtie1, and annotation via mirtop and (5) alignment of other small RNAs (tRNAs, lncRNAs, snoRNAs, snRNAs etc.) with Bowtie1, followed by quantification with RSEM and summarization with tximport. Low‐quality reads and adapters were filtered using a minimum trimmed length of 18 nucleotides. Reads passing quality control were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome (Ensembl GRCh38). Software versions used included: Nextflow v22.10.4, R v4.0.5, FastQC v0.11.9, Trim Galore! V0.6.6, Bowtie v1.3.0, Samtools v1.16.1, FASTX v0.0.14, miRTrace v1.0.1, RSEM v1.2.28, tximport v1.18.0 and mirtop v0.4.23. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with the DESeq2 package (Love et al. 2014) within the R software environment. Genes with absolute log2 fold change >1.5 (up‐) or ←1.5 (down‐regulated) and a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Omics Integration

Although contaminants such as platelets, lipoproteins, immunoglobulins and serum albumin were largely removed during sample preparation, residual contamination may have impacted the data. In silico pathway analyses were used to mitigate these effects. Integration of proteomic and transcriptomic data was conducted through KEGG and Reactome enrichment analyses, considering both p values and adjusted p values (http://ncpath.pianlab.cn/#/Enrichment). Overlaps among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized using a Venn diagram (Heberle et al. 2015) (https://www.interactivenn.net/index.html).

Functional Assay—Biological Activity of lEVs on PBMCs

PBMCs recovered from a leukoreduction chamber (healthy donor) were isolated via Ficoll‐Paque gradient. Then, PBMC cultures were stimulated with 22.5 µg of intact lEVs (final concentration: 0.15 µg/µL) from selected CTR (02, 07, 08 and 10) and PLWH (04, 05, 08 and 10) donors. lEV protein concentrations were previously quantified (see Section 9). A total of 5 × 105 PBMCs/well were incubated in a final volume of 150 µL; after 25 h, 120 µL of supernatant was collected. Inflammatory mediator levels were assessed using the Human Luminex Discovery Assay kit (R&D Systems—Cat#: LXSAHM‐12), as described in Section 5. LPS dose of 100 ng/mL for PBMC was based on prior literature and internal optimization for PBMC stimulation with EVs (Ngkelo et al. 2012).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses, including comparisons of cellular responses and inflammatory mediator levels, Pearson correlation coefficients, and principal component analyses (PCA), were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, USA). Volcano plots depicting differentially expressed lncRNAs and microRNAs were generated in R, while heatmaps representing protein marker enrichment were created in Prism. Statistical tests were selected based on data distribution, assessed by the D'Agostino‐Pearson omnibus test.

Parametric data were analyzed using unpaired Student's t‐tests; non‐parametric data with the Mann–Whitney test (two‐tailed, p ≤ 0.05). Group comparisons in the bioassay were conducted via one‐way ANOVA followed by uncorrected Fisher's LSD post‐hoc test. Sample sizes are detailed in figure legends, with p values shown in the figures.
