Detection of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in companion animals in Poland during the post-pandemic period (2022–2025)
Authors: Hanna Turlewicz-Podbielska, Ewelina Czyżewska-Dors, Łukasz Adaszek, Jakub Jędrzej Ruszkowski, Małgorzata Pomorska-Mól
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infects many species, including companion animals. Monitoring changes in pet seropositivity rate provides insight into viral circulation at the human–animal interface. This study assessed SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity rate in dogs, cats, and rabbits in Poland between 2022 and 2025, focusing on demographic and time-related factors.MethodsA total of 1037 serum samples were collected from dogs (n = 611), cats (n = 385), and rabbits (n = 41) in nine Polish provinces. Antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 were detected using a multispecies nucleocapsid-based ELISA. Data on sampling year, species, location, age and gender were analysed. Seropositivity rate estimates with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson score method. Associations were tested with Fisher’s exact test, and multivariable logistic regression was applied to assess predictors of seropositivity, including species, year, and age.ResultsThe overall seropositivity rate was 5.1% (53/1037; 95% CI: 3.93–6.62). Species-specific prevalence was 6.75% in cats (26/385; 95% CI: 4.65–9.71) and 4.42% in dogs (27/611; 95% CI: 3.05–6.35), while all rabbit samples were negative (0/41; 95% CI: 0.00-8.60). No significant temporal trend was observed across the study period, although prevalence was higher in 2024–2025 compared with 2022–2023. The proportion of seropositive animals was slightly higher in cats than in dogs (OR = 0.62 for dogs vs. cats, p = 0.096). Age was the strongest predictor: animals aged 4–7 years showed the highest seropositivity rate (7.98%, 19/238; OR = 3.94 compared to 0–3 years, p = 0.004). No significant associations were found with gender or geographic region.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that cats and dogs in Poland have been exposed to SARS-CoV-2, with seropositivity rate levels consistent with international reports. Cats were more frequently seropositive than dogs, and middle-aged animals had the highest likelihood of exposure, reflecting cumulative contact opportunities during peak human transmission. Rabbits did not show evidence of past infection, suggesting negligible involvement in viral ecology under natural conditions. These findings support the role of pets as sentinels of community transmission and highlight the importance of continued One Health surveillance.

Background

Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) is an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus taxonomically classified within the order Nidovirales, family Coronaviridae, and genus Betacoronavirus [1, 2],. The virus was first identified in December 2019 in Wuhan, China, and was subsequently recognised as the causative agent of a rapidly spreading respiratory disease in humans, later named Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) [3]. In March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) recognized COVID-19 as a global pandemic [4]. In Poland, the first confirmed case of COVID-19 was announced on March 4, 2020, involving a resident of Zielona Góra city, Lubusz Province [5]. In response to the rapidly growing number of infections, the government swiftly implemented control measures, including restrictions on public gatherings and travel. According to the Department of Epidemiology and Surveillance of Infectious Diseases in Poland, case numbers increased dramatically in the subsequent years: 1,289,293 confirmed cases were reported in 2020, 2,834,287 in 2021, and 2,345,903 in 2022. As the pandemic progressed, the emergence of less virulent viral variants together with increasing population-level immunity due to mass vaccination campaigns and natural infections contributed to a decline in reported cases, with 341,694 in 2023 and 276,065 in 2024 [6]. Nevertheless, the true scale of SARS-CoV-2 circulation in the population was likely higher than these figures suggest, since many infections were undiagnosed or unreported over time. While human-to-human transmission has declined recently, the number of reported infections among animals and susceptible species continues to increase [7]. At the onset of the pandemic, only sporadic cases of animal infections were detected worldwide. The first confirmed infection in a dog occurred in Hong Kong, where the virus was identified in nasal swabs and later confirmed serologically [8]. Although most infected pets do not display overt clinical symptoms, the capacity of cats and dogs to become infected by SARS-CoV-2 raises concern regarding their potential role as silent reservoirs. This could contribute to viral persistence, evolution of host-adapted variants, and even cross-species re-emergence [9–12].

While early pandemic efforts focused almost exclusively on human health, increasing evidence pointed to the capacity of SARS-CoV-2 to infect a wide range of animal species [13]. Given the multispecies host range of SARS-CoV-2, antibody surveys in dogs and cats are essential for clarifying transmission dynamics across human and animal populations [14]. In some cases, this reverse zoonotic transmission has led to local epizootics, as observed in farmed minks [15], zoo-housed tigers [16], hamsters [17], and wild white-tailed deer [18]. Moreover, interspecies transmission has also been reported, including events such as mink-to-cat transmission [19], which prompted drastic public health measures such as mass culling of infected animal populations [20]. The virus’s ability to infect hosts via angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 receptors is a key, but not the sole, step in viral entry, and facilitates cross-species transmission and has been implicated in both clinical and subclinical infections [21]. To date, 65 animal species have been identified by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) as naturally susceptible to SARS-CoV-2, including companion animals, livestock, and captive as well as free-ranging wildlife [13]. Among these, companion animals, such as dogs and cats, have exhibited variable rates of infection and seroconversion. Although the total number of reported infected animals is significantly lower than the number of human cases, serological studies consistently demonstrate that cats and dogs can be exposed and mount detectable immune responses [22–24].

Experimental studies have demonstrated that cats are more susceptible than dogs, with efficient viral replication in the nasal and tracheal epithelium and the potential to transmit the virus to contact animals. In contrast, dogs have lower susceptibility and typically fail to sustain replication [25]. Differences in susceptibility and immune response between cats and dogs have also been observed with respect to SARS-CoV-2 variants. In a large-scale serological study from Thailand, Suwanpakdee et al. [26] detected neutralizing antibodies in 0.23% (3/1,304) of cats and 0.28% (5/1,795) of dogs, indicating overall low seroprevalence in both species. However, qualitative differences in antibody profiles were noted: cats more frequently exhibited cross-neutralizing activity against multiple variants (Wuhan-Hu-1 and Delta), while most seropositive dogs reacted to a single variant only. Moreover, the neutralizing capacity against the Omicron BA.2 variant was markedly reduced or absent in all animals tested. Additionally, several studies have consistently reported higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in cats compared to dogs. For instance, Sánchez-Morales et al. [27] observed a prevalence of neutralizing antibodies of 4.6% in cats compared to 1.9% in dogs in the Community of Madrid, Spain. These results are in line with serological studies conducted in Minnesota [28], Italy [29], the United Kingdom [30], and France [31], all of which reported higher antibody prevalence in cats than in dogs. Collectively, these findings suggest that cats are generally more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection or more likely to be exposed to the virus within household environments.

The persistence of antibodies in companion animals has been investigated using various serological assays, which differ in antigenic targets and sensitivity. In a recent study, Kimmerlein et al. [32] employed an in-house ELISA based on recombinant nucleocapsid (N) and spike proteins of SARS-CoV-2 to monitor antibody responses in naturally exposed dogs and cats. Using this approach, IgG antibodies were detected in dogs between 16 and 828 days and in cats between 19 and 650 days after household exposure to COVID-19-positive owners. In contrast, Decaro et al. [33] followed seropositive pets for up to ten months using a commercial multispecies ELISA (ID.vet, France) and virus neutralization tests, showing that neutralizing antibodies persisted for at least eight months, while the N-based ELISA often yielded negative results despite ongoing neutralizing activity. These findings indicate that the duration of antibody detectability in pets depends strongly on the assay type and antigen used, with spike-based or neutralization assays offering higher sensitivity for detecting long-term immune responses.

This study aimed to evaluate the seropositivity rate of SARS-CoV-2 in companion animals (cats, dogs, and rabbits) within Poland during the post-pandemic phase, from 2022 to 2025. Our previous cross-sectional study, which included samples collected from domestic cats, dogs, and rabbits between June 2020 and February 2021, identified relatively low levels of seropositivity: 1.79% (5/279) in cats, 1.17% (4/343) in dogs, and 0% (0/29) in rabbits [34]. These findings reflected the early stages of the pandemic and were consistent with the limited number of documented animal infections worldwide at that time. However, a later study by Kaczorek-Łukowska et al. [35] has shown considerably higher seroprevalence, particularly during periods of intense human transmission. The authors reported 18.9% (46/243) seropositivity in cats and 16.0% (62/388) in dogs sampled between October 2021 and February 2022 in two Polish cities (Gdańsk and Olsztyn), aligning with Poland’s fourth pandemic wave. This increase in seropositivity likely corresponds with the greater transmission pressure among the human population.

The primary objective of this investigation was to elucidate trends over time and species-specific patterns, thereby enhancing our understanding of the role of companion animals in the epidemiology of SARS-CoV-2. Additionally, by juxtaposing our findings with existing data from Europe and other regions, we aimed to contribute to a One Health perspective concerning the transmission dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 at the human-animal interface.

Materials and methods

Samples

Samples represented a convenience sampling of residual sera obtained from client-owned animals during routine veterinary visits, collected from dogs, cats, and rabbits between May 2022 and May 2025, in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [36]. Owners were informed of the purpose of the study and the data protection policy and provided written consent for each pet. Blood was originally collected for clinical purposes (e.g., hematological or biochemical analyses), and a portion of the remaining serum, which was no longer required for diagnostic use, was included in this study. Samples originated from ten veterinary clinics and one veterinary diagnostic laboratory. Samples were obtained from cats and dogs residing in nine Polish provinces: Greater Poland, Kuyavian-Pomeranian, Lesser Poland, Lublin, Łódź, Masovian, Podlaskie, Warmian-Masurian, and West Pomeranian. Samples from rabbits were obtained from two provinces: Greater Poland and Lublin (Table 1; Fig. 1). A total of 1037 serum samples were included in the study, comprising 611 samples from dogs, 385 from cats, and 41 from rabbits. The following data were recorded for all samples: animal species, sampling location, and sampling date. Additionally, information on gender and age at the time of sampling was available for the majority of samples, with missing data for 6.2% (64/1037) of animals for gender and 12.1% (125/1037) for age. Samples were collected during routine veterinary visits or diagnostic procedures, regardless of any known or suspected exposure to SARS-CoV-2. All sera were aliquoted and stored at − 70 °C until further serological analysis. Samples were anonymised and coded to ensure confidentiality and consistency across sites before laboratory processing.

Antibody detection

Detection of antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 in serum samples was performed using a commercially available multispecies enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; ID Screen® SARS-CoV-2 Double Antigen Multispecies, IDvet, France), which targets antibodies directed against the nucleocapsid (N) protein of the virus. This assay was successfully applied in our previous SARS-CoV-2 serological study [34]. The procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 25 µl of dilution buffer and 25 µl of serum sample were added to each well of the ELISA plate and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. After incubation, plates were washed three times, followed by adding 100 µl of horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antigen. The plates were then incubated at 21 °C for 30 min, washed again three times, and 100 µl of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate solution was added. After a 20-minute incubation at 21 °C, the reaction was stopped with 100 µl of Stop Solution, and optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm. For each sample, the sample to positive percentage (S/P%) was calculated using the formula: S/P% = (OD_sample – OD_negative control)/(OD_positive control – OD_negative control) × 100. Samples were interpreted as follows, based on the manufacturer’s criteria: S/P% ≤ 50% – negative; S/P% > 50% and < 60% – doubtful; S/P% ≥ 60% – positive. Samples that yielded doubtful results in the initial ELISA screening were subjected to two additional independent repetitions. For each such case, the mean OD across all three replicates was calculated and used for final classification of the sample as positive or negative according to the manufacturer’s criteria.

Notably, potential cross-reactivity with other coronaviruses known to infect companion animals was excluded in a prior study [34]. The validation panel included sera from cats and dogs naturally infected with feline coronavirus or canine respiratory coronavirus, archival pre-pandemic samples collected in 2018 from dogs, cats, and rabbits, and positive control sera from COVID-19–positive humans. No cross-reactivity was observed with non–SARS–CoV–2 coronaviruses, confirming the assay’s specificity for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in multiple species.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.1.2) [37]. Data were imported with readxl and processed using dplyr [38] and tidyr [39]. As diagnostic sensitivity and specificity data for this assay in the tested populations were unavailable, the true prevalence could not be estimated; therefore, proportions of seropositive animals are reported as seropositivity rates. The seropositivity rate was reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) calculated using the Wilson score interval, which provides accurate coverage even for small sample sizes or proportions close to 0 or 1 (binom.confint, package binom) [40]. Confidence intervals were computed to describe the internal precision of seropositivity rate estimates within the analyzed sample. Associations between SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity and categorical predictors (year of sampling, province, age category, gender) were initially screened using Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation. Age was categorized into the following groups: 0–3 (young) years, 4–7 years (middle-aged), and ≥ 8 years (senior), reflecting clinically relevant life stages in dogs and cats, with a separate group for unknown ages (included in descriptive summaries but not used as the reference). Rabbits were excluded from the analyses due to small sample size and the absence of seropositive individuals. Samples from pet rabbits were included as an exploratory component to assess potential post-pandemic circulation in a species that is experimentally susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection [41]. Given the limited number of samples, data for rabbits were analysed descriptively and not incorporated into the logistic regression model.

We initially fitted a multivariable logistic regression model to assess patterns over time, including year of sampling, province, species, age category and gender. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% profile-likelihood confidence intervals were derived, and model coefficients were exponentiated to ORs.

Visualisations (trends over time and age-stratified prevalence plots with 95% CI) were generated with ggplot2 [42]. The geographic distribution map was created with MapChart (https://www.mapchart.net) and finalized using Canva (https://www.canva.com).

Results

A total of 1037 serum samples were collected in Poland between 2022 and 2025 (dogs n = 611; cats n = 385; rabbits n = 41). The overall seropositivity rate of SARS-CoV-2 was 5.1% (53/1037; 95% CI: 3.93–6.62). By species, seropositivity rate was 6.75% in cats (26/385; 95% CI: 4.65–9.71) and 4.42% in dogs (27/611; 95% CI: 3.05–6.35); none of the rabbit sera were positive (0/41; 95% CI: 0.00–8.60.00.60), indicating that SARS-CoV-2 exposure in pet rabbits remains extremely rare under natural conditions.

Year-specific prevalence (all species combined) was 3.1% (2/65) in 2022, 0% (0/19) in 2023, 5.3% (33/618) in 2024, and 5.4% (18/335) in 2025. When analysing the association between year of sampling and SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity, no significant differences were found (p = 0.859). Species showed a trend toward higher seropositivity rate in cats than in dogs (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.090). Differences between provinces were not significant (Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation, p = 0.23). Seropositivity rate did not differ by gender (p = 0.95), whereas age category was significant in univariable analysis (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.026). However, when stratified by species, the association with age was no longer statistically significant (dogs: p = 0.099; cats: p = 0.457), likely due to smaller subgroup sizes and reduced statistical power. Discussion

The detection of specific antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 provides insight into past infections and helps evaluate transmission dynamics at the human–animal interface. In the global context, SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in cats and dogs has varied widely across countries and time periods. The overall seropositivity rate for SARS-CoV-2 in our study was 5.11% (53/1037), with rates of 6.75% (26/385) in cats and 4.42% (27/611) in dogs. Regarding cats, our results were similar to those obtained in Serbia (5.56%; 2/34) [43], France (8%; 17/212) [44], USA (7.9%; 19/239 and 5.7%; 40/706) [28, 45], Brazil (7.3%; 40/547) [46] or Colombia (6.67%; 9/135) [47]. Regarding dogs, our results were comparable to those reported in Italy (4.5%; 5/112) [48], France (4.7%; 15/315) [44], and Colombia (3.67%; 11/300) [47]. Detailed information on SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in Europe and worldwide is presented in Table 5 for cats and Table 6 for dogs. Differences in reported seroprevalences between studies and countries likely reflect a combination of factors, including study design, choice of serological method, characteristics of the sampled populations, and year of sampling. Variability in animal populations, such as owned versus stray animals, or animals sampled in shelters versus veterinary clinics, can substantially influence the likelihood of prior exposure to SARS-CoV-2. Furthermore, the timing of sample collection relative to local peaks in human infection plays a critical role, as animals are most likely to be exposed during periods of high community transmission. However, comparison of seropositivity trends over time between countries should be interpreted with caution, as the exact sampling periods were not reported in several studies [47, 49], potentially limiting the alignment of datasets in time. Another important consideration is the serological method used. Different assays (e.g., ELISA, surrogate virus neutralisation tests (sVNT), virus neutralisation test (VNT)) vary in their sensitivity, specificity, and ability to detect neutralising versus non-neutralising antibodies. It can be observed that cats have generally shown higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence than dogs, particularly in later pandemic phases. In our study, cats showed a trend toward higher seropositivity compared to dogs (p = 0.090), a pattern supported by our multivariable regression (OR = 0.62 for dogs vs. cats) and consistent with reports from other countries [45].

We observed an increase in the overall SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity rate in dogs and cats compared with our previous investigation, based on samples collected in 2020–2021, in which the values remained below 2% [34]. In the current study (2022–2025), the proportion of seropositive animals was about two- to threefold higher, reaching 5.3% in 2024 and 5.4% in 2025, with lower levels in 2022 (3.1%) and no positive cases detected in 2023. The observed seropositivity level is higher than those recorded in our previous investigation; however, it is considerably lower than the values reported by Kaczorek-Łukowska et al. [35], who collected samples from companion animals between October 2021 and February 2022. This period coincided with the dominance of the Delta variant, which has been shown to possess a high transmission potential to companion animals and to infect both cats and dogs efficiently [65]. In contrast, the Omicron variant and its sublineages, which circulated predominantly during our sampling period (2022–2025), have been associated with markedly lower susceptibility of companion animals to infection. Experimental and field data indicate that Omicron replicates less efficiently in cats and dogs, resulting in reduced viral shedding and seroconversion rates [65]. Therefore, the seropositivity rate observed in our study, compared with the Delta period, likely reflects this reduced host susceptibility. In contrast, the slight increase compared with 2020–2021 is most plausibly due to prolonged and repeated opportunities for human-to-animal exposure over time. Descriptive analyses indicated a gradual increase in SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity among the sampled pet population; however, no significant differences in SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity were detected between years. This finding is consistent with the logistic regression results, where “year of sampling” did not emerge as a significant predictor. In other studies, an upward trend in seroprevalence was observed in cats. In Germany, the seroprevalence rate increased from 0.65% (April–September 2020) to 1.36% (September 2020–February 2021), with the highest monthly values appearing in early 2021, closely following a sharp rise in human cases [51, 52]. In Portugal, a notable increase was seen in cats from COVID-19–positive households, where the seroprevalence rose from 14.7% in an earlier study to 30.3% between November 2021 and March 2022 [66, 67]. Likewise, in Minnesota, USA, a marked rise in seroprevalence, from 0 to 5% before mid-May 2020 to 11–12% afterwards, coincided with a spike in human infections [28]. In Portugal, comparing two studies conducted in the Lisbon metropolitan area illustrates a clear temporal increase in SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in cats. While the earlier study, involving cats with unknown exposure status, reported a seroprevalence of 14.7% and a neutralising antibody rate of 20.4% [50], a subsequent study conducted between November 2021 and March 2022 in 76 cats from 50 COVID-19–positive households found significantly higher values: 30.3% seropositive by ELISA, and 69.6% of these with potent neutralising antibodies [67]. Although the animals in the later study came exclusively from SARS-CoV-2–positive households (potentially increasing the baseline risk of exposure), the marked rise in seroprevalence and neutralising antibody detection suggests a broader time-dependent increase in infection pressure. This pattern may reflect cumulative viral exposure in households and higher transmissibility of emerging variants. In dogs, longitudinal data from the United States revealed a dramatic increase in seropositivity over time. While only 1.16% of samples tested positive in the third quarter of 2021, this value rose sharply to 23.26% in the third and fourth quarters of 2024 [64]. These results highlight that, as with cats, canine exposure to SARS-CoV-2 also intensified over time, likely due to the same household exposure dynamics. These findings support the hypothesis that SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in pets tends to rise in parallel with human epidemic curves, particularly in periods following significant transmission waves. However, discrepancies between countries, such as the stable or even declining trend observed in Spain despite virus circulation (3.51% of seropositive cats in period from January to October 2020 vs. 1.57% in the period from October 2020 to January 2022) [58], indicate that local context, human behavior, animal management, and diagnostic approaches can all influence seroprevalence dynamics.

Age emerged as the strongest predictor of SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity in our study. Animals (cats and dogs together) aged 4–7 years showed the highest prevalence (8.44%, 19/225) and nearly fourfold higher odds of being seropositive compared to those aged 0–3 years (OR = 3.94, Table 4). Cats and dogs ≥ 8 years also had increased prevalence (5.76%, 23/399) and 2.6-fold higher odds of seropositivity (OR = 2.61, Table 4). When dogs and cats were analysed jointly, the association between age category and seropositivity was significant (p < 0.05). The multivariable logistic regression confirmed the biological relevance of age, which is in line with other reports highlighting age as a risk factor. Similarly, Nilsson et al. [68] showed that adult dogs had a 4.16-fold higher likelihood of seropositivity than young ones. Patterson et al. [29] and Newman et al. [69] also observed increased rates in adult canines. Barua et al. [45] found higher seropositivity among cats and dogs older than 1 year compared to juveniles in a large dataset from the United States. In contrast, some studies conducted on cats did not demonstrate age-related effects [49, 50]. In our study, animals aged 4–7 years showed the highest SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity. However, the age-dependent pattern observed here should not be interpreted as cumulative antibody accumulation with age. Previous studies have demonstrated that IgG antibodies persist for several months but tend to wane over time [32, 33]. Hence, the higher seropositivity observed among animals aged 4–7 years cannot be attributed to prior infection history. This age group most likely represents individuals with the highest current exposure risk due to lifestyle-related factors. Middle-aged dogs and cats are generally the most socially active, have frequent contact with people outside their household, and are often taken to veterinary clinics for preventive care or minor ailments — all situations that increase the likelihood of recent exposure. In contrast, younger animals (0–3 years) may have more limited human contact, spend more time indoors, or interact with fewer household members, while older pets (≥ 8 years) often show reduced mobility and shorter outdoor activity. In addition to behavioural factors, age-related differences in immune competence may also influence SARS-CoV-2 antibody detection. Middle-aged animals are likely to exhibit both optimal immune responsiveness and higher exposure potential, which together could explain the higher seropositivity observed in this group. However, current evidence on the kinetics and magnitude of SARS-CoV-2–specific antibody responses across age groups in companion animals remains scarce. Further controlled studies are needed to characterise how immune maturity and senescence affect antibody persistence and detectability in dogs and cats of different ages. Altogether, these factors provide a plausible explanation for the highest seropositivity being observed in the 4–7-year age group, despite the transient nature of SARS-CoV-2 antibody responses.

Gender, on the other hand, was not associated with SARS-CoV-2 antibody detection in our cohort, consistent with findings from Barroso et al. [70] and Ulloa et al. [49]. Although Patterson et al. [29] noted higher seropositivity in male dogs, most studies, including ours, found no significant differences between males and females, indicating that gender is unlikely to play a significant role in susceptibility to infection. Geographic distribution did not play a significant role in shaping SARS-CoV-2 exposure among companion animals in Poland. Differences in seropositivity between provinces were not statistically significant, suggesting that the virus circulated relatively homogeneously across regions. This likely reflects the nationwide character of human outbreaks, which acted as the primary source of spillover infections to pets, rather than any strong regional drivers of transmission.

An additional aspect of this study was the inclusion of pet rabbits, a species experimentally shown to be susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 replication, yet rarely infected under natural conditions [41]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that although rabbits can support viral replication, infections typically remain asymptomatic [41], suggesting that while infection is biologically possible, it seldom occurs or persists under household conditions. In our study, none of the pet rabbit sera tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies (0/41), which is consistent with our earlier findings where all 29 rabbits sampled between June 2020 and February 2021 were also seronegative [34]. These results suggest that the persistent absence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in pet rabbits in Poland, even years after the emergence of multiple viral variants, indicates that this species has played a negligible role in the epidemiology of SARS-CoV-2. Our findings contrast with a study from France, which reported the first evidence of natural SARS-CoV-2 infection among 144 pet rabbits, with a very low observed seroprevalence ranging between 0.7% and 1.4% [71]. The authors suggested that the low seroprevalence among tested rabbits, compared to the seroprevalence among cats and dogs in other studies, is related to the lower contact intensity between rabbits and their owners, which could explain the reduced transmission risk. These data indicate that while rabbits can be experimentally infected and rarely acquire infection naturally, their contribution to human-to-animal, animal-to-animal, or animal-to-human transmission chains is likely minimal.

Our study contributes to the growing evidence that companion animals can be infected with SARS-CoV-2. Although no statistically significant year-to-year differences were detected, descriptive trends suggest an increase in exposure during later phases of the pandemic. Age emerged as the strongest predictor of seropositivity, with middle-aged animals showing the highest risk. However, this pattern should not be interpreted as antibody accumulation over time, but rather as behavioral and epidemiological factors that increase opportunities for exposure during periods of high viral circulation. Neither gender nor geographic region was associated with infection status. The absence of antibodies in rabbits further suggests their role in SARS-CoV-2 epidemiology under natural conditions is minimal, despite experimental evidence of susceptibility. Taken together, these findings reinforce the concept of pets as sentinels of community SARS-CoV-2 circulation, emphasise the value of temporal surveillance for detecting changes in exposure risk, and underscore the need for harmonised serological monitoring and complementary assays.

The conclusions of our study should, however, have to be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the number of samples collected in 2022 and 2023 was relatively small compared to the numbers in later years. Although the small sample sizes limited the statistical power for these early years, their inclusion still provides valuable context and supports the conclusion that sporadic seropositivity persisted in pets beyond the initial phase of the pandemic. Second, the sampling was not random; instead, it was based on a convenience sample of residual sera from animals attending veterinary clinics that participated in the study. Therefore, the results may not fully represent the entire pet population in each region, although they provide valuable insights into post-pandemic exposure trends among client-owned animals. Another limitation of this study is the exclusive use of an ELISA assay targeting antibodies against the N protein of SARS-CoV-2. As discussed previously, N-based assays may differ in sensitivity and longevity of antibody detection compared to spike- or RBD-based assays [32, 33]. This methodological aspect may have influenced the estimated antibody prevalence, since N-based ELISA tests might not entirely capture the overall serological response. Future studies should therefore combine N- and S/RBD-based serological assays or include neutralization tests (e.g. sVNT, VNT) to more accurately assess both exposure and potential protective immunity in companion animals. Continued monitoring, ideally combining N- and S/RBD-based assays with neutralization tests, will be essential to clarify the long-term role of companion animals in the epidemiology of SARS-CoV-2 and in preparedness for potential future zoonotic events.
