Introduction

Fraud and misconduct in health systems represent complex, multifaceted challenges that undermine sustainability, efficiency and equity in healthcare delivery worldwide.1 2 These phenomena can take diverse forms, including insurance fraud (false claims, overbilling, duplicate reporting), informal payments to providers, procurement and supply chain corruption such as counterfeit or smuggled medicines, and even academic or research misconduct in medical education and practice.3 4 Together, these practices erode public trust, create inefficiencies and distort the allocation of scarce resources.

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the risks of health-related fraud are magnified due to limited resources, fragmented governance and insufficient oversight mechanisms. Global organisations, including the WHO and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), have emphasised the need for multispectral, system-wide reforms that integrate legal, technological and cultural approaches.4 5 Evidence from several LMICs also illustrates that weak institutional safeguards and growing demand for services can encourage systemic abuse.6,8

In Iran, the health system has undergone decades of reform, including the expansion of public health insurance and the adoption of performance-based financing models.9 While these reforms have improved access and coverage, they have also introduced vulnerabilities such as fragmented provider contracting, inconsistent monitoring and weak interagency coordination.10 Fraud in Iran has been reported in multiple forms: unnecessary prescriptions, inflated billing, collusion between providers and patients and manipulation of beneficiary records.11 12 The health insurance system, which covers the majority of the population, has been particularly susceptible to such risks.12

Despite the growing policy relevance of this issue, the Iranian literature on fraud prevention remains limited and fragmented. Existing studies have mostly addressed technical or legal dimensions in isolation, without integrating them into a comprehensive governance framework.12 13 This gap highlights the importance of developing a national, multidimensional strategy that draws on expert knowledge, contextual realities and international experiences.

This study addresses this need by designing and validating a practical framework for fraud prevention in Iran’s health system. Using a sequential mixed-methods approach—qualitative content analysis followed by a modified Delphi consensus process—the study engages experts from health policy, insurance and governance institutions to generate a context-sensitive, stakeholder-informed model for systemic reform.

Method

This study employed a sequential mixed-methods design, consisting of an exploratory qualitative phase followed by a two-round Delphi consensus process.

Phase 1: qualitative study

Participants were selected using purposive and snowball sampling to ensure maximum variation in professional backgrounds. The study included health system experts, senior policymakers, academic scholars and managers from both public and private sectors. Eligibility criteria required a minimum of 5 years’ professional experience in healthcare and familiarity with organisational ethics, service delivery or health governance.

A total of 12 interviews were conducted, achieving saturation based on the depth of discussion and diversity of participant perspectives. Saturation was further supported by triangulation with findings from the subsequent Delphi phase.

Semistructured interviews were conducted using a pretested interview guide (attached as online supplemental file 1). Interviews lasted 40–60 min and were held either in person or online. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and confidentiality was strictly maintained.

The interviews were analysed using framework analysis following the systematic steps proposed by Ritchie and Spencer.14 This approach was selected because it allows both inductive and deductive interpretation of qualitative data while facilitating integration with later policy-oriented frameworks. Initially, meaning units were coded and grouped into categories and themes to summarise the content of each interview. Although principles of qualitative content analysis guided this initial data reduction, the subsequent process adhered to the five stages of framework analysis: (1) familiarisation with the transcripts; (2) identification of a thematic framework based on both interview data and study objectives; (3) indexing and coding of text segments; (4) charting of data into a matrix organised by emerging categories and (5) mapping and interpretation to generate higher-order strategic areas.

The six strategic areas (cultural, educational, legal, technological, insurance-related and structural) were derived by grouping related subthemes within this analytical matrix. These areas were subsequently consolidated into three overarching domains—capacity building, oversight strengthening and modernisation of operational infrastructure.

Phase 2: Delphi consensus

A purposive sample of experts was recruited from health governance, policy, insurance, ethics and law. Eligibility criteria included ≥5 years of professional experience and active involvement in decision-making or oversight. 31 Experts participated in Round 1, of whom 27 completed Round 2. Panellists’ demographics (age, gender, education) are reported in the Results section.

Round 1 items were derived from the qualitative findings. Experts rated items on a 5-point Likert scale and provided open-ended comments. Items with a mean ≥4 and ≥75% agreement advanced to Round 2. In Round 2, participants re-rated items after reviewing group feedback. Consensus was defined as ≥75% of experts rating items 4 or 5.

Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, percentage agreement) were calculated using SPSS. Items below consensus threshold were revised or removed. The process ensured iterative refinement of strategies towards a validated national framework.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUMS.REC.1402.087), covering both the qualitative and Delphi phases. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline16 to draft this manuscript, and the STROBE checklist17 when editing, included in online supplemental file 2.

Linking the two phases to framework analysis

The integration of the two research phases was designed to ensure both depth and rigour in developing a national framework for combating fraud and misconduct in Iran’s health system. In the first phase, framework analysis was applied to explore underlying drivers, mechanisms and contextual factors influencing fraudulent and unethical behaviour, based on the experiences of diverse experts. These findings provided a grounded and context-sensitive basis for generating framework components.

In the second phase, a modified Delphi technique was employed to validate and prioritise these components through structured expert consensus. Panellists reviewed, rated and refined items across two rounds, ensuring convergence towards strategies with the highest feasibility and consensus.

The combination of inductive exploration (Phase 1) and iterative validation (Phase 2) produced a comprehensive, expert-informed framework that reflects both theoretical insight and practical applicability.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. The study focused on expert perspectives related to policy and institutional mechanisms for fraud prevention within the health system.

Phase 1: qualitative findings

A total of 12 experts were interviewed, including managers and auditors from the Social Security Organization (n=7) and the Health Insurance Organization (n=5). Participants had an average of 11 years of professional experience in areas such as insurance claims review, fraud detection and healthcare governance. Interviews reached thematic saturation after 12 sessions. This was achieved not by the number of interviews alone but by the depth and diversity of insights gathered from participants across insurance, governance and audit domains. Further triangulation with the Delphi phase confirmed saturation.

Data were analysed using framework analysis, leading to the identification of six strategic categories: cultural, educational (capacity-building), legal, technological, insurance-related and structural interventions. Each category is presented with representative themes and expert quotations, accompanied by a synthesis of practical recommendations.

For example, experts highlighted the role of cultural interventions in raising public awareness about appropriate insurance use and the necessity of educating providers to avoid unnecessary prescriptions. Educational strategies emphasised capacity-building through training managers and frontline staff in fraud detection techniques. Legal reforms were seen as essential, including clear definitions of fraud, enforceable penalties and consistent sanctioning mechanisms. Technological measures, such as electronic health records (EHRs) and electronic identity verification systems, were repeatedly cited as critical for improving transparency.

Through framework analysis, six strategic categories emerged as the most salient clusters of preventive strategies. During the Delphi phase, experts re-examined these categories and grouped them under three broader domains representing systemic levels of intervention. This hierarchical synthesis from qualitative coding to strategic domains ensured both conceptual grounding and practical structure in the final framework.

Phase 2: Delphi validation

In Round 1, 31 experts rated the proposed strategies. Items with a mean score ≥4 and ≥75% agreement advanced to Round 2. After refinement, 27 experts participated in Round 2, confirming consensus on most strategies.

High levels of agreement were reached on critical measures, including the need for clear legal definitions, enforceable sanctions and electronic identity verification systems (≥90% consensus, with some items reaching 100%). Items that did not achieve consensus, such as ‘setting expenditure ceilings for insurance services’, were excluded.

Quantitative analysis confirmed consistency across rounds, with reliability supported by stable agreement levels. The iterative process also allowed refinement of items such as staff training, which was clarified and retained under the technology category.

The final validated framework consisted of three overarching domains—(1) capacity building, (2) strengthening oversight systems and (3) modernisation of operational infrastructure—operationalised through six strategic pillars. These domains and strategies are summarised in table 3.

Quantitative analysis using SPSS confirmed both the stability of agreement among Delphi panellists and the reliability of their responses across rounds. Strategies were systematically refined based on expert feedback, with less-supported items revised or removed to enhance clarity and relevance. The final validated framework not only reflects a strong alignment between expert consensus and national policy priorities but also provides practical guidance for real-world implementation and offers strategic value for health system reform in Iran and other LMICs.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop a comprehensive and context-specific framework to address fraud and misconduct in Iran’s health system. Drawing on expert perspectives and validated through a two-round Delphi process, the findings revealed that fraud is a complex, multidimensional challenge that requires simultaneous attention to cultural norms, regulatory enforcement and operational systems.

The proposed framework was structured around three overarching domains: (1) capacity building for both providers and beneficiaries, (2) strengthening oversight and regulatory systems and (3) reforming operational infrastructure. Within these domains, six strategic pillars were identified, encompassing cultural interventions, educational initiatives, regulatory and insurance reforms, technological upgrades and structural realignment.

This layered structure reflects an important insight: fraud in health systems cannot be addressed solely through punitive mechanisms or technological tools. Rather, it requires a systemic approach that incorporates both behavioural change and institutional reform.5 6 The strategies that received the highest consensus—such as the need for clear legal definitions, consistent sanctions for misconduct and electronic identity verification—highlight the dual importance of enforceable policy and digital infrastructure.

Our findings are aligned with global evidence indicating that effective fraud prevention frameworks must address both individual behaviours and systemic vulnerabilities.6 18 The WHO and the OECD have emphasised the importance of comprehensive strategies that integrate legal action, public education and data-driven monitoring.19 Similarly, studies from countries such as Brazil, Turkey and South Korea have demonstrated that anti-fraud efforts are most effective when supported by regulatory clarity, professional training and technological surveillance systems.2 19 20

Notably, our framework adds to the literature by emphasising the role of cultural and educational interventions as foundational—not peripheral—components of fraud prevention. While many frameworks prioritise reactive detection and sanctioning mechanisms, the Iranian experts participating in this study highlighted the necessity of proactive measures, such as ethical education for healthcare professionals and increased public awareness about the proper use of health insurance.

This preventive orientation aligns with system-strengthening approaches advocated in global health governance and offers a potentially more sustainable model for resource-constrained settings.

The framework developed in this study provides actionable directions for national health authorities, insurance organisations and health policy planners in Iran. It emphasises a proactive approach to fraud prevention, integrating educational, regulatory and technological strategies in a way that is both responsive to current system gaps and aligned with international best practices.

Firstly, the cultural and educational dimensions highlight the need for a shift in societal and professional attitudes towards insurance misuse. Public awareness campaigns, integrated into health education curricula, can foster a sense of ownership and responsibility among beneficiaries. Simultaneously, ethical education for physicians and healthcare providers should be institutionalised through continuing medical education and professional development programmes.

Secondly, the legal and regulatory strategies underscore the urgent need for clear, enforceable anti-fraud policies. Existing legal ambiguities and fragmented enforcement mechanisms weaken deterrence. Implementing unified guidelines, defining misconduct categories and standardising sanctions across institutions will be critical.

Thirdly, the findings underscore the transformative potential of technology in fraud detection. EHRs, real-time data analytics and biometric identity systems can greatly enhance transparency and accountability. However, successful implementation requires investment in infrastructure, user training and inter-agency data-sharing protocols.

Lastly, structural reforms—particularly regarding tariff-setting, performance monitoring and inter-agency collaboration—can reduce systemic loopholes that enable fraud. These reforms must be driven by strong political will and supported by evidence-based policy design.

These measures, if implemented in a coordinated manner, can substantially reduce resource leakage and improve public trust in the health system. This framework may also be applicable in other LMICs facing similar regulatory and operational challenges, offering a replicable model for health system governance reform.

A major strength of this study lies in its mixed-methods design, combining qualitative exploration with structured expert validation through the Delphi method. This approach enabled both inductive theory-building and deductive consensus generation, increasing the robustness and credibility of the proposed framework.

The inclusion of a diverse panel of experts from various institutional and professional backgrounds enhances the generalisability and practicality of the recommendations. Furthermore, the use of a two-round Delphi process allowed for reflection, revision and agreement building among participants.

However, the study has certain limitations. The Delphi panel was limited to 27 participants, and although diverse, may not fully capture the views of all stakeholders, particularly patients and frontline staff. Additionally, the framework was not pilot-tested in practice, and its feasibility and cost-effectiveness remain to be assessed. The findings are also context-dependent and may require adaptation before application to other national or subnational settings.

Future research should focus on the operationalisation and pilot implementation of the proposed framework in selected regions or health insurance institutions. Real-world testing will allow for the identification of implementation barriers, stakeholder resistance and unintended consequences.

Moreover, quantitative impact assessments—tracking metrics such as claim rejection rates, recovery of fraudulent payments and user satisfaction—can offer valuable insights into effectiveness. Complementary qualitative studies, involving healthcare providers and beneficiaries, can further elucidate perceptions, challenges and contextual adaptations needed for success.

Longitudinal evaluations can also assess sustainability over time and explore the effects of institutional learning and feedback loops. Comparative studies with other LMICs may help refine the model and contribute to a global evidence base on fraud prevention in resource-constrained health systems.

Conclusion

This study developed a multidimensional, expert-validated framework for addressing fraud and misconduct in Iran’s health system. The findings demonstrate that combating fraud requires more than reactive enforcement; it calls for proactive, systemic reforms across educational, regulatory, technological and structural domains.

The proposed framework integrates upstream strategies—such as public and provider education—with downstream mechanisms, including legal sanctions and digital monitoring systems. Its strength lies in its comprehensive design, tailored to the operational realities of Iran’s health infrastructure.

By adopting this framework, policymakers and insurance institutions can enhance transparency, accountability and public trust in health service delivery. Moving from fragmented oversight towards integrated governance is not only feasible but imperative for protecting limited resources and improving equity in healthcare access.

Future implementation and evaluation of this model will be essential to inform evidence-based health system reforms in Iran and other LMICs facing similar challenges.
