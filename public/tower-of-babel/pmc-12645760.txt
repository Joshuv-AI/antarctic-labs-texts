The moderated mediation effect of cognitive flexibility on fear of negative evaluation in the relationship between social comparison orientation and social interaction anxiety among nursing students
Authors: Yon Hee Seo, Kyong Ah Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial comparison orientation, fear of negative evaluation, and social interaction anxiety are key psychological factors that can affect nursing students’ well-being. This study investigates the role of cognitive flexibility as a moderator and mediator in the relationship between social comparison orientation, fear of negative evaluation, and social interaction anxiety.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional observational study. A total of 198 nursing students from two universities in South Korea participated in this study. Data were collected using self-reported questionnaires between November 1 and December 2, 2024. The data were analyzed using SPSS/WIN 26.0, employing independent t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, Pearson’s correlation coefficients, and Hayes’s PROCESS macro methods (Models 4 and 7) for testing moderated mediation.ResultsSocial comparison orientation significantly positively predicted fear of negative evaluation (B = 0.471, p < .001), but had no significant direct effect on social interaction anxiety. Fear of negative evaluation, however, had a significant positive effect on social interaction anxiety (B = 0.682, p < .001). The interaction between social comparison orientation and cognitive flexibility was statistically significant (B = 0.180, p = .045). Furthermore, cognitive flexibility significantly moderated the mediation effect between social comparison orientation and fear of negative evaluation (B = 0.123, 95% CI [0.023–0.246]).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that cognitive flexibility plays a critical role in moderating and mediating the relationships among social comparison orientation, fear of negative evaluation, and social interaction anxiety. These results highlight the importance of enhancing cognitive flexibility to reduce anxiety and improve social interactions in nursing students.Keywords: Cognitive flexibility, Social comparison orientation, Fear of negative evaluation, Social interaction anxiety, Nursing student

Background

Humans often experience anxiety in social situations, especially when they feel unable to meet others’ expectations or are being evaluated. In South Korea, the exam-oriented education system increases the frequency of such evaluative settings, heightening the risk of social anxiety [1]. Medical and nursing students, who balance both theoretical duties and patient-related work, tend to have one of the highest workloads among university students, which leads them to confront evaluation-related fear and anxiety more frequently [2, 3]. Social anxiety refers to the discomfort or distress that arises in actual or imagined social situations where one perceives the presence of evaluative scrutiny by others [4]. It often manifests as a fear of negative evaluation and avoidance of social contexts [5]. This condition is characterized by excessive fear of being negatively evaluated in social situations, which can lead individuals to withdraw from social activities, devalue themselves, and avoid future interactions [6]. Social anxiety is also linked to a social comparison orientation, where individuals frequently compare themselves to others perceived as higher in status [7], and it is particularly common among nursing students [8]. Nursing students must establish professional interactions not only with their peers but also with patients and healthcare professionals from the early stages of their training. Within the hierarchical structure of the clinical environment, they are constantly observed and evaluated by various assessors, such as clinical instructors, nurses, and physicians. This ongoing scrutiny can intensify the fear of negative evaluation, a core component of social anxiety. Additionally, due to the nature of their work, nursing students, who are responsible for patient health, may experience anxiety from the fear of making mistakes that could harm patients [4].

Nursing students are frequently placed in environments where they compare themselves to their peers in terms of academic performance, clinical competence, social skills, and physical appearance, which increases their tendency toward social comparison [9]. The social comparison orientation refers to the cognitive tendency to compare one’s abilities with those of others [9]. In other words, it is the unconscious tendency to frequently compare oneself to others [10]. According to social comparison theory, humans are inherently social beings, and there is an internal drive—referred to by psychologist Festinger as the ‘inner drive’—that compels individuals to compare themselves to others [11]. Especially upward comparisons can lead to feelings of inadequacy and trigger fear of negative evaluation, which is common among nursing students who are frequently exposed to hierarchical and performance-based environments. When people find themselves in uncertain situations, this inner drive compels them to make comparisons in various areas, seeking to gain more control over their environment, which often leads to social comparisons. Individuals with a higher social comparison orientation tend to have lower self-esteem, higher levels of depression and neuroticism, and lower levels of happiness [9, 12, 13]. In particular, social comparison orientation can automatically evoke feelings of inferiority when comparing oneself to others [14]. In other words, anxiety related to comparisons with others can create excessively high standards, and failure to meet these standards may lead to strong negative feelings of inferiority. When such conditions persist, individuals may frequently experience negative emotions and even develop maladaptive thought patterns, which in turn can trigger social anxiety [13].

Nursing students are continuously exposed to evaluative situations from professors, clinical supervisors, and peers during both theoretical education and practical training. Watson and Friend [15] emphasized the fear of negative evaluation (FNE) as a core element of social interaction anxiety, stating that higher levels of FNE are associated with increased social interaction anxiety [16]. FNE can lead to lower self-efficacy and social avoidance behaviors, as well as a fear of making mistakes or avoiding questions, which in turn may result in missed learning opportunities and psychological burnout [17]. Therefore, FNE is a critical psychological factor that requires early assessment and intervention in nursing education.

Cognitive flexibility, the ability to manage challenges and generate alternative solutions [18, 19], is crucial for managing interaction anxiety [20], but it is often lower in individuals with high social anxiety [21]. It involves adapting to new environments and responding effectively to social cues [22, 23]. Studies [24, 25] show that cognitive flexibility significantly impacts social anxiety, as socially anxious individuals tend to overfocus on others’ judgments and disconnect from their emotions, impairing their flexibility. In contrast, high cognitive flexibility fosters present-moment awareness, effective coping, and anxiety reduction by enhancing self-awareness and regulating behavior. In nursing education, low cognitive flexibility can hinder adaptation to dynamic clinical settings and complex interpersonal demands, contributing to stress and the fear of negative evaluation (FNE), while high flexibility supports problem-solving, resilience, and better stress management.

Nursing students experience varying levels of stress and anxiety depending on their course level [26]. Second-year students, in particular, are at a critical stage in internalizing their professional identity. Having not yet experienced clinical practice, they face anticipatory stress related to future clinical experiences. They may experience anticipatory anxiety, including uncertainty, fear of negative evaluation, and concerns about their competence, which are triggered by the expectations surrounding clinical practice and assessments. This can lead to social interaction anxiety [8, 27]. Therefore, research is needed to explore these mechanisms before direct clinical stressors affect second-year students, as well as to support early interventions that improve academic achievement, practical skills, and readiness for supervised instruction [8].

While previous studies have examined the relationships among social comparison tendencies, fear of negative evaluation, cognitive flexibility, and social interaction anxiety, few have analyzed these variables together or explored the moderating and mediating roles of cognitive flexibility. Existing studies often focus on general college students or examine only specific variables, neglecting the evaluation-centered environment and psychological stress unique to nursing students. Additionally, the question of whether cognitive flexibility can simultaneously play both a mediating and moderating role remains underexplored. This issue is particularly important in the Korean nursing education context, where an evaluation culture is strongly embedded. Focusing on second-year students is particularly valuable, as it allows us to identify cognitive and emotional mechanisms before full clinical stressors emerge, providing crucial insights for developing early intervention strategies.

Therefore, this study constructed a moderated mediation hypothesis model to address three main research questions based on existing literature: (1) Does social comparison orientation directly affect social interaction anxiety in nursing students? (2) Does FNE mediate the relationship between social comparison orientation and social interaction anxiety in nursing students? (3) Does cognitive flexibility moderate this mediation model? Fig. 1 illustrates the research hypotheses. The aim of this model is to confirm the moderated mediation effect of cognitive flexibility on the relationships between nursing students’ social comparison orientation, FNE, and social interaction anxiety.

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional observational research design and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [28]. The study model was developed based on Hayes [29] PROCESS macro models 4 and 7, with the corresponding analytical approaches as follows: Model 4 (Mediation analysis): Applied to test whether a mediator variable explains the relationship between the independent and dependent variables. Model 7 (Moderated mediation analysis): Utilized to evaluate whether the path from the independent variable to the mediator is moderated by another variable, thereby determining if the mediation effect differs across levels of the moderator. This approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between variables.

Participants

Participants were recruited from two universities located in two regions of South Korea using convenience sampling. Participants were second-year nursing students from Gwangju Metropolitan City and Gyeongbuk, South Korea. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) current enrollment as a second-year nursing student, (2) age 18 years or older and (3) provision of informed consent to participate in the study. Second-year students were chosen because they represent a transitional stage before clinical practicum, where anticipatory anxiety is most pronounced. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete survey responses, (2) withdrawal from the study at any time. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty and that their decision would not affect their academic standing or their relationship with the researchers. Data were collected from November 1 to December 2, 2024. The sample size for this study was calculated using the G*Power 3.1 program with a significance level of ⍺ = 0.05, a power of 1-β = 0.95, a medium effect size of 0.15, and 9 predictors for the overall measurement variables in the regression analysis. The minimum required sample size was 166; considering the dropout rate, 200 responses were collected, with 198 valid responses included in the final analysis.

Measurements

Social comparison orientation

Social comparison orientation was measured using the Iowa-Netherlands Comparison Orientation Measure (INCOM) developed by Gibbons and Buunk [9]. This tool consists of 11 items across two subscales: six items related to ability and five items related to opinion. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a greater tendency for social comparison. As per Gibbons and Buunk [9], the tool’s reliability had a Cronbach’s α of 0.83, while in this study, it was 0.70.

Fear of negative evaluation (FNE)

FNE measures the fear of negative evaluations from others using the fear of negative evaluation scale brief (BFNE) developed by Watson and Friend [15], revised by Leary [30], and adapted by Lee and Choi [31]. This tool consists of 12 items, with responses recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree); higher scores indicate greater FNE. The reliability of the tool in Lee and Choi’s [31] study had a Cronbach’s α of 0.90, whereas in this study, it was 0.73.

Social interaction anxiety

The Social Interaction Anxiety Scale was developed by Mattick and Clarke [32] to measure the level of anxiety experienced in situations that require social interaction during early adulthood and was adapted by Kim [33]. This scale comprises 19 items, with responses recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (always agree); higher scores indicate higher levels of anxiety in social interaction situations. In Kim’s [33] study, Cronbach’s α was 0.90, and in the present study, it was 0.86.

Cognitive flexibility

The Cognitive Flexibility Scale was developed by Dennis and Vander Wal [18] and adapted by Heo [34]. It consists of 19 items across two subscales: the Alternative Subscale with 11 items and the Control Subscale with 8 items. Responses are recorded a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (completely agree), with higher scores indicating higher cognitive flexibility. In Heo [34] study, Cronbach’s α was 0.86, and in the present study, it was 0.78.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gwangju University (IRB No. 202406–HR–006 − 01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed of the study’s purpose and procedures. They were assured that refusal to participate would not result in any negative consequences. Surveys were completed anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was collected.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 26.0 and SPSS. The participants’ general characteristics were analyzed using frequency analysis and descriptive statistics. Differences in the study variables based on general characteristics were analyzed using independent t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and post-hoc analysis using the Scheffé test. Correlations between variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. To test the moderated mediation effect of cognitive flexibility on the relationship between social comparison orientation, FNE, and social interaction anxiety, SPSS PROCESS Macro models 4 and 7 proposed by Hayes [29] were used, with 5,000 bootstrap samples.

Regarding economic status, 123 participants (62.1%) reported average status; 43 (21.7%) reported good status, and 32 (16.2%) reported poor economic status. In terms of academic performance, 57 participants (28.8%) had grades of A or higher; 107 (54.0%) had grades B + and B, and 34 (17.2%) had grades of C or lower. The analysis of differences in social comparison orientation, FNE, social interaction anxiety, and cognitive flexibility based on the participants’ general characteristics revealed that cognitive flexibility was significantly higher in those with grades A or higher than in those with grades C or lower (F = 5.578, p = .004; Table 1).

Mediating effect of fear of negative evaluation in the relationship between social comparison orientation and social interaction anxiety

The mediating effect of fear of negative evaluation on the influence of social comparison tendency on social interaction anxiety was analyzed using PROCESS macro model 4 [29]. Social comparison tendency was found to have a statistically significant positive effect on both social interaction anxiety (B = 0.286, p < .001) and fear of negative evaluation (B = 0.471, p <. 001). In the next step, fear of negative evaluation was entered as a mediator. As a result, social comparison tendency did not have a significant direct effect on social interaction anxiety, but fear of negative evaluation emerged as a predictor of social interaction anxiety (B = 0.682, p < .001). After adding fear of negative evaluation to the equation, the explanatory power of social interaction anxiety increased from 5.6% to 30.5%. Using bootstrapping analysis, we confirmed the indirect effect of fear of negative evaluation on the influence of social comparison tendency on social interaction anxiety. The result was found to be statistically significant because the 95% confidence interval of the indirect effect did not include 0 (B = 0.321, CI [0.213–0.440]) (Table 4).

Moderated mediation effect of cognitive flexibility on fear of negative evaluation in the relationship between social comparison orientation and social interaction anxiety

The moderating effect of cognitive flexibility on the influence of social comparison orientation on fear of negative evaluation was analyzed using PROCESS macro model 7 [29]. Cognitive flexibility was found to moderate the relationship between social comparison orientation and fear of negative evaluation (B = 0.180, p = .045). This suggests that the influence of social comparison orientation on fear of negative evaluation varies depending on an individual’s level of cognitive flexibility. Given the significant interaction term, a specific value selection method was used to explore the conditional effect of cognitive flexibility. Examining the relationship between social comparison tendency and fear of negative evaluation across three levels of cognitive flexibility (-1SD, Mean, +1SD), the indirect effect of social comparison tendency on social interaction anxiety was B = 0.217 (95% CI = 0.098 − 0.344) at low cognitive flexibility (-1SD). At average cognitive flexibility, the indirect effect was B = 0.288 (95% CI = 0.173 − 0.416). At high cognitive flexibility (+ 1SD), the indirect effect increased to B = 0.354 (95% CI = 0.213-0.511) (see Fig. 2). In other words, the higher the cognitive flexibility, the stronger the indirect effect of social comparison tendency on social interaction anxiety via fear of negative evaluation.

Moderating effect diagram of cognitive flexibility

Finally, the significance of the moderated mediation effect was confirmed, confirming that cognitive flexibility served as a significant moderator in the mediating path (B = 0.123, 95% CI = 0.023-0.246). This means that cognitive flexibility moderates the mediating effect of fear of negative evaluation in the process in which social comparison tendency influences social interaction anxiety (Table 5).

Discussion

This study examined the moderated mediation effect of cognitive flexibility on the relationships between social comparison orientation, fear of negative evaluation (FNE), and social interaction anxiety in nursing students. These results are consistent with previous research indicating that individuals with strong comparison tendencies are more sensitive to external evaluations and therefore more vulnerable to social anxiety symptoms [1, 7, 13]. Individuals with high social comparison orientation frequently interpret ambiguous situations with cognitive bias, anticipating negative judgments even in the absence of evidence [1, 15]. Such tendencies manifest as FNE, a core cognitive mechanism underlying social interaction anxiety [35]. Nursing students with strong comparison tendencies may therefore internalize negative evaluations more deeply, resulting in heightened interpersonal anxiety. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions to reduce cognitive distortions, including cognitive reappraisal, minimizing focus on negative feedback, and fostering objective self-reflection.

Notably, this study confirmed the moderating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between social comparison orientation and FNE, consistent with prior literature [20]. Specifically, the strength of the relationship between social comparison orientation and FNE differed based on levels of cognitive flexibility (see Table 5; Fig. 2). The strength of this relationship was greatest when cognitive flexibility was low, suggesting that individuals with less adaptive thinking are particularly vulnerable. However, the indirect effects of social comparison orientation on social interaction anxiety via FNE remained statistically significant across all levels of cognitive flexibility (B = 0.217 at − 1SD, B = 0.288 at mean, and B = 0.354 at + 1SD), indicating that comparison-based thinking continues to exert influence even when cognitive flexibility is moderate or high. This pattern suggests that while cognitive flexibility buffers the intensity of evaluative stress, it does not fully eliminate the impact of comparison-based processing.

Regarding the scope of cognitive flexibility’s moderating effect, the current model revealed a significant moderation only between social comparison orientation and FNE (see Table 4; Fig. 2).In contrast, the direct effects of social comparison on social interaction anxiety and FNE on social interaction anxiety were non-significant. These findings raise the question of whether cognitive flexibility functions purely as a moderator or as a quasi-moderator in this context. The sustained predictive power of social comparison orientation, even at moderate and high levels of cognitive flexibility, suggests that comparison-based thinking exerts a pervasive influence. This is especially relevant in competitive academic settings, such as nursing programs, where frequent exposure to social comparison may contribute to FNE irrespective of an individual’s cognitive flexibility. Therefore, these findings underscore the need for further research into the specific social comparison contexts and evaluative perceptions encountered by nursing students, as well as their psychological responses.

First, the link between FNE and social interaction anxiety is well-established, as FNE is a core cognitive mechanism underlying social anxiety symptoms [15, 16]. FNE represents a crystallized fear of negative evaluation. Once activated, it may influence social interaction anxiety automatically, independent of an individual’s cognitive flexibility. This interpretation aligns with previous findings identifying FNE as a strong predictor of social avoidance regardless of coping style [1].

Second, the direct path from social comparison orientation to social interaction anxiety may involve affective and behavioral components, such as learned avoidance or low self-esteem, which are less influenced by cognitive flexibility alone. Consequently, cognitive flexibility appears to be most effective at the cognitive interpretation stage—specifically, between social comparison and internalized evaluation (FNE)—where reinterpretation and reappraisal processes are most active.

These findings refine the theoretical understanding of cognitive flexibility by highlighting its differential impact depending on the cognitive level of anxiety processes. Cognitive flexibility can reshape interpretations of social comparisons, thereby reducing FNE. However, once FNE becomes a stable emotional response pattern and manifests behaviorally, cognitive flexibility alone may be insufficient for regulation. This underscores the need for phase-specific interventions: cognitive flexibility training to prevent FNE development and other emotional regulation strategies to manage its consequences.

Lastly, these findings underscore the importance of incorporating psychological training programs in nursing education that enhance cognitive flexibility, including mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, and stress inoculation training. For example, mindfulness-based stress reduction programs have demonstrated improvements in attentional control and present-moment awareness—key components of cognitive flexibility. Research with clinical populations, such as adults with social anxiety disorder, shows that both MBSR and cognitive-behavioral therapies enhance cognitive reappraisal and mindfulness skills. Recent randomized controlled trials have confirmed the effectiveness of these approaches in reducing anxiety symptoms and enhancing emotion regulation [36]. In Korea, Choi and Kim [37] found that a mindfulness-based cognitive therapy program effectively reduced stress among nursing students. Similarly, cognitive reappraisal techniques—training individuals to reinterpret stressful social experiences—facilitate more adaptive meaning-making and reduce fear-based evaluations, mechanisms critical in social anxiety reduction. Complementing this, studies involving nursing students corroborate the applicability of such interventions in nursing education to help students cope more adaptively with evaluative stress, ultimately reducing the risk of chronic anxiety symptoms in clinical settings [38]. Therefore, educational programs targeting these cognitive-emotional regulation skills hold promise for enhancing nursing students’ psychological resilience and clinical performance.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; future longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporal dynamics among social comparison orientation, FNE, social interaction anxiety, and cognitive flexibility. Second, reliance on self-report measures may have introduced response biases. Incorporating mixed-method approaches, including qualitative interviews or behavioral observations, could provide deeper insights. Finally, as this study focused on Korean nursing students, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to other cultural or educational contexts.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that FNE has a mediating effect on the relationship between social comparison orientation and social interaction anxiety and that cognitive flexibility plays a role in moderating this mediation effect. These results suggest that while cognitive flexibility may buffer the vulnerability associated with comparison-based thinking, it cannot fully offset the negative impact of FNE. Accordingly, psychological interventions in nursing education should address both dimensions: enhancing students’ cognitive flexibility and reducing the frequency and intensity of negative social comparisons. To advance this line of research, longitudinal and cross-cultural studies are needed to clarify the temporal and contextual dynamics of these relationships. In addition, integrating evidence-based programs such as mindfulness training or cognitive reappraisal techniques into nursing curricula holds promise for improving students’ resilience, interpersonal confidence, and ultimately their clinical performance.
