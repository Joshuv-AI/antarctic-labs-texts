Effect of erector spinae block on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing lung surgical procedures: a systematic review and trial sequential analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Vitaliy Voznyy, Mouad Elganga, Leya Tawfik, Salem Abu Al-Burak, Yamini Subramani, Paymon Azizi, Lee-Anne Fochesato, Pavel S. Roshanov, Rahul Nayak, Richard Malthaner, Mahesh Nagappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung surgery is often accompanied by significant pain, leading to high opioid use and related side effects. This systematic review and meta-analysis examine the effect of the erector spinae plane block (ESPB) in reducing postoperative pain, opioid consumption, and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) after lung surgery.MethodsA systematic database search was conducted to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ESPB with a control in patients undergoing lung surgical procedures. The primary outcome was the early (6 h), intermediate (12 h), and late (24 h) pain scores at rest and during movement. The secondary outcomes were 24 h postoperative opioid consumption and postoperative nausea and vomiting. Mean differences (MD) and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using a random-effects model.Results20 RCTs with 1,391 patients were included in the meta-analysis (ESPB vs. control: 668 vs. 723). ESPB significantly reduced resting pain at 6 h (MD: 1.26; 95% CI: 0.78–1.75; p < 0.00001), 12 h (MD: 1.22; 95% CI: 0.41–2.03; p = 0.003), and 24 h (MD: 0.61; 95% CI: 0.33–0.90; p < 0.0001). Similarly, movement-evoked pain was reduced at 6 h (MD: 1.54; 95% CI: 0.71–2.37; p < 0.0001), 12 h (MD: 1.32; 95% CI: 0.88–1.76; p < 0.00001), and 24 h (MD: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.36–1.46; p = 0.001). Ten RCTs showed reduced 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption (MD: 16.74; 95% CI: 7.28–26.19; p = 0.0005) and decreased PONV incidence (OR: 0.32; 95% CI: 0.20–0.51; p < 0.001) in the ESPB group. Although statistically significant, the observed reductions in pain scores correspond to modest absolute differences of approximately 1–1.5 points on a 10-point scale in the early postoperative period and less than 1 point at 24 h. The certainty of evidence across all outcomes was low, primarily due to substantial clinical heterogeneity and variability in study protocols.ConclusionESPB appears to provide modest but consistent improvements in postoperative pain control, opioid consumption, and PONV after lung surgery. However, given the low certainty of evidence (GRADE) and substantial between-study heterogeneity, these findings support ESPB as an adjunct rather than a definitive regional analgesic strategy for lung surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03927-4.

Introduction

Lung resections performed through open thoracotomy or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) are known to cause significant postoperative pain [1, 2]. Insufficient pain management after lung surgery can substantially hinder recovery and quality of life, raising the risk of pulmonary complications, extending hospital stays, and leading to chronic pain and post-thoracotomy pain syndrome [3–5]. Adequate control of postoperative pain with multimodal analgesia is therefore essential for the proper rehabilitation of these patients, as highlighted by Enhanced Recovery After Surgery guidelines [6].

Historically, postoperative pain management in lung surgery has depended on systemic opioids and thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA). However, because of the well-known dose-limiting side effects of opioids, especially respiratory depression in this group, there has been a recent move towards multimodal anesthesia to help decrease perioperative opioid consumption [7]. While TEA remains the preferred standard for thoracic surgery pain control, its risk of serious neurological, cardiovascular, and infectious complications has led to the search for safer regional techniques [8]. One of the newer options is the erector spinae block (ESPB).

The ESPB is an ultrasound-guided interfascial block where local anesthetic is injected deep to the erector spinae muscle, just superficial to the transverse processes of the vertebrae. By targeting this plane, usually at the T4–T6 levels for thoracic surgery, the injectate can spread cranio-caudally and ventrally to affect the nearby nerve roots, effectively blocking sensory input from the anterior, posterior, and lateral aspects of the thoracic and abdominal walls [9].

The ESPB has attracted significant attention in anesthesiology because of its simplicity and safety profile. Recently, more clinical trials have explored the effectiveness of ESPB for postoperative pain management in lung surgery. The emerging evidence suggests that ESPB can significantly improve postoperative pain relief in lung surgery; however, there is considerable variation in sample sizes, study designs, and reported outcomes [10]. Despite increasing adoption of ESPB in thoracic anesthesia, uncertainty remains regarding the magnitude, consistency, and clinical relevance of its analgesic effects in lung surgery, particularly given variations in block technique, comparator strategies, and perioperative analgesic regimens. Previous meta-analyses have either included mixed surgical populations or failed to explore study-level factors that may influence outcomes. Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression of randomized controlled trials to clarify the effectiveness of ESPB in lung surgery and to explore factors contributing to between-study variability.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-regression protocol was registered with the International Registry of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251103707) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11].

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed to identify RCTs assessing the effect of ESPB on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing lung surgery. The search strategy was developed by a member of the clinical research team (SA) with input from other members. Systematic searches were conducted across the following databases: EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) (via OVID), MEDLINE, and Web of Science, from their start date to September 2025. Full-text articles were collected after an initial pilot search to identify relevant keywords. The Yale MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) analyzer helped select suitable controlled vocabulary terms [12]. The search strategy included synonyms and controlled vocabularies, such as MeSH in Medline and EMTree in Embase, along with free-text keywords and alternative spellings. Search terms included: “erector spinae plane block,” “lung surgery,” “thoracic surgery,” “lobectomy,” “wedge resection,” “segmentectomy,” “video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS),” “postoperative outcomes,” “postoperative analgesia,” “pain scores,” “postoperative opioid consumption,” and “postoperative complications.” Each database had a tailored search strategy with relevant thesaurus terms and fields, adjusted as needed. Searches were limited to studies published in English, regardless of country. All retrieved records were imported into Covidence, a systematic review software, for screening and data management. To reduce the chance of missing relevant publications, the reference lists of included articles were checked. Additionally, a citation search of key studies was performed to ensure all pertinent articles were captured. The search strategy is detailed in Supplementary File S1 and the study protocol in Supplementary File 2.

Study selection

RCTs involving adult (≥ 18 years old) patients undergoing lung surgery were included, provided that at least one group received unilateral ESPB. Included studies compared patients who received ESPB with those who received a control. Control groups varied across included trials and consisted primarily of sham blocks or no regional analgesia, with one study employing an active comparator. This heterogeneity reflects contemporary clinical practice but may bias pooled estimates toward benefit and was therefore explored through subgroup, sensitivity, and random-effects analyses. All included studies reported at least one of the following postoperative outcomes: resting or coughing pain scores, opioid consumption, or incidence of PONV. Studies involving alternative surgical populations or pediatric patients (< 18 years old) were excluded. Editorials, case reports, commentaries, reviews, abstracts without full-text access, and prospective or retrospective cohort studies were also excluded. Four independent reviewers (VV, ME, LT, SA) conducted duplicate title and abstract screening in Covidence. Full-text articles were subsequently retrieved and assessed in duplicate by two reviewers (VV, ME) against predefined inclusion criteria, with reasons for exclusion documented. Disagreements were first resolved by consensus between the two reviewers; if consensus could not be reached, the third reviewer (MN) resolved the conflict.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (VV, LT) extracted data in duplicate using a standardised Microsoft Excel form. The form recorded study-level characteristics, including study ID, publication year, study title, country, sample size per group (ESPB vs. Control), type of surgery, and patient demographics (age, gender distribution, and body mass index [BMI]). Details of the block included the block level, type of local anesthetic, use of adjuvants, and the concentration and volume administered. Baseline variables included surgical duration, anesthesia time, and intraoperative opioid use. Outcomes assessed were pain scores at rest and during coughing/movement (summarized as movement pain scores), 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption, nerve block–related complications, and PONV. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MN).

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent authors (VV, LT) assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias of the included RCTs using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [13]. Six domains were evaluated: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, selection of reported results, and overall risk of bias. For each study, individual domains were rated and classified as low, some concerns, or high risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined by the highest concern level across the six domains.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous data, such as age, BMI, pain scores, and postoperative opioid consumption, were reported as means with standard deviations. In contrast, categorical data, such as sample size, sex, and PONV, were presented as frequencies and percentages. Pain intensity was measured using either the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) or the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), with all values standardized to the NRS. Postoperative opioid consumption from the included studies was converted to oral morphine milligram equivalents (MME) to facilitate pooled analysis. When studies reported opioid use across different agents and routes (such as IV, IM, and oral), all doses were converted to oral MME using established equianalgesic conversion factors, using the Opioid Calculator (supplementary file on MME conversion) [14]. When outcomes were not reported in tables, values were extracted from figures using an online web plot-digitizer (available at https://plotdigitizer.com/app). For data reported as medians with ranges or interquartile ranges, means and standard deviations were estimated using established formulas described by Wan et al. [15].

Meta-analysis: summary measure

For each study, the MD was calculated and subsequently combined using a frequentist random-effects framework. Continuous outcomes were summarized as weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% CIs, and dichotomous outcomes were reported as ORs with corresponding 95% CIs. To account for between-study variability, a random-effects inverse-variance model was used for continuous data. In contrast, the Mantel–Haenszel method was applied to dichotomous data, thereby broadening the generalizability of the pooled results. For sparse or zero-event data, a continuity correction of 1 was applied to all cells before pooling. Further, a Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to examine the robustness of the findings within a probabilistic framework and to estimate credible intervals for the pooled effect. We statistically summarized the pooled estimates using Bayesian random–effects models via Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) simulations with non-informative prior distributions and Gibbs sampling for the mean and variance parameters, with 95% Credible Intervals (CrI) for all outcomes. Predictive intervals were generated for each outcome to reflect expected ranges in future studies. Heterogeneity was assessed and quantified using τ² and I² statistics, with further exploration at the individual-study level. Potential publication bias for the primary outcome (resting pain score at 6 h) was evaluated through funnel plot inspection and Begg’s, Egger’s, and fail-safe N tests. All analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) Version 5.4 (The Nordic Cochrane Center, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, 2020) and R statistical software. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses were conducted to assess how different variables influenced the pooled effect for the primary outcome (resting pain score at 6 h). Resting pain score at 6 h postoperatively was selected as the dependent variable for meta-regression analyses because it was the most consistently reported primary endpoint across studies and reflects early postoperative analgesic efficacy. Meta-regression was performed for baseline confounders treated as continuous variables (e.g., local anesthetic concentration and volume) and for predefined subgroups treated as categorical variables (e.g., local anesthetic type [ropivacaine: yes vs. no], adjuvant use [yes vs. no], and level of block T5 [yes vs. no]). A leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the stability of the pooled estimates by sequentially removing each study.

Trial sequential analysis (TSA)

The methodology of the Copenhagen Trial Unit was employed to conduct the Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) and to evaluate the potential Type I error risk for the primary outcome, specifically the resting pain score at 6 h. The TSA software applied the O’Brien-Fleming approach to calculate the alpha-spending function and to establish group-sequential boundaries (also known as threshold bounds or Z boundaries) for assessing the impact of ESPB relative to no ESPB on pain scores. When each RCT is incorporated into the analysis in chronological order, these boundaries are utilized to determine the statistical significance of the observed effect. The Required Information Size (RIS), which approximates the necessary sample size under the assumption that the effect curve does not cross any boundaries, was also calculated. The Type I and Type II error rates were set at 5% and 20%, respectively. The minimum clinically important mean difference (MCID) in TSA was set at approximately 1 point on the 0–10 NRS, consistent with the thoracic postoperative pain literature [16]. Analyses were conducted using TSA viewer software version 0.9.5.5 beta.

GRADE appraisal of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each postoperative outcome in this meta-analysis was assessed according to the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) working group’s guidance. Certainty was evaluated across five domains: risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. Evidence was downgraded by one level for serious concerns in any domain and by two levels for very serious concerns. The table summarizing the GRADE assessment was created using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool [17].

Results

The database search identified 3,283 records. After removing duplicates, 2,415 unique articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 2,366 were excluded for failing to meet the inclusion criteria, leaving 49 studies eligible for full-text assessment. Seventeen studies were subsequently excluded for reasons such as ineligible study design, conference abstracts, non-English language, unpublished status, inappropriate patient population, or use of non-lung procedures as the intervention. Another 12 studies were excluded due to the lack of an appropriate control group. These studies used the ESPB to assess the impact of adjuvants in the block, the use of single- versus bi-level ESPB, or the effect of different volumes and concentrations in the block. Ultimately, 20 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis [18–37]. Baseline characteristics of included studies

Tables 1 and 2 summarize baseline patient and study characteristics. This study included 20 prospective RCTs comprising 1,391 participants—723 in the control group and 668 in the ESPB group—for the meta-analysis. Patients in the ESPB group had a mean age of 54.3 ± 7.5 years, a mean BMI of 25.1 ± 9.6 kg/m², and were 52.3% male, while those in the control group had a mean age of 54.1 ± 7.3 years, a mean BMI of 25.5 ± 9.7 kg/m² and were 52.6% male. The mean duration of surgery was similar between groups (143.2 ± 57.8 min in the ESPB group vs. 143.9 ± 57.7 min in the control group) [18–21, 23, 24 ,26, 27, 29, 31–37], as was the mean anesthesia time (146.8 ± 36.5 min vs. 145.7 ± 38.0 min, respectively) [18, 19, 21, 22, 24, 27, 28, 37]. One study did not report BMI, [32] four studies did not report surgery time, [22, 25, 28, 30] and 12 studies did not report anesthesia time [20, 23, 25, 26, 29–36]. Nineteen studies compared ESPB to a passive control (sham block), [18–30, 32–37] while one compared ESPB with an active control (No sham block) [31]. These studies, published between 2019 and 2025, were conducted across multiple countries, including China (n = 9),[18–21, 23, 24, 28, 31, 35]. Turkey (n = 5), [27, 30, 34, 36, 37] Canada (n = 2), [22, 25] Korea (n = 1), [26] India (n = 1), [29] Egypt (n = 1),[33] and Brazil (n = 1) [32]. The most frequently performed surgical procedure was lobectomy (n = 16), [18–21, 23 ,25–28, 30–35, 37] followed by wedge resection (n = 8) [22, 25, 27, 28, 31, 34, 35, 37] and segmentectomy (n = 8) [21, 23, 25, 28, 31–33, 35]. Some studies included multiple lung procedures [25, 34, 36, 37]. Eighteen studies used a VATS approach, [18–35] while two used a thoracotomy approach [36, 37]. All these studies performed a preoperative, ultrasound-guided, unilateral erector spinae block [18–37]. Eighteen studies performed the erector spinae block at the T5 level, [18–23, 25–31, 33–37] while two studies performed it at the T4 level [24, 32]. Ten studies used ropivacaine, [18–20, 22–24, 26, 28, 35] nine studies used bupivacaine [25, 27, 29, 30, 32–34, 36, 37], and one study used lidocaine [21]. Sixteen studies used no adjuvants, [18–20, 23, 24, 26–28, 30–37] while four used epinephrine as an adjuvant with local anesthetics during the erector spinae block [21, 22, 25, 29]. The most common local anesthetic concentration was 0.5% (n = 10), [18, 19, 22–26, 30, 33, 34] then 0.25% (n = 5), [27, 29, 32, 36, 37] 0.375% (n = 2), [20, 31] 1% (n = 1), [21] 0.4% (n = 1), [28] and 0.1875% (n = 1) [18–37]. Reported local anesthetic volumes were 20 mL (n = 10), [19, 24, 27, 30–34, 36, 37] 25 mL (n = 4), [21, 23, 26, 28] 30 mL (n = 3), [18, 20, 25, 35] 15 mL (n = 1), [29] and one study used both 20 (< 50 kg) and 30 mL (> 50 kg), depending on participant weight [22]. Postoperative early pain scores

Resting pain scores at 6 hours

Seventeen studies (n = 1,170) reported resting pain scores at 6 h [18–21, 23, 24, 26–30, 32–37]. Pooled analysis showed a significantly lower mean pain score in the ESPB group (n = 579; 2.0 ± 0.81) compared to controls (n = 591; 3.28 ± 1.14), with a mean difference of 1.26 (95% CI: 0.78–1.75; p < 0.00001; I² = 97%; Bayesian estimate: 1.27; 95% CrI: 0.68–1.86; predictive interval: -0.91 to 3.43; certainty of evidence: Low) (Fig. 2A). Although effect sizes varied widely across studies, the consistent trend favored ESPB, suggesting a reproducible analgesic advantage despite significant heterogeneity. The high heterogeneity observed across outcomes (with I² often exceeding 90%) was expected due to variations in block technique (such as block level), local anesthetic (type, concentration, and volume), surgical method (thoracotomy versus VATS), comparator groups (sham, no block, or active control), and background multimodal analgesic protocols. When two studies on the thoracotomy approach [36, 37] were removed and re-analyzed for the VATS procedure, the results remained consistent, showing no significant change (15 studies; ESPB vs. Control: 506 vs. 518; MD: 1.33; 95% CI: 0.75–1.9; p < 0.00001; I2: 98%) (not shown in the figure). Visual inspection of the funnel plot, Begg’s test (p = 0.109), Egger’s test (p = 0.096), and the fail-safe N-test (3762) were not significant, thus ruling out publication bias (Supplementary file S4).

Movement pain scores at 6 hours

Ten studies (n = 691) reported pain scores during movement at 6 h [18–21, 23, 27, 33–35, 37]. Pooled analysis showed a significantly lower mean pain score in the ESPB group (n = 343; 3.13 ± 0.88) compared to controls (n = 348; 4.68 ± 1.09), with a MD of 1.54 (95% CI: 0.71–2.37; p < 0.001; I2 = 98%; Bayesian estimate: 1.54; 95% CrI: 0.71–2.37; predictive interval: -1.62 to 4.70; certainty of evidence: Low) (Fig. 2B). When one study on the thoracotomy approach [37] was removed and re-analyzed for the VATS procedure, the results remained consistent, showing no significant change (9 studies; ESPB vs. Control: 324 vs. 329; MD: 1.6; 95% CI: 0.72–2.47; p = 0.0004; I2: 98%) (not shown in the figure). Egger’s test was significant (p = 0.002). In contrast, the funnel plot and Begg’s test were not significant (p = 0.216), and the fail-safe N-test (1042) was high, indicating the absence of any publication bias (Supplementary file S4).

Postoperative intermediate pain scores

Resting pain scores at 12 hours

Fifteen studies (n = 1,018) reported resting pain scores at 12 h [18–20, 23, 24, 26–30, 32, 33, 35–37]. Pooled analysis showed a significantly lower mean pain score in the ESPB group (n = 503; 1.95 ± 0.73) compared to controls (n = 515; 3.17 ± 1.09), with a MD of 1.22 (95% CI: 0.41–2.03; p = 0.003; I2 = 99%; Bayesian estimate: 1.22; 95% CrI: 0.37–2.07; predictive interval: -2.32 to 4.76; certainty of evidence: Low) (Supplementary file S5). Although Egger’s test suggested potential small-study effects (p = 0.032), the Funnel plot and Begg’s test were not significant (p = 0.379), and the high fail-safe N value (3250) indicates the absence of any publication bias (Supplementary file S4).

Movement pain scores at 12 hours

Eight studies (n = 539) reported pain scores during movement at the 12-hour mark [18–20, 23, 27, 33, 35, 37]. Pooled analysis showed a significantly lower mean pain score in the ESPB group (n = 267; 2.94 ± 0.65) compared to controls (n = 272; 4.28 ± 0.74), with a mean difference of 1.32 (95% CI: 0.88–1.76; p < 0.001; I2 = 88%; Bayesian estimate: 1.33; 95%CrI: 0.70–1.97; predictive interval: -0.04 to 3.56; certainty of evidence: Low) (Supplementary File S5). The funnel plot, Begg’s test (p = 0.061), Egger’s (p = 0.155) tests, and a high fail-safe N-test value of 746, all showed absence of publication bias (Supplementary file S4).

Postoperative late pain score

Resting pain scores at 24 hours

Sixteen studies (n = 1,124) reported resting pain scores at 24 h [18–21, 23, 24, 27–30, 32–37]. Pooled analysis showed a significantly lower pain score in the ESPB group (n = 555; 2.10 ± 1.08) compared to controls (n = 569; 2.72 ± 0.99), with a MD of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.33–0.90; p < 0.001; I2 = 93%; Bayesian estimate: 0.24–0.99; predictive interval: -0.60 to 1.82; certainty of evidence: Low) (Fig. 3A). The funnel plot, Begg’s test (p = 0.096), Egger’s test (p = 0.059), and the fail-safe N-test (944) were not significant, ruling out any evidence of publication bias (Supplementary file S4).

Movement pain scores at 24 hours

Ten studies (n = 691) reported movement pain scores at 24 h [18–21, 23, 27, 33–35, 37]. Pooled analysis showed a significantly lower mean pain score in the ESPB group (n = 343; 2.91 ± 1.10) compared to controls (n = 348; 3.81 ± 0.75), with a MD of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.36–1.46; p = 0.001; I2 = 94%; Bayesian estimate: 0.16–1.66; predictive interval: -1.15 to 2.97; certainty of evidence: Low) (Fig. 3B). The funnel plot showed no publication bias with a non-significant Begg’s (p = 0.291) and Egger’s (p = 0.234) test, as well as a high fail-safe N-test (552) (Supplementary file S4).

Although the pain scores at various time intervals were statistically significant, the observed reductions correspond to modest absolute differences of approximately 1–1.5 points on a 10-point scale in the early postoperative period and less than 1 point at 24 h. Clinically, while pain scores decreased, they may fall below the minimal clinically important difference, especially beyond the first postoperative day. These smaller differences in pain scores, although statistically significant, may have limited clinical impact.

24-hour postoperative opioid consumption

Ten studies (n = 594) reported postoperative opioid consumption at 24 h [22, 25, 27, 29, 30, 32–34, 36, 37]. The 24-hour MME was significantly lower in the ESPB group (n = 297; 29.93 ± 27.42) compared to controls (n = 297; 43.19 ± 29.91), with a mean difference of 16.74 mg (95% CI: 7.28–26.19; p = 0.0005; I2 = 99%; Bayesian estimate: 16.5; 95% CrI: 0.03–33.1; predictive interval: -51.78 to 18.30; certainty of evidence: Low) (Fig. 4) (Supplementary file S4). When two studies on the thoracotomy approach [36, 37] were removed and re-analyzed for the VATS procedure, the results remained consistent, showing no significant change. (8 studies; ESPB vs. Control: 253 vs. 253; MD: 19.9; 95% CI: 7.25–32.56; p = 0.002; I2: 100%) (not shown in the figure). Ciftci et al. 2020 was identified as an outlier [27]. When this study was removed and the pooled estimate recalculated, the final significance did not change (9 studies; ESPB vs. Control: 267 vs. 267; MD: 10.92; 95% CI: 2.05–19.80; p = 0.02; I2: 99%) (not shown in the figure). The funnel plot, Egger’s test, Begg’s Test and fail-safe N-test all showed the absence of publication bias (Supplementary file S4).

Postoperative nausea and vomiting

Sixteen studies (n = 1,107) reported PONV [18–30, 33, 35, 36]. The incidence of PONV was significantly lower in the ESPB group (n = 552; 18.7%) compared to controls (n = 555; 39.5%), with an absolute risk reduction of 21% and a number needed to treat of 4.8 (95% CI: 3.8–6.4). The OR for the ESPB group compared to control was 0.32 (95% CI: 0.20–0.51; p < 0.001; I2 = 53%; Bayesian estimate: 0.30; 95% CrI: 0.17–0.52; predictive interval: 0.06 to 1.51; certainty of evidence: Low) (Supplementary file S6).

Exploratory outcomes

Quality of recovery (QoR)

Only 3 studies reported QoR using the QoR-40 questionnaire, and all 3 compared QoR-40 scores between the ESPB and control groups in the postoperative period [22, 23, 30]. None of the studies reported the delta QoR (Preoperative QoR-40 vs. Postoperative QoR-40). Klaibert et al. [22] reported the QoR-40 questionnaire on day 1 postoperatively. In this study, the QOR-40 was lower in the ESPB group than in the control group [22] Yao et al. [23] administered the QoR-40 questionnaire preoperatively and on postoperative days 1 and 2 [23]. While Ergun et al. [30] reported the QoR-40 questionnaire at hospital discharge [30]. In the latter two studies, the QOR-40 was significantly higher in the ESPB group than in the control group (Supplementary file S7) [23, 30].

Length of hospital stay

4 studies provided data on length of stay [24, 28, 30, 31]. Two studies reported shorter LOS in the ESPB group, [24, 30] while two reported longer LOS than in the control group; [28, 31] however, there was no significant difference between the groups (Supplementary file S7).

Time to rescue analgesia

One study found that time to rescue analgesia was significantly more prolonged in the ESPB group than in the control group (Supplementary file S7) [29].

Cochrane risk of bias assessment

The RoB 2 tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias for each included study. A summary of these evaluations is available in Supplementary file S6. Most studies were rated as “low risk,” except for six studies, which were rated as having “some concerns”, [18, 20, 24, 29, 35, 36] and one study, which was rated as having “high concern” overall [34]. All concerns were related to domain 1 (randomization process) and domain 5 (selection of reported results). Three studies received a rating of “some concerns” in domain one because it was unclear whether the allocation sequence was concealed until patients were enrolled [20, 24, 36]. One study was rated as having “high concern” in domain one due to significant baseline differences between the control and ESPB groups, even though these differences would have occurred by chance [34]. Additionally, all 20 studies were rated as having “some concerns” in domain five due to multiple eligible outcome measures, including different time points for pain scores (Supplementary file S8) [18–37].

Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses of the various subgroups

Meta-regression analysis was conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity for the primary outcome (6-hour resting pain). Meta-regression analyses were conducted across subgroups defined by local anesthetic type ropivacaine (yes [18–20, 23, 24, 26, 28, 35] vs. no [21, 27, 29, 30, 32–34, 36, 37]), adjuvants used (yes [21, 29] vs. no [18–20, 23, 24, 26–28, 30, 32–37]), T5 level of block (yes [18–21, 23, 24, 26–30, 33–37] vs. no [32]), and local anesthetic concentration and volume (Supplementary File S9).

In univariate analyses, local anesthetic concentration was significantly associated with the magnitude of analgesic effect (coefficient = 2.82, 95% CI: 0.18 to 5.46, p = 0.036), as was volume (coefficient = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.004 to 0.25, p = 0.044). Other variables, including local anesthetic type, use of adjuvants, and block level, were not statistically significant. However, these variables explained little of the between-study heterogeneity (R² = 0–5%), with residual heterogeneity remaining high (I² = 97.5%).

In multivariable meta-regression, local anesthetic concentration remained a significant predictor of analgesic effect (coefficient = 4.03, 95% CI: 2.06 to 5.99, p < 0.001). Additionally, use of adjuvants (coefficient = − 1.53, 95% CI: − 2.63 to − 0.42, p = 0.0069) and block level at T5 (coefficient = − 1.73, 95% CI: − 3.05 to − 0.42, p = 0.0096) were independently associated with reduced pain scores. The multivariable model explained a substantial proportion of between-study heterogeneity (R² = 65%), although residual heterogeneity remained high (I² = 35%).

Trial sequential analysis

A trial sequential analysis was conducted by sequentially incorporating studies in chronological order and performing cumulative analyses at each new study. The model presupposed a Type I error rate of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and a mean difference of -1.27. The Z-value constitutes the test statistic, and a Z-score of 1.96 corresponds to a p-value of 0.05. In Fig. 5, the uppermost Z-curve illustrates the cumulative accumulation of information as data from various trials are incorporated, with each square symbolizing an individual trial. In this instance, the Z-values derived from the accumulated trial data (i.e., the Z-curve) surpass the “Efficacy boundary for analgesic benefit” and cross the designated information size. The resting pain score at six hours surpassed the Z-boundary, signifying ‘firm evidence’ of a more substantial mean difference in pain scores for the ESPB group (Fig. 5). Our trial sequential analysis confirmed that ESPB was associated with reduced resting pain scores at six hours.

GRADE certainty of evidence

The overall certainty of evidence for all outcomes was assessed using the GRADE framework. Across most outcomes, resting pain scores (6 h, 12 h, and 24 h), movement-related pain scores (6 h, 12 h and 24 h), 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption, and PONV, the certainty of evidence was rated as low. This was primarily due to concerns about inconsistency, clinical variability across trials and the smaller sample size (Table 3).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, we demonstrate that ESPB is associated with significant yet clinically modest reductions in postoperative pain, opioid consumption, and PONV following lung surgery. While these findings are directionally consistent across studies, the magnitude of analgesic benefit was modest in the early postoperative period, with an absolute difference of approximately 1–1.5 points on a 10-point scale, diminishing to less than 1 point at 24 h. Similarly, the magnitude of the benefit in opioid consumption was modest in the 24 h postoperative period, with an absolute difference of approximately 16 mg MME. Although the pooled estimates favour ESPB, the observed effect sizes often approach or fall below commonly accepted minimal clinically important differences (MICD) for postoperative pain. This suggests that ESPB may not provide transformative pain relief; however, it could still be valuable as a component of multimodal analgesia rather than serving as a sole alternative to traditional regional methods.

We observed mean differences of 1.26, 1.22, and 0.61 at 6, 12, and 24 h, respectively, on a 10-point pain scale. Nair et al. [38] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of more than 1,100 patients undergoing cardiac surgery and reported that ESPB significantly reduced resting pain scores compared to placebo [38]. They observed similar mean differences of 1.05, 1.00, and 0.27 at 6, 12, and 24 h, respectively, on a 10-point pain scale, compared with our study. Several studies have reported comparable benefits of ESPB in other surgical settings, including breast, lumbar, and abdominal procedures, aligning with our findings in lung surgery patients [39–41]. The consistent direction and modest effects across diverse populations strengthen the evidence that ESPB is an effective component of postoperative pain management. This underscores its clinical importance in thoracic surgery.

Similarly, for the movement-evoked pain, we observed mean differences of 1.6, 1.32, and 0.91 at 6, 12, and 24 h, respectively, on a 10-point pain scale. Improvements in movement-evoked pain have also been reported in other surgical settings [40, 42]. The emphasis on early mobilization in other surgical procedures, such as breast, lumbar, and other thoracic procedures, has shown that ESPB significantly reduced movement-evoked pain scores compared with controls. Across these time points, patients in the ESPB group had significantly fewer episodes of moderate-to-severe pain and a greater proportion reporting mild or no pain during movement than did patients in the control group. The observed pattern mirrors the reductions in movement-evoked pain scores reported in our analysis at various postoperative intervals. The consistency of these findings across different surgical procedures highlights the analgesic effect of ESPB, which extends beyond resting pain to include pain during functional activities, an essential consideration for postoperative recovery and early mobilization, particularly in lung surgery [43]. This broad efficacy reflects the underlying anatomical mechanisms of ESPB, which may account for its effectiveness across a range of surgical contexts.

Cadaveric investigations of ESPB have shown that when a local anesthetic is injected between the erector spinae muscle and the transverse process, it spreads anteriorly to involve the dorsal and ventral rami, as well as sympathetic fibers through the rami communicants [44]. This extensive spread results in multi-dermatomal somatic and visceral analgesia, providing coverage of the anterior, lateral, and posterior thoracoabdominal walls [45, 46]. Both imaging and anatomical studies suggest that the injectate may also reach the paravertebral and epidural spaces, which could account for the block’s broad analgesic range and its ability to alleviate both resting and movement-related pain [47]. In addition, it maintains a favorable safety profile, with reported complication rates as low as 2 per 10,000 procedures [48].

Our study showed a modest decrease of 16 mg MME over 24 h. The benefits of the postoperative opioid consumption should be interpreted in the context of baseline opioid use, changing clinical practice and study variability. In other words, the clinical implications may vary by patient population and analgesic protocols. Jiao et al. [49] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis examining the opioid-sparing effects of ESPB across a wide range of surgical populations, including thoracic, orthopedic, breast, and abdominal procedures [49]. ESPB was associated with a substantial decrease in 24-hour opioid use compared with controls, with effect sizes greater than 2.1 standardized mean differences [50]. These findings are supported by additional meta-analyses in more specific surgical populations, including colorectal, spine, and abdominal procedures, all of which consistently report significant reductions in 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption [39, 51, 52]. These results highlight the consistent opioid-sparing effect of ESPB across a range of surgical populations. This effect is particularly relevant for patients undergoing lung surgery, where higher opioid consumption has been linked to increased complication rates, longer hospitalizations, and poorer overall survival [53, 54]. In addition, minimizing opioid use can facilitate enhanced recovery protocols by promoting earlier return of bowel function and reducing sedation, thereby supporting faster mobilization [55]. While cost-effectiveness was not specifically assessed, these clinical benefits suggest potential economic advantages, including reduced need for rescue analgesia and shorter lengths of stay.

Cai et al. [56] performed a systematic review and meta-analysis including 1,041 patients undergoing chest, abdominal, and spine surgery, reporting that ESPB reduced the risk of PONV by 68% compared with controls [56]. This notable reduction mirrors the pattern observed in our analysis, where ESPB was consistently associated with a lower incidence of PONV in lung surgery patients. The effect is likely attributable to ESPB’s opioid-sparing properties, as opioids activate µ receptors and increase vestibular sensitivity, thereby contributing to emesis. Given that PONV is among the most bothersome postoperative complications, minimizing its occurrence can meaningfully improve patient comfort and facilitate earlier recovery.

The substantial heterogeneity across outcomes underscores the impact of technical and clinical factors on ESPB effectiveness. Variations in local anesthetic concentration and volume at the block site, along with differences in perioperative analgesic protocols, likely contributed to the observed disparities in effect size. While these variations mirror real-world clinical practice, they may influence pooled estimates and limit direct comparisons between trials.

A likely under-recognized source of heterogeneity is the variability in how VATS is defined and performed across studies. Although studies were labelled as VATS, the term has historically been used inconsistently, especially during early adoption stages. In some centers, VATS procedures may involve larger incisions, multiple ports, or limited rib spreading, unlike modern uniportal or minimally invasive techniques. This variation in surgical approaches can lead to markedly different postoperative pain outcomes, sometimes resembling mini-thoracotomy. Such misclassification or differences in surgical invasiveness can either diminish or exaggerate the perceived analgesic benefits of ESPB and may explain the high heterogeneity observed across studies in our analysis.

Another possible reason for high heterogeneity is that many of the included RCTs may have involved low-risk patients undergoing less invasive VATS procedures, usually within optimized ERAS pathways that incorporate multimodal analgesia and local infiltration. In these cases, the additional benefit of a single regional technique may naturally be reduced, which probably explains the modest effect sizes observed.

Results indicated that although certain factors, such as local anesthetic concentration, contributed to heterogeneity, they did not significantly alter the overall direction or interpretation of the outcomes. However, these clinical heterogeneities may affect the generalizability and certainty of the pooled estimates. Notably, even though local anesthetic concentration and volume were significant modifiers of analgesic effect, considerable heterogeneity persisted, suggesting that unmeasured clinical and methodological factors likely influence variability across studies.

Strength of Study

This is the first and largest systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis exclusively of RCTs assessing postoperative outcomes of ESPB in lung surgery. A similar review by Koo et al. [56] included only 17 RCTs, of which 11 focused exclusively on lung surgery, and did not perform meta-regression analyses, limiting the study’s ability to explore the influence of study-level variables on outcomes [56]. Our review includes 20 RCTs and employs meta-regression, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and Bayesian analyses, offering a more comprehensive and methodologically rigorous evaluation. To better understand factors influencing the effectiveness of ESPB, we conducted meta-regression analyses examining the impact of clinical characteristics on resting pain scores at 6 h. Due to the high heterogeneity in outcomes, we performed subgroup analyses, which showed that local anesthetic concentration significantly affected the magnitude of ESPB’s analgesic effect. In contrast, the type of local anesthetic, volume, adjuvant use, and block level did not alter the final inference of our results. These findings reinforce the potential benefits of this regional nerve block procedure across diverse clinical environments in lung surgery patients. The use of Bayesian modelling and predictive intervals further strengthens the interpretability of our findings by providing clinically relevant estimates of expected effects in future studies.

Limitations

This review has several significant limitations. Many of the included RCTs enrolled relatively small numbers of participants, which may have reduced statistical power and increased the risk of type II errors. Additionally, key patient-centred outcomes, such as hospital length of stay, quality of recovery, and longer-term pain control beyond 48 h, were reported inconsistently, hindering meaningful analysis. These outcomes are increasingly regarded as essential measures in perioperative research. Minimal clinically important differences for pain outcomes were not formally assessed, limiting the interpretation of clinical relevance. Incomplete blinding of anesthesia teams in several trials introduces the potential for performance bias. Further, this review included only English-language studies, which may introduce language bias and exclude relevant non-English RCTs of ESPB in lung surgery. Another Important limitation is that none of the included studies reported chronic post-thoracotomy pain (CPTP) beyond the immediate postoperative period, a key outcome in thoracic surgery; future research should evaluate ESPB’s long-term analgesic effects on CPTP. In addition, future studies should use standardized assessment tools, such as the QoR-15 or other validated patient-reported outcome measures, to better assess the effects of ESPB on recovery, functional outcomes, and patient satisfaction.

Future directions

Future RCTs should adopt a multicenter design with larger sample sizes and standardized protocols, including consistent local anesthetic concentration and volume, block level, and type of lung surgery. Extending outcome assessments to 48 h and including measures of patient satisfaction would also yield important information. The newer studies may also focus on other regional techniques, such as the paravertebral block. A network meta-analysis comparing all regional techniques may provide a clearer picture of the best regional technique to choose for thoracic procedures. Cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to determine whether ESPB can achieve meaningful reductions in healthcare expenditures.

Key takeaway

ESPB provides dependable, but modest, pain relief during lung surgery. Its true clinical value largely hinges on patient selection and the specific surgical situation. Existing RCTs likely underestimate their importance in complex or high-risk cases. ESPB serves best as a safe, practical addition rather than a replacement for multimodal analgesia. Future research should focus less on proving efficacy in ideal settings and more on evaluating its real-world effectiveness, especially in high-risk populations.

Clinical implications

Within ERAS pathways, ESPB may be considered a reasonable adjunct for postoperative analgesia in lung surgery, particularly when neuraxial techniques are contraindicated or undesirable. By decreasing postoperative pain, minimizing opioid requirements, and reducing the incidence of PONV, ESPB may facilitate a smoother recovery and align with the objectives of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) programs. Anesthesiologists may consider ESPB as an effective regional adjunct within multimodal analgesia for patients undergoing lung procedures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ESPB is associated with modest but consistent improvements in postoperative pain, opioid consumption, and PONV after lung surgery. However, given the low certainty of evidence (GRADE) and substantial between-study heterogeneity, these findings support ESPB as an adjunct to multimodal analgesia rather than as a definitive regional analgesic strategy for lung surgery.
