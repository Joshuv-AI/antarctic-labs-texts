Congenital bulbourethral diverticulum with huge stones: a case report
Authors: Guangjie Li, Zhiqiang He, Shulian Chen, Hengxin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMale congenital urethral diverticulum with huge stones is an extremely rare benign disease. The patient was a 67-year-old male with a huge stone (4.5cm × 4.3cm × 4.1cm) in his bulbourethral diverticulum. The stones in the bulbourethral diverticulum have different treatment methods due to the different size of the stones. Since the number of such cases is very small, there is no clear standard treatment plan for the treatment of giant bulbourethral diverticular calculi (stone diameter greater than 4.0 cm). The treatment plan we give is transperineal incision of urethral diverticular incision, urethral diverticular resection and urethroplasty.Keywords: Urethral diverticulum calculi, Urethral diverticulum, Bulbar urethra, Congenital

Introduction

The urethral diverticula refer to sac-like structures opening into the urethra,1 classified into congenital and secondary types. Urethral diverticula are commonly found in females, with an incidence rate of 1% to 6% in females.2 The incidence of urethral diverticulum in males is currently unclear, but it is generally believed to be lower in males. Congenital urethral diverticulum in males typically occurs on the ventral side of the anterior urethra.3 The prevailing view is that this is due to developmental defects in the urethral folds, leading to segmental defects in the urethral wall, commonly seen in the bulbar urethra.4 We report a case of congenital bulbar urethral diverticulum with a large stone (4.5 cm × 4.3 cm×4.1cm). We performed a urethrocystoscopy, during which we observed a urethral diverticulum opening into the bulbar urethra. However, due to the large size of the stone, intraurethral lithotripsy and stone removal were deemed too challenging. To completely remove the urethral diverticulum stone and to determine the histopathological nature of the urethral diverticulum tissue, we performed an open perineal incision urethral diverticulum stone removal surgery, urethral diverticulum resection, and urethral reconstruction surgery. After incising the urethral diverticulum, we observed a massive urethral diverticulum stone.

Case presentation

The patient, a 67-year-old male, was admitted to the hospital on 7 July 2023 with perineal pain that had been present for a duration of one month. One month prior to the onset of symptoms, the patient experienced perineal discomfort without any discernible cause. This discomfort was characterised as paroxysmal and distending, persisting for several minutes before subsiding spontaneously. The presence of dysuria, haematuria or other such symptoms was not observed. The patient denied a previous history of urethral manipulation. Physical examination: the scrotum exhibited a red and swollen appearance, with no evidence of skin ulceration. The skin temperature was found to be within normal parameters, and a swelling measuring approximately 5.0cm × 4.5cm was observed in the scrotum below the root of the penis. The surface of this swelling was described as smooth, and its texture was noted as hard. The boundaries were described as clear, and no fluctuation sensation was detected. No obvious swelling was detected in the testes and epididymis bilaterally. The laboratory examination revealed the following results: a white blood cell count of 5446/μl in the urine, a red blood cell count of 13/μl in the urine, and a blood sedimentation rate of 76 mm/h. The results of the relevant investigative procedures revealed that the patient's blood exhibited no signs of anti-tuberculosis antibodies, while urine analysis revealed no evidence of tubercle bacilli. Furthermore, tumour markers and other indicators were found to be within normal limits.CTU: An ovoid high-density mass measuring approximately 4.5cm × 4.3cm×4.1cm was observed in the scrotum, situated beneath the cavernous body of the penis (Fig. 1). The mass exhibited a clear boundary and an average CT value of approximately 1341HU (mean value determined by summing horizontal, coronal and sagittal CT values). No obvious abnormal enhancement was observed on the enhanced scan. In order to clarify the location and nature of the high-density mass, urethral cystoscopy was performed after control of the urinary tract infection. The opening of the urethral diverticulum was seen at the ventral side of the bulbous urethra at an approximate distance of 8.0 cm, with a yellow stone visible at the opening (Fig. 2A), which could not be seen in its entirety within the urethral diverticulum (Fig. 2B). The presence of a substantial stone within the bulbous urethral diverticulum rendered intracavitary lithotripsy a challenging procedure. To address this challenge, a decision was made to excise the diverticulum and send it for pathological examination to ascertain the nature of the diverticulum. A transperineal incision was performed on the urethral diverticulum, lithotripsy was conducted, and a urethral diverticulectomy and urethroplasty were carried out. Upon incision of the diverticulum, a substantial stone was observed (Fig. 3). Thirdly, to prevent postoperative urinary retention, suprapubic cystocentesis and indwelling catheterisation were also performed. Following the surgical procedure, the cystostomy tube and urinary catheter were removed. Postoperative pathology revealed the presence of urethral diverticulum tissue, which exhibited signs of acute and chronic inflammatory changes, accompanied by local calcification. The composition of the substantial urethral diverticulum stone was found to be carbonate apatite. Postoperative CTU revealed the complete resolution of the bulbous urethral diverticulum, with no evidence of urethral stricture. The patient was observed over a period of two years, during which time normal urination was recorded and perineal pain ceased.

Discussion

The urethral diverticulum refers to a sac-like structure that opens into the urethra1 (as shown in Fig. 4). It can be either congenital or secondary. Urethral diverticula are common in women, with an incidence rate of between 1% and 6%.2 The incidence rate in men is not yet known, but it is generally believed to be rarer than in women.1 The overall incidence of urethral diverticulum in humans is currently estimated to be between 0.5% and 5%,5 of which approximately 4% to 10% of patients have concomitant urethral diverticulum stones.6 Congenital urethral diverticulum in males typically affects the ventral part of the anterior urethra.3 The current mainstream view is that this is caused by a developmental defect in the urethral folds leading to segmental defects in the urethral wall, which are commonly seen in the bulbar urethra.4 Secondary urethral diverticulum occurs in cases of stricture, infection, trauma or iatrogenic injury.4 The patient in this case had a congenital bulbous urethral diverticulum containing a large stone measuring 4.5 cm × 4.3 cm × 4.1 cm. Urethral cystoscopy revealed an opening to the diverticulum in the bulbar urethra. Due to the stone's large size, it was difficult to fragment it in the cavity. In order to remove the urethral diverticulum for pathological examination to determine its nature, open surgery was performed. After incising the diverticulum, a large stone was found inside it. Postoperative pathology revealed acute and chronic inflammatory changes in the urethral diverticulum tissue.

Examinations used to diagnose urethral diverticulum include urethrocystoscopy, retrograde urethrography, Doppler ultrasound, CT and MRI scans.7,8 It is difficult to distinguish between urethral diverticulum stones and urethral stones in male patients using imaging techniques.9 However, urethrocystoscopy can be used to distinguish between the two types of stone, thereby facilitating the formulation of a better surgical plan.

For urethral diverticulum, urethral diverticulectomy combined with urethroplasty is one of the most common and effective surgical methods, with an 83–97% cure rate. If the diverticulum is too large, local flaps or distant grafts (cheek mucosa) can be used for repair.10,11 Sun et al.12 used pneumatic and ultrasound lithotripsy to treat urethral diverticular stones. However, endoscopic lithotripsy can result in abrasions, tears and avulsions of the urethral wall, as well as false passage formation or urethral stricture.12 Furthermore, this method cannot treat urethral diverticulum in one stage nor remove diverticular tissue for complete pathological examination to determine the nature of the diverticulum. Therefore, open surgery is used to incise and remove the stones in cases of urethral diverticulum complicated by stones. At the same time, the diverticular tissue can be removed for pathological examination to determine whether cancerous changes are present. Studies have shown that urethral diverticula may become cancerous, particularly when stones chronically irritate the mucosa of the diverticulum. Types of cancer that may occur include adenocarcinoma, transitional cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma,13 clear cell carcinoma,14 and small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the urinary tract.15 In this case, the postoperative pathology of the resected urethral diverticulum tissue showed acute and chronic inflammatory changes, but no cancerous changes.

Conclusions

In summary, for congenital bulbourethral diverticulum with large stones, the most effective treatment method is perineal incision, urethral diverticulum stone removal, urethral diverticulum resection and urethroplasty. This method can also determine whether the urethral diverticulum tissue has become cancerous.
