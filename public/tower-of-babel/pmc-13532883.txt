Psychological Resource Profiles and Learning Engagement Among Rural Left-Behind Adolescents in China: A Latent Profile Analysis
Authors: Xinying Chen, Jialian Li, Yiwei Li, Siyu Tao, Qian Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWith increasing labor mobility between urban and rural regions, rural left-behind adolescents in China have become a group requiring attention in both educational and psychological domains. This study aimed to identify distinct psychological resource profiles among rural left-behind adolescents and examine their differences in learning engagement.Patients and MethodsThis study employed latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify distinct psychological resource profiles based on four key dimensions: mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support.ResultsFour latent profiles were identified among the participants: “High Mindfulness–High Support”, “Isolated High Mindfulness”, “Moderate Resource Integration”, and “Low Mindfulness–High Motivation”. Significant differences in learning engagement were observed across these profiles. Specifically, adolescents in the “High Mindfulness–High Support” and “Low Mindfulness–High Motivation” groups showed higher levels of learning engagement, whereas those in the “Isolated High Mindfulness” group showed the lowest level of engagement. Age was significantly associated with profile membership, whereas gender showed no significant association.ConclusionThese findings highlight the heterogeneity of psychological resource configurations among rural left-behind adolescents and suggest that different profiles are associated with distinct levels of learning engagement. The results may provide a descriptive basis for future longitudinal and intervention studies on learning engagement in this population.Keywords: Chinese rural adolescents, mindfulness, psychological development, learning engagement, latent profile analysis

Introduction

Rural left-behind adolescents constitute a distinct group of young individuals who reside in rural areas and whose one or both parents have migrated to urban areas for work, resulting in long-term parental absence from daily caregiving. These adolescents are typically raised by grandparents or other extended family members. In China, the size of this population is substantial, and they often face a series of developmental challenges, such as weakened parent–child bonds and inadequate social support networks.1 These risk factors have been associated with both mental health difficulties and academic development challenges.2 During adolescence—a critical period for psychological and academic growth—learning engagement serves as a key indicator of academic adaptability. High levels of learning engagement reflect students’ behavioral and emotional involvement in academic tasks and are closely related to academic achievement and positive psychosocial outcomes.3 Moreover, engagement in learning reflects an individual’s intrinsic motivation, capacity for self-regulation, and resilience in the face of academic challenges.4 Nevertheless, a considerable number of adolescents, particularly those in under-resourced rural settings, commonly display academic disengagement, characterized by inattention, low motivation, and avoidance behaviors.5 For left-behind adolescents, such issues may be more pronounced due to their vulnerable familial and social environments. According to Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, individual development is shaped by the dynamic interaction of various systems, including family, peers, school, and broader sociocultural contexts.6 Given their disrupted family structures and fragile support systems, rural left-behind adolescents may face constraints on learning engagement that stem from both external environmental factors and internal psychological limitations. Against this background, the present study aimed to identify distinct psychological resource profiles among rural left-behind adolescents and examine how these profiles were associated with learning engagement.

Prior research has revealed that left-behind adolescents often exhibit marked differences in learning motivation, strategy use, and emotional regulation, with some individuals displaying symptoms of academic burnout and anxiety.7 These disparities are closely linked to the distribution and functional adaptability of psychological resources, which are closely related to academic adjustment.8 As such, it is important to examine the underlying psychological resource structures related to learning engagement and to explore how internal psychological resources are associated with academic behavior. Importantly, left-behind adolescents should not be viewed as a monolithic group; their responses to external stressors and resource limitations vary considerably, reflecting individual differences in upbringing, personality traits, and access to social support.9 A purely variable-centered approach that focuses on linear associations between isolated psychological factors and academic engagement may overlook the complex, multidimensional nature of these adolescents’ psychological profiles. In contrast, identifying latent heterogeneity in psychological resources—and mapping out distinct configurations of psychological characteristics—offers a more nuanced and empirically grounded basis for promoting educational equity and informing personalized support strategies tailored to the needs of rural left-behind youth.10,11

From the perspective of ecological systems theory and integrative psychological resources, learning engagement among rural left-behind adolescents may be shaped by the joint configuration of multiple internal and external resources. Mindfulness reflects present-moment awareness and emotion-related self-regulation; grit captures sustained effort and persistence toward long-term goals; growth mindset reflects adaptive beliefs about the malleability of ability; and perceived social support represents external relational resources from family, peers, and significant others. Together, these resources cover internal self-regulatory capacity, motivational persistence, adaptive belief systems, and interpersonal support. Examining these dimensions within a unified framework allows for a more nuanced understanding of whether rural left-behind adolescents show cumulative, compensatory, or mixed configurations of psychological resources.

Mindfulness refers to the awareness that emerges from deliberately attending to present-moment experiences with an open and non-judgmental attitude.12 It is commonly conceptualized in two forms: state mindfulness and trait mindfulness. State mindfulness reflects an individual’s momentary awareness of the present in specific contexts, whereas trait mindfulness denotes a relatively stable psychological disposition to remain attentive and aware in everyday life.13 Trait mindfulness has been shown to be associated with a range of psychological outcomes, including emotion regulation, stress coping, and academic adjustment. For instance, Brown and Ryan argued that mindfulness, as a form of metacognitive capacity, enables individuals to detect internal distractions and emotional fluctuations in a timely manner, thereby facilitating purposeful attention regulation and sustained contextual focus.14 For adolescents, the ability to consistently remain present can mitigate academic anxiety, increase task engagement, and enhance cognitive flexibility, positioning trait mindfulness as a key psychological resource for navigating complex learning demands. Empirical evidence suggests that students with higher levels of trait mindfulness tend to maintain emotional balance under academic pressure and display stronger learning motivation and self-efficacy.15,16 Moreover, the positive association between trait mindfulness and learning engagement has been supported by meta-analytic findings. A meta-analysis by Dunning et al demonstrated that mindfulness levels among primary and secondary school students were significantly associated with classroom engagement, learning motivation, and overall psychological well-being.17

In addition to mindfulness, grit involves both enduring effort and the capacity to stay focused on long-term aspirations, making it an important psychological resource during adolescent development. Research has demonstrated that grit predicts academic success even more strongly than IQ, as it encapsulates one’s capacity to remain focused and resilient in the face of adversity.18 It has been shown to significantly influence adolescents’ learning persistence and positive academic emotions.19 For rural left-behind adolescents living in resource-scarce and high-stress environments, grit may serve as an important internal resource for overcoming unfavorable conditions and maintaining engagement in academic tasks. Closely associated with grit is the growth mindset, which reflects an individual’s fundamental belief that abilities are not fixed but can be developed through effort and learning. Adolescents who endorse a growth mindset are more likely to adopt adaptive learning strategies when facing challenges rather than avoiding them,20 and this belief system has been found to reduce academic anxiety and increase learning persistence.21 By cultivating greater tolerance for failure and fostering a stronger sense of efficacy in the learning process, a growth mindset is related to students’ motivation and plays a vital role in supporting their sustained engagement in academic pursuits.

Perceived social support, as a crucial dimension of external psychological resources, has been found to be related to regulatory and buffering processes in adolescents’ learning adaptation. An individual’s perception of being supported by family, friends, and significant others can enhance their sense of self-worth and belonging, while simultaneously mitigating emotional distress induced by academic stressors.22 This perceived support, in turn, indirectly fosters academic confidence and perseverance.23 For rural left-behind adolescents—who often face disruptions in family functioning and experience prolonged absence of parental companionship—perceived social support becomes an indispensable source for maintaining psychological stability and academic adjustment.24

From the perspective of integrative psychological capital, it is suggested that when individuals face challenges, various positive psychological resources do not function in isolation but instead operate in concert to foster resilience.25 Furthermore, developmental adaptation frequently stems from the dynamic accumulation and interplay of various protective resources over time.26 Therefore, incorporating mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support into a unified analytical framework allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how psychological resource configurations are structurally associated with learning engagement among rural left-behind adolescents.

To more precisely capture individual differences across multiple psychological dimensions, researchers have increasingly employed Latent Profile Analysis (LPA)—a person-centered statistical technique that does not rely on preset classifications. Instead, LPA uncovers latent subgroups by identifying distinct patterns within the data, thereby revealing the structural configurations of psychological resources at the individual level. This method has gained broad application in education, psychology, and sociology for its capacity to model heterogeneity among populations. For example, Ma et al applied LPA to perceived social support among adolescents and identified four distinct profiles.11 These latent profiles showed distinct developmental patterns and were differentially associated with social–emotional competence, underscoring the practical value of LPA in uncovering nuanced relationships between psychological traits and developmental outcomes.

However, within the Chinese context—particularly regarding rural left-behind adolescents—there remains a notable gap in LPA research based on multidimensional psychological resources. Owing to long-term parent–child separation and the constraints of resource-scarce ecological environments, the psychological development of rural left-behind youth is especially complex. While these adolescents often face significant resource deprivation, they may also develop unique adaptive patterns in response to prolonged life stressors.27 Therefore, this study aims to employ Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to identify typological patterns in psychological resources—specifically mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support—among rural left-behind adolescents. Additionally, the study will examine differences in learning engagement across the identified latent profiles, and further explore how demographic variables such as gender and age are associated with profile membership. Through this approach, the study seeks to provide a descriptive basis for understanding heterogeneity in psychological resources among rural left-behind adolescents and to inform future longitudinal and intervention research on learning engagement in this population.

Methods

Participants

The present study adopted convenience sampling to recruit rural left-behind adolescents aged 10–18 from Guangxi, Henan, Shandong, Guangdong, and Hainan provinces in China. In this study, rural left-behind adolescents were defined as adolescents who resided in rural areas and whose one or both parents had migrated to urban areas for work, resulting in prolonged absence from daily caregiving. “Rural” was determined based on participants’ self-reported current residence in rural areas. Questionnaires were administered either as paper-based questionnaires completed collectively in classroom settings or through Wenjuanxing links distributed to students in classrooms. A total of 491 questionnaires were collected, including 50 paper-based questionnaires and 441 online questionnaires. No questionnaires showed straight-line response patterns. After excluding two questionnaires with more than 10% missing data, 489 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 99.59%. Among the participants, 211 were male (43.10%) and 278 were female (56.90%). The participants’ ages ranged from 10 to 18 years, with a mean age of 14.40 years (SD = 1.75).

Measures

Mindfulness

Mindfulness was measured using the Child and Adolescent Mindfulness Measure (CAMM) developed by Greco et al.28 The CAMM contains 10 items and is divided into two dimensions: awareness and non-judgment (Items T1, T2, T3, T6, T7, T8; 6 items), and acceptance (Items T4, T5, T9, T10; 4 items). All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never true, 5 = always true). In the original scoring of the CAMM, higher raw scores indicate lower levels of mindfulness. Therefore, all CAMM items were reverse-coded before analysis so that higher scores indicated higher levels of mindfulness in the present study. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.90.

Grit

Grit was assessed using the Short Grit Scale (Grit-S) developed by Duckworth et al,29 which consists of 8 items divided into two dimensions: perseverance of effort and consistency of interests (4 items each). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all like me, 5 = very much like me). All items in the consistency of interests dimension are reverse-coded. The internal consistency coefficient in this study was Cronbach’s α = 0.70, indicating acceptable reliability.

Growth Mindset

Growth mindset was measured using a 6-item scale developed by Dweck et al.30 Items 1–3 assess fixed mindset (eg, believing intelligence is unchangeable), and Items 4–6 assess growth mindset (eg, believing intelligence can be developed). Responses are rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree), and Items 1–3 are reverse-coded. After conversion, the average score is calculated across all items. Higher scores indicate a stronger growth mindset. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.76).

Perceived Social Support

Perceived social support was assessed using the Chinese version of the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSS), originally developed by Zimet et al and adapted by Yan and Zheng. The scale consists of 12 items across three dimensions: support from friends, family, and significant others.22 Responses are given on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting greater perceived support. The internal consistency of the scale in this study was Cronbach’s α = 0.89, indicating good reliability.

Learning Engagement

Learning engagement was measured using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale–Student version (UWES–S), developed by Schaufeli et al and revised by Fang and Shi for use among Chinese students.31 The scale includes 17 items covering three dimensions: vigor, dedication, and absorption. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = never, 7 = always), with higher scores indicating greater engagement. In this study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96).

Procedure

After obtaining permission from teachers and relevant school administrators, the researchers explained the study’s purpose and procedures to participants and their guardians. Informed consent and voluntary participation were ensured. Questionnaires were distributed in both paper and online formats. The surveys were anonymous, and the response time was limited to 10–15 minutes per participant.

Results

Common Method Bias Test

Harman’s single-factor test was used to assess common method bias in this study. The findings indicated that seven factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and no single-factor structure was observed. The largest factor accounted for 35.15% of the variance, which is well below the 40% threshold. Therefore, no serious common method bias was detected in this study.

Naming and Characteristics of Latent Profiles

Based on four psychological resource indicators—mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support—this study conducted a latent profile analysis of rural left-behind adolescents. Four latent profiles with distinct psychological resource structures were ultimately identified. The conditional means of each profile across the four variables are presented in Figure 1.

The first group exhibited outstanding performance in both mindfulness and perceived social support, while also showing above-average levels of grit and growth mindset. Overall, their psychological resources were well balanced, combining strong internal awareness with adequate external support. This group accounted for 23% of the total sample and was classified as the “High Mindfulness–High Support Group”. The second group scored higher than the other types on mindfulness but markedly lower on grit, growth mindset, and social support. Given this pattern, it was labeled the “Isolated High Mindfulness Group”, representing 4% of the sample. The third group, which made up the largest proportion of participants (71%), scored at moderate levels across all four indicators, reflecting a relatively balanced but non-prominent developmental pattern. Because of its relatively integrated resource structure without significant weaknesses, this group was named the “Moderate Resource Integration Group”. Finally, the fourth group recorded the lowest mindfulness scores but the highest levels of grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support. This group, comprising 2% of the sample, was classified as the “Low Mindfulness–High Motivation Group”.

Discussion

The present study utilized Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to identify distinct psychological resource profiles among rural left-behind adolescents, based on four key dimensions: mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support. Furthermore, it examined the extent to which these latent profiles were associated with differences in learning engagement. The findings revealed substantial heterogeneity in the composition of psychological resources across individuals, indicating that rural left-behind adolescents do not represent a psychologically homogeneous group. Importantly, statistically significant differences in learning engagement were observed across profiles, supporting the value of a person-centered approach in understanding variations in educational outcomes.

Individuals in the High Mindfulness–High Support group (C1) exhibited elevated scores across all four dimensions of psychological resources, with particularly higher levels of mindfulness and perceived social support. High trait mindfulness reflects awareness and non-judgmental acceptance of present experiences, which may support emotional regulation and stress coping abilities.14 Simultaneously, social support provides external resources that may enhance adolescents’ confidence in navigating academic and personal challenges.22 Adolescents with both internal and external resources are more likely to demonstrate sustained attention, intrinsic motivation, and engagement in academic settings. Prior research has suggested that learning motivation is often influenced by the combined effects of multiple psychological strengths rather than a single factor alone.8 The present findings are consistent with this perspective, indicating that integrated psychological resources may be associated with higher learning engagement.

The Isolated High Mindfulness group (C2) showed relatively high mindfulness but lower levels of grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support. This profile suggests that although adolescents in this group may have heightened awareness of internal experiences, they may have fewer motivational and contextual resources. Such a configuration may be associated with limitations in overall adaptive functioning. While mindfulness may support emotional regulation and attentional control, its adaptive role may depend on the presence of complementary psychological and social resources.33 In this group, the combination of relatively high awareness and limited external support may be associated with lower levels of learning engagement.

The Moderate Resource Integration group (C3), which constituted the largest proportion of the sample, exhibited moderate levels across all four psychological resource dimensions. This profile reflects a balanced but non-differentiated pattern of psychological resources. In terms of learning engagement, this group showed lower levels compared with the High Mindfulness–High Support and Low Mindfulness–High Motivation groups, but higher levels than the Isolated High Mindfulness group. This suggests that moderate psychological resources may be associated with moderate levels of academic engagement.34

The most noteworthy finding pertains to the Low Mindfulness–High Motivation group (C4), which exhibited the lowest level of mindfulness but relatively higher levels of grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support, and showed relatively high levels of learning engagement among the four profiles. This finding suggests that different configurations of psychological resources may be associated with variations in learning engagement. It should be noted that this profile accounted for a relatively small proportion of the sample (approximately 2%), suggesting limited statistical stability for this subgroup. Previous studies have highlighted grit as an important factor related to sustained effort and goal persistence;18 a growth mindset as being associated with adaptive responses to challenges;20 and perceived social support as a resource linked to stress buffering and psychological adjustment.23 The “high-motivation” configuration observed in this group suggests that, even when mindful awareness is relatively low, other psychological resources such as grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support may be jointly associated with relatively high learning engagement. This finding is consistent with the resource substitution model proposed by Luruli et al, which highlights the functional complementarity among different psychological assets in promoting adaptive outcomes.35

The present study also found that age was significantly associated with latent profile membership. Older adolescents were more likely to be classified into the High Mindfulness–High Support and Moderate Resource Integration groups, suggesting that developmental factors may be associated with differences in psychological resource configuration.36 This finding is consistent with developmental psychology perspectives, which suggest that cognitive and socio-emotional capacities typically evolve with age.37 In contrast, gender was not significantly associated with profile membership, indicating that psychological resource configurations among rural left-behind adolescents may be more closely related to developmental and contextual factors than to gender differences. Contrary to some prior findings reporting gender differences in mindfulness and perceived social support,38 such differences may be attenuated in the rural left-behind context, where structural disadvantages such as parental absence and limited caregiving resources play a dominant role.

From an educational perspective, these findings suggest that learning engagement among rural left-behind adolescents may be better understood from a structural perspective rather than attributing it to single psychological traits. This perspective highlights the importance of differentiated and context-sensitive support strategies. For example, adolescents in the Isolated High Mindfulness group may benefit from support aimed at strengthening goal-setting abilities and social connectedness. The Moderate Resource Integration group may benefit from programs that enhance overall psychological resources. These suggestions remain exploratory and should be further examined in future intervention studies.

The present study investigated the heterogeneity of psychological resources among rural left-behind adolescents using a person-centered latent profile analysis approach, thereby addressing limitations of traditional variable-centered methods that often mask intra-group variation. By concurrently examining four key psychological dimensions—mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support—the study developed an integrated resource framework to elucidate the dynamic interplay between internal awareness and external support systems. This approach deepens the understanding of psychological mechanisms underlying learning engagement and reveals distinct profiles with explanatory value for educational research and practice.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretations of the observed associations between psychological resource profiles and learning engagement. Future research could employ longitudinal designs to examine developmental trajectories over time. Second, the sample was drawn from specific rural regions through convenience sampling, which may limit generalizability. In addition, although the questionnaires were administered in organized classroom contexts, the use of online self-report questionnaires may still have introduced potential response or selection biases. Expanding the geographic and demographic scope would enhance external validity. Third, although key psychological resources were included, other potentially relevant variables such as academic self-efficacy, emotional regulation, school belonging, and family functioning were not considered. Fourth, all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to social desirability bias and variability in item comprehension. Although Harman’s single-factor test suggested no serious common method bias, this approach may not fully rule out the possibility of common method variance. Finally, although distinct profiles were identified, future studies should further explore how profile-based findings can be translated into empirically tested intervention strategies. In addition, the smallest class, C4, included only 8 participants and accounted for 1.64% of the sample, suggesting limited statistical stability. Future studies are encouraged to examine the stability and replicability of this profile in larger and more diverse samples.

Conclusion

This study employed latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify distinct psychological resource configurations among rural left-behind adolescents across four core dimensions: mindfulness, grit, growth mindset, and perceived social support. It further examined differences in learning engagement across these latent profiles. The results revealed meaningful heterogeneity in psychological resource structures, yielding four interpretable subgroups. Notably, the High Mindfulness–High Support Group and the Low Mindfulness–High Motivation Group showed relatively higher levels of learning engagement, suggesting that different combinations of psychological resources may be associated with variations in academic engagement. In contrast, the Isolated High Mindfulness Group and the Moderate Resource Integration Group exhibited relatively lower engagement, indicating that the presence of individual psychological resources alone may not be sufficient to support sustained academic involvement.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering the structural configuration of psychological resources and individual heterogeneity when examining learning engagement among rural left-behind adolescents. The study supports a shift from traditional variable-centered approaches toward person-centered analytical frameworks in understanding adolescent learning behavior. From an applied perspective, these findings may inform future efforts aimed at enhancing learning engagement by strengthening mindfulness skills, fostering adaptive beliefs, supporting perseverance, and expanding perceived social support. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, these implications should be interpreted as preliminary and associative rather than causal.
