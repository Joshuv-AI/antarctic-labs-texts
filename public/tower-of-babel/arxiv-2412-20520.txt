Re(l)ality:
Nicola Bamonti

Abstract




Abstract

This paper investigates the mathematical and philosophical foundations of relational observables and reference frames using the fibre bundle formalism.
Two paradigms are contrasted: the
View from Nowhere
, which interprets frame-dependent observables as
perspectives
on an invariant equivalence class, and the
View from Everywhere
which interprets each frame-dependent observables as a fully-fledged fundamental reality.
The ontology endorsed by the View from Everywhere, termed
Relality
, is inherently fragmented, yet not solipsistic: inter-translatability between distinct, frame-dependent realities is possible.
The paper challenges perspectivalist metaphysics and critiques the reliance on frame-free structures, arguing they obscure both empirical content and physical information.
Central to the discussion is the challenge of providing a
perspicuous
and
parsimonious
characterisation of the gauge-invariant content of the theory.

1
Introduction

In gauge-theoretic frameworks such as General Relativity (GR),
observables
are defined as quantities that remain unchanged under specific transformations called
gauge transformations

(
Dirac 1964
)
.
These are transformations that leave the physical content of the theory, also referred to as the ‘observable content’, unchanged.
Consequently, gauge transformations are typically understood as mathematical re-descriptions of the theory’s observable content, indicating a
representational redundancy
according to which distinct mathematical formulations correspond to the same physical state.
1
1

1

For a thorough overview of this issue in both physics and philosophy, see
Berghofer et al. 2023
.

A standard assumption in physics holds that a procedure of ‘gauge-fixing’ is required to remove descriptive redundancy, thereby ensuring a
unique
description of physical configurations through the specification of gauge-invariant observables.
This paper investigates how such observables can be constructed and interpreted within GR.

For a theory to achieve empirical validity, its gauge-invariant observables
must
correspond to measurable quantities, facilitating meaningful comparisons between theoretical predictions and experimental observations. This correspondence is a critical criterion for the viability of any scientific theory.
2
2

2

Although, following
Lakatos 1969
, no theory can be wholly confirmed or falsified by experimental evidence, empirical adequacy remains crucial for avoiding charges of
ad hocness

(
Leplin 1975
)
.

Because all measurements are confined to specific regions of spacetime, empirical practice typically involves evaluating field variables at particular spacetime points—or within infinitesimally localised regions..
As
Gary and Giddings 2007
emphasise, “
all we can truly observe is localised — we have no access to infinity.
”
In GR, where gauge symmetries are often expressed as diffeomorphisms, this local character of measurement presents a difficulty: any quantity defined pointwise in spacetime is not invariant under such gauge transformations, raising challenges for constructing local, gauge-invariant observables.

In a diffeomorphism-invariant theory like GR, observables are typically classified into two main types:
(1)
non-local
quantities, defined over extended or global regions of spacetime—such as integrals over entire domains, and
(2)
relationally local
quantities, which are constructed by correlating field values. This work focusses primarily on the latter, often referred to simply as
relational observables
, as they offer a promising strategy for recovering locality without sacrificing gauge-invariance.

A well-established approach to constructing (local) relational observables in GR was introduced by
Rovelli 2002b
and later formalised by
Dittrich 2006
;
Dittrich 2007
.
This approach involves correlating two partial observables—quantities that are individually gauge-variant—to form a gauge-invariant complete observable.
These complete observables embody a relational notion of locality: rather than being anchored to a spacetime manifold, physical quantities are defined relative to the values of other fields.
Put simply, a complete observable represents the value of one partial observable when the value of another is specified.

This relational framework naturally aligns with that of reference frames in GR, as discussed in
Bamonti 2023
.
Reference frames offer a structured way to define local gauge-invariant quantities relationally.
In practice, the construction of relational observables often involves treating one partial observable as a spatiotemporal reference frame for another, thereby highlighting the inherently relational nature of locality in this framework.
As further argued by
Bamonti and Gomes 2024b
, gauge-invariance requires
dynamically coupled partial observables
(that is, dynamically coupled reference frames).
In this case, when a pair of partial observables — such as a metric field and a reference frame field —jointly satisfy the full, coupled equations of motion of the theory, only the
diagonal
action of a dynamical symmetry (such as diffeomorphisms in GR) preserves the solution. This ensures
deterministic evolution
, which is essential for gauge-invariance of the relational observable (see
Bamonti and Gomes 2024a
).

This paper addresses a central question: how should we interpret two distinct local relational observables when expressed relative to distinct reference frames?
Two primary views aim to answer this question.

The
View from Nowhere
is the dominant perspective in the literature (§
4
). According to this view, changing the reference frame does not affect the underlying physical content, as each reference frame is understood to provide merely a
perspective
on a shared, objective reality.
This idea has its roots in Special Relativity, where different inertial reference frames offer distinct perspectives on a Lorentz-invariant physical reality.
3
3

3

To be more historically precise, the origin can be traced back to Galilean physics, according to which different uniformly-moving reference frames in space, determine only different points of view on the same physically objective, Galilean-invariant quantities. However, it is with Special Relativity that this idea is placed in the relativistic context of relativistic spacetime.

Prominent representatives of this view include the
perspective-neutral framework
introduced by
Vanrietvelde et al. 2020
and related work by
Giacomini et al. 2019
and
Kabel et al. 2024
.
Explicitly,
Kabel et al. 2024
state “if we assume that the covariance of physical laws under classical changes of reference frame extends linearly to encompass changes of QRF, then the latter
should not affect the physical situation
. Thus, whether a system is in superposition or is entangled, and how these properties change dynamically, becomes a mere matter of perspective [emphasis added]”.

In contrast, the
View from Everywhere
—developed and defended in this paper (§
5
)—challenges the very assumption of a frame-independent reality. Rather than interpreting reference frames as mere perspectives on an invariant underlying structure, this view holds that each frame-dependent representation constitutes a complete and fundamental physical reality. Within this paradigm, there is no ‘external’ standpoint from which all perspectives are unified; instead, reality is inherently local, constituted entirely by the relational observable content relative to each distinct frame.

Clarifying Terminology.

I introduce a set of distinctions that underpin the terminology employed throughout this paper.
In particular, I differentiate between non-relational and relational quantities. The former are categorised as (i)
spatiotemporally explicit
and (ii)
spatiotemporally implicit
; the latter as (I)
frame explicit
and (II)
frame implicit
. Each category admits further subdivision by locality and gauge status, as outlined below.
Furthermore, I emphasise the importance of carefully distinguishing between the notions of
invariance
,
independence
, and
freedom
.

Within the domain of
non-relational quantities
, we may distinguish between the following categories:

(i)

Spatiotemporally explicit quantities:
Defined by a single function over a region
U\subseteq\mathcal{M}
. They can be further subdivided into:

(i.a)

Spatiotemporally
local
:
Determined solely by the properties at a single
point
— formally, an infinitesimal region of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
with a zero
Lebesgue measure
. For example, the metric tensor
g_{ab}(p)
specifies geometry exactly at point
p
. Such objects are not gauge-invariant.

(i.b)

Spatiotemporally
non-local
:
Defined over an extended region of
\mathcal{M}
, typically as integrals. They depend on more than just localised pointwise data. An example is the total volume computed by integrating the metric over a region—this is non-local as it aggregates information across a domain. Such quantities are gauge-invariant.

(ii)

Spatiotemporally implicit quantities:
Not representable by a single function over
\mathcal{M}
, but only via a collection of local functions often forming as an equivalence class. Such quantities are conventionally denoted using square brackets, e.g
[g_{ab}]
and are gauge-invariant.

Introducing reference frames enables the construction of
relational quantities
.
For now, I simply define a reference frame as a set of four scalar fields
\phi^{(I)}\mid_{I=1,\dots,4}
wich
uniquely
assign to each point
p\in\mathcal{M}
a quadruple
x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. These can be viewed similarly to the coordinate maps of differential geometry, but with a physical instantiation and dynamical properties that cannot be neglected unless approximations are made.

It is possible to distinguish relational quantities, which are frame-dependent, between the following categories:

(I)

Frame explicit quantities:
Defined by a single function over a region parametrised by the reference frame. These include:

(I.A)

Frame
local
:
Determined solely by the properties at a single point
p:=\phi^{-1}(x)
, or an infinitesimal region
\phi(U)\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
, where
U
has zero Lebesgue measure. For example,
g_{IJ}(\phi)
represents metric components expressed in terms of the frame fields.
4
4

4

One might wonder whether
g_{IJ}(\phi)
is not a
functional
, since each
\phi
is a scalar field (a functions of point
p\in\mathcal{M}
). However, even though
\phi^{(I)}(p)
are scalar functions defined on the manifold, when we write
g_{IJ}(\phi)
we are expressing the metric components in terms of the values provided by each
\phi
at each point. In other words, each point
p
is mapped to a coordinate
x=\phi(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and
g
is a function that assigns the corresponding metric tensor
g_{IJ}
to each such
x
. This is different from a functional, which would take an
entire
function as input and yield a number. Thus,
g_{IJ}(\phi)
is a function, not a functional.
These quantities are gauge-invariant when the reference frame is dynamically coupled to the metric. Without coupling, they may remain relational but not fully gauge-invariant (see
Bamonti and Gomes 2024b
).

(I.B)

Frame
non-local
:
Defined over an extended region parametrised by the reference frame.
An example is the volume integral of a frame locally defined, gauge-invariant metric
g_{IJ}(\phi)
. Interestingly, also these quantities are gauge-invariant only if the reference frame is dynamically coupled. Otherwise, we could
independently
apply a diffeomorphism either to the integration extremal points defined by the frame or to the metric, resulting in the integral value changing (see, e.g., formulas 15 and 17 in
Rovelli 2014
).

(II)

Frame implicit quantities:
Not representable by a single function, but only as a collection of frame-local quantities—typically an equivalence class
In such a case, I still denote the equivalence class using square brackets as
[g_{IJ}]
, which is a gauge-invariant object. These are gauge-invariant and discussed further in Section
5
, where the relevant equivalence relation between elements is formalised.

N.B.
: Naturally, any frame explicit object is also spatiotemporally explicit, insofar as it is defined via relational locality through reference frames.
However, to maintain clarity, I draw a deliberate distinction between
frame-explicit
and
spatiotemporally explicit
quantities. The term ‘
spatiotemporal
’ is reserved for objects expressed directly in terms of manifold points (if it helps, the reader may also interpret a spatiotemporally explicit object as ‘
manifold explicit
’).
5
5

5

The concept of spacetime lacks a universally agreed definition, and its interpretation varies across different frameworks. It may be understood as: (i) the manifold
\mathcal{M}
; (ii) the combination
(\mathcal{M},g_{ab})
of the manifold equipped with the metric field representing the gravitational field; or (iii) the gravitational field
g_{ab}
alone, treating
\mathcal{M}
as a purely mathematical construct without ontological status.

Finally, building on
Wallace 2019
distinction between coordinate
independence
and
freedom
, I introduce the following classification concerning reference frames:

1.

Reference frame-dependence:
A quantity is frame-dependent if its definition explicitly relies on the choice of reference frame in which it is expressed.

2.

Reference frame-independence:
A quantity is frame-independent if its definition remains unaffected by the choice of reference frame, even if it is formulated within one.

3.

Reference frame-freedom:
A quantity is frame-free if it can be defined without invoking any reference frame at all.

2
Debunking Perspectivalism

Throughout this paper, I adopt the term
perspective
in the sense used by
Adlam 2024b
, namely
physical perspectivalism
, where a perspective is defined by a physical observer functioning as a reference frame.
This usage contrasts with alternative interpretations common in the philosophy of science. For instance,
Massimi 2022
conceives of perspectives as historically and culturally situated epistemic lenses, while
epistemic perspectivalism
refers to views in which a perspective denotes the partial knowledge of a conscious observer. These latter notions are not the focus here.

It is worth clarifying the connection I draw between the
View from Nowhere
and perspectivalism.
As noted by
Adlam 2024b
and others (e.g.
Ruyant 2020
), perspectivalists frequently endorse the mantra that ‘there is no view from nowhere’.
However, I contend that this slogan rests on a misuse of the term
perspective
.
A perspective, properly understood, is always a perspective
on
something that remains unchanged—that is, it presupposes a shared referent.
The
perspective neutral structure
introduced in
Vanrietvelde et al. 2020
exemplifies precisely this ‘invariant
something
’ upon which multiple perspectives are defined.

This misunderstanding of what constitutes a
perspective
carries important implications for the debate on physical perspectivalism.
Adlam advocates a moderate version, which concedes the existence of perspective-neutral facts but denies them empirical significance.
In this framework, the
View from Nowhere
is
not
rejected outright; it is simply stripped of its epistemic utility. As Adlam puts it:

It appears that considerations from quantum mechanics and relativity do provide support for the idea that empirically meaningful descriptions typically have to be relativized to a perspective. But this in and of itself does not tell us whether we should adopt moderate or strong physical perspectivalism. To make that choice, we must decide whether these considerations suggest that all facts about physical reality must be relativized to a perspective, or whether they instead support the existence of some perspective-neutral facts about physical reality, over and above descriptions relativized to perspectives
(
Adlam 2024b
, p.6)
.

Moderate perspectivalism thus envisions each perspective as a partial, situated glimpse into a more complete, though empirically inaccessible, reality.
This is also evident from Adlam’s claim that moderate perspectivalism is motivated by the
epistemic humility
typically associated with epistemic perspectivalism—the idea that our knowledge is inherently limited by our standpoint.
As such, the moderate perspectivalist endorses a kind of
ontological humility
, acknowledging an underlying structure while denying full access to it.

By contrast, the strong version of perspectivalism denies the
existence
of any perspective-neutral facts: there are only perspectives.
This view, however, collapses under its own definition: if we understand (as I do) a perspective as always a perspective
on
something, then to deny that
something
is to dismiss the very concept of a perspective.
On this basis, I maintain that, so conceived, the strong view is conceptually incoherent.
It is therefore moderate perspectivalism—the only coherent form—that will be critically examined in this paper as the philosophical basis to the View from Nowhere.
7
7

7

Adlam’s analysis does not clearly distinguish between
perspective-independence
and
perspective-freedom
, relying instead on the ambiguous notion of ‘neutrality’. This ambiguity leads her to interpret any structural connection between perspectives as incompatible with strong perspectivalism. However, as I will argue in §
5
, if such connections are understood as perspective-
independent
facts, the purported incompatibility dissolves.

My critique of moderate perspectivalism will set the stage for the
View from Everywhere
developed in this paper.
While moderate perspectivalism retains the
existence
of an underlying ‘objective’ structure, albeit one without empirical significance, the View from Everywhere directly challenges this assumption.
It thus holds that frame-dependent observables in GR are not merely incomplete perspectives on a more (or equally) fundamental structure; they
are
the only fundamental structure. Each reference frame constitutes a distinct, fully-fledged physical reality. No ‘meta-empirical vantage point’ exists from which all frames can be globally reconciled. No God’s view is allowed
(
van Fraassen 2008
)
.

This philosophical shift requires a formal framework that treats reference frames as internal to the theory rather than imposed externally. The next section introduces a formal framework, the fibre bundle formalism, which neatly offers a mathematically precise basis for constructing relational, local gauge-invariant observables.
There, reference frames will be characterised as sections of a principal bundle, thereby enhancing their role as an internal element of the theory.

3
The Fibre Bundle Formalism: A Gauge Perspective on Reference Frames

In this section, I introduce the fibre bundle formalism to characterise reference frames and relational observables. Widely used in foundational studies of gauge theories (see e.g.
Healey 2007
;
Weatherall 2016
), this formalism has been further developed in the context of GR by
Gomes 2023a
;
Gomes 2023b
.
Let
M
denote the space of models
m
of the theory. This space can be structured as a
fibre bundle
with
S
as its structure group and
[M]:=\{[m]\mid m\in M\}
as its base space, where each equivalence class
[m]
contains models related by
\mathcal{S}
-transformations (the action of the symmetry group
\mathcal{S}
).

In this formalism, selecting a reference frame corresponds to defining a smooth
section map

\sigma:[m]\to\sigma([m])\in M
, which selects a
single
representative model within each equivalence class.
Geometrically, this amounts to specifying a subspace of
M
that intersects each
fibre

\mathbb{F}_{m}:=\mathrm{pr}^{-1}([m])

exactly
once, where
\mathrm{pr}:m\in M\to[m]\in[M]
is the
projection map
.

This formalism naturally aligns with the structure of gauge theories, since selecting a reference frame is often understood as fixing a gauge (see
Dittrich 2007
;
Bamonti and Thébault 2025
for applications in FLRW cosmology).
Indeed, choosing a gauge is formally equivalent to selecting a section in the fibre bundle.
In gauge-theoretic contexts, the symmetry group
S
generates
gauge orbits
: sets of models related by gauge transformations generated by the first-class constraints of the theory.
Each fibre in the bundle corresponds to one such orbit, with a one-to-one correspondence between each gauge orbit and the equivalence class of models under the action of
S
.
More concretely, for a model
m\in M
and
s\in S
, the associated orbit is defined as
\mathcal{O}_{m}=\{m_{s}\mid s\in S\}
, where
m_{s}
denotes the model obtained by applying the transformation
s
to
m
.
Since the theory is symmetric under
S
, these models are physically equivalent: each orbit contains redundant, isomorphic representations of the same physical state.
8
8

8

Gauge freedom need not be dismissed as merely ‘descriptive fluff’
(
Earman 2004
)
. Rather, the interplay between gauge-fixing and reference frame choice may reveal the relational structure of physics: gauge-variant degrees of freedom act as formal ‘handles’ enabling the construction of relational, gauge-invariant observables
(
Rovelli 2014
;
Adlam 2024a
)
.

The physical content of the theory is therefore encoded in any single representative per orbit, which is precisely what the section map—or gauge choice—selects.
In GR, this corresponds to fixing a reference frame through a coordinate gauge condition (see
Bamonti 2023
;
Gomes 2024b
).

To make these ideas more concrete, consider the fibre bundle of Lorentzian geometries.
Let
M=\text{Lor}(\mathcal{M})
denote the set of all Lorentzian metrics
g_{ab}\in\mathcal{M}
.
I restrict attention to ‘vacuum’ GR, so each model can be written as a pair
\langle\mathcal{M},g_{ab}\rangle
.
9
9

9

A more general model taking into account matter
m=\langle\mathcal{M},g_{ab},\phi\rangle
consists of a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, a (Lorentzian) metric
g_{ab}
, and some matter field
\phi
.

The bundle structure arises from the action of the diffeomorphism group
\mathcal{S}=\text{Diff}(\mathcal{M})
and the base space is the set of equivalence classes
[\text{Lor}(\mathcal{M})]:=\{[g_{ab}],g_{ab}\in\text{Lor}(\mathcal{M})\}
.
10
10

10

In GR, defining a (even local) product structure
[\text{Lor}(\mathcal{M})]\times\text{Diff}(\mathcal{M})
is generally hindered by so-called
Gribov obstructions

(
Gribov 1978
;
Henneaux and Teitelboim 1994
)
.

Concretely, each equivalence class
[g_{ab}]
consists of all diffeomorphism-related metrics:

[g_{ab}]:=\{g_{ab},(d^{*}g)_{ab},\dots\},\quad d\in\rm Diff(\mathcal{M}).

(1)

Consider four scalar quantities,
\Re^{(I)}_{g}
,
I=1,\ldots,4
, defined as curvature invariants constructed from the metric
g_{ab}
.
These are often known as
Kretschmann-Komar scalars
, named after
Kretschmann 1918
and
Komar 1958
(see also
Bergmann and Komar 1960
;
Bergmann and Komar 1962
).
11
11

11

Komar 1958
) derived these scalar invariants via an eigenvalue problem involving the Riemann tensor
R_{abcd}
and an anti-symmetric auxiliary tensor
V_{cd}
:
R_{abcd}-(g_{ac}g_{bd}+g_{ad}g_{bc})V_{cd}=0
.

The set
\{\Re^{(I)}_{g}\}
defines a
local
diffeomorphism:

\Re_{g}^{(I)}:=(\Re_{g}^{(1)},\cdots\Re_{g}^{(4)}):U\subseteq\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4},

(2)

which assigns a
unique
quadruple of real values to each point in a neighbourhood
U\subseteq\mathcal{M}
.
This set provides a spatiotemporal reference frame for the metric field itself, aligning with the relational strategy: “rather than fixing an observable at specific coordinates, its location is defined relative to features of the state”
(
Harlow and qiang Wu 2021
)
.

Within this reference frame, tensors such as the metric
g_{ab}
can be locally expressed in frame-dependent, but gauge-invariant, form. Specifically, for all isomorphic models, we define:

g_{IJ}(\Re_{g}):=\left[\Re_{g}^{-1}\right]^{*}g_{ab},

(3)

which yields a set of 10 scalar functions indexed by
I
and
J
, constructed from the metric and its derivatives.
12
12

12

For the set
\{\Re_{g}^{(I)}\}
to define a
viable
reference frame, they must be at least locally invertible, which requires
linear independence
of their gradients. This ensures that the Jacobian is non-zero. This condition may fail in spacetimes with continuous symmetries (e.g., those admitting Killing vectors).
Importantly, linear independence (a pointwise condition) differs from
functional independence
(a global condition): a set of scalars may be linearly independent, even if functionally dependent, and still serve as a viable, local reference frame.

The quantity
g_{IJ}(\Re_{g})
is invariant under diffeomorphisms, as follows from the transformation properties of each
\Re_{g}
:

\forall d\in\rm Diff(\mathcal{M}),\quad[\Re_{g}^{-1}]^{*}g_{ab}=[\big(d^{*}\Re_{g}\big)^{-1}]^{*}(d^{*}g)_{ab}.

(4)

Since the scalars
\Re_{g}^{(I)}
are constructed from the metric, they evolve consistently with it: given initial data for
g_{ab}
, the dynamical evolution of
g_{IJ}(\Re_{g})
is
uniquely determined
, ensuring its gauge-invariance, even though
g_{IJ}(\Re_{g})
is explicitly frame-dependent.
Explicitly, diffeomorphisms must act
diagonally
to preserve solutionhood: if
(g_{ab},\Re_{g}^{(I)})
is a possible solution, then
only

\big((d^{*}g)_{ab},d^{*}\Re_{g}^{(I)}\big)
is a still a possible solution.
This reflects the fact that the reference frame is
dynamically coupled
to the field whose observables it helps define.

The choice of
\Re_{g}
as a reference frame can be formalised as a gauge choice, understood as the choice of a section in the fibre bundle formulation of GR.
This amounts to imposing a condition that selects a unique representative from each equivalence class and it is achieved by defining a smooth function
F_{\Re_{g}}\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
, such that:

\forall g_{ab}\in Lor,\exists!f_{\Re_{g}}\in\rm Diff(M)\quad|\quad F_{\Re_{g}}(g_{ab})=0.

(5)

Here,
F_{\Re_{g}}
defines the section, and the diffeomorphism
f_{\Re_{g}}:g_{ab}\to f_{\Re_{g}}^{*}g_{ab}
acts as a projection operator onto the image of that section.
13
13

13

The projection corresponding to the choice of a reference frame can be written down in terms of coordinate charts. For instance, in GR two common gauge-fixings are the De Donder gauge in the Lagrangian sector which corresponds to the condition
F(g)=\partial_{\mu}(g^{\mu\nu}\sqrt{g})=0
, and the CMC gauge in the Hamiltonian sector:
F(h^{ij},\pi_{ij})=h^{ij}\pi_{ij}=const
. The former corresponds to the use of coordinates that satisfy a relativistic wave equation
\Box x^{\mu}=0
. The latter, selects global simultaneity 3-hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{\tau}
, parametrised by a universal time
\tau
. Notice that both are
only partial
gauge-fixings.

Concretely, this map identifies the unique representative within each fibre satisfying the gauge condition. (
5
).
It thus serves as an embedding from the fibre bundle manifold of models onto the submanifold defined by the
image
of the section map.
Geometrically, the condition
F_{\Re_{g}}(g_{ab})=0
defines a ‘level surface’ along each fibre: the submanifold where the reference frame condition holds.
Thus, selecting a reference frame amounts to selecting such a hypersurface across the bundle. This construction closely parallels standard gauge-fixing procedures in gauge theory, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

Proof.

From the diagonal action of the diffeomorphisms

4.1
The Philosophical Foundations of The View from Nowhere: Sophistication

The
View from Nowhere
has its conceptual foundation in the so-called
Sophistication
approach to symmetries, which stands in marked contrast to
Eliminativism
(or reductionism)

According to
Eliminativism
, a theory should be reformulated solely in terms of its symmetry-invariant quantities—typically expressed as equivalence classes—thereby eliminating all redundant, symmetry-related models from its ontology.
By contrast,
Sophistication
adopts a structuralist stance: it retains the full set of symmetry-related models, treating them as isomorphic representations of a single underlying structure that carries genuine ontological significance. As
Jacobs 2021
(drawing on Klein’s Erlangen Programme
Klein 1893
) explains, Sophistication is best characterised as a
symmetry-first
(or
external
) approach (see also
Dewar 2019a
).

A key philosophical issue concerns how to justify treating symmetry-related models as physically equivalent. As
Møller-Nielsen 2017
outlines, two interrelated strategies have emerged: the
interpretational approach
and the
motivational approach
.

The interpretational approach maintains that physical equivalence can only be granted once a clear and explicit—
perspicuous
—characterisation of the invariant ontology is provided. Only by specifying the intrinsic structure shared across models can one justify their equivalence.

In contrast, the motivational approach adopts a more pragmatic and less ontologically demanding stance. It requires only a compelling reason for treating the models as equivalent, even if an exhaustive intrinsic description of the shared structure is unavailable.

In this light, Eliminativism aligns naturally with the interpretational approach, as it seeks to eliminate all non-invariant features in favour of a purely invariant formulation.
Sophistication, by contrast, tends to align with the motivational approach: it accepts the physical equivalence of symmetry-related models without requiring a full intrinsic account of their shared invariant structure.

Nevertheless, proponents of Sophistication—most notably Dewar—argue that the interpretational standard can still be met.

Dewar 2019a
maintains that the symmetries themselves, by revealing the underlying invariant structure, are sufficient to warrant ontological commitment to the space
[M]
of equivalence classes, even in the absence of a fully specified intrinsical characterisation.

This position, however, has been met with criticism.

Martens and Read 2020
, for example, argue that without a perspicuous account of the structure shared across models, Sophistication amounts to a
cheap
strategy: one that posits structure without clearly articulating its content.

Responding to this critique,
Gomes 2024a
defends Sophistication by introducing reference frames—termed
representational conventions
.
In his work, these are formalised using the fibre bundle formalism via a projection operator
f_{\sigma}:M\to M
within the fibre bundle structure of the model space
M
, as I showed above.
According to Gomes, this construction
does
provide a
perspicuous
(as gauge-invariant), albeit choice-dependent, characterisation of
[M]
.
The adoption of
f_{\sigma}

in lieu
of the section map
\sigma:[M]\to M
supports the Sophistication claim that an intrinsic parametrisation of elements
[m]\in[M]
is unnecessary.
Gomes’ approach thus aims to reconcile both interpretational and motivational strategies. By showing that the answer to the motivational question—
why
symmetry-related models represent the same physical reality—can also provide a perspicuous account of the underlying structure, he seeks to shield Sophistication from accusations of being ‘cheap’.

Nonetheless, I will argue that Gomes’ approach, while insightful, remains ultimately insufficient (see §
5.2
).
Even if one characterises each equivalence class by selecting a ‘representative’ — a relational, gauge-invariant observable
f_{\sigma}^{*}(\bullet)
— this does
not
justify the underlying ontological commitment to the equivalence classes themselves.
In effect, selecting a representative merely offers one
perspective
on the broader structure. It does not resolve the deeper question of what, if anything, the equivalence class
[m]\in M
actually is.
As such, the
full
ontology of the theory remains insufficiently perspicuously and, arguably, inadequately specified.

4.2
Empirical Challenges to the View from Nowhere

Although philosophically coherent, the
View from Nowhere
faces serious challenges in experimental contexts. Its abstract, frame-free ontology misaligns with the relational character of empirical data, which inherently depend on specific reference frames. As a result, translating this theoretical framework into an operationally meaningful discourse becomes a major challenge.

The relational nature of empirical measurement has long been recognised in the literature. Observations rely on relationships between the system under investigation and the reference frame in which measurements are performed.

Anderson 1967
emphasises that all measurements fundamentally involve comparisons between physical systems.
Similar claims are made by
Rovelli 1991
and
Landau and Lifshitz 1987
, who underscore the indispensable role of reference frames in any measurement process.
Further support for this relational stance comes from the
Unobservability Thesis
, which holds that symmetry-related models are empirically indistinguishable
(
Wallace 2022
)
.
Related discussions suggest that the inclusion of observers (namely, reference frames) yields an
immanent
conception of empirical distinctness, whereby models are empirically distinguishable only if exhibit relational difference
(
Pooley and Read 2021
)
.
This reliance on reference frames underscores a central difficulty for the View from Nowhere: its abstract, frame-free ontology stands in tension with the operational structure of empirical science, which is inherently frame-dependent.

This operational structure aligns closely with Einstein’s original articulation of the point-coincidence argumen, twhich asserts that the physical content of a theory lies in the spacetime coincidences of material points (see
Giovanelli 2021
).
Specifically, Einstein stressed that spacetime
verifications
invariably amount to determining such coincidences
Einstein 1916
and that physical
experiences
are always assessments of these pointwise relations
Einstein 1919
.
21
21

21

For a detailed operational interpretation of Einstein’s point-coincidence argument, see
Bamonti and Gomes 2024b
.

These arguments strongly support the idea that observables are inherently relational, casting doubt on the adequacy of a frame-free ontology.
Attributing ontological significance solely (or even
also
, as in moderate perspectivalism) to abstract equivalence classes poses serious obstacles for experimental validation. Addressing these challenges requires more than simply
asserting
the existence of frame-free, gauge-invariant structures; proponents of the View from Nowhere must provide practical guidance for experiment-based characterisation of these structures—a daunting task, in my opinion.
22
22

22

Building on
Martens and Read 2020
criticism on Sophistication (see above), I regard moderate perspectivalism’s
assertion
of the physical reality of a frame-free structure—without engaging in the explanation of its empirical meaning—as a
cheap
proposal.

4.3
Loss of Physical Information and the Limits of Equivalence Classes

I have already raised many of my qualms about the
View from Nowhere
, and in the following section I will propose an alternative interpretation of the formalism of relational observables.
Before that, however, I wish to address a further issue: the potential loss of physical information that occurs when local, frame-dependent observables are regarded merely as perspectives on an equivalence class.

By
physical information
I refer to the set of empirically meaningful, gauge-invariant observables—primarily local in character—that are defined from within a given reference frame.
What makes such quantities physically informative is not only their gauge invariance, but also their empirical significance: they yield testable, spatiotemporally situated measurement outcomes.

The View from Nowhere treats these observables as perspectives on a more fundamental invariant structure—namely, the equivalence class—which purportedly encapsulates
the entirety
of physical information.
23
23

23

This issue is vividly captured by what Tong says about the choice of a gauge:

The [gauge] redundancy allows us to make manifest the properties of quantum field theories, such as unitarity, locality, and Lorentz invariance, that we feel are vital for any fundamental theory of physics but which teeter on the verge of incompatibility. If we try to remove the redundancy by fixing some specific gauge, some of these properties will be brought into focus, while others will retreat into murk. By retaining the redundancy, we can flit between descriptions as is our want, keeping whichever property we most cherish in clear sight.
(
Tong 2018
, p.1)

It is essential, however, to distinguish this case from that of non-local, frame-
independent
observables, such as volume integrals like
\int\limits_{\phi(U)}\sqrt{\rm det(g_{IJ}(\phi))}d^{4}\phi
.
Although such quantities have a clear empirical significance within each frame, the measurement outcomes they provide remain invariant across all reference frames.
As such, they do not constitute a ‘perspective’in the same way local relational observables do.
In this sense, the loss of physical information associated with frame-explicit content applies specifically to local, frame-dependent observables, not to non-local, frame-independent ones (refer to Tab
1
for terminology).

To summarise, this section underscored both the conceptual and empirical limitations in the View from Nowhere, though further exploration may reveal additional nuances.
In the next section, I introduce an alternative perspective—the
View from Everywhere
—which seeks to
perspicuously
characterise the theory’s invariant ontology without resorting to equivalence classes, as the sophisticated approach advocates.
This view treats each frame not as a perspective on a more fundamental structure, but as a complete and self-contained physical reality, thereby also avoiding the problem of informational loss altogether.

5
The View from Everywhere: Frame Representations Are All That Exists

Revisiting the two GPS reference frames discussed earlier, we may now offer an alternative interpretation of the two local observables
g_{IJ}(\phi_{b})
and
g_{IJ}(\phi_{r})
.
Rather than treating these as mere perspectives on an underlying equivalence class
[g_{ab}]
, as the View from Nowhere prescribes, the View from Everywhere regards
each
observable as a distinct, gauge-invariant, and empirically accessible physical world, defined by a specific reference frame.

This position is captured by the following definition:

The View from Everywhere
:
Each
frame-dependent, gauge-invariant representation constitutes a complete and self-contained physical reality. Reference frames define distinct relational worlds, not mere perspectives on an underlying invariant structure. This may also be conceived as a
view from each reference frame
.

In

m
and called

\{\phi_{r}^{(I)}\}
and
\{\phi_{b}^{(I)}\}

This map functions analogously to a coordinate transformation, but with an important difference: unlike an
ordinary

By identifying
\phi_{r}^{(I)}:=X^{I}_{r}
and
\phi_{b}^{(I)}:=X^{I^{\prime}}_{b}
, the gauge-invariant observables
g_{IJ}(\phi_{r}):=g_{IJ}(X^{I}_{r})

g_{IJ}(\phi_{b}):=g_{I^{\prime}J^{\prime}}(X^{I^{\prime}}_{b})
are related by the external diffeomorphism
m
, which acts as follows:

\text{\bf{m}}:g_{IJ}(X^{I}_{r})\rightarrow g_{I^{\prime}J^{\prime}}(X^{I^{\prime}}_{b})=\cfrac{\partial X^{I}_{r}}{\partial X^{I^{\prime}}_{b}}\cfrac{\partial X^{J}_{r}}{\partial X^{J^{\prime}}_{b}}g_{IJ}(X^{I}_{r}).

(8)

Clearly, this transformation is analogous to to a

24

24

g_{IJ}(\phi_{r})

. This transformation, however, is not universally defined for all scalar fields: its validity depends on the absence of self-interactions that could obstruct invertibility (e.g., via singular Jacobians). This is particularly true when scalars backreact on the metric.

This illustrates that in GR,
local
gauge-invariant observables are
covariant
under changes of reference frame.
Accordingly, , the adoption of a gauge choice—in the form of a specific reference frame—does not compromise the covariance of the theory. Rather, it reveals a deeper form of covariance: one that holds not merely across coordinate systems, but across reference frames themselves.

Although the map
m
provides a ‘shared vocabulary’ for translating between observables, these correspond to distinct and

25

25

According to the View from Everywhere, there is no deeper, invariant structure to which frame-dependent, local observables refer. Under this view, no invariant, frame-free structure is ontologically required.

Even this ontology is explicitly local and frame-dependent, it remains compatible with the existence of frame-independent quantities, since each representation within a specific frame is still not reduced to a perspective on the frame-independent structure, but it remains fundamental.

Importantly, the absence of a shared, total reality in the View from Everywhere—replaced by a plurality of local, frame-dependent realities—does not entail conceptual incoherence.

Fragmentation does not imply incoherence.

The different physical situations represented by the gauge-invariant observables
g_{IJ}(\phi_{r})
and

This inter-frame structure also addresses potential concerns about

Each observer—identified with a reference frame—is not isolated in their representation of observable reality.
Communication between observers remains possible: through the map
m

Crucially, however, this does not imply that each observer merely accesses a perspective of a more complete, overarching reality.

Moreover, it is crucial to distinguish here between

The existence of inter-frame mappings such as

As stated above, this does not contradict the principles of the

5.1
Relality

The existence of the external diffeomorphism
m
enables advocates of the View from Everywhere to define an equivalence class
[g_{IJ}]:=\{g_{IJ}(\phi_{r}),g_{IJ}(\phi_{b}),\dots\}
, which differs fundamentally from the equivalence class
[g_{ab}]:=\{g_{ab},(d^{*}g)_{ab},\dots\}
employed by the View from Nowhere.
I adopt the view that the equivalence class
[g_{IJ}]

supervenes
on its constituent frame-dependent observables in a
strongly reductive
sense: nothing exists ‘over and above’ the local realities that constitute it.
28
28

28

This aligns with standard forms of ontological reductionism (or, in some contexts,
eliminativism
, or
fictionalism
).
According to this positions, higher-level (supervenient) properties do not exist as independent entities, rather they emerge from systematic patterns within the underlying base-level entities.
However, this anti-realist stance is not intrinsic to the concept of supervenience
per se
. Authors such as
Butterfield 2011
and
Dewar 2019b
advocate a neutral stance on the ontological status of supervenient entities, neither affirming nor denying their reality.
According to this stance, supervenience is a formal relation of dependency, not a metaphysical claim about the existence of a deeper ontological hierarchy. For an overview of the notion of supervenience, see
McLaughlin and Bennett 2023
.

That is,
[g_{IJ}]
does not represent a more fundamental unified ontology, but a structural shorthand for the interrelation among local, frame-dependent observables, which are the only
bona-fide
physical realities.

It is worth noting that
[g_{IJ}]
is a frame-independent object in that it spans all possible reference frames. So, differently from
[g_{ab}]
, it is not a frame-free object.

Formally
, the View from Everywhere retains a one-to-one correspondence between
[g_{IJ}]
and the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{g}:=\{g_{IJ}(\phi_{r}),g_{IJ}(\phi_{b}),\cdots\}
.
However, unlike
[g_{ab}]
, the class
[g_{IJ}]
does not exist independently of its elements; it simply
is
the collection of its elements, i.e. the models
m\in M
.
In this context, a ‘model’ refers to a fully constructed local observable.
Essentially,
[g_{IJ}]
and
\mathcal{O}_{g}

coincide
. See Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
The set
[M]
of equivalence classes is part of the space of models itself, underscoring that
[g_{IJ}]
is not an independent structure but supervenes on the models themselves. The fibre
is
the collection of frame-dependent observables, connected by elements of the group
\rm Diff(\mathbb{R}^{4})
, namely the external diffeomorphisms
m
.

Whereas in the View from Nowhere the equivalence class
[g_{ab}]
expresses a
shared ontology
, in the View from Everywhere, the equivalence class
[g_{IJ}]
provides a shared vocabulary across distinct realities.

This distinction marks a decisive conceptual break.
The View from Everywhere denies the existence of any frame-free structure and instead grounds ontology in the plurality of
local
, frame-dependent observables.
Accordingly, the term
perspective
becomes misleading in this context, since it implies a single underlying reality that is represented from different standpoints—an assumption the View from Everywhere does not adopt.

I refer to the collection of such local realities as
Relality
.
This term encapsulates the formal construction of
[g_{IJ}]
which, as mentioned above, does
not
denote an ontologically independent collective reality beyond the individual frame-dependent realities themselves.
So, Relality only names the idea that these local, fundamental realities are mutually translatable via the map
m
; that is, they are interconnected, though ontologically distinct.

For this reason,
Relality
should not be conflated with a ‘first-person plural’ perspective, introduced in some versions of epistemic perspectivalism, where a collective viewpoint is seen as more complete than individual ones.
In fact, there is really no ‘something’ that can be ‘something more’ than the individual frame-dependent realities,
neither epistemically nor ontologically
.
Moreover,
Relality
makes no ontological claims about frame-independent, non-local observables; it pertains strictly to local, frame-dependent ones.
This clarification also helps address a potential objection of circularity in the definition of the map
m
. In fact, one might worry that
m
presupposes the frame-independent, collective structure
[g_{IJ}]
for its definition, which in turn presupposes the existence of
m
in order to be defined.
However, this objection misunderstands the nature of
m
, whose frame-independence simply reflects that it is defined consistently relative to
any
frame—without invoking a collective structure relative to which it is defined.

Drawing on the Kantian perspective I used above to frame the View from Nowhere, I argue that the View from Everywhere fundamentally rejects the notion of a shared, implicit noumenal reality that underlies phenomenal manifestations. There is no ‘empirically hidden’, frame-free structure beyond frame-dependent observables.

The interpretative framework offered by the View from Everywhere directly challenges the core assumptions of the Sophistication approach. In what follows, I argue that the View from Everywhere achieves greater
ontological parsimony
, avoiding unnecessary metaphysical commitments, while also providing a more perspicuous characterisation of the ontology underpinning relational observables. For these reasons, I contend that Sophistication should not be regarded as the final or most adequate framework for interpreting the formalism of gauge-invariant relational quantities.

5.2
Beyond Sophistication

As previously noted, the Sophistication approach holds that physical reality must be fundamentally described in terms of equivalence classes. The View from Everywhere directly challenges this assumption, arguing that frame-dependent observables—such as
g_{IJ}(\phi_{r})
and
g_{IJ}(\phi_{b})
—are distinct, fully-fledged physical scenarios. No further ‘empirically hidden’ structure need be posited beyond these frame-dependent ‘worlds’.

This interpretation accords with a principle of
ontological parsimony
, which I endorse.
The View from Everywhere is ontologically more conservative, committing only to what is expressible within individual reference frames.
If parsimony is understood as minimising the number of elements in one’s ontological inventory, then the View from Nowhere is
less
parsimonious.
It requires positing at least two categories of entities: the frame-dependent perspectives and the equivalence class they are said to represent.
This ontological duality is explicit in moderate perspectivalism, where both perspectives and the equivalence class are treated as equally fundamental (cf. fn.
17
), but it remains implicit even when only the equivalence class is considered ontologically primary (that is the position I strictly attribute to the View from Nowhere).
In that case, the perspectives—while not fundamental—still exist as physically meaningful, gauge-invariant observables.
By contrast, the View from Everywhere entails a single ontological commitment: the existence of each individual, frame-dependent reality. For the sake of clarity, it is worth repeating that
Relality
, as previously clarified, is not an entity in its own right, but a structural expression of the inter-translatability between local realities. It encodes no metaphysical surplus.

As discussed in §
4.1
, combining Sophistication with a motivational strategy raises difficulties, especially in meeting the demand for a
perspicuous
characterisation of ontology.

Among recent proposals, Gomes’ motivational strategy is especially promising; yet, I maintain that within the framework of Sophistication, it ultimately falls short, because selecting a representative from a gauge orbit does not justify the (non-parsimonious) ontological commitment to the equivalence class itself.
However, when reinterpreted through the lens of the View from Everywhere, Gomes’ approach gains renewed strength.
By dispensing with the need for a shared ontology grounded in equivalence classes, the very construction of the gauge-invariant, frame-dependent observables
already
provides a perspicuous account of the ontology.

This raises a subtle question: how can Gomes’ method—based on projecting gauge-variant models onto fibres—be accommodated within a framework where the only relevant ‘fibre’ consists of gauge-
invariant
observables connected by an external diffeomorphism
m
(see Figure
3
)?
At first glance, this might suggest that the View from Everywhere amounts to a version of Eliminativism, thereby excluding Gomes’ proposal altogether.

This conclusion, however, is incorrect. The View from Everywhere fully accommodates the
formal construction
of frame-dependent observables within the standard fibre bundle formalism.
For instance, a frame-dependent observable like
g_{IJ}(\phi_{r})
, can still be constructed by projecting a gauge-variant model onto a section of the bundle.
Endorsing the View from Everywhere does
not
require
removing
gauge structures from the formalism. Rather, it withholds ontological commitment from these structures,
including equivalence classes
. Nonetheless, equivalence classes, along with projection maps and fibres, remain
essential
for constructing frame-dependent observables within the fibre bundle framework.

5.3
Physical Information, Symmetry, and the Empirical Significance of Reference Frames in the View from Everywhere

Previously, I outlined one key advantage of the View from Everywhere: its ability to provide a
perspicuous
characterisation of the ontology of the theory, in line with a motivational approach.
Here, I outline two further benefits.
First, it avoids both the loss of physical information characteristic of the View from Nowhere (§
4.3
) and the breaking of diffeomorphic symmetry that often occurs when a specific reference frame—or gauge—is selected.
Second, it reinterprets the nature of reference frame choice, not as a conventional or pragmatic selection (as in the View from Nowhere), but as an empirical and ontologically meaningful act.

I. No Symmetry Breaking and No Information Loss

A common worry in gauge theories is that fixing a gauge—or selecting a specific reference frame—may break the theory’s underlying symmetries. of the theory.
The View from Everywhere defuses this concern through the external diffeomorphism
m
, which relates different reference frame choices and ensures GR remains fully
m
-covariant.

Moreover, according to the notion of
Relality
introduced earlier, all physically meaningful information is already encoded in each frame-dependent, gauge-invariant observable.
There is no need to posits a ‘total’ or ‘complete’ structure to account for missing content.
Even frame-independent quantities, such as volume integrals
{\int\limits_{\phi(U)}\sqrt{\text{det}(g_{IJ}(\phi))}d^{4}\phi}
should not be understood as a ‘repository’ of more complete information.

II. The Nature of Frame Choice: From Convention to Reality

A further strength of the View from Everywhere lies in its treatment of reference frame choice, which is entirely arbitrary, raising the question of what guides it.

It is often claimed that reference frames are chosen for pragmatic or conventional reasons, for instance to simplify calculations or provide more intuitive descriptions of a shared physical reality.
This stance is perfectly in line with the View from Nowhere, in which the choice of reference frame merely offers a
perspectival
description.

By contrast, in the View from Everywhere, the choice of reference frame is
empirical

This means that, since each frame-dependent, gauge-invariant observable specifies a distinct physical reality, the choice of frame is thus ontologically constitutive: it determines what exists.
This reconceptualisation of frame choice also addresses the apparent lack of empirical means for validating a chosen reference frame.
Since all measurements occur within some frame, then there is no
frame-free

However, since each reference frame corresponds to a complete reality, there is no need for cross-frame

6
Conclusion

This paper has employed the fibre bundle framework as a tool for analysing the role and interpretation of reference frames and relational observables in GR, with a particular focus on two contrasting paradigms: the
View from Nowhere
and the
View from Everywhere
.
Below, I summarise the key findings and outline directions for future research.

Section
2
clarified the concept of
perspective
, arguing it inherently presupposes an invariant, perspective-neutral structure upon which perspectives are defined.
This directly challenges the common perspectivalist assertion that ‘there is no view from nowhere,’ demonstrating that such slogans stem from a conceptual misuse of the term. Both strong and weak perspectivalism were examined, concluding that strong perspectivalism is incoherent, as it negates the essential referent of a perspective, and only moderate perspectivalism remains a viable option.

In Section
3
, I introduced the fibre bundle formalism, where the space of models is structured as a fibre bundle and gauge orbits encode the theory’s symmetry.
Reference frames, construed as section maps, enable the construction of gauge-invariant relational observables and clarify the link between reference frame choice and gauge-fixing.

In Section
4
, I developed the
View from Nowhere
, which interprets frame-dependent observables as mere
perspectives
.
Grounded in the
Sophistication
approach to symmetry, this view locates fundamental ontology in abstract, frame-free structures: the equivalence classes of models.
Although this framework, which moderate perspectivalism adheres to, demonstrates coherence, it struggles with empirical adequacy, as it appeals to observables that lack empirical accessibility.

Section
5
, presented the
View from Everywhere
, which rejects the assumption to assign ontological significance to frame-free structures and instead upholds frame-dependent, gauge-invariant observables as the only fundamental realities.
To express this alternative, the concepts of
Relality
and the
external diffeomorphism

m
were introduced, highlighting the possibility of inter-translatability between frame-dependent, local realities.
By adhering to a principle of ontological parsimony, the View from Everywhere avoids unnecessary metaphysical commitments to frame-free structures and naturally provides a
perspicuous
characterisation of the ontology, with significant implications for both theoretical structure and experimental viability.
This enables it to refine and ultimately supersedes the Sophistication approach on symmetries,
while retaining its structural insights and motivational spirit.
In addition, the View from Everywhere dissolves concerns about the loss of physical information and loss of covariance, commonly associated with gauge-fixing.
Crucially, it redefines frame choice: not as a pragmatic convenience, but as an ontologically determinative act.

These findings open several promising avenues for further investigation.
A deeper philosophical analysis of the concept of
Relality
may illuminate its implications for
objectivity
and
perspectivalism
in GR.
Additionally, exploring the role of external diffeomorphisms in the context of quantum reference frames may offer a bridge between this classical analysis and the quantum theory.

In conclusion, this paper has examined the complex interplay between reference frames, gauge symmetries, and ontology in GR.
By contrasting the View from Nowhere and the View from Everywhere, I have sought to clarify the conceptual foundations of relational observables and their interpretation.
The analysis underscores the indispensable role of reference frames in articulating physical reality and invites for continued philosophical and formal inquiry into their foundational status within modern physics.
