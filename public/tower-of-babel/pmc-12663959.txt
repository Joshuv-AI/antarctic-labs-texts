Alleviation of negative emotional and pain responses through amygdala-hypothalamic social circuit activation
Authors: Xinqi Lu, Guohua Chen, Weizhe Hong, Shuaishuai Hu, Rongfeng K. Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pain is a multifaceted experience that not only impacts individuals physiologically but also reshapes their emotional well-being and social dynamics. The expression of pain and postpain emotional states is profoundly influenced by social contexts. Recent research has highlighted the pivotal role of social rewards—including empathy, support, and acceptance—in modulating both pain perception and subsequent negative emotional responses. However, how direct manipulation of brain circuits that govern social behaviors and social rewards impacts pain and negative emotional responses remains largely unexplored. Here, we investigated whether direct activation of a newly identified noncanonical social reward circuit—specifically, the projections from the medial amygdala (MeA) to the medial preoptic area (MPOA)—can mitigate pain-related emotional and behavioral responses. Our findings demonstrated that both optogenetic and chemogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons in the MeA significantly reduced freezing and anxiety-like behaviors after shock period as well as pain responses in the hotplate test in both male and female mice. Furthermore, optogenetic activation of the MeA-to-MPOA circuit produced similar behavioral outcomes. Together, our study expands the understanding of neural circuits underlying pain modulation and highlights the critical need to incorporate social and psychological factors into therapeutic approaches designed to improve human health and well-being.

Pain, defined by its unpleasant sensory and emotional qualities, is often followed by the development of negative emotional states (1–3). Both the initial pain and these subsequent emotional states are profoundly influenced by a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors (4). Previous studies have demonstrated that social rewards—derived from positive social interactions—are linked to the release of neurochemicals such as dopamine and endogenous opioids, which can reduce pain sensitivity and enhance feelings of well-being (1, 3, 5). In contrast, negative social experiences—or social isolation—can exacerbate pain and emotional responses, increasing sensitivity, promoting maladaptive coping strategies, and raising the risk of chronic pain conditions (6, 7). However, the underlying mechanisms by which direct regulation of social reward-related brain circuits modulates pain and negative emotional responses are still unclear.

The MeA has been implicated in the regulation of social behaviors (8). Moreover, our recent work suggests that the MeA plays a vital role in regulating social reward and promoting prosocial behaviors (9–11). Although numerous studies have linked the MeA to the regulation of emotion and pain (12, 13), no research has directly examined how specific neuronal subpopulations within the MeA and their associated downstream circuits modulate emotional states and pain perception. To address this gap, we investigated the behavioral effects of activating GABAergic neurons in the MeA and their downstream axon terminals in the MPOA on pain-related behaviors.

Results and Discussion

Building on our previous work showing that GABAergic neurons in the MeA mediate social reward and prosocial behaviors (9–11), we focused exclusively on the functional role of these neurons in regulating pain-related behaviors. First, we employed an optogenetic approach to activate these neurons by injecting Cre-inducible channelrhodopsin 2 (ChR2) or control vectors into the MeA of Vgat-Cre mice, positioning an optic fiber above this brain region (Fig. 1 A and B). To confirm the rewarding effects, we utilized real-time place preference (RTPP) and intracranial self-stimulation (ICSS) assays (Fig. 1 C and F). As previously demonstrated, optogenetic activation of MeA GABAergic neurons elicited strong reward responses in both sexes of mice (Fig. 1 D, E, and G) and did not alter locomotion (Fig. 1 H and I). Recognizing that footshock induces fear and anxiety-like behaviors after delivery in mice (14), we investigated the effects of activating these neurons on these emotional behaviors (Fig. 1 J and K). Our results showed that optogenetic activation in ChR2-expressing mice significantly reduced freezing and anxiety-like behaviors after shock delivery compared to mCherry-expressing controls (Fig. 1 K–M), highlighting a buffering effect against postshock negative emotional responses. In addition, we observed that optogenetic activation showed no effect on jumping latency during shock (Fig. 1N), likely because the strong footshock overrode the neuronal modulation effect. Next, we assessed its impact on pain sensitivity using the hotplate test. We observed a significant increase in pain latency to the nociceptive response in ChR2 group compared to the control group (Fig. 1O), suggesting that this activation lowers animals’ pain sensitivity. Collectively, these findings indicate that activating MeA GABAergic neurons can alleviate negative emotional and pain responses.

Next, to explore the effects of prolonged activation of these neurons on negative emotional behavior and pain perception, we utilized DREADD (Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs) technology to selectively activate them (Fig. 1 P–R). Consistent with our findings from optogenetic activation, chemogenetic activation of MeA GABAergic neurons led to a significant reduction in postshock negative emotional behaviors and pain sensitivity (Fig. 1 S, T, and V). Notably, chemogenetic activation led to a significant increase in jumping latency in females relative to the control (Fig. 1U).

Finally, we investigated whether activating the MeA-to-MPOA social reward circuit could elicit behavioral effects similar to those observed with direct activation of the MeA. To test this hypothesis, we utilized an optogenetic approach by injecting Cre-inducible ChR2 into the MeA of Vgat-Cre mice and placing an optic fiber above the MPOA (Fig. 1 W–Y). Our results demonstrated that optogenetic activation of this circuit resulted in a significant reduction in negative emotional and pain responses (Fig. 1 Z–Z3).

In summary, activation of GABAergic neurons in the MeA and the MeA-to-MPOA circuit significantly alleviates postshock freezing and anxiety-like behaviors while reducing pain perception in both males and females. The amygdala consists of several subregions, such as the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA) and the MeA, both of which are implicated in the processing of emotions, particularly those related to fear and social behaviors (15). In contrast to the CeA, a brain region that encodes negative emotions and exacerbates them upon activation (15), our study demonstrates that the MeA can mitigate these negative emotions. This research expands our understanding of the amygdala’s role in pain and emotion regulation. Overall, this study broadens our understanding of the neural circuits involved in pain modulation and offers significant implications for clinical applications.

Materials and Methods

We activated MeA GABAergic neurons or their downstream circuits in adult Vgat-Cre mice of both sexes. Rewarding effects were evaluated using real-time place preference and intracranial self-stimulation assays. The foot-shock paradigms assessed the modulation of postshock negative emotional states by MeA-mediated social reward, while the hotplate test independently evaluated baseline pain perception (SI Appendix). Raw data and codes are available at a Zenodo repository (https://zenodo.org/records/16735045).
