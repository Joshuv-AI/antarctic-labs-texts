Smart multi-shell core composites with responsive dissolution for chemical inflow control
Authors: Haneen Omar, Bader H Alqahtani, Pranay Asthana, Nourah Almusharraf, Ahmed Bukhamseen, Edreese H Alsharaeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective management of oil and water production is a persistent challenge in hydrocarbon recovery, where premature water breakthrough can severely impact production efficiency. Inflow Control Devices (ICDs) are widely used to regulate fluid entry; however, achieving selective and responsive control over oil and water inflow remains a complex challenge. This study introduces a novel multi-shell core (MSC) composite designed with smart, selectively dissolvable coatings that adapt their wettability in response to fluid type intended for application in chemical autonomous inflow control devices (C-AICDs). The MSC structure incorporates sequential layers of metal oxide nanoparticles (SiO2, TiO2 and Fe2O3) via a scalable ball milling process. These inorganic layers were selected to create fluid-selective dissolution behavior in response to reservoir environments, particularly during transitions from oil to water production phases. Characterizations via XRD, FTIR, DSC, SEM, XPS, nanoindentation, and contact angle confirmed the successful coating structure, composition, mechanical enhancement, and tailored wettability. The coated layer thickness varies with the application and ICD dimensions. We successfully achieved a total thickness of 60 μm using three layers, each averaging 20 μm, which sets a strong foundation for future optimization with higher thicknesses. Dissolution tests under hot seawater and hydrothermal conditions demonstrated delayed oxidation, consistent with staged activation as observed in cyclic voltammetry. The sequential response of the three coating layers (SiO₂ (hydrophilic), TiO₂ (intermediate), and Fe₂O₃ (hydrophobic)) illustrates a clear responsive dissolution mechanism. Each layer dissolves selectively according to the contacting fluid phase, enabling a chemical inflow control process that autonomously regulates flow based on the reservoir environment. Introduction

Inflow control devices (ICDs) are widely used in well completions to regulate zonal inflow and delay unwanted fluids such as water or gas. As fixed hydraulic restrictors (e.g., calibrated nozzles or helical channels), passive ICDs create an additional pressure drop that balances inflow from different zones and mitigates early breakthroughs; however, because they are static and phase-blind, they cannot adjust once installed and may still pass high-mobility water/gas after breakthrough, risking well flooding1. These limitations led to autonomous inflow control devices (AICDs) that self-regulate based on fluid properties, preferentially choking low-viscosity phases (water/gas) while permitting oil. Field deployments report more uniform inflow, lower water cut, and improved recovery versus passive systems for example, AICD completions in Norway’s Troll Field increased cumulative oil by ~ 20% in the first 18 months relative to passive ICDs1–3.

AICDs achieve phase sensitivity via (i) rate-controlled/levitating-disk valves that lift and throttle under water/gas but remain open in viscous oil, (ii) floating-ball concepts that seat under denser water to reduce area, and (iii) fluidic-diode/cyclone paths (no moving parts) that impose greater pressure loss on low-viscosity fluids2–6. While effective, most designs rely on viscosity/density contrast and may include moving elements susceptible to erosion, scaling/plugging, or oscillatory behavior; they also typically maintain a minimum flow rather than fully shutting off 2–8. To address this, autonomous inflow control valves (AICVs) were introduced; using an internal pilot mechanism, AICVs can fully close for water/gas and reopen for oil, performing well even in light-oil reservoirs with limited viscosity contrast9–13.

In parallel, materials strategies embed selectivity at the material level, reducing reliance on moving parts: water-swellable elastomers/gels for shut-off and conformance control, degradable/dissolvable components for staged opening/closure, and wettability- or stimuli-responsive membranes/coatings that gate oil/water by chemistry, salinity, or temperature14–18. This has motivated chemical AICDs (C-AICDs) in which the reservoir fluid itself is the trigger for regulation.

In this study, we build on the C-AICD concept of Al-Musharraf, Asthana and co-workers,5,6 engineering multi-shell core (MSC) composites that deliver phase-selective, sequential dissolution. Each composite comprises a non-dissolvable Al core coated with three inorganic shells chosen for distinct wettability and dissolution kinetics: an outer Fe₂O₃ shell (oil-responsive), a middle TiO₂ shell (moderately wetting; responsive in mixed oil–water), and an inner SiO2 shell (water-responsive)19,20. Dissolution proceeds layer-by-layer: Fe2O3 is removed first under oil-dominant conditions; after its removal, TiO2 dissolves under mixed oil–water conditions; once that stage is complete, the SiO2 inner shell dissolves under water-dominant conditions, completing the sequence. This staged response directly underpins chemical inflow control by modulating permeability with changing fluid phase without moving parts.

Fabrication of thick, well-defined oxide shells is non-trivial; here we use sequential ball milling (SPEX Mixer/Mill 8000) as a simple, scalable route to deposit uniform, well-adhered coatings on spherical cores21–24. The approach allows tuning of layer identity and areal loading; in this proof-of-concept we demonstrate a total coating thickness of ~ 60 μm (≈ 20 μm per layer), suitable for exploring phase-triggered response while providing a basis for future thickness optimization to match device dimensions and target lifetimes11,14–16.

Coatings were characterized by XRD, FTIR, SEM, and contact-angle measurements to confirm structural, chemical, and functional integrity. Dissolution behavior was probed via cyclic voltammetry (CV) in hot saline media (80 °C) under ambient and elevated pressure to emulate producer-well conditions (typically 76–105 °C, ~ 2000–4000 psi, and 40–200 kppm TDS). The gradual, cycle-by-cycle increase in integrated CV area and the pressure-accelerated yet controlled layer loss are consistent with the intended exterior-to-interior sequence and with downhole operation3,4. Taken together, the results demonstrate feasibility of phase-responsive, sequential dissolution and support a chemical inflow-control mechanism: as each oxide layer dissolves in its preferred fluid regime (oil, mixed, or water), the coating autonomously adjusts wettability/permeability to regulate inflow.

This foundational work positions MSC coatings as a complementary path to mechanical AICD/AICV solutions. Future development will target application-specific shell thicknesses and chemistries for extended lifetimes and harsher brines, and explore related uses (e.g., fluid-responsive barriers in completions tools or temporary, phase-selective flow gates). The following sections detail the coating process, materials characterization, and dissolution testing under conditions relevant to C-AICD operation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Titanium (IV) Oxide (TiO2) was obtained from Winlab Limited. Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6 H2O) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from Loba Chemie. Urea and Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) were sourced from SRLchem, and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich. Distilled water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ·cm) obtained from an ELGA laboratory water purification system was used for all aqueous solution preparations and washing steps. Commercial aluminum balls (Al balls), solid, with a diameter of 3.0 mm (± 0.1 mm), were used as cores.

Methods

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Thermo Scientific spectrometer (Nicolet iS10). Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using Rigaku MiniFlex 600 using Cu Kα radiation (40 V, 15 mA, λ = 1.54056 Å) in a θ–θ mode from 5° to 90° (2 θ). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were studied using Hitachi DSC7020 from − 20 °C to 350 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 50 ml/min. The thermo-gravimetric analysis (TGA) tests were carried out by PerkinElmer TGA 8000™ Thermogravimetric analyzer. The study was performed in an inert nitrogen atmosphere from 25 °C to 500 °C with a uniform heating rate of 5 °C/min. The nanotest 3 nano-indentation platform (Product of Micromaterials, UK) was used to determine the samples’ elastic modulus and hardness values. The load was measured as a function of deformation depth. Berkovich-type diamond indenter was used with a maximum load of 20 mN. The experiment was done in the following sequence: (1) approaching the surface, (2) loading to the peak load of 20 mN at a rate of 5 mN/s, (3) holding the indenter at peak load for 30 s, (4) unloading from the peak load at a rate of 20 mN/s. The holding step was included to eliminate the creep effect. Elastic modulus and hardness values were obtained through analysis using Nanotest software. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a JEOL JSM-IT710HR field-emission scanning electron microscope operated at 5–15 kV to examine surface morphology and coating thickness. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were recorded using a Gamry 3000 electrochemical workstation at a scan rate of 50 mV s–1 within a potential range of 0–3 V. The measurements were performed in the frequency range of 1 Hz to 100 kHz using a standard three-electrode configuration consisting of a platinum working electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and a graphite counter electrode. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was carried out using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha instrument equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV) source to determine surface composition and chemical bonding states.

Preparation of iron oxide nanoparticles

Typically, a solution was prepared by dissolving 15.13 g FeCl3·6H2O and 90.78 g urea in 25 mL of distilled water. The mixture was stirred until a uniform solution was formed. This solution was then transferred to a crucible and subjected to calcination in a temperature-controlled furnace at 400 °C for 2 h with a heating rate (1 °C/min). Once the calcination process was completed, the crucible was allowed to cool naturally in the furnace, and the resulting product was ground and collected to obtain magnetic α-Fe2O3 heterogeneous nanoparticles.

Preparation of silica nanoparticles

Silica nanoparticles were synthesized using a modified CTAB-assisted sol-gel method. CTAB (250 mg, 0.67 mmol) was dissolved in 120 mL of deionized water containing 875 µL of 2 M NaOH solution and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. The mixture was then heated to 60 °C, after which TEOS (1.25 mL, 5.6 mmol) was slowly added under vigorous stirring. The reaction temperature was raised to 85 °C and maintained for 90 min to complete the hydrolysis and condensation process. The resulting product was allowed to cool to room temperature, collected by centrifugation, washed twice with methanol, and dried at 60 °C.

Preparation of different formulations of coated balls

Coating procedure (sequential milling, SPEX 8000). Coatings were applied in three stages (inner SiO2, middle TiO2, outer Fe2O3) using a SPEX SamplePrep Mixer/Mill 8000 (shaker-type). For each stage, a batch of 15 Al balls was combined with 100 mg of the corresponding oxide and one binder option: either 11 mg PVDC or 50 µL APTES. The mixture was milled for 5–15 min total (run as 5-min cycles with a brief pause to redistribute contents and limit heating). After milling, coated balls were sieved to remove loose agglomerates. The next shell was deposited using the same recipe. (The exact binder used for each formulation and the chosen 5- or 15-min run are listed in Table 1).

Coating a flat substrate

To coat a substrate using the casting method, a liquid material is applied to the surface of the substrate and allowed to solidify into a thin film. This involves preparing the liquid material, cleaning the substrate, pouring it onto the surface, spreading it evenly, solidifying the film, and inspecting for defects. Additional coatings may be applied as needed. Here, the Al foil was first cleaned and then coated with a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder with a coating ratio of 90:10 (metal oxide material: binder). After that, the metal oxide coating material slurry was poured onto the substrate surface using the doctor blade and spread evenly across the substrate. Then, allow it to dry in a vacuum oven. Each layer is allowed to dry before applying the next layer. The thickness of each coating layer is controlled by applying multiple coats after the previous layer has solidified.

Results and discussion

In this study, an MSC was developed using a 3 mm Al core ball coated with three distinct material layers. The number and composition of these layers were selected based on anticipated operational sequences during well operation. As shown in Scheme 1, the MSC consists of a central Al core (black), an inner hydrophilic layer (yellow), a middle amphiphilic layer (purple), and an outer hydrophobic layer (green), each designed to respond to specific fluid environments.

MSC design and fluid-responsive layers.

The MSC composite is intended for use within a C-AICD device featuring a funnel-shaped design (Scheme 2), which includes multiple inflow and a single outflow port. Reservoir fluids enter through the inflow ports and interact with the MSC composite. As the reservoir environment changes, each coating layer dissolves selectively, aqueous fluids dissolve the hydrophilic inner layers, while oil interacts with the hydrophobic outer layer. This progressive dissolution allows the MSC to migrate deeper into the funnel until it reaches the narrowest point. At this stage, the remaining Al core, now stripped of all coatings, blocks the outflow port, effectively stopping fluid flow.

C-AICDs funnel concept with an MSC composite.

The coating process employed a SPEX Mixer/Mill 8000 to deposit metal oxide nanoparticle layers (TiO2, Fe2O3, SiO2), each approximately 20 μm thick, resulting in a total coating thickness of approximately 60 μm. Al was selected as the core material because of its mechanical robustness, high thermal conductivity, and smooth surface finish, which support uniform coating adhesion and reproducibility. It is also lightweight, cost-effective, and chemically stable under both saline and hydrocarbon environments, making it suitable for reservoir-like testing. In this proof-of-concept study, the shell-layer thickness was fixed at approximately 20 μm for each oxide coating (SiO2, TiO2, and Fe2O3), corresponding to the maximum uniform layer attainable using the ball-milling method under the selected processing parameters. This thickness ensured complete coverage and mechanical stability while demonstrating the feasibility of sequential, responsive dissolution. Future optimization will target thicker coatings for extended dissolution duration and broader AICD design compatibility.

The first (innermost) layer was applied using a binder, either polyvinylidene chloride (PVDC), (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES), or polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), to enhance adhesion between the core and the inorganic shell. These binders improve interfacial strength, promote uniform nanoparticle dispersion, and withstand the mechanical stress of milling, thereby preserving coating integrity21,25–28.

The selection of TiO2, Fe2O3, and SiO2 was based on their favorable properties for oilfield applications, including high surface area, strong interfacial adhesion, and tunable solubility. These metal oxides ranging from 10 to 200 nm in size are widely produced at industrial scale, making them cost-effective and scalable. Their high specific surface area also contributes to enhanced surface interaction and reduced interfacial tension (IFT) with reservoir fluid29,30.

The three-layer architecture was successfully fabricated onto commercial Al cores using a sequential ball-milling approach (Fig. 1). Each layer responds to distinct reservoir conditions:

Al balls with different coating formulations.

Inner Layer (Yellow): Hydrophilic and water-soluble, this layer dissolves through hydrolysis when exposed to formation water. The binder ensures strong adhesion to the Al core and prevents premature detachment.

Middle Layer (Purple): Amphiphilic in nature, this layer dissolves in mixed environments (e.g., seawater–oil mixtures) through hydrolysis or ion exchange. It is applied without a binder to facilitate controlled dissolution.

Outer Layer (Green): Hydrophobic and oil-responsive, this final layer interacts with hydrocarbon-rich fluids, gradually dissolving as oil production increases.

This layer-by-layer design aligns with the evolving fluid profile of a reservoir from initial water injection to primary oil production and eventual water breakthrough. The sequential dissolution of the coatings enables the MSC to adapt its surface properties dynamically, thus regulating flow behavior. The timing and rate of dissolution for each layer can be tailored by adjusting their thickness, composition, and nanoparticle type, as verified through lab-based dissolution experiments.

First, XRD, FTIR, and DSC analyses were performed to characterize the starting materials (TiO2, Fe2O3, and SiO2) and confirm their phase purity and thermal stability. The coated ball systems were then analyzed using XRD, SEM, TGA, XPS, and contact angle measurements to examine interfacial interactions and verify the successful sequential coating of the MSC composite layers. Figure 2 displays the characterizations of the starting materials TiO2, Fe2O3, and SiO2.

XRD, FTIR, and DSC results of TiO2, Fe2O3, and SiO2.

The XRD pattern for TiO2 in Fig. 2 exhibits sharp and high diffracted intensity peaks assigned to an anatase phase, indicating that all particles are crystalline. For synthesized Fe2O3 NPs. XRD analysis showed a series of diffraction peaks at 2θ of 24, 33, 36, 49, 54, 62, 64, 72 and 76°. These peaks correspond to syn-hematite. The XRD pattern of SiO2 displays a broad peak, which confirms the amorphous nature of the synthesized SiO2 nanoparticles. There is no diffraction peak except for a broad band centered at 24.09°, which is a characteristic peak for SiO2 nanoparticles in amorphous nature. The FTIR spectra displayed all the characteristic peaks of TiO2, Fe2O3, and SiO2, and the DSC results indicated highly thermally stable materials.

To investigate interfacial interactions and confirm the successful sequential coating of the MSC composite layers, XRD analysis was conducted, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The measured diffraction patterns were compared with standard reference databases to identify peak positions and verify the crystalline phases of the coated materials. As the coating layers were applied, a progressive decrease in the intensity of peaks corresponding to the underlying layers was observed. This attenuation is attributed to physical interactions and the shielding effect of the outer coatings, indicating proper layer formation and coverage. Phase identification confirmed the presence of target metal oxide nanoparticles TiO2, Fe2O3, and SiO2, within their respective layers. These results validate the intended chemical composition and crystal structure, which are critical for achieving the desired dissolution behavior and mechanical stability in the C-AICD application.

The pattern shows distinct peaks for each oxide phase, and the decreasing intensity of previous layers confirms successful multi-layer deposition and interaction. These XRD findings demonstrate that the coatings were well-integrated, and the structural characteristics observed correlate with the composite’s expected performance in regulating fluid flow and mechanical durability throughout the well’s lifecycle.

Thermal stability is a crucial physicochemical parameter that significantly enhances the potential applications of composite materials. To assess the thermal stability of the synthesized materials, we conducted a TGA analysis (Fig. 4 (a)). The thermal degradation of titania, iron oxide, and silica nanoparticles is a standard behavior of any inorganic system, which generally has high thermal stability in the temperature range analyzed here. A modest mass loss between 50 and 200 °C is attributed to desorption of physisorbed water. Above ~ 230 °C the mass is essentially stable, consistent with the high thermal stability of TiO₂, Fe₂O₃, and SiO₂. Minor baseline drifts reflect binder residues and interfacial changes introduced by ball milling rather than oxide decomposition. The coating process of Al balls using ball milling changes the thermal stability of coated materials versus the raw materials. This can be explained due to the coating method applied and the interaction between the materials.

(a) TGA curves and (b) XPS spectra of raw and coated metal oxide nanoparticles.

The element composition of TiO2@Fe2O3@SiO2 composite and the formation of chemical bonds between components in single, binary and ternary oxide mixtures was investigated using the XPS method. For this purpose, detailed spectra of raw metal oxides were investigated and compared (Fig. 4(b)). As displayed in Fig. 4(b), Fe 2p signals dominated the binary TiO2@Fe2O3 spectra, indicating that the Fe₂O₃ layer shields the underlying TiO₂; Ti signals were attenuated below the detection threshold in survey scans. Meanwhile, in the binary system Fe2O3@SiO2 spectra and the ternary system of TiO2@Fe2O3@SiO2 spectra, two main peaks for silicon (Si 2s and Si 2p) and one peak of iron (Fe 2p) with lower intensity were detected.

In order to examine the interfacial interactions between different layers within the system and study the mechanical properties of the coated layers. we have opted to apply the coating on a flat substrate, specifically Al foil, using the casting method as illustrated in Fig. S2. The XRD pattern of the flat substrate with two and three coating systems clearly showed all the characteristic peaks of each metal oxide material in the pattern, as illustrated in Fig. S3.

Contact angle measurements were conducted to assess the wettability of the MSC composite layers, providing critical insights into their interaction with reservoir fluids (Fig. S4). A contact angle less than 90° indicates a hydrophilic surface with a strong affinity for water, while an angle greater than 90° signifies a hydrophobic surface that repels water. To perform these measurements, flat Al substrates were cleaned, coated with a PVDF binder, and layered with various metal oxides using the doctor blade method. Each layer was applied at a 90:10 oxide-to-binder ratio and dried in a vacuum oven before subsequent coatings. Layer thickness was controlled through sequential applications. The measured contact angles for individual materials were; SiO2 at 30.8° (strongly hydrophilic), TiO2 at 85.1° (moderately hydrophilic), and Fe2O3 at 128.0° (hydrophobic). The high contact angle of Fe2O3 indicates a strongly oil-wet surface, consistent with its assignment as the oil-responsive outer shell.

Composite configurations exhibited intermediate wettability, reflecting the combined influence of their constituents, where (TiO2 + Fe₂O₃) at 88.2°, (Fe2O3 + SiO2) at 59.9°, and ((TiO2 + Fe2O3) + SiO2) at 40.8°. These results clearly distinguish the surface behavior of each oxide and their combinations, which is crucial for tailoring the layer sequence in MSC systems. Based on these findings, the final MSC design was optimized with the first (inner) layer is SiO₂ (hydrophilic), the second (intermediate) layer is TiO₂ (moderately hydrophilic), and the third (outer) layer is Fe₂O₃ (hydrophobic). This configuration aligns with the fluid contact sequence expected in petroleum reservoirs, ensuring a stepwise and controlled response to changing environmental conditions.

The mechanical characteristics of coated systems were investigated using the nanoindentation techniques. The nanoindentation test was performed at several points on the flat substrate. A total of 10 indents were averaged to determine the mean hardness (H) and elastic modulus (E) values. The calculated results are shown in Table S1. The Load-displacement curves were obtained in a nanoindentation experiment as depicted in Fig. S5.

The study utilized SEM to visualize the surface of coated flat substrates with one to three layers of titania, iron oxide, and silica, as shown in Fig. S6 to S8. The SEM images effectively highlight the shape, morphology, and thickness of each metal oxide coating layer, revealing that the balls were successfully coated with three layers, each approximately 20 μm thick. Additionally, EDS and elemental mapping results demonstrated the distribution of the metal oxides on the Al foil surface. The SEM images also indicated that the coatings were dense and non-porous.

Dissolution study

To evaluate the dissolution behavior and stability of the multi-shell coatings under simulated reservoir conditions, cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments were conducted at 80 °C in seawater under both ambient and hydrothermal (downhole) pressures. Temperature was maintained using a thermostatic bath, and continuous stirring ensured uniform exposure of the samples to the test environment. CV analysis provided insight into progressive electrochemical degradation of the MSC layers by monitoring changes in current density and the integrated area under the CV curves.

A gradual increase in the integrated CV area over successive cycles indicated slow, controlled dissolution of the coating materials, which is desirable for sustained functionality in harsh downhole environments. The rate increased under elevated pressure (~ 47.4 kPa), confirming the role of pressure in accelerating layer degradation.

The hydrophobic Fe₂O₃ outer shell and the moderately wetting TiO₂ middle shell act as a kinetic barrier to brine ingress, delaying exposure of the water-responsive SiO₂ inner shell. Consistent with this design, the integrated CV area increases gradually on a cycle-by-cycle basis at 80 °C, without abrupt transitions that would indicate simultaneous penetration to the core. Accordingly, water reaches the inner SiO₂ only after the outer layers have fulfilled their service life by design and in a controlled, time-sequenced manner.

These results indicate operational suitability for downhole use: (i) temperature tolerance at 80 °C; (ii) brine compatibility; (iii) pressure-accelerated yet controlled layer loss; and (iv) fluid-type selectivity that enables stepwise chemical inflow regulation rather than reliance on moving parts.

This electrochemical evolution reflects a responsive dissolution process, where the hydrophilic inner layer (SiO₂) degrades first in water-dominated media, followed by the amphiphilic middle layer (TiO₂), while the hydrophobic outer layer (Fe₂O₃) dominates as oil production increases. Accordingly, water reaches the inner SiO₂ only after the outer layers have fulfilled their service life i.e., by design and in a controlled, time-sequenced manner.

Thus, the layer-by-layer degradation provides chemical regulation of fluid entry in the C-AICD, where flow modulation arises from chemical reactivity rather than mechanical restriction.

In this work, “dissolution” is used operationally to denote phase-selective loss of coating integrity/mass under the contacting fluid, as captured by the progressive increase in the integrated CV area. Mechanistically, the SiO₂ inner layer experiences water-driven surface hydrolysis in hot saline media (water-responsive, fast), the TiO2 middle layer exhibits slower aqueous degradation via surface hydroxylation/ion exchange (water-responsive, slow), and the Fe₂O₃ outer layer, being hydrophobic (contact angle ≈ 128°), is oil-responsive, it resists aqueous attack but is preferentially destabilized under oil-wet conditions by interfacial debonding/erosive loss rather than true molecular dissolution. This sequence provides the staged, fluid-dependent removal of coatings required for chemical inflow control.

Experimental CV results for three MSC formulations: APTES system ((APTES + TiO2)@Fe2O3@SiO2), (PVDC + TiO2) system ((PVDC + TiO2)@Fe2O3@SiO2), and(PVDC + Fe2O3) system ((PVDC + Fe2O3)@TiO2@SiO2) are presented in Fig. 5. All systems exhibited a gradual increase in the integrated CV area across cycles, confirming layer-by-layer dissolution with no rapid degradation. Under downhole pressure, dissolution rates increased (larger integrated areas than at ambient), validating the coatings’ suitability for a progressive, lifecycle-aligned response. These results indicate that the multilayer coatings maintain structural integrity over time, enabling prolonged core protection and controlled flow regulation in C-AICD devices.

Dissolution results of multi-shell coated Al balls at 80 °C under room pressure and hydrothermal pressure using CV technique.

Based on CV and complementary analyses (e.g., contact angle), the optimal MSC configuration was refined to include; first (inner) layer is SiO2 with binder, the second (middle) layer is TiO2, and then the third (outer) layer is Fe2O3. This configuration provides balanced responsiveness to changing reservoir fluids and pressures, ensuring gradual dissolution and optimal downhole performance.

Conclusion

To conclude, we have successfully developed multi-shell coated (MSC) spheres featuring three distinct layers, designed to selectively dissolve under simulated downhole conditions. Utilizing a ball milling technique, we incorporated binders such as PVDF and PVDC to enhance the adhesion between the Al core and the inorganic shells. These binders contributed to uniform coating, improved mechanical integrity, and mitigated the agglomeration of metal oxide nanoparticles during processing.

The coated spheres underwent comprehensive characterization using techniques such as XRD, FTIR, and DSC. Interfacial interactions among the layered shells were assessed through XRD patterns, while coating stability and dissolution kinetics were examined via cyclic voltammetry (CV). The CV results demonstrated a controlled, layer-by-layer dissolution behavior under elevated temperature and pressure, highlighting robust coating performance under reservoir-like conditions.

This innovative approach presents a transformative solution for managing multiphase flow in petroleum wells. The MSC composite autonomously regulates flow by selectively responding to exposure to oil and water, effectively minimizing unwanted water production. The sequential application of SiO2 (hydrophilic inner layer), TiO2 (intermediate layer), and Fe2O3 (hydrophobic outer layer) facilitates intelligent dissolution profiles and tailored wettability.

The developed multilayers, with a total thickness of approximately 60 μm, exhibited excellent structural integrity, surface responsiveness, and mechanical stability, making them highly compatible with C-AICDs. This work establishes a proof-of-concept for smart, responsive MSC composites intended for next-generation downhole flow control systems.

Beyond oilfield applications, the versatility of this platform, including customizable surface chemistry and degradation behavior, opens up possibilities for use in various industries that require stimuli-responsive materials. Future efforts will concentrate on optimizing layer thickness, refining composite compositions, and simulating full production lifecycle conditions to further enhance the deployment of smart materials in real-world petroleum operations. Taken together, the temperature-, salinity-, pressure-, and phase-responsive behaviors establish a practical proof-of-concept for MSC coatings tailored to producer-well conditions (76–105 °C, ~ 2000–4000 psi, TDS 40–200 kppm). Overall, this study establishes the first experimental validation of responsive dissolution-driven chemical inflow control, bridging materials science and petroleum engineering to create a truly adaptive AICD system.
