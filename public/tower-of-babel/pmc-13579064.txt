Medication-related osteonecrosis of the external auditory canal and treatment with teriparatide
Authors: Daheng Xia, Matthew J M Ting, Marcus D Atlas, Rudolf Boeddinghaus, Bronwyn G A Stuckey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedication-related osteonecrosis of the external auditory canal (MROEAC) is a rare complication of antiresorptive therapy, and guidance for diagnosis and management remains limited. We describe 2 patients with prolonged antiresorptive exposure who developed MROEAC and were treated with teriparatide. An 81-year-old woman developed bilateral external auditory canal erosions after 10 years of denosumab therapy. An 8-week course of teriparatide was associated with clinical improvement and a transient rise in bone formation markers, but computed tomography findings remained unchanged. Denosumab withdrawal led to delayed rebound bone resorption, prompting denosumab reintroduction. A 67-year-old woman developed left-sided MROEAC after 15 years of alendronate followed by a short course of denosumab. Four months of teriparatide was associated with symptomatic and biochemical improvement, but radiologic abnormalities persisted, and definitive surgery was required. These cases highlight the difficulty of differentiating MROEAC from more common otologic disorders, the discordance between clinical, biochemical, and radiologic response, the importance of multidisciplinary management, and therapeutic challenges created by denosumab discontinuation. Teriparatide was well tolerated and was associated with increased bone turnover markers, but radiologic resolution was incomplete. Surgical management may still be necessary.

Introduction

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the external auditory canal (MROEAC) is a rare but increasingly recognized adverse effect of antiresorptive therapy [1]. It is defined as exposed bone in the external auditory canal persisting for more than 8 weeks in patients receiving bisphosphonates without prior radiotherapy exposure, after exclusion of alternative causes like cholesteatoma or metastatic disease [2]. Recent authors have also implicated denosumab, and reviews suggest MROEAC is underrecognized rather than exceptional [1, 3, 4].

MROEAC may present with otalgia, otorrhea, pruritus, hearing loss, aural fullness, or exposed bone in the external auditory canal (EAC). Diagnosis is often delayed because these features mimic chronic otitis externa, EAC cholesteatoma, skull base osteomyelitis, or malignancy [1, 3]. MROEAC is probably a multifactorial disorder in which prolonged antiresorptive exposure suppresses bone remodeling, while local inflammation, infection, trauma, and possibly impaired vascularity contribute to failure of EAC bone healing. Potential cofactors include oncology bone-modifying therapy, antineoplastic treatment, glucocorticoid exposure, smoking, and local trauma or infection [1, 3, 4].

Given the paucity of experience, there is no established standard of care. Reported strategies include cessation of the implicated medication, local otologic care, observation, and surgery for persistent or structurally advanced disease [1, 5]. Teriparatide has emerged as a potential adjunctive treatment because of its anabolic effects on bone and its evidence base in medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) [6, 7]. Evidence in MROEAC is limited to case-level experience [3].

In denosumab-associated disease, treatment sequencing introduces additional challenges since denosumab discontinuation is followed by rebound bone turnover, rapid loss of bone density, and vertebral fractures [8, 9]. We present 2 cases of MROEAC managed with teriparatide, highlighting diagnostic challenges, discordance between clinical and radiologic response, and the complexity of post-denosumab osteoporosis management.

Case presentations

Case A

An 81-year-old woman presented with bilateral otitis externa after 10 years of denosumab therapy, with her last dose in January 2025. She had no prior fragility fracture. Bone mineral density T-scores were −0.7 at the lumbar spine and −2.5 at the femoral neck. She was a smoker and had giant cell arteritis previously treated with prednisolone and tocilizumab.

She reported a 3-year history of right-sided ear pruritus and otalgia and a 15-year history of progressive bilateral sensorineural hearing loss.

Fourteen months earlier, she had been treated for necrotizing otitis externa, although with diagnostic uncertainty because the erosion was localized to the EAC. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed otitis externa without osteomyelitis, and a bone gallium scan, initially reported as early osteomyelitis, was non-specific. Cultures grew Staphylococcus aureus and mixed bacterial flora. Despite prolonged antibiotics, symptoms progressed.

Case B

A 67-year-old woman presented in 2021 with left-sided aural fullness and otalgia. She had received alendronate weekly for 15 years after a fibula fracture, with a 12-month interruption. In July 2020, therapy changed to denosumab, with two denosumab injections before presentation. She was an active smoker. Bone mineral density showed a lumbar spine T-score of −0.7 and a forearm T-score of −0.4. She had had prior bilateral hip replacement for osteoarthritis.

Case B

Otoscopy demonstrated an eroded, necrotic inferior and posterior ear canal with an intact tympanic membrane. CT demonstrated a lobulated soft-tissue mass narrowing the bony EAC, with extensive erosion of the canal floor and extension into adjacent mastoid air cells (Fig. 2). MRI and biopsy excluded neoplasm and cholesteatoma, demonstrated resolution of the soft-tissue swelling, but showed persistent temporal bone erosion, supporting MROEAC.

Treatment

Case A

Management involved a multidisciplinary team including otolaryngology, endocrinology, and radiology. Denosumab was discontinued (last dose January 2025).

Subcutaneous teriparatide 20 mcg daily was commenced in July 2025 and continued for 8 weeks, based on MRONJ literature [6]. Local treatment included aural toilet, topical therapy, and repeated courses of oral antibiotics.

Case B

Management was coordinated between otolaryngology, radiology, and endocrinology. Denosumab was discontinued (last dose February 2021) and subcutaneous teriparatide 20 mcg daily was commenced in July 2021 and continued for 4 months.

Outcome and follow-up

Case A

Clinically, bilateral otitis externa improved. Left-sided symptoms improved 20 days after completion of teriparatide, whereas right-sided disease persisted initially. A CT in September 2025 demonstrated persistent extensive bilateral EAC erosions and bony sequestra, with no change from prior imaging. MRI confirmed persistent bilateral MROEAC without skull base osteomyelitis. Surgery was considered but was ultimately not required.

Bone turnover markers remained suppressed for 9 months after the last denosumab dose, with delayed C-terminal telopeptide (CTX) recovery (Table 1). Treatment was well tolerated, aside from transient hypercalciuria. Bone resorption rose 4 weeks after teriparatide cessation. Transdermal estradiol was initiated to mitigate rebound bone loss, but CTX rose markedly in December 2025. Oral and intravenous bisphosphonates were avoided in this patient due to dyspepsia, renal impairment, and potential need for rapid reversal should MROEAC progress later. Denosumab was ultimately restarted to reduce fracture risk.

Case B

N-terminal telopeptide and procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide increased during teriparatide therapy (Table 2). Clinically, pain and discharge improved, but follow-up CT in September 2021 showed stability rather than improvement. Hypercalciuria occurred during teriparatide therapy. Three months after cessation of teriparatide, the patient had recurrent debris, blockage, and hearing loss. CT demonstrated persistent canal erosion extending to the mastoid segment of the facial nerve.

She underwent left modified radical mastoidectomy, myringoplasty, middle temporal artery flap, mastoid obliteration, ossicular chain reconstruction, and meatoplasty in February 2022. Healing was slow, requiring split skin grafting to the left ear canal. By August 2023, the mastoid cavity had healed with normal hearing. Further antiresorptive therapy was withheld, and bone turnover markers remained stable. The recent complication of MROEAC weighed against retreatment.

Discussion

These cases illustrate the complexity of diagnosing MROEAC and the difficulty balancing local disease control alongside ongoing skeletal protection.

Both patients had prolonged antiresorptive exposure, suppressed bone turnover at presentation, and diagnostic delay, having initially been treated as inflammatory or infective otologic conditions. In each case, teriparatide was associated with biochemical evidence of increased bone turnover and clinical improvement, but incomplete radiologic resolution. Rebound bone turnover was more marked in Case A, reflecting differences between the patients in prior bisphosphonate exposure and denosumab duration.

Diagnosis is challenging because presenting symptoms overlap with more common otologic disorders and radiologic findings are not pathognomonic. Awareness of antiresorptive exposure is pivotal in diagnosis. Published management includes medication withdrawal, local otologic care, and surgery for persistent disease [1, 3-5]. The rationale for teriparatide is indirect, drawing on MRONJ and atypical femoral fracture literature [6, 7, 10, 11]. However, whether these benefits extend to MROEAC remains uncertain, and a recent denosumab-associated case similarly found no improvement in bone erosion on imaging after teriparatide [3].

High-resolution CT of the temporal bones is the key modality for demonstrating bony EAC erosion and sequestration, while MRI and nuclear medicine studies help exclude alternative diagnoses such as an EAC soft-tissue mass or more extensive skull base inflammation, respectively. MRI is more sensitive in detecting soft tissue changes but is not useful for showing the main distinctive changes of MROEAC: cortical bone erosions and bone sequestra, which are shown well on CT. Important differentials include osteoradionecrosis, EAC cholesteatoma, cutaneous malignancy, and necrotizing otitis externa.

Published reports suggest that serial otoscopic examination for healing—reduction in exposed bone, re-epithelialization, and less debris or suppuration—is the parameter that most closely tracks clinical improvement in MROEAC. CT changes evolve slowly and may lag behind symptoms, while bone turnover markers have been reported too inconsistently to serve as established response endpoints [1, 3, 5, 12]. The initial clinical response in our patients is likely multifactorial, including arrest of further erosion, in the setting of an increase in anabolic markers, and otoscopic ear toilet.

Our cases support biologic plausibility rather than proof of efficacy. The proposed mechanism for the therapeutic effect of teriparatide is through its action via WnT signaling pathway, promoting osteoblastic response. Other proposed actions include promotion of angiogenesis and downregulation of inflammatory markers [13]. The duration of 8 weeks of teriparatide treatment was based on the evidence from Sim et al [6]. An extension for Case B was obtained based on the review by Anabtawi [14]. Despite an initial biochemical response, she ultimately required definitive surgery, and it is possible that a longer course of teriparatide would have achieved a more sustained response, but was cost-prohibitive. Case A had persistent radiologic abnormalities and prolonged right-sided symptoms. Symptomatic or biochemical improvement may therefore not reliably predict radiologic resolution, and surgery may still be required in selected patients [3, 5].

Denosumab-associated disease poses an additional sequencing challenge. After denosumab cessation, bone turnover rebounds, especially with the introduction of teriparatide, with loss of bone density gains and increased vertebral fracture risk [8, 15]. Case A illustrates this. CTX remained suppressed for 9 months before rising sharply. The antiresorptive options were considered. Transdermal estradiol was prescribed but did not adequately control rebound resorption. Denosumab was therefore restarted to mitigate skeletal risk. Case B demonstrated some recovery of bone turnover after denosumab cessation and teriparatide therapy, but without marked rebound bone resorption. Differences in prior bisphosphonate exposure and duration of denosumab therapy have likely contributed [16, 17]. Current guidance emphasizes the importance of planned sequential therapy after denosumab withdrawal, but specific recommendations for patients with MROEAC are lacking [9, 18]. Smoking is a reported risk factor for MRONJ and may have adversely influenced local healing in both cases [19].

This report has limitations. It describes only 2 cases; treatment duration was relatively short and partly constrained by medication availability, imaging follow-up was not standardized, and concurrent local therapies and surgery limit causal attribution. Outcome assessment was also heterogeneous, incorporating symptoms, imaging, and biochemical markers, and there is currently no agreed-upon standard endpoint for treatment response in MROEAC. This makes it difficult to define a treatment target, compare outcomes across cases, or determine when medical therapy has been sufficient. Nevertheless, these cases add useful clinical data to a sparse literature, highlighting the discordance between symptomatic, biochemical, and radiologic response, and the difficulty of osteoporosis treatment sequencing after denosumab withdrawal.

MROEAC is a rare but important complication of antiresorptive therapy that should be considered in patients with persistent otologic symptoms and exposed bone in the external auditory canal. These cases suggest that teriparatide is feasible and may be associated with biochemical and clinical improvement, but the contribution of teriparatide cannot be isolated from concurrent management or cessation of antiresorptive therapy. Radiologic resolution may be incomplete, and surgical management may still be required. Denosumab-associated cases pose additional therapeutic challenges, as withdrawal of antiresorptive therapy to facilitate healing must be balanced against the risk of rebound bone turnover and vertebral fracture. Further case data are needed to better define the role of anabolic therapy, optimal treatment sequencing, and meaningful outcome measures in MROEAC.
