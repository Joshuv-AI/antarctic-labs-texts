Concomitant psoriasis may be associated with a more severe disease course in inflammatory bowel disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jimin Baek, Yu Kyung Jun, Joo Sung Kim, Jong Pil Im, Byeong Gwan Kim, Hyun Jung Lee, Seong-Jin Jo, Sang Woong Youn, Sooyun Park, Hyunsun Park, Seong-joon Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) share genetic and immunological features, but the impact of concomitant psoriasis on the clinical course of IBD is not well defined. We investigated whether concomitant psoriasis is associated with increased treatment burden and worse outcomes in patients with IBD. This retrospective cohort study included 75 patients with IBD from two academic centers in South Korea (IBD with psoriasis [IBD–PS], n = 25; IBD-only, n = 50). The IBD–PS group included only patients in whom psoriasis was diagnosed prior to IBD. Demographic data and clinical outcomes were analyzed. The primary endpoints were biologic and immunomodulator (IMM) use, and secondary endpoints included IBD-related surgery and emergency room (ER) visits. Time-to-event outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier methods. Biologic and IMM use were significantly higher in the IBD–PS group than in the IBD-only group (biologics: 40% vs. 16%; IMMs: 60% vs. 20%). Treatment escalation to biologics or IMMs occurred earlier in patients with concomitant psoriasis (log-rank P = 0.0021). ER visits were numerically more frequent among patients with severe psoriasis, and the time to first hard clinical event (IBD-related surgery or ER visit) tended to be shorter in the IBD–PS group, although this difference was not statistically significant. Overall, concomitant psoriasis may be associated with increased treatment intensity and a more aggressive IBD course. Introduction

Psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) are chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorders that share several clinical and immunological features. Both conditions are associated with dysregulated T-helper 17 (Th17) cell responses and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and IL-23, which play a pivotal role in disease pathogenesis1,2. Moreover, genome-wide association studies have identified overlapping susceptibility loci, including IL23R, IL12B, and TNFAIP3, further supporting a common genetic predisposition3,4.

Accumulating evidence indicates that psoriasis and IBD share overlapping immunological mechanisms, particularly involving the IL-23/Th17 pathway. Therapeutic developments parallel this understanding, with biologic agents targeting TNF-α, IL-12/23, and more recently IL-23–selective pathways demonstrating clinical efficacy across these conditions. In addition to immune pathway convergence, both psoriasis and IBD are increasingly linked to intestinal barrier dysfunction and gut dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial diversity and increased intestinal permeability. The resulting translocation of microbial products may further amplify systemic immune activation, supporting the concept of a gut–skin–immune axis underlying the shared pathogenesis of psoriasis and IBD4–6.

Clinical evidence suggests that patients with psoriasis are at an increased risk of developing IBD and vice versa7. The coexistence of these two diseases can potentially complicate disease management, given the need for systemic immunosuppression and the possibility of paradoxical reactions to biologic agents. However, few studies have been conducted to directly examine whether the presence of psoriasis affects the clinical course or treatment escalation of IBD, including the use of biologic agents and immunomodulators (IMMs).

Although interleukin-17 (IL-17) antagonists such as ixekizumab and secukinumab have shown high efficacy in treating psoriasis, their use in patients with IBD requires caution. Clinical trials evaluating anti-IL-17 therapies have reported exacerbations of IBD, including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). In one ixekizumab study involving 3,736 patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis, four cases of CD and seven cases of UC were reported. Similarly, a study of 3,430 patients treated with secukinumab revealed exposure-adjusted incidence rates of 0.11 and 0.15 per 100 patient-years for CD and UC, respectively. These findings highlight a potential divergence in the immunopathological roles of IL-17 in skin versus gut, suggesting a potentially protective role in the intestinal mucosa. Therefore, prescribing anti-IL-17 agents to patients diagnosed with IBD or those with a history suggestive of IBD warrants careful consideration. This also underscores the importance of providing more precise and individualized treatment strategies for patients with both psoriasis and IBD3,8.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of concomitant psoriasis on the treatment burden and clinical outcomes in patients with IBD. We hypothesized that patients with concomitant psoriasis would experience a greater treatment burden, such as the use of biologics and IMMs, and exhibit a more severe disease course compared with those without psoriasis.

Results

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 75 propensity score-matched patients with IBD were included: 50 in the IBD-only group and 25 in the IBD–PS group, with similar ages (51.8 ± 16.4 vs. 51.4 ± 19.4 years; P = 0.835). The mean age at IBD diagnosis did not differ significantly (38.6 ± 17.0 vs. 38.9 ± 18.4 years; P = 0.870). The proportion of male patients was 62.0% in the IBD-only group and 56.0% in the IBD–PS group (P = 0.803). Detailed baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. However, the post-matching SMD for sex (0.122) slightly exceeded the predefined threshold of 0.1, indicating residual imbalance in this variable.

Kaplan–Meier analysis of treatment escalation in IBD with or without concomitant psoriasis

As the primary endpoint, treatment escalation to biologics or IMMs, reflecting increased therapeutic intensity as a surrogate marker of disease progression, was analyzed separately (Fig. 2). Among the 75 patients included in the matched cohort for logistic regression analyses, 74 patients (50 in the IBD-only group and 24 in the IBD–PS group) had valid documentation of age at IBD diagnosis and were therefore included in the Kaplan–Meier analyses. The median follow-up duration for the overall cohort was 14 years (interquartile range [IQR], 9–17 years). When stratified by psoriasis status, the median follow-up duration was 15 years (IQR, 9–17 years) in the IBD-only group and 10 years (IQR, 8.8–17.2 years) in the IBD–PS group. Follow-up duration was calculated as the difference between age at the last follow-up and age at IBD diagnosis. Time to treatment escalation was significantly shorter in the IBD–PS group than in the IBD-only group (log-rank P = 0.0021). The survival curves diverged early during follow-up, indicating that treatment escalation occurred earlier and more frequently in patients with psoriasis.

The IBD–PS group showed a significantly shorter time to treatment escalation compared with the IBD-only group (log-rank P = 0.0021). Association between psoriasis status and hard clinical outcomes of IBD

As a secondary outcome, we evaluated the association between psoriasis severity and hard clinical outcomes of IBD. Psoriasis severity was classified as mild to moderate (patients not meeting severe criteria) or severe (defined as a PASI score ≥ 10 or use of systemic treatments such as cyclosporine, methotrexate, acitretin, phototherapy, or biologics for psoriasis). PASI scores were available in only three patients (11.7, 27.9, and 0.6) and psoriasis severity was primarily classified based on treatment history. Based on this definition, 14 patients were categorized as severe and 7 as mild to moderate. Four patients lacked sufficient clinical documentation to reliably classify psoriasis severity and were therefore excluded from the severity-stratified analysis. In two patients, psoriasis severity and IBD escalation occurred concurrently, whereas psoriasis severity preceded IBD escalation in the remaining patients. Hard clinical outcomes of IBD were defined as IBD-related surgery and ER visits due to IBD flare.

When stratified by psoriasis severity, differences were observed in the proportions of ER visits and surgical interventions (Fig. 3A). ER visits occurred in 28.6% of patients with mild-to-moderate psoriasis (2/7) and 50.0% of those with severe psoriasis (7/14). Surgical interventions occurred in 14.3% of patients with mild-to-moderate psoriasis (1/7) and 7.1% of those with severe psoriasis (1/14). Although ER visits were numerically more frequent in patients with severe psoriasis, surgical interventions were rare in both groups. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups based on Fisher’s exact test, likely reflecting the limited sample size and low number of events.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to compare the time from IBD diagnosis to the earliest surgery, or ER visit according to concomitant psoriasis status (Fig. 3B). The survival curves suggested a trend toward earlier ER visits or surgical events in patients with concomitant psoriasis compared with those with IBD alone; however, the between-group difference was not statistically significant (log-rank P = 0.39).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study showed that patients with concomitant psoriasis had significantly higher use of both biologics and IMMs compared with patients with IBD alone. These findings suggest that the presence of psoriasis may be associated with greater disease complexity in IBD, potentially requiring more intensive therapeutic strategies. These observations are consistent with prior reports suggesting potential mutual aggravation among immune-mediated diseases5,8.

Among patients with psoriasis, a trend toward more severe clinical manifestations, including higher rates of ER visits, was observed in those with severe psoriasis. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size, the observed directional trend suggests a possible association between psoriasis severity and IBD disease activity. This observation should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of patients. Consistently, our data also suggest a trend toward a more aggressive clinical course of IBD in patients with concomitant psoriasis, with a tendency for earlier occurrence of the first clinical event. However, in contrast to this overall pattern, the proportion of surgical events was numerically higher in the mild-to-moderate psoriasis group than in the severe group. Given the very small number of events, this finding is most likely attributable to random variation. Alternatively, patients with severe psoriasis may have received earlier or more intensive systemic therapy, which could have mitigated progression to surgical outcomes. IL-17 inhibitors were not used for psoriasis in the IBD–PS group prior to IBD hard outcome events, making paradoxical IBD exacerbation due to anti-IL-17 therapy unlikely to explain the observed association.

A plausible biological explanation is the presence of shared inflammatory mechanisms between psoriasis and IBD. Both diseases involve overlapping immune pathways, particularly those related to the IL-23/Th17 axis1,2,9,10. In addition, previous studies have reported alterations in gut microbial composition in psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis (PsA). In an untreated, recent-onset PsA cohort, both PsA and skin-limited psoriasis showed reduced gut microbial diversity and depletion of Coprococcus, while PsA also exhibited lower levels of Akkermansia, Ruminococcus, and Pseudobutyrivibrio, which are generally considered beneficial taxa9. Similar microbial alterations have been described in IBD and may contribute to barrier dysfunction and pro-inflammatory immune skewing, including IL-23/Th17 activation.

Although our study did not include mechanistic analyses such as cytokine profiling, genetic testing, or microbiome characterization, these shared immunologic and microbial features across the psoriasis–PsA–IBD spectrum provide a plausible biological context for the observed association. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and future prospective studies integrating microbiome profiling with clinical outcomes may help clarify potential shared biological pathways linking psoriasis and IBD.

Our findings suggest that patients with IBD and concomitant psoriasis may be more likely to require earlier escalation to systemic therapy. In this context, emerging data on ustekinumab, which showed high rates of steroid-free remission and endoscopic healing in pediatric UC refractory to anti–TNF agents, suggest that IL-23–targeted therapies may represent promising alternatives for this high-risk subgroup11. Real-world evidence from Korea has also demonstrated that ustekinumab is effective and well-tolerated as an induction therapy in CD, with more than half of patients achieving clinical remission and over 70% achieving clinical response within 20 weeks12.

However, these data are derived from distinct populations, including pediatric UC or CD-specific cohorts, and therefore extrapolation to the present adult mixed-IBD cohort should be interpreted cautiously. Rather than providing definitive guidance, our findings highlight the potential relevance of shared therapeutic pathways in patients with coexisting psoriasis and IBD and support the value of integrated, multidisciplinary management. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether early selection of specific biologic agents improves clinical outcomes in this population.

These findings have important clinical implications. First, they highlight the need for coordinated, multidisciplinary care involving dermatologists and gastroenterologists. Second, they support early consideration of systemic immunosuppressive therapy in patients with IBD and comorbid psoriasis. Finally, they raise the possibility that the severity of an immune-mediated disease may serve as a proxy for the overall immune dysregulation burden.

However, these findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. This study is limited by its retrospective design and relatively modest sample size, which restrict causal inference. In addition, only patients in whom psoriasis was diagnosed prior to IBD were included in the IBD–PS group to define temporal directionality, which may limit generalizability to patients whose psoriasis develops concurrently with or after IBD diagnosis. Subgroup analyses according to IBD subtype were performed with a small sample size, particularly in the CD subgroup, and the lack of statistical significance may reflect insufficient power (type II error). However, this finding should not be attributed solely to limited statistical power. The relatively high baseline use of biologics in the CD subgroup likely reflects the more aggressive disease course of CD compared with UC, resulting in a narrower difference between groups. Psoriasis severity could not be reliably classified in a subset of patients due to insufficient clinical documentation, and these patients were excluded from the severity-stratified analysis. This may introduce selection bias and limit the interpretability of severity-related findings. In addition, multiple comparisons may increase the risk of type I error. Psoriasis severity was primarily defined based on treatment history, including the use of systemic agents; this approach introduces potential circularity, as biologic therapy may simultaneously reflect disease severity and influence IBD treatment decisions. This creates a form of confounding by indication that cannot be fully disentangled in a retrospective design. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Furthermore, time-to-event analyses were based on age rather than calendar time due to incomplete availability of exact diagnosis dates. This approach may obscure differences in treatment availability across calendar eras in a cohort spanning 2004–2023, which could influence the timing of clinical events and treatment escalation. In addition, because age at diagnosis may differ by IBD subtype, this approach may introduce residual confounding in subtype-specific analyses. These factors should be considered when interpreting the Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank test results. In addition, although propensity score matching improved overall covariate balance, a small residual imbalance remained for sex (post-matching SMD = 0.122), which exceeded the predefined threshold and may have introduced residual confounding. Several baseline variables, including smoking status and body mass index, were not consistently available in the electronic medical records.

Additionally, standardized disease activity indices, the Mayo score for UC or the Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (CDAI) were not uniformly available in this cohort. Consequently, direct assessment of disease severity was not possible, and proxy measures reflecting disease burden and treatment intensity were used instead. Although these proxies provide clinically relevant information, they may not fully capture the underlying inflammatory activity.

Finally, the long study period (2004–2023) represents an additional limitation, as treatment paradigms for IBD evolved substantially with the introduction of newer biologic agents such as ustekinumab and vedolizumab. Because only a small number of patients in our cohort received these agents, stratified analyses by treatment era were not feasible, and potential temporal changes in treatment practices should be considered when interpreting our findings. In addition, confounding by indication may exist, as patients with concomitant psoriasis may have been more likely to receive biologics or IMMs that are effective for both conditions.

Despite these limitations, this study provides clinically relevant insights into the association between concomitant psoriasis and treatment escalation in IBD. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, standardized disease activity assessments, and detailed longitudinal follow-up are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

In conclusion, our study suggests that concomitant psoriasis may be associated with a more severe disease course and a higher treatment burden in patients with IBD. These findings underscore the importance of integrated management strategies and further investigations of shared pathophysiological mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two academic medical centers in South Korea: Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) and Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH). Patients who visited SNUH or SNUBH for IBD between October 2004 and January 2023 were included. A total of 25 patients with both IBD and psoriasis were identified from medical records (IBD–PS group). To better define the temporal relationship between the two conditions, only patients with psoriasis diagnosed prior to IBD were included in the IBD–PS group. We additionally reviewed the cohort for the presence of PsA and patients with confirmed PsA were excluded from the analysis to minimize potential confounding effects on systemic treatment use. The control group was defined as patients with IBD without a diagnosis of psoriasis (IBD-only group). Propensity score matching was performed at a 1:2 ratio between patients in the IBD–PS and IBD-only groups based on age, sex, and IBD type. All comparative analyses were conducted on the matched cohort (Fig. 4).

A flow diagram illustrating the patient selection process.

All diagnoses were confirmed by gastroenterologists and dermatologists based on standard clinical, endoscopic, and histologic criteria. Given the clinical heterogeneity between UC and CD and temporal changes in treatment paradigms, disease subtype-specific and time-adjusted analyses were planned.

Data collection

Clinical information was retrospectively collected from the electronic medical records of patients with IBD, with or without concomitant psoriasis. The electronic medical record database was reviewed through August 1, 2025 to obtain follow-up information for the included patients. The extracted data included demographic characteristics such as age and sex, IBD-related variables including disease subtype (CD or UC), age at diagnosis, history of IBD-related surgery or ER visits due to IBD flare, and treatment history with biologics or IMMs. Biologics included anti-tumor necrosis factor agents (infliximab, adalimumab, and golimumab), vedolizumab, and ustekinumab, while IMMs included thiopurines (azathioprine) and methotrexate. Only the use of biologics and IMMs for IBD treatment was analyzed. We determined the treatment indication based on clinical documentation in the medical records, including physician notes, indication statements in prescription records, and contemporaneous clinical assessment. Biologic therapies prescribed specifically for psoriasis were not included in the IBD outcome analyses, which focused on treatments used for IBD management. For patients with psoriasis, additional clinical information included age at diagnosis; use of systemic treatment, including phototherapy, oral systemic agents, or biologics; and available Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores. Psoriasis severity was defined as severe when the PASI score was greater than 10 or when systemic treatment had been administered. Any administration of biologics or IMMs for psoriasis management was considered only for the classification of psoriasis severity and was not included in the analysis of biologic or IMM use in relation to IBD course. All data were reviewed and organized to allow for comparison between patients with and without concomitant psoriasis in relation to treatment outcomes and clinical characteristics.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints were the use of biologics and IMMs for IBD treatment. Secondary endpoints included IBD-related surgery and ER visits due to IBD flare. These endpoints were selected to evaluate whether concomitant psoriasis in patients with IBD is associated with a higher requirement for advanced pharmacologic therapy, as well as increased surgical and acute care interventions.

Statistical analysis

In propensity score matching, patients with psoriasis were matched to patients without psoriasis using nearest-neighbor matching at a 1:2 ratio without replacement, implemented using the MatchIt package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Matching was performed based on age, sex, and IBD subtype. IBD subtype was included as a binary variable (UC vs. CD) in the propensity score model. Patients with indeterminate IBD subtype were excluded from analyses involving IBD subtype due to insufficient sample size but were retained in the overall and descriptive analyses. The adequacy of matching was assessed by calculating standardized mean differences (SMDs) for baseline covariates before and after matching. An SMD of < 0.1 was considered indicative of acceptable balance between groups.

For descriptive baseline comparisons, continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test without continuity correction. For inferential analyses of treatment use according to psoriasis status, categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test with Yates’ continuity correction due to the small sample size and the presence of contingency tables with low expected cell counts (< 5). Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify associations between concomitant psoriasis and use of biologics or IMMs. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the independent association between concomitant psoriasis and treatment use. Models were adjusted for age, sex, IBD subtype (UC vs. CD), and age at IBD diagnosis as a patient-level biological variable. For severity-stratified analyses with small sample sizes and low expected cell counts, Fisher’s exact test was applied to compare categorical outcomes between groups. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p-values < 0.05.

For time-to-event analyses, survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with time expressed in months from IBD diagnosis. Event time was defined as the earliest occurrence of (1) hard clinical outcome (IBD-related surgery or ER visit due to IBD flare) (2) treatment escalation (initiation of biologics or IMMs [azathioprine or methotrexate]); events recorded before diagnosis were disregarded. Because exact calendar dates of IBD diagnosis were not consistently available in the electronic medical records, follow-up duration was calculated as the difference between age at the last follow-up and age at IBD diagnosis. Only patients with a clearly documented age at IBD diagnosis were included to ensure an accurate index for time-to-event estimation, and patients with missing or inconsistent documentation were excluded from the survival analyses. Patients without a post-index event were censored at the age at last available follow-up or at the administrative end of the study period (August 1, 2025), whichever occurred first. Kaplan–Meier curves were presented with confidence bands and numbers at risk, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc, IL, USA) and R version 4.5.1.
