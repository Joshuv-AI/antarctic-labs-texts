Quality and efficacy of alcohol-based hand rubs used in healthcare facilities of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Abayneh Melaku, Tesfaye Admasu, Argaw Ambelu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHand hygiene is critical for infection prevention and quality of care. Alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs) are widely recommended for their broad antimicrobial activity and ease of use; however, concerns remain regarding product quality and efficacy. This study evaluated the physicochemical properties and antimicrobial performance of ABHRs used in healthcare facilities in Addis Ababa.MethodsA laboratory-based experimental design was conducted between May and August 2025. Thirty ABHRs from hospital-produced, locally manufactured, and imported sources were tested using a customized EN 1500 protocol. The volunteers’ hands were contaminated with Escherichia coli K12 and treated with 3 mL of the product for 30 s. Pre- and post-treatment bacterial counts were compared, with log10 reduction factor as the primary outcome. Non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal–Wallis, and Mann–Whitney U) were applied.ResultsMeasured ethanol concentrations ranged from 55 to 86% v/v compared with labeled 60–87% v/v. Nearly one-quarter of hospital-produced and locally manufactured products fell below the WHO minimum of 60% v/v. Antimicrobial testing showed log10 reductions of 2.63–4.63; 50% achieved the EN 1500 benchmark of ≥ 4 log reduction, 26.7% demonstrated moderate efficacy (3–<4 log reduction), and 23.3% fell below 3 log reduction. Imported formulations (73–86% v/v; median 79% v/v) consistently achieved ≥ 4 log reductions, significantly outperforming hospital-produced (p = 0.009) and locally manufactured products (p = 0.047).ConclusionOne-fourth of ABHRs and half of the tested products failed to meet the WHO concentration and EN 1500 efficacy benchmarks. Policymakers, hospital administrators, and the Ethiopian Food and Drug Authority must urgently establish mandatory quality audit systems and enforce strict adherence to international guidelines to safeguard healthcare workers, patients, and infection prevention efforts in Ethiopia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01748-1.

Introduction

Hand hygiene is universally acknowledged as a fundamental practice to reduce the transmission of infectious diseases and save lives, serving as a primary method for controlling pathogens and a critical tool in preventing infections in both healthcare and community settings [1–3]. The effectiveness of hand hygiene depends not only on the act itself but also on the quality and proper use of hand hygiene products such as alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs), soaps, and detergents. Among these, ABHRs have gained prominence due to their broad antimicrobial spectrum, ease of use, safety profile, and high acceptability, particularly during outbreaks such as the COVID-19 pandemic [4, 5]. However, the efficacy of ABHRs can be compromised by multiple factors, including alcohol concentration, contact time, formulation quality, product type, user compliance, appropriateness of use, pH, temperature, and volume used influence the overall effectiveness of hand hygiene practices [6–12].

Sustaining high levels of hand hygiene compliance among healthcare personnel remains challenging, with barriers such as lack of time, limited facilities, forgetfulness, and adverse effects like skin irritation and dryness frequently reported. Skin tolerability and user acceptance are therefore critical for promoting compliance, as products that are pleasant to use and non-damaging to the skin encourage consistent practice [13]. Supporting this O’Donoghue in 2019 demonstrated that both foam and gel formulations met WHO criteria for acceptability and tolerability, with foam scoring higher for ease of use, and neither causing damage to skin integrity [14].

Globally, regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration classify hand sanitizers as over-the-counter (OTC) drugs to ensure their safety, efficacy, and quality. International standards require minimum alcohol concentrations of at least 60% v/v ethanol or 70% v/v isopropyl alcohol for effective pathogen control [15, 16]. In Ethiopia, the Ethiopian Food and Drug Authority (EFDA) is legally mandated to regulate the production and distribution of high-quality hand hygiene products, including ABHRs, to safeguard public health [17].

Despite these requirements, studies in Addis Ababa and other Ethiopian cities have revealed significant quality concerns with locally manufactured and commercially available hand sanitizers. Selam et al. [5], found that many locally manufactured ABHRs in Addis Ababa contained ethanol content as low as 54.4% v/v, while Manaye and colleagues [18] reported that four out of five sanitizers sold in Southwestern Ethiopia had ethanol concentrations below 80% and correspondingly weak bactericidal activity. Similarly, in Eastern Ethiopian cities such as Haramaya, Dire Dawa, and Harar, four out of five tested sanitizers contained ethanol below WHO recommendations, with some as low as 51.3% v/v [19]. The antimicrobial efficacy of these substandard products was compromised, with bactericidal effects ranging only from 10% to 80%, far below the 99.9% efficacy claimed by manufacturers [5]. Selam et al. [20] also highlighted that locally produced formulations frequently deviate from WHO formulations. Similar irregularities have been documented in Nigeria and Uganda, where locally produced sanitizers often showed inappropriate pH, poor stability, or deviations from WHO-recommended formulations [21, 22]. These discrepancies between labeled and actual alcohol content raise serious concerns about product reliability and the potential for contributing to the spread rather than the prevention of infectious diseases. This inconsistent product quality poses a direct risk to the quality of care in hospitals and to public health. It promotes a false sense of security, which compromises infection prevention efforts. Substandard formulations may fail to adequately reduce microbial transmission, including antimicrobial-resistant organisms, in both healthcare and community settings, where hand hygiene is a primary defense mechanism. This makes product reliability essential. Such issues may be exacerbated by high demand during public health emergencies, limited awareness of the consequences of poor product quality, and profit-driven motives among producers and supply chain actors. Moreover, many hospitals in Addis Ababa have initiated in-house production of ABHRs to meet their demand, further underscoring the need for monitoring and regulation.

Therefore, evaluating the efficacy and safety of hand hygiene products is essential to ensure compliance with international standards and to protect public health and achieve the intended benefits of infection prevention and control. This study systematically evaluates the physicochemical quality and antimicrobial efficacy of hospital-produced, locally manufactured, and imported ABHRs used in Addis Ababa hospitals and marketed in the city, addressing critical gaps in product reliability and infection prevention.

Methods and materials

The study area

Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia and the headquarters of the African Union, is centrally located and bordered by the Oromia Region. Administratively, the city is divided into 11 sub-cities and 123 woredas (districts), which serve as the lower-level administrative units. According to the World Population Review and the World Bank, Addis Ababa has experienced rapid annual growth, with an estimated population of 5,957,000 in 2025, representing more than 25% of Ethiopia’s total urban population [23]. According to the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, Addis Ababa city has 37 hospitals (13 public, 24 private) and more than 100 health centers [24]. The city’s water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure service delivery shows significant gaps, with 86% of healthcare facilities having basic water, 14% limited, and 88.4% limited hand hygiene services [24, 25].

The study design and period

A laboratory-based experimental study employing a pre–post design was conducted from May to August 2025. The hands of volunteers were artificially contaminated with E. coli K12, followed by application of the test product using standardized rubbing techniques, and subsequent recovery of bacteria to determine log reduction values. The procedure was adapted from the European Norm EN 1500, a CEN standard widely applied across European countries and recommended by the World Health Organization for evaluating hand hygiene products [1, 26, 27]. Due to resource constraints, a customized EN 1500 approach was adopted: each product was tested on a single volunteer, with triplicate repetitions performed to enhance reproducibility and reliability. Similar customized protocols have been used in previous evaluations of ABHR efficacy [28].

Sample size determination

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to represent the dominant alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) formulations used in Addis Ababa. Products were drawn from three major sources: hospital-produced formulations for in-house use, locally manufactured products commercially marketed within the city, and imported formulations manufactured abroad. In total, 30 distinct ABHR formulations were included, balancing resource availability, financial constraints, and time limitations with the need to capture product diversity in routine use. Randomization was applied to the order of product testing to minimize bias. Although larger samples are generally recommended to increase statistical power, non-parametric methods remain valid and robust with modest group sizes, particularly when data distributions are skewed. Based on observed availability and usage patterns, the sample was proportionally allocated as follows: eight hospital-produced products, twelve locally manufactured products, and ten imported products.

Sample collection

Alcohol based hand rub collection

Sample collection was conducted in multiple stages. First, a market survey was performed to identify alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) products most commonly used in Addis Ababa hospitals and commercial outlets. Subsequently, samples were obtained from three sources: hospital production units preparing formulations for internal use, locally manufactured products available in pharmacies, and imported products distributed through retail outlets. Each product was labeled and assigned a unique code. Detailed product information, including manufacturer, batch number, type of alcohol, expiry date, and labeled alcohol concentration, was systematically recorded.

Sample collection

Volunteers’ hands were artificially contaminated with a standardized suspension of E. coli K12, the reference strain specified by EN 1500, to simulate transient microbial flora. Baseline contamination levels were determined by sampling viable organisms from the fingertips before product application, followed by serial dilution and plating on nutrient agar. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and colony-forming units (CFUs) were enumerated to calculate log10 reductions.

Volunteers then applied 3 mL of the assigned hand rub and rubbed their hands according to the EN 1500 technique for 30 s. After drying, post-application fingertip samples were collected using a validated neutralizing broth to recover surviving bacteria. Microbial counts before and after application were compared to calculate log10 reduction values. Each product was tested in triplicate to ensure precision and reproducibility. One volunteer was assigned per product, meaning 30 volunteers were used for 30 products. Pre and post-application tests were conducted, and log reduction was used as the outcome measure instead of reference product comparison. Other procedures followed the EN 1500 protocol.

Quality control

The research team provided one-day structured training for volunteers and research assistants, and laboratory staff to ensure uniform adherence to study procedures. All research assistants held at least a Bachelor of Science degree and had extensive experience in sample collection and microbiological analysis. Aseptic techniques were strictly maintained throughout sampling, handling, and laboratory procedures to prevent contamination and ensure the validity of results. Culture media were prepared and sterilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with sterility checks performed before use.

To ensure methodological reliability, neutralizer validation was conducted to confirm that the recovery medium did not inhibit bacterial growth or interfere with ABHR activity according to EN 1500 guidelines. Negative controls (broth without inoculum) and positive controls (broth inoculated with E. coli K12 without ABHR exposure) were included in each experimental run to verify the accuracy of recovery and plating. Each ABHR product was tested in triplicate to ensure consistency and reproducibility. Instruments (pipettes, incubators, and an alcohol meter) were calibrated before use, and colony counts were independently verified by two trained analysts.

Sample analysis

Pre- and post-application bacterial counts were determined by sampling fingertips in validated neutralizing broth, followed by serial dilution and plating on nutrient agar to quantify viable colonies. Plates were incubated at 36 °C for 24–48 h, after which colony counts were performed. The primary outcome measure was the logarithmic reduction factor (RF) in bacterial load. Products achieving measurable reductions were considered effective in reducing transient contamination.

In addition to microbiological evaluation, physicochemical analyses were conducted to verify product composition. Alcohol concentration was measured using a calibrated alcoholmeter and gas chromatography, hydrogen peroxide by spectrophotometry, glycerol by liquid chromatography, and pH by pH meter. Appearance was assessed visually for color and clarity. Products were stored under recommended conditions until testing to prevent degradation.

Data analysis and interpretation

Data from microbiological and physicochemical analyses were coded and entered into SPSS version 25.0 for statistical processing. Descriptive statistics (means, frequencies, and percentages) were calculated to summarize quality parameters, including alcohol concentration, pH, and antimicrobial efficacy, expressed as logarithmic reduction factors (RF). The primary analysis compared log10 reduction factors before and after product application to evaluate the efficacy of each alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR).

Because group sizes were modest (8–12 formulations per category) and bacterial counts were skewed, parametric assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance could not be assured. Accordingly, non-parametric methods were applied. The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used to assess pre- and post-treatment reductions within each product source, while the Kruskal–Wallis H Test compared median log10 reductions across hospital-prepared, locally manufactured, and imported formulations. Where the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant or borderline differences, pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni adjustment were conducted to identify specific group contrasts.

A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the data were also visualized through tables and charts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from Addis Ababa University, Ethiopian Institute of Water Resources (EIWR) Ethical Review Committee (Ref: EIWR/25/2024, November 2025). Verbal informed consent was obtained from all volunteers participating in the hand rub efficacy testing. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing product samples and volunteer data throughout the study to ensure privacy and compliance with ethical standards.

Results

Product sources and distribution of alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs)

Of the 30 ABHR products, 8 (28%) were hospital-produced, 10 (32%) imported, and 12 (40%) locally manufactured. Twenty-six (86.7%) were liquid, and 4 (13.3%) were gel. Labeled alcohol concentrations ranged from 60% to 87%, hydrogen peroxide from NR to 3% v/v (most at 0.125%), and glycerol up to 1.45% v/v. Several imported products combined high ethanol concentrations (≥ 85%) with elevated hydrogen peroxide levels (3% v/v). Bacterial load and Log10 reduction analysis

Evaluation of ABHRs revealed variable antimicrobial performance after a standardized 30-s contact time. The Log10 reductions ranged from 2.63 to 4.63. Of the 30 products, 15 (50%) achieved the ≥ 4 log benchmark, 8 (26.7%) showed moderate efficacy (3–<4 logs), and 7 (23.3%) fell below 3 logs, indicating weak activity. Hospital-produced and locally manufactured formulations, with median ethanol concentrations of ~ 65% v/v, produced inconsistent reductions (2.6–4.3 logs). In contrast, imported formulations (73–86% v/v) consistently achieved reductions of ≥ 4 log, underscoring their superior efficacy and stronger quality control (Fig. 2)

Non-parametric analysis of Log10 bacterial reductions

Wilcoxon signed rank tests confirmed significant pre- to post-treatment reductions within each product source (HPF: p = 0.012; LPF: p = 0.002; IPF: p < 0.001). The Kruskal–Wallis test (H (2) = 5.87, p = 0.053) indicated a trend toward superior efficacy of imported formulations. Pairwise Mann–Whitney U comparisons showed no difference between HPF and LPF, but imported formulations achieved significantly greater reductions than both HPF (p = 0.009) and LPF (p = 0.047) (Table 4).

Discussions

The study revealed that hospital-produced for in house use, locally produced, and imported alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs) showed marked inconsistencies in both physicochemical properties and antimicrobial efficacy. Variability was most pronounced in alcohol concentration, hydrogen peroxide content, pH, and antimicrobial effectiveness, underscoring potential challenges for infection prevention and control in Addis Ababa hospitals.

Labeled alcohol concentrations ranged from 60% to 87% v/v, with imported products frequently reporting higher values (≥ 85%) combined with elevated hydrogen peroxide levels (3% v/v). However, measured ethanol concentrations exposed discrepancies, ranging from 55.0% to 86.0%. Almost one-fourth of alcohol-based hand rub contained less than the WHO-recommended minimum of 60% v/v ethanol, confirming serious quality control deficiencies. This observation aligns with WHO guidance, which underscores that ethanol concentrations below 60% v/v are inadequate for effective hand disinfection [10, 16, 29]. Substandard formulations were identified among both hospital-produced and locally manufactured products, underscoring significant quality control gaps.

Beyond low concentrations, excessively high ethanol levels may also compromise efficacy by accelerating evaporation, thereby reducing contact time with microorganisms. Variability in hydrogen peroxide content may influence product stability and safety, since insufficient levels fail to inactivate bacterial spores, while excessive concentrations can cause skin irritation. Similarly, deviations in pH (6.0–7.6) may affect both antimicrobial activity and user tolerability, with formulations outside the neutral range potentially reducing compliance or altering product performance [30]. Physicochemical analysis in our study highlighted these inconsistencies, pointing to the need for strengthened regulatory oversight and standardized quality assurance mechanisms.

Similar findings have been reported in Ethiopia and other low-resource settings. From the studies done in Ethiopia, Selam et al. [5] found that many locally manufactured ABHRs in Addis Ababa contained ethanol below WHO standards, which had 54.4% v/v ethanol content, while Manaye and colleagues reported that sanitizers sold in Southwest Ethiopia had ethanol concentrations below 80% and correspondingly weak bactericidal activity [18]. Studies conducted in selected cities of Eastern Ethiopia also revealed that locally manufactured ABHRs frequently contained ethanol below WHO standards, and correspondingly weak bactericidal activity [19]. Selam et al. [20] also highlighted that locally produced formulations frequently deviate from WHO formulations. Similar irregularities have been documented in Nigeria and Uganda, where locally produced sanitizers often showed inappropriate pH, poor stability, or deviations from WHO-recommended formulations [21, 22]. This inconsistent product quality poses a direct risk to public health by promoting a false sense of security, which compromises infection prevention efforts, as substandard formulations may fail to adequately reduce microbial transmission, including antimicrobial-resistant organisms, and suboptimal or inconsistent reductions in bacterial load may contribute to resistance development in healthcare and community settings, where hand hygiene is a primary defense mechanism.

A ≥ 4 log10 reduction is generally regarded as clinically significant, reflecting effective antimicrobial activity and aligning with the EN 1500 standard for hygienic hand rubs. Antimicrobial efficacy reflected the physicochemical inconsistencies observed across products. Log10 reductions in bacterial load ranged from 2.63 to 4.63 after a standardized 30-s contact time. Overall, only half of the formulations achieved the EN 1500 benchmark of ≥ 4 log reduction, while more than one-quarter demonstrated moderate efficacy and nearly one-quarter fell below 3 logs, indicating inadequate antimicrobial activity. Imported products consistently performed best, with 70% meeting the benchmark and none falling below 3 logs. In contrast, hospital-produced and locally manufactured formulations displayed weaker and more variable efficacy, with up to one-third failing to reach even moderate thresholds. These findings reinforce and align with the direct relationship between ethanol concentration and antimicrobial performance, as products with measured ethanol below 60% consistently failed to achieve the required ≥ 4 log reductions [31]. The implications are significant that substandard ABHRs risk undermining infection prevention programs, eroding public confidence in hand hygiene, and compromising patient safety. Strengthened regulatory oversight, adherence to WHO-recommended formulations, and routine quality assurance are therefore essential to ensure reliable efficacy across all product sources.

Imported alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs) demonstrated superior antimicrobial performance, reflecting the benefits of standardized international manufacturing practices and stronger quality assurance systems. In contrast, locally manufactured and hospital-produced formulations showed greater variability, underscoring gaps in formulation accuracy and regulatory oversight. These findings are consistent with reports from Sierra Leone [32, 33], Uganda [22], and global systematic reviews [33, 34], which emphasizes that adherence to Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) and WHO-recommended formulations is critical to ensuring product reliability. Strengthening national regulatory frameworks and institutional accountability is therefore essential to ensure that all ABHRs, regardless of source, meet established efficacy benchmarks and contribute effectively to infection prevention efforts.

The surge in demand for ABHRs during public health emergencies, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, has driven increased local production, including within hospital settings. While this expansion mitigates supply shortages, it also introduces risks when production standards are not consistently upheld. These findings highlight the urgent need for rigorous quality assurance and regulatory enforcement. Similar global studies emphasize the importance of strict adherence to Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP), routine batch testing, and compliance with WHO-recommended formulations to safeguard product safety and efficacy [15, 16, 35, 36]. Importantly, Bauer-Savage et al. [37] demonstrated that local production of WHO-recommended alcohol-based hand rubs is feasible and can offer significant advantages in resource-limited settings, but also highlighted barriers such as variability in raw material quality, costs, and regulatory oversight. In this context, the Ethiopian Food and Drug Authority (EFDA) should strengthen oversight to ensure that both commercial and hospital-produced products meet established quality and efficacy benchmarks.

Study limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has some limitations. First, its scope was restricted to Alcohol based hand rubs in Addis Ababa, limiting generalizability to other regions of Ethiopia. Second, only one sample per brand was tested and analyzed in triplicate, which does not capture batch variability. Third, efficacy testing was conducted with a single volunteer in triplicate, ensuring intra-individual consistency but not capturing the inter-individual variability required by EN 1500. Fourth, the 30-s contact time achieves its intended killing effect only when the correct EN 1500 hand-rubbing technique is applied; however, this study did not validate volunteers’ technique, which may have influenced outcomes. Fifth, product shelf life, storage temperature, humidity, and storage duration since manufacture were not controlled for and could have affected efficacy measurements.

Conclusion

This study revealed substantial variability in the quality and efficacy of alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs) in Addis Ababa. Ethanol concentrations ranged from 55.0% to 86.0% v/v, with nearly one-quarter of formulations below the WHO minimum of 60% v/v. Only half met the EN 1500 benchmark of ≥ 4 log10 bacterial reduction, while imported products consistently outperformed hospital-produced and locally manufactured formulations, exposing deficiencies in quality control. These findings underscore critical gaps in infection prevention and control, as substandard ABHRs pose direct risks to healthcare workers and patients.

Policymakers and hospital administrators must urgently establish mandatory quality audit systems and enforce strict adherence to WHO and other international guidelines to safeguard healthcare workers, patients, and infection prevention efforts in Ethiopia. The Ethiopian Food and Drug Authority (EFDA) should lead this oversight, support local producers with training, and implement strong enforcement with awareness campaigns to eliminate substandard products, ensuring only effective ABHRs circulate in healthcare facilities and thereby safeguarding patient safety and public trust.
