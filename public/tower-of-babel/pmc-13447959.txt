A Misplaced Pocket of Air: Intramural Gastric Air Most Consistent With Gastric Emphysema
Authors: Priyanka Panwar, Lavender Otom, Subo Marsa, Gerald Odong, Steven Idet, Irene Muchiri, Hussein Abdulsamad, John Odhiambo, Christopher Opio, Amos Mwasamwaja, Saleem Abdulkarim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric emphysema (GE), or gastric pneumatosis, is a rare, noninfectious cause of intramural gastric air that must be distinguished from emphysematous gastritis (EG), a highly lethal infectious entity. Despite similar radiographic appearances, these conditions differ substantially in etiology, clinical course, management, and prognosis.CasesWe report three hemodynamically stable patients with CT‐confirmed intramural gastric air most consistent with GE. Two had associated portal venous gas. The cases occurred in distinct clinical contexts: infectious gastroenteritis with retching, transient midgut volvulus with obstructive gastric overdistension, and alcohol‐related upper gastrointestinal bleeding with forceful vomiting and a Mallory–Weiss tear. Although early EG could not be excluded with absolute certainty, the absence of sustained systemic toxicity, peritonitis, endoscopic necrosis, or clinical deterioration supported conservative management.ConclusionIntramural gastric air should be interpreted in clinical context rather than by CT appearance alone, particularly when portal venous gas or early antibiotic exposure complicates diagnostic certainty. Stable patients without systemic toxicity, peritonitis, endoscopic necrosis, or clinical deterioration may be managed conservatively with multidisciplinary input, while escalation should be reserved for suspected ischemia, perforation, sepsis, or clinical decline.

1. Introduction

Gastric emphysema (GE), also referred to as gastric pneumatosis, is a rare radiologic finding defined by the presence of gas within the gastric wall [1, 2]. It must be distinguished from emphysematous gastritis (EG), an infectious condition caused by gas‐forming organisms that carries a markedly worse prognosis [1, 3, 4].

Although these entities share similar imaging appearances, their etiology, clinical presentation, course, and management differ substantially. GE usually results from noninfectious mucosal disruption due to increased intragastric pressure, severe vomiting or retching, obstruction, instrumentation, trauma, ischemia, or air dissection from the mediastinum [1, 5–8]. In contrast, EG typically presents with severe abdominal pain, hematemesis, peritoneal signs, and systemic toxicity including fever, leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis, and hemodynamic instability and may require intensive care, broad‐spectrum antibiotics, and surgical intervention [1, 3, 4].

GE remains exceedingly uncommon, with fewer than 100 cases reported in the English‐language literature to date, and most evidence consists of isolated case reports or small series [2, 4, 9, 10]. However, the diagnosis is not always binary in real‐world practice, particularly when CT findings overlap with early EG or when antibiotics are administered before diagnostic certainty is achieved. We describe three cases of intramural gastric air most consistent with GE, highlighting the role of clinical–radiologic approach correlation, endoscopic timing, antibiotic exposure, and individualized follow‐up.

2. Cases

2.1. Case 1

A man in his thirties with dyslipidemia on rosuvastatin and omega‐3 supplementation presented with a one‐day history of sudden‐onset severe epigastric pain that rapidly became generalized. This was associated with abdominal distension, bloating, nausea, frequent belching and retching, and two episodes of nonbloody diarrhea. He denied vomiting, hematemesis, melena, fever, NSAID use, recent antibiotic exposure, or prior abdominal surgery. There were no significant dietary exposures or sick contacts.

On examination, he appeared uncomfortable and mildly dehydrated but remained hemodynamically stable and afebrile (blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, pulse 78 bpm, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air). The abdomen was distended but soft, with epigastric tenderness and preserved bowel sounds, without guarding or peritonism.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated mild leukocytosis (10.47 × 109/L; reference range: 4.00–10.00 × 109/L) and an elevated C‐reactive protein (18.5 mg/L; reference range: 0.00–10.00 mg/L), with, otherwise, normal renal, hepatic, pancreatic, and cardiac markers. Blood cultures showed no growth.

Contrast‐enhanced CT of the abdomen performed on the day of admission demonstrated dilated jejunal loops with air‐fluid levels and collapsed large bowel, suggestive of small bowel obstruction (Figure 1). Additionally, dependent intramural gas was identified within the gastric fundus and body, with left gastroepiploic venous and portovenous gas, raising concern for EG (Figure 2).

CT abdomen demonstrates dilated jejunal loops consistent with small bowel obstruction. Although a smooth transition point was identified on the study, it is not visualized on this particular image.

CT abdomen demonstrating intramural gas within the gastric fundus (red arrow) with associated left gastroepiploic venous (green arrow) and portovenous gas (purple arrow).

He was admitted for bowel rest, intravenous fluids, gastric decompression, and empiric intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g once daily following infectious diseases input. Because the initial radiologic appearance raised concern for EG, he was managed cautiously while further clinical, microbiologic, and endoscopic assessment was undertaken. The surgical team reviewed the patient early and recommended conservative, nonoperative management given his stable clinical status and absence of peritoneal signs. Despite the alarming radiologic findings, he remained afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and clinically nontoxic.

By hospital Day 2, his abdominal pain, retching, and diarrhea had resolved. However, concern persisted for possible EG, as the CT findings were not sufficiently definitive to exclude this diagnosis. Following a multidisciplinary discussion involving gastroenterology, surgery, radiology, and infectious diseases, a decision was made to perform an upper endoscopy on hospital Day 3 to further evaluate for EG. Endoscopy revealed thickened gastric folds with mucosal edema and superficial erosions, without necrosis, ulceration, purulent discharge, or features of invasive infection (Figure 3). Histology showed mild chronic gastritis without Helicobacter pylori. Gastric cultures grew scant viridans streptococci, considered likely oral commensal contamination by the infectious diseases team given the benign clinical course, negative blood cultures, rapid improvement, and absence of endoscopic necrosis. Ceftriaxone had been administered for one day and was subsequently de‐escalated.

Upper endoscopy demonstrating thickened gastric folds with mucosal edema and superficial erosions without necrosis or ulceration.

Stool testing detected sapovirus and enteroaggregative Escherichia coli, supporting infectious gastroenteritis with repeated retching and raised intragastric pressure as the probable precipitating mechanism. Repeat blood cultures remained negative. Repeat CT scan performed 6 days after presentation demonstrated near‐complete resolution of intramural gastric air and portovenous gas, with normalization of bowel caliber (Figure 4). Oral intake was resumed, and he was discharged home asymptomatic.

CT abdomen demonstrating near complete resolution of intramural gastric air and portovenous gas.

Overall, the absence of systemic toxicity, negative blood cultures, endoscopic necrosis or purulence and rapid clinical and radiologic resolution favored GE associated with acute infectious gastroenteritis and retching. However, early EG cannot be excluded with absolute certainty because antibiotics were administered.

2.2. Case 2

A man in his seventies with a history of prior stroke, hypertension, dyslipidemia, neurogenic bladder with a chronic suprapubic catheter, and recurrent urinary tract infections presented with profuse vomiting, reduced oral intake, and acute confusion. Initial evaluation revealed symptomatic hyponatremia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L; reference range: 136.00–145.00 mmol/L) and suspected urosepsis. He was admitted to a high‐dependency unit and treated with hypertonic saline, intravenous fluids, and intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g once daily following infectious diseases input for suspected catheter‐associated urosepsis, with gradual neurologic recovery.

During admission, he developed progressive abdominal distension, persistent vomiting, and failure to pass stool. Examination revealed a distended abdomen with generalized tenderness and reduced bowel sounds.

Laboratory testing showed mild leukocytosis (12.84 × 109/L; normal range 4.00–10.00 × 109/L) and inflammatory marker elevation (CRP 32.70 mg/L; reference range 0.00–10.00 mg/L). Blood cultures remained negative. Urine cultures grew mixed organisms consistent with catheter‐associated infection. After 4 days of ceftriaxone, antimicrobial therapy was escalated to intravenous meropenem 1 g thrice a day for 5 days in consultation with the infectious diseases team while surgical and gastroenterology teams assessed the need for intervention.

Urgent contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrated intramural gas within the gastric wall and portovenous gas, with proximal small‐bowel dilatation (Figure 5). CT angiography further demonstrated mesenteric twisting with a “whirlpool” sign consistent with evolving midgut volvulus and obstruction, with secondary GE and concern for ischemia (Figure 6).

CT abdomen demonstrating intramural gas within the gastric fundus (red arrow) with associated portovenous gas (purple arrow).

CT angiogram demonstrating mesenteric swelling (whirlpool sign) indicating midgut volvulus.

He was managed conservatively with bowel rest, nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluids, proton‐pump inhibitor infusion, electrolyte correction, and broad‐spectrum antibiotics for suspected urosepsis rather than confirmed EG. Nasogastric decompression returned dark, coffee‐ground aspirates, suggestive of mucosal injury or ischemic gastritis. Given preserved hemodynamics and absence of peritonitis or ongoing ischemia, surgery and upper endoscopy were deferred, and he was managed conservatively.

Clinical improvement followed with decreasing nasogastric output and return of bowel function. Repeat CT approximately 4 days later demonstrated resolution of the volvulus and complete disappearance of intramural gastric gas and portovenous gas (Figure 7).

CT abdomen demonstrating complete disappearance of intramural gastric gas and portovenous gas.

Oral intake was resumed gradually, and he was discharged in stable condition.

This episode favored GE related to transient obstructive gastric overdistension. Although meropenem may have provided bacterial coverage if early EG had been present, the absence of shock or peritonitis, negative blood cultures, improvement after decompression, and radiologic resolution supported GE as the most likely diagnosis.

2.3. Case 3

A man in his fifties with alcohol use disorder, decompensated alcohol‐related cirrhosis, and prior pulmonary tuberculosis complicated by chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis presented with hematemesis followed by a generalized tonic–clonic seizure. He had relapsed into daily binge drinking after a brief period of abstinence. There was no associated fever, abdominal pain, melena, or hematochezia.

On examination, he was alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15 after the event. He was tachycardic but normotensive and afebrile. He had jaundice, bilateral ankle edema, and hepatomegaly. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, nontender abdomen with preserved bowel sounds.

Initial laboratory investigations showed a hemoglobin of 13.7 g/dL (normal range: 13.00–17.00 g/dL), MCV 98.50 fL (normal range: 83.00–101.00 fL), borderline thrombocytopenia of 151 × 109/L (normal range: 150.00–410.00 × 109/L), mild coagulopathy with an INR of 1.26 (normal range: < 2.00), hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, elevated lactate, and a liver injury pattern consistent with alcohol‐related liver disease, including markedly elevated gamma‐glutamyl transferase, AST greater than ALT, elevated alkaline phosphatase, and direct hyperbilirubinemia. CT head showed no acute intracranial abnormality.

Contrast‐enhanced CT abdomen demonstrated locules of gas within the wall of the gastric fundus, suggestive of GE or ulceration‐related intramural gas, without active contrast extravasation to suggest ongoing gastrointestinal bleeding (Figure 8). Additional findings included diffuse hepatic steatosis with surface nodularity suggestive of cirrhosis and short‐segment mural thickening of the ascending colon. No portovenous gas was noted on imaging.

CT abdomen demonstrating locules of gas (red arrows) within the wall of the gastric fundus. No portovenous gas was noted on imaging.

Upper endoscopy performed, thereafter, demonstrated a small nonbleeding Mallory–Weiss tear, a Hill Grade II hiatus hernia, features of portal hypertensive gastropathy, mild D1 duodenitis, and a normal second part of the duodenum (Figure 9). There were no esophageal varices, active bleeding, or endoscopic evidence of gastric necrosis or purulent mucosal change. Gastric biopsies showed mild active chronic gastritis and were negative for Helicobacter pylori.

Upper endoscopy demonstrating a small nonbleeding Mallory–Weiss tear, severe acute‐on‐chronic gastritis with corpus‐predominant nodularity and petechial hemorrhagic spots, likely related to portal hypertensive gastropathy, and mild D1 duodenitis. No esophageal or gastric varices were identified.

He was managed conservatively with high‐dependency monitoring, proton‐pump inhibitor infusion, intravenous fluids, electrolyte correction, thiamine supplementation, and treatment for alcohol withdrawal. Intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g once daily was administered following infectious diseases input as prophylaxis against bacterial infection in the setting of cirrhosis and upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Because aspiration pneumonitis, aspiration pneumonia, and hospital‐acquired pneumonia were considered, ceftriaxone (administered for four days) and azithromycin (500 mg once daily orally for three days) were used initially and later escalated to intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam 4.5 g four times a day for seven days. Antibiotics were, therefore, administered for prophylactic and pulmonary indications, rather than for confirmed EG.

His hospital course was complicated by delirium tremens, transient paroxysmal atrial fibrillation that resolved after electrolyte correction, hepatic encephalopathy treated with lactulose and rifaximin, and a suspected aspiration‐related respiratory illness managed as above. He remained hemodynamically stable with no recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, worsening abdominal symptoms, fever, or signs of peritonitis. His mental status improved, oxygen was weaned, and oral intake resumed, and he was discharged in stable condition.

Given his preserved hemodynamic status, absence of peritonitis, improving clinical trajectory, and our prior institutional experience with conservatively managed GE, repeat cross‐sectional imaging was deferred. Instead, close clinical monitoring and serial abdominal examinations were performed, which remained reassuring throughout admission.

Overall, this episode was most consistent with GE related to mucosal injury from forceful vomiting and alcohol‐related gastritis with Mallory–Weiss tear. Although antibiotic exposure limits absolute exclusion of early EG, the absence of systemic toxicity, portovenous gas, endoscopic necrosis or purulence, and favorable clinical course supported GE as the most likely diagnosis.

3. Discussion

GE is characterized by noninfectious intramural gas resulting from mechanical or pressure‐related disruption of the gastric mucosa [2, 7]. Recognized causes include severe vomiting, gastric outlet obstruction, trauma, nasogastric tube placement, instrumentation, and ischemia [3, 5, 11–13]. In clinical practice, however, the distinction between GE and early EG is not always absolute, and diagnosis requires the integration of clinical status, laboratory findings, imaging pattern, endoscopic appearance, microbiologic data, procedural history, and response to treatment.

This case series highlights the diagnostic uncertainty that may arise when intramural gastric air is accompanied by alarming radiologic findings but the patient remains clinically stable. Portal venous gas has historically been regarded as an ominous finding, particularly when associated with bowel ischemic or sepsis; however, it has also been described in benign GE and may resolve with conservative treatment in stable patients [2, 5, 11, 14]. In our series, two patients had portal venous gas without clinical deterioration, and both improved with nonoperative management. Antibiotic exposure also complicated diagnostic certainty, requiring cautious interpretation rather than definitive attribution. In Case 1, ceftriaxone was started because early EG could not initially be excluded, while in Cases 2 and 3, antimicrobial therapy was given for separate clinical indications, including suspected urosepsis, bacterial infection prophylaxis in setting of cirrhosis and upper gastrointestinal bleeding, and suspected pulmonary infection. Procedural timing was another important consideration, as postendoscopic insufflation is a recognized confounder; however, CT preceded upper endoscopy in Cases 1 and 3, while endoscopy was not performed in Case 2. Therefore, endoscopic insufflation was unlikely to explain the intramural gastric air in these patients.

The three cases suggest different plausible mechanisms. In Case 1, infectious gastroenteritis with repeated retching may have increased intragastric pressure and promoted air dissection into the gastric wall [5]. In Case 2, transient midgut volvulus likely caused obstructive gastric overdistension and pressure‐related mucosal injury [2, 5, 6]. In Case 3, forceful vomiting with alcohol‐related gastritis and Mallory–Weiss tear may have produced mucosal disruption and intramural air [5, 15]. Alternative explanations, including early infection, ischemic mucosal injury, and multifactorial mucosal disruption, remain possible.

Differentiating GE from EG remains critical (Table 1) [16]. Imaging features may overlap; therefore, accurate diagnosis requires the integration of clinical status, laboratory findings, risk factors, and endoscopic assessment when indicated, rather than reliance on radiology alone [4, 5].

A practical diagnostic approach should begin with hemodynamic assessment. Patients with shock, peritonitis, severe metabolic acidosis, persistent lactate elevation, or clinical deterioration should be managed as possible EG or ischemia until proven otherwise. In stable patients, CT findings should be interpreted alongside fever, leukocytosis, lactate trend, blood cultures, risk factors, and procedural history. Endoscopy may help assess for necrosis, ulceration, or purulent mucosal changes but should be interpreted in relation to CT imaging and timing.

Management strategies consistently emphasize conservative therapy for stable patients. Recommended measures include bowel rest, nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluids, proton‐pump inhibitors, antibiotics, and close monitoring, with surgery reserved for hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, or suspected ischemia [1, 4, 5, 9, 15]. Antibiotics are not standard therapy for uncomplicated GE, which is generally noninfectious and may resolve with supportive care alone. However, antibiotics have been used in reported cases of GE managed conservatively when infection, ischemia, or early EG could not be excluded [17–19]. In this series, antibiotics were used because EG could not initially be excluded in Case 1 or because there were separate infectious or prophylactic indications in Cases 2 and 3. Therefore, antibiotic exposure limits diagnostic certainty and may have contributed to the favorable clinical course. Reported timelines of spontaneous improvement further support conservative management. Liang et al. report symptomatic resolution within several days, with radiologic normalization typically occurring between 5 and 14 days [1]. Iwamuro et al. similarly observed complete resolution within approximately 1 week in most conservatively treated cases [10]. Other reports document full radiographic resolution within days to 2 weeks once precipitating factors are addressed [3, 9, 15]. All three patients were treated nonoperatively with supportive measures tailored to the underlying precipitating condition and improved without surgical intervention, consistent with reported timelines and outcomes of conservatively managed GE.

The necessity of routine repeat imaging in clinically improving patients remains debated. Several authors suggest that follow‐up imaging is not mandatory in hemodynamically stable patients who demonstrate clear clinical improvement and biochemical normalization, as GE is typically self‐limited once the underlying precipitating factor is addressed [1, 2, 5]. In these reports, radiologic resolution closely parallels clinical recovery, and repeat imaging was often performed for confirmation rather than because of ongoing concern. Conversely, repeat CT is generally recommended when symptoms persist, worsen, or when there is diagnostic uncertainty regarding ischemia or EG [3, 4]. Therefore, current literature supports an individualized approach: in stable patients with reassuring clinical trajectories, close monitoring may suffice, whereas interval imaging should be reserved for those with concerning features or incomplete resolution. In this series, repeat CT documented resolution in two patients, while imaging was deferred in Case 3 because of sustained clinical improvement.

Case 3 also illustrates the clinical overlap between aspiration pneumonitis and aspiration pneumonia. Antibiotics were used because bacterial pneumonia or hospital‐acquired pneumonia could not be confidently excluded, rather than as evidence of confirmed EG. This distinction is important because uncomplicated aspiration pneumonitis does not routinely require antibiotics, whereas bacterial aspiration pneumonia or hospital‐acquired pneumonia does [20].

This case series has limitations. First, the diagnosis of GE was based on multidisciplinary clinical‐radiologic assessment rather than histologic confirmation of intramural gas, and early EG cannot be excluded with absolute certainty, particularly because antibiotics were administered. Antimicrobial therapy may also have contributed to the favorable clinical course. Second, the proposed mechanisms are inferential and based on temporal association, clinical context, and exclusion of competing causes rather than direct proof of causality.

Taken together, these cases support a cautious, probabilistic approach to intramural gastric air. Stable patients without systemic toxicity, peritonitis, endoscopic necrosis, or clinical deterioration may be suitable for supportive nonoperative management, while patients with shock, ischemia, perforation, or progressive clinical decline require urgent escalation.

4. Conclusion

GE, or gastric pneumatosis, is an uncommon cause of intramural gastric air that may closely mimic EG on imaging. This case series highlights three clinical contexts in which probable GE was managed successfully without surgery: infectious gastroenteritis with retching, transient obstructive gastric overdistension, and alcohol‐related upper gastrointestinal mucosal injury. The diagnosis is often probabilistic, particularly when antibiotics are administered early. Careful integration of clinical stability, CT findings, procedural timing, endoscopic assessment, antibiotic indication, and follow‐up strategy can help distinguish probable GE from early EG and avoid unnecessary invasive intervention.
