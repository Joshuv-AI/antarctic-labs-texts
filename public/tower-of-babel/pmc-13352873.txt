Collaboration Between local health authorities and community leaders in health emergency response in low- and middle-income countries: a scoping review of evidence from the COVID-19 era
Authors: Emmanuel Kwang, Monica Ansu-Mensah, Ernest Osei, Desmond Kuupiel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCollaboration between local health authorities and community actors is widely recognised as central to effective emergency preparedness and response, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where health systems face persistent resource and governance constraints. However, empirical evidence on how such collaboration is conceptualised, operationalised, and evaluated remains fragmented. This scoping review mapped and synthesised available evidence on collaboration between local health authorities and community leaders (including community-facing actors) during health emergencies, using the COVID-19 pandemic as an illustrative context to inform further research.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley framework and reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar for studies published between January 2020 and October 2025. Eligible studies were primary empirical research conducted in LMICs that examined collaboration between local health authorities and community-facing actors involved in emergency responses. Data were extracted using a structured charting tool and synthesised using descriptive and narrative approaches.ResultsThree qualitative studies from Bangladesh and the Philippines met the inclusion criteria. Collaboration involved partnerships between local health authorities and non-governmental organisations, universities, and community-based actors, often formalised through coordination mechanisms. Reported strategies included joint planning, co-implementation of services, resource and workforce sharing, capacity building, and community outreach. These approaches were associated with enhanced surge capacity, continuity of essential services, strengthened system resilience, and improved access and community engagement. However, community leadership roles were rarely explicitly defined, with leadership functions often embedded within community-facing or intermediary actors.ConclusionThe evidence base on collaboration during health emergencies in LMICs remains limited and conceptually underdeveloped. Available studies suggest that collaboration between local health authorities and community-facing actors may support coordinated and resilient responses. However, important gaps persist in how community leadership is conceptualised, operationalised, and evaluated. Future research should more explicitly define and examine community leadership roles, alongside strengthening institutionalised and sustainable collaborative mechanisms for emergency preparedness and response.Trial registrationNot applicable. This scoping review does not involve a healthcare intervention or human participant enrolment. Background

Health emergencies including infectious disease outbreaks, pandemics, natural disasters, and other public health crises pose persistent and evolving threats to global health security. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, in particular, exposed profound vulnerabilities in health systems worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where limited resources, weak governance structures, and fragmented service delivery systems often constrain effective emergency response [1–4]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for coordinated, inclusive, and context-responsive approaches to health emergency preparedness and response.

Effective response to health emergencies depends not only on the technical capacity of health systems but also on the quality of coordination and leadership among multiple stakeholders. Poor coordination among response actors has been repeatedly identified as a major barrier to timely and effective emergency management, resulting in duplication of efforts, inefficient resource use, and reduced public trust [2, 5]. In LMICs, where health systems are frequently overstretched by recurrent epidemics, natural disasters, and socioeconomic instability, the consequences of weak coordination are often amplified [3, 4].

Collaboration between local health authorities and community leaders has emerged as a critical strategy for strengthening health emergency response in such contexts. Local health authorities are responsible for implementing policies, coordinating services, and overseeing public health interventions. In contrast, community leaders including traditional leaders, religious leaders, and leaders of community-based organisations play a central role in mobilising communities, facilitating communication, and ensuring the cultural acceptability of interventions [6–8]. When effectively engaged, community leaders potentially can enhance compliance with public health measures, counter misinformation, and support the mobilisation of local resources during emergencies [6, 9].

Community engagement has been widely recognised as a cornerstone of effective emergency response [6, 9]. It facilitates inclusive decision-making, strengthens trust between communities and institutions, and enables the co-development of locally appropriate solutions [6, 9]. However, public health emergency preparedness and response efforts have historically prioritised institutional and technical capacities over community knowledge, social networks, and leadership structures [9–11]. This imbalance can undermine response effectiveness, particularly in settings where community trust in formal institutions is fragile.

The importance of collaboration is further highlighted by experiences from previous health emergencies. During the Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) outbreak in West Africa, limited engagement with community structures in the early stages of the response contributed to widespread mistrust, resistance to public health measures, and the rapid spread of misinformation [12, 13]. These experiences demonstrated that neglecting community voices can weaken social mobilisation efforts and compromise emergency control strategies. In contrast, collaborative approaches that leverage trusted community leaders have been shown to improve risk communication, adherence to public health and social measures, and overall response effectiveness [10, 14, 15].

The COVID-19 pandemic has reinforced these lessons. Across many LMICs, health authorities increasingly relied on partnerships with community leaders, non-governmental organisations, faith-based organisations, and community health workers to support surveillance, contact tracing, health promotion, and service continuity [10, 15, 16]. These collaborations enabled more adaptive and locally responsive interventions but also revealed significant challenges, including governance weaknesses, digital divides, socio-cultural resistance, and substantial psychological and operational burdens on frontline workers.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of collaboration, evidence on how partnerships between local health authorities and community leaders’ function during health emergencies in LMICs remain fragmented. While several reviews have examined community engagement in infectious disease prevention, emergency preparedness, and outbreak response more broadly [17–19], there is limited synthesis focused specifically on collaborative strategies, their effectiveness, associated barriers, and lessons learned from partnerships between formal health authorities and community leadership structures during health emergencies.

To address this gap, this scoping review aims to map and synthesise existing evidence on collaboration between local health authorities and community leaders including community-facing actors during health emergencies, using the COVID-19 pandemic as an illustrative context. By synthesising evidence on collaborative strategies, enablers, barriers, and best practices, this review seeks to inform policy, practice, and future research aimed at strengthening inclusive, resilient, and community-centred health emergency response systems.

Methods

This scoping review’s protocol was developed a priori and published [20]. It mapped the available evidence on collaboration between local health authorities and community leaders in responding to health emergencies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [21], with enhancements by Levac et al., [22] and was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [23, 24]. These frameworks were selected to ensure a systematic, transparent, and reproducible approach to identifying, charting, and synthesising evidence.

Identify relevant studies

A comprehensive search strategy was developed in collaboration with a subject librarian to identify relevant literature. Key search terms and keywords related to collaboration, local health authorities, community leaders, health emergencies, and LMICs were identified and combined using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Boolean operators (AND/OR). For example, (“local health authority” OR “district health officer” OR “Ministry of Health”) AND (“community leader” OR “traditional leader” OR “religious leader”) AND (“COVID-19” OR “pandemic” OR “health emergency” OR “epidemic”) AND (“collaboration” OR “partnership” OR “joint response”).

Electronic database searches were conducted in MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, covering publications from database from January 2020 to October 2025. To enhance coverage, grey literature sources including reports from international organisations such as the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention were also searched. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible publications.

No restrictions were placed on publication language during the search and initial screening stages. Titles and abstracts were screened irrespective of language, with Google Translate available where necessary. However, all studies identified as eligible during full-text screening were published in English; therefore, only English-language full texts were included in the final review. All retrieved citations were imported into EndNote X20, where duplicates were identified and removed. The full search strategy and results are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

Study selection was conducted in two stages. First, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (EK and MA-M) against the eligibility criteria (Phase I). Studies that appeared potentially relevant were advanced to full-text screening. In the second stage (Phase II), full-text articles were independently assessed by the same reviewers. Discrepancies at either stage were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (EO) consulted when consensus could not be reached. A structured screening tool was developed using Google Forms and pilot-tested 10% of potentially eligible studies to ensure consistency and clarity. The study selection process was documented using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram, ensuring transparency and reproducibility [26].

Collating, summarising, and reporting the results

Data synthesis followed three complementary approaches: descriptive analysis, thematic analysis, and narrative synthesis. Descriptive analysis was used to summarise study characteristics and contextual features, while thematic analysis was applied to qualitative data to identify recurring patterns related to collaborative strategies, effectiveness, barriers, enablers, and lessons learned. A narrative synthesis was then used to integrate findings across studies and highlight key evidence gaps.

Consistent with scoping review methodology, no critical appraisal of methodological quality was undertaken, as the aim was to map the extent and nature of existing evidence rather than assess study quality. Findings were reported in line with the PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist [24].

Result

Study selection

Of the 140 records identified across databases, three (n = 3) studies met the eligibility criteria for this scoping review and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). This low inclusion yield (~ 2.1%) represents a notable finding of the review rather than merely a procedural outcome, indicating a substantial gap between the volume of published literature on COVID-19 and the limited number of studies explicitly examining collaboration between local health authorities and community-level actors in LMIC contexts. Notably, all included studies were conducted in only two countries Bangladesh and the Philippines with no eligible studies identified from Africa, Latin America, or other LMIC regions, highlighting important geographic gaps in the evidence base.

Summary of collaboration characteristics, outcomes, and lessons from included studies

Actors and collaboration context

Across the three included studies, collaboration during the COVID-19 pandemic in LMIC settings was consistently characterised by partnerships linking local health authorities with community-facing organisations and institutions, although the composition and depth of these partnerships varied [27–29]. A common pattern across all studies was the reliance on intermediary actors, particularly NGOs and institutional partners as key bridges between formal health systems and communities. In Bangladesh, collaboration centred on an NGO working through formal memoranda of understanding with the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) and district hospitals, positioning the NGO as an embedded implementation partner within public service delivery [27]. In contrast, studies from the Philippines demonstrated more networked and multi-actor configurations, including NGO–government coordination involving community-based actors and partnerships between local government units and universities, which emphasised workforce support, capacity building, and system recovery functions [28, 29]. Despite these contextual differences, a shared feature across all studies was the indirect representation of community leadership. None of the studies explicitly conceptualised traditional or religious leaders as distinct actors; instead, leadership and community engagement functions were embedded within NGOs, community-based workers, or institutional intermediaries.

Collaborative strategies

Across the included studies, collaborative strategies converged around three core functions: coordination, co-implementation of services, and resource and workforce sharing, although the form and emphasis of these strategies varied by context [27–29]. A common cross-cutting feature was the use of coordination mechanisms to align actors and sustain service delivery during the pandemic. However, the degree of operational integration differed. In Bangladesh, collaboration was more tightly integrated within service delivery, with joint planning and co-management of COVID-19 units embedded within district hospitals, reflecting a service-oriented and implementation-focused model [27]. In contrast, the Philippine studies demonstrated more distributed and supportive collaboration models, where coordination was coupled with community outreach, infection prevention and control guidance, and workforce protection, particularly for community-based actors [28], as well as capacity building and flexible redeployment of personnel through university–LGU partnerships to support response and recovery [29]. Across all studies, resource sharing including staff, infrastructure, and technical expertise was a key enabling strategy, though its application ranged from direct service provision to indirect system support. Notably, digital tools were consistently used in a supportive capacity, primarily facilitating communication, coordination, and training rather than functioning as central mechanisms of collaboration [28, 29]. Overall, while collaborative strategies were contextually adapted, they collectively illustrate a shift towards flexible, multi-actor arrangements that prioritised continuity of services and system resilience, albeit with limited explicit focus on community leadership as a distinct strategic component.

Effectiveness and overall outcomes

Across the three included studies, collaboration was consistently associated with positive contributions to emergency response, particularly in maintaining service delivery under conditions of system strain [27–29]. A common outcome across all contexts was the enhancement of operational continuity, although the pathways through which this was achieved differed. In Bangladesh, effectiveness was closely linked to the direct integration of NGO–government collaboration within service delivery, enabling expansion of surge capacity and sustained provision of COVID-19 care [27]. In contrast, the Philippine studies reflected more indirect and system-supportive effects, where collaboration contributed to maintaining community-based services [28] and strengthening recovery capacity through institutional partnerships [29]. Despite these contextual differences, effectiveness across studies was predominantly framed in functional and operational terms such as continuity of services, system responsiveness, and stability of delivery structures rather than in terms of measurable health outcomes or comparative effectiveness. This highlights a shared limitation in the evidence base, where the benefits of collaboration are largely inferred from descriptive accounts rather than evaluated using standardised or quantitative indicators. As a result, while the studies collectively suggest that collaboration supports resilient emergency response, the extent and magnitude of its impact remain difficult to assess across settings.

Health system outcomes

Across the included studies, collaboration was consistently associated with strengthening health system functionality during the COVID-19 response, particularly through enhanced surge capacity, continuity of essential services, and improved coordination across actors [27–29]. A key cross-cutting pattern was the role of collaboration in stabilising service delivery under crisis conditions, although the mechanisms through which this occurred varied. In Bangladesh, health system strengthening was driven by direct integration of NGO–government collaboration within service delivery, resulting in expanded treatment capacity, including the establishment and operation of COVID-19 units and ICUs at district level [27]. In contrast, the Philippine studies reflected more decentralised and system-supportive effects, where collaboration enabled continuity of services through community-based workers operating under restrictive conditions [28], and through multi-sectoral partnerships, particularly between LGUs and universities that supported workforce capacity and system recovery [29]. Despite these differences, a shared outcome across all studies was the reinforcement of system resilience, defined primarily in terms of the ability to maintain service delivery and adapt to evolving demands. However, these outcomes were largely reported descriptively, with limited use of standardised indicators to assess system performance or resilience comparatively across settings.

Community outcomes

At the community level, collaboration was consistently associated with improved access to services, continuity of support, and the maintenance of trust and engagement, particularly among underserved populations [27–29]. Across studies, a common mechanism underlying these outcomes was the use of community-facing actors to extend health system functions into local settings. However, the pathways through which community outcomes were achieved differed. In Bangladesh, improvements in access were primarily driven by strengthened facility-based services at the district level [27], whereas in the Philippines, outcomes were more closely linked to community outreach, referral systems, and ongoing engagement facilitated by local actors and community-based workers [28]. Similarly, university–LGU partnerships contributed indirectly to community-level benefits through institutional outreach and support mechanisms [29]. Despite these positive patterns, community outcomes were generally reported in broad and descriptive terms, with limited disaggregation by population subgroup, equity dimensions, or context-specific vulnerabilities. This limits deeper understanding of how collaborative approaches affect different population groups and highlights a gap in the evidence regarding the equity implications of such collaborations.

Barriers and challenges

Across the included studies, barriers to effective collaboration were consistently rooted in systemic, workforce, and contextual constraints, although their manifestation varied by setting [27–29]. A common cross-cutting challenge was the limited preparedness of health systems at the onset of the pandemic, reflected in shortages of funding, staffing, and essential supplies such as personal protective equipment, as well as fragmented or rapidly evolving guidance that complicated coordination efforts. These system-level constraints created a challenging environment for establishing and sustaining collaborative arrangements.

Workforce-related challenges emerged as a prominent and shared theme across studies, particularly in the Philippine contexts, where burnout, psychological stress, and fear among frontline and community-based workers affected service delivery and continuity [28, 29]. In addition, social barriers such as stigma and community-level resistance further constrained implementation, highlighting the interaction between health system efforts and broader socio-cultural dynamics [27, 28]. Digital inequities represented another important cross-cutting barrier, particularly in settings where collaboration relied on virtual platforms for coordination, communication, or training. Uneven access to connectivity and digital devices limited participation and effectiveness of these approaches [28, 29]. Overall, these findings suggest that barriers to collaboration were multi-level, spanning structural, operational, and socio-cultural domains, and often interacted to shape the effectiveness and sustainability of collaborative response efforts.

Enablers and facilitators

Across the included studies, enablers of effective collaboration operated at relational, structural, and operational levels, with several common factors underpinning successful partnerships [27–29]. A consistent cross-cutting enabler was the presence of pre-existing relationships and trust between actors, which facilitated rapid coordination and reduced barriers to joint action during the emergency. These relational factors were often reinforced by formalised collaboration mechanisms, such as agreements or established coordination structures, which helped clarify roles and support more organised and sustained engagement.

At the operational level, practical support measures were critical in enabling collaboration to translate into sustained service delivery. Across contexts, provision of essential resources including personal protective equipment, access to testing, training, and psychosocial support played a key role in maintaining workforce participation and mitigating the challenges experienced by frontline and community-based actors [27, 28]. In addition, adaptive practices emerged as an important enabling factor, particularly in dynamic and resource-constrained environments. These included flexible redeployment of personnel, context-specific adjustments to guidelines, and the ability to modify implementation approaches in response to evolving needs [28, 29].

While the specific configurations of these enablers varied across settings, a shared pattern was their interdependence: trust and relationships facilitated coordination, formal structures provided stability, and operational supports enabled continuity. Together, these factors highlight that effective collaboration during health emergencies is not driven by a single element, but by the alignment of relational, institutional, and adaptive capacities within local systems.

Key lessons and gaps

Across the included studies, a consistent lesson is that collaboration in LMIC health emergencies is most effective when it is institutionalised, adequately resourced, and embedded within local systems, particularly where it supports workforce protection and continuity of essential services [27–29]. Despite differences in context and partnership models, the studies collectively suggest that collaborations function best when relational trust, formal coordination structures, and operational support mechanisms are aligned to enable adaptive and sustained response efforts.

However, several critical gaps were evident across the evidence base. Most notably, there was a consistent lack of explicit conceptualisation and analysis of community leaders as distinct actors. Instead, leadership functions were often implicit or mediated through NGOs, community-based workers, or institutional partners, limiting understanding of the specific roles, influence, and agency of formally recognised community leaders within emergency response systems. In addition, evidence on effectiveness was predominantly descriptive, with minimal use of rigorous evaluative designs or standardised outcome measures, constraining comparative assessment across settings.

A further gap relates to the temporal scope of existing studies, which focused largely on immediate response phases, with limited attention to the sustainability of collaborative arrangements beyond the acute emergency period. Taken together, these gaps highlight the need for future research to more clearly define and operationalise community leadership, adopt more robust evaluative approaches, and examine the long-term integration and impact of collaborative models across diverse health emergency contexts.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped the available empirical evidence on collaboration between local health authorities and community actors during health emergencies in LMICs, using COVID-19 as an illustrative context. Despite the extensive volume of COVID-19–related literature, only three studies met the inclusion criteria, representing approximately 2.1% of the records identified. This low yield is itself a significant finding, suggesting not only limited evidence base but also a potential misalignment between how collaboration is conceptualised in practice and how it is reported in academic literature. It may reflect a combination of factors, including (i) terminological variation, where collaboration with community actors is described under broader constructs such as “community engagement” or “health systems response” [30]; (ii) a genuine gap in empirical research explicitly examining authority–community collaboration in LMIC settings; and (iii) a tendency for such collaborations to be embedded within broader programme descriptions rather than analysed as a distinct object of study.

In addition, the geographic concentration of included studies in only two countries (Bangladesh and the Philippines) further limits the extent to which the findings can be interpreted as representative of LMIC contexts more broadly. The absence of eligible studies from regions such as Africa and Latin America, despite their extensive experience with health emergencies, is itself an important finding. This may reflect disparities in research production, differences in publication practices, or the under-documentation of collaboration in peer-reviewed literature.

A further conceptual issue identified in this scoping review relates to the distinction between “community leaders” and “community-facing actors.” While the scoping review initially focused on formal community leaders such as traditional, religious, and community-based organisation leaders with recognised social authority, the included studies primarily examined collaboration with community-facing institutional actors, including NGOs, universities, and local government units [27–29]. These actors differ in their roles, accountability structures, and relationships to both communities and health systems consistent with broader public health emergency preparedness literature [31]. Such arrangements facilitated rapid mobilisation, role clarification, and continuity of essential services during periods of system strain [31]. In the context of this review, they were included where they performed community mobilisation, coordination, or intermediary functions. However, this distinction is important, as it highlights that the evidence base reflects collaboration mediated through institutional actors rather than direct engagement with formally recognised community leadership. This conceptual gap underscores the need for future research to more clearly define and differentiate these actor categories.

The collaborative strategies identified coordination and joint planning, co-implementation of services, resource and workforce sharing, capacity building, and community outreach were associated with positive health-system outcomes, including enhanced surge capacity, sustained service delivery, and strengthened system resilience [27–29]. At the community level, collaborations supported improved access to services and the maintenance of trust and engagement during the pandemic. These findings are broadly consistent with existing literature suggesting the importance of leadership, coordination, and multi-sectoral action in effective emergency response [32].

However, important gaps were evident. Community leaders were rarely conceptualised or analysed as distinct actors; instead, leadership roles were often implicit and embedded within NGOs, community-based workers, or institutional intermediaries, limiting conceptual clarity and analytical depth [28, 29]. This limits understanding of how traditional, religious, or civic leaders shape decision-making, compliance, and accountability during health emergencies an issue widely recognised in the community engagement literature [30]. Furthermore, effectiveness was largely reported descriptively, with limited use of comparative designs or standardised outcome measures, constraining assessment of impact and transferability.

The conceptual synthesis (Fig. 2) based on the included studies illustrates how collaboration between local health authorities and community-facing actors is operationalised through specific strategies to generate health-system and community-level outcomes. This synthesis suggests the potential role of institutionalised partnerships, trust, and adaptive practices in extending health-system resilience into communities during crises, consistent with established resilience frameworks [28, 33].

Implications for policy, practice and research

For policymakers, these findings underscore the importance of formalising authority–community collaboration within emergency preparedness and response frameworks. Policies should explicitly recognise community leaders and community-facing institutions as strategic partners, with clearly defined roles, accountability mechanisms, and sustained resourcing beyond crisis periods. Embedding collaborative structures within national and subnational emergency plans may strengthen preparedness and reduce reliance on ad hoc arrangements during future health emergencies.

From a practice perspective, the review highlights the value of pre-existing partnerships, trust, and coordination mechanisms at local levels. Strengthening routine collaboration between health authorities and community actors including NGOs and educational institutions may enhance readiness and facilitate rapid mobilisation during emergencies. Attention to workforce protection, communication, and capacity building remains critical for sustaining service delivery and community trust under crisis conditions.

The limited number of eligible studies reveals a significant gap in empirical research explicitly examining collaboration between local health authorities and community leaders. Future research should adopt clearer conceptual definitions of community leadership, examine governance and power dynamics within collaborative arrangements, and employ more rigorous evaluative designs to assess effectiveness and sustainability. Comparative studies across emergency types and geographic contexts would further strengthen the evidence base and inform scalable collaborative models.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review was conducted using a systematic and transparent approach guided by the Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review methodological framework [21], with enhancements by Levac et al., [22], and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines, enhancing methodological rigour and reproducibility. By synthesising empirical evidence on collaboration during health emergencies in LMICs and presenting findings through structured charting, narrative synthesis, and a conceptual figure, the review contributes to a clearer understanding of collaborative processes relevant to population-level emergency response.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Only a small number of studies met the inclusion criteria; however, this should be interpreted not only as a limitation but also as a key finding of the review. The low inclusion yield reflects important gaps in how collaboration is conceptualised, operationalised, and reported in the peer-reviewed literature. In addition, all included studies were conducted in only two countries, limiting geographic representation and reducing the extent to which findings can be generalised across diverse LMIC settings. This geographic concentration highlights disparities in the distribution and documentation of research on health emergency collaboration.

A further limitation relates to conceptual heterogeneity. While the review aimed to examine collaboration involving community leaders, the included studies primarily focused on community-facing institutional actors, with leadership roles often implicit rather than explicitly defined. This constrains the ability to draw conclusions about the distinct roles of formally recognised community leaders and reinforces the need for clearer conceptualisation in future research.

Despite these limitations, the review provides a valuable public health contribution by mapping existing evidence, identifying critical gaps, and informing future policy, practice, and research on collaborative health emergency responses in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

This scoping review synthesised a limited body of empirical evidence on collaboration between local health authorities and community-facing actors, including but not consistently limited to explicitly defined community leaders, during health emergencies in LMICs, using COVID-19 as an illustrative context. The limited available evidence suggests that collaborative arrangement involving local authorities and community-facing organisations may support coordination, continuity of essential services, and local system resilience during crises. At the same time, the review highlights persistent gaps in how such collaboration is conceptualised and evaluated. Strengthening future preparedness and response will require policies that institutionalise collaborative mechanisms, practice-level investment in sustained local partnerships, and research that rigorously examines governance, effectiveness, and sustainability across diverse emergency contexts. However, the small number of studies and their concentration in two countries highlight substantial gaps in both the geographic and conceptual coverage of the literature, underscoring the need for more diverse and rigorous research in this area.
