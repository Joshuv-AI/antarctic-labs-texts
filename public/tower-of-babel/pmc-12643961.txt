Maternal Satisfaction With Universal Postnatal Nurse Home Visiting Services and Its Covariates: A Cross-Sectional Survey of 22,040 Mothers With Young Children in Seoul, Korea
Authors: Young-Ho Khang, Yu-Mi Kim, Ji Yun Lee, Kyung Ja June, Sung-Hyun Cho, Hong-Jun Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUniversal postnatal nurse home visitation services were introduced in Seoul, Korea in 2013 and are now being expanded nationally with government funding. This study examined maternal satisfaction with these services and identified factors associated with satisfaction among mothers of young children.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted with 22,040 mothers who participated in universal home visitation services between 2017 and 2019 in Seoul, Korea, approximately one month after service delivery. Maternal satisfaction was measured using a modified version of the Parent Satisfaction Questionnaire-18. The study estimated least square means of maternal satisfaction scores and prevalence ratios (PRs) of scoring 33 or higher (fidelity indicator) according to covariates.ResultsThe average satisfaction score was 36.27 (standard deviation: 4.32) out of 40. About 80.7% scored 33 or higher, and only 0.2% scored below 16. Among the four dimensions, “communication” had the highest score (9.34/10), whereas “general satisfaction” had the lowest (8.77). Satisfaction was lower among mothers aged 23 or younger (P = 0.002), from culturally diverse backgrounds (P < 0.001), with high Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale scores (P < 0.001), experiencing suicidal ideation (P < 0.001), or with childhood abuse experience (P = 0.019). These groups also had lower PRs of scoring 33 or higher. Mothers with two or more home visits had higher satisfaction scores (P < 0.001) and were more likely to score 33 or higher (PR, 1.05; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–1.07; P < 0.001). Participation in a mothers’ group program was also linked to higher satisfaction (P < 0.001) and increased PRs of scoring 33 or higher (PR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.02–1.09; P < 0.001). These relationships remained consistent after adjusting for other covariates.ConclusionMothers with young children who received universal postnatal nurse home visits reported high satisfaction. Increasing visit frequency and expanding mothers’ group program opportunities could further improve satisfaction.Keywords: Cross-Sectional Survey, Maternal Satisfaction, Nurse Home Visiting, Satisfaction, Universal Home Visiting, Republic of Korea

INTRODUCTION

Early childhood universal home visitation often led by nurses, or universal visits, supports families with young children without requiring eligibility criteria, helping identify those needing additional assistance.1,2,3 This universal approach ensures the impartial determination of recipients and the fair allocation of services,4 providing support for all families during a critical transition period, thereby improving program reach and reducing stigma.1,2,5 Such services have a long history in Australia, Europe, and UK6 and are also implemented in Japan7 and parts of the USA.2,3,5 Previous randomized controlled trials showed that universal postnatal nurse home visits reduced emergency care and child maltreatment while increasing exclusive breastfeeding rates.8,9,10

In Korea, universal postnatal nurse home visitation services began in 2013 in three districts under the Seoul Healthy First Step Project, an early childhood home visitation initiative developed by the Seoul Metropolitan Government.11 By 2020, it expanded to all 25 districts in Seoul12 and is now being rolled out nationwide as part of the Early Life Health Management Program, funded by the Ministry of Health and Welfare.13,14 Therefore, universal home visits for families with young children in Korea are anticipated to expand further.

Patient satisfaction and experiences are key indicators of healthcare quality.15 Satisfaction reflects personal expectations and subjective opinions, while experience focuses the care episode and patient perception.16 Donabedian’s framework emphasizes patient satisfaction as a critical outcome,17 influencing loyalty and treatment adherence, which in turn improves clinical outcomes.18,19 High satisfaction in universal visits can enhance sustainability of the services and identify families needing further support. While several studies mainly from Australia have reported maternal satisfaction with sustained nurse home visitation services,20,21,22 research on maternal satisfaction with universal nurse home visiting services remains limited.

This study aimed to evaluate maternal satisfaction with universal postnatal nurse home visits and identify factors influencing satisfaction among 22,040 mothers participating in the Seoul Healthy First Step Project, with a focus on visit frequency and service content. Specifically, we hypothesized that the characteristics of nurse home visits could affect maternal satisfaction measured after home visits, while other covariates, such as sociodemographic and psychosocial factors, and pregnancy and delivery characteristics, may act as confounders (Supplementary Fig. 1).

METHODS

Study design

The study design is a cross-sectional analysis of online satisfaction surveys conducted via smartphone ensuring confidentiality.

Study setting and universal postnatal nurse home visiting services

Seoul, the capital of Korea, has a population of about 10 million, or 20% of the national population. The city consists of 25 districts. By 2019, about 100 nurses were employed in the District Public Health Centers and satisfaction surveys were conducted in 22 districts in 2017–2018 and 24 districts in 2019.23

The Seoul Healthy First Step Project includes two types of nurse home visits: universal visits and sustained visits for vulnerable families.11,12,23 Universal visits, offered to all families with newborns who consent, were provided to 19.6% of families in Seoul with newborns in 2017, 23.9% in 2018, and 31.8% in 2019.12 Vulnerable families, identified by factors like low income, young maternal age, or other social challenges, receive sustained visits with 25 sessions from prenatal to age 2.12,23 The Maternal and Early Childhood Sustained Home-visiting (MECSH) program was incorporated into the Project to support these sustained visits.11,24 Between 2013 and 2019, 5.4% of families received sustained visits.23 While satisfaction with universal visits was measured online via smartphone, sustained visits used paper surveys with a slightly different 10-item questionnaire.12,23

Universal visits are typically conducted within the first six weeks postpartum. Most families (92.4%) receive one visit, while 6.7% receive two, and 0.9% receive three or more.23 According to the service protocol, follow-up visits are provided to families with unmet needs (e.g., breastfeeding, infant crying) after the initial universal visits.12 Visits last about 80–85 minutes and cover topics such as infant physical exams, growth assessment, breastfeeding, baby crying, child safety, and maternal health.12 Nurses undergo about 80 hours of training, including lectures, hands-on practice, and case conferences. A dedicated database tracks visit activities and provides feedback to nurses, aiming to improve performance.12

The Seoul Healthy First Step Project also offers a mothers’ group program for a small subset of mothers (less than 5%) following the universal visits.11,12,23 The mothers’ group program is typically offered in the Spring and Fall for mothers with infants of similar age (e.g., 10 weeks postpartum) living in comparable residential areas.12 Nurses at each District Public Health Center usually organize one class of mothers’ group each season. It consists of five two-hour sessions covering topics such as soothing techniques, baby sleep, baby massage, child development and play, and nutrition. The sessions are held at public facilities and accommodate 5–10 mother-baby pairs.

Study participants

Between 2017 and 2019, 43,396 mothers in Seoul utilized universal postnatal nurse home visitation services (Supplementary Table 1). Of these, 22,317 completed maternal satisfaction surveys via smartphone. According to the Pew Research Center’s 2018 survey, 95% of Korean adults owned smartphones, with 99% among those aged 18–34.25 The survey response rate was 51.4%, ranging from 46.8% in 2018 to 56.9% in 2019. For our analysis, a total of 22,040 (98.8% of 22,317) were included for our analysis, after excluding 277 cases with missing data on key variables except for pregnancy and delivery characteristics.

Maternal satisfaction questionnaires

A modified version of the Parent Satisfaction Questionnaire-18 (PSQ-18) was used to measure maternal satisfaction. The PSQ-18 has been used as a service satisfaction measure in sustained nurse home visitation services21,22 and adopted for assessing service satisfaction in universal home visits within the Seoul Healthy First Step Project.11,12,23 The original PSQ-18 assesses seven satisfaction dimensions: general satisfaction, interpersonal manner, communication, time spent, accessibility and convenience, technical quality, and financial aspects.26 Previous studies adapted the PSQ-18 by replacing “patient” with “parent,” “doctor” with “child health nurse,” and removing technical quality and financial aspects.20,21,27,28 For this study, we further excluded accessibility and convenience (Supplementary Table 2) for the satisfaction survey of universal visits, as they apply more to multiple visits, and replaced “child health nurse” with “home visiting nurse” to match Korean terminology.

Supplementary Table 2 shows satisfaction survey questionnaires used in the Seoul Healthy First Step Project, outlining their dimensions and scoring framework, which have been similarly employed in previous literature.20,21,22,26 The questionnaires, translated and back-translated for accuracy, consist of eight items across four dimensions. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale. For positive statements (e.g., “The nurse was good at explaining aspects of child care”), a “strongly agree” response gets a score of 5, and a “strongly disagree” gets 1. For negative statements (e.g., “The nurse acted to impersonal toward me”), the scoring is reversed. A one-point difference in PSQ scores corresponds to a shift of one grade in the level of agreement (e.g., from “strongly agree” to “agree” or from “agree” to “uncertain”). Given the limited practical utility of PSQ mean scores, we focused on the proportion of mothers with scores of 33 or higher on the PSQ. A PSQ score of 33 or higher was considered an indicator of fidelity for universal visits,11,12,23 representing scores greater than the average of 4 points per item on the 5-point Likert scale across the 8 items of PSQ. Meanwhile, a PSQ score of 16 or lower (below 4 per dimension) indicated concern.21

Covariates

Questions (or risk factor identification process) and response options (or risk factor categories) for all covariates were presented in Supplementary Table 3. All covariates from sociodemographic and psychosocial factors as well as start of obstetric care of pregnancy and delivery characteristics are vulnerability criteria for sustained visits. Pregnancy planning and delivery method were included as covariates since they were expected to be associated with maternal mental health.29,30 Nurse home visitation characteristics were selected for their potential as intervention targets.

Sociodemographic factors

This study considered five sociodemographic factors as covariates: maternal age, economic status, disability, single motherhood, and culturally diverse families. These factors were based on a baseline registration form completed by mothers using the services. Maternal age was categorized as 23 or younger, and 24 or older. Economic status was classified as low income (receiving basic living security program benefits or earning ≤ 50% of the national median household income) or not. Disability included any physical or mental disabilities. Single mothers were those who were widowed, divorced, or legally unmarried. Culturally diverse families were identified based on whether the mother was born or raised abroad.

Psychosocial factors

This study considered five psychosocial factors: the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), suicidal ideation, history of emotional treatment, childhood abuse, and need for domestic violence assistance. These were assessed using a baseline registration form completed by the mothers. The 10-item EPDS, with scores ranging from 0 to 30, was used to screen for peripartum depression,31,32,33 with scores categorized as < 10 (none or minimal depression), 10–12 (mild depression), and ≥ 13 (moderate or severe depression). Our prior review indicated that 10 and 13 points of EPDS were widely used as cut-points for maternal depression.34 The 10th item assessed suicidal ideation, where a score of 1 or higher indicated its presence. A history of treatment due to emotional issues was identified if the mother answered “yes” to the question, “Have you ever been treated for emotional issues?” Childhood abuse and the need for domestic violence assistance were defined by affirmative responses to the questions, “Have you ever been physically, emotionally, or sexually abused during childhood?” and “Do you need any help with domestic violence?” respectively.

Pregnancy and delivery characteristics

The study examined three pregnancy and delivery characteristics: start of obstetric care, pregnancy planning, and delivery method. These factors were derived from nurses’ records during universal visits. Missing data arose due to time constraints during visits or a lack of awareness among nurses in asking the relevant questions. Mothers reported the gestational age at which they began obstetric care, with prenatal care considered delayed if initiated at 20 weeks or later. They were also asked whether their pregnancy was planned, and responses were recorded accordingly. Additionally, mothers indicated their delivery method, specifying either vaginal birth or cesarean section.

Nurse home visitation characteristics

We analyzed five nurse home visitation characteristics as covariates: the number of visits, education on feeding, education on baby crying, participation in the mothers’ group program, and the experience of the home visiting nurses. The first four characteristics were obtained from the Seoul Healthy First Step Project’s database, while nurse experience data came from internal records maintained by the Project’s Support Team. The number of visits was categorized as either one or two or more visits. Education on feeding and baby crying, as well as mothers’ group participation, were recorded by the nurses in the database. Nurse experience was classified into two groups: those with 3 or more years of experience and those with less than 3 years.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with a P value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. We reported the percentage of mothers in different satisfaction score categories, along with the mean scores and standard deviations (SDs) for the eight survey items and four dimensions of maternal satisfaction (Table 1). The distribution of total maternal satisfaction scores (ranging from 8 to 40) by calendar year was also provided (Supplementary Table 4). Mean differences in satisfaction scores across covariates were assessed using t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA). Considering the nonparametric distribution of the satisfaction scores (Supplementary Table 4), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was also applied for comparing rank distributions of two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparing three groups. When the outcome is common (e.g., PSQ scores being 33 or over is over 80%), odds ratios by logistic regression may deliver a misleading information regarding the magnitude of association.35 Therefore, PROC GENMOD was used to compare prevalences of mothers scoring 33 or higher according to covariates (Tables 2, 3, and 4). We examined changes in prevalence ratios (PRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between nurse home visitation characteristics and maternal satisfaction after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, psychosocial factors, and pregnancy and delivery characteristics (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5). In the final model, the clustering among the 24 District Public Health Centers was considered (Table 4). Supplementary Table 6 presented least squares means and their 95% CIs for maternal satisfaction scores, calculated using analysis of covariance after adjusting for sociodemographic and psychosocial factors and pregnancy and delivery characteristics.

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board of Kangwon University approved the study (KWNUIRB-2020-06-003). Participants (mothers) provided informed consent for themselves and gave parental permission for their babies regarding the home visiting services and the subsequent satisfaction surveys.

RESULTS

Scores of maternal satisfaction survey questionnaires

Supplementary Table 4 shows the distribution of total maternal satisfaction scores by calendar year from 2017 to 2019. Among the 22,040 mothers who received postnatal nurse home visitation services, the average score was 36.27 (SD: 4.32) out of 40. Notably, 80.7% of mothers scored 33 or higher, while only 0.2% scored below 16. Satisfaction scores remained consistent across the years. Table 1 presents scores for eight satisfaction survey items and their four corresponding dimensions. Among the dimensions, “communication” had the highest score (9.34/10), while “general satisfaction” had the lowest (8.77). The item “the nurse ignored what I said” received the highest rating (4.71/5), while “the care I received from the nurse was just about perfect” had the lowest score (4.31). Despite this, 86.1% of mothers (46.9% strongly agreed, 39.2% agreed) felt that “the care I received from the nurse was just about perfect.” Items scoring 1 or 2 were rare, with the highest percentage for “The nurse acted too impersonal toward me” (2.0% strongly agreed, 1.2% agreed).

Relationships between mothers’ characteristics and satisfaction

Table 2 presents the distribution of maternal sociodemographic and psychosocial factors. The proportion of maternal sociodemographic risk factors—such as being 23 years old or younger, having low income, having a disability, being a single mother, or belonging to a culturally diverse family—was generally low, with most factors at 1% or less. However, the prevalence of EPDS scores within the ranges of 10–12 and 13 or higher was 10.1% and 4.9%, respectively. Additionally, 3.4% of mothers reported suicidal ideation (a score of 1–3 on the EPDS 10th item), and 3.2% had a history of treatment due to emotional issues. Childhood abuse was reported by 2.5% of mothers, and approximately 0.3% indicated a need for assistance with domestic violence.

Table 2 also provides details on pregnancy and delivery characteristics. A small proportion of mothers (0.2%) reported starting obstetric care at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later. Additionally, 17.6% of mothers reported that their pregnancy was unplanned, and 40.7% had a cesarean section. Missing data were noted for these pregnancy and delivery characteristics: 19.7% for the start of obstetric care, 6.7% for planned pregnancy, and 6.4% for delivery type.

The table further shows the results of comparisons of maternal satisfaction scores (via t-test and ANOVA for mean scores and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and the Kruskal-Wallis test for rank distributions) and the PRs of achieving satisfaction scores of 33 or higher (via PROC GENMOD), based on sociodemographic and psychosocial factors. Mothers aged 23 years or younger, and those from culturally diverse backgrounds, had significantly lower satisfaction scores and lower PRs of scoring 33 or higher compared to their counterparts. Families with low incomes tended to have a lower PR of high satisfaction scores (PR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.75–1.01; P = 0.076). Additionally, mothers with high EPDS scores, suicidal ideation, and childhood abuse experience had significantly lower satisfaction scores and lower PRs of scoring 33 or higher compared to those without these issues.

Table 2 also showed that significant differences in mean satisfaction scores existed based on the gestational age at which obstetric care began (P = 0.015 from ANOVA) and the delivery method (P = 0.019 from ANOVA). Mothers who had a cesarean section also had a lower PR of high satisfaction scores (PR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.97–1.00; P = 0.037). Mothers who did not plan their pregnancy also had a lower PR of high satisfaction scores (PR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.96–1.00; P = 0.028).

Relationships between characteristics of nurse home visits and satisfaction

Table 3 presents the relationships between the characteristics of home visiting services and maternal satisfaction scores, as well as PRs of achieving high satisfaction scores (33 or higher). Mothers who received home visiting services two or more times reported significantly higher mean satisfaction scores (P < 0.001) and higher PRs of achieving high satisfaction scores (PR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.03–1.07; P < 0.001). Similarly, mothers who participated in a mothers’ group program also reported higher mean satisfaction scores (P < 0.001) and increased PRs of achieving high satisfaction scores (PR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.02–1.09; P < 0.001). Similar significant differences in rank distributions of satisfaction scores were detected from the Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. However, no significant differences were observed in mean satisfaction scores (via t-test) and rank distributions of the scores (via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) or PRs of high satisfaction scores based on whether education on feeding or baby crying was provided, or on the experience level of the home visiting nurses.

Confounder-adjusted relationships between characteristics of nurse home visits and satisfaction

Table 4 presents the relationships between the characteristics of home visiting services and maternal satisfaction, after confounder adjustment and consideration of cluster effects of regions (24 District Public Health Centers). These statistical considerations did not affect the relationships between the characteristics of home visiting services and maternal satisfaction. After full statistical adjustments and consideration of cluster effects, mothers who received two or more home visits reported significantly higher PRs of achieving high maternal satisfaction scores compared to those who received only one visit (PR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.03–1.08, P < 0.001). Additionally, participation in a mothers’ group program was associated with increased PRs of achieving high satisfaction scores (PR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.02–1.08; P < 0.001). However, other characteristics of home visiting services were not significantly associated with high maternal satisfaction scores. Supplementary Table 5 shows consistent PRs of high satisfaction scores after adjusting for each of covariates. Supplementary Table 6 displays the relationship for adjusted least squares means of maternal satisfaction scores and revealed that the results are consistent with those in Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5.

DISCUSSION

Previous systematic reviews have highlighted the global interest in patient satisfaction,36,37,38 but research on parent satisfaction with nurse home visiting services remains limited.22 Several studies have reported maternal satisfaction with sustained nurse home visitation,20,21,22 but there is a paucity of information on mothers’ satisfaction with universal nurse home visitation. Few studies have used the PSQ to assess maternal satisfaction with these services.20,21,27,28,39

In this study, the average total maternal satisfaction score was 36.27 out of 40, with 80.7% of mothers scoring 33 or higher. The highest satisfaction score was observed among mothers participating in the mothers’ group program (36.96), while the lowest was among those with delayed obstetric care (34.34) (Tables 2 and 3). Compared to previous studies using the short-form PSQ for maternal satisfaction,20,21 these results are notably high. For example, a recent study of 352 mothers in a sustained nurse home visiting program reported a total score of 36.06 out of 40,21 and another study on sustained home visitation from the late 1990s found a score of 36.37 for the same dimensions.20 It should be noted that our prior report indicated that PSQ scores were higher for sustained home visitation compared to universal visits.11 In this study, the average score per dimension was 9.07 (36.27/4 = 9.07) out of a possible 10 points. The lowest mean score, 4.3, was for the item “The care I received from the nurse was just about perfect” (Table 1), corresponding to responses exceeding ‘agree.’ Given the phrasing, this score does not indicate a low level of satisfaction. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, only 0.2% of scores were below 16, similar to the 0.4% found in an Australian study.21

Two previous studies on maternal satisfaction with nurse home visiting services reported dimension-specific scores.20,21 In our study, the highest score was in the “communication” dimension, while the “general satisfaction” dimension received the lowest score, aligning with the recent MECSH-based study.21 However, a study from the late 1990s found the highest score in “interpersonal manner” and the lowest in “time spent.”20 The similarity between our results and the Australian study could be due to the incorporation of the MECSH program in the Seoul Healthy First Step Project,11,24 both of which utilize the Family Partnership Model,40 emphasizing active listening and understanding in home visiting practices.

The characteristics of nurse home visitation, such as visit frequency, timing, and intensity, can significantly influence maternal satisfaction. A systematic review found that these factors can impact postpartum intervention outcomes, including satisfaction with services.41

In this study, mothers who received two or more home visits reported higher satisfaction scores than those who received only one. Similarly, mothers who participated in a mothers’ group program had higher satisfaction scores than those who did not. These differences remained significant after adjusting for sociodemographic, psychosocial, and pregnancy and delivery characteristics (Table 4 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Other studies, including a cluster randomized controlled trial in Northern Ireland, showed that mothers who received multiple home visits reported greater satisfaction.42 Randomized trials have also indicated that mothers who received nurse home visits rated their care more positively than those receiving clinic visits or hospital follow-ups.43,44 A Korean study found that parenting support group programs improved parenting competency and reduced parenting stress,45 while a Finnish study showed that positive interactions with other mothers improved life satisfaction through social support.46

This study found that certain demographic and clinical factors were associated with lower maternal satisfaction scores. Younger mothers, mothers from culturally diverse backgrounds, those experiencing depression, suicidal ideation, or childhood abuse, and those who received delayed obstetric care or underwent cesarean sections tended to report lower satisfaction. These groups also had reduced PRs of achieving a satisfaction score of 33 or higher (Table 2). Additionally, mothers with low incomes or unplanned pregnancies tended to report lower satisfaction.

Systematic reviews suggest that lower satisfaction is often linked to younger age, poorer health, lower education, and lower social class.36,37,38 The analysis results indicated that mothers facing greater adversity reported low satisfaction, which can be attributed to several factors. Mothers experiencing adversity often have more complex needs that are challenging to fully address within constraints of home visiting services.21,22 As a result, it may be that these mothers report lower satisfaction levels. Women with mental health issues may not fully engage with universal visits, leading to decreased satisfaction. These individuals often require more customized and sustained support than universal visits to address specific risk factors and the unique needs of their families.21 Additionally, language barriers and cultural differences can impact satisfaction with services. An Australian study found that non-English speaking mothers reported lower satisfaction due to language barriers and cultural differences.21,47 To enhance maternal satisfaction in Korea, increasing the use of interpreter services or smartphone-based translation tools could be beneficial. In addition, understanding the relationship of satisfaction with maternal sociodemographic and psychological risk factors as well as pregnancy and delivery characteristics can help home visiting service providers address potential dissatisfaction.

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design prevents causal conclusions about the relationships between home visitation characteristics and maternal satisfaction. Despite controlling for various covariates, however, the associations between visit frequency, mothers’ group participation, and satisfaction remained robust (Table 4 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Second, the response rates for satisfaction surveys (46.8% in 2018 to 56.9% in 2019) may limit the generalizability of the findings, and data from non-respondents were not available. A previous Korean study using the 2017 National Health Insurance Database found that, among 64,410 mothers in Seoul, 1.4% were aged 23 or younger, 0.4% received basic living security, and 0.3% were disabled.48 These proportions were higher than those reported in the current study (0.7%, 0.3%, and 0.1%, respectively). Disadvantaged mothers were more likely to receive sustained home visits rather than universal visits. Third, although participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential, mothers may respond differently to socially sensitive questions, such as those regarding childhood abuse and the need for help with domestic violence. This may have influenced the strength of the relationship between these questions and the outcome variable (maternal satisfaction with universal visits). Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. It includes a large sample of 22,040 mothers from Korea. We used the PSQ, an internationally recognized tool for measuring satisfaction, which was translated specifically for this study, allowing comparisons with other countries.

This study revealed high maternal satisfaction among Korean mothers in Seoul who participated in universal postnatal nurse home visitation services. Mothers who received at least two visits or participated in a mothers’ group program reported greater satisfaction than those who received only one visit or did not participate. Increasing the frequency of visits and expanding participation in group programs are likely to improve maternal satisfaction further.
