Enhancing leucine aminopeptidase activity in Aspergillus oryzae LL1 through multi-round UV irradiation mutagenesis with potential application in debittering
Authors: Chiu-Yen Wen, Bo-Chou Chen, Huan-Yu Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo enhance the applicability of Aspergillus oryzae LL1 in the food industry, the strain underwent ultraviolet irradiation through random mutagenesis to enhance the enzyme activity of extracellular leucine aminopeptidase (LAP). A. oryzae LL1 was subjected to three rounds of ultraviolet mutagenesis, and a number of 210, 280, and 290 mutant clones were selected based on LAP activity and stability analysis. The mutant clone with the highest LAP activity was selected for each mutagenesis, and then proceeded to the next round of mutagenesis. The activity of the three selected mutant strains showed significantly increased LAP activity compared to the wild-type strain. The LAP specific activity of A. oryzae LL1 was 319.9 mU/mg. The LAP specific activities of the mutant strains U01, U02, and U03 were 386.3, 449.9, and 556.5 mU/mg, respectively. After three rounds of mutagenesis, the U03 strain exhibited 1.74-fold greater specific activity of LAP than the LL1 strain. A further study evaluated the debittering effect of crude enzyme of the mutant strain U03 on isolated soy protein through hydrolysis and a decrease in the content of hydrophobic amino acids. The mutant strain U03 demonstrated a significantly higher efficiency in hydrolyzing soy protein compared to the LL1 strain, as evidenced by the degradation into small peptide fragments observed in SDS-PAGE analysis, the significant increase in peptide peaks in reverse-phase HPLC analysis, and the higher total free amino acid contents. These results suggest that the U03 strain exhibits more effective hydrolytic activity and holds potential for applications in the food industry, particularly in reducing bitterness in protein hydrolysates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10529-025-03666-5.Keywords: Leucine aminopeptidase, Aspergillus oryzae, UV mutagenesis, Debittering, Soy protein hydrolysis

Introduction

Aminopeptidase (EC 3.4.11-) is widespread in prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and it acts on N-terminal peptide bonds in polypeptide chains, releasing free amino acid residues after recognition and catalytic hydrolysis (Naveed et al. 2021). Most aminopeptidases are metalloenzymes that require one or two divalent metal ions in their active sites to specifically release the N-terminal amino acid residues (Mucha et al. 2010). Based on the specificities of aminopeptidases at the substrate site, aminopeptidases can be categorized into those with strict substrate specificity and those with broad-spectrum substrates (Gao et al. 2018). According to the catalytic mechanism, aminopeptidases can be divided into leucine aminopeptidases, phenylalanine aminopeptidases, lysine aminopeptidases, arginine aminopeptidases, proline aminopeptidases, alanine aminopeptidases, valine aminopeptidases, and others (Wang et al. 2023).

Aminopeptidases play a crucial role in the food industry, particularly in sensory applications and the development of food flavor profiles. They are essential for debittering protein hydrolysates by eliminating hydrophobic amino acids from the N-terminus of peptide hydrolysates, which are known to contribute to the bitterness of the flavor profile (Nampoothiri et al. 2005; Stressler et al. 2013; Lin et al. 2020). Fungal aminopeptidases derived from Aspergillus oryzae are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the food industry, with a long history of utilization in debittering applications (Baltulionis et al. 2021).

Leucine aminopeptidase (LAP, EC 3.4.11.1) is a metal exopeptidase that can catalyze the hydrolysis of the peptide bonds of the NH2-terminal amino acids of proteins or polypeptides, with a preference for leucine, resulting in the release of free amino acids. The potential of LAP has been reported in various industrial processes, including the conversion of L-homophenylalanyl amide into L-homophenylalanine, an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor precursor (Kamphuis et al. 1992; Pedroche et al. 2002; Pertiwi et al. 2020), and the preparation of debittered hydrolysates in food industries (Lin et al. 2008, 2020; Cheung et al. 2015). Four extracellular LAPs (I, II, III, and IV) were found in A. oryzae used in soy sauce production (Nakadai et al. 1973; Nakadai et al. 1977; Ito et al. 2021). LAP produced from Streptomyces gedanensis improved the antioxidant activity, water retention and solubility of enzymatic hydrolysate, and enhanced its free radical scavenging activity and reduction ability (Rahulan et al. 2012). The residues Thr-346 and Leu-352 in LAP of Bacillus kaustophilus were essential for the catalytic reaction, and Ala-348 and Gly-350 were important for maintaining a stable active-site environment (Chi et al. 2006, 2008; Shen et al. 2011). LAPs produced from A. oryzae strain NZTM-EX and Aspergillus sp. strain AE-ME were evaluated by genotoxicity tests, repeated dose 90-day oral toxicity study, and allergenicity assessment, and these do no give rise to safety concerns under the conditions of intended use (Lambré et al. 2023, 2024).

Ultraviolet (UV) germicidal irradiation may cause mutagenic effects on microorganisms, including morphological alteration, variable pigmentation patterns, and mycotoxin production, and abnormal reproductive activities (Lindenauer and Darby 1994). The two main photoproducts that arise during exposure to UV light include cyclobutane-pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6–4 photoproducts (6-4PPs). In addition, UV light may also indirectly cause DNA damage via the production of singlet oxygen or free radicals in photodynamic reactions. One of the most reactive oxygen species (ROS) is the hydroxyl radical (OH*), with its high DNA-damaging capacity (Winterbourn 2008; Cadet and Wagner 2013). UV wavelengths may induce small amounts of DNA breaks, as well as DNA–protein and interstrand cross-links (Kciuk et al. 2020). Improvement of various microbal stains by UV exposure is beneficial for producing many enzymes. Lipase production increased 2.53 times after UV mutagenesis compared with the parental strain of Aspergillus niger (Sandana et al. 2001). The activity of the alkaline protease of Pseudomonas aeruginosa was increased 1.75 times by UV radiation compared to the wild-type strain (Rakib et al. 2020). LAP and protease production in Aspergillus sojae were improved by random mutagenesis, including ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS), UV, and 1-methyl-2-nitro-1-nitrosoguanidine (NTG), and the mutant showed a 9.6-fold increase in LAP and a 3.8-fold increase in protease production (Lim et al. 2019).

In previous studies, we screened of 175 fungi strains and identified two strains of Aspergillus oryzae LL1 and LL2, which exhibit high extracellular LAP activities. The LAP activities produced by A. oryzae LL1 and LL2 were 0.385 and 0.250 U/mL, respectively. Zn2+ increased the LAP activity of the A. oryzae LL1, while Cu2+, Pb2+, and Fe2+ inhibited its enzyme activity (Lin et al. 2010). The scalability of the A. oryzae LL1 fermentation process for industrial applications was evaluated through a scaled-up from 250 mL shake flask to 5 L, 50 L, 250 L, and ultimatedly a 2-ton bioreactor. The LAP activity of A. oryzae LL1 ranged from 0.122 to 0.219 U/mL in fermentations. Compared to commercial enzymes, the LAP activity of A. oryzae LL1 was 0.054 U/mg, while that of Flavourzyme was 0.068 U/mg. From these studies, LAP LL1 has significant potential for application in the food industry.

To improve the applicability of Aspergillus oryzae LL1 in the food industry, the strain was treated with UV irradiation through random mutagenesis to enhance the enzyme activity of extracellular LAP. In this study, A. oryzae LL1 underwent three rounds of ultraviolet mutagenesis, and the developed mutants were screened and evaluated for enzyme catalytic activity. The mutant strains showed significantly increased LAP activity and a significantly higher efficiency in hydrolyzing soy protein, which could be used in traditional fermentation and food processing industries.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

Aspergillus oryzae LL1 was obtained from Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC) in Taiwan. It was cultured and maintained in BS medium at pH 5.5 consisting of wheat bran and defatted soybeans. The preparation of BS medium was descripted previously (Lin et al. 2008). Aspergillus oryzae LL1 was cultured on PDA slants at 25 ℃ for 7–14 days. One loop of spores was incubated in 50 ml BS medium, gently shaking, at 30 ℃ for 4 days. Cultures were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and supernatants were measured for leucine aminopeptidase activity.

Mutagenesis with ultraviolet treatment

Spores of the strains cultured for 7 days were collected in 0.85% NaCl, and the concentration was diluted to 105 to 106 for UV irradiation. Next, 5 mL of the spore suspension was placed on a sterilized Petri dish (9 cm diameter), at a distance of 15 cm from the germicidal lamp, and exposed to UV light at 254-nm wavelength for a total dose of 15–20 J/m2 using the UVP Cross-linker (CL-3000, Analytik Jena, Germany). Then, a portion of the treated spore suspension was plated on PDA plates and incubated in the dark. The colonies formed after incubation were transferred into PDA slants for further culture, and potential mutants were selected based on their LAP activity.

Leucine aminopeptidase activity

Aminopeptidase activity was assayed by spectrophotometric determination of N-succinyl-L-Leu-p-nitroanilide (L-Leu-pNA, Sigma) release. First, 50 µL of crude enzyme was mixed with 25 µL of 16.4 mM L-Leu-pNA substrate in in a test tube, and the reaction mixture was adjusted to a final volume of 1.5 mL with 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0. The mixture was incubated at 37 ℃ for 20 min. Then, the reaction was terminated by adding 0.5 mL 30% (v/v) acetic acid. The absorbance of p-nitroanilide (pNA) was measured using a spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter DU-800, USA) at 410 nm. One unit (U) of LAP activity is defined as the amount of the enzyme that produces 1 µmol of p-nitroaniline per minute at 37 ℃.

Protein concentration

The quantitation of total protein was determined using Bradford method (Bradford 1976). Protein concentrations were determined with Bio-Rad protein assay reagent (Bio-Rad Lab., Hercules, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 5 mL Bio-Rad Bradford dye reagent (Coomassie brilliant blue G-250) was added to each tube and then mixed thoroughly with 0.1 mL standard or crude protein. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter DU-800, USA) after incubation at room temperature for 10 min in a dark place. A standard curve was prepared by using serially diluted bovine serum albumin (BSA) protein concentrations (6.25, 12.5, 25 50, and 100 µg/mL). The protein concentration was plotted against the corresponding absorbance, resulting in a standard curve used to measure the total protein concentration in unknown samples.

Colony PCR and sequence analysis of LapA gene

The LapA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the specific primers (forward primer LAP-F: 5-ATG CGT TTC CTC CCC TGC ATC-3; reversed primer LAP-R: 5-TTA CAG CGA ATC TGC GAA GGC-3). The gel was stained with ethidium bromide, and the banding profile was recorded using UV-gel documentation system. The amplified DNA product was purified and sequenced. Alignment of the sequences of the LapA gene from the wild-type and mutant strains was carried out using programs available at the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information, Bethesda, MD, USA) database.

Enzymatic hydrolysis

The crude enzyme extract from LL1 or U03 strain cultures was used for protein hydrolysis. Isolated soy protein (purchased from Gemfont Co., Taiwan) was diluted to 10% in 50 mM phosphate buffer, pH7.0. The enzyme–substrate (1% w/w) mixture was incubated at 37 ℃ for 0.2, 6, 24, and 72 h. The mixtures were then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 ℃ and stored at -20 ℃ for further analysis.

Electrophoresis of hydrolysate

After hydrolysis of the soy protein extract, the hydrolysates were separated by SDS-PAGE analysis. The protein samples (7.5 µL) were dissolved in sample buffer (2.5 µL), loaded 10 µL onto bis–tris SDS–polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE, SurePAGE™ 4–12% BT, GenScript, USA), and run with the Tris-MOPS-SDS running buffer (GenScript, USA). Gel electrophoresis was carried out at 50 V for 30 min, followed by 150 V for 1.5 h. The gels were stained with 0.1% Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 at room temperature. BlueRAY Prestained Protein Ladder (PM006-0500, GeneDireX, Taoyuan, Taiwan) was used as a protein standard.

Free amino acid analysis

The content of free amino acids was evaluated using an automatic amino acid analyzer L-8900 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were accurately weighed and extracted by 0.02 mol/L hydrochloric acid, then transferred into a 10 mL volumetric flask. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane. The analytical column (4.6 mm × 60 mm, #2622SC-PF, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) and the ammonia filter column (4.6 mm × 40 mm, #2650L, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) were maintained at 50 ℃ and 135 ℃, respectively. The flow rates for pump 1 and pump 2 were set at 0.35 mL/min and 0.30 mL/min, respectively. The peak time and peak area of the reference material were used to calculate the content of free amino acids. Standard amino acid mixtures, including Type AN-II and Type B, were used for quantification. The amount of each amino acid in the samples was calculated with reference to the standard solution sample, and the content of each amino acid was expressed as a percentage of the total sample weight. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Reversed-phase HPLC analysis

The samples were analyzed by high performance liquid chromatography Agilent 1200 series (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) on a Symmetry C18 5 μm (4.6 mm × 250 mm) column. Solvent A (0.1% trifluroacetic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% trifluroacetic acid in 60% acetonitrile) were used as mobile phase. The separation was carried out at flow rate of 1 ml/min and gradient elution of 0–5 min 100% solvent A, 5–45 min solvent A from 100 to 0%, 45–50 min solvent A 0%. Detection of peptides was carried out at 220 nm. Hydrolysate samples were filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters and 10 μL was applied to the column.

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as the means ± S.E.M. The P-values were two-tailed and performed using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was specified as P < 0.05.

Results

Improvement of LAP activity by mutagenesis

Aspergillus oryzae LL1 was screened and selected with high extracellular LAP activities in previous study. To further enhance the enzyme activity of LAP, random mutagenesis by UV irradiation was applied to obtain mutants with improved LAP production. A. oryzae LL1 strain was cultured on PDA plates at 30 ℃ for 3 days, after which the spores were subjected to UV mutagenesis, and screening based on LAP activity was conducted (Fig. 1). First, the spores of A. oryzae LL1 were irradiated with 15 J/m2 of UV-C exposure, followed by culturing on PDA for 3 days. Subsequently, a total of 210 single colonies were selected and cultured on PDA slants for 7 days, and then LAP activity was measured under BS medium with wheat bran and defatted soybeans. The average LAP activity of the 210 mutant strains was a 1.08-fold increase compared to the wild-type strain. However, among the mutant strains, the U1044 strain displayed the highest activity, exhibiting a 2.9-fold increase, while the U1181 strain demonstrated the lowest activity, with only a 0.15-fold increase (Fig. S1a). The survival rate of A. oryzae LL1 after the first UV irradiation was 16.9% (Table 1).

The stability of a strain is an essential characteristic in industrial strains. In order to assess the stability of mutant strains, twenty mutant strains with a level of LAP activity exceeding a 1.7-fold increase were selected for subculture, and activity stability tests were further examined. A total of 200 subclones of the twenty mutant strains were analyzed for LAP activity (Fig. S1). Notably, only subclones of the U1044 strain displayed higher LAP activity. After subculture, ten sub-clones, including U1044-1, U1044-2, U1044-3, U1044-4, U1044-7, U1044-8, U1044-9, U1044-10, U1061-4, and U1103-7, were selected for a further second round of activity stability test. The U1044-10–20 subclone displayed a 1.6-fold increase in LAP activity. This clone, renamed U01, was selected for the next round of UV treatment based on its stable LAP activity during two passages (Fig. S1c).

Clone U01 was subjected to a second round of mutagenesis with 15 J/m2 of UV irradiation. The survival rate of A. oryzae LL1 after the second UV irradiation was 53.1% (Table 1). The average LAP activity of the 280 mutant strains increased by 1.23-fold compared to clone U01 (Fig. S2a). The top 22 mutant strains with the highest activity were selected for two rounds of activity stability testing (Fig. S2b and S2c). The U2010-11–10 clone displayed a 1.2-fold increase in LAP activity compared to clone U01. The clone U2010-11–10, renamed U02, was selected for the next round of UV treatment (Fig. S2c).

Since the survival rate of the strains after the second UV irradiation had increased, the third round of mutagenesis used 20 J/m2 of UV irradiation. However, the survival rate was still high at 46.0% (Table 1). Screening of 290 mutant strains showed that LAP activity had increased by an average of 1.23-fold compared to clone U02 (Fig. S3a). The top 21 mutant strains with the highest activity were selected for two rounds of activity stability testing (Fig. S3b and S3c). The U3169-2–11 clone displayed a 1.3-fold increase in LAP activity compared to clone U02. The clone U3169-2–11, renamed U03, was selected and preserved (Fig. S3c).

LAP activity of selected mutants

After three rounds of UV mutagenesis and activity screening, three mutant clones (U01, U02, and U03) were isolated and cultured for comparison with wild-type strains. Compared to A. oryzae LL1, the mutant clones U01, U02, and U03 exhibited significantly increased LAP activity by 1.39, 1.44, and 1.96 times, respectively, along with significantly increased specific activity by 1.21, 1.41, and 1.74 times, respectively (Table 2). After three rounds of mutagenesis, the clone U03 exhibited 1.74-fold greater specific activity of LAP than the original strain. Mutant clones developed through ultraviolet mutagenesis have improved LAP activity and may enhance applicability in the traditional fermentation and food processing industries.

For further comparison of the leucine aminopeptidase A (LapA) gene from wild-type and mutant strains, specific primers were used in PCR and DNA sequencing to identify possible mutations in the LapA gene of A. oryzae strain. The LapA gene sequence of A. oryzae was available in GenBank (accession No. AP007171). The complete DNA sequence of the LapA gene is 1323 bp in length, spanning four open reading frames. To investigate LAP production between the wild-type and mutant strains, LapA sequences of strains U01, U02, and U03 were analyzed and compared to that of the WT. However, the entire LapA sequences were identical (data not shown). Although the specific LAP activities of the mutants were higher than those of the wild-type strain, no mutations were identified in the LapA gene. There may be other factors affecting the expression or secretion of LAP.

Soy protein hydrolysis

To evaluate the debittering effect of the mutant strain, isolated soy protein was used as a substrate for digestion by aminopeptidase to release amino acids. After hydrolyzing soy protein isolates for 0.2, 6, 24, and 72 h, the hydrolysates were collected and analyzed. First, the protein profile from the hydrolysis of soy protein isolates by the LL1 and U03 strains was analyzed using SDS-PAGE. It was observed that after hydrolysis, the electrophoresis pattern of soy protein changed, showing an increased intensity in low molecular weight polypeptides. The protein profile of soy protein isolate treated with the U03 crude enzyme extract displayed extensive degradation at 6 h compared to the LL1 crude enzyme extract (Fig. 2).

The free amino acid content in the hydrolysate of soybean protein isolates was analyzed. When the crude enzyme extract of the LL1 strain was used to hydrolyze the soybean protein for 6 h, 686.3 mg/L of total free amino acids were released, including 80.8 mg/L leucine, 57.2 mg/L phenylalanine, 30.3 mg/L isoleucine, and 31.1 mg/L tyrosine. When the crude enzyme extract of the U03 strain was used, the total content of free amino acids in the hydrolysates was 996.3 mg/L, including 113.4 mg/L leucine, 76.1 mg/L phenylalanine, 51.0 mg/L isoleucine, and 39.7 mg/L tyrosine. The total amount of free amino acids obtained by the U03 strain was 1.45 times that obtained by the hydrolysis of soybean protein with the crude enzyme extract from the LL1 strain (Table 3). The mutant strain U03 demonstrated a significantly higher efficiency in hydrolyzing soy protein compared to the LL1 strain as evidenced by the extensive degradation observed in SDS-PAGE analysis, the significant increase in peptide peaks in reverse-phase HPLC analysis, and the higher total free amino acid contents. These results suggested that the U03 strain exhibits more effective hydrolytic activity and could be an option to improve the taste and nutritional value of soy protein products.

Discussion

In this study, A. oryzae LL1 was treated with three rounds of UV irradiation, and the mutant strains with higher LAP activity were screened and selected through random mutagenesis. The mutant clones U01, U02 and U03 showed significantly increased LAP activity. The U03 strain exhibited 1.74-fold greater specific activity of LAP than the wild-type strain. The U03 strain demonstrated a significantly higher efficiency in hydrolyzing soy protein compared to the wild-type strain, suggestingmore effective hydrolytic activity. The mutant strain can be applied in traditional fermentation and food processing industries.

A mutation is one of the tools to improve strain because changes in producing genes effectively increases product yield. Random mutagenesis is a classic approach for inducing and producing mutants with random mutations. In this study, the mutant clones were generated by UV treatment only. The LAP activity of A. oryzae LL1 was improved through three rounds of UV irradiation. Notably, only the first round of UV treatment yielded clones with higher LAP activity. The subsequent two rounds of UV exposure did not produce clones with higher LAP activity as in the first round. Mutagenesis greatly depends on exposure time, intensity of UV exposure, and the physiological state of the microorganisms. In the final round, cells were treated with 20 J/m2 of UV-C, a higher dose compared to the first two rounds (Table 1). UV mutagenesis conditions would lead to higher enzyme activity and more stable variants. A cell survival of 40–50% is too high for effective mutagenesis, and reducing the titer of spore suspension can further enhance the mutagenic effect.

Several studies have shown that combining chemical and UV mutagenesis produces a better mutant with higher enzyme activity. Mutagenesis can be generated by individual physical or chemical mutagen, combined physical and chemical mutagens, or initiated by protoplast preparation (Suryadi et al. 2022). For example, the mutant developed from a UV exposure exhibited a 1.06-fold increase in extracellular β-glucosidase production compared to the wild-type strain Bacillus subtilis PS. The mutant developed from UV exposure and EMS mutagen combination showed a 1.2-fold increase (Agrawal et al. 2013). Similarly, the mutant exposed only to UV radiation indicated an increase in serrapeptase activity of 3234.9 EU/mL in comparison to Serratia marcescens wild-type strain (2770 EU/mL). Combined exposure of the wild-type to UV and EMS produced a better mutant with serrapeptase activity of 3437.6 EU/mL (Gopinath et al. 2020). UV irradiation and EMS mutagenesis were sequentially employed to create Halomonas smyrnensis AAD6T mutants with increased levan productivity. The productivity for the new strains was approximately 2.5 fold higher than the parental strain with notable PHB production (Erkorkmaz et al. 2022). Therefore, other mutagenesis methods can be further used to improve the LAP activity of A. oryzae U03.

UV-induced molecular lesions are attributable to pyrimidine dimers, cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and pyrimidine-pyrimidone photoproducts, leading to random mutations in DNA (Livneh et al. 1993). Therefore, UV-irradiation is commonly used to accelerate natural mutation rates and to generate improved phenotypes for growth performance or production of target molecules. Microorganisms have the ability to repair DNA lesions (Lindenauer and Darby 1994). To counteract a variety of mutagenic and cytotoxic DNA lesions, organisms have developed a number of highly conserved repair mechanisms such as photoreactivation, base excision repair (BER), nucleotide excision repair (NER), and mismatch repair (MMR). Additionally, double-strand break repair (by homologous recombination and nonhomologous end joining), SOS response, cell-cycle checkpoints, and programmed cell death (apoptosis) are also operative in various organisms at the expense of specific gene products. NER is critically important in the repair of UV-induced DNA lesions and is one of the most versatile and flexible repair systems found in most organisms and highly conserved in eukaryotes (Rastogi et al. 2010).

Soy protein isolate is widely used in various foods as a functional and nutritional ingredient and has been applied in the food industry. The enzymatic hydrolysis of soy protein isolate enhances its functional properties, including solubility, foaming, emulsifying and gelation capabilities. However, some proteins, especially soy protein, whey, and casein, develop a bitter taste during the hydrolysis process. The bitterness of partially hydrolyzed protein mainly results from the degradation of its molecular weight and the increased hydrophobicity of the peptides (Seo et al. 2007; Jiang et al. 2023). A leucine aminopeptidase Lap1, derived from Aspergillus sojae GIM3.30 and expressed in Pichia pastoris, was found to improve the degree of hydrolysis of casein and soy protein hydrolysates and also decreased their bitterness (Huang et al. 2015). The thermostable leucine aminopeptidase from Thermomyces lanuginosus jas been shown to significantly increase the degree of hydrolysis and remove more hydrophobic amino acids from the N-terminal region of the polypeptide to decrease the bitterness (Lin et al. 2020). In addition to leucine aminopeptidase, various extracellular enzymes in Aspergillus species hydrolyze soybean protein. An aminopeptidase from Aspergillus oryzae significantly reduced the bitterness of casein and cod protein hydrolysates by releasing hydrophobic amino acids (Nakamura et al. 2023). In previous study, the protease activity of A. oryzae LL1 was 50.9 U/mg, whereas Flavourzyme exhibited a protease activity of 35.6 U/mg. When applied to chicken breast meat hydrolysis, the degree of hydrolysis was 50.56% for LAP LL1, compared to 25.56% for Flavourzyme (Lin et al. 2008). In this study, we used 50 mg of soybean protein lysates with 0.5 mg of crude enzyme, approximately 100 mU LAP activity. The crude enzyme extract of the U03 mutant strain exhibited faster and more efficient hydrolysis of soy protein isolate compared to the LL1 strain (Fig. 2–4). Based on the free amino acid composition of hydrolyzed soy protein, the crude enzyme extract, in addition to LAP, is likely to contain other proteolytic enzymes (Table 3). According to literature, industrial applications typically use enzyme to substrate ratios in the range of 0.6–1.5% (e.g. Protamex) or activity-based dosages. For example, in a study on soybean protein hydrolysis, Flavourzyme was applied at 20 LAPU/g soybean protein, with hydrolysis durations optimized up to several hours. Bitterness value was reduced to 2 after Flavourzyme hydrolysis reaction in optimal hydrolysis conditions (Ma et al. 2014). While organoleptic evaluation of bitterness was not performed in this study. The sensory evaluation would provide direct evidence of debittering and plan to address this aspect in future work.

The proteolytic system of A. oryzae is known to be complex and comprises multiple enzymes beyond LAP, which collectively contribute to protein hydrolysis and potential affect debittering efficiency. A total of 157 proteolytic enzymes are recorded in the local database of Aspergillus species. Among them, A. oryzae has the highest abundance, with 70 enzymes, accounting for approximately 45% of all known proteolytic enzymes (Mu et al. 2024). The proteolytic complex of A. oryzae ATCC 42149 (RIB 40), the source strain of the commercial Flavourzyme, has been reported to contain three endopeptidases (two neutral proteases NP1 and NP2, and the serine protease oryzin ALP1), two leucine aminopeptidases (LAP1 and LAP2), and two dipeptidyl peptidases (DPP4 and DPP5) (Merz et al. 2015). These findings suggest that the enhanced soy protein hydrolytic activity observed in our UV-derived mutant strain may not only result from alterations in LAP activity but could also be associated with changes in other proteases. SDS-PAGE or proteomic profiling of the secreted enzyme complex from the mutant strain would be conducted in future studies to assess possible changes in other components of the protease complex.

Conclusion

In this study, Aspergillus oryzae LL1 was subjected to three rounds of UV irradiation through random mutagenesis to enhance the enzyme activity of extracellular leucine aminopeptidase (LAP). The mutant strains developed showed significantly increased LAP activity and higher efficiency in hydrolyzing soy protein compared to the wild-type strain. The mutant strain U03, in particular, exhibited a 1.74-fold greater specific activity of LAP. The crude protein of U03 strain demonstrated a significantly higher efficiency in hydrolyzing soy protein. The mutant strain U03 has enhanced enzyme activity and has potential applications in traditional fermentation and food processing industries. These improvements suggest potential applications in traditional fermentation and food processing industries.
