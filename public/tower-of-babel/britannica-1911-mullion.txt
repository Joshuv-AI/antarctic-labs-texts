MULLION (corrupted from “ munnion ”; this is derived from
Fr. moignon, stump), in architecture, the English term for the
perpendicular pieces of stone, sometimes like columns, sometimes
like slender piers, which divide the bays or lights of
windows or screen work from each other; equivalents are Fr.
meneau, Ital. regolo, Ger. Fensterpfoste. H. Wedgwood (Dict.
of Eng. Etym.) points out that the mullion is “the stump of the
division before it breaks out into the tracery of the window.”
In all styles, in less important work, the mullions are often

simply plain chamfered, and more commonly have a flat hollow
on each side. In larger buildings there is often a bead or bowtel
on the edge, and often a single small column with a capital; these
are more frequent in foreign work than in English. Instead
of the bowtel they often finish with a sort of double ogee. As
tracery grew richer, the windows were divided by a larger order
of mullion, between which came a lesser or subordinate set of
mullions, which ran into each other.
