Dietary yeast culture supplementation alleviates transportation stress in goats
Authors: Pei Qi, Lizhi Wang, Wanguo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe addition of yeast cultures to diets has been demonstrated to maintain the homeostasis of the rumen endo-environment and to enhance the body’s immunity. However, the effect of yeast cultures on alleviating transportation stress remains unclear. A total of 16 goats were randomly distributed into 2 dietary treatments (8 replicates/treatment) as follows: Basic diet group (CON); basic diet + 0.3% YC group (YC). The experiment lasted for a total of 46 days, consisting of a 30-day growth trial, followed by a one-day transportation stress treatment, and subsequently a 14-day feeding trial. Samples were taken on days 31, 32, 39 and 46. The results showed that YC improved growth performance, as evidenced by an increase in the average daily gain and a reduction in feed intake and weight gain ratio in goats (P < 0.05). There was a significant interaction between time effect and dietary effect on the rumen histamine (His) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) content (P < 0.05). There were significant interactions between diet and time on serum interleukin (IL) -1β, IL -6, Tumor Necrosis Factor-α, Glutamic pyruvic transaminase, His, LPS and immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM) concentrations (P < 0.05). At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes showed significant interactions between time and diet (P < 0.05). These evidences suggests that the supplementation of YC in the diet has been demonstrated to significantly enhance the immune function of goats, while simultaneously maintaining the homeostasis of the intra-rumen environment. This has the beneficial effect of alleviating the physiological stress associated with transportation.Keywords: yeast cultures, immunity, ruminal fermentation, transportation stress, microbial abundance

Introduction

Transportation stress represents a major challenge in livestock production, imposing physiological and psychological burdens that compromise immune competence, increase disease susceptibility, and reduce animal welfare, ultimately leading to significant economic losses (Takemoto et al. 2017; Xu et al. 2023). Key stress manifestations include elevated cortisol levels and tachycardia, all of which adversely affect meat quality and growth performance (Uetake et al. 2009; Kumar et al. 2023). Therefore, addressing transportation stress is crucial for improving both animal welfare and productivity. In the present study, animal welfare is defined as the ability of goats to maintain physiological homeostasis, immune function, oxidative balance, and rumen stability when exposed to transportation stress, as reflected by changes in growth performance, stress-related hormones, inflammatory markers, antioxidant capacity, and rumen fermentation parameters. To mitigate transportation stress, various strategies have been explored, encompassing management practices as well as pharmacological interventions. Effective handling, well-designed vehicles, and minimizing transport duration have demonstrated some success in alleviating stress (Loudon et al. 2019). Pharmacological approaches, such as tranquilizers and anti-inflammatory drugs, can alleviate certain stress responses but often come with drawbacks, including withdrawal periods and potential side effects (Wehling 2014). Nutritional approaches, including vitamin/mineral supplementation and bioactive compounds, have shown potential in modulating stress responses (Nwunuji et al. 2014; Gupta et al. 2020). Notably, probiotics and prebiotics, which enhance gut health and immune function, provide indirect stress relief (Gupta et al. 2018; Luise et al. 2022). Among these nutritional strategies, yeast culture (YC) have emerged as particularly promising due to their diverse beneficial properties. YC, particularly those from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, are recognized for their probiotic and immunomodulatory effects (De et al. 2014; Rohani et al. 2022). They have been shown to improve gut health, feed efficiency, and immune responses in livestock (Pang et al. 2022; Ali et al. 2023; Qi and Wang 2024). Additionally, YC can produce bioactive compounds such as beta-glucans and mannooligosaccharides, which have been linked to stress reduction and enhanced immune function (Emu et al. 2021; Sivinski et al. 2022). Building on this evidence, we hypothesize that YC supplementation in goat diets could alleviate transportation-induced stress.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary yeast culture supplementation on alleviating the negative impacts induced by transportation stress in goats. This research is significant as it explores a sustainable, non-pharmacological approach to improving animal welfare and productivity in livestock. Elucidating YC’s role in stress mitigation could inform optimized management practices and nutritional protocols, ultimately improving the health and performance of transported animals.

Materials and methods

Animal ethic statement

The research protocol for this study received approval from the Animal Policy and Welfare Committee of the Agricultural Research Organization of Sichuan Province, China (approval code: SCAUAC201408-3), and was in compliance with the guidelines set forth by the Animal Care and Ethical Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University.

Experimental animals and diets

The experiment took place at Sichuan Agricultural University’s Animal Nutrition Institute’s experimental farm in Ya an, China. A total of 16 healthy fattening Jintang black goats (a local breed of Sichuan province, China, 6 months of age, male, vaccinated and dewormed, uncastrated, BW = 26.31 ± 0.46 kg) with similar body condition were randomly distributed into 2 dietary treatments (8 replicates/treatment) as follows: Basic diet group (CON); basic diet + 0.3% YC group. The YC was supplied by Vitech Ultra Bioscience Corporation (Orange, California, USA). Following the guidelines of the Chinese Feeding Standard for Meat-producing Sheep and Goats (NY/T816-2004), the experimental diet was designed, as detailed in Table 1.

Animal management

The experiment lasted for 56 d, with the first 10 d being the adaptation period and the last 46 d being the formal experimental period (data and sample collection period). A feeding trial was conducted on days 1 through 30 during the formal trial. Additionally, a one-day transportation stress treatment trial was conducted on days 31 through 32, and an additional 14-day feeding trial was conducted on days 32 through 46 during the formal trial. Goats received a Total Mixed Ration (TMR). Each goat was housed in individual pens that included feeders and water containers. The experimental diet was provided twice daily at approximately 9:00 am and 5:00 pm for ad libitum intake. During the pre-feeding period, the quantity of feed and the quantity of leftovers were recorded daily for each test goat. The quantity of the following day’s feed was then adjusted according to the quantity of leftovers from the previous day, with the objective of ensuring that approximately 5% of the feed remained in the trough daily. Fresh water was continuously available. The goats were transported from the Sichuan Agricultural University Meat Goat Experimental Base in Ya’an to Panzhihua at 9:00 am on the 31-day, and then returned to Ya'an Experimental Base at approximately 9:00 am on the 32-day of the experiment at a speed of 35–45 km/h, with no breaks. The entire journey covered approximately 1000 kilometers and took roughly 24 hours. During transportation, the goats were housed together in a shared space within the vehicle, reflecting common local commercial transportation practices, with a loading density of 1.5 m² per goat. The weather was sunny during the transportation stress treatment, with the internal temperature of the vehicle hovering around 25°C to 30°C. The goats were fasted and dehydrated throughout the transportation period.

Product performance

On the 1st, 39th and 46th day of the experiment, the body weight (BW) before the morning feed was recorded at 8:00 am The BW was also recorded on the day 31 prior to the commencement of the transportation process, and on day 32 immediately after the transportation. A daily record was kept of the amount of feed consumed, and the average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were calculated during the feeding trial.

Feed sampling, and chemical analysis

Collection of feed samples: Appropriate samples were collected from the diets to be tested using the tetrad method, and the feed samples collected in each period were mixed and stored using a sealing bag for nutrient determinations. The samples were dried at 55°C and then analyzed for dry matter content at 105°C (DM, method 934.01), crude protein (CP, method 984.13), and ether extract (EE, method 973.1) in accordance with the protocols established by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC 2003). The levels of neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were quantified using the methodology described by Van Soest et al. (1991).

Physiological indicators

Physiological indicators were recorded on the 31st (pre-transportation), 32nd (post-transportation), 39th (post-transportation), and 46th (post-transportation) days of the experiment. Rectal temperatures and respiratory rates were measured after the morning feeding. Breaths per minute were recorded while the goats were at rest. Pulse rates were determined post-feeding using a stethoscope, and the number of pulses per minute was recorded.

Sample collection of blood and laboratory analyses

The blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of the goat on the day 31 before the commencement of the transportation process, and on day 32 immediately after the transportation. On the 39th, and 46th days, prior to the morning feed, the blood samples were collected again. Centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 minutes was performed to obtain serum, after which the samples were frozen at −20°C for the testing index.

The serum periprotein carbonyl (Pco), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity, and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were determined using the kit method, which was purchased from Beijing Solepol. The specific procedures were described in the kit manual.

Serum Immunoglobulin (Ig)G, IgA, IgM, Tumor Necrosis Factor-α(TNF-α), Interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-10, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), Thyriod hormone(T4), cortisol (COR), and epinephrine (EPI)were detected by double antibody sandwich ELISA (ELISA kit: Meimian Industrial Co.,Lt, China).

The concentrations of glutamic pyruvic transaminase (ALT), glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (AST), urea creatinine (CREA), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) in serum were detected by automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 3100, Hitachi Limited, Japan) using standard kit (Zhongsheng Beiqin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China).

For kit-mediated antioxidant indicators, the intra- and inter-assay CVs were 6.5% and 9.2%, respectively. For all ELISA-detected parameters (immunoglobulins, cytokines, LPS, and hormones including cortisol), the intra-assay CV ranged from 5.8% to 7.5%, and the inter-assay CV ranged from 8.2% to 11.0%.For serum biochemical parameters measured by the automatic biochemical analyzer, the intra- and inter-assay CVs were less than 3.5% and 5.0%, respectively.

Sample collection of ruminal content and laboratory analyses

Rumen content was collected via orogastric intubation on Day 31 (pre-transport) and Day 32 (post-transport). To avoid contamination from reticulum fluid, saliva, or surface bacteria, the initial sample was discarded. A subsequent sample of 300 mL of rumen content was then collected. The rumen content was divided into two portions, one of which was squeezed through four layers of gauze. The rumen fluid was collected, and the pH was tested immediately, Metaphosphoric acid (0.25 mL) was added to 1 mL rumen fluid and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min, and gas chromatography (GC-MS, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used to detect acetic, propionic and butyric acid concentrations in the rumen fluid. Microbial crude protein (MCP) content was determined using differential centrifugation, with protein quantified via a BCA assay kit.

For DNA extraction, 1 mL of rumen fluid was centrifuged (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), and the pellet was processed using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) following Guo et al. (2015) and manufacturer guidelines. DNA quality was verified before 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Amplified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Novogene, China) following Caporaso et al. (2011, 2012). Raw reads were processed in QIIME (v1.8.0) (Caporaso et al. 2010), filtering low-quality sequences (Q < 20, ambiguous bases, or barcode mismatches). Chimeric sequences were removed via Uchime (USEARCH v7.0), followed by pre-clustering to minimize sequencing noise (Edgar et al. 2011). The sequences were then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using the Uclust method at 97% similarity, with a representative sequence selected for each OTU (Schloss et al. 2009). The representative sequences were then aligned against the Greengenes database (http://greengenes.lbl.gov, (accessed on December 6, 2023)) and assigned taxonomy via the RDP Classifier (Huse et al. 2010). Six alpha-diversity indices of the bacterial communities (Chao1, Dominance, goods_coverage, observed_features, pielou_e, Shannon and Simpson) were calculated.

Statistical analysis

The data were preliminarily organized in Excel (2019) and analyzed by two-factor (two-way ANOVA) analysis of variance (ANOVA) for physiological indicators, blood indicators, rumen fermentation indicators and the relative abundance of rumen dominant microorganisms using SPSS27.0 statistical software, with factor one being the time effect, and factor two being the dietary effect. Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to identify significant differences between groups at different time points, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results were visualized using GraphPad Prism, and graphs were generated to represent the changes in physiological and biochemical markers over time. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to ascertain whether there were any significant differences between the control and YC groups in their production performance indicators. Statistical results were expressed as Mean ± SE, with P < 0.05 as significant differences.

Production performance

Table 2 presented the production performance of goats. During days 1 to 30, dietary YC supplementation increased the goats’ average daily gain (P < 0.001) and average daily feed intake (P = 0.009) while reducing the feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) (P = 0.005). The mean daily weight gain of the YC group was significantly greater (P < 0.05) than that of the control group from days 39 to 45. However, no significant differences were observed between groups in overall body weight, total feed intake, or cumulative feed efficiency (F/G) across the entire trial period (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of dietary yeast culture supplementation in alleviating the negative effects of transportation stress in goats. Results demonstrated that YC supplementation mitigated some of the adverse effects associated with transportation stress, as evidenced by improved growth performance, enhanced physiological responses, and favorable changes in specific blood parameters.

Effects of transportation stress on physiological, oxidative, and immune responses in goats

Transportation stress significantly influenced various physiological and biochemical indicators in goats, with post-transport assessments showing elevated body temperature, pulse rate, and respiration rate, indicating substantial stress. By the 38th day of the trial, these indicators normalized, suggesting a recovery period of approximately one week. Transportation also markedly increased oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and protein carbonyl levels in the blood while decreasing antioxidant capacities, including total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) activity. Furthermore, transportation stress led to a reduction in serum immunoglobulin levels (IgA and IgM), indicating an immunosuppressive effect (Li et al. 2024a). The levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, increased following transportation, indicating an inflammatory response triggered by the stressor (Alcalde et al. 2017; Pascual-Alonso et al. 2017).

Potential mechanisms underlying the alleviation of the negative effects induced by transportation stress by dietary yeast culture supplementation

The beneficial effects of YC supplementation on ruminant health and productivity are attributed to several interconnected mechanisms. One key mechanism is the modulation of the rumen environment, where YC helps stabilize rumen pH by reducing the population of lactic acid-producing bacteria and promoting lactic acid-utilizing bacteria (Sun et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024). Furthermore, YC has significant immunomodulatory effects, enhancing the immune response by increasing immunoglobulin concentrations (IgA, IgG, and IgM) and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Cai et al. 2022; Treon et al. 2024). The presence of β-glucans, mannans, and B-complex vitamins in yeast cell walls is believed to play a key role in these effects, contributing to the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties observed in ruminants supplemented with YC (Carpinelli et al. 2021). These mechanisms not only improve animal health but also enhance growth performance and production efficiency, especially under environmental and pathogenic stress conditions (Wang et al. 2023).

The practice of fasting and water deprivation during transportation of goats can induce several physiological stress responses, including accelerated metabolism, weakened immune function, and an increased risk of diarrhea, all of which negatively impact growth performance (Kannan et al. 2000). Previous studies have shown that YC supplementation improves goat growth performance, although performance during transportation stress differs. In this study, the average daily weight gain from days 39 to 45 in the YC group increased by 14.38% compared to the control group, suggesting that YC supplementation before and after transportation alleviates stress and supports growth performance (Ahmad et al. 2022).

Transportation stress typically results in a decrease in rumen pH and an increase in volatile fatty acids (VFAs) concentrations, as observed in the control group. Specifically, the pH of rumen fluid decreased, while TVFA, acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, and valeric acid levels increased, aligning with previous findings (Liu et al. 2020). This shift suggests a disruption in the normal fermentation process due to stress. The addition of YC in the diet mitigated some of these adverse effects, stabilizing rumen pH and reducing harmful metabolic disturbances. The effects of YC on rumen microbial fermentation and gas production were also notable. While transportation stress increased gas production, potentially leading to bloating, YC supplementation appeared to modulate microbial metabolic pathways, reducing gas accumulation and thus alleviating some stress-related impacts on the rumen (Garcia Diaz et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2024b). Furthermore, the present study revealed a significant increase in both histamine concentration and lipopolysaccharide concentration in the rumen of the CON group following transportation. However, this phenomenon was not observed in the rumen of the YC group. This discrepancy may be attributed to the ability of YC to maintain rumen homeostasis and reduce the production of inflammatory factors following their action within the rumen. This stabilization of the rumen environment helps in mitigating the negative effects of transportation stress (Rawal et al. 2024). The analysis at the phylum level of rumen microbial composition revealed that the predominant phyla remained the Anabaena and the Thick-walled phyla. The anabolic phylum was the most prevalent in the rumen and included bacteria capable of degrading hemicellulose, amylase, and protein. These anabolic bacilli demonstrate exceptional nutrient utilization and possess the capacity to adapt to environmental alterations and stress conditions (Mao et al. 2022). In particular, during the transportation process, the abundance of the anabolic bacilli phylum typically increases, which demonstrates their adaptability to stressful conditions. It has been demonstrated that the relative abundance of the Bacteroides phylum in the intestine increases significantly when weaning stress occurs in piglets, and a similar phenomenon has been observed in poultry subjected to heat stress (Li et al. 2019). The thick-walled bacillus phylum is a rich source of fiber-degrading bacteria, which enables it to possess a robust fiber-degrading ability. Another study demonstrated that heat stress resulted in a reduction in the prevalence of fibrolytic bacteria within the rumen of dairy cows, while the abundance of amylolytic bacteria increased considerably (Hooper et al. 2012; Deng et al. 2017). With regard to the impact of transportation stress on bacterial flora, the YC group, which included the addition of YC, appeared to demonstrate a degree of resilience. Following transportation, the control group exhibited an increase in the abundance of the Anabaena phylum, while the YC group demonstrated no significant change. This indicates the potential for the inclusion of YC to exert a stabilizing influence on the rumen flora, thereby mitigating the impact of transport stress on the rumen microbial composition.

It is noteworthy that there was a notable interaction between the time effect and the dietary effect. A less pronounced decline in GPX levels was observed in the experimental group with the inclusion of YC. This suggests that YC may play a role in the alleviation of transport-induced oxidative damage. Consequently, oxidative damage caused by transport on animal health may be mitigated to a certain extent by incorporating YC into the diet (Baiomy 2010; Li et al. 2024b). The significant interaction between diet, transportation, and their combined effects on histamine and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) concentrations (P < 0.001) suggests that YC supplementation had a stabilizing influence on these stress markers. In the YC group, histamine and LPS concentrations showed no significant differences before and after transportation (P > 0.05), indicating that YC may help maintain rumen homeostasis and reduce the production of these inflammatory factors under stress conditions. In contrast, the control group exhibited significantly elevated levels of both histamine and LPS following transportation (P < 0.05), reflecting a more pronounced inflammatory response (Zhang et al. 2022). The notable interaction effect can be attributed to YC’s ability to mitigate the impact of transportation stress on the rumen environment by reducing the production of histamine and LPS, which are typically associated with inflammatory and stress responses. This suggests that YC may help buffer the physiological changes induced by transportation stress, resulting in a less severe inflammatory response in the YC group compared to the control. The IgA levels in serum were markedly elevated in the YC group of goats relative to the control group following transportation. This phenomenon can be attributed to the inherent properties of YC, which have been demonstrated to bolster immune function and enhance resilience to stress in goats (Faeza et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2024a). The significant interaction (P < 0.05) between meal and time on IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IgM, IgG, and IgA levels suggests that the effects of YC supplementation become more pronounced in response to the stress of transportation. This interaction may stem from the ability of YC to modulate the immune response, as evidenced by its effects on proinflammatory cytokine and immunoglobulin levels (Slaats et al. 2016). In the control group, transportation stress elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, whereas in the YC group, these elevations were significantly reduced, suggesting that YC helps to mitigate the immune response to stress (Zhang and Dhalla 2024). Similarly, the increase in immunoglobulin levels (IgM, IgG, IgA) in the YC group indicated enhanced immune recovery of the animals, which may be attributed to the immunomodulatory properties of β-glucan and mannan in the yeast cell wall. This significant interaction reflects the fact that the immune-enhancing and anti-inflammatory effects of YC were more effective at specific time points after transport stress, thereby reducing the overall impact on the inflammatory response and promoting rapid immune recovery (Meena et al. 2013; Zhu et al. 2023). During long-distance transportation, goats are subjected to a series of continuous and intense stimuli, which in turn activate the HPA axis and SNS. These regulatory systems then oversee the secretion of appropriate hormones by endocrine glands. In instances of prolonged stress, serum cortisol levels may persist at elevated levels until the stressor is removed or the animal has adapted to the environment (Joseph and Whirledge 2017; Leistner and Menke 2020). Cortisol plays a role in the synthesis and release of glucose, as well as in raising blood glucose levels to meet the body’s energy need. The results of this experiment demonstrated that the concentration of serum cortisol in goats in the CON group increased by 86.7% following transportation, while the concentration of serum cortisol in goats in the YC group increased by only 19.8%, suggesting YC’s potential role in moderating stress-related hormonal responses (Kannan et al. 2003).

Conclusions

Transportation stress in goats leads to disruptions in physiological homeostasis, including elevated body temperature, increased respiratory rate, and altered immune and oxidative stress responses. Dietary YC supplementation helps mitigate transportation stress by enhancing rumen stability, improving immune function, and reducing oxidative stress. Although the present study did not include a detailed economic evaluation, the observed improvement in growth performance suggests that the additional cost of yeast culture supplementation may be partially offset. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive cost–benefit analyses to assess its practical application.
