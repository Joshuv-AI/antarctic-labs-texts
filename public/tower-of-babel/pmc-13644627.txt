Adult vaccination coverage and missed preventive care opportunities among Filipino adults with diabetes: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ranee Joy B Jacobo, Michael L Villa, Maria Jocelyn C Isidro, Sahra May O Paragas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdults with diabetes mellitus are at increased risk of vaccine-preventable infections and complications. Despite recommendations for adult immunization, uptake remains suboptimal in many low- and middle-income countries, including the Philippines, where data remain limited. This cross-sectional study assessed adult immunization coverage and associated clinical and sociodemographic factors among Filipino adults with diabetes recruited from inpatient, outpatient, and community outreach settings at a tertiary hospital. Uptake of influenza, pneumococcal, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, tetanus-diphtheria-pertussis (Td/Tdap), and COVID-19 vaccines was evaluated using prespecified operational definitions. Crude associations and parsimonious multivariable logistic regression models were examined; COVID-19 vaccination was not modeled because only six participants were unvaccinated. Among 284 participants (mean age 61.5 ± 12.7 y, 55.6% females), uptake was 44.4% for influenza, 49.6% for pneumococcal, 18.1% for herpes zoster, 15.5% for hepatitis B, 23.2% for Td/Tdap, and 97.9% for the primary COVID-19 series. Males had lower adjusted odds of influenza and pneumococcal vaccination, while increasing age was associated with pneumococcal uptake. Middle and high household income and diabetes duration of 5–10 y were associated with influenza vaccination. Among adults aged ≥50 y, male sex and high income were associated with herpes zoster vaccination. High income was also associated with hepatitis B and Td/Tdap uptake. Provider recommendation was associated with all outcomes, while lack of provider recommendation, failure to prioritize vaccination, and financial constraints were common reasons for non-vaccination. Uptake was uneven and low for most recommended adult vaccines. Integrating vaccine assessment and counseling into diabetes care while addressing financial and access barriers may improve uptake.KEYWORDS: Diabetes mellitus, adult immunization, vaccine uptake, preventive healthcare, healthcare provider recommendation

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects approximately 830 million people globally and is still a primary cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1 In the Philippines, diabetes affects around 7.5% of adults, or over 4 million people, and it is the fifth leading cause of death.2 Aside from metabolic problems, chronic hyperglycemia weakens both innate and adaptive immunity, increasing susceptibility to infections and worsening outcomes.3

Adults with diabetes are at increased risk of severe complications from vaccine-preventable diseases, including influenza, pneumococcal disease, COVID-19, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, and tetanus. Diabetes has been associated with higher risk of hospitalization for influenza or pneumonia (OR, 5.08; 95% CI, 3.45–7.50)4 and COVID-19 mortality (summary risk ratio, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.44–1.64).5 Type 2 diabetes is also associated with increased incidence of herpes zoster (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.82–1.85), higher healthcare utilization, and complications.6,7 Diabetes has not been consistently associated with acquisition of hepatitis B. However, coexisting diabetes and chronic hepatitis B are associated with increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma and all-cause mortality.8 Patients with diabetes, especially those with chronic wounds, may also be more susceptible to tetanus.9

Vaccination is therefore a key preventive strategy in this high-risk population. Influenza vaccination has been shown to reduce hospitalization and mortality, while pneumococcal, COVID-19, herpes zoster, and hepatitis B vaccines are associated with reduced infection-related complications and improved outcomes.10–14 Accordingly, the American Diabetes Association, the World Health Organization, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, recommend routine adult immunization as part of standard diabetes care.15 Philippine guidelines provide recommendations for the general adult population and selected high-risk groups but do not specify a separate comprehensive schedule for adults with diabetes. They recommend annual influenza vaccination, pneumococcal vaccination for older and eligible high-risk adults, two doses of herpes zoster vaccine for immunocompetent adults aged ≥50 y, hepatitis B vaccination for specified high-risk groups, and Td vaccination every 10 y, with Tdap for adults who are unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated. The guidelines also address MMR vaccination for immunocompetent adults, meningococcal vaccination for travelers and high-risk groups, and typhoid vaccination in endemic areas.16

Despite these recommendations, adult vaccination coverage among people with diabetes remains suboptimal worldwide because of limited awareness, inadequate access, and lack of healthcare provider recommendation.17–21 In the Philippines, pneumonia remains a leading cause of death, while chronic hepatitis B continues to have intermediate endemicity.22–25 Vaccine financing varies by vaccine and population: the Department of Health provides selected vaccines, including influenza, pneumococcal, and tetanus-containing vaccines, to priority groups through government facilities, but availability differs by locality and supply.26 Because PhilHealth generally does not cover standalone preventive adult vaccination, individuals unable to access government programs often pay out of pocket.

Outpatient consultations, inpatient admissions, and other healthcare visits provide opportunities for vaccination assessment and counseling. However, vaccination may not be systematically incorporated into these encounters because of patient hesitancy, limited provider recommendation, fragmented records, and financing or reimbursement constraints.27 Philippine data remain limited regarding adult immunization coverage, factors associated with vaccine uptake, and barriers to vaccination among Filipino adults with diabetes.

This study, therefore, assessed reported uptake of influenza, pneumococcal, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, Td/Tdap, and COVID-19 vaccines among Filipino adults with diabetes. It also examined related clinical and sociodemographic factors, provider recommendation, and reasons for non-vaccination to inform strategies to integrate adult immunization into routine diabetes care.

Materials and methods

The cross-sectional study was carried out in St Luke’s Medical Center Global City, a tertiary hospital in Taguig City, Philippines from September to December 2025. Adults with physician-diagnosed type 1 or type 2 diabetes encountered in inpatient wards, outpatient clinics, and affiliated community outreach activities were screened and invited successively during scheduled data-collection periods using convenience sampling. Eligible participants were aged ≥18 y and provided written informed consent. Individuals were excluded if they declined participation, could not provide informed consent or complete the interview because of cognitive or communication limitations, or could not provide sufficient vaccination information (Figure 1).

Study flow diagram.

Using the single-proportion formula, the minimum required sample was 235 participants at a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% anticipated vaccination uptake. The primary endpoints were the proportions reporting receipt of influenza, pneumococcal, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, Td/Tdap, and COVID-19 vaccines according to the study definitions. Secondary endpoints included provider recommendation, reasons for non-vaccination, and associations of vaccine uptake with sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.

Structured interviews were conducted in person, and the available medical records were reviewed using a standardized data collection form (Supplementary Table 1). The variables collected included the clinical profile, sociodemographic features, diabetes-related factors, comorbidities, lifestyle factors, frequency of medical visits, provider recommendation for vaccination, and reasons for non-vaccination. Medication adherence was based on participant self-report and defined as taking prescribed diabetes medications on more than 5 d per week. Glycemic control was categorized using the most recent HbA1c result within the preceding 3–6 months as <7%, ≥7%, or no recent test. Regular physician follow-up was defined as at least two visits per year, while comorbidity burden was classified as fewer than three versus three or more reported comorbidities. Provider recommendation was assessed by participant self-report for each vaccine and referred to advice received from any healthcare provider during any previous healthcare encounter, regardless of study site.

Adult vaccination practices in the Philippines are guided by local recommendations from the Philippine Society for Microbiology and Infectious Diseases, which generally align with international guidance but do not provide a separate comprehensive schedule for adults with diabetes. Vaccination status for influenza, pneumococcal, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, tetanus-diphtheria-pertussis (Td/Tdap), and COVID-19 vaccinations was determined based on the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care (Supplementary Table 2). Vaccination history was obtained through structured participant interviews and cross-checked against vaccination cards or available medical records when possible. Documentation status was classified at the participant level as vaccination card shown, card not shown with vaccination status based on self-report, or documentation uncertain. A card being shown did not necessarily confirm that each reported vaccine was individually documented.

Influenza vaccine uptake was defined as receipt within the preceding 12 months. Pneumococcal vaccine uptake was defined as receipt of any adult pneumococcal vaccine, although product-specific completeness could not be determined. The uptake of the herpes zoster vaccine was assessed among participants aged ≥50 y and was defined as receiving ≥1 dose. The uptake of hepatitis B vaccination was defined as receiving ≥1 dose of hepatitis B vaccine or a booster dose after the diagnosis of diabetes, regardless of previous childhood immunization status. COVID-19 vaccination was defined as completing the initial primary series with or without booster shots, rather than adherence to current periodic vaccination recommendations. Td/Tdap vaccine uptake was defined as receipt of a tetanus-containing booster within the past 10 y. The operational definitions measured reported vaccine receipt and did not necessarily indicate completion of the recommended vaccine series. A potential missed preventive care opportunity was defined as a healthcare encounter in which an eligible, unvaccinated participant did not receive vaccination despite an opportunity for assessment, counseling, vaccination, or referral because of possible provider-, patient-, access-, cost-, or availability-related factors. Reasons for non-vaccination were obtained using an open-ended question and categorized thematically.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and vaccination status. Continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Vaccine uptake was reported with 95% confidence intervals for all six vaccines; herpes zoster uptake was assessed only among participants aged ≥50 y. Provider recommendation, reasons for non-vaccination, and uptake according to participant-level documentation status were summarized descriptively for each vaccine.

Bivariate and multivariable analyses

Bivariate analyses were performed separately for each vaccine to examine associations between vaccination status and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Age was compared between vaccinated and non-vaccinated participants using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate based on cell frequencies. Crude odds ratios (cORs) with 95% CIs were estimated using binary logistic regression. Parsimonious multivariable logistic regression models were subsequently fitted for influenza, pneumococcal, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, and Td/Tdap vaccination to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs. Variables for adjusted analyses were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, potential confounding, and the number of outcome events to reduce the risk of overfitting. Reference categories were specified for categorical predictors, including low household income, female sex, and diabetes duration <5 y, as applicable. Multivariable logistic regression was not performed for COVID-19 vaccination because only six participants were unvaccinated, resulting in sparse outcome data and potentially unstable regression estimates.

Missing data and software

All valid and available data were included in the analysis. Missing values were not replaced or estimated and were reflected in the varying denominators across variables and follow-up time points. Analyses involving change from baseline to follow-up were limited to records with complete paired measurements. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. Data analysis was performed using R version 4.2.2.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012, and pertinent national ethics regulations. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the St Luke’s Medical Center Institutional Ethics Review Committee, under ethical approval number SL-25210. After a comprehensive explanation of the study, all participants provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary and had no impact on their medical treatment. We preserved the confidentiality of the research by using anonymized study identifiers and storing identifying information in secure, access-restricted files. The study was conducted in accordance with the policies of the institutional and ethics committees, and it involved minimal risk. The study consisted solely of structured interviews and medical record review, and no invasive procedures were performed.

Results

The study included 284 adults Filipinos with diabetes mellitus, with a mean age of 61.50 ± 12.72 y; 55.63% were female, 81.69% had attained college-level education or higher, and 59.15% belonged to the middle-income group. Nearly all participants had type 2 diabetes (99.65%), and 41.90% had been diagnosed for more than 10 y. Most reported taking their medications more than 5 d per week (81.69%) and visiting a physician at least twice annually (80.99%). Fewer than half had an HbA1c <7% (46.48%), while 84.86% had three or more comorbidities. Additional participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Vaccination uptake varied considerably across vaccines (Table 2). COVID-19 vaccination had the highest reported uptake, with 278 of 284 participants (97.89%; 95% CI, 95.46%–99.22%) having completed the primary vaccine series with or without booster doses. Pneumococcal vaccination was reported by 141 participants (49.65%; 95% CI, 43.69%–55.62%), while 126 (44.37%; 95% CI, 38.50%–50.35%) had received an influenza vaccine within the preceding 12 months. Lower uptake was observed for Td/Tdap vaccination, reported by 66 participants (23.24%; 95% CI, 18.45%–28.59%), and hepatitis B vaccination after diabetes diagnosis, reported by 44 participants (15.49%; 95% CI, 11.49%–20.24%). Among the 227 participants aged ≥50 y who were eligible for herpes zoster assessment, 41 (18.06%; 95% CI, 13.28%–23.69%) reported receipt of at least one dose.

Participant-reported provider recommendation was significantly associated with uptake of all six vaccines (all p < .001; Table 3). Influenza uptake was 52.51% among participants who recalled receiving a recommendation compared with 30.48% among those who did not. For pneumococcal, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, and Td/Tdap vaccines, no uptake was reported among participants who did not recall receiving a provider recommendation. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because recommendation and vaccination status were measured simultaneously through participant self-report.

Lack of provider recommendation or awareness was the leading reported reason for non-vaccination against pneumococcal disease, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, and Td/Tdap (Table 4). Other commonly reported reasons included failure to prioritize vaccination despite willingness, perceived lack of need, and financial constraints. Among the six participants who had not received a primary COVID-19 vaccine series, the principal reasons were disbelief in vaccination and fear of adverse effects.

The vaccine-specific bivariate analyses are presented in Tables 5–10. Influenza vaccination was significantly associated with educational attainment (p = .014) and monthly household income (p < .001). Participants with a high school education or lower constituted a greater proportion of the vaccinated than the unvaccinated group (24.60% vs. 13.29%). High-income participants were also more prevalent among vaccinated participants (31.75% vs. 15.82%), whereas low-income participants were less prevalent (10.32% vs. 24.05%). Compared with unvaccinated participants, those who received pneumococcal vaccination were older (median age, 66 vs. 59 y; p < .001), more frequently female (63.12% vs. 48.25%; p = .012), more likely to report medication adherence (86.52% vs. 76.92%; p = .036) and an HbA1c <7% (53.19% vs. 39.86%; overall p < .001), and less likely to have obesity (34.04% vs. 46.15%; p = .037).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study found substantial variation in vaccination coverage among 284 Filipino adults with diabetes mellitus. COVID-19 vaccination was nearly universal, whereas fewer than half had received pneumococcal or recent influenza vaccination, and fewer than one-fourth had received Td/Tdap, herpes zoster, or hepatitis B vaccination according to the study definitions. Household income was the most consistent factor associated with vaccine uptake in adjusted analyses, particularly for influenza, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, and Td/Tdap vaccination. Age and sex were associated with selected vaccine outcomes, while most diabetes-related clinical characteristics were not consistently associated with uptake. Reported healthcare provider recommendation was strongly associated with all six vaccination outcomes, and lack of provider recommendation or awareness was a leading reason for non-vaccination.

Influenza uptake in our study (44.4%) was lower than the 62.6%–64.8% reported among US adults with diabetes,17 but comparable to estimates from Spain (40.0%)20 and Thailand (39.6%),28 and higher than that reported in Malaysia (6.4%).29 Pneumococcal uptake (49.6%) was higher than estimates from Spain (2.0%),20 Türkiye (14.7%),30 and Thailand (17.4%).28 Hepatitis B uptake (15.5%) was also higher than estimates from Spain (2.0%)20 and Malaysia (9.8%),29 although it remained low. Herpes zoster uptake among participants aged ≥50 y (18.1%) was comparable to the 21.5% reported among adults with diabetes in Saudi Arabia.31 Differences across studies may reflect variations in vaccine availability, financing, healthcare access, study populations, and operational definitions.

The contrast between COVID-19 vaccination and the other vaccines suggests that high adult vaccination coverage is achievable when vaccines are supported by coordinated public campaigns, extensive communication, convenient delivery sites, and reduced financial barriers. COVID-19 coverage may therefore reflect the exceptional conditions of the pandemic rather than routine preventive practices among adults with diabetes. Historical completion of the primary COVID-19 vaccine series in our study (97.9%) was higher than the pooled 76.1% acceptance reported among persons with diabetes.18 However, acceptance and actual receipt are distinct outcomes, and our definition did not measure adherence to current recommendations for periodic or updated doses. The markedly lower uptake of the other vaccines indicates that adult immunization may not yet be systematically integrated into diabetes care, despite participants’ frequent contact with healthcare services and substantial burden of comorbidities.

Provider recommendation may represent an important point of intervention. Uptake was consistently higher among participants who recalled receiving a recommendation, while lack of provider advice or awareness predominated among reasons for non-vaccination against pneumococcal disease, herpes zoster, hepatitis B, and Td/Tdap. Similar findings were reported in Brazil, where lack of information from healthcare providers contributed to incomplete vaccination,21 and in Türkiye, where 52.5% of patients who received a vaccine recommendation subsequently obtained the recommended vaccine.30 Regular medical attendance alone may therefore be insufficient when vaccination status is not actively reviewed, discussed, and acted upon during consultations. Nevertheless, provider recommendation alone may not ensure vaccine receipt because patient hesitancy, failure to prioritize vaccination, cost, limited access, and vaccine availability may remain important barriers. Recommendation and vaccination history were measured simultaneously and were self-reported; consequently, the temporal sequence cannot be established, and the results should not be interpreted as showing that provider recommendation caused vaccination.

The consistent association between household income and uptake of several non-COVID vaccines suggests that affordability and access may contribute to vaccination disparities. Compared with low-income participants, high-income participants had approximately five times the adjusted odds of influenza vaccination, 14 times the odds of herpes zoster vaccination, nine times the odds of hepatitis B vaccination, and more than three times the odds of Td/Tdap vaccination. Comparable socioeconomic differences have been reported in the United States, where living at or above the poverty level was associated with greater influenza vaccination among adults with diabetes,17 and in Brazil, where influenza vaccination was associated with both age and income.21 These patterns are plausible where vaccines require out-of-pocket payment or are not routinely available within diabetes services. Although financial constraints were not the most frequently reported reason for every vaccine, they were particularly evident for influenza, pneumococcal, and herpes zoster vaccination. The wide confidence intervals for herpes zoster and hepatitis B estimates, however, indicate considerable uncertainty regarding the exact magnitude of these income-related differences.

Age and sex showed vaccine-specific rather than uniform associations. Each additional year of age was associated with higher odds of pneumococcal vaccination, possibly reflecting age-based recommendations, greater perceived susceptibility, or more frequent preventive discussions among older adults. This is consistent with findings from Thailand, where age ≥65 y, diabetes duration ≥15 y, chronic respiratory disease, and greater comorbidity burden were associated with receipt of both influenza and pneumococcal vaccines.28 In contrast, diabetes duration and comorbidity burden were not consistent determinants in our study. Males had lower adjusted odds of influenza and pneumococcal vaccination than females but higher odds of herpes zoster vaccination. These conflicting findings may be explained by variations in preventive healthcare utilization, vaccine awareness or residual confounding and need to be confirmed in larger samples. Diabetes severity and management measures, including glycemic control and general comorbidity burden, were not consistently associated with vaccination, suggesting that vaccination decisions may be more dependent on access, awareness, and healthcare delivery practices than clinical risk alone.

Several findings warrant cautious interpretation. The very high COVID-19 coverage left only six unvaccinated participants, precluding reliable multivariable analysis and making its bivariate associations unstable. Some estimates for vaccines with relatively few vaccinated participants were imprecise, particularly for herpes zoster and hepatitis B vaccination. The significant bivariate associations involving individual comorbidities, such as obesity with pneumococcal vaccination and heart disease with Td/Tdap vaccination, should be considered exploratory because numerous comparisons were conducted and these conditions were not retained as predictors in the primary adjusted models. Moreover, odds ratios, particularly large estimates for less common outcomes, should not be interpreted as equivalent increases in probability.

The study’s strengths include its assessment of six adult vaccines, use of vaccine-specific denominators, reporting of confidence intervals, and application of parsimonious adjusted models. However, single-center convenience sampling limits generalizability to adults in public facilities, primary care clinics, rural areas, and other regions. Vaccination history was predominantly self-reported, as only 92 participants (32.39%) showed a vaccination card, creating potential recall and misclassification bias, particularly for vaccines received years earlier or requiring multiple doses. Participant-level documentation also did not confirm each reported vaccine individually, and the absence of significant differences by documentation status does not exclude recall bias. Furthermore, the operational definitions measured reported vaccine receipt but did not consistently establish completion of all age-, risk-, product-, and dose-specific schedules; thus, the estimates should not be interpreted as complete adherence to current recommendations. Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about causality or the direction of the observed associations.

Diabetes care services should incorporate routine, vaccine-specific assessment using electronic or paper checklists, clear provider recommendations, and same-visit vaccination or referral whenever possible. Existing efforts like the Philippine College of Physicians Adult Vaccine Booklet can help in this effort by allowing documentation of immunization history and encouraging compliance with adult vaccination recommendations. Missed opportunities may be reduced by provider training, improved documentation, patient education, standing orders, and reminder systems. Broader national adult immunization policies should improve financing, access, communication, and documentation to reduce income-related disparities across diabetes and other adult risk groups. Future multicenter studies should include public and private facilities, verify vaccine-specific records where possible, and prospectively assess if provider prompts and accessible on-site vaccination increase uptake. Overall, vaccination coverage among Filipino adults with diabetes was uneven: COVID-19 uptake was exceptionally high, but substantial gaps persisted for routinely recommended adult vaccines. Integrating vaccination into diabetes care and reducing financial and informational barriers may help translate frequent healthcare contact into more complete preventive care.

Conclusion

Among the Filipino adults with diabetes enrolled in this study, uptake of routinely recommended vaccines was uneven: influenza and pneumococcal vaccination were reported by fewer than half, while hepatitis B, herpes zoster, and Td/Tdap uptake remained below 25%. Household income was the most consistent correlate of uptake, while age and sex showed vaccine-specific associations. Provider recommendation was strongly associated with vaccination; however, potential missed preventive care opportunities may also reflect patient hesitancy or failure to prioritize vaccination, affordability, limited access, and vaccine availability. The high historical completion of the primary COVID-19 vaccine series should be interpreted separately from routine adult vaccination because it occurred under exceptional pandemic-related policies and does not indicate adherence to current periodic vaccination recommendations. Integration of vaccine-specific assessment, counseling, documentation, and referral into routine diabetes care, and addressing financial and access barriers, may help to improve adult vaccination uptake.
