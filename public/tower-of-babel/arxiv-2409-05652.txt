Abstract
. We study the field concentration phenomenon between two closely spaced perfect conductors with imperfect bonding interfaces of low conductivity type. The boundary condition on these interfaces is given by a Robin-type boundary condition. We discover a new dichotomy for the field concentration depending on the bonding parameter γ \gamma . Specifically, we show that the gradient of solution is uniformly bounded independent of ε \varepsilon (the distance between two inclusions) when γ \gamma is sufficiently small. However, the gradient may blow up when γ \gamma is large. Moreover, we identify the threshold of γ \gamma and the optimal blow-up rates under certain symmetry assumptions. The proof relies on a crucial anisotropic gradient estimate in the thin neck between two inclusions. We develop a general framework for establishing such estimate, which is applicable to a wide range of elliptic equations and boundary conditions.

1.
Introduction and Main results

Our study is motivated by the phenomenon of field concentration in high-contrast composites, a central topic in composite material research. When two inclusions within a composite material are positioned very close to each other and their material properties significantly differ from the surrounding matrix, it may lead to a significant build-up of stress or field intensity in the narrow gap between the inclusions. Understanding the field concentration phenomenon quantitatively is important since it may cause material failure (see, for example,
[
4
,
25
]
). In some other cases, the inclusions are designed to create the field concentration to achieve desired enhancement of the field (see, for example,
[
32
,
22
]
). Theoretical analysis related to the field concentration phenomenon originated from the effective medium theory. In the pioneering work
[
24
]
, the electrical or thermal resistance of the narrow gaps between the inclusions was analyzed in order to estimate the effective conductivity of a medium containing a dense (nearly touching) array of perfectly conducting or insulating cylinders (2D) or perfectly conducting spheres (3D), all with perfect interfaces. See also
[
17
]
in the setting of elasticity. There have been significant progress over the past three decades in quantitatively analyzing the field concentration phenomenon in scenarios where the inclusions and the background matrix are perfectly bonded: optimal estimates for the local fields in the narrow gaps between the inclusions and asymptotic characterizations capturing the field concentration phenomenon have been derived.

Motivated by the significant physics work
[
33
]
and recent pioneering paper
[
18
]
, we investigate the case of non-ideal bonding. This model holds significant practical relevance, as it takes into account the contact resistance due to roughness and possible debonding at the interface, and it also approximates the membrane structure in biological systems. It is known in physics literature that interfacial effects are very important in a variety of systems. For example, they may dramatically alter the effective properties (
[
33
,
31
,
11
,
8
,
16
,
20
]
). In
[
33
,
31
]
, lower and upper bounds for the effective conductivity of a medium containing randomly packed equisized spheres with imperfect bonding interfaces were deduced by constructing trial fields that account for the complex interactions between the spheres and using minimum energy principles. It is noteworthy that the work
[
33
,
31
]
could not provide estimates for the local fields or capture the field concentration phenomenon when the spheres are closely located, because, as stated in
[
33
]
, “the complexity of the microstructure prohibits one from obtaining the local fields exactly”. However, in this paper, we will establish optimal estimates for the local fields under certain symmetry assumptions.

Let us describe the mathematical setup. Since the region of interest is the local narrow area in between two inclusions which are closely located, the mathematical problem is formulated with two inclusions. Let
n\geq 2
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
contain two relatively strictly convex open sets
D_{1}
and

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}\Big(a(x)\nabla{u}\Big)=0&\mbox{in}~\Omega,\\
u=\varphi(x)&\mbox{on}~\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.1)

represents the voltage potential, and

u|_{+}=u|_{-}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left.\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial\nu}\right|_{+}=k_{i}\left.\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial\nu}\right|_{-}\quad\mbox{on}~\partial D_{i}.

(1.2)

Here and throughout the paper, the subscripts

When

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\mbox{in}~\widetilde{\Omega},\\
u=U_{j}&\mbox{on}~\partial D_{j},j=1,2,\\

u=\varphi&\mbox{on}~\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

It was known that

\begin{cases}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1/2}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&\mbox{when}~n=2,\\
\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq C|\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon|^{-1}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&\mbox{when}~n=3,\\
\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&\mbox{when}~n\geq 4;\end{cases}

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\mbox{in}~\widetilde{\Omega},\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0&\mbox{on}~\partial D_{j},j=1,2,\\

While the optimal blow-up rate in two dimensions was captured about two decades ago in

When the bonding between the inclusions and the background matrix is non-ideal, at least one of the transmission conditions (
1.2
) fails. We consider the inclusion with imperfect bonding interfaces of low conductivity type (LC-type), which can be understood as an approximation of an inclusion with a low conductivity shell as the shell thickness approaches zero. Specifically, consider an inclusion with a core-shell structure, where the shell has a thickness denoted by
t
. Let
k
and
k_{s}
be the conductivities of the core and shell, respectively. As

, if

\gamma^{-1}:=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{k_{s}}{t}

exists and is positive, then the transmission conditions become

\left.\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial\nu}\right|_{+}=k\left.\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial\nu}\right|_{-}=-\gamma^{-1}(u|_{+}-u|_{-}),

and we say that the inclusion has an imperfect bonding interface of LC-type, where
\gamma
is called the bonding parameter. In this scenario, the continuity of potential no longer holds. It is important to note that there is another type of imperfect bonding interface known as the high conductivity type (HC-type), where the continuity of flux fails. In two dimensions, The HC-type problem is indeed the dual problem of the LC-type problem. For a detailed discussion and derivation of the transmission conditions, we refer the reader to
[
18
,
7
]
.

1.

Upper bound for gradient

Absence of field concentration for small

Remark 1.7
.

It is noteworthy that Theorems

and

are the first blow-up results in the context of field concentration for imperfect bonding interfaces. Note that

Therefore, the blow-up exponent in (

Remark 1.8
.

When

and

are balls of radius

and

respectively, it holds that

as in Theorems

and

is exactly the special bonding parameter ensuring that the inclusions are neutral to uniform fields, i.e., the insertion of the inclusions does not perturb the uniform fields (see

Step 1: The flattening map.

To ensure that the solution after the change of variables still satisfies Robin boundary conditions, we adapt the flattening map introduced in

*Section 3 to our setting, which preserves the normal directions on the upper and lower boundaries.

More precisely, we define

\tilde{f}(x^{\prime}):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(x^{\prime})&&\mbox{when}~~|x^{\prime}|\leq 10\,r_{0},\\
&0&&\mbox{when}~~|x^{\prime}|>10\,r_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

\tilde{g}(x^{\prime}):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &g(x^{\prime})&&\mbox{when}~~|x^{\prime}|\leq 10\,r_{0},\\

by

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y^{\prime}-h(y),\\

and

\displaystyle\nparallel\kern-1.99997ptu\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4s}\setminus\Omega_{s})}

\displaystyle\leq\nparallel\kern-1.99997pt\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4s}\setminus\Omega_{s})}+\nparallel\kern-1.99997ptv\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4s}\setminus\Omega_{s})}

\displaystyle\leq Cs^{1+\beta}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2s_{0}})}+Cs|U_{1}|\quad\mbox{for}~s\in[\sqrt{\varepsilon},s_{0}).

Then by the estimate (
1.13
) with
U_{1}=-U_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x)|\leq C|x^{\prime}|^{\beta}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2s_{0}})}+C|U_{1}|\quad\mbox{for}~x\in\Omega_{s_{0}}\setminus\Omega_{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.

To obtain the estimate for
x\in\Omega_{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
, we choose
k
so that
2^{-k-1}s_{0}<4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq 2^{-k}s_{0}
. Then (
4.3
) with
s=2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and (
4.7
) imply

\displaystyle\nparallel\kern-1.99997ptu\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4\sqrt{\varepsilon}})}

\displaystyle\leq\nparallel\kern-1.99997pt\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4\sqrt{\varepsilon}})}+\nparallel\kern-1.99997ptv\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4\sqrt{\varepsilon}})}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{-\frac{n-1}{4}}A_{k-1}^{1/2}+C\varepsilon\,|U_{1}|\leq C\varepsilon^{(\beta+1)/2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2s_{0}})}+C\varepsilon^{1/2}|U_{1}|,

which gives, by the estimate (
1.13
) with
U_{1}=-U_{2}
again,

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x)|\leq C\varepsilon^{\beta/2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2s_{0}})}+C|U_{1}|\quad\mbox{for}~x\in\Omega_{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
A dimensional reduction argument

In this section, we adapt a dimensional reduction argument in
[
13
]
to reduce the equation (
1.18
) to
n-1
dimensions, under the assumption that
f,g
satisfy (
1.8
) and (
1.16
). This argument plays an important role in proving Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

The weak formulation of (
1.18
) is given by

\int_{\Omega_{R_{0}}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx+\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{\Gamma_{\pm}}u\varphi\,d\sigma=0

for all smooth
\varphi
that vanishes on
\{|x^{\prime}|=R_{0}\}
.
Flatten the boundaries by

\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{\prime}&=x^{\prime},\\
y_{n}&=2\varepsilon\left(\frac{x_{n}-g(x^{\prime})+\varepsilon/2}{\varepsilon+f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{2}\right),\end{aligned}\right.\quad\forall(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\Omega_{R_{0}},

(5.1)

and let
v(y)=u(x)
. Note that

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{+}}u(x^{\prime},x_{n})\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}u(x^{\prime},\varepsilon/2+f(x^{\prime}))\varphi(x^{\prime},\varepsilon/2+f(x^{\prime}))\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime})|^{2}}\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}v(y^{\prime},\varepsilon)\varphi(y^{\prime},\varepsilon)\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}f(y^{\prime})|^{2}}\,dy^{\prime}.

Similarly,

\int_{\Gamma_{-}}u(x^{\prime},x_{n})\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})\,d\sigma=\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}v(y^{\prime},-\varepsilon)\varphi(y^{\prime},-\varepsilon)\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}g(y^{\prime})|^{2}}\,dy^{\prime}.

Therefore,
v
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\partial_{i}(a^{ij}(y)\partial_{j}v(y))&=0\quad\mbox{in }Q_{R_{0},\varepsilon},\\
2\varepsilon\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}f|^{2}}v(y)+a^{nj}(y)\partial_{j}v(y)&=0\quad\mbox{on }\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\},\\
2\varepsilon\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}g|^{2}}v(y)-a^{nj}(y)\partial_{j}v(y)&=0\quad\mbox{on }\{y_{n}=-\varepsilon\},\end{aligned}\right.

(5.2)

where the coefficient matrix
(a^{ij}(y))
is given by

\displaystyle(a^{ij}(y))=

\displaystyle\frac{2\varepsilon(\partial_{x}y)(\partial_{x}y)^{t}}{\det(\partial_{x}y)}

(5.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon+\mu|y^{\prime}|^{2}&0&\cdots&0&a^{1n}\\
0&\varepsilon+\mu|y^{\prime}|^{2}&\cdots&0&a^{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\varepsilon+\mu|y^{\prime}|^{2}&a^{n-1,n}\\
a^{n1}&a^{n2}&\cdots&a^{n,n-1}&\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|a^{in}|^{2}}{\varepsilon+f(y^{\prime})-g(y^{\prime})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\begin{pmatrix}e^{1}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&e^{2}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&e^{n-1}&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle a^{ni}=a^{in}

\displaystyle=-2\varepsilon\partial_{i}g(y^{\prime})-(y_{n}+\varepsilon)\partial_{i}(f(y^{\prime})-g(y^{\prime}));

\displaystyle e^{i}

\displaystyle=f(y^{\prime})-g(y^{\prime})-\mu|y^{\prime}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for}~i=1,2,\ldots,n-1,

and

Q_{s,t}:=\{y=(y^{\prime},y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~\big|~|y^{\prime}|<s,|y_{n}|<t\}.

(5.4)

Note that there is an extra
2\varepsilon
factor appearing in the boundary condition of (
5.2
). This is due to the extra
2\varepsilon
factor of
(a^{ij}(y))
defined in (
5.3
).
We define

\bar{v}(y^{\prime}):=\fint_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}v(y^{\prime},y_{n})\,dy_{n}.

(5.5)

By (
5.2
),
\bar{v}
satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}((\varepsilon+\mu|y^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla\bar{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a^{nj}\partial_{j}v)(y^{\prime},\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a^{nj}\partial_{j}v)(y^{\prime},-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial_{i}(\overline{a^{in}\partial_{n}v})-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial_{i}(e^{i}\partial_{i}\bar{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}f|^{2}}v(y^{\prime},\varepsilon)+\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}g|^{2}}v(y^{\prime},-\varepsilon)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial_{i}(\overline{a^{in}\partial_{n}v}+e^{i}\partial_{i}\bar{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\gamma^{-1}\bar{v}(y^{\prime})\underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial_{i}(\overline{a^{in}\partial_{n}v}+e^{i}\partial_{i}\bar{v})}_{:=\operatorname{div}F}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}f|^{2}}v(y^{\prime},\varepsilon)+\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}g|^{2}}v(y^{\prime},-\varepsilon)-2\gamma^{-1}\bar{v}(y^{\prime})}_{:=G},

where
\overline{a^{in}\partial_{n}v}
denotes the vertical average of
a^{in}\partial_{n}v
with respect to
y_{n}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. We will adapt this notation throughout this article.

Summarizing this section, we have shown the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f,g
satisfy (
1.8
) and (
1.16
),

be a solution of (

). Perform the change of variables (

), and let
v(y)=u(x)
,
\bar{v}
be defined as in (

). Then
\bar{v}
satisfies
\bar{v}(0)=0
and the equation

\operatorname{div}((\varepsilon+\mu|y^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla\bar{v})-2\gamma^{-1}\bar{v}=\operatorname{div}F+G\quad\mbox{in}~B_{R_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

(5.6)

where

\displaystyle|F(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\varepsilon|y^{\prime}||\overline{\partial_{n}v}|+|e^{1}||\nabla_{y^{\prime}}\overline{v}|\Big),

(5.7)

\displaystyle|G(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\varepsilon\max_{y_{n}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}|\partial_{n}v|+|y^{\prime}|^{2}|\overline{v}|\Big),

and
C
is some positive constant depending only on
n
,
\gamma
,
\|f\|_{C^{2,\sigma}}
, and
\|g\|_{C^{2,\sigma}}
.

Proof.

The derivation of equation (

satisfy (

), we have

\displaystyle|F(y^{\prime})|\leq C\Big(|\overline{a^{in}\partial_{n}v}|+|e^{i}\partial_{i}\bar{v})|\Big)\leq C\Big(\varepsilon|y^{\prime}||\overline{\partial_{n}v}|+|e^{1}||\nabla_{y^{\prime}}\overline{v}|\Big).

For the

\displaystyle|G(y^{\prime})|\leq

\displaystyle\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}f|^{2}}|v(y^{\prime},\varepsilon)-\bar{v}(y^{\prime})|+\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}g|^{2}}|v(y^{\prime},-\varepsilon)-\bar{v}(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\gamma^{-1}\Big(\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}f|^{2}}+\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{y^{\prime}}g|^{2}}-2\Big)|\bar{v}(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\varepsilon\max_{y_{n}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}|\partial_{n}v|+|y^{\prime}|^{2}|\overline{v}|\Big).

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\mu=1
. Namely, we consider

f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})=|x^{\prime}|^{2}+O(|x^{\prime}|^{2+\sigma})\quad\mbox{for}~~0<|x^{\prime}|<R_{0}.

In this case,

\alpha=\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}+4(n-2+2/\gamma)}}{2}.

(6.1)

For general
\mu>0
, we only need to replace
\varepsilon
and
\gamma
in the proof by
\varepsilon/\mu
and
\mu\gamma
respectively, in view of equation (
5.6
). The strategy of the proof is similar to the proof of
[
13
]
*Theorem 1.1. However, some modifications are needed to address the case

and the extra zero-order term

in the equation (
5.6
).

For
\varepsilon>0
,
\sigma,s\in\mathbb{R}
, an open set
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, we introduce the following norm

\|F\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma,s,D}:=\sup_{y^{\prime}\in D}\frac{|F(y^{\prime})|}{|y^{\prime}|^{\sigma}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})^{1-s}}.

(6.2)

Throughout this section,
B_{R}
always lies in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. For any
0<R<R_{0}
, we denote

to be the average of
u
over
\partial B_{R}

First, we establish estimates for
\bar{v}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For
n\geq 2
,
0\leq s\leq 1/2
,
\gamma>0
,
\sigma>0
,
1+\sigma-2s\neq\min\{\alpha,1\}
, and
\varepsilon>0
,
let
\bar{v}\in H^{1}(B_{1})
be a solution of

\operatorname{div}((\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla\bar{v})-2\gamma^{-1}\bar{v}=\operatorname{div}F+G\quad\text{in}\,\,B_{R_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

where
F,G
satisfy

\|F\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma,s,B_{R_{0}}}<\infty,\quad\|G\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma-1,s,B_{R_{0}}}<\infty.

(6.3)

Then for any
R\in(0,R_{0}/2)
, we have

\displaystyle\nparallel\kern-1.99997pt\bar{v}-(\bar{v}_{\partial B_{R}})\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{R})}

(6.4)

\displaystyle\leq C(\|F\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma,s,B_{R_{0}}}+\|G\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma-1,s,B_{R_{0}}}+\nparallel\kern-1.99997pt\bar{v}-(\bar{v})_{\partial B_{R_{0}}}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{R_{0}})})R^{\tilde{\alpha}},

where
\tilde{\alpha}:=\min\{\alpha,1,1+\sigma-2s\}
,
\alpha
is given in (
6.1
), and
C
is some positive constant depending only on
n
,
\sigma
,
s
, and is independent of
\varepsilon
.

For the proof, we use an iteration argument based on the following lemmas.

Lemma 6.2
.

For
n\geq 2
,
\gamma>0
, and
\varepsilon>0
, let
v_{1}\in H^{1}(B_{R_{0}})
satisfy

\operatorname{div}((\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla v_{1})-2\gamma^{-1}v_{1}=0\quad\text{in}\,\,B_{R_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

(6.5)

Then for any
0<\rho<R\leq R_{0}
, we have

\left(\fint_{\partial B_{\rho}}|v_{1}(y^{\prime})-(v_{1})_{\partial B_{\rho}}|^{2}\,dS\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left(\frac{\rho}{R}\right)^{\widehat{\alpha}}\left(\fint_{\partial B_{R}}|v_{1}(y^{\prime})-(v_{1})_{\partial B_{R}}|^{2}\,dS\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\widehat{\alpha}:=\min\{\alpha,1\}
, and
\alpha
is given in (
6.1
).

Proof.

By the elliptic theory,
v_{1}\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
. By scaling, it suffices to prove the lemma for
R=1
. We first provide a proof for the case when
n\geq 3
. Denote
y^{\prime}=(r,\xi)\in(0,1)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
. We can rewrite (
6.5
) as

\partial_{rr}v_{1}+\left(\frac{n-2}{r}+\frac{2r}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\right)\partial_{r}v_{1}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}v_{1}-\frac{2}{\gamma(\varepsilon+r^{2})}v_{1}=0\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{1}\setminus\{0\}.

Take the spherical harmonic decomposition

v_{1}(y^{\prime})=V_{0}(r)Y_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N(k)}V_{k,i}(r)Y_{k,i}(\xi),\quad y^{\prime}\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

(6.6)

where
Y_{0}=|\mathbb{S}^{n-2}|^{-1/2}
,
Y_{k,i}
is a
k
-th degree spherical harmonics, that is,

-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}Y_{k,i}=k(k+n-3)Y_{k,i}

and
\{Y_{k,i}\}_{k,i}\cup Y_{0}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-2})
.
Then

V_{0}(r)Y_{0}=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}v_{1}(r,\xi)\,d\xi=(v_{1})_{\partial B_{r}},

V_{k,i}(r)\in C^{2}(0,1)
is given by

V_{k,i}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}v_{1}(y^{\prime})Y_{k,i}(\xi)\,d\xi

and satisfies

\displaystyle L_{k}V_{k,i}:=

\displaystyle V_{k,i}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{n-2}{r}+\frac{2r}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\right)V_{k,i}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{k(k+n-3)}{r^{2}}+\frac{2}{\gamma(\varepsilon+r^{2})}\right)V_{k,i}(r)=0\quad\mbox{in}~~(0,1)

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,N(k)
. Since
v_{1}\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
and
\{Y_{k,i}\}_{k,i}\cup Y_{0}
is an orthonormal basis, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N(k)}|V_{k,i}(r)|^{2}=\fint_{\partial B_{\rho}}|v_{1}(y^{\prime})-(v_{1})_{\partial B_{r}}|^{2}\,dS\to 0\quad\mbox{as}~r\to 0.

This implies

|V_{k,i}(r)|\to 0\quad\mbox{as}~r\to 0

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,N(k)
.

Next, we consider two separate cases
0<\gamma\leq 1
and
\gamma>1
.

Case 1:
When
0<\gamma\leq 1
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by a direct computation,

L_{k}r=\frac{n-2}{r}-\frac{k(k+n-3)}{r}+\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\frac{2r}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\leq 0.

Since

\pm V_{k,i}(r)-|V_{k,i}(1)|r\to 0\quad\mbox{as}~~r\searrow 0,

and clearly,

\pm V_{k,i}(r)-|V_{k,i}(1)|r\leq 0\quad\mbox{when}~~r=1.

By the maximum principle,

|V_{k,i}(r)|\leq r|V_{k,i}(1)|\quad\mbox{for}~~0<r\leq 1.

(6.7)

Case 2:
When
\gamma>1
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\alpha_{k}:=\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}+4[k(k+n-3)+2/\gamma]}}{2}>0.

Note that
\alpha_{1}=\alpha
, where
\alpha
is defined in (
6.1
).
By a direct computation,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L_{k}r^{\alpha_{k}}=&\alpha_{k}(\alpha_{k}-1)r^{\alpha_{k}-2}+\left(n-2+\frac{2r^{2}}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\right)\alpha_{k}r^{\alpha_{k}-2}\\
&-\left(k(k+n-3)+\frac{2}{\gamma}\frac{r^{2}}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\right)r^{\alpha_{k}-2}\\
=&-2\varepsilon\left(\alpha_{k}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\frac{r^{\alpha_{k}-2}}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\quad\quad\mbox{for}~r\in(0,1),\end{aligned}

where in the second line, we used the equality

\alpha_{k}^{2}+(n-1)\alpha_{k}-(k(k+n-3)+2\gamma^{-1})=0

(6.8)

followed from the definition of
\alpha_{k}
. By (
6.8
) again, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}-\frac{1}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\alpha_{k}^{2}+(n-1)\alpha_{k}-k(k+n-3)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{k}+k+n-3)(\alpha_{k}-k)>0,

since

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle<\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}+4k^{2}+4(n-1)k+8-8k}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1+2k)^{2}}}{2}\leq k.

Therefore,

L_{k}r^{\alpha_{k}}<0\quad\quad\mbox{for}~r\in(0,1).

Since

\pm V_{k,i}(r)-|V_{k,i}(1)|r^{\alpha_{k}}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}~~r\searrow 0,

and clearly,

\pm V_{k,i}(r)-|V_{k,i}(1)|r^{\alpha_{k}}=0\quad\mbox{when}~~r=1.

By the maximum principle,

|V_{k,i}(r)|\leq r^{\alpha_{k}}|V_{k,i}(1)|\quad\mbox{for}~~0<r<1.

(6.9)

Combining these two cases, it follows from (
6.6
),(
6.7
), and (
6.9
),

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{\rho}}|v_{1}(y^{\prime})-(v_{1})_{\partial B_{\rho}}|^{2}\,dS

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N(k)}|V_{k,i}(\rho)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{2\widehat{\alpha}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N(k)}|V_{k,i}(1)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{2\widehat{\alpha}}\fint_{\partial B_{1}}|v_{1}(y^{\prime})-(v_{1})_{\partial B_{1}}|^{2}\,d\sigma.

This completes the proof for
n\geq 3
.

When
n=2
, we use
(\bar{v}(r)-\bar{v}(-r))/2
in place of
V_{1,1}(r)
, and repeat the arguments for the estimates (
6.7
) and (
6.9
) to obtain

\left|\frac{\bar{v}(\rho)-\bar{v}(-\rho)}{2}\right|\leq\rho^{\widehat{\alpha}}\left|\frac{\bar{v}(1)-\bar{v}(-1)}{2}\right|.

The lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

For
n\geq 2
,
s\leq 1/2
,
\sigma>0
, and
\varepsilon>0
, suppose that
F,G
satisfy (
6.3
), and
v_{2}\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
satisfies

\operatorname{div}((\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla v_{2})-2\gamma^{-1}v_{2}=\operatorname{div}F+G\quad\text{in}\,\,B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

(6.10)

Then we have

\|v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq C(\|F\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma,s,B_{1}}+\|G\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma-1,s,B_{1}}),

(6.11)

where
C>0
depends only on
n
,
\sigma
, and
s
, and is in particular independent of
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume

\|F\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma,s,B_{1}}+\|G\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma-1,s,B_{1}}=1.

When
\varepsilon\geq 1
, we divide the equation (
6.10
) by
\varepsilon
and obtain

\operatorname{div}((1+\varepsilon^{-1}|y^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla v_{2})-2(\varepsilon\gamma)^{-1}v_{2}=\varepsilon^{-1}\operatorname{div}F+\varepsilon^{-1}G\quad\text{in}\,\,B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

By assumptions,

|\varepsilon^{-1}F|\leq C|y^{\prime}|^{\sigma},\quad|\varepsilon^{-1}G|\leq C|y^{\prime}|^{\sigma-1},

so that (
6.11
) follows from the standard Moser iteration. Therefore, we will focus on the case
0<\varepsilon<1
, where we will use Moser iteration in weighted
L^{p}
spaces.

For
p\geq 2
, we multiply the equation (
6.10
) with
-|v_{2}|^{p-2}v_{2}
and integrate by parts to obtain

\displaystyle(p-1)\int_{B_{1}}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})|\nabla v_{2}|^{2}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}+2\gamma^{-1}\int_{B_{1}}|v_{2}|^{p}\,dy^{\prime}

(6.12)

\displaystyle=(p-1)\int_{B_{1}}F\cdot\nabla v_{2}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}-\int_{B_{1}}Gv_{2}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}.

By the definition in (
6.2
),

\displaystyle|F(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|y^{\prime}|^{\sigma}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})^{1-s}\|F\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma,s,B_{1}},

\displaystyle|G(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|y^{\prime}|^{\sigma-1}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})^{1-s}\|G\|_{\varepsilon,\sigma-1,s,B_{1}}\quad\mbox{for}~~y^{\prime}\in B_{1}.

To estimate the first term on the right-hand side of (
6.12
), we use Young’s inequality,
s\leq 1/2
, and
\varepsilon<1
,

\displaystyle(p-1)\left|\int_{B_{1}}F\cdot\nabla v_{2}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(p-1)}{4}\int_{B_{1}}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})|\nabla v_{2}|^{2}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}

\displaystyle+C(p-1)\int_{B_{1}}|y^{\prime}|^{2\sigma}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})^{1-2s}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(p-1)}{4}\int_{B_{1}}(\varepsilon+|y^{\prime}|^{2})|\nabla v_{2}|^{2}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}+C(p-1)\int_{B_{1}}|y^{\prime}|^{2\sigma}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}.

Then by Hölder’s inequality and
\sigma>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}|y^{\prime}|^{2\sigma}|v_{2}|^{p-2}\,dy^{\prime}\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{B_{1}}|y^{\prime}|^{\sigma(n+1+2\varrho)-(n-1+2\varrho)}\,dy^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{2}{n+1+2\varrho}}\times

(6.13)
