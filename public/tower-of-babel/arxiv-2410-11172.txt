Asynchronous 3-Majority Dynamics with Many Opinions

Abstract
We consider 3-Majority, a probabilistic consensus dynamics on a complete graph with n n vertices, each vertex starting with one of k k initial opinions. At each discrete time step, a vertex u u is chosen uniformly at random. The selected vertex u u chooses three neighbors v 1 , v 2 , v 3 v_{1},v_{2},v_{3} uniformly at random with replacement and takes the majority opinion held by the three, where ties are broken in favor of the opinion of v 3 v_{3} . The main quantity of interest is the consensus time, the number of steps required for all vertices to hold the same opinion. This asynchronous version turns out to be considerably harder to analyze than the synchronous version and so far results have only been obtained for k = 2 k=2 . Even in the synchronous version the results for large k k are far from tight. In this paper we prove that the consensus time is Θ ~ ​ ( min ⁡ ( n ​ k , n 1.5 ) ) \tilde{\Theta}(\min(nk,n^{1.5})) for all k k . These are the first bounds for all k k that are tight up to a polylogarithmic factor.

Introduction

Consensus dynamics
is a time-evolving distributed process on a network consisting of
anonymous
nodes (i.e., they do not possess their own ID) which hold their own value called
opinion
.

The nodes update their own opinions according to a common protocol and the aim of the protocol is to reach
consensus
, a configuration where all vertices hold the same opinion.
We usually consider protocols specified by a simple algorithm that consists of elementary local calculations (cf. light-weight algorithms
[
HP01
]
).
This algorithmic perspective describes real-world dynamics as the outcome of the interaction of simple local algorithms and appears in a broad range of areas including interacting particle systems
[
Lig85
]
, distributed computing
[
HP01
]
, chemical reaction networks
[
Dot14
,
CHKM20
]
, biological systems
[
FK13
]
, to name a few.
See
[
BCN20
]
for an excellent survey of this general topic.

To state it more formally, consider a graph
G=(V,E)
, where each vertex
v\in V
initially holds a value
\sigma_{0}(v)\in\Sigma
.
This vector
\sigma_{0}=(\sigma_{0}(v))_{v\in V}
is called a
configuration
.
During the dynamics, a subset of vertices is chosen to be activated, and the activated vertices update their opinions according to a common update rule.
This paper focuses on discrete-time dynamics.
A consensus dynamics is
synchronous
(or parallel) if all vertices become active at every discrete time step and
asynchronous
1
1

1

The usage of the term “asynchronous” here is standard in the literature of consensus dynamics (e.g.,
[
BCN20
]
) but different from some literature of distributed computing, where the word refers to unbounded/unpredictable delays.
(or sequential) if exactly one uniformly random vertex is chosen to be activated.
This yields a sequence of configurations
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and we are interested in the convergence time of
\sigma_{t}
.

Voter
is one of the simplest protocols studied in distributed computing
[
HP01
]
and physics
[
Lig85
]
.
In this protocol, an activated vertex selects a uniformly random neighbor
v
and updates its opinion to match the opinion of
v
.
The

exhibits

in the sense that the local update algorithm can be written as a (random) linear function of the current configuration vector.
That is, if
\sigma_{t}\in\Sigma^{V}
is the current configuration, then the next configuration
\sigma_{t+1}\in\Sigma^{V}
can be written as
\sigma_{t+1}=P_{t}\sigma_{t}
, where
P_{t}
is a random matrix chosen from an appropriate distribution
[
CR16
]
.
Linear dynamics contains several important dynamics, including the push voting model
[
DW83
]
, Moran process
[
DGMRSS14
]
, and averaging dynamics
[
BGPS06
]
.
This class of dynamics is widely recognized as a tractable class, as we can invoke martingale theory
[

]
.
In particular,
Voter
exhibits an elegant duality relationship with coalescing random walks, which enables us to bound the convergence time (e.g.,
[
CEOR13
]
).

While linear dynamics has been extensively investigated, a more recent line of research addresses dynamics based on nonlinear operations.
Such nonlinear dynamics has a wide range of applications in the consensus problem
[
BCNPS15
]
, community detection
[
CNS19
,
SS21
]
, chemical reaction network
[
CHKM17
]
, among others.
One prominent example is the
3-Majority
dynamics

,
BCNPT17
,
BCEKMN17
,
GL18
,
BCGHKR23
]
.
In this dynamics, an activated vertex
u
chooses three neighbors
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
uniformly at random (with replacement).
If
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
have the same opinion,
then
u
replaces its opinion with the opinion of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
Otherwise,
u
updates its opinion to the opinion held by
v_{3}
.
2
2

2

Observe that this is equivalent to
u
taking on the majority opinion
held by the three vertices
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
, or, if they all have different opinions, the opinion of
v_{i}
for random
i\sim\{1,2,3\}
.

Such majority-like update rules appear naturally as models of opinion forming.
For example, suppose that you visit an unfamiliar city for a conference and plan to have lunch in a restaurant.
To decide which restaurant to go to, you can ask some of your friends living in the city and then take the majority among the recommendations, where ties are broken at random.

1.1
Main Result

We study the convergence time of the asynchronous version of
3-Majority
on an
n
-vertex complete graph (with self-loops).
See
Definition

3.1
for the formal definition.
The quantity of interest is the
consensus time
, which is the number of time steps required to reach consensus.

In the case of binary opinions (
k=2
),
[
BCGHKR23
]
proved that the consensus time is
O(n\log n)
with high probability.
3
3

3

Throughout this paper, “with high probability” means with probability
1-O(n^{-\beta})
, for
some constant
\beta>0
.

Although the case of many opinions (
k>2
) is much less understood in the asynchronous model,

3-Majority
dynamics in the synchronous model have been well studied.

[
BCNPST17
]
proved that the dynamics reaches consensus within
O\left(k\log n\right)
rounds with high probability if there is initially a sufficiently large gap in the number of supporters between the most and second most popular opinions.
They also proved that the dynamics requires
\Omega(k\log n)
rounds to reach consensus with high probability for some initial configuration if
k\leq(n/\log n)^{1/4}
.
The condition of a large gap in the initial configuration was removed by
[
BCNPT16
]
, where they proved that the consensus time is
\tilde{O}\left(k^{3}\right)
with high probability for any initial opinion configuration if
k\leq n^{1/3-\epsilon}
and
\epsilon>0
is an arbitrary constant.
4
4

4

\tilde{O}\left(\cdot\right)
hides a
(\log n)^{O(1)}
-factor.

They asked for an improved bound on the consensus time, which was subsequently given by

[
GL18
]
.
They proved that the consensus time is
O\left(k\log n\right)
with high probability if
k=O(n^{1/3}/\sqrt{\log n})
.
The question is: Does this
linear-in-
k
dependence
[
BCN20
]
hold for all
k
?

Interestingly,
[
BCEKMN17
]
proved the following remarkable result: Starting from any initial configuration and any
k
(say,
k=n
),
the number of remaining opinions decreases to at most
\kappa
after
O\left(n\log n/\kappa\right)
rounds with high probability.
Combining this result with the bound from
[
GL18
]
for
\kappa=O(n^{1/3}/\sqrt{\log n})
,
we have that the consensus time of synchronous
3-Majority
dynamics is at most
\tilde{O}\left(n^{2/3}\right)
with high probability.

In summary, the linear-in-
k
dependence of the consensus time
holds for
k\ll n^{1/3}

[
GL18
]
but does not hold for
k\gg n^{2/3}

[
BCEKMN17
]
.
This raises the following natural question:

What is the range of
k
in which the linear-in-
k
dependence holds?

This paper determines the range of such
k
in the
asynchronous
setting.
More generally, we obtain the first consensus time bound that is tight for
all

2\leq k\leq n
up to
(\log n)^{O(1)}
factors.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

For any
k\geq 2
, the asynchronous
3-Majority
dynamics with
k
opinions on an
n
-vertex complete graph reaches consensus within
\tilde{O}\left\lparen\min\left\lparen kn,n^{1.5}\right\rparen\right\rparen
steps with high probability.
Moreover, there exists an initial configuration on which the dynamics requires
\tilde{\Omega}\left\lparen\min\left\lparen kn,n^{1.5}\right\rparen\right\rparen
steps to reach consensus with high probability.

More precisely, the hidden
(\log n)^{O(1)}
factors in
Theorem

1.1
are as follows: The upper bound is
O\left\lparen\min\left\{kn(\log n)^{2},n^{1.5}(\log n)^{1.5}\right\}\right\rparen
and the lower bound is
\Omega\left\lparen\min\left\lparen kn,\frac{n^{1.5}}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right\rparen\right\rparen
.

Theorem

1.1
means that the linear-in-
k
dependence holds for
2\leq k\ll\sqrt{n}
and not for
k\gg\sqrt{n}
.
The main ingredient of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is to bound the consensus time for
k\ll n^{1/2}
.
This extends the previous range of
k\ll n^{1/3}
in the synchronous setting
[
GL18
]
.

We also obtain a lower bound on the consensus time for the asynchronous
2-Choices
dynamics, which is a fundamental nonlinear consensus dynamics.
In the
2-Choices
dynamics,
the activated vertex
u
randomly picks two vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
(with replacement) and updates its opinion to match that of
v_{1}
if both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
have the same opinion (otherwise, the opinion of
u
does not change).
Specifically, we show that asynchronous
2-Choices
requires
\Omega(kn)
steps to reach consensus with high probability provided that
k\ll n/\log n
(see
Theorem

6.2
for the detail).
This establishes a polynomial gap between
3-Majority
and
2-Choices
in the asynchronous setting:
The asynchronous
3-Majority
reaches consensus within
\tilde{O}(n^{1.5})
steps, whereas
2-Choices
requires
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{2})
steps for
k\approx n/\log n
.
Such gap was previously known in the synchronous setting
[
BCEKMN17
,
GL18
]
.

This work gives an improved analysis of
3-Majority
dynamics in the asynchronous update setting.
We leave open whether the analogous consensus time bound holds for the synchronous
3-Majority
dynamics.
A general reduction between synchronous and asynchronous models is not known.
Indeed some dynamics are known to exhibit a huge synchronous-asynchronous gap
[
BCPTVZ24
]
.
However, we believe that there is no significant gap between asynchronous and synchronous
3-Majority
dynamics and our proof has the potential to be adapted to prove optimal consensus time bound for the synchronous
3-Majority
dynamics.

The overview of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
and our technical contributions are given in
Section

2
.

Related Work

The convergence time in consensus dynamics,
where each vertex repeatedly updates its opinion based on the opinions of others with the goal of reaching consensus,
has been studied for various opinion updating rules, including
3-Majority
.
This research topic spans multiple contexts, including distributed computing, physics, social science, and biology.
See, for example,
[
BCN20
]
for an overview of recent studies in this field.

Voter
(also known as the pull voting) is one of the simplest and most well-known consensus dynamics
[
Pel02
,
BGKM16
,
CR16
,
NIY99
]
.
It has been shown that, for any
k
, the expected consensus time is
O(n)
for synchronous
Voter

[
BGKM16
]
and
O(n^{2})
for the asynchronous
Voter

[
CR16
]
.

2-Choices
(also known as two-sample voting or best-of-two)
has also been extensively studied in the synchronous setting
[
DGMSS11
,
GL18
,
BCEKMN17
,
CER14
,
CERRS15
,
CRRS17
,
SS20
,
EFKMT17
]
.

[
GL18
]
showed that the consensus time is
O(k\log n)
with high probability if
k=O(\sqrt{n/\log n})
.
On the other hand,
[
BCEKMN17
]
showed that the consensus time is
\Omega(n/\log n)
with high probability if each vertex initially holds a different opinion (
k=n
).

General classes of opinion updating rules have been studied especially for the binary opinions case (
k=2
)
[
CR16
,
SY18
,
SS20
,
BCGHKR23
]
.

[
BCGHKR23
]
studied
j
-Majority
dynamics with binary opinions,
where each vertex
v
randomly picks
j
vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{j}
(with replacement) and
v
updates its opinion to match the majority opinion among
u_{1},\dots,u_{j}
(with ties broken randomly).
They showed that the consensus time of
j
-Majority
stochastically dominates the consensus time of
(j+1)
-Majority
for any
j\geq 2
.

[
SY18
]
studied asynchronous “majority-like” voting, including
(2j+1)
-Majority
for
j\geq 1
.
They showed that the expected consensus time is
O(n\log n)
for a dense Erdős-Rényi random graph.

For the case of synchronous updates, several studies focus on scenarios where an adversary capable of changing the opinions of vertices exists
[
BCNPT16
,
BCNPST17
,
GL18
,
BCEKMN17
]
.
For example,
[

Undecided State Dynamics is a well-known model within the field of consensus dynamics, which uses a special state called “Undecided”.
In this model, each “Undecided” vertex updates its state to match that of an interacted vertex, and each “not-Undecided” vertex becomes “Undecided” upon interacting with a vertex of a differing state.
The consensus time for Undecided State Dynamics has been well studied for

,

]
and,
more recently, for the general case of

[

,

]

in both synchronous and asynchronous model.

It is worth mentioning that the asynchronous model

In this section, we present the proof overview of
Theorem

1.1

2.1
Key Results

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
consists of two parts.
Firstly, we show that, starting from any initial configuration, the number of remaining opinions in the asynchronous
3-Majority
quickly decreases.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider the asynchronous
3-Majority
starting from any initial configuration.
There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that,
for any
1\leq\kappa\leq n
, the number of remaining opinions becomes at most
\kappa
within
Cn^{2}\log n/\kappa
steps with high probability.

In the synchronous setting, results analogous to
Theorem

2.1
were obtained in
[
BCEKMN17
, Section 3.2]
.
However, their proof crucially relies on the synchronicity.
Indeed, extending their approach to the asynchronous setting is “the most interesting open question” of
[
BCGHKR23
]
and we resolve it for
3-Majority
.

Secondly, we show that, the linear-in-
k
dependence of the consensus time holds for
k\ll\sqrt{n}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the asynchronous 3-Majority dynamics with
k\leq c\sqrt{n/\log n}
opinions on an
n
-vertex complete graph, where
c>0
is a sufficiently small constant. Then, for any initial configuration, the consensus time is
O(kn\log n)
with high probability.
Moreover, if the dynamics starts from the balanced initial configuration (that is, the configuration in which all opinions are supported by the same number of vertices), then the consensus time is
\Omega(kn)
with high probability.

The upper bound of
Theorem

2.2
addresses a wider range of
k
compared to
[
GL18
]
, who proved that the analogous upper bound in the synchronous setting holds for
k\ll n^{1/3}
.
Indeed, the common argument in previous works for the synchronous model essentially requires
k\ll n^{1/3}
(see
Section

2.3
for the detail).
We overcome the “
n^{1/3}
-barrier” by invoking sharp martingale concentration inequalities.
The lower bound of
Theorem

2.2
improves the analysis of
[
BCNPST17
]
who proved the analogous lower bound in the synchronous setting for
k\leq(n/\log n)^{1/4}
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
is straightforward from
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
using
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
.

We first prove the upper bound.
Let
k
be the number of opinions in the initial configuration and

\kappa=c\sqrt{n/\log n}
(for a sufficiently small constant
c>0
).
If
k\leq\kappa
, then from
Theorem

2.2
, the consensus time is
O(kn\log n)
.
If
k>\kappa
, from
Theorem

2.1
, the number of remaining opinions becomes at most
\kappa
within
O((n\log n)^{1.5})
steps.
Then from
Theorem

2.2
, the dynamics reaches consensus within additional
O(\kappa n\log n)=O(n^{1.5}\sqrt{\log n})
steps with high probability.
Therefore, for any
k\geq 2
, the consensus time is
O\left\lparen\min\left\lparen kn(\log n)^{2},(n\log n)^{1.5}\right\rparen\right\rparen
with high probability.

We prove the lower bound.
If
k\leq\kappa=c\sqrt{n/\log n}
and the dynamics starts with the balanced configuration, then the consensus time is
\Omega(kn)
from
Theorem

2.2
.
Even if
k>\kappa
, for the balanced initial configuration with
\kappa
opinions (i.e.,
\kappa
opinions supported by the same number of

vertices and the remaining opinions not supported by any vertex),
the consensus time is
\Omega(\kappa n)=\Omega(n^{1.5}/\sqrt{\log n})
with high probability from

2.2
.
This proves the claim.
∎

The rest of this paper is devoted to proving

and

2.2
Many Opinion Case

To prove
Theorem

2.1
, we borrow the framework of comparing
Voter
and
3-Majority
from
[
BCEKMN17
]
.
Specifically, we couple
Voter
and
3-Majority
so that the number of remaining opinions (i.e., an opinion that is supported by at least one vertex) in
3-Majority
is no more than that of
Voter
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider the asynchronous
Voter
and
3-Majority
dynamics starting from the same initial configuration.
There exists an explicit coupling of these dynamics such that, the number of remaining opinions in the
3-Majority
dynamics is no more than that of
Voter
.

An analogous result of
Lemma

2.3
in the synchronous setting was shown by
[
BCEKMN17
]
.
However, their proof crucially relies on the synchronicity of the update schedule.
In the asynchronous setting,
Lemma

2.3
was shown by
[
BCGHKR23
]
for the special case of
k=2
.
Indeed, they left the case of general
k\geq 3
as an open question and
Lemma

2.3
resolves it.
Moreover, a remarkable point is that the proof of
Lemma

2.3
is
constructive
in the sense that the coupling is explicit.
Actually, the coupling of both
[
BCEKMN17
,
BCGHKR23
]
rely on a tool from Majorization theory (Strassen’s theorem) and are nonconstructive.
Unfortunately, our analysis essentially relies on the asynchronicity.
We leave the constructive proof of
Lemma

2.3
in the synchronous setting as an open question.

We then prove an analogous result of
Theorem

2.1
for the asynchronous
Voter
.
This can be done by exploiting the well-known duality between
Voter
and coalescing random walks
[
AF
, Section 14.3]
.

Explicit Coupling of
Voter
and
3-Majority
(
Section

7
).

The proof strategy for
Lemma

2.3
is to construct a coupling that preserves the
majorization order
, following the previous approach of
[
BCEKMN17
]
for the synchronous model.
Let
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
denote the set of possible opinions.
For each opinion
i\in[k]
, let
c_{i}
be the number of vertices that hold opinion
i
in the current configuration.
Since the underlying graph is a complete graph,
we treat
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
as a configuration;
thus,
3-Majority
(and
Voter
) is a sequence of random configurations
(\mathbf{c}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
We always rearrange the elements of a configuration
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
in descending order, i.e.,
c_{1}\geq\dots\geq c_{k}
.
For two configurations
\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, we say
\mathbf{c}

majorizes

\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, denoted by
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, if for all
i\in[k]
,
\sum_{j=1}^{i}c_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{i}\widetilde{c}_{j}
.
For example,
(4,\,3,\,2,\,1)\succeq(3,\,3,\,2,\,2)
,
(5,\,5,\,0,\,0)\succeq(4,\,4,\,1,\,1)
but
(4,\,3,\,2,\,1)\not\succeq(5,\,3,\,2,\,0)
.
Note that, if
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
then the number of remaining opinions in
\mathbf{c}
is no more than that of
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
.
We construct a coupling of “one-step simulations” of
Voter
and
3-Majority
that preserves the majorization relation.

To state it more formally, let
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
(
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})
) be the random configuration after the one-step update of
Voter
(resp.
3-Majority
) on configuration
\mathbf{c}
(here we also keep the descending order by rearranging).
We show that, for any two configurations
\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
such that
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, there exists a coupling of
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})
and
\mathsf{Voter}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
such that
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})\succeq\mathsf{Voter}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
holds.
From this claim, we can immediately prove
Lemma

2.3
by repeatedly simulating the coupling.
To prove the claim,
we construct (i) a coupling of
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
and
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})
for any
\mathbf{c}
so that
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})\succeq\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
,
and (ii) a coupling of
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
and
\mathsf{Voter}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
for any
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
so that
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})\succeq\mathsf{Voter}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
.
The claim immediately follows from these two couplings as
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})\succeq\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})\succeq\mathsf{Voter}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
for any
\mathbf{c}
.

The most technical part is the coupling of (ii).
The key idea is to decompose the process of generating
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
into
deletion process
and
addition process
.
Specifically, in
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
,
a uniformly random vertex
u
is deleted, and then a new vertex, which shares the same opinion as another uniformly random vertex
v
, is added.
We show that these deletion and addition processes preserve the majorization relation.
To this end, we decompose the configuration vector
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}=(\widetilde{c}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{c}_{k})
into smaller components called the
block structure

and then construct a coupling for each block.
Since both deletion and addition processes change exactly one vertex, it suffices to apply the coupling for one of the blocks.
See
Section

7
for details.

2.3
Small Opinion Case

In the proof of
Theorem

2.2
,
we consider two phases.

•

Phase I: gap emergence.
We show that, starting from any initial configuration with

Phase II: gap amplification.
Once a significant gap arises between the most and second most popular opinions, we show that the dynamics reaches consensus within
O(kn\log n)
steps (
Lemma

5.1
).

The analysis of the gap-emerging phase is the most technical part of this paper
and, in fact, the most challenging part of previous work
[
BCNPT16
,
GL18
]
.
In what follows, we explain why previous techniques for synchronous setting require the condition
k\ll n^{1/3}
and how to overcome this barrier.

The
n^{1/3}
-barrier in the synchronous model.

Before describing the ingredients of our analysis,
we explain why previous works require the condition that
k\ll n^{1/3}
.
Consider the synchronous
3-Majority
dynamics.
Let
A_{t}(i)
denote the number of vertices that hold opinion
i
at the beginning of the
t
-th round, and let
\alpha_{t}(i)=\frac{A_{t}(i)}{n}
.
We treat
\alpha_{t}=(\alpha_{t}(i))_{i\in[k]}
as a
k
-dimensional vector in
[0,1]^{k}
.
For any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, it is not hard to see that

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}[\alpha_{t}(i)]=\alpha_{t-1}(i)\left(1+\alpha_{t-1}(i)-\lVert\alpha_{t-1}\rVert^{2}\right)

(1)

where

\E_{t-1}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation conditioned on the opinion configuration at round
t-1

and

\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the
\ell^{2}
-norm (cf.
[
BCNPST17
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Suppose we have a configuration which is still fairly balanced but
the differences in the support of various opinions start emerging.
More precisely, we start with a configuration
so that
\alpha_{0}(j)=\Theta(1/k)
for all
j\in[k]

and some opinion
i\in[k]
satisfies
\alpha_{0}(i)=\lVert\alpha_{0}\rVert^{2}+\Theta(1/k)
.
Fix such an opinion
i
.
Then,
1
, we have

\E_{0}[\alpha_{1}(i)]=\alpha_{0}(i)\left(1+\Theta\left(1/k\right)\right)=\alpha_{0}(i)+\Theta\left(1/k^{2}\right)
.
In other words,
\alpha_{1}(i)
has an additive drift of
\Theta\left(1/k^{2}\right)
in expectation.
In each round, the
n
vertices simultaneously update their opinions using their coin flips.
Therefore, the random variable
\alpha_{1}(i)
can be written as the sum of
n
independent random variables.
By the central limit theorem, we have
\alpha_{1}(i)\approx\E[\alpha_{1}(i)]\pm O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{0}(i)}{n}}\right)\approx\alpha_{0}(i)+\Theta\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)\pm O\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{kn}}\right)
.
To mitigate the influence of the variance term in the one-step transition due to the central limit theorem, it is necessary to satisfy
\frac{1}{k^{2}}\gg\sqrt{\frac{1}{kn}}
, or equivalently,
k\ll n^{1/3}
.

Tracking one-step change of
\alpha_{t}(i)
is essential in the proof of previous works
[
BCNPT16
,
BCNPST17
,
GL18
]
.
In
[
GL18
]
, the authors considered three classes of opinions:
An opinion is strong at round
t
if
\alpha_{t}(i)\geq\frac{\left\|\alpha_{t}\right\|_{\infty}}{5}
,
weak if
\alpha_{t}(i)<\frac{\left\|\alpha_{t}\right\|_{\infty}}{5}
,
and super-weak if
\alpha_{t}(i)\leq\frac{1}{10k}
.
Here,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
denotes the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm.
Their proof crucially relies on the
“weak remain weak” property: Once an opinion becomes weak (super-weak), then the opinion remains weak (resp. super-weak) with high probability in the next round.
Since this property refers to the concentration of the one-step change of
\alpha_{t}(i)
, for
k\gg n^{1/3}
, their analysis cannot move beyond “fairly balanced” configurations.

2.3.1
Our Tool: Drift Analysis via the Freedman Inequality

Drift Analysis.

Unlike synchronous models, we cannot ensure the one-step concentration of
\alpha_{t}(i)
as in the asynchronous model since only one vertex updates its opinion.
However, we can prove a multi-step concentration of
\alpha_{t}(i)
by exploiting martingale concentration.
Such an argument is known as
drift analysis
and is a key component in previous works on consensus dynamics in the asynchronous or population protocol model of relevant dynamics
[
BCGHKR23
,
AABBHKL23
]
.

A prototype argument of drift analysis
[
Köt16
]
goes as follows:
Consider a sequence of nonnegative random variables
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that satisfy
\E_{t-1}[X_{t}]\geq X_{t-1}+\varepsilon
(i.e.,
X_{t}
has an additive drift).
Then
Y_{t}:=X_{t}-\varepsilon t
forms a submartingale and we can apply the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality to ensure the linear growth of
Y_{t}
.
Similarly, if
(X^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies
\E_{t-1}[X^{\prime}_{t}]\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})X^{\prime}_{t-1}
(i.e.,
X^{\prime}_{t}
has a multiplicative drift), then
Y^{\prime}_{t}:=\frac{X^{\prime}_{t}}{(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})^{t}}
is a submartingale and we can repeat the same argument to ensure the exponential growth of
Y^{\prime}_{t}
.

The naive approach does not work.

If we try to apply this drift argument to asynchronous
3-Majority
, we obtain the following multi-step concentration of
\alpha_{t}(i)
:
If
\alpha_{t-1}
is an almost balanced configuration (i.e.,
\alpha_{t-1}(i)=\Theta(1/k)
for all
i\in[k]
) and if
\alpha_{t-1}(i)=\lVert\alpha_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+\Theta(1/k)
,
the normalized population
\alpha_{t}(i)
has an additive drift as

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}[\alpha_{t}(i)]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t-1}(i)\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{t-1}(i)-\lVert\alpha_{t-1}\rVert^{2}}{n}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t-1}(i)+\Omega\left(\frac{1}{nk^{2}}\right).

Thus, the sequence
\left(\alpha_{t}(i)-\frac{ct}{nk^{2}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a submartingale for some small constant
c>0
unless the configuration is almost balanced.
In the asynchronous model, in one step at most one vertex updates its opinion.
Therefore,
\lvert\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{t-1}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{n}
.
By the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality for submartingales, we obtain a lower tail that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\alpha_{t}(i)-\frac{ct}{nk^{2}}\leq\alpha_{0}(i)-\lambda\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2t}\right).

(3)

For example, suppose we are trying to show that
\alpha_{t}(i)\geq\alpha_{0}(i)
with high probability for
t\approx kn
.
If we set
t=kn
and
\lambda=\frac{ct}{nk^{2}}=\frac{c}{k}
, the upper tail is
\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{n}{k^{3}}\right)\right)
; which again requires
k\ll n^{1/3}
.

Our solution: The Freedman inequality.

The argument in the previous paragraph does not work for our setting of
k\approx n^{1/2}
since the tail bound from the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality is not sharp enough.
By exploiting the variance of the martingale difference, we can obtain a sharper bound.
Such a bound, namely, the Freedman inequality
[
Fre75
]
, can be seen as a Bernstein type bound for martingales.
Roughly speaking, it shows that for any martingale
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\lvert X_{t}-X_{t-1}\rvert
is small enough and
\E_{t-1}[(X_{t}-X_{t-1})^{2}]\leq S
, then we have
X_{t}=X_{0}\pm O(\sqrt{ST\log n})
for all
t=0,\dots,T
with high probability.
Recall that the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality yields that, if
\left|X_{t}-X_{t-1}\right|\leq D
,
then
X_{t}=X_{0}\pm O\left(\sqrt{D^{2}T\log n}\right)
with high probability.
Thus, we can obtain a sharper tail bound than the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality from the Freedman inequality if
S\ll D^{2}
.
For
X_{t}=\alpha_{t}(i)
, we have
S=\E_{t-1}\left[\left(\alpha_{t}(i)-\alpha_{t-1}(i)\right)^{2}\right]\leq n^{-2}\cdot\Pr_{t-1}[\alpha_{t}(i)\neq\alpha_{t-1}(i)]=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}k}\right)
if
\alpha_{t-1}(i)=O(1/k)
.
This improves the tail bound (
3
) in the previous subsection by a factor of
k
in the exponent.

2.3.2
Overview of Gap Emerging Phase

Weak and strong opinions.

We consider two classes of opinions:
An opinion
i\in[k]
is
strong
at step
t
if

\alpha_{t}(i)\geq\frac{7}{8}\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}
,
and is
weak
at step
t
if

\alpha_{t}(i)\leq\frac{3}{4}\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}
.
Note that there always exists a strong opinion since
\max_{i\in[k]}\left\{\alpha_{t}(i)\right\}\geq\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}
.
We also note that being non-weak just means
\alpha_{t}(i)\geq\frac{3}{4}\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}
(not necessarily being strong).

Suppose that the initial configuration is balanced (i.e.,
\alpha_{0}(1)=\dots=\alpha_{0}(k)=1/k=\lVert\alpha_{0}\rVert^{2}
).
Then, all opinions are initially strong.
Our key technical lemma is the
unique strong opinion lemma
,
which claims that after
O(kn\log n)
steps,
there remains only one strong opinion with high probability.
To state it more formally, let
I_{t}\in[k]
be the opinion such that
\alpha_{t}(I_{t})=\max_{i\in[k]}\left\{\alpha_{t}(i)\right\}
.
Then, the unique strong opinion lemma claims that
\max_{i\neq I_{T}}\left\{\alpha_{T}(i)\right\}\leq\frac{7}{8}\lVert\alpha_{T}\rVert^{2}
with high probability for some
T=O(kn\log n)
.

The proof of the unique strong opinion lemma consists of two parts:

1.

First, we prove that once an opinion
i\in[k]
becomes weak, then
i
is very unlikely to become strong in the future (weak cannot become strong).

2.

Second, we prove that, for
any two non-weak distinct opinions

i,j\in[k]
, either
i
or
j
becomes weak within
O(kn\log n)
steps with probability
1-O(n^{-2})
.

By the union bound over
i,j\in[k]
, we have that for any pair of strong opinions, either
i
or
j
becomes weak within
O(kn\log n)
steps with probability
1-O(k^{2}n^{-2})=1-O(n^{-1})
.
This proves the claim above.

In what follows, we present our approach to the proof of
Items

1
and
2
.

Proof outline of
Item

1
(
Section

4.1
).

This statement can be seen as a relaxation of the “weak remains weak” property of
[
GL18
]
.
The crucial difference is that we allow a gap between the population of weak and strong opinions.
We show that if
\alpha_{0}(i)\leq\frac{3}{4}\lVert\alpha_{0}\rVert^{2}
, then
\alpha_{t}(i)<\frac{7}{8}\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}
for all
t=1,2,\dots,n^{10}

with high probability.
The proof relies on the martingale-based argument for the Gambler’s ruin problem (see, e.g.,
[
Dur19
, Theorem 4.8.9]
).
To state it more formally, fix a weak opinion
i

and let
R_{t}=\frac{\alpha_{t}(i)}{\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}}
.
Note that
R_{0}\leq\frac{3}{4}
since the opinion
i
is weak.
By calculation, we can show that
\E_{t-1}[R_{t}]\leq R_{t-1}-\Omega\left\lparen\frac{1}{kn}\right\rparen
.
Let
\tau=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon R_{t}\not\in\left[\frac{5}{8},\frac{7}{8}\right]\right\}
.
Then,
(R_{t})_{0\leq t<\tau}
can be seen as an asymmetric random walk over the interval
\left[\frac{5}{8},\frac{7}{8}\right]
starting from
R_{0}\leq\frac{6}{8}
.
We can show that, for some
\phi=\omega(\log n)
, the sequence
(\exp(\phi R_{t\land\tau}))_{t\geq 0}
is a supermartingale; where
a\land b=\min\{a,b\}
.
By the optimal stopping theorem (
Theorem

A.3
), we have
\E[\exp(\phi R_{\tau})]\leq\exp(\phi R_{0})
.
This gives
\Pr\left[R_{\tau}\geq\frac{7}{8}\right]=n^{-\omega(1)}
.

Proof outline of
Item

2
(
Section

4.2
).

We shall look at the bias of two opinions.
Fix two distinct non-weak opinions
i,j\in[k]
at step
0
and let
\delta_{t}=\lvert\alpha_{t}(i)-\alpha_{t}(j)\rvert

and

\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}
be the first time that either
i
or
j
becomes weak, that is,

\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon\min\left\{\alpha_{t}(i),\alpha_{t}(j)\right\}\leq\frac{3}{4}\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}\right\}.

By calculation, if
t\leq\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}
, from
2
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}[\delta_{t}]\geq\left\lvert\E_{t-1}[\alpha_{t}(i)-\alpha_{t}(j)]\right\rvert=\delta_{t-1}\left\lparen 1+\frac{\alpha_{t-1}(i)+\alpha_{t-1}(j)-\lVert\alpha_{t-1}\rVert^{2}}{n}\right\rparen\geq\left\lparen 1+\frac{1}{2kn}\right\rparen\delta_{t-1}.

In other words, the bias
\delta_{t}
is multiplied by at least
\left\lparen 1+\frac{1}{2kn}\right\rparen
in expectation for any
t\leq\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}
.
Using the Freedman inequality with bounding the second moment of the one-step difference
\E_{t-1}[(\delta_{t}-\delta_{t-1})^{2}]
,
we can show that either
\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}\leq t
or otherwise
\delta_{t}\approx\left\lparen 1+\frac{1}{kn}\right\rparen^{t}\delta_{0}
with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(n\delta_{0}^{2}))
.
If
\delta_{0}\geq\sqrt{\log n/n}
, then this tail bound is strong enough.

To ensure that
\delta_{T}\geq\sqrt{\log n/n}
for some
T=O(kn\log n)
,
we exploit an additive drift of
\delta_{t}
.
By calculation, we can see that

\Var_{t-1}\left[\delta_{t}\right]\geq\frac{\alpha_{t-1}(i)+\alpha_{t-1}(j)-5\delta_{t-1}^{2}}{n^{2}}.

If
\delta_{t}
is small enough and
t\leq\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}
, then
\delta_{t}^{2}
admits an additive drift of
\Omega\left\lparen\frac{1}{n^{2}k}\right\rparen
.
Then, from the optimal stopping theorem, we can show that
\delta_{t+nk}\geq\delta_{t}+\Omega\left\lparen\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right\rparen
or otherwise
\tau^{\mathrm{weak}}\leq t+nk
with constant probability.
Using drift analysis result of
[
DGMSS11
]
, we show that
\delta_{t}\approx\sqrt{\log n/n}
with high probability for some
t=O(nk\log n)
.

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
denote the set of non-negative integers.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[n]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For a sequence of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

\E_{t}[\cdot]

\E\left[\cdot\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]
, where
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the natural filtration associated with
(X_{j})_{0\leq j\leq t}
.
When it is clear from the context, we will omit the term
“with probability
1
”.
For convenience, we sometimes use the notation
a\land b=\min\{a,b\}
.
For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
and
p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}

\lVert x\rVert_{p}=\left\lparen\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}^{p}\right\rparen^{1/p}

\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{v\in V}\{x_{v}\}
.
In particular, we use the shorthand notation
\lVert x\rVert=\lVert x\rVert_{2}

\ell^{2}

We use the standard asymptotic notation,
O(\cdot),o(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot)
, etc.

We present the formal definition of the asynchronous
3-Majority
as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
be parameters such that
1\leq k\leq n
and
V=[n]
.
The
asynchronous 3-Majority process over
k
opinions on
V

is the discrete-time Markov chain
(\sigma_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
over the state space
[k]^{V}
, where
\sigma_{t}
(for
t\geq 1
) is
obtained from
\sigma_{t-1}
by the following procedure:

1.

Select uniformly random
v,w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in V
, independently and with replacement.

2.

Define
\sigma_{t}\in[k]^{V}
by

\sigma_{t}(u)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{t-1}(u)&\text{if $u\neq v$},\\
\sigma_{t-1}(w_{1})&\text{if $u=v$ and $\sigma_{t-1}(w_{1})=\sigma_{t-1}(w_{2})$},\\
\sigma_{t-1}(w_{3})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For simplicity, we shortly call such process
(\sigma_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

asynchronous 3-Majority process
.
The
consensus time

\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}
is the stopping time defined by

\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon\text{for some $i\in[k]$ and all $v\in V$, $\sigma_{t}(v)=i$}\right\}.

The
fractional population
is the sequence of random vectors
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
where each
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]^{k}
is defined by

\alpha_{t}(i)=\frac{1}{|V|}\left\lvert\{v\in V\colon\sigma_{t}(v)=i\}\right\rvert.

For
t\geq 0
and
i,j\in[k]
,
the
bias

\delta_{t}(i,j)
is defined as
\delta_{t}(i,j)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lvert\alpha_{t}(i)-\alpha_{t}(j)\rvert
.
Let
\gamma_{t}=\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert^{2}
.

An element of
V
is called a
vertex

and an element of
[k]
is called an
opinion
.
We always use
n
and
k
to denote the number of vertices and opinions, respectively.
We consider the asymptotic behavior of
\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}
for
n\to\infty
.
Thus,
k=k(n)
is usually a function on
n
.

\alpha_{t}(i)\geq\frac{7}{8}\gamma_{t}
.
Note that there can be an opinion that is neither weak nor strong.
Note also that the most popular opinion is always strong since

.
It can be the case that there are two or more strong opinions.
In this section, we prove that, after

Lemma 4.1

(Unique Strong Opinion Lemma)
.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
I_{t}\in[k]
be an opinion that attains the maximum population, i.e.,
I_{t}\in\argmax_{i}\{\alpha_{t}(i)\}
, where ties are broken to the opinion with the smallest index.
Then, for some

T=O(nk\log n)
,
we have
\Pr\left[\max_{i\neq I_{T}}\alpha_{T}(i)\leq\frac{7}{8}\gamma_{t}\right]\geq 1-O(n^{-8})
.

If
\alpha_{t}(i)=\alpha_{j}(t)=\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert_{\infty}
for some
i\neq j
, then
\max_{i\neq I_{t}}\alpha_{t}(i)=\lVert\alpha_{t}\rVert_{\infty}
.
Therefore,
Lemma

4.1
means that a huge gap emerges between the most and the second most popular opinions at the
T
-th step.

The proof of
Lemma

4.1
consists of two parts.
First, we prove that, once an opinion becomes weak,
then the opinion is very unlikely to become strong in the future.
The proof can be found in
Section

4.1
.

Lemma 4.2

(Weak cannot become strong)
.

For any weak opinion
i\in[k]
at step
0
,
we have
\Pr\left[\forall 0\leq t\leq n^{10},\alpha_{t}(i)\leq\frac{3}{4}\gamma_{t}\right]\geq 1-n^{-\omega(1)}
.

Second, we prove that, for any two distinct opinions
i,j\in[k]
,
either
i
or
j
becomes weak within
\tilde{O}(nk)
steps with high probability.
The proof can be found in
Section

4.2
.

Lemma 4.3

(Pair of Non-Weak Opinions Vanishes)
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
that satisfies the following:
For any distinct non-weak opinions
i,j\in[k]
at step
0
(i.e.,
\alpha_{0}(i),\alpha_{0}(j)>\frac{3}{4}\gamma_{0}
), with probability
1-O(n^{-9})
,
either
i
or
j
becomes weak within
Cnk\log n
steps.

Lemma

4.1
immediately follows from the two results above.

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
assuming

4.2
and
4.3
.

From
Lemma

4.3
, the following event occurs with probability
1-O(n^{-9})

For any pair of non-weak opinions, at least one of them becomes weak within
Cnk\log n
steps, where
C>0
is some universal constant.
Moreover, with probability
1-n^{-\omega(1)}
, any weak opinion cannot become strong during
n^{10}
steps after being weak.
By the union bound, with probability
1-O(k^{2}n^{-9})=1-O(n^{-8})
,
the following event occurs:
For any pair of distinct non-weak opinions
i,j\in[k]
, either
i
or
j
is weak at step

.
In other words,
there exists only one strong opinion at step

.
This completes the proof.

This section is devoted to proving

To this end, we borrow the theory of majorization

Configuration and majorization.

For
i\in[k]
, let
e_{i}
be the unit vector.
That is,

(e_{i})_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

A
configuration
is a vector
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
such that
c_{1}\geq\dots\geq c_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i\in[k]}c_{i}=n
.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
be the set of all possible configurations, i.e.,

\mathcal{C}_{n,k}=\left\{\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\colon\sum_{i\in[k]}c_{i}=n\text{ and }c_{1}\geq\dots\geq c_{k}\geq 0\right\}.

Unlike the normalized population
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]^{k}
,
we always keep the non-increasing order in elements of
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
.
If
n
and
k
are clear from the context, we omit the subscription and simply write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
.

For
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}
, let
\mathbf{c}^{\leq 0}=0
and
\mathbf{c}^{\leq i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=c_{1}+\dots+c_{i}
be the partial sum.
Given a configuration
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
, we think that vertices
1,\dots,c_{1}
hold opinion
1
,
vertices
c_{1}+1,\dots,c_{1}+c_{2}
hold opinion
2
, and so on.
Specifically, the opinion of a vertex
u
is the index
i\in[k]
such that
\mathbf{c}^{\leq i-1}<u\leq\mathbf{c}^{\leq i}
.

Definition 7.1

(Majorization)
.

For two configurations
\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
, we say
\mathbf{c}

majorizes

\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, denoted by
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, if
\mathbf{c}^{\leq i}\geq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

We invoke the following useful characterization of majorization.
Recall that a matrix
P\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
is
doubly stochastic
if every row and column sum up to
1
.

Lemma 7.1

(Theorem B.2 in Section 2 of
[
MOA
]
)
.

For any two configurations
\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
if and only if there exists a doubly stochastic matrix
P\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
such that
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}=\mathbf{c}P
.

Using this lemma, it is easy to see that concatenating preserves majorization ordering.
For two vectors
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, let
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m},v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
be the vector obtained by concatenating
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
as a vector.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\mathbf{c}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{1},k_{1}}
and
\mathbf{c}_{2},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{2},k_{2}}
be configurations such that
\mathbf{c}_{i}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
Let
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{1}+n_{2},k_{1}+k_{2}}
(and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{1}+n_{2},k_{1}+k_{2}}
) be the configuration obtained by concatenating
\mathbf{c}_{1}
and
\mathbf{c}_{2}
(resp.
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}
) as a vector and then rearranging elements in descending order.
Then, we have
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
.

Proof.

Let
[\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
be the vector obtained by concatenating
\mathbf{c}_{1}
and
\mathbf{c}_{2}
(without rearranging).
Similarly, define
[\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}]
as the concatenation of
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}
.
From
Lemma

7.1
, there are two doubly stochastic matrices
P_{1},P_{2}
such that
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=\mathbf{c}_{i}P_{i}
.
Let
P\in[0,1]^{(n_{1}+n_{2})\times(n_{1}+n_{2})}
be the doubly stochastic matrix defined by

P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{1}&O\\
O&P_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, we have
[\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}]=[\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}]P
.
Since
\mathbf{c}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
are obtained by rearranging
[\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}]
and
[\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}]
, respectively,
we can write
\mathbf{c}=[\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}]\,\Pi
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}]\,\widetilde{\Pi}
for some permutation matrices
\Pi,\widetilde{\Pi}
.
Therefore, we have

\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2}]\,\widetilde{\Pi}=[\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}]\,P\,\widetilde{\Pi}=\mathbf{c}\Pi^{-1}P\,\widetilde{\Pi}.

Since
\Pi^{-1}\,P\,\widetilde{\Pi}
is doubly stochastic, from
Lemma

7.1
, we obtain the claim.
∎

Lemma 7.3

\Pi

-th element.
Then, we have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{c}P)_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}c_{\ell}&\text{if }\ell\not\in\{i,j\},\\

\lambda c_{j}+(1-\lambda)c_{i}&\text{if }\ell=j\end{cases}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{c}-e_{i}+e_{j})_{\ell}.

Voter and 3-Majority.

For a configuration
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
and unit vectors
e_{i}
, we define
\mathbf{c}-e_{i}
(and
\mathbf{c}+e_{i}
) as the configuration
obtained by computing
\mathbf{c}-e_{i}
(resp.
\mathbf{c}+e_{i}
) as a vector and then rearranging the components in descending order.
We promise that
c_{i}>0
and
c_{j}<n
whenever we consider
\mathbf{c}-e_{i}
or
\mathbf{c}+e_{j}
.

For a configuration
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in\mathcal{C}
, let
\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{c}}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in[0,1]^{k}
be the relative population sizes defined as
p_{i}=\frac{c_{i}}{n}
.
We omit the subscript
\mathbf{c}
and simply write
\mathbf{p}
if
\mathbf{c}
is clear from the context.
Note that
\mathbf{p}
specifies a distribution over
[k]
as
\sum_{i\in[k]}p_{i}=1
and
p_{i}\geq 0
.
By
i\sim\mathbf{p}
we mean that an opinion
i\in[k]
is chosen according to the distribution
\mathbf{p}
.

For a configuration
\mathbf{c}
, let
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
be a
\mathcal{C}
-valued random variable obtained by simulating one step of the asynchronous Pull Voting on the configuration
\mathbf{c}
.
That is,
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
is the random variable obtained by choosing
i,j\sim\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{c}}
and then
outputting
\mathbf{c}-e_{i}+e_{j}
, an event of probability
(c_{i}/n)(c_{j}/n)
.
In this case, we call
i
a
loosing opinion
and
j
a
gaining opinion
.
Note that the components of
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
are rearranged in descending order.
The same value of
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
can arise in several ways.
For example, it can be the case that
\mathbf{c}-e_{1}+e_{3}=\mathbf{c}-e_{2}+e_{4}
, which occurs if
c_{1}=c_{2}
and
c_{3}=c_{4}
.

Remark 1
.

Generally, for any fixed
i,j\in[k]
, we have

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{c}-e_{i}+e_{j}\right]=\frac{c_{i}k_{i}}{n}\cdot\frac{c_{j}k_{j}}{n},

where
k_{i}
denotes the number of opinions of size equal to
c_{i}
(in the configuration
\mathbf{c}
).
Therefore, to sample the distribution
\mathsf{Voter}(\mathbf{c})
for given
\mathbf{c}
, it suffices to determine the
sizes
of loosing opinion
i
and gaining opinion
j
.
It does not matter specifically which opinion with the selected size is chosen.

Similarly, let
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})
be the random variable obtained by simulating one step of the 3-Majority starting from
\mathbf{c}
.
To state it more formally,
for
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
, let
f\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
be a function defined by
f(\mathbf{c})=(f_{1}(\mathbf{c}),\dots,f_{k}(\mathbf{c}))
, where

\displaystyle f_{i}(\mathbf{c})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=c_{i}\left\lparen 1+\frac{c_{i}}{n}-\frac{\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert^{2}}{n^{2}}\right\rparen=np_{i}\left\lparen 1+p_{i}-\lVert\mathbf{p}\rVert^{2}\right\rparen

(20)

and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the
\ell^{2}
norm (cf.
Item

1
of
Lemma

3.1
).
It is not hard to see that
f(\mathbf{c})\in\mathcal{C}
if
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Then,
\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})
is defined as a
\mathcal{C}
-valued random variable obtained by
choosing
i\sim\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{c}},j\sim\frac{f(\mathbf{c})}{n}
and then outputting
\mathbf{c}-e_{i}+e_{j}
.
Note that

\Pr\left[\mathsf{3Maj}(\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{c}-e_{i}+e_{j}\right]=\frac{k_{i}c_{i}}{n}\cdot\frac{k_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{c})}{n}
, where
k_{i}
denotes the number of opinions of size equal to
c_{i}
.

Lemma 7.4

[

Let

be the function defined in

Then, for any configuration
\mathbf{c}

Stochastic majorization.

Let
X,Y
be
\mathcal{C}
-valued random variables.
We say that

stochastically majorizes

, denoted by

, if
there exists a coupling
D
of
X
and
Y
such that
X\preceq Y
with probability
1
over
D
.
It is known that this relation is transitive.

Lemma 7.5
.

If
X\leq_{\mathrm{st}}Y
and
Y\leq_{\mathrm{st}}Z
, then
X\leq_{\mathrm{st}}Z
.

For completeness, we include a proof here.

Proof.

Let
D_{XY}
and
D_{YZ}
be the couplings that certify

and
Y\leq_{\mathrm{st}}Z
, respectively.
Consider the following coupling of
X
and
Z
:
Sample

and then
(x,y)\sim D_{XY}|_{Y=y}
, and
(y,z)\sim D_{YZ}|_{Y=y}
. Output

.
Since
\Pr_{(x,y)\sim D_{XY}}\left[x\preceq y\right]=1

\Pr_{(y,z)\sim D_{YZ}}\left[y\preceq z\right]=1

This proves the claim.
∎

Proof.

Consider the following coupling:

1.

2.

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}c_{\ell}\geq X>\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j^{\prime}-1}f(c_{\ell})\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j^{\prime}-1}c_{\ell}

To prove

If

Let

The most technical part is to show that both

Lemma 7.9
.

For any configurations

\mathsf{Del}(\mathbf{c})\geq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{Del}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})

.

For any configurations
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}

\mathsf{Add}(\mathbf{c})\geq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{Add}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})

Proof of
Lemma

7.8
using

7.9
and
7.10

From

7.9
and
7.10

To see this, we can couple

\mathsf{Voter}^{\prime}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})

\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}=\mathsf{Del}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})

\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime\prime}=\mathsf{Add}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime})

Then, we have

Using this coupling,

\mathbf{c}
and

Block structure.

To prove
Lemmas

7.10
and
7.9
, we introduce the notion of
block structure
.
Given configurations
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, we can arrange them into a
2\times k
matrix
M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
as

\displaystyle M=M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}&\dots&c_{k}\\
\widetilde{c}_{1}&\widetilde{c}_{2}&\dots&\widetilde{c}_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
(S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{\ell})
be a partition of the columns of
M
into sub-matrices
S_{i}
. We call these
S_{i}

minimal blocks
, if each
S
has the property that the sum of the first and second rows of
S
are equal and
S
contains no smaller sub-block with this property. For example let

S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{i}\\
\widetilde{c}_{1}&\widetilde{c}_{2}&\cdots&\widetilde{c}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Here
S_{1}
is a block of length
i>1
,
where
\mathbf{c}^{\leq 1}>\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq 1},\mathbf{c}^{\leq 2}>\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq 2},...,\mathbf{c}^{\leq i-1}>\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq i-1}
but
\mathbf{c}^{\leq i}=\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq i}
so that both rows add up to the same number.
That is to say
c_{1}>\widetilde{c}_{1}
,
c_{1}+c_{2}>\widetilde{c}_{1}+\widetilde{c}_{2}
etc., but
c_{1}+\cdots c_{i}=\widetilde{c}_{1}+\cdots\widetilde{c}_{i}
.

As an example take
\mathbf{c}=(5,4,3,2,2,0)
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}=(4,4,4,2,1,1)
.
Using
|
as a block delimiter, the block structure is

\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}5&4&3\\
4&4&4\end{array}\right|\left|\begin{array}[]{c}2\\
2\end{array}\right|\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}2&0\\
1&1\end{array}\right|.

The partial sum structure of the first block is
(5>4,9>8,12=12)
, and the last is
(2>1,2=2)
.

An important observation is that, for any vertex
u\in V
,
the opinion
i
of
u
at configuration
\mathbf{c}

and the opinion
j
of
u
at configuration
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}

belong to the same block.

Proof of
Lemma

7.9
.

Let
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
be the initial configurations.
We first prove
Lemma

7.9
in the special case that

consists of a single minimal block.
Then, we reduce the general multiple block case to the single block case.

Case I. Single block case.

Suppose the matrix
M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
consists of a single minimal block.
That is,
\mathbf{c}^{\leq i}>\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq i}
for all

and
\mathbf{c}^{\leq k}=\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq k}=n
.
Consider the following coupling:

1.

Choose a uniformly random vertex

and let

be the opinions of
u
in the configuration

2.

Output

Therefore,
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
is equal (as a vector) to

For any

and
u\in V
are chosen uniformly at random with replacement.
If there is a particle on
v
, then the particle moves to
u
.
If two or more particles are on the same vertex, they coalesce into a cluster, which moves as a single particle.

For any
C\subseteq V
and
u\in V
, the following relation between
Voter
and CRW is well known (Section 14.3 of
[
AF
]
):

\displaystyle\Pr\left\lparen\textrm{In {Voter}, at time $t$, all vertex $v\in C$ have the same opinion initially held by $u$}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\Pr\left\lparen\textrm{In {CRW}, all particles initially on $C$ have coalesced before $t$ and their cluster is on $u$ at $t$}\right\rparen.

Let
\tau^{\mathrm{voter}}_{\kappa}
denote the time taken for the asynchronous
Voter
to reach a configuration in which the number of remaining opinions is at most
\kappa
.
Similarly,
let
\tau_{\kappa}^{\mathrm{crw}}
denote the time taken for CRW to reach a configuration where there are
\kappa
clusters.
From the above relation, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(\tau^{\mathrm{voter}}_{\kappa}\leq t)=\Pr(\tau_{\kappa}^{\mathrm{crw}}\leq t)

for any
\kappa\in[n]
and
t
.
Furthermore, since

\tau_{i-1}^{\mathrm{crw}}-\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{crw}}
follows the geometric distribution with success probability
\frac{(i-1)i}{n^{2}}
(Section 14.3.3 of
[
AF
]
),
we have

\displaystyle\E[\tau_{\kappa}^{\mathrm{crw}}]=\sum_{i=\kappa+1}^{n}\E[\tau_{i-1}^{\mathrm{crw}}-\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{crw}}]=\sum_{i=\kappa+1}^{n}\frac{n^{2}}{(i-1)i}=n^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}-\frac{1}{n}\right)\leq\frac{n^{2}}{\kappa},

i.e.,
\Pr(\tau_{\kappa}^{\mathrm{crw}}\geq\mathrm{e}n^{2}/\kappa)\leq 1/\mathrm{e}
from the Markov inequality.
Combining all, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\tau^{\mathrm{voter}}_{\kappa}\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}n^{2}}{\kappa}h\log n\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\tau_{\kappa}^{\mathrm{crw}}\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}n^{2}}{\kappa}h\log n\right]\leq\frac{1}{n^{h}}

for any positive integer
h
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove
Lemmas

2.3
and
2.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{c}
be the initial configuration and
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{pull}}_{t},\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{maj}}_{t}
be the configuration of the asynchronous
Voter
and
3-Majority
at the
t
-th time step.
By applying
Lemma

7.6
for
t=1,2,\dots
, we can couple
(\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{maj}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{pull}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{pull}}_{t}\succeq\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{maj}}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
If
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{pull}}_{t}
contains at most
\kappa
remaining opinions, then so does
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{maj}}_{t}
since
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{maj}}_{t}\succeq\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{pull}}_{t}
.
This proves the claim.
∎

Proof of

.

This directly follows from

and

.

Appendix A
Tools

Recall that a sequence of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is
supermartingale
if
\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}\right]\leq X_{t-1}

and
is

submartingale
if
\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}\right]\geq X_{t-1}
.

Theorem A.1

(Optimal stopping theorem. See e.g. Theorem 4.8.5 of
[
Dur19
]
)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a submartingale (resp. supermartingale) such that
\E_{t-1}\left[|X_{t}-X_{t-1}|\right]<\infty
.
and
\tau
be a stopping time such that
\E[\tau]<\infty
.
Then,
\E[X_{\tau}]\geq\E[X_{0}]
(resp.
\E[X_{\tau}]\leq\E[X_{0}]
).

Theorem A.2

(Freedman’s inequality. See Theorem 4.1 of
[
Fre75
]
)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a real-valued supermartingale
such that
X_{t}-X_{t-1}\leq D
for all
t\geq 1
.
Then, for any
\lambda,W>0
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[X_{T}-X_{0}\geq\lambda\textrm{ and }\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{t-1}\left[(X_{t}-X_{t-1})^{2}\right]\leq W\textrm{ for some $T\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2W+2D\lambda}\right).

In the following,
we show that if a sequence of real-valued random variables
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

has a negative drift, small variance, and bounded difference,
then
(X_{t})
is likely to decrease to
X_{t}\leq L
before growing up to
X_{t}\geq U
.
Here, we are interested in the value of
X_{\tau}
, rather than the bound of
\tau
.

Theorem A.3

(Gambler’s Ruin under Negative Drift)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of real-valued random variables.
Let
0\leq L\leq U
be parameters.
Let
\tau=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\leq L\textrm{ or }X_{t}\geq U\}
be the stopping time and suppose
\E[\tau]<\infty
.
Let
S,D,\theta>0
be constants that do not depend on
t
and suppose that the following condition holds for all
t\geq 1
:

(i)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\left\lparen\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}-X_{t-1}\right]+\theta\right\rparen\leq 0
,

(ii)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[(X_{t}-X_{t-1})^{2}\right]\leq S
,

(iii)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}|X_{t}-X_{t-1}|\leq D
.

Let
\phi=\frac{6\theta}{3S+2D\theta}
. Then, for any
L<X_{0}<U
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U]\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\phi X_{0}}-\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)}}{\mathrm{e}^{\phi U}-\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)}}.

Proof.

Let
Y_{t}=X_{\tau\wedge t}
for convenience.
Then,
Y_{t}-Y_{t-1}=\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}(X_{t}-X_{t-1})
.
Hence, we have
|Y_{t}-Y_{t-1}|\leq D
and
\E_{t-1}[Y_{t}-Y_{t-1}]=\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}(\E_{t-1}[X_{t}]-X_{t-1})\leq-\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\theta
.
Since
0\leq\phi<3/D
,
from the well-known fact
\mathrm{e}^{z}\leq 1+z+\frac{z^{2}/2}{1-|z|/3}
for any
|z|<3
(see e.g., p.39 of
[
Ver18
]
)
with
z=\phi\cdot(Y_{t}-Y_{t-1})
,
we have

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\phi\cdot(Y_{t}-Y_{t-1})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\phi\E_{t-1}[Y_{t}-Y_{t-1}]+\frac{\phi^{2}/2}{1-\phi D/3}\E_{t-1}[(Y_{t}-Y_{t-1})^{2}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\phi\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\theta+\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\frac{\phi^{2}S/2}{1-\phi D/3}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\phi\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\theta+\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\phi\frac{3\theta S}{3S+2D\theta}\frac{3S+2D\theta}{3S}\right)

\displaystyle=1,

i.e.,
Z_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{\phi Y_{t}}=\mathrm{e}^{\phi X_{t\wedge\tau}}
is a supermartingale.
Furthermore, we have
\lvert Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}\rvert<\infty
.
Hence, we can apply
Theorem

A.1
and
\E\left[Z_{\tau}\right]\leq\E[Z_{0}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\phi X_{0}}
holds.
Then, from

\displaystyle\E\left[Z_{\tau}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[Z_{\tau}\mid X_{\tau}\geq U\right]\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U]+\E\left[Z_{\tau}\mid X_{\tau}\leq L\right]\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq L]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\mathrm{e}^{\phi X_{\tau}}\mid X_{\tau}\geq U\right]\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U]+\E\left[\mathrm{e}^{\phi X_{\tau}}\mid X_{\tau}\leq L\right](1-\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U])

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{\phi U}\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U]+\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)}(1-\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U])

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{e}^{\phi U}-\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)})\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U]+\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)},

we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr[X_{\tau}\geq U]\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\phi X_{0}}-\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)}}{\mathrm{e}^{\phi U}-\mathrm{e}^{\phi(L-D)}}.

∎

Theorem A.4
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of random variables such that
\lvert X_{t}\rvert<\infty
for every
t\geq 0
and
\tau
be a stopping time
such that
\E[\tau]<\infty
.
Suppose there exists a constant
S>0
such that, for all
t\geq 1
,

(i)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\left(\Var_{t-1}[X_{t}]-S\right)\geq 0
,

(ii)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\left(\E_{t-1}[X_{t}]^{2}-X_{t-1}^{2}\right)\geq 0
.

Then, we have
\E[\tau]\leq\frac{\E\left[X_{\tau}^{2}\right]-\E\left[X_{0}^{2}\right]}{S}
.

Proof.

Let
Y_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=X_{t}^{2}-St
and
Z_{t}=Y_{t\wedge\tau}
.
Then,
Z_{t}
is a submartingale since

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}\left[Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{t-1}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}(Y_{t}-Y_{t-1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}^{2}-X_{t-1}^{2}-S\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\left(\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}^{2}\right]-\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}\right]^{2}-S+\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}\right]^{2}-X_{t-1}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Furthermore,
\E_{t-1}[Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}]<\infty
.
Hence, applying
Theorem

A.1
yields

\displaystyle\E[X_{\tau}^{2}]-S\E[\tau]=\E[Z_{\tau}]\geq\E[Z_{0}]=\E[X_{0}^{2}].

∎

Lemma A.5

(Multiplicative drift)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of real-valued random variables and
\tau
be a stopping time.
Suppose the following holds for some positive constants
a
,
D
,
S
,
U
and for all
t\geq 1
:

(i)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[X_{t}-aX_{t-1}\right]\geq 0
,

(ii)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}|X_{t}-X_{t-1}|\leq D
,

(iii)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[(X_{t}-X_{t-1})^{2}\right]\leq S
,

(iv)

\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}|X_{t-1}|\leq U
.

Then, for any
\lambda,T\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\bigvee_{t=0}^{T}\left\{X_{t\wedge\tau}\leq a^{t\wedge\tau}(X_{0}-\lambda)\right\}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}/2}{2A\left(S+(a-1)^{2}U^{2}\right)+\lambda B\left(D+|a-1|U\right)}\right)

holds, where
A=\sum_{t=1}^{T}a^{-2t}
and
B=\max\{1,a^{-2T}\}
.

Proof.

Let
Y_{t}=-a^{-t}X_{t}
and
Z_{t}=Y_{t\wedge\tau}
.
First, we observe

\displaystyle Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}=\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}(Y_{t}-Y_{t-1})=a^{-t}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\left(aX_{t-1}-X_{t}\right)

holds for any
t\geq 1
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}\left[Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=a^{-t}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[aX_{t-1}-X_{t}\right]\leq 0

holds, i.e.,
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a supermartingale.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left|Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}\right|

\displaystyle=a^{-t}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\left|X_{t-1}-X_{t}+(a-1)X_{t-1}\right|\leq B(D+|a-1|U)

and

\displaystyle\E_{t-1}\left[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=a^{-2t}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[\left(X_{t-1}-X_{t}+(a-1)X_{t-1}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq a^{-2t}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t-1}\E_{t-1}\left[2\left(X_{t-1}-X_{t}\right)^{2}+2(a-1)^{2}X_{t-1}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2a^{-2t}(S+(a-1)^{2}U^{2}).

(21)

Note that
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
holds for any
x,y
.
For any
T\geq 0
,
21
implies

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{t-1}[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2A(S+(a-1)^{2}U^{2}).

Here,
A=\sum_{t=1}^{T}a^{-2t}
.
Now, we apply
Theorem

A.2
to
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

with
D\leftarrow B(D+|a-1|U)
and
W\leftarrow 2A(S+(a-1)^{2}U^{2})
.
Since
Z_{t}-Z_{0}=X_{0}-a^{-(t\wedge\tau)}X_{t\wedge\tau}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\bigvee_{t=0}^{T}\left\{X_{t\wedge\tau}\leq a^{t\wedge\tau}(X_{0}-\lambda)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\bigvee_{t=0}^{T}\left\{Z_{t}-Z_{0}\geq\lambda\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\bigvee_{t=0}^{T}\left\{Z_{t}-Z_{0}\geq\lambda\textrm{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{t}\E_{t-1}[(Z_{t}-Z_{i-1})^{2}]\leq W\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2W+2Dh}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}/2}{2A(S+(a-1)^{2}U^{2})+\lambda B(D+|a-1|U)}\right)

holds for any
\lambda,T\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma B.1
.

Let
M>0
be a positive constant.
For two random variables
X
and
Y
such that

, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\E\left[\frac{X}{Y}\right]-\frac{\E\left[X\right]}{\E\left[Y\right]}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{M\E[Y]}\left\lparen\frac{\E[X]}{\E[Y]}\Var[Y]+\sqrt{\Var[X]\Var[Y]}\right\rparen.

Proof.

First, we observe for any reals
x,a
and non-zero reals
y,b
that

\displaystyle\frac{x}{y}-\frac{a}{b}-\frac{x-a}{b}+\frac{a(y-b)}{b^{2}}=\frac{a(y-b)^{2}}{yb^{2}}-\frac{(x-a)(y-b)}{yb}

holds.
Setting

and

\displaystyle\left|\E\left[\frac{X}{Y}\right]-\frac{\E\left[X\right]}{\E\left[Y\right]}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\E\left[\frac{X}{Y}-\frac{\E\left[X\right]}{\E\left[Y\right]}-\frac{X-\E[X]}{\E\left[Y\right]}+\frac{\E[X](Y-\E[Y])}{\E[Y]^{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\E\left[\frac{\E[X](Y-\E[Y])^{2}}{Y\E[Y]^{2}}-\frac{(X-\E[X])(Y-\E[Y])}{Y\E[Y]}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\E[X]\E[|Y-\E[Y]|^{2}]}{M\E[Y]^{2}}+\frac{\E[|X-\E[X]||Y-\E[Y]|]}{M\E[Y]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\E[X]\Var[Y]}{M\E[Y]^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{\Var[X]\Var[Y]}}{M\E[Y]}.

∎

In the following, we bound
\E_{t-1}[(R_{t}-R_{t-1})^{2}]
in terms variance.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be non-negative random variables such that
Y\geq M
for a positive constant
M
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\left\lparen\frac{X}{Y}-\frac{\E[X]}{\E[Y]}\right\rparen^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}}\left\lparen\sqrt{\Var[X]}+\frac{\E[X]}{\E[Y]}\sqrt{\Var[Y]}\right\rparen^{2}.

Proof.

Let
x=\E[X]
and
Y=\E[Y]
.
From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we immediately obtain

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\frac{X}{Y}-\frac{x}{y}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\left(\frac{(X-x)y+x(y-Y)}{Yy}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}y^{2}}\left(y^{2}\E\left[(X-x)^{2}\right]+x^{2}\E\left[(y-Y)^{2}\right]+2xy\left|\E\left[(X-x)(y-Y)\right]\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}y^{2}}\left(y^{2}\E\left[(X-x)^{2}\right]+x^{2}\E\left[(y-Y)^{2}\right]+2xy\sqrt{\E\left[(X-x)^{2}\right]\E\left[(y-Y)^{2}\right]}\right).

∎

B.3
Proof of
Lemma

7.10

In this subsection, we prove
Lemma

7.10
.
The proof is the same as the proof of
Lemma

7.10
except for considering addition instead of subtraction.

Proof of
Lemma

7.10
.

Let
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
be the initial configurations.
We first prove
Lemma

7.10
in the special case that
M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
consists of a single minimal block.
Then, we reduce the general multiple block case to the single block case.

Case I. Single block case.

Suppose the matrix
M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
consists of a single minimal block.
Consider the following coupling:

1.

Choose a uniformly random vertex
u\sim V
and let
j,\widetilde{j}
be the opinion of
u
in the configuration
\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
, respectively.

2.

Output
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbf{c}+e_{j}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}+e_{\widetilde{j}}
.

Note that
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
is the configuration obtained by first computing
\mathbf{c}+e_{i}
as a vector and then rearrange the elements in descending order.
Therefore,
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
is equal (as a vector) to
\mathbf{c}+e_{\ell}
,
where
\ell\in[k]
is the leftmost opinion whose size is equal to the size of
u
’s opinion in the configuration
\mathbf{c}
(this preserves the descending order).
Similarly, let
\widetilde{\ell}
be the left-most opinion in
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
so that
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{c}+e_{\widetilde{\ell}}
.

Compare the partial sums of
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}
.
For any
m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{\prime\leq m}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{c}^{\leq m}

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\leq m}+1

\because
M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
consists of a single block

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime\leq m}

\because
Addition increases the partial sum by at most one

and thus
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}
.

Case II. Multiple block case.

Suppose that the matrix
M(\mathbf{c},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
consists of two or more blocks
S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell}
(for
\ell\geq 2
).
Then,
\mathbf{c}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
can be seen as the concatenation of subvectors
\mathbf{c}[S_{1}],\dots,\mathbf{c}[S_{\ell}]
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{1}],\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{\ell}]
, where
\mathbf{c}[S_{b}],\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{b}]
are configurations.
Note that
\mathbf{c}[S_{b}]\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{b}]
since
\mathbf{c}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}
.

Consider the following coupling:

1.

Choose a block index
b\in[\ell]
randomly, where the probability of choosing a specific block
S_{a}
is proportional to the sum of
c_{i}
s belonging to
S_{a}
.

2.

Simulate the coupling of Case I on configurations
\mathbf{c}[S_{b}]
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{b}]
. This yields configurations
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}[S_{b}]
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}[S_{b}]
such that
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}[S_{b}]\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}[S_{b}]
.

3.

Output the concatenations

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left[\mathbf{c}[S_{1}],\dots,\mathbf{c}[S_{b-1}],\mathbf{c}^{\prime}[S_{b}],\mathbf{c}[S_{b+1}],\dots,\mathbf{c}[S_{\ell}]\right],

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{1}],\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{b-1}],\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}[S_{b}],\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{b+1}],\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}[S_{\ell}]\right].

Note that the probability that this coupling changes the
b
-th block
S_{b}
is equal to the probability that a uniformly random vertex
u\sim V
has an opinion in the block
S_{b}
.
Therefore,
(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime})
is a coupling of
\mathsf{Add}(\mathbf{c})
and
\mathsf{Add}(\widetilde{\mathbf{c}})
.
Since each corresponding subvectors satisfy the majorization (i.e.,
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}[S_{a}]\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}[S_{a}]
for all
a\in[\ell]
),
from
Lemma

7.2
, we have
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\succeq\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}
.
∎
