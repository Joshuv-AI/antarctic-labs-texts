Epidemiology of pain, delirium, psychiatric disorders, discomfort and sedation-analgesia management in the intensive care unit: a one-day nationwide study
Authors: Florian Blanchard, Angelina Barage, Belaïd Bouhemad, Camille Miard, Isaure Breteau, Frédéric Martino, Matthieu Biais, Paul-Henri Wicky, Pauline Perez, Bernard Allaouchiche, Christophe Huz, Vincent Susset, Samir Jaber, Vincent Legros, Sigismond Lasocki, Aurore Rodrigues, Marwan Bouras, Charles-Edouard Luyt, Maxime Beilvert, Mouhamed Djahoum Moussa, Clément Devautour, Fanny Vardon, Arthur James, Gerald Chanques, Jean-Michel Constantin, ESPRIT Study group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe administration of sedatives and analgesics in intensive care units (ICUs) has evolved significantly over the past 20 years, shifting from deep to light sedation strategies to minimize adverse effects. Despite this shift, substantial variability persists in sedation-analgesia practices. This study aimed to provide an updated national overview of sedation-analgesia management with a focus on discomfort assessment practices, including pain, delirium, anxiety, thirst, mood, and sleep disorders.MethodsThis was a one-day, multicenter, cross-sectional study conducted in French ICUs. Data were collected from all adult patients hospitalized in the ICU on the study day. A Unit-level survey documented ICU characteristics and sedation-analgesia protocols. Patient-level data included sedation levels, pain scores, and assessments of discomfort conditions. Statistical analyses were performed using descriptive methods and multilevel logistic regression.ResultsAmong 258 French ICUs contacted, 128 units (50%) participated, enrolling 2,063 patients. Most ICUs were university-affiliated (54%) and mixed medical-surgical (58%); 63% had a written protocol for sedation-analgesia. Sedation and pain were assessed in 96% and 91% of ICUs, respectively. Light or no sedation was observed in 84% of patients, while 15% were deeply sedated – 63% of whom were misaligned with usual indications. Pain assessment was performed at rest in 90% of patients and during care in 62%. Pain prevalence increased with lighter sedation levels and during care. Hypnotics were used in 31% of patients, Mainly propofol and midazolam. Discomfort was reported in 44% of patients, mainly anxiety, sleep disorders, and thirst. Written protocols for sedation and analgesia were not associated with sedation depth, drug use, or delirium screening, but were linked to more frequent pain assessment at rest. Multivariable analyses showed that higher SOFA scores were associated with deep or misaligned deep sedation. The presence of a written protocol for sedation and analgesia reduced the risk of unassessed pain but was not associated with deep or misaligned deep sedation.ConclusionThe shift toward lighter sedation has been successfully achieved; however, a broad spectrum of stressful symptoms persists, including pain, anxiety, thirst, and sleep disruption. These findings underscore the need for more effective strategies to optimize pain and overall patient comfort in non-deeply sedated ICU patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05654-7.Keywords: Critical care, Discomfort, Sedation, Analgesia, Delirium

Introduction

The administration of sedatives and analgesics in intensive care units (ICUs) has undergone significant evolution over the past 20 years [1]. Deep sedation is associated with adverse effects, including prolonged ventilation duration, extended length of stay, ICU weakness, delirium, and long-term psychiatric disorders [2]. In light of these unfavorable outcomes, numerous guidelines have aimed to standardize practices, prompting a paradigm shift from deep and prolonged sedation toward lighter and shorter sedation strategies. Deep sedation is now discouraged and should be reserved for specific clinical indications, such as brain injury with intracranial hypertension or severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [3].

Between 2000 and 2015, several practice surveys were conducted in Canada [4], Denmark [5], Spain [6], the United Kingdom [7], and France [2, 8], consistently revealing marked variability in sedation practices across and within countries. In this context, the Guidelines for the Prevention and Management of Pain, Agitation/Sedation, Delirium, Immobility, and Sleep Disruption in Adult Patients in the ICU (PADIS guidelines) advocate the use of written protocols for managing sedation, targeting light to moderate sedation levels [9, 10].

The shift toward lighter or no sedation has brought new challenges, notably increased patient discomfort in the ICU setting [9]. Common sources of discomfort include pain, anxiety, mood disorders, sleep disturbances, thirst, and dyspnea. These discomforts are multifactorial in origin, influenced by environmental issues, pharmacological treatments, and underlying disease severity [11]. Numerous studies have explored ICU patients’ self-reported experiences of discomfort, with each type being well characterized independently [12–17].

Despite the clinical importance of these issues, most existing data derive from declarative surveys of clinical practices, which are prone to reporting bias and often lack granularity regarding individual patient experiences. While the Dolorea study provided valuable insights into discomfort and symptom management, it was limited to mechanically ventilated patients and did not capture the broader ICU population [18]. The aim of this study was to gather recent epidemiological data on the assessment of sedation, pain, psychiatric symptoms and discomfort across a wide ICU population, in order to guide future improvements in patient-centered care in the ICU.

Methods

Study design

This was a one-day, multicenter, observational, cross-sectional study. ICUs were contacted through the Société Française d’Anesthésie et de Réanimation (SFAR) research network. Eligible ICUs included all French ICUs with or without intermediate care units. Units with exclusively intermediate care, step-down care, or pediatric and neonatal ICUs were excluded. Following enrollment, each center completed a survey on their own practices. On the dedicated day, between February 5 and February 18, 2024, all patients present in the ICU were enrolled. If an ICU bed was occupied by two different patients during the 24-h study period, both patients were included. Eligible patients were all adults (aged over 18 years) hospitalized on the study day, with no opposition from the patient or their relatives to participate in the study. Each unit-level survey and patient case report form (CRF) was completed under the supervision of a local co-investigator. The list of co-investigators is provided under the ESPRIT Study Group (see Supplemental File).

Data collection

The unit-level survey gathered information about the ICU characteristics and practice patterns related to prevention and management of agitation/sedation, pain, delirium, mood disorders, sleep disturbances, and thirst. Patient data recorded on an electronic dedicated CRF were extracted from medical charts and included demographic information, sedation levels, pain scores, and assessments of delirium, mood disorders, sleep disturbances, thirst. All data were compiled using a certified data collection service specializing in health data (SKEZIA, https://skezia.io/).

Definitions

Sedation, pain, delirium, thirst, and sleep disturbances were assessed over the 24 h preceding inclusion. Depression and anxiety were evaluated over the entire ICU stay. Given that different sedation and pain scales could be used, the vigilance and pain evaluations were standardized as follows. Sedation level was classified as agitation (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale [RASS] ≥ + 2; Ramsay Sedation Scale [RAMSAY] 1), no sedation or light sedation (RASS + 1 to −2; RAMSAY 2–3), or deep sedation (RASS ≤ −3; RAMSAY 4–6) [19]. For each patient, the local investigator recorded the sedation target set by the care team for the study day and the actual sedation level at the time of assessment. Sedation was considered aligned when the actual level matched the target, oversedated when it was deeper than the target (e.g., actual RASS = − 3 for a prescribed RASS of − 1 to 0), and undersedated when lighter than the target (e.g., actual RASS = 0 for a prescribed RASS of − 3). For patients in deep sedation (RASS ≤ − 3), alignment was further evaluated according to clinical indication: deep sedation was considered aligned if indicated for acute respiratory distress syndrome or intracranial hypertension, and misaligned otherwise. [1, 9, 20–22]. A patient was classified as having pain if they met at least one of the following criteria: a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) > 30 mm, a Numerical Rating Scale (oral: NRS-O; visual: NRS-V) > 3/10, a Verbal Rating Scale (VRS) > 2/5, a positive response to the binary pain assessments (yes/no), a Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS) > 4/12, or a Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT) > 2/8 [18]. Delirium, anxiety, thirst, sleep and mood disorders assessment as well as additional information relative to the CRF are available in the Supplemental Appendix.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the distribution (prevalence) of actual sedation levels observed on the study day across all ICUs, regardless of mechanical ventilation status. Secondary outcomes included the prevalence of sedation levels specifically among mechanically ventilated patients; the prevalence of pain at rest and during care; the prevalence of delirium; and the overall prevalence of discomforts, as well as the prevalence of each individual component (delirium, anxiety, thirst, sleep disturbances, and mood disorders). The usefulness of written protocols for sedation and analgesia was assessed using both bivariate and multivariable analyses, with a particular focus on the occurrence of deep sedation, misaligned deep sedation and the assessment of pain.

Ethics

In accordance with French regulations, the study was approved by the Comité éthique pour la recherche en anesthésie réanimation, France (Institutional Review Board IRB 00010254‐2023–098) [23]. As this was a prospective study utilizing routinely collected and anonymized data, written consent was not required.

Statistics

Descriptive analyses were conducted on the entire cohort. Quantitative variables were expressed as medians (interquartile range [IQR], 25–75%) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (%) and compared using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

An alluvial plot was created to visualize the trajectories across three key domains: sedation level (“Deep sedation” vs. “Light or no sedation”), pain at rest (“Yes,” “No,” “No evaluation”), and pain during care (“Yes,” “No,” “No evaluation”). Each flow represents a group of patients transitioning between these stages, with stream width proportional to the number of individuals. An upset plot was created to visualize the prevalence and overlap of discomfort conditions in ICU patients, including anxiety, sleep disorders, mood disorders, and thirst. The presence or absence of each condition was encoded as a binary variable, and the intersections were sorted in descending order based on their prevalence.

Multivariable analyses were performed to identify factors associated with deep sedation, misaligned deep sedation, and lack of pain assessment at rest. Given the hierarchical structure of the data, with patients nested within centers, multilevel logistic regression models were used to account for clustering at the center level. A random intercept was included for each center to capture unmeasured heterogeneity in sedation practices across institutions. Fixed effects included patient-level covariates (e.g., age, SAPS2, SOFA) and center-levels covariates (presence of a written protocol for sedation and analgesia, type of hospital, type of ICU, patient-to-physician ratio). The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Given that missing data accounted for less than 10%, the analysis was performed on complete cases only. All p-values were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.2 for macOS® (https://www.r-project.org, accessed October 2024). Additional information regarding the statistics is available in the Supplemental Appendix.

Results

Study population

Of the 400 ICUs in France, 258 (65%) were contacted, and 128 (50%) were enrolled in the study (Figure S1, Figure S2, Supplemental file). The characteristics of the participating ICUs are summarized in Table 1. The majority were university-affiliated hospitals (54%) with a mixed medical-surgical patient population (58%). A written protocol for sedation and analgesia was present in 80 out of 128 ICUs (63%). Within these protocols, 89% (71/80) included a therapeutic Management algorithm, 13% (10/80) recommended daily sedation interruption, and 41% (66/80) advocated for increased analgesic administration during patient care. At least one written procedure unrelated to sedation-analgesia was implemented in 121 out of 127 units (95%), as reported in Table 1.

Sedation and Pain

The characteristics of sedation and pain are detailed in Table 3 and Fig. 1. Sedation and pain were systematically assessed in 96% (122/127) of ICUs. Sedation assessment primarily utilized the RASS (107/116, 92%). No sedation or light sedation was observed in 84% of patients, 15% of patients were under deep sedation, and 1% were agitated (Fig. 1). The temporal distribution of sedation levels remained stable until day 7, after which a decrease in deep sedation was observed (Fig. 2, Table S1, Supplemental file). Among the deeply sedated patients, 93/304 (30%) had ARDS, and 21/304 (7%) presented with brain injuries associated with intracranial hypertension. However, 192/304 (63%) were misaligned for deep sedation. Oversedation and undersedation occurred in 5% and 2% of patients, respectively. Hypnotics were administered to 31% of patients, with propofol, midazolam, ketamine, and dexmedetomidine being the most frequently used agents. Among ventilated patients, no sedation or light sedation, deep sedation, and agitation were observed in 62%, 37%, and 1% of patients, respectively (Table S2, Supplemental file).

Delirium

Delirium assessments were predominantly conducted on demand (59%), with systematic evaluations performed in 13% of ICUs (Table 3). Among the patients with a non-normal mental state reported by healthcare providers, 44% underwent evaluation for delirium within the past 24 h, and 58% were suspected to have delirium.

Discomfort

The prevalence and characteristics of discomfort are detailed in Table 3, Fig. 4, and Figure S3 (Supplemental file). Among all ICU patients, 23% were non-evaluable, 33% had no discomfort, and 44% had at least one discomfort (Fig. 4A). Of the patients with at least one discomfort, 49% experienced a single discomfort, 33% had two, 14% had three, and 4% reported four discomforts (Fig. 4A). Figure 4B reports the prevalence of discomfort in all admitted patients using an upset plot. Anxiety was the most frequent, followed by sleep disorders, thirst and mood disorders (Fig. 4B and Table 3).

Usefulness of written protocols for sedation and analgesia

A written protocol for sedation and analgesia was implemented in 62% of ICUs (Table 1). A protocol was more frequently implemented in non-university-affiliated hospitals and mixed medico-surgical ICUs. Patients admitted to ICUs with a written protocol exhibited a higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, increased use of vasopressors, and were more likely to have therapeutic limitations (Table 2). Comparisons between ICUs with and without a written protocol revealed no significant differences in sedation levels, adherence to the prescribed sedation level, or sedative drug use (Table 3). Pain assessment at rest was higher in ICUs with a written protocol with no difference during care (Table 3).

Discussion

This large-scale study provides a comprehensive evaluation of sedation and analgesia practices across 128 French ICUs, encompassing 2,063 critically ill patients evaluated on a given day. Key findings reveal that sedation was systematically assessed in 96% of ICUs, with 84% of patients receiving no or light sedation, while 15% were under deep sedation, of whom 63% were misaligned with usual indications. Pain assessment was systematically conducted in 91% of ICUs; however, it was often evaluated using a behavioral scale at the expense of self-assessment scales and remained frequently under-evaluated, particularly during care procedures, where it was documented in only 62% of patients. Notably, 44% of patients experienced at least one form of discomfort, with anxiety being the most frequent and sleep disturbances being the most frequently reported as detrimental to patient participation in rehabilitation. Despite the implementation of written sedation-analgesia protocols in 63% of ICUs, their presence did not significantly impact sedation depth, sedative drug use, or adherence to prescribed sedation levels. Units with protocols performed pain assessment at rest more frequently, yet the prevalence of reported pain remained unchanged, probably because pain at rest is relatively uncommon and much less frequent than historically reported.

The PAD and PADIS guidelines [9, 24] recommend systematic sedation and analgesia assessment and the use of a sedation-analgesia protocol. The implementation of these protocols has progressively increased over time (29% in 2010 [2], 55% in 2013 [8], and 63% in our study). These protocols are associated with increased clinician assessment. The Dolorea study [18] in 2007 reported sedation assessment in 45% of cases, whereas in our study, nearly all patients (96%) were monitored, predominantly using the RASS scale. The systematic implementation of standardized assessment tools improves sedation management [25] but may reduce the additional benefit of sedation protocols. Multivariable analysis of factors influencing deep sedation and misaligned deep sedation did not find any significant benefit associated with the use of a sedation protocol, suggesting that healthcare professionals’ training allows appropriate drug titration, as previously shown in Australian nurses working without protocols [26]. Sedation strategies have evolved rapidly [9, 10, 24], with a significant increase in the proportion of patients under no or light sedation. Between 2007 (Dolorea study [18]) and our study, this proportion rose from 40 to 62% in ventilated patients and as high as 84% in the whole cohort. Despite ARDS and intracranial hypertension being the only clear indications for deep sedation, 63% of deeply sedated patients still lacked a justified indication. Multivariable analysis revealed that only severity scores were associated with misaligned deep sedation. SOFA, along with plasma lactate levels, has previously been identified as an independent predictor of deep sedation [27]. These findings highlight the persistent belief that severely critically ill patients require deep sedation while awaiting organ failure resolution, although extensive evidences demonstrate the adverse outcomes associated with this approach [28, 29]. This level of compliance with recommended sedation practices may be partly attributable to structural factors in French ICUs, including the availability of trained intensivists, established nurse-to-patient ratios, and systematic use of validated sedation monitoring tools. In contrast, data from low- and middle-income countries show that staff shortages, high nurse workloads, and limited access to monitoring tools are major barriers to the adoption of light sedation strategies [30]. These differences underscore the importance of tailoring sedation strategies to the local context and resources.

The shift toward lighter sedation uncovers a wide range of symptoms. As sedation decreases, the number of patients experiencing pain requiring Management increases, with pain prevalence rising from 0.9% in deeply sedated patients to 4.3%, 15.0%, and 25.0% in those under light sedation, no sedation, and agitation, respectively. Despite being a key indicator of quality care [31], pain remains underassessed in the ICU, especially during procedures, which is the moment of highest risk of experiencing pain. Pain assessment is predominantly performed using behavioral scales rather than self-reporting tools in patients able to communicate, even though this is not appropriate [9, 32, 33]. This can lead to an under-recognition of pain in patients who are able to self-report their pain intensity. In deeply sedated patients, where self-report is not possible, behavioral scales remain the recommended standard despite their limitations. Objective nociception monitoring tools such as the ANI or NOL have shown promising results [34, 35], but their usefulness in the context of deep sedation still needs to be established. These tools may, in the future, represent a potential alternative for pain detection in this specific population.

Among all ICU patients, 44% were described with at least one form of discomfort. In our study, anxiety and sleep disturbances were the most prevalent symptoms. A recent study, focusing on 603 patients self-reporting discomfort, found that thirst and anxiety were the most distressing symptoms in ICU [11]. However, mood disorders were not assessed, and sleep disturbances were evaluated based on tiredness, which May not capture all facets of sleep-related issues and May partly explain the discrepancy with our results. Despite nearly half of ICU patients experiencing discomfort, our results likely underestimate the true burden of these symptoms due to a lack of systematic evaluation. Previous studies, with systematic evaluation, have reported higher prevalence rates for individual discomforts in ICU patients: 66% for sleep disturbances [36], 70% for thirst [37], and 45% for mood disorders [38].

This study has both strengths and limitations. One of its key strengths is its cross-sectional design, providing a comprehensive snapshot of sedation-analgesia Management and patient discomforts in ICU. It is a large-scale, multicentric study that evaluates real-world practices in over 2,000 patients, rather than relying solely on self-reported institutional protocols. Additionally, the short duration of data collection helped minimize missing data. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, there is a potential risk of underestimating patient discomfort due to the lack of systematically standardized assessment methods across centers. Given the study’s one-day cross-sectional design, large sample size, and the objective to maximize ICU participation nationwide, the use of uniform evaluation tools for all sites was not feasible. The CRF was therefore deliberately simplified and made available in advance, allowing local adaptation to existing clinical workflows while maintaining core variable definitions. This pragmatic “real-life” approach ensured feasibility in busy ICU settings and reflected routine clinical assessment practices, even if it inevitably introduced some heterogeneity in data collection methods. Although such an approach May have led to an underestimation of discomfort prevalence compared with studies using strictly standardized research assessments, it offers a realistic and representative snapshot of current sedation, analgesia, and discomfort evaluation practices across a diverse range of French ICUs. Second, despite the significant number of participating ICUs, only 32% of French ICUs were included, raising the possibility of selection bias. Nevertheless, the characteristics of our ICU sample are comparable to national ICU data, supporting the representativeness of our findings. Third, for sedation and pain variables, investigators could report either the last recorded value or the mean of all values over the previous 24 h, depending on which was more readily available in the medical chart. This choice was predefined in the CRF and left to the investigator’s discretion to accommodate variability in local documentation practices. We acknowledge that the last recorded value does not capture fluctuations over the day and may not fully reflect the patient’s overall experience, while the mean value is not statistically optimal for ordinal scales such as the RASS and may mask episodes of over- or under-sedation (or pain). However, in the context of a pragmatic “real-life” nationwide survey, this flexibility was deemed necessary to reduce missing data and ensure broad participation. Fourth, the low rate of systematic delirium assessment (13% of ICUs) despite long-standing international guidelines likely contributed to an underestimation of its prevalence, particularly of hypoactive forms, which may be misinterpreted as depression or the effect of sedation. This gap may be explained by persistent barriers such as time constraints, perceived complexity of screening tools, competing clinical priorities, and variability in staff training. Given this limited use of validated tools, we classified patients according to a “normal” versus “non-normal” mental state, rather than relying solely on a formal delirium diagnosis. In both categories, we analyzed whether patients had been assessed with a validated delirium tool and whether the investigator reported a subjective impression of delirium, acknowledging the potential bias and variability inherent to such subjective assessments. This approach does not allow differentiation of delirium subtypes and limits the generalizability of our findings.

The efforts Made over the past 20 years to improve sedation practices, guided by national and international guidelines, have profoundly transformed the profile of ICU patients. While further improvement in sedation Management is still possible, we believe that the future of sedation should focus on addressing patient discomfort. Among these discomforts, pain remains a primary concern. If BPS is now widely and well used in non-communicating patients, using validated self-reporting scales to assess pain in patients able to communicate is the next priority. Anxiety, sleep disturbances, and thirst also play central roles in the experiences of ICU survivors. Recognizing the importance of these discomforts is essential, as reflected by the inclusion of sleep disturbances in the 2018 PADIS guidelines [9] and, more recently, by the 2025 update [10], which adds a conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence that melatonin may be considered as part of a multimodal approach to manage sleep disturbances and delirium.

Conclusion

In this large national study, the vast majority of patients were under no or light sedation, revealing significant discomforts, primarily pain, anxiety, and sleep disturbances. These findings underscore the need for more effective strategies to optimize pain management and overall patient comfort in non-deeply sedated ICU patients.
