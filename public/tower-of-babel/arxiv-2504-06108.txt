Causal Inference in Connected Populations with Contagion
Subhankar Bhadra, Department of Statistics, The Pennsylvania State University

Abstract
We address a gap in the literature on causal inference in connected populations: while there is a growing body of work on estimating causal effects in connected populations, little is known about how contagion and other network processes impact causal effects and inference. Contagion and other network processes imply that the outcomes of units affect the outcomes of other units, which enables the effects of interventions to propagate throughout connected populations and complicates insight into causal effects and inference. We offer novel insight into how contagion and other network processes impact causal effects and inference based on closed-form expressions for causal effects under spillover and contagion. These closed-form expressions reveal that the main effects of interventions, spillover, and contagion are intertwined even in the simplest possible settings, and that contagion can decrease or increase causal effects. We discuss statistical implications, including asymptotic bias of model-based estimators ignoring contagion, the violation of neighborhood exposure assumptions by unrestricted contagion and its effect on design-based estimators, and possible remedies.

1.1
Contributions

An empirical study by
Lee & Ogburn (2021)
highlights the consequences of ignoring contagion and other network processes.
We provide theoretical insight into how network processes—including spillover and contagion—can impact causal effects and inference by deriving closed-form expressions for the causal effect

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{E}&\coloneqq&\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(x_{i}=1;\bm{X}_{-i})-Y_{i}(x_{i}=0;\bm{X}_{-i})]\\

\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N}

\tau_{A}
,

The household example implies that the degrees of units are bounded and contagion is restricted to households,

\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\sum_{j=1:j\neq i}^{N}P_{i,j}\geq p_{\min}\,N\rho_{N}

,

:

Corollary 1
.
Under Conditions

3.2

The ego effect is

2.

The total causal effect is

\delta

4
Statistical Implications of Contagion

4.1
Model-Based Estimators

We discuss statistical implications of contagion,
starting with ordinary least squares estimators of
\tau_{E}
and
\tau_{T}
ignoring contagion.

Consider the household example with
causal effects

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{E}(\beta,\gamma,\delta)&=&(1-\chi)\,\beta+\chi\,\dfrac{\beta+\gamma\,\delta}{1-\delta^{2}},\;\quad\tau_{T}(\beta,\gamma,\delta)&=&(\beta+\gamma)\,\left(1+\chi\,\dfrac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right).\end{array}

If contagion is ignored by setting
\delta=0
,
the causal effects can be estimated by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{E}(\widehat{\beta}^{OLS},\,\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS},\,\delta=0)&=&\widehat{\beta}^{OLS},\;\quad\tau_{T}(\widehat{\beta}^{OLS},\,\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS},\,\delta=0)&=&\widehat{\beta}^{OLS}+\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS},\end{array}

where
\widehat{\beta}^{OLS}
and
\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS}
are ordinary least squares estimators of
\beta
and
\gamma
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\widehat{\beta}^{OLS}\\
\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS}\end{array}\right)&\coloneqq&(\bm{D}(\bm{X},\,\bm{Z})^{\top}\bm{D}(\bm{X},\,\bm{Z}))^{-1}\,\bm{D}(\bm{X},\,\bm{Z})^{\top}\bm{Y}.\end{array}

The
N\times 2
matrix
\bm{D}(\bm{X},\bm{Z})
has rows

(X_{1},\,X_{n(1)}\,Z_{1,n(1)})
,

\ldots
,

(X_{N},\,X_{n(N)}\,Z_{N,n(N)})
.

Proposition 1
.
Let
X_{i}\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny iid}}\text{Bernoulli}(\pi)
with
\pi\in(0,1)
,

Z_{i,j}\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny ind}}\text{Bernoulli}(\chi_{i,j})
with
\chi_{i,j}\coloneqq\chi\in(0,1)
when
j=n(i)
and
\chi_{i,j}\coloneqq 0
otherwise,
and
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{-i},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{z})]
be specified by Equation (
2
),
where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
\delta\in(-1,+1)\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}&&\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{E}(\widehat{\beta}^{OLS},\,\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS},\,\delta=0)\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\tau_{T}(\widehat{\beta}^{OLS},\,\widehat{\gamma}^{OLS},\,\delta=0)\end{pmatrix}\right|\;(\bm{X},\bm{Z})\right]\\
&\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mbox{\footnotesize a.s.}}&\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{(1-\chi)\,\beta}{1-\chi\,\pi^{2}}+\dfrac{\chi\,(1-\pi^{2})}{(1-\chi\,\pi^{2})\,(1-\delta^{2})}\,(\beta+\gamma\,\delta)\vskip 5.69046pt\\

4.1
demonstrates that estimators of
\tau_{E}
and
\tau_{T}

5
.
An alternative is estimators based on Markov random fields capturing both spillover and contagion
(
Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. 2021

Ogburn et al. 2020

.
Having said that,
estimators based on Markov random fields often rely on Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations (unless
N
is small) and are sensitive to model misspecification.

4.2
Design-Based Estimators

To avoid asymptotic bias of estimators based on misspecified models,
design-based estimators can be used.
Estimating the ego effect
\tau_{E}
is possible,
but estimating the total causal effect
\tau_{T}
is more challenging:
if the neighborhood exposure assumption
Y_{i}(x_{i};\bm{x}_{-i})=f_{i}(x_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}/N_{i})
is satisfied for random functions
f_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,N
),
an unbiased Horvitz-Thompson estimator of the total causal effect
\tau_{T}
exists,
but its variance grows without bound if
\mathbb{E}(Z_{i,j})\gg 1/\sqrt{N}

(
Li & Wager 2022
, Proposition 5,)
.

Li & Wager (2022)
suggest a PC balancing estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{T}^{PC}
to address the variance issue.
But the neighborhood exposure assumptions underlying
\widehat{\tau}_{T}^{PC}
and other design-based estimators are violated by unrestricted contagion,
as demonstrated in Figure

.
Simulation results in Section
5
suggest that
\widehat{\tau}_{T}^{PC}
has non-vanishing bias in such settings.
Possible remedies include bias reduction methods and additional structure restricting contagion (e.g., disjoint subpopulations).

&\leq&\ \max\limits_{1\leq j\leq\,N}\left\{\sqrt{1/N_{j}}\;\,\mathbb{I}(N_{j}\neq 0)\right\}\,\max\{1,\;\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\bm{M}(\delta,\bm{Z})\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|_{2}^{2}\}.\end{array}

By assumption,

1/\min_{1\leq j\leq N}N_{j}=o_{P}(1)
and
\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\bm{M}(\delta,\bm{Z})\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|_{2}^{2}

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\dfrac{1}{N}\,\left|\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\dfrac{M_{i,j}(\delta,\,\bm{Z})\,Z_{i,j}}{N_{j}}\right|&=&o_{P}(1).\end{array}

Vitali’s convergence theorem completes the proof of Theorem 1:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\dfrac{M_{i,j}(\delta,\,\bm{Z})\,Z_{i,j}}{N_{j}}\right]&=&0.\end{array}
