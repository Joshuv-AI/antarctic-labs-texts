Patient blood management protocol reduces allogeneic transfusion, morbidity, and mortality in coronary artery bypass surgery

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllogeneic blood is a limited and globally scarce resource. Protocols based on the Patient Blood Management (PBM) program, incorporating therapeutic alternatives to reduce the use of red blood cells (RBCs), fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelet concentrates (PCs), have become urgently needed. The aims of this study were: as a primary objective, to evaluate whether the implementation of a PBM protocol reduces allogeneic transfusion in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); and as a secondary objective, to assess the impact of transfusion on clinical outcomes and mortality following CABG surgery.MethodsThe study analyzed patients ≥ 18 years undergoing isolated and elective on-pump CABG, including both arterial and venous grafts. Data were retrospectively collected for two distinct periods, forming two groups: Pre-PBM (2010) and Post-PBM (2012), based on the implementation of a multidisciplinary PBM protocol aimed at optimizing erythropoiesis, hemostasis, and physiological tolerance to anemia. Statistical analyses included between-group comparisons, correlation analyses, and multiple regression to identify independent predictors of increased mortality. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 3,564 patients undergoing CABG were stratified into the Pre-PBM (n = 2,150) and Post-PBM (n = 1,414) groups. Implementation of a PBM protocol was associated with a significantly reduced allogeneic transfusions—RBC (62.7% to 48.4%), FFP (13.1% to 6.7%), and PC (5.4% to 3.0%) (p < 0.001). Mortality decreased from 4.5% to 3.1% (p = 0.042), with lower deep sternal wound infection rates (p < 0.001). Transfusion was an independent risk factor for increased mortality, with RBC (OR 3.63, 95% CI 2.20–5.99, p < 0.001), FFP (OR 5.69, 95% CI 3.91–8.27, p < 0.001), and PC (OR 6.45, 95% CI 4.07–10.24, p < 0.001) therapy demonstrating a dose-dependent association. Even in low-risk patients (EuroSCORE ≤ 2), allogeneic transfusion was associated with a significantly higher mortality (p < 0.001).ConclusionsImplementing a PBM protocol was associated with significant reductions in blood transfusions, infection rates, and mortality following CABG. Allogeneic transfusion was a strong predictor of adverse outcomes, including higher mortality, supporting the routine use of PBM strategies in CABG.Keywords: Coronary artery bypass grafting, Blood transfusion, Patient blood management, Clinical outcomes

Background

Blood transfusions are among the most common medical interventions and a significant financial burden [1], despite allogeneic blood being a limited global resource [2]. Cardiac surgeries account for substantial blood use, with transfusions required in up to 50% of procedures [3, 4]. Allogeneic transfusions have been associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and hospital costs due to their inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects [5–8].

Developing and implementing strategies, protocols, and hospital care programs that offer therapeutic alternatives to reduce or eliminate the use of red blood cells (RBC), fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelet concentrates (PC) has become an urgent need [9–13]. These comprehensive strategies are centered around the Patient Blood Management (PBM) program, which focuses on ensuring patient safety and using their physiological reserves. Together, these strategies aim to deliver patient-centered care by promoting the screening, diagnosis, and optimized treatment of anemia in the preoperative period, preventing bleeding and/or recovering the patient’s own blood in the intra- and postoperative periods, and optimizing the patient’s physiological reserves during the postoperative period, through a combination of medications, equipment, and/or surgical techniques [10, 13]. In addition to improved clinical outcomes, this program reduces both direct and indirect costs [10, 11]. The implementation of PBM-based protocols is crucial for preserving autologous blood, minimizing allogeneic transfusions, and improving outcomes by reducing morbidity and mortality in coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).

Thus, the aims of this study were: as a primary objective, to evaluate whether the implementation of a PBM protocol reduces allogeneic transfusion in patients undergoing CABG; and as a secondary objective, to assess the impact of transfusion on clinical outcomes and mortality following CABG surgery.

This study provides real-world evidence of PBM implementation in a large single-center cohort, demonstrating a dose–response relationship between transfusion exposure and mortality across multiple blood components (RBC, FFP, and PC), including in low-risk patients, and integrates a multidisciplinary educational intervention with clinical outcomes, offering insight into institutional implementation strategies. These findings complement existing literature by emphasizing institutional implementation and patient-level risk stratification.

Methods

Single-center retrospective cohort study. This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Beneficência Portuguesa de São Paulo (São Paulo, SP, Brazil; approval number: 4.830.588; date: July 6, 2021). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the data.

Study population

The study analyzed 4,923 patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent CABG in 2010 and 2012. Exclusion criteria included combined procedures (valvular or aortic surgery), congenital heart disease, off-pump CABG, and emergency or urgent surgeries. Consequently, a total of 1,359 patients were excluded from the study.

A total of 3,564 adult patients undergoing isolated (internal thoracic and/or radial artery and vein grafts), elective, on-pump CABG were included in the study. Data were retrospectively collected for two distinct periods, constituting two groups:

Pre-PBM protocol group (Pre-PBM): included data collected in 2010 over 12 months from a total of 2,150 (860 were excluded) patients who underwent CABG under the institution’s traditional blood transfusion practices.

Pre-PBM protocol group (Pre-PBM): included data collected in 2010 over 12 months from a total of 2,150 (860 were excluded) patients who underwent CABG under the institution’s traditional blood transfusion practices.

Post-PBM protocol group (Post-PBM): included data collected in 2012 over seven months from 1,414 (499 were excluded) new CABG patients after the Transfusion Medicine Education Program (TMEP) and implementation of a clinical protocol with main strategies from the PBM program.

Post-PBM protocol group (Post-PBM): included data collected in 2012 over seven months from 1,414 (499 were excluded) new CABG patients after the Transfusion Medicine Education Program (TMEP) and implementation of a clinical protocol with main strategies from the PBM program.

All patients undergoing on-pump CABG, performed through a median sternotomy by using the left internal thoracic artery, complemented with additional internal thoracic and/or radial artery and saphenous vein grafts. This observational study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE statement, in conjunction with the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Health Data (RECORD) extension. Data were derived from routinely collected hospital records and clinical databases. Particular attention was given to the definition of exposure groups (Pre-PBM vs. Post-PBM), the characterization of the study population, the validation of key outcomes (transfusion, morbidity, clinical outcomes, and mortality), and the specification of the data collection periods. Missing data were addressed by excluding non-essential variables and acknowledging relevant limitations. In addition, reporting was aligned with PBM-specific considerations, including a clear description of the intervention, transfusion metrics, and clinically relevant outcomes. Implementation of PBM strategies

A TMEP was implemented in the Cardiac Surgery department, targeting teams responsible for CABG. This Program consisted of weekly clinical-scientific meetings over 18 months, during which discussions focused on high-impact current scientific publications regarding the effects of blood transfusions on cardiac surgery patients, therapeutic alternatives to blood transfusion provided by the PBM program, and the hospital’s capacity to adopt PBM strategies. Beneficência Portuguesa de São Paulo Hospital (São Paulo, SP, Brazil) is a large, high-complexity tertiary care facility. The Program involved healthcare professionals directly engaged in cardiac surgery care, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, intensivists, hematologists, clinicians, nursing staff, nutritionists, physiotherapists, and biomedical scientists (80 professionals in total). The assessment of the impact of this program was primarily focused on clinical outcomes.

During TMEP, an evidence-based clinical protocol with main strategies from the PBM program was developed and implemented. The protocol followed the three pillars of PBM (Fig. 2).

Protocol incorporating strategies from the PBM program to conserve autologous blood and reduce allogeneic transfusions. Legends: aPTT - activated partial thromboplastin time; EPO – erythropoietin; IV – intravenous; Hb – hemoglobin; IM – intramusculary; MAP - mean arterial pressure; INR - international normalized ratio; SC – subcutaneous

Pillar I: Optimize erythropoiesis and coagulation status in the preoperative period;

Pillar II: Optimize hemostasis and minimize/recover blood loss during the intra- and postoperative periods;

Pillar III: Optimize the patient’s physiological tolerance to anemia in the postoperative period.

Within the PBM framework described above, specific transfusion-related measures were implemented to optimize hemostasis and minimize blood loss. In the preoperative period, strategies included screening for and treatment of anemia and coagulopathies, with iron supplementation and optimization of erythropoiesis (erythropoietin administration) or coagulation (discontinuation of platelet aggregation inhibitors and/or anticoagulants; administration of a thrombopoietin receptor agonist) when indicated, in the last month before surgery. Intra- and postoperative measures focused on bleeding prevention and control through standardized surgical hemostasis, use of antifibrinolytic agents (tranexamic acid), management of coagulation status (vitamin K; coagulation factors), and intraoperative blood recovery (cell salvage). Additionally, a more restrictive transfusion approach was encouraged, with transfusion decisions guided by clinical assessment and PBM principles rather than solely by hemoglobin. These strategies are consistent with previously described PBM frameworks in cardiac surgery, including those reported by Céspedes et al. and Santos et al. [14,15], which emphasize multimodal interventions targeting anemia, coagulation, and physiological tolerance to anemia.

For each patient who benefited from the PBM program described above, the necessary strategies were employed to strengthen the patient’s physiological blood reserves or to prevent their loss. The combination of strategies was tailored to each individual, but within the framework described above.

Regarding transfusion parameters, in the Pre-PBM group, transfusion practice was predominantly liberal (hemoglobin < 10 g/dL) and based on individual clinical judgment by surgeons, anesthesiologists, and intensivists. Following PBM implementation, cardiac surgery teams adopted a shift toward a more patient-centered and restrictive transfusion strategy (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL). However, due to the retrospective nature of the study, strict numeric transfusion thresholds were not consistently documented across all cases.

Clinical and surgical characteristics

The sample characterization involved analyzing variables extracted from the patient’s electronic medical record. Identification variables included age, sex, and dates of hospital admission and discharge.

Preoperative variables comprised diabetes mellitus (DM), dyslipidemia, hypertension, stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), atrial fibrillation (AF), congestive heart failure (CHF) stratified by left ventricular ejection fraction using the Simpson method (LVEF > 50%, 30–50%, < 30%), extracardiac arteriopathy, previous cardiac surgery, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic renal failure (CRF) with plasma creatinine ≥ 2.0 mg/dL, and mortality risk stratification using the European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE I).

Intra- and postoperative clinical outcome variables included the most reported complications and events, as outlined in the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) guidelines. RBC, FFP, and PC transfusions were key variables assessed, as well as the use of polytransfusions (> 3 units RBC). Additional variables included intensive care unit (ICU) discharge and ICU stay duration, prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV) > 24 h, perioperative acute myocardial infarction (AMI), stroke, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), AF requiring intervention, infections such as pneumonia and deep sternal wound infection, acute kidney injury (AKI), CRF with plasma creatinine ≥ 2.0 mg/dL, and the final discharge outcome categorized as survival or deaths up to 30 days from the date of surgery (30-day mortality).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses and statistical testing were performed using R 3.0.1, with a significance level set at 5% (p < 0.05). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to verify the normality of data distribution.

A comparative analysis of the variables between the two groups (Pre-PBM and Post-PBM) was conducted. Quantitative variables were described using means and standard deviations or medians, while qualitative variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. For group comparison, the Student’s t-test, ANOVA, and the Mann-Whitney non-parametric test were used. The Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was employed to test the homogeneity of proportions. Logistic regression models were used to analyze the effects and interactions between variables (age, sex, DM, dyslipidemia, CHF, MI, CRF, hypertension, stroke, AF, COPD, previous cardiac surgery, EuroSCORE I, RBC, FFP and PC, ICU stay duration, MV > 24 h, AMI, AKI, ARDS, infections, discharge outcome), following CABG surgery (Pre-PBM and Post-PBM groups). Variable selection was conducted using p-value–based backward elimination, with sequential removal of non-significant variables while assessing coefficient stability and clinical plausibility at each step; variables with p < 0.05 were retained in the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics, with VIF values close to 1 and tolerance > 0.90 indicating the absence of relevant collinearity; correlation matrices were also examined to confirm low inter-variable correlations. Model fit and performance were evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, pseudo-R² measures (Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke), − 2 log-likelihood, and overall classification accuracy.

The Post-PBM group had more patients without RBC transfusion (37.3% vs. 51.6%, p < 0.001) and fewer requiring polytransfusions (≥ 3 units RBC) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Comparison of red blood cell transfusion between Pre-PBM and Post-PBM groups

In total, 2,075 patients (58.2%) received at least one allogeneic blood component: 1,381 (66.6%) in the Pre-PBM group and 694 (33.4%) in the Post-PBM group. Conversely, 1,489 patients received no transfusion. The proportion of transfused patients was higher in the Pre-PBM group (66.6% vs. 51.6%; p < 0.001), while the Post-PBM group had more patients who were not transfused (48.4% vs. 33.4%; p < 0.001).

Using the multivariate regression model with variable selection process, patients who underwent surgery before the PBM protocol had a odds ratio 2.2 of receiving transfusion of RBC, FFP and/or PC compared to the patients in the Post-PBM group [Odds Ratio (OR) 2.22, Confidence Interval (CI) 95%: 1.90–2.58, p < 0.001].

For isolated RBC transfusion, more Pre-PBM patients received RBCs (66.4% vs. 52.3%, p < 0.001), while more Post-PBM patients were not transfused (47.7% vs. 33.6%, p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis showed that Pre-PBM patients had a 2.1-fold higher odds of RBC transfusion (OR 2.13, 95% CI 1.83–2.48, p < 0.001).

Predictive factors for 30-day mortality following CABG

In this cohort, 140 deaths occurred (96 Pre-PBM vs. 44 Post-PBM). Mortality was significantly higher among patients receiving transfusions of RBC (86.4% vs. 55.8%, p < 0.001), FFP (41.3% vs. 9.4%, p < 0.001), and PC (22.5% vs. 3.7%, p < 0.001).

Multivariate regression analysis identified independent predictors of increased mortality.

According to Table 5, RBC, FFP, and PC transfusions were independent risk factors associated with increased mortality (OR 3.63, 95% CI 2.20–5.99; OR 5.69, 95% CI 3.91–8.27; OR 6.45, 95% CI 4.07–10.24, respectively; all p < 0.001) compared to patients not receiving these allogeneic components.

Discussion

The strengths of the study include a large sample size, a single-center design with consistent surgical teams, and robust multivariable regression analysis. The study also evaluated dose-dependent effects of transfusion, incorporated clinically relevant subgroup analyses, and implemented a structured institutional Patient Blood Management protocol. This protocol included preoperative anemia optimization, intraoperative hemostatic strategies such as antifibrinolytics and blood recovery, and a restrictive transfusion approach, although individual interventions were not systematically recorded.

Implementation of an institutional PBM protocol for autologous blood conservation significantly reduced transfusions of RBC, FFP, and PC in CABG. In the Pre-PBM group, transfusion practices followed a predominantly liberal institutional approach (hemoglobin < 10 g/dL) and decisions were guided by individual clinical judgment, often aiming to maintain postoperative hemoglobin above 8 g/dL. In the Post-PBM group, a shift toward a more restrictive (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL) and patient-centered strategy, with decisions based on clinical context and physiological tolerance to anemia rather than fixed values. This change was associated with a significant reduction in transfusion rates of RBC, FFP, and PC. Preoperative assessment, typically conducted within 30 days of elective surgery, enabled the identification and management of modifiable risk factors, such as anemia, although these interventions were not standardized and may have introduced variability. Despite persistently high transfusion rates, consistent with the inherent bleeding risk of on-pump coronary artery bypass grafting, PBM was associated with clinically meaningful reductions in blood product use. Favorable outcomes likely reflect improvements in perioperative care, hemostatic management, and more judicious, evidence-based transfusion practices.

Post-PBM, fewer patients received polytransfusions (> 3 units of RBC), and more remained transfusion-free, reflecting per-patient blood savings and fewer patients transfused. The protocol primarily reduced postoperative mortality in CABG patients.

The cohorts in the pre-PBM and post-PBM groups were comparable in age, sex, and major comorbidities (DM, stroke, AF, prior cardiac surgery, extracardiac arteriopathy, CRF, and CRF requiring dialysis), particularly with regard to EuroSCORE-based mortality risk stratification (p = 0.215). The EuroSCORE is a simple, objective, and up-to-date system for evaluating cardiac surgery risk, based on one of the largest and most accurate databases in European cardiac surgical history, and is widely recommended [16]. Although the Pre-PBM group had a higher proportion of patients with dyslipidemia, hypertension, MI, CHF with LVEF > 50%, and COPD, the estimated risk of death according to EuroSCORE was similar between the groups, providing greater reliability to the comparative analyses. Observed mortality in this cohort exceeded that predicted by the EuroSCORE I, likely reflecting limitations in model calibration, as it was derived from earlier datasets and may not fully capture contemporary or institution-specific risk factors. High transfusion rates may also have contributed to increased postoperative risk, although causality cannot be established. Importantly, the model was applied uniformly across both groups, enabling consistent risk adjustment and valid comparative analysis.

Due to the retrospective design and use of an institutional database, key procedural variables such as cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, blood loss, and intraoperative medication details were not consistently available and were not included in the analysis. A data element not incorporated into the reported results is that a higher proportion of patients in the Pre-PBM group were receiving antiplatelet therapy with acetylsalicylic acid (70.3% vs. 64.4%, p < 0.001), which may have influenced bleeding and transfusion practices. Tranexamic acid was the primary intraoperative hemostatic agent, administered according to the institutional protocol, while other practices followed the standardized PBM approach. Although comparison of procedural characteristics could provide additional insights, the study was restricted to a homogeneous cohort of elective, isolated on-pump coronary artery bypass grafting to reduce heterogeneity and improve internal validity.

Autologous blood conservation reduces transfusion needs and improves CABG outcomes [11, 12]. In over 600,000 patients, PBM strategies lowered RBC, FFP, and PC use by 41%, 47%, and 27%, with reductions in mortality (28%), infections (21%), MI/stroke (31%), and length of stay (15%) [10]. A meta-analysis of 235,779 surgical patients reported a 39% reduction in RBC transfusions, along with fewer complications and lower mortality [17]. Similarly—yet in a structured, simple, practical, and fully reproducible manner—our PBM protocol reduced the use of RBC, FFP, and PC by 23%, 48%, and 44%, respectively; increased the proportion of non-transfused patients by 38%; and reduced 30-day mortality by 31% and deep sternal wound infections by 76%.

In this study, baseline differences between groups should be considered when interpreting the results, particularly the higher prevalence of prior myocardial infarction and CHF with LVEF > 50% in the Pre-PBM group. These differences may reflect random variation inherent to observational designs and could have influenced both bleeding risk and transfusion practices. However, in the multivariable analysis, prior myocardial infarction and CHF were not independent predictors of postoperative mortality, suggesting a limited impact on the primary outcome in the adjusted model. In addition, the overall baseline risk, as estimated by EuroSCORE, was similar between groups, although this metric does not fully account for all potential confounders. Therefore, the potential for residual confounding should be acknowledged, though the study employed rigorous adjustment methods to minimize bias.

The Post-PBM group had higher rates of prolonged ICU stay (p = 0.035). However, ICU discharge rates (p = 0.566) and total length of stay (p = 0.209) did not differ between the two groups. The longer intensive care unit stay in the post Patient Blood Management group likely reflects institutional practices rather than increased clinical severity, as a more conservative discharge policy of 48 to 72 h was adopted. This approach emphasized enhanced postoperative monitoring and early rehabilitation strategies, including early mobilization, cardiopulmonary physiotherapy, and progressive ambulation initiated shortly after extubation. Thus, prolonged stay likely represents recovery optimization rather than a higher complication burden. Importantly, deep sternal wound infection and 30-day mortality were lower, consistent with literature showing improved outcomes when allogeneic blood use is minimized [10–12, 18, 19].

Among patients receiving allogeneic transfusions (n = 2,075), transfusion was significantly associated with postoperative complications, including prolonged MV (> 24 h), AMI, stroke, AF requiring intervention, pneumonia, deep sternal wound infection, AKI, and CRF with dialysis, consistent with prior reports [6, 10, 12, 18]. Additionally, allogeneic blood transfusion was an independent risk factor for mortality following. Individually, RBC, FFP, and PC transfusions increased death risk 3.6-, 5.7-, and 6.5-fold, respectively. Other independent predictors included DM, age > 60, CRF, COPD, and AF, consistent with prior studies [6, 12, 18, 20–22].

Ming et al. [23] analyzed 8,238 patients undergoing cardiac surgery and found that transfusion of RBC, FFP, or PC independently increased the risks of mortality and infection, with combined transfusions producing dose-dependent adverse outcomes. Another study similarly demonstrated that mortality risk is directly proportional to the number of RBC units transfused in patients undergoing CABG [5]. Even patients with very low EuroSCORE risk showed higher mortality after allogeneic transfusion, consistent with another study reporting 6.0% vs. 0.4% deaths in low-risk patients (p < 0.001) [24].

Our study found that transfusions of RBC, FFP, or PC were associated with increased mortality in a dose-dependent manner, with higher volumes posing a greater risk. In some cases, the high odds ratios for RBC, FFP, and PC transfusions remained significant after adjustment, although their magnitude may partly reflect unmeasured illness severity. These associations likely indicate residual confounding by indication, as more severely ill patients are both more likely to receive transfusions and to die. The use of 30-day mortality may further accentuate the impact of acute complications. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as non-causal and may overestimate the independent effect of allogeneic transfusion, despite consistency with prior evidence. In fact, previous studies [5, 23] confirm this association.

Elevated EuroSCORE, preoperative anemia, and allogeneic transfusions, alone or combined, are also linked to reduced long-term survival [24, 25]. Mortality may result from multi-organ dysfunction, infection, malignancy, thromboembolism, delirium, hemolytic reactions, transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), and/or alloimmunization [26]. World Health Organization (WHO) and medical societies recommend institutional protocols to minimize allogeneic transfusions and improve postoperative outcomes [4, 9–11, 27–29]. This study highlights the role of PBM in patient-centered optimization of anemia, thrombocytopenia, bleeding, and autotransfusion management.

Propensity score matching could enhance causal inference in observational studies; however, its application in this dataset is limited by the absence of key variables required for adequate matching, including cardiopulmonary bypass duration, intraoperative factors, and detailed coagulation parameters. Use of propensity score matching under these conditions could result in incomplete adjustment and misleading estimates. Therefore, multivariable logistic regression, including available clinically relevant covariates, was used to adjust for confounders and identify independent predictors. Given the large sample size and model robustness, propensity score methods were unlikely to provide substantial additional benefit and could introduce instability without improving causal inference.

As limitations, we acknowledge that historical bias is inherent to before-and-after designs, even when the same surgical teams perform procedures, because unmeasured temporal changes in surgical technique, perioperative care, intensive care, and infection control may have influenced outcomes independently of Patient Blood Management. A concurrent shift toward more patient-centered care, including greater attention to hemostasis and postoperative management, may also have contributed. To mitigate this, the interval between periods was minimized, data collection was standardized, and multivariable regression was used to adjust for measurable confounders; however, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Findings should therefore be interpreted as associative rather than causal, although consistent with prior literature reporting reductions in transfusion rates of approximately 20 to 40% [17, 30] and with conclusions from the 2018 Frankfurt Consensus Conference [31] highlighting the observational nature of much of the evidence. Another limitation is the lack of consistent documentation of transfusion thresholds. Although a shift from a liberal to a more restrictive strategy was encouraged after Patient Blood Management implementation, decisions were not uniformly standardized or recorded, potentially affecting transfusion frequency and volume and limiting attribution of effects to the intervention. In addition, bleeding-related outcomes such as intraoperative blood loss, drain output, and reoperation were not systematically captured. However, the institutional reoperation rate for bleeding was low, at approximately 0.8%, compared with 2.7 to 3.4% reported in the literature [32], which indirectly suggests effective hemostatic management. Residual imbalance between groups cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

Implementing a PBM protocol was associated with a significant reduction in allogeneic blood transfusions, infection rates, and mortality following CABG. Allogeneic blood transfusions were an independent risk factor for increased morbidity and mortality, supporting the routine use of PBM strategies in CABG.
