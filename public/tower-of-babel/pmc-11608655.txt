Interventional study of the effect of trunk support provision on the body pressure and ventilation volume in the prone position in healthy young and older women
Authors: Akihiro Sato, Akiko Ajimi, Yuko Omiya, Jun-Ichi Shimizu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] This study aimed to determine and compare the effects of prone trunk support on facial and chest pressures and ventilation volumes between young and older women. [Participants and Methods] This study included 12 healthy young women and 10 healthy older women. We recorded the maximum body pressures on the face and chest, tidal volume, and lung capacity in three diﬀerent prone positions and compared the results between the two age groups. [Results] Trunk support provision decreased the facial pressure and increased the thoracic pressure in both groups. Facial pressure was significantly higher in older women than in younger women when trunk support was not provided. No significant change was observed in the tidal volume in both groups when trunk support was provided; however, the lung capacity decreased significantly in the younger group compared to the older group. In contrast, the chest pressure, tidal volume, and lung capacity were not correlated in either group. [Conclusion] Trunk support provision effectively reduces pressure on the face, which is associated with a high risk of pressure ulcers. Although trunk support increases pressure on the chest, it has limited effects on the respiratory movements. Thus, providing trunk support in the prone position can reduce the risk of facial pressure ulcers and facilitate medical management.Keywords: Prone positioning, Body pressure, Ventilation volume

INTRODUCTION

Prone therapy has been reported to be effective in improving oxygenation and survival rates in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and has recently been used as a treatment option for coronavirus disease (COVID-19)1,2,3). A meta-analysis of the effects of prone therapy in the treatment of ARDS reported improved mortality when patients were placed in the prone position for ≥12 h/day4). Furthermore, a meta-analysis of the effects of prone positioning in COVID-19 patients reported lower mortality and better oxygenation with prone than with supine positioning5). Therefore, prone positioning is a cost-effective and beneficial modality for treating ARDS and COVID-19. However, there is no standard method for the prone positioning of patients (e.g., positioning of the upper and lower limbs, use of support cushions), and the actual method employed has been shown by individual healthcare facilities. Pressure ulcers are a common complication of prone therapy. Pressure ulcers can be divided into medical device-related pressure ulcers (MDRPU) and weight-bearing pressure ulcers. MDRPU is the main contributor to pressure ulcers in the prone position6). As ventilators are often used in prone therapy, diﬀerent methods of prone positioning, such as the use of support cushions, may aﬀect local body pressure and influence the development of pressure ulcers.

The use of trunk supports in the prone position is common7,8,9,10,11), but the purpose of using supports has rarely been specified. The main purpose may be to ease medical procedures, such as intubation, but some reports suggest that trunk support should not be used because of concerns about its negative effects during ventilation12,13,14). However, there is very little basic information on prone positioning, making it difficult to decide whether trunk support should be used. Multidimensional information, such as the influence of the prone position on body pressure and respiratory movements, is important in deciding the proper prone position.

Thus, we focused on trunk supports and investigated the effects of their use on body pressure on the face, chest, and ventilation (TV: tidal volume; LC: lung capacity). As structural changes, such as changes in joint and muscle thickness, occur with age, younger and older adults may show different trends. Therefore, the young and older participants were compared in terms of trunk support use.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

Young female participants were recruited through a university bulletin board, whereas older female participants were recruited from the Silver Human Resource Center. The Silver Human Resource Center is a nonprofit organization that helps older adults work. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) female sex, (ii) absence of pain or other conditions that could interfere with prone positioning, and (iii) BMI <30. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) male sex or BMI not meeting the above criteria, and (ii) history of orthopedic or neurological disease that could interfere with the prone position. Men and women have different body shapes due to anatomical variations, such as the presence of breasts in women, which can influence body pressure distribution15). Therefore, this study focused exclusively on women to investigate these effects. Finally, 12 healthy young women group (YG) and 10 healthy older women group (OG) participated in the study. The aim and procedure of the present study were explained to the participants verbally and in writing, and written informed consent was obtained. This study was approved by the Tokyo Kasei University Ethics Review Committee (approval number: R4-20).

After participants self-reported their BMI and underwent head rotation range of motion measurement, they rested for 2 min in each of the three prone positions, which were presented in a random order. During these periods, the maximum body pressure on the face and chest was recorded. After the 2-min rest, ventilation volume was measured in the same position.

The basic test posture was a prone position with the face turned to the left and the right upper limb elevated, which is known to increase facial pressure16). In this study, three test postures were used: the basic prone position without trunk support (no-TS), and two variations with TS of different heights, low-TS (80 mm) and high-TS (1,300 mm), were added to the basic posture (Fig. 1). The low-TS configuration used a single low-resilience urethane foam cushion (length × width × height: 600 × 500 × 80 mm) originally designed for the prone position (Fig. 2)17). For the high-TS, a rigid urethane cushion (500-mm high) was placed under the low-TS cushion, resulting in a total TS height of 1,300 mm.

The inspection method for each survey item is outlined as follows. The passive range of motion for head rotation was measured, focusing solely on left head rotation, as the participant’s posture during the experiment was positioned for left rotation. The measurement procedure was as follows (Fig. 3): The basic axis of head rotation was defined as a line perpendicular to the line connecting the right and left acromion of the scapula, whereas the movement axis was defined as a line connecting the parietal and external occipital ridges. The examiner fixed the basic axis and rotated the participant’s head to the left, until the maximum pain-free extent was reached. During the measurement, the participant was video recorded from above, and the angle between the basic and motor axes was calculated to the nearest 1° using video analysis software (Media Blend, DKH [now Q’sfix], Tokyo, Japan). The passive range of left head rotation was measured twice for each participant in a sitting position, and the average of these measurements was used as the index.

Method for measuring the range of head rotation.

Basic axis (solid yellow line): A line perpendicular to the line (dashed yellow break line) connecting the left and right acromions.

Moving axis (solid white line): A line connecting the top of the head and the external laryngeal prominence.

The examiner stabilized the participant’s trunk and rotated their head to the left to the maximum extent within a pain-free range. The range of head rotation is indicated by the angle between the basic and moving axes.

A body pressure measurement sensor sheet (SR Soft Vision, Sumitomo Science & Engineering Co., Aichi, Japan) was placed either on the mattress (Everfit C3, Paramount Bed Co., Tokyo, Japan) or on the TS. Body pressure data were collected for 2 min from the start of each measurement. From these data, the maximum pressure values on the face and chest were recorded as indicators of body pressure. The sensor sheet used for measuring upper body pressure had a pressure-sensitive area of 1,800 mm (length) × 700 mm (width), spatial resolution of 28 mm2, and measurement range of 15–110 mmHg. Values below 10 mmHg were displayed as 0 mmHg, and values between 10–15 mmHg were displayed as 10 mmHg. All participants’ measurements were within the required range. After resting for 2 min in each prone position, ventilation was measured using a spirometer (Auto Spiro A-307, MINATO Medical Science Co., Osaka, Japan). The measured indicators included tidal volume (TV) and lung capacity (LC).

Comparisons between the YG and OG for each indicator were made using t-tests after checking for normality. Within-posture comparisons of body pressure, TV, and LC were performed using sphericity tests, followed by an analysis of variance. Multiple comparisons were performed when the analysis of variance showed significant differences. Correlations between head rotation and facial pressure, and between TV and LC and chest pressure were tested using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. All tests were performed using the Windows version of R.4.2.2 (CRAN, Freeware), with significance set at <5%.

In the effect of TS insertion on body pressure, significant differences were found only in the prone position without TS in the between-groups comparison; in OG, facial pressure was very high in the prone position without TS (73.2 ± 17.7 mmHg); TS insertion tended to decrease facial pressure; the higher the TS, the lower the facial pressure. This effect was similar in both groups. In contrast, chest pressure showed the opposite trend; chest pressure was lower in the prone position without TS insertion and increased with TS insertion. This was more pronounced at higher TS heights. Chest pressure was not significantly different between the YG and OG.

The effect of TS insertion on TV or LC was not significant between the YG and OG. A comparison between postures in the YG showed a decrease in the LC in the prone position with high-TS insertion compared with the prone position without TS insertion but no significant change in the LC in the OG. TS insertion did not significantly change the lung capacity in the prone position in either group.

To investigate the possible effect of head rotation angle on facial pressure, we examined the correlation between head rotation angle and facial pressure. In the YG, a negative correlation was found between the head rotation angle and facial pressure in the prone position with high-TS insertion, whereas in the OG, no correlation was found between the head rotation angle and facial pressure in all prone positions (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Prone positioning is effective in improving ventilation. In the practice of prone therapy, the use of trunk supports has been reported in many cases; however, few reports have clearly indicated the purpose of their use. There have been negative reports on the use of trunk supports because of increased chest compressions and decreased thoracic compliance12,13,14). In our study, we also observed that TS use increased thoracic compression; the higher the TS, the higher the thoracic compression. In contrast, TS insertion tended to decrease facial pressure. In the OG, the mean prone facial pressure without TS was >70 mmHg, whereas a decrease of >20 mmHg was observed with TS insertion. Pressure ulcers are particularly common on the face during prone therapy18). Although there is no agreement on the criteria for pressure to cause a pressure ulcer, with some studies using 32 mmHg and others using higher criteria19,20,21,22,23,24); thus, the facial pressure of approximately 70 mmHg for the OG observed in our experiments may represent a high risk for pressure ulcers. Facial ulcers in the prone position are one of the factors associated with medical device-related pressure ulcers and facial pressure is considered an important factor in the development of pressure ulcers. Therefore, the use of the TS in the prone position is considered important, not only to improve ventilation but also to reduce the risk of ulcers by reducing facial pressure. The effect of TS on facial pressure reduction may be more pronounced in older women. We hypothesized that there is a correlation between a low range of head rotation and increased facial pressure. In the YG, we found a significant correlation between the range of head rotation and facial pressure at a high TS, as hypothesized. This may be due to increased tone in the antagonist muscles associated with head rotation and increased resting tension in the ligaments and joint capsules involved in neck rotation. However, in the OG, wherein the range of head rotation was small, no significant correlation was observed between the range of head rotation and facial pressure. This may be because the head rotation required for breathing is compensated for by the rotation of the trunk and pelvis, resulting in a smaller increase in soft tissue tension, such as muscles and ligaments, with head rotation.

It is important to determine whether the use of TS affects ventilatory kinetics because prone therapy is used to improve ventilation. With an increasing number of recent studies demonstrating the efficacy of prone therapy in awake and non-intubated patients25,26,27), it is difficult for caregivers to determine the patient’s ventilatory kinetics. Therefore, it is crucial to determine in advance how diﬀerence in the prone position affects the ventilatory dynamics.

Furthermore, the ventral ribs move significantly during breathing. In the prone position, the ventral ribs are compressed, which can decrease thoracic compliance and impair respiratory motion. Increased thoracic pressure due to TS insertion may further impair respiratory motion. However, in our experiments, TS in the prone position increased the thoracic pressure but did not significantly change the TV in either group. Guérin et al.14) suggest that thoracic and lung compliance should be considered separately. This indicated that any decrease in thoracic compliance in the prone position was offset by an improvement in lung compliance. In the prone position, the dorsal lung, which has a higher parenchymal volume, is larger than the ventral lung, facilitating improved ventilation14). Although thoracic compliance was reduced in the prone position in our experiment, ventilation was compensated for by lung compliance, which may explain why the TV did not change.

In contrast, TS insertion significantly decreased the LC in the YG. This may be because greater thoracic movement is required for LC than for TV and younger subjects were more susceptible to LC than older participants because they had greater thoracic flexibility.

Overall, there was no significant correlation between chest pressure and TV or LC, and the increase in chest pressure due to TS insertion had little effect on ventilatory volume, suggesting that TS insertion can be positively used to reduce the risk of facial pressure ulcers, as described above. The results of this study may provide basic information about prone positioning.

This study had some limitations. In this study, we only included women to clarify the effects of different prone positions on body pressure and tidal volume. Since body shape characteristics differ between sexes, future studies that include men are anticipated to provide more comprehensive insights. In addition, compensatory trunk movements and pelvic rotation likely contributed to the observed changes in body pressure. While we did not include postural analysis, investigating compensatory movements at the joint motion level that influence body pressure is crucial for achieving optimal positioning. Future research incorporating postural analysis will provide more comprehensive insights, enhancing our understanding of body pressure dynamics.

In conclusion, TS insertion increases chest pressure and decreases facial pressure; however, the effect on ventilation is small, and TS insertion did not affect TV or LC in older women. Thus, TS insertion in the prone position not only facilitates intubation and management but also reduces facial pressure, may reduce the risk of facial pressure ulcers, and creates a few disadvantages in terms of ventilation. Little information has thus far been available regarding the risk and benefit of TS insertion in prone positioning and shape of TS applied during prone therapy. Our study may provide important information on prone positioning.
