Epigenetic control of transposable elements in brain and brain disorders
Authors: Corinne Smith, Peter J Hamilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOnce considered somewhere between inert (‘junk DNA’) and parasitic (‘selfish genetic elements’), transposable elements (TEs) are increasingly appreciated as inseparable from the healthy functioning of eukaryotic genomes. The ubiquitous TE sequences that make up about half of the human genome are sources of the physical genetic material necessary for establishing distinct domains of chromatin architecture, generating non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), expanding transcription factor regulatory networks, and providing cis-regulatory elements to enable complex gene expression patterns. Given the critical roles for TEs in core genomic functions, the circumstances in which TEs become dysregulated are increasingly being explored in the context of human disease. While most TEs are silenced by host-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional mechanisms, certain tissues and cell types are noted to experience considerable TE transcription. The brain and nervous tissues notably exhibit highly dynamic TE activity, both in response to experience and across the lifespan. Here, we review the current state of research into epigenetic regulation of and by TEs with a focus on the brain. We explore the intersection of epigenetics and TEs in brain cell types, with emphasis on DNA and histone methylation, ncRNAs and small RNAs, and the function of TE-regulating transcription factors. We explore how epigenetic mechanisms involved in brain TE regulation may become dysfunctional in the context of neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, and neuropsychiatric disorders. We present and synthesize the mounting evidence implicating the epigenetic control of brain TEs as an under-explored mechanism in these complex disorders.KEYWORDS: Transposable elements, brain disorders, transcription, psychiatric disorders

Introduction

What is a TE?

Transposable elements (TEs), also called transposons or ‘jumping genes,’ are repetitive DNA sequences that are capable of mobilizing their nucleic acid sequence to be re-inserted elsewhere in the genome, a process called transposition. First described by Barbara McClintock in 1950 [1], TEs have since come to be appreciated as ubiquitous DNA elements that compose a substantial portion of most eukaryotic genomes and approximately half the human genome [2,3]. While some TEs are ancestrally derived from retroviruses and retain viral-like proteins and mechanisms of replication [4,5], TEs are a diverse category of genetic elements that vary in their structure, genome distribution, activity, and method of mobilization [2]. Indeed, only a handful of TE subtypes encode the functional proteins necessary for transposition in human genomes, whereas the majority of human TEs have lost the ability to transpose as a consequence of accumulation of mutations and truncations over evolutionary history [6], as well as host-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional mechanisms of TE repression [7–10].

TE sequences are constantly contributing to basic genomic functions via mechanisms independent of their replication and transposition. Since TE sequences have considerably expanded the amount of DNA material per genome [11], TE-derived DNA can serve as potent sources of genetic material useful in the generation of cis-regulatory elements and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), expansion of transcription factor binding sites, and targeting of chromatin modifications, especially heterochromatin [12–15]. Further, TEs are enriched at insulators and functional boundaries that define topologically associating domains (TADs) in three-dimensional chromatin organization [16,17]. Given that these transposition-independent TE functions benefit from stable regulation of genomic TE loci, this poses interesting questions about how these genomic functions might be affected in conditions and cell types in which the TE-targeted epigenetic safeguards are weakened and TEs become mobilized. This possibility is especially provocative considering the robust body of evidence that demonstrates the highly dynamic nature of the cell types of the nervous system in terms of chromatin organization, epigenetic post-translational modifications to DNA and histones, and transcription factor expression and function [18–31]. With this in mind, TEs may be particularly sensitive to, and possibly contribute to, the dynamic epigenetic changes that occur within brain cell types and may play an under-appreciated role in the epigenetic and transcriptional adaptations that are well-noted to occur in these cells. In this review, we will discuss what is known about epigenetic regulation both of and by TEs in the brain, and we will explore the potential implications of epigenetic control of TEs in brain disorders. To identify relevant primary research, we queried the literature for studies focused on epigenetic mechanisms of TE expression and repression in neural tissue and cell types, both in general and in disordered conditions, focusing on human samples whenever possible. In interpreting these studies, we aim to resolve the contributions of TE-centric molecular mechanisms to brain disorders in this still-developing field of research.

Evolution and specificity of TE regulation

There are two major classes of TEs: Class I RNA retrotransposons, which use the reverse transcriptase enzyme to form RNA intermediates that are copied into a new genomic location during transposition, and Class II DNA transposons, which transpose by excising the segment of DNA and reintegrating it at a new genomic location [2]. Class I retrotransposons are further subdivided into long terminal repeat (LTR) or non-LTR retrotransposons based on their structure. LTR retrotransposons generally contain LTR flanking sequences and viral-like protein-coding sequences, including gag, pol, pro, and env [2,32]. The major family of LTRs is endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), of which there are numerous sub-families, including ERV-K, ERV-W, and MaLR [32]. There are also species-specific ERV classifications, such as human ERVs (HERVs) and murine ERVs (MERVs), within each sub-family, as well as species-specific non-ERV LTRs [32].

Non-LTR retrotransposons also utilize an RNA intermediate, but do not contain the same structural elements as LTRs. The two major divisions of non-LTRs are long interspersed elements (LINEs) and short interspersed elements (SINEs). LINEs generally contain 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs), which can act as promoters [33,34], and two open reading frames (ORFs), which encode a transcriptional chaperone and a reverse transcriptase enzyme, named ORF1p and ORF2p, respectively [2]. Certain evolutionarily young non-LTRs, such as the human-specific LINE-1 (L1) subfamily (L1HS), are transposition-competent in humans due to their functional reverse transcriptase enzymes, which perform target-primed reverse transcription [35]. Smaller non-LTRs, such as the SINE and Alu families, are not capable of autonomous transposition in humans, but are able to co-opt human L1 machinery to achieve transposition [36,37]. The coordination between transposition-competent and transposition-incompetent TEs illuminates the complex synergy that can occur upon the co-expression of diverse TE gene products.

While this review will emphasize transposition-independent mechanisms of TEs, transposition may also have important consequences in the context of brain development and disorders. Human brain tissue has been shown in multiple studies to have higher rates of TE transposition events than other tissues, which includes studies comparing brain and peripheral tissues from the same individual [38,39]. Because neurons, the cellular functional unit of the brain, are largely post-mitotic [40], transposition events are unlikely to pass to daughter cells or be inherited. However, post-mitotic neurons have access to a smaller repertoire of DNA repair mechanisms than dividing cells, which is particularly true for repair mechanisms related to DNA double-stranded breaks (DSBs) [41]. The transposition of L1 elements is known to result in DSBs, which may be particularly influential in neurons (this concept is reviewed in [42]). Therefore, neuronal cell types may especially depend on molecular mechanisms to limit transposition events as a way of preventing genomic instability in the brain. While repression of TEs is largely accomplished via epigenetic mechanisms, discussed in more detail in the following sections, epigenetic dysregulation is a feature of many brain disorders [18,20–24,27], as is genomic instability [42]. These brain disorder-related outcomes may be more completely understood by considering the role TE transposition events in the context of these molecular mechanisms.

Since TE sequences are ubiquitous in the genomes of nearly all cells, tissues, and species, TEs are well-documented to contribute to cell type-specific, tissue-specific, and species-specific epigenetic and transcriptional patterns [43–48]. TE sequences are highly enriched in genomic regulatory regions in a cell type-specific manner at both transcriptionally active and repressive loci [45,47]. Tissue-specific TE epigenetic repression or activation, including both histone and DNA methylation, has been shown to associate with tissue-specific gene expression patterns across tissue types [45,49]. TEs have been shown to be a source of binding sites for several important DNA-binding factors, including CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) and estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) [11,17,49], as well as other key neural transcription factors, including Sox2 and Brn2 [47]. Interestingly, the TE sequences that serve as Sox2 binding sites differ between human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and neural progenitor cells (NPCs), and the transition between ESC and NPC is accompanied by a change in chromatin state at these TE loci [47]. This highlights the importance of epigenetic regulation of and by TEs in human brain development.

TEs are also well-known to contribute to the evolution of traits across phylogeny. Notable examples include not only their original characterization in maize [1], but also regulation of the agouti gene in mice [50], eusociality in Synalpheus snapping shrimp [51], and the development of trichromacy in primates [52]. In the brain, TEs are hypothesized to have evolved to be especially influential on the regulation of neuronal gene expression [47,48,53–58]. In the mouse embryonic brain, a SINE-like TE known as MER130 acts as an enhancer for neocortex development, and this is conserved in humans [57]. MER130 acts as an enhancer site at forebrain-related genes in tetrapod genomes at higher rates than more evolutionarily distant genomes, suggesting a role in mammalian brain evolution. Expansion of the human hippocampus compared to non-human primates is also linked to TE-driven gene expression networks [58]. Thus, given the evolutionary importance of TEs and their dynamic nature in the human brain, TE-derived mechanisms merit investigation as potential drivers of human neurological conditions.

Epigenetic control of TEs in brain

KZFPs and TRIM28

Epigenetic control of genomic TEs is largely accomplished via chromatin-remodeling histone modifications [15,43,59–62], as well as DNA methylation, particularly in early development [39,63–66]. One well-characterized mechanism by which TE epigenetic control is initiated, particularly in the brain, is via the Krüppel-associated box (KRAB) zinc finger protein (KZFP) family of transcription factors, along with their cognate binding partner, tripartite motif containing protein 28 (TRIM28) (also called KRAB-associated protein 1 or KAP1) (Figure 1A) [7,67–69,71–73]. TRIM28 recruits histone deacetylases and histone methyltransferases, including Setdb1 and NuRD, to promote H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin formation at KZFP genomic binding sites [69,71,72]. KZFPs exhibit binding to TE loci, with the exact binding profile of each KZFP differing slightly to target subpopulations of TEs [69,74]. This has led to the so-called ‘evolutionary arms race’ hypothesis of TE expression and KZFP binding site mutations [74]. H3K9me3 occurs so frequently at TE loci that it has been hypothesized that this repressive histone marker evolved in response to TE integration into the genome in order to stabilize the genome and prevent further transposition [75].

Transposable elements are integral to the chromatin landscape. (A) TEs act as transcription factor binding sites, particularly for KRAB-zinc finger proteins (KZFPs) and their cognate binding partner, TRIM28 [67–69]. This recruits H3K9 trimethylation (H3K9me3), leading to heterochromatin formation at TE loci [7]. (B) TEs are enriched at CTCF protein binding sites [17,45,49]. They may act as topologically associated domain (TAD) boundaries, which dictate 3D chromatin structures, including DNA loops. (C) TEs act as cis-regulatory elements which are influential to the expression of proximal genes. In part, this is enabled by euchromatic histone modifications, such as H3K27ac [64], H3K4me [55], and H4K16ac [61], which mark active promoter regions. (D) TEs are targets of DNA methylation, silencing their expression and creating enriched regions of DNA methylation [39,64–66,70].

Both TRIM28 and the KZFP transcription factor family are expressed and functional in brain. KZFPs and TRIM28 in the brain display spatiotemporal patterning over the course of development in humans and non-human primates, with slightly differing expression levels across brain regions and a decline in postnatal expression [14]. In multiple comparative analyses of gene expression in the cortical brain regions comparing humans and non-human primates, KZFPs and TE-KZFP networks were found to be over-represented in the human samples, suggesting the importance of KZFP-mediated transcriptional control of TEs in human-specific brain evolution [25,48]. Thus, the co-evolution of TEs and their KZFP regulators may have helped expand the transcriptional lexicon in human brain cells to aid in the development of specialized human brain functions.

Other protein complexes are also known to colocalize and cooperate with the KZFPs and TRIM28 to silence TEs. The human silencing hub (HUSH) complex cooperates with TRIM28 to repress TEs in embryonic stem cells [8]. HUSH contributes to both H3K9me3 and DNA methylation, in particular at evolutionarily younger TE loci [8,62,76]. A paralog of the HUSH complex, HUSH2, primarily binds at promoters for interferon-stimulated genes and KZFPs and causes de-repression of these genes, highlighting another point of coordination between different methods of TE repression [77]. The lysine methyltransferase Suv39h also establishes H3K9me3 at intact retrotransposons with some overlap to loci silenced by TRIM28 through interactions with Setdb1, although this occurs more prominently in embryonic stem cells compared to neural progenitor cells [78]. TRIM28 can also direct de novo DNA methylation via coordination with DNA methyltransferases [10,65,79]. The KZFP family of transcription factors and their co-factor TRIM28 are a major source of genomic TE control, especially in the brain. The ways in which the KZFP-TE mechanistic relationship is impacted in brain disorders is a growing area of research and the current state of the field will be discussed in more detail in the following sections.

PIWI/piRNAs

First discovered in Drosophila, the P-element–induced wimpy testis (PIWI) protein and PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), which are a class of small ncRNAs, act as a mechanism of epigenetic and post-translational modification [9,80–85]. In Drosophila, approximately 86% of PIWI-piRNA complexes bind at TE loci, generally in heterochromatic regions [9]. Interestingly, PIWI protein-coding RNAs may be derived from TE sequences themselves [86]. PIWI and piRNAs direct both DNA and histone methylation at TEs [82,85] and tend to target full-length L1 copies, which occurs in both germline and somatic cells [82]. In the mouse hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (PFC), the PIWI/piRNA system promotes DNA methylation at L1 promoters, which has the effect of TE silencing [86]. In these regions, a knockout of one mouse PIWI gene, Mili, leads to hyperactivity and decreased anxiety-like behavior in the elevated plus maze in male mice only. PiRNAs have been characterized in human brain as well, and may be relevant for neurodegenerative disorder pathology, as discussed in more detail below [80,87,88].

DNA methylation proteins

In addition to histone methylation, DNA methylation is highly implicated in TE silencing. In particular, methylation arising from MeCP2, DNMT1, and DNMT3 proteins is common at TE loci, especially L1s (Figure 1D) [39,64–66,70]. Loss of MeCP2 increases L1 retrotransposition in the mouse brain [39]. In postmortem human tissue from an individual with Rett Syndrome (a neurodevelopmental disorder with autism-like behaviors caused by a MeCP2 gene mutation [89]), there were greater L1 copy numbers in the brain compared to a control brain, and within the individual when comparing to heart tissue [39]. In human NPCs, a CRIPSR-mediated knockout of DNMT1 leads to global demethylation and increased expression of evolutionarily young, hominoid/human-specific L1 elements, such as L1HS, via increased RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) recruitment [64]. Despite the loss of DNA methylation, these young L1s still showed TRIM28-mediated H3K9me3, indicating that both DNA and histone methylation are necessary to fully silence certain TE lineages.

Activating histone modifications

Epigenetic control of TEs is not solely repressive. Activation-associated histone modifications, which often mark promoters and enhancers, also mark TEs. Indeed, TEs can function as cis-regulatory elements for canonical gene expression, especially as promoters and enhancers (Figure 1C) [12,47,90]. In the brain, this occurs in a cell-type specific manner, with differing motifs in each cell type [12]. Histone acetylation (H3K27ac and H4K16ac, specifically) has been demonstrated at L1 and ERV loci in human ESCs and NPCs, which is proposed to enable TE-derived cis-regulation [61,64]. H3K27-acetylated L1 loci have been hypothesized to act as alternative promoters of canonical genes in human NPCs given the upregulation of genes proximal to H3K27ac-marked, young, demethylated L1s—particularly genes implicated in ASD, cognitive impairment, and epilepsy [64]. In addition, L1 promoters across species often carry the active promoter histone marker H3K4 methylation, indicating some level genomic pressure for maintaining active L1 promoters [46,55,91].

Epigenetic control by TEs in brain

In addition to their capacity to form cis-regulatory genetic elements, TEs influence the epigenome in the form of ncRNAs [55,92,93] (Figure 2). For instance, Alu elements, also called SINE B2 in the mouse, are able to act as transcriptional controllers by binding at promoters and preventing RNA Pol II from establishing proper contact with the promoter sequence [97,98]. This prevents elongation of transcribed RNA, which silences transcription. In addition to acting as ncRNAs in their own right, TEs contribute to other established RNA-based forms of epigenetic regulation, including long non-coding (lnc) RNAs and miRNAs. TEs are believed to comprise 42% of lncRNA sequences and TE-derived sequences are found in over 80% of lncRNAs [94]. Moreover, one hypothesis, known as the Repeated Insertion Domains of lncRNAs or RIDL hypothesis, posits that the functional domains within lncRNAs that bind DNA, RNA, or protein are derived from TE sequences [13,99]. These are primarily derived from ERV sequences [94] but can also be L1-derived [55].

TEs contribute to the epigenetic landscape as non-coding RNAs. (A) TEs are embedded within long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) regions. The TE-derived regions of lncRNAs contribute to transcriptional regulation of genes [55,94]. (B) Within TE DNA sequences, there are regions that encode microRNAs (miRNAs) [95]. These TE-embedded miRNAs can variably regulate gene expression [96]. (C) Alu elements are transcribed, and the RNA transcripts are able to repress activity of RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II), preventing transcriptional elongation [97,98].

In addition to lncRNAs, some miRNAs are derived from TE sequences, including piRNAs, as previously discussed [86,95,96]. The origins of these TEs tend to be enriched for DNA transposons and non-LTR retrotransposons, with LINEs and SINEs both being represented [95]. These TE-embedded miRNAs, similar to other miRNAs, are present across postnatal to adolescent human brain development in a spatiotemporally dynamic manner [96]. The cerebellum, for instance, exhibits the greatest expression of TE-embedded miRNAs, with higher levels of expression in childhood (ages 1–5) compared to adolescence (ages 9–20) [96].

Genomic TEs sequences have also expanded target sequences for DNA-binding factors that are integral to overall chromatin structure (Figure 1B). TEs act as targets for CTCF binding, demarcating TADs and contributing to chromatin megastructure [16,17,19,93,100,101]. TE-derived chromatin controlling domains are species-specific, both in their TE subfamily composition and in the proportion of CTCF binding sites derived from TEs [16,101]. In a Hi-C analysis of chromatin in the mouse cerebral cortex, ERV-rich regions were demonstrated to be enriched in the heterochromatic B2 domain, which was also enriched for H3K9me3, while the euchromatic A domains were enriched for SINEs, H3K27ac, and H3K79me2 [19]. This implies that different TE classes may have been differentially co-opted within the genome, marked by unique epigenetic landmarks, or may have been stratified in terms of their successful re-insertion into organizational sites throughout evolution. The varying levels of success of TEs at chromatin organization sites are also present at the developmental level. HERV-H is particularly enriched in TAD boundaries that are differentially regulated across differentiating human embryonic stem cells, and only transcriptionally active HERV-H copies function as TADs in this context [16]. Specifically, the HERV-H promoter is enriched at TAD sites, and the 3’ end of HERV-H sequences is enriched for CTCF binding despite lack of canonical CTCF binding sequences. Moreover, transcriptionally active L1 loci have been shown to bind RNA Pol II and generate TADs in cultured cells [102]. This L1 activity is counteracted by the SAFB protein, with SAFB-bound L1s exhibiting weaker boundary strength. Together, this indicates that transcriptionally active retrotransposons in particular are enriched at active TAD sites, indicating that they may influence genomic organization. This highlights the nuanced and dynamic relationship between TEs, transcription, and genome organization.

What happens when the epigenetic control of TEs goes awry?

TEs in brain development and neurodevelopmental disorders

TEs are highly dynamic in early development, as evidenced by numerous studies of ESCs [61,78], and they are particularly relevant in brain development. Several studies of NPCs have demonstrated active TE expression and transposition as a result of epigenetic modification [59,64,103,104]. TRIM28 and KZFPs are highly expressed in the embryonic developing brain [14,59], and a CRISPR-mediated TRIM28 knockout in NPCs of the mouse fetal forebrain resulted in an upregulation of ERVs that was distinct across differentiated cell types in the adult brain [105]. L1 expression changes dramatically across development and lifespan, with L1HS subfamilies exhibiting completely distinct expression and H3K4 methylation patterns in fetal forebrain samples compared to aged brain samples [55]. ERVs and SINE families also show distinct developmental patterns, both in expression levels and in region-specific transcription [14].

Prenatal and early life experience can also modulate TE expression, transposition, and methylation, which may be particularly relevant to neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders. Maternal immune activation has been demonstrated to modulate TE repression and expression in the rodent brain [60,106–108], and it is known to be a risk factor of neurodevelopmental disorders including autism and schizophrenia [109]. Early life stress or maternal separation also lead to greater L1 copy number and lower L1 methylation in limbic regions of the mouse brain [110,111]. TE regulation is therefore essential to healthy development, and several neurodevelopmental disorders show signs of brain epigenetic TE dysregulation (Figure 3).

Changes in TE regulation and expression in neurodevelopmental disorders. (Top) Evolutionarily young TEs, such as L1s and HERVs, are typically repressed across the genome, but they exhibit increased expression in neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism (ASD) [112–115] and schizophrenia (SCZ) [116–123]. These evolutionarily young TEs also transpose back into the genome, and the locations into which they transpose are enriched for ASD and SCZ risk genes [124–127]. (Bottom) In frontocortical regions, those with neurodevelopmental disorders tend to exhibit increased expression of TEs. Intronic Alu elements and Alu elements in ASD gene network regulatory regions tend to be hypomethylated [112], and both Alu elements and LINE elements have increased expression [112,113].

Autism spectrum disorder

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a common neurodevelopmental condition with a strong social component that affects approximately 1 in every 31 children and is usually diagnosed in childhood [128]. Postmortem transcriptomic sequencing studies of brain tissue from individuals with ASD across the lifespan demonstrate upregulation of differentially expressed TEs (DETEs) across brain regions, including the PFC [112], anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [113], and cerebellum [114,115], all of which display a concurrent decrease in both DNA and histone methylation at the TE subtype studied. Both Alu elements and L1s have received attention specifically. Given the transposition competence of certain L1 families and other non-LTRs that rely upon L1 machinery [36,37], this appears consistent with evidence that ASD brains experience greater rates of transposition [112,124]. In whole genome sequencing of ASD patients with unaffected parents, de novo TE insertions are particularly prevalent in known ASD genes and ASD gene regulatory regions, in some cases resulting in loss-of-function mutations in these genes [124]. In RNA-seq of ASD brain, TE transcripts of both transposition-capable, evolutionarily young TE families and transposition-incapable, evolutionarily older TE families are identified as differentially regulated, implicating both transposition and transposition-independent mechanisms as dysregulated in ASD. Some studies show Alu element transcripts along with evolutionarily older L2 and L3 are more highly dysregulated in the PFC [112], while others found human- and primate-specific L1 enrichment in the ACC but no differential expression in the PFC [113]. This may be a result of brain-region-specific regulation or differences across studies, but the trend of non-LTR TE transcript upregulation in ASD brain is consistent across studies.

Epigenetic control of brain TEs and ASD-related gene expression are intertwined. In the PFC of individuals with ASD, hypomethylated Alu elements exist largely in introns and appear throughout the genome in cis and trans regulatory regions [112]. The genes associated with these regulatory regions were predicted by an upstream regulator analysis to be enriched for ASD-related pathways and for cell and synapse survival pathways. In vitro neuronal knockouts of ASD-related genes, including ATRX, which regulates chromatin accessibility and maintains H3K9me3, upregulated L1 mRNA [113]. Most differentially expressed L1s were intronic to active genes and were expressed independently from the expression of the exons, pointing to independent mechanisms of intronic TEs and gene transcription.

Interestingly, TE hypomethylation is reported to be correlated with ASD severity. When dividing patient groups by language impairment (i.e., verbal and non-verbal subtypes), non-verbal ASD samples showed more pronounced hypomethylation at TE loci, both in brain tissue and in patient-derived cell lines, compared to controls and verbal ASD samples [112,114,129,130]. Further, the ‘communication’ diagnostic domain of the Autism Diagnostic Interview (ADI-R) was highly correlated with PFC Alu hypomethylation in both verbal and nonverbal ASD groups [112]. This is consistent with preclinical evidence from our group that dysregulation of PFC TEs by inversion of KZFP/TRIM28 function causes social cognition-specific behavioral deficits in laboratory rodents [30,31]. Altogether, there is evidence that epigenetic TE dysregulation, specifically L1 and Alu, in the brain may play a causal role in ASD pathology and may prove useful for parsing the range of social phenotypes in ASD as well.

Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia (SCZ) affects approximately 1 in every 345 people worldwide, and although its typical onset is in late adolescence to early adulthood, it is increasingly appreciated as a neurodevelopmental condition [131,132]. Research into TE dysregulation in SCZ, particularly of HERVs, has been a long-standing interest in the field [116]. In particular, HERV-W, HERV-K, and other evolutionarily recent TEs are frequently associated with SCZ [116–123]. In cerebrospinal fluid samples, patients with recent onset of SCZ symptoms had greater detectable HERV expression, particularly HERV-W, compared to long-term SCZ patients, who experience greater expression compared to controls [119]. A recent retrotranscriptome-wide association study (rTWAS) identified three HERVs associated with schizophrenia, one of which (a MER4 originating from chromosome 20) shared an association with bipolar disorder [133]. Transgenic mice constitutively expressing HERV-W display cognitive and social deficits associated with SCZ and impaired sensorimotor gating [91]. In addition, these HERV-W transgenic mice had several downregulated hippocampal genes associated with H3K4 methylation and with ASD and SCZ compared to their wild-type littermates. Restoring H3K4me2 via inhibition of lysine-specific demethylase-1 (LSD1) ameliorated both the altered H3K4 methylation pattern in the HERV-W transgenic mice and the behavioral phenotypes without affecting wild-type controls or previously unaffected behaviors.

L1 elements are also associated with SCZ [125–127,134–137], with increased retrotransposition [125–127] and altered methylation [134–137] in both peripheral samples and in the brain. DNA methylation at L1s was increased in one study of the dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) and hippocampus [137] and generally but not uniformly decreased in three studies of peripheral blood samples [134–136], highlighting the importance of tissue-specific inquiry. L1 insertions into genes have been demonstrated to occur at higher-than-expected frequencies in SCZ brain samples. This includes insertion into known SCZ-associated genes, including NPAS1, which was identified as having an identical L1 intronic insertion in two studies of dlPFC tissue from SCZ patients [125,127]. Other TE insertions have been found in regulatory sequences of SCZ-associated genes, such as GABBR1 [118] and PRODH [138], indicating potential cis-regulation of these genes by TEs. Similar to ASD, transposition contributes to genomic changes in SCZ that may contribute to disease severity.

TEs in the aging brain and neurodegenerative disorders

Across lifespan, and particularly in aging-associated neurodegenerative disorders, brain epigenetic dysregulation is well-known to occur [42,139]. This allows for increased expression of brain TEs with age, potentially contributing to genomic instability and DNA damage that is noted to occur during aging [21,22,42,140–142]. Several recent studies have demonstrated that these changes are cell type and brain region-specific [142]. Cortical excitatory neurons exhibit the most pronounced heterochromatin dynamics, such as a loss of heterochromatin and increased accessibility of CTCF binding sites [21,22,141]. There is also a reciprocal relationship between L1 expression, epigenetic silencing capacity, and lifespan in progeroid (accelerated aging) syndromes. In both patient-derived cell lines and in mouse models, depleting or blocking L1 transcripts promotes chromatin integrity and survival [143,144]. Outside of normal aging, TE expression and loss of epigenetic regulation in the human brain have been demonstrated to be particularly heightened in aging-associated neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Parkinson’s Disease [42,48,142,145] (Figure 4).

Changes in regulation of and by TE transcripts in neurodegenerative disorders. (Top) In the whole brain, PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) repress TE expression. TE transcripts that are not repressed can be sequestered by the TDP-43 protein, preventing transposition. When hyperphosphorylated tau is present, such as in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), there is a demonstrated relationship between loss of piRNA function and increased tau toxicity [87,88]. When TDP-43 is dysfunctional, such as in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, it is no longer able to sequester TE transcripts [146]. Overall, this leads to widespread TE transcription and presence of TE transcripts in the brain [146,147]. (Bottom) TEs are typically repressed within heterochromatin, marked by H3K9me3. One exception is Alu elements, whose RNA is able to regulate the activity of RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II). In neurodegenerative disorders, evidence from the human hippocampus indicates that heterochromatin is relaxed, both via increased histone acetylation and decreased histone methylation, enabling TE transcription [148–150]. Moreover, Alu elements are processed and degraded, allowing for increased RNA Pol II activity [150,151]. This largely occurs at AD-relevant genes.

Alzheimer’s disease

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a common form of dementia, and two of the neuropathological hallmarks of AD are accumulation of hyperphosphorylated microtubule-associated protein tau and of amyloid-β protein plaques [152]. Pathological tau has been demonstrated across several model systems and postmortem human brain tissue to be associated with epigenetic changes in AD and with TE activation [87,148,149,153,154]. A Drosophila model with increased tau expression and neurotoxicity showed heterochromatin relaxation and loss of function in heterochromatin-producing proteins [87,149]. In tandem, there was increased expression of Ago3, the ortholog of human PIWIL, which regulates piRNAs. Similarly, loss of function mutations in the Drosophila gene flamenco, which encodes several piRNAs, worsened tau neurotoxicity without impacting tau protein levels [87]. In contrast, overexpression of the PIWI protein in transgenic tau-expressing Drosophila attenuated both neuronal death and differential TE expression [87]. In several mouse models of tauopathy, this pattern of increased TE expression with age was accelerated, but interestingly, transgenic amyloid-β mice did not exhibit this same degree of differential TE expression [154]. This aligns with reports that in the mouse and human brain, pathological tau expression, but not amyloid-β, is associated with the epigenetic changes that lead to neurodegeneration [148]. Analysis of human postmortem brain tissue also confirms that piRNAs and TEs are dysregulated in AD patients and patients with other tauopathies [87,88].

Similar to the bidirectional relationship between L1 expression and heterochromatin integrity in aging cells, Alu elements are both dysregulated by and cause dysregulation in AD pathology. AD brains demonstrate increased RNA Pol II activity and H3K9 acetylation related to greater processed Alu elements [150]. As previously discussed, full-length Alu and SINE B2 elements in humans and mice, respectively, are known to bind RNA Pol II and prevent transcription [97,98]. When processed, however, their ability to repress transcription is impaired. In particular, the hippocampus of AD brains showed a more marked increase in Alu processing than the dlPFC, and the levels of processed Alu elements, but not full-length Alu elements, were correlated with AD-associated upregulated genes [150]. This pattern mirrored AD progression, where intermediate stage AD patients or patients with mild cognitive impairments had intermediate Alu processing compared to healthy controls and to severe AD patients [150]. This work highlights the essential role of TEs to healthy brain aging on a transcriptional level.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and other TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43)-related neurodegenerative conditions such as frontotemporal dementia (FTD) have also received significant attention in the exploration of epigenetic regulation of TEs. TDP-43, an RNA-regulating protein that is a pathological hallmark for several neurodegenerative disorders [155], has been demonstrated across species to be enriched at reads mapped to TE transcripts in protein-RNA interaction immunoprecipitation sequencing under healthy conditions [146]. In Drosophila, TDP-43 dysfunction identified in glia leads to neuronal cell death, mediated by the Gypsy ERV [156,157]. Studies have also identified TE dysfunction in human postmortem brains from patients with TDP-43-related pathology. A study of ALS frontal cortex tissue indicated three distinguishable pathologies among patients, one of which was characterized by TDP-43 dysfunction and upregulation of retrotransposons, particularly non-LTR retrotransposons [147]. This group accounted for 20% of cases, and while there were no phenotypic differences noted, the molecular signatures were distinct. In FTD patient postmortem brain samples and TDP-43-deficient mouse brain samples, there was a decrease in TDP-43 binding to TE RNA, and a corresponding increase in the number of TE transcripts identified as TDP-43 binding partners [146]. Thus, post-transcriptional regulation of TEs, such as binding by TDP-43, may play an additional role in TE control in the brain and in neurodegenerative disease.

Parkinson’s disease

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by the degradation of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra, resulting in progressive loss of motor control and other functions [158]. Similar to other neurodegenetative disorders, TEs are increasingly implicated in PD pathology, although the causal and epigenetic mechanisms remain unclear. One avenue of research centers on primate-specific non-LTR TEs known as SINE-VNTR (variable number tandem repeat)-Alu (SVA) elements, which have been associated with PD in multiple whole genome sequencing studies. An SVA insertion in the TAF1 gene has been implicated in X-linked dystonia parkinsonism, and an SVA-containing lncRNA derived from inside the microtubule‑associated protein tau (MAPT) gene locus has been shown in multiple studies to be associated with PD [159–161]. The MAPT-associated SVA insertion, called SVA_67, can modify the expression of MAPT, though whether this occurs via cis or trans regulation remains to be determined [160]. Moreover, increased SVA expression and VNTR repeat length in PD patients are correlated with PD symptom progression, pointing to its potential utility as a PD biomarker [161,162]. L1 expression is also noted in PD patient-derived samples. High copy number of highly active, retrotransposition-competent L1s is more common in PD patient genomic sequencing compared to controls, and within the PD patient group, higher copy number of these L1s is significantly associated with PD symptom progression [163]. Increasing L1 expression in the substantia nigra via hemizygous loss of the Engrailed1 gene in mice results in cell death in the dopamine-producing neurons [164]. This characteristic neuropathology of PD was halted by expressing PIWIL1, which reversed the increased L1 expression.

HERV-K has also received significant attention in PD research. In whole blood samples from PD patients, HERV-K RNA, specifically the envelope protein, was negatively correlated with expression of miRNAs that were predicted in silico to bind HERV-K sites [165]. The authors of this study suggest that these miRNAs act as post-transcriptional control of HERV-K in response to immune activation, resulting in a decreased HERV-K expression. In cortical regions, HERV-K ENV protein has been shown to colocalize with the astroctic marker GFAP. In PD, these HERV-K ENV-positive astrocytes tend to be quiescent, while in controls they tend to be reactive [166]. Both GFAP and HERV-K ENV were decreased in PD samples in both brain and plasma, and the lower levels in plasma were correlated with greater PD progression. Collectively, this body of PD research points to several classes of retrotransposons as regulators of PD-related gene expression and as potentially useful PD biomarkers.

TEs in neuropsychiatric disorders

Epigenetic-related molecular processes are well-known to be disrupted in brain cell types across neuropsychiatric conditions such as mood disorders, stress-related disorders, and substance use disorders (SUDs) [23,84,167–174]. Compared to neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders, there are relatively fewer studies focused on epigenetic regulation of TEs in neuropsychiatric disorders, particularly as it relates to research from human brain samples. However, research in this area is accelerating and TEs are increasingly appreciated as dysregulated and differentially expressed in these disorders [133,167,175,176] (Figures 5 and 6). Thus, given evidence from other classes of brain disorders, TEs may prove a useful new avenue for research into the neurobiological underpinnings of these complex disorders [171].

Substance use disorders alter TE-associated chromatin landscape. (Top) In human brain, long-term substance use increases permissive histone methylation [173] and histone acetylation [168]. There is also upregulation of L1 and HERV elements [173,177]. L1 elements have been shown to have insertions into genes, particularly substance use-related genes (SUD genes) [177]. (Bottom) In the rodent brain, in regions including the nucleus accumbens and dentate gyrus there is a general loss of methylation, including H3K9me3, H3K9me2, and DNA methylation at L1 elements [178–180]. There is also an increase in gene-TE fusion transcripts [181]. Cocaine use has also been shown to induce CREB-related ZFP189 transcription, potentially contributing to downstream TE regulation [28].

Chronic stress reorganizes TE chromatin accessibility in rodent brain. In rodent models of chronic stress, in the hippocampus, there is an increase in H3K9me3 corresponding to a decrease in TE expression [182]. There is an exception to this at the B2 SINE TE, which has decreased H3K9me3 [183], and at L1 elements, which may be upregulated in a region- and strain-specific manner [184,185].

Substance use disorders

In 2023, approximately 17% of the U.S. population age 12 and older met the criteria for a SUD [186]. SUDs, including alcohol use disorder, stimulant use disorders, and opioid use disorder, are well characterized to induce changes in brain chromatin state [23,168,172,187]. There is increasing evidence that TEs are dysregulated in humans who have engaged in disordered drug use as well [173,177,188,189] (Figure 5), though few studies have examined the intersection of these two areas, particularly in the brain.

In a neuroblastoma cell line, both cocaine and methamphetamine exposure induced L1 transposition without a corresponding increase in L1 mRNA production or double-stranded breaks in the DNA [190]. The same effect was not present in a hepatocellular carcinoma cell line and was particularly localized to the heterochromatin fraction of the neuroblastoma cells in a subcellular fractionation. Interestingly, an siRNA-mediated knockdown of CREB activity prevented transposition [190]. The involvement of CREB in epigenetic response to stimulants is consistent with reports that CREB regulates ZFP189 [28], a KZFP that exhibits preferential binding to L2s in humans [69]. In mouse nucleus accumbens (NAc), including both D1 and D2 medium spiny neurons, Zfp189 transcripts accumulate in response to chronic cocaine exposure, and CRISPR-mediated recruitment of CREB to the Zfp189 promoter was sufficient to alter cocaine-related behaviors [28].

In rodent models of stimulant use disorders, epigenetic repression of TEs, particularly L1s, has been shown to be decreased. In rats, neurotoxic doses of methamphetamine caused hypomethylation of L1 promoters, leading to increased L1 expression, specifically in regions associated with adult neurogenesis, such as the dentate gyrus [178]. Mice treated with seven days of cocaine differentially expressed TE-gene fusion transcripts of several genes implicated in reward in the NAc compared to saline-treated controls, suggesting changes to transcriptional regulation in response to cocaine [181]. ChIP-seq of the mouse NAc performed after seven days of cocaine administration showed a decrease in H3K9me3, specifically at intergenic TE loci [179]. While no studies have currently resolved a brain epigenetic mechanism of TE dysregulation in human patients with stimulant use disorders, blood samples from people who use nicotine products, both vaping and smoking, and who exhibited methamphetamine-induced paranoia have shown DNA hypomethylation at L1 loci [135,184,189,191].

Despite the lack of insight into the brain epigenetic mechanisms that control TEs in stimulant use disorders, there is emerging evidence from human brain samples that transposition does occur in response to chronic drug use. In the human mPFC, people who died from a cocaine use disorder (CUD)-related cause had greater novel L1 insertions within genes, while controls had greater novel insertions between genes. These intragenic insertions in CUD patients were found to be preferentially located in genes related to signal transduction and were present in both neuronal and non-neuronal cells [177]. This was similarly seen in peripheral samples of general intravenous drug users, who had greater rates of HERV-K HML-2 insertions in the dopamine-related gene RASGRF2 compared to controls [188]. Combined with the established link between drug use and chromatin dynamics, this hints at a connection between drug-use-induced changes to chromatin accessibility and biased TE transposition at SUD-relevant genes.

In addition to stimulant use, epigenetic regulation of TEs has also been studied in the context of alcohol and opioids. Opioid exposure drives euchromatin formation in human brain, including increased H3K27 acetylation [168]. A microarray study of postmortem human brain tissue from patients with alcohol use disorder revealed an increase in ERV transcription, along with a decrease in DNA methylation and an increase in H3K4me3 in the frontal cortex [173]. Interestingly, TRIM28 was also upregulated across the amygdala and frontal cortex, which the authors posit is a compensatory response to the ERV upregulation [173]. Opioids, particularly morphine, similarly cause epigenetic responses at TE loci. In cultured human neuroblastoma cells, short-term morphine administration leads to hypermethylation of DNA while long-term morphine exposure leads to hypomethylation, which held true at L1 loci and was reflected in L1 mRNA levels [185,192]. In addition, as with cocaine and methamphetamine, morphine and fentanyl have both been shown to induce L1 transposition in human neuroblastoma cells specifically, and this occurred in a dose-dependent manner and required functioning Toll-like receptor 4 activity [193]. In the mouse NAc, repeated morphine exposure leads to a decrease in H3K9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) that was largely seen at TE loci, particularly ERVs and L1s [180]. Several of the genomic loci with differential H3K9me2 post-morphine were genes involved in glutamatergic neurotransmission. Overall, several lines of evidence from SUD research point to a link from epigenetic regulation of TEs to TE expression and transposition to genes associated with substance use, making this an important area for future study.

Mood disorders

Mood disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD), affect an estimated 9.5% of American adults each year [194]. Inspired by the established association of SCZ with HERVs, particularly HERV-W, studies have also assessed HERV transcript levels in BD and MDD using microarrays [117,120] and more recently rTWAS [133]. In the ACC and hippocampus of individuals with BD, MDD, or SCZ, there was a trend of downregulation of HERV-W GAG proteins compared to healthy controls [117]. There also appear to be a few modest but significant differences in HERV expression levels detected in a microarray panel of dlPFC samples from BD patients compared to controls, with both up- and down-regulated TEs detected [120]. Blood bisulfite sequencing of BD patient samples shows a hypomethylation at the 5’ UTR of L1 elements at a CpG site distinct from one hypomethylated in SCZ samples [134], suggesting that the location of an epigenetic modification within a TE may influence neurological outcomes.

In MDD, brain TE transcripts are downregulated in patient postmortem brain RNA-seq compared to controls in a brain-wide analysis [195]. At the cell type-specific level in the dlPFC, however, there is a broad bidirectional dysregulation of TE transcripts, most prominently in excitatory neurons, but also in inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes. While this study used RNA-seq data and thus does not directly interrogate epigenetic factors, there were noted upregulations of KZFP encoding transcripts both at the whole brain and the cell type levels, suggesting an imbalance in the relationship between KZFPs and TEs in MDD brain. Moreover, repressed TEs were highly enriched as introns of immune genes, largely interferon-related genes [195]. This may connect existing bodies of literature implicating TEs in immune regulation [30,31,73,196] and immune dysregulation in MDD [197,198].

Despite a current lack of understanding of directly TE-related epigenetic mechanisms in MDD and BD, there is other evidence for epigenetic changes that indicate mood disorders may be more fully understood by studying TEs. For example, lncRNAs in the brain act as chromatin remodelers and are associated with both MDD and BD [199,200]. LncRNAs, while a distinct class of genetic elements, are known to contain TEs and TE-derived sequences that may act as the primary functional units within lncRNAs [13]. DNA methylation imbalances between the dlPFC and temporal pole cortex have also been demonstrated in MDD and BD patients compared to controls, suggesting general epigenetic differences [170]. Moreover, recent preliminary evidence suggests that postmortem dlPFC tissue from BD patients shows hallmarks of accelerated epigenetic aging [201], potentially contributing to the increased risk of BD patients for ALZ, another potential link to epigenetic regulation of TEs in BD pathology.

Post-traumatic stress disorder

To date, no human studies of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have examined epigenetic regulation of TEs in the brain, although several studies have examined the effects of stress in animal models on brain TE epigenetic regulation. Acute restraint stress in rats caused an upregulation of Suv39h-associated H3K9me3 at TE loci and at canonical gene introns in the hippocampus [182]. This corresponded to decreased overall TE mRNA expression observed specifically in the hippocampus but not in the frontal cortex, cerebellum, or in tissues outside the brain. In contrast, shock-enhanced fear learning in rats produced L1 upregulation in the amygdala, and chronic restraint stress upregulated L1s in the hippocampus in certain mouse strains [202,203], indicating that acute and chronic stress effects may differ at certain TE loci. Acute peripheral corticosterone administration increased global H3K9me3 in the rat hippocampus but decreased it at specifically B2 SINE elements, corresponding to an increase in B2 SINE mRNA [183]. Further associating B2 SINE in particular with the stress response, B2 SINE RNA contains a glucocorticoid response element (GRE)-like site [204]. This alters glucocorticoid receptor binding at canonical GREs, causing differential regulation at glucocorticoid receptor regulated genes in glioblastoma cell culture. This suggests B2 SINE elements are both regulated by and play a regulatory role in glucocorticoid-mediated stress effects. Thus, certain TEs and their epigenetic regulation seem to be stress-responsive in a brain-region-specific manner.

In human serum samples from U.S. soldiers pre- versus post-deployment, bisulfite sequencing revealed that those who went on to develop PTSD showed differential DNA methylation at L1 and Alu elements, indicating that peripherally, PTSD diagnosis was associated with epigenetic TE control [205]. Interestingly, while there were no differences in L1 methylation pre-deployment, only those who did not develop PTSD showed an increase in L1 methylation post-deployment, while those who developed PTSD showed no change. This suggests that peripheral silencing of L1s may be involved in, or be a marker of, stress resilience. Additionally, Alu element methylation increased in PTSD cases post-deployment and was higher than controls pre-deployment [205], suggesting that existing TE regulation may be related to vulnerability to developing PTSD. Taken together, the rodent brain and human peripheral sample studies suggest that dynamic epigenetic regulation of TEs is occurring in response to extreme stress, but this mechanism warrants further study in the human brain.

Conclusions

Brain TEs are dynamic, experience-responsive, and capable of causing lasting alterations to genomic functions via transposition and transposition-independent mechanisms. TEs and associated epigenetic mechanisms are therefore under-appreciated facilitators of the transcriptional and functional plasticity which is a defining function of the nervous system. TEs are emerging as important markers and mediators of brain disorders, and thus should not be overlooked in studies aimed at uncovering the nuclear phenomena associated with brain diseases [206].

TEs are able to facilitate a variety of chromatin and gene expression changes. Transposition events can alter the DNA landscape, producing both beneficial and deleterious mutations [38,103,207]. TEs act as crucial loci of chromatin organization, both appearing at TAD boundaries and acting as sites for histone modifications [15,17,100]. TEs are well-documented participants in cis regulation of important brain genes [11,12,47,49,61]. Epigenetic changes that occur in response to experiences like infection, aging, drug exposure, and stress all impact TE activity [21,22,60,106,110,179,182,208], and disruption to brain TE regulation impacts the behavioral response to many of these experiences as well [29–31]. Altogether, this positions epigenetic control of brain TEs as a sensitive and dynamic mechanism involved in facilitating molecular and behavioral adaptations.

In addition to direct regulation of and by TEs in the brain, TE gene products can trigger downstream mechanisms that may be relevant to the brain disorders discussed. In particular, inflammation is a hallmark of many brain disorders [158,198,209,210]. TEs, many of which are derived from viral sequences, are capable of triggering innate immune reactions when their transcripts or gene products are cellularly expressed [105,211–213]. Interferon-related activity is noted to be sensitive to L1 RNA in particular [212–214]. The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) system can be triggered by L1 ORF1p in the cytoplasm, mobilizing to prevent L1 transposition [214]. In contrast, we have noted that KZFP-mediated de-repression of brain TE loci is associated with a rapid downregulation of interferon-related gene transcription [30,31,215], likely via impeded cis regulatory contacts between TEs and these immune genes on the genomic level. These opposing results suggest that genomic TE DNA sequences and TE gene products may contribute to immune regulation via distinct mechanisms and at multiple levels. This warrants a nuanced conversation on the role of TEs in immune processes, which are central to brain disorders.

Despite the growing appreciation for TE-related mechanisms, TEs are still typically excluded in bioinformatic pipelines due to practices that exclude multi-mapping reads, as well as several technical challenges due to the repetitive and polymorphic nature of TEs (see Lanciano and Cristofari (2020) for an in-depth review of these challenges and possible bioinformatic solutions [216]). However, several analytical methods currently exist to include or probe TEs in large bioinformatic datasets (for a comprehensive guide, see for example O’Neill et al. [217]). These include sequencing pipelines such as L1-seq, which can identify L1 insertions [218], or long read RNA sequencing techniques such as PacBio Iso-Seq or CELLO-seq (single cell long read sequencing), which are able to eliminate some ambiguity of mapping repetitive sequences [55,219]. Moreover, a novel analysis pipeline, TE-seq, has been developed that has the option to provide a long-read sequenced reference genome specific to the animals under investigation, which is then used to map short-read RNA-seq data with higher confidence [220]. Several analytical pipelines for identifying TE transcripts in bulk RNA-seq data currently exist, including TEtranscripts [221] and RepEnrich2 [222]. These accessible methods are particularly useful as they can be applied to existing short-read, bulk RNA-seq datasets. Bulk RNA-seq can also be analyzed in novel ways that can identify TEs with higher confidence, such as with de novo genome guided assembly [122], or can identify fusion transcripts between TEs and canonical gene exons [181]. Given the demonstrated importance of cell type specificity, analysis of single cell and single nuclei sequencing data that accounts for TEs is likely to yield more nuanced findings, including identifying cell-specific somatic transposition events that are obscured by bulk analysis [223]. Methods for analysis of single cell sequencing currently include analysis pipelines SoloTE [224] and scTE [225], which make use of scRNA-seq data. Though their repetitive, multi-mapping nature presents some bioinformatic challenges, methods for accurately analyzing and mapping TEs add a depth of insight into transcriptomic datasets, enabling further exploration.

TEs and TE epigenetic regulation may be a useful and novel area for biomarker research. There is considerable evidence that epigenetic control of TEs and corresponding TE expression distinguishes subtypes or severity of a variety of brain disorders [112,114,147,150,161–163]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a novel avenue for assessing brain biomarkers, particularly showing promise in neurodegenerative disorders [226–228]. L1HS elements have been observed to be enriched in postmortem human AD brain EVs [226]. Peripherally measured EVs from both humans and canines suggest that increased TE RNA presence is a hallmark of age-related neurodegenerative conditions [227,228]. If change in TE expression in peripheral EVs can be reliably correlated to observed brain TE dysregulation, this may provide a novel avenue for TE-based biomarkers of brain disorders. DNA methylation at TEs in blood or serum may also distinguish brain disease states, such as in the case of PTSD [205]. Development of TE-based biomarkers may add not only to diagnosis but also to our understanding of the genetic mechanisms of a variety of disorders.

Given the ubiquity of TE expression and regulation changes in brain disorders, TEs also provide a compelling target to enhance our treatment of these disorders. Retrotransposons, in particular L1s, are responsive to reverse transcriptase inhibitors currently on the market, and studies have demonstrated functional improvements in animal models, particularly of aging and neurodegeneration, as a result of their use [87,140,143,229]. A recent Phase 2A clinical trial of 3TC, a nucleoside analog reverse transcriptase inhibitor therapy, in people with mild cognitive impairments demonstrated improved CSF and serum biomarker concentrations and no change in cognitive status over six months [230]. In this trial, the drug was well-tolerated, suggesting targeting retrotransposition may be a feasible treatment avenue. On the other hand, several common antipsychotic drugs have also been shown to increase TE expression via epigenetic mechanisms such as inhibition of histone deacetylase or DNA demethylation [231,232]. Given that SCZ patients tend to display upregulation of several TE subclasses, targeting the epigenetic mechanisms involved in aberrant TE expression may yield therapeutic outcomes distinct from traditional antipsychotics. Determining the tractability of TEs as therapeutic targets for brain disorders must be carefully evaluated in future studies.

In sum, TEs and TE-related epigenetic mechanisms are a sensitive, dynamic, and under-studied molecular axis involved in brain function and dysfunction. TE-centric molecular processes warrant further investigation as potential markers of and contributors to human brain disorders.
