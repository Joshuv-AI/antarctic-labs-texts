Field Epidemiology Training Program global evaluation: application of graduates’ skills
Authors: Emily Moore, Whitney L. Rémy, Lauren Courtney, Briana Lucido, Meredith Pinto, Sara Albanna, Lise Martel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP) is a three-tier (Frontline, Intermediate, and Advanced) practical training that strengthens the capacity of the global public health workforce to detect and respond to epidemics. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has supported the development of FETPs worldwide for over 40 years. A global cross-sectional evaluation was conducted with graduates from CDC-supported FETP Intermediate and Advanced programs to understand the application of FETP in their current job and its impact on their career progression.MethodsAn online survey with Intermediate and Advanced FETP graduates was conducted from March to May 2022. Purposive and snowball sampling was used to recruit survey participants. Each participant received the survey link via email. The survey included questions on demographics, FETP competencies and functional areas, and the impact of training on career. Poststratification weights were applied based on the known proportions of the study population, and univariate and bivariate frequency distributions were calculated. Bivariate cross-tabulations for association were tested using the Rao–Schott Chi-square test of independence.ResultsThe sample included 1493 graduates representing CDC-supported FETP programs from 49 countries. Most graduates reported that FETP helped them achieve their current job (87%) and career aspirations (90%) and has been helpful to the organizations for which they worked (95%).ConclusionsThe FETP competencies are relevant and helpful to graduates’ professional work. Background

For over 40 years, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has supported the development of Field Epidemiology Training Programs (FETP) worldwide. FETP is a training that strengthens the capacity of countries’ local public health workforce to detect and respond to epidemics through practical application of learning (75% field work vs. 25% classroom instruction) to everyday work [1, 2]. FETP trainees and residents conduct surveillance activities and outbreak investigations to prevent, detect, and control communicable diseases [1]. There are three levels of FETP: advanced (2-year program), intermediate (9-month program), and frontline (3-month program). Since the inception of FETP, CDC has supported the training of nearly 20,000 FETP graduates in over 80 countries [2]. The impact of FETP has been documented through country and regional program evaluations [3–8].

A global cross-sectional evaluation of graduates from CDC-supported FETP Intermediate and Advanced programs was conducted to understand the application of FETP in their current job and its impact on their career progression. This survey offered information about CDC-supported FETPs from the perspective of FETP graduates. It describes to what extent FETP graduates are applying competencies learned from their training, particularly in emergency or outbreak response, what skills FETP graduates need to acquire or learn after their graduation from FETP to have a successful career in public health, and how the completion of FETP has affected graduates’ career progression.

Methods

Participants

Participants were graduates from CDC-supported FETP Intermediate and Advanced programs in 49 countries. Participants were offered an incentive of $9-$11USD cellular phone credit via Ding, an international mobile recharge service. The availability of Ding was limited; thus, only countries where Ding was available were included in the study.

Sampling

Available data kept by the CDC indicated that 6811 individuals had graduated from the Intermediate (1653) and Advanced (5164) programs across the globe as of August 2021. A target sample size of 1500 respondents was calculated using a proportional allocation approach to ensure reliable estimates for each stratum by training level and region with a margin of error of ± 5%.

Without a central repository of graduate contact information, nonprobability sampling approaches were used to create a sampling frame. First, purposive sampling was used by obtaining graduates’ email addresses from country FETP programs. Next, a prompt at the end of the survey asked respondents to recommend additional participants and their email addresses. Contact information for 3805 graduates was collected using these approaches (3710 from FETP programs and 95 from the snowball sampling).

Survey instrument

The online survey was modeled on surveys conducted with FETP programs in the Democratic Republic of Congo and the United Kingdom [9, 10]. A final draft of the survey was pretested in English, Spanish, and French, and cognitive interviews in each language were conducted to ensure the validity of the tool. The survey was translated into four additional languages to expand the reach to CDC-supported graduates: Arabic, Indonesian, Portuguese, and Russian. Next, the survey was piloted with 36 FETP graduates in English, Spanish, French, Arabic, Portuguese, and Indonesian to further evaluate the questions and test the feasibility of distribution. Minor changes were made to the survey to incorporate the pilot feedback.

The finalized questionnaire (see Additional File 3) included 74 questions: open-ended (11), multiple selection (4), single selection (33), and dichotomous (26). After participants provided written consent to participate in the survey, they were presented with study questions organized in the following topic areas: demographics (21), FETP competencies and functional areas (45), and FETP impact on career (8).

Measures

Demographics. The respondents were categorized into two groups based on the highest level of FETP completed: advanced graduates who completed a 2-year FETP program and Intermediate graduates who completed a 9-month FETP program. Information was collected on the respondents’ sex, age, country where FETP was completed, years since graduation, current type of work, supervisory experience, and participation in the COVID-19 response.

FETP competencies and functional areas. This section of the survey assessed five FETP competencies of CDC-supported programs listed in Table 1. For each of these FETP competencies, the respondents rated their confidence in performing the competency, its relevance to their current work, and, when applicable, how helpful it was for their COVID-19 response work on a 5-point Likert scale. Respondents rated how often they perform each competency in their current job by selecting frequency options on a 6-point Likert scale. Respondents were also asked about their supervisory experience and to indicate which of the five FETP competencies they supervise in their work. Respondents were then asked to assess whether the 17 functional areas (see Additional File 1) were helpful or not helpful in their current job and to select the top 5 most helpful. Lastly, respondents were asked open-ended questions about additional skills and knowledge that were helpful or should be included.

FETP impact on career. In the final section of the survey, respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale with the following statements: FETP training helped me achieve my current job or role; taking part in FETP training helped me achieve my career aspirations; and taking part in FETP training has been beneficial to organizations that I work/worked for. Respondents also indicated whether they have worked outside of the government since completing FETP in this section

Data collection

The survey was administered on a rolling basis via Qualtrics from March 1, 2022, to May 15, 2022, by sending a confidential, personalized link to each graduate. Participants received one to three reminder emails over 2 months to complete the survey. Those who completed the survey were eligible for a $9–$11 USD cellular phone credit via Ding.

Ethics, consent and permissions

Access to the survey required informed consent, and participation was voluntary, with the option to terminate involvement at any time. Answers to the survey questions were kept confidential, and the records were de-identified for analysis. This study was determined to be non-human research and was therefore exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) oversight, also receiving approval through the CDC’s COVID-19 Public Health Emergency Paperwork Reduction Act waiver.

To identify response variability across groups, univariate and bivariate weighted frequency distributions were calculated in SAS using SURVEYFREQ. Variables included in bivariate cross-tabulations were tested for association using the Rao–Schott Chi-square test of independence. Chi-square tests with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Open-ended responses were translated using DeepL and reviewed by a professional translation agency, then analyzed using a content analysis approach [11]. Two analysts completed the analysis by grouping similar content into corresponding codes and then grouping the codes into themes. The first analyst coded the responses using inductive coding stored in a qualitative codebook and reviewed them for validity. In contrast, the second analyst used the validated codebook to code the responses without seeing the first analyst’s codes. The analysts then convened to discuss and resolve coding discrepancies. The codes for each record were merged with the large dataset to calculate the weighted frequency distribution of the codes by participant demographics.

Results

The final sample included 1493 individuals for a response rate of 39%. Of those who responded, 94% completed the entire survey. Participants completed the survey in seven languages, with 951 (60%) completing the survey in English. Respondents who completed both training levels (n = 33) were sorted into advanced because it was their highest FETP tier completed.

Survey respondents represented CDC-supported programs in 49 countries and included 1117 (75%) advanced graduates and 376 (25%) intermediate graduates. Respondents represented four training regions, with the highest proportion from Africa (41%) and Asia–Pacific (APAC; 33%). Most completed their training 2 to 9 years ago (74%), but intermediate graduate participants tended to have graduated more recently than advanced graduates (mean difference of 3 years for Intermediate graduates vs. 6 years for advanced).

Most respondents supervised others (83%) and worked in surveillance/data or administration, education, and communication. Most respondents reported working on the COVID-19 response (82%).

Confidence performing FETP competencies

Eighty-seven percent of all participants were confident performing each competency independently or as experts. Although these results are not intended to compare groups but to assess alignment with expected outcomes of each training tier, advanced graduate participants tended to rate themselves as experts more often across all competencies (Fig. 1). More participants were confident in analyzing surveillance data (95%) and conducting an outbreak investigation (95%) than in conducting a planned epidemiologic study (87%).Fig. 1Participants reported confidence in performing each competency by tier

Participants reported confidence in performing each competency by tier

Participants who trained in APAC and Latin America and Caribbean (LAC) regions tended to rate themselves as experts less often than those from programs in other regions. For example, 54% of participants from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) and 53% from Africa rated themselves as experts in conducting an outbreak investigation compared to 37% of APAC and 24% of LAC participants.

Participants who primarily supervised others were more confident conducting a planned epidemiologic study (90%) than non-supervisors (78%) and rated themselves as experts more often than non-supervisors across all other competencies.

Relevance of FETP competencies

Over 70% of all participants reported that each competency was very or extremely relevant to their current work. The competencies rated very or extremely relevant most often by graduates were analyzing surveillance data (83%) and communicating public health information (83%), while conducting a planned epidemiology study (71%) was rated very or extremely relevant least often among graduates.

Participants from Africa rated all competencies as very or extremely relevant more often than participants from other regions (Table 5). Supervisors rated all competencies as more relevant than non-supervisors. For example, 50% of supervisors found analyzing surveillance data extremely relevant to their current job compared to 35% of non-supervisors.

Frequency of performing FETP competencies

Analyzing surveillance data was reported to be performed monthly by 70% of participants compared to conducting a planned epidemiology study, which was performed once a quarter or less by 86% of graduates.

Communicating health information was reported to be performed less often by APAC participants than by graduates from other regions. Forty percent of APAC participants reported performing the competency at least monthly, as compared to 57–69% from other regions.

Supervisors indicated that they performed all the FETP competencies more frequently than non-supervisors. The largest difference was found in analyzing surveillance data, with 74% of supervisors versus 54% of non-supervisors performing the competency monthly or more frequently.

Helpfulness of FETP competencies for COVID-19 response

Most participants who worked on the COVID-19 response found all competencies helpful to their response work, with at least 82% rating each competency as very or extremely helpful. Analyzing surveillance data and conducting an outbreak investigation were rated as most helpful, as 92% indicated both competencies were very or extremely helpful.

Participants from APAC rated the competencies as slightly less helpful for their COVID-19 response work compared to graduates from other regions.

Supervision of FETP competencies

Participants who primarily supervise or manage other staff selected which competencies they supervise. Analyzing surveillance data (63%) was the competency supervisors oversaw the most, while conducting a planned epidemiologic study (41%) was the competency that supervisors oversaw the least. These results mirror the results rating the relevance and performance of the competencies.

Helpful functional areas and skills

All 17 functional areas were rated as helpful in preventing, detecting, and responding to disease outbreaks, including COVID-19, by at least 83% of participants. Most participants (95% or more) rated biostatistics, emergency management or response, leadership and management, and teaching or instructing others in field epidemiology as helpful in their work. Three of these four areas were also selected in participants’ top five most helpful functional areas in their work: biostatistics (42%), emergency management or response (41%), and leadership and management (31%).

Through qualitative data collection, participants listed additional knowledge and skills they thought should be included in the FETP. Participants indicated they would like additional training in the following topic areas: epidemiology and surveillance (28%), including advanced statistical analysis, statistical modeling, informatics, and spatial epidemiology; communication and dissemination (9%), including general communication, risk communication, scientific writing, and developing manuscripts; and software (7%), including R, SAS, SPSS, or STATA. Nine percent of the participants identified wanting additional training opportunities in general such as longer or more training, continued or follow-up training, and a desire to participate in exchange programs with other countries. Twenty percent of participants indicated they had no additional training needs. See additional data in Supplemental material.

FETP impact on career

Most participants indicated that FETP had helped them achieve their current job position and career aspirations and was helpful to the organizations for which they worked (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Participants’ agreement with statements about FETP’s impact on their career

Participants’ agreement with statements about FETP’s impact on their career

Over 80% of participants have worked for one to three organizations since they graduated from FETP (37% for one, 29% for two, 17% for three, and 13% for four or more).

Forty percent of participants reported that they have worked outside the government since graduating from FETP. The trend was higher for advanced graduate participants (45%) than for intermediate graduate participants (27%). Moreover, working outside the government was associated with having graduated from FETP more recently.

A larger proportion of supervisors indicated that FETP helped them achieve their current job position (88%), or career aspiration (92%), and has been beneficial for organizations they work for (96%) than non-supervisors (84%, 85%, and 93%, respectively).

Although no significant difference between women and men was found in the use of competencies, more women (92%) than men (85%) agreed that training helped them achieve their current job position.

Discussion

Advanced and intermediate graduate participants from CDC-supported FETPs reported that they are applying their FETP to their work, the competencies are relevant to their work, and they are confident in performing the competencies independently, and often as experts. Graduates were most confident in analyzing surveillance data and performed this competency most frequently, which has occurred in another similar smaller scale evaluation [12]. Furthermore, participants’ confidence in performing competencies increased with training level. Advanced graduates reported high confidence and more often reported themselves as experts. These results match expectations that self-reported confidence level would be higher amongst the advanced-level group. Additionally, the current high confidence levels (≥ 78% of participants reporting expert or independent confidence levels for each category) of intermediate participants are reassuring that the Intermediate curriculum is generally successful in teaching the FETP competencies.

Although more graduates were confident in conducting outbreak investigations than communicating public health information, the latter was perceived as more relevant, performed more frequently, and supervised by more graduates. This highlights a potential need to improve training materials on public health communication in FETP training.

Graduates indicated that FETP had a beneficial impact on their careers in terms of achieving their current job positions and career aspirations. They also indicated that FETP has been beneficial for the organizations in which they work. Together, these results validate that FETP is likely recognized as a meaningful professional development program. Additionally, FETP graduates who serve as supervisors reported a greater impact on their careers than non-supervisors. Further research to explore the impact of FETP on graduates’ careers could identify how many FETP graduates are promoted from a non-supervisory role to a supervisory role after they complete FETP, if there is any association between completed FETP tier and job position or promotions and whether people who were supervisors before FETP are earning more promotions than those who were not supervisors before. These findings could identify the point in a person’s career that FETP would be most beneficial, which could influence FETP recruitment and explore whether a leadership and management module specifically tailored to the needs of FETP graduates in supervisory positions could have any additional benefit for graduates or the organizations in which they work.

The finding that 40% of participating graduates have worked outside the government is concerning as FETP is primarily designed to expand the field epidemiology and public health capacities of governments [1]. Graduates may leave the government for multiple reasons, such as for competitive salaries and benefits outside of the government [13], but further research would be needed to confirm what is pulling FETP graduates away from their government positions and how the government can better ensure that FETP graduates are retained.

Women and men participants shared similar perceptions of the training program and the use of FETP competencies. Nevertheless, women were significantly more likely to agree that the FETP helped them achieve their current roles than men. Further research is needed to better understand why this is the case.

Supervisors were more confident in their skills than non-supervisors and found the FETP competencies more relevant to their current work. Supervisors performed competencies more frequently and found competencies more helpful to COVID-19 response work than those who do not supervise others. These findings validate that the competencies highlighted by the FETP program are valuable for field epidemiologists, especially those growing their careers as managers. During the COVID-19 pandemic, FETP graduates were highly sought out for their expertise. A large proportion of FETP graduates contributed to the COVID-19 response, echoing what has been cited by others [14, 15]. Bell et al. found that during the COVID-19 response, FETP graduates contributed to response efforts focused on country-level coordination, surveillance, rapid response teams, case investigations, activities at points of entry, and risk communication and community engagement [15]. Interestingly, graduates who trained in Africa, and secondarily in MENA, reported FETP competencies as highly relevant for their current job and helpful for COVID-19 response work more often when compared to graduates from LAC and APAC. This could be a result of differing backgrounds, job responsibilities, or cultural behaviors that influenced how participants answered the survey questions, but it also suggests that there are likely different region or country priorities, public health structures, and needs. Since each FETP is encouraged to adapt its curriculum and objectives over time to align with its country’s public health priorities [13, 16], the regional differences found in this survey, including confidence level, could indicate that it may be important to standardize and update the quality of the core FETP curriculum to ensure more even quality across all regions. It also highlights an opportunity to further evaluate the impact of FETP curriculum adaptations to better understand what adaptations have been most effective and why, which could then inform future curriculum updates. On the other hand, the COVID-19 pandemic response highlights that despite regional differences, FETP graduates are important on a global scale, acting as frontline public health forces that protect their communities from disease outbreaks and respond to public health emergencies [14, 15].

Study limitations

The possible effects of some biases on the generalizability of these results must be acknowledged. During the survey distribution, 401 of 3805 links were undeliverable, raising some concern for nonresponse bias. Emails may have been undeliverable or missing in disproportionate numbers from specific subgroups, such as those who graduated less recently. However, the 39% response rate is acceptable for online surveys, and the extremely high completion rate of 94% reduces those concerns. In addition, there is a bias due to the self-reporting nature of the survey as respondents may rate their skills differently based on the varying epidemiological capacity levels between countries.

Although the sample target of 1500 was met, there were difficulties gathering contact information for Intermediate graduates, raising some concern for sampling bias, as this group was somewhat underrepresented in this sample (600 Intermediate responses were expected, but 379 responses were received). To reduce this bias, a poststratification approach was used to proportionally match the sample population to the known proportions of the population and applied weights to calculate more representative frequencies. Overall sex of participant percentages of the FETP population were not known, so sex was not used when applying weights; thus, women and men may not be represented accurately in our sample.

The survey was designed for both advanced and intermediate graduates, but each population was analyzed separately. Tailoring the survey to each tier could have provided more detailed information about each tier’s specific competencies and functional areas.

When the survey asked graduates about the functional areas, only spatial epidemiology and social epidemiology included definitions in their descriptions; thus, other functional areas were open to graduates’ interpretation.

Conclusions

Responses from advanced and intermediate FETP graduates indicate that FETP teaches applied epidemiology competencies that are relevant and helpful in their careers. The overall confidence in performing these competencies supports that the intermediate and advanced FETP curricula generally teach the competencies well. However, there is still room for improvement, such as adding more epidemiology, surveillance, and software skills, either during FETP or as post-FETP continuing education, and more effectively teaching health communication competencies.

Graduates see FETP as helpful for achieving their career goals and beneficial to the organizations for which they work. Considering that many FETP graduates get promoted and are in supervisory positions, over time, there is a potential for growth in the impact of FETP graduates on leadership decisions and the overall public health system of a country. However, the high attrition rates of FETP graduates working outside of the government need to be further explored and addressed.
