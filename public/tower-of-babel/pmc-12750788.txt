Impact of late ventricular fibrillation on postdischarge 1-year mortality of patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction: a nationwide retrospective study
Authors: Yael Israeli Hadar, Moti Haim, Gal Tsaban, Yigal Abramowitz, Blake Filderman, Rodrigue Garcia, Hilmi Alnsasra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the impact of in-hospital late ventricular fibrillation (VF) (>48 hours) on the 1-year mortality risk among patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) who survived the index hospitalisation.DesignRetrospective cohort study estimating the incidence rates of late VF following AMI and the associated 1-year risk of all-cause mortality.SettingCardiac intensive care units (CICUs) in Israel between the years 2000 and 2018.ParticipantsPatients presenting with AMI (ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation MI (NSTEMI)) who were admitted to CICUs.ResultsA total of 14 280 consecutive AMI patients of whom 118 developed late VF and 68 of these survived the index hospitalisation. Patients with late VF had higher mortality rates within 1 year following AMI overall (54.8% vs 10.2%, p<0.01), among STEMI patients (54.7% vs 10%, p<0.01) and among those with NSTEMI (55.2% vs 10.5%, p<0.01). Late VF post-MI was found associated with increased risk of 1-year mortality (adjusted HR (aHR) 3.25, 95% CI (1.98 to 5.34), p<0.01). While this association was statistically significant among STEMI patients (aHR 3.6, 95% CI (2.06 to 6.30), p<0.01), it was not statistically significant among NSTEMI patients (aHR=1.87, 95% CI (0.6 to 5.84), p=0.29).ConclusionsLate VF was found to be associated with increased 1-year mortality risk among patients presenting with AMI. However, this association was only significant among STEMI patients, but not NSTEMI patients.

Introduction

Approximately 5% of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients still experience sustained ventricular arrhythmias (VA).13 Most notably, ventricular fibrillation (VF) is the most common cause of sudden death among patients with AMI. The incidence of VF is greater in patients with ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) than in patients with non-STEMI (NSTEMI).2 4 Sustained VA occurs more often in AMI patients who do not undergo timely reperfusion and in those with left ventricular dysfunction.1

With the implementation of timely reperfusion, the incidence of VF post-MI has significantly declined over the years, with a greater reduction observed for late VF (>48 hours after the onset of AMI) in particular.12 58 While in-hospital VF remains a lethal complication of AMI, there is still a lot of uncertainty regarding its actual impact on long-term survival.3 A substudy of the VALIDATE-SWEDEHEART trial demonstrated that early (<48 hours) VA occurring after primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for STEMI was associated with increased mortality compared with VA that occurred before PCI. However, the incidence of VA between 24 and 48 hours after STEMI was very low, which limited the ability to assess their independent prognostic significance.8 The FAST-MI study highlights that the increased risk associated with AMI-related VF is mainly driven by in-hospital mortality and does not extend beyond discharge.4

Bougouin et al demonstrated that patients developing VF in the setting of AMI are at increased risk of in-hospital mortality but not long-term mortality. However, only 24 patients had late VF in this study, of whom one third died during the index hospitalisation, limiting the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding the long-term prognostic implications of post-MI late VF.9

Although several clinical studies have examined VA in PCI-treated STEMI, including reports of VA occurring before, during and after reperfusion,1012 important gaps in knowledge remain. Many studies focus on early VF or VA as a broad category, whereas specific information on late VF is limited, especially among patients discharged alive from the index hospitalisation. Furthermore, data on VF in NSTEMI patients is considerably more limited and much of the existing studies focus primarily on STEMI patients.101215 Moreover, there is no differentiation in most post-MI VF treatment guidelines for these two categories (STEMI vs NSTEMI).25 6 1618

Using a large national registry of AMI patients during the PCI era, this study aims to achieve two primary objectives: (1) assess the incidence of late in-hospital VF post-AMI among patients who survived the index hospitalisation and (2) gain deeper insight into the prognosis of patients who experience late in-hospital VF.

Materials and methods

We analysed data from the Acute Coronary Syndrome Israeli Survey (ACSIS) conducted between 2000 and 2018. The ACSIS Registry is a 2-month nationwide survey that has been conducted biennially for over 20 years. The ACSIS prospectively collects data from all consecutive acute coronary syndrome (ACS) admissions across all 25 coronary care units in Israel. A diagnosis of AMI was confirmed by the attending physician using all available data according to the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction.19

Dedicated study personnel recorded demographic, historical and clinical data, including medical management, on prespecified study forms. The Central Data Coordinating Centre was responsible for collecting all case report forms, and the Israel Heart Society was responsible for keeping the survey database. 1-year mortality was ascertained by hospital chart review, telephone contact and use of the Israeli National Population Registry.

We aimed to compare the effects of late in-hospital VF (over 48 hours after the onset of AMI) on mortality in patients admitted with STEMI and NSTEMI. All AMI patients with late in-hospital VF were included. Patients who developed late VF were compared with those who did not experience late VF, both while considering all patients who presented with AMI, and separately when distinguishing between STEMI and NSTEMI patients.

The patients were divided into two subgroups (late VF vs no late VF) during the index hospitalisation. Baseline characteristics including sociodemographic and clinical parameters were compared between the two groups. The primary outcome was the 1-year mortality, assessed for the entire cohort (including in-hospital mortality) and separately for patients who survived the index hospitalisation.

Patients and public involvement

The public was not involved in the design, conduct, analysis or reporting of this study.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are presented as both the number (n) and corresponding percentage for categorical variables. For continuous variables, either the median and IQR, or the mean and SD are shown. To analyse categorical variables, we used either the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Testing for normality was employed for each continuous variable. For continuous variables, we used a T-test when normally distributed and a Mann-Whitney U test with a non-normal distribution. Survival curves were presented to assess the relationship between late VF and mortality in patients who survived the index hospitalisation, using the Kaplan-Meier pairwise log-rank tests. The associations between the occurrence of late in-hospital VF and 1-year mortality were evaluated in the total cohort and separately among STEMI and NSTEMI using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Multivariable adjustment was performed to account for other variables which could influence the association between late VF and mortality. The models were adjusted for age, beta-blocker treatment at baseline, chronic renal failure at baseline, chronic heart failure (CHF) at baseline, diabetes, gender, hypertension, Killip class at presentation, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), prior MI and number of diseased vessels. All tests were conducted at a two-sided overall 5% significance level (p=0.05). All analyses were performed using R statistical software (R-studio, V.4.0.3, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, 14 280 consecutive patients presented with AMI, of which 118 patients (0.82%) experienced in-hospital late VF. Only 68 patients with late VF were discharged alive from the hospital. Baseline characteristics of patients stratified by the occurrence of in-hospital VF are shown in table 1 and online supplemental table S1. Among patients who experienced late VF, 87 (73.7%) of them presented with STEMI and 31 (26.3%) patients had NSTEMI. Patients who experienced late VF were more likely to have a history of CHF (14.4% vs 7.8%, p=0.01), to have higher admission Killip class at presentation, and were more likely than their counterparts to present with LVEF below 30% (36.2% vs 8.4%, p<0.01). In-hospital complications including cardiogenic shock (46.2% vs 3.6%, p<0.01), acute renal failure (29.7% vs 6.3%, p<0.01), pulmonary oedema (30.8% vs 6.9%, p<0.01) and de novo mild-moderate CHF (24.3% vs 9.7%, p<0.01) were also more likely to develop in patients with late VF. Ultimately, all patients with late VF had at least Killip 2 during their hospital course (table 1).

The rates of late VF in all patients, STEMI and NSTEMI patients

Patients who experienced late VF were more likely to die during the first year after AMI. 54.8% of AMI patients who experienced late VF died and 10.2% of those without late VF died during the first year (p<0.01). In subgroup analysis, STEMI and NSTEMI patients demonstrated a similar pattern. Notably, the increased 1-year mortality rates in the late VF group were mainly related to death events occurring prior to hospital discharge, as only 68 of patients with late VF survived the index hospitalisation (table 2).

Discussion

This study provides several insights regarding the prognostic significance of late in-hospital VF among AMI patients admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU). Late VF was found to be strongly associated with decreased survival, especially among patients presenting with STEMI.

The lower rate of late VF in our study could be attributed to the widespread utilisation of early reperfusion and guideline-directed therapies, thus reducing the incidence of late VA.15 20

In our study, AMI patients who experienced late VF often presented with a more complex medical history compared with AMI patients who did not. Notably, these patients were more likely to have chronic renal failure, history of CHF, higher admission Killip class and low LVEF. This complexity in their medical history may be a contributing factor to their increased mortality risk. No significant differences were observed between the groups in our study with respect to smoking status, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, history of ischaemic heart disease or cerebrovascular events. However, Tran et al2 identified that patients who developed VF were less likely to have a medical history of diabetes, hypertension, transient ischaemic attack or cerebrovascular accident. However, they included patients with both early and late ventricular tachycardia (VT)/VF.

Previous studies generally agree that late VF worsens the outcome of AMI patients, predominantly in terms of in-hospital outcomes. However, the extent to which this influence persists after the patient has been discharged from the hospital remains uncertain.2 3 5 15 21 In a recent study, the 1-year mortality rate was found to be 40.4% in all AMI patients complicated with in-hospital VF, compared with 11.3% in patients without in-hospital VF.4 However, they argued that the increased mortality among patients with VF was mainly driven by in-hospital mortality. Since this represented around 90% of the 1-year mortality, the actual long-term impact was inconclusive.4 The SWEDEHEART study highlights that VA occurring after PCI is associated with a significantly higher mortality risk than arrhythmia before PCI. However, their analysis primarily focused mainly on early VF and included very limited data on late VF.8 Our study not only supports the finding that late VF is associated with an increased 1-year mortality rate in AMI patients, but our results additionally suggest that this risk persists beyond the initial admission period. This is supported by the reduced 1-year survival rate of AMI patients who experienced late VF and were discharged alive from the hospital.

Järvensivu-Koivunen et al reported that patients with mildly impaired or normal LVEF (>40%) who experienced VA (VF or VT) following AMI had a significantly increased long-term risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD) and higher overall mortality over a median follow-up of 7.6 years. Unlike our study, they included all VA occurrences during AMI hospitalisation without distinguishing between early and late VA. Their study raises the important question of whether implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) therapy could be beneficial in preventing SCD in this patient population.22

There is a paucity of data regarding the frequency of late VF and the outcomes of STEMI vs NSTEMI patients.2 5 6 16 17 VF is thought to be less common in patients with NSTEMI in comparison to STEMI patients.2 4 Interestingly, a study demonstrated that late VF was as frequent in both STEMI and NSTEMI patients (0.5% vs 0.4%).21 A recently published study14 showed that the risk of late VF or VT is 2.4% in the overall population and 1.7% in a cohort with uncomplicated STEMI. Compared with our study, the previous study14 included only STEMI patients treated with primary PCI, analysed the risk of VT and defined late VF as occurring at least 1 day after primary PCI.

When comparing crude mortality rates, we found that patients with late VF had higher mortality rates in both STEMI and NSTEMI groups. However, the association between late VF and 1-year mortality rate of NSTEMI patients did not reach statistical significance among the patients who survived beyond the hospitalisation. This may potentially be a result of the small sample of NSTEMI patients who developed late VF in this study as reflected by the wide CIs.

Given the higher Killip class and the lower LVEF observed among patients with late VF, it remains unclear whether late VF is acting as a marker of more advanced disease rather than an independent prognostic factor. Moreover, it is uncertain whether the excess mortality risk is directly attributed to VF itself, since causes of mortality in this population may be arrhythmic and non-arrhythmic. Our findings underscore the need for better risk stratification tools to reduce mortality among VF patients. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the potential benefit of ICD in this high-risk population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective cohort study with the limitations typically attendant to such studies. The data have been collected by different physicians at different medical centres across the country, which may have influenced the uniformity of this data. However, data collection was prospective for all cases, which minimises this limitation and enhances the validity of the study. Furthermore, the database includes only patients admitted to the CICU with no data on AMI patients hospitalised in internal medicine departments, thus limiting the generalisability of our findings. Additionally, residual confounding cannot be ruled out as reperfusion timing, infarct size or adherence to therapy were not available for the current analysis. Moreover, follow-up data on ICD implantation is lacking and the exact timing of late VF was not available. Lastly, the use of all-cause mortality instead of SCD/arrhythmia-specific mortality limits causal interpretation.

Conclusions

In this nationwide registry, we found that late VF occurring after AMI is associated with a higher risk of postdischarge 1-year mortality. Further research is needed to elucidate whether a cardioverter defibrillator implantation would mitigate the risk of death in this population.
