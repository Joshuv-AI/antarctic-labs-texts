Method for assessing inter-individual variability in the carbohydrate utilization potential of gut microbiota ex vivo
Authors: Rachel E Asbury, Taryn A Hoffman, Cathy Chu, Radhakrishnan Mahadevan, Bradley A Saville
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe microbial utilization of carbohydrates produces metabolic byproducts that are associated with health benefits, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). The rapid absorption of SCFAs by intestinal epithelial cells makes them challenging to study in vivo. Culturing methods address this limitation by providing controlled conditions that allow for monitoring substrate utilization and the production of fermentation byproducts. In this paper, we describe an easily implemented, cost-effective method for screening inter-individual differences in carbohydrate degradation potential among gut microbiota from different individuals using batch cultures in 96-deep well plates. In addition to culturing methods, we describe procedures for obtaining stool samples from human volunteers, as well as for processing and storing them. We demonstrate that complete or near-complete substrate utilization is typically reached within 24 hours of culturing in buffered media in 96-deep well plates, and inter-individual differences in substrate utilization rate and SCFA production are observable by 12 hours.Keywords: Gut microbiome, Human gut microbiota, Gut microbial communities, Microbial culture, Prebiotics, Non-digestible oligosaccharides, Carbohydrates, High-throughput culturomics

Background

The fermentation of carbohydrates by gut microbiota into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) has important implications for health, and gut microbial communities between different individuals vary widely in their carbohydrate utilization abilities [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. While the ability to degrade polysaccharides that are inaccessible to human degradative enzymes exists widely across gut microbial communities, the abundance of carbohydrate-degrading microbes and the diversity of utilization pathways results in heterogeneous responses to dietary interventions with non-digestible carbohydrates [3,9,10].

As the primary byproducts of carbohydrate fermentation, SCFAs are an important metric for evaluating inter-individual variability in carbohydrate utilization by gut microbial communities [3,5,11,12]; however, their rapid absorption by intestinal epithelial cells makes them challenging to study in vivo [13,14]. Therefore, culturing systems where synthetic microbial communities (in vitro) or communities directly from stool samples (ex vivo) are grown in media supplemented with carbohydrates are superior for assessing metabolic potential, allowing substrate utilization and metabolite production to be assessed in real time [2].

One challenge in gut microbial culturing systems is managing the decrease in pH that coincides with the production of SCFAs and other organic acids during fermentation processes. In the proximal colon, where most carbohydrates are fermented, the absorption of acidic byproducts and bicarbonate secretion maintains pH conditions between 5.5–6.5 [4,15], but within lab conditions, the decrease in pH can lead to culture death and incomplete substrate utilization. Bioreactors, such as batch or chemostat systems, offer dynamic pH control [2,16]; however, these systems can be costly and challenging to scale for the high-throughput studies needed to assess inter-individual variability.

In developing this method, we found that pH can be maintained over 48 hours at a biologically optimal range (5.5–7.5). This was achieved using buffered media supplemented with carbohydrates in a simple culturing setup, which allowed for complete substrate utilization. We validate this method with cultures supplemented with manno-oligosaccharides (MOS) and show that it is feasible to assess inter-individual variation in substrate utilization rates, SCFA production profiles, and community composition through 16S rRNA bacterial gene sequencing. We demonstrate that our culturing method can recover greater bacterial diversity than is detected in baseline stool samples alone. We also use this method to compare SCFA production profiles between individual microbial communities supplemented with MOS and other commercial prebiotics. In addition to culturing methods, we present a full workflow for obtaining stool samples from human participants, along with procedures for sample processing and storage. We also describe how this protocol can be used alongside other measures known to be associated with gut microbial composition, such as participant characteristics including anthropometric data and dietary intake, to examine how these factors contribute to carbohydrate utilization and SCFA production.

This article describes a single, scalable workflow for measuring carbohydrate utilization, taxonomic composition and metabolite production of gut microbial communities at high-throughput using a simple culturing setup within 96-deep well plates, alone or alongside participant characteristics such as anthropometric data and dietary intake.

Transfer media to a 1 L glass bottle.3.4.2.Prepare 50% glycerol by diluting glycerol with equal parts ultrapure water in a 1 L beaker. Volume can be adjusted depending on how many samples there are to process (∼100 mL per sample).3.4.3.Mix and transfer to individual serum bottles (∼100 mL per bottle). Use a separate glycerol stock for each sample to avoid contamination between samples.3.4.4.Heat sterilize BHI media and glycerol stock by autoclaving at 121C for 20 min. Allow to cool.3.4.5.Add l-cysteine: prepare 500 mg/mL stock of l-cysteine and filter-sterilize using a 0.22 µm syringe filter. Sterilize both the inside and the outside of the resealable plastic bag and masticator bag with ethanol and allow to dry completely. Sterilize all consumables before transferring into the anaerobic chamber. Prepare a waste collection bag within the chamber.3.5.4.Transfer the stool sample into the anaerobic chamber. Using a disposable spatula, transfer sample from the collection container into the masticator bag. Carefully fold the masticator bag, removing as much air as possible, then insert the folded bag containing the sample into the plastic zip-loc bag (minimize sample exposure to oxygen). Transfer the sample out of the anaerobic chamber through the airlock.3.5.5.Weigh the sample (which is within the masticator bag and the resealable plastic bag) on a calibrated analytical balance and record the results. Return the sample to the anaerobic chamber.3.5.6.Calculate the volume of media required to dilute the sample to 20% w/v. Fold the masticator bag and place back into the plastic bag. Completely seal the plastic bag and transfer back out through the airlock.3.5.9.Homogenize the sample in the paddle blender (3x for 10 s each). Remove the bag from the blender and complete the homogenization by hand, especially in the corners, for ∼1 min.3.5.10.Return the bagged stool back into the chamber through the airlock. Carefully open the resealable plastic bag and masticator bag. The sample may have leaked from the masticator bag into the plastic bag. Method validation

We developed this method using stool culture samples from individual research participants that were selected from a larger group recruited using the methods described herein. First, we determined the optimal media composition by comparing the metabolite production and taxonomic composition of stool cultures using the methods described, using different media compositions supplemented with manno-oligosaccharides (MOS) (Fig. 2). Sequencing metrics for determining bacterial taxonomic composition with 16S rRNA gene sequencing indicated high quality sequencing across samples, and detailed metrics including sequencing depth and rarefaction curves are included in Supplementary S2. Analyses of taxa recovery across culturing conditions are included in Supplementary S3. Brain-Heart Infusion (BHI) media is well supported for its ability to grow a wide range of gut microorganisms, especially Bacteroides, which is an important genus for carbohydrate degradation [19,26]. Lau et al. showed that a base media of BHI with supplements effectively supported the growth of gut microbes during culturing, even those thought to be unculturable [1]. While existing literature demonstrated that supplementing media with fetal bovine serum or mucin supports microbial growth [1], we found that the SCFA production and taxonomic composition at phylum and genus levels were comparable between fetal bovine serum or mucin-supplemented and non-supplemented media. Further, cultures grown within BHI media were associated with greater richness and alpha-diversity than other media conditions (Supplementary S2 and S3). Therefore, the base media of BHI broth supplemented with 0.5 g/L-cysteine hydrochloride, 10 mg/L hemin and 1 mg/L vitamin K was selected for this culturing method.

Next, a range of bicarbonate buffers was tested for their ability to maintain pH within an optimal range of 5.5–7.5. Stool cultures were grown anaerobically using BHI media supplemented with MOS. We demonstrate that a starting buffer concentration of 250 mM, and in some cases, 100 mM, is sufficient to maintain the pH within this range over 48 hours of carbohydrate fermentation (Fig. 3). For these reasons, the final media composition selected was BHI media with supplements and 250 mM bicarbonate.

We validated that this method can be used to assess inter-individual variability in substrate utilization and metabolite production. Independent stool cultures were supplemented with fermentable MOS, and the concentrations of metabolites in the supernatant were quantified at various timepoints using HPLC. We demonstrated that our culturing conditions achieves complete or near-complete substrate utilization over 24 hours, as evidenced by the concentration of MOS oligomers and monomers in the culturing supernatant (Fig. 4A). Inter-individual variability in metabolite production and substrate utilization can be observed (Fig. 4B-C). In addition to the production of SCFAs, we also assessed the production of lactic acid, which has been shown to vary meaningfully between individuals due to the amount and types of lactate-utilizing bacteria [27]. In our study, we found a high amount of variability in the production of all acids, with the highest inter-individual variability in the production of lactic acid. Notably, this work demonstrates the importance of sampling at multiple time points: the greatest variability in substrate utilization is observed at the 8- and 12-h time points. While trends in acetic acid and propionic acid are seen from the first time-point, variability is only observed after 8 hours for lactic acid, and after 24 hours for butyric acid. For some cases such as for high-throughput studies where only one time point is feasible, we recommend a time point of 8 or 12 hours if variability in carbohydrate utilization is the primary endpoint, and 24 hours where variability in SCFA production is the primary endpoint.

Finally, we validated that this method can be used to screen the inter-individual variability in SCFA production between different prebiotic substrates. Independent stool cultures were supplemented with 25 mg/mL of MOS, fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) or xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS), as well as glucose (control). Differences in SCFA production were observed between different stool cultures as well as between different carbohydrate substrates within the same individual (Fig. 5). Notably, the highest concentrations of lactic acid were observed for cultures supplemented with glucose and FOS. During the pH measurement experiments, cultures supplemented with FOS exhibited the greatest pH drop across all buffer concentrations and were only controlled within the target pH range with the highest buffer concentration (Fig. 3). The acidic pH observed in the FOS-supplemented culture supernatant may be related to the increase in lactic acid, a stronger acid than the short-chain fatty acids. This demonstrates that cultures grown with certain substrates may achieve adequate pH control with lower concentrations of NaHCO3-, while others may require higher concentrations due to the accumulation of acidic fermentation byproducts.

A detailed breakdown of taxa enriched and depleted across culturing conditions is provided in Supplementary data S3. Interestingly, our culturing method yielded a greater genus-level richness than what was seen in the baseline sample of one culture: 117 distinct genera were detected after culturing S003 in BHI medium without prebiotics, compared to 102 distinct genera found within the uncultured S003 stool sample. Among the genera detected in the cultured sample was Bifidobacterium and many members of Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) including Bacillus, Clostridium and Eubacterium. This finding provides further rationale for culturing, as culturing in the proper media conditions can enrich bacteria that are present within samples at a low or undetectable abundance.

Some taxa detected at baseline were not recovered under any culturing condition. These lost taxa were generally present in low abundance (mean <1% relative abundance across baseline samples), except for Alloprevotella and Prevotella, which had a mean relative abundance in the baseline samples of 3.1% and 2.5%. Nevertheless, culturing recovered taxa representing 92.6% of the cumulative relative abundance at baseline. Across all culturing conditions, 189 distinct genera were identified with 16S rRNA bacterial gene sequencing. Of these, 158 were detected at baseline. Culturing maintained 134 of the 158 genus-level taxa found at baseline (85%), and a further 31 genera were enriched during culturing.

Overall, this work demonstrates an easily implemented, high-throughput method to screen for inter-individual variability in gut microbial carbohydrate utilization, taxonomic composition, and SCFA production, while maintaining culture pH and obtaining complete, time-dependent substrate utilization.

Limitations

This work describes a method to assess carbohydrate/prebiotic utilization and metabolite production at a community level within 96-well plates, using bicarbonate buffers to maintain the pH of cultures within a biologically acceptable range. This method was developed using stool samples from five healthy individuals, a sample size that was selected to demonstrate technical feasibility rather than to capture the full extent of inter-individual variability in carbohydrate utilization in human gut microbial communities. Further, this method has not been evaluated using stool samples from individuals with potential gut microbiome perturbations or dysbiosis, including those taking antibiotics or probiotics, and additional validation may be required for these sample types. Nonetheless, we expect that this framework can be used under these conditions albeit possibly with a greater number of samples required to assess differences.

Research objectives assessing the utilization of different carbohydrates or cross-feeding by different metabolites may require different media compositions. Further, some taxa were not recovered by our culturing method. Research objectives for targeting specific taxa such as fastidious or “hard-to-culture” microorganisms may require additional adjustments to the media composition and culturing conditions. Finally, little information is available on the impact, if any, of high concentrations of bicarbonate buffers on the viability of microorganisms. For example, high buffer concentrations may lead to osmotic stress on bacterial cells. While this was not explored in our study, we observed complete or near-complete substrate utilization over 24 hours, indicating that the culture conditions supported active microbial metabolism. Finally, as with all ex vivo culture systems, this method is limited in its generalizability to in vivo conditions.

Nonetheless, some advantages of ex vivo systems include the ability to directly monitor fermentation products and capture time-dependent changes in microbial taxa that would otherwise be inaccessible in vivo. Further, as demonstrated in our method validation, culturing can promote the growth of taxa that are undetectable in stool samples at baseline, providing a more comprehensive picture of taxonomic composition and metabolic potential. This method offers a pathway to screen microbial communities for their carbohydrate utilization capacity and to assess differences in metabolic potential between individuals. We envision that this simple and scalable culturing system can be readily adapted to explore the utilization of any soluble carbohydrate substrate, facilitating greater mechanistic insight into inter-individual variability in carbohydrate utilization by human gut microbial communities.
