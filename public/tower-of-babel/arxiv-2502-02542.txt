OverThink : Slowdown Attacks on Reasoning LLMs
Abhinav Kumar, Jaechul Roh, Ali Naseh, Marzena Karpinska, Mohit Iyyer, Amir Houmansadr, Eugene Bagdasarian, University of Massachusetts Amherst Simon Fraser University

Abstract
A reasoning language model (RLM) generates costly reasoning tokens, often hidden from the users, that help it excel at many tasks. Our OverThink attack targets RLM-based applications (such as chatbots or coding agents) that rely on external context by forcing these models to generate substantially more reasoning tokens while still producing contextually correct answers. An adversary conducts the attack by injecting decoy reasoning problems into available content, optimized to elicit a large number of tokens. We craft decoy challenges (using Markov decision processes, language translation, or graphic comprehension) that appear benign individually yet have an adversarial impact when inserted in the context, allowing them to easily evade safety filters. We evaluate OverThink on proprietary and open-source reasoning models across the FreshQA, SQuAD, and MuSR datasets, where we observe 13 × \times , 46 × \times , and 12 × \times increases, respectively. We also explore multimodal attacks using images, which cause up to a 2.7 × \times increase in reasoning, as well as attacks on coding agents by injecting decoys into skills, README files, and code, resulting in up to a 17 × \times increase. We explore several defenses and evaluate their efficacy against different attack strategies, highlighting that defending against OverThink is nontrivial. Finally, we show that newer generations of RLMs, while showing a drastic increase in per-token cost, also exhibit up to a 2.3 × \times increase in reasoning tokens, leaving them more vulnerable to OverThink attacks.

Background and Related Work

2.1
Reasoning in LM and agents

For performance improvements, classical LM scaling has focused on increasing compute, model parameters, and the amount of training data
[
14
]
. Yet,
recent work shows that scaling parameters becomes less effective when pretraining data is not increased proportionally
[
10
]
.

A reasoning language model (
P_{\theta}
) mitigates these limitations by allowing the model to “think” i.e., it generates intermediate reasoning tokens (
y_{r}
), typically separated with reasoning tags (also known as a scratchpad), before producing the final answer (
y_{a}
)
[
19
]
to the user query
(
q
). This can improve performance by enabling the model to allocate additional resources at test-time for harder problems, resulting in longer reasoning traces, an effect often referred to as test-time scaling
[
70
]
. One key reason RLMs benefit from longer reasoning chains is their ability to recognize errors and revert to the last known correct step, a behavior known as backtracking
[
51
]
.

Beyond single-turn queries, RLMs also become the backbone of modern agentic systems. This extends the output space by producing tool calls alongside reasoning and answer tokens, and the input space by incorporating tool outputs as input to generate the next step. Reasoning models allow agents, such as coding agents, to autonomously write, execute, and debug real-world systems
[
42
]
and iterate over different strategies
[
38
]
.
While this expanded interaction space enables more capable reasoning, it also increases reasoning token consumption, as agents may require multiple rounds of tool use, feedback, and deliberation
[
7
]
.

2.2
Related Attacks

Prompt Injection in LMs.
In prompt injection attacks, an
adversary manipulates the input prompt of LMs to influence the
generated output. This manipulation can either
directly
alter
the input prompt
[
59
,
6
]
or

indirectly
inject malicious prompts into external sources
retrieved by the model
[
26
]
. By contrast, our work
introduces a novel attack vector where the adversary’s goal is not to
provoke harmful output (that can trigger jailbreaking
defenses
[
5
,
87
]
) but to increase the
number of reasoning tokens, thereby inflating the financial cost of
using deployed reasoning models.

Denial-of-Service (DoS) Attacks.

DoS attacks
[
9
,
29
,
47
]
on ML models
[
68
]
focused on maximizing energy consumption while not preserving the correctness of user outputs. Similarly, prior work
[
31
,
15
]
has proposed slowdown attacks on early-exit models
[
35
,

]
that degrade both accuracy and efficiency. Other attacks
[

22

24

fine-tune LMs with malicious data or craft malicious prompts but generate outputs that are visible to users and can be easily flagged.

Comparison with concurrent work.
An earlier version of this work, publicly released in February 2025, introduced the DoS attacks on reasoning LMs [anonymous, 2025]. Follow-up work on attacking reasoning LMs has mostly gone one of two ways: forcing excessive token consumption, or directly corrupting the chain-of-thought. Yet,
OverThink
operates under a weaker threat model, supports more modalities, tasks, and proposes a general framework (see Table
1
).

On token consumption, Si et al.
[
69
]
optimize a suffix that drives up to 9x more reasoning steps, but this requires white-box gradient access over long reasoning chains, making it costly against closed deployments. Liu et al.
[
44
]
and Li et al.
[
41
]
move to black-box settings where both need repeated victim queries to converge, which is slow and leaves a detectable query pattern. Wang et al.
[
77
]
use a genetic algorithm to distort a problem’s logical structure until the model overthinks it, but the resulting inputs are visibly mangled and easy to catch with a consistency check. Li et al.
[
39
]
extend this to agents, first triggering excessive tool calls and then optimizing reasoning payloads, but again relies on iterative optimization against the live agent and targets tool-use budgets specifically.
On CoT corruption, Rajeev et al.
[
62
]
show that a fixed, irrelevant trigger (e.g., “cats sleep most of their lives”) reliably confuses reasoning models and more than triples the odds of a wrong answer.

These attacks are effective but generally assume a stronger attacker (white-box access, large query budgets, or visibly modified inputs) and are each narrow in scope, tuned to one pipeline or setting. Our
OverThink
attack is general-purpose by comparison as the same mechanism works against standalone reasoning LMs, agentic pipelines, and across modalities.

2.3
Reasoning Model Deployment

Most current LMs leverage inference-time scaling
[
51
,
19
,
4
,
25
]
and are integrated into state-of-the-art AI tools and products.
They power not only chatbots, but also newer coding assistants, self-driving car VLAs
[
49
]
, agentic browsers (for example, OpenAI’s Codex and AI pair programmers like Cursor leverage large language models for code editing, while ChatGPT Atlas and Perplexity’s Comet are full web browsers built around LM-based reasoning
[
79
]
.

The services access a reasoning model
P_{\theta}
via cloud APIs that are priced based on token consumption.
However, these reasoning models have also introduced some extremely expensive models. One of the most expensive RLMs, GPT 5.5-pro, costs
\$60
per million input tokens and
\$270
per million output tokens. Even the open-source alternatives, due to their size, become compute expensive to deploy (e.g., the 2.8 trillion parameter kimi k3 model
[
73
]
).

Crucially for our threat model, user-facing applications like ChatGPT, Copilot, Atlas or Comet do not bill end-users per query or per reasoning step. Instead, they offer free or fixed-cost access (e.g. free tiers or flat subscriptions), insulating users from the underlying token-based pricing. In other words, whether an assistant answers a simple question or undertakes extensive reasoning, the user’s cost remains unchanged – the extra expense of multi-step “thinking” is absorbed by the service provider rather than passed on per query.

3
The
OverThink
Attack

We focus on agentic applications that use RLMs on untrusted data like chatbots operating on web pages or coding agents using third-party libraries. We assume that the user or a developer of that agent pay to the model provider for generated tokens.
Inspired by
algorithmic complexity attacks
[
18
]
, we create inputs that cause RLMs to generate

unnecessary
reasoning tokens, thus increasing operating costs.

Crucially,
OverThink
attack has to maintain answer quality to user queries. We focus on inflating count of reasoning tokens as they are often hidden or rarely inspected, whereas generated answers are presented to the user
and incorrect ones could be flagged by the user.

3.1
Threat Model.

Attack’s Potential Scenarios.

Users might send questions to chatbots like chatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or
coding agents like codex or claude code that already support RLM. These
applications can retrieve information from untrusted external sources
such as webpages or documents and provide them as inputs to the RLM
along with the user’s query. While users enjoy an application for free or
at a fixed cost, the application is responsible for costs associated
with RLM’s answer and reasoning token generation. An adversary can target a webpage or repository that the application retrieves data from or alter
documents or emails used by an AI assistant. In all these cases, the
user invests in getting the right answer on their query but might not
be interested in detailed inspection of the source nor access to
reasoning. This makes these scenarios a perfect candidate for our
proposed attack.

Adversary’s Target.

In this attack, the adversary targets
the agents
that uses

P_{\theta}
while utilizing external resources. Unlike existing prompt injection
attacks, the adversary does not target the user directly, e.g. modify
outputs; instead, the focus is on increasing application’s costs to
operate the reasoning model.

Adversary’s Objectives.

The adversary aims to increase the reasoning chain
y_{r}
generated by
P_{\theta}
for a
specific class of user queries that rely on external context, which is
integrated into the final input to the RLM.
These attacks apply to instructions that depend on external information (e.g., updated documentation for new coding libraries used by coding agents, or daily news updates from news platforms).
The adversary needs to ensure that the modified external context used by
P_{\theta}
answers the user’s
original query
q
correctly while omitting any trace of the context
manipulation.

Adversary’s Capabilities.

In this attack, the adversary has black-box access to target RLM
P_{\theta}
and can access the external context retrieved by the RLM. In cases where the
adversary cannot access the target RLM, we assume that they have
access to a proxy RLM, on which they can perform black-box queries.

3.2
Problem Statement

An
RLM

P_{\theta}
maps an input sequence containing user’s question
q
and external context
z
, to an
output sequence consisting of reasoning tokens
y_{r}
and answer tokens

y_{a}
.

We define a function
\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(q,z)
as a non-deterministic function returning number of reasoning tokens generated by
P_{\theta}(q,z)
:

\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(q,z)=||y_{r}||

where
P_{\theta}(q,z)=(y_{r},y_{a})
.

With user’s question
q
and adversary-created context
z^{*}
, the
adversary aims to achieve the following:

1.

Longer Reasoning
. The adversary aims to significantly increase the length of reasoning chains:

\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(q,z^{*})\gg\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(q,z)

where
z
is the benign context.

2.

Answer Stealthiness
. The output of
P_{\theta}
when utilizing
z^{*}
(
y^{*}_{a}
) has to remain
similar to the original output
y_{a}
and cannot include any
information related to the modification included in
z^{*}
.

Therefore the attack objective can be formulated as:

z^{*}=\underset{\tilde{z}}{\text{argmax}}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(q,z^{*})\bigg|\mathbbm{1}_{y^{*}_{a}\approx y_{a}}\right]\quad

where,
\tilde{z}
is all possible variants of z leading to

y^{*}_{a}\approx y_{a}
.

Conducting a successful
OverThink
attack is challenging in this setting, because both gradient-based methods, such as GCG
[
91
]
, and standard jailbreak-style black-box methods
[
40
,
58
]
become ineffective. To use gradient-based optimization methods, the adversary needs specific target tokens or strings so that a loss can be defined for backpropagation. Similarly, jailbreak-style black-box optimization
[
3
]
requires a known set of tokens that are not defined for slowdown attacks.

3.3
Why Does This Attack Matter?

Increase in Inference cost.
Across multiple generations, LMs have gotten significantly more costly. GPT-3.5 (released in March 2023) cost $1.5 per million output tokens, whereas GPT-5.5 Pro costs $270 per million output tokens (180
\times
).

Increase in token consumption.
The increase doesn’t just exist at the token cost level, as reasoning models can take more than 7
\times
as many tokens as non-reasoning models for the same task
[
17
]
. With the increasing agentic systems, utilizing these expensive RLMs with drastically rising costs, in everyday applications and high-risk settings, overthinking attacks may substantially increase operating costs and introduce new risks. Token bloat is already a significant issue for these systems, as agents often generate long reasoning traces, repeatedly invoke tools, and process large amounts of intermediate context
[
7
]
.
OverThink
attacks can worsen this problem by prolonging or restarting iterative steps such as file inspection, code generation, execution, and revision. Since output tokens, including reasoning tokens, are generally more expensive than input tokens, this additional activity can significantly increase costs
[
54
]
. Users may also exhaust output-token or context-window limits, potentially paying for incomplete or empty responses.

Computation and latency impact.
Increased reasoning and agentic steps also lead to longer running times, which can be problematic for latency-sensitive applications. Inference latency is closely related to output length, and unusually long generations can also increase queuing delays for other requests
[
84
]
. Although cost and latency are existing challenges, overthinking attacks can make them substantially worse and render agent execution impractical or economically unsustainable. They may also consume shared computational resources for extended periods, delaying benign requests and increasing energy use and carbon emissions
[
56
]
. Thus, overthinking attacks can amplify the already significant token, cost, and latency overheads of coding agents and general-purpose RLMs and agentic systems.

Our primary objective is to find perturbations that induce significantly longer reasoning chains while preserving accuracy. Since the search space of possible perturbations is sparse (i.e., most of the nearest neighbors to the query will generate a similar number of tokens or significantly affect accuracy
[
28
]
), starting from a base challenge that is both difficult and elicits long reasoning chains substantially narrows the search space. Therefore, our first step is to select a hard challenge, namely a decoy challenge (Subsection
4.1
). Because the chosen decoy challenge significantly reduces the search space, we can then iterate on it to find stronger variants (Subsection
4.2
).

However, decoy challenges that produce long reasoning chains without degrading accuracy may still fail to achieve contextual correctness, the output can explicitly reveal that the decoy task was solved, making the attack easy to detect. To address this, we craft instructions that make the output stealthier and improve contextual correctness (Subsection
4.2
). Finally, depending on the adversary’s goal, they may mount a large-scale attack affecting multiple sources or instead target a single source. These different scenarios motivate different decoy insertion methods (Subsection
4.3
).

4.1
Decoy Problem Selection

A Reasoning LM
P_{\theta}
allocates different numbers of reasoning tokens based on their assessment of a task’s difficulty and confidence in the response
[
19
]
. Leveraging this, we introduce a
decoy challenge (
d
)
designed to increase reasoning token usage.
The main challenge in selecting an effective decoy challenge is to accurately estimate problem complexity from the model’s perspective.
Table
2
shows that tasks perceived as difficult by humans do not always result in higher token usage. For example, models generate solutions to IMO 2024 problems with an average of 8,923 reasoning tokens, significantly fewer than the 21080 tokens required on average for a simple Sudoku puzzle across all three reasoning LMs. We hypothesize that this occurs because challenges beyond a certain difficulty threshold, or of a certain nature, cannot effectively leverage the self-verification capabilities of reasoning LMs
[
88
]
. If reasoning LMs are unable to verify the correctness of their steps, they lack sufficient information for extended exploration and backtracking.

Keeping these criteria in mind, we design
d
to trigger
multiple
rounds of backtracking
[
85
]
. The most effective decoys involve long tasks with many small, verifiable steps. Examples include Sudoku puzzles and finite Markov decision processes (MDPs), which require numerous operations, each validated against clear criteria, thereby increasing the complexity of model’s reasoning.

4.2
Decoy Optimization

Once we select a class of decoy tasks we optimize them to further increase the number of reasoning tokens as the same family of decoy challenges can lead to vastly different number of reasoning tokens. As discussed previously in Section
3.2
, due to lack of available reference output strings and budget constraints, both gradient based and black-box optimization techniques fail to achieve
OverThink
attack objective.
As a result, the adversary needs a sample-efficient procedure that can search over a large space of candidate decoys while accounting for noisy measurements (based on number of reasoning tokens generated).

Optimization Objective and Reasoning tokens Distribution.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the pool of all decoy challenges:

\mathcal{D}=[d_{0},d_{1},....,d_{N}]

where N is number of all possible decoy challenges in the given class. The goal of the optimization process is to find the a challenge (
d_{i}
) that leads to long reasoning chains. Since we use
c_{r}
to measure the candidate decoy’s attack utility, the feedback during each run varies due to the stochasticity of the decoding algorithm in LMs. This leads to a noisy utility signal. Due this reason we treat the attack utility of a given decoy challenge as a function of
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(q,d_{i}+z)
.

Therefore, the Decoy Optimization problem can be defined as:

d^{*}=argmax_{d_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(q,d_{i}+z)\Big]

We observe that the Decoy Optimization problem can be structured as a
multi-armed bandit (MAB)
problem where each decoy
d_{i}
is an “arm,” each model call corresponds to an arm pull, and the observed reward is a function of
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(q,d_{i}+z)
. This framing also lets us express the return of an arm via a quality function, where the quality function maps a decoy challenge to its utility. Moreover, the bandit perspective naturally captures the
exploitation
(re-testing decoys already known to elicit high reasoning) versus
exploration
(trying new decoys that may generalize better or yield even higher reasoning) trade-off that arises during the optimization process.

ICL-evolve: ICL-based decoy challenge search.

While an adversary can train a new model to search for decoy challenges, a cost affective alternative for an adversary is to utilize in-context learning (ICL) as it can generates new samples using samples provided in the input prompt, making it an attractive alternative to gradient based optimization techniques.

To solve the optimization problem, Inspired by evolutionary search
[
82
,
43
]
, we introduce
ICL-evolve
(see Algorithm
1
). It samples a small
population
of samples from the set of existing decoy challenges:

\mathcal{D}_{b}=[d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}...d_{k}]

and generated a new variants conditioned on the population using ICL.
Since for every decoy challenge,
d_{i}
, the feedback is sampled from a non-deterministic function
\mathcal{R}(d_{i})
,
the quality function for a decoy challenge
d_{i}
is

Q(d_{i})=\mathbb{E}\Big[log\Big(\mathcal{R_{\theta}}(\hat{q},d_{i}+\hat{z})\Big)\Big]

where
<\hat{q},\hat{z}>
is dummy query, context pair.
Due to the high variance in token efficiency in models
[
21
]
, the algorithm can get really sensitive to the minor token count increase, giving a noisier signal and making the decoy challenge search more difficult. Using the log scale allows us to mitigate the impact of the noisy signal.

) where we take inspiration from nucleus sampling
[
30
]
, as used in LM decoding. Specifically, we balance exploration and exploitation by randomly sampling decoy challenges from
\mathcal{D}_{b}
such that the cumulative sum of their normalized Q-values is at least a threshold
\tau
. Adjusting
\tau
controls the diversity–quality trade-off as lower thresholds concentrate sampling on high-scoring decoys, which can reduce diversity, whereas higher thresholds broaden the sampling pool to include more diverse, but potentially lower-quality, samples.

We start sampling by creating array of tuples containing the decoy challenges and its respective normalized Q-value:

H=\Bigg[\Big(a,\frac{\mathcal{Q}(d)}{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathcal{Q}(d_{j})}\Big)~\forall d\in\mathcal{D}_{b}\Bigg]

We find the smallest index after which the total normalized Q-value is higher than the selected threshold
\tau
, called
k
.

We then select all the elements with the index lower than or equal to
k^{*}
.

H_{n}=[(d_{j},Q_{j})\in H_{n}:j\leq k^{*}]

We normalize
H_{n}
again and sample m samples, where we treat the normalized Q-value as the probability of that decoy challenge being sampled. The sampled decoy challenges are

\mathcal{D}_{m}=[d_{0},d_{1},....,d_{m}]

Using
D_{m}
, we construct

H_{m}=[(d,Q(d))\forall d\in\mathcal{D}_{m}]

which is returned by the sampling function.

New population generation.

Once we obtain the input population
H_{m}
, we provide it to an LM
\pi
to generate a new population. However, we observe that the generated samples often lack diversity, which leads to stagnation in search quality. Limited diversity in LM generation is a phenomenon that has been noted in prior work
[
46
]
.
To mitigate this issue, we construct a prompt that asks the model, rather than generating decoy challenges directly, to produce each challenge together with an estimated probability of sampling that challenge from the set of all possible challenges
D_{m}
. The model is further instructed to output only challenges whose estimated probability is below
0.2
(to promote diversity). This approach—prompting the model to generate challenge–probability pairs—is known as verbalized sampling
[
89
]
and can be formulated as:

(d_{1},p_{1}),(d_{2},p_{2}),...(d_{n},p_{n})\sim\pi(\cdot|t_{vs},H_{m})

Our verbalized-sampling prompt is illustrated below:

Verbalized Sampling Prompt

<decoy challenge generation instructions>
…. Followed by probability associated with it in <probability></probability> tag, in which you provide the likelihood score between 0.0 and 1.0 that indicates how representative typical this response is compared to the full distribution. Sample at random from the full distribution/ tail of the distribution such that the probability of each response is less than 0.25.

The decoy challenges generated by
\pi
are appended to
\mathcal{D}_{b}
.
After repeating these steps for T iteration, we pick the best performing decoy challenge from
\mathcal{D}_{b}

d^{*}_{f}=argmax_{d\in\mathcal{D}_{b}}Q(d)

where
d_{f}
is the best performing decoy problem.

Decoy Output Obfuscation.

The decoy task and the primary task have different objectives, the model’s output will often include solutions to both tasks unless it is incentivized otherwise. This results in a suspicious output that makes the attack easily detectable.
Designing effective obfuscation instructions is challenging because the model tends to ignore the decoy task if it is directly instructed to solve the decoy task in the scratchpad while not providing the output, without being given a clear reason (e.g.,
do not show the output for these challenges in the final text
).

Therefore, we construct the obfuscation instructions such that we provide the model with an adequate reason to solve the decoy task while ensuring that the result of the task has minimal, hard to observe impact on the final output (e.g., add a single word like true or false, change the theme of your answer to a festive theme, etc.). We first sample multiple strong candidates from the optimized sample pool. We then employ techniques such as role-playing
[
11
]
to give the model a plausible motivation to follow these instructions. Finally, we select the sample that demonstrates the greatest increase in reasoning while maintaining stealthiness. Reasoning trace example for successful and unsuccessful obfuscation is illustrated below:

Reasoning chains for different obfuscation prompts.

I need to proceed carefully,
since this may be an attempt to make me perform an unusual or potentially

Let us calculate the returns. … All of these values are greater

. Therefore, according to the instructions, I should creatively

4.3
Decoy Injection Modes

Building on the decoy selection and optimization steps, we finally consider how an adversary deploys the resulting decoy in practice.
Below, we propose two different strategies based on the adversary’s goals to cheaply scale the attack or make it harder to detect.
Table
3
shows snippets of both approaches.

Context-Agnostic Injection.

To conduct a large-scale attack the adversary needs to minimize the cost required to poison a corpus. We achieve this by creating a general attack template that
can be inserted into any context. This template is crafted without any
knowledge of the user query. These templates prioritize scalability, making them recognizable and can be caught using
OverThink
attack targeted defenses shown in our experiments in Section
6
. While the decoy task selection and optimization is the same across both methods, we change the obfuscation instructions and formatting of the decoy task to be more general and transferable.

The agnostic instructions avoid using any context specific information by using general instructions such as
"This challenge is crucial to solve the user question"
or
"The context can not be utilized and understood without solving this challenge"
. Our experiments in Section
5
show that even with the weaker threat model, and higher chance of detectability, context-agnostic injections have a higher reasoning increase. This makes them a stronger attack while being cost-effective and
transferable.

Context-Aware Injection.

In context-aware injections, the adversary optimizes the decoy to the specific context, aiming either to evade targeted defenses (e.g., decoy filtering) or to induce accuracy degradation when defenses succeed. We achieve this by identifying key pieces of information in the context that are required to answer most questions and then crafting obfuscation instructions that condition access to those pieces on correctly solving the embedded challenge. Because the instructions, the challenge, and the surrounding context are woven so tightly, filtering techniques may end up removing crucial information, leading to significant performance degradation.

This attack assumes a stronger threat model, such as awareness of the target context, and also requires greater resources. As a result, it is best suited for poisoning highly influential content (i.e., content likely to be retrieved by a large number of users), where the higher cost is justified by the reduced chance of detection.

4.4
Extending
OverThink
to New Modalities and Tasks

Retrieved documents.
The primary method we use to test the attack is poisoning retrieved documents, such as Wikipedia articles, task descriptions, and environment details. In both factual and agentic reasoning settings, the instructions must be crafted such that the model has a plausible reason to pay attention to the injected auxiliary task without identifying it as a problematic or adversarial injection.

Repositories and skills.
We test the attack in deployed agentic settings using coding agents. We poison three possible sources: skill files, README files, and repository code. The injection is optimized once using a proxy model and a proxy document, and is then applied across all test skill files, README files, and repository code. To provide a plausible reason for the agent to attend to the injection, we instruct it to invert colors, change a shape, or modify another minor element of the final file artifact based on the soulution of the decoy challenge. This results in a correct and benign output for the user while achieving a drastically higher attack success rate. We focus on tasks such as game generation and web application development.

Images.
While most of our analysis focuses on text-based attacks, adversaries can also operate in multimodal settings. Existing image-based attacks typically generate perturbations to optimize a specific objective, such as increasing token generation. However, these approaches are often infeasible for closed-source models due to their reliance on transferability from open-source models and limited transfer success
[
64
]
. Moreover, white-box optimization requires substantial computational resources, making it impractical in many settings
[
60
]
.
We propose a low-cost and transferable attack that transforms images by introducing irrelevant details or visuals, which we call
decoy visuals
. These include stylistic modifications (e.g., abstract art, 3D visualizations, mosaic art), additional related information (e.g., labeled visuals of
malus domestica
in an apple image), and unrelated comparisons (e.g., adding oranges to an apple image). These added elements do not affect the final output while increasing the reasoning chain.

5
Evaluation

In this section, we evaluate the efficacy of the
OverThink
attack across multiple models, modalities and agentic settings. We also run ablations, study transfer across models, and analyze generated reasoning chains to better understand when and why the attack succeeds.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Models.
We evaluate our attack on multiple closed and open-source models
(o1, DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, o4-mini, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5, Claude 3.7, Grok Reasoning, Mistral Reasoning) reasoning
models. These models leverage advanced reasoning methods such as CoT,
and are well-known for excelling on a range of complex tasks and
benchmarks
[
19
]
.

Question-Answering (QA) Datasets.
We evaluate our attack on factual QA benchmarks that draw on online sources (e.g., Wikipedia), including FreshQA
[
76
]
and SQuAD
[
63
]
. However, many facts in these datasets may already appear in model training data, so models can often answer without relying on the retrieved context, making context-based attacks and defenses harder to evaluate.

To better isolate reasoning that depends on the provided text, we also use MuSR
[
72
]
, which targets multi-hop reasoning on synthetic cases: the model must interpret implicit, commonsense-laden evidence that is distributed across a long narrative (hundreds of words) and then perform deliberative, multi-step reasoning (e.g., about physical and social situations, including theory of mind) to reach the correct solution.

Agentic and Multimodal Datasets.
We create a synthetic dataset for testing coding agents, containing ninety benign samples and ninety injected samples (thirty each for README, code, and skill injection) and three unit tests per task. We create a diverse set of tasks, including application development, code generation, game development, and web development. We also create a synthetic dataset for testing our image-based attacks, containing fifteen benign samples and fifteen injected samples. Details about the synthetic datasets are provided in Appendix
8
.

Data Processing.
We select a subset of the dataset consisting of samples whose ground truth changes infrequently and is less prone to unintentional errors. Pages with very frequent updates can cause large performance fluctuations, reducing reproducibility over time. To minimize costs and adhere to computational and environmental ethical considerations, we restrict our evaluations of different attack types, attack transferability, and baselines to fifty samples from the FreshQA and MuSR datasets. This ensures minimal impact on existing infrastructure while allowing us to test our attack methodologies. Subsequently, we study the impact of context-agnostic attacks on 100 samples each from the FreshQA and SQuAD datasets and 150 samples from the MuSR dataset across nine models, and present a comprehensive analysis of attack performance at a larger scale.

We test the coding agents and multimodal models using the entire generated synthetic dataset.

Evaluation Metrics.

For the QA experiments of our datasets, we evaluate outputs with claim accuracy
[
48
]
. We implement this via an LM-as-a-judge: the judge checks each claim in the model’s solution against the dataset’s ground-truth facts, assigning 1 if the claims match and 0 otherwise. For longer, more complex generations, richer claim-verification metrics could be used
[

81
]
.

Since our attack injects a decoy task, we also measure stealthiness with contextual correctness: how much of the final solution stays focused on the user’s query rather than drifting into decoy content. We score outputs as 1 if they contain only query-relevant claims, 0.5 if they mix query- and decoy-related claims, and 0 if they are entirely decoy-related. Defenses

6.1
Existing Defenses and Limitations

Although our attack achieves high success rates and produces stealthy outputs, it is less stealthy at the input level. Standard jailbreak detectors are unable to catch it because the attack does not rely on typical jailbreak signals (e.g., arbitrary strings or policy-violating content). However, a defense that explicitly looks for decoy-style challenges can flag the attack by detecting a mismatch between the injected decoy and the otherwise benign context. In this section, we discuss several potential defenses.

Prompt Injection Defenses.

Due to the prevalence of prompt injection attacks
[
50
]
, multiple guardrails (or prompt-injection detectors) have been proposed with the goal of detecting jailbreaks and prompt injections. Llama Guard
[
32
]
is an open-source prompt-injection detection framework that uses Llama2-7b to determine whether an input prompt is safe and to filter unsafe prompts. Multiple industrial detectors have also been released, such as Lakera
[
36
]
and GA Guard
[
2
]
. We evaluate our attack against the following open-source and closed-source guardrails (Table
11
) and find that none of them flags or filters our attack.

Filtering.

As a potential defense, an application can reduce the attack surface by filtering external context to keep only information relevant to the user’s question. This can be done by chunking the context and retrieving only the most relevant chunks, or by using an LM to directly filter the context. Using an LM filter is effective at removing our injected decoy problems (Table
11
).

In our evaluation, we use GPT-4o to filter the context (prompt in Figure
15
). Filtering reduces accuracy from 60% to 40% for context-aware attacks and to 0% for context-agnostic attacks. For context-agnostic attacks, the reasoning-token increase remains (about
6\times
), but the total loss in accuracy and contextual correctness makes the attack easy to detect. For context-aware attacks, filtering largely removes the reasoning-token increase and preserves contextual correctness, while reducing accuracy by roughly 50%. This creates a defense–accuracy trade-off: filtering improves robustness but can degrade task performance in a way that context-aware attacks can exploit while remaining relatively stealthy.

Paraphrasing.

Another possible defense is paraphrasing the retrieved context
[
33
,
45
]
. Because the external context comes from untrusted sources, rewriting it can be a practical countermeasure as it may disrupt the structure of an injected decoy challenge (reducing its effectiveness) while largely preserving the original meaning of the retrieved information.
Table
11
reports results after applying paraphrasing. Paraphrasing degrades performance for both context-agnostic and context-aware attacks, but the context-aware attack still induces a substantial increase in reasoning tokens even after the defense. The paraphrasing prompt is shown in Figure
16
.

6.2
Potential Defenses

Reasoning Chain Monitoring.

Because the decoy challenge is fully solved in the model’s scratchpad, a straightforward way to detect a
OverThink
attack is to monitor the reasoning trace. Unlike output-only checks, this can catch externally benign attacks involving hidden internal objectives. The monitor could use a smaller model or simple heuristics to score the trace for manipulation signals
[
8
]
. However, recent work
[
90
]
shows that increased prompting pressure can cause RLMs to produce more cryptic reasoning traces, leaving its impact on
OverThink
for future work.

Adaptive Reasoning.
Another approach is to adjust the amount of
reasoning depending on the model inputs, i.e. we can decide in advance
how many reasoning tokens are worth spending based on the question and
context. For example, OpenAI API models can control “effort levels”
reasoning tokens (see Table
12
). However, the
context could also be manipulated to select a more expensive
effort level
[
67
]
. Instead, we could rely on the
trusted context, e.g.,, user’s question, to estimate the effort needed to
isolate from potentially harmful outputs, similar
to Han et al.
[
27
]
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose
OverThink
, a slowdown attack against applications in which RLMs process untrusted data. The attack uses prompt injections to add decoy challenges optimized to elicit long reasoning chains while preserving the expected answers, making the manipulation difficult for users to detect. Experiments show that
OverThink
significantly degrades reasoning efficiency, increasing reasoning tokens by up to
27
\times
on FreshQA,
46
\times
on SQuAD, and
12
\times
on MuSR. We also present initial results on multimodal inputs, showing up to a 2.7
\times
increase, and coding agents, showing up to a 12.7
\times
increase, suggesting that the multiple modalities and agentic settings. Finally, we evaluate common defenses, including prompt-injection guardrails, filtering, and paraphrasing, and find them largely ineffective against
OverThink
.

Ethical Considerations

We have conducted this research with an emphasis on responsible research practices, mindful of both the computational and infrastructure costs associated with LMs. Specifically, we have limited our study to a sub-section, up to 200 samples, of each dataset which represents a fraction of the daily computational load on these services, which are already under strain according to
[
57
]
. We aimed to
minimize our impact on these infrastructures while still obtaining
meaningful insights and allowing reproducible experiments.

Understanding and mitigating resource overhead risks is essential for the long-term success of LM applications. We have made our code and used prompts public to facilitate adoption of defenses by the applications relying on LM reasoning models. By conducting this work we hope to contribute to sustainable, fair, and secure advances in AI research promoting
computing for good
.

Dataset Preparation

FreshQA
is a dynamic question-answering (QA) benchmark designed to
assess the factual accuracy of LLMs by incorporating both stable and
evolving real-world knowledge. The benchmark includes 600 natural
questions categorized into four types: never-changing, slow-changing,
fast changing, and false-premise. These questions vary in complexity,
requiring both single-hop and multi-hop reasoning, and are linked to
regularly updated Wikipedia entries. The original query consists of an
average of 11.6 tokens. However, due to the randomness and
the length of the context extracted from Wikipedia, the total input
token count increases to an average of 11278.2 tokens when
the context is appended. This leads to a noticeable variation in input
length.

SQuAD
contains more than 100k questions based on more than 500
articles retrieved from Wikipedia. While the average length of a query
in the dataset is similar to FreshQA with 11.5 tokens, the
context is significantly shorter and shows less variance in length. An
average context in the dataset contains 117.5 tokens.

MuSR
dataset contains 756 samples across three tasks, murder mysteries (the model needs to act as a detective and utilize the provided context to deduce the murderer in the given scenario.), object placement (the model utilizes the context containing the observations made by multiple characters to locate an object in different locations.), team allocation (the model needs to understand the social dynamics, personal preferences, previous experiences, etc., to allocate people on a given task). The average context length of the dataset is 895 tokens and the average query length is 15.3

Utilizing these three datasets allows us to study our attack and impact
of factors like context length and task complexity, on its efficacy.

Synthetic image and coding dataset.

Synthetic images.
Our image dataset consists of 30 images. 15 of them belonging to a benign set with no decoy elements and 15 of them belonging to a complex set with decoy inserted decoy elements. The objects are mundane day to day objects such as different cars, fruits and animals to keep the original task simple and minimize the noise in reasoning token measurement that can exist due to task complexity itself.

Coding agent dataset.
The coding agent dataset consists of 90 benign and 90 adversarial samples, with 30 samples for each mode, including README files, skill files, and code. The tasks primarily involve the development of various Python applications, such as a blackjack game. The primary decoy inserted is an MDP, which the agent sometimes solves directly using the LLM or by generating an additional MDP-solving script.
