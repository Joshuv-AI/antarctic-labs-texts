Dunkl and Cherednik operators
Oleg Chalykh

Abstract
. This survey article, written for the Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics , 2nd edition, is devoted to the remarkable family of operators introduced by Charles Dunkl and to their q q -analogues discovered by Ivan Cherednik. The main focus is on the rôle of these operators in studying integrable many-body systems such as the Calogero–Moser and the Ruijsenaars systems. To put these constructions into a wider context, we indicate their relationship with the theory of the rational Cherednik algebras and double affine Hecke algebras. While we do not include proofs, references to the original research articles are provided, accompanied by brief historical comments.

If

\Lambda\subset V

with

\mathbb{C}(V)
in the obvious way.

Cherednik operators are a non-trivial generalisation of the Dunkl operators: they are commuting elements of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
which can be viewed as a deformation of the operators
t(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Their theory is intrinsically linked to the theory of the double affine Hecke algebras (DAHAs) and Macdonald polynomials, while the Dunkl operators are similarly related to the rational version of DAHAs, the rational Cherednik algebras. There also exists an intermediate theory of degenerate DAHAs, related to the trigonometric Dunkl and rational Cherednik operators.
Last but not least, both the Dunkl and Cherednik operators admit elliptic generalisations important for the study of the elliptic versions of the Calogero–Moser and Ruijsenaars systems. In particular, they can be used to construct quantum and classical Lax matrices for these systems.

2.
Dunkl operators for Coxeter groups and rational Calogero–Moser system

2.1.
Dunkl operators

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group with its reflection representation
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, equipped with a
W
-invariant complex Euclidean form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Let
R\subset V
be the root system of
W
. Each root
\alpha
defines a linear function from
V
to
\mathbb{C}
by
x\mapsto\langle\alpha,x\rangle
. This identifies
V
with
V^{*}
, and the canonical pairing between
V,V^{*}
with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Let us choose a
W
-invariant
multiplicity function

k\,:\,R\to\mathbb{C}
,
\alpha\mapsto k_{\alpha}
. It is customary to partition
R
into
R_{+}\sqcup-R_{+}
. Note that
k_{\alpha}=k_{-\alpha}
. If
W
is irreducible,
k
takes on at most two different values.
The
Dunkl operators
are the following elements of
\mathcal{D}(V)*W
:

y_{\xi}:=\hbar\partial_{\xi}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}s_{\alpha}\,,\quad\xi\in V.

(2.1)

Here
\hbar\neq 0
is a parameter
2
2

2

To follow the standard conventions of quantum mechanics,
\hbar
should be replaced
-\mathrm{i}\hbar
and
k_{\alpha}
should be purely imaginary.
which is only needed to handle the classical limit, otherwise one may assume
\hbar=1
.
The main two properties of the operators (
2.1
) are:

(1)

commutativity:

\,y_{\xi}\,y_{\eta}=y_{\eta}\,y_{\xi}\,

\forall\ \xi,\eta\in V
,

(2)

W
- equivariance:

\,w\,y_{\xi}w^{-1}=y_{w.\xi}\,

\forall\ w\in W
.

As a result, the assignment
\,\xi\mapsto y_{\xi}\,

extends to a
W
- equivariant algebra map

\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\to\mathcal{D}(V)*W\,,\quad q\mapsto q(y)\ .

(2.2)

The following version of the Dunkl operators is often used:

T_{\xi}:=\partial_{\xi}+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}(1-s_{\alpha})\,,\quad\xi\in V.

(2.3)

The family
\{T_{\xi}\}
is also commutative and
W
- equivariant, and has the advantage of preserving the polynomial subspace
\mathbb{C}[V]
when acting on
\mathbb{C}(V)
.

2.2.
Rational Calogero–Moser system

The operators (
2.1
) can be used to prove the integrability of the
rational Calogero–Moser system
. The following calculation is important for that.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\partial_{i}=\partial_{\xi_{i}}
and
y_{i}=y_{\xi_{i}}
, where
\{\xi_{i}\,|\,i=1\dots n\}
is an orthonormal basis in
V
. For
\langle y,y\rangle:=y_{1}^{2}+\dots+y_{n}^{2}
, a direct calculation shows that

\langle y,y\rangle=\hbar^{2}\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\frac{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle^{2}}k_{\alpha}(k_{\alpha}-\hbar s_{\alpha})\,,\qquad\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}^{2}\,.

(2.4)

Introduce the linear map

\mathrm{Res}:\ \mathcal{D}(V)*W\,\to\,D(V)\,,\qquad\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}w\mapsto\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}\,.

(2.5)

Combining this map with (
2.2
), define

L_{q}=\mathrm{Res}\,q(y)\,,\qquad q\in\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}\,.

(2.6)

If
q
is homogeneous,
L_{q}
is a differential operator with the leading symbol
q(\hbar\partial)
. The
W
-invariance of
q
and the commutativity of the Dunkl operators imply that (1) each
L_{q}
is
W
-invariant, (2)
L_{q}L_{q^{\prime}}=L_{qq^{\prime}}
for any
q,q^{\prime}
, (3) the family
\{L_{q}\,,\ q\in\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}\}
is commutative. Hence, the following result.

Theorem 2.2
.

The map
q\mapsto L_{q}
defines an algebra embedding
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}\to\mathcal{D}(V)^{W}
.

For any Coxeter group
W
, the algebra
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}
is a polynomial algebra on
n=\dim V
generators, so this theorem produces
n
algebraically independent differential operators
L_{q}\in\mathcal{D}(V)^{W}
. For
q=\langle\xi,\xi\rangle
, we readily find from (
2.4
) that

L_{\langle\xi,\xi\rangle}=\hbar^{2}\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\frac{k_{\alpha}(k_{\alpha}-\hbar)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle^{2}}\,.

(2.7)

This is known as the Hamiltonian of the quantum rational Calogero–Moser system associated to the group
W
.

Corollary 2.3
.

The quantum rational Calogero–Moser system (
2.7
) is completely integrable, that is, it admits
n=\dim V
pairwise commuting, algebraically independent Hamiltonians.

The same construction, using
T_{\xi}
instead of
y_{\xi}
, demonstrates the integrability of the Calogero–Moser Hamiltonian in “radial form”

L=\Delta+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\frac{2k_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}\,.

(2.8)

Example 2.4
.

Consider
W=S_{n}
acting on
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
by permuting the basis vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
; it is generated by permutations
s_{ij}
,
i\neq j
. The ring
\mathbb{C}(V)
is the ring of functions of
n
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. The root system

R=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\ |\ 1\leq i\neq j\leq n\}

is of type
A_{n-1}
, and the multiplicity function
k:R\to\mathbb{C}
amounts to a single number
k\in\mathbb{C}
. Taking
y_{i}=y_{e_{i}}
, we obtain

n
commuting Dunkl operators

y_{i}=\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}-k\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{x_{i}-x_{j}}s_{ij}\,,\qquad i=1\dots n\,.

In this case the Calogero–Moser operator (
2.7
) becomes

L=\hbar^{2}\Delta-\sum_{i<j}\frac{2k(k-\hbar)}{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}\,.

The Dunkl operators can also be used to construct the so-called
shift operators
that intertwine operators (
2.6
) for different values of
k
. Let us write
L_{q}(k)
to indicate the dependence of (
2.6
) on the multiplicity function,
k
. Modify (
2.5
) by defining
\mathrm{Res}^{-}\left(\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}w\right)=\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)a_{w}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Set
\hbar=1
and
define
S=S(k)\in\mathcal{D}(V)
by
S=\mathrm{Res}^{-}\left(\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}y_{\alpha}\right)
. Then

L_{q}(k+1)S(k)=S(k)L_{q}(k)\qquad\forall\ q\in\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}

(2.9)

where the multiplicity
k+1
is defined by
(k+1)_{\alpha}=k_{\alpha}+1

\forall\ \alpha
.

2.3.
Classical case

The classical limit corresponds to taking
\hbar\to 0
. More precisely, we view the Dunkl operators as elements of the algebra

A_{\hbar}*W=\mathbb{C}(V)[\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{p}_{n}][[\hbar]]*W\,,

where the quantum momenta
\hat{p}_{k}=\hbar\partial_{\xi_{k}}
satisfy the commutation relations
[\hat{p}_{k},f]=\hbar\,\partial_{\xi_{k}}f
for
f\in\mathbb{C}(V)
. We have an algebra isomorphism

\eta_{0}:\ (A_{\hbar}*W)/\hbar(A_{\hbar}*W)\to A_{0}*W\,,\qquad f\mapsto f\,,\ \ \hat{p}_{k}\mapsto p_{k}\,,\ \ w\mapsto w\,,

where

A_{0}=\mathbb{C}(V)[p_{1},\dots,p_{n}]=\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]
is the classical version of
A_{\hbar}
. Therefore,
A_{\hbar}
(resp.
A_{\hbar}*W
) is a formal deformation of
A_{0}
(resp.
A_{0}*W
). Note that
A_{0}
is commutative, with the Poisson bracket defined by
\{\eta_{0}(a),\eta_{0}(b)\}=\eta_{0}(\hbar^{-1}[a,b])
for
a,b\in A_{\hbar}
. For any
a\in A_{\hbar}*W
, we call
\eta_{0}(a)
the
classical limit
of
a
. Thus, the classical limit of (
2.1
) is the
classical Dunkl operator

y_{\xi,c}=p_{\xi}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}s_{\alpha}\,.

Here
p_{\xi}
is the classical momentum in direction
\xi
. The operators
y_{\xi,c}
are commuting elements of
A_{0}*W
, and we have a classical variant of (
2.2
):

\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\to A_{0}*W\ ,\quad q\mapsto q(y_{c})\,.

(2.10)

Theorem 2.6
.

The map
q\mapsto L_{q,c}=\mathrm{Res}\,q(y_{c})

defines an algebra embedding
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}\to A_{0}^{W}
, whose image is a Poisson-commutative subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{c}\subset A_{0}^{W}
.

As a corollary, this proves integrability (in Liouville sense) of the classical rational Calogero–Moser system described by

L_{\langle\xi,\xi\rangle,c}=\langle p,p\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\frac{k_{\alpha}^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle^{2}}\,.

(2.11)

2.4.
Rational Cherednik algebras

These constructions can be put into a wider context. Given
V
,
W
and the multiplicity function
k
as above, we define the
rational Cherednik algebra

H_{k}=H_{\hbar,k}
as the subalgebra of
\mathcal{D}(V)*W
generated by
\mathbb{C}W
,
\mathbb{C}[V]
,
and the image of
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]
under (
2.2
). Abstractly,
H_{k}
can be described by generators
\xi\in V
,
\eta\in V^{*}
,
w\in W
and relations

\displaystyle[\xi,\,\xi^{\prime}]=0\ ,\quad[\eta,\,\eta^{\prime}]=0\ ,\quad w\xi w^{-1}=w.\,\xi\,,\quad w\eta w^{-1}=w.\,\eta\ ,

\displaystyle[\xi,\eta]=\hbar\langle\xi,\eta\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\alpha^{\vee},\eta\rangle s_{\alpha}\,.

(2.12)

The above realization of
\,H_{k}
inside
\mathcal{D}(V)*W
, which sends

\xi\mapsto y_{\xi},\quad\eta\mapsto\eta,\quad w\mapsto w\,,

(2.13)

is referred to as the
Dunkl representation
. (Another representation of
H_{k}
can be defined by sending
\xi
to
T_{\xi}
.) The family
\{H_{k}\}
is a deformation (in fact,
universal deformation) of
\,H_{0}=\mathcal{D}[V]*W\,
.
In particular,
H_{k}
has the PBW property: a vector space isomorphism
H_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathbb{C}[V]\otimes\mathbb{C}W\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]
.
Recall the identification
*\,:\,V\to V^{*}
defined by
\alpha^{*}=\langle\alpha,-\rangle
, whose inverse is also denoted by
*
.
The symmetric nature of (
2.12
) implies that the mapping

V\ni\xi\mapsto\xi^{*}\,,\quad V^{*}\ni\eta\mapsto-\eta^{*}\,,\quad w\mapsto w

(2.14)

extends to an automorphism of
H_{k}
, which can be viewed as an (abstract) Fourier transform.

The
spherical subalgebra
of
H_{k}
is defined as
e\,H_{k}\,e\,
, where
\,e=|W|^{-1}\sum_{w\in W}w\,
is the symmetrising idempotent. Restricting (
2.5
) onto the spherical subalgebra, one obtains an algebra map

\mathrm{Res}:\,eH_{k}e\hookrightarrow\mathcal{D}(V)^{W}\,.

(2.15)

The family of algebras
B_{k}:=\mathrm{Res}(eH_{k}e)\subset\mathcal{D}(V)^{W}
represent the universal deformation of
B_{0}=\mathcal{D}[V]^{W}
. The algebra
B_{k}
has two commutative subalgebras:
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
and the algebra of operators (
2.6
); the automorphism (
2.14
) acts on
B_{k}
, exchanging them.

The algebras
H_{k}
and
B_{k}
have rich representation theory. The important
category
\mathcal{O}
is formed by the
H_{k}
-modules on which each
\xi\in V
acts locally nilpotently. The structure of this category is determined by the
standard
modules
, which play a rôle similar to Verma modules in Lie theory.
To define such modules we fix an irreducible representation
\tau
of
W

and extend the
W
-structure on
\tau
to a
\,\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]*W
-module
structure by letting
\,\xi\in V\,
act trivially.
The
standard
H_{k}
-module of type

\tau
is then given by

M(\tau):=\mathrm{Ind}^{H_{k}}_{\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]*W}\,\tau\,=\,H_{k}\underset{\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]*W}{\otimes}\tau\,.

(2.16)

The PBW property of
H_{k}
implies that
\,M(\tau)\cong\mathbb{C}[V]\otimes\tau\,

as a
\mathbb{C}[V]
-module. An important example is the trivial representation
\tau
, in which case
\,M(\mathrm{triv})\cong\mathbb{C}[V]\,
defines the so-called
polynomial representation
of
H_{k}
. One can check that the action of any
\xi\in V
on this space is given by the Dunkl operator (
2.3
).

2.5.
KZ connection

The Dunkl operators and Cherednik algebra can be used to construct a family of flat connections known as Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov (KZ) connections. Let

\delta(x)=\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\langle\alpha,x\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad V_{\rm{reg}}=\{x\in V\,|\,\delta(x)\neq 0\}\,.

Correspondingly, we have the algebras
\mathbb{C}[V_{\rm{reg}}]
and
\mathcal{D}[V_{\rm{reg}}]
of regular functions and differential operators on
V_{\rm{reg}}
.
Write
H_{k}[\delta^{-1}]\,
for the
localization of
H_{k}
on (powers of)
\delta
. From the Dunkl representation (
2.13
) it follows easily
that
one may identify
H_{k}[\delta^{-1}]\,\cong\mathcal{D}[V_{\rm{reg}}]*W
. Applying the same localisation to the standard module
M=M(\tau)
(
2.16
) produces the space
M[\delta^{-1}]\cong\mathbb{C}[V_{\rm{reg}}]\otimes\tau
, with an action of
\mathcal{D}[V_{\rm{reg}}]*W
. This allows us to view
M[\delta^{-1}]
as the space of sections of
a trivial vector bundle on
V_{\rm{reg}}
of rank
\dim\tau
, and a
\mathcal{D}[V_{\rm{reg}}]
-module. A direct calculation shows that the
\mathcal{D}
-module structure on
M[\delta^{-1}]
is
described by

\hbar\partial_{\xi}(f\otimes v)=\hbar\partial_{\xi}(f)\otimes v+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}f\otimes s_{\alpha}v\ ,

(2.17)

which defines a regular flat connection
on
\mathbb{C}[V_{\rm{reg}}]\otimes\tau
. It is
called a
KZ connection
with values in
\tau\,
: its
horizontal sections
\,\varphi:V_{\rm{reg}}\to\tau\,
satisfy the

KZ equations

\hbar\partial_{\xi}\varphi+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}s_{\alpha}(\varphi)=0\ ,\quad\forall\,\xi\in V\ .

(2.18)

An important
difference compared to (
2.1
) is that in (
2.18
), the reflections
s_{\alpha}
act on the
values
of
the functions involved, while in (
2.1
) they act on their arguments.

2.6.
Dunkl transform

In this section we assume that
k_{\alpha}\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in R
.
Given
y\in V
, consider the joint eigenvalue problem for the Dunkl operators (
2.3
):

T_{\xi}f=\langle\xi,y\rangle f\,,\quad\forall\ \xi\in V_{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(2.19)

Theorem 2.7
.

The system (
2.19
) has a unique solution
f:x\mapsto E_{k}(x,y)
which is real-analytic on
V_{\mathbb{R}}
and satisfies
f(0)=1
. Moreover, the mapping
(x,y)\mapsto E_{k}(x,y)
extends to a holomorphic function on
V\times V
.

The function
E_{k}
is called the
Dunkl kernel
. It has the following properties:

(1)

E_{k}(x,y)=E_{k}(y,x)
,

(2)

E_{k}(\lambda x,y)=E_{k}(x,\lambda y)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

(3)

E_{k}(w.x,w.y)=E_{k}(x,y)
for any
w\in W
,

(4)

\overline{E_{k}(x,y)}=E_{k}(\overline{x},\overline{y})
,

(5)

E_{0}(x,y)=\exp(\langle x,y\rangle)
.

Let

w_{k}(x)=\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}|\langle\alpha,x\rangle|^{2k_{\alpha}}\,,\qquad c_{k}=\int_{V_{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}w_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(2.20)

The
Dunkl transform
associated with
W
and the multiplicity function
k
is defined by

\widehat{f}(\xi)=c_{k}^{-1}\int_{V_{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)E_{k}(-i\xi,x)w_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad(\xi\in V_{\mathbb{R}})\,.

(2.21)

The
inverse transform
is defined by
f^{\vee}(\xi)=\widehat{f}(-\xi)
.

Theorem 2.8
.

(1)
The Dunkl transform is a homeomorphism of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(V_{\mathbb{R}})
.

(2)
(Plancherel theorem) The Dunkl transform has a unique extension to an isometric isomorphism of
L^{2}(V_{\mathbb{R}},w_{k})
.

(3)
(
L^{1}
-inversion) If both
f
and
\widehat{f}
belong to
L^{1}(V_{\mathbb{R}},w_{k})
, then
f=(\widehat{f})^{\vee}
almost everywhere.

2.7.
Generalisation for complex reflection groups

For this section only,
W
is
a finite subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(V)
generated by complex
reflections. An element
s\in W
,
s\neq\mathrm{id}
, is
a
complex reflection
if it
acts as
identity on some hyperplane
H_{s}
in
V
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of complex reflections
s
in
W
; the group
W
acts on
\mathcal{S}
by conjugation.
Write
\mathcal{A}
for the set of reflection hyperplanes
\,\{H_{s}\}\,
for reflections in
W
.
If
H\in\mathcal{A}
, the (pointwise) stabilizer of
H
in
W

is a finite cyclic subgroup
W_{H}\subseteq W
.
For each
H\in\mathcal{A}
we fix a covector
\alpha_{H}\in V^{*}
with
\ker\alpha_{H}=H
.

Given a
W
-invariant multiplicity function
k\,:\,\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
,
s\mapsto k_{s}
, we define the
Dunkl operators

as the following elements of
\mathcal{D}(V)*W\,
:

y_{\xi}:=\hbar\partial_{\xi}-\sum_{H\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\alpha_{H}(\xi)}{\alpha_{H}}\sum_{s\in W_{H}\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}}k_{s}s\ ,\quad\xi\in V\ .

(2.22)

These operators have the same basic properties as in the Coxeter case, namely, commutativity and
W
-equivariance. Similarly, the assignment
\,\xi\mapsto y_{\xi}\,

extends to an injective algebra homomorphism

\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\hookrightarrow\mathcal{D}(V)*W\ ,\quad q\mapsto q(y)\ .

(2.23)

With these definitions, Theorem
2.2
remains valid.
The algebra
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}
is, again, a polynomial algebra on
n=\dim V
generators, so this gives
n
algebraically independent differential operators (
2.6
). If
W
is non-Coxeter (and irreducible), all elements in
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}
have degrees
>2
and, in general, the quantum Hamiltonians
L_{q}
are too complicated to allow any explicit expression.

One also introduces the rational Cherednik algebra, category
\mathcal{O}
and KZ connection in the same way as it was done in the Coxeter case.

The Dunkl operators for Coxeter groups were introduced by Dunkl in

Rational Cherednik algebras were introduced and studied by Etingof and Ginzburg in

Classical limit of the Dunkl operators and the algebra

.
The category

for

]
. The link between Dunkl operators and KZ equations in the trigonometric setting goes back to Cherednik

. There is a correspondence between solutions to the KZ equations and the eigenfunctions of the operators (

[

]
, see also

]
for an interpretation of the shift operators from that viewpoint. Dunkl operators and Cherednik algebras can be used to study deformed Calogero–Moser systems and generalisations, see

The Dunkl kernel and Dunkl transform are due to Dunkl

, see also Opdam’s work

3.
Affine Hecke algebras and Cherednik operators

3.1.
Affine Weyl groups and Hecke algebras

Let
R\subset V_{\mathbb{R}}
be a reduced, irreducible crystallographic root system,
and
W
be the Weyl group of
R
, generated by the orthogonal reflections
s_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in R
. As before, we extend the action of
W
onto
V
by
\mathbb{C}
-linearity.
We write
R^{\vee}=\{\alpha^{\vee}\}
for the dual system formed by the coroots
\alpha^{\vee}=2\alpha/\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle
.
Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
be a fixed basis of simple roots in
R
, associated with a decomposition
R=R_{+}\sqcup R_{-}
. We have the coroot and coweight lattices:
Q^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}a_{i}^{\vee}
and
P^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}
, where the fundamental coweights
b_{i}\in V
are defined by
\langle a_{i},b_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
. We write
Q^{\vee}_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}a_{i}^{\vee}
and
P^{\vee}_{+}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,b_{i}
for the cones of
positive
coroots and
dominant
coweights, respectively.

The
affine Weyl group
is defined as
W_{a}=W\ltimes t(Q^{\vee})
, where
t(Q^{\vee})
denotes the group of translations
t(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in Q^{\vee}
acting on
V
by

t(\lambda).x=x-c\lambda
, where
c
is a fixed parameter. The
extended
affine Weyl group is
\widehat{W}=W\ltimes t(P^{\vee})
. The group
\widehat{W}
acts naturally on functions on
V
. In particular, a translation
t(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in P^{\vee}
acts by

t(\lambda).f(x)=f(x+c\lambda)
, i.e.,
t(\lambda)=e^{c\partial_{\lambda}}
.

Let
\widehat{V}
denote the space of affine-linear complex-valued functions on
V
. We identify
\widehat{V}
with
V\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta
, where vectors in
V
are considered as linear functionals on
V
via the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and where
\delta\equiv c
on
V

(so
e^{\delta}=q:=e^{c}
).
Let

R_{a}=\{\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha+m\delta\,,m\in\mathbb{Z}\,,\alpha\in R\}\subset\widehat{V}

(3.1)

be the affine root system associated with
R
. The action of
\widehat{W}
on
\widehat{V}\subset\mathbb{C}(V)
permutes affine roots. For any
\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha+m\delta
we have the orthogonal reflection with respect to the hyperplane
\widetilde{\alpha}(x)=0
in
V
,

s_{\widetilde{\alpha}}.\,x=x-\widetilde{\alpha}(x)\alpha^{\vee}\,,\quad x\in V\,.

We extend the set of simple roots
a_{i}
to a basis in
R_{a}
by adding
a_{0}=\delta-\varphi
, where
\varphi
is the highest root in
R_{+}
.
The simple reflections
s_{i}=s_{a_{i}}
,
i=0,\dots,n
generate the group
W_{a}
, and the
length

l(w)
of
w\in W_{a}
is defined as the length
l
of a reduced (i.e., shortest) decomposition

w=s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{l}}\,,\quad\text{with}\ 0\leq i_{k}\leq n\,.

(3.2)

Let
\Omega
be the subgroup of the elements
\omega\in\widehat{W}
which map the basis
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
to itself. It is known that
\Omega
is an abelian group, isomorphic to
P^{\vee}/Q^{\vee}
, and the extended affine Weyl group is isomorphic to
W_{a}\rtimes\Omega
. Each
\tilde{w}\in\widehat{W}
admits a unique presentation as
\tilde{w}=w\omega
with
w\in W_{a}
and
\omega\in\Omega
. We use this to extend the notion of the length from
W_{a}
to
\widehat{W}
by setting
l(w\omega)=l(w)
, so
l(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

The
braid group

\mathfrak{B}
of
\widehat{W}
is the group with generators
T_{w}
,
w\in\widehat{W}
, and relations

T_{v}T_{w}=T_{vw}\quad\text{if}\quad l(v)+l(w)=l(vw)\,.

(3.3)

Write
T_{i}:=T_{s_{i}}
for
i=0,\dots,n
. Then for any reduced decomposition
w=s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{l}}\omega
we have

T_{w}=T_{i_{1}}\dots T_{i_{l}}T_{\omega}
. It follows that
\mathfrak{B}
is generated by
T_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,n
and
T_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, subject to the following relations:

\displaystyle T_{i}T_{j}\dots=T_{j}T_{i}\dots\qquad\text{for $i\neq j$, with $m_{ij}$ factors on either side}\,,

(3.4)

\displaystyle T_{\omega}T_{\omega^{\prime}}=T_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}\quad\text{for}\ \omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega\,,

(3.5)

\displaystyle T_{\omega}T_{i}T_{\omega}^{-1}=T_{j}\qquad\text{if}\ \omega s_{i}\omega^{-1}=s_{j}\,.

(3.6)

Here
m_{ij}=2,3,4,6
is the order of
s_{i}s_{j}\in W_{a}
.

The braid group
\mathfrak{B}
contains an important abelian subgroup
\{Y^{\lambda}\,|\,\lambda\in P^{\vee}\}
.
Namely, if
\lambda,\mu\in P^{\vee}
are dominant, then from (
3.3
)
T_{t(\lambda)}T_{t(\mu)}=T_{t(\mu)}T_{t(\lambda)}=T_{t(\lambda+\mu)}
. Hence,
Y^{\lambda}:=T_{t(\lambda)}
with
\lambda\in P_{+}^{\vee}
form an abelian semigroup, and we extend it by setting
Y^{\lambda}=Y^{\mu}(Y^{\nu})^{-1}
whenever
\lambda=\mu-\nu
with
\mu,\nu\in P^{\vee}_{+}
.

Choose nonzero parameters
\tau_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,n
such that
\tau_{i}=\tau_{j}
if
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
are conjugated in
\widehat{W}
. We use
\tau
to denote the set of parameters. Note that
\tau_{i}
’s take on at most two different values. The (extended)
affine Hecke algebra

\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is the quotient of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{B}
by relations

(T_{i}-\tau_{i})(T_{i}+\tau_{i}^{-1})=0\,,\qquad i=0,\dots n\,.

(3.7)

For
\tau_{i}=1
we have
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\cong\mathbb{C}\widehat{W}
. The subalgebra
\mathfrak{H}\subset\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
generated by
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
is known as the (finite)
Hecke algebra
of
W
.
The elements
T_{w}
,
w\in\widehat{W}
(
w\in W
, respectively) form a
\mathbb{C}
-basis of
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
(
\mathfrak{H}
, respectively). An alternative basis for
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is given by the elements
T_{w}Y^{\lambda}
with
w\in W
,
\lambda\in P^{\vee}
.

3.2.
Basic representation and Cherednik operators

Setting
q=e^{c}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)\subset\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}(V))
be the algebra of difference operators for the lattice
\Lambda=P^{\vee}
. We view the group algebra
\mathbb{C}\widehat{W}
as a subalgebra of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
, by sending

\widehat{W}\ni wt(\lambda)\mapsto we^{c\partial_{\lambda}}\qquad(w\in W,\ \lambda\in P^{\vee})\,.

(3.8)

The algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
can also be realized as a subalgebra of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
. This is called the
basic representation
of
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
. To describe it, extend the set of parameters
\tau_{i}
to
\tau_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in R_{a}
so that
\tau_{\alpha}=\tau_{w(\alpha)}
for
w\in\widehat{W}
, and introduce
\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}(V)
as follows:

\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}=\frac{\tau_{\alpha}^{-1}-\tau_{\alpha}e^{\alpha}}{1-e^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\alpha\in R_{a}\,.

(3.9)

Theorem 3.1
.

The extended affine Hecke algebra admits a faithful representation
\beta:\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
such that

\displaystyle\beta:\,T_{i}\mapsto\tau_{i}+\mathbf{c}_{i}(s_{i}-1)\,,\qquad\mathbf{c}_{i}=\mathbf{c}_{a_{i}}\qquad(i=0,\dots,n)\,,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\beta:\,T_{\omega}\mapsto\omega\quad\text{for}\quad\omega\in\Omega\,.

(3.11)

The
Cherednik operators
are, by definition, the images of
Y^{\lambda}
under
\beta
. They form a commutative family of difference-reflection operators, and should be viewed as
q
-analogues of the Dunkl operators. In comparison, they are rather complicated. To find
Y^{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in P_{+}^{\vee}
, for example, one first finds a reduced decomposition
t(\lambda)=s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{l}}\omega
and then writes the product
Y^{\lambda}=T_{i_{1}}\dots T_{i_{l}}T_{\omega}
in the basic representation.

From now on, we identify
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
with its image under
\beta
, in particular, making no distinction between
Y^{\lambda}
and the Cherednik operators. It is sometimes more convenient to write
Y^{\lambda}
in terms of the following elements
R(\alpha)
(“
R
-matrices”):

R(\alpha)=\tau_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}+\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}(1-s_{\alpha})\,,\quad\alpha\in R_{a}\,.

(3.12)

These elements satisfy the property

wR(\alpha)w^{-1}=R(w.\alpha)\,,\qquad\text{for any}\ w\in W_{a}\,.

Using this and the fact that
T_{i}=R(a_{i})s_{i}
for
i=0,\dots,n
, it is straightforward to rewrite
Y^{\lambda}
in terms of
R(\alpha)
instead of
T_{i}
.

The commutative subalgebra generated by the Cherednik operators will be denoted as
\mathbb{C}[Y]
, so elements
f(Y)\in\mathbb{C}[Y]
are arbitrary linear combinations of
Y^{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in P^{\vee}
. Inside
\mathbb{C}[Y]
we have the subalgebra
\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}
, spanned by the orbitsums
f=\sum_{\mu\in W\lambda}Y^{\mu}
.

3.3.
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-case

For the root system
R
of type
A_{n-1}
the above theory has another variant, referred to as the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-case.
We start with
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, with the orthonormal basis
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}
and the associated coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. The roots in
R
are
\alpha=\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}
with
i\neq j
, and the group
W={S}_{n}
acts on
V
by permuting the basis vectors. Instead of
P^{\vee}(R)
we choose the lattice
\Lambda
to be

\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\epsilon_{i}
. There is only one parameter
\tau
so
\tau_{i}=\tau
. The algebra of difference operators
{\mathcal{D}}_{q}=\mathbb{C}(x)\ltimes t(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, associated with
\Lambda
, is generated by
\mathbb{C}(x)
and
t(\epsilon_{k})=e^{c\partial_{k}}
(
1\leq k\leq n
).
The simple roots are
a_{i}=\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i+1}
(
1\leq i<n
), with simple reflections
s_{a_{i}}=s_{i,i+1}
.

The extended affine Weyl group
W_{a}=S_{n}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is generated by
s_{i,i+1}
and
\omega
acting on
f\in\mathbb{C}(x)
by

(\omega.f)(x)=f(x_{2},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}-c)\,.

(3.13)

The subgroup
\Omega
(of elements of length zero) is generated by
\omega
. The algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is generated by
T_{1},\dots,T_{n-1}
and
T_{\omega}
, subject to relations (
3.5
), (
3.7
), the relations that
T_{\omega}^{n}
is central in
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
and that
T_{\omega}T_{i}=T_{i+1}T_{\omega}
for
1\leq i<n
. The basic representation
\beta\,:\,\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}*S_{n}
is defined by

\beta(T_{\omega})=\omega\,,\quad\beta(T_{i})=\tau+\mathbf{c}_{i,i+1}(s_{i,i+1}-1)\,,\qquad\mathbf{c}_{ij}=\frac{\tau e^{x_{i}}-\tau^{-1}e^{x_{j}}}{e^{x_{i}}-e^{x_{j}}}\,.

(3.14)

The commuting Cherednik operators
Y_{i}=Y^{\epsilon_{i}}
are

Y_{i}=T_{i}T_{i+1}\dots T_{n-1}\,T_{\omega}\,T_{1}^{-1}\dots T_{i-1}^{-1}\quad(i=1,\dots,n)\,.

(3.15)

Using the elements

R_{ij}=\tau s_{ij}+\mathbf{c}_{ij}(1-s_{ij})\,,

(3.16)

these can be rewritten as

Y_{i}=R_{i,i+1}R_{i,i+2}\dots R_{i,n}\,t(\epsilon_{i})\,R_{1i}^{-1}\dots R_{i-1,i}^{-1}\quad(i=1,\dots,n)\,.

(3.17)

3.4.
C^{\vee}C_{n}
case

Another special case is related to the non-reduced affine root system of type
C^{\vee}C_{n}
. Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the standard orthonormal basis
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
and the associated coordinates
\{x_{i}\}
. Let
R
be the root system of type
C_{n}
,

R=\{\pm 2\epsilon_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq n\}\cup\{\pm\epsilon_{i}\pm\epsilon_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq n\}\,.

(3.18)

The Weyl group
W={S}_{n}\ltimes\{\pm 1\}^{n}
of
R
consists of the transformations that permute the basis vectors
\epsilon_{i}
and change their signs arbitrarily. As in
3.1
, we write
\widehat{V}=V\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta
for the space of affine-linear functions on
V
, with
\delta\equiv c
.
Let
R_{a}
be the affine root system associated with
R
(
3.1
). As a basis of simple roots, we choose

a_{0}=\delta-2\epsilon_{1}\,,\qquad a_{i}=\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i+1}\quad(i=1,\dots,n-1)\,,\qquad\alpha_{n}=2\epsilon_{n}\,.

For this case,
\widehat{W}=W_{a}=W\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is generated by
s_{i}=s_{a_{i}}
,
i=0,\dots,n
. Their action of the generators in coordinates is by

\displaystyle s_{0}\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(c-x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\,,

\displaystyle s_{i}\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},x_{i},\dots,x_{n})\quad(i=1,\dots,n-1)\,,

(3.19)

\displaystyle s_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},-x_{n})\,.

As in the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-case, we consider the algebra
\mathcal{D}_{q}
of difference operators in
n
variables, and view
\mathbb{C}\widehat{W}
as a subalgebra in
{\mathcal{D}}_{q}*W
.

The algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is generated by
T_{0},\dots,T_{n}
subject to the relations

\displaystyle T_{i}T_{i+1}T_{i}T_{i+1}=T_{i+1}T_{i}T_{i+1}T_{i}\quad(i=0,i=n-1)\,,

(3.20)

\displaystyle T_{i}T_{i+1}T_{i}=T_{i+1}T_{i}T_{i+1}\quad(i=1,\dots,n-2)\,,

(3.21)

\displaystyle T_{i}T_{j}=T_{j}T_{i}\,,\quad|i-j|\geq 2\,,

(3.22)

\displaystyle(T_{i}-\tau_{i})(T_{i}+\tau_{i}^{-1})=0\quad(i=0,\dots,n)\,,

(3.23)

where
\tau_{i}
are deformation parameters. Here it is assumed that
\tau_{1}=\dots=\tau_{n-1}=\tau
, so
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
depends on three parameters:
\tau_{0},\tau_{n}
and
\tau
.

The basic representation
\beta:\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\to{\mathcal{D}}_{q}*W
involves two additional parameters,
\tau_{0}^{\vee},\tau_{n}^{\vee}
. Let us introduce parameters
\tau_{\alpha}
and functions
\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in R_{a}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\tau\,,\quad\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}=\frac{\tau^{-1}-\tau e^{\alpha}}{1-e^{\alpha}}\,\quad\text{for\ }\alpha=k\delta\pm\epsilon_{i}\pm\epsilon_{j}\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z},\ i\neq j)\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\tau_{0}\,,\quad\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}=\tau_{0}^{-1}\frac{(1-\tau_{0}\tau_{0}^{\vee}e^{\alpha/2})(1+\tau_{0}(\tau_{0}^{\vee})^{-1}e^{\alpha/2})}{(1-e^{\alpha})}\,\quad\text{for\ }\alpha=(2k+1)\delta\pm 2\epsilon_{i}\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z})\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\tau_{n}\,,\quad\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}=\tau_{n}^{-1}\frac{(1-\tau_{n}\tau_{n}^{\vee}e^{\alpha/2})(1+\tau_{n}(\tau_{n}^{\vee})^{-1}e^{\alpha/2})}{(1-e^{\alpha})}\,\quad\text{for\ }\alpha=2k\delta\pm 2\epsilon_{i}\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z})\,.

With this notation, we define
\beta
on generators (and extend by multiplicativity) by

\beta:\,T_{i}\mapsto\tau_{i}+\mathbf{c}_{a_{i}}(s_{i}-1)\,,\quad i=0,\dots,n\,.

This defines a subalgebra of
{\mathcal{D}}_{q}*W
, isomorphic to
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
and depending on five parameters
\tau_{0},\tau_{0}^{\vee},\tau_{n},\tau_{n}^{\vee},\tau
.
The commutative subalgebra
\mathbb{C}[Y]
is generated by the operators
Y_{i}^{\pm 1}
, where
Y_{i}=Y^{\epsilon_{i}}
is

Y_{i}=T_{i}\dots T_{n-1}T_{n}T_{n-1}\dots T_{1}T_{0}T_{1}^{-1}T_{2}^{-1}\dots T_{i-1}^{-1}\,,\quad i=1,\dots,n\,.

3.5.
Macdonald–Ruijsenaars operators

Similarly to Dunkl operators, symmetric combinations of the Cherednik operators produce commuting operators. The map (
2.5
) becomes

\mathrm{Res}:\ \mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W\,\to\,D_{q}(V)\,,\qquad\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}w\mapsto\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}\,.

(3.24)

Theorem 3.2
.

The map
f\mapsto L_{f}=\mathrm{Res}\,f(Y)
defines an algebra embedding
\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)^{W}
.

The commutative family
\{L_{f}\,,\,{f\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}}\}
defines a quantum completely integrable system on
V
. Being difference operators, the Hamiltonians
L_{p}
depend exponentially on quantum momenta
\widehat{p}=-\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial
.

Example 3.3
.

In the notation of
3.3
, let
f_{r}=e_{r}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
be the elementary symmetric combinations of
Y_{i}
,
1\leq r\leq n
. Then the operators
L_{r}=\mathrm{Res}\,f_{r}(Y)
have the following form:

L_{r}=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}}{|I|=r}}\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i\in I}{j\notin I}}\mathbf{c}_{ij}\,\prod_{i\in I}t(\epsilon_{i})\,.

(3.25)

Up to a gauge transformation, these are commuting Hamiltonians of the
trigonometric Ruijsenaars system
, a relativistic version of the Calogero–Moser system.

Standard generators of
\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}
are the orbitsums for the fundamental coweights
b=b_{i}
, so we denote

f_{b}=\sum_{\pi\in Wb}Y^{\pi}\,,\qquad L_{b}=\mathrm{Res}\,(f_{b})\,.

(3.26)

The operators
L_{b}
are complicated in general. The following result gives explicit expressions for some of them, known as
Macdonald operators
.

Theorem 3.4
.

(i) Let
b\in P^{\vee}_{+}
be minuscule, so that
\langle\alpha,b\rangle
is either
0
or
1
for any
\alpha\in R_{+}
. Then

L_{b}=\sum_{\pi\in Wb}\,A_{\pi}\,t(\pi)\,,\qquad A_{\pi}=\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha\in R}{\dpr{\pi, \alpha}>0}}\,\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}\,.

(3.27)

(ii) Let
b\in P^{\vee}_{+}
be quasi-minuscule, i.e.
b=\varphi^{\vee}
, with
\varphi\in R_{+}
the highest root. In this case,
\langle\alpha,b\rangle\in\{0,1\}
for any
\alpha\in R_{+}\setminus\{\varphi\}
. Then

\displaystyle L_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in Wb}\,A_{\pi}\,(t(\pi)-1)\,,\qquad A_{\pi}=\mathbf{c}_{\delta+\pi^{\vee}}\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha\in R}{\dpr{\pi, \alpha}>0}}\,\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}\,.

(3.28)

Remark 3.5
.

Strictly speaking, the above expressions for
L_{b}
omit an additional constant summand.

Remark 3.6
.

In the
C^{\vee}C_{n}
-case, one takes
L_{r}=\mathrm{Res}\,f_{r}
, where
f_{r}
is the
r
-th elementary symmetric polynomial of
Y_{i}+Y_{i}^{-1}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Explicit expressions for
L_{r}
exist. The simplest one,
L_{1}
, is known as the
Koornwinder operator
.

3.6.
DAHAs

In the setting of
3.2
, let
P
be the weight lattice of
R
, defined by
\langle P,Q^{\vee}\rangle=\mathbb{Z}
. The group algebra of the lattice
P
will be denoted as
\mathbb{C}[X]
. It is spanned by
X^{\mu}
with
\mu\in P
, with
X^{\mu}X^{\nu}=X^{\mu+\nu}
. We view
\mathbb{C}[X]
as a subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}(V)
, by
X^{\mu}\mapsto e^{\mu}
.

Definition 3.7
.

The algebra
\mathbb{H}
, generated by
\mathbb{C}[X]
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
viewed as subalgebras of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
, is called the
double affine Hecke algebra
(DAHA) of type
R
.

Equivalently,
\mathbb{H}
is generated by
\mathbb{C}[X]
,
\mathbb{C}[Y]
and
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
. Moreover, the elements
X^{\mu}T_{w}Y^{\lambda}
with
\mu\in P
,
\lambda\in P^{\vee}
,
w\in W
form a linear basis in
\mathbb{H}
. Hence,
\mathbb{H}
has the PBW property: a vector space isomorphism
\mathbb{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathbb{C}[X]\otimes\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}[Y]
. In the above definition of
\mathbb{H}
, the commutative subalgebras
\mathbb{C}[X]
and
\mathbb{C}[Y]
seem to be of a rather different nature. Nevertheless, one has the following non-trivial result.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{\vee}
denote the DAHA associated to the dual root system
R^{\vee}
, i.e. with the rôles of
P
and
P^{\vee}
interchanged, and with the same parameters
\tau
. The
\mathbb{C}
-linear mapping
\omega\,:\,\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}^{\vee}
defined by

\omega(X^{\mu}T_{w}Y^{\lambda})=X^{-\lambda}T_{w^{-1}}Y^{-\mu}\quad(\mu\in P,\ \lambda\in P^{\vee},\ w\in W)

(3.29)

is an anti-isomorphism of algebras.

The
spherical DAHA
is defined as
e_{\tau}\mathbb{H}e_{\tau}
, where

e_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\sum_{w\in W}\tau_{w}^{2}}\sum_{w\in W}\tau_{w}T_{w}

(3.30)

is the idempotent in the Hecke algebra
\mathfrak{H}
corresponding to the one-dimensional character
T_{w}\mapsto\tau_{w}
determined by setting
T_{i}\mapsto\tau_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. It has two commutative subalgebras,
e_{\tau}\mathbb{C}[X]^{W}e_{\tau}
and
e_{\tau}\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}e_{\tau}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

The map (
3.24
) restricts to an algebra embedding
\mathrm{Res}\,:\,e_{\tau}\mathbb{H}e_{\tau}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)^{W}
.

This produces a subalgebra
\mathbb{B}:=\mathrm{Res}(e_{\tau}\mathbb{H}e_{\tau})
of
W
-invariant difference operators. It has two commutative subalgebras,
\mathbb{C}[X]^{W}
and
\mathrm{Res}(e_{\tau}\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}e_{\tau})=\mathrm{Res}(\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W})
constructed in Theorem
3.2
. The map (
3.29
) induces an anti-isomorphism
\omega\,:\,\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}^{\vee}
, exchanging these subalgebras.

3.7.
Classical case

The classical limit corresponds to
q=e^{c}\to 1
, and the procedure is similar to
2.3
. Namely, we set
c=\hbar\beta
, with some fixed
\beta
, and consider the algebra

A_{\hbar}*W=\mathbb{C}(V)[t_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,t_{n}^{\pm 1}][[\hbar]]*W\,,\qquad t_{k}:=e^{\hbar\beta\partial_{b_{k}}}\,,

where
\{b_{k}\}
is a basis for
\Lambda
(e.g., the fundamental coweights when
\Lambda=P^{\vee}
). We have

[t_{k},f]=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\hbar\beta)^{l}\partial_{b_{k}}^{l}(f)t_{k}\,,\qquad\forall\ f\in\mathbb{C}(V)\,.

The algebra
A_{\hbar}
is a formal deformation of the algebra
A_{0}=\mathbb{C}(V)[\Lambda]
whose elements are finite linear combinations
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}f_{\lambda}e^{\beta p_{\lambda}}
, where
f_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}(V)
and
p_{\lambda}
is the classical momentum in direction
\lambda
. The algebra
A_{0}
is commutative, with the induced Poisson bracket

\{e^{\beta p_{\lambda}},f\}=\beta\partial_{\lambda}(f)e^{\beta p_{k}}
. We have an algebra isomorphism (”classical limit” map)

\eta_{0}:\ A_{\hbar}*W/(\hbar A_{\hbar}*W)\to A_{0}*W\,,\qquad f\mapsto f\,,\ \ t_{k}\mapsto e^{\beta p_{b_{k}}}\,,\ \ w\mapsto w\,.

The classical Cherednik operators
Y^{\lambda}_{c}:=\eta_{0}(Y^{\lambda})
can be defined directly using the classical version of the basic representation,
\beta_{c}=\eta_{0}\circ\beta\,:\,\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\to A_{0}*W
. For any
f\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}
, the classical Macdonald–Ruijsenaars Hamiltonian
L_{f,c}=\eta_{0}(L_{f})
can be obtained as
L_{f,c}=\mathrm{Res}(f(Y_{c}))
, and the family
\{L_{f,c}\,,\,{f\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{W}}\}
is Poisson-commutative.

3.8.
Nonsymmetric Macdonald polynomials

For brevity, our discussion here is restricted to the settings of
3.2
,
3.6
. From (
3.10
), (
3.11
) it is clear that the algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
preserves the polynomial subspace
\mathbb{C}[X]
when acting on
\mathbb{C}(V)
. The
nonsymmetric Macdonald polynomials
form a basis in
\mathbb{C}[X]
, diagonalising the action of the Cherednik operators. To state the result, we need a particular partial ordering on the weight lattice
P
.

Let
Q_{+}
and
P_{+}
denote the cones of positive roots and dominant weights, respectively. We write
P_{-}:=-P_{+}
. For
\lambda\in P
, denote by
\lambda^{\pm}
the unique elements of
P_{\pm}\cap W\lambda
, and by
v(\lambda)\in W
the shortest element such that
v.\lambda=\lambda_{-}
. For
\lambda,\mu\in P_{+}
, we say that
\lambda<\mu
if
0\neq\mu-\lambda\in Q_{+}
. For
\lambda,\mu\in P
we say that
\lambda\leq\mu
if either
\lambda^{+}<\mu^{+}
or
\lambda^{+}=\mu^{+}
and
v(\lambda)\geq v(\mu)
with respect to the Bruhat order on
W
.
Note that any antidominant
\lambda\in P_{-}
is highest in its
W
-orbit.

Proposition 3.10
.

For generic parameters
q=e^{c}
and
\tau
, and for any
\mu\in P
, there is a unique
E_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}[X]
of the form
E_{\mu}=X^{\mu}+\mathrm{l.o.t.}
which is a common eigenfunction of the Cherednik operators:

Y^{\lambda}(E_{\mu})=\gamma_{\lambda,\mu}E_{\mu}\quad(\mu\in P,\ \lambda\in P^{\vee})

(3.31)

with
\gamma_{\lambda,\mu}\in\mathbb{C}
.

The polynomials
E_{\mu}
(
\mu\in P
) are called the monic
nonsymmetric Macdonald polynomials
. Their coefficients are rational functions of
q,\tau
.
The
symmetric Macdonald polynomials
are obtained from
E_{\mu}
by applying the symmetriser (
3.30
). They form a basis of
\mathbb{C}[X]^{W}
that diagonalises the action of the operators
L_{f}
from Theorem
3.2
.

3.9.
Affine
q
-KZ equations

The DAHAs can be used to construct a
q
-analogue of the KZ equations (
2.18
). Pick a module
\tau
over the affine Hecke algebra,
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
, and consider the space
M(\tau):=\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes\tau
. It will be viewed as
\tau
-valued meromorphic functions on
V
, acted upon by
(\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W)\otimes\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
. For
a\in\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)
,
w\in W
,
h\in\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
, we abbreviate the action of
aw\otimes h
on
M(\tau)
as
awh
. (Note that the actions of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
on
M(\tau)
commute.) Recall that the group algebra of
\widehat{W}
sits inside
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
by (
3.8
), and together with
f\in\mathbb{C}(V)
it generates
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
.

Proposition 3.11
.

The assignment

\displaystyle\nabla(f)

\displaystyle=f\,,

\displaystyle\nabla(s_{i})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{c}_{i})^{-1}s_{i}T_{i}+\frac{\mathbf{c}_{i}-\tau_{i}}{\mathbf{c}_{i}}s_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\nabla(\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega T_{\omega}

for
f\in\mathbb{C}(V)
,
i=0,\dots,n
,
\omega\in\Omega
extends uniquely to an algebra map
\nabla\,:\,\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W\to\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}M(\tau)
, that is, an action of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
on
M(\tau)
.

To arrive at the above definition of
\nabla
, one argues similarly to the calculation of the KZ connection (
2.17
). Namely, one first uses the embedding
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\to\mathbb{H}
and the PBW property of
\mathbb{H}
to induce a structure of an
\mathbb{H}
-module on
\mathbb{C}[X]\otimes\tau
. A localisation
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{reg}}
to a suitable subset
V_{\rm{reg}}\subset V
identifies
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{reg}}\cong\mathcal{D}_{q}[V_{\rm{reg}}]*W
, which in its turn induces a structure of a
\mathcal{D}_{q}[V_{\rm{reg}}]*W
-module on
M=\mathbb{C}[V_{\rm{reg}}]\otimes\tau
. A direct calculation
then shows that
s_{i}
acts on
M
by the above formula (for
f\in\mathbb{C}[V_{\rm{reg}}]
and
\omega\in\Omega
this is obvious). By analytic continuation, the same action is well defined on
M(\tau)=\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes\tau
.

Definition 3.12
.

The affine
q
-KZ equations (with values in a
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
-module
\tau
) is the following system of equations for
F\in M(\tau)
:

\nabla(t(\lambda))F=F\qquad\quad\forall\ \lambda\in P^{\vee}\,.

(3.32)

The action of
\nabla(w)
,
w\in W
makes the solution space to (
3.32
) into a
W
-module.

A convenient way of expressing
\nabla(t(\lambda))
and, more generally,
\nabla(w)
for any
w\in\widehat{W}
is by

\nabla(w)=C_{w}w\,,\quad C_{w}\in\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}\,.

Here
\{C_{w},\ w\in\widehat{W}\}
satisfy the cocycle condition

C_{ww^{\prime}}=C_{w}\,(w.C_{w^{\prime}})\qquad\forall\ w,w^{\prime}\in\widehat{W}\,.

(3.33)

(The action of
w
on
C_{w^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is by
w\otimes 1
.) From Proposition
3.11
we find

C_{s_{i}}=(\mathbf{c}_{i})^{-1}T_{i}+\frac{\mathbf{c}_{i}-\tau_{i}}{\mathbf{c}_{i}}=\frac{T_{i}^{-1}-e^{a_{i}}T_{i}}{\tau_{i}^{-1}-\tau_{i}e^{a_{i}}}\,,\qquad C_{\omega}=T_{\omega}\,,

so together with (
3.33
) this uniquely determines
C_{w}
.

3.10.
Trigonometric Dunkl operators, Calogero–Moser–Sutherland system, and degenerate DAHA

The rational Cherednik algebra can be regarded as a rational version of DAHA. There is also an intermediate version, referred to as the
degenerate
DAHA. In that version, the affine Hecke algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is replaced with the
graded Hecke algebra
, breaking the symmetry between
\mathbb{C}[X]
,
\mathbb{C}[Y]
.

We begin in the setting of
3.1
. For a
W
-invariant multiplicity function
k\,:\,R\to\mathbb{C}
,
set

\rho_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\alpha\,.

(3.34)

Definition 3.13

(Dunkl–Cherednik operator)
.

For
\xi\in V
, define

T_{\xi}=\partial_{\xi}+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\frac{1}{1-e^{-\alpha}}(1-s_{\alpha})-\langle\rho_{k},\xi\rangle\,.

(3.35)

This is a trigonometric analogue of the operators (
2.3
). Their key property is, again, the commutativity:
[T_{\xi},T_{\eta}]=0
for
\xi,\eta\in V
, hence the mapping
V\ni\xi\mapsto T_{\xi}
extends to an algebra map

\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\to\mathcal{D}(V)*W,\qquad q\mapsto T_{q}\,.

(3.36)

Proposition 3.14
.

The map
q\mapsto L_{q}:=\mathrm{Res}\,T_{q}
,
q\in\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}
defines an algebra embedding
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]^{W}\to\mathcal{D}(V)^{W}
.

By a direct calculation,

L_{\langle\xi,\xi\rangle}=\Delta+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{1+e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}\partial_{\alpha}+\langle\rho_{k},\rho_{k}\rangle\,.

(3.37)

This is a trigonometric analogue of (
2.8
). Using a suitable gauge transformation, it can be transformed into the hyperbolic Calogero–Moser–Sutherland Hamiltonian

L=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}(k_{\alpha}-1)\frac{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{4\sinh^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}}\,.

(3.38)

The Dunkl–Cherednik operators are manifestly not
W
-equivariant. An alternative definition is

S_{\xi}=\partial_{\xi}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\frac{1+e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}(1-s_{\alpha})\,.

(3.39)

These are called the
Dunkl–Heckman
operators. They are
W
-equivariant, but do not commute. Nevertheless, they can be used to show the integrability of the Hamiltonian (
3.37
).

Proposition 3.15
.

Given
v\in V
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, define

L_{v,r}=\mathrm{Res}\,\left(\sum_{\xi\in Wv}(S_{\xi})^{r}\right)\,.

Then the operators
L_{v,r}
for
v\in V
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
form a commutative family, containing the Hamiltonian (
3.37
).

Example 3.16
.

For
W=S_{n}
, the positive roots are
\alpha=e_{i}-e_{j}
with
i<j
, and
k_{\alpha}=k
for all
\alpha
. The Dunkl–Heckman operators
S_{i}=S_{e_{i}}
take the following form:

S_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{k}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\frac{e^{x_{i}}+e^{x_{j}}}{e^{x_{i}}-e^{x_{j}}}(1-s_{ij})\,.

Written in coordinates
z_{i}=e^{x_{i}}
, these become

S_{i}=z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}+\frac{k}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\frac{{z_{i}}+{z_{j}}}{{z_{i}}-{z_{j}}}(1-s_{ij})\,.

The operators

L_{r}=\mathrm{Res}\,\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}^{r}\right),\qquad r=1,\dots,n

(3.40)

generate the algebra of commuting quantum Hamiltonians in this case. A slightly different family of operators was introduced by Polychronakos:

\pi_{i}=z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}+{k}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\frac{{z_{i}}}{{z_{i}}-{z_{j}}}(1-s_{ij})\,.

(3.41)

They satisfy the commutation relations

[\pi_{i},\pi_{j}]=-k(\pi_{i}-\pi_{j})s_{ij}

which can be used to show that the operators

I_{r}=\mathrm{Res}\,\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}^{r}\right)\,,\qquad r=1,\dots,n

(3.42)

pairwise commute. Although
I_{r}\neq L_{r}
in general, the algebras generated by
L_{r}
and
I_{r}
coincide.

Let
\widehat{W}^{\vee}=\widehat{W}(R^{\vee})=W\rtimes P
be the extended affine Weyl group for the dual root system, and
\Omega^{\vee}\cong P/Q
be the abelian subgroup of elements of length zero. The group algebra of
\widehat{W}^{\vee}
can be realised inside
\mathcal{D}(V)*W
by sending
W\ni w\mapsto w
and
P\ni\lambda\mapsto e^{\lambda}
(viewed as a function
x\mapsto e^{\langle\lambda,x\rangle}
).

Definition 3.17
.

The subalgebra
\mathbf{H}_{k}
of
\mathcal{D}(V)*W
, generated by
w
,
e^{\lambda}
,
T_{\xi}
(
w\in W
,
\lambda\in P
,
\xi\in V
), is called the
degenerate DAHA
associated to the root system
R
and multiplicity function
k
.

The algebra
\mathbf{H}_{k}
has the PBW property: a vector space isomorphism
\mathbf{H}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\otimes\mathbb{C}\widehat{W}^{\vee}
. The elements
T_{p}e^{\lambda}w
(
p\in\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]
,
\lambda\in P
,
w\in W
) from a linear basis in
\mathbf{H}_{k}
.

Another realisation of the same algebra is constructed inside
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
(with the lattice
\Lambda=P
). Recall that one can realise
\widehat{W}^{\vee}
inside
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
, sending
W\ni w\mapsto w
,
P\ni\lambda\mapsto t(\lambda)=e^{c\partial_{\lambda}}
. This representation will be denoted
\pi_{0}
and it can be deformed as follows.
Introduce the elements

r_{i}=s_{i}-\frac{k_{i}}{a_{i}^{\vee}}(1-s_{i})\,,\quad(i=0,\dots,n)\,,\quad s_{i}=s_{a_{i}^{\vee}}\,.

(3.43)

Here
a_{0}^{\vee}=\delta-\psi^{\vee}
,
a_{1}^{\vee},\dots,a_{n}^{\vee}
are simple roots of
R_{a}^{\vee}
,
k_{i}=k_{a_{i}}
(
i=1,\dots,n
),
k_{0}=k_{\psi}
, with
\psi
the highest short root of
R
. This determines a representation of
\widehat{W}^{\vee}
by

\pi_{k}\,:\,\mathbb{C}\widehat{W}^{\vee}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W\,,\quad s_{i}\mapsto r_{i}\,,\quad\Omega^{\vee}\ni\omega\mapsto\omega\quad(i=0,\dots,n)\,.

(3.44)

(Note that
\pi_{k}(\omega)=\pi_{0}(\omega)
for
\omega\in\Omega
.) As a consequence, we have a realisation of the lattice
P
by
\lambda\mapsto\pi_{k}(\lambda)
. The difference operators

represent a rational limit of the Cherednik operators

.

Let us identify
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathbb{C}[V]
,
q\mapsto q^{*}
using the Euclidean form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Proposition 3.18
.

The algebra
\mathbf{H}_{k}
is isomorphic to the subalgebra
\mathbf{H}^{\prime}_{k}
of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
generated by
\mathbb{C}[V]
,
r_{i}
(
i=0,\dots,n
), and
\omega\in\Omega^{\vee}
. The isomorphism
\mathbf{H}_{k}\to\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\prime}
sends
W\ni s_{i}\mapsto r_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,n

\sigma_{\mu}(z)

\sigma_{\mu}(z)=\frac{\theta(z-\mu)\theta^{\prime}(0)}{\theta(z)\theta(-\mu)}\,,\qquad\mu,z\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(4.1)

Here
\theta(z)=\theta_{1}(z|\tau)
is the odd Jacobi theta function associated with the elliptic curve
\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau
.

For
\lambda\in V
, the
elliptic Dunkl operators
are the following elements of
\mathcal{D}(V)*W
:

y_{\xi}=\hbar\partial_{\xi}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle}(\langle\alpha,x\rangle)s_{\alpha}\ ,\quad\xi\in V\ .

(4.2)

The auxiliary
spectral variable

\lambda
is a distinctive feature of the elliptic case,
and we write
y_{\xi}(\lambda)
when need to emphasize the dependence on
\lambda
. Note that as a function of
\lambda
, (
4.2
) has poles along the hyperplanes
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle=m+n\tau
with
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Once again, two main properties of the Dunkl operators are their commutativity and equivariance:
for all
\,\xi,\eta\in V\,
and
w\in W
,

y_{\xi}\,y_{\eta}=y_{\eta}\,y_{\xi}\,,\qquad\,w\,y_{\xi}(\lambda)=y_{w\xi}(w\lambda)\,w\,.

(4.3)

Note that in the second relation the group action now changes both
\xi
and
\lambda
. As before, the assignment
\,\xi\mapsto y_{\xi}\,

extends to an algebra map

\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\to\mathcal{D}(V)*W\ ,\quad q\mapsto q(y)\,.

(4.4)

By taking suitable symmetric combinations of the elliptic Dunkl operators, one can construct commuting Hamiltonians of the elliptic Calogero–Moser system.
We first illustrate the procedure for the quadratic Hamiltonian. Similarly to Example
2.1
, we calculate
\langle y,y\rangle=y_{1}^{2}+\dots+y_{n}^{2}
to find

\langle y,y\rangle=\hbar^{2}\Delta-\hbar\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\sigma^{\prime}_{\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle}(\langle\alpha,x\rangle)s_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k^{2}_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\left(\wp(\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle)-\wp(\langle\alpha,x\rangle)\right)\,.

(4.5)

Here
\sigma^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\sigma_{\mu}(z)
, and
\wp(z)
is the Weierstrass
\wp
-function with periods
1,\tau
.

The resulting expression is singular at
\lambda=0
, but can be regularised by subtracting a
\lambda
-dependent term. Using that

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\sigma^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)=-\wp(z)-2\zeta(\frac{1}{2})
, one finds that

\langle y,y\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\frac{k^{2}_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle^{2}}\ \to\ \hbar^{2}\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}(k_{\alpha}-\hbar s_{\alpha})\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\wp(\langle\alpha,x\rangle)+C\quad\text{as $\lambda\to 0$}\,,

(4.6)

with
C=2\zeta(\frac{1}{2})\hbar\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle s_{\alpha}
. Applying the map (
2.5
), we define

L_{\langle\xi,\xi\rangle}:=\mathrm{Res}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\,\left(\langle y,y\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\frac{k^{2}_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle^{2}}\right)\,.

(4.7)

Up to a constant, this produces the Hamiltonian of the quantum elliptic Calogero–Moser system,

L=\hbar^{2}\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}k_{\alpha}(k_{\alpha}-\hbar)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\wp(\langle\alpha,x\rangle)\,.

(4.8)

4.4.
Elliptic difference case

The setting is the same as in

4.7.
Lax matrices

The Dunkl and Cherednik operators can be used to construct
Lax matrices
for the Calogero–Moser systems. We will first state the results in the case (
4.8
).

In the setting of
4.1
, we have
\mathcal{D}(V)
acting on
\mathbb{C}(V)
by differential operators. Consider the induced module

M=\mathrm{Ind}_{\mathcal{D}(V)}^{\mathcal{D}(V)*W}\,\mathbb{C}(V)\,.

We write elements of

as

with

, thus identifying

as vector spaces. The algebra
\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
is then identified with
\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}W)\otimes\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}(V))
, i.e. with

matrices of size

. As a result, the action of

on

gives a representation

\varrho\,:\ \mathcal{D}(V)*W\to\mathrm{Mat}(|W|,\mathcal{D}(V))\,.

(4.29)

It is compatible with taking the classical limit, so we also get

\varrho_{c}\,:\ A_{0}*W\to\mathrm{Mat}(|W|,A_{0})\,,\qquad A_{0}=\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]\,.

(4.30)

Note that if
W^{\prime}\subset W
is a subgroup and
e^{\prime}=\frac{1}{|W^{\prime}|}\sum_{w\in W^{\prime}}w

W^{\prime}
-invariant element acts on
M
, preserving the subspace

are
f=e^{\prime}\sum_{u\in W^{\prime}\backslash W}uf_{u}

4.29
), (

\varrho\,:\ (\mathcal{D}(V)*W)^{W^{\prime}}\to\mathrm{Mat}(r,\mathcal{D}(V))\,,\qquad\varrho_{c}\,:\ (A_{0}*W)^{W^{\prime}}\to\mathrm{Mat}(r,A_{0})\quad(r=|W|/|W^{\prime}|)\,.

Recall that

is the classical limit of
\mathcal{D}(V)
, and it comes with the canonical Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
.

Definition 4.7
.

(1)
Given

and

of

. That is,
[\mathcal{L}_{ij},\mathcal{H}]=[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A}]_{ij}
for
i,j=1,\dots,r
.

(2)
Given
H\in A_{0}
and
L\in\mathrm{Mat}(r,A_{0})
, a classical
Lax partner
of
L
relative to
H
is an element
A\in\mathrm{Mat}(r,A_{0})
such that
\{L_{ij},H\}=[L,A]_{ij}
for
i,j=1,\dots,r
. Equivalently,
\dot{L}=[L,A]
where
\dot{L}
denotes the time-derivative for the Hamiltonian flow defined by

.

.

The same definition applies with
\mathcal{D}(V)
and

replaced by the algebra of difference operators,

, and its classical limit,

Now pick a weight

Its classical limit

\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)
(associated with the lattice
\Lambda=P^{\vee}
) acting on the space of meromorphic functions,
\mathbb{C}(V)
. Replacing
\mathcal{D}(V)
with
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)
in the above constructions, we consider the module

M=\mathrm{Ind}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)}^{\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W}\,\mathbb{C}(V)\,.

The (left) action of
\mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W
on
M
gives a representation

\varrho\,:\ \mathcal{D}_{q}(V)*W\to\mathrm{Mat}(|W|,\mathcal{D}_{q}(V))\,.

It is compatible with taking the classical-limit map
\eta_{0}
, see
3.7
, so we also get

\varrho_{c}\,:\ A_{0}*W\to\mathrm{Mat}(|W|,A_{0})\,,\qquad A_{0}=\mathbb{C}(V)[P^{\vee}]\,.

Lemma 4.11
.

For a fundamental coweight
\lambda\in P^{\vee}_{+}
, write
W^{\prime}
for the stabiliser of
\lambda
in
W
and
e^{\prime}
for the corresponding symmetriser. Consider the elliptic Cherednik operator
Y:=Y^{\lambda}
, in which the spectral variable
\xi
is specialised to
\xi=-\rho_{k}+z\lambda
, where
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\rho_{k}
is given by (
3.34
). Then the action of the Cherednik operator
Y
on
M
preserves the subspace
M^{\prime}=e^{\prime}M
.

Using the lemma, we introduce the
quantum Lax matrix

\mathcal{L}
by restricting

Y=Y^{\lambda}
onto
M^{\prime}
and set the
classical Lax matrix

L
to be the classical limit of
\mathcal{L}
. The resulting matrices
\mathcal{L}\in\mathrm{Mat}(r,\mathcal{D}_{q}(V))
and
L\in\mathrm{Mat}(r,A_{0})
are of size
r=|W|/|W^{\prime}|
and depend on spectral parameter,
z
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
L_{b}
be one of the elliptic difference operators constructed in Theorem
4.5
, where
b\in P^{\vee}_{+}
is (quasi-)minuscule. Write
L_{b,c}
for the classical limit of
L_{b}
.
The Lax matrix
\mathcal{L}\in\mathrm{Mat}(r,\mathcal{D}_{q}(V))
(resp.
L\in\mathrm{Mat}(r,A_{0})
) has a quantum (resp. classical) Lax partner relative to
\mathcal{H}=L_{b}
(resp.
H=L_{b,c}
). Hence, the Hamiltonian flow associated with
H=L_{b,c}
induces an isospectral deformation of
L
.

This result is expected to remain valid for all commuting Hamiltonians
L_{b}
constructed in Theorem
4.4
. This would imply that the classical Lax matrix remains isospectral under
all
commuting Hamiltonian flows. This is known to be true for
R=A_{n-1}
and in the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-case (Ruijsenaars system), simply because all fundamental coweights are minuscule in that case. It has also been verified for the
C^{\vee}C_{n}
-case (van Diejen system).

4.8.
Historical comments

The elliptic Dunkl operators (
4.2
) were introduced, and their commutativity proved, by Buchstaber, Felder and Veselov in
[
BFV
]
. Etingof and Ma constructed Dunkl operators in a more general case, for any abelian variety
X
with an action of a finite complex reflection group
W

[
EM
]
. They also considered and studied Cherednik algebra for such pairs
X,W
. The corresponding integrable systems made recent appearance in quantum filed theory (see
[
ACL
]
for the detailed study of rank 1 case and further references). Theorem
4.1
is proved in
[
EFMV
]
for this more general case (including the Inozemtsev system
[
I
]
). For the real crystallographic case (
4.8
), another proof was given earlier by Cherednik in
[
C6
]
.

The constructions in Sections
4.4
–
4.6
are due to Komori and Hikami
[
KH2
]
, based on Cherednik’s idea of Yang–Baxter equations on affine root systems
[
C7
]
. Our account follows
[
Ch
]
closely. The functional
R
-matrices (
4.20
) go back to Shibukawa and Ueno
[
SU
]
. The Hamiltonians (
4.22
), (
4.23
) were found (and showed to commute) by Ruijsenaars in
[
R
]
, see also
[
RS
]
for the classical system and
[
Ha
]
for a link to lattice models. For the
C^{\vee}C_{n}
-case (Van Diejen’s system), van Diejen proposed in
[
vD1
]
the Hamiltonian (
4.27
)–(
4.28
)
under an additional constraint on the parameters. That constraint was removed and the integrability first proved in
[
KH1
]
by a method, different to
[
KH2
]
. Another, geometric proof was found recently by Rains within his geometric approach to elliptic DAHAs
[
Ra
]
.

Material in Section
4.7
is based on
[
Ch
]
. The subject of Lax pairs for classical Calogero–Moser systems has a long history, see
[
Ch
]
and references therein. The classical matrices (
4.33
) reproduce those from
[
DHP
]
, but their isospectrality under all commuting flows was not known prior to
[
Ch
]
, except in type
R=A_{n-1}
. For the elliptic difference case with

, but nothing was known beyond
A_{n-1}
-case before
[
Ch

PG

. Notably, in

Ch

the classical (as well as quantum) Lax matrix

was found for the

-case (van Diejen system), and its isospectrality under all commuting Hamiltonain flows was established.
