Sequential Cohort Selection
Hortence Phalonne Nana, Christos Dimitrakakis

Abstract
We study the problem of fair cohort selection from an unknown population, with a focus on university admissions. We start with the one-shot setting, where the admission policy must be fixed in advance and remain transparent, before observing the actual applicant pool. In contrast, the sequential setting allows the policy to be updated across stages as new applicant data becomes available. This is achieved by optimizing admission policies using a population model, trained on data from previous admission cycles. We also study the fairness properties of the resulting policies in the one-shot setting, including meritocracy and group parity.



1
Introduction

Decision-making in selection processes such as university admissions, hiring, or public resource allocation
(
Gibney and Shang 2007
;
Schmitt and Kim 2007
;
Yoshimura et al. 2006
)
requires balancing fairness considerations, such as meritocracy and equal opportunity, with selecting a successful student cohort. We focus on a version of this problem where the admission policy instead is fixed ahead of seeing the pool of candidates. This ensures that the admission criteria are transparent.

We address the problem of selecting a subset of individuals from an
unknown
population. For that reason, we optimise our admission policy by assuming the candidate pool as coming from some—perhaps unknown—probability distribution. This reflects real-world situations, where we must make choices with incomplete or uncertain information about the candidate pool. We formalise the selection problem as an expected utility maximization task, measuring fairness constraints such as equitable representation across demographic groups.

Contribution.

We propose a framework for fair decision-making under uncertainty that jointly learns and optimizes selection policies for evolving and unknown applicant populations. Our approach models the applicant distribution using a generative model and learns outcomes conditioned on features and decisions. It personalizes selection by assigning individual-specific probabilities under selection framework and also measure fairness constraints such as demographic parity and equality of opportunity. We evaluate the method in two settings: (i) a
one-shot
setting, where the policy is trained on known data and tested on new samples, and (ii) a
multi-stage
setting, where the population distribution is unknown and updated over time. Across both settings, our learned policies are adaptive, generalize well, and support equitable outcomes.

Setting and Notation

In our setting, we need to make a decision for a population of
N
individuals. Each individual
i
is described through features
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
. For the running example of college admissions, these can include socio-demographic attributes (e.g. age, gender, citizenship, country) as well as performance metrics (high school GPA, science points, language points).
We denote the complete population’s features as
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
. While the candidate population is unknown, we assume that we have access to a probability distribution
P
over possible populations. This can be achieved through probabilistic inference given past admission data.

We must define the acceptance policy
\pi
before we see the population. This reflects practical scenarios where policies must be deployed without knowledge of the population. The form of admission policy
\pi
that we will focus on generates a probability for accepting each individual independently, based on their features. That is, for every individual
i
, it calculates a conditional probability
\pi(a_{i}|x_{i})
, where
a_{i}=1
means that the individual
i
is accepted and
a_{i}=0
means that they are rejected. For a given population feature matrix
\mathbf{x}
, the policy generates an acceptance vector
\mathbf{a}
so that:

\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\pi(a_{i}\mid x_{i}).

This policy then defines how the selection will be made for
any
possible observed population
\mathbf{x}
.

Once a person
i
is accepted, we can observe the outcome
y_{i}
of our decision: in the university admissions case, this can be grades achieved in different subjects taken, with
y_{i,j}
being individual
i
’s outcome in the
j
-th course, with the course results of population
\mathbf{x}
denoted by
\mathbf{y}
.

Our policy optimisation target is to maximise a utility function in expectation, over a probability distribution over possible populations.

P(\mathbf{x})
: the probability of having a specific candidate population. Secondly
P(y_{i}\mid x_{i},a_{i}=1)

The outcomes for rejected individuals remain unobserved.

Our utility function
u(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})
is defined in terms of our selection
\mathbf{a}
and the outcomes
\mathbf{y}
obtained.
For concreteness, we define the following log-linear utility function:

u(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\left(\sum_{i\in N}a_{i}\cdot y_{i,k}\right)-c\cdot\|\mathbf{a}\|_{1},

(1)

where
y_{i,k}
is the outcome of candidate
i
in course
k
,
\|\mathbf{a}\|_{1}
is the total number of selected candidates and
c
is the cost of selecting a candidate. The first term
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\left(\sum_{i\in N}a_{i}\cdot y_{i,k}\right)
rewards having students who perform well across multiple courses, and the second term
-c\cdot\|\mathbf{a}\|_{1}

penalizes selecting too many candidates, helping us to effectively manage resources.

While we want our selection to be optimal in expectation, we have to consider the uncertainty in the features and outcomes of the population.
When outcomes
\mathbf{y}
are unknown, but the decision maker observes features
\mathbf{x}
, the utility of taking an action
\mathbf{a}
is the expected utility (reflecting the uncertainty about
\mathbf{y}
) over possible outcomes:

U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[u(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right].

(2)

As we do not know the population
\mathbf{x}
, we have to optimise the policy in terms of the expected utility under a distribution
P
of possible populations; the expected utility of policy
\pi
is then given by:

U(\pi,P)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\right].

(3)

The decision maker’s primary goal is to maximize the expected utility
\max_{\pi}U(\pi,P)
.
While utility maximisation is not necessarily aligned with fairness considerations,
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
showed that it can satisfy a type of meritocratic fairness related to stability.

3
Related Work

The problem of subset selection under fairness constraints has been extensively studied in decision-making and machine learning literature
(
Stoyanovich, Yang, and Jagadish 2018
)
.
In this paper, we are mainly focusing on the
sequential
setting, where candidate populations appear at different time periods and we must decide who to admit.
Usually, the decision maker can observe the candidates, allowing them to make selections based on their characteristics
(
Gupta and Sobel 1958
)
. However, in some scenarios, the admission policy has to be defined before seeing the candidate population. This is the focus of this paper.

Meritocratic selection has been widely explored in decision theory and economics. Classical models often assume that productivity or individual merit is well-defined and observable
(
Mas-Colell et al. 1995
;
Rawls 1971
)
. The
expected marginal contribution
(EMC) framework has been proposed as a means of quantifying merit by assessing the contribution of each individual to the overall utility of a selected subset
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
. Our work builds on this by integrating EMC within a fixed policy selection framework, ensuring that selection decisions remain consistent with meritocratic principles.

Fairness in automated decision-making has been a growing area of interest, with multiple formalizations proposed to ensure equitable outcomes across different demographic groups. For instance, demographic parity mentioned by
(
Caton and Haas 2024
;
Chouldechova 2017
;
Corbett-Davies et al. 2017
;
Dwork et al. 2012
;
Dwork and Ilvento 2018a
;
Dwork and Ilvento 2018b
;
Kearns et al. 2019
;
Kusner et al. 2018
)
ensures that selection probabilities are proportional across groups, regardless of underlying performance differences. However, demographic parity has been criticized for potentially disregarding individual qualifications. To address this,
equality of opportunity
introduced by
(
Hardt, Price, and Srebro 2016
)
and also used by
(
Caton and Haas 2024
;
Corbett-Davies et al. 2017
)
ensure that individuals with similar qualifications receive comparable treatment. Our work incorporates both fairness notions, balancing trade-offs between utility and equitable representation, as
(
Bagriacik and Otero 2024
)
did in their paper, but with a classification fairness metric. We aim to find an optimal balance that respects both fairness and efficiency.

Optimizing decision policies in uncertain environments has been studied in reinforcement learning and robust optimization. The use of fixed policies, where parameters are optimized prior to evaluation, is common in offline reinforcement learning
(
Levine et al. 2020
;
Sutton 2018
)
. Fairness in decision-making under uncertainty is addressed by
(
Zhang and Weiss 2021
;
Zhang and Shah 2015
)
, particularly in settings where class labels are censored. The authors propose a new fairness framework that explicitly accounts for censored data, ensuring decisions do not disproportionately disadvantage certain groups. Our approach aligns with these methods by treating the selection process as an expected utility maximization problem under unknown population characteristics. The log-linear utility function we employ follows prior work in set selection for university admission, where selection is modeled as an optimization problem
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
.

The tension between fairness and utility has been a major concern in algorithmic decision making
(
Corbett-Davies et al. 2017
;
Berk et al. 2021
)
. Their studies have shown that enforcing strict fairness constraints can lead to reduced overall utility, raising questions about the optimal balance between fairness and efficiency. Our study explores a weighted combination of fairness penalties, allowing decision makers to well balance fairness and utility according to policy objectives. Additionally, we investigate the use of a max-operator formulation to ensure that the most severe fairness violations are mitigated.

4
Policy Optimisation

Given a population distribution
P(\mathbf{x})
and an outcome distribution
P(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})
, we have to find an optimal policy. We do this by using policies with some parameters
\theta
through gradient ascent, for any arbitrary utility function
u(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})
.

Policy Structures and Parametrs.

The simplest policy structure we have, denoted by
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})
, uses a linear-sigmoid function of the features:

\pi_{1}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{1}(a_{i}\mid x_{i}),\pi_{1}(a_{i}=1\mid x_{i})=\frac{e^{(\theta_{1}^{\top}x_{i})}}{1+e^{(\theta_{1}^{\top}x_{i})}},

(4)

where
x_{i}
is the feature vector for candidate
i
, and
\theta_{1}
is the parameter vector that defines the policy.

The second policy structure,
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})
, uses a neural network that outputs admission probabilities for each candidate conditioned on the features:

\pi_{2}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{2}(a_{i}\mid x_{i}),\qquad\pi_{2}(a_{i}=1\mid x_{i})=f_{\theta_{2}}(x_{i}),

(5)

where
f_{\theta_{2}}(\cdot)
is a neural network parametrized by
\theta_{2}
with a sigmoid output activation to model admission probabilities. Unlike the first policy, this architecture can allow us for modeling complex nonlinear relationships between features and admission decisions.

In the remainder, we will use
\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})
and
\theta
when there is no need to distinguish between the two types.

Gradient Calculation.

The policy optimisation problem is not a typical supervised learning problem. Consequently, we must explicitly approximate the expected utility gradient. We start with the expected utility of the policy:

U(\pi,P)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}}P(\mathbf{x})\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}),

(6)

where
U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})
, given by equation
2
, is approximated by sampling from the outcome prediction model
P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})
.

To optimize the policy using gradient ascent, we need to compute the gradient of
U(\pi,P)
with respect to the parameters
\theta
of the policy. This gradient provides a direction for adjusting
\theta
to maximize
U(\pi_{\theta},P)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U(\pi_{\theta},P)}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}P(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}P(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\log(\pi_{\theta}(a_{j}\mid x_{j}))}{\partial\theta_{i}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}P(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi_{\theta_{1}}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{i,j}

\displaystyle\quad[\mathbf{1}_{a_{j}=1}\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{j}=0\mid x_{j})-\mathbf{1}_{a_{j}=0}\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{j}=1\mid x_{j})]\bigg].

In practice, summing over all possible
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{a}
is intractable, we approximate these expectations using Monte Carlo sampling. We first sample
n_{\mathbf{x}}
instances
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\sim P(\mathbf{x})
from the population distribution. For each sampled
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
, we then sample
n_{\mathbf{a}}
actions
\mathbf{a}^{(k,m)}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{x}^{(k)})
from the policy. Using these samples, the gradient is approximated as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U(\pi_{\theta},P)}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{n_{\mathbf{x}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{1}{n_{\mathbf{a}}}\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{n_{\mathbf{a}}}U(\mathbf{a}^{(k,m)},\mathbf{x}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{j}^{(k,m)}\mid x_{j}^{(k)})\bigg],

(8)

or more specifically for the first policy,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U(\pi_{\theta_{1}},P)}{\partial\theta_{i_{1}}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{n_{\mathbf{x}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{1}{n_{\mathbf{a}}}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{\mathbf{a}}}U(\mathbf{a}^{(k,m)},\mathbf{x}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{i,j}^{(k)}\bigg[\mathbf{1}_{a_{j}^{(k,m)}=1}\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{j}^{(k,m)}=0|x_{j}^{(k)})

\displaystyle-\mathbf{1}_{a_{j}^{(k,m)}=0}\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{j}^{(k,m)}=1|x_{j}^{(k)})\bigg].

(9)

n_{\mathbf{x}}
is the number of population samples,
n_{\mathbf{a}}
is the number of action samples per
\mathbf{x}
, and

\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{j}^{(k,m)}=1|x_{j}^{(k)})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\theta_{1}^{T}\cdot x_{j}^{(k)})}.

As mentioned previously, the objective is to maximize the expected utility and this is done by iteratively updating the parameter
\theta
using gradient ascent:

\theta^{t+1}\leftarrow\theta^{t}+\eta\cdot\nabla_{\theta}U(\pi_{\theta},\mathbf{x}).

(10)

where
\eta
is the learning rate

When using neural networks, this optimization is performed via stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or one of its variants (e.g., Adam), using automatic differentiation frameworks such as PyTorch or TensorFlow. To match the framework’s default of minimizing losses, we define the loss function as the negative expected utility:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})}[U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})],

and minimize it using backpropagation:

\theta^{t+1}\leftarrow\theta^{t}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta),

which is mathematically equivalent to gradient ascent on the utility objective.

Batch and Sample Sizes.

In our setting, the utility
cannot
be broken down into a sum over individuals, as it is a utility over sets. This means that the
batch size
, is equivalent to the size of the applicant pool, i.e. the number of individuals in each sampled
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
. In addition, the number of samples for the population (
n_{\mathbf{x}}
) and actions (
n_{\mathbf{a}}
) may not be the same. Their choice influences the variance of the estimate.

5
Fairness Metrics.

Here we introduce the fairness metrics to quantify and address trade-offs between utility maximization and fairness objectives. Specifically, we focus on demographic parity and equality of opportunity. To define the latter, we make use of the concept of expected marginal contribution.

5.1
Demographic Parity

Demographic parity
(
Caton and Haas 2024
)
ensures that subpopulations (based on gender) are equitably represented in the selected set, even if the groups differ in performance. So, selection probabilities for different groups will be proportional to their representation in the population. For a policy
\pi
, it’s defined as:

|\pi(a_{i}=1|G=g_{1})-\pi(a_{j}=1|G=g_{2})|\leq\epsilon,\,\forall g_{1},g_{2}

(11)

G
is the group membership ( male or female), and
\epsilon
is a tolerance parameter. This metric prevents one group from being disproportionately favored.

5.2
Expected Marginal Contribution (EMC).

The EMC is a metric for assessing each candidate’s impact on the selection set. It quantifies how much a candidate improves the overall utility when they are selected under a specific policy. This measure is particularly useful in where decision makers must evaluate candidates based on uncertain or probabilistic outcomes. Under a fixed policy
\pi
, the EMC of candidate
i
is defined as:

\displaystyle\text{EMC}_{i}(U,\pi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})}[\left(U(\mathbf{a}+i,\mathbf{x})-U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\right)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}P(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\left(U(\mathbf{a}+i,\mathbf{x})-U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})\right)

(12)

in this expression,
\mathbf{a}+i
represents the selection set
\mathbf{a}
after adding candidate
i
. The expectation is taken over both the distribution of feature
P(\mathbf{x})
and the policy selection probabilities
\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{x})
, to capture the uncertainties in both the candidate pool and the selection process.

This formulation is closely inspired by the EMC definition introduced by
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
. However, an important distinction in our approach is that the EMC is now evaluated under a fixed selection policy while accounting for population uncertainty, and could capture the probabilistic nature of decision-making. Then, the formulation acknowledges that candidate evaluations may vary due to randomness, selection strategy or external influences.

5.3
Equality of Opportunity

Also known as equal acceptance rate defined in
(
Verma and Rubin 2018
)
, equality of opportunity ensures that individuals with similar qualifications (EMC) have equal probabilities of selection across groups. Unlike demographic parity (DP) that enforces equal selection probabilities regardless of differences in qualifications or utility contributions( this can result in individuals being selected based on group membership rather than their actual qualifications), equality of opportunity, however, ensures that individuals with similar qualifications (EMC above a threshold
\tau
) have equal selection probabilities across groups. This avoids penalizing high-performing individuals while still addressing fairness concerns.

\displaystyle|\pi(a_{i}=1\mid EMC_{i}\geq\tau,G=g_{1})

\displaystyle-\pi(a_{j}=1\mid EMC_{j}\geq\tau,G=g_{2})|\leq\epsilon,\forall g_{1},g_{2}

(13)

In our utility function,
c
represents the cost of selecting an individual. Larger
c
discourages selecting many candidates. By setting
\tau=c
, the cost of admitting each individual, we align selection costs with fairness considerations, meaning we distinguish between those worthy and unworthy of selection.

To incorporate multi-dimensional fairness
(
Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan 2016
)
penalties into our decision-making framework, we merge both fairness metrics into a single overall fairness penalty. One approach is to use a weighted sum, allowing us to balance the relative importance of each fairness criterion. In this case, the overall fairness penalty
P_{\text{overall}}
is defined as

P_{\text{overall}}=\lambda_{\text{dem}}\,P_{\text{dem}}+\lambda_{\text{eq}}\,P_{\text{eq}},

(14)

P_{\text{dem}}
is the demographic parity penalty,
P_{\text{eq}}
is the equality of opportunity penalty, and
\lambda_{\text{dem}}
and
\lambda_{\text{eq}}
are non-negative weights that reflect the importance of each fairness measure.

An alternative approach is to adopt the maximum operator, which ensures that the most significant fairness violation dominates the overall penalty. In this method, the overall fairness penalty is given by

P_{\text{overall}}=\max\left(P_{\text{dem}},\,P_{\text{eq}}\right).

(15)

This approach is particularly useful when it is critical to guard against any severe deviation from fairness, as it directly penalizes the worst-case scenario among the fairness metrics.

6
Experiments in the One-shot Setting.

Experiments are performed
in simulation
, as the interactive nature of the task means that evaluations on static datasets are impossible. We base the simulator
P^{*}
on a university admission data
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
. This allows us to study realistic admission dynamics while maintaining control over the environment. Using a simulator based on actual data, we can test different policies in a consistent and repeatable way, without needing access to real-time decision-making or sensitive student records.

We test both policies ( logistic and neural network) defined in
section
4
in our experiments. For the
neural
network
policy, we use a feedforward network that maps features to
admission probabilities. The architecture consists of two hidden
layers with ReLU activations, each followed by dropout to prevent
overfitting. The final layer outputs a probability via a sigmoid
activation, representing the likelihood of admitting a given
applicant. This structure offers greater flexibility than a simple
linear model, to capture more complex and nonlinear patterns in the
feature space.

The
outcome prediction model
(
P(y\mid x,a)
) is defined here as a Bayesian multivariate linear regression model
(
Minka 2000
)
trained on historical data from previously admitted students and their academic outcomes. To account for uncertainty in future outcomes, we use
posterior predictive sampling
during policy evaluation to sample possible outcomes from the posterior predictive distribution of the model. It lets us evaluate the admission policy using different possible student outcomes, without needing to retrain or change the model.

6.1
Utility of
Logistic vs Neural Policies

To compare the performance of both policies after optimization, We examine how the average utility evolves over time for each policy type under two different values of the penalty parameter
c\in\{0.001,0.1\}
, which controls the trade-off between admitting more applicants and maximizing expected utility
The outcomes are shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Comparison of logistic and neural network admission policies over 1000 Iterations under varying admission costs (
c
). To ensure robustness, each configuration is repeated across 10 independent trials.

The left plot (with
c=0.001
) reflects the setting where the cost of admitting students is minimal, allowing the policy to focus on maximizing utility. Here, the neural policy clearly outperforms the logistic one, converging faster and achieving a higher utility. In contrast, the right plot (with
c=0.1
) represents a more conservative scenario where admitting students is more costly. Even under this constraint, the neural network policy demonstrates superior performance, indicating its greater flexibility in capturing complex admission strategies. These results suggest that when decision-makers face uncertain outcomes and variable admission costs, more expressive policy classes (like neural networks) may yield better utility outcomes than simpler models like logistic regression.

6.2
Effect of Batch Size and Selection Cost
c

To evaluate the trade-offs between utility and fairness, we systematically vary the batch size and selection cost (
c
), and compute utility and fairness penalties for both neural network and logistic policies for test set. The batch size controls how many applicants are evaluated per iteration, while the selection cost (
c
) penalizes large admission volumes. The plots in figure
2
show how both utility and fairness metrics evolve with these parameters.

Figure 2:
Expected utility (blue and red curves) and fairness penalties (green and purples curves) with respect to batch size for different values of cost
c
. The plots illustrate how
c
controls the selection process in this admissions in function of batch size for both policies.

Impact of
c
values on utility.
When
c=0.001
, selection is cheap, so both policies are encouraged to admit more candidates. As the batch size increases, we see a steady improvement in utility for both models, especially for the neural policy which eventually achieves a utility close to 10. For the logistic policy, utility also increases with batch size but at a slightly slower rate. When
c=0.1
, selection becomes costly, which limits the number of admitted candidates. Here, the neural network still maintains a strong upward trend, showing its adaptability under stricter constraints, while the logistic policy starts with negative utility and only slowly improves, plateauing around 6. This confirms that higher selection costs discourage large admissions and make it harder for simpler models to perform well.

Impact of batch size on fairness penalties.
Fairness penalties drop significantly as batch size increases. At small batch sizes, both policies suffer from noticeable fairness violations, especially the logistic model. The neural network policy shows a sharp improvement: its fairness penalty drops rapidly and reaches zero after only 100 student evaluations. In contrast, the logistic policy reduces its fairness penalty more gradually, only approaching zero when the batch size exceeds 200. This suggests that expressive models like neural networks can more efficiently adapt to fairness constraints when provided with sufficient batch-level information, whereas simpler models like logistic regression require larger batches to achieve similar levels of fairness.

7
Experiments in the Sequential Setting

We consider a
multi-stage
policy learning setting inspired by a real-world scenario, such as a college admissions office that revises its admission strategy yearly. The objective is to improve decision-making over time by learning from past outcomes. Each stage corresponds to an admissions cycle (year), and the admissions policy is updated based on observed performance of previously admitted students. All our experiments are performed on simulations, because of the interactive nature of the problem: a fixed dataset only includes data for students that have been admitted.

The process works as follows: At each stage
t
, a
population
x_{t}=(x_{t}^{1},\ldots,x^{n}_{t})
of size
n
is drawn from

P^{*}(x)
. Then, we apply our admission policy
\pi_{t-1}
, where

\pi_{0}
is an arbitrary policy, to select a subset of the population

a_{t}
. Afterwards, for each individual
i\in a_{t}
we observe

y^{i}_{t}\sim P^{*}(y^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i})
and obtain utility

U(a_{t},y_{t})
At the end of each stage, we estimate a new applicant
model
P_{t}(x)
from
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
and a new prediction model

P_{t}(y^{i}|x^{i})
from
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}
for
all previously accepted students. We then use
P_{t}
to find a new
policy
\pi_{t}
maximising expected utility, i.e.
\pi_{t}=\arg\max_{\pi}U(\pi,P_{t})
.

The following sections describe our choices for the simulator
P^{*}
, models
P_{t}
and policies
\pi_{t}
.

Data Generation for each Stage.

At the beginning of each stage, a synthetic applicant pool is generated from a fixed simulator
P^{*}(x)
, which is a Conditional Tabular GAN (CTGAN) trained on a university admission dataset. For each applicant, academic outcomes ( GPA in 3 courses) are predicted using a multi-output Bayesian ridge model
P^{*}(y^{i}|x^{i})
, trained on the initial dataset. This is not updated in subsequent stages and serves only to simulate academic outcomes for newly generated applicant pools. Admission decisions are made based on a learned policy, and only admitted students’ outcomes
y^{i}
are revealed to the learner.

7.1
Model Updates

Our framework employs two models that are updated after each stage:

•

Applicant Distribution Model (
P_{t}(x)
):
A CTGAN trained on all students ever considered (both admitted and rejected), updated at each stage. It refines the feature distribution for future policy updates to reflect trends in who is being selected over time.

•

Outcome Prediction Model (
P_{t}(y\mid x,a)
):
We employ a Bayesian multivariate linear regression model that is continuously updated across stages using the outcomes of admitted students. Unlike simpler model like basic linear regression
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
, it maintains a full posterior distribution over regression weights and outcome noise, thereby capturing both parameter uncertainty and outcome variability. In each stage, we use
Thompson Sampling
by drawing a single model from this posterior to guide decision-making. This approach naturally balances exploration and exploitation: early stages explore different admission policies via model uncertainty, while later stages exploit accumulated knowledge. The model is retrained after every stage with newly observed data, continuously refining its estimates as more outcomes become available.

7.2
Policy Learning over Admission Stages

We are using both policy architectures defined in section
4
and describe in section
6
.

In the first stage, no historical admissions data is available to inform decision-making, as this is the initial year of the admissions process. Therefore, we use an heuristic initial policy
\pi_{0}
: students are admitted if their predicted average GPA across the three courses exceeds a fixed threshold. It then serves as a reasonable starting point, providing a basic selection criterion before more sophisticated decision-making frameworks can be developed.

Once the first cohort of students is admitted and their academic outcomes are observed, the applicant distribution model and the outcome prediction model are trained for the first time. From the second stage onward, the following iterative process proceeds: the decision-maker updates the policy
\pi_{t}
parameters using policy gradient methods, aiming to maximize expected utility, which includes a penalty for too many admissions, using the cost parameter
c
. It applies this updated policy to admit students, and their actual academic outcomes are observed. These new data points are then incorporated into both the applicant distribution model and the outcome prediction model, allowing them to adjust their estimations. Over time, this smarter and self-improving framework allows the university to make progressively well-informed decisions, ensuring that admissions strategies remain aligned with university goals while adapting to real-world changes in student quality and reputation.

Delay policy updated.

To better understand how the frequency of policy updates affects performance, we run an experiment on delayed policy updates. Instead of adjusting the admission strategy after every stage, we compare outcomes when updates happen every 1, 2, or 3 stages. In practice, universities may not revise their policies each year due to resource or administrative constraints. Testing how often the policy needs to change could show whether updating less frequently still gives good results and help universities to balance between adaptability and practicality.

Compare against static baselines.

To better understand the benefits of adaptive admission policies, we compare them against static baselines that do not change over time. In real-world university settings, some institutions might prefer fixed policies because they are easier to implement and maintain. However, such static rules might fail to adapt to changing applicant pools or new data. In this experiment, we include three static baselines: two based on logistic and neural network models trained once and used throughout, and another based on a simple GPA threshold. We can clearly see the advantage of allowing the system to learn and adjust over time. A commonly used baseline like top-k admission, which selects the best candidates based on their outcomes, is not suitable here because it assumes full knowledge of applicant success in advance, something universities never have in practice. By contrast, our setup reflects realistic uncertainty, where outcomes must be estimated from observed data.

8
Discussion and Future Works

In this paper, we tackled the challenge of designing fair and effective university admission policies under uncertainty. Using a policy gradient framework, we explored how decisions can adapt over time to unknown applicant populations, balancing utility with fairness goals like demographic parity and equality of opportunity through EMC. We showed that the cost parameter
c
(penalization of admitting too much students while having small resources) plays a key role: low values give high utility but may hurt fairness, while higher values improve fairness at the expense of utility. This trade-off highlights the importance of choosing the right balance.

Through multi-stage experiments, we looked at cases where policy updates are delayed, and found that even with less frequent updates, neural network still well performs but not logistic policy. Finally, comparing against simple baselines like GPA or fixed models confirmed the value of letting the system learn and adjust. Among all tested approaches, the neural network-based policy consistently outperformed others, including the logistic one, showing stronger ability to adapt and improve over time. Overall, our work shows how universities can design more fair and responsive admission strategies that adapt to real-world changes while still focusing on student merit.

Future research could expand this work to online learning as sequential decision-making where we update the policy incrementally as each applicant is evaluated, rather than waiting for a full annual cycle. Another future work could be the matching problem.

Dataset description

All experiments are conducted on data generated from a simulator
P^{*}
trained on Norwegian university admission data
(
Kleine Buening et al. 2022
)
. A simulator is
necessary
, as an evaluation cannot be performed on static datasets. This allows us to study realistic admission dynamics while maintaining control over the environment, and using a simulator based on actual data, we can test different policies in a consistent and repeatable way, without needing access to real-time decision-making or sensitive student records. The original dataset is not publicly available. It was obtained under a data-sharing agreement for research purposes and cannot be redistributed.. Academic features include high school GPA, science, language, and other points. Admission decisions are binary (0 = rejected, 1 = admitted), with a 70% acceptance probability.
To enhance realism for this set of experiments, we train a Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial Network (CTGAN)
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
over 100 epochs and generate 1,000 synthetic candidates, ensuring statistical consistency. We then filter for admitted students and generate three course-specific GPA scores based on their academic background clipped between 0 and 4. We then preprocess the data by applying one-hot encoding to categorical variables and normalizing numerical attributes using Min-Max Scaling. Course GPAs are also normalized for consistency. Finally, we split the dataset into 70% training and 30% testing for robust model evaluation.
The experiments were conducted on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M2 chip, 8 GB of RAM, and macOS Ventura. All models were implemented in Python using PyTorch (v2.0), NumPy (v1.24), scikit-learn (v1.2), and CTGAN from the SDV library (v0.7.0). No GPU acceleration beyond the M2’s integrated hardware was used. The experiments were lightweight and reproducible on standard personal machines.

Appendix B
Baseline comparison for the one-shot setting

To evaluate the effectiveness of our policy utility, we compare it against two baseline selection methods: Threshold-based Selection and Greedy Selection. These baselines provide useful benchmarks for assessing how well our policy approaches optimize admissions decisions in figure
3
.

The Threshold-based Selection

approach admits candidates whose predicted GPA or performance in three key courses exceeds a predefined threshold. It ensures that only applicants meeting a minimum academic standard are selected, offering a straightforward but rigid method that does not consider the overall distribution of applicants’ abilities. While it maintains academic quality, it may exclude candidates who could perform well despite falling just below the threshold.

The Greedy Approach (Based on Total Outcome)

prioritizes applicants with the highest predicted academic performance by selecting those with the highest sum of grades across all courses. Unlike the threshold method, which applies a fixed cutoff, the greedy approach ranks candidates and selects the top performers, assuming that maximizing total academic achievement will yield the best outcomes. However, this method may favor candidates with strong overall scores while neglecting other factors that contribute to long-term success, such as diversity in skills or adaptability to different learning environments. In both methods, the number of selected applicants remains the same.
