Development and preliminary evaluation of a measure ofself-efficacy of Self-Management in ARThritis: SMART
Authors: Ramali Mendis, Tom Lynch, Asaduzzaman Khan, Ranjeny Thomas, Nicola W. Burton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-management is a critical component of care of autoimmune inflammatory arthritis, but current measures do not address all five core self-management domains. We aimed to develop and evaluate a novel questionnaire of self-management efficacy in arthritis.MethodsA rapid review was conducted to identify existing self-management questionnaires for chronic disease and arthritis. Information on item focus, format, scoring and psychometric attributes was extracted. Results were used to inform development of a draft questionnaire with 20 items across five domains: medication, exercise, diet, smoking cessation and wellbeing. Feedback on comprehensibility, perceived usefulness and comprehensiveness from a convenience sample of people with inflammatory arthritis (n = 12), and a sample of medical clinicians (n = 8), was used to refine questionnaire instructions and items. The refined measure was completed online by 135 people with inflammatory arthritis enrolled in the A3BC biobank (n = 392) and data were used to assess structural validity (exploratory factor analysis), internal consistency (Cronbach alpha), and 2-week test-retest reliability (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC; n = 112).ResultsExploratory factor analysis supported a multidimensional structure. Each subscale was strongly unidimensional when examined independently. The items demonstrated strong internal consistency in all five subscales of self-management (Cronbach alpha 0.92–0.98). The test-retest reliability was acceptable with subscale ICCs 0.61–0.89.ConclusionThe novel Self-Management in Arthritis (SMART) questionnaire incorporates 20 items to assess efficacy for five domains of self-management of arthritis. Preliminary psychometrics are promising with unidimensional subscales, strong internal consistency, and acceptable test-retest reliability. Further evaluation can contribute to the use of SMART in research or clinical practice settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03812-y.

Background

Autoimmune inflammatory arthritis, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), is a chronic condition of the immune system potentially resulting in irreversible joint damage if untreated. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects an estimated 17.6 million people worldwide with a female predominance [1]. It leads to significant disability and healthcare expenditure. The annual health care expenditure for people living with RA is higher, and the quality of life lower, than for people without RA [2].

Self-management involves taking an active role in managing one’s condition by managing treatments, lifestyle changes and symptoms. The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) have published recommendations on the importance of self-management and have recognised that it is insufficiently addressed in the care of people with arthritis [3]. EULAR guidelines highlight the lifestyle behaviours of a healthy diet, physical activity and smoking cessation complementing medical management as well as the importance of managing emotional wellbeing [3, 4]. These domains have been targets in self-management and educational interventions in arthritis [5–7]. Self-management interventions have been shown to improve quality of life and depressive symptoms, and to be more effective than routine care in patients with chronic diseases [8], and beneficial for inflammatory arthritis related outcomes [9].

Self-efficacy pertains to confidence and capability to do specific tasks, including in challenging situations [10]. Self-efficacy of arthritis self-management is one’s confidence to carry out tasks and behaviours important in managing one’s autoimmune inflammatory arthritis. Self-efficacy has an important role in determining whether self-management actions are initiated, how much effort is exerted, and how long the effort is sustained in the face of obstacles and failures [10]. Patients with high self-efficacy in coping with their chronic disease have a self-perceived ability to manage challenges related to their diseases and a sense of control over their lives. Self-efficacy has been recognised to have a positive benefit on aspects of living with inflammatory arthritis and reduced health burden to the individual [3]. Given the importance of self-management of RA, an understanding of self-efficacy for self-management can be used to identify people who may benefit from targeted support to enable self-management, which in turn can prevent escalation of symptoms and related complications, and ultimately improve health outcomes and quality of life.

Measures assessing self-management and efficacy have been developed for chronic disease as well as specific to inflammatory arthritis. However, existing measures are limited as they do not comprehensively cover the core EULAR specified self-management domains of exercise, diet, medication management and smoking cessation, as well as psychosocial wellbeing (see Table 1). This research therefore aimed to develop a measure of self-efficacy of self-management in patients with rheumatoid and other types of inflammatory arthritis, with a focus on exercise, diet, medication, smoking cessation, and psychosocial wellbeing and as the key domains.

Methods

Approvals

Ethics approval was granted through the Northern Sydney Local Health District HREC as a sub-study (2024/ETH02589) of the A3BC Registry & Biobank (2019/ETH10386; ResID21-PID1).

Study design

This project was conducted in five phases– 1) a rapid review of available self-management of chronic disease measures 2) development of draft items; 3) feedback on perceived comprehensibility, usefulness and comprehensiveness from a sample of patient participants with inflammatory arthritis as well as medical clinician feedback on utility and coverage; 4) refinement of items based on patient and clinician feedback; and 5) assessment of factor structure, internal consistency and 2 week test-retest reliability.

Rapid review of other questionnaire measures (Phase I)

A literature review was performed to identify English language questionnaire measures described as evaluating self-management with respect to arthritis or chronic diseases. The search was conducted in Clinical Knowledge Network, PubMed and Embase databases for publications from 2000 to April 2024. Multiple sets of search terms were applied to capture both the population of interest and outcomes related to self-management. The first set of terms related to the disease area including: rheumatoid arthritis, seropositive RA, seronegative RA. Broader arthritis terms were also searched such as psoriatic arthritis, osteoarthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, axial spondyloarthritis. Articles focusing primarily on fibromyalgia, chronic pain syndromes, or somatisation were excluded.

The second set of terms captured concepts of self-management and related constructs including: self-management, self-care, self-regulation, self-monitoring, self-efficacy, lifestyle, nutrition, diet, exercise, falls prevention, physical activity, physio, peer support, educational program, joint protection. The phrase “disease-specific self-management tasks” was also included.

A third group of terms focused on measurement and evaluation including: measure, scale, metric, score, assessment (tool), efficacy, effectiveness, evaluation, criteria, self-reported. To avoid capturing studies outside the scope of the review, quality of life measures were excluded in some searches.

Publications of relevant self-management measures cited in the included studies were manually searched to identify additional publications. Publications detailing the development of the available measures, or if not available, subsequent texts detailing psychometrics, were reviewed for data extraction. Data were extracted on the measure focus, format, number of items, subscales and psychometrics and are presented in Table 1. The self-management domains addressed were identified to determine if exercise, diet, medications, smoking cessation or wellbeing were included (Table 2).

Measures evaluating only one self-management domain (e.g. only exercise), not specific to chronic disease (e.g. generalised self-efficacy) or those that did not have psychometric data available for an English questionnaire version were excluded from the data extraction process.

Development of draft questionnaire measure (Phase II)

Existing questionnaire measures relevant to self-management of chronic disease and inflammatory arthritis were reviewed to inform content validity of the new measure. Building on this foundation, draft items were generated to provide balanced coverage across the five key domains of self-management in inflammatory arthritis: diet, exercise, medication use, smoking cessation, and general wellbeing. Equal representation of the number of items across these domains was prioritised to ensure balanced assessment of self-management behaviours and to avoid overemphasis on any single area. Items were worded consistent with the Self-Determination Theory Perceived Competence Scale which has been previously adapted for use in diabetes and smoking [18–20], and addresses knowledge, confidence, perceived ability, and capacity to manage challenges. The draft measure was developed to assess the construct of self-management self-efficacy of participants. It does not assess actual self-management in terms of frequency, quality or consistency of behaviours. A final question with a free text response asks respondents to indicate what specific assistance with self-management they want.

Patient participant and clinician feedback on draft questionnaire (Phase III)

Patient participant feedback – feasibility and variability

The Australian Arthritis & Autoimmune Biobank Collaborative (A3BC) is a national registry and biobank of participants with arthritis and autoimmune diseases. Sixty participants from the A3BC with inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis and spondyloarthropathy) who previously indicated a willingness to be contacted about research opportunities were invited by email to complete the draft questionnaire online and provide feedback through an online survey. Participant consent was implied as participants were advised that submission of the online survey would be taken as informed consent. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap hosted at The University of Sydney [21, 22].

Exact time taken to complete the draft questionnaire was captured by the online platform. Following completion, participants were asked to complete a feedback questionnaire with six items about usefulness, comprehensibility, completion time and relevance. Responses were recorded using a 5-point Likert-scale level indicating strength of agreement/disagreement. Participants were then asked to rate the usefulness of the measure using a 4-point Likert Scale (poor, fair, good, very good). There was also the opportunity to provide optional free text feedback regarding the positive aspects, problems and missing areas of the measure.

Clinician feedback – face validity

Clinician feedback was sought to assess face validity. The questionnaire was reviewed at an in-person Rheumatology Departmental Meeting at the Princess Alexandra Hospital (Brisbane, Australia) attended by consultant rheumatologists, rheumatology advanced trainees and resident medical officers. Led by author RM, this involved providing the questionnaire to clinicians, in-person discussion and hard copy written feedback at the conclusion of the session about the perceived usefulness and breadth of content. The clinicians completed a feedback form with 6 questions with responses recorded using a 5-point Likert-scale level indicating strength of agreement/disagreement. They were then asked to rate the usefulness of the questionnaire on a 4-point Likert scale (poor, fair, good, very good). There were 4 optional free text questions pertaining to the length of the measure, usefulness, good aspects and problems with the measure.

Refinement of questionnaire post patient and clinician feedback (Phase IV)

Patient feedback and clinician feedback was considered and the draft questionnaire was revised accordingly.

Psychometric testing: validity, internal consistency, and test-retest reliability (Phase V)

For psychometric testing, we recruited additional participants from the A3BC registry and biobank. We included participants over the age of 18 with diagnosed inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, axial spondyloarthropathy, undifferentiated inflammatory arthritis), and excluded those who had previously participated in the feasibility feedback (Phase II) and were not diagnosed with inflammatory arthritis. The study invitation was distributed by email. The questionnaires were administered online via a REDCap survey. Participant consent was implied as participants were advised that submission of the online survey would be taken as informed consent.

A demographic questionnaire assessing age, country of birth, diagnosis and disease duration was completed after the first administration. The online questionnaire captured time taken to do the measure at initial administration. The test was open for 4 weeks with a reminder was sent 2 weeks after the initial invitation for non-responders. These data were used to examine validity and internal consistency.

For test-retest reliability, participants who completed the first administration were sent he invitation to retest 2-weeks after. A reminder was sent to complete the retest after 2 weeks for non-responders. The test rest interval minimum 2 weeks (with the longest possible interval of 4 weeks) is commonly used in psychometric analyses was considered long enough to minimise memory effects from the first administration responses and short enough that significant changes in self-efficacy were unlikely. Data in Table 1 demonstrate that self-efficacy measures are relatively stable and would typically be not expected to change in two weeks [11, 15, 16].

Reporting of validity and reliability were aligned with COSMIN guidelines [23] (Supplementary data).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted in R and descriptive statistics were evaluated using Microsoft Excel.

The test data (initial responses in Phase V, n = 135) were used to assess unidimensionality of the whole measure as well as each subscale. The 20-item measure was evaluated using exploratory factor analysis with maximum likelihood estimation and Pearson correlation matrix with NA responses treated as missing for the medication and smoking cessation subscales. Subsequently, each subscale (reflecting the five self-management domains) was evaluated separately to assess unidimensionality. Factor loadings of each item on a factor of > 0.3 with explained variance of > 50% were considered acceptable [24].

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach alpha to assess how inter-related the items in the measure are. Using previously published criteria, a Cronbach alpha of > 0.7 was considered acceptable [25]. Test re-test reliability (n = 112) was assessed through the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC, two-way random effects model for agreement). This assesses the stability of the measure over the time interval. Using previously published criteria adequate test-retest reliability was determined as > 0.7 [26, 27]. The Standard Error of Measurement (SEM = standard deviation* √(1-ICC)) and minimal detectable change (MDC = SEM*1.96*√2) are reported.

Results

Phase I - rapid review of self-management questionnaire measures

The summary, structure and psychometrics of the nine questionnaire measures identified through rapid review are presented in Table 1. These included the Arthritis Self-Efficacy Scale (ASES) [11], Arthritis Self-Efficacy-8 [12], Patient Activation Measure (PAM) [13], Patient Activation Measure-13 (PAM-13) [14], Rheumatoid Arthritis Self-Management Behaviour Scale (RA-SMBS) [15], Rheumatoid Arthritis Self Efficacy Scale (RASE) [16], Self-Care Behaviors Scale (SCBS) [17], Self-Efficacy to Manage Chronic Disease Scale (SEMCD) [28] and PROMIS (The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System) Measures of Self-efficacy for Managing Chronic Conditions [29].

Excluded measures that are not specific to chronic disease and self-management, included the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) and Self-Management Ability Scale (SMAS 30) [30, 31]. The Joint Protection Self Efficacy Scale (JP-SES) and Self-Efficacy for exercise (SEE) were excluded as they were specific to only one domain [32, 33]. The Swedish Rheumatic Disease Empowerment Scale (SWE-RES-23) was excluded as the measure was developed in Swedish language without psychometrics of an English version [34].

The included measures ranged from having 6 items [28] to 36 items [29] with no subscales [12–14, 16, 28] and up to seven subscales [11, 15, 17]. Common item response formats were Likert scale agreement/ disagreement [13–17, 29] or visual analogue scales [11, 12, 28]. Responses were scored by using the sum [15–17, 29] or mean of items [11, 12, 28] or sum of items transformed to a specific metric [13, 14]. Most measures were scored by the total of all items, with higher scores indicating higher self-efficacy or self-management. One questionnaire scored subscales separately [11].

There were no measures that evaluated efficacy of self-management across all domains of smoking cessation, diet, exercise, medications, and wellbeing (Table 2). Seven evaluated self-efficacy [11–14, 28, 29], and two measured actual self-management behaviour [15, 17]. Wellbeing self-management was covered by most measures and had the largest number of assessment items across the domains [11, 12, 15–17, 28, 29]. Eight measures included items pertaining to exercise or forms of movement [11–17, 29] and seven included medications [13–17, 28, 29]. Diet was covered by four measures [13, 14, 17, 29] and smoking cessation was covered by one [17]. Diet and smoking as self-management domains were covered the least. Other components of self-management assessed by the measures included pain [11, 12, 15–17, 28], sleep [11, 12, 16, 29], fatigue [11, 12, 16, 28], social interaction [15–17, 29] and interaction with healthcare [13–15, 17].

Reported internal consistency was generally acceptable or high across measures, with Cronbach alpha above 0.7. In ASES Cronbach alpha ranged from 0.75 to 0.90 across subscales [11]. The shorter ASES-8 version Cronbach alpha was 0.89 [12]. The RA-SMBS also had high internal consistency, with Cronbach alpha 0.908 [15]. The SCBC was 0.74 [17], SEMCD6 0.900 [28] and PROMIS item banks and short forms ranged from 0.85–0.97 [29].

Test-retest reliability was assessed in only three measures. ASES had acceptable test-retest reliability ranging from 0.85 to 0.90 by Pearson correlation [11]. In RA-SMBS, test-retest reliability was moderate with an ICC of 0.628 and Pearson correlation of 0.78 [15]. Test-retest reliability for RASE at a one week interval was 0.694 by correlation coefficient and 0.889 at four weeks [16].

The PAM and PAM-13 were developed with use of Rasch analysis to create a unidimensional measure [13, 14].

Structural validity was assessed in ASES by an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) followed by a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [11]. An EFA was conducted for RA-SMBS and subsequent CFA demonstrated an acceptable model fit on the final 23 item version [15]. An EFA followed by CFA was conducted for the SCBS with seven factors identified [17]. A unidimensional structure was demonstrated in ASES-8 with an EFA [12]. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for all full PROMIS question banks demonstrated moderate to high model fit indices with each bank demonstrating high comparative fit indexes and satisfactory factor loadings [29].

Principal component analysis was used to explore construct validity in RASE with eight factors identified [16]. Principal component analysis confirmed uni-dimensional structure for the SEMCD [28].

Phase II – development of draft measure

After review of the current available measures, a draft measure was developed– this was called SMART (Self-Management in ARThritis). The draft measure had four items for each of the five self-management domains (total of 20 items). The domains were presented in the order of wellbeing, exercise, diet, medications and smoking cessation, and included examples of relevant activities. The response format for each item was a 5-point Likert scale., with response labels of 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = unsure, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree. Questionnaire instructions included a brief introduction to self-management, and descriptions of the assessed efficacy constructs of knowledge (having relevant information and understanding of self-management requirements), confidence (feelings and thoughts to do of that self-management activity), perceived ability (skills and physical capability for that self-management activity), and capacity to manage challenges (coping with difficulties related to that self-management activity in the long term, including feelings, motivation, other people, resources, stress and life situations).

Phase III – participant feedback

Patient participant feedback

Data were obtained from 12 participants (20% response) with diagnosed autoimmune inflammatory arthritis. Median time taken to complete the measure was less than 5 min (2.39 IQR: 1.61–2.84). Feedback was generally positive. A high majority of participants agreed the instructions and questions were easy to understand (91%), the measure was easy to complete (100%), completion time was reasonable (83%), the measure could be answered accurately for their situation (91%) and they could understand why people would be asked to complete this questionnaire (93%). There was a mixed response regarding the usefulness of the questionnaire in assessing the confidence for self-management of arthritis with 50% rating the score “good” or “very good”, and 50% reporting “fair”.

Clinician feedback

Feedback was obtained from four consultant rheumatologists, two rheumatology advanced trainees and two resident medical officers (100% response). All agreed that the measure covered all relevant areas of arthritis and healthy lifestyle self-management (100%). The measure overall was considered useful with a 100% rating of “good” or “very good”. There was a very high level of agreement that the questionnaire included relevant factors for arthritis management (100%), was relevant to a range of patients (88%), provided valuable information (88%) and would be useful to understand patients’ confidence about arthritis self-management (100%).

Phase IV - refinement of measure post feedback

In the open-ended text for suggested improvements, three patient participants suggested that a valuable inclusion would be to assess the impact and adjustment required of living with arthritis. The overarching title for the “Wellbeing” domain was therefore revised to “Adjustment and Coping”. The Medication subscale was revised to include medication management during flares of arthritis in response to clinician participant feedback that this would be a valuable inclusion Based on participant feedback medications and Smoking cessation subscales were altered to include the option to omit these subscales by choosing “No” for the subscale if not applicable (i.e. patients were not on medication or were not current smokers).

The order of the self-management domains was questioned during the clinician feedback session. Clinicians indicated that wellbeing was a challenging subscale to begin with and it would be better placed after exercise. In light of this feedback, the order of self-management domains was changed. Clinicians suggested wording of items could be simplified to clearly explain relevant terms. Accordingly, the language used in each item was also revised.

The final measure is presented in Supplementary Materials 1. Items within each self-management domain sub-scale are scored separately with a sum out of 20, with higher scores indicating higher self-efficacy.

Measurement error

The standard error of measurement (SEM) for each subscale ranged from 1.89 to 2.93, indicating reasonably precise measurements. Corresponding minimal detectable change values ranged from 5.25 to 8.13. The minimal detectable changes (MDC) of 8.13 and 7.78 were higher in the smoking and adjustment and coping subscales respectively (Table 8).

Floor/ceiling effects

There were no floor effects: the proportion of the lowest score ranged from 0.74% to 2.96%. There were subscale ceiling effects ranging from 20.74 to 39.23%. The smoking subscale had a significant ceiling effect of 75% (Table 8).

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and evaluate the psychometric properties of a novel measure of self-efficacy of self-management specific to patients with inflammatory arthritis (SMART). The measure was developed after a review of previous measures, reflected the previously developed Perceived Competence Scales [19, 20], and was revised in response to patient and clinician feedback. Results indicated unidimensional subscales, strong internal consistency and acceptable test-retest reliability.

Our rapid review of existing measures of self-management identified gaps in content, with no measure addressing all five domains of self-. The SMART questionnaire is therefore unique as it addresses five domains of exercise, diet, smoking cessation, medication, and wellbeing, which represent key targets in arthritis self-management [3, 4]. Existing measures informed response format and scoring with a Likert scale and scores derived as a sum of relevant items used for SMART.

A key component of this study was the integration of clinician and participant feedback on a draft version. For example, in response to feedback, item and instruction wording and ordering was revised such that the final version included the wording of “adjusting to and coping with arthritis” rather than generalised wellbeing. Furthermore, clinician feedback prompted the reordering of subscales to start with exercise, and then have wellbeing (relabelled adjustment and coping), diet, medication and smoking. This enhances practicality and usability of the measure, while providing valuable insights into perceived usefulness. However further content validation is required. Participant feedback indicated that half the participants rated the usefulness of the measure as only “fair” (with the other half rating the usefulness as “good” or “very good”). Future research could examine this perception in more detail. Input from patients is integral for sufficient content validity, and could include in-depth interviews to examine patient perceptions in more detail [24]. Cognitive interviewing of patients as they complete the questionnaire could provide valuable information on perceptions and interpretation of items.

The clinician feedback was limited to rheumatologists and medical staff, however we acknowledge the importance of a multi-disciplinary approach to understand self-management of arthritis. The measure was developed as part of a multidisciplinary project with collaborators in dietetics, clinical exercise physiology, tobacco control clinical health psychology and public health. However further input from other disciplines such as nursing, occupational therapy and physiotherapy could be beneficial. The feedback in the current study thus represents preliminary content and face validity only and should be addressed further in future iterative refinement of the measure.

The measure was tested in a small sample of Australian people with inflammatory arthritis and so results may not be applicable across other settings and conditions. Due to the recruitment method, which used a database of people who had previously agreed to be contacted about research opportunities, a potential limitation of this study is self-selection bias. Participants who opted in may have been more motivated or engaged in self-management than the broader arthritis population, potentially influencing feedback on the measure’s usability and perceived usefulness. Additionally, most participants had long-standing diagnoses (12% diagnosed for 6–10 years, 57% diagnosed more than 10 years ago), which may limit the applicability of findings to newly diagnosed patients, who may have less experience with self-management strategies, engaging with healthcare, and who may not be well established on medical treatment. The measure also needs to be studied further to determine comprehensibility and utility in groups with a non-English speaking background or those with limited literacy, as they were likely not captured in this cohort. The measure evaluates important self-management targets of diet, exercise, smoking cessation, medications and wellbeing. Other self-management components in arthritis include sleep quality, fatigue, and pain [3], which were not specifically identified in SMART. We conceptualised “wellbeing” (relabelled as adjustment & coping in response to participant feedback) as a broad multidimensional construct and described it as encompassing “coping, positive thoughts and feelings, good relationships and quality of life”. This could include fatigue, sleep quality, stress management, and pain management. This approach is more consistent with the contemporary positive psychology movement which focusses on flourishing and enablement rather than the reduction of negative experiences. However, future in depth qualitative research could examine how patients interpret this domain, and what they perceive it includes.

The multidimensional factor structure supporting the five self-management domains has implications for scoring. Scoring of each subscale results in five separate scores rather than one total score as was first considered. The advantage of this approach is that specific areas to address with an intervention (e.g. efficacy for smoking cessation) are more readily apparent than when using a single score that encompasses all the self-management areas. The medication and smoking subscales can be omitted if not relevant to the participant (i.e. never smoker or not on any medications).

Ceiling effects were observed in several subscales. This was highest in the smoking cessation subscale, where 75% of participants achieved the highest possible score. As a result, this measure may be less sensitive to detect an improvement in participants with well-established self-management behaviours. This outcome may also have been influenced by self-selection bias within the sample, as discussed earlier, as few participants were current smokers. However, the scale demonstrated no floor effects and may still be useful to identify individuals with lower self-management self-efficacy and to detect potential improvements in this group. The minimal detectable change values also indicate that substantial changes in the subscale scores may be required to detect a clinically meaningful change that exceeds measurement error. While it may be challenging to interpret small changes in subscale scores, moderate to large changes or consistent changes in self-management self-efficacy over time should be interpretable.

SMART demonstrated strong internal consistency and acceptable test-retest reliability. Reported internal consistency was acceptable across the other nine measures identified with Cronbach alpha values ranging from 0.74 to 0.97 [11–17, 28, 29]. SMART values were also high with subscale values ranging from 0.92 to 0.98. The test-retest reliability for SMART subscales was comparable to the RA-SMBS [15], but not as robust as compared to ASES and RASE [11, 16]. The test-retest reliability results (ICC 0.61–0.89) indicate that there was acceptable stability in responses over a retest time interval of 2–4 weeks. This is considered an appropriate time interval for the evaluation of patient reported outcome measures (PROM) [24]. Test-retest reliability was lowest in the Adjustment and Coping (wellbeing) domain (ICC = 0.61). This is likely a reflection of the intrinsically lower stability of this domain generally, where perceptions of being able to cope with and adjust to living with arthritis manage may be more heavily influenced by external factors and day to day events [35].

Further psychometric testing could be conducted with a larger and more diverse sample of patients and to examine additional forms of validity, such as convergent validity. This could involve comparing responses from the SMART-assessed group with scores from other self-efficacy measures, such as the widely used Arthritis Self-Efficacy Scale (ASES), Patient Activation Measure (PAM) and Short Version Patient Activation Measure (PAM-13). Sensitivity to change could also be examined in the context of an intervention. This would provide further evidence for the utility of SMART as a measure of self-management efficacy.

SMART is quick and easy to administer and has potential research and clinical applications. In research, it can be applied to assessment of effectiveness of interventions such as education programs or self-management interventions. In clinical practice, the measure could be used to identify patients with inflammatory arthritis who could benefit from directed support for self-management which could include specialists e.g. smoking cessation interventionist, clinical/health psychologist, dietician, exercise physiologist or from a holistic multidisciplinary program. Given the test-retest reliability results, frequent administration would not be required. We would consider re-administration over a longer interval such as six months, in response to specific triggers (such as adverse life events), or if clinicians were concerned about self-management.

Conclusion

The SMART measure is a 20-item self-report measure and preliminary psychometrics indicate acceptable validity, strong internal consistency and acceptable test-retest reliability in individuals with inflammatory arthritis. The measure had positive feedback from patients for ease of use, and from medical clinicians for usefulness. The SMART measure potentially offers a novel way to assess self-efficacy in arthritis self-management across five key domains of exercise, wellbeing, diet, medications and smoking cessation in clinical and research settings. Further research to validate this measure would be useful.
