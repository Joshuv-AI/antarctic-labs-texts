Out-of-home activities motivating older adults’ participation in the community
Authors: Sapphire H. Lin, Fang Zhao, Xin Yang, Yi-Ching Lynn Ho, Belinda Yuen, Yee Sien Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Life space—the geographical extent of people’s movement within their communities—is closely linked to the physical and cognitive functions of older adults. It serves as a potential marker for mobility and health among aging populations, corresponding to the ability to age in place. Central to maintaining and expanding older adults’ life spaces are their out-of-home travel and activities. In a study with 1118 community-dwelling older adults in Singapore, participants’ travel patterns were tracked over a period of two weeks to identify how and when they travel, where they travel to, and why they travel (or what they do at out-of-home locations). Data was collected via a mobile application with precise location tracking and an accompanying log for participant input. This paper tests the feasibility and comprehensiveness of a list of activities that older adults engage in when they visit places outside their homes, using it to characterize travel motivations amongst older persons. Furthermore, it identifies the specific activities that are associated with larger life spaces, while controlling for sociodemographic differences in a hierarchical linear regression model. The findings suggest that the proposed list adequately represents older adults’ motivations for travel and activities within the community, making it suitable for applications in future research and analyses. Crucially, the results indicate that although trips to meet day-to-day needs were performed more frequently, trips for employment and social activities were the key drivers of larger life spaces or greater extents of travel.

Introduction

Life space is a spatial measure of people’s mobility within the community. This measure can range from movements within one’s home to further distances such as locations outside their town of residence [1, 27]. Life space is particularly relevant when studying older adults’ physical health and social engagement. When people age, their life spaces often become increasingly constricted [27], which can negatively impact their well-being. Indeed, life space is associated with older adults’ social participation [17, 25], and constrictions in life spaces are correlated with negative health outcomes amongst older adults [34]. Life space, therefore, potentially serves as a health marker for aging populations, given its predictive value for mortality, morbidity, and healthcare utilization amongst older adults, along with its economic impact for the society [15, 25].

Hitherto, the most widely used life space measure is the self-reported University of Alabama at Birmingham’s Life-Space Assessment (UAB-LSA; [15]). It summarizes the distance, frequency, and independence of travel on a scale of 0–120, with higher scores representing greater mobility [27]. However, this measure is subject to recall bias, and does not capture where people go and why they make those trips. This causes the UAB-LSA score to lack contextual meaning regarding older adults’ motivations for travel and active aging [35]. Given these limitations, objective and comprehensive capture of these activities becomes an attractive approach to measure life space [2].

For older adults, maintaining or expanding life space depends on their ability and motivation to engage in out-of-home activities and travel. While the broader literature has identified major motivations for travel [7, 8, 23], this is seldom studied within the life space framework, limiting its translation to wider applications in healthcare and urban planning. Indeed, the literature lacks a consistent typology of older adult travel and activity within life space studies.

To address these gaps, this paper tests the feasibility and comprehensiveness of a typology of activities that stratify older adults’ out-of-home trips according to their purposes and clarifies their motivations for out-of-home travel. Additionally, we examine how different travel motivations relate to an objective measure of life space. Establishing an objective and consistent list of activity points that comprehensively summarizes older adult travel patterns will be beneficial for future research and practice. Importantly, understanding the relationship between travel motivations and life space for this target population can inform scholars and policymakers about older adults’ travel motivations and preferences, enabling improvements to policies, services and experiences that keep older adults active within the community.

Travel motivations and life space

In the context of life space and mobility studies, activity points (sometimes termed nodes, opportunities or points of interest) refer to significant and meaningful locations that people visit [29]. Strictly speaking, the study of activity points does not constitute life space assessment, as there is no actual spatial parameter. However, in contemporary life space research though, the study of activity points receives greater attention, especially in geographic information system (GIS) studies employing global positioning system (GPS) data. These methods provide much more granular information on community participation [2].

Over time, the study of activity points was incorporated into the behavioral sciences through the concepts of travel diaries and ecological momentary assessments [4]. These methods, often aided by technology such as smartphone applications, can capture real-time data and offer advantages over traditional retrospective self-reporting methods. They increase accuracy and significantly reduce self-recall bias, making them desirable tools for understanding travel behavior, patterns, preferences, and related factors [33].

Research has shown that perceptions of accessibility to facilities, transit access, and supportive built environment features increased out-of-home travel amongst older adults in Singapore [12]. Objectively, older adults in denser environments with increased transit accessibility are more likely to engage in active social and recreational activities [19]. These findings underscore the importance of designing neighborhood environments and transportation networks that support active and healthy aging by facilitating positive person-environment interactions and enhancing access to out-of-home activities [5].

Beyond frequency of travel, the extent of travel (life space) and participation in out-of-home activities are deeply interconnected, yet this connection has received inadequate attention in present literature. In one of the few studies that explored the associations between out-of-home activities and life space mobility, researchers found that activities such as work, business, shopping, leisure, and pickup, were all positively associated with UAB-LSA scores. Conversely, leaving the home for hospital/clinic visits demonstrated a negative association [31]. While this study supports the relationship between out-of-home activities and life space, it highlights the need for more nuanced categorizations of travel motivations to better understand which specific activities contribute to an expanded life space.

Activity points that characterize out-of-home travel motivations

There are multiple ways in which scholars have categorized activity points or places at which people stop during their travel within their local communities. Recker et al. [28] initially attempted to characterize travel and activity patterns by examining sociodemographic and temporal distributions across distances and purposes of travel. They broadly categorized activities into five categories: Home; Work/Work-related/Education; Shopping; Social/Entertainment/Recreation/Other; Travel.

Developing a more prioritized framework of activities, Siła-Nowicka et al. [29] discovered that people tend have sets of hierarchically ranked places that they visit frequently or where spend a significant amount of time. Typically, the most important location is the home, followed by work or school. Third or further ranked places include activity points where instrumental activities of daily living (e.g., grocery shopping) occur, alongside locations for physical activity, leisure and spiritual activities. These locations largely constitute an individual’s social capital [22].

In a bid to identify the ‘third places’ where people spend their time, apart from home and work, Siła-Nowicka and colleagues [29] conducted an analysis of points of interest, which were further conceptualized into 10 activity types. This list included: Accommodation, Eating and Drinking; Commercial Services; Attractions; Sports and Entertainment; Education and Health; Public Infrastructure; Manufacturing and Production; Retail; Transport (parking, petrol stations). The authors later excluded Manufacturing and Production from analysis.

From a socio-scientific perspective, Mokhtarian et al. [23] attempted to categorize 10 or more travel motivations using ’s hierarchy of needs [21] as a framework. To address physiological needs, people travel for Grocery Shopping and Eating Out. To address safety/security needs, people travel for Work, Medical, Exercise, Banking/Investments, Religious Services, or Therapy. To address social needs, people travel for Social Activities, or Volunteer/Club/Religious Activities. Further up the hierarchy of needs were more abstract motivations for travel such as Escape, Therapy, and Adventure.

In Singapore, there have also been attempts to categorize different activity stops to understand local usage and satisfaction levels. Instead of classifying them as travel motivations, the local public housing authority [13] approached the subject from a place-centric angle and listed 12 types of facilities available in every precinct in the country. Although this list is different from lists of travel motivations reviewed above, it provides an authoritative perspective on the local land-use mix within urban towns. The stops include Commercial Facilities such as General Retail Shops, Markets or Market-Produce Shops/Stalls, and Eating Facilities; Elderly-Friendly Facilities; Playgrounds; Parks & Greenery; Transportation Facilities; Sports Facilities; Recreational & Leisure Facilities; Precinct Facilities; Community Facilities; Education Facilities; Health/Medical Facilities; Financial Facilities.

Taking into consideration the lack of consistency in activity lists characterizing older adult travel patterns and the under-investigated relationship between out-of-home activities and objective extents of life space, we define the aims for this study. This paper first aims to develop a unified, comprehensive list of activity points pertaining to out-of-home travels by integrating prior work on travel diaries, ecological momentary assessments and life space mobility sciences. This provides a consistent, robust and standardized reference of facilitating future research and analysis. Furthermore, we test this list with a large diverse sample of older adults in Singapore to determine its feasibility and ease of use on a digital GIS platform. Finally, we analyze how these activity points and travel motivations are related to an objective spatial measure of life space.

Methods

This paper presents a focused analysis of travel activities and life space mobility, using data from the Elderly Life Activities–Space Envelopes (EASE) Project—a large, comprehensive study on the life spaces of older adults ≥50 years old in Singapore and their associated multidisciplinary determinants [18, 30, 32]. The primary data source for this study was travel diaries collected from 1118 older adults over a 14-day period. In this study, we employed an a priori approach of developing a list of activities before data collection to ensure structured input from participants. Following this, we collected the travel diaries with a GPS tracking mobile application or with paper logs. The extent of travel (calculated with coordinates) and the purpose of travel (selected by participants) were then used for data analysis.

Derivation of list of activities

Through several consensus meetings, the research team, comprising experts in healthcare, social science, urban planning and travel behavior, collaboratively derived a list of 14 activities. This was done after cross-referencing existing literature on travel motivations [23, 28, 29] and the types of estate facilities available in Singapore [13]. To ensure completeness, a free-text option for “Others” was included, acknowledging that the list might not be exhaustive. We also considered clarity of how these activities were worded and feasibility of list length, given that the participants were older adults who may not be comfortable with long lists on small smartphone screens. Recruitment

A sample of community-dwelling older adults ≥50 years old (N = 1131) was recruited through direct advertisements and partnering organizations engaging with older adults. Quota sampling was applied to ensure the sample was representative of Singapore’s population in terms of age groups and housing types (which serves as a proxy for socioeconomic status). Participants completed comprehensive assessments of life space, as well as related health, social and environmental determinants, followed by objective life space tracking with a 14-day travel diary. There were 13 participants who did not complete 14 days of the travel logging and were considered to have withdrawn from the study (1.1% attrition). Therefore, only data from 1118 participants who successfully completed all components of the study were analyzed.

This study was approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB Reference Number: 2021/2566). All participants gave their informed consent before any data was collected. The research team maintained strict privacy and confidentiality standards throughout the data collection processes, adhering to local constitutions such as the Personal Data & Protection Act (PDPA) and Human Biomedical Research Act (HBRA).

Data collection

The data used in this paper arise from a 14-day travel diary that participants maintained with the help of an app, X-ING, or with paper logs. Most of the 1118 participants used the X-ING app, while 5% (n = 56) used paper logs. The X-ING app, based on the Future Mobility Sensing technology [38], is an automated travel survey system that tracks user locations and other sensor data to generate a trip diary with inferred modes and activities for the users to verify at the end of each day. This software platform has been successfully deployed and validated in large-scale travel surveys in Singapore and other countries [11, 24, 39]. It provides participants with an easy way to record their travels for two weeks. Figures 1 and 2 provide examples of the app and paper diaries.

All participants using the X-ING app were guided through the installation and setup process. Detailed instructions were provided both in writing and verbally, supplemented by a video guide. The research team maintained a hotline throughout the study period and remained contactable to participants who had questions or encountered difficulties. The research team also monitored the travel logging process through the backend system and initiated contact every 2–3 days with participants who appeared to have difficulties. For older adults who opted to maintain paper diaries, the on-the-ground research team contacted them once every 2–3 days either in-person or via phone calls/messages to ensure their diaries were being completed in a timely and accurate manner.

At the end of the data collection period, the research team performed data entry to convert the 56 paper diaries into electronic records, which allowed integration of the travel diaries of all 1118 participants onto a single unified platform for analysis. The only difference was that the entries from the paper diaries does not include information on the actual routes traveled, which is available for the app users through GPS tracking.

Measures

From the travel diaries, we derived the frequency of stops, which was the total number of times participants visited places for specific activities over the 14 days of data collection. This could range from 0 to any number of times participants logged that activity across the 14-day travel diary.

We further derived the convex hull from the coordinates logged in the travel diaries (see Fig. 3 for illustration). The convex hull is a geometric calculation that identifies the smallest convex polygon enclosing a set of points, with the outermost geospatial points serving as the vertices [26]. This polygon represents the spatial range of the destinations visited and provides a fundamental measure of the maximal geographical area of people’s activities [3]. The area of the convex hull acts as a proxy for the extent of life space [9]. In this study, the area of the convex hull was determined by finding the smallest convex polygon encompassing all the coordinates participants traveled to over the 14 days of data collection and calculating its area (km2). This served as an objective and spatial measure of life space.

Example of Convex Hull

Other sociodemographic variables, such as age, sex, marital status, employment status, housing type and possession of a driving license, were collected through a questionnaire. Given the established association between physical health and older adults’ travel behaviors [19], the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) was also conducted for all participants. The SPPB provides a composite score of lower-extremity performance based on a gait speed test, a balance test (in three positions), and a five-times sit-to-stand test. Each component is scored from 0 to 4, leading to a maximum of 12 points, where higher scores indicate better performance. These variables were included as covariates and controlled for in the statistical analysis.

Data analysis

Before data analysis, we performed data cleaning on the travel diaries. There were 13 participants who had < 14 days of travel logs. To address missing data, we applied mean imputation to the frequency variables based on each participant’s available data. This step ensured that the calculated frequencies were not artificially deflated due to incomplete logging.

To test our proposed typology of out-of-home travels/activities for older adults in Singapore, we conducted a descriptive analysis to summarize: (1) the travel motivations of participants across all participants’ entries in their travel diaries, and (2) the unadjusted and adjusted average frequencies to stops for specific activities per participant after data imputation.

Subsequently, we applied hierarchical multiple regression with two blocks of variables to identify the different travel motivations affecting the convex hull while controlling for sociodemographic differences. In the first block, we included control variables as predictors, namely, age, sex (0 = male, 1 = female), marital status (0 = not presently married, 1 = presently married), employment status (0 = not presently working, 1 = presently working), housing type (0 = public, 1 = private), and driving license (0 = no, 1 = yes). In the second block, we included the total frequency of each of the 14 activities as additional predictors. The convex hull over 14 days was the dependent variable. The variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values were inspected to confirm that multicollinearity was not present among the variables. Data analysis was conducted with JASP 0.19.0 [14].

Findings

Sample description

Participants were between 50 and 88 years old, and the mean age was 63.8 (SD = 7.6). More than half were females (68%). A similar percentage were married individuals (68%). Approximately half were not actively employed (57%). The majority lived in public housing apartments (76%) and more than half (62%) had driving licenses. The sample was relatively robust, with an average SPPB score of 11.4 (SD = 1.4). The outcome measure, convex hull, averaged 101.7km2 (SD = 84.6).

Comprehensiveness of activity list

This paper first aims to test a unified, comprehensive list of activities pertaining to out-of-home travels. Table 2 documents the frequencies of stops made for various purposes across the sample, along with the number of unique participants who logged at least one occurrence of such stops. The findings show that only 3.8% of the stops fell into the ‘Others’ category, while 96.2% of the stops fit into the list of activities provided to participants. This demonstrates that the list managed to capture virtually all the activities participants engaged in. We noted 66.8% of participants had to select an ‘Others’ category at least once through the 14 days of data collection.

Other free-text entries were interpreted as stops that were incidentally picked up by the algorithm, for example, when participants got stuck in traffic jams or other underground transit, or were waiting a long time for the lift or traffic lights, etc. These stops should have been deleted during the verification of trip diaries by the users, however, some of the users chose to keep the stops and explain the situation in free text. Such occurrences can be reduced in future studies by improving the stop detection algorithm and ensuring the participants are proficient in the use of the app.

Importantly, we also tracked the following indicators which support the construct validity of the activity list, for future older adult populations and GIS studies with smartphones. These are: (1) the low attrition rate of 1% (only 13 of 1131 did not complete the study), (2) the minimal preference for paper logs (only 56 of 1118 participants), and (3) the comprehensiveness of the list in capturing 96% of all out-of-home activities.

Discussion

This study contributes to the literature by providing empirical validation and demonstration that a comprehensive, activity-based category list (see Table 1) captures out-of-home travel motivations consistently and reliably, which helps future comparability across studies. Indeed, we observed that amongst all the free-text entries that did not fit into the prescribed list, only PUDO occurred more frequently than the least frequent item. ‘Financial Services’ had the lowest frequency across all the activities in the list, with an occurrence rate of 0.4% across the sample. All other categories derived from the free-text entries—viz. errands, rest stops, ad-hoc meetings/appointments, special events, and community involvement—occurred at a lower frequency than ‘Financial Services’ (i.e., at 0.2% of the time or less).

When building the list, we considered including PUDO but decided against it for several reasons. First, it is often an incidental activity and the durations at these stops might frequently be poorly estimated in the travel logs. For instance, dropping a child off at the school gate may take less than a minute if there were no jams and might likely be missed from the travel log. These instances would lead to an inaccurate representation of PUDOs and cause stress to participants who try to record every PUDO instance.

Second, the literature we consulted [13, 23, 28, 29] did not include PUDO in their list of activities. This could be because it cannot be categorized as the purpose for any one stop and would potentially overlap with every other category of activity. In other words, one could do a PUDO at a healthcare stop, or an education stop, or any other stop for that matter.

Lastly, to minimize respondent burden, we had to balance the number of activities in the list against a catch-all list that would require much scrolling in the app before participants managed to find the entry they were looking for. This last reason applies to all the other categories derived from the free-text entries as well. We considered that 14 activities (instead of 20, assuming all were included) is a balance between having a comprehensive list vis-à-vis a burdensome list for the older adults. Indeed, the low attrition rate, minimal preference for the paper log, and the high rate of capture for out-of-home activities, showed that the list is feasible amongst the target demographic.

We also leveraged the list to provide a descriptive analysis of the travel motivations in this demographic. The common ‘third places’ [29] for older adults, apart from home and work, include places where they dine out, shop for groceries, and engage in exercise and leisure. These locations are most frequented among participants, aside from their homes and workplaces. To improve and maintain community mobility amongst older adults, it is crucial that these ‘third places’ remain comfortable and accessible to older adults as they age. That said, frequency does not reflect the spatial extent of out-of-home travels. The activities significantly associated with larger convex hulls differed somewhat from the most frequented places.

Our analysis also identified the activities that make a significant impact on the extent of participants’ travel, specifically measured by the convex hull, an objective and spatial measure of life space. Findings show that employment and social activities—not just routine errands—drive larger life spaces and shows that life-space expansions are linked most strongly to engagement beyond basic needs. As life-space is a known predictor of physical and cognitive health, this work links specific travel motivations to larger, healthier life spaces, informing age-friendly urban planning and public health by pinpointing which activity types and infrastructure components best support healthy aging-in-place.

As Singapore builds more integrated community hubs and brings amenities closer to residents’ homes with meticulous land use planning [6], daily necessities like groceries and retail needs can be easily met within close proximity. Consequently, the motivations that encourage older adults to travel further distances are primarily social reasons, such as dining out, meeting with friends, participating in religious activities, engaging in leisure pursuits, and exercising.

We recognize that a conundrum sometimes exists between different policies or initiatives at enhancing the lives of older adults. For instance, healthcare practitioners may advocate for increased activity by encouraging older adults to walk longer distances, and urban developers may promote better quality of life by bringing amenities closer to them. Although both recommendations are rooted in the same intention of improving quality of life, they appear to lead to opposing outcomes at first glance. In worst cases, policies could contradict evidence and create obstacles to building healthy cities, as highlighted by case studies in cities such as Baltimore, Bangkok, and Brisbane [10, 20].

This study is a significant step towards reconciling the apparent contradictions between different policies aimed at improving the lives of older adults. It highlights that policies from various perspectives can coexist and complement each other through a comprehensive understanding of the target audience’s needs and behaviors. Specifically, the findings demonstrate that older adults can have their daily necessities met within accessible reach yet still travel further distances for socialization. This underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration among healthcare practitioners, urban planners, and social scientists to resolve these seeming conflicts and derive insights that lead to well-rounded improvements in the lives of older adults. By working together, these professionals can create policies and interventions that enhance both the physical and social aspects of older adults’ mobility, fostering healthier and more socially connected communities.

Practically, the findings support local authorities efforts towards 20-minute towns, where all journeys to respective neighborhood centers can be completed under 20 min [16]. Such planning towards diversity of land use mix within the neighborhood appears to support productive out-of-home participation through the frequency of out-of-home trips. At the same time, built environment features such as sheltered walkways, safe pedestrian crossings, access to public transport, proximity to shops and parks, and street furniture such as benches are critical to enabling older adults to navigate high-rise neighborhoods beyond their immediate surroundings ([36], [37]). They support older adults’ ability to conduct social and work-related travel, which are shown to be associated with larger life spaces. Additionally, because employment-related trips are tied to larger life spaces—and better health, by extension—policies can aim to keep older adults active in the workforce [18].

A limitation of this paper is the use of a non-random sample, as participants were allowed to voluntarily sign up for the study if they heard about it and were not randomly drawn from a sampling frame. Despite the non-probability sampling method, we strived to obtain a large nationally representative sample from the community that matches the local population in terms of age bands, housing type, and frailty. The combination of these quotas is also indicative of a good distribution of socioeconomic status. Although the study sample spans a broad age range (50–88 years), age-related differences were statistically adjusted for through covariates in the analysis. We believe this approach offers a parsimonious and interpretable assessment of the factors associated with older adults’ community mobility.

In conclusion, this paper tests and validates a comprehensive list of activities for future travel surveys of older adults. This list provides a consistent and standardized means to facilitate future research and analysis. The paper also enhances understanding of the motivations behind older adults’ out-of-home travel, considering both frequency and extent, each of which carries distinct implications. While daily necessities drive frequent travel, social interactions encourage older adults in Singapore to travel further and expand their life spaces. Access to and within these spaces are crucial for maintaining the older adults’ mobility and participation outside their homes. This underscores the need for interdisciplinary collaboration between healthcare, urban planning, and social sciences to develop well-rounded interventions and policies that holistically improve the quality of life for older adults, fostering healthier and more socially connected communities.
