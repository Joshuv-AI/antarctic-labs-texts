Gestational diabetes in multifetal versus singleton pregnancies: A multicenter cohort study
Authors: Rinat Gabbay‐Benziv, Esther Maor‐Sagie, Amos Stern, Inna Bleicher, Simon Shenhav, Enav Yefet, Yifat Wiener, Michal Kovo, Maya Frank Wolf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with increased neonatal morbidity in singleton pregnancies, but evidence in multifetal pregnancies is limited. Current diagnostic thresholds are largely extrapolated from singleton data; It remains unclear whether plurality modifies GDM‐associated neonatal and long‐term offspring outcomes. We wanted to evaluate neonatal complications and long‐term offspring healthcare utilization associated with GDM in multifetal versus singleton pregnancies, and to assess effect modification by plurality and the impact of gestational age at delivery.Material and MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study utilized electronic medical records across six university‐affiliated medical centers in Israel (1/1/2010–6/30/2024). All live births at 22 weeks' gestation or later or with birthweight of >500 g were included. Four exposure groups were defined by plurality and GDM status (ICD‐9 code 648.8x): singleton without GDM, singleton with GDM, multifetal without GDM, and multifetal with GDM. Short‐term neonatal complications (neonatal hypoglycemia, neonatal ICU admission, respiratory distress, intraventricular hemorrhage, and growth abnormalities) and long‐term offspring outcomes were assessed by hospital‐based health‐care utilization (e.g., neurology, ophthalmology, cardiology). Multivariable Firth logistic and ordinary least squares regression models were adjusted for maternal age, body mass index, parity, and fertility treatment. Additional models adjusted for gestational age were performed. False discovery rate correction and GDM‐by‐plurality interaction terms were applied.ResultsOf 134 517 pregnancies (136 579 live‐born offspring) to 104 023 mothers, 10 605 singleton (7.9%) and 256 multifetal (0.2%) pregnancies were complicated by GDM. In multifetal pregnancies, GDM was not associated with increased short‐term neonatal complications nor with lower adjusted odds of neonatal hypoglycemia (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.30; 95% CI: 0.18–0.49), neurologic (aOR, 0.37; 95% CI: 0.24–0.59), or ophthalmologic (aOR, 0.49; 95% CI: 0.31–0.77) health‐care utilization. Adjustment for gestational age attenuated associations for neonatal intensive care unit admission but did not fully explain interactions for hypoglycemia or selected long‐term outcomes. Conversely, GDM in singleton pregnancies was associated with increased neonatal morbidity.ConclusionsAssociations between GDM and neonatal and offspring outcomes differ by plurality. Risk stratification strategies derived from singleton pregnancies may not fully capture risk profiles in multifetal pregnancies.Keywords: gestational diabetes mellitus, long‐term neurodevelopment, maternal morbidity, multifetal pregnancies, neonatal outcomes

Key message

Gestational diabetes in multifetal pregnancies was associated with a different pattern of observed maternal and neonatal outcomes than in singleton pregnancies, warranting further investigation into whether plurality influences risk stratification.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) increases neonatal morbidity in singleton pregnancies, and current diagnostic thresholds and management targets are derived from studies of this population. 1 , 2 However, multifetal pregnancies, now comprising 3%–4% of all US births, exhibit distinct metabolic and placental physiology that may modify the neonatal consequences of hyperglycemia. 3 , 4 Observational studies have yielded conflicting findings regarding GDM effects on perinatal outcomes in twins, with some suggesting attenuated neonatal risk compared with singletons. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Moreover, women with multifetal GDM appear to have a lower risk of subsequent type 2 diabetes compared with women with singleton GDM, suggesting that glucose intolerance in twins may reflect transient gestational physiology rather than chronic dysregulation. 5 , 12 Lastly, while singleton GDM is linked to long‐term offspring outcomes, including childhood obesity, metabolic syndrome, neurodevelopmental impairments, 13 , 14 and allergic or respiratory disorders, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 it remains unclear whether these associations exist in multifetal pregnancies.

These observations raise important questions about whether the impact of GDM on neonatal and long‐term offspring outcomes differs between singleton and multifetal pregnancies.

Furthermore, earlier gestational age at delivery, a hallmark of multifetal pregnancies, may partly explain many observed outcome differences, independent of hyperglycemia itself. 7 Using harmonized electronic health records mapped to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model across six Israeli medical centers, we conducted a national multicenter cohort study of 134 517 pregnancies.

We evaluated statistical interactions between GDM and multifetal pregnancies for neonatal and long‐term offspring outcomes and assessed the impact of gestational age at delivery on the observed associations. These analyses clarify whether GDM‐associated risk profiles derived from singleton pregnancies are applicable to multifetal pregnancies.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and data source

This multicenter retrospective cohort study used harmonized electronic medical records from six university‐affiliated medical centers in Israel, mapped to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model within the national Kinneret collaborative research platform. 19 , 20 , 21

Study population

We included all live births ≥22 weeks' gestation or birthweight ≥500 g between January 1, 2010, and June 30, 2024. Exclusions were stillbirths, pregestational diabetes, and major congenital anomalies. Pregnancies were categorized into four groups by plurality and GDM status: singleton without GDM, singleton with GDM, multifetal without GDM, and multifetal with GDM. GDM was identified using ICD‐9 code 648.8x recorded during the index pregnancy. Although all participating centers use a similar two‐step screening strategy for GDM, including a 50‐g glucose challenge test followed by a diagnostic 100‐g oral glucose tolerance test when indicated, the harmonized dataset did not include laboratory glucose values, treatment modality, or measures of glycemic control. Repeated pregnancies from the same mother were included. Analyses of neonatal and long‐term offspring outcomes were performed at the offspring level; therefore, both members of multifetal pregnancies contributed observations to the primary analyses. Sensitivity analyses were subsequently restricted to one offspring per mother to minimize within‐mother and within‐pregnancy correlation.

Primary outcomes

Primary outcomes included short‐term neonatal complications (hypoglycemia, neonatal intensive care unit admission, respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage [any grade and by severity], and neonatal growth abnormalities, including small for gestational age and large for gestational age) and long‐term offspring outcomes assessed by hospital‐based health‐care utilization by specialty (neurology, ophthalmology, cardiology, nephrology, and pulmonology/immunology). Long‐term offspring outcomes were assessed using linked mother–child records within the participating hospitals' electronic medical record systems. Follow‐up began at birth and continued until the last recorded encounter within the participating centers or June 30, 2024, whichever occurred first (median follow‐up 3.3 years, range 0–14 years). Follow‐up duration was similar across all four exposure groups (range of group medians: 3.25–3.42 years). Long‐term outcomes were defined as hospital‐based specialty clinic utilization and should be interpreted as indicators of healthcare use rather than confirmed diagnoses. Models evaluating long‐term outcomes were additionally adjusted for birth year to account for temporal trends in coding and healthcare utilization.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included maternal and perinatal outcomes: hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (including gestational hypertension, preeclampsia with or without severe features, and superimposed preeclampsia), cesarean delivery, preterm delivery, fetal growth restriction (FGR), and other perinatal outcomes. All variables were defined using standardized OMOP and ICD‐9 criteria (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Univariable analyses used χ 2/Fisher–Freeman–Halton tests for categorical variables and ANOVA/Kruskal–Wallis for continuous variables, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. 22 To denote statistically significant pairwise differences between exposure groups, comparison letters (a–f) were assigned to specific group contrasts and annotated directly in the descriptive tables (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4). Each letter appears only once per row for visual clarity. The complete mapping of letters to exposure group comparisons is presented in Table S2.

Multivariable analysis of perinatal, neonatal, and long‐term offspring outcomes by exposure group.

Note: The reference group for all comparisons is singleton pregnancies without gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Models were adjusted for maternal age, body mass index (BMI), parity, and use of fertility treatment.

Multivariable analyses used Firth logistic regression for binary outcomes 23 and ordinary least squares regression for continuous outcomes, addressing small subgroup sizes (multifetal GDM, n = 256). Two model specifications were prespecified: (1) group‐specific adjusted odds ratios (aORs) using indicator variables (singleton pregnancies without GDM as the reference group), and (2) a main‐effects model including a GDM‐by‐plurality interaction term to formally test effect modification by plurality. Covariates included maternal age, body mass index, parity, and fertility treatment, selected a priori. In sensitivity analyses, neonatal models additionally adjusted for gestational age at delivery to evaluate the extent to which observed associations could be explained by differences in gestational age at delivery.

Continuous variables were z‐score standardized. Missing covariate data were limited overall (maximum 4.9% for maternal BMI and <0.2% for all other covariates); therefore, mean imputation was used to preserve sample size and minimize data loss.

Results are reported as aORs or β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals derived from profile likelihood or penalized likelihood ratio tests. FDR correction was applied separately by outcome domain (perinatal, neonatal, long‐term). Interactions were significant at q < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis included bootstrap resampling (n = 1000 iterations) of significant interaction terms, oversampling smaller exposure groups (multifetal GDM first, then multifetal without GDM, singleton GDM, without GDM) to ensure proportional representation while limiting each mother to one pregnancy per iteration (Table S3). FDR correction was reapplied within each iteration. Because offspring‐level analyses included both members of multifetal pregnancies, resampling was restricted to one offspring per mother in each iteration to reduce potential clustering arising from shared maternal and pregnancy characteristics.

Software

All analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9.7). Univariable tests, including ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, Chi‐squared, and Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact tests, were implemented using the scipy (version 1.7.3) and rpy2 (version 3.4.5) packages. Multivariable Firth logistic regression was conducted using the firthlogist package (version 0.5.0), and OLS regression and FDR correction were performed using the statsmodels package (version 0.14.0).

RESULTS

A total of 134 517 pregnancies (136 579 live‐born offspring) from 104 023 mothers were included: 90.6% (121 857) singleton without GDM, 7.9% (10 605) singleton with GDM, 1.3% (1799) multifetal without GDM, and 0.2% (256) multifetal with GDM. Multifetal GDM comprised 256 pregnancies (512 neonates). Among the multifetal pregnancies, seven were triplet gestations, none of which were complicated by GDM. Maternal baseline characteristics according to the four exposure groups are presented in Table 1.

Primary outcomes: Short‐term neonatal complications

Mean birthweight was significantly lower in multifetal pregnancies compared with singletons, irrespective of GDM status. Respiratory and intraventricular hemorrhage were more frequent among multifetal pregnancies than singleton pregnancies, independent of GDM status.

Within multifetal pregnancies, GDM was not associated with higher rates of short‐term neonatal complications compared with multifetal pregnancies without GDM. Mean birthweight was similar between multifetal pregnancies with and without GDM (2448 g vs. 2401 g), as were neonatal intensive care unit admission (30.7% vs. 32.4%) and rates of growth abnormalities. The proportion of neonates classified as small for gestational age was lower among multifetal pregnancies with GDM compared with those without GDM (8.4% vs. 12.9%; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Primary outcomes: Long‐term offspring morbidity

The median duration of follow‐up was 3.33, 3.42, 3.25, and 3.33 years for the singleton GDM, singleton without GDM, multifetal GDM, and multifetal without GDM groups, respectively. Long‐term neonatal outcomes differed according to the four exposure groups (Table 2). Across 17 categories of healthcare utilization, offspring from multifetal pregnancies, regardless of GDM, had higher utilization rates than offspring from singleton pregnancies. Within multifetal pregnancies, GDM was not associated with higher long‐term utilization. Neurology‐ and ophthalmology‐related visits were more frequent among offspring from multifetal pregnancies without GDM than among those from multifetal pregnancies with GDM (neurology, 6.3% vs. 3.3%; ophthalmology, 7.5% vs. 4.7%; both p < 0.001).

Secondary outcomes: Maternal and perinatal complications

Women with GDM were older and had a higher body mass index than those without (p < 0.001). Fertility treatments were markedly more common in multifetal GDM (20%) vs. multifetal without GDM (13%) or singletons (1%–3%, p < 0.001). Median gestational age at delivery decreased across exposure groups, from 39.0 weeks among singleton pregnancies without GDM to 34.7 weeks among multifetal pregnancies with GDM (p < 0.001). Cesarean delivery predominated in multifetal pregnancies (73.4% in multifetal pregnancies with GDM vs. 64.4% without GDM). Among singleton pregnancies, the corresponding rates were 30.7% in those with GDM and 18.5% in those without GDM (p < 0.001). Hypertensive disorders were more frequent in GDM pregnancies and highest among multifetal GDM (any preeclampsia 16.4% vs. 11.0% in multifetal without GDM, p < 0.001). Cholestasis of pregnancy followed a similar gradient, reaching 5.1% in multifetal GDM. Fetal growth restriction was more prevalent in multifetal than singleton pregnancies but similar between multifetal with and without GDM (5.5% vs. 6.8%) (Tables 1 and 3).

Multivariable outcomes analysis

After adjustment for maternal age, body mass index, parity, and fertility treatment and application of FDR correction, statistically significant interactions between GDM and multifetal pregnancies were limited (Table 4). Multifetal pregnancies with GDM were delivered slightly later than expected (β = 0.73 weeks; q < 0.001) and had lower odds of preterm premature rupture of membranes (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.62; q = 0.019). Neonates of multifetal pregnancies complicated by GDM had a lower‐than‐expected risk of hypoglycemia (aOR = 0.30; q < 0.001), reduced neonatal intensive care unit admission (aOR = 0.70; q = 0.002), and shorter length of hospital stay (β = −1.0 days; q < 0.001) compared with expectations based on main effects. Long‐term outcomes also demonstrated lower adjusted odds of neurology‐related visits (aOR = 0.37; q < 0.001), ophthalmology‐related visits (aOR = 0.49; q = 0.002), and social work utilization (aOR = 0.31; q = 0.033). No significant interactions were observed for severe neonatal morbidity or growth outcomes. After additional adjustment for gestational age at delivery (Table S4), associations for neonatal intensive care unit admission and length of hospital stay were attenuated, suggesting that prematurity may partly explain these associations. In contrast, inverse associations for hypoglycemia and neurology‐ and ophthalmology‐related utilization persisted. A modest interaction for neonatal growth restriction emerged after gestational age adjustment, indicating higher risk only after accounting for prematurity.

Bootstrap‐based sensitivity analysis

Bootstrap‐based sensitivity analyses confirmed the high reproducibility of key findings both before and after adjustment for gestational age (Tables S5 and S6). High reproducibility was observed for hypoglycemia (93.5%), length of hospital stay (93.0%), neurologic utilization (95.2%), and ophthalmologic utilization (82.6%). Moderate reproducibility was observed for neonatal intensive care unit admission (77.7%). After gestational age adjustment, neurologic utilization remained robust (77.7% of iterations with q < 0.05), whereas reproducibility was lower for hypoglycemia (54.6%) and ophthalmologic utilization (47.9%). Neonatal growth restriction demonstrated limited reproducibility (<30%).

DISCUSSION

In this national multicenter cohort leveraging OMOP‐standardized data, GDM in multifetal pregnancies was associated primarily with increased maternal morbidity but was not associated with excess short‐term neonatal complications. In contrast to multifetal pregnancies without GDM, multifetal pregnancies with GDM demonstrated lower adjusted odds of neonatal hypoglycemia and lower rates of neurology‐ and ophthalmology‐related health‐care utilization. These GDM‐by‐plurality interaction effects persisted after adjustment for gestational age at delivery and bootstrap‐based sensitivity analyses, supporting effect modification by plurality beyond differences attributable to prematurity. One possible explanation for the lower observed rates of neonatal hypoglycemia and lower neurology and ophthalmology‐related health‐care utilization among offspring is that the metabolic physiology of multifetal pregnancies may differ from that of singleton pregnancies. Greater placental mass and altered maternal insulin resistance may lead to glucose elevations that meet current singleton‐based diagnostic thresholds. However, these elevations may not be associated with the same degree of fetal exposure or adverse outcomes. Our findings raise the possibility that the relationship between currently defined GDM and adverse outcomes may differ in multifetal pregnancies. However, alternative explanations should be considered, including differences in clinical surveillance, management practices, residual confounding, and selection effects inherent to observational studies. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require prospective validation.

Prior meta‐analyses reported increased rates of hypertensive disorders and cesarean delivery in multifetal pregnancies complicated by GDM. However, neonatal associations were inconsistent, with some studies suggesting attenuated perinatal risk compared with singleton pregnancies. 8 , 10 , 11 , 24 , 25 Our findings align with those of Greco et al., 10 who reported lower neonatal mortality (RR 0.50) and lower rates of small‐for‐gestational‐age neonates (RR 0.89) among twins with GDM. We extend these observations through formal interaction testing and assessment of long‐term offspring health‐care utilization. However, long‐term outcomes in our study were based on healthcare utilization rather than confirmed clinical diagnoses and should be interpreted accordingly.

The lower risk of neonatal hypoglycemia contrasts with the classic Pedersen hyperglycemia hyperinsulinemia paradigm and may reflect limited transplacental glucose transfer in multifetal placentation, resulting in reduced fetal hyperinsulinism. 2 , 26 In contrast, GDM in singleton pregnancies has been consistently associated with increased neonatal morbidity in large landmark studies, including HAPO 27 and IADPSG. 28 Together, these findings suggest that the associations between gestational hyperglycemia and offspring outcomes may differ according to plurality, although residual confounding and differences in clinical management cannot be excluded.

These findings suggest that the clinical implications of gestational hyperglycemia may differ by plurality and warrant prospective studies to determine whether alternative diagnostic thresholds could improve risk stratification in multifetal pregnancies. Current diagnostic criteria may identify glucose intolerance in multifetal pregnancies that do not confer comparable neonatal risk, potentially increasing intervention intensity without clear neonatal benefit. Approaches that emphasize enhanced maternal surveillance while accounting for plurality in neonatal risk stratification warrant prospective evaluation.

This study's strengths include its large, multicenter design encompassing six hospitals harmonized under the OMOP Common Data Model (Kinneret platform), allowing standardized analytics and comparison across exposure groups. Rigorous statistical modeling, including Firth logistic regression for rare outcomes, OLS for continuous variables, FDR correction, and bootstrap sensitivity testing (neurologic visits 95.2% pre‐adjustment, 77.7% post‐adjustment) supports the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered. The retrospective design and reliance on electronic medical record coding. Although all participating centers use similar diagnostic approaches for GDM, the harmonized dataset did not include laboratory glucose values, treatment modality, or measures of glycemic control, precluding assessment of disease severity.

The relatively small number of multifetal pregnancies with GDM (n = 256) limited power for detailed subgroup analyses and may have reduced the precision of interaction estimates. Therefore, interaction findings should be interpreted cautiously despite their consistency across sensitivity analyses. A major limitation of this study is the lack of chorionicity data. Because monochorionic and dichorionic twin pregnancies differ substantially in placentation and perinatal risks, the inability to account for chorionicity may have introduced residual confounding and should be considered when interpreting the findings. While short‐term perinatal outcomes were directly retrieved from the medical records, long‐term offspring outcomes were ascertained from hospital outpatient specialty visits, which reflect healthcare utilization rather than confirmed diagnoses. Accordingly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of reduced neurologic or ophthalmologic disease burden. Follow‐up was restricted to data from the six participating hospitals and did not include community medical records or pharmacy data, potentially resulting in incomplete outcome ascertainment, misclassification, and limited generalizability.

Additionally, lack of postpartum data precludes assessment of type 2 diabetes progression. Several neonatal differences were attenuated after gestational age adjustment, suggesting residual confounding by prematurity despite extensive sensitivity testing.

Overall, in this national cohort, GDM in multifetal pregnancies was associated predominantly with maternal morbidity and demonstrated a distinct neonatal and offspring outcome profile compared with singleton pregnancies. These findings provide evidence that the observed associations between gestational diabetes and offspring outcomes differ by plurality and support further prospective studies to determine whether these differences have implications for risk stratification and clinical management.
