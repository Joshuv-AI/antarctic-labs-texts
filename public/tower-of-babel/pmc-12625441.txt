Comorbidities among women presenting for obstetric care in Uganda
Authors: Leevan Tibaijuka, Adeline A Boatin, Asiphas Owaraganise, Lisa M Bebell, Musa Kayondo, Mark J Siedner, Francis Bajunirwe, Yarine Farjardo Tornes, Joseph Ngonzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal mortality rates are high in low- and middle-income countries, with Uganda’s ratio at 189 deaths per 100,000 live births. Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) account for 15% of indirect maternal deaths worldwide. We examined medical comorbidities during pregnancy at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH) in south-western Uganda.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study at MRRH from January to December 2022. Each month, we randomly selected 200 women admitted to the obstetrics department and extracted data on sociodemographic characteristics and diagnoses. We summarised the prevalence and distribution of medical comorbidities using frequencies and percentages. We performed multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities.ResultsWe enrolled 2,336 women over 12 months with a mean age of 26 ± 5.9 years. Of these, 16.6% (n = 387) had ≥ 1 non-pregnancy-related comorbidity: 6.4% (n = 149) had NCDs, and 11.2% (n = 261) had an infectious comorbidity. NCDs included anaemia, 77 (3.3%); chronic hypertension, 35 (1.5%); pre-gestational diabetes, 16 (0.7%); asthma, 9 (0.4%); and cardiac disease, 6 (0.3%). Infectious comorbidities included HIV, 207 (8.9%); severe malaria, 50 (2.1%); tuberculosis, 7 (0.3%); and COVID-19, 4 (0.2%). Two hundred ninety-six (12.7%) women had ≥ 1 pregnancy-related comorbidity, 11.3% (n = 265) had pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, 10 (0.5%) had venous thromboembolic disease, and 5 (0.2%) had gestational diabetes. Factors associated with non-pregnancy-related comorbidities included maternal age over 34 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.56, 95%CI: 1.16, 5.62), 2–4 prior deliveries (aOR = 1.62, 95%CI: 1.08, 2.42) or grand multiparity (aOR = 2.08, 95%CI: 1.21, 3.59), being unmarried (aOR = 5.63, 95%CI: 1.93, 16.43), and alcohol use (aOR = 3.75, 95%CI: 1.45, 9.70).ConclusionsNCDs are common among women admitted for delivery in Uganda. They are associated with advanced maternal age, increasing parity, alcohol use and unmarried status. Clinicians at all healthcare levels, should be aware of these possible underlying conditions and promptly diagnose them. This study highlights a high burden of NCDs, even among younger people, in low-resource regions and calls for further study of the impact of such conditions on pregnancy and childbirth.Keywords: Non-communicable diseases, Maternal health, Pregnancy, Medical comorbidities, Sub-Saharan africa

Background

Maternal mortality is high globally, an estimated 287,000 maternal deaths occurred in 2020, with low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) accounting for 95% of the deaths [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for two thirds of the global mortality [1]. Pre-existing medical conditions aggravated by pregnancy account for 28% of maternal deaths globally with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) contributing to 15% of these deaths [2]. In Africa, indirect causes represent 20% of total maternal deaths [3]. In Uganda, the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) is 189 deaths per 100,000 live births [4] while at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), the MMR is 375, and the indirect causes account for up to 19% of the maternal deaths [5]. Maternal mortality is largely attributed to the direct causes mainly due to obstetric conditions and complications such as preeclampsia/eclampsia, antepartum and postpartum haemorrhage, obstructed labour, abortion and sepsis. These are targeted by the emergency obstetric interventions and maternity services [2].

Despite the importance of these indirect causes of maternal mortality largely due to medical comorbidities including various NCDs during pregnancy, they receive little attention from the key policy and strategy documents of leading national and international maternal health governmental and non-governmental organisations and UN agencies [6, 7]. The NCDs include cardiovascular disease such as chronic hypertension (HTN) and chronic rheumatic heart disease; endocrine or metabolic disease such as diabetes mellitus (DM), hypo and hyperthyroidism; haematological disease such as anemia, sickle cell disease or thalassemia; mental illness such as depression; and neoplasms [8]. Medical comorbidities and NCDs during pregnancy may significantly aggravate obstetric conditions and are associated with adverse effects on maternal health and pregnancy outcomes [8–10]. Also pre-existing NCDs or medical comorbidities developed during pregnancy are aggravated by the physiologic effects of pregnancy [11]. Examples of the significant role of medical comorbidities and NCDs in pregnancy on direct causes of maternal death include ruptured uterus due to macrosomia in un-diagnosed or un-treated diabetes, severe pre-eclampsia due to pre-existing hypertension and death from obstetric haemorrhage in the context of anemia [8].

Medical comorbidities and NCDs in pregnancy in the LMIC are neglected in the areas of (1) under diagnosis and poor detection, (2) limited management and treatment prenatal through birthing (3) poor follow up and suboptimal NCD management in the post pregnancy phase. As a result, there is paucity of reliable data on the burden, diagnosis and management of the medical comorbidities and NCDs in pregnancy in LMIC [12]. This subsequently contributes to the adverse maternal-fetal outcomes due to mismanagement of these conditions [8]. Therefore, more attention is needed to adequately address medical comorbidities and NCDs in pregnancy and post-delivery especially in developing countries like Uganda.

This study set out to determine the burden, describe the distribution and factors associated with the non- pregnancy related medical comorbidities in obstetric admissions at MRRH.

Materials and methods

Study population and setting

This study was conducted at MRRH—a government funded public referral and teaching hospital affiliated to Mbarara University of Science and Technology. It is located in south-western Uganda, an area with a population of nine million people across several districts, including Mbarara, Isingiro, Bushenyi, Buhweju, Ibanda, Kiruhura, Mitoma, Ntungamo, Rwamapara, Sheema, and Rubirizi. Additionally, patients from neighboring countries such as Rwanda, Tanzania, and the Democratic Republic of Congo also seek medical care at the hospital. The maternity department registers approximately 10,000 deliveries every year and has a maternal mortality rate of 375 per 100,000 live births and perinatal mortality rate of 33 per 1000 live birth [5, 13]. The department has 14 obstetricians and gynaecologists, 38 postgraduate students, intern doctors, nurses and midwives.

MRRH provides a wide range of preventive and curative care, and serves as a critical referral center, it houses a high-risk ward dedicated to managing mothers with complicated pregnancies, making it a crucial destination for those with life-threatening medical and obstetric conditions. Furthermore, it boasts the only functional adult intensive care unit in the region capable of providing life support. The hospital also houses a functional neonatal intensive care unit which addresses the needs of newborns with complications [14].

Study design

We employed a cross-sectional study design from January 2022 to December 2022 at MRRH. We retrospectively enrolled a random sample of 200 women each month of 2022 from the obstetrics admissions register. We abstracted from the individual patients’ charts into a Redcap-based database. A waiver of individual consent for this data collection was sought from the institutional review board since there was only clinical register and chart review, but no patient interaction.

Participant recruitment/enrolment

We included a random sample of all obstetric admissions (pregnant and post-delivery women) at MRRH Obstetrics Department during 2022. We excluded participants whose medical records/charts were either missing entirely or contained less than 50% of the required study variables.

Data collection procedure

We used Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools—a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies [15], to extract the required retrospective data from hospital patient records (the maternity clinical registers and individual patient charts). The extracted data included demographic information (age, parity, district of origin), referral history, and diagnoses during admission including medical co-morbidities. The primary outcome was presence of one or more pre-existing or non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities (i.e., chronic hypertension (HTN), pre-gestational diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiac disease, chronic kidney disease, asthma, epilepsy, anemia, HIV, Tuberculosis, malaria, and COVID-19). We also collected data on pregnancy-related medical comorbidities (i.e., pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and venous thrombo-embolic (VTE) disease—deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE)). Medical comorbidities were measured based on documentation of any of these diagnoses at admission or during the patient’s time of admission. Pre-existing comorbidities (chronic hypertension, pre-gestational diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, asthma and epilepsy) were obtained from the patient’s chart as recorded by the clinical care team following a self-report from the participants, with additional evidence of on-going treatment related to these conditions. HIV diagnosis was made per clinical chart review or self-reported HIV positive status. Anemia was defined as haemoglobin estimation of < 11.0 g/dL as noted from the participant’s chart [16–18]. Tuberculosis was defined as presence of cough and other symptoms and signs of tuberculosis with a positive sputum GeneXpert MTB/Rif (Xpert MTB/RIF) for Mycobacterium tuberculosis [19, 20]. Severe malaria was defined as presence of fever and other symptoms of malaria, positive malaria rapid diagnostic test or blood slide for malaria with danger signs [21, 22]. COVID-19 disease was considered if the definitive diagnosis was made following either Polymerase Chain Reaction or rapid diagnostic test in a participant with clinical features of COVID-19 disease [23, 24]. Gestational hypertension was defined as new-onset hypertension without proteinuria [25, 26]. Pre-eclampsia was defined as new onset hypertension with proteinuria (≥ 2 + protein) or without proteinuria but with severe features of pre-eclampsia including severe hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 160mmHg or diastolic ≥ 110mmHg), persistent epigastric pain, persistent headache, visual changes, elevated creatinine and elevated liver transaminases [25, 26]. Eclampsia was defined as presence of grand mal seizures in participants with signs and symptoms of pre-eclampsia [27]. GDM was defined as glucose intolerance that was first detected during the second or third trimester of the current pregnancy [28–30]. DVT was defined as presence of a blood clot in the lower extremities following a compression Doppler ultrasonography [31, 32], PE was defined as presence of dyspnea, abrupt onset of chest pain, cough and other signs in participants with a diagnosis of DVT [31–33]. The determinants/factors collected included maternal age, parity, marital status, employment status, level of education, referral status, smoking, alcohol use.

Sample size calculation and sampling

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi, Version 3, employing the sample size determination for cross-sectional studies. The calculations were based on the following assumptions: a 95% confidence level, 80% power, a prevalence of comorbidities as 20.7% as noted at the High dependence Unit (HDP) of Mulago national referral hospital [34] and considering approximately 10,000 women delivering annually at MRRH. We enrolled a random sample of 200 women each month of 2022 (n = 2400) from the obstetrics admissions register at MRRH. We collected data from 2336 participants that met the inclusion criteria and excluded 64 participants whose medical records/charts were either missing or had less than 50% of the covariates.

Data analysis

Data were exported to STATA 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for cleaning and analysis. We described the participants’ characteristics using proportions. The prevalence of comorbidities was reported as a percentage. The distribution of comorbidities was summarized as frequencies and percentages and presented as bar-charts. To assess the factors associated with comorbidities among pregnant women admitted at MRRH, we performed univariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis and reported both crude and adjusted odds ratios. In the final multivariable model, we included all exposure variables with a p-value < 0.2 at univariable analysis, as well as smoking, based on biological plausibility. To assess collinearity in our multivariable model, we employed the variance inflation factor (VIF) method, considering VIF > 5 as indicative of collinearity. The factors associated with comorbidities in the multivariable model were determined based on variables with a p-value < 0.05, indicating statistical significance.

Results

Of the 8,571 obstetric admissions at MRRH from January 2022 to December 2022, 2400 patient charts were randomly selected for inclusion into the study. We included a total of 2,336 women and excluded 64 women (43 with missing clinical charts and 21 charts had less than 50% of the required variables).

Characteristics of study participants

The mean age was 26 ± 5.9 years; most participants fell within the 20–34 years category (78.7%). The highest proportion were married (98.6%) and had informal employment (51.8%). Regarding gravidity, half (51.1%) of the participants had 2–4 pregnancies. The majority of participants attended antenatal care (ANC) (99.1%). Close to a third of the participants had been referred/transferred from another health facility to MRRH (28.7%). Most participants had a gestation period between 37 and 42 weeks (64.2%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of medical comorbidities among obstetric admissions at Mbarara regional referral hospital

The prevalence of medical comorbidities in pregnancy and post-delivery among women admitted at the maternity ward of MRRH was 16.6% (95% CI 15.1–18.1). Out of the 2336 participants, 387 had one or more non-pregnancy related medical comorbidities. Non-communicable medical comorbidities (NCDs) accounted for 6.4% (n = 149), while the infectious/communicable medical comorbidities accounted for 11.2% (n = 261) of the medical comorbidities. Thirty-four women (1.5%) had both NCDs and infectious medical comorbidities.

Factors associated with non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities among obstetric admissions at Mbarara regional referral hospital

In the multivariable analysis (Table 2), the factors independently associated with non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities were maternal age > 34 years, being unmarried, multiparity (2–4) and grand multiparity (≥ 5), and alcohol consumption. Women aged > 34 years were 2.6 times (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.56, 95%CI: 1.16, 5.62) more likely to have non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities compared to those aged between 20 and 34 years. Also, unmarried participants were 5.63 times (aOR = 5.63, 95%CI: 1.93, 16.43) likely to have non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities compared to the married. Higher parity was also associated with non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities, compared to primigravidas, multiparous (2–4) women were 1.62 times (aOR = 1.62, 95%CI: 1.08, 2.42) likely to have non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities while the likeliness was even higher at 2.1 times) (aOR = 2.08, 95%CI: 1.21, 3.59) for grand multiparous women (≥ 5). Similarly, participants who reported a history of alcohol consumption were 3.8 times (aOR = 3.75, 95%CI: 1.45, 9.70) more likely to have non-pregnancy-related medical comorbidities compared to participants with no history of alcohol consumption.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the prevalence, distribution and factors associated with medical comorbidities among obstetric admissions at a tertiary hospital in a low-resource setting in south-western Uganda. We found that medical comorbidities and NCDs are prevalent, and were independently associated with advanced maternal age, being unmarried, higher parity and alcohol use. Our findings highlight the need to implement medical comorbidity surveillance through timely screening and identification to optimise their diagnosis, accelerate linkage to management and treatment for better maternal and neonatal outcomes.

The high prevalence of medical comorbidities in our study could be partly explained by the rising prevalence of comorbidities in the general population, particularly in women of reproductive age [35–39]. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [8, 36, 38, 40, 41], and aligns with global concerns about the rising burden of NCDs especially in low resource settings [35, 36, 38, 39].

Advanced maternal age (>34 years) was associated with medical comorbidities, consistent with findings from previous hospital-based and population studies among pregnant and post-delivery women [42, 43]. This may be explained by biological changes associated aging—such as hyperlipidemia, obesity/overweight, hypertension, and diabetes—as well as selected behavioral risk factors including alcohol use, smoking, and reduced physical activity which increase with age [44]. Notably, most NCD and communicable disease risk factors are related to behaviors initiated in early adulthood [44].

Higher parity was also associated with medical comorbidities, echoing findings from other settings that link grand multiparity as a contributor to various medical comorbidities, including a five-fold cardiovascular risk [42, 45, 46]. Repeated pregnancies expose women to cumulative cardiometabolic changes—including weight gain, dyslipidemia, increased plasma glucose and insulin resistance, and stressors like endothelial dysfunction and inflammation—which, while generally beneficial to the mother and fetus, may contribute to long-term cardiovascular disease risk [45, 47, 48].

Alcohol use was found to be associated with medical comorbidities, similar to other studies that identified alcohol, smoking, and unhealthy diet as modifiable behavioral risk factors for NCDs [49]. Global alcohol consumption has increased in recent decades, particularly in developing countries [50]. Women may be under-screened for alcohol-related complications due to gendered assumptions, which can lead to earlier onset of disease at lower doses than in men [50–53].

Being unmarried was also associated with medical comorbidities in this study. This aligns with literature suggesting that married women tend to have better physical and mental health outcomes, and is similar to other studies that reported that married women tended to be healthier, both physically and mentally, with fewer medical comorbidities [54–56]. The protective effects of marriage may stem from increased social support and health-promoting behaviors [56–58]. Elevated cortisol levels and flatter diurnal cortisol slopes—more common among unmarried individuals—have been linked to metabolic syndrome, atherosclerosis, and poor glycemic control [59–62].

A key strength of this study is its large sample size, drawn from a full calendar year of obstetric admissions. This comprehensive timeframe enhances the reliability of prevalence estimates and minimizes the influence of seasonal variation. Additionally, the study was conducted at a tertiary referral hospital that manages complex obstetric cases from across southwestern Uganda. This setting offers valuable insight into the burden of medical comorbidities among women requiring higher-level care and reflects patterns that may be relevant to similar referral contexts in low-resource settings.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be noted. First, we reported the prevalence of medical comorbidities among obstetric admissions (pregnant and post-delivery women) at MRRH. This approach excluded women attending antenatal and postnatal clinics who may also experience significant comorbidities, potentially underestimating the overall burden. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral hospital in southwestern Uganda, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other regions or levels of care. However, MRRH receives referrals from multiple peripheral health units and manages complex cases, making it a relevant setting for understanding severe maternal morbidity. Third, the retrospective design relied on routinely documented patient charts, which varied in completeness and quality. We excluded 64 participants whose records were missing or contained less than 50% of the required covariates; however, several variables still had substantial missing data, with some exceeding 50% missingness. This may have introduced selection bias, reduced statistical power, and affected the precision of our estimates. Fourth, although no COVID-19 lockdowns were in place during the study period, residual impacts of the pandemic may have influenced maternal health service utilization in our region. Disruptions from earlier phases — such as reduced antenatal care attendance, delayed care-seeking, and shifts in the proportion of facility-based deliveries — may have persisted, potentially affecting both the composition of our study population and the observed comorbidity patterns [63–65]. Lastly, the study did not include follow-up data or clinical outcomes, which limits our ability to assess the long-term impact of comorbidities on maternal and neonatal health.

Conclusions and recommendations

Medical comorbidities and NCDs are common among women admitted for delivery in Uganda and are associated with advanced maternal age, increasing parity, alcohol use, and unmarried status. While these findings are specific to a referral hospital context and may not be generalizable to all pregnant women, clinicians at all levels of healthcare should remain alert to these potential underlying medical conditions and ensure timely screening and diagnosis during obstetric care. This study underscores the significant burden of NCDs, even among younger women, in low-resource settings, and highlights the need for further research into the long-term impact of comorbidities on pregnancy trajectories and health system responsiveness.
