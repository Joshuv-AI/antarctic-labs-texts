Acute development and rupture of a pancreaticoduodenal artery aneurysm after endovascular aneurysm repair in a patient with median arcuate ligament syndrome
Authors: Ayumi Ito, Gaku Matsumoto, Fumiaki Iwase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 80-year-old man underwent endovascular aneurysm repair at our institution. Preoperative imaging demonstrated celiac artery occlusion caused by median arcuate ligament syndrome and markedly developed pancreaticoduodenal arcades; however, a pancreaticoduodenal artery (PDA) aneurysm (PDAA) was not identified. On postoperative day 2, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed no aneurysm, hematoma, or vascular injury involving the arcade vessels. On postoperative day 3, sudden abdominal pain developed. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a retroperitoneal hematoma with active extravasation, and emergency angiography demonstrated a newly formed ruptured 4-mm aneurysm of the anterior inferior PDA. Coil embolization was successfully performed. This case demonstrates that PDAAs may form and rupture rapidly. Clinicians should explore this rare but potentially fatal condition even when recent imaging studies show no evidence of aneurysm formation.Keywords: Median arcuate ligament syndrome, Pancreaticoduodenal artery aneurysm, Endovascular aneurysm repair

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenal artery (PDA) aneurysms (PDAAs) account for approximately 10% of all visceral artery aneurysms [1,2]. Regardless of their size, PDAAs are associated with a high risk of rupture, and they are often discovered only after rupture has occurred [3]; therefore, they are clinically significant vascular lesions. Stenosis and occlusion of the celiac artery are known to contribute to true PDAA formation. Hemodynamic alterations, specifically increased retrograde blood flow through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade caused by celiac artery stenosis, may lead to aneurysm formation [4]. However, predictive factors for rupture are unclear. We report a rare case of a PDAA that acutely developed and ruptured after endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) in a patient with a markedly enlarged pancreaticoduodenal arcade secondary to median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS).

Case report

An 80-year-old man was diagnosed with a 27-mm left common iliac artery aneurysm with localized dissection and a 20-mm right common iliac artery aneurysm; therefore, EVAR was planned. Preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) was performed using an 80-row multidetector CT scanner, which revealed celiac artery occlusion at its origin and the characteristic “hooked” appearance of MALS. The pancreaticoduodenal arcade vessels were markedly dilated and had a maximum diameter of 7.0 mm. Careful review of axial and multiplanar reconstruction images revealed no aneurysm formation (Figs. 1A and B). EVAR was performed by the cardiovascular surgery team at our institution using bilateral femoral arteries as access sites. A 23-mm main body stent graft (Excluder; W. L. Gore & Associates, Newark, DE) was deployed just below the left renal artery. The right and left common iliac arteries were covered with 20-mm and 27-mm contralateral legs, respectively. An Excluder iliac branch endoprosthesis (W. L. Gore & Associates) was used for the left common iliac artery. A 12-mm iliac branch component was inserted in the external iliac artery, and a 12-mm internal iliac component was deployed to the internal iliac artery. Intraoperative misplacement of the guidewire in the visceral arteries as well as intraoperative and immediate postoperative complications were not observed. Routine contrast-enhanced CT performed on postoperative day (POD) 2 revealed no endoleaks and confirmed adequate perfusion of the abdominal branches. No significant changes in the celiac artery or pancreaticoduodenal arcade were noted (Fig. 1C). The patient was asymptomatic.

On POD 3, the patient reported abdominal pain. His blood pressure and heart rate were 101/68 mmHg and 91 bpm, respectively. The patient’s blood pressure improved after fluid resuscitation. Emergent contrast-enhanced CT revealed a retroperitoneal hematoma with active extravasation (Fig. 2A). Subsequent angiography via the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) identified a 4-mm aneurysm in the anterior inferior PDA (Figs. 2B and C). A 4-Fr COBRA catheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was positioned in the SMA, and a microcatheter (Excelsior SL-10; Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) was inserted in the anterior inferior PDA branching from the first jejunal artery. Embolization was performed using detachable microcoils (Target 360 Detachable Coils; Stryker) (Fig. 2D), and hemostasis was successfully achieved. The patient was discharged without any rebleeding after surgery. PDAA recurrence was not observed during follow-up.

Discussion

PDAAs are classified as true aneurysms or pseudoaneurysms; however, pseudoaneurysms typically arise secondary to pancreatitis, trauma, or surgical intervention [1,2], and true PDAAs have been associated with stenosis or occlusion of the celiac artery. Increased retrograde flow through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade caused by celiac artery stenosis may contribute to aneurysm formation [4]. In a dilated arcade, abnormal hemodynamics may lead to aneurysm development and enlargement [5]. Atherosclerosis, MALS, and celiac artery dissection can cause celiac artery stenosis [6,7]. However, because PDAAs are rare, their natural history is poorly understood.

Several cases of PDAAs that acutely developed and subsequently ruptured have been reported [8,9]; however, to the best of our knowledge, cases involving a PDAA that was clearly absent during imaging the day before rupture have not been reported. This case demonstrates that some PDAAs can develop and rupture within 1 day. Stoecker et al. [10] reported that the presence of calcification in the aneurysm wall was associated with intact PDAAs. Acutely formed aneurysms without calcification may have a higher tendency to rupture, even if they are small, whereas those that enlarge gradually and develop calcification may have a lower tendency to rupture. This may be related to the observation that the risk of PDAA rupture is not correlated with aneurysm size. Our patient had an aneurysm with a diameter of 4 mm. Because of its acute onset and fusiform morphology, a dissecting aneurysm caused by arterial dissection cannot be excluded. However, because most ruptured PDAAs are treated endovascularly, pathological evaluation often is not feasible.

Because this case occurred after EVAR, iatrogenic vascular injury related to intravascular manipulation was a concern. However, the ruptured vessel could not be accessed from the occluded celiac artery; additionally, access from the SMA required passage through the first jejunal branch, making iatrogenic injury unlikely. Furthermore, CT performed the day before rupture showed no findings suggestive of vascular injury. Shibahara et al. [11] reported a case of PDA rupture after EVAR in a patient with celiac artery stenosis caused by MALS, similar to our case. EVAR-related occlusion of the inferior mesenteric artery may have altered mesenteric collateral circulation. Additionally, redistribution of blood flow after excluding iliac artery aneurysms may have acutely changed the hemodynamics of both the visceral and mesenteric circulation. These acute hemodynamic shifts may have contributed to the formation and subsequent rupture of a PDAA. Therefore, it is conceivable that interventions that alter mesenteric hemodynamics in patients with underlying celiac artery stenosis or occlusion, such as that observed with MALS, may be associated with rapid de novo aneurysm formation. Such changes could occur within 24 hours after the intervention in rare instances.

Previous studies have indicated that PDAAs are associated with a high risk of rupture despite their size [6]. Therefore, the current guidelines recommend treating all identified PDAAs regardless of their size [12]. However, cases involving an aneurysm that forms and ruptures within an extremely short timeframe, such as ours, highlight the limitations of screening and prophylactic interventions for rupture prevention. Radiologists should maintain heightened vigilance when interpreting imaging results obtained after interventions that may acutely alter mesenteric hemodynamics, particularly in patients with celiac artery stenosis or occlusion, and carefully evaluate the pancreaticoduodenal arcade vessels because recent negative imaging findings do not definitively exclude the possibility of impending aneurysm formation.

Conclusion

This case highlights that PDAAs can form and rupture within an extremely short time interval despite negative imaging findings. Clinicians should remain vigilant for this rare but potentially fatal condition, particularly after interventions or disease states that acutely alter PDA hemodynamics.
