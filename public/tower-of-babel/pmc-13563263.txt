Cefepime and Mortality
Authors: Zahra N. Sohani, You Jia Zhong, Avideh Afshar, Bander A. Assiri, Genevieve Gore, Matthew P. Cheng, Anthony Lieu, Jordana Serero, Fergus Hamilton, Alexander Lawandi, Mical Paul, Dafna Yahav, Arthur M. Albuquerque, Guillaume Butler-Laporte, Emily G. McDonald, Todd C. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the association of cefepime with all-cause mortality compared with other β-lactam antibiotics?FindingsIn this systematic review and bayesian meta-analysis of 110 randomized clinical trials including 22 608 patients, cefepime was associated with a 94.4% posterior probability that the pooled odds ratio for mortality exceeded 1 compared with other β-lactams. The estimated number needed to harm was between 111 and 227.MeaningThese findings suggest that the potential mortality signal associated with cefepime should be considered when framing its role in clinical practice guidance.

Introduction

Cefepime is a broad-spectrum cephalosporin with activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and gram-positive organisms,1 as well as relative stability against hydrolysis by many ampicillin resistance locus C (AmpC)–producing β-lactamases.2,3 It is recommended as a first-line agent for the treatment of febrile neutropenia by the Infectious Diseases Society of America4 and the European Society for Medical Oncology5 and in the treatment of infections due to organisms with moderate risk of substantial AmpC production.6

Concerns regarding cefepime’s safety arose after a 2007 meta-analysis by Yahav et al7 that reported a 26% relative increase in risk of mortality compared with other β-lactams. Proposed explanations included inadequate drug exposure, which has been associated with worse outcomes,8,9,10,11 and cefepime-associated neurotoxicity,9,12 particularly at higher doses, which may contribute to longer hospitalization and higher in-hospital mortality.13,14,15 Following the Yahav et al7 study, a subsequent meta-analysis conducted by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) that incorporated unpublished industry-sponsored data found no statistically significant increase in mortality.16

Since then, the safety of cefepime has remained controversial, with numerous commentaries,17,18 meta-analyses,9,19,20 and letters21,22,23 offering differing interpretations of the evidence. Interest in this question has recently intensified following a large observational study that suggested lower mortality with cefepime than with piperacillin-tazobactam.24 Given ongoing uncertainty and the widespread use of cefepime in serious infections, we conducted a systematic review and bayesian meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) comparing cefepime with other β-lactams for all-cause mortality across all indications. This study incorporates published and unpublished trials and applies a bayesian framework to estimate the probability of harm, thereby offering a clinically interpretable assessment of cefepime’s potential association with mortality.

Methods

This systematic review and bayesian meta-analysis was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023455194). Institutional review board approval and participant informed consent were not needed for this study because it did not constitute human participants research. After registration, but prior to analysis, 2 protocol modifications were made. First, an evidence-based prior distribution was specified for the between-study heterogeneity parameter (τ), consistent with methodological recommendations for bayesian meta-analysis.25 Second, the prespecified secondary outcomes of clinical success and microbiological failure were not meta-analyzed because reporting was inconsistent across studies, and outcome definitions varied substantially among trials.

Eligibility Criteria and Outcomes of Interest

We included RCTs conducted among children and adults comparing either (1) cefepime monotherapy vs any β-lactam monotherapy or (2) cefepime plus a second drug vs β-lactam plus the same second drug. Studies evaluating cefepime as prophylaxis or cefepime and β-lactamase inhibitor combinations were excluded. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality defined as 30-day mortality or the reported mortality end point closest to 30 days. Because mortality time points varied across trials, all-cause mortality was selected as the most consistently reported and comparable outcome.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A search strategy was developed by a research librarian (G.G.) and outlined in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. Medline was used to develop the initial search and subsequently adapted to other databases. The following databases were searched from inception to May 25, 2026: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Web of Science Core Collection, World Health Organization–International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, ClinicalTrials.gov, and LILACS. The reference lists of previous meta-analyses were screened for relevant studies. No restriction by language or geography was made. In addition, unpublished industry-sponsored trials identified through the FDA were considered, in which 25 of 41 FDA-provided studies16,26 met inclusion criteria (eTable 2 in Supplement 2). Sixteen of the FDA-provided trials did not meet our eligibility criteria because they used non–β-lactam comparators or included concomitant antibiotics that were not balanced between treatment arms.

Selection and Data Collection Process

Using Covidence, 2 reviewers (including Z.N.S., Y.J.Z., A.A., M.P.C., A. Lieu, J.S., A. Lawandi, and T.C.L.) screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility. Data extraction was performed independently by 2 reviewers (including Z.N.S., Y.J.Z., A.A., and B.A.A.) using a piloted extraction form. All disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (Z.N.S. or T.C.L.). Extracted data included study characteristics, patient population, cefepime and comparator regimens, concomitant therapies, demographic (age and sex) and clinical (disease, pathogen, and fever) characteristics, and mortality outcomes. Data on clinical, microbiological, and adverse event outcomes were also collected, with adverse event analyses to be reported separately. Data on race and ethnicity were not collected as these were not consistently reported in studies.

Risk of Bias and Certainty of Evidence

Risk of bias was assessed independently by 2 reviewers (including Z.N.S., Y.J.Z., A.A., and B.A.A.) using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool, version 2.0,27 with disagreements resolved by consensus or third-reviewer (Z.N.S. or T.C.L.) adjudication. Trials were categorized as low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias according to standard guidance.28 The overall assessment of bias was classified as (1) low if all domains were judged to be low risk of bias; (2) some concerns if at least 1 domain was judged to have some concerns, but no domain was judged to be at high risk of bias; and (3) high risk of bias if at least 1 domain for this result or if a study was judged to have some concerns for multiple domains in a way that substantially lowered confidence in the result. Risk-of-bias assessment could not be completed for studies published in languages other than English or French, as no reviewers were fluent in the publication language. It could also not be performed for unpublished studies. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation framework.

Statistical Analysis

Synthesis Methods

Odds of mortality were synthesized using a hierarchical bayesian random-effects model on the log-odds scale and results exponentiated on the odds ratio (OR) scale with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). We chose a vaguely informative prior normal distribution with a mean (SD) of 0 (2.7) for the treatment effect. Weakly informative priors were selected to regularize estimation, particularly for the between-study heterogeneity parameter, while allowing the observed trial data to remain the primary driver of the posterior estimates. To model between-study heterogeneity (τ), we applied an evidence-based prior specific to binary outcomes in which both comparator arms were pharmacologic treatments and the outcome was all-cause mortality.

We estimated a pooled OR in 3 analytic sets: (1) all eligible RCTs, including published, unpublished, and FDA-provided trials; (2) peer-reviewed published RCTs; and (3) trials without a high risk of bias. For each analysis, we report the pooled OR, 95% CrI, and posterior probability that the OR exceeded 1. The posterior probability should be interpreted alongside the 95% CrI; the CrI reflects the range of plausible estimates, whereas the posterior probability reflects the proportion of the posterior distribution suggestive of higher mortality with cefepime.

Subgroup Analyses

The following a priori subgroup analyses were performed: (1) adults vs children; (2) cefepime dose (high, ≥2 g every 8 hours in adults and ≥50 mg/kg every 8 hours in pediatrics; intermediate: ≥2 g every 12 hours in adults and ≥50 mg/kg every 12 hours in pediatrics; low: <2 g every 12 hours in adults and <50 mg/kg every 12 hours in pediatrics); (3) by comparator β-lactam; and (4) by clinical indication. An array of infections were grouped as mixed infections, including gynecologic infections, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, and skin and soft tissue infections. Posterior probabilities that OR exceeded 1 are reported for each subgroup.29 Subgroups were modeled using a meta-regression approach.

Number Needed to Harm

We estimated the number needed to harm using crude event rates to establish the absolute risk increase, calculated as experimental event rate minus control event rate. Number needed to harm was calculated as the inverse of the absolute risk increase.

Publication Bias

Funnel plots were visually inspected for asymmetry, and Egger regression testing was performed to assess small-study effects among published studies. Because these approaches have limitations in heterogeneous meta-analyses, we also compared pooled estimates from published trials alone with those obtained after inclusion of unpublished trials. Differences between published and unpublished sources were interpreted as potential evidence of bias.

We contacted authors of the primary trial reports to provide missing information.30,31,32,33 Trials with missing data were excluded for the corresponding outcome of interest. All analyses were conducted using the brms package34,35 in R, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Certainty of Evidence

The overall certainty of evidence was judged to be moderate, downgraded per Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation guidance118 due to heterogeneity across studies and possible publication bias, particularly given that unpublished FDA-provided trials showed estimates in the opposite direction from the published literature. Mortality estimates and probability of harm with cefepime use did not vary by risk of bias (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). There were no major concerns regarding indirectness or imprecision. Discussion

This systematic review and bayesian meta-analysis of 110 trials found a 94.4% posterior probability that cefepime was associated with increased odds of death compared with other β-lactams. Detailed data were available only from published peer-reviewed trials, which showed a 98.6% posterior probability of harm with cefepime use and an OR of 1.17 (95% CrI, 1.02-1.34). In subgroup analyses, this signal was observed in adults but not children and was most pronounced in trials of febrile neutropenia and severe bacterial infections. Higher cefepime doses were also associated with a greater posterior probability that the OR exceeded 1.

The absence of patient-level data limits definitive interpretation of the observed mortality signal. These findings may be driven by a combination of dose-related toxic effects and challenges with pharmacologic exposure in the context of higher minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). Higher cefepime doses have repeatedly been described to be associated with neurotoxic effects in older adults, patients with kidney dysfunction, and critically ill patients.119 In turn, cefepime-associated neurotoxic effects increased risk of in-hospital mortality in retrospective cohorts, although whether neurotoxic effects themselves are causal or a marker of kidney dysfunction and excessive drug exposure remains uncertain.120,121,122 Similarly, our data show that higher doses had a higher posterior probability of being associated with increased mortality.

With respect to pharmacologic exposure, some studies suggested that outcomes among patients with gram-negative bacteremia treated with cefepime vary by MIC, even when isolates are categorized as susceptible.8,123 Among patients with P aeruginosa bacteremia treated with cefepime, mortality was higher when the cefepime MIC was 8 µg/mL compared with 4 µg/mL or less.10,123 For these isolates, standard dosing may not reliably achieve target exposure unless extended infusion or higher dose strategies are used, which may correspondingly increase risk of toxic effects.124,125 Clinical data are mixed for the Enterobacterales. Cefepime susceptible dose–dependent Enterobacter cloacae bacteremia has been associated with higher mortality in some cohorts,126 whereas more contemporary observational studies using high-dose cefepime for AmpC-risk Enterobacterales have not shown clear excess mortality.3,127 In addition, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute revised cefepime breakpoints for Enterobacteriaceae in 2014 as follows: isolates with MICs of 4 to 8 µg/mL, previously categorized as susceptible, are now classified as susceptible dose dependent, and this would necessitate higher dose therapy.128 It is difficult to determine whether our observed association with mortality reflects an inherent safety concern with cefepime or an issue with pharmacologic exposure, which could potentially be mitigated with consideration of the organism’s MIC, optimized dosing, and therapeutic drug monitoring for exposure-related toxic effects. Such an approach should, in the context of the potential for increased mortality reported in this analysis, be the subject of rigorous prospective validation.

Inclusion of unpublished studies, most of which were industry-sponsored trials provided to the FDA during its investigation, attenuated the posterior probability that the OR exceeded 1 from 98% to 94%. These trials yielded estimates in the opposite direction from the published literature, consistent with the earlier FDA analysis.16 This discrepancy may reflect selective publication, whereby trials with estimates in the harmful direction may have been more likely to be published or there were systematic differences in the FDA-provided trials with respect to enrolled populations, illness severity, cefepime dosing, comparator selection, concomitant antibiotics, or outcome assessment. The latter is supported by a bayesian probability greater than 98% that these trials were sampled from a different distribution than the published literature. Because these trials were not peer reviewed and had limited methodological detail, we could not determine which factors explain the discrepancy. Greater transparency regarding the FDA-provided trials would help determine whether there are populations in whom cefepime is not associated with excess mortality. A prior bayesian reappraisal of the FDA analysis similarly emphasized that frequentist nonsignificance did not exclude a clinically relevant mortality signal21; our analysis extends this work by incorporating a larger evidence base while applying prespecified eligibility criteria. We show that even with inclusion of the relevant unpublished studies, the probability that the OR exceeded 1 remains high.

Strengths and Limitations

Key strengths of our analysis are the use of a bayesian model that allowed us to estimate the probability of harm more intuitively for clinical interpretation and to account for between-study heterogeneity introduced by various clinical indications, age groups, comparators, and cefepime dosing strategies. Additionally, we included conference abstracts and unpublished trials to minimize risk of publication bias and included only RCTs to strengthen the interpretability of our data. Discussion surrounding the safety of cefepime was recently revived with the publication of an observational cohort of patients administered cefepime during a 15-month shortage of piperacillin-tazobactam.24 Using instrumental variable analysis, this study suggested that cefepime was associated with reduced mortality compared with piperacillin-tazobactam. While observational studies better reflect clinical practice, such as empiric combination therapy, deescalation, and clinician response to illness severity, they remain vulnerable to substantial biases. Subsequent methodological critiques of that observational study have indicated that the reported mortality difference may have been influenced by collider bias from adjustment for metronidazole and by exposure classification that grouped clinically distinct antibiotic regimens.129 The presence of such biases underscores the critical importance of randomized data to answer this question.

This meta-analysis also had some limitations. First, many trials were open label, allowing clinicians to modify doses, durations, or concomitant therapies after randomization; however, the pooled estimate remained in the direction of higher mortality with cefepime after excluding high risk of bias and trials in which there were inadequate details to assess the risk of bias. Second, limited transparency of unpublished trials reduced our ability to assess their methods and explain why their estimates differed from the published literature. Finally, all-cause mortality is an imperfect safety outcome, particularly in patients with cancer, in whom death may reflect underlying disease progression. However, it was the most consistently reported outcome, and more specific outcomes were inconsistently defined or reported. Furthermore, in RCTs, noninfectious causes of death should also be balanced between groups on average. Therefore, the mortality estimate should be interpreted as a global safety signal rather than evidence of a specific causal mechanism.

Conclusions

This systematic review and bayesian meta-analysis found that cefepime was associated with a higher probability of death compared with other β-lactams. While this association was attenuated with inclusion of unpublished trials, the posterior probability of harm remained greater than 94%. This analysis does not imply that cefepime should no longer be used; rather, this analysis should prompt a cautious and nuanced interpretation of cefepime’s role in a given patient’s therapy, stimulate prospective investigation into optimized dosing for both efficacy and safety, and be considered in the creation of future guidance statements.
