Abstract
. We study Hamamoto’s expanding square argument towards a 1-D minimisation problem related to the sharp solenoidal uncertainty principle. Working in the right function space, we recast the involved interpolation type inequality into an exact equality, where the vanishing of the remainder term characterises the extremisers via the confluent hypergeometric functions. In the process we also remove some unnecessary constraints on the prescribed parameters.

1.
Introduction

In
[
Maz18
]
, V. Maz’ya proposed an
Open Problem
for the sharp dimensional constant in the following Heisenberg-Pauli-Weyl uncertainty principle inequality

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\mathbf{u}|^{2}|x|^{2}\,dx\geq C_{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx\right)^{2}

for solenoidal (namely, divergence-free) fields
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
It turns out

C_{N}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}(N+2)^{2},\quad&N\in\{1,2\},\\
\frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{(N-2)^{2}+8}+2\right)^{2},\quad&N\geq 3,\end{cases}

see Hamamoto
[
Ham23
]
for
N\geq 3
and Cazacu-Flynn-Lam
[
CFL22
]
for
N\in\{1,2\}
.
In particular, by poloidal-toroidal and spherical-harmonics decompositions, Hamamoto reduced the question to the following elegant 1-D minimisation problem:

(1)

\inf\limits_{f\not\equiv 0}\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-\varepsilon f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx\right)}{\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx\right)^{2}}.

Here
\varepsilon
and
\mu
are reasonable constants coming from dimensional reduction.

Let us now introduce the natural weighted Sobolev space in which we shall solve the minimisation problem (
1
)
(clearly, one should also assume
f^{\prime}\not\equiv 0
).
For a twice differentiable real-valued function
f
on the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,+\infty)
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, set

\|f\|:=\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(x^{\mu+1}+x^{\mu-1})(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu-1}f^{2}\right)dx\right)^{1/2}.

Let
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be the Hilbert space completion with respect to
\|\cdot\|
of

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):\,\|f\|<+\infty\}.

By working in
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we see immediately that (
1
) is
de facto
an interpolation problem

.

\|f\|^{\prime}:=\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(x^{\mu+1}+x^{\mu})(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu-1}f^{2}\right)dx\right)^{1/2},

see
[

.
However, a careful analysis shows that his expanding square argument

We need the following properties of the space
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Lemma 1
.

For every
f\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, it holds that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\{0,+\infty\}}f^{\prime}(x)x^{\frac{\mu+1}{2}}=\lim_{x\rightarrow\{0,+\infty\}}f^{\prime}(x)x^{\frac{\mu}{2}}=\lim_{x\rightarrow\{0,+\infty\}}f(x)x^{\frac{\mu}{2}}=0.

In particular, we also have

\lim_{x\rightarrow\{0,+\infty\}}f^{\prime}(x)f(x)x^{\mu}=0.

Our main results are stated as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\mu
and
\varepsilon
are two reals with
\varepsilon\leq\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}
. Let
b=\frac{\mu-\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}}{2}
.
Then

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(xf^{\prime\prime}+xf^{\prime}+\mu f^{\prime}+(\mu-b)f\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-\varepsilon f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle-\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}+1\right)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx+\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx

and

(3)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-\varepsilon f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}+1\right)^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx\right)^{2}

hold for all
f\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Note that (
3
) was already established in
[
Ham23
, Theorem 5.2]
by Laguerre polynomial expansion and then in
[
Ham24
, Theorem 1]
, though with a flaw as indicated above.
We shall prove (
3
) in the framework of equalities in which (
2
) is a special case.
These equalities allow us to characterise the extremisers in a clear-cut way.

Corollary 3
.

Suppose
\mu\neq 0
and
\varepsilon
are two reals with
\varepsilon\leq\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}
. Let
b=\frac{\mu-\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}}{2}
.
The equality in (
3
) holds if and only if for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
,

f(x)=Ce^{-\lambda x}\,{}_{1}F_{1}(b,\mu,\lambda x)

in the case
\mu>0
, or

f(x)=C(\lambda x)^{1-\mu}e^{-\lambda x}\,{}_{1}F_{1}(b+1-\mu,2-\mu,\lambda x)

in the case
\mu<0
. Here,

{}_{1}F_{1}(b,\mu,x):=1+\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{b(b+1)\cdots(b+k-1)}{\mu(\mu+1)\cdots(\mu+k-1)}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}

denotes Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric function that solves Kummer’s ODE

xw^{\prime\prime}+(\mu-x)w^{\prime}-bw=0.

2.
Proofs of the main results

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We start with the tedious arguments for completeness. Set

G_{1}(x):=(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}x^{\mu+1},\quad G_{2}(x):=(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}x^{\mu},\quad G_{3}(x):=(f(x))^{2}x^{\mu}.

Simple reasoning shows that it suffices to check

(4)

\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}G_{1}(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}G_{3}(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}G_{2}(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}G_{3}(x)=0.

(i) By using the elementary inequality
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left|\frac{dG_{1}(x)}{dx}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left|(\mu+1)x^{\mu}(f^{\prime})^{2}+2x^{\mu+1}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left|(\mu+1)x^{\mu}(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime})^{2}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left(\left|\frac{\mu+1}{2}\right|\left(x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu-1}(f^{\prime})^{2}\right)+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime})^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty.

This implies that

G_{1}(+\infty)=G_{1}(1)+\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{dG_{1}(x)}{dx}dx

is convergent and hence a nonnegative constant. If
G_{1}(+\infty)>0
, then

+\infty=\int_{1}^{+\infty}G_{1}(x)dx\leq\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty.

From this contradiction we deduce
G_{1}(+\infty)=0
.

(ii) To verify the second limit in (
4
), we can use similar arguments:

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left|\frac{dG_{3}(x)}{dx}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left|\mu x^{\mu-1}f^{2}+2x^{\mu}f^{\prime}f\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left(\left|\mu\right|x^{\mu-1}f^{2}+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu-1}f^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty

and hence that

G_{3}(+\infty)=G_{3}(1)+\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{dG_{3}(x)}{dx}dx

is convergent and hence a nonnegative constant. If
G_{3}(+\infty)>0
, then

+\infty=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{G_{3}(x)}{x}dx=\int_{1}^{+\infty}x^{\mu-1}f^{2}dx\leq\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty,

which is a contradiction. Therefore, we deduce
G_{3}(+\infty)=0
.

(iii) Now we verify the limits in (
4
) when
x\rightarrow 0
. Note as in (ii) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{dG_{3}(x)}{dx}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\mu x^{\mu-1}f^{2}+2x^{\mu}ff^{\prime}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\left|\mu\right|x^{\mu-1}f^{2}+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu-1}f^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty

and hence that

G_{3}(0)=G_{3}(1)-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dG_{3}(x)}{dx}dx

is a nonnegative constant. If
G_{3}(0)>0
, then as in (ii) we have

+\infty=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{G_{3}(x)}{x}dx=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\mu-1}f^{2}dx\leq\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty,

which is a contradiction. Thus we deduce
G_{3}(0)=0
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{dG_{2}(x)}{dx}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\mu x^{\mu-1}(f^{\prime})^{2}+2x^{\mu}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\left|\mu\right|x^{\mu-1}(f^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu+1}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}+x^{\mu-1}(f^{\prime})^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty

and hence that

G_{2}(0)=G_{2}(1)-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dG_{2}(x)}{dx}dx

is a nonnegative constant. If
G_{2}(0)>0
, then

+\infty=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{G_{2}(x)}{x}dx=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\mu-1}(f^{\prime})^{2}dx\leq\left\|f\right\|^{2}<+\infty,

which is a contradiction. Thus we deduce
G_{2}(0)=0
.

Collecting all the justifications above then proves the lemma.
∎

Now, using the expanding square argument, we prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

For every
f\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, and any
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}

\displaystyle\big(xf^{\prime\prime}+\alpha xf^{\prime}+\beta f^{\prime}+\gamma f\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(f^{\prime\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(xf^{\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(f^{\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle+\gamma^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx+2\alpha\beta\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(f^{\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle+2\alpha\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}x^{\mu+1}dx+2\beta\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}x^{\mu}dx+2\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}fx^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle+2\alpha\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu}dx+2\beta\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu-1}dx.

Using Lemma
1

and integration by parts for the first four cross-terms, we get
3
3

3

Here, the involved “integration by parts” and “differentiation” are valid even if
\mu\in\{-1,0\}
.

\displaystyle 2\alpha\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}x^{\mu+1}dx

\displaystyle=\alpha\left[(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}\right]_{0}^{+\infty}-\alpha(\mu+1)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle=-\alpha(\mu+1)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx,

\displaystyle 2\beta\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}x^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle=\beta\left[(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}\right]_{0}^{+\infty}-\beta\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=-\beta\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx,

\displaystyle 2\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}fx^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle=2\gamma\left[f^{\prime}fx^{\mu}\right]_{0}^{+\infty}-2\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx-2\gamma\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=-2\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx-2\gamma\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu-1}dx,

and

\displaystyle 2\alpha\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle=\alpha\gamma\left[f^{2}x^{\mu}\right]_{0}^{+\infty}-\alpha\gamma\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=-\alpha\gamma\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx.

Then (
5
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}

\displaystyle\big(xf^{\prime\prime}+\alpha xf^{\prime}+\beta f^{\prime}+\gamma f\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(f^{\prime\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(xf^{\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(f^{\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx+2\alpha\beta\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(f^{\prime}\big)^{2}x^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle\qquad-\alpha(\mu+1)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx-\beta\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx-2\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx

\displaystyle\qquad-2\gamma\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu-1}dx-\alpha\gamma\mu\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx+2\beta\gamma\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}fx^{\mu-1}dx.

To cancel out the only remaining cross-term, we choose
\beta=\mu
.
Furthermore, we shall also specify the choice
\gamma=(\mu-b)\alpha
, where
b=\frac{\mu-\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}}{2}
.
This leads to

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(xf^{\prime\prime}+\alpha xf^{\prime}+\beta f^{\prime}+\gamma f\big)^{2}x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-b(\mu-b)f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle-\alpha(\mu+1-2b)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx+\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-\varepsilon f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx

\displaystyle-\alpha\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}+1\right)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx+\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx=:g(\alpha).

From the non-negativity of the above quadratic function
g:\alpha\mapsto g(\alpha)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle 4\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-\varepsilon f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}x^{\mu+1}dx\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}+1\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx.

This together with the equality in (
6
) for
\alpha=1
proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 4
.

Note that (
6
) for
b=\frac{\mu+\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}}{2}
leads to a weaker version of (
3
).

Proof of Corollary
3
.

The equality in (
3
) is attained if and only if the quadratic function
g
in (
6
) has only one real root, denoted by
\lambda
, namely,

\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon}+1}{2}\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f^{\prime})^{2}x^{\mu}dx}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(x^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}-\varepsilon f^{2}\right)x^{\mu-1}dx}.

Note that by the weighted Hardy inequality,
\lambda\geq 0
, and we can assume
\lambda>0
otherwise
f
is constant, which is not in
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and is excluded from the optimisation problem.
Suppose
f\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfies the equality in (
3
), then we see from (
6
) that

(7)

xf^{\prime\prime}+\lambda xf^{\prime}+\mu f^{\prime}+(\mu-b)\lambda f=0.

Now, to solve (
7
) we introduce

w_{\lambda}(x):=w(\lambda x):=e^{\lambda x}f(x).

Then the ODE (
7
) is transformed into

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=x(e^{-\lambda x}w_{\lambda}(x))^{\prime\prime}+(\lambda x+\mu)(e^{-\lambda x}w_{\lambda}(x))^{\prime}+(\mu-b)\lambda e^{-\lambda x}w_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=x(\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda x}w(\lambda x)-2\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda x}w^{\prime}(\lambda x)+\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda x}w^{\prime\prime}(\lambda x))

\displaystyle\quad+(\lambda x+\mu)(-\lambda e^{-\lambda x}w(\lambda x)+\lambda e^{-\lambda x}w^{\prime}(\lambda x))+(\mu-b)\lambda e^{-\lambda x}w(\lambda x)

\displaystyle=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\left(\lambda xw^{\prime\prime}(\lambda x)+(\mu-\lambda x)w^{\prime}(\lambda x)-bw(\lambda x)\right).

Letting
t=\lambda x
, we get Kummer’s (second order) ODE

tw^{\prime\prime}(t)+(\mu-t)w^{\prime}(t)-bw(t)=0,

which is known to have two independent solutions (see for example Viola
[
Vio16
]
):

\varphi(t)={}_{1}F_{1}(b,\mu,t)\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(t)=t^{1-\mu}{}_{1}F_{1}(b+1-\mu,2-\mu,t).

Recall that
{}_{1}F_{1}(b,\mu,z)
is entire in
b
and
z
, and meromorphic in
\mu
with poles
\mu=0,-1,-2,\cdots
.
Noticing that when
\mu>0
, the function
e^{-t}\varphi:t\mapsto e^{-t}\varphi(t)
belongs to
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. However,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(e^{-t}\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)\right)^{2}t^{\mu+1}dt=+\infty,

since
(\psi^{\prime\prime}(t))^{2}=O(t^{-2-2\mu})
as
t\rightarrow 0
.
In the case
\mu<0
,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(e^{-t}\varphi(t)\right)^{2}t^{\mu-1}dt=+\infty,

thus
e^{-t}\varphi\notin\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Noticing that

(\psi^{\prime\prime}(t))^{2}t^{\mu+1}=O(t^{-1-\mu}),\quad(\psi^{\prime}(t))^{2}t^{\mu-1}=O(t^{-1-\mu}),\quad(\psi(t))^{2}t^{\mu-1}=O(t^{1-\mu})

as
t\rightarrow 0
, we can deduce that
when
\mu<0
,
e^{-t}\psi\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Tracking the above discussions back to
x
variable, we proved the corollary.
∎
