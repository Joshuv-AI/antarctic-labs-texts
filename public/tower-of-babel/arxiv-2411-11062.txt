Abstract
. In the theory of dynamic programming, an optimal policy is a policy whose lifetime value dominates that of all other policies from every possible initial condition in the state space. This raises a natural question: when does optimality from a single state imply optimality from every state? Working in a general setting, we provide sufficient conditions for this property that relate to reachability and irreducibility. Our results have significant implications for modern policy-based algorithms used to solve large-scale dynamic programs. We illustrate our findings by applying them to an optimal savings problem via an algorithm that implements gradient ascent in a policy space constructed from neural networks.



1.
Introduction

Dynamic programming (DP) is a major branch of optimization theory with a vast
range of applications. Within economics alone, applications extend from monetary
and fiscal policy to asset pricing, unemployment, firm investment, wealth
dynamics, commodity pricing, sovereign default, the division of labor, natural
resource extraction, human capital accumulation, retirement decisions, portfolio
choice, and dynamic pricing. Dynamic programs that include uncertainty are
often called Markov decision processes (MDPs) and the theory of such processes
has been extensively developed (see, e.g.,
Bäuerle and Rieder (2011)
,

Hernández-Lerma and Lasserre (2012)
,
Bertsekas (2012)
, or

Bertsekas (2022)
).

Much of the recent surge in interest in MDPs has been fueled by artificial
intelligence and reinforcement learning (see, e.g.,
Bertsekas (2021)

or
Kochenderfer et al. (2022)
). In recent years, researchers solving
large-scale MDPs have moved away from value-based methods and towards
policy-based methods, such as policy gradient ascent (see, e.g.,
Sutton et al. (1999)
,

Lan et al. (2023)
,
Kumar et al. (2023)
,
Murphy (2024)
).
These policy-based methods seek to maximize a real-valued objective, such as

(1)

m(\sigma)\coloneq\int v_{\sigma}(x)\rho(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)\qquad(\sigma\in\Sigma).

Here
\sigma
is a policy for a given MDP, mapping some state space
\mathsf{X}
into
a specified action space,
v_{\sigma}(x)
represents the lifetime value of
following the fixed policy
\sigma
, conditional on initial state
x
, and

\rho
is a given “initial distribution.” In practice, each
\sigma
is
typically represented by a neural network. Policy-based methods often handle
large problems with continuous state and action spaces more efficiently than
traditional value-based methods such as value function iteration (VFI). The
framework has led to numerous successful algorithms for solving complex
decision-making problems, including Trust Region Policy
Optimization
(
Schulman et al., 2015
)
, Asynchronous Advantage
Actor-Critic
(
Mnih et al., 2016
)
, and Proximal Policy
Optimization
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
.

Despite these successes, there is one significant disadvantage of policy
gradient methods: unlike traditional DP algorithms, these methods are not
guaranteed to converge to an optimal policy, even in relatively straightforward
settings. To understand the issues at hand, recall
that an
optimal policy
is a
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that

v_{\sigma}(x)=\max_{s\in\Sigma}v_{s}(x)
for every
x\in\mathsf{X}
; that is, a
policy
\sigma
such that following this policy in every period leads to maximum
lifetime value from every initial state
x
. Even if one attains a global
maximum in (
1
), with maximization over all
\sigma\in\Sigma
,
there is no guarantee that the resulting policy will be an optimal policy.
Indeed, the result depends in general on the initial distribution
\rho
, which is not
one of the primitives of the DP problem.

Regarding this issue, the prevailing view is that relative optimality depends on
the initial distribution
\rho
having large support. For example, in a major
study of policy gradient methods,
Bhandari and Russo (2024)
state that “Policy
gradient methods have poor convergence properties if applied without an
exploratory initial distribution”
(
Bhandari and Russo, 2024
, p. 1910)
.

One disadvantage of choosing
\rho
with large support is that computing the
integral (
1
) becomes computationally expensive, since the function

v_{\sigma}(x)
is expensive to evaluate. Another is that, in general, this
integral cannot be computed exactly, and, moreover, the implications of a
relatively poor approximation to the integral for optimality theory are not
well-known. A third issue is that existing conditions for “sufficiently large
supports” are difficult to test in practice, making it hard to understand
whether or not the relevant theory applies. (We return to this last point
below.)

These observations motivate the following question: Does there exist a large and
important class of models for which it is permissible to choose
\rho
with

small
support, while still maintaining the property that maximization of this
criterion over all
\sigma\in\Sigma
yields a (globally) optimal policy?
At the extreme, when can
\rho
be concentrated at a single point
x
, so that
the maximization criterion
m(\sigma)
from (
1
) is just

v_{\sigma}(x)
? In other words, when does optimality at a single state imply
optimality at every state?

We show that, for standard MDPs on general state spaces, strong irreducibility of the
Markov dynamics generated by
\sigma
is sufficient for this property.
Specifically, if a policy is optimal at a single state and has a strongly irreducible
transition kernel, then this optimality propagates throughout the entire state
space, making the policy globally optimal. (Here “strong irreducibility” refers to
irreducibility of positive operators under the standard Riesz space definition.)
In addition, we show that weaker forms of irreducibility are also sufficient for this
result when
\sigma
is also continuous.

Since gradient policy methods are commonly applied to problems with continuous
state and action spaces, we avoid discreteness restrictions. In particular, for
the MDPs that we consider, the state and action spaces can be arbitrary metric
spaces. We also avoid placing restrictions on the class of MDPs under
consideration, adopting only mild regularity conditions that imply existence of solutions.
Focusing on problems where solutions exist is natural for this line of research, since
we wish to examine conditions under which local optima imply global optima.

Papers examining theoretical properties of policy gradient methods have some
connection to our work. For example,
Bhandari and Russo (2024)
examine when
policy gradient ascent (actually decent) yields a globally optimal policy. To
step from local to global optimality, they restrict the classes of MDPs under
consideration and require that the initial distribution
\rho
dominates the
discounted state occupancy measure under the optimal policy (i.e, the measure is
absolutely continuous with respect to
\rho
). This condition is difficult to
verify in practice, since the optimal policy is
a priori
unknown (and, in
fact, the object of the whole computational procedure). Here we avoid both
large support restrictions on
\rho
and explicit restrictions on the optimal
policy. (At the same time,
Bhandari and Russo (2024)
supply many valuable new
results concerning the policy gradient algorithm, and we make no direct
contribution to this analysis.)

Other papers that examine theoretical properties of gradient policy methods
include
Khodadadian et al. (2021)
,
Agarwal et al. (2021)
, and

Xiao (2022)
. However, in these papers, the focus is on proving
the convergence of
\int v_{\sigma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho
to its maximal value, rather than
obtaining global convergence from local convergence (as we do here). At the
same time, these papers provide valuable rates of convergence for specific
algorithms, which we do not discuss. A related line of research focuses on
average-optimal policies in finite-state MDPs by leveraging specific state space
structures under some policies. This includes exploring unichain, multichain,
communicating, and weakly communicating MDPs to study algorithmic convergence

(
Bartlett and Tewari, 2009
;
Puterman, 2014
)
. Our results focus on the
continuous state setting, where gradient policy methods are usually applied.

In an application connected to the income fluctuation problem, we examine policy
gradient methods under different irreducibility settings using neural network
representations of policy functions. In this we follow a recent trend in
economics towards solving large scale dynamic problems using neural networks to
represent equilibrium objects (see, e.g.,
Maliar et al. (2021)
or

Friedl et al. (2023)
). However, these papers do not examine the link between
local and global optimality. In fact they do not use the class of policy gradient methods
described above, focusing instead on minimizing Euler or Bellman residuals.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
provides background on
MDPs. Section
3
states our main results in a general setting.
Section
4
examines how the irreducibility condition from
Section
3
can be weakened while still obtaining some transmission of
optimality across states. Section
5.1
illustrates our theoretical results
in the context of a benchmark optimal savings problem. Section
6

outlines avenues for future work.

2.
Markov Decision Processes

In this section, we review essential properties of Markov decision processes and
state a technical lemma that will be applied in our main results.

2.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{A}
be metric spaces, let
\mathscr{B}
be the Borel subsets of

\mathsf{X}
, let
b\mathsf{X}
be the set of bounded Borel measurable functions from

\mathsf{X}
to
\mathbbm{R}
, and let
cb\mathsf{X}
be the continuous functions in
b\mathsf{X}
. Both

b\mathsf{X}
and
cb\mathsf{X}
are paired with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|
and the
pointwise partial order
\leqslant
. For example,
f\leqslant g
indicates that
f(x)\leqslant g(x)
for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
. A map
M
from
b\mathsf{X}
to itself is called

order preserving
if
f\leqslant g
implies
Mf\leqslant Mg
.
Absolute values are applied pointwise, so that

|f|
is the function
x\mapsto|f(x)|
.

A
transition kernel
on
\mathsf{X}
is a function
Q
from
\mathsf{X}\times\mathscr{B}
to

[0,1]
such that
x\mapsto Q(x,B)
is Borel measurable for all
B\in\mathscr{B}
and

B\mapsto Q(x,B)
is a Borel probability measure for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
. To any
such kernel
Q
we associate a bounded linear operator on
b\mathsf{X}
,
often referred to as its
Markov operator
and also denoted by
Q
, via

(2)

f\mapsto Qf,\qquad(Qf)(x)=\int f(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}).

Typically,
(Qf)(x)
represents the expectation of
f(X_{t+1})

given that
X_{t}=x
and
X_{t+1}
is drawn from
Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})
.

As usual, the positive cone of
b\mathsf{X}
, denoted here by
b\mathsf{X}_{+}
, is all

v\in b\mathsf{X}
with
v\geqslant 0
. Let
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
be the dual space of

b\mathsf{X}
and let
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}_{+}
to be the positive cone of
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
.
The set
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}_{+}
contains, among other objects,
the set
\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{X})
of Borel probability measures on

\mathsf{X}
. For simplicity, elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{X})
are referred to as

distributions
. For
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{X})
and
f\in b\mathsf{X}
we set

\left\langle{f,\rho}\right\rangle\coloneq\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho.

For each
x\in\mathsf{X}
, the
point evaluation functional
generated by
x

is the map
\delta_{x}
that sends
w\in b\mathsf{X}
into
w(x)\in\mathbbm{R}
. Below
it will be convenient for us to write this in dual notation, so that

\left\langle{w,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle=w(x)
for every
w\in b\mathsf{X}
. We will make use of the
following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Every point evaluation functional on
b\mathsf{X}
is a nonzero element of

b\mathsf{X}_{+}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathsf{X}
. Linearity of
\delta_{x}
is obvious: given
a,b\in\mathbbm{R}

and
v,w\in b\mathsf{X}
, we have

\left\langle{av+bw,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle=(av+bw)(x)=av(x)+bw(x)=a\left\langle{v,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle+b\left\langle{w,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle.

Regarding continuity, if
w_{n}\to w
in
b\mathsf{X}
, then
w_{n}\to w
pointwise
on
\mathsf{X}
, so
\left\langle{w_{n},\delta_{x}}\right\rangle=w_{n}(x)\to w(x)=\left\langle{w,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle
.
Regarding positivity, it suffices to show that
\left\langle{w,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle\geqslant 0

whenever
w\geqslant 0
. This clearly holds, since
w\geqslant 0
implies

w(x)=\left\langle{w,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle\geqslant 0
. Finally,
\delta_{x}
is not the
zero element of
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
because

w=\mathbf{1}
is in
b\mathsf{X}
and
\left\langle{w,\delta_{x}}\right\rangle=w(x)=1\not=0
.
∎

2.2.
Markov Decision Process

Let
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{A}
be metric spaces, as in Section
2.1
.
A
Markov decision process
(MDP) with state space
\mathsf{X}
and action space

\mathsf{A}
is a tuple
(r,\Gamma,\beta,P)
, where
r
is a reward function,

x\mapsto\Gamma(x)\subset\mathsf{A}
is a feasible correspondence,
\beta\in\mathbbm{R}
is a
discount factor and
P(x,a,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})
is a distribution over next
period states given current state
x
and action
a
. Let
\mathsf{G}\coloneq\{(x,a)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{A}:a\in\Gamma(x)\}
. We consider a relatively
standard environment, as considered in, say,
Bäuerle and Rieder (2011)
and

Hernández-Lerma and Lasserre (2012)
, where

(a)

\beta\in(0,1)
,

(b)

\Gamma
is nonempty, continuous, and
compact-valued on
\mathsf{X}
,

(c)

r
is bounded and continuous on
\mathsf{G}
, and

(d)

the map
(x,a)\mapsto\int v(x^{\prime})P(x,a,dx^{\prime})
is continuous on
\mathsf{G}
whenever
v\in cb\mathsf{X}
.

(The case of unbounded
r
is discussed in Section
6
.)

Let
\Sigma
denote the set of feasible policies, by which we mean all Borel
measurable functions
\sigma
mapping
\mathsf{X}
to
\mathsf{A}
with
\sigma(x)\in\Gamma(x)
for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
. For each
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
x\in\mathsf{X}
,
we set

r_{\sigma}(x)\coloneq r(x,\sigma(x))\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\sigma}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\coloneq P(x,\sigma(x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}).

Thus,
r_{\sigma}(x)
is rewards at
x
under policy
\sigma
and
P_{\sigma}
is
the transition kernel on
\mathsf{X}
generated by
\sigma
. Using the corresponding
Markov operator
P_{\sigma}
, as defined in (
2
), the
lifetime
value
of a policy
\sigma
, denoted by
v_{\sigma}
, can be expressed as

(3)

v_{\sigma}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(\beta P_{\sigma})^{t}r_{\sigma}

(see, e.g.,
Puterman (2014)
, Theorem 6.1.1).
We will use the fact that
v_{\sigma}
is also the unique fixed point
in
b\mathsf{X}
of the
policy operator

T_{\sigma}
defined by
T_{\sigma}\,v=r_{\sigma}+\beta P_{\sigma}\,v
. This operator can be written more explicitly as

(T_{\sigma}\,v)(x)=r(x,\sigma(x))+\beta\int v(x^{\prime})P(x,\sigma(x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\qquad(v\in b\mathsf{X},\;x\in\mathsf{X}).

(The lifetime value
v_{\sigma}
is the unique fixed point of
T_{\sigma}
because
the spectral radius of the linear operator
\beta P_{\sigma}
is
\beta
, so, by the
geometric series lemma,
\beta<1
implies that
v=r_{\sigma}+\beta P_{\sigma}\,v
has the unique solution given by the
right-hand side of (
3
).) Iterating on the definition
T_{\sigma}\,v=r_{\sigma}+\beta P_{\sigma}\,v
, we
find that

(4)

T_{\sigma}^{n}v=r_{\sigma}+\beta P_{\sigma}r_{\sigma}+\cdots+(\beta P_{\sigma})^{n-1}r_{\sigma}+(\beta P_{\sigma})^{n}v\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbbm{N}$}.

This expression will be useful in the theory below.

The
value function
is denoted
v^{*}
and defined at each
x\in\mathsf{X}
by

v^{*}(x)\coloneq\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}v_{\sigma}(x)
.
A policy
\sigma
is called
optimal
if
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)

for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
.

We define the Bellman operator by

(5)

(Tv)(x)=\max_{a\in\Gamma(x)}\left\{r(x,a)+\beta\int v(x^{\prime})P(x,a,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\right\}\qquad(v\in b\mathsf{X},\;x\in\mathsf{X}).

We will use the following facts:

Proposition 2.2
.

Under the stated assumptions,

(a)

the value function
v^{*}
is the unique fixed point of the
Bellman operator in
b\mathsf{X}
,

(b)

the value function
v^{*}
is well-defined and contained in

cb\mathsf{X}
, and

(c)

at least one optimal policy exists.

Proof.

See
Bäuerle and Rieder (2011)
or
Hernández-Lerma and Lasserre (2012)
.
∎

We also make use of the following technical lemma, which shows one implication
of local optimality at some given
x\in\mathsf{X}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
, then

(6)

\sum_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}\int(v^{*}(x^{\prime})-v_{\sigma}(x^{\prime}))P_{\sigma}^{n}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=0.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbbm{N}
. Applying the expression for
T_{\sigma}^{n}v
from
(
4
) twice, first with
v=v_{\sigma}
and then with
v=v^{*}
, we
get

(7)

T_{\sigma}^{n}\,v_{\sigma}-T_{\sigma}^{n}\,v^{*}=\beta^{n}(P_{\sigma}^{n}\,v_{\sigma}-P_{\sigma}^{n}\,v^{*}).

In addition, we have

(8)

v_{\sigma}=T_{\sigma}^{n}\,v_{\sigma}\leqslant T_{\sigma}^{n}\,v^{*}\leqslant T^{n}\,v^{*}=v^{*}.

In (
8
), the first inequality is due to the fact
T_{\sigma}
is order preserving
and
v_{\sigma}\leqslant v^{*}
, while the second follows from the
fact that
T_{\sigma}\,v\leqslant Tv
for all
v\in b\mathsf{X}
. (Since
T_{\sigma}\,v\leqslant T\,v
for all
v
,
T_{\sigma}^{2}\,v^{*}\leqslant T_{\sigma}T\,v^{*}\leqslant T^{2}\,v^{*}

and so
T_{\sigma}^{2}\,v^{*}\leqslant T^{2}\,v^{*}.
By induction, the second
inequality holds.) The claim in Lemma
2.3
follows from (
7
) and
(
8
). To see this, fix
x\in\mathsf{X}
with
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
. From this equality and
(
8
) we get
(T_{\sigma}^{n}\,v_{\sigma})(x)=(T_{\sigma}^{n}\,v^{*})(x)
.
Since
\beta>0
, combining this result with (
7
) yields

(P_{\sigma}^{n}\,v_{\sigma})(x)=(P_{\sigma}^{n}\,v^{*})(x)
.
Hence (
6
) holds.
∎

3.
From Local to Global Optimality

The standard definition of optimality, which was given in Section
2.2
,
is global in nature, since it concerns the lifetime value of the policy at every

x\in\mathsf{X}
. We seek conditions under which local optimality implies global
optimality. In particular, we seek conditions under which
the following three statements are equivalent:

(E1)

there exists an
x\in\mathsf{X}
such that

v_{s}(x)\leqslant v_{\sigma}(x)
for all
s\in\Sigma
,

(E2)

there exists a
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{X})
such that

\left\langle{v_{s},\rho}\right\rangle\leqslant\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle
for all

s\in\Sigma
,

(E3)

\sigma
is an optimal policy.

3.1.
Preliminary Results

We first note that, in the MDP set up we have described,
(E1)
and
(E2)
are always
equivalent, as the next lemma shows.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
\sigma\in\Sigma
, the statements
(E1)

and
(E2)
are equivalent.

Proof.

To show
(E1)
implies
(E2)
, assume
(E1)

and fix
x\in\mathsf{X}
with
v_{\sigma}(x)\geqslant v_{s}(x)
for all
s\in\Sigma
.
Let
\delta_{x}
be the point evaluation functional generated by
x
.
Since
\delta_{x}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{X})
and

\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\delta_{x}}\right\rangle=v_{\sigma}(x)\geqslant v_{s}(x)=\left\langle{v_{s},\delta_{x}}\right\rangle

for all
s\in\Sigma
, so
(E2)
holds. To show

(E2)
implies
(E1)
, fix
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{X})
with

\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle\geqslant\left\langle{v_{s},\rho}\right\rangle
for all
s\in\Sigma
.
Since at least one
s\in\Sigma
is optimal (Proposition
2.2
),
this yields
\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle\geqslant\left\langle{v^{*},\rho}\right\rangle
.
Now suppose to the contrary that for each
x\in\mathsf{X},
we can find a
\tau\in\Sigma

such that
v_{\tau}(x)>v_{\sigma}(x)
. Since
v^{*}(x)\geqslant v_{s}(x)
for all

s\in\Sigma
and
x\in\mathsf{X}
, we have
v^{*}(x)>v_{\sigma}(x)
for all

x\in\mathsf{X}
. Hence
\left\langle{v^{*},\rho}\right\rangle>\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle
.
This contradiction proves
(E1)
.
∎

Our second preliminary observation suggests one way to obtain
(E3)
from
(E2)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle=\left\langle{v^{*},\rho}\right\rangle
, then
v_{\sigma}=v^{*}

holds
\rho
-almost everywhere.

Proof.

By definition,
v^{*}\geqslant v_{\sigma}
on
\mathsf{X}
. If, in addition,
v^{*}>v_{\sigma}
on a set
E
of positive
\rho
-measure, then

\int(v^{*}-v_{\sigma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\geqslant\int_{E}(v^{*}-v_{\sigma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho>0.

This contradicts
\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle=\left\langle{v^{*},\rho}\right\rangle
.
Hence
v_{\sigma}=v^{*}
holds
\rho
-almost everywhere.
∎

Loosely speaking, this lemma shows that maximizing the scalar performance
measure
\left\langle{v_{\sigma},\rho}\right\rangle
with a highly exploratory initial distribution

\rho
promotes global optimality.

The disadvantage of this approach is that evaluating the scalar measure from a
such an initial distribution is costly. For this reason, our main
interest is in providing conditions under which
(E1)
implies
(E3)
. Our
conditions relate to irreducibility. The first condition, discussed in
Section
3.2
, uses a notion of irreducibility from the literature on
Banach lattices (see, e.g.,
Zaanen (2012)
), while the second,
discussed in Section
4.3
, uses an irreducibility concept from the Markov
process literature.

3.2.
Strong Irreducibility

Recall that a linear subspace

I
of
b\mathsf{X}
is called an
ideal
in
b\mathsf{X}
when
f\in I
and
|g|\leqslant|f|
implies
g\in I
. An ideal
I
is said to be
invariant
for a
linear operator
K
if
KI\subset I
. A linear operator
K
from
b\mathsf{X}
to
itself is called
positive
when
Kf\geqslant 0
for all
f\geqslant 0
. A positive
linear operator
K
is called
irreducible
if the only invariant ideals
under
K
are the trivial subspace
\{0\}
and the whole space
b\mathsf{X}
.
By Proposition 8.3 (c) of
Schaefer (1974)
, such a
K
is irreducible
if and only if, for each nonzero
f\in b\mathsf{X}_{+}
and each nonzero
\mu\in b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}_{+}
, there exists an
m\in\mathbbm{N}
with
\left\langle{\mu,K^{m}f}\right\rangle>0
.

In what follows, we call a transition kernel
P
on
\mathsf{X}

strongly irreducible
if its Markov operator (see
(
2
)) is irreducible on
b\mathsf{X}
in the sense just defined.
Here is our main result for the strongly irreducible case.
In the statement,
\sigma
is any feasible policy.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
P_{\sigma}
is strongly irreducible,
then
(E1)
–
(E3)
are equivalent.

For example, Theorem
3.3
tells us that, under the stated conditions,
we can obtain an optimal policy by fixing an arbitrary initial state
x\in\mathsf{X}

and maximizing the real-valued function
s\mapsto v_{s}(x)
over
\Sigma
. Alternatively, we can fix any
distribution
\rho
and maximize
s\mapsto\left\langle{v_{s},\rho}\right\rangle
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

In view of Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show that

(E1)
and
(E3)
are equivalent. That
(E3)
implies

(E1)
is immediate from the definition of
optimal policies. Hence we need only show that
(E1)
implies
(E3)
.
In line with the conditions of Theorem
3.3
, we assume that

\sigma
is a feasible policy and
P_{\sigma}
is strongly irreducible.
Using
(E1)
, we take
x\in\mathsf{X}
with
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
.

Let
h\coloneq v^{*}-v_{\sigma}
. By the definition of
v^{*}
we have
0\leqslant h
.
We claim in addition that
h=0
. To see this, suppose to the contrary that
h
is
nonzero. In this case, by strong irreducibility,
for each nonzero
\mu
in the positive cone of
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
we can find an

m\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
\left\langle{\mu,P_{\sigma}^{m}\,h}\right\rangle>0
.
Because
\delta_{x}
is a nonzero element of
the positive cone of
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
(Lemma
2.1
), we can set
\mu=\delta_{x}
to obtain
an
m\in\mathbbm{N}
with
(P_{\sigma}^{m}\,h)(x)>0
. This contradicts
(
6
), so
h=0
holds. In other words,

v_{\sigma}=v^{*}
. This proves
(E3)
.
∎

4.
Topological Conditions

The discussion in Section
5.4
shows that strong irreducibility cannot
be dropped without either (a) weakening the conclusions of
Theorems
3.3
, or (b) adding some side conditions. In this section,
we investigate both scenarios. In particular, we show that

(a)

even when irreducibility fails, optimality can pass across
some

states under a continuity condition and a type of “local irreducibility,” and

(b)

when seeking the full global conclusions of Theorem
3.3
, we
can drop strong irreducibility if we assume a weaker form of
irreducibility and pair it with continuity.

The first topic is treated in Section
4.1
. The second is treated in
Sections
4.2
and
4.3
.

4.1.
Reachable States

To begin, we return to the general MDP setting from Section
2.2
, where
\mathsf{X}
and

\mathsf{A}
are arbitrary metric spaces. Letting
Q
be any stochastic kernel on

\mathsf{X}
, a point
y\in\mathsf{X}
is called
Q
-reachable
from
x\in\mathsf{X}

when, for each open neighborhood
G
of
y
, there exists an
n\in\mathbbm{N}
with

Q^{n}(x,G)>0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\sigma
be any continuous policy.
If
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
and
y
is
P_{\sigma}
-reachable from
x
, then
v_{\sigma}(y)=v^{*}(y)
.

Proof.

As a preliminary step, we show that
h\coloneq v^{*}-v_{\sigma}
is
continuous under the stated assumptions. Since
\sigma
is continuous, our
conditions on
(r,\Gamma,\beta,P)
imply that the mappings
x\mapsto\int v(x^{\prime})P(x,\sigma(x),dx^{\prime})
and
x\mapsto r(x,\sigma(x))
are continuous on
\mathsf{X}
whenever
v\in cb\mathsf{X}
. This implies
that
T_{\sigma}
is invariant on
cb\mathsf{X}
. Moreover,
cb\mathsf{X}
is a closed
subset of the complete metric space
b\mathsf{X}
under the supremum norm (since
uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous). In addition, given
v,w\in b\mathsf{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\|T_{\sigma}v-T_{\sigma}w\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\beta\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\int|v(x^{\prime})-w(x^{\prime})|P(x,\sigma(x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leqslant\beta\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\int\|v-w\|P(x,\sigma(x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=\beta\|v-w\|.

Since
\beta<1
, the contraction mapping theorem implies that
T_{\sigma}^{n}w\to v_{\sigma}
for every
w\in b\mathsf{X}
. If we now fix
w\in cb\mathsf{X}
and
use the fact that
T_{\sigma}
is invariant on this set, we obtain a sequence

(T^{n}w)_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
converging to
v_{\sigma}
and entirely contained in

cb\mathsf{X}
. As
cb\mathsf{X}
is closed in
b\mathsf{X}
, this implies that

v_{\sigma}
is in
cb\mathsf{X}
. In particular,
v_{\sigma}
is continuous. As
v^{*}

is also continuous (see Proposition
2.2
), we see that
h
is continuous.

Now fix
x\in\mathsf{X}
. Seeking a contradiction, we suppose that
y
is

P_{\sigma}
-reachable from
x
and yet
h
obeys

h(y)>0
. By this continuity and
h(y)>0
, there exists an open
neighborhood
G
of
y
with
h>0
on
G
. Because
y
is

P_{\sigma}
-reachable from
x
, there exists an
n\in\mathbbm{N}
with

P_{\sigma}^{n}(x,G)>0
. As a result, we have

\int(v^{*}(x^{\prime})-v_{\sigma}(x^{\prime}))P_{\sigma}^{n}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\geqslant\int_{G}h(x^{\prime})P_{\sigma}^{n}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})>0.

But
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
, so this inequality contradicts Lemma
2.3
.
The contradiction proves Lemma
4.1
.
∎

4.2.
Open Set Irreducibility

The results in Section
4.1
discussed forms of “local”
irreducibility and their implications. In this section, we analyze settings where
these local conditions extend across the whole space and policies are
continuous.

In general, a transition kernel
Q
from
\mathsf{X}
to itself is called
open
set irreducible
if every
y\in\mathsf{X}
is reachable from every
x\in\mathsf{X}
.
For continuous policies that generate open set irreducible transitions, we have
the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
P_{\sigma}
is open set irreducible and
\sigma
is continuous, then

(E1)
–
(E3)
are equivalent.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show
(E1)
implies
(E3)
.
So fix
x\in\mathsf{X}
and suppose that
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
.
For any
y\in\mathsf{X}
, open set irreducibility implies that
y
is
P_{\sigma}
-reachable from
x
.
Hence, by Lemma
4.1
, we have
v_{\sigma}(y)=v^{*}(y)
.
In particular,
(E3)
holds.
∎

4.3.
\pi
-Irreducibility

We treat one more form of irreducibility, due to its importance in the
literature on Markov dynamics. In general, given a nontrivial measure
\pi
on

(\mathsf{X},\mathscr{B})
, a transition kernel
Q
on
\mathsf{X}
is called

\pi
-irreducible
if, for each
x\in X
and every Borel set
B\subset X
with
\pi(B)>0
, there exists an
n\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
Q^{n}(x,B)\coloneq(Q^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{B})(x)>0
. (See, e.g.,
Meyn and Tweedie (2012)
.) Here, we will say that

Q
is
weakly irreducible
if there exists a measure
\pi
on
(\mathsf{X},\mathscr{B})
such that

(ii)

\pi
assigns positive measure to all nonempty open sets, and

(i)

Q
is
\pi
-irreducible.

Lemma 4.3
.

The following implications hold for any transition kernel
Q
on
\mathsf{X}
.

(a)

If
Q
is strongly irreducible, then
Q
is weakly irreducible.

(b)

If
Q
is weakly irreducible, then
Q
is open set irreducible.

Proof.

Regarding (a), let
Q
be strongly irreducible and let
\pi
be any distribution on
\mathsf{X}
such
that
\pi(G)>0
whenever
G\subset\mathsf{X}
is open and nonempty.
1
1

1

Such a measure exists in many settings, such as when

\mathsf{X}
is a locally compact topological group – in which case we can take

\pi
to be the Haar measure. In many applications,

will be a subset of

\mathbbm{R}^{n}
and
\pi
will be Lebesgue measure.
Fix
B\in\mathscr{B}
with

\pi(B)>0
and fix
x\in B
.
We recall from Lemma

that
\delta_{x}
is a nonzero element of the
dual space
b\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
. Also,

is a nonzero element of

.
Hence, by strong irreducibility, there exists
an

with

. We can rewrite this as

. This proves that

\pi
-irreducible.

is weakly irreducible.

Regarding (b), let

Theorem 4.4
.

If

is weakly irreducible and

In view of Lemma

, it suffices to show

.
This is true by Theorem

.

5.
Applications

In this section, we consider three applications. The first involves an optimal
savings problem with stochastic returns on wealth, and our aim is to illustrate
the significance of our main theoretical results. The second application
provides an extension showing how results in the paper can be applied outside
the MDP framework. The third application, found
in Section
5.4
, demonstrates that the irreducibility assumptions in
Theorems
3.3
,
4.2
, and
4.4
cannot be
removed without changing their conclusions.

5.1.
Optimal Savings

Consider an agent who seeks to maximize lifetime utility by choosing
optimal savings and consumption. The evolution of wealth is governed by the equation

(9)

w_{t+1}=\eta_{t+1}(w_{t}-c_{t})+y_{t+1},\qquad t=0,1,\ldots,

where
w_{t}\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
is time-
t
wealth,
c_{t}
is the current-period
consumption,
y_{t+1}
is the next-period labor income, and
\eta_{t+1}

represents the stochastic return on savings. The sequences
(y_{t})
and

(\eta_{t})
are IID with distributions
\varphi
and
\psi
respectively. For now
we assume that both of these distributions have full support on
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
. To
simplify the notation, we use
w^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(w-c)+y^{\prime}
to denote the evolution
of wealth.

We formulate this problem as an MDP. The state space is
\mathbbm{R}_{+}

and the set of feasible actions at wealth level
w
is

\Gamma(w)=\{c\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}:c\leqslant w\}
.
A feasible policy in this setup is a Borel measurable function
\sigma
from

\mathbbm{R}_{+}
to itself satisfying
\sigma(w)\leqslant w
for all
w\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
.
The reward function is
r(w,c)\coloneq u(c)
,
where
u(c)
is the utility derived from consumption and
u
is
continuous, differentiable, and strictly concave on
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
.

The transition kernel
P(w,c,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d})
is given by

(10)

P(w,c,B)=\int\mathbbm{1}_{B}(\eta^{\prime}(w-c)+y^{\prime})\psi(d\eta^{\prime})\varphi(dy^{\prime}),

where
0\leqslant c\leqslant w
and
B
is a Borel set in
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
.
Given
\sigma\in\Sigma
, the corresponding policy operator
T_{\sigma}
is given
by

(T_{\sigma}v)(w)=u(\sigma(w))+\beta(P_{\sigma}\,v)(w)\coloneq u(\sigma(w))+\beta\int v(w^{\prime})P(w,\sigma(w),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w^{\prime}),

where
\beta\in(0,1)
is the discount factor.
Using this setup, we have the following result:

Lemma 5.1
.

Under the stated assumptions, the optimal savings transition kernel

P_{\sigma}
is open set irreducible for every
\sigma\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be the set of feasible policies. Fix
\bar{w}\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
and an open set

B\subseteq\mathbbm{R}_{+}
with
\pi(B)>0
.
Let
\alpha=w-\sigma(w)
. By a change of variable, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\sigma}(\bar{w},B)

\displaystyle=\int\mathbbm{1}_{B}(w^{\prime})\psi(\eta^{\prime})\varphi(w^{\prime}-\alpha\eta^{\prime})\,d\eta^{\prime}\,dw^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(\eta^{\prime})\varphi(w^{\prime}-\alpha\eta^{\prime})\,d\eta^{\prime}\right)dw^{\prime}.

We treat the inner integral first. Since
\eta^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
are independent
random variables on
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
with strictly positive densities
\varphi
and
\psi

respectively and
w^{\prime}=\alpha\eta^{\prime}+y^{\prime}
, we have
the probability density function
f
of
w^{\prime}
at any point
w^{\prime}>0
is given
by the convolution

f(w^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(\eta^{\prime})\psi(w^{\prime}-\alpha\eta^{\prime})d\eta^{\prime}

as in the inner integral.
Since
w^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(w-c)+y^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}>0
,
w^{\prime}-\alpha\eta^{\prime}>0
,
which implies that
0<\eta^{\prime}<\frac{w^{\prime}}{\alpha}
. Therefore, we have

f(w^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\frac{w^{\prime}}{\alpha}}\varphi(\eta^{\prime})\psi(w^{\prime}-\alpha\eta^{\prime})d\eta^{\prime}.

Note that
\varphi(\eta^{\prime})>0
for
\eta^{\prime}>0
and
\psi(w^{\prime}-\alpha\eta^{\prime})>0

with
\eta^{\prime}\in(0,\frac{w^{\prime}}{\alpha})
. Moreover,

|\frac{w^{\prime}}{\alpha}|>0
for every
w^{\prime}>0
. Hence
f(w^{\prime})>0

for all
w^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
. Since
B
is open, there is an nonempty open interval

(l,m)\subset B
. Thus,

P_{\sigma}(w,B)=\int_{B}f(w^{\prime})\;dw^{\prime}\geqslant\int_{l}^{m}f(w^{\prime})\;dw^{\prime}>0
.
That is,
P_{\sigma}
is open set irreducible.
∎

Let

B(w,c,v)=u(c)+\beta\int\int v(\eta^{\prime}(w-c)+y^{\prime})\psi(d\eta^{\prime})\varphi(dy^{\prime}).

Since
u
is strictly concave, the map
c\mapsto B(w,c,v)
is strictly
concave whenever
v
is concave on
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
. One can also show that
v^{*}
is
concave on
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
. Combining these facts with the Bellman equation,
it is straightforward to show that the optimal policy is
both unique and continuous. We record this in the proposition below.
More details on the arguments can be found in Chapter 12 of
Stachurski (2022)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Under the assumptions stated above, the optimal policy of the optimal
savings model is unique and continuous on
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
.

Given the open set irreducibility of the transition kernel at any feasible policy
stated in Lemma
5.1
, Theorem
4.2
implies that we can compute a
(globally) optimal policy by maximizing
v_{\sigma}(\bar{w})
at any fixed
\bar{w}\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
. We now explore this result in a computational experiment, where the
maximization of
v_{\sigma}(\bar{w})
is based on a simple gradient ascent algorithm
with a neural network to approximate the policy function
\sigma
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
be the network parameters that
characterize the policy network

\hat{\sigma}(\cdot;\theta)
with a fixed paramter size
d\in\mathbbm{N}
.
We want to find the policy parameterization that maximizes the expected
discounted sum of rewards defined by
u(\hat{\sigma}(w;\theta))

starting from the initial wealth level
\bar{w}
:

\max_{\hat{\sigma}\in\Sigma}v_{\hat{\sigma}}(\bar{w})\coloneq\max_{\hat{\sigma}\in\Sigma}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}\int u(\hat{\sigma}(w^{\prime};\theta))P^{t}_{\hat{\sigma}}(\bar{w},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w^{\prime}).

We can approximate this by Monte Carlo with large batch size
N

and rollout steps
T
:

(11)

\hat{v}_{\hat{\sigma}}(\bar{w})\coloneq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\beta^{t}u(\hat{\sigma}(w_{i,t};\theta))\quad\text{with}\quad w_{i,0}=\bar{w}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,N,

Here
w_{i,t}
is the wealth at time

for the
i

starting at
\bar{w}
and following policy
\hat{\sigma}
.
(In particular, we generate the rollout trajectories

for all

9

\hat{\sigma}(w_{i,t};\theta)

The fact that
w_{i,0}=\bar{w}
for all

During each episode, the model parameters are updated to reduce the value of the loss function

(12)

L(\theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\beta^{t}u(\hat{\sigma}(w_{i,t};\theta))

is equivalent to maximizing the finite time approximation of the expected lifetime value (
11

5.2.
A Reducible MDP

Now consider a modified version of the optimal saving MDP where both returns and
labor income are bounded:

(13)

w^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(w-c)+y^{\prime},\quad\eta^{\prime}\in[\underline{\eta},\overline{\eta}],\quad y^{\prime}\in[\underline{y},\overline{y}]

with
0<\underline{\eta}<\overline{\eta}<1
and
0\leqslant\underline{y}<\overline{y}<\infty
. For
w\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
and a Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbbm{R}_{+}

the stochastic kernel
P_{\sigma}
is

(14)

P_{\sigma}(w,B)=\int\mathbbm{1}_{B}(\eta^{\prime}(w-\sigma(w))+y^{\prime})\psi(d\eta^{\prime})\varphi(dy^{\prime})

where
\psi
and
\varphi

[\underline{\eta},\overline{\eta}]
and

At the same time, we deliberately continue to take
\mathbbm{R}_{+}

This leads to a failure of irreducibility, as clarified in the next proposition.

Proposition 5.3
.

For any
\sigma\in\Sigma
, the transition kernel
P_{\sigma}
is neither
weakly irreducible nor open set irreducible.

Proof.

Fix
\sigma\in\Sigma
. In view of Lemma
4.3
, we only
need to show that
P_{\sigma}
is not weakly irreducible.
To this end, let
\pi
be a distribution on
\mathsf{W}
that assigns positive
probability to open sets. Fix initial wealth
w_{0}\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
. For any
t

transition, let
\alpha_{t}\coloneq w_{t}-\sigma(w_{t})
be savings at step
t
.
Then

w_{t+1}\leqslant\overline{\eta}\alpha_{t}+\overline{y}\leqslant\overline{\eta}w_{t}+\overline{y}.

Iterating this inequality
n
times from
w_{0}

for all

. This shows that

is
not weakly irreducible.

5.3.
Firm Entry

The next example provides an extension showing how the results in the paper can
be used outside the MDP setting. This is significant because many recent
examples of dynamic programs are not MDPs (see, e.g.,

Sargent and Stachurski (2025a)
). The example concerns an optimal stopping problem
with state-dependent discounting. We prove that, under the assumptions stated
below, local optimality implies global optimality.

Specifically, we consider a stopping problem for a firm choosing if and when to enter a
market. Stopping corresponds to entering the market and continuing
corresponds to waiting until the next period and then deciding again. If the firm enters at time
t
it
receives one-off profit
\pi(X_{t})
, where
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a Markov
sequence on possibly unbounded interval
\mathsf{X}\subset\mathbbm{R}
with transition
kernel
Q
. If the firm continues then it pays a fixed cost
c
. We admit the
possibility that discounting is not constant. (This allows for the fact that
firms evaluate future profit opportunities based on cost-of-capital,
which fluctuates over time.) Thus, the Bellman equation for the firm is

(15)

v(x)=\max\left\{\pi(x),\;-c+\beta(x)\int v(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\right\},

where
\beta(x)\in(0,\infty)
is the discount factor associated with
cost-of-capital in state
x
. (Discount factor realizations above one are
admitted in order to accommodate occasionally negative interest rates.)

Let
ib\mathsf{X}
be the increasing functions in
b\mathsf{X}
and let
icb\mathsf{X}
be the
continuous functions in
ib\mathsf{X}
. We assume that the discount operator
K
defined by

(Kf)(x)=\beta(x)\int f(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\qquad(x\in\mathsf{X},\;f\in b\mathsf{X})

preserves monotonicity and continuity, in the sense that
K
is invariant on
both
ib\mathsf{X}
and
icb\mathsf{X}
, and that
r(K)<1
, where

r(K)
is the spectral radius of
K
. For simplicity, we also assume that
Q

always has full support on
\mathbbm{R}
, so that
Q(x,B)>0
for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbbm{R}
with
positive Lebesgue measure. (For example,
(X_{t})
might be driven by an AR(1)
sequence with normal shocks.) Finally, we assume that the profit function

\pi
is bounded, increasing, and continuous.

A policy is a map
\sigma
from
\mathsf{X}
to
\{0,1\}
, with
\sigma(x)=1

indicating the decision to stop. The lifetime value
v_{\sigma}
of policy
\sigma

is the unique fixed point of the operator
T_{\sigma}
on
b\mathsf{X}
given by

(16)

(T_{\sigma}\,v)(x)=\sigma(x)\pi(x)+(1-\sigma(x))\left[-c+\beta(x)\int v(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\right].

We let
\Sigma
be the set of all policies
\sigma
such that
T_{\sigma}
is
invariant on
ib\mathsf{X}
.

As for the MDP case, the
value function
is denoted
v^{*}
and defined at
each
x\in\mathsf{X}
by
v^{*}(x)\coloneq\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}v_{\sigma}(x)
. A
policy
\sigma
is called
optimal
if
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
. In the proofs below we will make use of the Bellman operator for
this problem, which takes the form

(Tv)(x)=\max\left\{\pi(x),\;-c+\beta(x)\int v(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\right\}\qquad(x\in\mathsf{X},\;v\in b\mathsf{X}).

The model has standard optimality properties, which we detail in the theorem
below.

Theorem 5.4
.

Under the stated assumptions, the following results hold:

(a)

The value function is the unique solution to the Bellman equation
in
b\mathsf{X}
,

(b)

the value function is increasing and continuous, and

(c)

at least one optimal policy exists.

We will prove in addition the following result, which holds for any
\sigma\in\Sigma
:

Theorem 5.5
.

If there exists an
x\in\mathsf{X}
such that
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
, then
\sigma
is optimal.

We begin the proof of Theorem
5.4
and Theorem
5.5

with a series of lemmas. In what follows, an operator
S
on
b\mathsf{X}
is called

globally stable
when
S
has a unique fixed point
\bar{v}
in
b\mathsf{X}
and

S^{n}v\to\bar{v}
as
n\to\infty
for all
v\in b\mathsf{X}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

For each
\sigma\in\Sigma
, the operator
T_{\sigma}
is globally stable on
b\mathsf{X}
.

Proof.

Fix
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
v,w\in b\mathsf{X}
. Applying the triangle inequality,
combined with the fact that
K
is a positive linear operator, we have

|T_{\sigma}\,v-T_{\sigma}\,w|\leqslant|K(v-w)|\leqslant K|v-w|
. Since
\rho(K)<1

and
b\mathsf{X}
is a Banach lattice, by Proposition 4.1 and Theorem 2.1 in

Stachurski and Zhang (2021)
, there exists some
N\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
T_{\sigma}^{N}

is a contraction. By Banach fixed point theorem,
T_{\sigma}^{N}
is globally stable and
it follows that
T_{\sigma}
is globally stable on
b\mathsf{X}
.
∎

The next proof uses several concepts from
Sargent and Stachurski (2025b)
. We refer
to that paper for the definitions, which are omitted here for brevity.

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

To prove Theorem
5.4
we apply Theorem 5.5 of

Sargent and Stachurski (2025b)
, which applies to the abstract dynamic program

(b\mathsf{X},\mathbbm{T})\coloneq(b\mathsf{X},\{T_{\sigma}:\sigma\in\Sigma\})
. First, in
b\mathsf{X}

with its usual partial order,
the statement
v_{n}\uparrow v
is equivalent to
v_{n}(x)\uparrow v(x)
in

\mathbbm{R}
for each
x\in\mathsf{X}
. From this fact and the monotone
convergence theorem, we obtain
Tv_{n}\uparrow Tv
whenever
(v_{n})\subset b\mathsf{X}
and
v_{n}\uparrow v\in b\mathsf{X}
. Thus,
(b\mathsf{X},\mathbbm{T})
is order continuous.
Also,
b\mathsf{X}
is countably-Dedekind
complete because the set of bounded Borel measurable functions is closed
under pointwise convergence. In addition, each
T_{\sigma}
is globally
stable (Lemma
5.6
) and hence order stable (see
Sargent and Stachurski (2025b)
, Example 2.1).
Finally, let
\hat{T}
be the operator given by
\hat{T}v=|\pi|+Kv
.
Since
\rho(K)<1
, there is a
u\in b\mathsf{X}_{+}
with
\hat{T}u=u
.
For this
u
we have
T_{\sigma}\,u\leqslant\hat{T}u\leqslant u
, so
(b\mathsf{X},\mathbbm{T})
is
bounded above. It now follows from Theorem 5.5 of
Sargent and Stachurski (2025b)

that the value function is the unique solution
to the Bellman equation and at least one optimal policy exists.

To prove the final claim we first note that
T
is globally stable on

b\mathsf{X}
, since, given
v,w\in b\mathsf{X}
, we have

|Tv-Tw|\leqslant|K(v-w)|\leqslant K|v-w|
, so the argument in the proof of
Lemma
5.6
applies. Now fix
v\in icb\mathsf{X}
. Since
K
is invariant on

icb\mathsf{X}
, the element
-c+Kv
is in
icb\mathsf{X}
. Moreover,
\pi\in icb\mathsf{X}

and so
\max\{\pi,-c+Kv\}\in icb\mathsf{X}
. This shows that
T
is invariant on
icb\mathsf{X}
.
Since
icb\mathsf{X}
is nonempty and closed under uniform limits, and
T
is globally
stable on
b\mathsf{X}
, we conclude that
v^{*}\in icb\mathsf{X}
. The proof of
Theorem
5.4
is now done.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

If
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
for some

x\in\mathsf{X}
, then
v_{\sigma}=v^{*}
almost everywhere.

Proof.

Since
v_{\sigma}\leqslant v^{*}
and
T_{\sigma}\,v\leqslant Tv
for all
v\in b\mathsf{X}
,
we have
v_{\sigma}=T_{\sigma}\,v_{\sigma}\leqslant T_{\sigma}\,v^{*}\leqslant Tv^{*}=v^{*}
. As a result,
v_{\sigma}(x)=v^{*}(x)
implies

(T_{\sigma}\,v_{\sigma})(x)=(T_{\sigma}\,v^{*})(x)
, which, using the
definition of
T_{\sigma}
in (
16
), yields

\beta(x)\int v^{*}(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=\beta(x)\int v_{\sigma}(x^{\prime})Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}).

As
\beta(x)>0
, we obtain
\int(v^{*}(x^{\prime})-v_{\sigma}(x^{\prime}))Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=0
. If
v^{*}>v_{\sigma}
on a set
E
of positive Lebesgue measure, then,
at the same time, we have

\int(v^{*}(x^{\prime})-v_{\sigma}(x^{\prime}))Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\geqslant\int_{E}(v^{*}(x^{\prime})-v_{\sigma}(x^{\prime}))Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})>0,

where the last inequality is due to the assumption that
Q(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})

assigns positive measure to any such
E
. From this contradiction, we
conclude that
v^{*}=v_{\sigma}
almost everywhere.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
f,g
be two increasing functions in
b\mathsf{X}
with
f\leqslant g
.
If
g
is continuous and
f=g
almost everywhere, then
f=g
.

Proof.

If
f
is continuous, the result follows from the fact that any two continuous measurable
functions equal almost everywhere on an
interval of
\mathbbm{R}
are equal. Now suppose instead that
f
is not
continous at
x_{0}\in\mathsf{X}
. Let
a=\lim_{x\uparrow x_{0}}f(x)
and

b=\lim_{x\downarrow x_{0}}f(x)
.
Monotonicity and discontinuiy of
f
implies
a<b
. Since
g
is continuous,

g((a,b))^{-1}
is open. We claim that
g((a,b))^{-1}
is nonempty.
Indeed, since
f=g
almost everywhere, there are
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathsf{X}
such that
g(y)\leqslant a

and
g(y^{\prime})\geqslant b
. Since
g
is continuous and increasing, there exist

y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathsf{X}
such that
g(y^{\prime\prime})\in(a,b)
. Thus
g((a,b))^{-1}
is
a nonempty open set. This proves the existence of a set of nonzero Lebesgue

. Contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Suppose there exists an

and a policy

, we have

By Theorem

, the function

is increasing and continuous.

5.4.
A Two-State Example

In this section, we show that the irreducibility assumptions used in
Theorem
3.3
, Theorem
4.2
, and Theorem
4.4

cannot be dropped: without irreducibility,
(E1)
–
(E3)
are not
generally equivalent. To make the example as simple as possible, we specialize
to the case where
\mathsf{X}
is finite and study the role of irreducibility in this
setting. In applying these theorems, we take the metric on
\mathsf{X}
to be the
discrete metric, so that all subsets of
\mathsf{X}
are open and
\mathscr{B}
is all subsets
of
\mathsf{X}
. We also assume that
\mathsf{A}
is finite and impose the discrete metric
on
\mathsf{A}
. As a result, every map and hence every policy from
\mathsf{X}
to
\mathsf{A}

is continuous.

Prior to stating our example, we note some elementary properties of
irreducibility on discrete states. Given a transition kernel
Q
on
\mathsf{X}
, we say that

y
in
\mathsf{X}
is
Q
-accessible
from
x\in\mathsf{X}
when there exists an
m\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
Q^{m}(x,y)>0
. The usual definition of irreducibility of a
transition kernel
Q
on finite
\mathsf{X}
is that, for every
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
,
x

is
Q
-accessible from
y
and
y
is
Q
-accessible from
x
. To distinguish
between different notions of irreducibility, we call this
discrete
irreducibility
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\sigma
be any feasible policy. When the state space is finite, the following
statements are equivalent:

(a)

P_{\sigma}
is strongly irreducible.

(b)

P_{\sigma}
is discretely irreducible.

Proof.

((a)
\implies
(b)) Fix
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{x}(z)
equal
1
when
z=x
and
zero elsewhere. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{y}
be defined analogously. Since
\mathbbm{1}_{x}\in b\mathsf{X}_{+}
and

\mathbbm{1}_{y}\in(b\mathsf{X}_{+})^{\prime}
, there exists an
m\in\mathbbm{N}
with

\left\langle{\mathbbm{1}_{x},P_{\sigma}^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{y}}\right\rangle>0
. But

\left\langle{\mathbbm{1}_{x},P_{\sigma}^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{y}}\right\rangle=P_{\sigma}^{m}(x,y)
, so
y
is

P_{\sigma}
-accessible from
x
. This proves that
P_{\sigma}
is discretely
irreducible.

((b)
\implies
(a)) Fix a nonzero
f\in b\mathsf{X}_{+}
and nonzero

\mu\in(b\mathsf{X}_{+})^{\prime}
. Since
\mathsf{X}
is finite, these element are just
maps from
\mathsf{X}
to
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
and, as both are nonzero, we can find
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\mathsf{X}
such that
f(\bar{y})>0
and
\mu(\bar{x})>0
. Moreover,
since
P_{\sigma}
is discretely irreducible, there is
m\in\mathbbm{N}
, such that

P_{\sigma}^{m}(\bar{x},\bar{y})>0
. As a result,

\left\langle{\mu,P_{\sigma}^{m}f}\right\rangle=\sum_{x}(P_{\sigma}^{m}f)(x)\mu(x)=\sum_{x}\sum_{y}f(y)P_{\sigma}^{m}(x,y)\mu(x)\geqslant P_{\sigma}^{m}(\bar{x},\bar{y})f(\bar{y})\mu(\bar{x})>0.

This proves that
P_{\sigma}
is strongly irreducible.
∎

Now consider a two-state MDP with
\mathsf{X}=\{1,2\}
and
\mathsf{A}=\{1,2\}
. The
feasible coprrespondence is defined by
\Gamma(1)=\{1,2\}
and
\Gamma(2)=\{2\}
. The reward function is defined by
r(i,j)=r_{ij}
with

\begin{pmatrix}r_{11}&r_{12}\\
r_{21}&r_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&2\end{pmatrix}.

We set
\beta=0.9
. The transition probabilities
P(x,a,x^{\prime})
are given by

\displaystyle P(1,1,\cdot)=(1,0),\quad P(1,2,\cdot)=(1,0),\quad P(2,1,\cdot)=(0,1),\quad P(2,2,\cdot)=(0,1).

By the definition of the feasible correspondence
\Gamma
, there are only two
feasible policies
\Sigma=\{\sigma,\pi\}
, where
\sigma(x)=2
and

\pi(x)=x
for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
. The transition probabilities following the
two policies are given by

P_{\sigma}=P_{\pi}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that
P_{\sigma}=P_{\pi}
fails to be discretely irreducible. By
Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
5.9
, this means that this transition
kernel is neither strongly irreducible, nor weakly irreducible, nor open set irreducible.

Let us compute the lifetime value functions for the optimal
\sigma
and
\pi
.
For policy
\sigma
, we have
r_{\sigma}=(r_{12},r_{22})=(1,2)
and

v_{\sigma}=(10,20)
. For
\pi
, we have
r_{\pi}=(r_{11},r_{22})=(0,2)

and
v_{\pi}=(0,20)
. Since,
v^{*}(x)\coloneq\sup_{s\in\Sigma}v_{s}(x)=v_{\sigma}(x)
, we see that
\sigma
is an optimal policy. On the other hand,

v_{\pi}(2)=v^{*}(2)
, but
v_{\pi}(1)<v^{*}(1)
, indicating that optimality in one
state does not guarantee global optimality. This confirms that irreducibility
cannot be dropped from the statements of Theorems
3.3
,

4.2
, and
4.4
.

Extensions and Future Work

Using MDP optimality results from
Bäuerle and Rieder (2011)
or

Bertsekas (2022)
, it should be possible to extend our results to the
case of unbounded rewards by replacing the ordinary supremum norm on
b\mathsf{X}

with a weighted supremum norm. Also, while our results have focused on standard
MDPs with constant discount factors, one useful variation of this model is MDPs
with state-dependent discount factors, so that
\beta
becomes a map from
\mathsf{X}

to
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
(see, e.g.,
Stachurski and Zhang (2021)
). We conjecture that
the main results we obtained for MDPs will extend to generalized ADPs
with state-dependent discounting under suitable stability and irreducibility
assumptions. Finally, it seems likely that results similar to Theorem
3.3
will
be valid for some continuous time MDPs, as well as at least some of the
nonstandard discrete time dynamic programs discussed in
Bertsekas (2022)

and
Sargent and Stachurski (2025a)
. These topics are also left for future work.
