Direct Spectral Acceleration of First-Order Methods for Saddle Point Problems with Bilinear Coupling
Meng Li, Paul Grigas

Abstract
We study convex–concave saddle point problems with bilinear coupling, covering linearly constrained convex optimization and more general nonsmooth or constrained models via a proximable term in the dual objective. In linearly convergent regimes, we characterize how spectral properties of the coupling matrix and objective conditioning jointly determine the attainable linear rates. We propose direct spectral acceleration for first-order primal–dual methods for a class of bilinear-coupled saddle point problems, including affinely constrained smooth strongly convex optimization and extensions with proximable dual terms. The resulting algorithms distinguish objective-dominated and coupling matrix-dominated regimes and attain optimal linear convergence without Chebyshev inner loops or double-loop designs. We further develop stochastic block-coordinate extensions in the affinely constrained case with separable objectives; we also establish optimal linear rates matching the block-coordinate lower bound. For both deterministic and stochastic methods, we provide matching worst-case lower bounds via explicit finite-dimensional hard instances.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider two variants of saddle point problems with bilinear coupling. The first is the following general problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad F(x,y)=f(x)+y^{\top}Mx-b^{\top}y-\phi(y),

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth convex function,

, the proximal term
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper convex and closed function, and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the coupling matrix. Another problem we consider is its block-wise extension in the equality-constrained case
(\phi\equiv 0)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}},i=1,\ldots,N}

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad F(x,y)\ =\ f(x)+y^{\top}Mx-b^{\top}y

(2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})+y^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}x_{i}-b^{\top}y,

where the coupling matrix
M=\left(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
m=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}
, and the primal objective
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})
is separable. Problems of these forms arise in many contexts, including convex games
(

;
6

, robust/adversarial training problems
(

(

Contributions.
For deterministic primal–dual methods, we propose new algorithms for (
1
) in the regime where
f(x)
is smooth and strongly convex,
M
has full row rank, and
\phi(y)
is proximable. We distinguish two regimes depending on whether the dominant difficulty comes from the conditioning of the objective, controlled by

, or from the conditioning of the coupling matrix, controlled by

. In the two regimes, we design direct acceleration schemes that act on the primal side and the dual side, respectively.
These accelerations yield the following advantages: (i) the algorithms have complexities matching the lower bound without requiring inner-loop Chebyshev acceleration, (ii) they exhibit better numerical performance compared with existing algorithms, which need to perform a fixed number of Chebyshev inner iterations (depending on the condition numbers). As far as we are aware, our algorithms are the first that do not require a double-loop structure in this setting.
Furthermore, our framework accommodates a proximable dual term
\phi

A second contribution is a stochastic extension of our direct acceleration schemes to the block-coordinate setting (

f
is separable, matching the corresponding lower bound we derive for the same class of first-order methods. Theoretically, these results sharpen the understanding of how stochastic block updates interact with coupling spectra to yield optimal linear rates; practically, they reduce per-iteration cost and lead to improved empirical performance on large-scale instances. In addition, in the regime where objective conditioning dominates, we extend the accelerated scheme to nonseparable objectives while preserving the optimal linear rate. Table

Finally, we complement our upper bounds with matching worst-case lower bounds that make the dependence on the coupling spectrum explicit. Although broader unified frameworks exist (e.g.,

Remark 2.5
.

Let us explain the intuition behind our direct acceleration designs. When the
x
-side condition number, or equivalently the condition number of
f
, dominates, namely when

\frac{s_{\max}}{s_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L}}=O(1),

x
-side acceleration is necessary to obtain a rate whose dependence on the condition number of
f
matches the
\sqrt{\frac{L}{\mu}}
scale, as in a simpler problem
\min_{x}f(x)
. On the other hand, when

\frac{s_{\max}}{s_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L}}=\Omega(1),

y
-side acceleration is necessary to obtain a rate whose dependence on the condition number of the coupling matrix matches the
\frac{s_{\max}}{s_{\min}}
scale, as in a simpler problem
\min_{y}\left\|M^{\top}y-c\right\|_{2}^{2}
.

From the
y
-side perspective, linear convergence of first-order methods can be understood through the strong concavity of the value function

\Phi_{y}(y):=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}F(x,y),

which is induced by the full row rank of the bilinear coupling matrix
M
. In particular,
\Phi_{y}
is
\frac{s_{\min}^{2}}{L}
-strongly concave. Therefore, when
\frac{s_{\min}}{s_{\max}}
is small, acceleration must act on the
y
-iterates or otherwise leverage the spectral structure of
M
. This viewpoint motivates our Lyapunov function, in which the term
\frac{1}{2}\left\|M^{\top}(y^{k}-y^{*})\right\|_{2}^{2}
plays a role analogous to the Bregman distance
D_{f}(x^{k},x^{*})
used in
x
-side acceleration. Moreover, the x-update step of the algorithm uses a smaller step size on the
x
-side (below the
\frac{1}{L}
scale) to balance the convergence of the two variables. Our direct spectral acceleration removes the inner-loop Chebyshev preconditioning, admits a straightforward incorporation of proximable dual terms
\phi
, and empirically converges faster in our experiments.
In contrast to our algorithm,
33
apply Chebyshev iterations with a fixed number of inner steps to precondition the linear system.

whose conditional expectation is hard to control since
y^{k+1}
depends on the same random index
i
. Independent sampling makes the expected Lyapunov decrease tractable; we use the same idea in Algorithm
4
.

The trade-off is a higher per-iteration oracle/matrix-access cost. Empirically, however, setting
j=i
performs similarly to independent sampling in our experiments (Figure
6
).

Remark 3.5
.

The
x
-updates in Algorithm
3
are reminiscent of accelerated coordinate descent methods
(
2
)
. In fact, consider a more general setting in which (
2
) is replaced by a problem with a non-separable objective
f(x)=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
. If we replace the component gradients
\nabla f_{j}(x_{j})
by the block partial gradients
\nabla_{j}f(x)
, then with modifications to the
x
-side steps of Algorithm
3
(see
Appendix

H
), one can establish a comparable convergence rate

O\left(N\frac{\bar{s}_{\max}}{s_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{L}{\mu}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right),

under the regime
\frac{\bar{s}_{\max}}{s_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L}}=O(1)
, where

is

-smooth in

. On the other hand, the
y
-side block-coordinate method Algorithm

does not extend naturally, since it relies on maintaining a stored quantity

while updating only a single block contribution

per iteration, a decomposition that is unavailable when

is non-separable.

Remark 3.7
.

Apart from the one-time cost of computing and storing
\nabla f\left(x^{0}\right)
and
M\left(x^{0}-t\nabla f\left(x^{0}\right)\right)
at initialization, each iteration of Algorithm

consists of a deterministic update plus a randomized refresh.

In the deterministic part, each iteration uses at most one oracle access (to update
\nabla f_{j}\left(x^{k+1}_{j}\right)
) and
O(1)
block matrix multiplications (block matrix–vector products), e.g.,
M_{i}M_{i}^{\top}(w^{k}-y^{k})
and
M_{j}^{\top}u^{k+1}
. Moreover,
M\left(x^{k}-t\nabla f\left(x^{k}\right)\right)
can be stored and updated incrementally since only the
j

and
\nabla f\left(x\right)
change.

For the refresh of
MM^{\top}y^{k+1}

p=\frac{1}{N}
. When
y^{k+1}
is refreshed (i.e.,
y^{k+1}=\tilde{y}^{k+1}

M/M^{\top}

O(1)
per iteration.
A similar
O(1)
per-iteration oracle/matrix-access bound holds for the
x
-side method; see Remark

Remark 3.8
.

The
y
-updates in Algorithm
4
are closely related to accelerated stochastic methods for finite-sum problems

(
3
;
28
;
26
)
. It is well known that, to obtain linear convergence in such settings, one typically needs a variance-reduction mechanism.

To illustrate the point, consider the quadratic finite-sum problem

\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \sum_{l=1}^{N}\left\|M_{l}^{\top}y-c_{l}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Using only stochastic gradients based on a single sampled index
l
generally does not yield the linear rate in terms of block matrix multiplications. In contrast, variance-reduced accelerated methods can achieve a complexity on the order of

O\left(N\frac{\bar{s}_{\max}}{s_{\min}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right)

block matrix multiplications.

In Algorithm
4
, we incorporate a variance-reduction step, similar in spirit to the loopless scheme in
(
26
)
, to obtain the optimal rate while preserving a loopless structure.

In comparison, Algorithm
3
does not require an explicit variance-reduction mechanism to achieve the optimal rate in the regime

\frac{\bar{s}_{\max}}{s_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\bar{L}}}=O(1)
.
In this regime, the accelerated coordinate-descent behavior on the
x
-side dominates the stochastic-gradient effects coming from the
y
-side.

6
Conclusions

We developed single-loop direct spectral acceleration for first-order primal–dual methods on bilinear saddle-point problems and showed how objective conditioning and the coupling spectrum jointly determine linear rates. Our deterministic methods handle a proximable dual term and achieve optimal complexity without double-loop Chebyshev preconditioning. We further obtained optimal stochastic block-coordinate rates for separable objectives in the equality-constrained case and provided matching lower bounds via explicit finite-dimensional hard instances, with experiments confirming the practical gains.

Promising directions include extending our stochastic block-coordinate direct acceleration beyond the equality-constrained separable setting—incorporating proximable dual terms and nonseparable objectives—and applying the same objective-conditioning/coupling-spectrum perspective to broader (block-wise) primal–dual problems.

Proof of Proposition
2

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Lemma 1 in
23
, notice that

\Phi_{y}(y)=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}F(x,y)=-b^{\top}y-\phi(y)+\inf_{x}\left[f(x)+x^{\top}M^{\top}y\right]=-b^{\top}y-\phi(y)-f^{*}\left(-M^{\top}y\right),

where
f^{*}
is the Fenchel conjugate of
f
, and thus
\frac{1}{\mu}
-smooth and
\frac{1}{L}
-strongly convex (by Theorem 6 of
21
). Therefore,
-f^{*}\left(-M^{\top}y\right)
is
\frac{s_{\max}^{2}}{\mu}
-smooth and
\frac{s_{\min}^{2}}{L}
-strongly concave. Hence, the solution

y^{*}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Phi_{y}(y).

exists and is unique. By the strong convexity of
f
, the solution
x^{*}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}F(x,y^{*})
is unique, and it satisfies
\nabla f\left(x^{*}\right)+M^{\top}y^{*}=0
. By Danskin’s theorem applying on
f^{*}\left(-M^{\top}y\right)=\sup_{x}\left[-f(x)-x^{\top}M^{\top}y\right]
,
\nabla_{y}f^{*}\left(-M^{\top}y\right)|_{y=y^{*}}=-Mx^{*}
. Hence,
Mx^{*}-b\in\partial\phi\left(y^{*}\right)
. Therefore,
(x^{*},y^{*})
satisfies (
4
).

Since
Mx^{*}-b\in\partial\phi\left(y^{*}\right)
,
y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y}\left[{x^{*}}^{\top}M^{\top}y-b^{\top}y-\phi(y)\right]
. Hence, by Danskin’s theorem,
M^{\top}y^{*}\in\partial_{x}\sup_{y}\left[{x}^{\top}M^{\top}y-b^{\top}y-\phi(y)\right]|_{x=x^{*}}
, and thus

0=\nabla f\left(x^{*}\right)+M^{\top}y^{*}\in\partial_{x}\sup_{y}F(x,y)|_{x=x^{*}}=\partial_{x}\Phi_{x}\left(x^{*}\right).

In conclusion, there exists a unique saddle point
(x^{*},y^{*})
, and it satisfies (
4
).

For
(x^{*},y^{*})
satisfying (
4
), we could know

\mathcal{H}_{y_{2}}^{4}&=\mathrm{span}\{h,A^{2}h\}\end{aligned}\right.,\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}_{x_{1}}^{5}&=\mathrm{span}\{h,A^{2}h\}\\
\mathcal{H}_{x_{2}}^{5}&=\mathrm{span}\{Ah,A^{3}h\}\\
\mathcal{H}_{y_{1}}^{5}&=\mathrm{span}\{Ah,A^{3}h\}\\
\mathcal{H}_{y_{2}}^{5}&=\mathrm{span}\{h,A^{2}h,A^{4}h\}\end{aligned}\right.,\dots{}

By induction, we have the following lemma:

Lemma J.19
.

For problem (
10
) with
f,M,g
specified in (
72
), if the sequence of iterates satisfies Definition
J
, then for any
k\geq 2
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}_{y_{1}}^{2k}&=\mathrm{span}\{A^{2i}Ah:i=0,\ldots,k-2\},\\
\mathcal{H}_{y_{2}}^{2k}&=\mathrm{span}\{A^{2i}h:i=0,\ldots,k-1\}\end{aligned}\right.,\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}_{y_{1}}^{2k+1}&=\mathrm{span}\{A^{2i}Ah:i=0,\ldots,k-1\},\\
\mathcal{H}_{y_{2}}^{2k+1}&=\mathrm{span}\{A^{2i}h:i=0,\ldots,k\}\end{aligned}\right..

Before presenting our results, we first establish the following useful lemma, which provides a closed-form expression for the inverse of a structured block matrix.

Lemma J.20
.

Let
S\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a non-singular matrix, and let
H,G\succeq 0
be symmetric matrices of the same dimension. Suppose that the matrix
S+HS^{-\top}G
is also non-singular. Then, the inverse of the block matrix

\begin{pmatrix}H&S\\
-S^{\top}&G\end{pmatrix}

is given by

\begin{pmatrix}H&S\\
-S^{\top}&G\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}S^{-\top}G(S+HS^{-\top}G)^{-1}&-S^{-\top}+S^{-\top}G(S+HS^{-\top}G)^{-1}HS^{-\top}\\
(S+HS^{-\top}G)^{-1}&(S+HS^{-\top}G)^{-1}HS^{-\top}\end{pmatrix}.

Using Lemma
J.3
, we obtain the following result for the
y^{*}
component of the saddle point.

Lemma J.21
.

Suppose
L_{x},L_{y}>0
and
s_{\max}\geq\sqrt{5}s_{\min}>0
. Then, the
y^{*}
component in the saddle point of problem (
10
) with the specification in (
72
) is given by

\displaystyle y_{1}^{*}=-\frac{\hat{s}}{s_{\min}}Ay_{2}^{*},

(73)

\displaystyle(A^{4}+\alpha A^{2}+\beta I)y_{2}^{*}=\frac{L_{x}s_{\min}^{2}}{\hat{s}^{4}}h,

where

\alpha=2\cdot\frac{s_{\min}^{2}}{\hat{s}^{2}}+\frac{L_{x}L_{y}}{\hat{s}^{2}},\quad\beta=\frac{s_{\min}^{4}}{\hat{s}^{4}}.

(74)

Proof.

The saddle point
(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*})
of problem (
10
) with (
72
) satisfies the first-order optimality condition:

\begin{pmatrix}L_{x}I&0&\hat{s}A&-s_{\min}I\\
0&0&s_{\min}I&\hat{s}A\\
-\hat{s}A&-s_{\min}I&L_{y}I&0\\
s_{\min}I&-\hat{s}A&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{*}\\
x_{2}^{*}\\
y_{1}^{*}\\
y_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
h\end{pmatrix}.

We rewrite the system in block form:

\begin{pmatrix}H&S\\
-S^{\top}&G\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x^{*}\\
y^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\bar{h}\end{pmatrix},

where

H=\begin{pmatrix}L_{x}I&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}A&-s_{\min}I\\
s_{\min}I&\hat{s}A\end{pmatrix}=M^{\top},\quad G=\begin{pmatrix}L_{y}I&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\bar{h}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
h\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
J.3
, the solution for
y^{*}
is

y^{*}=(S+HS^{-\top}G)^{-1}HS^{-\top}\bar{h}.

Now compute
S^{-\top}=M^{-1}
. Since
A
is symmetric, we have

M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}A(s_{\min}^{2}I+\hat{s}^{2}A^{2})^{-1}&-\left(s_{\min}I+\frac{\hat{s}^{2}}{s_{\min}}A^{2}\right)^{-1}\\
\left(s_{\min}I+\frac{\hat{s}^{2}}{s_{\min}}A^{2}\right)^{-1}&\hat{s}A(s_{\min}^{2}I+\hat{s}^{2}A^{2})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then,

S+HS^{-\top}G=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}A+\hat{s}L_{x}L_{y}A(s_{\min}^{2}I+\hat{s}^{2}A^{2})^{-1}&-s_{\min}I\\
s_{\min}I&\hat{s}A\end{pmatrix}.

Again, by applying Lemma
J.3
to
S+HS^{-T}G
,

(S+HS^{-T}G)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
-\left(s_{\min}^{3}I+\left(2s_{\min}\hat{s}^{2}+\frac{\hat{s}^{2}L_{x}L_{y}}{s_{\min}}\right)A^{2}+\frac{\hat{s}^{4}}{s_{\min}}A^{4}\right)^{-1}\left(s_{\min}^{2}I+\hat{s}^{2}A^{2}\right)&*\end{pmatrix},

where
*
denotes irrelevant blocks.

Hence, combining all parts, we obtain

\displaystyle y_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left[-\left(s_{\min}^{3}I+\left(2s_{\min}\hat{s}^{2}+\frac{\hat{s}^{2}L_{x}L_{y}}{s_{\min}}\right)A^{2}+\frac{\hat{s}^{4}}{s_{\min}}A^{4}\right)^{-1}\left(s_{\min}^{2}I+\hat{s}^{2}A^{2}\right)\right]

(75)

\displaystyle\cdot L_{x}\left[-\left(s_{\min}I+\frac{\hat{s}^{2}}{s_{\min}}A^{2}\right)^{-1}\right]h

\displaystyle=\left(A^{4}+\alpha A^{2}+\beta I\right)^{-1}\cdot\frac{L_{x}s_{\min}^{2}}{\hat{s}^{4}}h,

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are defined in (
74
). Finally,
y_{1}^{*}=-\frac{\hat{s}}{s_{\min}}Ay_{2}^{*}
follows from the second row of the first-order condition.
∎

The following lemma shows how to construct an “approximate” solution
\hat{y}^{*}
, where
\hat{y}_{2}^{*}
satisfies
\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}=O(q^{\ell})
and
\hat{y}_{2,i}^{*}=q^{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

Lemma J.22
.

Suppose
L_{x},L_{y}>0
and
s_{\max}\geq\sqrt{5}s_{\min}>0
. Let
\alpha,\beta
be defined as in (
74
). Then, there exists a real root
q\in(0,1)
of the quartic equation

1-(4+\alpha)q+(6+2\alpha+\beta)q^{2}-(4+\alpha)q^{3}+q^{4}=0,

(76)

satisfying

1-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{2\beta}+\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}<q<1-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}.

(77)

Let

\hat{h}=\left((2+\alpha+\beta)q-(3+\alpha)q^{2}+q^{3},\,q-1,\,0,\ldots,0\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},

and define

h=\frac{\hat{s}^{4}}{L_{x}s_{\min}^{2}}\hat{h}.

(78)

Then, an approximate solution
(\hat{x}^{*},\hat{y}^{*})
can be constructed with
\hat{y}_{2}^{*}
given by

\hat{y}_{2,i}^{*}=q^{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(79)

Moreover, the approximation error satisfies

\left\|y_{2}^{*}-\hat{y}_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}q^{\ell}.

Proof.

Let
r=\frac{1}{1-q}
. The quartic equation (
76
) transforms to the polynomial equation

P(r):=1+\alpha r+(\beta-\alpha)r^{2}-2\beta r^{3}+\beta r^{4}=0.

Let

\bar{r}=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{1}{4}},\quad\underline{r}=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{2\beta}+\frac{1}{4}}.

Then
P(\bar{r})=1>0
, and

P(\underline{r})=1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\beta}=1-\frac{\left(2\frac{s_{\min}^{2}}{\hat{s}^{2}}+\frac{L_{x}L_{y}}{\hat{s}^{2}}\right)^{2}}{4\cdot\frac{s_{\min}^{4}}{\hat{s}^{4}}}<0.

Therefore, there exists a root
r\in(\underline{r},\bar{r})
, which implies the existence of a root
q\in(0,1)
satisfying (
77
). (Actually, the root has a closed-form expression:
q=\frac{4+\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-4\beta}-\sqrt{\left(4+\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-4\beta}\right)^{2}-16}}{4}
.)

By Lemma
J.3
and the definition of
h
in (
78
), the vector
y_{2}^{*}
satisfies

(A^{4}+\alpha A^{2}+\beta I)y_{2}^{*}=\hat{h},

which corresponds to the linear system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(2+\alpha+\beta)y_{2,1}^{*}-(3+\alpha)y_{2,2}^{*}+y_{2,3}^{*}=(2+\alpha+\beta)q-(3+\alpha)q^{2}+q^{3},\\
&-(3+\alpha)y_{2,1}^{*}+(6+2\alpha+\beta)y_{2,2}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,3}^{*}+y_{2,4}^{*}=q-1,\\
&y_{2,1}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,2}^{*}+(6+2\alpha+\beta)y_{2,3}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,4}^{*}+y_{2,5}^{*}=0,\\
&\vdots\\
&y_{2,\ell-4}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,\ell-3}^{*}+(6+2\alpha+\beta)y_{2,\ell-2}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,\ell-1}^{*}+y_{2,\ell}^{*}=0,\\
&y_{2,\ell-3}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,\ell-2}^{*}+(6+2\alpha+\beta)y_{2,\ell-1}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,\ell}^{*}=0,\\
&y_{2,\ell-2}^{*}-(4+\alpha)y_{2,\ell-1}^{*}+(5+2\alpha+\beta)y_{2,\ell}^{*}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(80)

By construction (
79
), the approximate solution
\hat{y}_{2}^{*}
satisfies the first
\ell-2
equations exactly. The last two equations yield residuals:

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{2,\ell-3}^{*}-(4+\alpha)\hat{y}_{2,\ell-2}^{*}+(6+2\alpha+\beta)\hat{y}_{2,\ell-1}^{*}-(4+\alpha)\hat{y}_{2,\ell}^{*}

\displaystyle=q^{\ell+1},

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{2,\ell-2}^{*}-(4+\alpha)\hat{y}_{2,\ell-1}^{*}+(5+2\alpha+\beta)\hat{y}_{2,\ell}^{*}

\displaystyle=-q^{\ell}+(4+\alpha)q^{\ell+1}-q^{\ell+2}.

Therefore, the residual error satisfies

\beta\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|(A^{4}+\alpha A^{2}+\beta I)(\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*})\right\|_{2}\leq(7+\alpha)q^{\ell},

which implies

\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}q^{\ell}.

∎

By
Section

J.3
, the zero-chain property in
Section

J.1
, and the choice of
h
in (
78
), we can see that

\mathcal{H}_{y_{2}}^{2k-1},\mathcal{H}_{y_{2}}^{2k}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\left\{h,A^{2}h,\ldots,A^{2k-2}h\right\}=\mathrm{span}\left\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+1}\right\},

(81)

for any
k\geq 1
. This implies that the only possible nonzero entries of
y_{2}^{2k-1},y_{2}^{2k}
are within the first
k+1
components. This structure will be useful in lower bounding the errors
\left\|y_{2}^{2k}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}
, and similarly for
\left\|y_{2}^{2k-1}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}
.

Lemma J.23
.

Suppose
L_{x},L_{y}>0
and
s_{\max}\geq\sqrt{5}s_{\min}>0
. Assume
1\leq k\leq\frac{\ell}{2}-1
and
\ell\geq 2\log_{q^{-1}}\left(\left(2+2\sqrt{2}\right)\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}\right)
. Then,

\left\|y^{2k}-y^{*}\right\|_{2}\geq\left\|y_{2}^{2k}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{q^{k+1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{q^{k+1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{s_{\min}}{s_{\max}}\left\|y^{0}-y^{*}\right\|_{2},

(82)

where
y^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}^{0}\\
y_{2}^{0}\end{pmatrix}=0
is the initialization.

Proof.

By (
81
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|y_{2}^{2k}-\hat{y}_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=k+2}^{\ell}q^{2i}=q^{2k+2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-k-1}q^{2i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q^{2k+2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q^{2i}=\frac{q^{2k+2}}{2}\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{q^{2k+2}}{2}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-\hat{y}_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where the last inequality uses the fact that
k\leq\frac{\ell}{2}-1
and
q<1
.

Since
\ell\geq 2\log_{q^{-1}}\left(\left(2+2\sqrt{2}\right)\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}q^{\ell}\leq\frac{q^{k+1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\cdot 2\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}q^{\ell-k-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q^{k+1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}\left/\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right..

Then, we conclude

\displaystyle\left\|y_{2}^{2k}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|y_{2}^{2k}-\hat{y}_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}-\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q^{k+1}}{\sqrt{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-\hat{y}_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}-\left(1+\frac{q^{k+1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q^{k+1}}{\sqrt{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}-\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left\|\hat{y}_{2}^{*}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q^{k+1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{0}-y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

Finally, since
y_{1}^{*}=-\frac{\hat{s}}{s_{\min}}Ay_{2}^{*}
, we have

\left\|y_{1}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\hat{s}}{s_{\min}}\left\|y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}

so

\left\|y^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|y_{1}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{4\hat{s}^{2}}{s_{\min}^{2}}\right)\left\|y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{s_{\max}^{2}}{s_{\min}^{2}}\left\|y_{2}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

This completes the proof.
∎

J.3
, and
J.3
, we obtain the following lower bound for first-order methods on problems under Assumption

Theorem J.24
.

Let positive parameters
L_{x},L_{y}>0
and
s_{\max}\geq\sqrt{5}s_{\min}>0
be given. Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Then there exists a problem instance of the form (
10
), satisfying Assumption
4
, with
f,M,g
specified in (
72
) and
h
defined in (
78
), such that

\ell\geq\max\left\{2\log_{q^{-1}}\left((2+2\sqrt{2})\cdot\frac{7+\alpha}{\beta}\right),\ k+1\right\},

where
\alpha,\beta
are defined in (
74
) and
q
is the solution to (
76
) satisfying the bounds in (
77
).

Then, for this problem, any approximate solution
(x^{k},y^{k})\in\mathcal{H}_{x}^{k}\times\mathcal{H}_{y}^{k}
generated by a first-order method as described in Definition
J
satisfies the following lower bounds:

{\Phi_{x}}\left({x}^{k}\right)-{\Phi_{y}}\left({y}^{k}\right)\geq q^{k+3}\frac{s_{\min}^{4}\left\|y^{0}-y^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{16L_{x}s_{\max}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|y^{k}-y^{*}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{q^{\frac{k+3}{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{s_{\min}}{s_{\max}}\left\|y^{0}-y^{*}\right\|_{2}.

(83)

Remark J.25

(Lower bound and comparison)
.

As a result, if we require
\|y^{k}-y^{*}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
, then the number of iterations needed is at least

k\geq 2\frac{\log\left(\frac{s_{\min}\|y^{0}-y^{*}\|_{2}}{2\sqrt{2}s_{\max}\epsilon}\right)}{\log\left(q^{-1}\right)}=\Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{s_{\max}^{2}}{s_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{L_{x}L_{y}s_{\max}^{2}}{s_{\min}^{4}}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right),

(84)

where the equality follows from

\log\left(q^{-1}\right)=\Theta(1-q)=\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)=\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{s_{\min}^{4}}{s_{\max}^{2}s_{\min}^{2}+L_{x}L_{y}s_{\max}^{2}}}\right),

for sufficiently large
\frac{s_{\max}}{s_{\min}}
.
This lower bound is also implied by Theorem 2 of
22
in the convex–concave case; compared with their framework, our construction is more direct and yields an explicit finite-dimensional hard instance with fully specified parameters, which makes the dependence on
s_{\min},s_{\max},L_{x},
and
L_{y}
transparent.
Finally, note that a dependence on
\frac{s_{\max}}{s_{\min}}
(or
\frac{L_{xy}}{\mu_{xy}}
in the notation of
22
) is also needed in addition to
\sqrt{\frac{L_{x}L_{y}s_{\max}^{2}}{s_{\min}^{4}}}
, since it is possible that
L_{x}L_{y}\leq s_{\min}^{2}
.
