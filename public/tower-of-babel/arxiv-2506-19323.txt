PDE methods for extracting normal vector fields and distance functions of shapes
Takahiro Hasebe, Jun Masamune, Hiroshi Teramoto and Takayuki Yamada

Abstract
Partial differential equations can be used for extracting geometric features of shapes. This article summarizes recent methods to extract the normal vector field from an elliptic equation proposed by Yamada and from the heat equation, and also a method to extract the (signed) distance function from an elliptic equation that generalizes Varadhan’s in 1967.

Introduction

Extracting geometric features of shapes from partial differential equations (PDEs) has been developed in the literature, especially in the context of image analysis and engineering, see e.g.
BF
;
crane2013geodesics
;
han2004cortical
;
yamada2019geometric
;
yamada2019thickness
and references therein. In particular, a construction of signed distance functions using PDEs is broadly useful in topology optimization
AJT04
.

Motivated by the above situation and inspired by physical intuitions, we developed PDE methods to extract the normal vector fields of sets
M1
and the (signed) distance functions of sets
M
.
The present paper summarizes these results with some progress, including mathematical proofs or a sketch of proofs. Notice that “thickness” of shapes is another important geometric feature. Thickness is discussed in

;
yamada2019geometric
;
yamada2019thickness
and is omitted in the present article.

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}\left(a\nabla s-{\rm Id}\chi_{A}\right)+(1-\chi_{A})s=0&\text{in}\quad\Omega\\
s=0&\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1)

where

It is shown that

We also discuss a slightly different equation

\tilde{s}=0&\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2)

Section

analyzes a solution
h(t,x)
to the Cauchy problem of the heat equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h(t,x)=\Delta h(t,x),\\
h(0,x)=\chi_{A}(x),\qquad\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{cases}

(3)

In Subsection

, it is shown that

In Section

such that

.
Rate of convergence is also provided.

2
Normal vector field: elliptic equations proposed by Yamada

Let
A
and
\Omega
be open bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}~(N\geq 2)
such that the closure
\overline{A}
is contained in
\Omega
. The boundary of
A
is assumed to be Lipschitz in the sense of
(
Leo
, Definition 9.57)
, i.e., for each
x\in\partial A
there is a neighborhood
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
x
such that
\partial A\cap U
can be represented as the graph of a Lipschitz function in some orthogonal basis. Lipschitz continuity implies the differentiability almost everywhere and allows us to define the surface measure of
\partial A
denoted by
\sigma
; see e.g.
(
Leo
, Example 9.56)
. Then we can define the normal vector of the surface
\partial A
, oriented outwards from
A
and defined
\sigma
-almost everywhere. The normal vector at
x\in\partial A
is denoted by
n(x)
.

The standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is signified by
(\cdot,\cdot)
and the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product in the matrix space
M_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is denoted by
(A:B):=\text{Tr}(A^{T}B)
. The inner product in the
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued Sobolev space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
is given by

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}:=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\varphi:\nabla\psi)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\langle\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi_{j},\partial_{x_{i}}\psi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{N}),\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\dots,\psi_{N})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
.

Equation (
1
) is formulated in the weak form

a\int_{\Omega}(\nabla s:\nabla\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega\setminus A}(s,\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial A}(n,\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\qquad\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N},

(6)

where
a
is a positive parameter.
On the right hand side, the function
\varphi
on the surface
\partial A
, sometimes denoted by
\varphi|_{\partial A}
, is defined via the trace theory, see e.g.
(
Leo
, Theorem 18.1)
.
In particular, the inequality

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}}\leq C_{1}\|\varphi\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}

(7)

holds for some constant
C_{1}>0
independent of
a
and
\varphi
, so that the surface integral in (
6
) is a bounded linear functional on
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
. By Lax-Milgram’s theorem (see e.g.
(
B11
, Corollary 5.8)
), Equation (
6
) has a unique solution
s=s_{a}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
.

Theorem 2.1
(
M1
)

The following holds:

\lim_{a\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega\setminus A}(s_{a},\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial A}(n,\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\qquad\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}.

(8)

Moreover, the strong convergence
as_{a}\to 0
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
holds.

Remark 1

Assertion (
8
) means that the vector-valued function
s_{a}|_{\Omega\setminus A}
converges to the vector-valued measure
n(x)\delta_{\partial A}(\mathrm{d}x)
in the dual space of
\{\varphi|_{\Omega\setminus A}:\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}\}
, where
\delta_{\partial A}
stands for the finite Borel measure on
\Omega\setminus A

\delta_{\partial A}(B):=\text{Area}(B\cap\partial A)=\int_{B\cap\partial A}\mathrm{d}\sigma,

(9)

which is singular to the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega\setminus A
.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
2.1
)

By taking the test function
\varphi=s_{a}
, the weak form yields

a\|s_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}\leq a\|s_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}+\|s_{a}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus A)^{N}}^{2}=\int_{\partial A}(n,s_{a})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(10)

By the Schwarz inequality and (
7
), the last surface integral is further bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial A}(n,s_{a})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|n\|_{L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}}\|s_{a}\|_{L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}}=\sqrt{\text{Area}(\partial A)}\,\|s_{a}\|_{L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}}

(11)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\|s_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}},

where
C_{2}:=\sqrt{\text{Area}(\partial A)}\,C_{1}
is independent of
a>0
. Combining (
10
) and (
11
) together yields

\|as_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}\leq C_{2}.

(12)

Moreover, (
12
) and (
11
) imply that the surface integral is bounded by
C_{2}^{2}/a
, so that (
10
) (multiplied by
a
) gives

\|as_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{a}s_{a}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus A)^{N}}^{2}\leq C_{2}^{2}.

(13)

Assertion (
8
) is equivalent to that
as_{a}
weakly converges to
0
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
as
a\to 0^{+}
. By the uniform boundedness (
12
), we can extract a subsequence of
\{as_{a}\}_{0<a<1}
as
a\to 0^{+}
, simply denoted by
\{as_{a}\}
, that converges weakly to some
w
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
, see e.g.
(
B11
, Theorem 3.18)
. This implies that the same subsequence
\{as_{a}\}
also weakly converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{N}
to

because the continuous embedding
L^{2}(\Omega)^{N}\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
induces the dual continuous embedding
(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N})^{*}\to(L^{2}(\Omega)^{N})^{*}
. From (
13
), the subsequence
\{\sqrt{a}s_{a}\}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega\setminus A)^{N}
and so
\{as_{a}\}
converges to 0 in
L^{2}(\Omega\setminus A)^{N}
. This shows
w|_{\Omega\setminus A}=0
a.e. According to the definition of
w|_{\partial A}
via the trace theory,
w|_{\partial A}=0
a.e. too. By taking
\varphi=w
in the weak form (
6
), we get

\int_{\Omega}(a\nabla s_{a}:\nabla w)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

We further take the limit
a\to 0^{+}
along the subsequence we have selected above to obtain
\|w\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}=0
, i.e.,
w=0
a.e. By the standard compactness argument, without going to subsequences, the weak convergence
as_{a}\rightharpoonup 0
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
fully follows.

For the notational clarity we set
T\colon H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}\to L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}
to be the bounded linear operator
\varphi\mapsto\varphi|_{\partial A}
. The already established fact
as_{a}\rightharpoonup 0
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
implies the weak convergence
T(as_{a})\rightharpoonup 0
in
L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}
because
f\circ T\in(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N})^{*}
for all
f\in(L^{2}(\partial A)^{N})^{*}
. Hence,

a\int_{\partial A}(n,s_{a})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\langle n,T(as_{a})\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial A)^{N}}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad a\to 0^{+}.

(14)

Selecting the test function
\varphi=as_{a}
in the weak form gives

\|as_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{a}s_{a}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus A)^{N}}^{2}=a\int_{\partial A}(n,s_{a})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

which, together with (
14
), establishes the strong convergence as desired.

Next we discuss Equation (
2
). Its weak form is

a\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\tilde{s}:\nabla\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{s},\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial A}(n,\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\qquad\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}.

As before, a unique solution
\tilde{s}=\tilde{s}_{a}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
exists.

Theorem 2.2

It holds that

\lim_{a\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{s}_{a},\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial A}(n,\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\qquad\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}

(15)

and that
a\tilde{s}_{a}\to 0
strongly in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
.

Remark 2

Similarly to Remark
1
, assertion (
15
) implies that
\tilde{s}_{a}
converges to the
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued finite Borel measure
n(x)\delta_{\partial A}(\mathrm{d}x)
on
\Omega
, where
\delta_{\partial A}
is defined by the same (
9

The convergence primarily holds in the dual space
(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
, so that it particularly holds in the Schwartz distribution space

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
2.2
)

The proof is very similar to Theorem
2.1
. The main difference is that inequality (
13
) is improved into

\|a\tilde{s}_{a}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{a}\tilde{s}_{a}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}\leq C_{2}^{2}.

Therefore, the family

is uniformly bounded in

, which actually makes the arguments for proving

3
Normal vector field: the heat equation method

Let
N\geq 2
and
A
an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In this section,
\kappa\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a fixed
C^{1}
function such that

(
\kappa 1
)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\kappa(r)=0
and
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\kappa(\|x\|^{2})|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
,

(
\kappa 2
)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r\kappa^{\prime}(r^{2})=0
and
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\sup_{b\in K}\left(\|x+b\|\!\cdot\!|\kappa^{\prime}(\|x+b\|^{2})|\right)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
for any compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Let
\kappa_{t}(x):=t^{-N/2}\kappa(\|x\|^{2}/t)
and

h(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\kappa_{t}(x-y)\chi_{A}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad t>0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Assumption
(\kappa 2)
allows us to calculate the gradient

\nabla_{x}h(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{2(x-y)}{t^{N/2+1}}\kappa^{\prime}(\|x-y\|^{2}/t)\chi_{A}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(16)

The goal of this section is to demonstrate that
\nabla_{x}h(t,x)
is approximately oriented toward the normal direction on
\partial A
whenever
x
is a smooth point of the boundary. If
x
is a singular point of the boundary then the normal vector is not defined. In this case, we still investigate the asymptotic behavior of the gradient
\nabla_{x}h(t,x)
.

In the special case where
\kappa(r)=(4\pi)^{-N/2}\exp(-r/4)
, of course
h
is a solution to the heat equation (
3
). Since most of the results hold for general kernels
\kappa
and the proof is almost the same, we deal with general
\kappa
.

3.1
Smooth boundary points

Let

The boundary

Example 1
(Heat equation)

Proof

Remark 8

Equation (
36
) includes Equation (
35
) as the special case
f\equiv 0
and
g\equiv 1
. To be precise, this case is excluded due to Assumption
3
; however, our proof can be easily fixed to treat this case and the proof becomes much simpler. We can actually thus show, under the assumption that
A
consists of finitely many connected components of
C^{1,1}
-boundary, that

\sup_{x\in\overline{A}}\left|-\sqrt{a}\log q_{a}(x)-d(x,\partial A)\right|\leq C\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{a}\quad\text{for all}\quad 0<a<\frac{1}{2}

for some constant
C=C(A)>0
.

Remark 9

Similar problems are discussed in the context of the method of vanishing viscosity for viscosity solutions to Hamilton–Jacobi equations.
First, the Cole-Hopf transform
p_{a}(x)=-\sqrt{a}\log u_{a}(x)
transforms our equation (
4
) to

\begin{cases}-\sqrt{a}\Delta p_{a}+|\nabla p_{a}|^{2}-1=-f\exp(\frac{p_{a}}{\sqrt{a}})&\text{in}\quad\Omega,\\
p_{a}=-\sqrt{a}\log g&\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Recalling that
f=0
in
A
, we have

-\sqrt{a}\Delta p_{a}+|\nabla p_{a}|^{2}-1=0\quad\text{in}\quad A.

(38)

Equations that generalize (
38
) are analyzed in
FS86
and
Tra11
with zero boundary condition on
\partial A
. More precisely, an asymptotic expansion as
a\to 0^{+}
was obtained in
(
FS86
, Theorem 5.1)
and the rate of convergence
a^{\frac{1}{4}}
was obtained in
(
Tra11
, Theorem 3.3)
. In our problem, however, the values of
p_{a}
on
\partial A
are unknown and the major part of our proof is devoted to the estimate of these boundary values. Consequently we obtained the better rate of convergence
a^{\frac{1}{2}-\tau}
or
\sqrt{a}\log(1/a)
.
We also mention that in
(
L
, Proposition 6.1)
(see also the remark after the proposition) the rate of convergence
a^{\frac{1}{4}}
has also been obtained when the domain is the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

If
f
is a characteristic function, then we can also analyze
u_{a}
on
\Omega\setminus A
and extract the signed distance function from the PDE. The proof is just a slight modification of Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.3
(
M
)

We select
f:=C^{*}\chi_{\Omega\setminus\overline{A}}
with any constant
C^{*}\geq\sup_{x\in\partial\Omega}g(x)
and define

U_{a}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
as in (
5
). Then there exists
C=C(\Omega,A,C^{*},g)>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\Omega^{*}}|U_{a}(x)-\hat{d}(x,\partial A)|\leq C\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{a}\quad\text{for all}\quad 0<a<\frac{1}{2},

where
\hat{d}
is the signed distance function

\hat{d}(x,\partial A):=\begin{cases}-d(x,\partial A),&x\in\overline{A},\\
d(x,\partial A),&x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{A}\end{cases}

and
\Omega^{\ast}:=\{x\in\Omega\colon d(x,\partial A)\leq d(x,\partial\Omega)\}
.

4.2
Sketch of proofs

Because the original proof is lengthy, the proof is sketched below so that the idea can be grasped. The reader is referred to the original article
M
for further details.

[1. Positivity and uniform bound for
u_{a}
]
It is known that

\displaystyle 0<u_{a}(x)\leq M\quad\text{ for all }x\in\Omega,

(39)

where
M:=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in\Omega}f(x),\sup_{x\in\partial\Omega}g(x)\right\}
, see e.g.
(
B11
, Theorem 9.27 and footnote 34)
. A slightly different proof is given in
M
.

[2. Lower bound for
-\sqrt{a}\log u_{a}(x)
]
The proof follows the lines of Varadhan’s
V67
. Since
f=0
on
A
, the function
u_{a}
restricted to
A
satisfies

-a\Delta u_{a}+u_{a}=0\quad\text{in}\quad A

and so
u_{a}|_{\overline{A}}\in C^{2}(A)\cap C(\overline{A})
.
We fix
y\in A
, set
\varepsilon:=d(y,\partial A)
and consider a unique solution
w
in
C^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))\cap C(\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(y)})
to the equation

\begin{cases}-a\Delta w+w=0&\text{in}\quad B_{\varepsilon}(y),\\
w=M&\text{on}\quad\partial B_{\varepsilon}(y).\end{cases}

(40)

Since
u_{a}\leq w
on
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(y)
, the comparison principle yields
u_{a}\leq w
in
B_{\varepsilon}(y)
. The function
w
has the explicit form

w(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{M\int_{0}^{\pi}\cosh(|x-y|\cos\theta/\sqrt{a})\sin^{N-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{\int_{0}^{\pi}\cosh(\varepsilon\cos\theta/\sqrt{a})\sin^{N-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}&\text{if }N\geq 2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{M\cosh(|x-y|/\sqrt{a})}{\cosh(\varepsilon/\sqrt{a})}&\text{if }N=1.\end{cases}

(41)

Some straightforward estimates of
w
entail

\displaystyle-\sqrt{a}\log u_{a}(y)-d(y,\partial A)\geq c\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{a},\quad y\in A,~0<a<\frac{1}{2},

(42)

for some constant
c>0
independent of
a
and depending on the dimension
N
, the global bound
M
and the diameter of
A
.

[3. Upper bound for
-\sqrt{a}\log u_{a}(x)
]
Let

\beta_{a}:=\inf_{y\in\partial A}u_{a}(y).

Since
u_{a}
is continuous function on
\overline{A}
and is positive, the above infimum is attained at a point in
\partial A
and
\beta_{a}
is positive.

By contrast to the lower bound, we now take a ball outside of
A
and construct a comparison function. Let
y\in A
be fixed and
\sigma\in\partial A
be a point such that
|y-\sigma|=d(y,\partial A)
. For each
0<a\ll 1
, there exists a ball
B_{\sqrt{a}}(\tilde{y})\subseteq\Omega\setminus A
tangent to
\partial A
at
\sigma
. Let
z
be a unique classical solution to

\begin{cases}a\Delta z=z&\hskip-42.67912pt\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\sqrt{a}}(\tilde{y}),\\
z=\beta_{a}&\hskip-42.67912pt\quad\text{on}\quad\partial B_{\sqrt{a}}(\tilde{y}),\\
\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to+{\infty}}z(x)=0.&\end{cases}

By the maximum principle,
z\leq\beta_{a}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\sqrt{a}}(\tilde{y})
and hence on
A
.
Since now
u_{a}\geq\beta_{a}\geq z
on
\partial A
, we get
u_{a}\geq z
on
A
. Again,
z
has an explicit form, which is omitted here.
Estimating
z(y)
we arrive at

\displaystyle-\sqrt{a}\log u_{a}(y)-d(y,\partial A)\leq\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{a}}+c^{\prime}\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{a}

(43)

for all
y\in A
and
0<a<\frac{1}{2}
, where
c^{\prime}>0
is a constant depending only on the dimension
N
and the diameter of
A
.

The above arguments more or less followed the lines of
V67
too. In the case of
q_{a}
, the boundary value was
1
and so there was no need to study
\beta_{a}
. In our case, however, we need to estimate
\beta_{a}
, which requires substantially new ideas. The next steps 4–7 are devoted to
\beta_{a}
.

[4. Upper bound for
\sqrt{a}\log(1/\beta_{a})
]

For each
a>0
, we choose a point
y_{a}\in\partial A
such that
u_{a}(y_{a})=\beta_{a}
, which exists by the continuity of
u_{a}
.
We prove that, for any
0<\varepsilon<d(\partial A,\partial\Omega):=\inf\{|x-y|:(x,y)\in\partial A\times\partial\Omega\}
and
a>0
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{a}}\leq\varepsilon-\sqrt{a}\log\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y_{a}}({u_{a}}).

(44)

Again we begin with constructing a comparison function. For each
0<\eta<d(\partial A,\partial\Omega)
, let
u_{a}^{\eta}
be a unique classical solution to

\begin{cases}a\Delta u_{a}^{\eta}=u_{a}^{\eta}&\quad\text{in}\quad B_{\eta}(y_{a}),\\
u_{a}^{\eta}={u}_{a}&\quad\text{on}\quad\partial B_{\eta}(y_{a}).\end{cases}

Now the boundary values are not constant and so an explicit form of
u_{a}^{\eta}
is hopeless. Still, the value of
u_{a}^{\eta}
at the central point
y_{a}
is explicit as shown in
Kuz
. Actually, the formula for
u_{a}^{\eta}(y_{a})
is exactly formula (
41
) evaluated at
x=y=y_{a}
by replacing
M
with the sphere mean of
u_{a}
over
\partial B_{\eta}(y_{a})
, denoted by
\mathbf{m}_{\eta}^{y_{a}}({u}_{a})
. After some estimates, we can obtain

\displaystyle u_{a}^{\eta}(y_{a})\geq\frac{\mathbf{m}_{\eta}^{y_{a}}({u}_{a})}{\cosh(\eta/\sqrt{a})}.

(45)

As in step 1 above, applying
(
B11
, Theorem 9.27)
to the function
u_{a}-u_{a}^{\eta}\in H_{0}^{1}(B_{\eta}(y))
yields
u_{a}\geq u_{a}^{\eta}
in
B_{\eta}(y)
. Combining (
45
) with
u_{a}(y_{a})\geq u_{a}^{\eta}(y_{a})
, applying
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\,\eta^{N-1}\,\mathrm{d}\eta
, and recalling that the volume mean and sphere mean are related via

\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y}(h)=\frac{N}{\varepsilon^{N}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{m}_{\eta}^{y}(h)\eta^{N-1}\,\mathrm{d}\eta,

we obtain (
44
).

[5. Lower bound for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y_{a}}({u_{a}})
]
This part is proven via the weak form of
u_{a}
. The idea is that if we could take the test function
\varphi
in (
36
) to be the characteristic function
\chi_{B_{\varepsilon}(y_{a})}
, then the second term would yield the volume mean of
u_{a}
. Of course the characteristic function is not in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, but approximation via mollifiers works. We can neglect the first term of (
36
) thanks to the small coefficient
a
. Consequently, we obtain

\sup_{y\in\partial A}|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y}(u_{a})-\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y}(f)|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad a\to 0^{+}.

(46)

Since
\partial A
is the boundary of the open set
[f>0]
, the volume mean
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y}(f)
is positive for all
y\in\partial A
. By the continuity of
y\mapsto\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y}(f)
, it has a positive minimum on
\partial A
. Combined with (
46
), this gives a uniform lower bound
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y}(u_{a})\geq\alpha(\varepsilon)>0
for some
\alpha(\varepsilon)>0
independent of
a
.

[6. Conclusion: uniform convergence]

Considering (
44
) and the lower bound
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y_{a}}({u_{a}})\geq\alpha(\varepsilon)
, we obtain

\limsup_{a\to 0^{+}}\sqrt{a}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{a}}\leq 0.

This fact combined with inequalities (
42
) and (
43
) finishes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

[7. Rate of convergence]
In view of (
42
) and (
43
), a good upper bound for
\sqrt{a}\log(1/\beta_{a})
is desirable. For this, we choose
\varepsilon=a^{\frac{1}{2}-\tau}
in (
44
) as a function of
a
(
0<\tau<\frac{1}{2}
is arbitrarily small but fixed) and give a bound for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{y_{a}}(u_{a})
. This can be achieved by carefully estimating the convergence (
46
). In the estimation we use the Schwarz inequality where the norms
\|u_{a}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))},\|\nabla u_{a}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))}~(y\in\partial A)
appear. The mere use of the uniform bound
|u_{a}(x)|\leq M
results in
\|u_{a}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))}^{2}\leq C\epsilon^{N}
. This bound is kind of optimal if
\zeta=0
, e.g., in case
f=\chi_{\Omega\setminus\overline{A}}
. However, the function
u_{a}
is close to
f
near
\partial A
(see (
46
)) and therefore, in case
\zeta>0
, the values of
u_{a}
are likely to be close to
0
near
\partial A
, so that
\|u_{a}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))}^{2}\leq C\epsilon^{N}
might be far from optimal. In fact, by an iterative argument of local energy estimates, we can prove

\|u_{a}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))}^{2}+a\|\nabla u_{a}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\varepsilon}(y))}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{N+2\zeta},\qquad 0<a<1,y\in\partial A

for some uniform constant
C
independent of
y\in\partial A
and
a
. With this estimate, the convergence (
46
) can be finely estimated and then Theorem
4.2
follows.
