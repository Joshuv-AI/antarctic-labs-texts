Dengue haemorrhagic fever complicated with intravascular haemolysis, coagulopathy and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis: a case report
Authors: Malsha Dangedara, Nadeesha Muthumala, Jeewanthi Abeysinghe, Rahal Jayasundera, Bernadene Fernandopulle, Shehan Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF) may occasionally manifest with atypical or severe complications under the umbrella of expanded dengue syndrome (EDS). Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and intravascular haemolysis are rare but potentially fatal complications that require a high index of suspicion, particularly when clinical deterioration persists beyond the critical phase.Case presentationWe report a previously healthy 19-year-old woman who presented with DHF and subsequently developed persistent fever, worsening transaminitis, rising bilirubin levels, haemoglobinuria, and progressive cytopaenia despite completion of the critical phase. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated markedly elevated ferritin (> 40,000 ng/mL), hypertriglyceridaemia, hypofibrinogenaemia, and features of intravascular haemolysis, including indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, reticulocytosis, elevated LDH and a negative direct antiglobulin test. Ultrasonography revealed new splenomegaly. Bone marrow aspirate confirmed haemophagocytosis. She met six HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria, supporting a diagnosis of secondary HLH triggered by acute dengue infection. The patient was treated with intravenous dexamethasone (10 mg/m²/day) for 14 days with tapering, alongside transfusion support, N-acetylcysteine, and cautious fluid management. She achieved full clinical and biochemical recovery.ConclusionThis case highlights a rare but severe manifestation of EDS where DHF was complicated by intravascular haemolysis and secondary HLH. Persistent fever, worsening liver dysfunction, cytopaenia or coagulopathy beyond the expected course of dengue should prompt early evaluation for HLH. Timely diagnosis and corticosteroid therapy can be lifesaving.Keywords: Dengue, Expanded dengue syndrome, Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, HLH, Intravascular Haemolysis, Coagulopathy

Introduction

Dengue, a tropical arboviral illness, presents with a spectrum of manifestations ranging from of asymptomatic illness and mild febrile illness to severe complications such as dengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS) [1, 2]. Another well-described entity is expanded dengue syndrome (EDS) which includes atypical or unusual manifestations with involvement of organ systems or severe complications that cannot be solely explained by plasma leakage or bleeding [3]. These include acute hepatic failure, acute renal failure, dengue myocarditis and encephalitis [4–7]. EDS can also manifest as haematological complications such as coagulopathy, intravascular haemolysis (both immune and nonimmune), and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). The latter one is of a rare occurrence [8–11].

HLH is a hyperinflammatory syndrome caused by uncontrolled activation of macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells, which can be fatal if not identified early and treated appropriately [11]. Engulfment of host red blood cells by macrophages (haemophagocytosis) is a characteristic feature of HLH, which can be demonstrated in biopsies of immune tissue such as lymph nodes, spleen, liver and bone marrow [12, 13].

Here, we describe a presentation of DHF complicated with coagulopathy, acute liver failure with intravascular haemolysis and HLH, which was managed with IV dexamethasone, judicious fluid management, transfusion and other supportive measures.

Case report

A previously healthy 19-year-old woman presented with a four-day history of high-grade fever with chills, frontal headache radiating retro-orbitally, anorexia, reduced oral intake, and oliguria. She had no respiratory, gastrointestinal or urinary symptoms. On admission, she was tachycardic (110/min) and hypotensive (85/60 mmHg), improving transiently with 500 ml of normal saline. This suggested possible early plasma leakage or dehydration in dengue, prompting cautious fluid resuscitation according to national guidelines. Examination revealed right iliac fossa tenderness without peritonism, while urinary β-HCG was negative. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leucopaenia of 2.36 × 109/L (Neutrophils 68%, Lymphocytes 25%), thrombocytopaenia (93 × 10⁹/L) and normal haemoglobin (11.7 g/dL) with raised aspartate transaminase (337) and alanine transaminase (108.7) (see Table 1). Initial ultrasonography demonstrated right-sided abdominal lymphadenopathy without appendiceal inflammation or fluid leakage suggestive of DHF. She was managed according to national dengue guidelines in the pre-critical phase.

By hospital admission day 2/ fever day (H2/F5), she had persistent right iliac fossa pain (attributed to mesenteric adenitis), ongoing fever, mild transaminitis (AST > ALT), indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, and recurrent hypotensive episodes requiring repeated saline boluses. Serial haematocrit values were monitored but did not demonstrate significant haemoconcentration (> 20%) while ultrasonography remained negative for plasma leakage supporting the absence of classic plasma leakage despite clinical instability. On day H3/F6, she developed generalised abdominal tenderness with ultrasonic evidence of gallbladder wall oedema and mild ascites. Leucopaenia worsened (1.82 × 10⁹/L) with thrombocytopaenia (84 × 10⁹/L). Surgical evaluation excluded peritonitis. She was managed through the critical phase with 70 ml/h maintenance fluids.

Notably, the fever did not demonstrate a defervescence phase as typically expected in dengue; instead, it persisted continuously beyond the critical phase without an afebrile interval. Furthermore, there was worsening transaminitis, rising bilirubin, hyperlactataemia, haemoglobin drop, and passage of dark urine suggesting haemoglobinuria. Although traditionally used in paracetamol-induced hepatotoxicity, NAC has been shown to confer benefit in non-acetaminophen acute liver failure through antioxidant properties, replenishment of glutathione stores, improvement of hepatic microcirculatory blood flow, and modulation of cytokine-mediated injury [14, 15]. Given the severity of hepatic involvement and the potential for progression to acute liver failure in severe dengue, NAC was commenced as hepatoprotective supportive therapy.

Rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) demonstrated prolonged clotting times, reduced clot amplitude and low maximum clot firmness, consistent with consumptive coagulopathy requiring fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and platelet transfusions (see Table 1). Intravenous meropenem was initiated empirically due to persistent fever beyond the expected critical phase, rising inflammatory markers and concern for secondary bacterial infection.

By day H7/F10, her leukocyte count (14 × 10⁹/L) and platelet count (125 × 10⁹/L) improved; however, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia worsened. Markedly elevated LDH, ferritin, and moderately raised creatine phosphokinase, along with new splenomegaly, raised suspicion for haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). A blood picture indicated haemolysis with polychromasia, fragmented cells, and microspherocytes. The reticulocyte count was elevated (9.07%) while the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was negative, and urinary haemoglobin was confirmed.

Persistent fever by day H9/F12 was accompanied by worsening coagulopathy (prolonged PT, APTT, TT and severe hypofibrinogenaemia). She required additional FFP, cryoprecipitate, vitamin K, and red cell transfusion. Repeat ultrasonography showed hepatosplenomegaly with acute hepatic parenchymal changes. Laboratory trends demonstrated intravascular haemolysis, evidenced by rising indirect bilirubin, markedly elevated LDH, reticulocytosis, haemoglobinuria and a negative DAT, favouring a non-immune mechanism. The combination of hypofibrinogenaemia, prolonged clotting times and ROTEM features were more in keeping with HLH-associated coagulopathy rather than classic dengue-associated DIC.

Dengue infection was clinically suspected from admission based on the epidemiological setting and early laboratory findings of leucopaenia and thrombocytopenia. The dengue NS1 antigen test was performed beyond the early viraemic phase of illness. NS1 sensitivity is highest during days 1–5 of fever and declines thereafter due to immune complex formation and falling viraemia. The IgM result became available later in the illness course, reflecting both timing of antibody seroconversion and laboratory processing intervals rather than delayed diagnostic consideration. Dengue IgM positivity with negative IgG supported a diagnosis of acute primary dengue infection. In addition, circulating NS1 antigen may become undetectable during later stages due to antibody-mediated immune complex formation, particularly as IgM seroconversion occurs.

The fulfilment of multiple HLH-2004 criteria raised strong clinical suspicion for secondary HLH and prompted bone marrow examination for diagnostic confirmation. It revealed increased histiocytes with prominent haemophagocytosis, confirming HLH secondary to acute dengue infection (see Fig. 1). She was commenced on IV dexamethasone 10 mg/m² daily for 14 days with a gradual taper. This led to resolution of fever, normalisation of hepatic function and coagulation parameters, and recovery from acute kidney injury. At one-month and three-month follow-up, she remained clinically well with normal haematological (Leucocytes 6.3 × 109/L, platelets 165 × 109/L and haemoglobin 12.5 g/dL) and biochemical indices (see Fig. 2). Discussion

Hepatic involvement in DHF is common and may present as elevated transaminases due to direct viral injury, immune-mediated hepatocellular damage, or ischaemia due to shock [4]. Features such as persistent fever, right iliac fossa tenderness, thrombocytopaenia, with rising liver enzymes are attributable to this phenomenon. However, this patient had an unusual projection of EDS with intravascular haemolysis and HLH. Ongoing liver dysfunction after the resolution of the critical phase should prompt evaluation for additional aetiology such as HLH, co-infections or secondary bacterial sepsis.

HLH is a hyperinflammatory syndrome resulting from immune dysregulation, with excessive activation of macrophages and lymphocytes leading to a cytokine storm and tissue damage [16, 17]. The HLH-2024 guidelines include identifying a consistent molecular diagnosis or fulfilment of 5 out of 8 specific clinico-laboratory criteria [17, 18]. These include a persistent fever greater than 38.5 °C and the presence of splenomegaly. Cytopaenia affecting two or more of the three haematopoietic lineages in peripheral blood (haemoglobin < 9 g/dL, platelet count < 100 × 10/L, and neutrophils < 1.0 × 10/L in adults) is also indicative. Additional supportive features include hypertriglyceridemia (fasting values > 3.0 mmol/L (265 mg/dL)) and/or hypofibrinogenemia (≤ 1.5 g/L). Evidence of haemophagocytosis in the bone marrow, spleen, liver, lymph nodes, or other tissues further supports the diagnosis. Functional impairment of the immune system is demonstrated by low or absent NK cell activity. Elevated serum ferritin concentrations (≥ 500 µg/L) and increased levels of soluble CD25 (soluble interleukin-2 receptor) ≥ 2400 U/mL are also diagnostic parameters.

HLH can be triggered by many infections such as EBV, CMV, HIV, malignancies, rheumatological conditions and immune deficiency syndromes [19]. Dengue has been identified as a precipitant of HLH, especially in infants, with fewer reports in adults [20]. This patient met 6 out of 8 HLH-2004 criteria. Although bone marrow biopsy remains the gold standard for demonstration of haemophagocytosis, its absence does not preclude a diagnosis [21]. Furthermore, haemophagocytic activity is not pathognomonic of HLH as it may be seen in severe sepsis or other inflammatory states [13]. However, in the appropriate clinical context, it strongly supports the diagnosis. The decision to perform a bone marrow biopsy in this case, despite significant coagulopathy, was made after careful correction of clotting parameters, reflecting a risk-benefit consideration critical in such scenarios.

Intravascular haemolysis (both with immune and non-immune mediation) is also a rare complication of DHF [10, 22, 23]. In this patient, the laboratory evidence, including reticulocytosis, indirect hyperbilirubinemia and elevated LDH coupled with haemoglobinuria, and dropping haemoglobin levels, supported the presence of active haemolysis. The blood picture also revealed polychromasia and nucleated red cells, while the reticulocyte count was elevated, suggesting an active erythropoietic response. In this case, the negative direct antiglobulin test favoured a non-immune mechanism of haemolysis. Several pathophysiological processes may explain this phenomenon in severe dengue and HLH. First, dengue infection is associated with complement activation, which may lead to bystander red cell membrane injury and intravascular haemolysis even in the absence of detectable autoantibodies [24–26]. Second, endothelial dysfunction and capillary leak can promote microangiopathic shear stress, resulting in mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes, supported in this patient by the presence of fragmented red cells on blood film [27, 28]. Third, the hypercytokinaemic state characteristic of HLH, with markedly elevated ferritin and inflammatory mediators, may contribute to macrophage activation and increased erythrophagocytosis, as well as oxidative red cell membrane damage [29–31]. Reports of DAT-negative intravascular haemolysis in dengue further support the plausibility of non-immune mechanisms in this setting [10, 32]. The coexistence of dengue-associated endothelial injury and HLH-related cytokine storm likely created a synergistic environment promoting intravascular red cell destruction in this patient.

During the latter phase of the illness, the patient developed severe coagulopathy. The differential diagnosis included liver dysfunction, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and HLH associated coagulopathy. The D-dimer was markedly elevated, fibrinogen profoundly reduced, platelet count falling below 50 × 10⁹/L, and PT was prolonged. Based on these parameters, the calculated ISTH overt DIC score was 8, consistent with overt disseminated intravascular coagulation [33]. However, the concurrent presence of extreme hyperferritinaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, splenomegaly, bicytopaenia, and bone marrow-confirmed haemophagocytosis yielded a retrospective H-score exceeding 250, corresponding to a > 99% probability of secondary HLH [34]. These findings suggest that the coagulopathy was likely multifactorial, reflecting overlapping dengue-associated DIC and HLH-driven hyperinflammation rather than a single isolated process.

Management of HLH as per HLH-94 or HLH-2004 treatment protocols involves prompt recognition and immunosuppressive therapy to control cytokine production and macrophage activation with corticosteroids as first-line treatment [17]. However, in infection-associated secondary HLH, particularly when triggered by viral infections such as dengue, early recognition and treatment of the underlying trigger together with short-course corticosteroid therapy may be sufficient in selected patients. This patient showed clinical improvement with defervescence, normalisation of liver function and resolution of haematological indices with IV dexamethasone (10 mg/m²/day) for 14 days with tapering, supporting the importance of timely intervention. In severe or refractory presentations, etoposide, cyclosporine, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) may be necessary [21]. However, in infection-associated HLH, immunosuppression must be balanced against the risk of worsening the underlying infection. Several reported adult cases of dengue-associated HLH have demonstrated favourable outcomes with corticosteroid therapy alone alongside supportive care [16, 17, 35].

Thus, this patient had a severe and atypical course of dengue infection, complicated by the coexistence of HLH and intravascular haemolysis as a rare occurrence. Both conditions are associated with immune dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction and cytokine storm. Furthermore, they share similar manifestations of fever, cytopaenia, liver dysfunction and coagulopathy, which may confound diagnosis. Differentiating between evolving dengue complications and HLH requires astute clinical judgement, trending of biomarkers, and occasionally invasive diagnostics. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for secondary HLH in patients with prolonged fever and systemic inflammation after the initial critical phase of dengue, as early treatment may significantly reduce mortality.

Dengue-associated HLH has been reported less frequently in adults than in children, but available adult data suggest a clinically consistent pattern prompting evaluation for HLH. Adult case reports and small series describe improvement in many patients following early corticosteroid therapy alongside supportive care, while delays in recognition are associated with poor outcomes and escalation to second-line immunosuppression in severe cases [8, 20]. A recent narrative review and individual patient data meta-analysis by Sourris et al. reported a pooled mortality rate of 19.2% in dengue-associated HLH, highlighting the substantial risk associated with delayed recognition [35]. The authors noted that early diagnosis and timely initiation of corticosteroid therapy were associated with improved survival, and that many patients were successfully managed with supportive care and dexamethasone alone without the need for escalation to etoposide. These findings align with the favourable clinical course observed in our patient following early steroid initiation.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare but life-threatening manifestation of expanded dengue syndrome complicated by intravascular haemolysis, overt DIC, and secondary HLH. Persistent fever and escalating inflammatory markers beyond the critical phase should prompt early evaluation for HLH. The case report reinforces the need for heightened clinical vigilance and regular biomarker monitoring in dengue patients with protracted systemic inflammation or unexplained multi-organ involvement. Given the highly reported mortality prompt diagnosis and early initiation of appropriate therapy can be lifesaving, underscoring the importance of incorporating HLH into the differential diagnosis of complicated dengue, particularly in adults presenting with prolonged or unusual deterioration after the critical phase.
