Hidden and unlocked firearm storage among Colorado firearm owners
Authors: Allison J Nielsen, Rachel L Johnson, Erin Wright-Kelly, Joseph A Simonetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the proportion of Colorado firearm owners who store firearms hidden and unlocked by firearm-owner, firearm-related, and household characteristics, including the presence of children in the household.MethodsData are from the 2023 Colorado Firearm Injury Prevention Survey, an online state-representative survey. Sociodemographic characteristics, number and type of firearms owned, and storage practices were assessed for respondents who reported owning at least one firearm (n = 432). The primary outcome was the proportion of firearm owners storing any firearm hidden and unlocked. Findings are presented as weighted percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsAmong Colorado firearm owners in 2023, 47.2% (95% CI: 40.7, 53.9) stored at least one firearm of any type hidden and unlocked, 39.7% (95% CI: 33.3, 46.4) stored at least one handgun hidden and unlocked, and 25.7% (95% CI: 20.5, 31.8) stored at least one long gun hidden and unlocked. Among households with children present, 31.3% (95% CI: 21.6, 42.9) stored at least one firearm hidden and unlocked.ConclusionsStoring firearms hidden and unlocked is common among firearms owners in Colorado. Efforts to prevent firearm injuries, especially by children and adolescents, should emphasize the risks of relying solely on hiding firearms to prevent unauthorized access.Keywords: Firearms, Firearm storage, Injury prevention, Child health, Adolescent health

Introduction

In 2022, firearm injuries surpassed motor vehicle accidents as the leading cause of death for children and adolescents in the United States (Goldstick et al., 2022). Access to a firearm is an important risk factor for firearm injury, and in 2021, approximately 30 million children in the United States resided in households with firearms (Anglemyer et al., 2014; Miller and Azrael, 2022; Wilson et al., 2023). To reduce firearm injury risk, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that clinicians provide guidance to parents and guardians to securely store firearms to prevent unauthorized firearm access among children and adolescents in the household (Lee, 2022).

Despite this guidance, prior studies have demonstrated that a large proportion of firearm owners who reside in households with children also store their firearms unlocked and/or loaded with ammunition (Miller and Azrael, 2022; Schenck et al., 2022). For example, in 2021, a nationally representative study of United States adults found that only 44% of those with firearms and children in the household stored all firearms locked and unloaded (Miller and Azrael, 2022). In lieu of securing household firearms with locking devices, such as firearm lockboxes or safes, some parents or guardians may decide to hide household firearms based on an assumption that children will not find those firearms. However, several studies have demonstrated a significant gap between parental perceptions of firearm access and firearm accessibility among children (Salhi et al., 2021; Baxley and Miller, 2006; Berrigan et al., 2023). In a nationally representative survey of firearm-owning parents, of those who believed that their children could not access household firearms, 37% of their children reported being able to access a firearm within an hour (Salhi et al., 2021; Baxley and Miller, 2006). Consistent with this concern, the American College of Surgeons provides guidance on firearm injury prevention to parents and guardians that specifically discourages the practice of hiding firearms in households with children present (Firearm Safety and Patient Health, 2022).

To our knowledge, little research has focused on the practice of hiding firearms. In 2023, a multi-state study found nearly 60% of firearm owners stored at least one firearm hidden and unlocked, with no difference in the prevalence of hiding handguns versus long guns (Anestis et al., 2023). The current study extends prior research using state-representative survey data from Colorado; a state in which compared to the national average, has a disproportionally high rate of firearm ownership and firearm-related mortality (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026; Gun ownership in America, 2026). We estimated the proportion of Colorado firearm owners who store at least one firearm hidden and unlocked in their household. We differentiate by firearm type (i.e., handguns vs. long guns), number of firearms owned, motivations for firearm ownership, and by various respondent and household characteristics, including the presence of children in the household. Among households with children present, we disaggregate by the ages of those children. Altogether, our aim was to provide detailed insights into the practice of storing household firearms hidden and unlocked among adult firearm owners in Colorado.

Methods

Study design and population

Data for this study came from the publicly available 2023 Colorado Firearm Injury Prevention Survey, which was administered by the research firm Ipsos from May to June 2023. We included adults over 17 years of age who resided in Colorado, had email access, and were not currently institutionalized. The survey was administered electronically in English and Spanish. Participants were selected from the Ipsos KnowledgePanel (924 Colorado adults contacted, 70.3% response), an online panel comprised of approximately 60,000 members designed to represent the United States adult population (Ipsos, 2015). Participants were recruited through address-based sampling methods and selected randomly using a probability-based sampling design. To ensure the sample was representative of the Colorado adult population, another 893 non-random participants were enrolled (of 1189 non-random participants initially contacted, 75.1% qualified based on Colorado residency and age). In 2023, a total of 1520 qualified participants completed the survey; 612 reported the presence of at least one household firearm, and 432 identified as firearm owners.

For the data presented in this study, we included respondents who reported the presence of at least one firearm in the household and identified as a firearm owner (n = 432). Additional details on survey procedures are available elsewhere (Ipsos, 2015). This study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board. We report our findings using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology guidelines (von Elm et al., 2008).

Measures

Survey items assessed sociodemographic characteristics, including sex, age, race, ethnicity, number and ages of children who lived, visited, or stayed in the home, income, education, urbanicity, and military service. Firearm owners were asked about the type (handgun and/or long gun) and number of firearms in the household, and whether each firearm was stored locked, unlocked, and/or hidden. Owners were asked about motivations for owning firearms (check all that apply), including family tradition, family heirloom, occupation, hunting, exercising constitutional rights, managing property wildlife or pests, collecting firearms, and for protection of self, protection of family, and protection of community or property. Motivations for ownership was not assessed specifically for the types of firearms kept (e.g., handgun vs. long gun). We categorized responses as “protection of self, family, community, or property” versus all others.

The primary outcome is whether any household firearm was stored hidden and unlocked. Secondary outcomes included whether handguns were stored hidden and unlocked and whether household long guns were stored hidden and unlocked.

Statistical analysis

We describe the proportion of Colorado firearm owners who report storing firearms hidden and unlocked using weighted percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the sample overall, and by handgun and long gun ownership. We tested associations between the primary or secondary outcomes with demographics and firearm ownership characteristics, and motivations using weighted chi-square tests. Design, post-sampling, and post-stratification weights were applied to account for non-response and to standardize the sample so that findings are representative of the Colorado adult population in 2023, including among subgroups analyzed. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2.

Research ethics

This study used de-identified, publicly available data from the 2023 Colorado Firearm Injury Prevention Survey and did not involve direct interactions with participants. The original Colorado Firearm Injury Prevention Survey study was approved as exempt by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board and adhered to ethical standards for human research, including informed consent and protection for participant confidentiality.

The proportion of Colorado firearm owners who stored any type of firearm hidden and unlocked varied by sociodemographic and household characteristics (Table 2), ranging from 29.6% (95%CI: 16.7, 46.8) among households with children aged 13–17 years to 67.6% (95%CI: 44.9, 84.3) among individuals aged 75 years and older. The proportion of firearm owners who stored any type of firearm hidden and unlocked was significantly higher among those without versus with children in the household (56.7% [95%CI: 48.5, 64.7] vs. 31.3% [95%CI: 21.6, 42.9]) and those who were aged 75 years and older versus younger. Specific to firearm type, the proportion who stored firearms hidden and unlocked was significantly associated with sex (hidden long guns more common among males than females), age group (handguns, long guns), ethnicity (hidden handguns more among those identifying as non-Hispanic than Hispanic), the presence of children in the home (hidden handguns and long guns more common among those without children in the home than those with children in the home), the ages of children in the home (long guns), and education (hidden long guns more common among those with higher rather than lower educational attainment).

Unlocked and hidden firearms among firearm owners with both handguns and long guns

Among Colorado firearm owners with both handguns and long guns, the practice of storing firearms hidden and unlocked varied with the type and number of firearms owned, how other types of firearms were stored, and motivations for firearm ownership (Table 3). In comparison to those who owned 3–4 long guns, a significantly higher proportion of those who owned 1–2 long guns stored at least one long gun hidden and unlocked (16.9% [95%CI: 7.4, 33.8] vs. 37.9% [95%CI: 28.1, 48.9]). In comparison to those who did not store at least one handgun hidden and unlocked, a significantly higher proportion of those who stored at least one handgun hidden and unlocked also stored a long gun hidden and unlocked (11.5% [95%CI: 6.4, 19.9] vs. 60.6% [95%CI: 46.4, 73.2]). In comparison to those who did not store at least one long gun hidden and unlocked, a significantly higher proportion of those who stored at least one long gun hidden and unlocked also stored a handgun hidden and unlocked (24.0% [95%CI: 15.8, 34.7] vs. 78.8% [95%CI: 65.0, 88.2]). In comparison to those who did own firearms for protection, a significantly higher proportion of those who did not own firearms for protection stored a long gun hidden and unlocked (28.7% [95%CI: 21.0, 37.9] vs. 49.3% [31.1, 67.67]).

Discussion

In 2023, nearly half of Colorado firearm owners reported that they stored at least one firearm hidden and unlocked, and this practice was more common for storing handguns rather than long guns. Nearly 40% of firearm owners reported having children who lived, visited, or stayed in the home, and approximately one in three of those households had at least one firearm that was hidden and unlocked. To our knowledge, few studies have reported on the practice of storing firearms hidden and unlocked. Overall, this study finds that storing firearms hidden and unlocked is a common practice among Colorado firearm owners. These findings suggest that efforts are needed to assess this type of storage practice among a larger population of firearm owners and to develop interventions that encourage more secure approaches to storage among firearm owners.

Firearms are a leading cause of death among children in the United States, and access to a household firearm increases the risk for youth firearm injury and death (Goldstick et al., 2022; Anglemyer et al., 2014). As a result, recommendations from medical societies and firearm-related organizations encourage parents and guardians to take steps to prevent unauthorized firearm access by securing firearms in a locked position (Firearm Safety and Patient Health, 2022; Schaechter, 2025; Cassidy, 2021). It is conceivable that some approaches to hiding firearms can be effective in preventing unauthorized firearm access among children and adolescents. However, whether firearms can be effectively hidden is difficult for clinicians to assess when providing anticipatory guidance, as prior work has shown that children report access to firearms even when their parents state otherwise, and accidents have occurred when children gain access to unsecured firearms (Wilson et al., 2023; Salhi et al., 2021; Baxley and Miller, 2006; Berrigan et al., 2023). Accordingly, medical societies and firearm advocacy organizations have specifically advised against hiding firearms as an approach to preventing unauthorized access (Firearm Safety and Patient Health, 2022; Cassidy, 2021).

This study lends important insights into the factors that may impact firearm owners' decisions to hide firearms. Those who did not have children or adolescents in their households were significantly more likely to rely on hidden firearm storage. Among those with children and/or adolescents in their households, the proportion who stored firearms hidden and unlocked varied minimally by the ages of those children and/or adolescents, with the exception of handgun storage. Storing handguns hidden and unlocked was less common when 13- to 17-year-olds were present in the home in comparison to younger children. Those who stored long guns hidden and unlocked were more likely to do so with handguns (and vice versa). Interestingly, those who kept firearms for protection were less likely to store firearms hidden and unlocked. The reasons for this finding are unclear and may be related to the ease and speed with which the firearm owner can access such a handgun when it is hidden. Notably, there are a variety of other reasons that people choose to own firearms, and some firearms have significant financial value, which may influence decisions to hide or secure them. Future efforts should attempt to unpack the relationship between the various reasons for having firearms, the value of those firearms, and how those factors impact storage decisions.

Additional efforts are needed to identify and characterize the factors that firearm owners consider when deciding to hide firearms, and to identify interventions or intervention components that encourage more secure forms of storage. For example, given the high cost of some preferred locking devices (e.g., firearm safes), interventions could consider providing such devices at no or reduced cost (Khazanov et al., 2025; Khazanov et al., 2023; Betz et al., 2023). Given that hiding firearms appears to be common, future studies seeking to characterize household storage should specifically assess this practice by delineating between different approaches to hiding firearms and the extent to which those specific practices are likely to actually prevent unauthorized access (e.g., an antique long gun hidden in an attic box is likely less of a risk than a loaded handgun hidden in a bedroom dresser). Notably, in 2023, Colorado had in place a law requiring firearm owners to securely store firearms using a lock or safe to prevent access by juveniles (Child access prevention and safe storage in Colorado, 2026). The extent to which this or other similar laws, however, actually impact the storage decisions of firearm owners is unclear (Miller et al., 2022).

Youth may seek out household firearms due to curiosity, perceived need for protection, emotional crisis, or other reasons (Lee, 2022). Several studies have identified discrepancies between parental perceptions of household firearm access and reported access by children and youth within the home (Salhi et al., 2021; Baxley and Miller, 2006; Berrigan et al., 2023). For example, in a study conducted among 280 parent-child dyads, of the 70% of parents who said their child could not access a firearm, one in five youth reported being able to access a firearm within five minutes (Salhi et al., 2021; Berrigan et al., 2023). While not assessed in these studies, the practice of having hidden and unlocked firearms (which parents may perceive as secure storage) may help explain this difference.

This study has several limitations. The interpretation of “hidden” firearms is subjective and lacks a standardized definition, which may have introduced variability in responses. Self-reported storage practices may also be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Based on the structure of our survey items, we were unable to assess whether hidden firearms were stored loaded with ammunition. These data are representative of the Colorado firearm owner population and may not be generalizable to other state populations with different sociodemographic characteristics or firearm laws. The associations we identified could be impacted by confounding.

Conclusions

In summary, storing firearms hidden and unlocked is a common practice among firearm owners and households with children in Colorado, and is an altogether understudied practice within the research literature. These findings highlight the need to provide clearer definitions of hidden firearm storage in future work, and to develop interventions that address this practice, especially in households with children.
