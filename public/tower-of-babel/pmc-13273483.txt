Effect of copper and cultivation condition on the transport, metabolism, and potential risk of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings (Pisum sativum Linn.)
Authors: Wenhao Xiong, Qin Tang, Yayong Xia, Sanfei Du, Jing Shi, Kankan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPesticides and heavy metals are two major classes of environmental pollutants, and their environmental fate requires careful assessment. This study investigated the effects of copper (Cu) and cultivation methods on the distribution and metabolism of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings. Bioconcentration factor (BCF) analysis indicated that tolfenpyrad was poorly absorbed by soil-grown pea seedlings (BCF <1) but readily absorbed by hydroponically grown seedlings (BCF ≫ 1). Furthermore, the translocation of tolfenpyrad within the seedlings was limited, with translocation factors ranging from 0.001 to 2.831. The presence of Cu differentially influenced transport mechanisms, likely due to synergistic and antagonistic interactions between the two analytes. Tolfenpyrad dissipated rapidly in pea seedlings, with half-lives of 3.6–3.8 days. The exposure risk was considered negligible, as risk quotients were below 100%. These findings provide valuable insights into the influence of Cu and cultivation conditions on the environmental behavior of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings.Subject areas: soil science, environmental science, pollution, plant biology, plant physiology

Introduction

Tolfenpyrad, a novel pyrazoleamide insecticide and acaricide, acts as a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor. Its mechanism of action involves disrupting the mitochondrial electron transport chain, thereby inhibiting energy production in insects.1 Approved in over 25 countries, this compound is widely used in agriculture. However, its indiscriminate, inefficient, and continuous application has led to issues such as excessive residues in crops and the environment, as well as toxic effects on non-target organisms.2,3 While previous studies have addressed the residue dissipation and potential risks of tolfenpyrad in crops like tea, okra, and cabbage,1,4,5 research on its accumulation, distribution, and transport within crops remains scarce. For instance, Wang et al. reported that tolfenpyrad predominantly accumulates in the roots of tea plants with limited upward transport.6 Chen et al. demonstrated that while tolfenpyrad is readily absorbed by the roots of hydroponically grown romaine and edible amaranth, its translocation within these plants is restricted.7 Furthermore, Liao et al. found that tolfenpyrad is easily absorbed by endive roots in both soil and hydroponic systems, though the latter showed a greater propensity for downward translocation.8 Currently, information regarding the fate of tolfenpyrad in crop ecosystems is limited. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the accumulation, translocation, distribution, metabolism, and exposure risk of tolfenpyrad across a broader range of crops and to examine the influence of exogenous substances—such as heavy metals—and cultivation conditions, including soil and hydroponic systems.

With the advancement of industrialization and urbanization, heavy metal pollution stemming from anthropogenic activities—such as smelting, mining, applying mineral fertilizers and sludge, and using pesticides—has become increasingly severe.9 Heavy metal contamination is prevalent in agricultural soils; typical pollutants include cadmium (Cd, 0.1 mg/kg), mercury (Hg, 0.1 mg/kg), arsenic (As, 7.5 mg/kg), lead (Pb, 23.8 mg/kg), chromium (Cr, 41.0 mg/kg), and copper (Cu, 28.3 mg/kg).10 These heavy metals are predominantly toxic, persistent, and resistant to microbial degradation, leading to their translocation into plants via soil, which adversely impacts plant growth and quality.11,12 The coexistence of heavy metals and pesticides in crops and the environment is well-documented, with research primarily focusing on their chemical and biological toxicities, as well as potential synergistic and antagonistic interactions.13,14 Nonetheless, there is a paucity of research regarding the influence of heavy metals on the behavior and fate of pesticides within crops. Therefore, investigating the accumulation, distribution, translocation, and metabolism of pesticides in ecosystems where heavy metals and pesticides coexist is crucial. Such research is vital for enhancing our understanding of how heavy metals affect the persistence and behavior of pesticides within the crop ecological environment.

Pea seedlings (Pisum sativum L.), characterized by their high content of carotenoids, vitamin C, total phenols, and other bioactive compounds, represent young pea plants with significant potential for sustainable cultivation due to their short harvest cycle and minimal fertilization requirements.15 Cu(II) serves as an essential micronutrient for plant growth; however, excessive levels can exert severe toxic effects, inhibiting development and potentially causing plant mortality at critical soil concentrations.16,17 This study focuses on pea seedlings and Cu(II) as the selected crop and heavy metal, respectively. Initially, a modified QuEChERS (quick, easy, cheap, effective, rugged, and safe) pretreatment method, coupled with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), was developed and validated to quantify tolfenpyrad in pea seedling tissues. Metabolites formed during cultivation were identified using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS), while Cu levels were measured via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Subsequently, the accumulation, translocation, and metabolism of tolfenpyrad were examined in soil-cultivated pea seedlings following both root and foliar applications, with and without Cu exposure. Furthermore, the fate and distribution of tolfenpyrad were investigated in hydroponically grown pea seedlings. Finally, the residual dissipation and exposure risk of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings were evaluated under various treatment conditions. These findings aim to provide a theoretical foundation for comprehensively understanding the impact of heavy metals and cultivation conditions on the environmental fate—encompassing uptake, translocation, and metabolism—of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings.

Results

Effect of Cu and cultivation condition on accumulation, translocation, and distribution of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings

In the uptake trials, the data (Figure 1) indicated that tolfenpyrad was initially absorbed and predominantly distributed within soil samples following root application of the insecticide, both in the presence and absence of Cu. Subsequently, its concentration declined across all treatments. Specifically, in low-dose treatments, the concentration of tolfenpyrad in soil samples decreased from 43.825 mg/kg to 18.739 mg/kg without Cu and from 36.266 mg/kg to 21.952 mg/kg with Cu during the cultivation process. In hydroponic cultivation treatments, tolfenpyrad concentrations ranged from 0.002 to 0.020 mg/L in soil samples. In root samples without Cu, tolfenpyrad concentrations increased from 0.042 mg/kg to 0.375 mg/kg over the first six days and then declined to 0.061 mg/kg over the subsequent eight days. In root samples with Cu, concentrations rose from 0.101 mg/kg at day 0–1.041 mg/kg at day 6, before decreasing to 0.127 mg/kg by day 14 in low dose treatments (Figures 1A and 1C). The concentrations in stem and leaf samples initially increased and subsequently decreased. Additionally, Cu appears to enhance the root absorption capacity and translocation potential of tolfenpyrad. In high-dose treatments (Figures 1B and 1D), similar absorption and translocation potentials for tolfenpyrad were observed; however, tolfenpyrad levels decreased following Cu application. In the hydroponic trials (Figures 1E and 1F), a substantial amount of tolfenpyrad was absorbed and accumulated in the root samples of pea seedlings. Specifically, the concentrations of tolfenpyrad increased from 5.364 mg/kg at day 0 to 45.081 mg/kg at day 2 before decreasing to 7.915 mg/kg by day 14 in the low-dose treatment. In Figure 2, the bioconcentration factor (BCF) values for tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings were consistently below 1, both in the absence and presence of Cu treatments. However, these values increased following the addition of Cu, indicating that tolfenpyrad can be absorbed and accumulated in the roots of pea seedlings, with limited translocation to the stems and leaves. In contrast, for hydroponically grown pea seedlings, the BCF values of tolfenpyrad (Figure S1, supplemental information) were significantly greater than 1, indicating a superior absorption and accumulation capacity compared with soil-cultivated pea seedling roots. The uptake potential of tolfenpyrad was also notably pronounced in hydroponic pea seedlings.18 In Figure S2 (supplemental information), translocation factor (TF) values suggest that tolfenpyrad exhibits a notable potential for translocation between the tissues of soil-cultivated pea seedlings following the application of this insecticide, both in the absence of Cu (TF range: 0.385–1.776) and in its presence (TF range: 0.308–1.501). Conversely, in hydroponic experiments, the translocation of tolfenpyrad from the roots to the stems and leaves of pea seedlings was not significant, as indicated by TFstem/root and TFleaf/root values of less than 1. However, the transport from stems to leaves was more pronounced, with TFleaf/stem values ranging from 0.458 to 2.831.

In the downward translocation experiments, the concentration of tolfenpyrad in leaf samples decreased from 29.889 mg/kg to 13.220 mg/kg. In stem samples, the residue levels increased from 2.082 mg/kg at day 0 to 3.902 mg/kg at day 6 before decreasing to 2.266 mg/kg at day 14. Similarly, root samples exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease in tolfenpyrad concentration under low-dose treatment conditions (without Cu) (Figure 3A). The patterns of tolfenpyrad concentration changes in pea seedling tissues with high-dose treatments (without Cu) mirrored those observed with low-dose treatments, with an overall increase in concentrations (Figure 3B). As illustrated in Figures 3C and 3D, the presence of Cu inhibits the accumulation potential of tolfenpyrad in the leaves of soil-cultivated pea seedlings. Under high-dose treatment, tolfenpyrad concentrations decreased from 52.020 mg/kg to 25.192 mg/kg in the absence of Cu and from 37.990 mg/kg to 20.025 mg/kg in the presence of Cu. In hydroponic experiments (Figures 3E and 3F), the primary site of tolfenpyrad distribution was observed to be the leaves of pea seedlings, attributable to the method of application. Additionally, downward translocation was evident in both low and high dose treatments. For instance, tolfenpyrad concentrations changed from 23.584 mg/kg to 16.796 mg/kg at low doses and from 29.737 mg/kg to 18.534 mg/kg at high doses. The TF values of tolfenpyrad in pea seedling tissues from different treatments are illustrated in Figure 4, indicating that the downward translocation was restricted in both soil-cultivated and hydroponic pea seedlings, as evidenced by TF values less than 1. In soil cultivation trials (both with and without Cu), the transport of tolfenpyrad from leaves to stems was more significant than from leaves to roots or from stems to roots. In contrast, in hydroponic pea seedlings, the downward translocation of tolfenpyrad from upper stems to roots (TF values ranging from 0.265 to 0.654) was more pronounced than the other two translocation pathways (TF values ranging from 0.026 to 0.201).

Distribution of Cu in soil-cultivated pea seedlings after applying the mixture of tolfenpyrad and Cu

Following the root application of tolfenpyrad and Cu, the concentrations of Cu in pea seedling tissues were observed to range from 0.392 to 0.552 mg/kg in roots, 0.248 to 0.580 mg/kg in stems, and 0.211 to 0.748 mg/kg in leaves under low-dose treatment conditions (Figure S3A, supplemental information). Upon increasing the application dose of tolfenpyrad, the Cu concentration ranges were elevated to 0.462–0.649 mg/kg in roots, 0.281–0.605 mg/kg in stems, and 0.258–0.808 mg/kg in leaves (Figure S3B, supplemental information). This indicates an initial increase followed by a decrease in Cu levels within the roots, stems, and leaves of pea seedlings throughout the cultivation process. In experiments assessing downward translocation, a similar trend was observed, with Cu levels initially increasing and then decreasing; a slight increase in Cu concentration was noted with higher tolfenpyrad doses (Figures S3C and S3D, supplemental information). For instance, the concentrations of Cu in pea seedling leaves ranged from 0.622 to 1.366 mg/kg for the low dose and from 0.681 to 1.421 mg/kg for the high dose treatment.

Identification of potential metabolites of tolfenpyrad in pea seedling tissues and cultivation media

Sixteen transformation products of tolfenpyrad were detected in the samples, with their structures, names, and distribution detailed in Table 1. In the soil cultivation trials without Cu, OH-PT, CO-PAM, and CA-T-AM were detected in soil samples, while six metabolites, such as CO-PAM (in stems), CO-PCA (in leaves), CA-T-AM (in stems), T-AM (in roots and leaves), OH-T-AM (in leaves), and OH-T-CA (in roots), were identified in pea seedling tissues. In the soil cultivation (with Cu) trials, two metabolites, CO-PAM and OH-T-OH, were identified in soil samples, while eight transformation products, namely CO-PAM (in stem and leaf), CA-T-AM (in leaf), OH-T-OH (in root), CO-PT (in stem and leaf), DM-PT-OH (in leaf), PCA (in root and stem), OH-T-AM (in stem), and OH-T-CA (in stem), were detected in pea seedling tissues. The variation in metabolic processes between the two soil cultivation trials, with and without Cu, can be attributed to the specific types of enzymes and distinct enzyme activities present in the same crop subjected to different treatments.16,17 In the hydroponic trials, CO-PAM, CO-PT-CA, and PAM were found in nutrient solution samples. Additionally, nine transformation products, including DM-PT (in root), CP-PCA (in leaf), OH-T-OH (in root), CO-PT (in root and leaf), OH-PAM (in root and stem), T-AM (in root), T-CA (in stem), PAM (in root, stem, and leaf), and PCA (in root and leaf), were detected in pea seedlings. The primary metabolic pathways were proposed to involve hydroxylation, carbonylation, hydrolyzation, amination, and demethylation.

Residual dissipation and exposure risk of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultured pea seedlings

In addition to examining the impact of Cu and cultivation conditions on the accumulation, translocation, and metabolism of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings, this study further investigated the residual dissipation and exposure risk of this insecticide in soil-cultivated pea seedlings following foliar application at various doses and frequencies. As presented in Table S1 (supplemental information), the initial concentrations of tolfenpyrad in the edible parts of pea seedlings ranged from 0.87 to 3.65 mg/kg across four treatments, demonstrating a positive correlation with the dosage and frequency of tolfenpyrad application.19 Throughout the cultivation period, the residue levels of tolfenpyrad decreased significantly (p < 0.01), with dissipation percentages exceeding 50% after 14 days of cultivation. The dissipation of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings conformed to a first-order kinetic model, with determination coefficients (r2) ranging from 0.8548 to 0.9448, and half-life values (t1/2) between 3.6 and 3.8 days (Table S2, supplemental information). In pea seedling samples collected 14 days after application of tolfenpyrad at both low and high doses, residue levels were found to be 0.42 mg/kg. Furthermore, the chronic risk quotient (RQc) and acute risk quotient (RQa) metrics were employed to assess the exposure risk of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings for Chinese consumers.20 The acceptable daily intake (ADI) and acute reference dose (ARfD) values for tolfenpyrad were determined to be 0.006 and 0.01 mg/kg body weight, respectively.21 The median residue (STMR) and highest residue (HR) values for tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings ranged from 0.610 to 1.385 mg/kg and 1.680 to 3.771 mg/kg, respectively. According to dietary intake reports for Chinese consumers,22 RQc values (Figure 5A) varied between 15% and 71% across all pea seedling samples. Figure 5B illustrates that the acute exposure risk of tolfenpyrad for Chinese consumers warrants attention, with RQa values ranging from 44% to 116% in pea seedlings collected at 0 and 1 day after final application across all treatments. In contrast, the remaining samples exhibited negligible acute exposure risk, with RQa values between 22% and 96%.23

Discussion

This reduction of tolfenpyrad in soil samples collected from the uptake trials is likely attributable to the root absorption by pea seedlings and degradation by specific soil microorganisms.24 The presence of Cu was observed to diminish the soil’s absorption capacity for tolfenpyrad and extend its persistence. This effect may be due to the inhibitory impact of the heavy metal Cu on the accumulation pathways within soil samples,16 as well as a potential antagonistic interaction with tolfenpyrad, which will be the subject of our future research. In the hydroponic trials, the primary site of distribution was not the nutrient solution, regardless of whether low or high doses were applied. Instead, the majority of residues were detected in the roots of pea seedlings. This observation can be attributed to the compound’s high hydrophobicity (logKow = 5.6) and the well-developed root systems of hydroponically grown pea seedlings.7,25 In contrast, during soil cultivation treatments, tolfenpyrad was progressively absorbed by the roots and subsequently translocated to the aerial parts of the plant, including stems and leaves. The introduction of Cu enhanced the accumulation of tolfenpyrad residues in pea seedling tissues. The findings suggest that tolfenpyrad is absorbed by the roots of soil-cultivated pea seedlings and subsequently translocated to the aerial parts. The differing outcomes between the two dosage treatments may be attributed to the interplay between the synergistic and antagonistic effects of tolfenpyrad and Cu in soil-cultivated pea seedling tissues. At low tolfenpyrad doses, a more pronounced synergistic effect between the two analytes was observed, whereas at high doses, the antagonistic effect predominated, influencing the accumulation and uptake of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings.14 Higher concentration of tolfenpyrad was accumulated in hydroponic pea seedlings roots, which may be attributed to the more developed root systems of hydroponically grown pea seedlings compared with those cultivated in soil, resulting in greater accumulation of tolfenpyrad in the root samples.25,26 Furthermore, the uptake of tolfenpyrad was more efficient in hydroponic pea seedlings than in soil-cultivated counterparts, likely due to the distinct physiological and biochemical characteristics of seedlings grown under different conditions.8 Additionally, tolfenpyrad concentrations in pea seedling tissues, soil, and nutrient solution samples were positively correlated with the application dose, irrespective of Cu application. This observation is consistent with findings in other crops such as romaine, edible amaranth, tea, and endive.6,7,8 This limited translocation is attributed to the high hydrophobicity of tolfenpyrad (logKow = 5.6). Furthermore, the presence of Cu enhances the uptake of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings.27 Previous research has demonstrated that rice plant roots can absorb and accumulate difenoconazole (logKow = 4.4) from soils28 and that fludioxonil, with a logKow of 4.1, is readily absorbed by chrysanthemum roots, although its upward transport is restricted.18

Tolfenpyrad initially accumulated in the leaves of soil-cultivated pea seedlings and subsequently decreased after foliar application due to transport to stems and roots, as well as microbiological dissipation.29 Tolfenpyrad accumulation and downward translocation occurred in soil-cultivated pea seedlings (without Cu), and a positive correlation was observed between residue levels and the application dose of tolfenpyrad. Previous research has indicated similar results, demonstrating that tolfenpyrad predominantly accumulates in the leaves of soil-cultivated endive following foliar application. It is subsequently translocated to the stems and roots, with the distribution potential positively correlated with the application dose of tolfenpyrad.8 Furthermore, the downward translocation of tolfenpyrad was also impeded by Cu, resulting in decreased levels in stems, roots, and soil to varying extents. These outcomes may be attributed to the occupation of tolfenpyrad accumulation sites in pea seedling tissues and soil by Cu, partial obstruction of absorption and downward translocation pathways by Cu, and the existence of an antagonistic interaction between tolfenpyrad and Cu in soil-cultivated pea seedlings during cultivation.14,16 In the hydroponic trials, as the application dose increased, the accumulation of tolfenpyrad became more pronounced, enhancing its transport potential within the tissues of pea seedlings. This suggests a positive correlation between the application dose of tolfenpyrad and its accumulation and downward movement in hydroponically grown pea seedlings.30 Similar findings were reported by Chen et al.,7 who noted that tolfenpyrad was predominantly distributed in hydroponically grown romaine leaves and translocated basipetally to stem and root tissues. However, tolfenpyrad was not detected in nutrient solution samples in either treatment, likely due to the well-developed root systems of hydroponic pea seedlings and the compound’s low water solubility and hydrophilicity.6,31 Furthermore, the potential for downward translocation of tolfenpyrad from leaf to stem samples was more pronounced in soil-cultivated pea seedlings compared to hydroponic pea seedlings. Conversely, the basipetal transport to root samples exhibited the opposite trend. This phenomenon may be attributed to the distinct transport mechanisms of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings under varying cultivation conditions.32 The limited downward translocation potential may be related to the well-developed root system of hydroponic pea seedlings. Previous research has identified phloem as a critical pathway for the downward transport of pesticides in vegetables.33 Tolfenpyrad is capable of being transported downward through phloem in hydroponically grown pea seedlings; however, this transport is less efficient in soil-cultured pea seedlings. The current findings align with the results observed for tolfenpyrad transport in soil-cultivated and hydroponic endive samples,8 yet they differ from those reported for hydroponic romaine and edible amaranth samples.7 These discrepancies may be attributed to the distinct physiological and biochemical characteristics inherent to different crop species.29,34

The absorption and accumulation capacity of Cu in pea seedling tissues can be enhanced by increasing the application dose of tolfenpyrad. This may be due to the ability of Cu to be transported within pea seedlings, coupled with the synergistic interaction between these two analytes affecting Cu distribution in the tissues.13 Furthermore, Cu residues were found to be higher in leaf samples compared with other tissues in both root and foliar application trials. This is likely due to the leaves’ enhanced potential for promoting photosynthesis and their specific physiological and biochemical properties.16,35 The precise mechanisms and pathways underlying the synergistic effect on the fate of Cu in pea seedlings will be elucidated in our future research endeavors.

In the Joint FAO/WHO Meeting on Pesticide Residues (JMPR) reports concerning tolfenpyrad, 24 potential metabolites of tolfenpyrad have been identified in soils and various crops.21 Consequently, these 24 metabolites are monitored using UHPLC-high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS) in pea seedling tissues and environmental matrices, including soil and nutrient solution, collected from diverse soil cultivation and hydroponic trials. Sixteen possible metabolites and five potential pathways were proposed. Distinct metabolites were identified in various pea seedling tissues, potentially attributable to specific metabolic mechanisms, warranting further investigation. In contrast to prior studies,7,8 the transformation products of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings differed from those observed in romaine, edible amaranth, and endive under both soil cultivation and hydroponic conditions. These results align with the findings of Pszczolinska et al.,34 which demonstrated that the metabolic processes of the same pesticide can vary across different crops.

The rapid dissipation of tolfenpyrad may be attributed to the morphology and absorption characteristics of pea seedlings.36 The findings of this study are consistent with the dissipation rates of tolfenpyrad observed in soil-cultivated lettuce, which exhibit half-lives ranging from 3.5 to 4.5 days. However, these results differ from those reported for Shanghai green, Chinese cabbage, and spinach, with half-lives of 2.0–3.1 days, 4.0 to 6.8 days, and 5.3 to 6.6 days, respectively, under similar soil cultivation conditions.1 The observed variation in tolfenpyrad dissipation across different leafy vegetables may be attributed to the distinct physiological and biochemical characteristics inherent to each vegetable, including morphology, growth rate, and growth stage at the time of treatment.37 Notably, the maximum residue limit (MRL) for tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings has not been established by the Chinese regulatory authorities; however, the MRL for Chinese cabbage is set at 0.50 mg/kg.38 The residue data suggested that a pre-harvest interval (PHI) of 14 days is advisable.7 The chronic and acute exposure risk of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings is acceptable (<100%) for various Chinese consumers.5 The dissipation and exposure risk data suggest that pea seedlings cultivated in tolfenpyrad-treated soil and harvested at a PHI of 14 days are safe for Chinese consumers when adhering to the recommended application guidelines.

In summary, an analytical method for detecting tolfenpyrad in pea seedling tissues was developed and validated, demonstrating feasibility for field trials. Subsequently, tolfenpyrad was primarily distributed in the soil due to the application method, with limited upward translocation observed (BCF <1) in soil cultivation treatments without Cu during the uptake trials. The introduction of Cu reduced the accumulation of tolfenpyrad in soil samples and affected its uptake potential in pea seedlings, being attributed to the synergistic and antagonistic interactions between tolfenpyrad and Cu. The accumulation and upward translocation of tolfenpyrad in hydroponic pea seedlings were significantly more pronounced (BCF ≫ 1) compared with those in soil-cultivated pea seedlings. This difference can be attributed to the well-developed root system and distinct physiological characteristics of pea seedlings under hydroponic conditions. In the downward translocation experiments, tolfenpyrad initially accumulated in the leaf tissues of both soil-cultivated and hydroponic pea seedlings, then its concentration decreased gradually due to limited transport (TF < 1) and degradation processes. The presence of Cu inhibited the accumulation and downward translocation of tolfenpyrad, likely because Cu occupied the absorption sites and partially obstructed the translocation pathways of tolfenpyrad. During the cultivation process, sixteen metabolites of tolfenpyrad were identified in pea seedling tissues, soils, and nutrient solutions. The potential metabolic pathways included hydroxylation, carbonylation, hydrolysis, amination, and demethylation. Variations in the number and types of metabolites can be attributed to the specific enzyme types and distinct enzyme activities present in the same crop when subjected to different treatment methods. Furthermore, tolfenpyrad exhibited rapid dissipation in soil-cultivated pea seedlings, with half-lives ranging from 3.6 to 3.8 days. The residues in samples collected at a PHI of 14 days post-application were below 0.50 mg/kg (MRL in China). Consequently, the exposure risk of tolfenpyrad-treated pea seedlings is deemed acceptable for Chinese consumers. These findings contribute valuable insights into the impact of heavy metals and cultivation methods on the accumulation, distribution, and metabolism of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the experimental design relied on a single plant species (pea seedlings) and a specific heavy metal, i.e., Cu(II), which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other crop varieties or pollutants with different chemical properties. Second, the stark contrast in BCFs between soil-cultivated and hydroponically grown pea seedlings highlights a major constraint of the hydroponic model; while useful for mechanistic studies, it fails to account for the complex soil matrix effects, such as organic matter adsorption and microbial degradation, which significantly limit pesticide bioavailability in real-world agricultural settings. Third, the study focused on short-term dissipation (half-lives of 3.6–3.8 days) and immediate metabolic responses in seedlings, leaving the long-term fate of the identified metabolites and the potential for chronic toxicity or residue accumulation in mature edible tissues largely unexplored. Finally, while synergistic and antagonistic interactions were observed, the specific molecular mechanisms governing the competitive transport between Cu(II) and tolfenpyrad were not fully elucidated.

Method details

Plant cultivation

The pea seeds (Species: zhongwan No. 6, genotype: containing dominant alleles such as Le and R) were meticulously washed with deionized water and subsequently soaked for 12 h. They were then placed on moistened filter paper within several sterile glass dishes. The seeds were incubated in an artificial climate chamber, maintained at a temperature of 25 °C and a relative humidity of 70%, for approximately five days. Following this initial cultivation period, the pea seedlings were transferred to separate containers: either seedling barrels filled with soil for soil-based cultivation experiments or vessels containing nutrient solution for hydroponic experiments. These seedlings were then relocated to a greenhouse incubator, where they were subjected to controlled environmental conditions, including a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, with temperatures set at 15 °C during the night and 25 °C during the day, and a relative humidity of 70%. After 14 days of cultivation under these conditions, multiple batches of pea seedlings were prepared for subsequent experimental analyses.

Accumulation, translocation, distribution, and metabolism of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings under soil-cultivation and hydroponic conditions

In accordance with the application protocol for tolfenpyrad outlined by Liao et al.,8 the uptake trials via root application utilized doses of 50 mg/L (low dose) and 80 mg/L (high dose). In contrast, the downward translocation trials via foliar application employed doses of 67.5 g a.i./ha (low dose) and 112.5 g a.i./ha (high dose). The frequency of application was standardized to a single instance across all soil cultivation and hydroponic treatments. For soil-cultivated pea seedlings, the root application involved spraying the pesticide solution onto the soil surrounding the roots, whereas for hydroponic seedlings, the roots were immersed in a nutrient solution containing a specified concentration of tolfenpyrad. The foliar application method, consistent for both soil-cultivated and hydroponic pea seedlings, entailed spraying the tolfenpyrad solution directly onto the leaves. Samples of roots, stems, and leaves from the pea seedlings, as well as samples from the cultivation matrix (soil and nutrient solution), were collected at intervals of 0 (2 h), 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days following the final application of tolfenpyrad. All samples were collected in triplicate (n = 3) and preserved at −18 °C prior to analysis via LC-MS/MS to assess accumulation, translocation, and distribution of tolfenpyrad) and UHPLC-HRMS to identify metabolism of tolfenpyrad.

Effect of Cu on the accumulation, translocation, distribution, and metabolism of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings

The application dosages and frequencies of tolfenpyrad in soil cultivation trials with Cu were consistent with those in trials without Cu, as detailed in the last section. In addition to tolfenpyrad, a Cu concentration of 10 mg/L was incorporated into the pesticide solution for each treatment in the uptake and downward translocation experiments. Samples of roots, stems, and leaves from pea seedlings, as well as soil samples, were collected at intervals of 0 (2 h), 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days following the final application of tolfenpyrad and Cu. All samples were collected in triplicate (n = 3) and stored at −18 °C prior to analysis. The analyses included LC-MS/MS for the accumulation, translocation, and distribution of tolfenpyrad, ICP-MS for Cu concentrations, and UHPLC-HRMS for the metabolism of tolfenpyrad.

Residual dissipation and exposure risk assessment of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated pea seedlings after foliar application

Drawing upon our previous research,1,7 tolfenpyrad was applied to soil-cultivated pea seedlings at dosages of 67.5 g a.i./ha (low dose) and 112.5 g a.i./ha (high dose). The application frequencies were once and twice, utilizing foliar spraying to assess the residual dissipation and exposure risk of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings. The edible portions (above-ground parts) of the pea seedlings were collected in triplicate (n = 3) at intervals of 0 (2 h), 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days following the final application. Prior to instrumental analysis, all samples were preserved at a temperature of −18 °C.

Method validation, quality control and quality assurance

Detection of tolfenpyrad in different matrices: The analysis of this insecticide was performed on a Shimadzu LC-20AD XR LC system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a Sciex API 4000 Q-Trap MS system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA). The LC conditions were: Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm i.d., 5 μm); flow rate of 0.8 mL/min; injection volume of 5 μL; column temperature of 25 °C; mobile phases of 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and acetonitrile (B); elution process of A/B = 9/95 (v/v), retention time of 7 min. The parameters of mass spectrometry were set as follows: positive electrospray ionization source (ESI+); ion spray voltage of 5,500 V; ion source temperature of 600 °C; auxiliary gas 1 (N2, 99.9999%) pressure of 60 psi; auxiliary gas 2 (N2, 99.9999%) pressure of 50 psi; air curtain gas pressure of 25 psi.

Detection of the copper in different matrices: The analysis of this heavy metal was performed on an iCAP Q ICP-MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), the internal standard solution was Ge (0.01 μg/mL), and the instrument parameters were set as follows: collection mode: mass spectrometry; plasma RF power: 1,550 W; volume flow rate of plasma gas: 15 L/min; volume flow of atomizing gas: 1 L/min; volume flow of auxiliary gas: 1 L/min; volume flow of cooling gas: 1 L/min.

Detection and confirmation of the metabolites of tolfenpyrad in different matrices: The analysis was performed on a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC UHPLC system equipped with a Q-Exactive Orbitra HRMS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). The UHPLC conditions were: Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm i.d., 5 μm); flow rate of 0.2 mL/min; injection volume of 5 μL; column temperature of 30 °C; mobile phases of 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and acetonitrile (B); gradient elution program of 0–0.5 min (A/B = 80/20, v/v), 0.5–6 min (A/B = 0/100, v/v), 6–10 min (A/B = 80/20, v/v). The MS parameters were set as follows: ESI; ion spray voltage of 3,500 V (positive) and −2,500 V (negative); auxiliary gas of 10 arbitrary units (arbs); sheath gas of 25 arbs; capillary temperature of 320 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature of 300 °C; full scan mode (70–800 m/z); mass resolution of 70,000 (full width at half maximum); maximum injection time of 50 ms.

Extraction of tolfenpyrad: An aliquot of 2.00 ± 0.01 g of pea seedling leaf (stem, root) or 10.00 ± 0.01 g soil was weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. 20 mL of acetonitrile was added, the mixture was vortexed at 2,500 rpm for 3 min. Then 2 g of anhydrous MgSO4 and 1 g of NaCl were added, and the mixture was vortexed at 2,500 rpm for 3 min. After centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 6 min, 1 mL of supernatant was transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube containing purification sorbent (50 mg of PSA) and vortexed for 30 s. The sample solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before analysis.

Extraction of Cu: An aliquot of 1.00 ± 0.01 g of pea seedling leaf (stem, root) was weighed into a crucible. 1 mL perchloric acid and 3 mL nitric acid was added. Then place the crucible on a heating plate at 200°C for digestion until only white solid material remained. Put it into a 50 mL volumetric flask, add 2% nitric acid water, dissolve and constant volume. The sample solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm water membrane before analysis.

The developed method was validated by several parameters, including linearity, limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantitation (LOQ), and matrix effect (ME).19 In Table S3 (Supporting information), the correlation coefficient (R2) was 0.9953–0.9965, indicating that tolfenpyrad had good linearity at concentrations of 0.005, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, and 5 mg/L. LOD and LOQ was defined as a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of 3 and 10, respectively. The limit of quantitation and limit of detection of tolfenpyrad in all samples were 0.01 mg/kg and 0.003 mg/kg, respectively.39 ME value was calculated as the slope ratio of the matrix-matched standard curve to the solvent standard curve. ME value (<1) indicated that the matrix weakening effect was observed, and the matrix-matched calibration curve was more accurate to the quantitative analysis of tolfenpyrad in pea seedling tissue samples.40 Intra-day (n = 5) and inter-day (n = 15) recovery experiments were carried out at three spiked levels (0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg) to evaluate the accuracy and precision of the analytical method of tolfenpyrad in various pea seedling tissue samples. The data in Table S4 (Supporting information) showed that intra-day average recoveries of tolfenpyrad in pea seedling roots, stems, and leaves were 79.71%–106.17%, and relative standard deviations (RSDs) were 0.74%–5.32%. Inter-day average recoveries of tolfenpyrad in pea seedling tissue samples were 82.27%–102.40% with RSDs <5%. These results demonstrate that the analytical method meets the requirements of quality control and method validation procedures for tolfenpyrad residues in pea seedlings and can be used for subsequent field trials.41

Data processing

Besides the levels of tolfenpyrad in different pea seedling tissue and cultivation medium samples, BCF, including root concentration factor (RCF), stem concentration factor (SCF), and leaf concentration factor (LCF), and translocation factor (TF) values were used to examine the accumulation, translocation, and distribution of tolfenpyrad in soil-cultivated and hydrponic pea seedling ecosystems.27,28 The equations, RCF = Croot/Ccultivation medium, SCF = Cstem/Ccultivation medium, LCF = Cleaf/Ccultivation medium, TFstem/root = Cstem/Croot, TFleaf/root = Cleaf/Croot, and TFleaf/stem = Cleaf/Cstem, were applied to assess the absorption and uptake ability of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings. Additionally, the equations, TFroot/leaf = Croot/Cleaf, TFstem/leaf = Cstem/Cleaf, and TFroot/stem = Croot/Cstem, were used to evaluate the downward translocation potential of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings. In these equations, Croot, Cstem, Croot and Ccultivation medium represent the concentrations (mg/kg) of tolfenpyrad in pea seedling roots, stems, leaves, and cultivation medium (soil or nutrient solution), respectively.7

Residual dissipation of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings was assessed using the following equations: Ct = C0 × e–kt and t1/2 = ln2/k, where C0 is the initial concentration (mg/kg) of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings, Ct is the concentration (mg/kg) of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings at time t (d), k is the dissipation rate constant (day−1) and t1/2 is the half-life (d) of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings.20 RQa and RQc values were used to evaluate the acute and chronic exposure risks of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings, respectively, for different age and gender groups of Chinese consumers. These quotients were calculated by the equations: NESTI = FI × HR/b.w., RQa = NESTI/ARfD × 100%, IEDI = FI × STMR/b.w., and RQc = IEDI/ADI × 100%. In these equations, NESTI (mg/kg b.w.) represents the national estimated short-term intake of tolfenpyrad, FI (g/day) is the daily intake of pea seedlings, HR (mg/kg) is the highest residue level of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings, b.w. (kg) is the body weight of consumer, ARfD (mg/kg b.w.) is the acute reference dose of tolfenpyrad, IEDI (mg/kg b.w.) is the international estimated daily intake of tolfenpyrad, STMR (mg/kg) is the median residue level of tolfenpyrad in pea seedlings, and ADI (mg/kg b.w.) is the acceptable daily intake of tolfenpyrad.19,23,40

Quantification and statistical analysis

The data were processed using office Excel, and statistically analyzed with SPSS. Visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism. In addition, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test was conducted to investigate the significant differences between the samples (p < 0.01).32
