FBXW7 mitigates hepatic fibrosis by degrading NOTCH1 to transcriptionally silence SEMA3G in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells
Authors: Chengrong Yan, Jinling Dong, Yihong Sun, Xin Min, Junhui Liu, Shaozhou Ma, Shan He, Yuru Luo, Wenyang Hao, Yuan Fang, Hongyu Su, Weihao Xue, Rui Zhou, Baochang Lai, Sasa Liu, Jianyu He, Donghong Zhang, Xiaohong Zheng, Enqi Liu, John Y-J Shyy, Fanpu Ji, Hua Han, Yue Wu, Zuyi Yuan, Xu-Feng Zhang, Yidong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Overactivation of Notch signaling in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) is an established driver of hepatic fibrosis, yet its precise upstream regulators and downstream effectors remain poorly defined, limiting therapeutic translation. Here, we demonstrate that FBXW7, the primary E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for NOTCH1 degradation, serves as a critical suppressor of liver fibrosis. Using endothelial-specific Fbxw7 knockout mice subjected to three distinct fibrosis models, we show that FBXW7 loss exacerbates liver fibrosis, promotes LSEC capillarization, and enhances hepatic stellate cell activation. Transcriptomic analysis identified Sema3g as the top upregulated gene in FBXW7‑deficient LSECs, and conditioned medium from these cells stimulated HSC activation through SEMA3G‑dependent paracrine signaling. Moreover, nanoparticle-mediated delivery of Sema3g-targeting siRNA specifically to LSECs substantially ameliorated injury-induced liver fibrosis in Fbxw7 knockout mice. Mechanistically, FBXW7 loss stabilizes NOTCH1, leading to enhanced Sema3g transcription. Consistent with these findings, human cirrhotic samples and murine fibrotic livers exhibit reduced FBXW7 expression alongside elevated active NOTCH1 and SEMA3G in LSECs. Collectively, our results demonstrate that FBXW7 mitigates liver fibrosis by degrading NOTCH1 to transcriptionally silence SEMA3G, and suggest that targeting the FBXW7/NOTCH1/SEMA3G axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for fibrotic liver disease.

Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis, the progressive scarring of liver tissue resulting from chronic injury caused by viral hepatitis, alcohol abuse, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), poses a significant global health challenge with limited effective treatment options1–3. While hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) serve as the principal mediators of extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition4,5, growing evidence highlights the crucial role of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) in coordinating fibrogenesis through paracrine signaling networks5–9. In the healthy liver, LSECs maintain HSC quiescence through constitutive nitric oxide (NO) production and possess a distinctive fenestrated architecture that enables efficient blood-hepatocyte exchange10–13. Following injury, however, LSECs undergo capillarization, a pathological transition marked by the disappearance of fenestrations, formation of basement membrane, and acquisition of a profibrotic phenotype that potently activates HSCs14.

The Notch signaling pathway, an evolutionarily conserved mechanism of cell-cell communication, operates through ligand-receptor interactions between adjacent cells. Binding of Notch ligands (JAG1, JAG2, DLL1, DLL3, or DLL4) to Notch receptors (NOTCH1-4) triggers sequential proteolytic cleavage events that release the Notch intracellular domain (NICD)15,16. The liberated NICD subsequently translocates to the nucleus where it associates with RBPJ to form a transcriptional activation complex that induces expression of target genes, including the well-characterized Notch effectors HEY1, HEY2, HES1, and HES517. In LSECs, excessive Notch signaling has been firmly established as a promoter of hepatic fibrogenesis, largely through its suppression of NO-mediated quiescence signals18–20. Nevertheless, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling Notch activity and the key downstream effectors linking Notch activation to fibrotic progression remain poorly characterized.

FBXW7, the substrate recognition component of the SCF-type E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, controls the proteasomal degradation of numerous oncoproteins and signaling molecules, including NOTCH121,22. While Xia et al. recently demonstrated that hepatocyte-specific FBXW7 suppresses MASH progression by sustaining hepatic catabolism and systemic homeostasis23, its role in LSEC-mediated hepatic fibrosis remains unknown. Given that NOTCH1 protein stabilization amplifies fibrotic responses, we hypothesize that FBXW7 may function as a critical negative regulator of LSEC-dependent fibrogenesis by promoting NOTCH1 degradation. Moreover, the specific Notch-induced paracrine factors secreted by LSECs that directly activate HSCs remain unidentified, and their characterization is essential for developing targeted anti-fibrotic therapies.

In this study, through an integrated approach employing genetically engineered mouse models, primary cell cultures, transcriptomic analyses, and human cirrhotic specimens, we have identified the FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis in LSECs as a key regulator of hepatic fibrosis. Our findings not only uncover a previously unrecognized connection between ubiquitin-dependent Notch regulation and LSEC capillarization but also establish LSEC-derived SEMA3G as a profibrotic paracrine factor that activates HSCs. These results position SEMA3G signaling inhibition as a promising therapeutic approach for fibrotic liver diseases.

Results

Endothelial FBXW7 is dispensable for liver homeostasis

We initially assessed FBXW7 expression in vascular endothelial cells by reanalyzing publicly available single-cell RNA-seq datasets (E-MTAB-8077 and GSE136103)24,25. This analysis revealed abundant Fbxw7 transcripts across all endothelial cell populations in multiple organs of healthy adult mice, including the liver. Subsequent analysis demonstrated that, within the liver, Fbxw7 was ubiquitously detected across all endothelial subsets, including LSECs (Supplementary Fig. 1A–D). Notably, a similar expression pattern of FBXW7 was also observed in human liver endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 1E–J). To determine the role of endothelial FBXW7 in hepatic homeostasis, we generated an endothelial-specific conditional Fbxw7 knockout model (Fbxw7f/f: Cdh5CreERT2, referred to as Fbxw7EKO) by crossing Fbxw7f/f mice with Cdh5CreERT2 mice, while Fbxw7f/f mice were used as controls (referred to as CTL). To assess the role of FBXW7 in hepatic homeostasis, we intraperitoneally injected six-week-old CTL and Fbxw7EKO mice with tamoxifen to induce Fbxw7 deletion. Seven weeks post-induction, mice were euthanized, and liver tissues along with blood samples were collected for histological and molecular analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Notably, the liver-to-body weight ratio remained comparable between CTL and Fbxw7EKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Serum biochemical analysis showed no significant differences in alanine transaminase (ALT) or aspartate transaminase (AST) levels between control and Fbxw7EKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C), indicating preserved liver function. Histological assessment by H&E staining revealed nearly identical liver architecture in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Furthermore, no evidence of liver fibrosis was detected, as confirmed by Sirius Red staining and hepatic hydroxyproline quantification (Supplementary Fig. 3E–G). In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed comparable expression of LYVE1 (a marker of differentiated LSECs), CD34 (a marker of capillarized LSECs), and the basement membrane components EMCN and Collagen IV in control and Fbxw7EKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3H, I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that endothelial FBXW7 is dispensable for maintaining hepatic homeostasis under physiological conditions.

Deletion of endothelial Fbxw7 aggravates injury-induced liver fibrosis

To explore the role of endothelial FBXW7 in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis, chronic liver injury models were established in both control (CTL) and endothelial-specific Fbxw7-knockout (Fbxw7EKO) mice using three distinct methods: repeated low-dose carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) administration, bile duct ligation (BDL) surgery, or feeding with a methionine-choline-deficient (MCD) diet (Supplementary Fig. 2B–D). qPCR confirmed efficient Fbxw7 deletion in LSECs from Fbxw7EKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3J). In the CCl₄ and BDL models, the liver-to-body weight ratio was significantly higher in male Fbxw7EKO mice than in CTLs, whereas no such difference was observed in the MCD model (Fig. 1A). Across all three models, Fbxw7EKO mice developed more severe liver injury, as indicated by elevated serum levels of ALT and AST (Fig. 1B, C). Histological analysis further revealed exacerbated liver damage in knockouts, including increased inflammatory cell infiltration by H&E staining (Fig. 1D), enhanced fibrosis measured by hydroxyproline content (Fig. 1E), Sirius Red staining (Fig. 1F), and COL1A1 immunostaining (Fig. 1G). In the MCD model, Fbxw7EKO mice also exhibited greater hepatic lipid deposition compared to CTLs (Fig. 1D, H). Although female knockout mice displayed a similar but milder phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 4), the pronounced effects in males led us to focus subsequent studies on male mice. Fbxw7 deficiency promotes hepatic stellate cell activation

Following liver injury, quiescent HSCs undergo activation and transdifferentiation into proliferative myofibroblasts that drive extracellular matrix deposition4,5. To investigate this process, we isolated HSCs from both CTL and Fbxw7EKO mice. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that Fbxw7 deficiency significantly upregulated key markers of HSC activation (Acta2, Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb, and Ctgf) across all three fibrotic models (Fig. 2A–C). Consistent with these molecular findings, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated increased αSMA-positive myofibroblasts in Fbxw7EKO livers (Fig. 2D, E). Furthermore, co-immunostaining analysis showed enhanced proliferation of HSCs (DESMIN + ) in knockout mice, as evidenced by greater Ki67 positivity compared with the controls (Fig. 2F, G). Fbxw7 loss promotes LSEC capillarization

LSEC capillarization represents a critical pathological feature of chronic liver disease that drives HSC activation7,14. To characterize this process, we performed scanning electron microscopy on LSECs isolated from CCl₄-treated CTL and Fbxw7EKO mice. While CTL LSECs exhibited characteristic uniform fenestrations organized in sieve-like patterns, Fbxw7-deficient LSECs showed markedly reduced and disorganized fenestrations with irregular sizes (Fig. 3A). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated molecular hallmarks of capillarization in knockout mice, including decreased LYVE1 (a marker of differentiated LSECs) and increased CD34 expression (a marker of capillarized LSECs) (Fig. 3B). In addition, Fbxw7EKO mice showed enhanced deposition of basement membrane components (EMCN and Collagen IV) and increased LSEC proliferation (VE-cadherin and Ki67 double-positive cells) compared with the controls (Fig. 3C, D). FBXW7 inhibits Sema3g expression in LSECs

To identify LSEC-derived paracrine factors that might mediate HSC activation in Fbxw7 EKO mice, we performed RNA sequencing on primary LSECs isolated from CCl₄-treated Fbxw7 EKO and CTL mice. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 489 downregulated and 1,265 upregulated genes in Fbxw7-deficient LSECs (Fig. 4A). Strikingly, the most significantly upregulated transcript encoded semaphorin-3G (SEMA3G) (Fig. 4B), a secreted member of the class 3 semaphorin family family26. This finding was particularly intriguing given recent reports implicating SEMA3C in HSC activation and liver fibrosis27. We next examined the expression of class 3 semaphorins (Sema3a–g) and other fibrosis-associated semaphorins (Sema4a, Sema4g, and Sema7a)28–30 by qPCR. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, Sema3g exhibited the most pronounced upregulation in Fbxw7-deficient LSECs across all three fibrotic models (Fig. 4C–E). Accordingly, immunostaining confirmed elevated SEMA3G protein levels specifically within LSECs of Fbxw7EKO mice (Fig. 4F). Conversely, overexpression of Fbxw7 in primary LSECs isolated from CCl₄-injured wild-type mice significantly reduced Sema3g transcript levels (Fig. 4G). Fbxw7 loss promotes HSC activation through upregulating Sema3g in LSECs

To test whether LSEC-derived SEMA3G drives HSC activation, we prepared conditioned media (CM) from primary cultures of LSECs from CTL (CM-CTL) and Fbxw7EKO (CM-Fbxw7EKO) mice challenged with CCl₄ (Fig. 5A). ELISA analysis confirmed significantly elevated SEMA3G levels in CM-Fbxw7EKO compared with CM-CTL (Fig. 5B). When applied to HSCs, CM-Fbxw7EKO potently upregulated fibrogenic markers (Acta2, Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb, and Ctgf) (Fig. 5C) and enhanced HSC transdifferentiation into αSMA-positive myofibroblasts (Fig. 5D). In a complementary approach (Fig. 5E), we isolated LSECs from CCl₄-treated wild-type mice and generated conditioned media from these cells after infection with either Fbxw7-overexpressing (CM-oeFbxw7) or control (CM-oeCTL) lentivirus. Notably, CM-oeFbxw7 contained lower levels of SEMA3G protein and demonstrated reduced capacity to induce HSC activation compared with CM-oeCTL (Fig. 5F, G). To establish a causal relationship, we performed Sema3g knockdown in Fbxw7EKO LSECs using lentiviral shRNA (Fig. 5H). This intervention reduced SEMA3G concentration in the conditioned media (Fig. 5I), and attenuated the upregulation of HSC activation markers as well as the transdifferentiation of HSC-to-myofibroblasts induced by CM-Fbxw7EKO (Fig. 5J, K). Supporting these findings, gain-of-function experiments in the human HSC cell line LX-2 demonstrated that recombinant SEMA3G alone was sufficient to induce HSC activation (Fig. 5L, M). SEMA3G has been shown to bind to heterodimers formed by NRP2 and PlexinD131. The expression of Nrp2 but not PlexinD1 in HSCs was upregulated in Fbxw7EKO mice when compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Consistently, treatment with conditioned medium from Fbxw7EKO LSECs (CM-Fbxw7EKO) also increased Nrp2 expression in hepatic stellate cell line LX12, whereas PlexinD1 levels remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6C, D). Moreover, knockdown of Nrp2 in HSCs abolished the pro-fibrotic effects induced by CM-Fbxw7EKO (Fig. 5N). Targeted knockdown of Sema3g in LSECs attenuates Fbxw7 loss-induced liver fibrosis

To validate the FBXW7/SEMA3G axis in vivo, we subjected Fbxw7EKO mice to a CCl₄-induced liver fibrosis model and treated them with Sema3g-specific siRNA encapsulated in spherical nucleic acid (SNA) nanoparticles (Fig. 6A), which are designed to target fibrotic LSECs via scavenger receptor A32. Immunostaining confirmed reduced SEMA3G expression in LSECs of Fbxw7EKO mice treated with Sema3g siRNA compared to scramble siRNA-treated controls (Fig. 6B). Fbxw7EKO mice receiving Sema3g siRNA exhibited a significantly lower liver-to-body weight ratio than those treated with scramble siRNA (Fig. 6C). Knockdown of Sema3g ameliorated CCl₄-induced liver injury in Fbxw7EKO mice, as evidenced by reduced serum levels of ALT and AST (Fig. 6D, E). Histological analysis further revealed attenuation of liver damage following Sema3g knockdown, including reduced inflammatory cell infiltration on H&E staining (Fig. 6F), as well as decreased fibrosis measured by hydroxyproline content (Fig. 6G), Sirius Red staining (Fig. 6H), and COL1A1 immunostaining (Fig. 6I). Fbxw7 loss enhances Notch-dependent Sema3g transcription

To investigate whether FBXW7 regulates Sema3g expression through Notch signaling, we first analyzed our RNA-seq data and identified significant upregulation of the canonical Notch target gene Hey1 in Fbxw7-deficient LSECs (Fig. 7A), which was validated by qPCR (Fig. 7B). We next examined whether this FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis is conserved and functional in human cells. Consistent with the murine findings, knockdown of FBXW7 in human LSECs robustly enhanced Notch signaling and SEMA3G expression (Fig. 7G, H), effects that were abolished by DAPT treatment (Fig. 7I). Reciprocally, N1ICD overexpression in human LSECs potently upregulated SEMA3G (Fig. 7J, K). CM from human LSECs overexpressing N1ICD exhibited a greater capacity to activate human HSCs than CM from control cells, as indicated by elevated expression of fibrotic markers (Fig. 7J, L). Notably, this paracrine activation of HSCs was significantly attenuated by SEMA3G knockdown (Fig. 7M–O). In addition, N1ICD overexpression significantly activated reporter gene expression under the control of a 1-Kb murine Sema3g promoter, whereas such effects were abolished by mutating the putative RBPJ-binding site (Fig. 7P). These results establish a mechanistic cascade wherein Fbxw7 deficiency stabilizes N1ICD, leading to RBPJ-dependent transcriptional activation of Sema3g in LSECs.

The FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis exhibits conserved dysregulation in human cirrhosis and experimental fibrosis models

Immunostaining analysis of human cirrhotic livers revealed significant dysregulation of the FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis in LSECs, characterized by markedly reduced FBXW7 expression accompanied by increased levels of N1ICD and SEMA3G compared to healthy controls (Fig. 8A–C). In addition, cirrhotic patients had significant higher levels of serum SEMA3G than healthy controls (Fig. 8D). Moreover, serum SEMA3G levels were positively correlated with both serum AST levels and the ALT/AST ratio (Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). These clinical observations were further supported by single-cell RNA-seq data. Notably, cirrhotic patients exhibited elevated SEMA3G transcripts in liver endothelial cells compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 8E and Supplementary Fig. 7D–G). In addition, the livers of cirrhotic patients contained more HSCs positive for the SEMA3G receptors NRP2 and PlexinD1 than those of healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 7H). These results were further confirmed in mouse models of liver fibrosis (CCl₄ and BDL), which showed the same pattern of decreased FBXW7 alongside increased N1ICD and SEMA3G in LSECs (Fig. 8F–K). Discussion

LSEC capillarization is a key driver of fibrogenesis in chronic liver diseases10,14,34, making it a promising therapeutic target for this devastating condition. In this study, we employed cirrhotic liver samples, endothelial-specific conditional knockout mice, primary LSEC cultures, transcriptomic profiling, and molecular analyses to identify the FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis that critically regulates chronic injury-induced liver fibrogenesis by mediating LSEC-HSC crosstalk. Our findings demonstrate that endothelial-specific deletion of Fbxw7 exacerbates LSEC capillarization and HSC activation, resulting in aggravated liver fibrosis across three distinct murine models. Mechanistically, we show that while LSEC-derived SEMA3G promotes HSC activation via NRP2 signaling, FBXW7 attenuates hepatic fibrosis by facilitating NOTCH1 degradation, thereby transcriptionally silencing Sema3g (Fig. 9).Fig. 9Schematic showing the FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis mediates LSEC-HSC crosstalk and regulates liver fibrosis.Endothelial-specific deletion of Fbxw7 promotes LSEC capillarization and HSC activation, exacerbating injury-induced liver fibrosis. Notch overactivation in LSECs is evident in both human cirrhosis patients and murine fibrotic models18. Previous functional studies have established that Notch hyperactivation induces LSEC capillarization and the production of profibrotic paracrine signals, exacerbating liver fibrogenesis in CCl₄ and MASH models18,19. However, the mechanisms driving Notch activation in this context remain unclear. Our study addresses this gap by identifying FBXW7 as a critical negative regulator of Notch signaling in LSECs, mediated through NOTCH1 degradation—a mechanism consistent with its role in other cell types21. It is noteworthy that FBXW7 has also been demonstrated to inhibit HSC activation by blocking Notch signaling through the degradation of the Notch ligand DLL135. This highlights a dual mode of regulation by FBXW7—targeting the receptor (NOTCH1) in LSECs and a ligand (DLL1) in HSCs—underscoring the complexity and cell-type specificity of Notch pathway control in liver fibrosis. Consequently, future investigations should focus on dissecting the specific roles of individual Notch ligands and receptors in the multifaceted process of liver fibrosis. Such detailed understanding could facilitate the development of more precise strategies for targeting Notch signaling, thereby overcoming the major drawback of pan-Notch inhibitors, such as gamma-secretase inhibitors (GSIs), which broadly block signaling transduced by all Notch receptors and often lead to dose-limiting36.

Using tissue-specific Fbxw7 conditional knockout mice, we demonstrated that endothelial FBXW7 plays a pivotal role in suppressing injury-induced liver fibrosis by inhibiting Notch signaling. Notably, male Fbxw7 knockout mice exhibited more pronounced fibrotic phenotypes compared to their female counterparts, aligning with the sexual dimorphism in hepatic pathophysiology reported in prior clinical and experimental studies37–39. No significant differences in liver function or histology were observed between control and Fbxw7 knockout mice at 14 weeks under steady-state conditions, suggesting that FBXW7 is largely dispensable for normal LSEC homeostasis but becomes critically required under stress conditions. Injury-induced inflammatory or metabolic stressors are likely required to unmask the pro-fibrotic phenotype, indicating that FBXW7 functions as a conditional regulator of endothelial–stromal crosstalk rather than a fibrosis initiator. Whether prolonged aging would eventually reveal spontaneous phenotypes remains to be determined. Intriguingly, Xia et al. recently reported that hepatocyte-specific FBXW7 protects against MASH-associated fibrogenesis by modulating the transcriptional activity of nutrient-sensing nuclear receptors PPARα and ERRα23. These findings collectively support the therapeutic potential of FBXW7 overexpression in mitigating liver fibrosis.

A pivotal discovery of our study is the identification of SEMA3G as a key paracrine factor linking LSEC dysfunction to HSC activation. Although semaphorins are well-characterized in neuronal guidance and angiogenesis26,31,40,41, their role in liver fibrosis has only recently emerged. Clinical studies have reported elevated serum levels of SEMA3C, SEMA5A, and SEMA6D in patients with chronic liver diseases42. Yagai et al. demonstrated that hepatocyte-derived SEMA3E promotes LSEC and HSC activation, exacerbating CCl₄-induced liver fibrosis43. Additionally, SEMA3C was recently found to be upregulated in HSCs of cirrhosis patients, and its selective deletion in HSCs attenuated experimental liver fibrosis in mice27. Our transcriptomic analysis of liver endothelial cells from control and Fbxw7 knockout mice identified Sema3g as the most significantly differentially expressed gene among all SEMA family members, exhibiting the greatest fold change in expression. We further demonstrated that Sema3g is directly regulated by Notch signaling. Most importantly, our in vivo loss-of-function data demonstrate that targeted silencing of Sema3g in LSECs significantly ameliorates the exacerbated liver fibrosis caused by endothelial Fbxw7 deficiency, providing direct genetic evidence that SEMA3G is the primary downstream mediator of FBXW7 in this context. While we cannot exclude the possibility that other SEMA3 family members may contribute to liver fibrosis through partial functional redundancy or synergistic mechanisms, the magnitude of expression change, direct Notch regulation, and the in vivo rescue effect collectively support SEMA3G as the critical effector in this pathway. We also established that NRP2 in HSCs is essential for SEMA3G signaling, the identity of potential co-receptors remains to be elucidated. Similarly, SEMA3G has been shown to promote proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells by activating YAP via NRP2/PlexinA1 under diabetic condition44. Semas binding to the NRP2/Plexin complex regulates multiple downstream effectors, including Ras/Rap family GTPases, Rho proteins, intracellular tyrosine kinases (Pyk2, Syk, FAK, Fer/Fes, Fyn, and Src family members), and second messengers (cAMP and Ca²⁺)26. Future studies are required to identify which of these signaling molecules mediate SEMA3G-driven HSC activation. Collectively, these findings underscore the emerging importance of semaphorins in liver fibrogenesis and highlight the need to identify additional family members involved in this pathological process.

Mechanistically, we demonstrate that FBXW7 suppresses Sema3g expression in LSECs by inhibiting Notch signaling. This aligns with the work by Hyun et al., who reported that Notch signaling promotes organized revascularization via SEMA3G induction45. Furthermore, we show that Notch upregulates Sema3g by enhancing its promoter activity in a RBPJ-dependent manner.

In summary, our study identifies a FBXW7/Notch/SEMA3G axis mediating LSEC-dependent HSC activation in chronic liver injury. We further link this axis to human fibrogenesis by showing dysregulation of FBXW7, N1ICD, and SEMA3G in LSECs of cirrhotic patients. What drives FBXW7 downregulation in LSECs during liver injury remains unclear, but potential upstream drivers include inflammatory signaling46,47, hypoxia48, and metabolic stress23,49. Deciphering these mechanisms will help position FBXW7 in the pathogenic pathways of hepatic fibrosis. Together, these findings advance our understanding of LSEC-HSC crosstalk and propose SEMA3G signaling blockade as a promising therapeutic approach for fibrotic liver disease. Furthermore, given the broad expression of FBXW7 across endothelial populations, future studies are warranted to investigate whether this regulatory axis also contributes to fibrogenesis in other organs, including the heart, lung, and kidney.

Methods

Human liver and serum samples

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No. XJTU1AF2025LSYY-759). The committee waived the requirement for individual informed consent for the following reasons: (1) the research involved no more than minimal risk to the participants; (2) all serum samples were retrospectively collected residual specimens from routine clinical testing; (3) all samples were fully de-identified prior to analysis, with all personal identifiers irrevocably removed. A total of 85 de-identified serum samples were utilized, comprising 42 (21 males and 21 females) from normal individuals and 43 (17 males and 26 females) from patients diagnosed with liver cirrhosis. All data were processed anonymously and used exclusively for research.

Normal and cirrhotic liver tissues were obtained intraoperatively from specimens of patients who underwent liver resection for hemangioma, or orthotopic liver transplantation, respectively, at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, China. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No. XJTU1AF2017LSL-007).

Animal models and liver fibrosis inductions

For genetically modified mouse lines, all animals were on a C57BL/6 J background. Initial experiments included both male and female mice with sex-stratified analyses. Although fibrotic phenotypes were observed in both sexes, male mice exhibited more consistent responses. To exclude confounding effects of estrogen, only male mice aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 20–30 g were used for liver fibrosis induction in all subsequent experiments. All mice were humanely euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation prior to blood and liver collection, and were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at Xi’an Jiaotong University’s Experimental Animal Center. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University and conducted in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 86-23, revised 1985) established by the National Academy of Sciences.

Fbxw7f/f mice50 were crossed with Cdh5CreERT2 mice51 to generate endothelial cell-specific Fbxw7 knockout mice. Gene deletion was induced in 6-week-old mice via intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen (100 mg/kg) for five consecutive days. For liver homeostasis assessment, mice were analyzed 7 weeks post-induction, while fibrosis studies employed three established models: (1) CCl4-induced fibrosis (0.6 mL/kg, 3 times/week for 6 weeks) with sample collection 72 h post-final injection; (2) bile duct ligation (BDL) where mice underwent double ligation and transection of the common bile duct after 12 h fasting, followed by sample collection two weeks post-surgery; (3) MCD diet (catalog #A02082002B, Research Diets, Inc., New Brunswick, NJ, USA).-induced fibrosis (MCD diet for 8 weeks).

In vivo knockdown of Sema3g in LSECs with nanoparticles

Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) nanoparticles, which specifically target liver sinusoidal endothelial cells via scavenger receptor A and enhance nucleic acid stability as previously described32, were self-assembled through aurophilic interactions for oligonucleotide delivery. Conditional Fbxw7 knockout mice were administered tamoxifen for five consecutive days at six-week-old, and CCl₄-induced liver fibrosis modeling was initiated when the mice reached eight-week of age (see above for detailed procedures). Therapeutic intervention commenced three weeks after the start of CCl₄ treatment. Mice were randomly assigned to two groups: the SNAs-siCTL control group and the SNAs-siSema3g treatment group. siSema3g: GCUAUACACAGGUCUCACUGC.The SNA stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 2 OD nucleic acids in 10 mL of buffer. Tail vein injections were performed every three days throughout the treatment period, with each mouse receiving a single injection volume of 300 μL. Mice in the treatment group received SNA loaded with siSema3g (SNAs-siSema3g), whereas control mice received an equivalent amount of SNA encapsulating scrambled control siRNA (SNAs-siCTL). All mice were sacrificed three days after the final injection, and liver tissues were collected for subsequent biochemical and histological analyses.

Histology and immunostaining

Liver tissues were immediately divided into two portions post-dissection for parallel processing. For cryoembedding, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 2 h, equilibrated in 30% sucrose solution overnight at 4 °C, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and sectioned at 7 μm thickness using a cryostat. For paraffin embedding, tissues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70–100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin at 65 °C before sectioning at 5 μm. Standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Sirius Red staining were performed to assess liver histopathology and collagen deposition, respectively. For immunofluorescence, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) or Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM, pH 9.0) for 10 min. Sections were blocked with 5% horse serum in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (Table S1) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) and counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL). For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections were processed similarly, then incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies, developed using the VECTASTAIN® ABC kit (Vector Laboratories), and visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Fluorescent images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000 or Leica STELLARIS 5). Bright field images of H&E, Sirius Red, and IHC-stained sections were acquired using an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from samples using the TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop). For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, R223-01). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Genstar) on a QuantGene 9600 Fast Real-Time PCR system, utilizing gene-specific primers (Table S2). Gene-specific primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The 18S rRNA was used as an endogenous control for normalization, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2^(-ΔΔCT) method. Experiments included at least five independent biological replicates per condition, with statistical analysis performed on log2-transformed relative expression values.

Western blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted from LSECs using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) containing 10 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein concentrations were quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer, followed by incubation with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Clinx Science Instruments, China).

Isolation and culture of primary liver cell

Primary LSECs and HSCs were isolated from mice using an optimized two-step perfusion protocol as described previously20. Briefly, anesthetized animals underwent sequential portal vein perfusion with calcium/magnesium-free Hank’s balanced salt solution followed by digestion buffer containing 0.2 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 µg/mL DNase I (Roche) in calcium/magnesium-supplemented buffer. The excised liver was mechanically dissociated in C-tubes (Miltenyi Biotec) with additional enzymatic digestion (0.2 mg/mL collagenase IV and 10 µg/mL DNase I, 37 °C, 15 min), then filtered through 100 µm cell strainers. Hepatocytes were removed by three rounds of low-speed centrifugation (50 × g, 3 min, 4 °C), followed by non-parenchymal cell collection at 360 × g for 7 min at 4 °C. HSCs were purified by density gradient centrifugation (17.6–11.5% iodixanol, Axis-Shield; 1400 × g, 21 min, 4 °C, brake-off), with interface cells washed (350 × g, 5 min). LSECs were isolated by magnetic-activated cell sorting using anti-CD146 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec; 15 min incubation at 4 °C, light-protected) followed by MS column separation. Purified LSECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ScienCell) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. In addition, primary human LSECs (Cat#: IM-H399) and culture media (Cat#: IM-H399-1) were obtained from IMMOCELL (Xiamen, Fujian, China).

Preparation of conditioned medium and LSEC-HSC crosstalk study

Primary LSECs were cultured in serum-free endothelial cell medium (ECM) for 24 h to generate LSEC-conditioned medium (LSEC-CM). The supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, and stored at −80 °C until use. For SEMA3G-containing conditioned medium, HEK-293T cells were transduced with either SEMA3G-overexpressing or control lentivirus. After 48 h, the viral supernatant was replaced with fresh medium, and conditioned medium was collected after an additional 24 h of culture. To investigate LSEC-HSC paracrine signaling, immortalized mouse hepatic stellate cells (iCellBioscience, iCell-0094a) were treated with LSEC-CM. Parallel experiments were performed using human LX-2 HSCs (a gift from Dr. Dongmin Li at Xi’an Jiaotong University) cultured with HEK-293T-derived conditioned medium containing recombinant human SEMA3G. Following treatment, cells were harvested for gene expression analysis of HSC activation markers. All cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Plasmid construction

To generate shRNA expression plasmids, oligos of shRNA sequence against Sema3g or Nrp2 were synthesized and inserted into the pLKO.1 lentivirus vectors. shSema3g: GCTATACACAGGTCTCACTGC; shNrp2: CCTCACTTTGAAATCGAGAAA; a non-targeting sequence control shRNA: GTACGTACGTACGTACGTACT. A 1-kb fragment of the murine Sema3g promoter region was PCR-amplified and cloned into the pGL3-basic vector to generate the pGL3-Sema3gP reporter construct. The RBPJ binding site (TTCCCA, nucleotides +31 to +36) within this promoter was subsequently mutated to TGTAGT using site-directed mutagenesis by PCR. To generate SEMA3G overexpression plasmid, the coding sequence of human SEMA3G gene (NM_020163) was amplified from a commercial plasmid pCR-BluntII-TOPO-SEMA3G (MiaoLing plasmid, cat#: P20753) using PrimerSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa, R050A), and cloned into the pCDH vector. All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Lentivirus production and transduction

HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with shRNA constructs and the lentiviral packaging plasmids pSPAX2/pMD2.G (at a 4:3:1 ratio) using Lipofiter 3.0 transfection reagent (Hanbio, China). Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, cleared by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min, and either stored at −80 °C or processed for concentration. For viral concentration, supernatants were mixed with one-third volume of 40% PEG8000 solution, incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking (60 rpm), and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C. The viral pellets were then resuspended in cold serum-free DMEM and stored at −80 °C in single-use aliquots. For transduction, target cells were incubated with viral particles in the presence of 10 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma, H9268). Stable cell lines were selected using 1 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma, P9620) for 48 h, beginning 24 h post-transduction. Knockdown efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR analysis of target gene expression levels.

Biochemistry and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were obtained via orbital puncture and allowed to clot at 4 °C for 4 h. After centrifugation, serum was aliquoted and stored at –80 °C until further analysis. Serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) were measured using commercial kits (AST-C010-2-1, ALT-C009-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng bioengineering Institute, China). Similarly, SEMA3G concentrations in human serum and conditioned medium were quantified using ELISA kits (EH2152, EM0738, FineTest) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hydroxyproline levels in liver tissues were measured by using an ELISA kit (A030-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng bioengineering Institute, China).

Luciferase reporter assay

For transcriptional activity assays, HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with pGL3-Sema3gP (wild-type or mutant), the internal control plasmid pRL-TK, and pLV-NICD using polyethylenimine (PEI). Twenty-four hours post-transfection, dual-luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1980) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each experimental condition was performed in five independent biological replicates.

RNA-seq analysis

Primary LSECs were isolated from control and Fbxw7 knockout mice treated with CCl4 and subjected to RNA-sequencing analysis. Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were generated using Illumina’s proprietary base calling software. Initial quality assessment and adapter trimming were performed with fastp (v0.23.1) with default parameters. High-quality paired-end reads were then aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (GRCm39 assembly) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Transcript quantification was performed using the featureCounts module of Subread. Gene expression levels were normalized and expressed as FPKM to account for both transcript length and sequencing depth variations. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.36.0) in R (v4.2.1), applying a pre-filtering threshold of minimum 50 total reads across samples. Statistically significant differentially expressed genes were identified using a threshold of absolute |log2FC | >0.5 with p-value < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes and KEGG pathways was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.4.4).

scRNA-seq data analysis

Publicly available human liver scRNA-seq data (GSE136103)24, including cirrhotic (n = 5) and control (n = 5) specimens, were reanalyzed using Seurat (v4.1.0) in R. Initial quality control removed genes detected in fewer than 3 cells and retained high-quality cells meeting stringent criteria: 200–15,000 expressed genes per cell, 2000–25,000 UMIs, mitochondrial gene content <20%, and erythrocyte gene expression <10%. The data were normalized using log-normalization with a scale factor of 10,000, followed by identification of the top 2000 highly variable genes through the “FindVariableFeatures” method. Technical batch effects were corrected using reciprocal PCA integration (top 50 principal components) with default parameters. Significant principal components (determined by elbow plot inflection point) were selected for downstream nonlinear dimensional reduction. Cell clustering was performed using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph-based algorithm, with subsequent visualization via UMAP and t-SNE. Cluster annotation was performed based on canonical marker expression patterns, with endothelial populations identified by characteristic expression of CDH5, ERG, and VWF. SEMA3G expression patterns were visualized using the “FeaturePlot” function with default parameters.

In addition, mouse multi‑organ scRNA‑seq data (E-MTAB-8077)25 from healthy adult male mice (n = 30) were analyzed using the same preprocessing and clustering pipeline. Liver endothelial cells were extracted from the multi‑organ dataset and subclustered into four subtypes: arterial EC, LSEC, lymphatic EC, and cycling EC. Fbxw7 expression was examined in multi‑organ endothelial cells and in the 4 liver endothelial subtypes on UMAP.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0, with data presented as mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed exact Mann–Whitney U tests with Hodges–Lehmann median difference effect sizes were adopted for small non-normal datasets, and two- or one-way ANOVA for multiple-group comparisons, followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc multiple comparisons to evaluate pairwise differences between all four experimental subgroups. All n values represent independent biological replicates. Significance threshold was set at p < 0.05; non-significant comparisons were marked ns. Correlations between serum SEMA3G levels and liver biochemical indices (ALT, AST, ALT/AST ratio) were assessed via Pearson correlation analysis according to data distribution.

Schematic assembly, labeling and layout were performed using Adobe Illustrator 2024. Partial graphic elements were obtained from Figdraw (https://www.figdraw.com/#/paint_index_v2) with official publication authorization (Export IDs: PISTSe18e8, YAUUU9c40b, ROSYW4b4c9).
