First-Principles Prediction of Material Properties from Topological Invariants
Sebastián Alí Sacasa Céspedes

Abstract
Methods for predicting material properties often rely on empirical models or approximations that overlook the fundamental topological nature of quantum interactions. We introduce a topological framework based on string theory and graph geometry that resolves ultraviolet divergences as topological obstructions regularized via Calabi-Yau mappings while preserving symmetries and causal structures, where molecular and condensed matter systems are represented combinatorially through a graph where M-branes form vertices and open strings are twistor-valued edges, holomorphically encoding geometric data from the dynamical system. The resulting effective action is governed by a graph Laplacian whose spectrum dictates stability, excitations, and phase transitions. Applied to uniaxial nematic liquid crystals, the model not only recovers the phenomenological virtual volumes of the Jiron-Castellon model from first principles but also predicts anisotropic thermal expansion coefficients and refractive indices with precision exceeding 0.06%. The quantitative agreement with experiment, achieved without fitted parameters, demonstrates that principles from quantum gravity and string theory can directly yield accurate predictions for complex materials. Keywords: Topological String Theory, Liquid Crystals, Anisotropic Materials, Graph Laplacian, Topological Phase Transitions, Twistor Geometry, Calabi-Yau Manifolds.

This establishes that the universality class is governed not only by symmetry and dimensionality but also by the topology of the underlying molecular network.

\Delta n_{\text{topo}}=\frac{\lambda_{0}^{3}N_{A}\rho}{6\pi M}\chi(G)\left(\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial V}\right)_{T}\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{3}

\kappa_{ij}\approx\kappa_{0}\delta_{ij}

The additional term scales with the squared norm of brane tensions, reflecting that heat can be transported not only by lattice vibrations but also by collective excitations of the brane network. This contribution is inherently anisotropic and nonlocal, since it depends on graph connectivity rather than local geometry. Thus, thermal transport becomes a probe of hidden network structure, predicting observable deviations from Fourier’s law in systems where the brane-graph duality is realized.

Physically, cycles introduce frustration and long-range correlations, softening the approach to the isotropic phase and thus modifying critical exponents from their mean-field values. The remarkable consequence is that universality classes are classified not only by dimensionality and symmetry, as in the Wilson–Fisher paradigm, but also by topological invariants of the underlying network.

Taken together, these predictions demonstrate that topology controls measurable optical, thermal, and critical phenomena in a manner inaccessible to conventional field-theoretic approaches. This reinforces the central claim of the present work: that graph invariants and brane tensions form the true microscopic degrees of freedom governing stability and phase transitions in extended systems.
These predictions are experimentally testable through precision measurements of temperature-dependent birefringence, anisotropic thermal conductivity, specific heat near

4
Discussion

The topological string-graph framework developed here sits at the confluence of several major theoretical currents. Our approach synthesizes insights from high-energy theory, mathematical physics, and computational materials science to produce a novel paradigm for describing quantum matter. It is instructive to situate our work within this broader context to clarify its contributions and limitations.

The use of twistor-valued graph edges draws direct inspiration from Witten’s seminal work on reformulating perturbative gauge theory as a string theory in twistor space
[
35
]

geometric and causal structure
of interactions within a material. The twistors
\mathbb{T}_{ij}
on our graph edges are not auxiliary variables but represent the physical worldsheets of open strings, whose null structure is holomorphically encoded. This provides a direct link between the combinatorial data of the graph and the continuous geometry of spacetime. Concurrently, the concept of dynamical string tension, emphasized by Bars, Steinhardt, and Turok
[
1
]
in a cosmological context, finds a natural home in our framework. Equation (42) promotes the twistor norm
\|\mathbb{T}_{ij}\|
—and hence the interaction strength—to a dynamical variable dependent on brane moduli and the dilaton, moving far beyond static graph models.

Our graph-theoretic perspective is further bolstered by developments in computational materials science. Lazar
et al.

[
15

and Turcani
[

demonstrated the power of topological and graph-based descriptors for analyzing local structure and molecular topology. Bradlyn
et al.

Recent advances in topological string theory provide the essential mathematical underpinnings for our approach. The work of Costello and Li

The answer is an emphatic no; that is not its purpose. While established approaches—from Landau theory to the Vuks or Jirón-Castellón models—excel at describing macroscopic scales, they operate at a coarse-grained level, often requiring empirical input and lacking a fundamental microscopic description of the interacting molecular network. Thus, our approach does not invalidate prior models but rather subsumes them into a more complete, microscopic, and predictive theory, explaining their success from a deeper perspective and enabling prediction of topological effects for systems and its industrial applications.

5
Conclusions

We have developed and validated a topological string-graph framework that bridges fundamental concepts in quantum gravity with practical predictions in materials science. By reinterpreting UV divergences as topological obstructions regularized through Calabi-Yau mappins and cohomology classes, we establish a mathematically consistent and physically predictive theory that operates from first principles without empirical fitting.

The key achievements of this work include the theoretical framework, which integrates twistor geometry, string theory, and graph theory to create a combinatorial representation of molecular and condensed matter systems where M-branes form graph vertices and twistor-valued open strings encode geometric interactions from first-principles predictions thath naturally incorporates non-commutative deformations, dynamical string tensions, and topological invariants, enabling the derivation of material properties, such as anisotropic thermal expansion coefficients and refractive indices, without fitted parameters enabling experimental validation, applied to uniaxial nematic liquid crystals, the framework reproduces and extends the phenomenological virtual volumes of the Jiron-Castellon model, achieving quantitative agreement with experimental data at the 0.06% level for properties including density, refractive indices, and thermal expansion coefficients. The topological control of phase transitions demonstrates that critical exponents near the nematic-isotropic transition are governed by topological invariants of the interaction graph (
\chi(G)
,
b_{1}(G)
), establishing a direct link between graph topology and invariant critical behavior.

This work provides a concrete formulation and experimental validation of a dynamical systems approach to molecular and condensed matter behavior, offering more fundamental explanations than conventional multipole expansions, Landau theories, or bulk liquid crystal models. Our framework improves and extends these classical approaches by applying advanced theoretical concepts from high-energy physics to the design and understanding of real-world materials. The exceptional agreement between theory and experiment underscores the validity of topological regularization and string-theoretic methods in condensed matter systems.

Looking forward, this framework opens new avenues for predicting and designing functional materials with tailored anisotropic responses, from liquid crystals to topological insulators and quantum materials. Computationally efficient implementations of twistor-graph dynamics could further enable high-throughput screening of material properties based solely on molecular topology and symmetry.
