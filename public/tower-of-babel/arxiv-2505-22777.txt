MEDAL: A Framework for Benchmarking LLMs as Multilingual Open-Domain Chatbots and Dialogue Evaluators
John Mendonça, Isabel Trancoso, Alon Lavie

Abstract
As the capabilities of chatbots and their underlying LLMs continue to dramatically improve, evaluating their performance has increasingly become a major blocker to their further development. A major challenge is the available benchmarking datasets, which are largely static, outdated, and lacking in multilingual coverage, limiting their ability to capture subtle linguistic and cultural variations. This paper introduces MEDAL, an automated multi-agent framework for generating, evaluating, and curating more representative and diverse open-domain dialogue evaluation benchmarks 1 1 1 Code and data at github.com/johndmendonca/medal . . Our approach leverages several state-of-the-art LLMs to generate user-chatbot multilingual dialogues, conditioned on varied seed contexts. A strong LLM (GPT-4.1) is then used for a multidimensional analysis of the performance of the chatbots, uncovering noticeable cross-lingual performance differences. Guided by this large-scale evaluation, we curate a new meta-evaluation multilingual benchmark and human-annotate samples with nuanced quality judgments. This benchmark is then used to assess the ability of several reasoning and non-reasoning LLMs to act as evaluators of open-domain dialogues. We find that current LLMs struggle to detect nuanced issues, particularly those involving empathy and reasoning.

Introduction

The ubiquitous use of Large Language Models (LLMs) for the majority of NLP tasks has prompted the research community to largely shift its focus to their development
(
Minaee et al. 2025
)
. As such, newer models are being introduced at an increasingly rapid pace and frequently supplant state-of-the-art results on several established benchmarks
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2024b
)
. However, as these models rapidly advance, the capacity to evaluate their performance in meaningful ways has increasingly struggled to keep pace, becoming a significant impediment to the safe and effective adoption of new LLMs for tasks at large. As such, a critical reflection on whether the established benchmarks themselves continue to be effective and reliable measures of progress has become essential
(
Laskar et al. 2024
)
.

When examining open-domain dialogue evaluation within this rapidly evolving context, we observe significant deficiencies in the methodology used for meta-evaluation of LLM-Judges – LLMs acting as automated evaluators of chatbot performance. In particular, most existing benchmarks rely on obsolete chatbot responses conditioned on human-human interactions
(
Mendonça et al. 2024a
)
, creating a mismatch with contemporary human-chatbot interaction paradigms. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, efforts to diversify benchmarks beyond English have been largely limited to translating existing English datasets into other desired languages, rather than natively curating data in these languages
(
Mendonca et al. 2023
;
Rodríguez-Cantelar et al. 2023
)
, thus failing to capture potential linguistic and cultural nuances in dialogue quality. To address these gaps, this work presents an automated, multi-agent framework called

Our framework operates in three stages. First, we synthetically generate a large collection of dialogues in multiple languages, using several state-of-the-art LLMs acting as agents in the dialogue-generation process (§
3
). These LLMs act both as the chatbot and as the human user, and they are conditioned on diverse seed contexts designed to cover various axes of variability (topic, persona, affective state, and language). User-side generation is validated by an LLM-Judge to ensure coherence. By incorporating different LLMs as dialogue participants and varying seed contexts,
we aim to minimize biases and diversify the quality of the generated dialogue dataset.

In the second stage, we use a state-of-the-art LLM (GPT-4.1) as a strong automated multi-dimensional evaluator of the large multilingual dialogue dataset generated in the first stage (§
4
). This provides a scalable method for assessing chatbot performance across various quality dimensions (e.g., coherence, commonsense, empathy, and language). The large-scale automated annotation uncovered
significant performance differences across languages and LLM sizes
, underscoring the need to expand dialogue evaluation beyond English.

Finally, informed by the large-scale automated evaluation and the dialogue source characteristics (i.e., the participating LLMs), we automatically curate a novel, meta-evaluation benchmark annotated by human experts (§
5
). The curation process aims to maximize the diversity of rated chatbots represented in the curated benchmark, and to ensure a balanced distribution of dialogue quality across multiple dimensions.
One of the main strengths of MEDAL is that it is model agnostic and can be further extended to additional languages and/or LLMs with relative ease
.

Using the curated meta-evaluation benchmark, we conduct a comprehensive assessment of the ability of various state-of-the-art LLMs to perform as automated open-domain dialogue evaluators.
Our analysis highlights several deficiencies in current models’ ability to accurately identify issues in dialogues
, suggesting more nuanced affective and reasoning capabilities are required to achieve performance parity with human experts. Crucially, this methodology allows us, to the best of our knowledge, to perform the
first systematic assessment comparing the open-domain dialogue evaluation capabilities of reasoning and non-reasoning models within a multilingual context

LLMs have also been used as a drop-in replacement of humans via simulation

Meta-evaluation Benchmarks

The majority of currently used dialogue meta-evaluation benchmarks predate the widespread introduction of LLMs

;
Mehri and Eskenazi 2020b
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. This raises concerns regarding their effectiveness at evaluating current human-chatbot interactions, especially considering that the majority of the obtained turn-level annotations use the same dialogue datasets
(

)
.

Our work draws inspiration from two main bodies of work.
Finch et al. 2023b
investigates the ability of ChatGPT-3.5
(

)
for dialogue behaviour detection for nine categories in real human-chatbot dialogues.
Mendonça et al. 2024b
conducts a similar behavioural analysis with the curation of a large scale turn-level benchmark that evaluates dialogues from the
Soda
dataset
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
. This evaluation used GPT-4
(
OpenAI 2024
)
as a judge, and then validated a small portion using human annotators.

While these and other recent benchmarks (detailed in Appendix
A
) offer valuable insights, they predominantly focus on English. A notable exception is the work by
Rodríguez-Cantelar et al. 2023

In light of these limitations, MEDAL positions itself as the first truly multilingual meta-evaluation benchmark that explicitly assesses the performance of a wide range of recent open-access LLMs across multiple languages.

3
Dialogue Generation

3.1
Multi-agent Framework

Given the instruction following and creative writing abilities of LLMs, we prompt-instruct an LLM (acting as a human user and equipped with the topic) to begin and guide the conversation with a chatbot (another LLM, conditioned only on prior conversational context). Since our goal is to evaluate chatbots, we advocate that this approach constructs a realistic simulated representation of actual open-domain human-chatbot interactions
3
3

3

Although it is not the goal of this work, the generated dialogues could be used to finetune efficient chatbots.
. Additional details, including our specific prompts, are described in Appendix
C
.

3.1.1
LLM as a User

To maximise diversity and minimise inherit biases, we condition the generation of dialogues using several contextual cues, including a scene description obtained from a commonsense knowledge graph, persona, an affective state and a target language. The inclusion of detailed information for contextualization purposes has been shown to outperform conversations sampled without context in terms of specificity and interestingness
(
Kim et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
.

Scene description.

We use the templated sentence form of Atomic 10x
(
West et al. 2022
)
to describe scenes. Atomic 10x is a synthetically generated commonsense knowledge graph, consisting of symbolic triplets describing two events and the relation between them. Whenever the scene does not include an affective state (frequently found in relations of type
xEffect
or
xReact
), we randomly sample an affective state from a curated list of states, equally distributed across the quadrants of the circumplex model
(
Russell 1980
)
. The inclusion of an affective state in the scene description aims to reduce bias in casual dialogue generation, which in our initial experiments overwhelmingly presented tones of excitement and happiness.

Persona.

Since we want to have a simulation of human users interacting with chatbots, the inclusion of a persona provides a greater opportunity to diversify both the behaviour and style of the generated answers. We randomly sample personas from the publicly available subset of Persona Hub
(
Ge et al. 2024
)
, a collection of diverse personas automatically curated from web data. Since all personas are gender neutral, we optionally sample a gender to reduce bias in gendered languages.

Language.

To address the lack of resources in other languages, previous work in the curation of multilingual dialogue datasets has translated existing English dialogues into other languages
(
Lin et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2023b
)
. This approach to generating dialogues in non-English languages has serious limitations, the most obvious of which being the strong cultural biases introduced in the resulting translated dialogues (e.g., references to named entities, locations, and/or events). In our case, we keep the templated prompting sentence in English, but ask the LLM to act as a user from a specified country, and allow it to adapt the scene while generating directly in the specified native target language. This promotes better cultural diversity in contrast with translation
4
4

4

A cherry-picked example from our dataset highlights this diversity for a dialogue discussing the music genre ”funk”: In Portuguese it typically refers to
Funk carioca
, whereas in English, it is associated with the 60s North American
Funk
.
.

First Turn Generation.

Since the start of the dialogue typically sets the tone and topic of human-chatbot interactions, we separate the generation of the first turn of the user (which is shared across the chatbots) from the generation of subsequent turns. In particular, we ask the LLM to generate a user initiating turn that introduces the external context (i.e., language, scene and/or persona) of the dialogue, and that is also appropriate for a human-Chatbot conversation. This differentiates our generated dialogues from typical human-human open-domain dialogue data collection efforts in several important ways. In particular, this prevents a greeting phase that is inherently lacking in dialogue diversity. Also, this inhibits user utterances that attribute anthropomorphism features to chatbots, which in real conversations with Chatbots would typically result in a templated refusal to engage by the chatbot (
"As an AI, I don’t have the ability to…"
).

3.1.2
LLM as a Judge

In order to ensure maximum quality in the generation of user utterances, and inspired by prior work
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
, we include an automatic online feedback loop validation step which employs an LLM as a judge. In particular, this feedback loop is tasked with determining if: (1) the dialogue should end (thus preventing situations where the dialogue becomes redundant); and (2) the generated utterance is natural and appropriate w.r.t to the scene, persona, and language. If the generated utterance fails to meet the defined criteria, the user LLM is asked to regenerate the utterance response, conditioned on the rejected response and its feedback. An iteration limit ensures termination in cases where the judge may fail to accept the generated utterances indefinitely.

3.1.3
LLM as a Chatbot

We employ several open-access multilingual LLMs of different sizes as target chatbots. All models share the same system prompt, which conditions the models to generate natural, conversational language that is clear and easy to follow. In order to mimic other studies of open domain chatbot performance, the LLMs generating the chatbot responses have access to the dialogue history but are not explicitly provided with the external context (scene and persona) that was made available to the user agent.

Languages.

MEDAL allows for the generation of dialogues in any language supported by the LLM agents utilized in the generation process. Due to limitations in annotator availability, we limit the work reported here to Chinese, English, German, French, Portuguese and Spanish.

Other Hyperparameters.

We limit the feedback loop to 10 iterations in the first turn. This ensures dialogue generation for every context (scene-language-persona). However, for subsequent turns, we limit the loop to 5 iterations. Our analysis in a subset of dialogues found that 5 iterations was sufficient to ensure an average success rate of over 90%, and keeps costs below a factor of 4
6
6

6

Incorporating the feedback loop makes the process 4 times more expensive in terms of LLM queries, assuming response generation and evaluation steps have equal cost.
. Dialogue length is capped at 10 turns due to cost constraints.

Automated Filtering.

Despite the inclusion of a feedback loop, an initial small-scale evaluation experiment uncovered instances where the user LLM did not adhere to the specified language or failed to maintain its role as a human user (i.e by erroneously reversing the role and acting as the responding Chatbot). We therefore employ an additional filtering step using GPT-4.1 to automatically detect and remove these dialogues, while taking care to keep dialogues that exhibit chatbot deficiencies.

3.3
Results

In total, 38,400 dialogues were generated, stemming from 4,800 unique conversation starters (400 scenes, 2 user LLMs, and 6 languages). The automated filtering step then removed 6.44% of the dialogues, resulting in a final dataset of 35,927 instances. Statistics such as average turn length and diversity metrics are included in Appendix
C
.

Human Validation.

In order to evaluate the performance of the LLM as a user, we conduct a small scale human validation, where we ask annotators to rate the quality of the utterances generated by the LLM acting as a user in terms of their human-likeness, on a 1-5 Likert scale. Our annotator pool consists of a mix of graduate professionals in linguistics and computational linguistics, all with prior experience with annotations

In total, 100 dialogues per language were evaluated. Even when selecting the lowest score, 98% of the dialogues were rated 4 or 5 (5:79.3%; 4:18.7%; 3:1.5%; 2:0.3%; 1:0%), with an average agreement (measured with Krippendorff’s
\alpha

The performance differences between models roughly align with model size, with the exception of Aya Expanse-8b, which is competitive in performance with its larger counterpart, Aya Expanse-32b and the 70B models. We observe that, in general, all models perform best in English. Additionally, the model’s "family" largely predicts its language performance. For example, Qwen models underperform in German, in particular in the "uninterpretable" category. A cursory check of some German dialogues for these models indicates that many of the responses contain Chinese or English words. We also note that Phi-3.5 and Llama-3.1-8B generally struggle more with Chinese. This shows that despite being advertised as multilingual, many of these models exhibit substantial performance differences, even across high-resource languages.

5.1
Benchmark Curation

In order to construct a meta-evaluation benchmark that maximizes the representation of dialogues of diverse quality, we employed a multi-stage, balanced sampling strategy to select 100 dialogues per target language.

Filtering.

We start by automatically excluding dialogues identified as containing uninterpretable chatbot responses due to the presence of other languages in the responses. This is done with simple keyword matching of GPT-4.1 assessments. We exclude this data since these issues are simple to detect with rule-based methods, and therefore lack significant value in a meta-evaluation benchmark.

Balanced Selection.

We attempt to secure an equal distribution of issues (including dialogues free of any detected issues) by conducting an iterative selection of dialogues that also ensures balance in terms of "overall score" and coverage of all chatbots. This is achieved by (1) sampling a dialogue for each user-chatbot pair that contains no issues, and (2) sequentially selecting dialogues that contain each type of issue while minimizing "overall score" and chatbot representation imbalances. Additional details are found in Appendix
E
.

5.4.2
Issue Detection

Following
Finch et al. 2023b
, we calculate Accuracy and F1-scores per language for positive and negative occurrences by averaging results across the double human annotations, whenever available. We present F1 results in Figure
4
. Additional metrics, such as precision, recall, accuracy and predicted counts, are available in Appendix

Looking at F1- performance, all evaluated models achieve results comparable to human expert performance (F1 of over 0.8). This indicates that identifying issue-free dialogues is a relatively easy task for current LLMs. The F1+ results, however, reveal a significant gap between human and model performance across all failure categories. While human annotators retain strong F1+ scores across all categories, the evaluated models struggle to match this performance (with "uninterpretable" and "non-factual" as exceptions). For example, the weakest models (DeepSeek-V3 and Gemini-2.0) score F1- above 0.95 on "commonsense" and "empathy", while their F1+ scores on these two categories are below 0.20. In contrast, GPT-4.1 and o3 exhibit a more balanced performance, with notably higher F1+ values, while maintaining a strong F1-. In general, we find "empathy", "commonsense", and "relevance" issues to be the hardest to detect, with F1+ below 0.4 for even the top models. These likely require a more nuanced understanding of conversational dynamics – areas where current LLMs still struggle.

6
Findings and Contributions

This work introduces
MEDAL, a novel framework for generating and curating simulated multilingual open-domain human-chatbot conversations in support of evaluating current LLMs as both chatbots and evaluators. MEDAL operates in stages: first, it scales multilingual synthetic dialogue generation via a multi-LLM approach, with LLM agents acting as the user, chatbot, and judge. MEDAL then employs a strong LLM-judge for large-scale automated evaluation, yielding insights into the performance of small-to-medium size open-access LLMs as chatbots. These inform the automated curation of our meta-evaluation benchmark, which is used to evaluate several state-of-the-art LLMs acting as dialogue evaluators.

Our key contributions and findings are:

1.

MEDAL provides a novel framework for on-demand generation, evaluation and curation of multilingual dialogues, and evaluation benchmarks.
MEDAL can be further extended to include additional languages and/or LLMs (as users, chatbots, or judges).

2.

MEDAL offers native multilinguality.
Instead of translating existing dialogues, MEDAL generates natively, resulting in more authentic multilingual dialogue datasets.

3.

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of LLMs as both open-domain chatbots and as judges,
finding that: (1) there are significant cross-lingual performance differences between the primary open-access LLMs acting as chatbots; and (2) current LLMs still struggle with detection of nuanced dialogue issues such as "empathy" and "commonsense".

7
Limitations

Generation.

We recognize several limitations in our generation step. First, LLMs exhibit uneven global cultural awareness, which can introduce biased or stereotypical notions when left to generate culturally diverse content
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
(though we argue this approach is preferable than simply translating from English dialogues). Second, to reduce model-specific biases, we employed several small-to-medium sized LLMs spanning different sizes and families. Our framework remains model-agnostic so that any LLM may be integrated. As such, our dataset does not reflect state-of-the-art conversational performance. Finally, we acknowledge a potential bias in our user utterances, since they must conform to the notion of quality set by our LLM-Judge.

Evaluation.

Since our automated evaluation relies on GPT-4.1, we risk introducing model-specific biases. This could have been mitigated by including more LLMs
(
Qian et al. 2025
)
. However, our meta-evaluation indicates this evaluator achieves the highest overall recall in issue detection across languages, and all evaluated judges report middling precision. Therefore, we believe the inclusion of several LLMs in the decision-making process would likely still leads us to overestimate the true prevalence of issues in the dialogues, while some problematic instances may still go unnoticed. Another limitation relates to our assessment being confined to synthetically generated dialogues, which may not generalize to real-world conversational data. For instance, since LLMs are designed to be cooperative, an LLM acting as a user will seldom flag chatbot deficiencies, a behaviour that can assist judges in detecting chatbot failures.

Curation.

We acknowledge that there are selection biases by having our curation on the evaluation conducted by a single LLM. In particular, we sample dialogues that GPT-4.1 identified as having issues. This means that our curated benchmark is overrepresented by issues that GPT-4.1 was able to detect. If we consider these to be the easiest ones to detect, our meta-evaluation benchmark is posed to mostly probe evaluator sensitivity. Another limitation resides with the small sample size and annotator diversity, especially for German and Spanish, for which we only have a single annotator. This limitation, plus the fact that chatbots have different performances for each language, makes meta-evaluation analysis at the language level difficult to generalise.

Benchmarking.

While LLMs appear to showcase breakthrough performance across numerous benchmarks, many of these are rapidly becoming either compromised due to data contamination
(
Sainz et al. 2023
)
, or obsolete as models surpass their difficulty. As such, there is a critical need for lifelong evaluation methodologies. MEDAL offers a promising step in this direction, by enabling on-demand generation of novel, multilingual dialogues, therefore mitigating leakage and ensuring relevance as new LLMs emerge. However, MEDAL relies on automated assessments as a screening stage to reduce human annotation load. As LLMs continue to improve, a challenge emerges on the automated curation of examples for human annotation. Future work should look into more refined evaluation acquisition policies, such as active testing
(
Kossen et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2024c
)
.

8
Ethical Considerations

Annotators.

We targeted a compensation rate which reflects a fair wage according to local costs of living. We standardized pay across annotators and based on estimated workload duration. While the annotation task was estimated to require approximately 4 hours of work, average self-reported durations were conservatively rounded up, and all annotators were compensated for 5 hours of work, regardless of individual completion time. No personal identifiable information about the annotators was stored.

Safety.

Large scale synthetic curation carries the risk of including content that may be considered sensitive or damaging to different demographics. This is particularly relevant on the user-generated side considering that Atomic10X contains content alluding to self-harm or harm to others. We minimize the presence of harmful content by incorporating an LLM-judge that is tasked with refusing particular generations. For the chatbot side, however, part of our goal is to detect the frequency of such issues in their generations. As such, we decided against conducting safety filtering on chatbot responses. However, we acknowledge that our curated benchmark is not comprehensive enough to evaluate specialised content safety classification models.

Appendix C
Dialogue Generation

C.1
Seed Context

Figure 5:
Selected affective states from the circumplex model
(
Russell 1980
)
.

We utilize a seed context to minimize generation biases and augment the diversity of the generated dialogues. This context is composed of 4 different axes of diversity: Scene, Persona (including Gender), Affective State and Language. We then ask an LLM to generate the first utterance of the dialogue taking into account all this information. This composes a fallback strategy, where the LLM is free to chose one of our controlled axis as the topic of the conversation, focusing first on the scene and then on the persona and affective state as backup, or even a combination of several.

Scene.

We take inspiration from
Kim et al. 2023
, which show that Atomic10X scenes can be used as an effective source of social commonsense when scaling synthetic data generation. In detail, we take the same sentence expansions of Atomic10X triplets (CC-BY-4.0) as the scene description for our seed context.

Affective State.

Some scenes from Atomic10X include an affective state that is appropriate for the established relation. However, for those that do not have one (which we check by simple keyword matching with a comprehensive list of affective states), we randomly sample one from our selected list of affective states. We present our selected affective states from the Circumplex model
(
Russell 1980
)
in Figure
5
. This selection was based on sampling an equal number of states on each quadrant (Valence, Arousal), while also minimizing arousal which we believe better reflects the lower intensity of this dimension in textual interactions.

Persona.

We sample the personas from the publicly available subset of PersonaHub
(
Ge et al. 2024
)
. PersonaHub (CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0) is a collection of 1 billion diverse personas automatically curated from web data, which can be used to synthesize high-quality generations at scale.

Gender.

Using a simple rule-based analysis of the scenes and personas, we detect an uneven distribution of gender for Atomic 10X (54% male, 46% neutral for Atomic10X), whereas PersonaHub is purely gender-neutral. Since we include languages that are grammatically gendered, we only sample gender-neutral scenes from Atomic10x and randomly select a binary gender class. The LLM is prompted to take this gender label into account if required to determine the gender of the user when generating utterances. This attempts to minimize gender bias in user generations.

Appendix E
Benchmark Curation

E.1
Example Selection

Given our annotator budget, we were limited to annotating 100 dialogues per language. Given this relatively small sample size, we had to ensure our benchmark was composed of meaningful examples that represent the types of issues LLM-based chatbots incur. As such, we employ a sampling strategy that attempts to balance the distribution of quality in the benchmark.

We obtain this subset by randomly sampling one dialogue per user-chatbot that contains no issues and has a overall score of 5. For the remaining dialogues, we iterate over the types of issues until we obtain our desired benchmark size of 100 examples. For each iteration, we keep track of the distribution of overall score and chatbots and select a dialogue that increases the representation of the minority class. If the minority class is tied, we select one at random. If no examples that match the requirements are found, then we ignore that requirement in the selection

You are a creative writer specializing in crafting human-like casual open-domain interactions with chatbots. Your task is to generate the first message a human user might send to a chatbot, based on the following inputs:

1. Scene Description: A small social context or event description.

2. Persona: A brief description of the individual’s role, background, or identity.

3. Gender: Gender of the individual if not already provided before.

4. Language/Culture: The language or cultural context of the user.

Guidelines:

- Use natural, conversational language typical of casual, open-domain interactions. Messages should feel authentic and concise, limited to one or two small sentences.

- Do not address the chatbot in a manner that assumes it has a physical body, a personal history, or experiences typical of a human (e.g., having a family, personal secrets, or emotions linked to past events).

- Do not write messages that imply the chatbot is someone the user has met before or can relate to as if it were a human friend.

- Ask for advice, opinions, information, or share personal reflections, experiences, or questions that do not attribute human characteristics to the chatbot.

- Reflect the age, emotional state and language/culture of the individual in tone, word choice, and phrasing.

- Incorporate the context from the scene description without explicitly repeating it word-for-word but subtly integrating its essence or themes.

- Do not use placeholder terms like "PersonY". Use realistic names, pronouns, or generic references suitable for the context.

- If gender is required and not provided in the persona or scene, use the one provided as "Gender".

Output:

Provide only the message that the human might send to a chatbot. Do not include quotation marks, meta-commentary, or any additional text outside of the generated message (including "user:").
