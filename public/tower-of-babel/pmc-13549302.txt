Acupuncture Modulation of the Microbiota–Gut–Brain Axis in Major Depressive Disorder: Integrative Mechanisms, Emerging Evidence, and Future Therapeutic Perspectives
Authors: Jingwen Zhang, Linjing Wang, Shuai Shi, Zhihong Liu, Jiajie Niu, Jingwen Zhao, Chunying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and disabling psychiatric condition. Its pathophysiology extends beyond monoamine deficiency to encompass systemic dysregulation of the gut-brain axis—a bidirectional network integrating neural, immune, endocrine, and microbial signals. Accumulating evidence indicates that MDD patients frequently exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis, impaired intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, and systemic low-grade inflammation. These gut-derived pathological alterations may collectively contribute to the onset and progression of depression through complex interactions involving microbial metabolites, immune signaling, and neuroendocrine pathways. Although several recent reviews have addressed gut-brain axis dysfunction in depression or the therapeutic effects of acupuncture individually, a systematic synthesis integrating neuroimmune, neuroendocrine, and microbiome mechanisms within a unified framework remains lacking. This review integrates three key regulatory dimensions—neuroimmune regulation, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis homeostasis, and gut microbiota remodeling—through which acupuncture is proposed to modulate the gut-brain axis in MDD. Acupuncture, a core modality of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), offers unique advantages in intervening in this complex system due to its multi-target regulatory properties. Modern studies have linked classical TCM principles to the vagus-adrenal axis, the endocannabinoid system, the HPA axis, and the TLR4/NF-κB pathways. Specifically, preclinical evidence suggests that acupuncture may induce systemic anti-inflammatory responses, suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation, restore HPA axis negative feedback, and repair the intestinal barrier, while also reshaping gut microbiota composition, promoting short-chain fatty acid synthesis, and regulating tryptophan metabolism. These coordinated actions may generate synergistic multi-system antidepressant effects. Clinical evidence further suggests that acupuncture combined with pharmacotherapy may outperform medication alone, although high-quality clinical trials remain limited. This review critically appraises current evidence and methodological limitations, and proposes future directions including rigorous randomized controlled trials, multi-omics integrative approaches, and microbiome-guided precision acupuncture strategies, aiming to advance individualized acupuncture treatment for MDD.

Introduction

Amid profound transformations in global social structures and economic patterns, psychological stressors have accumulated steadily, leading to a marked increase in the prevalence of depressive disorders. These disorders have now become one of the most common types of mental illness. Characterized by high recurrence rates and significant disability, depressive disorders impose a heavy burden on both individuals and society as chronic mental illnesses.1 Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and suicidal ideation. Cognitive impairment and multiple physical symptoms are also common, and together these features severely impair daily functioning and social adaptation.2,3 According to the World Health Organization, approximately 280 million people worldwide are affected by depression, with a global prevalence of approximately 5.7% in adults.4 The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2019 further confirmed that mental disorders remained among the top ten leading causes of burden worldwide, with no evidence of global reduction since 1990.5 Following the COVID-19 pandemic, the global number of individuals with depression has rapidly increased, now approaching 246 million, highlighting its severity as a public health issue.6 Alarmingly, the World Health Organization (WHO) has projected that by 2030, depression will be the leading cause of disease burden globally.7 Although pharmacotherapy remains the first-line treatment, approximately one-third of patients respond poorly, developing treatment-resistant depression. Long-term medication use may also induce side effects such as sexual dysfunction, weight fluctuations, and gastrointestinal discomfort,8 underscoring the need to explore novel therapeutic pathways from a more comprehensive pathophysiological perspective.

Against this backdrop, the gut-brain axis, a bidirectional signaling network connecting the central nervous system and the gut,9 has emerged as a new focal point in mental disorder research. This modern medical concept aligns with TCM theories emphasizing the close relationship between emotional states and digestive function, such as “Spleen-Stomach deficiency gives rise to a hundred diseases” and “Liver qi stagnation invades the Spleen”. From early life stages, gut microbiota interact with the host to participate in neural system development and regulation, forming the foundation of brain-gut functional connectivity.10 Recent studies reveal that MDD patients commonly exhibit pathological features, including gut microbiota dysbiosis, intestinal barrier damage, and systemic low-grade inflammation. These alterations show significant positive correlations with depression severity,11 suggesting that gut-brain axis dysregulation may play a pivotal role in MDD pathogenesis. Gut microbes exert dual effects on the gut-brain axis: they regulate central neuroimmune and neurotransmitter systems via metabolites (eg, short-chain fatty acids) that cross the blood-brain barrier, and they also increase intestinal mucosal permeability. This latter change may be conceptually aligned with the TCM theory of impaired transport and transformation due to “spleen deficiency with dampness accumulation”. This can also facilitate the translocation of substances like bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS) into the bloodstream, activating systemic inflammatory responses. These substances then profoundly influence central stress circuits and neuroplasticity via afferent pathways such as the vagus nerve.12,13 However, the preponderance of current evidence remains correlative, and a causal relationship between gut microbiota alterations and MDD pathogenesis requires further validation.

Among numerous brain-gut axis intervention strategies, acupuncture, a treasure of TCM, demonstrates unique regulatory potential due to its safety, accessibility, and proven efficacy. Rooted in the traditional principles of “regulating liver qi and harmonizing the spleen-stomach,” this therapy has historically been employed to address emotional and behavioral dysregulation. Modern research further reveals acupuncture’s multi-targeted, holistic regulatory characteristics, particularly in modulating brain-gut interactions. A 2021 study by Liu et al published in Nature demonstrated that electroacupuncture stimulation at Zusanli (ST36) specifically activates deep fascial sensory neurons expressing Prokr2, subsequently inducing catecholamine release via the vagus–adrenal axis to exert systemic anti-inflammatory effects.12,13 Gao et al elucidated at the molecular level that electroacupuncture inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation via cannabinoid CB2 receptors, suggesting its potential therapeutic value for depression-related neuroinflammation.14 Additionally, Wang et al discovered that electroacupuncture enhances tight junction protein expression in the gut via the TLR4/NF-κB p65 pathway, repairing the intestinal mucosal barrier structure.15 This may represent a modern mechanistic correlate for acupuncture’s traditional function of “fortifying the spleen, supplementing qi, and protecting the intestines.” Although most of these mechanism studies originate from animal experiments, and issues such as their applicability to humans, acupoint specificity, and optimization of stimulation parameters require further validation, the existing findings provide an important scientific basis for understanding acupuncture’s role in treating mental disorders.

Although several recent reviews have individually examined gut-brain axis dysfunction in depression,12 acupuncture’s therapeutic effects in MDD,16 or its regulation of gut microbiota,17 none have integrated neuroimmune, neuroendocrine, and microbiome mechanisms within a unified framework. Most existing reviews remain descriptive and pathway-specific, without proposing a conceptual synthesis that bridges these dimensions. To our knowledge, a comprehensive integration encompassing neuroimmune regulation, HPA axis homeostasis, intestinal barrier integrity, microbial metabolism, multi-omics evidence, and precision medicine perspectives has not yet been attempted. This knowledge gap limits the translational potential of mechanistic findings and hinders the development of biomarker-guided, individualized acupuncture strategies. Therefore, this review aims to fill this gap by systematically integrating evidence from three key regulatory dimensions—neuroimmune regulation, HPA axis homeostasis, and gut microbiota remodeling—into a unified conceptual framework that positions acupuncture as a multi-system gut-brain axis modulator. By critically evaluating current research progress and methodological limitations, and by proposing future directions including rigorous randomized controlled trials, multi-omics integrative approaches, and microbiome-guided precision acupuncture strategies, we hope to provide new insights for the integration of acupuncture into evidence-based treatment paradigms for depression.

Pathophysiological Association Between Gut-Brain Axis Dysregulation and Major Depressive Disorder

Cascade Amplification of Neuroimmune Inflammation and Systemic Imbalance

The role of neuroimmune inflammation in the pathophysiology of MDD has evolved from a peripheral hypothesis to a pivotal pathological hub. This process is not an isolated central event but a systemic one involving a complex, ongoing dynamic dialogue between peripheral and central systems. Chronic stress drives brain microglia from a resting, surveillant phenotype toward an activated state, marked by elevated secretion of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α.18 This pro-inflammatory shift can compromise neuronal viability and destabilize the neurochemical systems that support mood and cognition—by impairing glutamatergic synaptic plasticity and by altering the synthesis, release, or reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine.19 This inflammatory state is not necessarily always the “cause” of depression; in certain contexts, it may also be the “result” of prolonged stress, metabolic abnormalities, or psychosocial factors. Its complex bidirectionality demands careful interpretation of the underlying causal networks.

Central neuroinflammation frequently interacts with peripheral immune activation, particularly gut-associated immunity, forming a self-reinforcing cycle. Microbial dysbiosis weakens intestinal epithelial tight junctions, creating a “leaky” barrier that permits luminal contents—including LPS—to enter the circulation. Systemic LPS engages Toll-like receptor 4 on immune cells and triggers MyD88-dependent signaling, which in turn promotes NF-κB nuclear translocation and the transcriptional activation of pro-inflammatory genes.20 In a rat model of depression, acupuncture has been demonstrated to improve depressive-like behaviors through modulation of the gut microbiota and the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, suggesting that this pathway may serve as a critical molecular interface linking gut dysbiosis to central inflammation.21 However, the degree of pathway activation varies significantly among individuals. Not all MDD patients exhibit typical “leaky gut” or elevated LPS levels, suggesting this mechanism may apply only to specific inflammatory subtypes rather than being a universal feature of MDD.

This gut-initiated immune activation can propagate these effects to the central nervous system through multiple pathways. Circulating inflammatory mediators may directly enter the brain through an inflammation-compromised blood-brain barrier or induce local production of inflammatory mediators by activating cerebral microvascular endothelial cells. Additionally, vagal afferent fibers, as rapid neural pathways, can detect intra-abdominal immune signals and transmit them to the solitary nucleus in the brainstem, thereby influencing higher-order brain functions and neuroendocrine responses.22 Miller and Raison note that this multi-pathway, multidimensional neuroimmune dialogue readily forms a self-reinforcing inflammatory cycle. This framework may help explain why some patients respond poorly to conventional antidepressants. It also provides a rationale for developing therapeutic strategies that target the immune system.19,23 Nevertheless, targeting neuroinflammation for therapeutic intervention remains challenging. Key questions include how to precisely modulate microglial function without compromising their essential immune surveillance role, and how to identify patient populations most likely to benefit from anti-inflammatory treatments.

Collectively, current evidence supports neuroimmune regulation as one of the most consistently reported biological effects of acupuncture in preclinical models of MDD. However, the majority of mechanistic findings originate from animal studies, and the causal relationships among neuroinflammation, gut microbiota alterations, and clinical improvement remain incompletely understood. Additionally, the heterogeneity in inflammatory profiles across MDD patients suggests that neuroimmune-targeted acupuncture may be most beneficial for specific subtypes, highlighting the need for biomarker-driven patient stratification in future clinical trials.

Bidirectional Regulation Between the HPA Axis and Microbiota

The HPA axis remains a cornerstone of MDD neurobiology. Classically, MDD has been linked to HPA hyperactivity, in which hypothalamic CRH neurons drive pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) release and sustain elevated cortisol levels.24 However, this understanding remains incomplete. HPA axis disruption involves a complex network encompassing receptor sensitivity, negative feedback efficacy, and epigenetic modifications. Impaired glucocorticoid receptor signaling in limbic regions like the hippocampus weakens its inhibitory control over the HPA axis, creating a state of persistent hypercortisolemia. This may represent a key molecular basis driving depression-related cognitive impairment and emotional disturbances.

The gut microbiota, as a crucial environmental modulator, exhibits profound connections with the developmental and functional shaping of the HPA axis. Early studies by Sudo et al in germ-free animals revealed that mice lacking microbial colonization exhibited markedly heightened HPA axis stress responsiveness, an abnormality that could be reversed by colonizing them with specific bacterial strains during infancy.25 This finding underscores the fundamental role of the microbiota in shaping HPA axis function, but the precise mechanisms remain unclear. Several lines of evidence point to potential mediators. For instance, specific probiotics may enhance glucocorticoid receptor expression and nuclear translocation in the hippocampus; short-chain fatty acids, through histone deacetylase inhibition, may epigenetically remodel stress-related gene expression; and microbial products such as GABA may act directly on hypothalamic neuroendocrine pathways.26,27 The complexity of this interaction lies in its pronounced bidirectionality. Chronic stress-induced elevations in glucocorticoids significantly alter gut microbiota composition and functional diversity, and also compromise intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. Mechanistically, glucocorticoid receptor activation downregulates tight junction proteins such as claudin-1, thereby increasing intestinal permeability.28 This creates a self-reinforcing pathological cycle: stress drives HPA axis activation, glucocorticoids disrupt microbial homeostasis and damage the intestinal barrier, dysbiosis then promotes systemic inflammation, and inflammatory signals further exacerbate HPA axis dysfunction by enhancing CRH release. Foster et al29 emphasize that this cycle may be a key mechanism underlying the chronicity and treatment resistance of depression.

Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that whether this closed-loop applies to all MDD patients remains controversial. Substantial interindividual variations in microbiota composition, differing histories of stress exposure, and diverse genetic backgrounds may all influence the specific form of HPA axis-microbiota communication. Most evidence remains derived from highly controlled animal models, whose direct correspondence to human pathophysiology requires further clarification. Translating microbiome interventions into clinically effective therapeutic strategies necessitates resolving key questions: whether optimal strain combinations exist, whether critical windows for intervention timing exist, and which biomarkers should inform personalized treatment protocols.

The HPA-microbiota loop offers a compelling model for understanding MDD chronicity, but its relevance likely varies across individuals due to differences in microbial ecology, genetics, and stress exposure. Interventions that target both systems—acupuncture being one example—could theoretically outperform single-target approaches, though clinical translation awaits robust stratification biomarkers. A note of caution: across studies, reported microbial changes are inconsistent, causality remains unproven, and confounders such as diet and medication use are often inadequately controlled.

Loss of Intestinal Barrier Integrity

Simply categorizing intestinal barrier dysfunction as a concomitant phenomenon of MDD may significantly underestimate its active role in disease progression. Intestinal barrier integrity reflects the integrated function of several layers: an apical tight junction protein network, a protective mucus coat, and an underlying immune surveillance system. Claudin-family proteins—particularly claudin-1—are essential components of the tight junction network that controls paracellular permeability. The selective channels they create strictly regulate material exchange between the intestinal lumen and submucosal tissues. In animal models exhibiting depressive-like behaviors and in postmortem tissues from MDD patients, the expression patterns of these structural proteins show significant disruption, indicating substantial impairment of barrier function.30

From a translational medicine perspective, alterations in serum biomarkers provide clinically relevant evidence for this mechanism. Elevated circulating levels of zonulin and fatty acid-binding protein 2 (FABP2) correlate dose-dependently with depressive symptom severity and have been shown to predict treatment response in longitudinal follow-up studies.31 However, the specificity of these biomarkers remains controversial — they also appear in other inflammatory bowel diseases, requiring careful consideration of their diagnostic specificity during interpretation. They may be more suitable as indicators of systemic barrier dysfunction rather than MDD-specific markers.

At the mechanistic level, psychological stress-induced gut barrier disruption is not a passive process but occurs through highly specific molecular pathways. Under chronic stress, excessive HPA axis activation drives corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) to act both centrally and peripherally—directly binding intestinal corticotropin-releasing factor 1 (CRF1) receptors as a peripheral signaling molecule. This activation triggers a myosin light chain kinase phosphorylation cascade, leading to cytoskeletal reorganization, tight junction protein internalization and degradation, and ultimately abnormal increases in epithelial barrier permeability.32 Of note, this process exhibits marked heterogeneity across intestinal segments. The ileum and colon regions demonstrate differing sensitivities to stress-induced injury due to variations in microbiota composition, immune cell infiltration patterns, and physiological functions.

The disruption of barrier integrity initiates a cascade of reactions: microorganisms and their metabolites translocate into the circulation, activating the systemic immune system; dysfunction of enteroendocrine cells affects the synthesis of neuroactive substances, including serotonin precursors; and impaired nutrient absorption leads to energy metabolism disorders. These alterations collectively impact the central nervous system through immune, neural, and metabolic pathways, disrupting bidirectional brain-gut communication.33,34 However, it is important to recognize that this systemic impact varies significantly between individuals. Gut barrier dysfunction is more likely a characteristic alteration specific to certain subtypes of MDD rather than a universal, inevitable phenomenon. This heterogeneity challenges simplistic causal explanations while also offering a potential direction for future patient stratification and treatment based on gut barrier biomarkers.

Intestinal barrier integrity emerges as a critical intermediary linking gut-derived inflammatory signals to central neuropathology in MDD. Serum biomarkers such as zonulin and FABP2 offer clinically accessible tools for assessing barrier function, though their diagnostic specificity requires careful interpretation. The heterogeneous nature of barrier dysfunction across MDD patients suggests that it may serve as a valuable stratification marker rather than a universal therapeutic target. This heterogeneity, while complicating simplistic causal models, provides a compelling rationale for personalized approaches that assess and target barrier integrity in selected patient subsets.

The Multisystem Regulatory Mechanisms of Acupuncture on the Gut-Brain Axis

Viewing acupuncture merely as a local stimulus no longer suffices to explain its systemic regulatory effects in treating MDD. Grounded in TCM’s holistic philosophy, acupuncture functions more like a precise systemic regulator, with its core role being to “disperse stagnation and harmonize pivotal mechanisms.” By stimulating meridian qi and blood flow and coordinating viscera functions, acupuncture synergistically intervenes across multiple pathways (neurological, immune, endocrine, and microbiome) to restore the dynamic equilibrium of “yin and yang in harmony.” This multi-level, multi-target action aligns profoundly with TCM’s therapeutic wisdom of “treating different diseases with the same approach” and “holistic regulation,” granting it unique advantages in addressing the multidimensional dysregulation characteristic of the complex gut-brain axis. Recent comprehensive reviews have further consolidated the understanding that acupuncture exerts antidepressant effects through multi-target mechanisms, including neurotransmitter balance regulation (5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), glutamate, dopamine), neuroplasticity enhancement, inhibition of key inflammatory pathways such as P2X purinoceptor 7 (P2X7R)/NLRP3 and NF-κB, HPA axis modulation, and tryptophan metabolism regulation.35

Precision Regulation of Neuroimmune-Inflammatory Dialogue

In the progression of MDD, neuroimmune-inflammatory processes appear to form a critical bridge linking gut dysfunction to central nervous system symptoms. Abnormal microglial activation drives this pathological pathway, disrupting neurotransmitter balance and synaptic plasticity through complex signaling cascades and ultimately producing the core clinical features of depression. Acupuncture may modulate this neuroimmune dialogue by acting on multiple nodes within the pathway. From a TCM perspective, this process aligns closely with the pathogenesis of “liver qi stagnation transforming into fire”, which subsequently disturbs mental clarity and obstructs the qi movement of the zang-fu organs. Thereby achieving the TCM therapeutic aims of “soothing the liver to relieve depression” and “clearing heat to purge fire”. By selecting acupoints such as LR3 (Taichong), GB34 (Yanglingquan), and HT7 (Shenmen), acupuncture regulates liver qi flow, drains excess fire, and calms the shen. This restores harmonious interaction among the zang-fu organs and reestablishes balance between yin and yang. Clinical evidence supports its efficacy in alleviating irritability, insomnia, and chest distension—core manifestations of this pattern. Emerging evidence has further highlighted that acupuncture’s antidepressant effects are mediated through the decoding of neuroimmune crosstalk, involving the restoration of HPA axis homeostasis, suppression of microglial and astrocytic activation, modulation of autophagy, inhibition of key inflammatory signaling pathways, and activation of vagus-mediated anti-inflammatory pathways.36

Acupuncture’s regulation of the neuroimmune system involves the activation of highly specific neural pathways. Electroacupuncture at Zusanli (ST36) selectively activates deep fascial sensory neurons expressing the Prokr2 protein, providing crucial neurobiological evidence for acupoint specificity.13,37 While these findings provide compelling preclinical evidence from well-controlled animal models, their direct translation to clinical practice in MDD patients requires confirmation through human mechanistic studies that can validate the same neural pathways. Activation of these specific neurons triggers a distinct neural pathway: signals are transmitted through the dorsal horn of the spinal cord to the ventromedial nucleus of the hypothalamus, subsequently activating the sympathetic-adrenal axis. This prompts the adrenal medulla to release catecholamines, achieving rapid and effective systemic anti-inflammatory effects.13,38,39 This rapid regulatory pathway from the body surface to visceral organs exemplifies the classical Chinese medicine theory that “where the meridians pass, the therapeutic effects reach.” More importantly, it provides experimental evidence for understanding the modern scientific implications of acupuncture’s regulation of qi mechanisms.

At the cellular and molecular level, acupuncture demonstrates precise regulatory capabilities over microglial function. Studies indicate that electroacupuncture intervention significantly suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome hyperactivation in the hippocampus induced by chronic stress, reduces caspase-1 enzyme activity, and decreases mature IL-1β production.14,40,41 In a post-stroke depression model, acupuncture at Baihui (GV20), Shenting (GV24), and bilateral Zusanli (ST36) was shown to increase gut microbial diversity, upregulate beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae, and simultaneously downregulate NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and caspase-1 protein expression in the colon, leading to reduced serum IL-1β and IL-18 levels.42 In addition, electroacupuncture at Zusanli (ST36) has been reported to attenuate depressive-like behaviors in post-stroke depression mice by inhibiting the hippocampal TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and promoting hippocampal neurogenesis.43 The molecular mechanism of this regulatory process involves the participation of the endocannabinoid system. The anti-inflammatory effects of electroacupuncture are significantly attenuated by CB2 receptor-specific antagonists. This suggests that acupuncture may promote the release of endogenous cannabinoids, acting in a paracrine manner on CB2 receptors on the surface of microglia to inhibit inflammasome activation.44,45 This finding offers a new perspective for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying acupuncture’s heat-clearing and fire-purging effects. It should be noted, however, that these observations are derived from rodent models of depression, and whether similar NLRP3 suppression occurs in the human brain following acupuncture treatment remains to be determined.

Beyond direct regulation of microglia, acupuncture also significantly improves astrocyte function. Under depressive states, impaired glutamate uptake by astrocytes leads to synaptic glutamate accumulation and excitotoxicity. Research indicates that electroacupuncture intervention can upregulate the expression of the glutamate transporter glutamate transporter-1 (GLT-1) in astrocytes, enhancing their ability to clear glutamate and thereby improving the neuronal microenvironment.27,46,47 This regulation of glutamatergic system homeostasis synergizes with the suppression of microglial inflammatory responses, collectively forming the cellular basis for acupuncture’s effects in resolving phlegm, opening orifices, calming the mind, and soothing the spirit.

Acupuncture’s regulation of the central nervous system immunity is closely linked to intestinal barrier function. Disruption of intestinal barrier integrity leads to translocation of microbial metabolites, a key driver of systemic inflammation. Experimental studies indicate that electroacupuncture at the Zusanli (ST36) point enhances intestinal tight junction protein expression via the vagus nerve-cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, thereby improving intestinal barrier function.13,15,48 This local intestinal regulation forms a positive feedback loop with central anti-inflammatory effects, establishing a complete bidirectional “gut-brain” regulatory circuit.

From a neurochemical perspective, acupuncture’s regulation of neuroimmunity ultimately manifests as improved neurotransmitter system function. Inflammatory factors activate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, redirecting tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway and reducing serotonin synthesis. Electroacupuncture intervention reverses this process while regulating the metabolism of monoaminergic neurotransmitters such as dopamine and norepinephrine, thereby correcting neurochemical imbalances associated with depression.49 Emerging evidence further indicates that electroacupuncture may ameliorate depression and comorbid constipation through the tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (TPH2)/5-HT pathway of the gut-brain axis, suggesting that the regulation of serotonin synthesis in the gut may be an additional mechanism contributing to acupuncture’s antidepressant effects.50 This multi-targeted modulation of the neurotransmitter system may represent a key mechanism through which acupuncture alleviates core depressive symptoms.

In sum, acupuncture appears to regulate neuroimmune inflammation through coordinated actions at multiple levels: it activates specific neural pathways (eg, Prokr2-expressing neurons), modulates microglial and astrocytic function, protects the intestinal barrier, and rebalances neurotransmitter systems. This multi-tiered profile may underpin its advantages in addressing the complex neuroimmune abnormalities characteristic of MDD. This holistic regulatory characteristic confers unique advantages in addressing neuroimmune abnormalities in MDD, providing scientific evidence for understanding acupuncture’s antidepressant effects via the gut-brain axis.

Thus, acupuncture appears to act as a systemic neuroimmune modulator via multiple coordinated mechanisms: Prokr2-mediated neural signaling, CB2-dependent NLRP3 suppression, astrocytic GLT-1 upregulation, and vagal protection of the gut barrier. This multi-target profile distinguishes it from purely local interventions. Yet most of this evidence comes from animal models; how these pathways translate to MDD patients—and which contribute most to clinical benefit—remains largely unknown.

Synergistic Mechanisms of HPA Axis Steady-State Restoration and Gut Barrier Repair

In the pathological progression of MDD, dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and gut barrier damage may form a mutually reinforcing vicious cycle. This process aligns with the pathogenesis evolution described in TCM as “liver qi stagnation with spleen deficiency and heart-kidney disharmony.” Disruption of this bidirectional regulatory pathway sustains neuroendocrine activation. This in turn compromises intestinal mucosal integrity and triggers systemic inflammation, creating a cascade that propels disease progression. The unique value of acupuncture treatment lies in its ability to simultaneously intervene at two critical junctures of this cycle, achieving synergistic effects in HPA axis homeostasis restoration and intestinal barrier repair to fulfill therapeutic objectives of soothing the liver and strengthening the spleen, as well as harmonizing the heart and kidney.

From a neuroendocrine regulation perspective, acupuncture exhibits significant central specificity in modulating HPA axis function. Studies indicate that electroacupuncture stimulation at Baihui (GV20) and Yintang (EX-HN3) acupoints markedly suppresses excessive excitation of corticotropin-releasing hormone neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, reducing their transcriptional activity.16,38,51–53 A comprehensive 2024 review by Zheng et al systematically summarized the molecular mechanisms by which acupuncture modulates HPA axis dysfunction, highlighting the involvement of multiple neurotransmitter receptors, neuropeptides, and microRNAs in the paraventricular nucleus, hippocampus, amygdala, and pituitary.54 The molecular basis for this regulatory effect may involve epigenetic modifications. Experimental evidence indicates that electroacupuncture promotes histone acetylation in the glucocorticoid receptor promoter region of the hippocampus, enhancing its transcriptional activity while downregulating the expression of the negative regulatory factor FKBP5.55,56 This epigenetic remodeling—by elevating glucocorticoid receptor expression and enhancing its nuclear translocation—appears to restore the negative feedback sensitivity of the HPA axis, an effect that aligns with acupuncture’s traditional function of calming the spirit (Shen) and rebalancing yin and yang.

Regarding intestinal barrier repair, acupuncture exhibits multi-pathway regulatory capabilities. Electroacupuncture at the ST36 (Zusanli) acupoint inhibits stress-induced internalization and degradation of tight junction proteins by suppressing myosin light chain kinase phosphorylation in intestinal epithelial cells.15,32,57 The molecular mechanism involves regulating the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway, where electroacupuncture reduces the activity of key molecules to maintain cytoskeletal stability. Concurrently, electroacupuncture appears to restore intestinal mucosal barrier integrity and upregulates tight junction proteins (eg, ZO-1, occludin) through the TLR4/NF-κB p65 pathway.15 Desmosomal proteins such as Desmocollin-2 (DSC2) also play a key role in maintaining intestinal epithelial integrity and facilitating mucosal repair.58,59 These findings provide modern biological interpretations for acupuncture’s traditional efficacy in strengthening the spleen, boosting qi, and fortifying the intestines.

The vagus nerve plays a pivotal role in this synergistic regulatory mechanism. Research indicates that vagus nerve efferent fibers activated by acupuncture release acetylcholine, which binds to α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on intestinal mucosal immune cells, thereby inhibiting the activation of the nuclear factor κB signaling pathway.22,39,60,61 Activation of this cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway reduces local intestinal inflammation. By lowering circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, this pathway may, in turn, improve HPA axis glucocorticoid resistance. The functional importance of this vagal pathway is supported by transection experiments, in which acupuncture’s protective effects on both the HPA axis and intestinal barrier are markedly diminished.13,62 This also corroborates the importance of TCM’s concept of “regulating qi” in therapeutic interventions.

From a metabolic perspective, acupuncture’s regulation of the HPA-gut barrier axis also involves alterations in short-chain fatty acid metabolism. Electroacupuncture intervention promotes butyrate production by gut microbiota. Butyrate has both local and systemic effects. Locally, it provides energy to intestinal epithelial cells; systemically, it acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor, enhancing hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression via epigenetic mechanisms.63 This metabolite-mediated gut-brain crosstalk offers new insights into acupuncture’s distal effects, embodying the traditional Chinese medical principle of “nourishing the innate with the acquired.” Additionally, butyrate activates G protein-coupled receptors to promote mucin secretion by intestinal epithelial cells, further strengthening the intestinal mucosal defense function,64 consistent with the therapeutic goal of “strengthening the spleen and resolving dampness.”

Acupuncture’s regulation of this synergistic pathway exhibits distinct acupoint specificity. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies reveal that stimulating different acupoints activates distinct brain functional networks: Baihui primarily modulates the prefrontal cortex-limbic system circuit, while Zusanli predominantly influences the solitary nucleus-vagus nerve pathway.51,52,65 These divergent central response patterns may explain the relative specificity of different acupoints in regulating the HPA axis and intestinal function, providing a scientific rationale for clinical syndrome differentiation and acupoint selection.

At the clinical translation level, this synergistic regulatory mechanism holds significant therapeutic implications. Studies indicate that acupuncture treatment can significantly reduce cortisol levels in the serum of MDD patients while simultaneously improving intestinal barrier function indicators.66,67 This dual improvement effect correlates positively with the degree of clinical symptom relief, suggesting that the synergistic action of HPA axis homeostasis restoration and intestinal barrier repair may contribute to acupuncture’s antidepressant efficacy. Acupuncture also exhibits effects on this pathway. Follow-up observations after treatment cessation indicate that improvements in relevant indicators can be maintained for extended periods,17,49 reflecting acupuncture’s therapeutic characteristic of strengthening the body’s inherent resilience. Although these clinical findings are promising, the number of studies remains small, and most lack rigorous control for placebo effects, underscoring the need for larger, well-designed trials to confirm these biomarker changes.

Taken together, these findings suggest that acupuncture achieves synergistic regulation of HPA axis homeostasis and intestinal barrier repair through multiple mechanisms involving epigenetic regulation, neuroimmune dialogue, and metabolite mediation. This multi-targeted, holistic regulatory profile confers unique advantages for addressing neuroendocrine-immune network dysregulation in MDD, offering new insights into acupuncture’s therapeutic mechanisms via the gut-brain axis.

The evidence reviewed above supports the view that acupuncture simultaneously targets the HPA axis and intestinal barrier through a coordinated mechanism involving epigenetic reprogramming of glucocorticoid receptor expression, vagus-mediated cholinergic anti-inflammatory signaling, and butyrate-dependent metabolic regulation. This dual targeting capability positions acupuncture as a particularly promising intervention for MDD patients with concurrent neuroendocrine and gastrointestinal dysregulation. However, the relative contribution of each pathway to clinical outcomes, and whether this synergistic effect can be optimized through specific acupoint combinations or stimulation parameters, warrants systematic investigation in future human studies.

Reshaping the Gut Microbiome Ecology and Metabolic Networks

The restructuring of gut microbial communities and their metabolic outputs represents another key mechanism of acupuncture’s regulation of the gut-brain axis. This process is closely related to the TCM concept of “spleen-stomach transformation and transportation” functions. TCM theory posits that “the spleen is the foundation of acquired constitution and the source of qi and blood production.” As a vital “microecological organ” within the human body, the proper functioning of the gut microbiota can be regarded as a micro-level manifestation of spleen-stomach transformation and transportation. A growing body of evidence indicates that acupuncture influences multiple diseases, including depression, through the regulation of gut microbiota composition and function, with specific microbial taxa and their metabolites serving as key mediators of the gut-brain communication.68

Analyses using 16S rRNA high‑throughput sequencing have revealed that the decline in α diversity induced by chronic stress was significantly reversed following electroacupuncture intervention. This structural adjustment entailed a favorable shift in gut microbial composition: probiotic-associated genera such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium increased, whereas pro-inflammatory genera such as Desulfovibrio decreased.44,45 In a post-stroke depression model, acupuncture at Baihui (GV20), Shenting (GV24), and bilateral Zusanli (ST36) similarly increased gut microbial diversity and upregulated beneficial taxa, including Bifidobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae, while simultaneously suppressing colonic NLRP3 inflammasome activation.42 A recent multi-omics study integrating 16S rRNA sequencing and UPLC-MS/MS metabolomics demonstrated that Mongolian medical warm acupuncture restored gut microbial diversity, increased SCFA-producing genera (Lactobacillus and Prevotella), normalized neuroactive metabolites including taurine and arginine, and upregulated intestinal tight junction proteins such as occludin, ZO-1/TJP1, and claudin-4.69 In addition, electroacupuncture at the “Siguan” acupoints (bilateral Hegu, LI4, and Taichong, LR3) has been shown to modulate gut microbiota composition by increasing beneficial Firmicutes and Clostridia while reducing potentially pathogenic Bacteroidetes and Bacteroidia in a rat model of post-stroke depression.70 From a TCM perspective, this optimized microbial structure directly reflects acupuncture’s therapeutic effects of “strengthening the spleen and harmonizing the stomach, supporting the body’s vital energy and expelling pathogens.” By restoring balance to the intestinal microecology, it rebuilds the foundational health principle of “when vital energy resides within, pathogens cannot invade.” Whether this altered community structure responds site-specifically to acupuncture points, and whether a causal relationship exists between these microbial changes and behavioral improvement, warrants further investigation—ideally through refined microbiota transplantation experiments. This requires validation through more refined microbiota transplantation experiments.

Metabolically, alterations in short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are particularly noteworthy. Following electroacupuncture treatment in depressive animal models, fecal concentrations of acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid showed an overall upward trend, with the most significant increase observed in butyric acid. Butyrate acts locally as a histone deacetylase inhibitor, providing energy for intestinal epithelial cells and enhancing barrier integrity. Beyond these local effects, it crosses the blood-brain barrier to exert neuroprotective actions. Research by Stilling et al confirmed that butyrate promotes the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor in the hippocampus through epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, thereby influencing neuroplasticity processes.27,46,47 From a TCM perspective, short-chain fatty acids, as products of gut microbial metabolism, align with the theory of “the spleen governs the ascent of clear essence.” This concept describes the spleen’s role in transporting nutrients to the entire body, including the brain, the “seat of the spirit.” However, these findings warrant caution: Can exogenous butyrate supplementation fully replicate the effects of electroacupuncture? Do butyrate’s actions vary across different brain regions? These questions remain inadequately addressed.

Within the tryptophan metabolic pathway, acupuncture’s influence is more complex and multidimensional. Electroacupuncture intervention significantly reduces the plasma kynurenine/tryptophan ratio in rats with lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced depression, diminishes the production of the neuroexcitotoxic quinolinic acid, and may simultaneously increase the bioavailability of serotonin synthesis precursors. This metabolic shift is partly attributed to acupuncture’s inhibition of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) activity. As a key molecular link between inflammation and the neuroendocrine system, IDO is strongly induced under stress conditions.48 Interestingly, this regulatory effect may be significantly influenced by baseline microbial composition. Inter-individual variations in microbiome structure could explain the considerable heterogeneity in treatment responses across different study subjects. From a TCM perspective, the restoration of tryptophan metabolic balance reflects acupuncture’s holistic regulatory principle of “harmonizing qi and blood, balancing yin and yang.” It exerts antidepressant effects by improving the material foundation of the “heart governing mental clarity.”

From a systems biology perspective, acupuncture’s influence on the microbiota-metabolite axis exhibits distinct spatiotemporal dynamics. Changes in community structure often precede normalization of the metabolite profile, while behavioral improvements typically follow these biological alterations. This cascade reaction pattern suggests that the reshaping of microbial ecology may be a fundamental link in acupuncture’s sustained antidepressant effects. This aligns closely with TCM’s emphasis on “the spleen and stomach as the source of transformation and transportation,” which addresses the root cause of disease by regulating the foundation of acquired constitution to improve overall health. However, in-depth research in this field still faces methodological challenges: most current microbial analyses remain at the genus level, with precise identification at the species or strain level awaiting technological breakthroughs; Biological interpretation of metabolomics data also requires rigorous functional validation experiments. Of note, the majority of these microbiome findings are derived from animal studies, and the extent to which they reflect changes in the human gut ecosystem remains uncertain.

Future research should focus on elucidating the specific mechanisms by which key metabolites mediate gut-brain signaling, identifying the specific regulatory patterns of different acupoint stimulations on microbial function, and exploring the feasibility of predicting acupuncture efficacy based on baseline microbial characteristics. Progress in these areas holds dual promise: it could advance our mechanistic understanding of acupuncture while also informing the optimization of personalized treatment protocols grounded in syndrome differentiation. This could propel acupuncture’s evolution from traditional empirical medicine toward precision medicine. The evidence summarized above indicates that acupuncture modulates gut microbial ecology in a manner characterized by increased SCFA-producing bacteria, reduced pro-inflammatory taxa, and favorable metabolic shifts in tryptophan catabolism. These microbial changes likely contribute to acupuncture’s antidepressant effects through metabolite-mediated signaling to the central nervous system. Nevertheless, the field faces significant methodological constraints, including the predominance of genus-level taxonomic resolution and the limited functional validation of metabolomic findings. Future research should prioritize metagenomic and metabolomic integration to establish causal relationships between specific microbial strains, their metabolic outputs, and behavioral outcomes.

Regulatory Characteristics and Acupoint Functional Networks in Spatiotemporal Dimensions

To examine acupuncture’s intervention on the gut-brain axis, it must be understood within a dynamic spatiotemporal framework, a perspective that aligns with the temporal and spatial patterns of qi circulation in TCM. Responses across different physiological systems exhibit distinct temporal hierarchies: adjustments in autonomic nervous activity and reductions in certain inflammatory mediators are observable immediately after needling, representing the rapid response phase that demonstrates acupuncture’s instantaneous “qi regulation” effects. Conversely, the restructuring of microbial communities and stable changes in their metabolic products emerge only after multiple treatment cycles, constituting a long-term regulatory process that reflects acupuncture’s cumulative “root-cause treatment” effects. Long-term follow-up studies by Tian et al revealed that this temporal cascade follows a pattern progressing from rapid neural regulation to intermediate immune-endocrine adjustments, culminating in long-term microbial remodeling.49 This progressive regulatory process reflects the TCM triad of “qi, spirit, and form”—a conceptual framework spanning the material, functional, and spiritual dimensions of health. This temporal framework supports the cumulative nature of acupuncture’s benefits, yet its practical utility for guiding treatment frequency is constrained by individual baseline states—such as vagal tone—that can substantially alter response dynamics. For instance, patients with initially low vagal tone may exhibit relatively slower systemic responses, underscoring the importance of personalized treatment protocols and embodying TCM’s principle of “treating each person according to their unique constitution.”

In the spatial dimension, acupoint stimulation demonstrates fascinating specificity and synergistic effects, profoundly reflecting the scientific essence of TCM’s meridian theory. Functional networks formed by distinct acupoints appear to exhibit relatively specific regulatory tendencies toward different segments of the gut-brain axis. Located on the Governor Vessel, Baihui (GV20) is traditionally used for mental disorders. Modern research demonstrates its significant regulatory role in the limbic system-HPA axis circuit, embodying the theoretical positioning that “the brain is the residence of the original spirit.” As a convergence point on the Stomach Meridian of Foot-Yangming, “Zusanli” (ST36) excels in regulating gastrointestinal motility, immune homeostasis, and systemic inflammatory networks, validating the ancient adage “For abdominal issues, seek Zusanli.” Meanwhile, the large intestine’s Mu point (Tianshu, ST25) acts more directly on the local intestinal microenvironment, influencing barrier function and microbial spatial distribution.16,51–53 This functional differentiation based on meridian pathways and point characteristics provides a modern scientific rationale for clinical syndrome differentiation and point selection. However, is this specificity absolute or relative? Reports from different research teams show considerable variation, potentially influenced by factors such as stimulation parameters, individual meridian sensitivity, and disease stage, reflecting the clinical wisdom of TCM’s “same disease, different treatment.”

More intricately, these systems do not operate as simple superpositions but form complex interactive networks, aligning closely with TCM’s “holistic concept.” Activation of the vagus nerve exerts broad effects on the gut-brain axis. Through cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathways, it suppresses inflammatory factors in peripheral immune organs; it also modulates gastrointestinal motility and mucus secretion. This alters the physicochemical properties of intestinal contents, thereby influencing microbial colonization ecology and metabolic activities. Bonaz et al propose that the vagus nerve is more than a passive signaling conduit—it actively integrates bidirectional signals between the gut and the brain.22,55,56 Intriguingly, short-chain fatty acids (particularly butyrate) produced by microbial metabolism further enhance vagal ganglion excitability and signaling efficiency, forming a positive feedback loop. This bidirectional dialogue among “brain-gut-microbiota” resonates intriguingly with traditional Chinese medical theories such as “the heart and small intestine are paired organs” and “the spleen and stomach govern the ascending and descending movements of qi”.

This multi-level, multi-system synergistic amplification effect endows acupuncture with unique advantages when intervening in systemic diseases like MDD. Regarding point selection and combination, pairing Baihui (GV20) with Zusanli (ST36) achieves simultaneous regulation of the mind-spirit (upper body) and spleen-stomach (middle jiao), while combining Taichong (LR3) with Zusanli (ST36) enables concurrent treatment of liver and spleen. Modern research confirms these classical point combinations produce synergistic effects where “1+1>2”. However, we must acknowledge that the current understanding of this networked regulatory mechanism remains in its infancy. Questions regarding the synergistic mechanisms of different acupoint combinations, the selection of optimal intervention time windows, and the optimization criteria for individualized acupoint protocols all require answers through rigorously designed systems biology research. In-depth exploration of these issues could advance the precision of acupuncture treatment and potentially inspire new approaches to complex systemic diseases. More broadly, such research may help bridge traditional medical wisdom with modern scientific methods.

The spatiotemporal dynamics of acupuncture’s effects—spanning rapid neural responses, intermediate immune-endocrine adjustments, and long-term microbial remodeling—highlight the importance of optimizing treatment frequency and duration based on individual baseline states. The functional differentiation among acupoints suggests that rational point selection can strategically target specific components of the gut-brain axis network. However, the relative versus absolute nature of acupoint specificity and the optimal combination strategies for synergistic effects require further elucidation through network pharmacology and systems biology approaches.

Evidence Integration and Development Pathways from a Clinical Translation Perspective

Construction and Validation of a Multidimensional Evidence Network

Building upon the preclinical mechanisms summarized in Table 1, we now turn to the clinical evidence that supports the translational potential of acupuncture in MDD. As our focus shifts from basic research to clinical practice, the evidence network for acupuncture’s intervention in MDD via the gut-brain axis is gradually forming its unique architecture. A meta-analysis incorporating 29 randomized controlled trials demonstrated that combining acupuncture with conventional antidepressant medication yielded an effect size of −1.78 (95% CI: −2.34 to −1.22) in improving Hamilton Depression Rating Scale scores, with this advantage becoming apparent early in treatment.57 More recently, a large-scale meta-analysis incorporating 66 randomized controlled trials with 5744 participants confirmed that acupuncture combined with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) significantly reduced Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores (SMD = −1.185), increased treatment response rates (RR = 1.391), and improved remission rates (RR = 1.597), with a favorable safety profile.71 Similarly, a systematic review of 60 studies involving 4,675 participants with mild-to-moderate MDD reported that acupuncture alone (SMD = −0.57) or in combination with antidepressants (SMD = −1.00) significantly alleviated depressive symptoms and improved insomnia, with only mild adverse events.72 Of note, this synergistic effect was more pronounced in patients with gastrointestinal discomfort, suggesting that regulating the gut-brain axis may represent a unique pathway through which acupuncture exerts its effects. This aligns closely with the TCM concept of “treating depression by regulating the spleen and stomach.” However, caution is warranted in interpreting these findings—most current studies have limited follow-up periods, and the long-term efficacy of acupuncture requires further validation through high-quality research.

Resting-state fMRI allows direct visualization of acupuncture’s central nervous system effects. Wang et al observed that after 4 weeks of acupuncture intervention, abnormalities in functional connectivity between the default mode network and salience network in MDD patients improved. This change synchronized with elevated serum BDNF levels and negatively correlated with reduced IL-6 concentrations, an inflammatory marker.15,37,58,59 This dual “imaging-molecular” validation strategy provides robust support for constructing the complete effect chain of “regulating the spirit, harmonizing the viscera, and unblocking the meridians,” reflecting acupuncture’s characteristic of treating both form and spirit. However, is this correlation specific? Could other treatments also induce similar changes? These questions require further clarification through the establishment of active control groups.

At the microbiome level, exploratory research by Chen et al observed increased relative abundance of short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria (eg, Prevotella, Ruminococcus) in patients’ gut microbiota following acupuncture intervention, with this change correlated to clinical symptom improvement.60 While these findings are encouraging, it is important to recognize that current clinical studies remain limited in number and sample size and lack standardized dietary control. These factors may compromise the reliability of the results. Future research with larger samples and stricter controls is needed to validate these preliminary findings and further explore the intrinsic link between acupuncture’s “tonifying the spleen and harmonizing the stomach” effect and microbiota regulation. This highlights a critical gap between preclinical mechanistic insights and clinical validation, emphasizing the need for translational studies that directly test whether animal-derived mechanistic hypotheses hold in human MDD patients.

Individualized Strategies Within a Precision Medicine Framework

Given the high heterogeneity of MDD, biomarker-based patient stratification emerges as a critical pathway for optimizing acupuncture efficacy. Research indicates that patients exhibiting “inflammatory subtype” characteristics (marked by high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) > 3 mg/L and elevated IL-6) have been reported to respond better to acupoint protocols emphasizing anti-inflammatory effects. (eg, Zusanli, Quchi), demonstrating significantly higher symptom improvement rates compared to non-inflammatory subtypes.62 This classification method, based on pathophysiological features, provides a modern scientific interpretation for traditional syndrome differentiation and treatment.

Autonomic nervous system function, particularly vagal tone, constitutes another promising predictive dimension. Heart rate variability analysis indicates that patients with higher baseline high-frequency power exhibit more pronounced responses to acupuncture treatment.63 This finding has prompted researchers to explore the possibility of tailoring treatment protocols based on autonomic function profiles, such as intensifying stimulation at points like Neiguan (PC6) and Zusanli (ST36) for individuals with low vagal tone. However, standardizing such personalized strategies remains challenging, involving issues like uniform assessment methods and precise control of intervention parameters.

Baseline gut microbiome characteristics offer a novel perspective for treatment individualization. Liu et al attempted to predict acupuncture efficacy through fecal microbiota analysis, finding predictive models based on the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio and butyrate-producing bacteria abundance demonstrated reasonable accuracy.64 This underpins the emerging concept of “microbiome-guided precision acupuncture.” However, its clinical implementation requires resolving numerous technical hurdles, including standardized sampling, unified analytical workflows, and consistent interpretation of results. This approach imbues the traditional TCM principle of “tailored treatment for each individual” with contemporary relevance.

Methodological Challenges and Future Directions

The field continues to face significant methodological challenges. Several methodological challenges warrant attention. First, adequate blinding is inherently difficult in acupuncture trials given the physical nature of the intervention,66 and sham acupuncture may not fully control for the complex placebo effects associated with needling sensations.67 Second, the acupuncture literature may be susceptible to publication bias favoring positive results. Third, study quality varies considerably across trials, with heterogeneity in randomization procedures, sample size calculations, and outcome assessments. The primary issue lies in balancing treatment standardization with individualization. Studies exhibit substantial variations in acupoint selection, stimulation parameters, and treatment frequency. Yang et al advocate for maintaining core operational standards while allowing appropriate adjustments based on individual responses.65 This “preserving fundamentals while innovating” research approach respects traditional acupuncture practice while meeting modern clinical research standards. However, implementing this “limited flexibility” protocol design remains challenging, particularly in maintaining operational consistency across multicenter studies.

Methodological challenges in implementing blinding also warrant attention. While traditional sham acupuncture controls have somewhat improved blinding success rates, neuroimaging studies by Lee et al indicate that even non-specific needling activates certain brain regions.66,67 This suggests we must re-evaluate the rationality of current control designs and explore more persuasive experimental approaches, such as innovative designs like “stepwise randomized controlled trials” or “individualized controls.”

Conclusion

Based on a systematic review of existing evidence, this review suggests that acupuncture may constitute a comprehensive, multi-level regulatory system for intervening in MDD via the gut-brain axis, although it must be emphasized that many of these mechanistic insights remain preliminary and require further validation. The proposed framework rests on three mechanistic pillars. Neuroimmunologically, acupuncture appears to dampen hippocampal NLRP3 inflammasome activity, suppress microglial cytokine release, and improve astrocytic glutamate handling via GLT-1 upregulation. At the neuroendocrine level, epigenetic modulation of glucocorticoid receptor expression and FKBP5 downregulation may restore HPA negative feedback sensitivity. In the gut microbiome, acupuncture seems to promote SCFA-producing bacteria, boost butyrate, and lower the kynurenine/tryptophan ratio—changes that could support serotonin synthesis and neuroplasticity. These mechanisms collectively suggest acupuncture’s multi-targeted and holistic therapeutic advantages.

These findings lend biological support to the traditional TCM approach of “regulating liver qi and harmonizing spleen-stomach functions” in depression, and point toward a biologically grounded rationale for integrating acupuncture into precision medicine. By integrating neuroimmune, neuroendocrine, and microbiome dimensions into a unified framework, this review positions acupuncture as a “multi-system gut-brain axis modulator”—a conceptualization that distinguishes it from previous reviews that have addressed these dimensions separately.

Nevertheless, three major limitations of the current evidence base warrant acknowledgment. First, the majority of mechanistic insights derive from preclinical animal studies, and their direct correspondence to human pathophysiology requires further validation. Second, acupuncture protocols exhibit considerable heterogeneity across studies in terms of acupoint selection, stimulation parameters, and treatment frequency, complicating cross-study comparisons and meta-analytic synthesis. Third, high-quality randomized controlled trials with adequate sample sizes, active comparators, and long-term follow-up remain scarce, limiting the strength of clinical recommendations.

Looking forward, the integration of modern multi-omics technologies—including metagenomics, metabolomics, and proteomics—with traditional syndrome differentiation and treatment principles holds promise for advancing patient stratification and personalized acupuncture protocols. The emerging concept of “microbiome-guided precision acupuncture” offers a particularly compelling direction for future research. Although challenges remain in standardizing treatment protocols and addressing individual therapeutic heterogeneity, these methodological and technological advances position acupuncture to offer a distinctive and evidence-based therapeutic option for depression in the era of precision medicine, provided that rigorous translational research continues to bridge the gap between bench and bedside.
