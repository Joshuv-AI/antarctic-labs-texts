Successfully treated a rare case of amebic mediastinal abscess complicated by a pseudoaneurysm of the right subclavian artery
Authors: Qun-Xian Zhang, Qiang Guo, Hua Liu, Tao Liu, Ye Yuan, Min Zeng, Ye Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmebiasis, caused by Entamoeba histolytica, is an intestinal disease that can lead to severe complications such as ulcers and abscesses. While the intestine is most commonly affected, extraintestinal involvement is also possible. However, mediastinal amebiasis remains rare. This case report details a patient with amebiasis who developed an amebic mediastinal abscess, which subsequently ruptured into the pleural cavity, resulting in bilateral empyema and a pseudoaneurysm of the right subclavian artery. Treatment was promptly initiated with metronidazole, alongside closed pleural drainage. Additionally, endovascular stent placement was performed to address the right subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm. The patient was discharged in good health on the 37th postoperative day. The treatment approach described offers valuable clinical insights for frontline healthcare providers.Keywords: Amebiasis, MA, Empyema, Amebic trophozoites, Stent placement

Introduction

Amebiasis, caused by the parasitic protozoan Entamoeba histolytica. The global prevalence of human amebic infections is approximately 3.6% [1]. Each year, an estimated 40,000 to 100,000 individuals worldwide succumb to amoebic disease, making it the fourth leading cause of death from protozoan infections. The mortality rate among patients without complications is less than 1%. However, in 5% to 10% of patients, the emergence of complications significantly increases the mortality rate [2]. Mediastinal amebiasis (MA) remains rare. This report presents a 12-year-old child who initially developed an amebic mediastinal abscess that invaded the right subclavian artery, leading to the formation and rupture of a pseudoaneurysm, which subsequently caused bilateral empyema. Amebic trophozoites were confirmed in the pleural fluid through culture. The patient was treated with intravenous metronidazole and oral chloroquine, supplemented with chest tube drainage and endovascular stenting of the right subclavian artery to treat the pseudoaneurysm. His condition improved significantly, with no recurrence observed during follow-up visits. The treatment approach outlined in this case provides valuable therapeutic insights and guidance for frontline healthcare providers.

Case description

The patient is a 12-year-old male who presented to the hospital with intermittent fever lasting for 5 days and chest tightness and pain for 2 days. Initially, he experienced intermittent high fever, with a peak temperature of 39.3 °C, and accompanied by pharyngodynia and paroxysmal cough. On the third day of treatment at the local hospital, his condition showed no significant improvement, and he developed retrosternal chest pain and chest tightness, both of which were exacerbated by breathing. At this time, the patient was directly transported to our hospital by ambulance. Upon arrival at our hospital, the patient had a temperature of 37.3 °C, heart rate of 156 beats/min, respiratory rate of 40 breaths/min, and blood pressure of 102/55 mmHg.

Laboratory results revealed the following: White blood cell (WBC) count: 15.49 × 109/L, Neutrophil percentage: 85.4%, C-reactive protein (CRP): 142.21 mg/L, Erythrocyte sedimentation rate: 41 mm/h, Procalcitonin: 9.27 ng/ml, Alanine aminotransferase: 124 U/L, Aspartate aminotransferase: 60.5 U/L. The electrocardiogram indicated sinus tachycardia. Chest CT scans revealed increased density in the thymic region, a small amount of pericardial effusion, minimal bilateral pleural effusion, and possible infectious lesions in the lower lobes of both lungs (Fig. 1A and B). The patient was diagnosed with septicemia and chest pain, and treatment with meropenem and linezolid was initiated for infection.

Changes in the patient’s pleural effusion during the first four days of hospitalization

On the second day of admission, Enhanced chest CT revealed an enlarged thymus with uneven enhancement. There was a moderate increase in bilateral pleural effusion, along with incomplete expansion of the lower lobes of both lungs and pleural inflammation (Fig. 1C and D). Bilateral thoracentesis was performed, draining 500 ml of chylous pleural effusion. Despite these interventions, the patient’s dyspnea and fever persisted, and he displayed drowsiness, poor responsiveness, increased heart rate, elevated respiratory rate, and elevated blood pressure. On the fourth hospital day, chest CT revealed moderate to large amounts of pleural effusion bilaterally and incomplete expansion of the lower lobes (Fig. 1E and F). Ultrasound-guided bilateral pleural catheter placement was performed, draining 1790 ml of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid culture identified a streptococcus infection. At this point, we considered the possibility of infection. Antibiotic treatment is administered based on the results of antibiotic sensitivity. Over the course of treatment, the patient’s PLT count progressively declined, and both WBC and CRP showed significant changes (Fig. 2A-C). On the seventh day of hospitalization, and after antibiotic changes and chest drain placement, amebic trophozoites were found using in vitro culture and microscopic examination of trophozoites and cysts (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, amebiasis complicated by empyema was suspected, prompting the addition of metronidazole to the treatment regimen.

Diagnosis of mediastinal amebiasis and its complications. (A) Amebic trophozoites in the pleural fluid; (B-E) Enhanced chest CT indicating the formation of an aneurysm

On the eighth day of admission, the patient presented with dyspnea, poor responsiveness, and bilateral chest tubes drained 1630 ml of dark red fluid. In addition, enhanced chest CT revealed a pseudoaneurysm at the proximal right subclavian artery (Fig. 3B-E). It is suspected that MA involves the blood vessels, eventually leading to the formation of a pseudoaneurysm in the right subclavian artery, which locally ruptures to form a hematoma. Aneurysms have the risk of rupture. An emergency procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Surgical Process: Angiography revealed a pseudoaneurysm measuring approximately 4 × 3 cm in size, located distal to the right subclavian artery (Fig. 4A). Based on these findings, a lifestream balloon-expandable vascular stent-graft was deployed (Fig. 4B).

Endovascular stent placement in the right subclavian artery. (A) Pre-angiography; (B) Post-stent placement

Postoperatively, the patient received a combination of metronidazole, cefoperazone-sulbactam, vancomycin, and minocycline for infection, along with metronidazole irrigation of the pleural cavity. On the 10th postoperative day, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted to include metronidazole and piperacillin for continued infection therapy. Following aggressive therapy, the patient’s condition gradually stabilized. Chest CT showed no significant pleural effusion, and the mediastinal hematoma demonstrated slight absorption compared to previous scans. The patient was discharged on day 37 after the operation. One year post-operation, enhanced chest CT revealed changes in the proximal pseudoaneurysm of the right subclavian artery related to the stent. A small amount of right-sided pleural effusion had decreased, and patchy or segmental atelectasis in the lower lobes showed improvement (Fig. 5A and B). CT angiography of the aorta demonstrated post-stenting changes in the proximal pseudoaneurysm of the right subclavian artery, with visible metallic stent shadows. The stent maintained its regular shape and patency (Fig. 5C-E).

Discussion

Amebiasis, caused by the parasitic protozoan Entamoeba histolytica, primarily affects children and school-aged individuals who are more susceptible to the infection [3]. Clinically, the intestine is the most commonly affected organ, while MA remains particularly rare. This rarity is largely due to the dissemination of trophozoites through the bloodstream via the venous system [2, 4]. The incidence of amebiasis is currently low in our country, with extraintestinal cases being rare, initial symptoms are often nonspecific, which can contribute to misdiagnosis. This patient experienced a 12-day delay from onset to diagnosis, during which early anti-infection treatments were ineffective, and the condition progressively worsened. This highlights the critical importance of timely diagnosis for effective treatment of amebiasis.

Subsequent enhanced chest CT revealed a new pseudoaneurysm at the proximal right subclavian artery. The formation of the pseudoaneurysm may be attributed to several factors: (1) Entamoeba histolytica enters the human body through the digestive tract and may invade the intestinal wall, entering the bloodstream. This invasion can damage the blood vessel wall by disrupting endothelial cells and the mesostructure of the vessels. (2) The amoeba infection triggers local inflammation, releasing inflammatory mediators that further compromise the integrity of the blood vessel walls. (3) Persistent inflammation and infection cause the blood vessel walls to weaken, making them unable to withstand normal blood pressure, leading to localized dilation. Central line placement may also damage blood vessels, but plain chest CT scan indicates that the catheterization position is good. Therefore, this factor can be ruled out. For ongoing treatment, endovascular stent placement was performed, and metronidazole irrigation of the pleural cavity continued. The patient’s condition gradually stabilized. Follow-up chest CT revealed no significant pleural effusion, and the mediastinal hematoma showed slight absorption. The child was successfully extubated and discharged. Follow-up CT angiography showed changes related to the stenting of the proximal pseudoaneurysm of the right subclavian artery. Mediastinal infections involving blood vessels and aneurysm formation can present life-threatening risks, particularly due to the high mortality rate associated with aneurysm rupture. Timely diagnosis and prompt management of complications are critical for achieving a favorable prognosis.

This child presented with a short history of illness and was diagnosed with primary MA, which made the diagnosis particularly challenging. Unlike other reports, the low incidence of pseudoaneurysms in amebiasis was overcome through our prompt diagnosis and effective treatment. When an infected individual presents with unexplained chest pain, fever, and signs of pleural infection or pleural effusion, the possibility of MA should be considered. Timely acquisition of pleural fluid for parasitic culture is essential, as delays in diagnosis can lead to severe complications. The treatment approach and lessons learned from this case aim to provide valuable therapeutic insights for frontline healthcare workers, ultimately reducing the serious complications associated with amebiasis and improving the quality of life and prognosis for affected children.
