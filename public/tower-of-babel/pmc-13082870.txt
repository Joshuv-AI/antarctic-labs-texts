Fatty acid metabolism—an emerging regulatory node in T‐cell immunometabolism
Authors: Brett Chapel Arenberg, Michael N Sack, Jing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the roles of glucose metabolism and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates in immune regulation are well established, the contribution of fatty acid metabolism remains less well defined. In this review, we examine current knowledge on the immunomodulatory functions of fatty acid metabolism, with a particular focus on T‐cell biology. We discuss the catabolic, anabolic and signalling aspects of lipid metabolism and their influence on immune function. Short‐chain fatty acids modulate T‐cell epigenetics through histone acetylation, thereby impacting gene expression. Medium‐ and long‐chain fatty acids augment fatty acid oxidation (FAO), which supports the expansion and function of regulatory T‐cell populations. Very‐long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids, when cleaved from the membrane, serve as precursors for both pro‐inflammatory and pro‐resolving signalling molecules. Additionally, de novo fatty acid synthesis contributes to membrane biogenesis and alters acetyl‐CoA availability, linking lipid metabolism to epigenetic regulation. The mechanisms described above present promising opportunities to modulate inflammation through targeted therapies. In this review, we focus on emerging targets within fatty acid metabolism pathways that show potential for influencing inflammatory responses in translational models. Specifically, we review key transcription factors, metabolic enzymes and dietary interventions, while also addressing current limitations and challenges in translating these findings to clinical settings. Although studies in murine models have yielded encouraging results, substantial gaps remain—particularly in applying these metabolic strategies to human T‐cell biology. We conclude by emphasising the importance of validating these targets in ex vivo human models as a critical step towards future clinical intervention.

Introduction

Over 100 years ago, Otto Warburg highlighted the importance of metabolic dynamism in tumor cells. Cancerous cells preferentially generate ATP by anaerobic glycolysis, 1 allowing the mitochondria to employ other fuel substrates for biosynthetic functions and tumor proliferation. This same metabolic remodelling is evident in immune cells, a concept termed immunometabolism; 2 where different metabolites confer regulatory effects on immune cell fate and function. To date, the effects of glucose, amino acids and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) intermediates on immunometabolism have been extensively studied, 3 , 4 , 5 whereas the role of fatty acid metabolism is less well characterised. 6

This review aimed to elucidate the gaps in our knowledge of fatty acid metabolism within the immune system, with a particular emphasis on T‐cell metabolism. T cells are selected as the focal point of this review because of the influence of fatty acid metabolism on T‐cell polarisation and the central role of these adaptive lineages in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases through mechanisms, including cytokine production, B‐cell activation and direct cytotoxic effects. Here we initially provide a concise overview of fatty acid metabolism in T‐cell immunoregulation. Subsequently, we highlight emerging and promising targets of fatty acid metabolism that warrant further clinical and translational investigation, emphasising their potential to modulate disease processes and highlight existing knowledge gaps in understanding their functions.

A brief perspective of fatty acid metabolism in immunoregulation

Fatty acids are complex carboxylic acids, with either saturated or unsaturated aliphatic chains. The hydrocarbon chain length is important in defining the nomenclature and function of fatty acids. Short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are generated during gut microbiome metabolism and then absorbed from the gut where they play a role in signalling and acetylation. Medium‐ and long‐chain fatty acids (LCFAs), predominantly replenished from the diet, contribute towards fatty acid oxidation (FAO). Very‐long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), also derived from the diet, are metabolised into signalling molecules with either pro‐inflammatory or pro‐resolving functions. Additionally, medium‐, long‐ and very‐long‐chain fatty acids can be synthesised de novo via fatty acid synthesis (FAS). Although not comprehensive, the section below outlines the major roles of these metabolites in immunometabolism.

Fatty acids

Short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

Indigestible polysaccharides are metabolised by the gut microbiome to generate SCFAs (C to C6 carbon chain lengths). 7 These molecules play an important role in modulating the gut‐immune system. High levels of SCFAs in the mouse gut decrease Th1 T‐cell subsets and increase regulatory T‐cell levels. 8 The SCFA pentanoate reduces murine Th17 differentiation while increasing T‐cell release of IL‐10 by inhibiting histone deacetylase activity. 9 Butyrate supplementation enhances murine gut Treg populations and polarises dendritic cells into pro‐resolving phenotypes. 10 , 11 , 12 These effects appear to be regulated at the epigenetic level, where enhanced H3 acetylation at the FOXP3 locus increases murine Treg differentiation. 10 Interestingly, butyrate supplementation reduces mouse T‐cell ATP citrate lyase (ACLY) levels, an enzyme that generates glucose derived acetyl‐CoA. 13 Inhibition of ACLY and the subsequent reduction in cytosolic acetyl‐CoA levels diminishes histone acetylation and blunts murine Th17 immune responsiveness. 14 In humans, elevated levels of the SCFA propionate similarly blunts histone deacetylase activity and immune responsiveness in Th1 and Th17 cells. 15 Additionally, butyrate drives inducible Treg (iTreg) cell differentiation in mouse by increasing CPT1A activity and fatty acid oxidation. 11 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SCFAs reshape T‐cell epigenetic landscapes to modulate gene expression programmes that control T‐cell differentiation and effector function. The role of the microbiome as a source of bacterial SCFA synthesis on T‐cell regulation remains poorly defined. Nevertheless, restoration of a more ‘primitive’ microbiome in individuals consuming a minimally processed, high‐fibre diet is associated with higher SCFA levels and reduced systemic inflammatory biomarkers. 16 Additionally, a human study shows that Ramadan‐linked intermittent fasting increases the gut microbiome diversity and bacterial families linked to butyrate production. 17 A further study, which did not directly assess the gut microbiome, demonstrates that fasting blunts Th1 and Th17 immune responsiveness. 18 Hence, although the composition of the gut microbiome, SCFA synthesis and T‐cell regulation have not been comprehensively or integratively defined, these early data implicate the importance of the gut‐immune‐SCFA‐signalling axis in T‐cell immunoregulation.

Medium‐ and Long‐chain Fatty Acids (MCFAs and LCFAs)

Dietary MCFAs (C6–C12 carbons) and LCFAs (C13–C21 carbons) are the predominant fuels supporting mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO). MCFAs passively diffuse through the mitochondrial membranes whereas LCFAs are transported across the mitochondrial membrane via the carnitine palmitoyl transferase (CPT) shuttle. Mitochondrial FAO produces reducing equivalents that power the electron transport chain. Each oxidation cycle decreases the hydrocarbon chain length by two carbons until acetyl‐CoA acetyltransferase (ACAT) cleaves acetoacetate into the final two acetyl‐CoA molecules. The role of FAO derived energy and metabolic intermediates in immunity is beginning to be explored. In murine studies, the transcriptional induction of FAO drives CD8+ T effector memory (T mem) cell and CD4+ T regulatory (T reg) cell polarisation. 19 , 20 The direct role of these dietary FAs in human T‐cell biology remains to be characterised.

Very‐long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs)

The PUFAs have a > 21 hydrocarbon chain length and their nomenclature is based on the location of their terminal carbon double bond from the methyl end. Omega‐3 fatty acids have a double bond at the third carbon from their methyl end and omega‐6 fatty acids have a double bond at the sixth carbon from the end.

PUFAs are either absorbed through the small intestine or hydrolysed from cell membranes to be metabolised into pro‐inflammatory or pro‐resolving signalling molecules. Among them, linoleic acid (LA; C18: 2n‐6) is the most abundant omega‐6 PUFA. 21 LA treatment promotes CD8+ T‐cell (CTL) mitochondrial fitness and redirects CTL towards a memory‐like phenotype, which results in improved tumor control. 22 Other examples include omega‐6 arachidonic acid, which can be metabolised into signalling molecules called eicosanoids. Eicosanoids are predominantly pro‐inflammatory and include prostaglandins, leukotrienes and thromboxanes. 23 They play a myriad of roles in modulating T‐cell biology, including roles in proliferation, apoptosis, cytokine secretion and chemotaxis. 24 In contrast, specialised pro‐resolving mediators (SPMs) are derived from omega‐3 fatty acids, specifically docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA). SPMs also play a role in regulating mouse T‐cell function and can restore the Treg/Th17 balance by inhibiting NLRP3 signalling within T cells. 25 Furthermore, evidence from CD5L‐deficient mouse T cells suggests that changing the ratio of saturated to PUFAs influences Th17 cell function and pathogenicity. 26 In humans, the conjugation of arachidonic acid with the amino acid glycine to form N‐arachidonylglycine blunt human Th1 and Th17 immune responses by acting through its cognate G‐protein‐coupled receptor, GPR18, and inhibiting canonical CD4+ T‐cell signalling. 27

A further class of lipid mediators are the endocannabinoids. Endocannabinoids are derived from DHA and EPA and act on the cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2. While these receptors typically act in the brain, they are also expressed in immune cells. The endocannabinoid anandamide (AEA) reduces human T‐cell proliferation and Th17 differentiation. 28 , 29 AEA's ability to modulate T‐cell biology may be independent of cannabinoid receptor and rather dependent on nuclear receptor 4 interactions. 30 A schematic of the divergent roles of PUFAs on T‐cell function are shown in Figure 2.

Fatty acid synthesis

Fatty acid synthesis generates fatty acids that are essential for cell membranes or act as signalling molecules in activated immune cells. However, its role in T‐cell regulation remains poorly defined. Nevertheless, ACLY, which is important in FAS and in the production of cytosolic acetyl‐CoA, is important for the epigenetic shift in gene expression for fatty acid biosynthetic function and aerobic glycolysis during murine T‐cell activation. 31 , 32 Acetyl‐CoA synthetase (ACSS) is another source of acetyl‐CoA, generating the molecule from the SCFA acetate. ACSS activity is important for CD8+ activation in both mice and humans. Furthermore, inhibiting FAS in murine and human T cells reduces Th2 and Th17 activation while promoting Treg polarisation. 33 , 34

New targets for clinical and basic research

Given these emerging immunoregulatory roles of fatty acid metabolism, we ask whether modulation of these metabolic pathways may improve T‐cell‐linked pathology. The direct mechanisms whereby fatty acid metabolism can be modulated include through transcriptional and metabolic enzyme regulation, and via the supplementation of specific metabolites. In the section below, we propose emerging targets within these mechanisms to modulate T‐cell function with potential future utility in immune‐linked disease. We highlight what is known about these targets and gaps in our understanding for further study.

Fatty acid metabolism‐related transcription factors

The regulation of fatty acid metabolism is controlled in part by nuclear receptors mediated transcription. The two main transcription factors controlling fatty acid metabolism are the family of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors (PPARs) and liver X receptor (LXR).

Peroxisome proliferator activated receptors (PPARs)

PPAR isoforms are constitutively expressed and their activity is modulated by fatty acid intermediates which function as ligands. 35 Furthermore, PPARs control energy homeostasis by regulating fatty acid catabolism, 36 and are known to modulate inflammatory responses.

PPAR activation tends to promote Treg function in mice and humans. PPARγ function in skin resident Tregs promotes IL‐10 production and attenuates imiquimod induced psoriasis in mice by upregulating CD36 fatty acid uptake. 37 Mechanistic studies suggest PPAR agonism enhances fatty acid oxidation by upregulating CPT1 and inhibiting the glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase. 38 Induction of PPARα with pemafibrate blunted experimental inflammatory disease and autoimmunity in mice and reduced the ratio of Th17/Tregs in human peripheral blood. 39

Liver X receptor (LXR)

LXR is a cholesterol‐sensing nuclear receptor expressed predominantly in the liver, but is also present in immune cells, where it is postulated to regulate membrane PUFA composition by controlling fatty acid elongation and desaturation. 40 While most often considered a target for diabetes or atherosclerotic disease, these transcription factors have also been investigated for their ability to modulate inflammation. 36 , 41 Two LXR isoforms are expressed with LXRα (NR1H3) being predominantly evident in metabolically active tissues, such as liver, adipose tissue and macrophages; whereas LXRβ (NR1H2) is ubiquitously expressed and is present in T cells.

Interestingly though, LXRα and LXRβ both contribute to the dampening of pro‐inflammatory T‐cell responses, particularly Th17 cells. Pharmacologic activation of LXRα and LXRβ reduces human Th17 activation by modulating IL‐17 expression. 42 In contrast, genetic deletion of either isoform in mice increases Th17 induction, and deletion of both isoforms confers resistance to suppression by LXR agonists. 43 In human CD4+ T cells, activation of LXRα and LXRβ similarly reduces Th17 activity whereas in mice the LXRβ depletion specifically increases both Th1 and Th17 populations. 44 Interestingly, LXRβ knockdown also decreased the T‐follicular helper cell population, demonstrating their more complex role in modulating and promoting immune response. 45

LXR activation also appears to modulate the immune system by influencing T‐cell proliferation and survival. Activation of both LXR isoforms inhibits proliferation and differentiation of T‐helper cells in mice and humans by promoting apoptosis and altering membrane composition. 46 , 47 Additionally, the endogenous LXR agonist 27‐Hydroxycholesterol reduces the population of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in a murine tumor model, 48 further supporting a broader immunosuppressive role for LXR signalling.

In contrast to their inhibitory effects in helper T cells, activation of LXRs promotes T regulatory (Treg) cell proliferation and immunosuppressive function. For example, LXR agonists stimulate the expansion of murine gut‐associated Tregs with enhanced suppressive ability against Th1 and Th17 cells. 49 Similarly, indirect activation of LXR via fenofibrate increases FOXP3+ Tregs while reducing IL‐17 and IFN‐γ. 50 This concept is delineated further where LXRβ was found to be essential for Treg survival and function, manifest by early‐onset murine systemic autoimmunity following complete or partial Treg LXRβ knockdown. 44 Collectively, these data underscore the intrinsic role of LXRs in maintaining immune tolerance. However, the effect of LXR activation on Tregs generation and infiltration remains complex as several human and mouse studies show that LXR activity restrains FOXP3+ infiltration and generation in inflamed tissues, implicating that the LXR‐mediated immune response may depend on the microenvironment and timing of this nuclear receptors activation. 51 , 52

The distinct immune‐regulatory roles of the two isoforms are further illustrated in models of autoimmune disease. In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a mouse model of multiple sclerosis (MS), LXRβ deficiency confers protection by reducing Th1/Th17‐driven inflammation, whereas LXRα deficiency exacerbates disease pathology, potentially through impaired endothelial function and blood–brain barrier integrity. This indicates that LXRα may mediate protective effects outside of the T‐cell compartment, while LXRβ acts more directly within T cells, particularly in maintaining Treg stability and limiting effector T‐cell activation. These effects align with the tissue expression patterns of these nuclear receptor isoforms.

Challenges in targeting nuclear receptors

Despite their immunomodulatory potential, targeting nuclear receptors in clinical settings presents significant challenges. A major concern is the risk of off‐target and systemic side effects arising from the widespread transcriptional control these nuclear receptors exert over fatty acid metabolism and other gene expression programmes across multiple tissues. For instance, PPARγ agonists like pioglitazone are relatively well tolerated, 53 whereas clinical studies of early‐generation LXR agonists have demonstrated significant adverse effects. 54 , 55 In particular, non–isoform‐selective LXR agonists have been associated with increased hepatic lipogenesis and hypertriglyceridemia, as well as central nervous system‐related adverse events. These lipogenic effects are thought to be primarily mediated by LXRα activation in the liver, while LXRβ is more broadly expressed and may mediate immunomodulatory effects with less impact on hepatic lipid metabolism. To address these limitations, several more refined strategies have been explored, including the development of isoform‐selective LXRβ agonists or tissue‐restricted modulators. Although no isoform‐selective LXR agonist has yet achieved clinical success, these approaches illustrate promising directions for dissociating the beneficial immunomodulatory effects of LXR activation from its metabolic and neurological liabilities. 41 Another potential approach would be to target the downstream genes regulated by LXR to reduce inflammation. For example, Niemann‐Pick C1‐Like 1 (NPC1L1) and ATP‐binding cassette A1 (ABCA1) are downstream targets of LXR, with NPC1L1 being downregulated and ABCA1 upregulated. The herbal remedy Si‐Ni‐San has been shown to decrease the expression of NPC1L1 and increase the expression of ABCA1 in mouse colons, while also reducing Th17 levels and improving symptoms in a dextran sodium sulphate‐induced colitis model. 56 Si‐Ni‐San is generally well tolerated at dosages used to treat post‐stroke depression 57 and therefore could be a promising approach for indirectly activating LXR in the treatment of inflammatory disease. However, more robust clinical studies are needed to demonstrate its efficacy and safety in the context of inflammation linked disease.

While clinical studies and drug development targeting LXR and PPAR are underway, there is still a need to broaden our understanding of their effects on T‐cell proliferation, polarisation and activation to inform drug design. Furthermore, downstream targets of LXR may provide specific and safer methods of immune control worthy of further study.

Modulation of fatty acid metabolic enzymes

The enzyme targets discussed include ACLY, ACSS, acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) and stearoyl Co‐A desaturase 1 (SCD1). ACLY breaks down citrate from the TCA cycle into oxaloacetate and acetyl‐CoA. ACSS generates acetyl‐CoA from the SCFA, acetate. These acetyl‐CoA molecules can either acetylate histones to drive changes in transcription or be funnelled into FAS. In the committing step of FAS, ACC catalyses the carboxylation of acetyl‐CoA to malonyl‐CoA. ACC has two forms: ACC1 and ACC2; ACC1 resides in the cytosol and ACC2 resides in the mitochondria. Cytosolic malonyl‐CoA fuels de novo lipogenesis while mitochondrial malonyl‐CoA downregulates fatty acid oxidation by inhibiting CPT, a rate limiting shuttle for long‐chain FAO. 58 SCD1 works downstream of ACC in FAS, adding double bonds to fatty acids to produce delta‐9 monounsaturated fatty acids. 59 How these enzymes interact with T‐cell immunity is summarised in Figure 3.

ATP citrate lyase

ACLY catalyses the conversion of citrate into oxaloacetate and acetyl‐CoA. Much of the research on ACLY in immunometabolism has been limited to the innate immune system, where it modulates inflammation and improves symptoms of autoimmune diseases. 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 ACLY has also been explored for its ability to treat cancer, both for its role in sustaining highly proliferating cells and for its ability to interfere with T‐cell immunotherapy. 65 , 66 , 67 The ability to modulate rapidly dividing cells and control immunity makes ACLY an attractive target to modulate inflammation and autoimmunity.

Pertaining to T‐cell immunity, inhibition of ACLY reduces Th2 and Th17 derived inflammation in a mouse colitis model. 13 ACLY genetic inhibition also attenuates experimental autoimmune encephalitis (EAE) pathology by reducing acetylation of Th17 related genes and blunting T‐cell effector pathogenicity. 14 Mouse CD8+ T cells rely on ACLY derived acetyl‐CoA to epigenetically shift metabolism from fatty acid oxidation towards glycolysis to support differentiation and function. 31 , 32 These later results were replicated in a human study, showing genetic deletion in CD4+ primary T cells decreased proliferation and differentiation into Th1 and Th17 lineages. 68 Interestingly, another study suggested that murine CD8+ T cells utilise acyl‐CoA synthetase as a ‘backup generator’ of acetyl‐CoA when ACLY is inhibited, implicating that both proteins may need to be inhibited for a more robust effect. 69

While most of these studies suggest that ACLY's main mechanism of immune modulation is the production of acetyl‐CoA for histone acetylation, its ability to control FAS may also regulate T‐cell function. Here, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of ACLY in murine T cells promotes differentiation towards Tregs. 68 This increase in Treg population was abrogated by the addition of malonyl‐CoA, suggesting the effects were a result of FAS inhibition. Furthermore, the decrease in murine T‐cell FAS was concurrent with increased fatty acid oxidation, likely from increased CPT1A activity as malonyl‐CoA levels fell. 70 In humans, the expression of IL‐17 in human T cells is dependent on FAS, and this can be dramatically reduced by ACLY inhibition. 14 This concept is further supported where glucose derived acetyl‐CoA fuels de novo FAS and facilitates Treg to Th17 cell polarisation in primary human cells. 34

Prior research has shown ACLY's role in the innate immune system, but our understanding of its role in adaptive immunity, and specifically T‐cell biology, is more limited. Additional research is required to understand which T‐cell enriched genes are regulated by ACLY‐dependent histone acetylation and how this influences inflammatory/autoimmune disease pathogenesis. Furthermore, a deeper understanding of ACLY's control of FAS in T cells could help inform not only treatment of inflammatory/autoimmune diseases but also to combat immune suppression within the tumor microenvironment.

Acetyl‐CoA synthetase 1 and 2

Acetyl‐CoA synthetase converts acetate into acetyl‐CoA, linking SCFAs with epigenetic histone acetylation. ACSS has two main isoforms. ACSS1 exists in the mitochondrial matrix while ACSS2 presides in the nucleus. ACSS2 has been most extensively studied in the context of immune regulation. ACSS2 plays a role in enhancing CD8+ T‐cell responsiveness. ACSS2 activity promotes TCR activation and production of IFN‐γ and granzyme B in human and mouse T cells in response to infection by driving histone acetylation. 71 , 72 Notably, these effects are mediated by the conversion of acetate into acetyl‐CoA for histone acetylation rather than FAS. 72 Furthermore, ACSS2 plays a role in preventing T‐cell exhaustion in the tumor microenvironment through epigenetic changes. ACSS2 genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition induces higher levels of exhaustion and reduces CD8+ T‐cell tumor lysis capacity. 73 , 74 ACSS1 also plays a role in T‐cell immunity, although its role is less well characterised. ACSS1 converts butyrate into butyryl Co‐A, which drives murine CPT1A activity, enhancing FAO and Treg differentiation. 11 Taken together, these studies suggest that ACSS2 may play a deleterious role in CD8+ mediated inflammatory diseases while ACSS1 plays a more protective role. However, ACSS1 may also drive CD8+ cytotoxicity among calorically starved T cells in cancer models. 75 While these initial studies are promising, more research is needed to better characterise these proteins' effects on immune function and immunometabolism.

Acetyl‐CoA carboxylase

Acetyl‐CoA Carboxylase catalyses the first step of FAS by carboxylating acetyl‐CoA into malonyl‐CoA. Among its isoforms, ACC1 has been shown to play a role in modulating immune responses. Genetic deletion of ACC1 in T cells impairs the production of T‐helper (Th) cytokines. In a murine model of allergic asthma, ACC1 deletion mice exhibited reduced levels of Th2 cytokines (IL‐5 and IL‐13) and decreased pulmonary T‐cell infiltration. 33

Pharmacological inhibition of ACC supports these findings. Treatment with soraphen A, an allosteric ACC inhibitor, led to reduced activity across Th1, Th2 and Th17 subsets in mice. 34 , 76 However, contrasting results were observed with another inhibitor, 5‐tetradecyl‐oxy‐2‐furoic acid (TOFA). In one study, TOFA treatment resulted in increased expression of Th1‐associated cytokines, including IFN‐α, and enhanced expression of interferon‐stimulated genes in mice. 77 This suggests that TOFA may promote Th1 activation, potentially by suppressing competing T‐helper subsets. The discrepancy between these studies could be due to the distinct mechanisms of action of the inhibitors: while soraphen A acts allosterically, TOFA functions through a different inhibitory pathway. This underscores the need for further investigation into how different modes of ACC1 inhibition influence T‐cell subset dynamics, particularly Th1 responses.

Some studies suggest that ACC1 genetic deletion alone is insufficient for optimal Treg induction in mice, with more robust effects observed upon dual knockout of ACC1 and ACC2. 34 , 78 However, these results have been challenged by studies demonstrating ACC1‐specific deletion can successfully promote Treg development under both steady state and inflammatory conditions. 79 These conflicting findings highlight the importance of further research to dissect the distinct contributions of ACC1 and ACC2 to T‐cell differentiation and function.

Importantly, ACC inhibition has shown therapeutic potential in several murine models of autoimmune disease, including asthma, experimental autoimmune EAE, colitis and psoriasis. 33 , 34 , 78 , 79 , 80 Furthermore, in a human ex vivo study, soraphen A administration to CD4+ T cells extracted from individuals with MS resulted in reduced pro‐inflammatory Th1, Th2 and Th17 cytokines, while increasing the anti‐inflammatory IL‐10. 81 Collectively, these findings support the potential of ACC as a target for modulating immune responses in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, warranting further investigation in human ex vivo models and clinical studies to explore its efficacy.

Stearoyl CoA desaturase 1

Stearoyl CoA Desaturase 1 (SCD1) introduces double bonds into fatty acids to generate omega‐9 monounsaturated fatty acids, such as oleic acid. 59 Given the correlation between the desaturation ratio in membranes and immune function, SCD1 provides an interesting target candidate to modulate inflammation. 82

SCD1 activity influences multiple CD4+ T‐cell lineages, including regulatory and effector helper subsets. Inhibition or genetic deletion of SCD1 has been shown to increase Treg populations. Adoptive transfer of SCD1−/− T cells into RAG knockout mice enhanced Treg populations and improved the EAE disease score. 83 Another mouse study showed that SCD1 knockout predisposes naïve T cells to differentiate into Tregs, and that supplementation with oleic acid, a product of SCD1, ameliorated this effect. 84

While SCD inhibition can increase Treg populations, several mouse studies have shown it may also cause inflammation by promoting T‐helper cell subsets. Genetic deletion increased the transcription of Th1‐associated interferon‐stimulated genes by activating transcription factors IRF3 and IRF9 (interferon regulatory factors 3 and 9). 77 , 85 In models of allergic inflammation, the effect of SCD1 also appears operational downstream of STAT3 signalling to reduce lung inflammation in mice by decreasing total inflammatory cell and eosinophil numbers, suggesting a modulatory role in Th2 immunity. 86 These changes have measurable effects on disease models. In a mouse model of colitis, genetic deletion of Scd1 in T cells worsened the disease score. 87 Conversely, other murine studies demonstrated that inhibition of SCD1 either has no effect or reduced the expression of Th1 cytokines in response to immune challenge. 88 , 89 The understanding of the role of SCD1 in human remains very limited. However, a primary culture study reported that SCD1 expression was highest in follicular helper T cells (Tfh) compared with non‐Tfh cells. 90 In that study, inhibition of SCD1 impaired Tfh cell maintenance and function. 90

Beyond CD4+ subsets, SCD1 also regulates CD8+ T‐cell responses. Genetic deletion of Scd1 augmented differentiation of CD8+ T cells into effector cells and increased IFN‐γ production. 91 Notably, many of these effects were reversed by oleic acid administration, a product of SCD1 activity, underscoring the functional importance of SCD1‐derived monounsaturated fatty acids in CD8+ T‐cell regulation. 91

Taken together, these data show that our understanding of the role of SCD in T‐cell mediated immune modulation is incomplete. More research should be done in human models to replicate murine study results to better characterise its effect on Treg, Tfh, Th1 and CD8+ T‐cell function and regulation. If the composite effects of SCD1 across different cell lineages regulate inflammation, it could open up opportunities for treatment through either pharmacological inhibition or activation of the enzyme or supplementation with its downstream product, oleic acid. Indeed, oleic acid has already demonstrated an ability to modulate Th1 and Th2 inflammation in mice and humans. 92 , 93 Overall, SCD1 provides a possible target for immune modulation although gaps in our understanding remain to be clarified.

Dietary manipulation of fatty acids

Given the emerging role of fatty acid metabolism in immunometabolism and immune regulation, a question arises as to whether dietary supplementation that modulates fatty acid levels can mirror these effects. In this review, we highlight pectin and omega‐3 fatty acid supplementation as potential dietary immune modifiers.

Pectin supplementation

SCFAs which are generated during gut microbial breakdown of dietary fibre have been shown to ameliorate human and murine inflammation. 9 , 94 , 95 Because of this, high‐fibre dietary supplements may offer a low‐cost possibility to ameliorate inflammation. Pectin is a complex polysaccharide fibre found in the cell wall of plants. Several studies have looked at pectin as a fibre supplement to treat inflammation. Mouse studies have demonstrated its ability to modulate inflammation, where pectin supplementation improved colitis by decreasing gut Th17 and macrophage activation. 96 Pectin also improved murine autoimmune uveitis by increasing Treg and reducing Th17 populations. 97 Furthermore, this supplement has been shown to attenuate Th2‐linked food allergy in mice. 98 Here, the pectin supplementation altered the gut biome by promoting Bacteroides growth, a genus that decreases murine Th2 activation through SCFAs production. 98 , 99

Interestingly, pectin's effect on mouse immune populations has shown to either preferentially blunt myeloid populations 96 or diminish Th1 and Th17 populations in response to different allergic triggers. 97 This may in part reflect the different triggers and or as pectin's immunomodulatory effects depend in part on metabolism into SCFAs by the gut biome, its effects on specific T‐cell subsets may be dependent on the hosts bacterial composition. These data collectively implicate, in part, the requirement for a more in depth phenotyping of the gut microbiome to understand the contexts wherein pectin supplementation modulates both myeloid and T‐cell immune responsiveness.

Despite these gaps in our knowledge, pectin supplementation may be unsuitable in inflammatory bowel disorders (IBDs), given that fermentable fibres exacerbate IBD symptoms and patients with IBD often avoid dietary fibres to control symptoms and improve their quality of life. 100 , 101 , 102 These observations highlight further complexities that need to be explored in the context of microbiome phyla, disease states and the role of high‐fibre supplementation. These studies additionally need to be interrogated in human disease, given the more blanket anti‐inflammatory profiles of pectin in murine studies. 103 , 104 , 105

Omega‐3 fatty acids

Because omega‐3 fatty acids are preferentially metabolised into pre‐resolving molecules, omega‐3 supplementation has been explored to treat inflammatory diseases. This has been tested in several clinical studies with varied results. In patients with psoriasis, dietary omega‐3 fatty acid supplementation had protective cardiovascular effects and resulted in some improvement in disease activity, while topical treatments showed no improvement in psoriatic lesions. 106 , 107 , 108 Clinical studies in patients with MS also have widely varied results. Some studies show an improvement in MS symptoms 109 while other studies show no change 110 or even a trend towards worsening MS lesions. 111 Interestingly, in a small clinical study with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), krill oil supplementation, which is enriched in omega‐3 fatty acids, improved the SLE disease activity index (SLEDAI) only among patients with a higher baseline disease activity. 112

While laboratory studies support that omega‐3 fatty acids may reduce inflammation, it is possible that nutritional supplementation of omega‐3 fatty acids in a clinical setting lacks the specificity needed to significantly resolve inflammation. Additionally, as omega‐3 PUFAs can be converted into arachidonic acid through desaturation and elongation to confer inflammatory signalling, 113 supplementation with long‐chain PUFAs, EPA and DHA, may be more effective anti‐inflammatory supplements. Indeed, supplementation with EPA and DHA in mice resulted in greater pro‐resolving mediator formation than omega‐3 supplementation. 114 These results have been replicated in humans where EPA and DHA oral supplementation promoted full remission of SLE in a small clinical cohort. 115 At the population level, multiple studies have shown that EPA supplementation is linked with a reduction in inflammation associated pathology, including coronary artery disease. 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 , 120 Together, these findings highlight the need for direct human immunophenotyping studies to elucidate how specific PUFAs influence distinct immune cell populations involved in immunity and inflammation, both in healthy and disease states.

Concluding remarks

With the global rise in chronic disease, it is crucial to prioritise research focused on prevention and the development of novel treatments to reduce disease burden. In this review, we have highlighted emerging metabolic targets that show promise in this area. However, much of the current evidence is based on studies conducted in mouse models. To facilitate the transition to clinical application, these findings must now be evaluated in ex vivo human models and in proof‐of‐principal studies in human populations.
