Projected public health benefits of a hypothetical HSV-1 vaccine in the United States: A mathematical modeling analysis
Authors: Bechir S. Naffeti, Houssein H. Ayoub, Christine Johnston, Laith J. Abu-Raddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection is highly prevalent worldwide and causes oral, genital, and neonatal herpes. Several HSV vaccine candidates are currently in development. This study used mathematical modeling to estimate the potential population-level impact of HSV-1 vaccination strategies in the United States. Infant vaccination with 70% efficacy and lifelong protection introduced in 2030 reduced oral and genital incidence rate by 47.3 and 58.3% by 2075, respectively, with annual new infections declining by 41.2 and 53.5%, corresponding to 28.5 million cumulative infections averted. The number needed to vaccinate to prevent one infection, defined from program initiation to a specified year, declined from 35.8 by 2040 to 7.2 by 2070. Addition of adolescent catch-up vaccination further reduced oral and particularly genital incidence. Lifelong protection produced approximately threefold greater impact than 15-year protection. HSV-1 vaccination could substantially reduce oral and genital HSV-1 burden, supporting prioritization of vaccine development and integration into public health strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection is a lifelong infection, most commonly acquired during childhood (1). In 2020, the World Health Organization estimated 122.2 million new infections among individuals aged 0 to 49 years, contributing to 3.8 billion prevalent cases and a global prevalence of 64.2% in this age group (1). Transmission occurs primarily through oral secretions during asymptomatic shedding (2, 3). The infection is characterized by frequent subclinical shedding, symptomatic reactivation manifesting as oral or facial lesions in some individuals, and occasional severe complications such as encephalitis or corneal blindness (2, 4).

The virus can also be transmitted through oral sex or sexual intercourse, resulting in genital herpes (5, 6). In high-income countries, including the United States (US), improved hygiene and living conditions have reduced childhood oral acquisition (5, 7, 8), leading to a decline in prevalence over recent decades (7–12). These declines are projected to continue in the US in the coming decades (6). However, the growing proportion of adolescents reaching sexual debut without prior oral exposure, and therefore without protective immunity against genital acquisition, has contributed to increased sexual transmission such that most incident genital herpes cases are now attributable to HSV-1 rather than HSV-2 (5, 6, 9–12). Although rare, transmission from an infected mother during delivery can result in neonatal herpes, a severe condition associated with high mortality and long-term morbidity (13, 14).

Options to control HSV-1 as well as HSV-2 infections remain limited despite their substantial global burden and impact on sexual and reproductive health (15–17). The World Health Organization (WHO), together with global partners, has called for intensified action to reduce HSV-related disease and has spearheaded the development of a business case for HSV vaccines (15, 16, 18). Vaccine development is progressing across multiple platforms and stages, although challenges remain (19–21).

This study evaluates the potential impact of a prophylactic HSV-1 vaccine in the US population using mathematical modeling. The US was chosen as the setting because it is the only country with repeated, nationally representative, population-based surveys spanning several decades—the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES) (6, 22, 23)—enabling characterization and modeling of the HSV-1 epidemic, past, present, and future. This analysis estimates the public health benefits achievable through national vaccination and aims to generate evidence to guide strategic, data-driven approaches that inform HSV-1 vaccine development, implementation, and policy engagement.

RESULTS

Impact of infant vaccination with lifelong protection

In scenario 1, vaccine coverage in the total US population increased approximately linearly as 80% of newborns were vaccinated, reaching 20.5% by 2050, 41.0% by 2075, and 58.6% by 2100 (fig. S1A). Because lifelong protection was assumed, vaccine coverage and effective vaccine coverage were identical.

With 70% vaccine efficacy (VEs), the oral infection incidence rate declined by 20.7% by 2050, 47.3% by 2075, and 66.2% by 2100 (Fig. 1A), while the genital infection incidence rate declined by 16.0, 58.3, and 80.7%, respectively (Fig. 1B). Annual new oral infections decreased by 18.8, 41.2, and 57.8% (Fig. 1C), and annual new genital infections decreased by 14.1, 53.5, and 75.9% (Fig. 1D) at the same time points.

DISCUSSION

These results highlight the importance of prioritizing HSV-1 vaccine development and integrating the vaccine into public health programs to mitigate the health burden of this infection. Meaningful population-level benefits are projected even if coverage does not reach the high levels historically achieved for routine childhood immunizations before the rise in vaccine hesitancy (24, 25).

The results further indicate that an HSV-1 vaccine could deliver rapid, large-scale population benefits even with modest efficacy, with relatively few vaccinations required to avert one infection over time. This projected benefit persists despite partial vaccine protection and a declining incidence rate over the coming decades. Collectively, these findings suggest that the vaccine has reasonable potential for cost-effectiveness, driven by the still substantial underlying incidence and prevalence of infection.

Momentum for HSV (HSV-1 or HSV-2) vaccine development is growing, driven by advances in basic science, improved understanding of HSV immunology, and promising antigen-adjuvant combinations (19–21, 26, 27). Further impetus comes from vaccine candidates currently in development (15, 19–21, 28), the success of prophylactic and therapeutic vaccines against varicella zoster virus, a closely related alphaherpesvirus (19, 29), effective veterinary herpesvirus vaccines (19, 30), evidence that intramuscular vaccination can induce genital mucosal immunity (19, 31), and partial protection against HSV-1 observed in the Herpevac trial (19, 27). Together, the study findings and these developments present a compelling case for accelerating investment to advance a prophylactic HSV vaccine to implementation.

Across scenarios, the impact on genital HSV-1 acquisition was greater than on oral acquisition, particularly over the long term. This reflects the concentration of genital acquisition in young adulthood (5, 6)—a period strongly protected by vaccination in the modeled programs. This finding is especially timely as HSV-1 epidemiology in the US and other high-income countries is undergoing a marked transition: Oral acquisition is declining, while genital acquisition is rising, leading to an increasing burden of genital disease and a falling burden of oral disease (6, 9–12, 32). Vaccination therefore represents a powerful intervention to counter this shift in HSV-1 epidemiology.

While infant vaccination is expected to be the cornerstone of protection against oral infection in childhood, its impact on sexual acquisition will take more than a decade to materialize as vaccinated cohorts gradually reach sexually active ages. To accelerate population-level benefits, catch-up vaccination of adolescents aged 10 to 14 years, as illustrated in scenario 2 (Fig. 2 and fig. S4), substantially amplifies the impact on incidence and reduces the NNV to prevent one infection. For instance, by 2050, with 70% VEs, the reduction in annual new genital infections reached 31.6% in scenario 2 compared with 14.1% in scenario 1 without catch-up vaccination, while the corresponding NNV values were 11.7 and 16.3, respectively.

A key finding of this study is that the duration of vaccine protection is a decisive determinant of long-term impact and, by extension, cost-effectiveness. This is most clearly illustrated by comparing scenario 1 and scenario 4, which differ only in the duration of protection—lifelong versus 15 years. Scenario 1 achieved three times the long-term impact of scenario 4 (Fig. 1 and fig. S3). By 2075, with 70% VEs, reductions in annual new oral and genital infections reached 41.2 and 53.5%, respectively, with an NNV of 6.3 in scenario 1, compared with only 14.0 and 16.0%, respectively, with an NNV of 14.1 in scenario 4.

This result is not unexpected: A 15-year average duration of protection in a population with a life expectancy of approximately 80 years (33) leaves vaccinated individuals unprotected for most of their lives once immunity wanes. This finding highlights the importance of developing vaccines that provide durable protection or implementing booster strategies—at minimum, a booster in adolescence before sexual debut—to maintain protection against genital and oral acquisition in adulthood, particularly genital acquisition in late adolescence and early adulthood.

As expected, vaccine impact increased with higher efficacy; however, even a modest efficacy of 50% produced substantial reductions in infection burden. Moreover, the impact estimates presented here may be conservative, suggesting that the true public health benefit of vaccination could be greater than reported. The model assumed that vaccination only reduces susceptibility to infection and did not account for potential effects among vaccinated individuals who experience breakthrough infections. Evidence from other pathogens shows that vaccination can lower pathogen load and shedding, thereby reducing infectiousness (34, 35), and may attenuate disease severity by decreasing the frequency of oral and genital lesions (15, 16, 36).

The model also assumed no benefit from vaccinating individuals already infected, although therapeutic effects of vaccination are possible (15, 16, 19, 36). Moreover, given the >83% genomic and >85% proteomic homology between HSV-1 and HSV-2 (37) and evidence of epidemiologic interaction (38, 39), an HSV-1 vaccine may confer partial cross-protection against HSV-2, and vice versa. Notably, the Herpevac trial vaccine, developed for HSV-2, reduced genital HSV-1 disease by 58% and HSV-1 infection by 32% (27), suggesting the potential for dual benefit. It is also plausible that future vaccines will be designed to target both HSV-1 and HSV-2 (15, 16, 19, 36). Last, this analysis focused solely on infection outcomes and did not consider additional benefits, such as prevention of severe HSV-1 complications and neonatal herpes (2, 4, 13, 14), further indicating that the benefits of vaccination are underestimated and strengthening the case for this vaccine.

This study has limitations. It relied on hypothetical vaccine characteristics as no vaccine is currently available, and the attributes of a future vaccine may differ from those modeled. However, the scenarios explored were designed to capture a broad and plausible range of vaccine characteristics and program strategies. Model parameters were based on high-quality HSV-1 natural history data from specific cohorts (table S2), whose generalizability to the overall population may be imperfect. Nonetheless, because the estimates reflect relative reductions, predictions are less sensitive to such uncertainties, as supported by the uncertainty analysis.

The assessed vaccine impact reflects the combined effects of direct vaccine protection and indirect effects arising from reduced onward transmission. However, the model structure does not readily permit separate quantification of direct and indirect effects. Individual-based modeling frameworks, in which individual infection events can be explicitly tracked, suggest that indirect effects may be comparable in magnitude to direct effects (40).

To reduce complexity, the model did not explicitly incorporate sex, distinguish between vaginal and anal intercourse, or include antiviral treatment. These omissions are unlikely to bias results given the largely similar exposure risks between males and females (9, 22) and the low coverage of antiviral treatment.

The model further assumed that vaccine-induced protection wanes according to an exponential distribution, such that the transition from efficacious protection to no protection follows an exponential function over time, although the true pattern of waning immunity remains uncertain. Aging between age groups was also modeled using exponentially distributed transitions, which may modestly increase and advance the predicted timing of infant vaccination impact on genital acquisition. However, this effect is likely small as aging from birth to sexual debut was represented by three sequential 5-year exponential compartments, yielding a Gamma-distributed waiting time of order 3 and substantially reducing early “leakage” compared with a single 15-year exponential compartment. Lastly, the results reflect the US context and may not be generalizable to settings with markedly different HSV-1 epidemiology, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (1, 32, 41–43).

This study has strengths. A sophisticated model was used to capture the complex dynamics of HSV-1 transmission across its four modes, grounded in high-quality data on the natural history and transmission parameters of this infection. The model incorporated heterogeneity in contact patterns and biological features of infection while also accounting for indirect population-level benefits beyond the direct protection of vaccine recipients. The analysis drew on standardized, population-based NHANES data spanning four decades, providing robust, nationally representative estimates that reflect sociodemographic diversity and patterns of acquisition. A range of vaccine characteristics and strategies were explored—including varying efficacies, durations of protection, and infant, catch-up, and booster programs—allowing for comprehensive scenario analyses.

In conclusion, this study provides a quantitative assessment of the impact of an HSV-1 vaccine, demonstrating its potential to substantially reduce oral and genital infections, avert millions of cases, and reverse the rising burden of genital HSV-1 disease. The results highlight that timely implementation, adolescent catch-up programs, and vaccines with durable protection are critical to maximizing public health benefits. These findings present a compelling case for accelerating investment, research, and coordinated global efforts to develop and implement HSV vaccines as a priority in public health strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mathematical model

A deterministic compartmental population-level model was developed to simulate HSV-1 transmission dynamics in the US under prophylactic vaccination (fig. S7). The model extended a previously developed framework characterizing the US HSV-1 epidemic from 1970 to 2100 (6), adapting it to incorporate vaccination, drawing on established vaccine modeling approaches (35, 36, 44–47).

The model structure was informed by current knowledge of HSV-1 natural history and epidemiology (2, 4, 6, 48) and consisted of coupled nonlinear differential equations that stratified the population by age, infection status and stage, oral and genital risk of exposure, and vaccination status (section S1). The model distinguished between oral and genital routes of acquisition (2, 4, 6, 48) and captured primary infection, latency, and episodic reactivation with virus shedding (6). After first acquisition, individuals progressed from primary infection into lifelong latency, punctuated by recurrent reactivations (2, 4, 6, 48).

The force of infection incorporated four age-dependent transmission modes. Oral acquisition occurred predominantly through oral-to-oral transmission, with a small contribution from genital-to-oral transmission via oral sex (6). Genital acquisition occurred through oral-to-genital transmission, which accounted for most new genital HSV-1 infections, and through genital-to-genital transmission via sexual intercourse, which contributed to a lesser extent (6). Children under 15 years were assumed to acquire HSV-1 exclusively through oral-to-oral transmission, whereas all four modes contributed among adolescents and adults (≥15 years).

The population was divided into 20 age groups, each spanning 5 years (0 to 4 to 95 to 99 years), with heterogeneity in sexual activity incorporated across age groups. Age- and risk-specific mixing patterns for the transmission modes were modeled using mixing matrices.

Further details of model structure are provided in section S1. Model implementation, parameterization, and analyses were performed in MATLAB R2019a (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Simulations were partially performed using the Red Cloud computational infrastructure at Cornell University.

Model parameterization and fitting

The model was parameterized using current empirical data on HSV-1 natural history and epidemiology, with parameter descriptions, justifications, and sources provided in section S2 and tables S1 and S2. Calibration was performed using age-stratified HSV-1 prevalence data from 10 biennial rounds of the nationally representative NHANES survey (1976 to 2016) (6, 22, 23), analyzed according to standardized NHANES survey methods and analytic guidelines with sampling weights applied (23).

Sexual risk behavior and its age dependence were parameterized using NHANES data on the reported number of sexual partners in the previous 12 months (6, 23). Demographic dynamics were fitted to reproduce US age-specific population projections from the United Nations Population Division (33). Model fitting was conducted using a nonlinear least-squares approach (6), implemented with the Nelder–Mead simplex algorithm (49).

Vaccine efficacy

VEs was defined as the proportional reduction in susceptibility to infection among vaccinated compared with unvaccinated individuals (35, 36, 50). The model incorporated two efficacies: VEsO for oral acquisition and VEsG for genital acquisition. In the absence of evidence that these differ, they were assumed equal. No additional “breakthrough effects” (35, 36, 50) were considered; vaccinated individuals who became infected were assumed to follow the same natural history and infectiousness as unvaccinated individuals.

Vaccine impact was assessed under varying average durations of immune protection (15), with waning immunity modeled using an exponential distribution (35), such that the transition from efficacious protection to no protection follows an exponential function over time. The assumed durations of protection may reflect not only intrinsic vaccine-induced immunity but also the potential for immune boosting through natural exposure, given the relatively high prevalence of this infection.

Measures of vaccine impact

The population-level impact of the vaccine was determined by both its direct effects (VEsO, VEsG, and duration of protection) and its indirect effects through reduced onward transmission (35, 51). Impact was assessed by comparing epidemiological outcomes—including prevalence, incidence rate, and annual new infections—under vaccination scenarios against a counterfactual without vaccination.

Vaccine impact was further evaluated using the NNV to prevent one infection over a specified time horizon, calculated as the ratio of vaccinations administered to infections averted (35, 51). This metric, used as a proxy for cost-effectiveness in the absence of cost data (51, 52), reflects the epidemiological effectiveness of vaccination. A programmatic perspective was adopted, quantifying the number of vaccinations required to prevent one infection from program initiation through a specified year.

Vaccination program scenarios

Four vaccination program scenarios were examined to inform policy deliberations. In all scenarios, vaccination was assumed to begin in 2030, with impact evaluated at three levels of efficacy (VEsO=VEsG=50, 70, and 90%). Scenarios differed by target population, duration of protection, and inclusion of catch-up or booster strategies. A summary is presented in Table 1.

Uncertainty analysis

To assess the impact of parameter uncertainty on model outcomes, an uncertainty analysis was conducted for the model’s biological and behavioral parameters (tables S1 and S2). For each program scenario and VEs, 500 simulations were performed using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) from a multidimensional parameter distribution (6, 54). LHS is a stratified sampling method that efficiently captures parameter variability with fewer simulations than simple random sampling (55). Parameter ranges were divided into 500 equally probable intervals, from which one value was randomly sampled without replacement. These values were then combined across parameters to generate 500 unique parameter sets.

A ± 30% uncertainty range was applied to the point estimates of the model parameters, with the model refitted to input data in each run (6). Across runs, 95% UIs for the NNV to prevent one infection were estimated.

The ±30% range was not intended to provide a formal probabilistic characterization of parameter uncertainty but rather to define a structured uncertainty envelope around point estimates in situations where empirical confidence intervals are unavailable, such as for biological parameters related to infection natural history. While this approach does not capture all potential sources of structural and parametric uncertainty and is not a substitute for fully data-driven distributions (56), it represents a pragmatic and commonly adopted strategy in transmission dynamics modeling (6, 57–59). Its purpose is to evaluate the stability and robustness of model conclusions under plausible parameter variation rather than to generate formal statistical confidence measures.
