Abstract
. Nonuniform deterministic finite automata (NUDFA) over monoids were invented by Barrington in [ Bar85 ] to study boundaries of nonuniform constant-memory computation. Later, results on these automata helped to indentify interesting classes of groups for which equation satisfiability problem ( PolSat \operatorname{\textup{{{PolSat}}}} ) is solvable in (probabilistic) polynomial-time [ GR02 , IKKW22b ] . Based on these results, we present a full characterization of groups, for which the identity checking problem (called PolEqv \operatorname{\textup{{{PolEqv}}}} ) has a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm. We also go beyond groups, and propose how to generalise the notion of NUDFA to arbitrary finite algebraic structures. We study satisfiability of these automata in this more general setting. As a consequence, we present full description of finite algebras from congruence modular varieties for which testing circuit equivalence CEqv \operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}} can be solved by a probabilistic polynomial-time procedure. In our proofs we use two computational complexity assumptions: randomized Expotential Time Hypothesis and Constant Degree Hypothesis.

Introduction

There are many interactions between mathematics and (theoretical) computer science.
Many branches of these sciences influence each other, sometimes in quite surprising ways.
Relatively recent example of such an influence is so-called algebraic approach to Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) which led to complete classification of computational complexity of CSP
[
Bul17
,
Zhu20
]
.
It is really impressive how in this case (universal) algebra, combinatorics, logic, computational complexity and algorithmic work together to give new results in each of these fields.

Another example of synergy between different fields of mathematics and theoretical computer science can be observed on the borderline of circuit complexity, automata theory and (universal) algebra.
The most significant example here is the role of monoids played in automata theory and formal languages.

Usually deterministic finite automaton (DFA) is determined by
an alphabet
\Sigma

acting over a set
Q
of states
by a function
\delta:\Sigma\times Q\ni(\sigma,q)\longmapsto\sigma\cdot q\in Q
.
This action can be extended (in an obvious way) to the action of the free monoid
\Sigma^{*}
.
To decide if a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is accepted by a particular DFA
we need to endow it with a starting state
q_{0}
and a set
F\subseteq Q
of accepting states.
Then
w
gets accepted if
w\cdot q_{0}\in F
.
For our purposes we prefer, first to treat the set
Q^{Q}
of functions
as the monoid with
f\cdot g=g\circ f
,
and then to treat the action
\delta
as a function
a:\Sigma\longrightarrow Q^{Q}

given by
a(\sigma)(q)=\sigma\cdot q
.
Now, the word
\sigma^{1}\ldots\sigma^{n}
gets accepted
if
a(\sigma^{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot a(\sigma^{n})\in S
,
where
S
consists of transitions determined by the words
w\in\Sigma^{n}

satisfying
w\cdot q_{0}\in F
.

Before restating Barrington’s definition of Non-uniform Deterministic Finite Automata(NUDFA) over monoids

]

{\mathbf{t}}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot x_{n}

In NUDFA over the monoid
{\bf{M}}
to accept the word from
\Sigma^{n}

we are going to relax the term

to an arbitrary semigroup term

and replace one action

by a bunch of functions

.
Now a
{\mathbf{t}}

•

a set of

•

and a set

Finally, a NUDFA over

Originally Barrington considered mainly the Boolean case where

To define these two notions of solvability and nilpotency outside the group realm we need a notion of a commutator. However we need to work with a commutator
\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
of two congruences
\alpha,\beta
(that in the group setting correspond to normal subgroups) instead of a commutator of elements of an algebra.
For more details on the definition and the properties of commutator, we refer to the book
[

]

Here we only note that this concepts of commutator of congruences works smoothly only in some restricted setting of the so called congruence modular varieties, i.e. equationally definable classes of algebras with modular congruence lattices.
Fortunately this setting includes groups, rings, quasigroups, loops, Boolean algebras, Heyting algebras, lattices and almost all algebras related to logic.
In groups, rings or Boolean/Heyting algebras the congruences are determined by normal subgroups, ideals or filters respectively.
Obviously this new concept of commutator of normal subgroups coincides with the old one.
The commutator of two ideals
I,J
of a commutative ring
is simply their algebraic product
I\cdot J

while the commutator of filters in a Boolean/Heyting algebra is their intersection.
Now we can say that a congruence

\left[\alpha,\alpha\right]=0_{{\bf{A}}}

\left[\ldots\left[\left[\alpha,\alpha\right],\alpha\right],\ldots\alpha\right]=0_{{\bf{A}}}

\left[\left[\left[\alpha,\alpha\right],\left[\alpha,\alpha\right]\right],\ldots\left[\left[\alpha,\alpha\right],\left[\alpha,\alpha\right]\right]\right]=0_{{\bf{A}}}

Here,

{\bf{A}}

The algebra

This decomposition prevents interaction between different primes, as they occur on different stalks/coordinates. And the lack of such interactions is crucial in bounding the arity of expressible

Also in our considerations the finite nilpotent algebras that decompose into a product of algebras of prime power order occurs naturally.
They are known as supernilpotent algebras

Algebraic preliminaries

An algebra is a set called universe together with a finite set of operations acting on it called basic operations of the algebra. We usually use boldface letter to denote the algebra and the very same latter, but with a regular font, to denote its universe.
{\rm Pol\>{\bf{A}}}
is polynomial clone of
{\bf{A}}
, that is the set of all polynomial operations of
{\bf{A}}

{\bf{B}}
. An induced algebra

{\bf{A}}
closed on

.

.

.

Every solvable (so in particular - nilpotent) algebra in the congruence modular variety is Malcsev, i.e.​ it possesses a polynomial operation
{\mathbf{d}}

{\mathbf{d}}(y,x,x)={\mathbf{d}}(x,x,y)=y

To define this slicing properly, we need to consider congruences. Recall that congruence
\sigma
of an algebra
{\bf{A}}
is an equivalence relation which is preserved by the operations of
{\bf{A}}
. Such relations are naturally associatted with surjective homomorphisms from
{\bf{A}}
to
{\bf{A}}/\sigma

to
[x]_{\sigma}
(equivalence class of

in
\sigma
), so congruencess are essentially generalization of normal subgroups of a group. Similarly as normal subgroups, congruences of an algebra form a lattice. From now on, we write
{\sf Con\>{\bf{{\bf{A}}}}}
for the set of all congruences of
{\bf{A}}

{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}
, will play a significant role in our analysis.

For
\alpha,\beta\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{{\bf{A}}}}}
, by
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
we mean a set of congruencess
\gamma
such that
\alpha\leqslant\gamma\leqslant\beta
. In case when
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]=\{\alpha,\beta\}
, i.e.​ there are no congruencess between
\alpha
and
\beta
, we call
\beta
a cover of
\alpha
, we call
\alpha
a subcover of
\beta
, and we call
\alpha,\beta
a covering pair. To highlight such a situation we write
\alpha\prec\beta
for short. Whenever
\alpha
is meet-irreducible (join-irreducible) congruence, then there is a unique congruence
\alpha^{+}
(
\alpha^{-}
) such that
\alpha\prec\alpha^{+}
(
\alpha^{-}\prec\alpha
).
For a nilpotent algebra
{\bf{A}}
from CM wheneverver its congruencess
\alpha,\beta
satisfy
\alpha\prec\beta
, the cosets (congruence classes) of
\beta/\alpha
in
{\bf{A}}/\alpha
have equal sizes, being a power of some prime (see
Lemma

7.1
and
Lemma

7.2
). We later denote this prime by
\mathsf{char}(\alpha,\beta)
and call it a characteristic of a congruence cover
\alpha\prec\beta
. Moreover for arbitrary
\alpha<\beta
, we write
\mathsf{char}{\left\{{\alpha,\beta}\right\}}
for the set of all possible prime characteristics of covering pairs, which are fully contained in
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
.
We say that a pair of congruences
\alpha<\beta
of a nilpotent algebra
{\bf{A}}
forms a Prime Uniform Product Interval (PUPI), if there are congruencess
\alpha<\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\leqslant\beta
such that

•

\bigvee\alpha_{i}=\beta

•

\alpha_{i}\wedge(\bigvee_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j})=\alpha
, for
i,j\in\{1..k\}
,

•

For every
i
we have
|\mathsf{char}{\left\{{\alpha,\alpha_{i}}\right\}}|=1
.

We call a congruence
\beta
supernilpotent whenever it is nilpotent and the interval
I\left[0_{{\bf{A}}},\beta\right]
is a PUPI, and we call an algebra
{\bf{A}}
supernilpotent, whenever
1_{{\bf{A}}}
is supernilpotent.
Each supernilpotent algebra is isomorphic to a direct product of nilpotent algebras of prime power size. In this sense supernilpotence generalizes nilpotence for groups.
Note that this definition is equivalent to a standard definition of supernilpotent algebras in congruence modular varieties
[
MS21
]
.

We say that a nilpotent algebra
{\bf{A}}
has supernilpotent rank
k
whenever
k
is the smallest number for which we can find sequence of congruences
0_{{\bf{A}}}=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\ldots<\alpha_{k}=1_{{\bf{A}}}
such that interval
I\left[\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}\right]
is a PUPI for each
0\leqslant i<k
. Note that for a finite nilpotent algebra
{\bf{A}}
we can always find such a finite sequence, since each covering pair forms a PUPI. This notion of supernilpotent rank of an algebra, later denoted by
{\mathsf{sr}\left({\bf{A}}\right)}
, proved to be extremely usuful in some very recent results on the computation complexity of circuit satisfiability problem
[
IKK20
,
Kom23
]
. In fact, results for
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgCSat}}}}/\operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}}
we present in this paper, are essentially about nilpotent algebras with
{\mathsf{sr}\left(A\right)}=2
.

3.
Polynomial equivalence

Our characterization of polynomial time cases of
\operatorname{\textup{{{PolEqv}}}}
is achieved through a reduction to some special instances of
\operatorname{\textup{{{PolSat}}}}
. It was noticed already in
[
GR02
]
that
\operatorname{\textup{{{PolSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}}\right)
for finite group

reduces in polynomial time to
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}}\right)
. Very recently, the full characterization, of groups for which
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgSat}}}}
can be solved in randomized polynomial time was shown under the assumptions of rETH and CDH in

[

]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\bf{G}}
be a finite group.
Assuming rETH and CDH the problem

\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}}\right)
is in
RP
if and only if

{\bf{G}}/{\bf{G}}_{p}
is nilpotent for some
normal
p
-subgroup
{\bf{G}}_{p}
of
{\bf{G}}

(with
p
being prime).

Theorem

3.1
together with a result from
[
IKKW22a
]
provides us with enough information to prove the
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof.

We start with recalling that
[
IKKW22a
, Theorem 1]
tells us that under ETH,

\operatorname{\textup{{{PolEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}}\right)\in\textsf{P}
forces
{\bf{G}}
to have a normal nilpotent subgroup
{\bf{H}}

with nilpotent quotient
{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}
.
The very same proof actually gives the same structure of
{\bf{G}}
under
rETH and
\operatorname{\textup{{{PolEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}}\right)\in\textsf{RP}
.

For the converse we start by observing that
the nilpotent normal subgroup
{\bf{H}}
of
{\bf{G}}
is a product of its Sylow subgroups,
say
{\bf{H}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{H}}_{s}
with
{\bf{H}}_{j}
being a
p_{j}
-group.
Each such subgroup
{\bf{H}}_{j}
is isomorphic to the quotient
{\bf{H}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}

for the normal subgroup
{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
of
{\bf{H}}
consisting of all elements in
H

with the order not divisible by
p_{j}
.
In particular
H^{\prime}_{1}\cap\ldots\cap H^{\prime}_{s}={\left\{{1}\right\}}
.
Moreover for an inner automorphism
h
of
{\bf{G}}
we have not only
h(H)=H

but also
h(H^{\prime}_{j})=H^{\prime}_{j}
, as
h
has to preserve the order of elements.
This means that
{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
is normal also in
{\bf{G}}
.

Now we know that the quotient
{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
has a nilpotent normal subgroup
{\bf{H}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}

with a nilpotent quotient
({\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j})/({\bf{H}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j})={\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}
.
Thus CDH and Theorem
3.1
give us that
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}}\right)
and consequently
\operatorname{\textup{{{PolSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}}\right)
are in
RP
.
Consequently
\operatorname{\textup{{{PolEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}}\right)\in\textsf{RP}
as deciding whether
{\mathbf{t}}={\mathbf{s}}
holds in

{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
reduces to check if none of the
\left|G/H^{\prime}_{j}\right|-1
equations of the form
{\mathbf{t}}{\mathbf{s}}^{-1}=a
,
with
a\in G/H^{\prime}_{j}-{\left\{{1}\right\}}
has a solution.

Finally,
H^{\prime}_{1}\cap\ldots\cap H^{\prime}_{s}={\left\{{1}\right\}}
tells us that an equation holds in
{\bf{G}}

iff it holds in all the quotients
{\bf{G}}/{\bf{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
, so that

\operatorname{\textup{{{PolEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{G}}}\right)\in\textsf{RP}
.
∎

5.
Circuit equivalence

In this section we will prove
Theorem

1.3
. To do it we will show that solving
\operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}}
for an algebra
{\bf{A}}
can be reduced to solving the very same problem for quotients of
{\bf{A}}
by a meet-irreducible congruences. Obviously, every quotient algebra by a meet-irreducible congruence has the smallest congruence bigger than identity relation. This observation plays a crucial role in the proof o
Theorem

1.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

First suppose that
\operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)
is tractable
to eliminate types
{\bf{3}}
and
{\bf{4}}
from the
{\rm typ}{\left\{{{\bf{A}}}\right\}}
.
It should be obvious how to do it for type
{\bf{3}}
.
For the lattice type
{\bf{4}}
we first note that the existence of a solution
(in the two element lattice)
to the systems of two equations of the form
{\mathbf{m}}(\overline{x})=1\ \&\ {\mathbf{j}}(\overline{x})=0
is
NP
-complete,
where
{\mathbf{m}}(\overline{x})
is in CNF and
{\mathbf{j}}(\overline{x})
is in DNF, both with only positive literals.
But obviously this system has no solutions iff

{\mathbf{m}}(\overline{x})\vee{\mathbf{j}}(\overline{x})={\mathbf{j}}(\overline{x})
holds identically in
({\left\{{0,1}\right\}};\wedge,\vee)
.
To put this into the minimal set
U
of type
{\bf{4}}
simply replace each variable
x
by
{\mathbf{e}}_{U}(x)

(where
{\mathbf{e}}_{U}
is an idempotent polynomial of
{\bf{A}}
with the range
U
)
and the operations
\wedge,\vee
by the corresponding polynomials of
{\bf{A}}

turning
{\bf{A}}|_{U}
into the two element lattice.
This shows that
{\rm typ}{\left\{{{\bf{A}}}\right\}}\subseteq{\left\{{{\bf{2}}}\right\}}
(i.e.
{\bf{A}}
is solvable) or
\operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)
is
co-NP
-complete.

Now the discussion made in
[
IKK22b
, Section 4]
between Problems 2 and 3
shows that (under (r)ETH) in fact
{\bf{A}}
has to be nilpotent and that
{\mathsf{sr}\left({\bf{A}}\right)}\leqslant 2
.
This shows the ‘only if’ direction of our theorem.

To prove the converse note that an identity holds in an algebra
{\bf{A}}

iff it holds in all quotients of
{\bf{A}}
by meet-irreducible congruences
(as the intersection of all meet-irreducible congruences is the identity relation
{\bf{0}}_{{\bf{A}}}
). Let
\alpha
be a meet-irreducible congruence of
{\bf{A}}
. To complete the proof it suffices to show that (under CDH)
\operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}/\alpha}\right)\in\textsf{RP}
.

Observe that if
{\bf{A}}
is nilpotent and
{\mathsf{sr}\left({\bf{A}}\right)}\leqslant 2

then each quotient of
{\bf{A}}
has these two properties as well. Moreover, identity relation in
{\bf{A}}/\alpha
(i.e.
0_{{\bf{A}}/\alpha}
) has the unique cover. Since congruence classes for covering pairs have equal sizes
[
FM87
, Colloraly 7.5]
, it is clear from definition of supernilpotency that every supernilpotent congruences of
{\bf{A}}/\alpha
has cosets of prime power sizes (as for every
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{A/\alpha}}}
, if
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0_{{\bf{A}}/\alpha}
, then
\beta_{1}\wedge\beta_{2}>0_{{\bf{A}}/\alpha}
). Therefore by
Theorem

1.2
,
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgCSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}/\alpha}\right)\in\textsf{RP}
. Finally
Fact

4.1
gives us
\operatorname{\textup{{{CEqv}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)\in\textsf{RP}
, as required.
∎

Algebra

We use the standard universal algebraic notation the reader can find e.g. in
[
BS11
]
. Our results heavily rely on Tame Congruence Theory and Modular Commutator Theory which have a detailed description in
[
HM88
]
and
[
FM87
]
respectively. One can find the not too long summary of needed notions and facts in
[
IK22
, Section 2]
. Here we just recall, for readers convenience, the most important notation.

For an algebra
{\bf{A}}
and
a,b\in A
congruence
\Theta(a,b)
is the smallest congruence containing the pair
(a,b)
. Note that every join-irreducible congruence is generated by a single pair of elements of the algebra. In particular, covers of the identity relation, called atoms, are generated by a single pair.

Minimal sets are the central notion of the Tame Congruence Theory. Formally, for an algebra
{\bf{A}}
and
\alpha,\beta\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{{\bf{A}}}}}
such that
\alpha<\beta
we define the
(\alpha,\beta)
-minimal set
U\subseteq A
as minimal, with respect to the inclusion, among sets of the form
{\mathbf{f}}(A)
for all unary polynomials
{\mathbf{f}}
of
{\bf{A}}
such that
{\mathbf{f}}(\beta)\not\subseteq\alpha
. In this paper we consider
(\alpha,\beta)
-minimal sets for
\alpha\prec\beta
only (i.e. for so-called prime quotients). For every minimal set
U
of
{\bf{A}}
there exists unary idempotent polynomial
{\mathbf{e}}_{U}
of
{\bf{A}}
such that
{\mathbf{e}}_{U}(A)=U
. A trace of the
(\alpha,\beta)
-mimal set
U
is a set
u/\alpha\cap U
for some
u\in U
such that
u/\alpha\cap U\not=u/\beta\cap U
. For
(\alpha,\beta)
-minimal set
U
(or its trace
N
) of algebra
{\bf{A}}
we usually consider an induced algebra
{\bf{A}}/\alpha|_{U/\alpha}
(
{\bf{A}}/\alpha|_{N/\alpha}
). The five ways of local behaviour of finite algebras mentioned in
Section

2
are exactly the types of algebras (or rather their polynomial clones) induced on minimal sets traces. Note that all traces of a minimal set are polynomially equivalent. In this paper we usually work with minimal sets of type
{\bf{2}}
, that is in which algebras induced on traces are polynomially equivalent to one-dimensional vector spaces. Traces of a minimal set of type
{\bf{2}}
are of prime power order, this prime is called a characteristic of the minimal set. Note that all minimal sets taken with respect to the same prime quotients induce polynomially equivalent algebras. It allows us to define the type of prime quotient as a type of minimal sets taken with respect to that quotient. In case of prime quotients of type
{\bf{2}}
a prime characteristic of the quotient defined in
Section

2
is equal to the characteristic of minimal sets taken with respect to it.

Algebras belonging to congruence modular variety are a wide class of algebras for which Tame Congruence Theory and Commutator Theory work particularly well. In this context term ,,modular” means that all algebras from the variety have modular congruence lattice i.e. for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}

\alpha\leqslant\beta
implies
\alpha\vee(\gamma\wedge\beta)=(\alpha\vee\gamma)\wedge\beta
. Equivalently, lattice is modular if it has no elements
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
for which
\alpha<\beta
,
\alpha\vee\gamma=\beta\vee\gamma
and
\alpha\wedge\gamma=\beta\wedge\gamma
. Such a sublattice
\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\alpha\vee\gamma,\alpha\wedge\gamma\}
, if found in
{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}
, is called a
pentagon
. If
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
and
I\left[\gamma,\delta\right]
are intervals such that
\beta\wedge\gamma=\alpha
and

\beta\vee\gamma=\delta
, then
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
is said to transpose up to
I\left[\gamma,\delta\right]
, written
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]\nearrow I\left[\gamma,\delta\right]
and
I\left[\gamma,\delta\right]
is said to transpose down to
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
, written
I\left[\gamma,\delta\right]\searrow I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
and the two intervals are called transposes of one another. Two intervals are said
to be projective if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of transposes. A fundamental fact in lattice theory is that a lattice is modular if and only if its projective intervals are isomorphic. In the case of congruence lattice of an algebra this isomorphism is even stronger since projective prime quotients have exactly the same minimal sets and in a consequence the same types and, in case of type
{\bf{2}}
, the same characteristics.

Malcev algebras are examples of algebras from congruence modular varieties. Such algebras form so called congruence permutable varieties. Congruences of algebras from this class commute i.e.​ for arbitrary congruences
\alpha
and
\beta
we have that
\alpha\circ\beta=\beta\circ\alpha
.

Supernilpotency plays a crucial role in our investigations. For a fixed algebra
{\bf{A}}
we write
\sigma
for a biggest supernilpotent congruence and
\kappa
for the smallest congruence such that
{\bf{A}}/\kappa
is supernilpotent. Moreover, we write
\sigma_{p}
for the biggest supernilpotent congruence with cosets of size being a power of
p
. The existance of such biggest/smallest congruences follows from term definition of supernilpotence and its properties
[
AM10
, Corollary 6.6]
.

Arithmetic operations and functions

Sometimes we use addition in two different abelian groups inside one formula. To avoid ambiguity, different symbols for different types of addition are used:
+,\sum
for one type of addition (for instance in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
) and
\oplus,\bigoplus
for the other (in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
). Moreover, for a natural number
m
with a prime decomposition
p_{1}^{\alpha}\cdot p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}
we write
\Delta\left(m\right)={\left\{{p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}}\right\}}
for the set of prime divisors of
m
and
\delta\left(m\right)=p_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{s}
for the largest square-free divisor of
m
. We also use
\Delta\left(A\right)
for
\Delta\left(|A|\right)
and
\delta\left(A\right)
for
\delta\left(|A|\right)
. Likewise,
\mu\left({A}\right)
denotes the maximal arity among basic operations of
{\bf{A}}
and
\mu\left({f}\right)
is the arity of the function
f
. For a set
X={\left\{{d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}}\right\}}

and a function
f:X^{k}\longrightarrow Y
we associate to
f
its
binary expansion
f^{\circ}:{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}^{\left|X\right|\cdot k}\longrightarrow Y
,
i.e., arbitrary function satisfying

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=f^{\circ}(x_{1}\mbox{\tt==}d_{1},\ldots,x_{1}\mbox{\tt==}d_{s},\ldots,x_{k}\mbox{\tt==}d_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\mbox{\tt==}d_{s}).

Programs

Recall that a program
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S)
computes a Boolean function
\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. From now on we write
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,S\right](\overline{b})
for evaluation of this function on a tuple
\overline{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Moreover, if
S=\{c\}
is one-element set, we simply write
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,c\right](\overline{b})
instead of
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,\{c\}\right](\overline{b})
. With each program
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S)
over
{\bf{A}}
we can naturally associate an inner function
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota\right]:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow A
satisfying
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota\right](\overline{b})\in S
iff
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,S\right](\overline{b})=1
. This function is computed by the expression

{\mathbf{p}}(a^{x_{1}}(b^{x_{1}}),\ldots,a^{x_{k}}(b^{x_{k}}))
, where all
a^{x_{i}},b^{x_{i}}
are provided by the instructions
\iota(x)=(b^{x},a^{x})
. For a congruence
\sigma\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{{\bf{A}}}}}
and a program
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S)
we can define the quotient program
({\mathbf{p}}/\sigma,n,\iota/\sigma,S/\sigma)
of an algebra
{\bf{A}}/\sigma
, by simply reinterpreting
{\mathbf{p}}
to be a circuit over
{\bf{A}}/\sigma
(algebras
{\bf{A}}
and
{\bf{A}}/\sigma
have the same signature), and taking
\iota(x)=(b^{x},a^{x}/\sigma)
, and
S/\sigma=\{[s]_{\sigma}:s\in S\}
.

Circuits

In next chapters we use different types of gates to build bounded-depth circuits computing Boolean functions. For instance we write
\mathsf{AND}_{d}
to denote a gate which takes at most
d
inputs and computes their conjunction and
\mathsf{OR}_{d}
for a gate computing at most
d
-ary disjunction. A
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}
-type boolean gate is any unbouded fan-in gate, which sums the inputs modulo
m
and returns
1
iff the sum belongs to some accepting set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. We allow different accepting sets for different gates. Other kinds of gates appearing in the paper are
\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}
gates which also rely on modulo counting, but in a more complex way. Each such a gate takes
n
inputs
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
, and computes their affine combination
\alpha_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}b_{n}+d
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{v}
. Here we interpret each
b_{i}
as
v
-dimensional vector
(b_{i},\ldots,b_{i})
. We allow each
\alpha_{i}
to be arbitrary endomorphism of the abelian group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{v}
, which can be also viewed as arbitrary
v\times v
matrix with coefficients from
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Hence
\alpha_{i}b_{i}
can be viewed as applying linear map
\alpha_{i}
to
(b_{i},\ldots,b_{i})
. In this way
\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}
gate computes a function of type
\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\nu}
. Additionally, for
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
let
\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}^{c}
denote a boolean variant of
\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}
gate which returns value
1
when the affine combination
\alpha_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}b_{n}+d
evaluates to
c
, and returns
0
otherwise. Note that for a field
\mathbb{F}
with underlying group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{v}
, every scalar
g\in F
defines a linear map
x\longmapsto g\cdot x
. Thus such
g
(or rather its associated endomorphism) can be used as a coefficient
\alpha_{i}
inside
\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}
gate. To simplify some of the later calculations, we will always assume that vector
(1,\ldots,1)
is the unit of such a field
\mathbb{F}
. Note that for a field
\mathbb{F}=(F,\cdot,+,0,1)
, for each
e\in F
we can define a new field
\mathbb{F}_{e}=(F,\circ,+,0,e)
, by defining
x\circ y
to be
x\cdot e^{-1}\cdot y
.

It is indeed a valid assumption, since for every non-zero element
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{v}
which is not a unit of the field
\mathbb{F}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{v}
, we can redefine multiplication to be
x\cdot g^{-1}\cdot y
in terms of old multiplication, and fix the new inverse of an element
x
to be
x^{-1}\cdot g^{2}
. One can easly check that such a rewriting of
\mathbb{F}
defines a new field in which
g
plays role of the unit (and we did not alter the underlying group).

Having all these gates, we build a bounded-depth circuits by listing types of gates which are allowed on each layer, starting with the input layer on the left, finishing with the output layer on the right. For instance
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
denotes a
3
-level circuit with inputs wired to some set of
\mathsf{AND}_{d}
gates, then this
\mathsf{AND}_{d}
gates have wires to
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}
gates and on the output level there is one
\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
gate. We allow multiple wires between two gates.

Useful algebraic facts

Before dealing with the remaining parts of the proofs, we present a number of lemmas describing certain useful aspects of the local behaviour of algebras.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
{\bf{A}}
be a finite algebra and
\beta
be an atom in
{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}
.
Then for each element
e\in A
the induced algebra
{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}

on the coset
e/\beta

is either trivial or simple.

Proof.

We show that collapsing in
{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}
two elements
a\neq b
we collapse any pair

(c,d)\in(e/\beta)\times(e/\beta)
.
Obviously we have
\Theta_{{\bf{A}}}(a,b)=\beta
so that
c
and
d
can be connected by the so-called Malcev chain, i.e. the projections of the pair
(a,b)
by unary polynomials of
{\bf{A}}
.
More precisely it means that there are unary polynomials
{\mathbf{p}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathbf{p}}_{s}
of
{\bf{A}}
with

c\in{\left\{{{\mathbf{p}}_{0}(a),{\mathbf{p}}_{0}(b)}\right\}}
,

{\left\{{{\mathbf{p}}_{j}(a),{\mathbf{p}}_{j}(b)}\right\}}\cap{\left\{{{\mathbf{p}}_{j+1}(a),{\mathbf{p}}_{j+1}(b)}\right\}}\neq\emptyset
and

d\in{\left\{{{\mathbf{p}}_{s}(a),{\mathbf{p}}_{s}(b)}\right\}}
.
This gives that each of the
{\mathbf{p}}_{j}
’s maps
e/\beta
into
e/\beta
,
i.e.
{\mathbf{p}}_{j}
is a basic operation of the induced algebra
{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}
.
But then the very same chain is a Malcev chain inside
{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}

showing that
(c,d)\in\Theta_{{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}}(a,b)
, as claimed.
∎

The next lemma is a specialized version of some much more general facts known in Universal Algebra (see for instance
[
FM87
, Corollary 5.8]
).

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
{\bf{A}}
be a finite nilpotent algebra from a congruence modular variety
with a Malcev term
{\mathbf{d}}(x,y,z)

and
\beta
be an abelian atom in
{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}
.
Then for each element
e\in A
the induced algebra
{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}
on the coset
e/\beta
is polynomially equivalent to a simple module in which the underlying group structure is determined by the binary operation
x+y={\mathbf{d}}(x,e,y)

and the corresponding group
(e/\beta;+,e)

is isomorphic to some power of the group
(\mathbb{Z}_{p};+,0)
.

Proof.

By
[
FM87
, Corollary 5.8]
we know that the coset
e/\beta
with the operation

x+y={\mathbf{d}}(x,e,y)
is a group.
Moreover, abelianity of the congruence
\beta
gives that the induced algebra
{\bf{A}}|_{e/\beta}
is abelian, and therefore, by
[
FM87
, Corollary 5.9]
is affine,
i.e. polynomially equivalent to a finite module
M
.
But
Lemma

7.1
ensures us that this finite module is simple.
This in particular gives that all non-zero (i.e. different from
e
) elements of
M
have the same order, as otherwise for
a,b\in M=e/\beta
of orders
m,n
respectively,
if
m<n
then the endomorphism
M\ni x\longmapsto mx\in M
is non-zero (as
mb\neq e
)
and is not surjective (as
ma=e
), contradicting simplicity of
M
.
This shows that the underlying group of the module
M
is a finite elementary abelian group,
i.e. a finite power of the group
(\mathbb{Z}_{p};+,0)
for some prime
p
.
∎

Now we present yet another simple observation.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
{\bf{A}}
be a finite nilpotent Malcev algebra
and
\delta\prec\theta
two of its congruences.
Then every element
e\in A
belongs to some
(\delta,\theta)
-minimal set.

Proof.

Start with any
(\delta,\theta)
-minimal set
V
and pick
(c,d)\in\theta|_{V}-\delta
.
By
[
FM87
, Corollary 7.5]
in nilpotent algebras all cosets of the same congruence have the same size. Thus there is
a\in A
with
(e,a)\in\Theta(c,d)-\delta
.
Since
{\bf{A}}
is Malcev it has the unary polynomial
{\mathbf{p}}(x)
such that

{\mathbf{p}}{{c}\choose{d}}={{e}\choose{a}}
.
In particular the polynamial
{\mathbf{p}}
does not collapse
\theta
to
\delta

so that, by
[
HM88
, Lemma 2.8]
,
{\mathbf{p}}(V)
is a minimal set and it contains our element
e
.
∎

Now we demonstrate that easiness of
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgCSat}}}}
can be transferred to quotients.

Fact 7.4
.

For an algebra
{\bf{A}}
and its congruence
\theta

there is a polynomial time reduction from
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgCSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}/\theta}\right)
to
\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgCSat}}}}\!\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S)
be a program over
{\bf{A}}/\theta
. Then for
\iota(x)=(b^{x},[a_{0}^{x}]_{\theta},[a_{1}^{x}]_{\theta})
we put
\iota^{\prime}(x)=(b^{x},a_{0}^{x},a_{1}^{x})
and
S^{\prime}=\bigcup S
(where
a_{0}^{x}
,
a_{1}^{x}
are arbitrary representants of the respective cosets in
{\bf{A}}/\theta
). Then since
\theta
is a congruence relation, for any
\overline{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we have
\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota\right](\overline{b})\in S\iff\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota^{\prime}\right](\overline{b})\in S^{\prime}
. So the program
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota^{\prime},S^{\prime})
over
{\bf{A}}
computes exactly the same Boolean function as
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S)
over
{\bf{A}}/\theta
, while both programs clearly have the same size. Obviosuly
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota^{\prime},S^{\prime})
can be computed from
({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S)
in polynomial (linear) time.

∎

The following simple fact provides us with a way to reason about characteristics of intervals below a join irreducible congruence in the congruence lattice of an algebra.

Fact 7.5
.

Let
{\bf{A}}
be a solvable Malcev algebra and
\alpha,\beta\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}
such that
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
is a PUPI .

Then, for every
\gamma\in{\sf Con\>{\bf{A}}}
join irreducible in
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
it follows that
|\mathsf{char}{\left\{{\alpha,\gamma}\right\}}|=1
.

Proof.

From the fact that
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
is a PUPI we immediately have that
\beta/\alpha
and hence
\gamma/\alpha
are supernilpotent in
{\bf{A}}/\alpha
. Therefore,
I\left[\alpha,\gamma\right]
is a PUPI . Hence,
\gamma=\bigvee_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}
where
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a set of maximal congruences from
I\left[\alpha,\gamma\right]
with
|\mathsf{char}{\left\{{\alpha,\alpha_{i}}\right\}}|=1
. But
\gamma
is join-irreducible in
I\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
, and hence in
I\left[\alpha,\gamma\right]
, and
\gamma=\bigvee_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}
implies that
\gamma=\alpha_{i}
for some
i
. Thus
|\mathsf{char}{\left\{{\alpha,\gamma}\right\}}|=1
.
∎

The next Lemma is modeled after Lemma 3.1 from
[
IKK18
]
. It provides us with a normal form for all the
s
-ary functions of type
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{s}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
m
be a square-free positive integer and
p\nmid m
be a prime.
Then every function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{s}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
can be expressed by

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})=\sum_{(\overline{\beta},u)\in{\bf{\mathbb{Z}}}_{m}^{s}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}}\mu_{\overline{\beta},u}\cdot\textsf{b}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}x_{i}\oplus u\right),

where

•

\textsf{b}:\mathbb{Z}_{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is given by
\textsf{b}(0)=1
and
\textsf{b}(x)=0
for
x\neq 0
,

•

\mu_{\overline{\beta},u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

•

\sum
is the addition from
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

•

\bigoplus
is the addition from
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Moreover, coefficients
\mu_{\overline{\beta},u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
are computable in
2^{O(s)}
steps.

Proof.

We start with assuming that
m
is a prime, say
\Delta\left(m\right)={\left\{{q}\right\}}
, to recursively define
the functions
\textsf{b}_{k}^{q}:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
by putting

\displaystyle\textsf{b}_{1}^{q}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\textsf{b}(x_{1})

\displaystyle\textsf{b}_{k}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=q^{-1}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-2},x_{k-1}\oplus i\cdot x_{k})-\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-2},x_{k}\oplus i\cdot 1_{q})\right),

where
q^{-1}
is the inverse of
q
in the field
GF(p)
.
Then we observe that

\textsf{b}_{k}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=1
if
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{k}=0
and
\textsf{b}_{k}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
otherwise.

To see this we induct on
k
and first assume that
x_{k}\neq 0
.
Then in each of the two big sums only one summand can be non-zero,
i.e. the one in which the last argument
x_{k-1}\oplus i\cdot x_{k}
(or
x_{k}\oplus i\cdot 1_{q}
, resp.)
is zero.
But then each big sum is
\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-2},0)
, so that
\textsf{b}_{k}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
.
On the other hand for
x_{k}=0
all summands in the first sum are equal to
\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})
.
Simultaneously all summand of the second sum are zero, as

\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-2},x_{k}\oplus i\cdot 1_{q})=\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-2},i\cdot 1_{q})

and
i\cdot 1_{q}
ranges here over all non-zero elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Thus

\textsf{b}_{k}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=q^{-1}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})-0\right)=\textsf{b}_{k-1}^{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}).

Now, for
m=q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{r}
first we observe that the group
\mathbb{Z}_{q_{j}}

is isomorphic to the subgroup
\frac{m}{q_{j}}\cdot\mathbb{Z}_{m}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,
e.g. under sending the unit
1_{q_{j}}
to
\frac{m}{q_{j}}\cdot 1_{m}
.
Now for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
we put

\textsf{b}_{k}^{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\textsf{b}_{k}^{q_{j}}(\frac{m}{q_{j}}\cdot x_{1},\ldots,\frac{m}{q_{j}}\cdot x_{k})

to get

\textsf{b}_{k}^{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=1
if
x_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{j}}}{{\equiv}}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{j}}}{{\equiv}}x_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{j}}}{{\equiv}}0

and
\textsf{b}_{k}^{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
otherwise.

Finally we put

\textsf{b}_{k}(\overline{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\textsf{b}_{k}^{j}(\overline{x})

to get the functions
\textsf{b}_{k}:\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{k}\longrightarrow{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
satisfying

\textsf{b}_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=1
iff
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{j}}}{{\equiv}}0
for all
i,j
.

Note that the recursive definition of the
\textsf{b}_{k}^{j}
’s puts them in a nice form

\sum_{(\overline{\beta},u)\in{\bf{\mathbb{Z}}}_{m}^{k}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}}\mu_{\overline{\beta},u}\cdot\textsf{b}\left(\frac{m}{q_{j}}\cdot\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}x_{i}\oplus u\right)\right),

i.e. they are
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-linear combinations of the expressions of the form
\textsf{b}(A\cdot\overline{x})
with

A\cdot\overline{x}
being
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-linear combinations
of the

{\mathbf{p}}_{u}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{i}\cap\alpha^{-}_{i}}}{{\equiv}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e,&\mbox{if $u(\pi_{i}(x_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{i}(x_{s}))=0$,}\\
a_{i},&\mbox{if $u(\pi_{i}(x_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{i}(x_{s}))=1$.}\end{array}\right.

,

{\mathbf{p}}_{u}

.

Now with the help of the binary polynomial

to get

.
Note that each

Note also that for

Thus the values

To conclude our proof we associate with each 3-CNF formula

to be the

.

Lemma 9.1
.

Every function of the form
{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{k}

can be computed by an
\mathsf{AND}_{n}\circ\Sigma_{(p,k)}
-circuit of size at most
2^{n}
.

Proof.

Our function can be extended to
GF(p^{k})\longrightarrow GF(p^{k})

and then represented by a polynomial of
GF(p^{k})
of degree
n\cdot(p^{k}-1)
.
However, due to the fact that for
x\in{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
and
j\geqslant 1
we have
x^{j}=x
, each nonconstant monomial can be replaced by the one of degree between
1
and
n
.
Such monomials, with unit coefficients, can obviously be represented by
\mathsf{AND}_{n}
-gates.
The other coefficients as well as the sums of monomials with arbitrary coefficients are covered by the
\Sigma_{(p,k)}
-gate, since multiplication by element of
GF(p^{k})
is a linear map when we treat
GF(p^{k})
as a
k
dimensional vector space over
GF(p)
.
∎

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
m
be a square-free positive integer,
p\nmid m
be a prime.
Then every function computable by a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{d}
-circuit
can be also computed by a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit of size
O(m^{d+1})
.

Proof.

The boolean function
f
computable by a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{d}
-circuit can be decomposed into

f(\overline{x})=\mathsf{AND}_{d}(\chi_{S_{1}}g_{1}(\overline{x}),\ldots,\chi_{S_{d}}g_{d}(\overline{x}))

for some
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-linear functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
and characteristic functions
\chi_{S_{i}}

of some subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.
With the help of
Lemma

8.1
the
d
-ary function

\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{d}\ni(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})\longmapsto\mathsf{AND}_{d}(\chi_{S_{1}}(y_{1}),\ldots,\chi_{S_{d}}(y_{d}))\in{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}

can be presented in the form

\sum_{(\overline{\beta},u)\in{\bf{\mathbb{Z}}}_{m}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}}\mu_{\overline{\beta},u}\cdot\textsf{b}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}y_{i}\oplus u\right).

Substituting
y_{j}
by
g_{j}(\overline{x})
we end up with a function of the same form
in which the inner sum
\bigoplus
increases in arity,
but the outer sum has the same number of summands bounded by
m^{d+1}
.
Obviously the expressions of the form
\textsf{b}\left(\bigoplus\ldots\right)

can be computed by
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}
-gates,
while the outer sum is
\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-computable.
∎

Lemma 9.3
.

Every function computable by a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
-circuit of size
\lambda

can be also computed by a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit of size
O(m^{p}\lambda^{p})
.

Proof.

This time a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
-computable function
f(\overline{x})
can be decomposed into
\chi_{T}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda-1}\alpha_{i}\cdot\chi_{S_{i}}g_{i}(\overline{x})\right)

where
T\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
S_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and the
g_{i}
’s are
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-linear functions.
Note that
\chi_{T}(z)
can be represented by a unary polynomial, of degree
p-1
over
GF(p)
.
Thus

\chi_{T}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda-1}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\right)

is also a polynomial over
GF(p)
of
\lambda-1
variables with degree
p-1
.
Thus this polynomial, when restricted to
{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
, is computable by

\mathsf{AND}_{p-1}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit of size
\lambda^{p-1}
,
i.e. the maximal possible number of monomials here.
Since the
\chi_{S_{i}}g_{i}(\overline{x})
’s are
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}
-computable
we end up with
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{p-1}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit.
However
Lemma

9.2
help us to replace
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{p-1}
-parts
by
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuits of size
O(m^{p})

so that the size of the final circuit computing
f
can be bounded by
O(m^{p}\lambda^{p})
.
∎

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
m
be a square-free positive integer,
p\nmid m
be a prime and
g
be a
k
-ary boolean function.
If the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
are computable by

\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
-circuits
of size
O(\lambda)

then also
g(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
is computable by such a circuit,
but of size
O(\lambda^{kp})
.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Lemma

9.1
the function
g
can be represented as a restriction of a polynomial
g^{\prime}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
of degree
k
over
GF(p)
to the set
{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}^{k}
.
On the other hand, by
Lemma

9.3
, each of the
f_{j}
’s is computable by a
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit of size
O(m^{p}\lambda^{p})
.
In particular
f_{j}(\overline{x})
can be decomposed into

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\alpha_{i}\cdot\chi_{S_{i}^{j}}g_{i}^{j}(\overline{x})

where
S_{i}^{j}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and the
g_{i}^{j}
’s being
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-linear.
Now plugging the linear polynomials

f^{\prime}_{j}(\ldots,y_{i}^{j},\ldots)=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\alpha_{i}\cdot y_{i}^{j}

over
GF(p)
, with at most
O(m^{p}\lambda^{p})
variables
y_{i}^{j}
, into
g^{\prime}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})

we get a polynomial of degree
k
which, as before,
on the arguments from
{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
is
\mathsf{AND}_{k}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-computable.
Note that the polynomial
g^{\prime}(f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{k})
may have up to
O(km^{p}\lambda^{p})
variables, as the
f_{j}
’s do not need to share variables.
Thus the size of
g^{\prime}(f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{k})
and therefore of the corresponding
\mathsf{AND}_{k}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit is bounded by the number of monomials of degree
k
over
O(km^{p}\lambda^{p})
variables, i.e. roughly by
O((km^{p}\lambda^{p})^{k})
.
To compute
g(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
we simply replace the
y_{i}^{j}
’s in the above circuit computing

g^{\prime}(f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{k})
by
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}
-circuits computing
\chi_{S_{i}^{j}}g_{i}^{j}(\overline{x})

to get
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{k}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit of size
O((km^{p}\lambda^{p})^{k})
.
However, in view of
Lemma

9.2
, this can be replaced by
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit, by the expense of increasing the size
O(m^{k+1})
times.
Finally we note that since
g
returns only
0
or
1
the
\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-gate can be replaced by

\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
type gate. In our applications we treat
m,k
and
p
as constants, so the size of the resulting circuit is
O(\lambda^{kp})
.
∎

Lemma 9.5
.

Let
m
be a square-free positive integer,
p\nmid m
be a prime and
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\nu}
.
Then every
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{d^{\prime}}\circ\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}^{c}
-circuit of size
\lambda

can be replaced a

\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
-circuit
of size
O(\lambda^{\nu d^{\prime}p^{3}})
.

Proof.

We start with noting that for a constant
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\nu}

an equation of the form
t=c
in the module
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\nu}

can be replaced by a system of
\nu
equations
t^{[j]}=c^{[j]}

(with
t^{[j]}
being the projection of
t
onto the
j
-th coordinate)
and then by a single equation
g(t^{[1]},\ldots,t^{[\nu]})=1
, where

g(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\nu})=\prod_{j=1}^{\nu}(1-(y_{j}-c^{[j]})^{p-1})

is a polynomial of degree
\nu\cdot(p-1)
over
GF(p)
.

Now we observe that our starting function can be actually computed by a

\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}\circ\Sigma_{(p,\nu)}^{c}
-circuit, by simply
using
Lemma

9.4
to eliminate the
\mathsf{AND}_{d^{\prime}}
-level.
Thus this function can be represented (with a sum in the group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\nu}
) as

\chi_{\left\{{c}\right\}}(f(\overline{x}))
for

f(\overline{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\cdot f_{i}(\overline{x})

with
\alpha_{i}
being the
k\times k
matrices over the field
GF(p)

and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
being
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
-computable.
Formally the values
f_{i}(\overline{x})
are boolean,
but we interpret them by the constant
\nu
-tuples
(f_{i}(\overline{x}),\dots,f_{i}(\overline{x}))\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\nu}
.

Observe also that
f(\overline{x})^{[j]}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{ij}\cdot f_{i}(\overline{x})
for
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a sum of the
j
-th row of the matrix
\alpha_{ij}
,
where this time the boolean values taken by
f_{i}
are interpreted by
0,1\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
The polynomial
g^{\prime}
obtained from
g
by

g^{\prime}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{s})=g(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i1}\cdot z_{i},\ldots,\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i\nu}\cdot z_{i})

has also degree bounded by
\nu p
so that, on the set
{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
,
it is
\mathsf{AND}_{\nu p}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-computable.
Thus checking if
f(\overline{x})=c
reduces to checking
g^{\prime}(f_{1}(\overline{x}),\ldots,f_{s}(\overline{x}))=1

which in turn can be done with the help of
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}\circ\mathsf{AND}_{\nu p}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-circuit.
Now
Lemma

9.4
and
Lemma

9.3
allows us to eliminate the
\mathsf{AND}_{\nu p}
-level,
then replace the two levels
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
by
\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\Sigma_{(p,1)}
,
and finally compress two
\Sigma_{(p,1)}
-levels to a single one and replace it by
\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
.

A careful analysis of this process gives the required bound for the final

\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{m}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{p}
-circuit.
∎

Now we present a characterization of Boolean functions that can be represented in supernilpotent algebras.

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
{\bf{A}}
be a finite supernilpotent Malcev algebra.
Then the functions computable by an
n
-ary boolean program

\left({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,S\right)
over the algebra
{\bf{A}}

can be also computed by

\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{\delta\left(A\right)}\circ\mathsf{OR}_{\left|S\right|}
-circuits of size
O(n^{d})
with

d\leqslant\left|A\right|^{1+\log\mu\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)}=(2\cdot\mu\left({{\bf{A}}}\right))^{\log\left|A\right|}
.

Proof.

First we reduce our setting to
\left|S\right|=1
as, after building appropriate
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{\delta\left(A\right)}
-circuits separately for each
c\in S
,
we eventually join them by the final gate
\mathsf{OR}_{\left|S\right|}
.

Next we note that the algebra
{\bf{A}}
, being supernilpotent,
decomposes into a product
{\bf{A}}_{1}\times\ldots\times{\bf{A}}_{s}
of supernilpotent algebras
{\bf{A}}_{i}
,
each of which is of prime power order, say
\left|A_{i}\right|=p_{i}^{\nu_{i}}
,
where
\Delta\left(A\right)={\left\{{p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}}\right\}}
.

In order to construct
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{\delta\left(A\right)}
-circuit computing the boolean function

\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,c\right]
we first produce an
n
-ary polynomial
w(\textsf{b}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{b}_{n})

of degree
d\leqslant\left|A\right|^{1+\log\mu\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)}
over the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{\delta\left(A\right)}

such that for all
\overline{b}\in{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
we have

\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,c\right](\overline{b})=1\mbox{\quad iff \quad}w(\overline{b})=\delta^{\prime},

where
\delta^{\prime}=\frac{\delta\left(A\right)}{p_{1}}+\ldots+\frac{\delta\left(A\right)}{p_{s}}
.
Given the polynomial
w(\textsf{b}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{b}_{n})
as above
we translate it into
\mathsf{AND}_{d}\circ\mathsf{MOD}_{\delta\left(A\right)}
-circuit
by imitating the monomials of
w
(of degree at most
d
) by
\mathsf{AND}_{d}
-gates
and then summing them up by an appropriate

\mathsf{MOD}_{\delta\left(A\right)}
-gate with accepting set
{\left\{{\delta^{\prime}}\right\}}
.
Obviously the bound for the degree of the polynomial
w
determines the upper bound for the size of just constructed circuit, as there are at most
O(n^{d})
monomials of degree
d
.

The polynomial
w
is going to be obtained separately for each factor in our decomposition

{\bf{A}}={\bf{A}}_{1}\times\ldots\times{\bf{A}}_{s}
.
In what follows an element
a
of
A
is to be presented as a tuple
(a(1),\ldots,a(s))
.
Moreover our decomposition of
{\bf{A}}
leads to the decomposition of the program

\left({\mathbf{p}},n,\iota,c\right)
over
{\bf{A}}
into
s
programs

\left({\mathbf{p}}_{j},n,\iota_{j},c_{j}\right)
, over
{\bf{A}}_{j}
respectively,
where

•

{\mathbf{p}}_{j}
is the polynomial of
{\bf{A}}_{j}
corresponding to
{\mathbf{p}}
,

•

\iota_{j}(x_{i})=(\textsf{b}^{x},a^{x}_{0}(j),a^{x}_{1}(j))
whenever
\iota(x)=(\textsf{b}^{x},a^{x}_{0},a^{x}_{1})
,

•

c_{j}=c(j)
.

Note here that for this decomposition we have

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,c\right](\overline{b})=1

\displaystyle\mbox{\quad iff \quad}{\mathbf{p}}(a^{x_{1}}_{b^{x_{1}}},\ldots,a^{x_{k}}_{b^{x_{k}}})=\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota\right](\overline{b})=c

\displaystyle\mbox{\quad iff \quad}\amper_{j=1}^{s}\ {\mathbf{p}}(a^{x_{1}}_{b^{x_{1}}}(j),\ldots,a^{x_{k}}_{b^{x_{k}}}(j))=\left({\mathbf{p}}_{j}\right)\!\left[\iota_{j}\right](\overline{b})=c(j)

\displaystyle\mbox{\quad iff \quad}\amper_{j=1}^{s}\ \left({\mathbf{p}}_{j}\right)\!\left[\iota_{j},c_{j}\right](\overline{b})=1

The plan is to construct, for each
j=1,\ldots,s
an appropriate polynomial
w_{j}(\textsf{b}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{b}_{n})

(of degree bounded by
\left|A\right|^{1+\log\mu\left({{\bf{A}}}\right)}
)
over the prime field
GF(p_{j})

satisfying

\left({\mathbf{p}}_{j}\right)\!\left[\iota_{j},c_{j}\right](\overline{b})=1\mbox{\quad iff \quad}w_{j}(\overline{b})=1

and then sum them up to get the polynomial

w=\frac{\delta\left(A\right)}{p_{1}}\cdot w_{1}+\ldots+\frac{\delta\left(A\right)}{p_{s}}\cdot w_{s}

over the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{\delta\left(A\right)}

that would clearly satisfy

w(\overline{b})=\delta\mbox{\quad iff \quad}\amper_{j=1}^{s}\ w_{j}(\overline{b})=1\mbox{\quad iff \quad}\left({\mathbf{p}}\right)\!\left[\iota,c\right](\overline{b})=1,

as promised.

Before we construct the
w_{j}
’s note that despite the fact that their variables
\textsf{b}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{b}_{n}
are denoted here to be boolean, they in fact may range over entire

with supernilpotent rank equal
2

{\bf{A}}

\operatorname{\textup{{{ProgCSat}}}}

Problem 1

Prove or disprove the Constant Degree Hyphothesis.

The natural next step in our investigations is to go outside congruence modular realm. The first problem in such a case is that Tame Congruence Theory and Commutator Theory (our heavily used tools) for arbitrary algebras do not work as well as for algebras from congruence modular varieties.
