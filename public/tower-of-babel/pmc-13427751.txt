LightGastroFormer: a lightweight multi-resolution transformer for gastrointestinal disease classification
Authors: Prateek Singh, Sudhakar Singh, Manoj Kumar Shukla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In order to promote early diagnosis and lessen the workload of doctors during endoscopic and capsule endoscopy tests, automated analysis of gastrointestinal (GI) images is essential. However, current deep learning techniques frequently fail to simultaneously capture long-range contextual information and fine-grained local patterns, especially in large-scale and highly imbalanced datasets. We suggest LightGastroFormer, a lightweight transformer-based architecture intended for reliable and effective GI disease categorization, as a solution to these problems.The suggested model combines a gated feed-forward network, effective self-attention, and a multi-resolution patchwise tokenizer to efficiently find minor lesion characteristics while preserving computational efficiency. Three public benchmarks are used to evaluate LightGastroFormer: Kvasir v1, Kvasir v2, and the extensive Kvasir-Capsule dataset. Across all evaluated datasets, the proposed approach demonstrates consistently strong performance, matching or surpassing existing state-of-the-art CNN- and transformer-based methods. In particular, without using explicit data balancing strategies, LightGastroFormer obtains an accuracy of 0.94 on Kvasir v1, 0.95 on Kvasir v2, and 0.97 accuracy with a 0.97 F1-score on the highly imbalanced Kvasir-Capsule dataset. Further ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of each architectural component and reveal important insights into model behavior under class imbalance. With only 6.42 million trainable parameters, LightGastroFormer offers a favorable balance between accuracy, robustness, and efficiency, making it well suited for real-world clinical deployment in gastrointestinal disease screening and diagnosis. The code is available here: https://github.com/Prateeksingh-moa/LightGastroformer.

Introduction

One of the main causes of illness worldwide is gastrointestinal (GI) diseases. These include bleeding disorders, infections, inflammatory conditions, and cancers, all of which frequently start with extremely minor alterations to the mucosal surface. According to World Health Organization reports, digestive disorders make up approximately 10% of the world’s disease burden, and GI cancers alone cause over three million deaths annually. The burden is particularly evident in India, where over the past ten years, the incidence of small bowel, gastric, and colorectal cancers has increased steadily. However, there are still not enough qualified gastroenterologists, which makes it difficult to provide prompt and precise diagnosis in both urban and rural healthcare systems.

While early detection has been enhanced by modern endoscopic and capsule-based screening techniques, they also present a new issue. Clinicians frequently have only minutes to interpret the 50,000–80,000 images that can be produced by a single capsule endoscopy. Many significant anomalies, like angiectasia, erosions, or early polyps, can be very subtle and show up in just a few frames. It is challenging to manually review such vast amounts of data due to fatigue and a lack of personnel, which raises the possibility of missing discoveries. Deep learning-based automated systems have shown promise, but conventional convolutional neural networks frequently have trouble with small lesion features and the extreme imbalance that naturally arises in real-world datasets. By capturing long-range relationships in an image, vision transformers have opened up new possibilities for medical imaging. Nevertheless, GI images are not taken into consideration when designing standard transformer architectures. They frequently need very large datasets to train efficiently, and they usually rely on fixed patch sizes that ignore small lesions. Additionally, they are often computationally demanding, which restricts their application in clinical settings where dependability and efficiency are crucial.

We introduce LightGastroFormer, a lightweight transformer architecture created especially for gastrointestinal image classification, in response to these practical difficulties. Developing a model that is precise, dependable, and useful for clinical application is the goal. LightGastroFormer presents a multi-resolution patch tokenizer that maintains fine lesion details and global mucosal structure, both of which are frequently lost in conventional transformer patching. It uses an auxiliary supervision pathway to help the model learn stable and discriminative representations even when rare disease classes only occur a few times, and it integrates a gated feedforward network to enhance feature selectivity under class imbalance.

We evaluate LightGastroFormer on three publicly available GI datasets: Kvasir Capsule, Kvasir v1 and Kvasir v2. The model achieves strong results on all of them, including a macro F1 score of 0.97 on the highly imbalanced Kvasir Capsule dataset without using any data balancing techniques. Ablation studies confirm that the multi resolution tokenizer plays a major role in this improvement.

Our work makes the following key contributions: A multi-resolution patchwise tokenizer designed to preserve subtle lesion morphology by combining coarse and fine-grained patch embeddings, enabling improved detection of small and rare GI abnormalities.A lightweight gated feedforward network tailored for medical image analysis, which enhances feature selectivity and improves robustness under class imbalance.An auxiliary supervision mechanism that improves optimization stability and enhances minority-class performance without requiring explicit data balancing.

Related work

Convolutional neural networks, transformer-based architectures, and multi-scale representation learning are some of the complementary research paths that have been used and studied for automated analysis of gastrointestinal images. Earlier CNN’s were used for GI disease detection for tasks like bleeding detection, polyp identification, and mucosal abnormality categorization. Although these techniques performed well on carefully selected datasets, they more had trouble in generalizing to actual clinical scenarios where lesions are small, subtle, and extremely variable. By the arrival of big public datasets like Kvasir v1, Kvasir v2, and Kvasir Capsule, deep learning for gastrointestinal classificaltion has expanded enormously. However, the same problems remain like class imbalance, fine-grained feature extraction, and long-range contextual modeling and extraction still pose serious hurdles. Medical imaging has recently seen the introduction of vision transformers, which provide improved global reasoning but frequently lose local details because of fixed patch tokenization. The creation of specialized architectures that can incorporate multiscale information while maintaining computational efficiency for clinical deployment has been spurred by these constraints.

CNN-based methods for GI image classification

Öztürk and Özkaya1 proposed a gastrointestinal image classification framework that combines an LSTM classifier with CNN feature extractors like AlexNet, GoogLeNet, and ResNet to address the issues of limited annotations and class imbalance. Even when the CNN backbone was not adequately trained, their LSTM-based model outperformed conventional ANN and SVM classifiers. During experiments using datasets of different sizes, the suggested method achieved an overall accuracy of 97.9%, demonstrating its effectiveness for GI tract classification, particularly when data is scarce.

To improve the effectiveness of gastrointestinal disease classification, Ganesh et al.2 presented a hybrid CNN–Transformer architecture. Their model achieves 94% accuracy on a large-scale endoscopic image dataset while consuming less storage, reducing the number of trainable parameters, and shortening the total training time. In terms of accuracy and computational efficiency, the proposed hybrid framework outperformed traditional pre-trained CNN models, making it more appropriate for practical diagnostic applications. Sathishkumar et al.3 investigated a number of CNN architectures, such as SqueezeNet, ResNet-101, and DenseNet-169, for the classification of lower gastrointestinal diseases using the Kvasir dataset. With DenseNet-169 achieving an accuracy of 97.8%, their experiments demonstrated the powerful capabilities of deep CNN models. Nevertheless, their method primarily relies on datasets that are fairly balanced and does not adequately address more complicated issues like extremely small lesions or severe class imbalance that frequently arise in real-world GI imaging scenarios. Siddiqui et al.4 proposed SNet, a CNN-based framework developed to tackle common difficulties in endoscopic image classification, such as low contrast images, irregular texture patterns, and limited annotated data. Their model integrates MRMR-based feature selection and demonstrates good cross-dataset generalization, achieving more than 98% accuracy on Kvasir v1 and v2 datasets. Although the results are impressive, the method is still largely CNN-focused and might not completely capture multi-scale contextual dependencies required for handling more complicated GI datasets. In order to enhance decision limits for endoscopic images, Pooja et al.5 suggested a hybrid GI disease classification framework that combines CNN-based feature extraction with an SVM classifier. They attained an accuracy of 84% by using preprocessing and feature learning before SVM-based classification. Even though it works well as a straightforward hybrid technique, its performance is still inferior to more sophisticated deep learning models intended for fine-grained GI disorders.

A two-stage deep learning system for GI illness analysis was presented by Şener and Ergen6. It combines a hybrid transformer-based classification pipeline with a bespoke segmentation network (GISegNet). On the Kvasir dataset, their segmentation model received high Dice and Jaccard scores, and their fused DeiT–ViT feature representation, which was improved using mRMR and classified using SVM, achieved 95.3% accuracy. The method is less efficient than end-to-end transformer topologies since it depends on multi-stage processing and manual feature selection, notwithstanding its effectiveness.

Transformer-based methods for GI image classification

In order to improve the categorization of GI anomalies, Subedi et al.7 devised a hybrid CNN–Transformer model that combines a Swin Transformer branch for global context with DenseNet201 for local feature extraction. Their method demonstrated robustness even under class imbalance, outperforming CNN-only and transformer-only baselines on Kvasir-Capsule and achieving good performance on the GastroVision dataset. In order to enhance interpretability and demonstrate the model’s dependability in generating decisions, the study also included saliency map visualizations. Tabassum et al.8 introduced GastroViT, an ensemble of lightweight pre-trained vision transformers designed to classify a wide range of GI diseases on the HyperKvasir dataset. Their ensemble outperformed individual MobileViT models and several SOTA baselines, achieving over 91% accuracy despite severe class imbalance. The study also incorporated Grad-CAM and SHAP for interpretability, showing the potential of transformer ensembles for reliable real-world GI diagnosis.

Using Bio-RoBERTa, a spatial-channel attention transformer, and cross-attention fusion processes, Sharmila and Geetha9 created a sophisticated multimodal transformer framework that combines clinical text with capsule endoscopy images. Their model demonstrated the advantages of mixing multimodal inputs with sophisticated feature alignment, achieving exceptionally high accuracy on six-class GI illness classification. Nevertheless, compared to simplified end-to-end designs, the method is computationally demanding due to its extensive multi-stage processing and many ensemble classifiers. In order to get around Swin Transformer’s shortcomings in capturing long-range dependency in gastrointestinal endoscopic pictures, Wang et al.10 developed a hybrid shifted-window vision transformer. Their model achieves 95.42% accuracy on Kvasir v2 and excellent performance on HyperKvasir, improving representation of widely distributed anatomical patterns like the Z-line and esophagitis.

In order to capture both local and global characteristics in endoscopic images, Wu et al.11 presented FLATer, a lightweight transformer model that combines residual CNN blocks, spatial attention, and ViT modules. Their two-stage classification approach demonstrated suitable for real-time clinical applications by achieving up to 99.7% accuracy while maintaining exceptionally fast inference speed. The study highlights the importance of effective hybrid transformer topologies for quick identification of GI disorders. Özbay12 developed a transformer-based architecture to enhance colorectal illness diagnosis in capsule endoscopy images by combining GI tract region segmentation with a long-range attention model.Using residual-Inception modules, split-token embedding, and cross-channel feature learning, the method obtained a 99.5% accuracy on the WCECCD dataset. This study demonstrates how segmentation and transformer-based feature extraction can be used in tandem to improve GI imaging diagnostic performance.

Despite the above studies having proven the consistent progress in the field of gastrointestinal image analysis, a number of limitations remain. CNN-based methods tend to be poor at capturing fine visual patterns that are needed to detect diseases at an early stage, whereas transformer-based models, although with good global modeling features, can be computationally intensive and fail to capture fine-grained local texture that is very important in clinical diagnosis. Hybrid and ensemble approaches seek to deal with these issues, although they generally add complexity to models and do not deal well with imbalanced clinical data.

These constraints draw attention to the necessity of a framework that is capable of learning multi-scale representations effectively and with computational efficiency and resistance to class imbalance. In response, we present a lightweight system to be used to analyze gastrointestinal images. Although the suggested scheme is based on the well-known transformer-related ideas, it incorporates a task-specific design by implementing a multi-resolution patchwise tokenizer and a lightweight gated feedforward network. This allows the model to simultaneously learn on a global context and the fine features of the lesions with severe computational limitations.

In contrast to conventional transformer architectures, the presented framework is tuned to the applications of imbalanced GI datasets and real-time inference, where fine-grained feature sensitivity and efficiency is critical. Thus, the novelties of this work are not only in the separate parts, but in their combined implementation, lightness of formulation and adherence to the difficulties of the gastrointestinal image classification.Fig. 1The suggested LightGastroFormer model’s general architecture is shown as follows. Methodology

LightGastroFormer (Fig. 1) is a lightweight transformer-based framework designed for accurate gastrointestinal disease classification under conditions of class imbalance and subtle visual variations. The overall pipeline consists of image preprocessing, multi-resolution tokenization, transformer-based feature encoding, and supervised classification. Each component is designed to preserve fine-grained mucosal patterns while maintaining computational efficiency.

Multi-resolution patchwise tokenizer

To overcome the limitations of fixed-size patch tokenization in standard vision transformers, we introduce a multi-resolution patchwise tokenizer that extracts complementary representations at different spatial scales.

A learnable class token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_{\textrm{cls}} \in \mathbb {R}^{1 \times d}$$\end{document} is prepended to the sequence, and positional embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{P} \in \mathbb {R}^{(N+1) \times d}$$\end{document} are added:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Z}_0 = \left[ \textbf{x}_{\textrm{cls}} \parallel \textbf{X}\right] + \textbf{P}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Implementation Details: For an input resolution of 224 × 224, the tokenizer generates N = 196 tokens. The primary branch uses a convolution with kernel size k = 16 and stride 16, producing a 14 × 14 feature map. The secondary branch uses a convolution with kernel size k = 8 and stride 8, followed by a 2 × 2 max-pooling operation to align spatial dimensions to 14 × 14. The resulting feature maps are concatenated along the channel dimension and linearly projected to obtain the final token embeddings.

The overall training objective is defined as a weighted combination of cross-entropy losses:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = \mathcal {L}_{CE}(\hat{\textbf{y}}, \textbf{y}) + \lambda \mathcal {L}_{CE}(\hat{\textbf{y}}_{\textrm{aux}}, \textbf{y}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}$$\end{document} denotes the ground-truth label, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}_{CE}$$\end{document} is the cross-entropy loss, and λ controls the contribution of the auxiliary supervision.

During inference, only the primary classifier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{y}}$$\end{document} is used for prediction.

Optimization

The network is trained in an end-to-end manner using the AdamW optimizer along with a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler and a batch size of 16. This setup helps in achieving stable convergence and provides good generalization performance across highly imbalanced gastrointestinal datasets, although some variations can still be observed depending on the data distribution.

Experimental setup

Datasets

Three publicly accessible gastrointestinal image datasets—Kvasir Capsule, Kvasir v1, and Kvasir v2—that collectively encompass both capsule endoscopy and traditional endoscopic imaging scenarios are used to assess the suggested LightGastroFormer.

117 capsule endoscopy videos were used to create the extensive capsule endoscopy dataset known as Kvasir Capsule13. Although more than 4.7 million frames can be retrieved from these videos, the dataset only offers 47,238 medically validated and annotated frames for supervised learning. The dataset is extremely difficult because of the severe class imbalance and modest lesion appearances. These images are categorized into 14 clinically important classes, which include normal mucosa as well as pathological features such angiectasia, erosion, ulcer, polyp, and hemorrhage. About 8,000 endoscopic images taken during standard gastrointestinal examinations make up the Kvasir v1 dataset14. Eight different classes, which indicate anatomical landmarks and clinical diseases such esophagitis, polyps, ulcerative colitis, colored resection margins, and normal mucosa, make up the dataset. The dataset has been very much utilized as a baseline for Gastrointestinal image classification tasks and is comparatively balanced. The Kvasir v214 dataset contains 8,000 images distributed across eight classes and represents an improved and expanded version of the original Kvasir dataset.It maintains the same class taxonomy while providing improved image quality and better consistency across samples, which makes it suitable for evaluating the generalization ability and robustness of classification models under similar clinical settings.

Following the commonly adopted evaluation protocol, we conducted a patient-level split using the provided video IDs in order to avoid data leakage and to ensure a more realistic evaluation of the model performance. The dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for testing, while keeping the class distribution relatively balanced across both subsets so that the evaluation remains fair.

Evaluation metrics

The model performance is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Because of the strong class imbalance in the Kvasir Capsule dataset, we mainly focus on the macro-averaged F1 score. In contrast to overall accuracy, the macro F1 score gives equal importance to each class, which provides a more balanced and dependable evaluation, especially for the minority categories that are underrepresented.

All the reported metrics are calculated on the held-out test sets with 5 fold cross-validation, strictly adhering to the standard splits that comes with each dataset, so that the comparisons remain fair and reproducible across different experiments.

Implementation details

All experiments were implemented using PyTorch and carried out on a single GPU. LightGastroFormer is constructed on a lightweight transformer backbone that contains 6 transformer blocks, an embedding dimension of 256, and 4 multi-head self-attention heads in each block. This configuration was selected to achieve a good trade-off between representational capacity and computational efficiency, so the model remains practical for deployment in resource-limited clinical environments.

For the purpose of proving the data efficiency of the proposed framework, LightGastroFormer was trained from scratch using truncated normal distribution initialization. The pre-trained weights from ImageNet were not used in this experiment. This is to ensure that the results obtained are accurate and representative of the capability of the proposed model to learn domain-specific features directly from the target datasets without relying on transfer learning from natural image domains, which sometimes result in domain mismatch.

For the purpose of robustness and generalization improvement, common data augmentation methods were used during training. These included random horizontal and vertical flip transformations with a probability of 0.5, random rotations within ±15 degrees, and color jittering with brightness and contrast modifications of 0.1 to simulate the lighting variations commonly seen in endoscopic procedures.

The proposed multi-resolution patch tokenizer is a mechanism that generates token embeddings at both coarse and fine spatial resolutions. This allows the model to simultaneously focus on the global mucosal context and fine lesion-level information. The features obtained from different resolutions are concatenated and projected into a common embedding space before being passed to the transformer encoder. Despite its small architecture, this approach significantly decreases the number of parameters compared to standard Vision Transformer models, while maintaining the discriminative power, especially when dealing with highly imbalanced gastrointestinal datasets.

Unless otherwise specified, the same architectural setup and optimization hyperparameters were used uniformly across the Kvasir Capsule, Kvasir v1, and Kvasir v2 datasets. Results and analysis

This section will give a thorough assessment of LightGastroFormer on three image datasets of the gastrointestinal system, namely Kvasir-Capsule, Kvasir v1, and Kvasir v2. We will examine the training process, results on a per-class basis, and various architectural ablations to gain insight into what role each plays in the final performance of the model. Particular attention will be paid to cases involving heavy class imbalance, as it is in these regimes that most models tend to fail. It is hoped that through these experiments, there can be a better understanding of how and why the proposed architecture performs so well.

Training dynamics and convergence behavior

In all the three datasets, LightGastroFormer maintains a stable and quite reliable training process. As it can be noticed from the training and validation curves (Figs. 2, 3, 4), both the loss and accuracy are changing smoothly with the increase in the number of epochs, without any abrupt spikes or divergences. This is an indication that the training process is well regulated, and there are no apparent signs of overfitting. Even with the presence of only 6.42M trainable parameters, the architecture remains compact, which is further supporting its goal of being efficient and useful in real-world deployment tasks.

In the Kvasir-Capsule dataset, which is the largest and also imbalanced, the training process is relatively slower but still stable throughout the training process. The training as well as validation losses are reducing consistently, while the accuracy is also increasing gradually and finally reaching up to 97%. The fact that the training and validation curves are so closely aligned is an indication that the model is not simply learning to reproduce the dominant Normal clean mucosa class, which is a typical problem in imbalanced datasets. Rather, it appears to be learning more robust and discriminative features that also benefit the minority classes.

In comparison, Kvasir v1 and Kvasir v2 converges faster. The validation accuracy reaches high values within the first few epochs itself, possibly because these datasets have cleaner distributions and less severe class imbalance. More importantly, the gap between training and validation accuracy remains minimal, which highlights the strong generalization capability of the model across datasets that differ in size, distribution and complexity.Fig. 2Training and validation loss and accuracy curves on the Kvasir-Capsule dataset.Fig. 3Training and validation loss and accuracy curves on the Kvasir v1 dataset.Fig. 4Training and validation loss and accuracy curves on the Kvasir v2 dataset.

In addition to comparisons with previously reported state-of-the-art methods, we also evaluated three widely adopted backbone architectures under the same experimental setting, namely ResNet-50, ViT-Base, and Swin-Base. All baselines were initialized with ImageNet pre-trained weights and fine-tuned using identical patient-level splits, augmentation strategies, and optimization settings to ensure a fair and controlled comparison.

As summarized in Table 6, transfer learning substantially improves the performance of all baseline models compared to training from scratch. ResNet-50 achieves a strong macro-F1 score of 0.82, demonstrating the robustness of convolutional inductive biases even in highly imbalanced medical datasets. Interestingly, ResNet-50 slightly outperforms ViT-Base, which attains a macro-F1 score of 0.79. This finding is in line with previous works that have shown that standard Vision Transformers, although they have an advantage in terms of ImageNet pre-training, still have difficulties in adapting to domain-specific and fine-grained lesion patterns if the data distribution is significantly different from natural images. In medical imaging applications, this domain difference is likely to be more apparent and impact the sensitivity.

Swin-Base is relatively better than ResNet-50 and ViT-Base, achieving a macro F1 of 0.84. This is likely because of its hierarchical feature learning and attention mechanism, which is beneficial for learning spatial patterns. However, this also increases the computational complexity and the number of parameters, which may not be suitable for a resource-limited environment.

On the flip side, LightGastroFormer is always significantly better than all the other competing baseline models, with a macro-F1 of 0.97, while still having a much lighter model architecture. This is a clear indication that the improvements are not only due to the transfer learning benefits but are mostly due to the task-specific architectural design choices proposed in this work. This is because the multi-resolution tokenizer is able to preserve the global mucosal information as well as the boundary details of the lesions, while the medical-gated feedforward network enhances the feature selection capabilities despite the presence of a large class imbalance problem.

The quantitative results confirm that the proposed architecture generalizes well on different datasets with varying complexity, size, and imbalance ratio. These results support its potential suitability for real-world GI image analysis tasks, where both reliability and efficiency are of equal importance.Fig. 8Attention map visualizations generated by LightGastroFormer for different gastrointestinal categories.

Recent multi-scale transformer architectures such as MSViT30, M2Former31, and Quadformer32 are known to provide strong hierarchical feature representations using cross-scale attention mechanisms. Rather than depending on heavy pyramidal stages, the proposed model employs a lightweight dual-branch tokenizer to capture multi-resolution features with very small additional overhead. This design choice helps in keeping the inference latency low, which is important for time-sensitive scenarios. As a result, the architecture becomes more suitable for real-time video capsule endoscopy applications, where computational resources are often limited and efficiency matters a lot. In contrast, many hierarchical Vision Transformers operate beyond 4 to 5 GFLOPs, limiting their practicality in deployment-sensitive environments. Additionally, the proposed Medical-Gated Feedforward Network is specifically designed to address the severe class imbalance commonly observed in gastrointestinal datasets. Such task-specific adaptation is often absent in generic multi-scale transformer backbones, further highlighting the practical advantages of the proposed approach.

Ablation study

To gain a better insight into how each architectural element actually impacts the final performance, we conducted an ablation study on the Kvasir-Capsule dataset (Table 8). In each experiment, only a single module is either removed or slightly altered, while the rest of the architecture remains exactly the same. This allows us to better understand the role of each element and how much it actually contributes to the final performance.

Removing the multi-resolution tokenizer leads to a noticeable decrease in validation accuracy and macro-F1 score. This further emphasizes the importance of multi-resolution tokenization in learning complementary feature representations. In capsule endoscopy images, lesions are generally small and spatially sparse. As such, it becomes highly important to capture both the global anatomical context and the local patterns together. Without these multi-resolution features, the model begins to have difficulty in sustaining high performance, particularly in the minority classes which already have few samples.

Similarly, the removal of the Medical-Gated Feedforward Network in favor of a regular feedforward layer results in a drop in overall accuracy and macro-F1 score. This further confirms that the gating function has a significant contribution in filtering out irrelevant background information and focusing more on diagnostically useful features, although the extent of this contribution is slightly different for different classes. By filtering out redundant background information and magnifying discriminative features, it improves the sensitivity to the underrepresented classes, which is extremely useful in highly imbalanced datasets.

The auxiliary classification head consistently improves macro-F1 scores and contributes to more stable training across datasets. By introducing additional supervision at the patch level, it encourages richer intermediate representations and supports better class-wise discrimination.

Overall, the complete LightGastroFormer architecture achieves the best balance between accuracy, stability, and class-wise performance. Conclusion and future directions

We presented LightGastroFormer in this paper, a lightweight transformer-based model to detect gastrointestinal diseases with high precision and accuracy through endoscopic and capsule endoscopy images. The suggested model combines a multi-resolution patchwise tokenizer and a gated feedforward component with self-attention to simultaneously learn both contextual information on a global scale and fine-grained local features that are highly needed in the reliable diagnosis of GI.

Evaluation on three publicly available benchmarks, namely Kvasir v1, Kvasir v2, and Kvasir-Capsule have shown that LightGastroFormer is performing well and consistently on a wide range of settings. Specifically, the model is robust on the highly imbalanced Kvasir-Capsule dataset with no explicit balancing of data strategies, suggesting that the model is capable of learning discriminating representations in challenging settings. The sensitivity of each architectural element is also supported by ablation experiments, with the multi-resolution tokenizer increasing sensitivity to minor lesions, and the gated feedforward network increasing feature selectivity and stability, particularly of minority classes.

Although these are good results, there are still a number of limitations. The evaluation is currently restricted to publicly available datasets and might not be representative of the variability seen in the clinical environment in reality, including variations in imaging devices, acquisition protocols and patient populations. Also, the model works frame-by-frame and does not directly use temporal data present in video capsule endoscopy sequences, potentially valuable in identifying transient or ambiguous lesions. Moreover, although the model is resistant to imbalance between classes, it does not explicitly use imbalance-aware optimization techniques, which can be further improved to sensitize the model to minorities.

Future studies will be directed at the expansion of the framework to more clinically relevant tasks such as lesion localization and segmentation and the integration of temporal modeling to take advantage of inter-frame correlations in video data. It will be necessary to test the model on multi-center and cross-device data to determine its generalizability and accuracy in practice. Furthermore, enhancing human-AI interaction by enhancing interpretable visualization methods and confidence-conscious decision support systems will be essential in an effort to integrate seamlessly with clinical processes. These guidelines will assist in closing the performance versus practice gap between algorithms and real-world application in gastrointestinal diagnostics.
