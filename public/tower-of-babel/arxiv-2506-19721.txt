MUSE-DARK-I: Dark matter halo properties of intermediate- z z star-forming galaxies
Ciocan, B. I, Bouché, N. F, Fensch, J, Mercier, W, Krajnović, D, Richard, J, Contini, T, Jeanneau, A

Abstract
Context. Disk-halo decompositions of star-forming galaxies (SFGs) at redshifts z > 1 z>1 typically focus on massive galaxies with stellar masses exceeding log ⁡ ( M ⋆ / M ⊙ ) > 10 \log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})>10 . Aims. In this study, we analyse the dark matter (DM) halo properties of 127 intermediate-redshift ( 0.3 < z < 1.5 0.3<z<1.5 ) SFGs down to low stellar masses ( 7 < log ⁡ ( M ⋆ / M ⊙ ) < 11 7<\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})<11 ). To do so, we use integral field unit observations from the MUSE Hubble Ultra Deep Field Survey, as well as photometry from the Hubble Space Telescope and James Webb Space Telescope. Methods: We employ a 3D forward modelling approach to analyse the morpho-kinematics of our sample, enabling us to measure individual rotation curves extended up to 2 − 3 2-3 times the effective radii. We performed a disk-halo decomposition with a 3D parametric model, which includes stellar, DM and gas components, as well as corrections for pressure support. We validated our methodology using mock data cubes generated from idealised disk simulations. We select the best-fitting DM model, among six different DM density profiles, including the Navarro-Frenk-White, Burkert, Einasto and the generalised α ​ β ​ γ \alpha\beta\gamma profile of Di Cintio et al. 2014 (DC14). Results. Our Bayesian analysis suggests that the mass-dependent density profile of DC14, which accounts for the response of DM to baryonic processes such as stellar feedback, performs as well as or better than the other six halo models in ≳ 65 % \gtrsim 65\% of the sample, while baryon-only models are rejected by the data. We performed consistency checks for DC14 and found that the stellar masses obtained from the disk-halo decomposition agree with the values inferred from the spectral energy distributions, and the recovered DM inner slopes, γ \gamma , agree with the theoretical expectations. We find that 89% of the galaxies have DM fractions larger than 50%, similar to what is observed in the local universe. For 70% of the SFGs, we infer γ < 0.5 \gamma<0.5 , indicating cored DM density profiles, however, we do not find any correlation between γ \gamma and the star-forming activity of the sample. The stellar–halo mass and concentration – halo mass relations inferred from our 3D modelling align well with the theoretical expectations, but with larger scatter. Our results confirm the anticorrelation between the halo scale radius and DM density with a slope of ∼ − 1.1 \sim-1.1 . The halo scale radii and DM surface densities increase with M ⋆ M_{\star} , while the DM densities remain constant. We find tentative evidence of an evolution of the DM density with z z , which suggests that the DM halos of z ∼ 0.85 z\sim 0.85 systems are denser (by ∼ 0.3 \sim 0.3 dex) than those of local galaxies. In contrast, the halo scale radii are z z -invariant. Conclusions. We measured DM halo properties of intermediate- z z SFGs down to low M ⋆ M_{\star} , finding that over half of the sample is consistent with cored DM density profiles. These findings supports core formation driven by baryonic processes in the context of Λ \Lambda CDM.

Introduction

The matter content of the Universe is dominated by DM from sub-galactic to cosmological scales. The concept of DM entered mainstream research in the 1970s when observations revealed that galaxy rotation curves (RCs) remain flat at large galactocentric distances (e.g.,
van de Hulst et al. 1957
,
Rubin & Ford 1970
), contrary to the expected Keplerian decline. These flat RCs could not be explained by the Newtonian gravity of the baryonic matter alone, but instead suggested the presence of a DM halo.

The presence of DM halos is central to the
\Lambda
cold dark matter (
\Lambda
CDM) cosmological model, which is a successful framework for predicting and explaining the large-scale structures of the Universe and their evolution with cosmic time. However, on scales smaller than
\sim 1
Mpc, this model faces several challenges (see
Bullock & Boylan-Kolchin 2017
for a review). For example, DM-only simulations predict that DM assembles into haloes that develop steeply rising inner radial density profiles, i.e. ’cusps’, in the absence of any baryonic effects. These simulated DM halos can be described by:
\rm{\rho(r)\propto r^{-\gamma}}
with
\gamma\sim 0.8-1.4
, and are well fitted by a Navarro–Frenk–White profile (NFW;
Navarro et al. 1997
,
Klypin et al. 2001
), independently of initial conditions and cosmological parameters.
On the other hand, observations of low surface brightness galaxies have demonstrated that these systems show density profiles that are consistent with a constant-density ’core’ at the centre, with
\gamma\sim 0-0.5
(e.g.
Flores & Primack 1994
,
Salucci & Burkert 2000
,
de Blok et al. 2001
,
Weldrake et al. 2003
,
Salucci et al. 2007
,
Oh et al. 2015
). This disagreement between observations and simulations is known as the core-cusp problem and has posed a major challenge to
\Lambda
CDM for the past two decades
(
de Blok 2010
)
. This discrepancy either hints at the inadequacy of DM-only simulations to capture the DM dynamics on small scales, due to the absence of phenomena connected to baryonic physics, or the fact that a modification of the whole
\Lambda
CDM paradigm is needed (e.g. self-interacting DM -
Spergel & Steinhardt 2000
, axion-like fuzzy DM-
Hu et al. 2000
).

Several baryonic mechanisms within the
\Lambda
CDM framework have been proposed to solve the core-cusp problem. For example, infalling gas clumps can transfer angular momentum to DM via dynamical friction, resulting in a shallower central density profile (e.g.
El-Zant et al. 2001
,
Romano-Díaz et al. 2008
,
Johansson et al. 2009
). Alternatively, energy could be transferred to the outer halo through resonant effects induced by a central stellar bar, which can also transform cusps into cores
(
Weinberg & Katz 2002
)
. Cores may also be created when baryons, after condensing at the centre of a halo, are suddenly expelled by feedback processes. In many high-resolution cosmological simulations, strong stellar feedback from massive stars and supernovae was shown to drive large-scale gas outflows during repeated starburst events, leading to an overall expansion of the DM halo and the creation of a cored DM density profile (
Navarro et al. 1996
,
Read & Gilmore 2005
,
Governato et al. 2010
,
Pontzen & Governato 2012
,
Teyssier et al. 2013
,
Brooks & Zolotov 2014
,
Freundlich et al. 2020a
,
Dekel et al. 2021
,
Li et al. 2023
,
Jackson et al. 2024
,
Azartash-Namin et al. 2024
). Core formation can also be linked to active galactic nuclei (AGN) activity in high-mass galaxies and galaxy clusters (
Martizzi et al. 2012
,
Peirani et al. 2017
).

In the stellar feedback scenario, many studies claim that core formation is strongly dependent on
M_{\star}/M_{\rm{halo}}
, with this mechanism being most efficient for
(M_{\star}/M_{\rm{halo}})\sim 3-5\>\rm{x}\>10^{-3}
, corresponding to a stellar mass regime of
10^{9}<M_{\star}/M_{\odot}<10^{10}
. In more massive haloes, the outflows become ineffective at flattening the inner DM density and the haloes have increasingly cuspy profiles. Similarly, at
M_{\star}/M_{\rm{halo}}<10^{-4}
, there is not enough supernova energy to efficiently change the DM distribution, and the halo retains the original NFW profile (
Di Cintio et al. 2014
,
Read et al. 2016
,
Lazar et al. 2020
). Similar results were obtained from simulations that also include AGN feedback in addition to stellar feedback ( e.g.
Macció et al. 2020
).
Tollet et al. 2016
have demonstrated that the relationship between the inner DM density slope and
M_{\star}/M_{\rm{halo}}
holds approximately up to
z\sim 1
, implying that in all haloes, the shape of their inner density profile changes over cosmic time, as they grow in stellar and total mass. Nevertheless, the dependency of the DM inner slope on
M_{\star}/M_{\rm{halo}}
is still debated (e.g.
Bose et al. 2019
).

RCs represent the fundamental tools to study the mass distribution in SFGs, as they are amongst the only observables one could use to probe the DM density distribution on galactic scales
(
Rubin et al. 1978
)
. Kinematic studies focussing on the local Universe have used a variety of dynamical tracers to analyse the DM halo properties of disk galaxies (
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
,
Katz et al. 2017
,
Allaert et al. 2017
,
Katz et al. 2018
,
Read et al. 2019
,
Korsaga et al. 2019
,
Salucci 2019
,
Li et al. 2019
,
Li et al. 2020
). Besides characterising the structure of DM halos of local galaxies, it is also crucial to understand the evolution of these halos over cosmic time. Thereby, dynamical studies of higher-
z
galaxies are essential to understanding the nature of DM.

Fortunately, over the past decade, Integral Field Unit (IFU) observations utilising instruments such as the Spectrograph for INtegral Field Observations in the Near Infrared (SINFONI,
Eisenhauer et al. 2003
), the Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE;
Bacon et al. 2010
) and the K-band Multi-Object Spectrograph (KMOS;
Sharples 2014
), along with sub-mm observations employing e.g. the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), have opened up a new avenue in probing the dynamics of high-
z
systems (e.g.
Förster Schreiber et al. 2009
,
Genzel et al. 2011
,
Wuyts et al. 2016
,
Bacon et al. 2017
,
Wisnioski et al. 2015
,
Girard et al. 2018
,
Le Févre et al. 2020
). Despite these advancements, it is important to highlight that this field is still in its nascent stages.
Indeed, studying the DM halo properties of distant SFGs at
\rm{z}\sim 1-2
was not considered possible without very deep observations (
Genzel et al. 2017
,
Genzel et al. 2020
,
Puglisi et al. 2023
,
Nestor Shachar et al. 2023
), or stacking techniques (
Lang et al. 2017
,
Sharma et al. 2021
,
Tiley et al. 2019
). These studies often found declining outer RCs and consequently low DM fractions, but it is important to note that they primarily focus on high-mass systems (
\log(M/M_{\odot})\gtrsim 10
). In a pilot study of nine
z\sim 1
SFGs,
Bouché et al. 2022
pushed this type of analysis to lower stellar masses (
8.5<\log(M/M_{\odot})<10.5
) using extremely deep MUSE observations. They found a variety of RC shapes, reminiscent of the diversity observed at
z=0
, with DM fractions reaching
>50\%
in the lower mass regime.

Given the difficulty in studying the kinematics in the outer parts of distant galaxies, high-redshift studies of DM fraction or DM halo shape are limited to small samples: with 22 SFGs in
Puglisi et al. 2023
, 41 SFGs in
Genzel et al. 2020
, and 100 in
Nestor Shachar et al. 2023
(with the exception of the 240 massive SFGs analyzed in
Wuyts et al. 2016
), all with
\log(M/M_{\odot})\gtrsim 10
. Hence, the literature still lacks a comprehensive investigation of the DM halo properties in a statistical sample of typical intermediate-
z
(
0.2<z<1.5
) and intermediate-to-low mass SFGs with
7<\log(M/M_{\odot})<11
. This MUSE project (”MUSE-DARK”) aims to fill this knowledge gap by performing a detailed morpho-kinematics analysis on
>400
intermediate redshift SFGs from the MUSE
Hubble
Ultra Deep Field (MHUDF,
Bacon et al. 2023
), the MUSCATEL (programme 1104.A-0026; PI: Wisotzki, L.) and the lensing cluster
(
Richard et al. 2021
)
data-sets.

In this first MUSE-DARK paper, our objective is to perform a 3D disk-halo decomposition on a sample of 127 late-type SFGs from
the MUSE HUDF data release
(
Bacon et al. 2023
)

using six different DM halo models, as well as baryon-only models. Our galaxies span a wide redshift (
0.28<z<1.49
) and stellar mass (
7<\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})<11
) range, enabling us to study the evolution of DM halo properties with
z
and
M_{\star}

z

(

These tools construct 3D disk models that can be directly compared to 3D observational data and

By considering both the Point Spread Function (PSF) and the Line Spread Function (LSF), these tools return the intrinsic ‘deconvolved‘ (structural and) kinematic parameters of the galaxies, which are corrected for instrumental effects and beam-smearing. All dynamical modeling approaches necessarily rely on a set of assumptions about the physical structure of galaxies — such as axial symmetry, the spatial distribution of mass components — as well as on technical choices in how the data are processed. In particular, 3D forward modeling tools like
GalPaK
3D

GalPaK
3D

This 3D forward modelling framework offers several advantages over traditional methods (such as fitting the 2D velocity field or 1D rotation curve) and is best suited for distant galaxies because: (1) it uses all the information contained in thousands of spaxels across the galaxy data cube; (2) it allows us to probe the low S/N regions in the outskirts of galaxies, where the S/N of the spectral line of interest drops below unity, by leveraging the collective signal of all low-S/N spaxels; (3) it constrains the intrinsic morphological and kinematical parameters of the disk simultaneously, thereby breaking the
v_{\rm{max}}
-inclination degeneracy; and lastly (4) it computes the likelihood directly from the 3D data without loosing information (traditional RC decomposition methods performed in 1D typically rely on a limited number of often correlated data points). The 3D forward modelling does assume an axisymmetric disk, hence our rather strict criteria for selecting a sample of regular, unperturbed rotation dominated galaxies (see section
4.2

Freundlich et al. 2020b
), Burkert
(

)
, coreNFW
(

(

\rho(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm{s}}}{\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{\gamma}\left(1+\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{\alpha}\right)^{(\beta-\gamma)/\alpha}},

(2)

where
{r_{\rm{s}}}
is the scale radius,
\rm{\rho_{\rm{s}}}
the scale density, and
\alpha,\>\beta,\>\gamma
are the shape parameters of the DM density profile.
6
6

6

E.g., a NFW
(
Navarro et al. 1997
)
profile has
\alpha,\beta,\gamma=(1,3,1)
, the Dekel-Zhao (

has
\alpha,\beta,\gamma=(0.5,3.5,a)
.
\beta

\gamma

\alpha(X),\beta(X),\gamma(X)

Finally, we also consider baryon-only models setting
v_{\rm{DM}}(r)=0

While the URC and no DM models have 12 - 13 free parameters, the disk-halo models have between 14 - 18 free parameters (depending on the used halo model and whether we include a bulge component or not), namely:

We use loose flat priors covering a wide physical range for all these parameters, except for the inclination,
i
, stellar masses,
M_{\star}
, and HI masses
M_{\rm{HI}}
for which we use more conservative priors. More specifically, for the inclinations, we use priors based on the values obtained with
Galfit
from the HST/F160W images (
van der Wel et al. 2012

), such that

4.3
), with
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})_{\rm{SED}}\pm 0.15

M_{\star}

{X}
. For the HI masses, we use as priors the scaling relations proposed by
Chowdhury et al. 2022
at
z\sim 1
(their Equation 1), such that
\log(M_{\rm{HI}}/M_{\odot})=0.32\cdot\log(10^{M_{\star}/10^{10}})+10.183\pm 0.2
.
We tested
GalPaK
3D
using flat and Gaussian priors for all the parameters, and found that the results are consistent, within the errors. We also tested whether using priors on other structural parameters besides
i
, such as for
R_{\rm{e}}
and
n
leads to different results, and found that they agree within the errors.

3.1
Description of the simulations and creation of synthetic MUSE data-cubes

To validate the 3D disk-halo decomposition introduced in
2.2
and to estimate potential systematic errors, we generated mock MUSE observations from two idealised disks simulated using the Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) hydro-dynamical code
RAMSES

(
Teyssier 2002
)
with specific initial conditions that control the DM profile shape using the
MAGI
code
(
Miki & Umemura 2018
)

as we will simulate two galaxy models, one representative of a cuspy and one of a cored DM distribution.

Specifically, the idealised disks are set in a cubic box of length
132
kpc and the gas cells have sizes between 1 kpc and 8 pc depending on the level of refinement. A cell is refined if either: (i) the number of initial conditions particles in the cell is above 50; (ii) its mass, including embedded new star particles, is above
5\times 10^{4}
M
⊙
; (iii) the local Jeans length is shorter than four times the cell size.
DM and initial conditions stars are modelled by
1\times 10^{4}
and
8.8\times 10^{4}
M
⊙
mass particles, respectively. Their effect on the gravitational potential is treated at a maximum refinement of 32 pc.
The numerical recipes are described in more detail in
Fensch & Bournaud 2021
and summarised here. Briefly, we use heating and atomic cooling at solar metallicity from
Courty & Alimi 2004
, and we allow cooling down to 100 K and prevent numerical fragmentation with a pressure floor at high densities. We model star-formation with a Schmidt law with an efficiency per free-fall time of 1% for gas cells denser than 300
H
/cc and cooler than
2\times 10^{4}
K where new stars have a mass of
4\times 10^{3}
M
⊙
. We include two types of star formation feedback: type II supernovae and H
II
regions, using the same model as
Medium Feedback
in
Fensch & Bournaud 2021
.

We ran two disk models, one representative of a cuspy and one of a cored DM distribution, respectively ID 3 and ID 982 from
Bouché et al. 2022
, using the shape parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
described in Table
1
.
The amount of ionised gas in these galaxies is not constrained by our data, therefore we use, for the initial conditions, a gas mass corresponding to the SFR measured in
Bouché et al. 2022
for these two galaxies. This gas mass corresponds to a (molecular) depletion timescale of 0.7 Gyr typical for intermediate redshift galaxies (
Tacconi et al. 2020
,
Saintonge et al. 2013
).
7
7

7

In the snapshot used for mock creation, the effective total gas depletion timescale is around 0.85 Gyr for the two models.

We do not attempt to simulate the neutral HI gas.

We first run the simulations with an isothermal equation of state, at temperature
5\times 10^{4}
K, for
\simeq\penalty\ 300
Myr, to let the gas component relax in the gravitational potential of the stars. After this first phase, we let the gas cool and activate star formation and its feedback. Measurements are made 100 Myr after the activation of gas cooling and star formation and feedback.

4.2
Sample selection

We used the MHUDFs main catalogue to select SF galaxies with
z
¡1.5 for our kinematic study, which have an effective signal to noise
\rm{S/N_{eff}}>10
for the brightest emission line (either
\rm{[O\textsc{ii}]}\>\lambda 3727
,
\rm{H\beta}
,
\rm{[O\textsc{iii}]}\>\lambda 5007
,
\rm{H\alpha}
). The effective S/N accounts for the fact that the surface brightness of the galaxy alone is not sufficient to determine the accuracy in the fitted disk structural and kinematic parameters, as the compactness of the galaxy with respect to the PSF also plays an important role (
Bouché et al. 2015
). The effective S/N is defined as follows:

\rm{S/N_{eff}}=(R_{\rm{e}}/R_{\rm{PSF}})\cdot\rm{S/N_{max}},

(3)

where
R_{\rm{e}}
stands for the stellar half-light radius,
R_{\rm{PSF}}=\rm{FWHM}/2
is the radius of the MUSE PSF, and
\rm{S/N_{max}}
is the S/N of the emission line of interest in the brightest spaxel of the MUSE data cube centred on the galaxy.
We also impose
R_{\rm{e}}/R_{\rm{PSF}}>0.5
for our sample selection, in order to further avoid unresolved galaxies.
Additionally, we only select systems which have an inclination
i>30^{\circ}
for our kinematic study.

\rm{S/N_{eff}}>10
,

, and

.

Moreover, we require our galaxies to be axisymmetric disks- i.e. unperturbed - in order to accurately model their morpho-kinematics and infer their DM halo properties. Therefore, we exclude merging systems from our sample. Additionally, we flag galaxies, which show signs of gravitational interactions such as tidal arms; clumpy SF regions; as well as large residuals in the
GalPaK
3D

Ventou et al. 2019

This leads to a sample of 173 galaxies.

Finally, for the disk-halo decomposition described in section
2.2
, we require the galaxies to be rotationally supported, therefore, we exclude dispersion-dominated systems from our analysis.
Using the URC described in Sect.

5.1
Model comparison and model selection

Using the 3D disk-halo decomposition methodology, we first identify which of the seven models–DC14, NFW, Burkert, Dekel-Zhao, Einasto, coreNFW, baryons-only– best describe the data by computing the Bayes factor for each pair of models. Namely, we use the model evidence
\log(\mathcal{Z})
or marginal probability log
P(y\rm{|}M)
, defined as:

\log(\mathcal{Z})=\ln\left(\int P(y|\theta,M)\,P(\theta|M)\,d\theta\right),

(4)

i.e. the integral of the posterior over the parameters space yielded by our MCMC fitting routine with
pyMultiNest
(
Buchner et al. 2014
).

\log(\mathcal{Z})
serves as a measure of how well the model explains the observed data, accounting for both goodness of fit and model complexity. This metric is closely related to the Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), which is BIC=
-2\ln\mathcal{\hat{L}}+k\ln(n)=\chi^{2}+k\ln(n)
where
\mathcal{\hat{L}}=P(x|{\widehat{\theta},M})
is the maximum likelihood,
k
the number of parameters, and
n
the number of data points.

Following
Kass & Raftery 1995
, we rescale the evidence
\log(\mathcal{Z})
by a factor of -2 so that it is on the same scale as the usual information criterion (BIC, Deviance Information Criterion). We then compute the Bayes factor, defined as the ratio of the marginal probabilities of two competing models
M_{1}
vs
M_{2}
:

B_{12}=\frac{P(y|M_{1})}{P(y|M_{2})}=\log(\mathcal{Z}_{M_{1}})-\log(\mathcal{Z}_{M_{2}})

(5)

for each pair of models (e.g.
Trotta 2008
).
Considering our scaling factor of -2 (see also
Kass & Raftery 1995
), positive evidence against the null hypothesis, i.e. that the two models are equivalent occurs when the Bayes factor is
>3
, whereas strong evidence occurs when the Bayes factor
>20
. This corresponds to a logarithmic difference
\rm{\Delta log(\mathcal{Z})}
of 2 and 6, respectively.

Before discussing the results for the entire sample, we present the residual maps in Fig.
8
for the same three galaxies from Fig.
6
and one extra representative galaxy in our sample.
This Fig. shows the residual maps (generated from the residual cube as in Fig.
6
) for each of the six DM profiles–DC14, NFW, Burkert, Dekel-Zhao, Einasto, coreNFW– and the baryon only model, along with the evidence (in black) and the Bayes factor (color-coded as in Fig.
9
) between the DC14 and and the other six models.

This figure clearly shows that when the Bayes factor indicates strong positive evidence in favour of a particular model with
\Delta\log(\mathcal{Z})\ll-6
,— e.g. DC14, in the example from the bottom row — the corresponding residual maps consistently exhibit lower residuals compared to the alternatives. For instance, for galaxy MOSAIC 888, we infer a Bayes factor of
\Delta\log(\mathcal{Z})=-1141

between the DC14 and NFW profiles, and

\Delta\log(\mathcal{Z})=-1522
between the DC14 and the baryon-only models, indicating a (very) strong positive evidence for DC14. This figure clearly shows that the baryon-only model is strongly disfavored, and similarly the NFW is also strongly disfavored, with the residual maps clearly indicating that these DM models provide the poorest fit to the data. However, when we infer a Bayes factor with a small value between two competing models (
-6<\Delta\log(\mathcal{Z})<6
- inconclusive/mild positive/mild negative evidence) e.g. second and third rows of this Figure, the residual maps look fairly similar.

5.3
Disk-halo decomposition cross checks

In this section, we perform several cross-checks of our 3D disk-halo decomposition.
We first compare (Sect.

5.3.1
Stellar gravitational field from mass maps

To independently estimate the stellar disk component’s contribution to the disk-halo decomposition, we adopted the MGE method (
Monnet et al. 1992
,
Emsellem et al. 1994
) on the stellar mass maps obtained from pixel SED fitting described in Appendix
C
. Given that the mass maps could only be computed for the higher-S/N galaxies (
\sim 40\%
of the sample), we also used the JWST NIRCAM/F090W images for the rest.

We then use the MGE approach (after masking foreground/background galaxies), employing the
mgefit
Python package of
Cappellari 2002
, considering the PSF, on the 2D stellar maps (or the NIRCAM/F090W images). Each MGE model comprises concentric 2D Gaussians characterised by peak intensity, dispersion, and axial ratio or flattening
10
10

10

Following
Scott et al. 2013
, we optimise the allowed range of axial ratios until the fits become unacceptable, using as a lower limit the axial ratio from Sec.
4.4
.

. Finally, based on the MGE parameters, we use the
mge-vcirc
module from the
jampy
Python package of
Cappellari 2008
, to calculate the gravitational acceleration (
v^{2}/r
) in the equatorial plane of each galaxy. The central idea behind this method is to use the MGE representation of the galaxy’s light distribution to infer the underlying mass distribution (valid for the NIRCAM images where we assume a constant M/L ratio, whereas for the mass maps, the MGE representation directly yields the mass distribution), assuming dynamical equilibrium. In Fig.
10
, the dotted red and pink curves show the predicted circular velocity of the stellar mass distribution, as derived from the mass maps and NIRCAM/F090W images, respectively. As this Fig. demonstrates, there is good agreement between the
v_{\star}(r)
determined from the mass maps/F090W images and the MUSE data. For approximately
80\%
of the galaxies in our sample, the RCs obtained from the two methods agree within the errors. Moreover, the half-light radii and Sérsic disk profile from the mass maps are very similar compared to those obtained from the ionized gas tracers.

Discrepancies in the order of
\sim 10-20
km/s, where present, are mostly confined to the central regions, and remain within the uncertainties of the
GalPaK
3D
velocities (yellow shaded regions in Fig.
10
). These central differences likely arise from the sensitivity of the MGE method to the precise modelling of the PSF. We note that the central parts are particularly sensitive to the assumed PSF model, as the small angular sizes of our galaxies (
R_{\rm e}\sim 0.5
–
1\hbox{${}^{\prime\prime}$}{}
) make them more prone to resolution-related systematics.

Crucially, the agreement between the MGE-based and
GalPaK
3D
stellar circular velocities provides an independent validation of our disk-halo decomposition. While
GalPaK
3D
assumes an Sérsic disk profile, the MGE approach does not impose a parametric form on the stellar mass distribution. The fact that both methods yield consistent stellar contributions implies that the stars in our sample galaxies are indeed in (near) equilibrium disks. The results obtained in this section were used as a qualitative check against the results obtained with our 3D parametric modeling.

5.6

The

\rm{\Lambda CDM}
framework is a well-known scaling relation describing the relationship between the mass of a DM halo and the stellar mass of the galaxies residing within it. This scaling relation reflects the SF efficiency, and thus the efficiency of feedback processes, providing crucial insights into galaxy formation models (e.g.
Vale & Ostriker 2004
,
Moster et al. 2010
). The stellar mass–halo mass relation was investigated using RCs of individual galaxies from the local Universe by e.g.
Read et al. 2017
,
Di Paolo et al. 2019
,
Posti et al. 2019
and
Li et al. 2020
amongst more recent studies, and these have found rather large scatter in the relation.

Figure

This Figure shows that our results are qualitatively in good agreement with the relation inferred by
Behroozi et al. 2019
, as well as the one derived by
Girelli et al. 2020
using the (sub)halo abundance matching technique, albeit with a larger scatter. The latter two studies quote a scatter in the
M_{\star}-M_{\rm{vir}}
relation of about
\sim 0.2-0.4
dex, which is much smaller than the scatter
\gtrsim 1.5
dex we measure for the MHUDF galaxies. Similar results were found by
Li et al. 2020
for the local SPARC sample, using DC14 without
\Lambda
CDM priors. It is also worth noting that our sample does not probe the high-mass end, thereby does not reach the bend in the
M_{\star}-M_{\rm{vir}}
relations.

In appendix
D
, we show in Fig.

the

, where

5.7
Concentration-mass relation

A fundamental prediction from N-body
\Lambda
CDM simulations is the concentration of dark matter halos, defined as
c_{\rm{vir}}=r_{\rm{vir}}/r_{\rm{s}}
, which is expected to scale with halo mass as
M_{\rm{vir}}\propto c_{\rm{vir}}^{-0.13}
and with redshift as
c_{\rm{vir}}\propto(1+z)^{-1}

(
Bullock et al. 2001
;
Wechsler et al. 2002
)
, reflecting the redshift evolution of the virial radius
r_{\rm vir}
. The
c_{\rm{vir}}
–
M_{\rm{vir}}
relation has been tested using RCs in the local Universe
(
Allaert et al. 2017
;
Katz et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, but remains poorly constrained at higher redshifts for galaxy-scale halos
(
Bouché et al. 2022
;
Sharma et al. 2022

5.8
Concentration-density relation

The
c_{\rm{vir}}-M_{\rm{vir}}
or
r_{\rm{s}}-M_{\rm{vir}}
relations can be transformed to the
r_{\rm{s}}-\rho_{\rm{s}}
relation.
Figure
17
shows the
r_{\rm{s}}-\rho_{\rm{s}}
relation for the MHUDF galaxies. We observationally confirm the well-known anticorrelation of these two halo parameters with a slope of
\approx-1
(e.g.
Salucci & Burkert 2000
,
Spano et al. 2008
,
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
). This anticorrelation results from the expected scaling relation of DM predicted by hierarchical clustering (e.g.
Gott & Rees 1975
).

Using
HyperFit

(
Robotham & Obreschkow 2015
)
, we fit a simple power-law model to the
r_{\rm{s}}-\rho_{\rm{s}}
relation, such that
\rho_{\rm{s}}\propto\rho_{0}\cdot r_{\rm{s}}^{x}\cdot(1+z)^{y}
, taking into account the errors. However, to investigate whether there is redshift evolution, we first need to asses the mass completeness of our sample across the probed
z
-range. We found that the MHUDF survey is complete down to a stellar-mass limit of
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=8.61
within
0.2<z<1.5
, whereas our sample is only complete above
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=8.9
, due to additional S/N selection criteria. To mitigate this bias, we imposed a uniform mass cut at
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=8.9
, which resulted in the exclusion of 29 galaxies from our sample. Restricting the analysis to this mass-complete sample, we find
\rho_{\rm{s}}\propto r_{\rm{s}}^{-1.091\pm 0.161}\cdot(1+z)^{0.328\pm 0.266}
(purple line in Fig.
17
). For example,
Djorgovski 1992
showed that the density of DM halos,
\rho
, and the size,
r
, follow
\rho\propto r^{-3(3+n)/(5+n)}
. The
r_{\rm{s}}
slope of
\sim-1.1
that we infer yields
n\sim-1.85
. This value for
n
is very close to
n\sim-2
expected in
\Lambda
CDM for halo masses of
\sim 10^{12}\>M_{\odot}
(e.g.
Shapiro & Iliev 2002
).

In terms of the
z
-evolution, this Fig. demonstrates that higher-
z
SFGs from our sample show slightly larger DM densities than their lower
z
counterparts and are shifted above the relation inferred for local galaxies. Therefore, we find tentative evidence that that
\rho_{\rm{s}}
increases with
z
, however, a larger data-set is required to confidently confirm these results.

To further explore this finding, we plot in the left-hand panel of Fig.
18
the halo scale radius – stellar mass relation, in the middle panel the DM density – stellar mass relation, and in the right-hand panel the DM surface density (
\Sigma_{\rm{s}}=\rho_{\rm{s}}\cdot r_{\rm{s}})
as a function of galaxy mass for the MHUDF sample (coloured data points) and SPARC sample (grey data points,
Li et al. 2019
), using the DC14 halo model. Similar to
Li et al. 2019
, we find that the halo scale radius and the DM surface density increase with
M_{\star}
, as opposed to e.g.
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
, who found constant DM surface densities in
z=0
disks.
Also, similarly to
Li et al. 2019
, we find that the DM density is more or less constant with stellar masses.

Investigating the linear fits of the two data sets, we find that the halo scale radii,
r_{\rm{s}}
, of MHUDF galaxies are consistent with those obtained for the local SPARC sample. This agrees with results inferred from DMO simulations by e.g.
Bullock et al. 2001
and
Dutton & Macció 2014
, who found that the halo scale radii remain constant with redshift. On the other hand, we find tentative evidence that the DM densities,
\rho_{\rm{s}}
, are
\sim 0.3
dex larger than those of local galaxies. This suggests that the DM halos of
z\sim 0.85
SFGs are slightly denser than those of their local counterparts. A more detailed discussion of the evolution of halo parameters with
z
can be found in Sect.
6.3
.

6
Discussion

6.1
Core formation

Our results in Section
5.1
show that in more than half of the sample, the mass-dependent DC14 profile—which accounts for the response of DM to baryonic processes—performs as well as or better than other halo models. Using this profile, we find that
\sim 70\%
of our galaxies are consistent with cored DM distributions (Section
5.5
). The formation of DM cores has been explained through various scenarios in the literature.

Alternative cosmologies such as warm DM
(
Bode et al. 2001
)
, self-interacting DM
(
Spergel & Steinhardt 2000
)
, and fuzzy DM
(
Hu et al. 2000
)
offer non-standard solutions to the core-cusp problem. Notably, SIDM and fuzzy DM models have been shown to reproduce a range of rotation curve shapes, including cores, in SFGs
(
Kamada et al. 2017
;
Khelashvili et al. 2023
)
.

Within the standard
\Lambda
CDM framework, baryon–DM interactions in the inner halo—driven by AGN feedback
(
Dekel et al. 2021
)
, infalling gas clumps
(
El-Zant et al. 2001
;
Romano-Díaz et al. 2008
;
Johansson et al. 2009
)
, central stellar bars
(
Weinberg & Katz 2002
)
, or stellar feedback
(
Read & Gilmore 2005
;
Governato et al. 2010
;
Teyssier et al. 2013
;
Brooks & Zolotov 2014
;
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Freundlich et al. 2020b
;
Jackson et al. 2024
)
—have been proposed as core-forming mechanisms.

Although DM cores have been observed up to
z\sim 2
(e.g.
Genzel et al. 2017
,
Sharma et al. 2022
,
Nestor Shachar et al. 2023
), a clear correlation between core formation and baryonic processes remains elusive. However, some studies have found links between the inner DM slope and SF activity in their small galaxy samples. Most notably,
Read et al. 2019
showed that dwarfs with no recent SF activity tend to retain cuspy profiles, while those with prolonged and bursty star formation histories display cores. Similarly,
Bouché et al. 2022
found that SFGs above the MS or with high
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
are more likely to have cores.

Motivated by these results, we now explore whether similar trends are present in our sample. Figure
19
(left) shows the kinematically inferred stellar masses as a function of the offset from the star-forming main sequence (
\Delta_{\rm MS}
) following
Boogaard et al. 2018
. The data points are color-coded by the inner slope of the DM density profile,
\gamma
. We find no clear relation between
\gamma
and
\Delta_{\rm MS}
, as both cored and cuspy galaxies span a wide range of SFRs. However, a clear mass trend emerges, as indicated by the gradient in the color coding: more massive galaxies tend to have cuspier DM profiles.

The right panel of Figure
19
shows the DM density at 150 pc as a function of
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\rm vir})
. Here, we also find a mass trend such that higher stellar-to-halo mass ratios correspond to higher central DM densities, i.e., cuspier profiles. However, no clear trend emerges with specific SFR, as galaxies with similar
\rho_{\rm DM}
(150 pc) show a broad range in sSFR.

We note, however, that this lack of correlation with current SF activity is not necessarily unexpected. Models predict that core formation is driven by repeated, rapid fluctuations in the gravitational potential, triggered by bursts of SF and gas outflows, rather than by the instantaneous SFR. Hence, a better test of the stellar feedback–core connection would require full SFHs, as employed by
Read et al. 2019
. We defer such an analysis to future work.

It is also important to note that some recent studies using hydrodynamical simulations report no significant correlation between bursty SFHs and the formation of DM cores in galaxies, or even a lack of cores in simulated SFGs. For instance,
Bose et al. 2019
analysed the DM density profiles of isolated dwarf galaxies in the APOSTLE and AURIGA hydrodynamical simulations, finding no evidence of a correlation between the evolution of the DM inner slope and the SFH, nor between the inner slope and the stellar-to-halo mass ratio.

Also, large volume cosmological simulations such as the IllustrisTNG suite (e.g. TNG50;
Nelson et al. 2019
;
Pillepich et al. 2019
) predominantly fail to form cored DM profiles in galaxies. Figure
20
shows the inner slopes of the DM density profiles,
\gamma
, as a function of stellar mass, for observed and simulated galaxies. Our MHUDF sample of SFGs at
z\sim 1
is shown in red, the SPARC z=0 sample is shown in gray
(
Li et al. 2020
)
, while the TNG50
z\sim 1
SFGs are shown in black. The samples are matched in stellar mass. As illustrated in the Fig., the TNG50 galaxies tend to exhibit cuspy inner profiles across the sampled
M_{\star}
range, while our observations preferentially indicate the presence of cores. This discrepancy suggests that TNG50 does not fully capture the baryonic processes responsible for core formation, at least in the mass and
z
regime probed here.

On the other hand, the SPARC
z=0
galaxies show intermediate inner slopes, with
\gamma
values between those predicted by TNG50 at
z\sim 1
and those measured in our intermediate-
z
sample. This suggests that SPARC galaxies exhibit a mix of core and cusp profiles, possibly reflecting a broader diversity in central DM structure at lower redshift.

6.2
Scaling relations between DM & baryons

The results that we inferred from Fig.
18
suggest a strong correlation between the properties of the DM halo and those of the baryonic component. We find that the halo scale radius,
r_{\rm{s}}
, and the DM surface density,
\Sigma_{\rm{s}}
, increase with
M_{\star}
. On the other hand, the DM density,
\rho_{\rm{s}}
, is constant with stellar mass. These results indicate that more massive galaxies must reside in bigger halos, but the size of the halo and its mass must progressively increase to keep the density constant. Similar results were found by
Li et al. 2019
for the local SPARC sample, as can be seen from the linear fits to the two data sets in Fig.
18
. The increase of the DM surface density with stellar mass was also confirmed observationally by
Zhou et al. 2020
for the local SPARC sample and theoretically by
Kaneda et al. 2024
.

However, for example
Donato et al. 2009
and
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
found that the DM surface density,
\Sigma_{\rm{DM}}\propto\rho_{0}\cdot r_{\rm{c}}
remains constant with
M_{\star}
. No explanation for the constant DM surface density with stellar mass was given within the
\Lambda
CDM framework by these authors. The differences between our results and those of the latter two studies most likely arise due to the different definitions of the DM halo parameters, as well as the different analysis methods and priors used in the kinematic modelling. For instance,
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
employed the maximum disc method and fitted pseudo-isothermal profiles on their sample. In their analysis,
\rho_{0}
represents the central volume density of cored DM halos, while in our case,
\rho_{\rm{s}}
denotes the characteristic volume density for the DC14 profile. Similarly,
r_{\rm{c}}
in their study represents the core radius, whereas in our case,
r_{\rm{s}}
is the halo scale radius.
Li et al. 2019
demonstrated that using comparable assumptions for the kinematic modelling of the SPARC sample as those employed by
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
results in a constant DM surface density with stellar mass. They argue that the maximum disk method used in
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
is inadequate for low and intermediate-mass galaxies, as it makes the stellar disc contribute as much as it can, which compensates for the contribution from DM halo.

6.3
Redshift evolution of DM halo properties

Our analysis in section
5.8
shows that there is tentative evidence that the DM density -
\rho_{\rm{s}}
- increases with
z
, as can be seen from the gradient of the data points from Figure
17
. Fitting a simple power-law model to our mass-complete sample, such that
\rho_{\rm{s}}\propto(1+z)^{y}
, we find that
y=0.328\pm 0.266
, which hints at an evolution of
\rho_{\rm{s}}
with
z
. The redshift evolution of
\rho_{\rm{s}}
can be seen more clearly in the middle panel of Fig.
18
, where we compare the DM densities of our sample to those of
z=0
SFGs.
We find that the DM halos of
z\sim 0.85
galaxies are denser than those of their local counterparts by
\sim 0.3
dex. However, we note that a larger dataset spanning a broader stellar mass and redshift range will be necessary to confidently confirm this tentative redshift evolution and disentangle selection effects from genuine physical trends.

Nonetheless, the increase of the characteristic density
\rho_{\rm{s}}
of DM halos with redshift is in agreement with theoretical expectations in the
\Lambda
CDM context, as shown by
Zhao et al. 2003
and
Zhao et al. 2009
. For example,
Zhao et al. 2009
used a large set of high-resolution N-body simulations of a variety of structure formation models to study the mass accretion histories, the mass and redshift dependence of concentrations, and the concentration evolution histories of DM halos. They develop a universal model for halo concentrations, which can be also used to study the evolution of
\rho_{\rm{s}}
with
z
(see their Fig. 20). They indeed find that
\rho_{\rm{s}}
increases with
z
, which is in qualitative agreement with our observational results. This trend arises naturally from the physics of hierarchical structure formation in an expanding Universe. At higher redshifts, the mean background density of the Universe is higher, leading halos that collapse earlier to do so in denser environments. Since the characteristic density
\rho_{\rm{s}}
is related to the density of the Universe at the time of halo collapse, halos forming at earlier epochs tend to have higher densities.

On the other hand, we find that the halo scale radii of the MHUDF galaxies are consistent, within the errors, with those of local galaxies, in accordance with the redshift invariant
r_{\rm{s}}
found by e.g.
Bullock et al. 2001
,
Diemer et al. 2013
and
Dutton & Macció 2014
from DMO simulations.

7
Summary and conclusion

In this paper, we analyse the DM halo properties of a large sample of 127 SFGs, spanning a wide redshift (
0.28<z<1.49
) and stellar mass (
7<\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})<11
) range. To do so, we use deep MUSE IFU observations from the MHUDF survey
(
Bacon et al. 2023
)
. We perform a disk–halo decomposition using a 3D forward-modelling approach that accounts for stellar, gas, DM (and, where applicable, bulge) components, including corrections for pressure support. For the DM component, we test six different density profiles, namely: (1) the DC14 profile
(
Di Cintio et al. 2014
)
, linking the DM profile shape to the stellar mass–halo mass ratio; (2) NFW
(
Navarro et al. 1997
)
; (3) Burkert
(
Burkert 1995
)
; (4) Dekel-Zhao
(
Freundlich et al. 2020b
)
; (5) Einasto
(
Navarro et al. 2004
)
; and (6) coreNFW
(
Read et al. 2016
)
, as well as baryon-only models. The robustness of our methodology is assessed through application to mock MUSE data generated from idealised disk simulations.

Our main findings are summarised below:

-

The 3D approach allows us to constrain RCs to
2-3\>R_{\rm{e}}
in individual SFGs revealing DM dominated RCs, and a diversity in shapes with rising and declining outer profiles, similar to what is observed in the local Universe (Fig.
10
).

-

Compared to NFW, Burkert, Dekel-Zhao, Einasto and coreNFW,
DC14 performs as well or better in 89%, 67%, 81%, 75%, 83% of the sample, respectively (Fig.
9
), while baryon-only models are generally rejected by the data.

-

Several internal consistency checks support the reliability of the 3D decompositions. These include independent derivations of the stellar gravitational potential from mass maps and a 1D RC decomposition, both of which yield results consistent, within the errors, with the 3D approach for the vast majority of galaxies.

-

Performing several self-consistency checks for the best fitting model, DC14, we found that the disk stellar masses
M_{\star}
derived from the kinematic modelling are consistent with the SED-derived
M_{\star}
, and the inferred DM inner slopes,
\gamma
, are consistent with the DC14 expectations (Fig.
12
).

-

89% of the sample has
f_{\rm{DM}}(<R_{\rm{e}})
larger than 50%, while only 11% of our galaxies are baryon dominated. Globally, the
f_{\rm{DM}}(<R_{\rm{e}})-\Sigma_{M_{\star}}
relation is similar
to the z = 0 relation (e.g.
Courteau & Dutton 2015
), and follows from the TFR (Fig
13
).

-

70% of the SFGs favour cored DM density profiles, with
\gamma
¡ 0.5, in line with with
z=0
studies probing the same
M_{\star}
range (
Katz et al. 2017
,
Allaert et al. 2017
). However, no correlation between core/cusp formation and the SF activity of the sample can be observed (Fig.
19
).

-

The stellar mass–halo mass relation of the sample follows the
Behroozi et al. 2019
and
Girelli et al. 2020
relations, but with a larger scatter (Fig.
15
).

-

The concentration–halo mass relation agrees qualitatively with the
Dutton & Macció 2014
relation inferred from DM-only simulations, but with a larger scatter (Fig.
16
-left-hand side). The sample follows a tighter sequence in the halo mass–halo scaling radius relation, in accordance with simulations
(
Dutton & Macció 2014
)
and observations
(
Di Paolo et al. 2019
)
, but with a slight offset towards larger
r_{\rm{s}}
values at fixed halo mass (Fig.
16
-right-hand side).

-

We observe an anti-correlation between halo scale radius and DM density (Fig.
17
). The MHUDF galaxies are offset above the
z=0
relations from
Kormendy & Freeman 2016
and
Di Paolo et al. 2019
, suggestive of evolution in
\rho_{\rm{s}}
with redshift.

-

The halo scale radius and the DM surface density increase with
M_{\star}
, while the DM density remains relatively constant across the mass range explored (Fig.
18
).

-

The DM densities of
z\sim 0.85
SFGs are on average
\sim 0.3
dex higher than those of z=0 galaxies, while the halo scale radii are consistent with those of local galaxies (Fig.
18
).

Taken together, these findings provide support for the presence of cored DM distributions in a significant fraction of the sample, and offer empirical constraints on several DM halo scaling relations across a redshift and mass regime that remains relatively under-explored. This study also highlights the power of utilising 3D disk-halo decomposition on deep IFU data. In future studies, we will expand this analysis to larger samples and explore alternative DM models, including self-interacting DM
(
Spergel & Steinhardt 2000
)
and fuzzy DM
(
Hu et al. 2000
)
.

Appendix A
DM models

In this work, we used the following six DM models, and a baryon-only model:

(1) DC14:
Di Cintio et al. 2014
introduce a mass-dependent density profile to describe the distribution of DM on galactic scales, which takes into account the response of DM to baryonic processes, and can thus represent both cored and cuspy profiles.
Within this context, the DM density profile is modelled as a generalised
\alpha-\beta-\gamma
double power-law (e.g.
Hernquist 1990
,
Zhao 1996
):

\rho_{\rm{DC14}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm{s}}}{\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{\gamma}\left(1+\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{\alpha}\right)^{(\beta-\gamma)/\alpha}},

(7)

where
{r_{\rm{s}}}
is the scale radius,
\rm{\rho_{\rm{s}}}
the scale density, and
\alpha,\>\beta,\>\gamma
are the shape parameters of the DM density profile.
\beta
is the outer slope,
\gamma
the inner slope and
\alpha
describes the transition between the inner and outer regions. The values of these shape parameters depend on the stellar-to-halo mass ratio,
X=\log(M_{\star}/M_{\rm{halo}})
(
Di Cintio et al. 2014
,
Tollet et al. 2016
) as:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2.94-\log[(10^{X+2.33})^{-1.08}+(10^{X+2.33})^{2.29}],

(8)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4.23+1.34\;X+0.26\;X^{2},

(9)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-0.06+\log[(10^{X+2.56})^{-0.68}+10^{X+2.56}].

(10)

The parameter
X
determines the shape of the DM halo profile and its associated
{v_{\rm{DM}}(r)}
. We restrict
X
to
[-3.0,-1.2]
to guarantee a solution in the upper branch of the core-cusp vs
X
parameter space (see Fig. 1 of
Di Cintio et al. 2014
).

The density
\rm{\rho_{\rm{s}}}
is set by the halo virial velocity
{v_{\rm{vir}}}
(or equivalently halo mass
M_{\rm{vir}}
), and following
Di Cintio et al. 2014
,
r_{-2}
can be converted to
{r_{\rm{s}}}
as:

r_{-2}=\left(\frac{2-\gamma}{\beta-2}\right)^{1/\alpha}r_{\rm{s}},

(11)

where
{r_{-2}}
is the radius at which the logarithmic DM slope is equal to -2.

The concentration
c_{\rm{vir}}
is defined as:

c_{\rm{vir},-2}=\frac{r_{\rm{vir}}}{r_{-2}},

(12)

where
r_{\rm{vir}}
is the virial radius of the halo. We note that the halo concentration parameter
c_{\rm{vir}}
can be corrected to a DM-only (DMO) halo following e.g. the prescriptions of
Katz et al. 2017
as:

c_{\rm{vir,DMO}}=\frac{c_{\rm{vir},-2}}{1+\cdot e^{0.00001[3.4(X+4.5)]}},

(13)

(2) NFW: N-body DMO simulations predict cuspy DM halo profiles, which can be well described by the NFW profile
(
Navarro et al. 1997
)
. This profile can be considered a special case of the generalised
\alpha-\beta-\gamma
profile with
\alpha,\beta,\gamma=(1,3,1)
. The DM density profile is given by:

\rho_{\rm{NFW}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm{s}}}{\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)\left(1+\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)\right)^{2}}.

(14)

which goes as
\rho\propto r^{-1}
at small radii and
{\rho\propto r^{-3}}
at large radii.

(3) Dekel-Zhao:
Freundlich et al. 2020b
introduce a halo profile with two shape parameters subject to baryonic effects, which is a special case of the generalised
\alpha-\beta-\gamma
profile with
\alpha,\beta,\gamma=(0.5,3.5,\gamma)
. This profile has variable inner slope,
\gamma
, and concentration parameter
c_{\rm{vir}}
, and the profile parameters are correlated with the stellar-to-halo mass ratio, thereby being appropriate for describing both cusped and cored density profiles. The DM density profile is given by:

\rho_{\rm{Dekel-Zhao}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm{s}}}{\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{\gamma}\left(1+\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2(3.5-\gamma)}},

(15)

with
\rho_{\rm{s}}=(1-\gamma/3)\overline{\rho_{\rm{c}}}
, while
\overline{\rho_{\rm{c}}}=c_{\rm vir}^{3}\mu\overline{\rho_{\rm{vir}}}
,
\mu=c_{\rm{vir}}^{\gamma-3}(1+c_{\rm{vir}}^{1/2})^{2(3-\gamma)}
and
\overline{\rho_{\rm{vir}}}=3M_{\rm{vir}}/4\pi r_{\rm{vir}}^{3}
and two shape parameters
\gamma
and the concentration parameter
c_{\rm{vir}}
.
The variable inner slope,
\gamma
, defined in
Freundlich et al. 2020b
as the absolute value of the logarithmic slope at 1% of the virial radius, (denoted as
s_{1}
) depends on the stellar mass- halo mass relation as follows:

s_{1}(x)=\frac{1.25}{1+\left(\frac{X}{1.30\cdot 10^{-3}}\right)^{2.98}}+0.37\log\left(1+\left(\frac{X}{1.30\cdot 10^{-3}}\right)^{2.98}\right)

(16)

with
X=M_{\star}/M_{\rm{vir}}
.
The inner slope corresponds to the NFW slope for
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\rm{vir}})<-4
, to flatter inner density profiles for
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\rm{vir}})
between -3.5 and -2 and to steeper- than-NFW inner density profiles for
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\rm{vir}})>-2
.

(4) Burkert:
Burkert 1995
introduced a halo profile characterised by a cored structure, i.e. with a finite density towards the centre, as opposed to the diverging density seen in the NFW profile. The density profile is given by:

\rho_{\rm{Burkert}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm{s}}}{\left(1+\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)\left(1+\left(\frac{r}{r_{\rm{s}}}\right)^{2}\right)}.

(17)

(5) coreNFW:
Read et al. 2016
studied the evolution of isolated dwarf galaxies using high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations, and showed that repeated bursts of star formation can transform cusps into cores. A general fitting function for the evolved DM profile, based on the NFW profile, was introduced:

M_{\rm{cNFW}}(<r)=M_{\rm{NFW}}(<r)f^{n}(r)

(18)

where

f^{n}(r)=\tanh(r/r_{\rm{c}})^{n}

(19)

This function suppresses the central cusp below a core radius,
r_{\rm{c}}
, defined as
r_{\rm{c}}=\eta R_{1/2}
, with
\eta=1.75
and
R_{1/2}
being the half mass radius.
The parameter
n
controls the shallowness of the core, where
n=0
corresponds to a cusp and
n=1
to a core. This parameter is tied to the star formation time scale
t_{\rm{SF}}
, set to be
\sim 5.8Gyr
for
z\sim 1
SFGs.
The resulting density profile for coreNFW is given by:

\rho_{\rm{cNFW}}=f^{n}(r)\rho_{\rm{NFW}}+\frac{nf^{n-1}(r)(1-f^{2}(r))}{4\pi r^{2}r_{c}}M_{\rm{NFW}}\\

(20)

(6) Einasto: Using high-resolution N-body simulations,
Navarro et al. 2004
demonstrated that the DM halos are better described by the Einasto profile
(
Einasto 1965
)
, which has the following density profile:

\rho_{\rm{Einasto}}(r)=\rho_{\rm{s}}e^{-\frac{2}{\alpha_{\epsilon}}\left(\left(\frac{r}{r_{s}}\right)^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}-1\right)}

(21)

where
\alpha_{\epsilon}
is a shape parameter describing the rate at which the logarithmic slope decreases towards the centre. When
\alpha_{\epsilon}>0
, the profile has a finite density. For example,
Dutton & Macció 2014
have shown that
\alpha_{\epsilon}
is dependent on the halo mass, however, we chose to leave this parameter free in our modelling and impose flat priors such that
\alpha_{\epsilon}\in[0.1,2]
.

(7) Baryons-only: We also tested baryon-only models, performing the RC decomposition by assuming maximal disks, i.e. without any contribution from a DM halo. We thereby set
v_{\rm{DM}}(r)^{2}=0
in equation
1
.

Appendix B
Impact of Different HI Parametrizations

To assess the impact of the HI surface density model used in our disk-halo decomposition (Section
2.2
) on the recovered DM halo parameters, we compare in Figure
21
the inferred inner DM slope
\gamma
obtained with three different assumptions for the HI distribution. Our model (i) assumes a constant HI surface mass density (
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
), yielding
v_{\rm gas}(r)\propto\sqrt{\Sigma_{\rm HI}r}
, and results in a slope
\gamma_{\rm sgas}
. We contrast this with results obtained using the models (ii) and (iii) assuming radially declining profiles from
Martinsson et al. 2016
(
\gamma_{\rm Martinsson}
, left panel) and
Wang et al. 2025
(
\gamma_{\rm Wang}
, right panel), respectively. All decompositions assume the DC14 profile for the DM halo.

We find good agreement within the uncertainties for 90% of the sample, indicating that our conclusions are robust against reasonable variations in the assumed HI profile. The uncertainties on
\gamma
incorporate the full posterior distribution from the MCMC sampling and therefore account for the propagated uncertainty in the gas contribution.

Dickinson et al. 2003

).
For JWST, we used the NIRCAM F090W, F115W, F150W, F200W, F277W, F335M, F356W, F410M, and F444W bands from the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey DR3 (JADES,
Bunker et al. 2023
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023a
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
;
Rieke et al. 2023a
;
Rieke et al. 2023b
;
d’Eugenio et al. 2024
). We also complemented the observations with additional JWST/NIRCam medium bands from the JWST Extragalactic Medium-band Survey (JEMS,
Williams et al. 2023a
;
Williams et al. 2023b
) as provided as part of JADES since its DR2. This includes the F182M, F210M, F430M, 460M, and 480M bands. We did not consider the HST/WFC3 F105W, F125W, F140W, and F160W bands from CANDELS since the images are only available at a coarser pixel scale of either
40\text{\,}\mathrm{mas}
or
60\text{\,}\mathrm{mas}
. In the end, we used five HST/ACS bands in the optical and 14 JWST/NIRCAM bands in the NIR (from roughly
400\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
to
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
) to reconstruct spatially resolved maps of the stellar mass of the galaxies. For the MGE modelling described in section
5.3.1
, we used the JWST NIRCAM/F090W images.

For each galaxy, we extracted
200\times 200
pixels cutouts and variance maps in all aforementioned bands and converted them to the same flux unit. For JWST, variance maps were taken as the square of the error map that includes all sources of uncertainties whereas, for HST, the variance maps were taken as the inverse of the weight maps
(
Casertano et al. 2000
)
and, by construction, do not include Poisson noise. Thus, we added in quadrature the contribution of Poisson noise to the HST variance maps following the prescription given in Sect. 8.1 of
Mercier 2023
.
Then, we convolved each cutout to the spatial resolution of the F480M band
12
12

12

The PSF FWHM was estimated to be around
0.16\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolArcsecond}
. As a comparison, the best resolution is achieved in the F115W band with a PSF FWHM of roughly
0.03\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolArcsecond}
.
using a matching kernel for the image and the square of the kernel for the variance map (see Sect. 8.1 and Appendix D of
Mercier 2023
).
The HST PSFs were produced using the
Photutils

(
Bradley et al. 2024
)
implementation of the ePSF empirical reconstruction method
(
Anderson & King 2000
;
Anderson 2016
)
using a maximum of 30 iterations and a sigma clipping of five. To reconstruct the best-fit ePSF model, we used a set of five nearby stars taken from the MXDF analysis that are located in the vicinity of the MHUDF. For JWST, we created PSF models for the JADES observations using
WebbPSF

(
Perrin et al. 2012
;
Perrin et al. 2014
)
. The same PSFs were also used for our MGE modelling. We did not estimate PSF variations across the field of view since the MHUDF is a relatively small field. After producing all the PSF models, we created for each band the matching kernel to F480M using the
PyPHER
software
(
Boucaud et al. 2016
)
and convolved the images and the variance maps in this band using this kernel.

We used the pixel-per-pixel SED fitting library
pixSED
to produce spatially resolved stellar mass maps from the PSF-matched HST and JWST images of the galaxies. An account of how the library works can be found in Sect. 8 of
Mercier 2023
. Effectively, it acts as a wrapper that takes as input the redshift along with multi-band images and variances maps of a galaxy and produces resolved maps of physical parameters (e.g. stellar mass or SFR) using already existing SED fitting codes as backend. In this analysis, we used the SED fitting code
Cigale

(
Boquien et al. 2019
)
. Each pixel was fitted separately assuming a Chabrier IMF
(
Chabrier 2003
)
,
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
single stellar populations, a delayed exponential star formation history with a final burst/quench episode
(
Ciesla et al. 2018
;
Ciesla et al. 2021
)
, and a
Charlot & Fall 2000
attenuation law. Nebular emission was also included in the fit with standard parameters and we added a systemic error of
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{dex}
in quadrature to account for model and calibration uncertainties. To speed up the fitting process, we masked beforehand pixels that belong to nearby galaxies using the segmentation maps provided as part of JADES, and we kept the background pixels to estimate the uncertainties on the stellar mass at the pixel level when fitting pure background fluctuations.

We show a typical example for galaxy 939 at
z\approx 1.3
in Fig.
22
.
When integrating the stellar mass map within the area defined by the JADES segmentation map, we find a total stellar mass equal to
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=10.01
, similar to what was obtained in
Bacon et al. 2023
with
Magphys
, namely
\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=10.14
. It is worth mentioning that we observe good agreement between the stellar masses obtained from the different SED fitting routines outlined in section
4.3
and in this section.
