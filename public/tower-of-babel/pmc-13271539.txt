Critical appraisal of intravitreal triamcinolone acetonide for serpiginous-like choroiditis in a tuberculosis-endemic setting
Authors: Srinivasan Sanjay
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I would like to congratulate Kawali et al.[1] for their article on the role of intravitreal triamcinolone in serpiginous-like choroiditis (SLC). I have a few concerns which I would like the authors to address. The retrospective, single-center design makes the study inherently vulnerable to selection and documentation bias, which is only briefly acknowledged in the limitations. Of 32 initially identified patients, only 14 (15 eyes) with at least 6 months of follow-up were included in the analysis, yet there is no systematic comparison between included and excluded patients, raising concern about attrition bias and potential overestimation of benefit if early failures or complications were more likely to be lost. SLC is treated as a relatively uniform entity, although morphology (dendritic, placoid, peripheral placoid), bilaterality, and lesion area vary substantially, and the absence of stratified outcomes by phenotype or baseline lesion load limits conclusions about which SLC subtypes may truly benefit from intravitreal triamcinolone acetonide (IVTA).

IVTA is described as “primary” or “adjunctive” therapy, but anti-tubercular treatment (ATT) timing (before, simultaneous with, after IVTA, or not given) and concurrent oral steroid regimens (10–20 mg with taper) are highly heterogeneous, making it difficult to isolate the specific contribution of IVTA to lesion control, recurrence, and paradoxical-worsening. The clinical indications for IVTA (foveal-threatening lesions, systemic contraindications, patient preference) are reasonable but introduce confounding by indication, as eyes with more severe or more central disease may be over-represented, which complicates the interpretation of both efficacy and safety. Only one patient was receiving immuno modulatory therapy (IMT), so the findings cannot be readily generalized to contemporary practice patterns in which long-term IMT is frequently employed in SLC, especially in TB-endemic regions.

Outcomes and Analysis

The chosen primary outcomes (time to clinical and fundus autofluoroscence (FAF)-based lesion resolution, paradoxical-worsening, recurrence, and need for repeat IVTA) are clinically meaningful, but there is no predefined hierarchy or sample size justification, and the study is clearly underpowered to provide robust estimates of recurrence or paradoxical-worsening. Visual acuity is analyzed using a paired t-test, without specifying whether best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was converted to logMAR or presenting confidence intervals; although the reported P value of 0.006 indicates statistical significance, the clinical gain from 20/50 to 20/32 is modest and not explored in relation to foveal involvement or macular edema. Recurrence is reported descriptively (three eyes, 20%; 1/11 on ATT vs 2/4 without ATT), but no formal comparative statistics are performed and the small numbers do not support strong inferences that ATT reduces recurrence in IVTA-treated SLC.

The absence of paradoxical-worsening in 15 eyes is likely over-interpreted, as paradoxical-worsening rates of 12.9–33% have been reported in larger ATT-treated cohorts,[2] and this study is not statistically powered to demonstrate risk reduction, particularly in the absence of a control arm or standardized ATT protocol. The discussion advances mechanistic explanations (such as lower bacillary load and a preferential role of ATT in preventing recurrence rather than halting acute progression) largely on the basis of prior literature; however, the present dataset does not directly test these hypotheses, and suggestions that IVTA monotherapy can “safely” abort lesions without ATT are not fully supported given the two relapses among the three IVTA-only cases. This should be addressed.

The manuscript describes the safety profile as “acceptable,” yet intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation above 21 mmHg occurred in one-third of eyes by one estimate and 45% by another, with a maximum IOP of 45 mmHg; this internal inconsistency, together with the lack of detailed IOP trajectories or characterization of steroid responders, weakens the safety assessment. Posterior subcapsular cataract developed in 38.4% of eyes, with one patient requiring surgery, which represents a substantial complication burden in a relatively young cohort (mean age 34 years) and should be more prominently acknowledged when IVTA is proposed as a “safer” alternative to systemic corticosteroids. Although ATT-related adverse effects are reported (27.2% transient events), the discussion does not systematically balance ocular steroid-related complications against systemic ATT toxicity, limiting the strength of the argument for reduced systemic burden.

The claim that this is the first series evaluating 2 mg IVTA for SLC in a TB-endemic setting appears credible and constitutes the main novelty of the study, but the discussion at times leans toward suggesting practice change (e.g., adopting 2 mg IVTA as a “valuable component” or “primary” therapy) without comparative data against Ozurdex [TM], periocular steroids, or conventional systemic regimens. No formal cost-effectiveness analysis is undertaken despite emphasizing affordability and resource-limited contexts, and the narrative comparison with dexamethasone implants relies on small historical series with differing inclusion criteria and follow-up, which weakens economic and safety conclusions. The authors appropriately call for prospective randomized controlled trials, but the discussion would be strengthened by more concrete proposals, such as standardizing ATT timing, stratifying by TB test status and lesion morphology, and defining paradoxical-worsening and recurrence endpoints a priori to enhance the translational impact of future work.
