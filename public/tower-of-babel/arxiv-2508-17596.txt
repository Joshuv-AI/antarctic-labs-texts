Connected Theorems: A Graph-Based Approach to Evaluating Mathematical Results
Haocheng Ju, Tianyi Xu, Bin Dong,

Abstract
The evaluation of mathematical results plays a central role in assessing researchers’ contributions and shaping the direction of the field. Currently, such evaluations rely primarily on human judgment, whether through journal peer review or committees at research institutions. To complement these traditional processes, we propose a data-driven approach. We construct a hierarchical graph linking theorems, papers, and fields to capture their citation relationships. We then introduce a PageRank-style algorithm to compute influence scores for these entities. Using these scores, we analyze the evolution of field rankings over time and quantify the impact between fields. We hope this framework can contribute to the development of more advanced, quantitative methods for evaluating mathematical research and serve as a complement to expert assessment.

Introduction

Evaluating mathematical results is a critical task, as it influences decisions such as publishing in top journals, recruiting and promoting faculty, assessing grant proposals, and nominating researchers for prestigious prizes. The evaluation of mathematical research is generally guided by two main principles: whether a result supports developments in fields beyond mathematics, and whether it contributes to mathematics itself. In practice, these assessments are conducted by humans through peer review or by panels of distinguished mathematicians.

To complement these traditional evaluation methods and provide a data-driven perspective, we propose a computational approach to quantify the influence of mathematical results from data. We collect published papers that have arXiv preprints with valid HTML versions, which make it possible to extract theorem IDs more conveniently, from over 500 journals and construct a hierarchical three-level graph comprising 2.39M theorems, 141k papers, and 13 fields. Edges are built based on citation relationships at each level. We then introduce a PageRank-style algorithm that propagates influence both within and across levels to compute influence scores for theorems, papers, and fields.

Using these scores, we rank theorems and papers and analyze the evolution of field rankings over time. We observe that DiffGeom, Analysis, PDE, and Physics often rise and fall together under different parameter settings, suggesting they may form a cluster. PDE and Probability exhibit steady growth over time, while Numerical Analysis, Statistics, and Optimization consistently remain among the lowest-ranking fields. We also quantify field-to-field impact from paper-level influences and find that many pairs exert nearly symmetric influence, with clusters emerging such as (Algebra, AlgGeom, DiffGeom, Topology) and (DiffGeom, Analysis, PDE, Physics). At the same time, asymmetries appear: for example, DynSys exerts a stronger influence on PDE and Physics than the reverse, and Numerical Analysis influences Optimization more strongly than the reverse. These asymmetries reflect a directional flow of knowledge, where fields developing general theories tend to influence more application-driven domains.

However, since we rely on the HTML versions of arXiv preprints to extract theorem IDs and build theorem-level citations, our dataset only includes published papers that have an arXiv version with a valid HTML format. Consequently, it represents only a subset of the entire body of published mathematical literature. In addition, because some citation information is missing or inaccurate, our findings may not fully generalize to the whole mathematical literature. We believe that incorporating additional papers and improving the accuracy of citation extraction at both the theorem and paper levels will refine the three-level graph and further enhance the ability of our approach to complement traditional research evaluation methods.

Related Work

2.1
Quantitative evaluation of mathematical research

Citation-based metrics developed for general scientific fields, such as the impact factor for journals and the h-index for authors, can be naively applied to mathematics. However, their use has been criticized in the mathematical context due to their short citation window, limited interpretability, and unstable rankings
1076a2be-10cc-3bdb-8624-1f5569e17b06
;
10.1093/reseval/rvv041
. Mathematics-specific measures include the Mathematical Citation Quotient (MCQ), defined as the total citations from MR-indexed journals to articles published in a given journal during the preceding five years, divided by the number of such articles; and the mean cumulative impact factor, a weighted average of rank-normalized impact factors that yields more stable journal rankings
10.1093/reseval/rvv041
. Beyond journals, researcher-level studies report that mathematicians’ output does not decline rapidly with age and that cross-field collaboration correlates with more successful careers
dubois2014productivity
. Work on prize effects finds that Fields Medalists’ productivity (papers per year, citation counts, and student mentoring) declines relative to close contenders after the award, in part because medalists shift to new research areas
borjas2015prizes
. At the subfield level, analyses using indicators such as the number of Fields Medalists, the share of papers from each subfield in top journals, and their presence in top ranked departments suggest that higher status subfields often have a widely recognized list of central open problems
schlenker2020prestige
.

2.2
Theorem dependency graph

Theorem dependency graphs can be categorized into two types: formal and natural language. A formal theorem dependency graph is a graph where each node represents a formal definition, axiom, theorem, or lemma, and edges are defined by references among these mathematical objects. Such graphs have been constructed for various formal languages, including Lean
bauer2023mlfmf
, Coq
coq-dpdgraph
, Isabelle
wenzel2014isabelle
, and Agda
bauer2023mlfmf
.

Natural language theorem dependency graphs include NaturalProofs
welleck2021naturalproofs
, AutoMathKG
bian2025automathkg
, and TheoremKB
delemazure:hal-02940819
. Theorems in NaturalProofs are sourced from ProofWiki, the Stacks Project, a real analysis textbook, and a number theory textbook. AutoMathKG
bian2025automathkg
constructed a knowledge graph consisting of definitions, theorems, and problems drawn from ProofWiki, textbooks, a small subset of arXiv papers, and TheoremQA
chen2023theoremqa
, and proposed an automatic update method using large language models.

The most closely related work to ours is TheoremKB
delemazure:hal-02940819
, which constructed a theorem dependency graph with over six million nodes extracted from nearly all mathematics papers on arXiv. Their work differs from ours in several respects. First, their dataset includes almost all mathematics papers on arXiv, many of which are not peer reviewed. In contrast, we restrict our attention to published papers and use their arXiv versions, as peer-reviewed papers are generally regarded as higher-quality research outputs. Second, TheoremKB focuses on building a theorem dependency graph, whereas we develop a three-level graph that integrates theorems, papers, and fields, enabling analysis at multiple levels of abstraction. Third, their work concentrates on the technical aspects of graph construction and provides limited analysis, mainly concerning the largest connected component and the most cited results. By comparison, our study not only details the model, algorithms, and data construction process, but also investigates the evolution of field rankings and the field-to-field impact.

•

Paper level: There is an edge from paper

f_{j}

The cross-level edges are defined as follows:

•

For each theorem
t\in\mathcal{T}
, there is a bidirectional edge between
t
and the paper it belongs to.

•

For each paper
p\in\mathcal{P}
, there is a bidirectional edge between
p
and its primary field.

We denote the following neighbor sets:

\begin{array}[]{ll}N_{T}(t)=\{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\mid t^{\prime}\to t\},\quad N_{P}(p)=\{p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}\mid p^{\prime}\to p\},\\
N_{F}(f)=\{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\mid f^{\prime}\to f\}.\end{array}

The weight matrices for each level are defined as follows. For the theorem level:

T(t_{i},t_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&t_{j}\notin N_{T}(t_{i}),\\
0.05&t_{j}\in N_{T}(t_{i})\text{ and }t_{i},t_{j}\text{ are from the same paper,}\\
0.1&t_{j}\in N_{T}(t_{i})\text{ and }t_{i},t_{j}\text{ are from different papers, but share at least one author,}\\
1&t_{j}\in N_{T}(t_{i})\text{ and }t_{i},t_{j}\text{ are from different papers with no shared authors.}\end{array}\right.

For the paper level:

P(p_{i},p_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&p_{j}\notin N_{P}(p_{i}),\\
0.1&p_{j}\in N_{P}(p_{i})\text{ and }p_{i},p_{j}\text{ share at least one author,}\\
1&p_{j}\in N_{P}(p_{i})\text{ and }p_{i},p_{j}\text{ have no shared authors.}\end{array}\right.

For the field level:

F(f_{i},f_{j})=\sum_{p_{1}\in f_{i},p_{2}\in f_{j}}\mathds{1}_{\{P(p_{1},p_{2})>0\}}.

3.2
Computing the influences

Let
u_{T}(t)
,
u_{P}(p)
, and
u_{F}(f)
denote the influence scores of theorem
t
, paper
p
, and field
f
, respectively. Let
\widetilde{T},\widetilde{P},\widetilde{F}
be the column-normalized weight matrices:

\widetilde{T}(t_{i},t_{j})=\frac{T(t_{i},t_{j})}{\sum_{k}T(t_{k},t_{j})},\qquad\widetilde{P}(p_{i},p_{j})=\frac{P(p_{i},p_{j})}{\sum_{k}P(p_{k},p_{j})},\qquad\widetilde{F}(f_{i},f_{j})=\frac{F(f_{i},f_{j})}{\sum_{k}F(f_{k},f_{j})}.

We define the following mappings:

•

\phi_{TP}(t)
: the paper that theorem
t
belongs to;

•

\phi_{PF}(p)
: the primary field of paper
p
;

•

\phi_{PT}(p)=\{t\in\mathcal{T}\mid t\text{ is in }p\}
: the set of theorems in paper
p
;

•

\phi_{FP}(f)=\{p\in\mathcal{P}\mid p\text{ belongs to }f\}
: the set of papers in field
f
.

We aim to propagate influence across the three levels. At the theorem level, each theorem gathers influence from the theorems that cite it and inherits influence from its corresponding paper. At the paper level, each paper gathers influence from citing papers, inherits influence from its field, and incorporates the influence of its most influential theorem. At the field level, each field aggregates influence from citing fields and from its constituent papers whose influence exceeds the average value
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}
. Based on this structure, we propose an iterative update of
u_{T}(t)
,
u_{P}(p)
, and
u_{F}(f)
following the PageRank algorithm:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{T}(t)=&\alpha_{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in N_{T}(t)}\widetilde{T}(t,t^{\prime})u^{(k)}_{T}(t^{\prime})+(1-\alpha_{T})\frac{u^{(k)}_{P}(\phi_{TP}(t))}{|\mathcal{T}|/|\mathcal{P}|}\\
\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{P}(p)=&\alpha_{P}\sum_{p^{\prime}\in N_{P}(p)}\widetilde{P}(p,p^{\prime})u^{(k)}_{P}(p^{\prime})+\beta_{P}\frac{u^{(k)}_{F}(\phi_{PF}(p))}{|\mathcal{P}|/|\mathcal{F}|}\\
&+(1-\alpha_{P}-\beta_{P})\max_{t\in\phi_{PT}(p)}u_{T}^{(k)}(t)\\
\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{F}(f)=&\alpha_{F}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in N_{F}(f)}\widetilde{F}(f,f^{\prime})u^{(k)}_{F}(f^{\prime})+(1-\alpha_{F})\sum_{p\in\phi_{FP}(f)}\left(u_{P}^{(k)}(p)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\right)_{+}\end{array}\right.

where
\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{T}(t),\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{P}(p),\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{F}(f)
are the unnormalized scores at iteration
k+1
, and
(x)_{+}=\max(x,0)
. We then normalize the updated scores to unit
\ell_{1}
norm:

u^{(k+1)}_{T}(t)=\frac{\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{T}(t)}{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{T}(t^{\prime})},u^{(k+1)}_{P}(p)=\frac{\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{P}(p)}{\sum_{p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}}\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{P}(p^{\prime})},u^{(k+1)}_{F}(f)=\frac{\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{F}(f)}{\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\hat{u}^{(k+1)}_{F}(f^{\prime})}.

Here,
u_{T}^{(k+1)}(t)
,
u_{P}^{(k+1)}(p)
, and
u_{F}^{(k+1)}(f)
denote the normalized influence scores at iteration
k+1
, and
\alpha_{T},\alpha_{P},\alpha_{F},\beta_{P}\in(0,1)
with
\alpha_{P}+\beta_{P}<1
. The scores are initialized uniformly as
u^{(0)}_{T}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|},u^{(0)}_{P}(p)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|},u^{(0)}_{F}(f)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}.
The stopping criterion is

\max\{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}|u^{(k+1)}_{T}(t)-u^{(k)}_{T}(t)|,\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}|u^{(k+1)}_{P}(p)-u^{(k)}_{P}(p)|,\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|u^{(k+1)}_{F}(f)-u^{(k)}_{F}(f)|\}<10^{-9}.

4.2
Evaluating the field-to-field impact

We aim to quantify the total influence that field
f
exerts on field
f^{\prime}
through paper-level citations. The underlying idea is that the more influential a citing paper
p^{\prime}
is (as reflected by a large
u_{P}(p^{\prime})
), the greater impact it assigns to the papers it cites. Accordingly, we define the impact of field
f
on field
f^{\prime}
as

I(f,f^{\prime})=\sum_{p\in f,p^{\prime}\in f^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}(p,p^{\prime})u_{P}(p^{\prime}).

The results are shown in Figure
4
, with hyperparameters set to
(\alpha_{T},\alpha_{P},\beta_{P},\alpha_{F})=(0.6,0.6,0.05,0.85)
and data up to December 2023. The heatmap reveals several patterns:

Near-symmetric field pairs.

Many field pairs have nearly symmetric influence, indicating balanced knowledge exchange. Examples include (DiffGeom, Analysis) and (Analysis, DiffGeom), (Numerical Analysis, Physics) and (Physics, Numerical Analysis), as well as (DiffGeom, Topology) and (Topology, DiffGeom).

Clusters of fields.

The heatmap also reveals field clusters in which each pair exhibits strong mutual influence, such as (Algebra, AlgGeom, DiffGeom, Topology) and (DiffGeom, Analysis, PDE). In addition, the (PDE, Physics) pair exhibits consistently high impact scores in both directions, reflecting their close interconnection.

Asymmetric relationships.

Despite overall symmetry, several pairs display imbalance. For example, the impact of Analysis on PDE is 27% higher than the reverse; DynSys on PDE is 70% higher than the reverse; DynSys on Physics is 75% higher than the reverse; and Numerical Analysis on Optimization is 93% higher than the reverse. These asymmetries are consistent with the characteristics of the fields. The stronger influence of Analysis on PDE is natural, since many foundational techniques in PDE originate from Analysis, whereas feedback from PDE to Analysis is comparatively weaker. Likewise, the influence of DynSys on both PDE and Physics reflects the fact that dynamical systems provide conceptual frameworks and tools that are widely applied in these areas, while the reverse influence is weaker. Finally, the stronger impact of Numerical Analysis on Optimization arises because Optimization depends heavily on Numerical Analysis for algorithm design and convergence analysis, while Numerical Analysis encompasses a broader scope beyond Optimization. Overall, the observed asymmetries highlight the directional flow of knowledge across mathematical domains.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a computational framework to quantify the influence of mathematical results, papers, and fields. We collect published papers that have arXiv preprints with valid HTML versions and construct a three-level graph comprising theorems, papers, and fields. Intra-level edges are defined by citation relationships, while inter-level edges capture hierarchical associations: theorems are linked to the papers they appear in, and papers are linked to their corresponding fields. To compute influence scores across all levels, we develop a PageRank-style algorithm that propagates influence both within and across levels. This allows us to assign influence scores to theorems, papers, and fields, and to rank entities accordingly. Using these scores, we examine the evolution of field influence over time and observe that DiffGeom, Analysis, PDE, and Physics often rise and fall together, suggesting that they form a tightly connected cluster. We also find that Numerical Analysis, Optimization, and Statistics consistently remain among the lowest-ranking fields across different hyperparameter settings. Furthermore, when examining field-to-field impact, we find that the resulting influence matrix is nearly symmetric, with identifiable field clusters such as (Algebra, AlgGeom, DiffGeom, Topology) and (DiffGeom, Analysis, PDE, Physics). Nonetheless, some asymmetries emerge. For example, DynSys exert a stronger influence on PDE and Physics than the reverse. These patterns reflect a directional flow of knowledge across mathematical domains: fields that develop general theories tend to exert greater influence on more application-driven fields. We hope this framework can complement traditional methods for evaluating mathematical research in the future.

Limitations

Our work has several limitations:

1.

Since we rely on the HTML versions of arXiv preprints to extract theorem IDs and construct theorem-level citations, our dataset includes only those published papers that have a corresponding arXiv version with a valid HTML format. This covers only a subset of the entire body of published mathematical literature. As a result, our findings may not fully generalize to all published mathematical works. Ideally, one should collect the complete set of papers indexed by MathSciNet and construct a more comprehensive graph.

2.

The citation information is imperfect. For paper-level citations, we rely on the Semantic Scholar Academic Graph
1
1

1

https://api.semanticscholar.org/api-docs/datasets

10.1145/3487553.3527147
, which contains missing references, leading to missing edges in the paper-level citation graph. For theorem-level citations, several issues arise:

(i)

Because theorem-proof associations are identified using rule-based methods, some proofs are not matched to their theorems, resulting in missing citations.

(ii)

Even when proofs are matched, our rule-based method extracts cited theorem IDs from the proof text, which may lead to incorrect citations, for example, when a theorem is mentioned for comparison rather than being used in the proof.

(iii)

We rely on the HTML versions of the arXiv preprints of published papers. However, theorem IDs in these HTML versions may differ from those in the original arXiv submissions or in the final published versions, which can lead to mismatches in citation links.

(iv)

In some cases, a theorem is used in the proof, but the reference is not made explicit within the proof itself. Instead, it may appear in other sections such as the introduction, or the author may cite only the paper without mentioning the specific theorem, resulting in additional missing citations.

The first two issues could be alleviated by applying large language models with strong long-context abilities to extract triplets of the form (theorem ID, cited theorem IDs, bibliography items) directly from the text. The third issue ideally requires access to the full published versions, though this faces technical challenges (e.g., imperfect pdf-to-text conversion) and copyright restrictions. The fourth issue may be addressed by examining the cited paper and using large language models to infer which specific theorem is being used implicitly in the proof.

3.

A small number of papers were misclassified due to issues with the MathSciNet API. In addition, some papers naturally belong to multiple fields, yet we assign each paper only to its primary MSC code. A more refined approach would incorporate secondary MSC codes and connect papers to multiple fields with weighted edges.
