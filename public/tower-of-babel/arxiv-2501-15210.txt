Thermodynamical limits for models of car-sharing systems: the Autolib’ example
Guy Fayolle, Christine Fricker January 2025

Abstract
We analyze mean-field equations obtained for models motivated by a large station-based car-sharing system in France called Autolib’ . The main focus is on a version where users reserve a parking space when they take a car. In a first model, the reservation of parking spaces is effective for all users (see [ 4 ] ) and capacity constraints are ignored. The model is carried out in thermodynamical limit, that is when the number N N of stations and the number of cars M N M_{N} tend to infinity, with U = lim N → ∞ M N / N U=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}M_{N}/N . This limit is described by Kolmogorov’s equations of a two-dimensional time-inhomogeneous Markov process depicting the numbers of reservations and cars at a station. It satisfies a non-linear differential system. We prove analytically that this system has a unique solution, which converges, as t → ∞ t\to\infty , to an equilibrium point exponentially fast. Moreover, this equilibrium point corresponds to the stationary distribution of a two queue tandem (reservations, cars), which is here always ergodic. The intensity factor of each queue has an explicit form obtained from an intrinsic mass conservation relationship. Two related models with capacity constraints are briefly presented in the last section: the simplest one with no reservation leads to a one-dimensional problem; the second one corresponds to our first model with finite total capacity K K .

1
Introduction

The paper investigates asymptotic properties for some time-inhomogeneous Markov processes obtained as mean-field limits. More precisely, in a system with
N
sites and
M_{N}
particles, they describe the limiting behavior of an arbitrary site in the system, when the numbers
N
and
M_{N}
get large together at the same speed, i.e. when
M_{N}/N
tends to a constant
U>0
as
N\to\infty
. The limit thus obtained is generally called the
thermodynamic limit
in statistical physics.

Such a Markov process is described by its transient probability given by the forward Kolmogorov’s equations. We prove the existence and uniqueness of a solution to these non-linear differential equations, as well as an exponential rate of convergence toward the stationary regime. This is a main issue, which appears to be crucial in the literature to obtain further results on the mean-field convergence as, for example, the so-called
refined mean-field
in
[
13
,
1
]
.

This question has also been investigated for finite state reversible dynamics
[
6
,
5
]
, involving the construction of Lyapounov functions by using relative entropy. Our study goes beyond this framework with a simple model emanating from car-sharing systems (CSS) (see
[
4
]
), which can also be represented as systems of interacting particles. Here, stations are viewed as sites and cars become particles. The main feature of these station-based CSS (e.g.
Autolib’
) is that users can reserve a parking slot at their destination.

1.1
A particle model with reservation

Let us describe the model as an homogeneous version of simplified car-sharing dynamics. Consider
M_{N}
particles randomly spread among
N
sites, each site having a capacity
K\leqslant+\infty
, so that
M_{N}\leq KN
when
K
is finite. Particles can be of two types, either a reserved parking space (reservation) or a car. At time
t=0
, the repartition of the particles among the sites is arbitrary.
From site
i
, after a random time exponentially distributed with parameter
\lambda
, a car decides to go to site
j
with probability
1/N
and a reservation appears at site
j
. If the reservation is possible, i.e. when the total number of particles at site
j
is less than
K
, then the car leaves
i
, and the reservation at
j
remains for a travel time exponentially distributed with parameter
\mu
(the case
i=j
is possible, with the same parameters). Otherwise, no car departure occurs.

It should be noted that another dynamics would be to allow the particle of type car to randomly search for an unsaturated site, but we shall not consider this model.

1.2
A queueing description of the model

We will recast the mean field system introduced above in a queueing context, and it will be useful to recall the classical notation, which can be found e.g. in
[
14
]
.

M/M/1/\infty
stands for a single server queue, with Poisson input, exponentially distributed service times, infinite capacity. In this study, the service discipline can be FIFO (first-in-first-out), LIFO (last-in-first-out) or RAND (random order). When the Poisson intensity is time-dependent, we shall write
M(t)/M/1/\infty
; otherwise, we shall write

./M/1/\infty
for an unspecified arrival point process.

Similarly,
M/M/\infty
(resp.
M(t)/M/\infty
) denotes a queue with infinitely many servers, Poisson input with constant (resp. time dependent) intensity and exponentially distributed service times.

In the queueing systems just defined, arrivals and service times are independent.

The model proposed in Section
1.1
can alternatively be viewed as a closed Jackson network (see
[
3
]
), with
M_{N}
customers. This means that each of the
N
sites can be replaced by a tandem with total capacity
K
. The customers in the first infinite-server queue of tandem
i
represent reservations, and customers in the second
M/M/1/\infty
queue represent available cars. The service times of the tandem are, respectively, the travel times of the cars and the inter-arrival times of the users. Both are independent and exponentially distributed, with respective parameters
\mu
and

\lambda
. Note that the routing considered here involves a blocking policy. Indeed, if the destination site (tandem) is saturated, the user request is rejected.

Thermodynamical limit in the unbounded capacity case

In this section and in Section

For any fixed

Then the process

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{N}=\{(r,v)=(r_{i},v_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}\in\mathbb{N}^{2N},\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{i}+v_{i})=M_{N}\},

with a unique invariant probability measure. Its infinitesimal generator is given by

\displaystyle G^{N}f(r,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\big(f(r+e_{j},v-e_{i})-f(r,v)\big)1_{v_{i}>0}

\displaystyle+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}\big(f(r-e_{i},v+e_{i})-f(r,v)\big),\quad\forall(r,v)\in\mathcal{S}^{N},

for any function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
with support on
\mathcal{S}^{N}
.
The so-called

approach is used to investigate the large-scale behavior of the system. We start from the empirical measure given, for any pair

and

\displaystyle\alpha^{N}_{j,k}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}1_{\{(R^{N}_{i}(t),V^{N}_{i}(t))=(j,k)\}}.

With this definition,

represents the proportion of tandems (sites) at time

Let
\vec{\alpha}^{N}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{\alpha^{N}_{j,k}(t)\}_{(j,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}(j+k)\alpha^{N}_{j,k}(t)=\frac{M_{N}}{N},\quad t\geq 0.

In the sequel, we are mainly interested in the limit behavior of the system as
N\to\infty
, assuming

U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{M_{N}}{N}.

Letting

denote the set of probability measures

on

\sum_{j,k\geqslant 0}(j+k)\rho_{j,k}=U,

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha_{j,k}(t)}{dt}+\left[\lambda b(t)+\mu j+\lambda\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{k>0\}}\right]\alpha_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda b(t)\alpha_{j-1,k}(t)\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{j>0\}}+\mu(j+1)\alpha_{j+1,k-1}(t)\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{k>0\}}

\displaystyle b(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}1-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{j,0}(t)

\displaystyle\delta(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\delta_{n}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{\delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{\delta_{n}}(t)

After having shown the existence of a solution to system (

Lemma 3.6

Any

Proof.

Letting

Then, inserting (

\delta=\mu p=\lambda q=\lambda(1-F(1,0)),

To proceed further with the proof of Lemma

The preceding arguments show that we are entitled to analyze the equations (

3.3
Speed of convergence of
\delta(t)

The following proposition provides an estimate of the speed of convergence of
\delta(t)
toward

Proposition 3.7
.

As

,
\delta(t)=\delta+\mathcal{O}(e^{-vt})
, with
v=\min\bigl(\mu,\bigl(\sqrt{\lambda}-\sqrt{\delta}\bigr)^{2}\bigr)
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n},n\geq 0,
be the following sequence of ergodic

M/M/1/\infty
queues with the following parameters for each
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}
:

•

the service rate is equal to

\lambda
;

•

the intensity of the Poisson arrival process has the constant value

\gamma_{n}<\lambda
.

Let
\widetilde{H}_{n}(t)
be the probability for

\mathcal{\widetilde{Q}}_{n}
to be empty at time
t
. It is well-known that
\widetilde{H}_{n}(t)
can be expressed in terms of Bessel integrals (see e.g.
[
16
, p. 23]
). Moreover, setting

c_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigl(\sqrt{\lambda}-\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}\bigr)^{2},

we have, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, the
classical estimate, valid here since
\lambda>\alpha
(see
e.g.
[
2
, p. 107]
),

\widetilde{H}_{n}(t)=1-\dfrac{\gamma_{n}}{\lambda}+\widetilde{h}_{n}t^{-3/2}e^{-c_{n}t}+o\bigl(t^{-3/2}e^{-c_{n}t}\bigr),

(3.21)

where
\widetilde{h}_{n}
is a uniformly bounded constant depending on
the initial conditions.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f(\cdot)
be an integrable function of
t
on

\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that

\displaystyle l=\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)
exists. Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\mu e^{\mu(s-t)}f(s)ds=l.

(3.22)

Proof.

Just splitting the integral into the sum

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}+\int_{T}^{t}
, where, for a given
\varepsilon
,
T
is such that

|l-f(t)|<\varepsilon,\quad\forall t\geq T,

the proof of the lemma is immediate by letting
t\to\infty
, and then
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Let us consider now the following scheme

\begin{cases}\gamma_{0}(t)=\gamma\in]0,\lambda[,\quad\forall t\geq 0,\\[5.69046pt]
\displaystyle\frac{d\gamma_{n+1}(t)}{dt}+\mu\gamma_{n+1}(t)=\lambda\mu(1-H_{\gamma_{n}}(t)),\\[8.5359pt]
\gamma_{n+1}(0)=0,\quad\forall n\geq 0,\\[5.69046pt]
\displaystyle H_{\gamma_{n}}(t)=\psi_{\gamma_{n}}(t)+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}D_{\gamma_{n}}(s,t)H_{\gamma_{n}}(s)ds,\end{cases}

(3.23)

which differs from (
3.14
) only by its first equation, but this is not a minor point!

The last equation of (
3.23
) can be seen as emanating from a
M(t)/M/1
ergodic queue, which we suppose to be empty at time
0

[

\widetilde{H}_{n}(t)\geq 1-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\lambda},\quad\forall\gamma_{n}\leq\lambda.

Using now Lemma
3.8
and (

) with
\delta_{n}

\gamma_{n}

\gamma_{1}(t)\leq\gamma(1-e^{-\mu t})\leq\gamma

, and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{n}(t)=\delta(t),\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\delta_{n}(t)\leqslant\delta(t)\leqslant\gamma_{n}(t),\quad\forall t,n\geqslant 0.

Suppose, and this is true for
n=0
,

\gamma_{n}(t)=\gamma_{n}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-a_{n}t}).

Using the monotonicity properties already mentioned and the estimate (
3.21
), we can write

H_{\gamma_{n}}(t)=1-\dfrac{\gamma_{n}}{\lambda}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\max(e^{-a_{n}t},t^{-3/2}e^{-c_{n}t})\bigr).

Then, the integrated form

\gamma_{n+1}(t)=\lambda\mu\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu(s-t)}(1-H_{\gamma_{n}}(s))ds

yields (some details are omitted),

\displaystyle\gamma_{n+1}(t)=\gamma_{n}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-a_{n+1}t})

where

a_{n+1}=\min{(a_{n},v_{n})},\quad\mathrm{with}\ v_{n}=\min{(\mu,c_{n})}.

Choosing now

concluding the proof of Proposition

Applying now Lemma

\delta(t)=\lambda\mu\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu(s-t)}(1-H_{\delta}(s))ds,

we get

Hence, Proposition

of Theorem

4
Two other related systems

In this section, we present two systems in the same context with capacity constraints. Both can be analyzed via the same methods, and the main results are stated without detailed proofs.

4.1
Model 2: mean-field limit viewed as a single
M(t)/M/1/K
queue with finite capacity
K

This model is the simplest one among the car-sharing class (see e.g.
[
12
]
), with no parking space reservation. It is particularly meaningful for bike-sharing systems, which do not permit reservation.
There are
M_{N}
cars moving among
N
stations of capacity
K
. A car leaves the station at rate
\lambda
and travels for a random time (exponentially distributed with parameter

\mu
). Then, it returns to a uniformly chosen station, if possible, or else sets off on a new journey.

As
N\to\infty
with
\displaystyle U=\lim_{N\to\infty}M_{N}/N
, the usual empirical distribution tends to a deterministic dynamical system
\vec{\alpha}(t)
described by the following equations, for
0\leqslant j\leqslant K,\,t\geq 0
,

\frac{d\alpha_{j}(t)}{dt}+\left[\lambda\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{j>0\}}+\mu(U-a(t))\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{j<K\}}\right]\alpha_{j}(t)=\mu(U-a(t))\alpha_{j-1}(t)+\lambda\alpha_{j+1}(t)\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{j<K\}},

(4.1)

where
\displaystyle a(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{K}j\alpha_{j}(t)
.
The underlying time-inhomogeneous Markov process is a

M(t)/M/1/K
queue with service rate
\lambda
and Poisson arrival rate
\mu(U-a(t))
, where
a(t)
is the mean number of customers in the queue at time
t
.

Setting

\displaystyle Q(z,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{K}\alpha_{j}(t)z^{j}
, for an arbitrary
complex variable
z
and
t\geq 0
, we get the nonlinear functional equation

\frac{\partial Q(z,t)}{\partial t}+\left[\mu(U-a(t))(1-z)+\lambda\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)\right]Q(z,t)=\lambda\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)Q(0,t)+\mu(U-a(t))\alpha_{K}(t)z^{K}(1-z),

(4.2)

with
\displaystyle a(t)=\frac{\partial Q(1,t)}{\partial z}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
t\geq 0
, there exists a unique distribution
\vec{\alpha}(t)
satisfying the nonlinear system (
4.1
). In addition, for any
\lambda,\mu>0
, there is a unique equilibrium point

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\vec{\alpha}(t)=\vec{\pi}>\vec{0}
given by

\pi_{j}=\frac{(1-\beta)\beta^{j}}{1-\beta^{K+1}},\quad 0\leq j\leq K,

where

\beta=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\bigl(U-a(t)\bigr)=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\Bigl(U-\sum_{j=0}^{K}j\pi_{j}\Bigr)

is the unique solution of the fixed point equation

\beta=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\biggl(U-\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}+\frac{(K+1)\beta^{K+1}}{1-\beta^{K+1}}\biggr).

Proof.

System (
4.1
) rewrites in the matrix form

\frac{d\vec{\alpha}(t)}{dt}=\vec{\alpha}(t)M(a(t)),

(4.3)

where
\vec{\alpha}(t)
is a row vector and
M(a(t))
a tridiagonal matrix, denoted in this way to emphasize the dependence on
a(t)
. Now we introduce the iterative vector scheme

\begin{cases}a_{0}(t)=\vec{\alpha}_{0}(t)=0,\quad t\geq 0,\\[2.84544pt]
\displaystyle a_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{K}j\alpha_{n,j}(t),\\[11.38092pt]
\vec{\alpha}_{n+1}(0)=\vec{\alpha}(0),\\[5.69046pt]
\displaystyle\frac{d\vec{\alpha}_{n+1}(t)}{dt}=\vec{\alpha}_{n+1}(t)M(a_{n}(t)),\quad n\geq 0,\end{cases}

(4.4)

which can be shown, using Appendix
A.1
, to be increasing in the sense that
a_{n}(t)<a_{n+1}(t)<U
.

4.2
Model 3: mean-field limit viewed as a system of two queues in tandem with total capacity bounded by
K<\infty

The model is the same as that described in Sections
1.1
and
1.2
, with a finite capacity constraint
K
. The first (resp. second) queue of the tandem is still an
M(t)/M/\infty

(resp.
./M(t)/1
) queue, but the total number of particles remains bounded by
K
. Then, for
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leqslant k+j\leqslant K
, the following mean-field equations hold (see
[
4
]
), for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha_{j,k}(t)}{dt}+\left[\lambda d(t)\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{j+k<K\}}+\mu j+\lambda c(t)\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{k>0\}}\right]\alpha_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda d(t)\alpha_{j-1,k}(t)+\mu(j+1)\alpha_{j+1,k-1}(t)

\displaystyle+\lambda c(t)\alpha_{j,k+1}(t)\hbox{\rm\small 1\kern-3.23753pt\normalsize 1}_{\{j+k<K\}},

(4.5)

where

d(t)=1-\sum_{j=0}^{K}\alpha_{j,0}(t),\quad\mathrm{and}\quad c(t)=1-\sum_{j=0}^{K}\alpha_{j,K-j}(t).

(4.6)

Setting

F(x,y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{0\leqslant j+k\leqslant K}\alpha_{jk}(t)x^{j}y^{k},\quad\forall t\geq 0,

for
x,y
arbitrary complex variables, the following functional equation holds.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F(x,y,t)}{\partial t}+\left[\lambda d(t)(1-x)+\lambda c(t)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{y}\Bigr)\right]F(x,y,t)

\displaystyle\quad=\mu(y-x)\frac{\partial F(x,y,t)}{\partial x}+\lambda c(t)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{y}\Bigr)F(x,0,t)+\lambda d(t)(1-x)F_{K}(x,y,t),

(4.7)

where
F_{K}(x,y,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{K}\alpha_{j,K-j}(t)x^{j}y^{K-j}
,
d(t)=1-F(1,0,t)
and
c(t)=1-F_{K}(1,1,t)
.

The analog of Theorem
2.3
can be stated as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

For each
t\geq 0
there exists a unique distribution
\vec{\alpha}(t)
satisfying the nonlinear system (
4.1
). In addition, for any
\lambda,\mu>0
, there is a unique equilibrium point

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\vec{\alpha}(t)=\vec{\pi}>\vec{0}
given by

\displaystyle\pi_{jk}=\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\rho_{R}^{j}}{j!}{\rho_{V}^{k}},\quad 0\leq j+k\leq K,

(4.8)

where
Z
is a normalizing constant and
(\rho_{R},\rho_{V})
is obtained as the unique solution of the system

\begin{cases}\rho_{R}&=\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\biggl(1-\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{K}\pi_{j0}\biggr),\\[11.38092pt]
U&=\displaystyle\sum_{j,k}(j+k)\pi_{jk}.\end{cases}

(4.9)

As previously announced, we only sketch out a few arguments. To this end, as in Section
3.1
, we write for Model 3 the following subsidiary equations, which emanate directly from (
4.7
).

(1)

Letting
G(x,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}F(x,x,t)
, with
G(1,t)=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G(x,t)}{\partial t}+\lambda\left(d(t)-\frac{c(t)}{x}\right)(1-x)G(x,t)=\lambda c(t)\left(1-\frac{1}{x}\right)F(x,0,t)+\lambda d(t)(1-x)x^{K}(t)(1-c(t)).

(4.10)

Then, dividing (
4.10
) by
1-x
and letting

, we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}G(1,t)=0,

or, equivalently,

\frac{\partial G(1,t)}{\partial x}=U,

(4.11)

which depicts a mass conservation valid for
t\geq 0
.

(2)

For
A(y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}F(1,y,t)
, with
A(1,t)=1
and
A(0,t)=1-d(t)
, equation (
3.3
) still holds, just replacing

\lambda c(t)
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A(y,t)}{\partial t}+\lambda c(t)\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)A(y,t)=\mu(1-y)\frac{\partial F(1,y,t)}{\partial x}+\lambda c(t)\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)A(0,t).

(4.12)

(3)

Analogously, letting
B(x,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}F(x,1,t)
, with
B(1,t)=1
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B(x,t)}{\partial t}+\lambda d(t)(1-x)B(x,t)=\mu(1-x)\frac{\partial B(x,t)}{\partial x}+\lambda d(t)(1-x)F_{K}(x,1,t),

which yields the equivalent of (
3.5
), just replacing

\lambda
by
\lambda c(t)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{dr(t)}{dt}=\lambda c(t)d(t)-\mu r(t),

(4.13)

where
\displaystyle r(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{\partial F(1,1,t)}{\partial x}
stands for the mean number of customers in the first queue of the tandem, at time

Remark 4.3
.

As equation (
3.5
), equation (

) seems quite natural. Indeed, it can be viewed as a fluid equation for the first queue of the tandem, which is a

queue with arrival rate
\lambda c(t)d(t)
and service rate

The following result is the analog of Lemma

.

Lemma 4.4
.

Any

of the tandem driven by equation (

) has the generating function
F(x,y)\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\lim_{t\to\infty}F(x,y,t)

(4.14)

where
\displaystyle Z=\sum_{j+k\leqslant K}\frac{p^{j}}{j!}\,q^{k}
is the normalizing constant and where

are uniquely determined by

with

\displaystyle d=1-Z^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{K}p^{j}/j!

Proof.

Letting
t\to\infty
in equation (

\displaystyle\left(\lambda d(1-x)+\lambda c\biggl(1-\frac{1}{y}\biggr)\right)F(x,y)

From the previous lemma, we get directly (

Appendix A

A.1

The probabilities

following set of

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dfrac{dp_{0}(t)}{dt}=-\beta(t)p_{0}(t)+\mu p_{1}(t),\\[8.5359pt]

(A.1)

which we rewrite in operator form

\dfrac{d\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(t),

(A.2)

where
\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(t)
is a generator (represented by an
infinite matrix) and
\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)
is an infinite row vector
belonging to the Banach space
\ell_{1}
of absolutely summable
sequences. It is known either from a probabilistic point of view
(e.g.
[
10
,
15
]
) or by an analytic argument (e.g.
[
7
]
),
that (
A.2
) has, for all
t\geqslant 0
, a unique solution in

\ell_{1}
. In addition, the generator
\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(t)
has a
continuous spectrum of eigenvalues, located on the negative real line.

Similarly, the distribution function

s_{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}(Z(t)\leqslant n),\quad\forall n\geqslant 0,

satisfies
the system

\begin{cases}\dfrac{ds_{0}(t)}{dt}=-(\beta(t)+\mu)s_{0}(t)+\mu s_{1}(t),\\[8.5359pt]
\dfrac{ds_{n}(t)}{dt}=\beta(t)s_{n-1}(t)-(\beta(t)+\mu)s_{n}(t)+\mu s_{n+1}(t),\quad n\geqslant 1,\end{cases}

(A.3)

which will be written as

\dfrac{d\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(t)\mathbf{L_{\beta}}(t),

(A.4)

where
\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(t)
denotes the row vector

\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(s_{0}(t),s_{1}(t),\ldots).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{P}_{\beta}}(t)
be the
solutions of (
A.2
) corresponding to respective initial
conditions
\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(0)
and

\mathbf{\widetilde{P}_{\beta}}(0)
. The following properties hold:

(i)

If
\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(0)\geqslant\mathbf{\widetilde{P}_{\beta}}(0)\geq 0
, then
\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)\geqslant\mathbf{\widetilde{P}_{\beta}}(t)\geq 0,\ \forall t\geqslant 0.

(ii)

Let
\beta(\cdot),\gamma(\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{+}[0,\infty]
, such
that

\beta(t)\leqslant\gamma(t),\quad\forall t\geqslant 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(0)\geqslant\mathbf{S_{\gamma}}(0).

Then
(stochastic dominance)

\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(t)\geq\mathbf{S_{\gamma}}(t),\ \forall t\geq 0.

In particular, with the notation of equation (
3.12
),

H_{\beta}(t)\geq H_{\gamma}(t),\ \forall t\geq 0
.

(iii)

For any
\beta(\cdot),\gamma(\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{+}[0,\infty]
,
with
\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(0)=\mathbf{P_{\gamma}}(0)
, we have
the
Lipschitz condition

|\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)-\mathbf{P_{\gamma}}(t)|\leqslant K\|{\beta}(\cdot)-{\gamma}(\cdot)\|,

(A.5)

where
K
is an
absolute constant independent of
t
and
|\cdot|
denotes, for an
arbitrary vector
\mathbf{X}
, the usual
\ell_{1}
-norm
|\mathbf{X}|=\sum_{i}|x_{i}|
.

(iv)

The
M(t)/M/1/\infty
queue with arrival
rate
\delta(t)
and the
M/M/1/\infty
queue with arrival
rate
\delta=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\delta(t)
have the same
stationary regime.

Proof.

As for point (i), it is not difficult to see that

\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(t)
and
\mathbf{L_{\beta}}(t)
are positive
operators. For instance, making in
A.3
the change of functions

s_{n}(t)=w_{n}(t)\exp\Bigl(-\mu t-\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds\Bigr),\quad n\geqslant 0,

leads to the system

\begin{cases}\dfrac{dw_{0}(t)}{dt}=\mu w_{1}(t),\\[8.5359pt]
\dfrac{dw_{n}(t)}{dt}=\beta(t)w_{n-1}(t)+\mu w_{n+1}(t),\quad n\geqslant 1,\end{cases}

which has the form

\dfrac{d\mathbf{W_{\beta}}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{W_{\beta}}(t)\mathbf{\widetilde{L}_{\beta}}(t),

where
\mathbf{\widetilde{L}_{\beta}}(t)
has only positive
coefficients. A similar argument can be used for the positivity of

\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(t)
.

The stochastic dominance in (ii) follows now from (i). Indeed, setting

\mathbf{R}(t)=\mathbf{S_{\beta}}(t)-\mathbf{S_{\gamma}}(t),

the row vector
\mathbf{R}(t)
satisfies the non homogeneous differential
equation

\dfrac{d\mathbf{R}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{R}(t)\mathbf{L_{\gamma}}(t)+\bigl(\gamma(t)-\beta(t)\bigr)\mathbf{D}(t),

(A.6)

where
\mathbf{D}(t)=(d_{0}(t),d_{1}(t),\ldots)
, with
d_{n}(t)=[s_{n+1}(t)-s_{n}(t)]_{\beta}\,.
By (i), the vector
\mathbf{D}(t)

has non-negative components and the operator
\mathbf{L_{\gamma}}(t)

is positive, whence it follows that the solutions of (
A.6
) are
also non negative.

To prove (iii), we shall use differential calculus in
Banach spaces. In this framework, most of the classical results for
the real line or the complex plane apply without substantial
modification.

For any
\beta(\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{+}[0,\infty]
, with
0\leqslant\|\beta\|\leqslant B
, take an arbitrary perturbation function
\Delta(\cdot)
, with

\beta(t)+\Delta(t)\in\mathcal{C}^{+}[0,\infty]
. When it exists, the
partial derivative with respect to
\beta(\cdot)
of a differentiable mapping

g:\mathcal{C}^{+}[0,\infty]\times[0,\infty]\rightarrow\ell_{1}

is a
functional (see
[
7
]
) written
\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(t)}{\partial\beta}
. With this notation, one sees easily that
\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)}{\partial\beta}
,
where
P_{\beta}
satisfies (
A.2
), must be a solution of the
following non-homogeneous linear differential equation

\dfrac{d\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}(t)\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(t)+\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(t)\mathbf{M},\quad\mathbf{Q}(0)=0,

(A.7)

where
\mathbf{M}
is a constant infinite matrix given by

\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0&0&\dots\\
0&-1&1&0&\dots\\
0&0&-1&1&\dots\\
0&0&0&-1&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\dots\end{pmatrix}.

Then the solution of (
A.7
) writes in the form

\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{P_{\beta}}(s)\mathbf{M}{\bf\Phi}(t,s)ds,

where
{\bf\Phi}(t,s)
is the so-called fundamental
solution (see
[
7
]
) of the homogeneous equation of type
(
A.2
). The preceding argument yields directly the rough estimate

\sup_{\beta}\|\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}\|=\sup_{\beta}\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\|\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}(t)\|\leqslant K,

(A.8)

where
K
is a bounded constant. Since
\mathbf{Q}_{\beta}
is the
derivative with respect to
\beta(\cdot)
of the function

\mathbf{P_{\beta}}
, defined on the Banach space
\mathcal{C}^{+}[0,\infty]
,
(
A.8
)) gives the Lipschitz condition (
A.5
).

The last property (iv) in the lemma can be viewed as an immediate consequence of the stochastic ordering contained in (i) and (ii), and details will be omitted.
The proof of the Lemma is concluded.
