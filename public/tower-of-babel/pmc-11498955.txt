Using cognitive load theory to tailor clinical reasoning training for preclinical medical students
Authors: Jihyun Si
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive load theory (CLT) provides strategies for tailoring clinical reasoning training for novice medical students. This commentary highlights the importance of adapting training to the level of preclinical medical students and explores these methods from a CLT perspective to help medical educators apply them effectively in classroom settings.Keywords: Clinical reasoning, Preclinical, Cognitive load theory, Instructional strategies

Introduction

Clinical reasoning training for preclinical medical students should be appropriately tailored to their level. However, the current literature does not seem to provide clear picture of how to tailor clinical reasoning training for each level of medical students [1]. Instead, much of the focus is on explaining how learners acquire clinical reasoning skills based on different theories or the content of the clinical reasoning training curricula [1, 2]. Discussions on how to customize this training to suit novice learners appear to be limited. To address this gap, I recently published an article titled, “Fostering Clinical Reasoning Ability in Preclinical Students Through an Illness Script Worksheet Approach in Flipped Learning: A Quasi-Experimental Study” [3]. In this article, I explored instructional strategies for tailoring clinical reasoning training to preclinical students from the perspective of cognitive load theory (CLT). CLT provides various strategies for adapting training methods to suit novices by managing cognitive load [4]. Based on my experience, employing CLT strategies to reduce cognitive overload is essential for successful clinical reasoning training. While my research offered valuable initial insights into CLT strategies for preclinical students, due to the word limits, the strategies were not thoroughly addressed from the perspectives of CLT in my previous research. A more comprehensive discussion of these strategies within the CLT framework would enhance their practical application. Therefore, to assist medical educators in effectively implementing these strategies, this commentary aims to emphasize the importance of tailoring clinical reasoning training to the cognitive level of preclinical medical students and to provide a more in-depth discussion of these methods from a CLT perspective.

Clinical reasoning training for preclinical students and CLT

Clinical reasoning training presents significant challenges for preclinical students, who typically have limited medical knowledge and clinical experience. Engaging in complex, new problem-solving tasks, such as clinical reasoning with clinical cases requires the students to integrate multiple pieces of information simultaneously, generate possible differential diagnoses, and use additional patient data to validate these diagnoses [5]. This high level of complex information processing often leads to substantial cognitive overload for novices, which is detrimental to effective clinical reasoning training [6]. CLT is particularly relevant in this context. It suggests instructional strategies based on human cognitive architecture, including the limitations of working memory and the processes of schemas (organized knowledge structure) construction and automation in long-term memory (LTM) [4]. CLT postulates that when the working memory load exceeds its capacity, successful learning cannot occur. This limitation is particularly pertinent when students deal with novel or unfamiliar information [4]. For preclinical students working with clinical cases, the limited capacity of working memory is a significant bottleneck to learning and CLT suggests instructional strategies that can facilitate successful learning, especially when students are faced with unfamiliar and complex tasks. Therefore, incorporating CLT-based strategies is essential to mitigate cognitive overload. However, most instructional recommendations for preclinical students have rarely considered this limitation. To ensure successful clinical reasoning training for preclinical students, it is crucial to account for CLT in the design of learning and teaching methods.

Effective strategies for preclinical students from the perspective of CLT

In my previous research, various CLT-based instructional strategies were employed to manage three types of cognitive loads when preclinical students engaged with clinical cases in the classroom. In learning situations, three types of cognitive load were involved: intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load. These loads are additive, so they collectively determine the total cognitive load imposed by a task [4]. Therefore, tailoring clinical reasoning training to the learner’s level involves optimizing learning by managing these three types of cognitive load.

To adjusting intrinsic cognitive load, which arises from the inherent complexity of learning tasks [4], simplified versions of clinical cases as learning tasks were presented to students. While authentic cases reflect real-life complexity and are beneficial for clinical reasoning training, they involve a high level of complexity for novices [7, 8]. To manage this, it is crucial to sequence clinical cases from simple to more complex, starting with the most typical or straightforward cases that still cover essential elements of real clinical scenarios [7]. Complexity can then be gradually increased by adding more patient information, unfamiliarity, or ambiguity. In addition, supportive information was provided in advance before each class to further reduce intrinsic cognitive load. This information, which bridges the gap between what learners know and what they need to know for effective clinical reasoning, helps novices perform clinical reasoning and prevent cognitive overload that can occur if students have to learn new concepts while solving complex cases [6]. Furthermore, a just-in-time process worksheet was used to provide step-by-step guidance during clinical reasoning activities in the classroom. This process worksheet, which consisted of four sections, a clinical case vignette and its initial problem representation, three possible differential diagnoses with corresponding illness script constructions, comparison and contrast of the illness scripts with the initial problem representation, and development of a list of tests with their justification in my research, helped manage intrinsic cognitive load by guiding learners with structured phases or expert rules of thumb. When it is presented precisely when needed, students do not have to process procedural information, thereby reducing cognitive overload [9]. Finally, collaborative learning was employed, which allows for the distribution of cognitive load across multiple learners, thereby expanding the effective working memory capacity of the group [4]. By distributing the task information and associated cognitive load across a larger reservoir of cognitive capacity, each member does not need to process all the information alone [4]. Thus, it can reduce intrinsic cognitive load.

To decrease extraneous cognitive load, which arises from ineffective instructional design [4], a worked-out example created by a professor was presented before the course began. This strategy provides a clear demonstration of the clinical reasoning process and can reduce unnecessary cognitive load associated with unguided clinical reasoning process [6, 10]. The “worked-out example-new case” approach [6] is effective in enabling students to focus on essential aspects of clinical reasoning without being overwhelmed by extraneous cognitive load.

To promote germane load, which refers to the cognitive resources used directly for learning [4], a series of 15 varied cases were presented over 15 classes. Increasing case variability helps students develop more sophisticated schemas by allowing them to recognize patterns and distinguish between relevant and irrelevant information [4, 8]. This high variability increases germane load, which positively impacts learning and transfer [4]. Additionally, after collaborative work, students were required to present their clinical decision-making processes to the class. Such group presentations can effectively substitute for self-explanation, a well-known strategy for inducing germane load [8], by encouraging refection on their reasoning processes and more sophisticated explanations as a group. The group presentation can facilitate understanding and knowledge construction in a way similar to self-explanation. Lastly, cognitive feedback was provided at the end of each class through a worked-out example completed by each professor. Cognitive feedback provides information on performance quality, promoting learning or schema construction by encouraging learners to reflect on the quality of their clinical reasoning processes [9]. A worked-out example completed by each professor offered learners sufficient feedback and allowed them to compare their reasoning processes with expert models, thereby promoting reflection and enhancing schema construction [3, 9].

Conclusion

This commentary aims to encourage further discussion on optimizing instructional strategies for preclinical students by integrating CLT into clinical reasoning training. My experience suggests that CLT-based strategies can significantly enhance the effectiveness of clinical reasoning training for novices. Given the limitations of working memory, novices often struggle to master complex, real-life clinical tasks without carefully structured guidance and practice [4]. Therefore, by designing instructional approaches that strategically manage cognitive loads, medical educators can create more supportive learning environments that facilitate the development of clinical reasoning skills among preclinical students.

It is also important to recognize that while sufficient support is essential for novices at the outset, this guidance should be progressively reduced as students’ expertise grows to prevent redundancy and unnecessary cognitive load [7]. As learners advance, the level of support that was once helpful can become counterproductive. Thus, a balance should be struck between providing enough guidance to aid initial learning and scaling back as students’ skills and confidence grow.
