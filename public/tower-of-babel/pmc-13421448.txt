Exploring the effect of higher loading of short carbon fibers on mechanical and physico - mechanical characterization of polyamide66 and polytetrafluroethylene blend composites
Authors: M P Sandeep, B M Rudresh, M K Prasanna Kumar, M R Praveen Kumar, B V Lingesh, N Channa Keshava Naik, Addisu Frinjo Emma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe influence of increased short carbon fiber (SCF) content on the mechanical properties of polyamide-66/polytetrafluoroethylene (PA66/PTFE) blend composites was examined. An 80 wt% PA66 and 20 wt% PTFE blend served as the composite matrix. The potential of SCF as a reinforcement phase with higher loading (10, 20 and 30 wt%) on these blend composites was studied. The hybrid composites were produced via twin-screw extrusion followed by injection molding. Their mechanical performance was assessed using tensile, flexural, and impact tests. The results revealed that increasing the carbon fiber content markedly improved the mechanical properties, although a marginal reduction in impact strength was observed. It was observed that 20 wt% of carbon fiber exhibits the highest strength of 134, 198 and 39 MPa respectively under tension, flexure and impact loading which is 10%, 25%, 41% increase over the neat blend (PA66/PTFE). The composites with 10 wt% SCF responds with lower strength among fiber filled ones. Carbon fiber loadings exceeding 30 wt% led to a deterioration in composite strength. A consistent decline in melt flow index was observed with increasing fiber content, reaching a minimum of 9.9 g/10 min at the highest SCF loading. The heat deflection temperature [HDT] exhibited an increase from 210.8 °C to 256 °C at 0.45 MPa and from 62.98 °C to 233.6 °C at 1.8 MPa across the investigated fiber concentrations. Conversely, the VICAT softening temperature remained unchanged with increased fiber addition. SEM examination of fractured surfaces identified fiber fracture, fiber bending, and matrix deformation as the dominant failure mechanisms.Keywords: PA66/PTFE, Carbon fibers, Mechanical, thermoplastic blend, synergismSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The growing interest in the mechanical behavior of polymers and their composites within materials engineering stems from their lightweight characteristics, superior strength-to-weight efficiency, ease of fabrication, outstanding specific strength and modulus, and enhanced aesthetic properties1. Polymer composites have shown superior mechanical performance over metallic materials in many structural applications. The mechanical strength of these composites is primarily influenced by the incorporation of reinforcement phases, leading to higher specific strength compared to metal-based materials. Polymer performance can be tailored through modification strategies such as polymer blending, reinforcement with fibers or fillers, and copolymerization2. Consequently, systematic investigation of fiber reinforcements and their volume fractions in thermoplastic matrices is essential for the development of high-performance structural composites. High-strength carbon fibers play a decisive role in enhancing composite mechanical properties, while short carbon fibers provide high strength, stiffness, and hardness, coupled with strong interfacial compatibility with thermoplastic polymers3. Glass, carbon, Kevlar, and basalt fibers have been extensively employed in structural applications and are widely recognized as effective reinforcement phases in composite materials. However, excessive fiber loading can adversely influence composite performance. Among these reinforcements, high-strength fibers such as carbon fibers, characterized by a high slenderness ratio, have proven to be particularly effective in enhancing composite strength. Numerous studies have investigated the use of various synthetic fibers and reported their influence on the mechanical performance of thermoplastic composites. Mechakra et al.4 examined the effect of incorporating alfa fibers into polypropylene composites fabricated using twin-screw extrusion. Their findings demonstrated that the inclusion of short alfa fibers led to a significant improvement in the mechanical properties of polypropylene composites. Additionally, the authors reported that flexural strength was predominantly governed by the reinforcement effect of alfa fibers, which was attributed to the strong interfacial interaction between the fibers and the polymer matrix.

Jiang et al.5 investigated the influence of long glass fiber content on the tensile and impact behavior of high-density polyethylene (HDPE) composites, considering the effects of fiber volume fraction, geometry, and composition. Their results indicated that the incorporation of long glass fibers significantly improved the strength, stiffness, and impact resistance of the composites. However, the reinforcement mechanism was found to restrict plastic deformation. In addition, the presence of long fibers enabled more uniform load transfer, thereby enhancing the flexural performance of the composites. Rudresh et al.2 examined the mechanical performance of PA66/PTFE (80/20) blend composites reinforced with 10 wt% short glass fibers (SGF) and 10 wt% short carbon fibers (SCF).

The composites were produced using twin-screw extrusion followed by injection molding. The results indicated that carbon fiber reinforcement provided greater enhancement in mechanical performance compared to glass fibers. However, carbon fiber–reinforced composites exhibited lower impact strength, which was attributed to increased brittleness. An improvement of approximately 45% in both tensile and flexural strength was achieved in carbon fiber–filled composites. Fractographic analysis revealed a higher concentration of stress concentration regions in carbon fiber–reinforced systems, leading to a reduction in composite toughness. Lingesh et al.6 critically examined the influence of 10 wt% short carbon fibers (SCF) and 10 wt% short glass fibers (SGF) on the mechanical properties of polyamide 66/polypropylene (PA66/PP) (80/20) blend composites. The composites were fabricated using twin-screw extrusion followed by injection molding. Their study demonstrated that fiber reinforcement significantly improved the mechanical performance of PA66/PP-based composites. Glass fiber–reinforced composites exhibited more ductile behavior, whereas carbon fiber–reinforced systems showed comparatively brittle characteristics. The enhanced mechanical strength was attributed to improved interfacial bonding between the fibers and the polymer matrix. However, a slight reduction in impact strength was observed in both composites, primarily due to increased brittleness. Ziaee et al.7 investigated the influence of reinforcement type and its concentration on the mechanical performance of PPS/SCF, PPS/SGF, and HDPE/SGF thermoplastic composites. Their study evaluated the effects of fiber reinforcement on tensile, flexural, and impact properties. The results indicated that while certain mechanical properties improved with fiber addition, excessive fiber content led to a decline in performance. A reduction in ductility and interfacial bonding strength was also observed at higher reinforcement levels. The authors emphasized that the optimal fiber content is dependent on both the matrix material and the type of reinforcement. Based on experimental findings, the optimal fiber concentration for tensile performance was reported to be approximately 68–69% for CF/PPS composites and 63–64% for GF/PPS systems. Jun Kwon et al.8 examined the influence of thermoplastic matrix content on the mechanical performance of fiber-reinforced thermoplastic composites. Their findings highlighted that fiber–matrix compatibility is strongly governed by the type and composition of the base thermoplastic, with brittle reinforcements such as carbon fibers exhibiting superior interfacial adhesion compared to other fibers. Chuanbao and Sanjiu Ying9 investigated the tensile behavior of polypropylene composites reinforced with short carbon fibers (SCF) at concentrations of 5, 15, and 25 wt%. The composites were fabricated using twin-screw extrusion followed by injection molding. Their results demonstrated that the incorporation of 25 wt% SCF led to nearly a twofold increase in tensile strength and approximately a threefold enhancement in tensile modulus compared to neat polypropylene. However, elongation at break decreased with increasing fiber content, with a reduction in ductility of nearly 50% observed at higher SCF loadings. Fractographic analysis revealed relatively smooth fracture surfaces with uniformly dispersed carbon fibers within the polypropylene matrix. Additionally, the influence of basalt fiber reinforcement on the mechanical properties of polypropylene and polycarbonate composites was reported in the literature10. These composites were manufactured using compression molding, and the results indicated substantial improvements in tensile and impact properties, particularly for polypropylene-based systems. The use of a compatibilizer enhanced the fiber–matrix interfacial bonding, with mechanical performance improving progressively up to a basalt fiber content of 40 wt%.

Wei et al.11 investigated the influence of acid and alkaline surface treatments applied to glass and basalt fibers on the mechanical performance of thermoplastic composites. Their study demonstrated that acid treatment was more effective in enhancing the mechanical properties of the composites compared to alkaline treatment. In a related study, the effect of incorporating sugarcane bagasse fibers on the mechanical behavior of polypropylene composites was reported12. To improve interfacial adhesion with the polymer matrix, the bagasse fibers were subjected to alkaline treatment prior to composite fabrication. Fiber content was varied from 0 to 30 wt%, and the results indicated a progressive improvement in tensile and flexural properties up to the maximum fiber loading. Additionally, a notable increase in hardness was observed with increasing fiber content. The findings highlighted the effectiveness of fiber–matrix interfacial bonding mechanisms in enhancing the mechanical performance of natural fiber–reinforced thermoplastic composites.

El-Shekeil et al.13 examined the mechanical performance of thermoplastic polyurethane composites reinforced with kenaf fibers. The composites were produced using a melt-mixing process followed by compression molding, with kenaf fiber contents of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 wt%. Their results showed that tensile and flexural strengths improved with increasing fiber content up to 30 wt%, beyond which a reduction in impact strength was observed due to excessive fiber loading. Additionally, composite hardness increased progressively as a function of fiber concentration. Zo et al.14 proposed and investigated polyamide-6/polyurethane blend composites reinforced with long carbon fibers, fabricated through a melt-mixing technique. Their study demonstrated that the incorporation of carbon fibers significantly enhanced the tensile, flexural, and thermal properties of the blend composites, highlighting the effectiveness of carbon fibers as a reinforcement in improving overall composite performance. The influence of carbon fiber reinforcement on the mechanical and thermal performance of polyphenylene sulfide (PPS) composites has been investigated and reported in the literature15. These composites were fabricated using compression molding techniques. The study demonstrated that the incorporation of carbon fiber layers significantly enhanced fracture resistance, tensile modulus, flexural modulus, and thermal stability of PPS composites. The results also confirmed strong compatibility between carbon fibers and the thermoplastic PPS matrix. The hybrid reinforcement effect on the mechanical behavior of polyamide 66/polypropylene (PA66/PP) blend composites has been examined by other researchers16. Their work focused on the combined influence of glass–carbon and glass–basalt fiber systems. The findings revealed that glass–carbon hybrid composites exhibited superior load-bearing capability during mechanical testing compared to glass–basalt hybrids. Improved interfacial compatibility between glass–carbon fibers and the PA66/PP blend resulted in enhanced flexural performance, which was attributed to stronger fiber–matrix bonding arising from the hybrid reinforcement effect. Ashori et al.17 explored the effects of bagasse, glass, and cellulosic fibers on the thermal, moisture, and mechanical properties of polypropylene composites fabricated using a melt-mixing process. Their study indicated that corn stalk fiber alone did not significantly improve tensile properties; however, the hybrid incorporation of bagasse and glass fibers resulted in substantial improvements in tensile, flexural, and impact strengths. The formation of an interconnected fiber network contributed to improved composite morphology and suitability for practical applications. Moisture absorption increased due to the presence of hybrid fibers, although only limited enhancement in impact strength was observed. Toro et al.18 reported on the processing and characterization of hybrid matrix composites reinforced with carbon fibers using polyether-ether-ketone (PEEK) and polyether-imide (PEI) matrices. The composites were fabricated via hot pressing using a multilayer architecture and evaluated through uniaxial tensile and low-velocity impact testing. The PEEK/CF and PEI/CF composites showed distinct behaviors, with the PEI/CF system exhibiting a 48% improvement in strain to failure when fibers were oriented at 45°. In contrast, carbon fiber reinforcement in PEEK composites increased crystallinity, which led to a reduction in both tensile and impact strength.

Kenari et al.19 investigated the influence of short glass fiber reinforcement on the mechanical performance of polypropylene composites. Their findings demonstrated that the inclusion of glass fibers significantly enhanced the load-bearing capacity of the composites while also improving resistance to thermal degradation. However, the presence of glass fibers reduced composite ductility, and excessive fiber content led to increased brittleness and a corresponding decrease in elongation at break. The effects of various fiber reinforcements on the mechanical, thermal, and morphological characteristics of PA66/PPS blend-based composites have been reported in the literature16,20. These studies showed that the incorporation of glass, carbon, and other fibers resulted in substantial improvements in mechanical properties, while also contributing to better blend morphology. Enhanced thermal stability was observed in fiber-reinforced blends, which was attributed to the combined influence of blend composition and fiber reinforcement. In addition, the influence of blend composition in PA66/PTFE and PA6/PTFE systems on thermal, mechanical, and rheological behavior has been examined21,22. The studies highlighted the role of moisture absorption on blend properties, revealing that controlled moisture uptake led to improvements in tensile strength. Furthermore, the effect of carbon fiber reinforcement on the thermal, moisture-related, and mechanical behavior of PTT/ABS composites has been reported23. The authors noted that while carbon fiber addition increased composite brittleness, it also contributed to higher hardness, enhanced thermal stability, and increased moisture absorption due to the reinforcement effect.

Carbon fiber reinforcement has been widely recognized for its strong influence on the mechanical, thermal, and physico-mechanical behaviour of thermoplastic composites. However, excessive carbon fiber incorporation can alter composite structure and performance. Polyamide 66 (PA66) is a high-performance engineering thermoplastic, while polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) is valued for its suitability in structural applications. Mechanical components severely undergo simultaneously both mechanical and tribological failures. It is not possible for the homopolymer to uphold both mechanical and tribological loading simultaneously. Therefore, polymer modification is required. Polymers can be modified by reinforcing fibres and fillers, co-polymerization and Polymer blending. Polymer blending is one of most economically and structurally strong method to modify the polymer composites. PA66 is high strength and polar polymer whereas PTFE is an excellent wear resistance and non – polar polymer. Further, high carbon fibres can exhibit high strength when they are reinforced with this thermoplastic blend. In light of existing research, the present work aims to systematically investigate the mechanical and related physico-mechanical characteristics of higher loading of short carbon fibres on PA66/PTFE blend composites.

Processing and fabrication of hybrid composites

For the fabrication of SCF filled composites, the raw materials: Polyamide 66 (PA66), Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) and short carbon fiber (SCF) (Tables 1 and 2)—were dried in an oven at about 80 °C for 48 h to remove moisture and to reduce plasticization effects. The PA66 and PTFE were first mixed using a mixer. The fabrication process was carried out in two stages. In the first stage, the PA66/PTFE blend was extruded using a twin-screw co-rotating Barbender extruder (Make: CMEI, Model: 16CME, SPL, chamber size: 70 cm³), as depicted in Fig. 1. The process of extrusion uses 5 heating zones to ensure uniform heating inside the extruder barrel. The temperatures were maintained at 220 °C (Zone 1), 235 °C (Zone 2), 240 °C (Zone 3), 265 °C (Zone 4), and 270 °C (Zone 5), while 220 °C die temperature was set. The screw was operated at a constant speed of 100 RPM, which corresponded to a material feed rate of 5 kg/h. The extruded filaments were rapidly cooled in a cold-water bath and subsequently pelletized using a pellet cutter. The resulting PA66/PTFE-blend pellets were again dried prior to blending with the fibres in a mixer for subsequent processing.

Processing and fabrication of hybrid composites: a) Twin screw extruder and.

In the subsequent stage, the PA66/PTFE blend pellets were combined with short fibers in accordance with the specified formulation. The mixture was melted and compounded by passing it through different heating zones of the extruder barrel. The extruded composite strands were cooled in a cold-water bath and subsequently converted into pellets. All test specimens were then fabricated using the pelletized polyblend composite produced from the co-rotating extruder by injection molding. The injection-molding machine was operated with barrel temperatures set to 265 °C in Zone 1 and 290 °C in Zone 2, while the temperature of 65 °C was maintained at the mold (Fig. 1(b)). An injection pressure in the range of 700–800 bar was employed, along with a screw speed of 10–15 rpm. The injection, cooling, and ejection times were fixed at 10 s, 35 s, and 2 s, respectively. All specimens molded in accordance with ASTM standards for mechanical testing were visually examined, and samples exhibiting the defects were excluded from further evaluation.

b) Injection moulding machine (GLS Polymers Ltd., Bangalore, India).

Testing of Hybrid Composites

The SCF filled composites were evaluated for tensile, impact, & flexural properties in accordance with ASTM standards. Tensile testing was performed as per ASTM D638 using a universal testing machine –UTM (JJ Lloyd, London, UK; 1–20 kN capacity) at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min, as illustrated in figure 2. Flexural properties were determined as per ASTM D790 using a UTM at a crosshead speed of 1.33 mm/min. Impact strength was assessed in accordance with ASTM D256. All mechanical tests were carried out at ambient conditions. Five specimens were tested and the average value was considered for data representation. The dimensions of the ASTM-standard specimens used for composite testing are shown in figures 2(a–c).

Results and discussion

The influence of increased short carbon fiber content on the characterization of PA66/PTFE blend composites was investigated. The composite behavior was examined using various experimental techniques, and the observed responses are interpreted and discussed in the subsequent sections.

Physico-mechanical characterization of PA66/PTFE blend composites: effect of higher loading of SCF

The influence of increased short carbon fiber content on the hardness and density of PA66/PTFE-based composites has been depicted in Tables 3 and 4. Hardness and density measurements were conducted in accordance with ISO 2039-2 and ISO 1183 standards, respectively. The Rockwell hardness was examined using M and R Scale. M Scale uses the major load of 100 kg with 6.35 mm ball indenter whereas R scale uses 60 kg major load with 12.7 mm ball indenter. The hardness of these composites depends on the area of indentation as well as type of indentor. The carbon fiber filled composites hardness on M and R scale was proportionately enhances due to increase in loading of carbon fibers. The hardness of PA66/PTFE blend exhibits the hardness of 82 and 120 respectively on M and R scale. The appreciable hardness of these composites was attributed to the presence of PA66 in the Blend. PA66 is basically recognized for its higher hardness, rigidity and crystalline nature2,6. PA66 is a semi crystalline thermoplastic exhibiting the superior and compact molecular structure. This rigid arrangement of molecular chains transforms to higher surface hardness, stiffness and structural rigidity. Further, PA66 has high density hydrogen bonds which lead to crate strong intermolecular forces which increase the resistance to deformation and hardness14. PTFE has poor hardness because of its molecular structure. The long chain of carbon atoms shielded by fluorine atoms results in weak intermolecular forces allowing the polymer chains to slide easily []. But when this soft PTFE filler is added to PA66, it reduces the overall surface hardness. Therefore, the hardness of neat Blend (PA66/PTFE) is less compared to carbon filled ones. But the modification to improve the hardness of these PA66/PTFE composites is by reinforcing the carbon fillers. It was observed that the incorporation of short carbon fibers enhanced the hardness of the composites on both M and R scales. However, increasing the fiber content beyond a certain level resulted in only marginal additional improvement. At a lower fiber loading of 10 wt% (C1), hardness values of 82 and 122 were recorded on the M and R scales, respectively. With higher fiber loading, these values increased slightly to 86 and 124. The C3 composite containing 30 wt% short carbon fibers exhibited modest improvements of 3.33% and 1.65% compared to the blend and C1 composites, respectively. The initial enhancement in hardness at lower fiber content can be attributed to the high tensile modulus and stiffness of the carbon fibers. When short carbon fibers are incorporated into the relatively soft polymer blend, effective interfacial interaction and their uniform dispersion enable the fibers to carry a substantial share of the applied load, thereby restricting surface deformation of the matrix. As a result, an increase in hardness is observed for the C1 composites. Further, reinforcing carbon fibers increases the hardness of composites. But higher loading of these fibers (30 wt%), could effectively resist the applied load. Carbon fibers exhibit high tensile strength and elastic modulus, and the incorporation of 30 wt% short carbon fibers into the polymer blend increases the volume fraction of rigid reinforcement within the softer matrix. This enhancement effectively improves resistance to surface deformation and indentation2,22. The C3 composites are identified as optimally reinforced systems, wherein strong interfacial bonding among the composite constituents facilitates efficient stress transfer under applied loading conditions15,20. In the reinforced composites, the applied load is primarily borne by the reinforcing phase rather than being directly transferred to the softer polymer matrix, leading to an increase in hardness due to effective fiber load-bearing. At higher carbon fiber contents, the mobility of the thermoplastic molecular chains is significantly restricted, resulting in increased stiffness and hardness of the composite material20. Moreover, the elevated fiber loading in C3 composites promotes enhanced crystallization of the PA66/PTFE blend, contributing to the development of a harder and denser surface. Among all the composites evaluated, the C3 composition exhibits the highest hardness values, irrespective of the measurement scale employed21.

The void content observed in the neat blend is attributed to the presence of high-density PTFE, which, when combined with polyamide 66, can induce localized stress concentrations that favor void formation. The incorporation of carbon fibers into the PA66/PTFE blend leads to an increase in composite density. Additionally, the void fraction data are presented to elucidate the influence of reinforcement on the physical properties of the composites22. The density of neat blend is 1.2615 g/cc. The density of the composites increased with the incorporation of short carbon fibers. Although higher fiber loadings can sometimes lead to a reduction in density due to increased porosity and inadequate interfacial bonding, the present study demonstrates a consistent increase in density with increasing reinforcement content20,21. C1 composites allows for good dispersion and better interfacial adhesion, minimizing void defects and hence increased the density. The incorporation of fibers results in a denser, stronger, and more rigid polymer matrix, thereby increasing the overall mass per unit volume of composites14. The relatively higher void fraction is observed in C1 composites can be attributed to the entrapment of air bubbles, which are readily introduced during the melt mixing and processing stages. Further, at lower filler concentrations, the viscosity of the polymer melt may be lower, which can allow more air to be trapped and dispersed during the melt-mixing (extrusion) process. Lower filler loading sometimes results in a less efficient, lower-pressure molding process that fails to fully force air out, leading to higher void formation. The difference in properties between PA66 and PTFE can create micro-gaps and less uniform packing2. During processing, any residual moisture or other volatiles can vaporize and become entrapped. This may lead to the formation of voids which results in higher void fraction21,22. But lesser void fraction for C2 composites is totally attributed to better packing and increased effective viscosity. Additionally, if the processing includes higher injection pressure, the higher loading can actually aid in more efficient evacuation of gases, as the filler helps fill in spaces and provides a matrix for holding the compound together. But at 30 wt%, the composites viscosity increases significantly due to high fiber loading, which might lead to slight increase in void content at higher than 30% levels, causing a reversal in density trends. This effect is mitigated by the strong interfacial interaction between the carbon fibers and the polymer blend matrix. At higher short carbon fiber loadings, a reduced void fraction is observed due to the diminished presence of non-resin regions. The effective fiber–matrix interaction further minimizes stress concentration sites through a synergistic reinforcement effect21. Therefore, at 10 wt% SCF loading (C1), the lower packing density allows more voids, whereas at 20 wt%, the higher fiber density combined with potential optimizations in melt processing leads to a more compact, lower-void structure16,22. This is the reason for the higher load lower void fraction for C1 and C2 composites respectively.

Mechanical characterization of PA66/PTFE blend composites: higher loading of SCF

Tensile behavior of PA66/PTFE composites: effect of higher loading of SCF

The influence of increased short carbon fiber content on the tensile behavior of PA66/PTFE blend composites is illustrated in Fig. 3a and b. Tensile properties were evaluated in accordance with ASTM D638 at room temperature. Figure 3a depicts the variation in tensile strength with increasing SCF loading, revealing a significant enhancement in tensile strength as the fiber content increases. An increase in tensile strength was observed for the C1 (10 wt% SCF) and C2 (20 wt% SCF) composites, whereas a contrasting trend was noted for the C3 composite containing 30 wt% SCF. A reduction in tensile strength was evident at the highest fiber loading level. The neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibited a tensile strength of 58 MPa, which can be attributed to the presence of 80 wt% PA66 and 20 wt% PTFE in the blend.

The tensile behavior of SCF reinforced PA66/PTFE based composites: a Tensile strength and b Tensile modulus.

Although PA66 contributes significantly to strength due to its polar nature and strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding2, PTFE is a comparatively soft and ductile polymer with lower intrinsic tensile strength. The incorporation of PTFE into PA66 leads to a reduction in tensile strength of the blend, primarily due to weak interfacial adhesion, the low inherent strength of PTFE, and alterations in the crystallinity of PA66. Furthermore, while PA66 exhibits strong polarity and hydrogen bonding between molecular chains, PTFE is non-polar in nature, characterized by weak intermolecular forces and low surface energy, which adversely affects stress transfer within the blend system21.

Blending PA66 with PTFE can result in weak interaction within the composite. Under applied stress, these interfaces are prone to crack initiation and propagation, leading to failure at lower loads compared to neat PA6622. The incorporation of PTFE reduces the overall load-bearing capacity of the system, as PTFE fibrils are ineffective in transferring tensile stress, thereby imposing a higher stress demand on the PA66 matrix. In addition, the presence of PTFE alters the crystallization behavior of PA66 during processing. Acting as heterogeneous nucleation sites, PTFE particles may accelerate crystallization but promote the formation of finer and more irregular crystalline structures, which can adversely affect tensile strength. Furthermore, the inclusion of PTFE introduces localized stress concentration zones within the PA66 matrix. These regions serve as preferential sites for microcrack initiation and growth under tensile deformation, ultimately resulting in premature failure of the blend composites2,21,22.

The effect of introducing 10 wt% SCF (C1) into the blend exhibits the tensile strength of 121 N/mm2 which is 108.62% increase over neat blend. Further loading of SCF (20 wt%) (C2) further increases the tensile strength to 134 N/mm2 which is 131% increase over matrix phase. But the drop in strength under tension was identified under the presence of 30 wt% SCF in the blend (C3). Tensile strength rises at 10 and 20 weight% short carbon fiber (SCF) in the PA66/PTFE blend because excellent fibers and PA66/PTFE matrix interaction and efficient reinforcing mechanisms that allowed the high-strength fibers to support the load5,6. Additionally, by serving as strengthening agents, the carbon fibers improve the load-bearing capacity at these stress levels. Carbon fibers are incredibly strong and stiff. The tensile strength is significantly increased by their uniform distribution for C1 and C2 composites due to presence of 10 and 20 weight% SCF which allows the matrix to effectively relocation stress to the fibers7,8. At these relatively low weight percentages (10 and 20 wt%), the fibers are uniformly embedded throughout the thermoplastic matrix, avoiding the problems with fiber aggregation and orientation that occur at much higher loadings. The high aspect ratio (length-to-diameter) of short carbon fibers increases the PA66/PTFE Blend strength. The fibers act as stiff reinforcements by limiting the mobility of thermoplastic PA66/PTFE polymer chains, increasing the structural strength and stiffness of the composite15. The 10 to 20% SCF range provides the ideal balance for reinforcing the PA66/PTFE blend and optimizing stress allocation efficiency through strong fiber dispersion before reaching high loadings that could cause structural issues19. In contrast, the C3 composites exhibited a reduction in tensile strength, indicating degradation of matrix integrity rather than further reinforcement. The incorporation of 30 wt% short carbon fibers appears to represent a critical threshold beyond which the detrimental effects outweigh the reinforcing benefits. At such high fiber contents, uniform dispersal within the thermoplastic matrix becomes increasingly difficult, leading to fiber agglomeration. These agglomerated regions promote the formation of voids and localized stress concentration sites, which act as initiation points for premature failure under tensile loading18. As the fiber volume fraction increases, the total surface area requiring wetting by the polymer matrix also increases. High shearing occurs during processing of high-loading substances. Fibers break into shorter lengths during processing due to increased viscosity caused by 30 wt% SCF concentration, which impairs their capacity to serve as reinforcements17. Fiber breakage in a PA66/PTFE blend at a 30 wt% PTFE concentration during processing occurs primarily because high PTFE concentrations act as a processing detrimental filler, reducing the ductility and cohesion of the polyamide matrix. At this high loading, PTFE particles cause massive stress concentration points during melt spinning or drawing, leading to premature fracture, a situation compounded by the poor interfacial compatibility between PA66 and PTFE. Increased porosity inside the composite results from higher fiber loading, which further reduces the strength because it is more difficult to remove air during manufacture8. Excessive reinforcing reduces the PA66/PTFE blend ductility, increasing the composite vulnerability to brittle fracture as opposed to ductile deformation. Tensile strength decreases as carbon fiber loading increases because the material fails due to premature crack propagation24,25. Among the composites examined, C2 composites exhibit the better tensile strength.

A similar trend was observed in the ductility behavior of the composites, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a). The neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibits superior ductility owing to the presence of a ductile phase. However, the incorporation of short carbon fibers into the blend leads to a progressive reduction in ductility. At higher fiber loadings, fiber agglomeration becomes more pronounced, resulting in localized stress concentration zones that adversely affect the ductile response of the composites. These act as initiation sites for microcracks, leading to premature failure rather than plastic deformation21. The bond between the thermoplastic matrix and the carbon fibers can be weak due to their differing polarities. This weak interaction allows the fiber to pull out easily under tension, reducing the energy absorbed and decreasing the strain at break. At higher short carbon fiber loadings, inadequate fiber bundling are commonly observed26. These agglomerations create discontinuities and micro voids in the polymer matrix, which drastically reduces the materials ability to undergo plastic strain27. A sudden drop in strain at break often occurs at a specific percentage, where the composite moves from a ductile failure mode to a brittle failure mode. Among the composites evaluated, the C3 composition exhibits the lowest ductility compared to the neat PA66/PTFE blend.

Furthermore, an increase in short carbon fiber content leads to a significant enhancement in the tensile modulus of PA66/PTFE composites, as shown in Fig. 3(b). The maximum tensile modulus is found in the C3 composites. This is mostly because the carbon fibers and thermoplastic blends work well together. The carbon fibers also have a very high degree of rigidity. The composites overall stiffness is immediately increased by the incorporation of this stiffer constituent. More stress transfer at the fiber–matrix zone is made possible by the addition of carbon fibers15. The fibers efficiently support the majority of the imposed tensile load which raises the modulus overall. The movement of polymer chains within the matrix is restricted by short carbon fibers. Controlling the molecular movement of thermoplastics makes the composite less flexible and more rigid, which raises the modulus18,24,25.

The morphology of the fractured surfaces during tensile test of all the carbon filled composites is studied using SEM images. Figure 4a–d illustrates the surface morphology of the fractured composites examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure 4a shows the SEM micrograph of the neat PA66/PTFE blend after tensile fracture. The fracture surface exhibits characteristics of a mildly ductile failure, with the presence of fine voids distributed across the surface. The morphology indicates that the matrix experienced significant straining rather than abrupt fracture. Protruding PTFE microfibrils are visible on the fractured surface, suggesting ductile deformation of the PTFE phase. Although PA66 acts as a relatively rigid and mechanically robust matrix, agglomeration of PTFE is evident due to differences in density between the two polymers. The fracture surface appears non-uniform and textured, reflecting the rigid nature of PA66. Additionally, shallow and deep grooves formed as a result of plastic deformation are observed, indicating severe matrix deformation under tensile loading.

SEM analysis reveals distinct fracture mechanisms for the composites under tensile loading (Fig. 4). For the C1 composites (Fig. 4b), fiber fracture, delamination, and fiber pull-out are observed. Fractured fiber debris and impressions of fiber pull-out are visible on the surface, accompanied by severe matrix deformation and a noticeable number of voids. Both fibers and the matrix appear to have undergone significant strain during fracture. The C2 composites (Fig. 4c) exhibit minimal void formation and an improved compatibility due to the higher fiber content8,15. This enhanced interaction reduces strain at fracture, as reflected in the relatively uniform and less deformed matrix. The fracture surface morphology indicates better stress transfer and superior structural integrity, correlating with the higher strength of these composites. In contrast, the C3 composites (Fig. 4d) display fiber agglomeration, misalignment, and disintegration. The structure appears loosely packed, and the high fiber volume fraction leads to extensive fiber pull-out during loading. Non-resin regions and fiber misalignment, creates localized stress concentration zones. Peeling and overlapping of fibers further compromise the structural integrity, resulting in reduced mechanical performance. Overall, among all composites studied, the C2 composites demonstrate the most robust fracture surface, with minimal voids, indicating superior structural integrity and resistance under tensile loading.

3.2.2 Impact of Higher Short Carbon Fiber Content on the Flexural Performance of PA66/PTFE Composites

The flexural behavior of PA66/PTFE composites reinforced with increasing short carbon fiber (SCF) content at room temperature is presented in Fig. 5a and b. The addition of SCF significantly enhances the flexural strength of the composites. An increase in fiber loading leads to improved bending performance; however, a reduction in strength is observed when the SCF content reaches 30 wt%. The neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibits the lowest flexural strength, whereas the C2 composite demonstrates the highest. These results indicate that effective fiber–matrix interaction and the aspect ratio (L/D) of the carbon fibers are critical factors in maintaining the structural integrity of the composites under flexural loading.

Figure 5(a) and (b) illustrates the flexural behavior of SCF-reinforced PA66/PTFE blend composites. The neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibited a flexural strength of 75.06 MPa. Incorporation of 10 wt% SCF (C1) increased the flexural strength to 154 MPa, representing a 105% improvement over the unfilled blend. Further addition of SCF to 20 wt% (C2) enhanced the strength to 198 MPa, corresponding to a 164% increase. However, at 30 wt% SCF (C3), a slight reduction in flexural strength to 182.8 MPa was observed. Among the composites studied, the neat blend demonstrated the lowest strength, while the C2 composite exhibited the highest flexural performance. The reduction in flexural strength of the neat PA66/PTFE blend relative to pure PA66 is attributed to differences in polymer polarity. PA66 is a polar polymer with strong intermolecular interactions, whereas PTFE is non-polar with weak intermolecular forces. Blending these polymers results in limited compatibility between the phases, producing weaker interfacial regions and micro voids, which adversely affect flexural strength2,21.

The flexural strength of the fiber-reinforced composites (C1, C2, and C3) is significantly higher than that of the neat PA66/PTFE blend. The incorporation of carbon fibers into the blend enhances bending performance by providing effective reinforcement. Carbon fibers interact favorably with the thermoplastic matrix, altering the composite’s structural morphology and improving mechanical performance. Addition of 10 and 20 wt% short carbon fibers (C1 and C2) increases the flexural strength of the PA66/PTFE blend, primarily due to increased interaction bonding, improved resistance to crack propagation, and efficient stress allocation from the matrix to the high-strength fibers13. At lower to moderate fiber loadings (10–20 wt%), the fibers are well-dispersed throughout the matrix, optimizing their reinforcing efficiency. During flexural loading, the carbon fibers carry a substantial portion of the tensile and compressive stresses. The fibers’ adequate length and uniform distribution allow significant improvements in both flexural strength and modulus14. Overall, the strong and stiff short carbon fibers reinforce the polymer matrix, markedly enhancing the composite’s bending performance. Strong bonding is made possible by the excellent contact between SCF and the thermoplastic blend, which controls the premature fiber pull-out24. The fibers can withstand greater loads because of the enhanced adhesion between the matrix and the fibers, which keep them entrenched. In these semi-crystalline thermoplastics, short carbon fibers can serve as nucleating agents, encouraging crystallization and enhancing matrix mechanical stability25.

However, at a higher SCF loading of 30 wt% (C3), the flexural strength of the PA66/PTFE composites decreases compared to the C2 composites. This reduction is attributed to the high volume fraction of fibers, which adversely affects the structural integrity of the PA66/PTFE/SCF system. Specifically, fiber aggregation and poor interfacial bonding at 30 wt% SCF result in stress concentration sites, leading to diminished flexural performance. In C3 composites, the short carbon fibers tend to agglomerate at the localized area together. These bundles act as stress concentration points and weak spots reducing the overall structural integrity. High filler loading increases the viscosity of PA66/PTFE blend leading to poor fiber impregnation by the polymer24. The reduced flexural strength of the C3 composites is also associated with increased void content and porosity, which compromise the structural integrity of the material. Under bending loads, fiber pull-out occurs, and as the fiber content increases, the composite becomes more brittle. Flexural strength, reflects the material’s ability to withstand both tensile and compressive stresses during bending, decreases when the matrix becomes too rigid to effectively support the fibers6,7. At high fiber loadings, the composites are susceptible to premature failure through inter-laminar shear. Consequently, while C3 composites still exhibit improved strength relative to the neat blend, their flexural performance is lower than that of the C2 composites. Among the composites studied, the C2 system demonstrates the highest flexural strength due to the optimal presence of short carbon fibers. Figure 5(a) shows the effect of higher SCF loading on the percentage deflection of PA66/PTFE composites. A clear decrease in ductility is observed with increasing fiber content, reflecting a transition in phase morphology from ductile to brittle behavior. At high SCF loadings, poor fiber impregnation within the polymer matrix and increased the number of non-resin zones create additional stress concentration sites, further reducing composite ductility. Among all composites, the neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibits the highest deflection due to the presence of the ductile phase.

The flexural modulus of the blend (PA66/PTFE) increases due to the reinforcement contribution of SCFs (Fig. 5(b)). The tensile modulus of carbon fibers is remarkably higher than that of thermoplastic matrices. The stiffness to withstand bending is increased by increasing the fiber volume fraction. The reinforced SCFs increase the composite overall resistance to deformation by limiting the motion of the thermoplastic molecular chains under stress. When a load is applied, the stiff SCF receives the stress from the weaker, more flexible thermoplastic matrix. A higher composite modulus is the result of a more efficient transfer, which is ensured by a higher fiber density. SCF can increase the material crystallinity by acting as a nucleating agent in thermoplastic matrices. A higher flexural modulus is typically associated with increased crystallinity8,9.

The SEM images of the fractured surfaces under bending loads are presented in Fig. 6(a–d). Figure 6(a) shows the fracture surface of the neat PA66/PTFE blend, which appears relatively smooth, indicating the ductile nature of the material. Small agglomerates of PTFE debris are observed on the surface, and no significant textured features are present, suggesting that the matrix primarily accommodated the deformation. Figure 6(b) illustrates the C1 composite (10 wt% SCF) under bending. The surface shows good fiber–matrix compatibility, with the matrix in a strained state and small voids resulting from reinforcement. The fibers are effectively embedded, and matrix deformation dominates over fiber damage25. In the C2 composites (Fig. 6c), fiber fracture becomes more pronounced than matrix deformation, and impressions of fiber pull-out are evident. Although some fiber overlap and localized stress concentration zones are observed, the fibers remain well-aligned and maintain strong interaction with the thermoplastic matrix. PTFE fibrils are also visible on the fractured surface, indicating localized matrix deformation under flexure. Overall, the C2 composites exhibit a predominantly brittle fracture, but maintain good structural integrity. For the C3 composites (Fig. 6d), higher SCF content results in fiber agglomeration and localization. Poor fiber–matrix interaction, an increased number of stress initiation sites, and extensive fiber breakage are evident. The surface is characterized by brittle fracture, with cracks propagating through the matrix network. The combined effect of broken fibers and matrix straining reduces the composite’s structural performance. Among the SEM images analyzed, the C2 composites demonstrate the best fiber–matrix interaction and structural integrity, correlating with their superior flexural strength.

The impact behavior of PA66/PTFE blend composites: effect of higher SCF incorporation

The influence of increasing short carbon fiber (SCF) content on the impact strength of PA66/PTFE blend composites was evaluated under both notched and un-notched conditions, and the results are presented in Fig. 7. It is observed that the composites exhibit higher impact strength in the un-notched condition compared to the notched condition. For the neat PA66/PTFE blend, the impact strength under notched conditions was 11.07 kJ/m². The incorporation of SCF slightly modified the impact behaviour, however, even at higher fiber loadings, the improvement under notched conditions was minimal. Specifically, the notched impact strengths of C1, C2, and C3 composites were 8, 9, and 9.4 kJ/m², respectively, indicating only marginal gains. The limited enhancement is attributed to the inherently brittle nature of the composites and the reduction in ductility with increasing fiber content. Relative to the neat blend, the notched impact strengths decreased by 27.73%, 18.69%, and 15.08% for C1, C2, and C3 composites, respectively. In contrast, under un-notched conditions, the effect of SCF reinforcement on impact strength was substantial. The neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibited an un-notched impact strength of 14 kJ/m². Incorporation of 10 wt% SCF (C1) increased the impact strength to 32 kJ/m², corresponding to a 128.57% improvement. Further increases in SCF content to 20 wt% (C2) and 30 wt% (C3) led to impact strengths of 39 kJ/m² and 34 kJ/m², representing enhancements of 178.57% and 142.85%, respectively, over the neat blend. These results indicate that while high SCF loadings can reduce ductility and limit improvements under notched conditions, the reinforcement effect is highly effective in un-notched scenarios, where the composites can absorb and dissipate energy more efficiently.

Impact performance of SCF-reinforced PA66/PTFE blend composites.

The improved energy dissipation mechanisms resulting from the insertion of short carbon fibers (SCF) increased the un-notched impact strength. The absence of an existing sharp stress concentrator in un-notched settings enables the composite to use fiber-related toughening mechanisms prior to catastrophic breakdown5,6. Short carbon fibers prevent cracks from spreading. The fibers bridge the microcrack created by an impact in the matrix, which makes it harder for the crack to open and spread7. Higher overall energy absorption is a result of the energy absorbed during the debonding and subsequent pull-out of the short fibers from the matrix, particularly in cases when interfacial adhesion is moderate. The failure of composites is controlled by the increased loading of fibers, which disperses the impact energy over a larger volume of the matrix instead of allowing it to concentrate at one location. Fibers can increase the matrix’s capacity to absorb energy before breaking by promoting plastic deformation8,15. As a result, compared to the plain blend, the composites C1 and C2 show superior impact strength.

The reduction in impact strength of the C3 composites relative to C2 is attributed to poor fiber–matrix adhesion, which arises from the higher fiber volume fraction. By decreasing the composite’s capacity for plastic deformation, the incorporation of 30% SCF makes it extremely stiff. Instead of yielding, the composites typically fail by brittle fracture. When a 30% SCF composite is struck, it breaks down quickly with little energy absorption and frequently exhibits a steep decline in load-bearing capability following the peak19. It gets harder to evenly distribute the fibers with greater loadings. Short carbon fibers tend to act as stress concentrators and have a tendency to agglomerate at higher loadings. Excessive fiber content can also lead to insufficient matrix wetting, resulting in weak fiber–matrix interfacial bonding18. Premature fiber debonding and pull-out under impact are encouraged by weak interfaces. However, adding 30% concentration frequently results in internal voids or interfacial flaws that serve as fracture starting sites. Furthermore, at high SCF loadings, the volume of polymer available to undergo plastic deformation in the stress zone is restricted. During the injection molding of 30 wt% SCF composites, high shear stresses may fracture the carbon fibers, leading to a reduction in effective fiber length24,25. Shorter fibers are less capable of bridging cracks and dissipating energy through pull-out mechanisms, resulting in a diminished contribution to toughness. Consequently, the increased fiber volume fraction in the C3 composites leads to a reduction in impact strength.

Common fracture mechanisms observed in the fiber-reinforced composites include fiber breakage and fiber pull-out. The neat PA66/PTFE blend (Fig. 8a) exhibits a predominantly ductile fracture, characterized by smooth surfaces resulting from impact loading. Minimal void formation is observed, indicating low stress concentration, and only slight plastic deformation of the matrix is evident compared to the overall impact fracture. The textured surface with waviness is observed due to impact. The poor deformability of the blend is observed as result of less absorption of energy due to absence of reinforced phase. But the not much deference in the molecular structure is observed for the blend25. The incorporation of PTFE into PA66 introduces a slightly ductile phase, as observed in the SEM images. The PA66 matrix provides rigidity, resulting in a relatively resistant surface, and the impact-induced fracture remains moderate rather than severe.

The SEM images of SCF-reinforced PA66/PTFE composites fractured under impact loading are shown in Fig. 8(b–d). In the C1 composites (10 wt% SCF, Fig. 8b), the fibers are well-dispersed within the matrix, demonstrating good fiber–matrix interaction. However, due to the relatively low fiber content, the matrix undergoes significant deformation, and several stress concentration zones are evident. Fiber fracture is commonly observed on the surface, along with localized dislocations caused by the impact load. For the C2 composites (20 wt% SCF, Fig. 8c), the SEM images reveal a stronger fiber–matrix interaction. Impressions from fiber pull-out are more pronounced, and while some fibers are misaligned, broken fibers are embedded in the matrix due to effective resin impregnation. Minimal void formation is observed, and the surface exhibits increased brittleness compared to C1, reflecting enhanced load transfer from the matrix to the fibers. Melt flow index (MFI), heat deflection temperature (HDT), VICAT softening point (VST), moisture absorption of PA66/PTFE blend composites: effect of higher SCF loading

The melt flow index (MFI) of PA66/PTFE-based composites was evaluated at 275 °C with a 2.16 kg load, following the ISO 1133 method. The neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibited an MFI of 26.27 g/10 min. Incorporation of 10 wt% SCF reduced the MFI to 15.65 g/10 min, representing a 40.42% decrease compared to the neat blend. Further increasing the SCF content led to a continuous decline in MFI, reaching 13.89 g/10 min at 30 wt% SCF. The reduction in MFI is attributed to the increase in melt viscosity caused by the addition of carbon fibers, which impedes polymer flow23. The high aspect ratio of SCFs forms a physical network within the matrix, interacting with polymer chains and restricting their mobility. Strong adhesion and good interfacial interaction between the PA66/PTFE matrix and fiber surfaces further hinder chain movement, increasing the melt resistance10. As SCF content rises, fibers occupy a larger volume fraction, increasing intermolecular friction both between fibers and between fibers and polymer chains23. The rigid carbon fibers act as obstacles to the smooth slippage of molten polymer chains, while their high surface area promotes entanglement with the matrix, forming a physical network that limits polymer mobility and elevates viscosity21. Among all the composites examined, the neat PA66/PTFE blend exhibits the highest MFI due to the absence of fiber-induced restrictions.

The heat deflection temperature (HDT) of SCF filled PA66/PTFE based composites was studied at a load of 0.45 MPa and 1.80 MPa as per ISO75. The HDT of PA66/PTFE based composites increases under both the loading conditions due to addition of SCF. The heat deflection temperature (HDT) of the PA66/PTFE blend and SCF-reinforced composites was evaluated under both low (0.45 MPa) and high (1.8 MPa) loads. At 0.45 MPa, the neat blend exhibited an HDT of 210.8 °C. Incorporation of SCF into the blend increased the HDT, with C1, C2, and C3 composites showing enhancements of 15.18%, 18.9%, and 21.5%, respectively. Similar trends were observed at the higher load of 1.8 MPa. The neat blend showed an HDT of 62.8 °C under this load, whereas the HDT increased to 211.6 °C, 212.9 °C, and 233.6 °C for composites containing 10, 20, and 30 wt% SCF, respectively. The improvement in HDT is primarily attributed to the high stiffness and load-bearing capability of the carbon fibers, which act as a rigid skeleton that restricts the mobility of polymer chains at elevated temperatures14,16,23. SCFs reinforce the thermoplastic matrix, enhancing both tensile and flexural properties, and providing resistance against deflection under load. Since HDT corresponds to the temperature at which a specimen deflects by 0.25 mm under a specified load, the increased rigidity of SCF-reinforced composites allows them to resist bending at higher temperatures. Additionally, the high thermal conductivity of SCFs promotes uniform heat distribution, minimizing localized softening and premature deformation in the polymer matrix28,29. The intrinsic thermal resistance of carbon fibers further preserves structural integrity under heat. Among the composites studied, C3 exhibits the highest HDT, demonstrating the combined effect of increased fiber content and improved matrix–fiber interaction on thermal performance.

The VICAT softening temperature (VST) of SCF filled PA66/PTFE blend composites was studied using ISO 306. The VST test measures the temperature at which 1 mm2 needle penetrates 1 mm into the specimen. If the polymer matrix at the surface softens, the needle will penetrate regardless of the reinforcing fibers located slightly deeper in the bulk material. But the VST of these composites don’t have much variation over the blend16,23. Average 255 0 C of VST is exhibited by all composites when these SCF filled composites were under test. Because the VICAT softening temperature (VST) test primarily measures the surface softening of the polymer matrix rather than the overall structural rigidity of the bulk composite, it frequently stays relatively constant or only slightly improves after the addition of SCF into a thermoplastic blend16. SCFs reinforcement impact solely improves the structural integrity of the PA66/PTFE composite but it has no inherent influence on the polymer matrix crystalline, amorphous, or chemical softening point10.The thermal characteristics of SCF-filled composites may be diminished by poor adhesion which is caused by increased SCF loading. Therefore, the VST remains almost unaltered under elevated SCF loading.

The effect of moisture absorption of hard carbon fibers filled PA66/PTFE blend composites according to ASTM D6980. When short carbon fibers (SCFs) are added to the PA66/PTFE blend, the moisture percentage absorption rises mainly because the fibers increase the interfacial surface area and open up additional channels for water infiltration. One crucial weak point is the link between SCFs and the PA66/PTFE matrix. More higher-rate absorption channels are created when moisture permeates the composite and moves along the fiber-matrix contact16,21. During the molding process, the fiber addition may cause additional micro-voids, pores, and flaws in the matrix. Water fills these spaces, which function as reservoirs. A mismatch in volumetric expansion at the fiber-matrix interface results from the PA66/PTFE matrix absorption of water and subsequent swelling28,30,31. Increased water penetration is made possible by the microcracks created by this mismatch. The higher volume of fibers embedded in the matrix causes the composites to absorb more percentage of water.
