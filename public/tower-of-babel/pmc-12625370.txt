DPEP2 suppresses metastasis via NF-κB-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition and M2 polarization in p53-loss non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yongting Liu, Yulong Zhao, Ziyu Dai, Jingyi Wang, Jingyi Sun, Shasha Yang, Ruixin Ma, Qiong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe TP53 gene (encoding the human p53 protein) is mutated in about 50% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Loss of p53 is closely related to tumor metastasis and immune regulation, contributing to malignant progression and poor prognosis. However, therapeutic strategies for p53-loss NSCLC are still relatively limited. We found that DPEP2 is a diagnostic marker and predicts better outcome and prognosis in NSCLC with mutant TP53, but not with wild-type TP53. Loss of p53 induced DPEP2 downregulation at cellular and tissue levels. Functionally, DPEP2 inhibited the invasion and migration abilities, and decreased F-actin fibers. Mechanistically, DPEP2 impeded epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via the MAP3K7-mediated NF-κB signaling. Immunological analysis suggested that low level of DPEP2 and high level of M1 macrophages led to poor prognosis, whereas patients with high level of DPEP2 and low level of M2 had the best survival. Subsequently, a co-culture system was established to verify the effect of DPEP2 on M2 macrophages. The results showed that DPEP2 inhibited M2 macrophages polarization in a NF-κB-dependent manner. In addition, we found that DPEP2 antagonized the tumor-promoting effects of M2 macrophages by depleting LTD4. In vivo experiments indicated that high DPEP2 levels resulted in fewer lung metastases and less M2 macrophage infiltration. Overall, our studies suggest that DPEP2 might be a promising therapeutic target for highly metastatic p53-loss NSCLC by inhibiting tumor metastasis and M2 macrophage capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04054-9.Keywords: P53-loss NSCLC, Tumor metastasis, Tumor-associated macrophages, DPEP2, NF-κB pathway

Introduction

Lung cancer has been recognized as the primary cause of cancer-related deaths, of which non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is daunting in its high incidence and mortality [1]. The genome instability or mutation is a fundamental incentive of tumorigenesis and pathogenesis. Mutations in the tumor suppressor gene TP53 (encoding the human p53 protein) occur in approximately 50% of patients with NSCLC [2]. Loss of p53 is a key inflection point in NSCLC progression and has a close connection with tumor metastasis and immune remodeling [3, 4]. Consequently, it is imperative to alleviate the malignant characteristics of p53-loss NSCLC.

The interaction between tumor cells and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) has been considered a hallmark of cancer [5]. The tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are generally recognized as major inflammatory components in TIME and crosstalk with tumor cells by secreting multiple cytokines such as leukotriene D4 (LTD4) [6]. Extensive research has demonstrated that TP53 status significantly influences the differentiation and infiltration of TAMs. For instance, compared with wild-type p53, patients with mutant p53 have higher macrophage infiltration in ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma and the opposite result is observed in gastric adenocarcinoma [7]. Moreover, upregulated p53 inhibits the polarization of M2 macrophages in melanoma [8], while the deficiency of p53 drives M2 polarization in liver cancer [9]. However, the macrophage landscape and regulatory mechanisms in p53-loss NSCLC have not been fully elucidated.

In this study, we found that loss of p53 induced DPEP2 downregulation, contributing to more malignant progression and worse prognosis in p53-loss NSCLC. Previous studies have believed that DPEP2 is widely distributed in the lung tissues. The most common substrate of DPEP2 is LTD4, and activation of LTD4-CysLTRs signaling is conducive to malignant progression of lung cancer [10, 11]. Here, we found that DPEP2 might act as a bridge to participate in tumor-macrophage communication. In short, the dual inhibitory effects of DPEP2 on tumor cells and macrophages make it a promising therapeutic target for p53-loss NSCLC.

Methods and materials

Data acquisition

NSCLC transcriptomic profiles and matched clinical information were obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GEO database. Protein expression data in different normal tissues were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). For the gene expression among different NSCLC cell lines, we applied the “Expression 21Q3 Public” file downloaded from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) website.

Immunological analysis

The ESTIMATEScore [12] and TIDE [13] were used to evaluate immune infiltration and immunotherapy effects. The expression and distribution of DPEP2 in TIME at the single-cell level were analysed according to TISCH2 [14]. The association between gene expression and immune infiltration was analyzed by TIMER2.0 [15]. The immune cell subsets were determined by Cibersort using the LM22 gene set.

Cell lines and culture

All cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 or DMEM medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The specialized culture medium for THP-1 cells was supplemented with 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma). TP53 knockout A549 cells were purchased from Ubigene Biosciences. All cells were cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and were free of mycoplasma contamination. For NF-κB signaling activation, cells were incubated with 1µM NF-κB activator 1 (E1351, Selleck) for 24 h. For NF-κB signaling blocking, cells were incubated with 10µM JSH-23 (S7351, Selleck) for 24 h. The U-73,122 (1µM, S8011, Selleck) was used to inhibit phospholipase C activity. The FIPI (20µM, S7321, Selleck) was used to inhibit phospholipase D activity.

Induction of human macrophages

THP-1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate-12 acetate (PMA) (HY-18739, MCE) for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 for another 24 h of culture. Under the microscope, THP-1 cells were observed to change from suspended to adherent, and the cell shape became irregular, indicating the activation of M0 macrophages. To induce M2 polarization, M0 macrophages were stimulated with 20 ng/mL IL4 (HY-P70445, MCE) and IL13 (HY-P70568, MCE) for 24 h, as previously described [16, 17].

Lentivirus infection assay

Lentivirus infection was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (GeneChem, China). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured to 20–30% confluence. The cells were then incubated in 10%FBS medium containing lentivirus (MOI = 30) and HiTransG (infection enhancer) for 16 h. The multiplicities of infection (MOI) = (lentivirus titer × lentivirus volume)/cell number. 48 h after infection (70–80% confluence), the cells were selected in medium containing puromycin (2 µg/ml) (Solarbio, China).

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in the absence of RNase. Quantification of RNA concentration using NanoDrop™ One. 2 µg RNA was transferred to cDNA using Evo M-MLV RT Kit (AG11728, Accurate Biotechnology). The 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (B21703, Bimake) was used to perform amplification on QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR software. 2 −ΔΔCt value was calculated to perform quantitative analysis. Primer sequences were shown in Table S1.

Western blot and Co-IP assay

For Western blot assays, samples were lysed for protein extraction using RIPA buffer (WB3100, NCM) containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P002, NCM). After ultrasonic treatment, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with loading buffer and denatured by heating at 95 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA).

For Co-IP assays, the protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MedChemExpress) were pre-washed 3 times with PBST and incubated with the indicated antibodies or control IgG overnight at 4 °C. The cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were incubated rotationally with magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. The immunoprecipitates were washed with PBST several times and subjected to western blot. The antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

Migration and invasion assay

The wound-healing assay was used to verify migration ability. Cells were seeded in ibidi™ Culture-Inserts. After the cells had adhered to the wall, the inserts were removed. The serum-free medium was replaced, and the culture was maintained for 48 h. For the invasion assay, cells were seeded in transwell chambers. Briefly, 200 µL of serum-free medium was added to the upper chamber, and the lower chamber was completely immersed in 500 µL of medium with 20% FBS. Three fields were randomly selected and evaluated under a microscope to determine the migration and invasion abilities. Leukotriene D4 (LTD4) was purchased from MedChemExpress. Tumor cells were stimulated with 100 nM LTD4 to assess invasion and migration.

Phalloidin staining

To visualize cytoskeleton, F-actin was labeled with phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 488 (C2201S, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded into 24-well glass slides and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Next, cells were washed with 0.1% Triton X-100 three times for 5 min each to increase membrane permeability. The working solution was prepared by diluting phalloidin with PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100. Next, the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. DAPI was used to label cell nuclei.

Flow cytometry

To assess macrophage polarization, the cells were collected and dispersed into single-cell suspensions. The CD16/32 antibody (Cat #: BE0307, BioXCell) was used to block nonspecific Fc receptors at 4℃ for 30 min. Next, cells were incubated with FITC anti-human CD86 Antibody (Cat #: 374203, Biolegend) for 30 min in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were fixed and permeabilized, followed by incubation with PE anti-human CD206 (MMR) Antibody (Cat #: 321105, Biolegend) at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were finally analyzed using BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and the FlowJo.

ELISA

The level of universal leukotriene D4 (LTD4) was analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Sango, China). For cell samples, the cell culture medium was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove impurities and cell debris, and the supernatant was collected for detection. For tissue samples, tissues of equal mass were taken, minced, and resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors. The tissue homogenate was fully ground or ultrasonically disrupted, then centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5–10 min, and the supernatant was collected for detection. Standards and samples were added to a microplate that had been pre-coated with LTD4 antigen. After incubation, the anti-LTD4 antibody with HRP-conjugated streptavidin and the TMB were added to produce a blue color, which was then converted to yellow under the action of acid. Finally, the absorbance value was measured at 450 nm. The concentration of LTD4 was calculated by a standard curve.

Histological staining

Samples were fixed in 10% formalin overnight, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned at 4 μm-thickness. The samples were subjected to deparaffinisation and dehydration. After antigen retrieval with 15 min of heat treatment in 10 mM sodium citrate and 30% H2O2, sections were blocked in 10% goat serum for 30 min at 37 °C. The diluted primary antibodies CD206 (1:100, AG2660, Beyotime) were incubated overnight at 4 °C followed by incubation with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and then sections were mounted on glass slides and subjected to microscopy.

Mouse model

BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (China). All animal studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Appl. No. XY20240513003). Both the experimental group and the control group used n = 4 BALB/c nude mice. For metastasis assay, 1 × 106 H1299 cells (transfected with lentivirus) were injected through the tail vein of 4-week-old nude mice. On day 21, the mice were euthanized and the lungs were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The tumor nodules on the surface were visually counted under a dissection microscope.

Statistical analysis

The data presented in this study are shown as the mean ± standard deviation using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Comparisons between two independent samples and multiple samples were employed using two-tailed Student’s t tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), respectively. Spearman correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation between two genes. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Integrated genomic analysis of TP53-related effectors in metastatic NSCLC

Given that p53 is one of the most relevant regulators of tumor metastasis, we assessed the differentially expressed genes in TCGA-NSCLC cohorts that were annotated based on TP53 status and M stage (Fig. 1A). Integration analysis showed that 40 candidate effectors were differentially expressed (Fig. 1B). Next, the LASSO regression (Fig. 1C-D) and Cox regression analysis (Fig. 1E) were used to reduce the overfitting high-dimensional genes. In this manner, DPEP2, TCN1, and STXBP6 were evaluated as valuable candidate effectors. Schoenfeld residuals plots (Figure S1A-C) and Kaplan-Meier curves (Figure S1D-F) showed that the survival probability was higher in patients with high DPEP2 (HR = 0.73) and STXBP6 (HR = 0.70) and low TCN1(HR = 1.29).

Loss of p53 induces DPEP2 down-expression in NSCLC

We compared the mRNA and protein levels of TCN1, STXBP6 and DPEP2 in TP53-wild type (WT) A549 cells and TP53-null H1299 cells. The results suggested that DPEP2 was significantly downregulated in the absence of TP53 (Fig. 1F-G). In addition, ectopic TP53 expression greatly promoted DPEP2 expression in TP53-null H1299 cells (Fig. 1F-G) and TP53-knockout (KO) A549 cells (Fig. 1H-I). However, TP53 had no statistically significant effect on the expression of TCN1 and STXBP6 (Figure S2A-F). Therefore, DPEP2 was selected for the subsequent research.

TP53 exhibited the highest mutation rate among the annotated somatic mutations and there was a significant negative correlation between TP53 mutation and DPEP2 level in TCGA-NSCLC (Fig. 1J). Further analysis revealed that DPEP2 mRNA and protein levels were lower in TP53-MT tissues than that in TP53-WT tissues based on TCGA and CPTAC samples (Fig. 1K-L). Similar results were also confirmed in different lung cancer cell lines (Fig. 1M). Collectively, these observations support the proposition that loss of p53 induces DPEP2 down-expression in NSCLC.

DPEP2 is a diagnostic marker and predicts better prognosis in p53-loss NSCLC

Based on Consensus and HPA databases, DPEP2 is enriched in the lungs among systemic organs (Fig. 2A-B). To gain more insight into the differential expression of DPEP2 in NSCLC, we extracted mRNA microarray or sequencing cases with detailed clinical information from TCGA and GEO databases (Fig. 2C). The western blot analysis on 8 pairs of tumor specimens confirmed the decreased protein expression of DPEP2 in NSCLC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2D).

Next, we plotted ROC curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) to estimate the diagnostic performance of DPEP2. The AUC values were up to 0.983 and 0.991, respectively in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) (Fig. 2E, F). Considering the impact of TP53 status on disease prognosis, the NSCLC cohorts were divided into TP53-WT and TP53-MT groups. Interestingly, we found that, compared with progressive disease (PD) and stable disease (SD) patients, DPEP2 levels were higher in patients with complete response (CR) and partial response (PR) in TP53-MT group, but not in TP53-WT group (Fig. 2G-H). Likewise, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that higher DPEP2 were associated with better overall survival (OS) in TP53-MT patients, but not in TP53-WT patients (Fig. 2I-J). In addition, the multivariate Cox regression analysis of overall survival (OS) confirmed DPEP2 as an independent prognostic factor in TP53-MT LUAD and LUSC patients (Tables 1 and 2).

DPEP2 regulates the metastatic ability of p53-loss tumor cells via inhibiting the epithelial-mesenchymal transition

The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was used to determine the potential relationship between DPEP2 and tumor metastasis-related pathways in TP53-MT NSCLC. DPEP2 may play a role in cell adhesion, focal adhesion, and extracellular matrix (ECM) receptor interaction (Figure S3A). We selected 4 NSCLC cell lines with different TP53 status for further investigation (Fig. 3A). DPEP2 was then stably overexpressed by lentivirus (Fig. 3B, S3B). Transwell and wound healing assays were used to reflect the invasion and migration abilities of tumor cells, respectively. The results showed that overexpression of DPEP2 moderately inhibited the invasion and migration of TP53-WT A549 cells, but had no effect on TP53-MT H1975 cells (Figure S3C-D), but had a significant inhibitory effect on TP53-null H1299 and H358 cells (Fig. 3C-D). As the basis of cell movement, F-actin cytoskeleton plays an important role in tumor metastasis [18]. Phalloidin staining revealed that DPEP2-overexpressed cells had a decreased parallel F-actin fibers in TP53-null cells (Fig. 3E).

The mechanism by which DPEP2 regulates metastatic capacity of p53-loss tumor cells is the focus of subsequent studies. Western blot results suggested that overexpression of DPEP2 decreased the levels of Snail and Vimentin, and increased the level of E-cadherin, which are the marker proteins of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Fig. 3F, S3E). Numerous studies have confirmed that NF-κB signaling is a crucial regulator of EMT. Our results revealed that DPEP2 overexpression reduced the levels of phosphorylated NF-κB and IKKα in both H1299 and H358 cells. And the changes of EMT markers induced by DPEP2 were rescued by NF-κB activator 1 (Fig. 3G-I). In addition, NF-κB activator 1 could partially reverse the inhibitory effect of DPEP2 on the metastasis ability (Fig. 3J-K). To further demonstrate that the NF-κB pathway is essential for the tumor suppressor effect of DPEP2, we used JSH-23 to block NF-κB signaling. The results suggested that following treatment with JSH-23, DPEP2 lost its ability to regulate the phosphorylation of p65 and tumor metastasis in H1299 and H358 cells (Fig. 3L-M, S3F-G). Thus, DPEP2 regulates the metastatic capacity of p53-loss cells in an NF-κB-dependent manner. Further Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays showed that DPEP2 could bind to MAP3K7 in H1299 and H358 cells (Figure S4A-B). Moreover, overexpression of DPEP2 impaired the interaction between MAP3K7 and IKKα, leading to a reduction in the level of phosphorylated IKKα (p-IKKα) (Figure S4C).

Assessing immune-related characteristics of DPEP2 in NSCLC

There is growing appreciation that p53 status affects the immune landscape in a snowball-like manner [19]. Thus, the immune characteristics of DPEP2 warrant further in-depth study. The tumor purity was firstly evaluated in TCGA cohort using ESTIMATE algorithm. The results suggested that the expression of DPEP2 in NSCLC was positively correlated with the StromalScore (R = 0.608), ImmuneScore (R = 0.732), and ESTIMATEScore (R = 0.720) (Fig. 4A). We further analyzed the infiltrated immune cells according to the LM22 signature matrix. We identified increases in CD4 resting T cells, M0 macrophages, resting dendritic cells and mast cells in DPEP2-high tumors, whereas plasma cells and M2 macrophages showed significant decreases (Fig. 3B). In addition, DPEP2 was highly correlated with most immune checkpoints. Here, we noted that DPEP2 had the strongest correlation with SIGLEC9 in both LUAD (R = 0.67) and LUSC (R = 0.77), which was recently reported as a novel macrophage immune checkpoint [20] (Fig. 4C). We analyzed three independent scRNA-seq datasets and found that the abundance of DPEP2 was the highest in macrophage clusters among immune cells (Fig. 4D). We then introduced TIDE (tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion) to evaluate the immunotherapy predictive efficacy. The TIDE and T-cell exclusion scores were higher in the DPEP2-low group (Fig. 4F), which represented a higher potential for immune escape. Similarly, DPEP2 levels were negatively correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration in LUAD (R = −0.158, P = 4.18e-04) and LUSC (R = −0.325, P = 3.35e-13) (Fig. 4G). As we all know, the complexity of macrophages is reflected in the delicate balance between their pro-tumor and anti-tumor functions [21]. Survival analysis reflected the effect of DPEP2 levels interfering with macrophages on prognosis. Low DPEP2 and high M1 macrophages led to poor prognosis, whereas patients with high DPEP2 and low M2 had the best survival (Fig. 4H).

DPEP2 suppresses M2 macrophages polarization via NF-κB pathway

The evidence is increasingly compelling that cancer cells can induce the phenotypic transition of macrophages to adapt to the complex tumor microenvironment. We compared the infiltration of M1 and M2 macrophages in TP53-WT and TP53-MT LUAD and LUSC tissues (Figure S5A-B). The findings showed more M2 macrophages infiltration in TP53-MT LUAD (R = 0.371) and LUSC (R = 0.249) tissues. Likewise, data from cBioPortal showed that patients with TP53-MT had lower levels of M1 macrophage markers and higher levels of M2 markers compared to TP53-WT patients (Figure S5C-D). Other compelling evidence also suggests that suppressed p53 signaling drives M2-like polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in breast cancer, liver cancer, and colorectal cancer [22–24]. Therefore, we hypothesized that p53-loss promoted M2 macrophage polarization in the tumor microenvironment of NSCLC.

To test this hypothesis, THP-1 cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) to induce M0 macrophages. Before co-culture, the identification of M0 macrophages had been completed. The results were manifested as increased cell volume, irregular morphology, adherent growth, and flow cytometry showing high expression of CD68 and CD11b (Figure S6A-B). Then M0 were co-cultured with H1299/H358 cells for 5 days in a transwell apparatus (Fig. 5A). The levels of macrophage markers were measured on days 2 and 5 as described in previous studies [25, 26]. Analysis of mRNA expression showed that the M1 markers (IL6, NOS2, TNF) were downregulated, whereas M2 markers (IL10, TGFb, ARG1) were upregulated (Fig. 5B-C). The flow cytometry results indicated that after 5 days of co-culture with H1299/H358 cells, the proportion of CD86 + macrophages decreased, while the proportion of CD206 + macrophages increased significantly (Fig. 5D-E). These results showed that p53 loss induced the phenotypic transition of macrophages to a tumor-supportive type (M2-type) in the tumor microenvironment of NSCLC.

Considering the strong correlation between DPEP2 and macrophage infiltration, we investigated whether DPEP2 interferes with macrophage polarization in p53-null cells. We manipulated DPEP2 expression in H1299/H358 cells and collected the corresponding conditioned medium (CM). The CM from DPEP2-overexpression cells (DPEP2 CM) suppressed the polarization of macrophages towards M2 phenotype, as evidenced by decreased levels of M2 markers (Fig. 5F). Flow cytometry confirmed that macrophages with DPEP2 CM showed lower proportion of CD206+ (M2-type) cells (Fig. 5G).

DPEP2 is one of the glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins (GPI-APs). The extracellular release and intercellular transfer of GPI-APs are universal and spontaneous features between somatic cells [27]. Here, we examined the expression of DPEP2 in THP1 cells. Western blot indicated that DPEP2 was transferred between macrophages and tumor cells (Fig. 5H). It is widely recognized that the cleavage of the GPI anchor by phospholipases (PLC or PLD) represents the most typical extracellular release form of GPI-APs. Then we collected the conditioned medium and measured the concentration changes of DPEP2 under different phospholipase activities. It was found that the phospholipases C inhibitor (U-73122), but not the phospholipases D inhibitor (FIPI), reduced the concentration of DPEP2, suggesting that the release of DPEP2 is mediated by phospholipases C (Fig. 5I). Subsequently, we upregulated DPEP2 in THP1 cells to explore the mechanism (Fig. 5J). A large body of evidence has highlighted the important role of NF-κB signaling in regulating macrophage plasticity [28]. Similarly, we found that DPEP2 reduced the phosphorylation levels of p65 and IKKα in THP-1 cells cultured in p53-loss CM, suggesting the inhibition of NF-κB signaling (Fig. 5K-L). Further studies revealed that overexpression of DPEP2 led to a decrease in ARG1 protein level and a reduction in the proportion of CD206 + cells, suggesting that DPEP2 negatively regulates M2 polarization (Fig. 5M-N). Moreover, the DPEP2-mediated ARG1 down-regulation could be rescued by NF-κB activator 1 (Fig. 5M). To verify the indispensability of NF-κB signaling, JSH-23 was used to block NF-κB signaling. The results of flow cytometry indicated that the blockade of NF-κB signaling abrogated the inhibitory effect of DPEP2 on M2 polarization, as reflected by no significant difference in the proportion of CD206 + cells (Fig. 5N).

DPEP2 impairs the pro-tumor effect of M2 macrophages by depleting LTD4

It is generally accepted that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) mostly exhibit M2 phenotype and enhance tumor metastasis [29]. Subsequently, we induced M2 differentiation under the stimulation of IL3/IL14 to reveal the role of DPEP2 in the metastasis of p53-loss NSCLC. Figure S6C showed that the proportion of CD206 + cells increased from 6.58% to 74.8%, suggesting that M2 macrophages were successfully induced. These M2 macrophages then were co-cultured with Vector/DPEP2 overexpressed cells, and the transwell and wound healing assays were performed to examine the invasion and migration. The results suggested that M2 macrophages improved the metastatic ability, which were consistent with previous reports. In addition, upregulated DPEP2 weakened the pro-metastatic role of M2 macrophages (Fig. 6A-B). The best-known function of DPEP2 is to hydrolyze LTD4, and LTD4 is mainly derived from macrophages in the tumor microenvironment. In addition, LTD4 stimulation benefits the progression of a variety of cancers after binding to cysteinyl leukotriene receptors [10, 30, 31]. Further experiments showed that overexpression of DPEP2 depleted LTD4 in the co-culture system (Fig. 6C). Moreover, LTD4 treatment rescued the effect of DPEP2 on the pro-metastatic role of M2 macrophages (Fig. 6A-B).

DPEP2 inhibits p53-loss NSCLC metastasis and M2 macrophages infiltration in vivo

To further confirm the above results, we established lung metastasis mouse models with BALB/c-nude mice. H1299 Vector/DPEP2 cells with luciferase were injected via tail vein for metastatic dissemination investigation. The luciferase images demonstrated that DPEP2 resulted in a significant decrease on tumor size (Fig. 6D). Metastatic nodules on the lung surface were reduced when DPEP2 was upregulated (Fig. 6E). Next, the lung metastatic nodules of equal mass were obtained, and LTD4 concentration was measured 3 times according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results showed that DPEP2 reduced LTD4 concentration in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 6F), which was consistent with the results in co-culture system. In addition, the attenuation of CD206 fluorescence intensity suggested that DPEP2 inhibited M2 macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

Mutations in TP53 are present in human cancers of multiple cellular origins, with an overall frequency of up to 50%. The intricate and diverse functions of mutant p53 proteins (MT-p53) in regulating cancer cell fate have been previously reported [32, 33]. It is currently believed that the function of mutant p53 in driving the malignant progression of tumors has been attributed to three non-mutually exclusive processes: (i) loss-of-function (LOF), that is, loss of wild-type p53 protein (WT-p53) activity; (ii) dominant-negative effects (DNE), that is, low levels of MT-p53 repressing WT-p53 function; and (iii) gain-of-function (GOF) activities, meaning that MT-p53 has acquired additional activity, usually through interaction with specific cofactors [34–36]. A recent study highlighted that LOF, but not GOF, of MT-p53 is critical for the proliferation, survival, and metastasis of a broad range of human cancers [37]. Therefore, we aimed to explore the tumor pathogenesis and potential therapeutic approaches for p53-loss NSCLC.

At the beginning of the study, overlapping differentially expressed genes were screened by analyzing the expression profiles from TCGA cohort with WT/MT TP53 status or M0/M1 stage. Subsequently, three valuable candidates (TCN1, STXBP6 and DPEP2) closely related to p53 were determined by LASSO regression and Cox regression analysis. Further cytological and histological studies demonstrated that the loss of p53 markedly downregulated DPEP2 expression in NSCLC and could be rescued by ectopic p53 expression. Prognostic analysis found that higher DPEP2 were associated with better outcome and OS in TP53-MT patients, but not in TP53-WT patients. Extensive evidence supports that p53 signaling directly regulates over 300 target genes and indirectly mediates thousands of genes, exerting complex and significant effects on both tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment [19]. The differences of DPEP2 in NSCLC patients with different TP53 status in this study may be due to the strong and widespread p53 signaling masking its effects.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the loss of p53 is closely related to the high aggressiveness of tumors. For example, Shreya et al. demonstrated that the loss of p53 epigenetically modulated epithelial to mesenchymal transition in colorectal cancer [38].

Loss of p53 also antagonized the effect of androgen receptor inhibitors and facilitated the bone-metastatic capacity of prostate cancer [39, 40]. Similar to previous reports, we found that overexpression of DPEP2 significantly impaired the metastasis of p53-loss NSCLC by inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). In addition, MAP3K7 is a key activator of the NF-κB pathway through its interaction with the IKK complex [41–43]. By validating the interaction between DPEP2 and MAP3K7, our study revealed the molecular mechanism by which DPEP2 regulates the NF-κB signaling in p53-loss NSCLC. These results provide evidence that DPEP2 is a vital tumor suppressor in p53-loss NSCLC.

Tumor cells have evolved various strategies to limit or circumvent immune attacks. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent a pivotal target owing to their heterogeneous subtypes and functions in tumor immunity [44]. DPEP2 has been shown to protect against viral myocarditis by acting as a suppressor of macrophage inflammation [45]. In addition, DPEP2 inhibited MAK3K7 induced inflammatory signals in intestinal inflammation [46]. Multiple lines of evidence have indicated that DPEP2 is an immune-related prognostic marker of LUAD that distinguishes cold and hot tumors [47, 48]. Drawing on previous research, this study presents novel findings regarding DPEP2’s influence on the polarization and infiltration of M2-type TAMs. This study preliminarily verified the phospholipases C-dependent transfer of DPEP2, as a GPI-AP, between cancer cells and macrophages. In fact, lipolytic release from plasma membrane and intercellular transfer are identified as the main (patho) physiological roles exerted by GPI-APs [49, 50]. The modes of intercellular transfer of GPI-APs usually involves physical contact, enzyme mediation, and carrier mediation (such as extracellular vesicles, lipoprotein-like particles, or lipid-containing micelle-like complexes) [51]. Validation of the specific transfer mechanism will be a multidisciplinary and challenging project.

Despite the systematic analysis and validation of DPEP2 in p53-loss NSCLC, this study has some limitations. First, the subtypes of M1 and M2 macrophages oversimplify the true phenotype of TAMs, although it is still the most commonly used model for studying TAM heterogeneity [29, 52, 53]. With the development of single-cell genomics, more precise TAM subtypes need to be considered. Second, establishing orthotopic transplantation models or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models in TP53 knockout mice better retains the original characteristics of tumors. This also constitutes the key focus and challenge of subsequent research. Additionally, co-occurring genomic alterations have emerged as core determinants of different malignant features and heterogeneous responses [54, 55]. In fact, TP53/EGFR co-mutations have been reported to be associated with shorter progression-free survival and mixed clinical responses to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) therapy [56]. Therefore, whether DPEP2 plays different roles in co-mutated cancers is a more complex and worthwhile topic to explore. Finally, given the prevalence of TP53 mutations in different cancers, a challenge for the future is to extend these implications to other tumor types.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that DPEP2 impaired the metastatic capacity of p53-loss NSCLC both in vivo and vitro. In addition to inhibiting tumor cells, DPEP2 also interfered with the tumor microenvironment, as reflected by the inhibition of M2 macrophage infiltration, polarization, and pro-metastatic ability. Collectively, these novel findings support an important role for DPEP2 in tumor-immune cells crosstalk and reveal that DPEP2 may be a potential therapeutic target for highly metastatic p53-loss NSCLC.
