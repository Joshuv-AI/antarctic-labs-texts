Precision medicine and patient outcomes in intensive care unit: Culture sensitivity, antibiotics, and APACHE score IV
Authors: Sidra Zubair, Subia Jamil, Sakina Fatima, Syed T. Sibtain, Hira Raees, Asma Eraj, Amber Nawab
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:In the intensive care unit (ICU), complex medical conditions require specialized care; the threat of antibiotic resistance is significant due to frequent antibiotic use. This study investigates the pivotal role of culture sensitivity testing in shaping antibiotic prescription practices and patient outcomes in ICUs.MATERIALS AND METHODS:By using a prospective observational-analytical design, medical data from 640 patients at a Karachi hospital for one year in 2022 were utilized. The study focused on antibiotic therapy, including empiric and targeted approaches, culture sensitivity testing, the use of the APACHE scale for mortality prediction, and changes in antibiotic regimens based on culture reports. Statistical analysis involved Fisher’s Exact and ANOVA tests.RESULT:The age category above 60 emerged with the highest survivorship. Contrary to expectations, age did not seem to correlate significantly with mortality rates. Staphylococcus species was the most prevalent microorganism in this study, disproportionately affecting nonsurvivors 66.7%. The clinical profile comparison between survivors and nonsurvivors offered a multifaceted perspective, with significant differences in vital parameters such as temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, urine output, FiO2, mean arterial pH, and blood sugar levels. Survivors (44.60%) underwent complete antibiotic regimen changes, a strategy for improved patient outcomes.CONCLUSIONS:Complex antibiotic combinations were prescribed for broad clinical conditions. These insights pave the way for future research on antibiotic stewardship in ICUs, navigating more effective and targeted treatments.

Introduction

Patients with serious illnesses and complicated medical conditions receive specialized care in intensive care units (ICUs), which constitute essential healthcare facilities.[1] Patients are more prone to infections in the ICUs because of weakened immune systems, invasive medical procedures, and extended hospital stays.[2] Since a variety of antibiotics are frequently used to treat these infections, antibiotic resistance is becoming a growing issue in ICU settings.[34] To improve the care and survival rates of critically ill patients in the ICU, it is essential to comprehend the dynamics between microorganisms, antibiotic therapies, and patient outcomes.[3]

The proper administration of antibiotics to fight infections is a crucial component of caring for ICU patients. These medications are given hypothetically while awaiting culture results or targeted when particular pathogens are found.[5] Antibiotic-resistant bacteria have developed as a result of the increasing use of antibiotics in ICUs, posing a serious threat to patient health and complicating treatment plans. To ensure efficient treatment plans and reduce negative effects, it is crucial to look into the relationships between antibiotic consumption, microbial infections, and patterns of antibiotic resistance.[35]

The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) scale is another frequently used instrument in ICUs for determining the severity of an illness and predicting patient mortality.[67] Risk stratification can be improved, and clinical decision-making in the ICU can be strengthened by knowing how microbial infections and antibiotic resistance link to APACHE scores. The purpose of this study is to examine how microbial infections, antibiotic use, antibiotic resistance, and APACHE scores in ICU patients interact.[8]

Inappropriate antibiotic prescribing can lead to increased healthcare costs and the emergence of antibiotic resistance. The present study addressed the need for evidence-based insights into current practices to guide improvements in patient care, optimize antibiotic use, and alleviate the challenges associated with antimicrobial resistance. The study’s novelty lies in its comprehensive exploration of antibiotic prescribing practices, incorporating unique aspects such as age categories and APACHE scores. The present study is crucial for modifying antibiotic prescriptions to specific age-related considerations, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes in the ICU setting. Moreover, the analysis of antibiotic changes in relation to APACHE scores adds depth to the study, shedding light on the prescribing practices concerning the severity of patients’ conditions.

Materials and Methods

In the ICU of a hospital, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the impact of culture sensitivity testing on antibiotic prescribing practices and patient outcomes. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Board of Jinnah University for Women (Reference # JUW/IERB/034/2021). The data collection period span was 1 year, from February 2022 to February 2023.

Study design and setting

A prospective observational-analytical design was used. Data from patient medical records were gathered from a tertiary health care hospital, located in Karachi, Pakistan. To keep the confidentiality of the hospital, its name has been withdrawn. The primary variables of interest were the number of antibiotics prescribed (Empiric and targeted therapy), culture sensitivity (C/S) testing, the use of the APACHE scale for mortality prediction, and any changes in antibiotic prescription based on the culture reports.

Study participants and sampling

The ICU’s patient medical files were examined, covering 640 patients’ data in total. The sample size was calculated using the WHO calculator, and N was found to be 385 with a confidence interval of 95%, and 50% of the population proportion was used to calculate the sample size. Population size was considered unlimited. However, to increase the statistical power of the result, the sample size actually increased to 640 subjects.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic of the study population, APACHE scores, culture sensitivity testing rates, and antibiotic prescription patterns. Suitable statistical techniques by using IBM SPSS 22, such as Fisher’s Exact test and ANOVA test, were used to analyze the data.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Ethical Review Board gave its approval, and the study was carried out in conformity with ethical guidelines. All data were anonymized and safely preserved, and patient confidentiality and privacy were maintained throughout the study.

Result

This study was carried out on a total of 640 patients with various medical conditions. The most prevalent medical conditions were respiratory diseases affecting 140 patients (21.9%), followed by multiorgan diseases in 119 patients (18.6%), and neurological disorders accounted for 93 cases (14.5%). The patients’ outcomes varied, with 46.3% being discharged, and the mortality rate was 30.6%. Further details are mentioned in Table 1.

Discussions

In the 640 sample, 69.4% survived, and 30.6% died. It is notable that the statistical study showed no connection between age and death rate. These results highlight the complexity of factors influencing mortality by indicating that age alone may not be a reliable predictor of survival outcomes in this study population.[9] More research into additional characteristics and risk factors may help us understand these results more thoroughly.

The extensive spectrum of bacteria found in this study emphasizes how complicated infections are in the ICU environment. Effective patient management and the avoidance of nosocomial infections depend on an understanding of the incidence and spread of these bacteria. The most common bacteria in this study, Staphylococcus spp., are an opportunistic pathogen with a history for causing a variety of illnesses, including bloodstream infections and surgical site infections.[1011] The prevalence was higher in nonsurvivors than in survivors, occurring in 5.6% of all cases.[12] This demonstrates that Staphylococcus infections may be related to less favorable results. Localized infections, which are usually linked to abscesses or wound infections, are indicated by the detection of pus cells in 2.5% of samples. Antibiotic resistance and the ability of pathogens like Acinetobacter spp. and Candida albicans to seriously infect immunocompromised patients.[1314] Being vigilant and treating them are crucial to stop these infections from spreading within the ICU. Pseudomonas aeruginosa was only discovered in nonsurvivors, which is significant since it shows how important a role it could potentially play in poor outcomes.[15] Gram-negative bacteria are recognized for their ability to lead to serious bloodstream, urinary, and respiratory infections that typically need aggressive treatment. Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Aspergillus, and E. coli are a few examples of these bacteria.

This study suggests that the APACHE score remains an effective and valuable tool in predicting patient mortality in an ICU setting.[7] The second relationship between the APACHE score and antibiotic prescription practices such as monotherapy and combination therapy is that the data shows a predominance of monotherapy in patients with lower APACHE scores. Remarkably, this trend re-emerges in extremely high score categories (>140), suggesting that the complexity of care and severity of illness may necessitate simpler antibiotic strategies. Conversely, the use of combination therapies is most notable in the mid-range APACHE scores (21-120), with an observable peak in the usage of two antibiotics in the 61-70 range, which might be due to severe infection.

Among survivors, a substantial proportion experienced a complete change in their antibiotic treatment regimen, suggesting that altering antibiotics may be a potentially beneficial strategy for improving patient outcomes. Additionally, the combination of discontinuing one antibiotic while adding another underscores the complexity of treatment decisions in critically ill patients.[16] In contrast, nonsurvivors exhibited a different pattern. A higher percentage underwent a complete change in their antibiotic regimen, possibly indicating a more aggressive approach to treatment.

In mono-antibiotic, ceftriaxone, a broad-spectrum cephalosporin, was frequently chosen for empiric and targeted therapy, highlighting its versatility in managing a range of infections.[17] Furthermore, meropenem and pipercillin-tazobactam also played significant roles in empiric therapy, possibly reflecting their effectiveness against severe infections or suspected multidrug-resistant organisms.[18] Colistin was notably more prevalent in targeted therapy, indicating its reserved use for severe infections or multidrug-resistant strains. Two antibiotic combinations are frequently used to deal with a broader variety of infections.[19] In targeted therapy, meropenem + colistin appear more regularly, possibly indicating that it is used to treat severe cases or infections resistant to many drugs.

Limitations

Possible limitations of this study include its observational nature, potential confounding variables that were not controlled for, and the single-center design, which might limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

The observational nature and single-center design of this study are two limitations that may restrict its generalizability. However, findings shed important light on the microbiological environment of ICU patients and highlight the significance of individualized infection prevention and treatment techniques in this high-risk setting. These results highlight the value of individualized, data-driven healthcare and provide new directions for future study on antibiotic stewardship in hospital settings, particularly in ICUs.
