Improving adherence to appropriate use criteria for paediatric supracondylar humerus fractures: a three-cycle quality improvement project
Authors: Osama Embaby, Mahmoud Mersal, Mohamed Elalfy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe appropriate use criteria (AUC) developed by the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) provide evidence-based guidance for managing paediatric supracondylar humerus fractures (SCHFs), yet adherence varies widely in clinical practice. This quality improvement project aimed to improve the rate of ‘appropriate’ management for paediatric SCHF at a tertiary referral centre in Egypt by systematically implementing the AAOS AUC through a structured, multicycle intervention.MethodsWe conducted a three-cycle plan–do–study–act quality improvement project at Mansoura University Emergency Hospital between January 2021 and May 2023, including all children under 18 years with acute SCHFs. Cycle 1 (n=58) established baseline adherence. Cycle 2 (n=62) implemented educational workshops and a departmental protocol. Cycle 3 (n=58) introduced reinforcement training, regular case discussions, visual reminders and individualised audit feedback. The primary outcome was the proportion of cases managed ‘appropriately’ according to the AUC.ResultsAmong 178 patients, appropriate management increased from 62.1% at baseline to 75.8% after initial education and 87.9% after reinforcement interventions, representing a 25.8% absolute improvement. Rarely appropriate management decreased from 19.0% to 3.4%. The greatest improvements occurred in Gartland type II fractures. Key baseline barriers included the lack of AUC awareness (78% of surgeons unfamiliar), absence of standardised protocols and variable surgeon preferences. Following sustained educational interventions and audit feedback, AUC tool utilisation increased from 45% to 95%.ConclusionsA phased quality improvement initiative combining education, protocolisation, reinforcement training and audit feedback significantly improved adherence to evidence-based guidelines for paediatric SCHFs. Sustained educational interventions with regular case-based discussions and individualised feedback are essential for long-term practice change. Organisational factors, particularly lack of awareness and inconsistent application of guidelines, represent the primary modifiable barriers to adherence.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study provides a practical, replicable framework for implementing evidence-based guidelines in orthopaedic trauma care that can be adapted to diverse healthcare settings, including resource-limited environments. The findings suggest that sustained educational interventions with ongoing reinforcement and audit feedback are essential for long-term practice change, rather than one-time educational sessions alone. The study’s emphasis on low-cost interventions integrated into existing workflows offers a pragmatic approach for institutions seeking to improve guideline adherence without requiring substantial additional resources. Introduction

Supracondylar humerus fractures (SCHFs) are the most common elbow injuries in children, accounting for up to 70% of paediatric elbow fractures and approximately 15% of all childhood fractures worldwide.1 2 These injuries typically occur between the ages of 5 and 7 years, most commonly following a fall on an outstretched hand.3 Despite their frequency, SCHF remains clinically significant due to the high risk of complications, including neurovascular injury, compartment syndrome, malunion and cubitus varus deformity.4 5

Management strategies for SCHF vary considerably across institutions and individual surgeons. While stable, non-displaced fractures (Gartland type I) are typically managed conservatively with immobilisation, displaced fractures (types II and III) often require reduction and percutaneous fixation.6 7 However, controversy persists regarding the optimal management of borderline cases, particularly Gartland type II fractures, where decisions between casting and surgical pinning are influenced by multiple factors, including fracture stability, swelling severity and neurovascular status.8 9

To address this variability and standardise care, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) developed appropriate use criteria (AUC) for paediatric SCHF management in 2014.10 The AUC synthesise available evidence and expert consensus into scenario-based recommendations, categorising treatment options as ‘Appropriate’, ‘May Be Appropriate’ or ‘Rarely Appropriate’ based on fracture type (Gartland classification), vascular status, neurological status, soft-tissue condition, associated injuries and swelling severity.11 Through a web-based tool, surgeons can rapidly match a patient’s clinical presentation to recommended treatment strategies, theoretically reducing unwarranted practice variation and improving outcomes.12

However, real-world validation and implementation of the AAOS AUC outside North America remains limited. Most available reports are retrospective or simulation based, and few prospective quality improvement studies have systematically evaluated interventions to improve AUC adherence.13 Moreover, there is scarce data from low- and middle-income countries, where variations in resources, training and healthcare infrastructure may affect guideline adoption and concordance.14

Prior to this project, our institution, a high-volume tertiary referral centre in Egypt receiving over 150 paediatric SCHF annually, had no standardised protocol for applying the AAOS AUC. An initial internal review suggested that management decisions were predominantly based on individual surgeon preference and training background rather than evidence-based guidelines, leading to potential overuse of ‘rarely appropriate’ treatments, particularly for Gartland type II fractures.

This quality improvement project was, therefore, initiated with the primary aim of improving the proportion of paediatric SCHF cases managed ‘appropriately’ according to the AAOS AUC. We hypothesised that a multifaceted, phased intervention involving education, protocol implementation and system-level changes would lead to significant and sustained improvement in adherence to these evidence-based standards. Secondary aims included identifying the key barriers to AUC adherence and developing sustainable strategies to maintain improvement over time.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective, three-cycle plan–do–study–act (PDSA) quality improvement project at the Emergency Hospital of Mansoura University, a tertiary referral centre in the Nile Delta region of Egypt. The hospital serves a catchment population of approximately 5 million and receives paediatric trauma referrals from multiple governorates. The project spanned from January 2021 to May 2023. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (2.0) guidelines.15

Participants

All children under the age of 18 years presenting to our institution with an acute (less than 14 days from injury) SCHF during the three audit periods were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they had pathological fractures, presented more than 14 days after injury (classified as neglected fractures), or had incomplete medical records that prevented accurate AUC assessment.

PDSA cycles and interventions

The project was structured around three distinct PDSA cycles, each building on the lessons learnt from the previous cycle. Cycle 1 served as a baseline assessment phase to quantify current practice patterns and identify barriers to guideline adherence, without implementing any interventions. Subsequent cycles (Cycles 2 and 3) incorporated the four components of the PDSA methodology with planned interventions, implementation, evaluation and adaptation based on findings.

Cycle 1: baseline assessment (January 2021–June 2021)

Plan

The initial phase aimed to quantify the baseline adherence to the AAOS AUC for paediatric SCHF management at our institution. We planned to collect prospective data on all eligible patients over a 6-month period to establish current practice patterns and identify the primary areas of non-concordance with AUC recommendations.

Do

Data were prospectively collected for 58 consecutive patients meeting inclusion criteria. For each case, detailed clinical information was recorded, including fracture type (Gartland classification based on true lateral radiographs), vascular status (non-perfused, perfused but pulseless or perfused with palpable pulse), neurological status (median, radial and ulnar nerve integrity), soft-tissue envelope (closed vs open), swelling severity (typical vs severe) and associated injuries. The treating surgeon’s management plan was documented. A senior orthopaedic resident, not involved in the patient’s direct care, then independently entered each clinical scenario into the official AAOS AUC web-based tool to determine the appropriateness rating for the actual treatment provided.

Study

Analysis of the baseline data revealed that only 62.1% (36/58) of cases were managed ‘appropriately’ according to the AUC. The rate of ‘rarely appropriate’ management was 19.0% (11/58), with the highest non-concordance found in the management of Gartland type II fractures. Specifically, 65% of type IIa fractures were managed with casting alone, which the AUC rates as ‘rarely appropriate’ for unstable or significantly displaced type II fractures. Postcycle surveys and focus group discussions with the surgical team identified key barriers: 78% of surgeons were unfamiliar with the AAOS AUC web tool, there was no departmental consensus on type II fracture management and treatment decisions were based primarily on individual surgeon training and preference.

Act

Based on these findings, we planned a multimodal intervention aimed at increasing awareness of the AUC, providing clear guidance on evidence-based management and establishing a departmental protocol.

Cycle 2: educational intervention and protocol implementation (September 2021–February 2022)

Plan

We hypothesised that a targeted educational programme combined with the introduction of a standardised departmental protocol would significantly increase the rate of ‘appropriate’ management. The intervention was designed and implemented between July and August 2021.

Do

The intervention consisted of two components.

Educational Workshop (July 2021): A mandatory 2-hour educational session was conducted for all orthopaedic residents and consultants. The workshop covered: (a) the evidence base for the AAOS AUC, including review of key studies; (b) live demonstration of the AUC web-based tool with case-based exercises; (c) discussion of common scenarios and areas of controversy, particularly type II fracture management and (d) distribution of pocket reference cards summarising AUC recommendations.

Departmental Protocol Development (August 2021): A multidisciplinary team, including senior consultants, residents and the quality improvement lead, developed a departmental protocol for SCHF management that explicitly integrated AUC recommendations. The protocol included a flowchart for type II fracture decision-making based on stability, displacement and swelling.

Data were then prospectively collected for 62 patients in the 6 months following the intervention (September 2021–February 2022).

Study

The rate of ‘appropriate’ management increased to 75.8% (47/62), representing a 13.7% absolute improvement. The rate of ‘rarely appropriate’ cases decreased from 19.0% to 9.7% (6/62). For type IIa fractures, ‘rarely appropriate’ management decreased from 65% to 25%. However, analysis revealed persistent barriers: documentation of the AUC rating was inconsistent (completed in only 45% of cases), some senior surgeons expressed resistance to changing their established practices and the web-based tool was perceived as time-consuming in the emergency setting.

Act

To address these remaining barriers and ensure sustainability, we planned a comprehensive reinforcement strategy, including refresher training and regular case discussions.

Cycle 3: reinforcement education and sustained improvement (November 2022–May 2023)

Plan

We aimed to sustain and further improve AUC adherence through reinforcement education, regular case-based learning and visual reminders. The intervention was implemented between March and October 2022.

Do

The intervention included three components.

Reinforcement Training Workshops (March–April 2022): A series of three refresher workshops were conducted for all orthopaedic staff, focusing on challenging case scenarios, particularly Gartland type II fractures. Interactive case-based discussions were used to reinforce AUC principles. AUC training was incorporated into the mandatory orientation for new residents.

Data were subsequently collected for 58 patients over a 7-month period (November 2022–May 2023).

Study

The rate of ‘appropriate’ management further increased to 87.9% (51/58), a 12.1% improvement from Cycle 2 and a total 25.8% improvement from baseline. The rate of ‘rarely appropriate’ cases fell to just 3.4% (2/58). For type IIa fractures, ‘rarely appropriate’ management decreased to 10%. The use of the AUC tool for decision-making was documented in over 95% of cases, indicating successful integration into routine practice. Postcycle surveys revealed high satisfaction with the reinforcement interventions, with 91% of surgeons reporting increased confidence in applying the AUC and 85% finding the weekly case discussions ‘very helpful’.

Act

The changes were adopted as standard departmental policy. Weekly case review meetings and visual reminders. AUC training was permanently incorporated into resident orientation, and the departmental protocol was updated to include mandatory AUC documentation for all SCHF cases.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 178 patients meeting the inclusion criteria were included across the three audit cycles: 58 in Cycle 1, 62 in Cycle 2 and 58 in Cycle 3. The demographic and clinical characteristics remained consistent across all three cycles, ensuring comparability of results.

The mean age of the total cohort was 6.0±3.3 years (range 1–16 years), with 94 children (52.8%) aged 5 years or younger. There was a male predominance, with 120 males (67.4%) and 58 females (32.6%), consistent with the epidemiology of paediatric SCHF reported in the literature. Gartland type III extension fractures were the most common presentation, accounting for 109 cases (61.2%). Type I fractures were seen in 21 patients (11.8%), type IIa in 25 patients (14.0%), type IIb in 17 patients (9.6%) and flexion-type fractures in 6 patients (3.4%).

The vast majority of patients presented with perfused hands and palpable distal pulses (168 patients, 94.4%). Eight patients (4.5%) had perfused hands without palpable pulses (‘pink pulseless hand’) and two patients (1.1%) presented with non-perfused hands requiring urgent intervention. No neurological deficits were documented at initial presentation in any patient. Nearly all fractures were closed (175 patients, 98.3%), with only three open fractures. Associated ipsilateral forearm or hand fractures were rare, occurring in nine patients (5.1%). Typical swelling was noted in 152 patients (85.4%), while severe swelling was present in 26 patients (14.6%). Primary outcome: appropriateness of management across cycles

The primary outcome—the proportion of patients receiving ‘appropriate’ management according to the AAOS AUC—increased significantly across the three cycles. In Cycle 1 (baseline), 62.1% (36/58) of cases were managed appropriately. Following the educational intervention and protocol implementation in Cycle 2, this increased to 75.8% (47/62), representing a 13.7% absolute improvement. After the reinforcement training, visual reminders, case discussions and individualised feedback in Cycle 3, the rate of appropriate management reached 87.9% (51/58), a further 12.1% improvement and a total absolute improvement of 25.8% from baseline.

Concurrently, the rate of ‘rarely appropriate’ management, which was the primary target for reduction, decreased from 19.0% (11/58) at baseline to 9.7% (6/62) in Cycle 2, and finally to 3.4% (2/58) in Cycle 3, representing an absolute reduction of 15.6%. The rate of ‘may be appropriate’ management also showed a steady decline, from 19.0% (11/58) in Cycle 1 to 14.5% (9/62) in Cycle 2, and to 8.6% (5/58) in Cycle 3. Treatment modalities

Closed reduction with percutaneous cross pinning was the most common treatment modality across all cycles, used in 133 patients (74.7%). Immobilisation without reduction was employed in 37 patients (20.8%), lateral pinning in 6 patients (3.4%) and open reduction with internal fixation in 2 patients (1.1%). Traction and external fixation were not used in any cases. The distribution of treatment modalities remained relatively stable across cycles, but the appropriateness of the chosen treatment for the specific fracture pattern and clinical scenario improved significantly.

Discussion

This three-cycle quality improvement project successfully demonstrates that a systematic, multifaceted approach can significantly improve adherence to the AAOS AUC for paediatric SCHF in a real-world clinical setting. By progressing from education and protocolisation to sustained reinforcement with visual reminders, case discussions and individualised feedback, we achieved a 25.8% absolute increase in ‘appropriate’ management and a corresponding 15.6% decrease in ‘rarely appropriate’ care. Our findings underscore the importance of moving beyond passive educational strategies to actively embedding evidence-based guidelines into routine practice through continuous reinforcement and audit feedback.

Comparison with existing literature

The initial baseline of 62.1% appropriate care in our study is comparable with other single-centre studies conducted before targeted interventions. Brandt et al reported 68% concordance with AAOS AUC in a retrospective review of 156 patients at a paediatric level 1 trauma centre in the USA.16 Similarly, Douleh et al found 71% concordance in a simulation-based study.17 Our baseline is notably lower than these reports, possibly reflecting differences in baseline awareness of the AUC in different healthcare settings and the challenges of guideline implementation in resource-limited contexts.

The main source of non-concordance in our study—the conservative management of Gartland type II fractures—is a well-documented area of clinical uncertainty and practice variation.8 9 The AAOS clinical practice guideline acknowledges that evidence for type II fracture management is limited, with most recommendations based on expert consensus rather than high-quality randomised trials.18 Our first intervention, combining education with a formal protocol, led to a significant 13.7% improvement, highlighting that the lack of awareness and absence of clear guidance were key initial barriers. This aligns with quality improvement literature emphasising the role of education as a foundational step in changing clinical practice.19

However, the substantial jump to 87.9% appropriate care was only achieved after implementing sustained reinforcement interventions: refresher training, visual reminders, regular case discussions and individualised audit feedback. This multifaceted approach addressed the persistent barriers of inconsistent documentation, resistance to change and lack of ongoing reinforcement. This finding is consistent with a growing body of evidence suggesting that sustained educational interventions combined with audit and feedback are among the most effective strategies for promoting guideline adherence and reducing practice variation.20 21 Ivers et al demonstrated in a Cochrane systematic review that audit and feedback can lead to significant improvements in professional practice, particularly when feedback is provided regularly and includes specific, actionable recommendations.22

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its prospective design, relatively large cohort for a single-centre quality improvement project, systematic application of the AUC tool to all cases and the use of a rigorous PDSA methodology with iterative learning. The three-cycle approach allowed us to test and refine interventions progressively, identifying what worked and what did not. The sustained educational interventions and regular audit feedback represent sustainable, system-level changes that are likely to maintain improvements over time. Additionally, this is one of the first quality improvement studies to evaluate AAOS AUC implementation in a low- and middle-income country setting, providing valuable insights for similar contexts.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a single-centre study in a specific healthcare context, our findings may not be directly generalisable to all settings, particularly those with different educational resources, staffing models or case volumes. We did not collect data on clinical outcomes (such as the rates of malunion, neurovascular complications or need for reoperation), so we cannot definitively state that the increase in ‘appropriate’ care led to improved patient outcomes, although this is the implicit goal of the AUC. The Hawthorne effect may have influenced behaviour, as clinicians were aware they were a part of a quality improvement project, potentially leading to better performance than would occur in routine practice. The time gap between Cycle 2 and Cycle 3 (March–October 2022 for intervention implementation) means that some improvement might be attributable to maturation effects or secular trends rather than the specific interventions. Finally, we did not assess inter-rater reliability in AUC classification, although we used the official web-based tool that provides standardised ratings.

Implications for practice

Our findings have several important implications for clinical practice and quality improvement efforts. First, they demonstrate that the AAOS AUC can be successfully implemented in diverse healthcare settings, including low- and middle-income countries, with appropriate adaptation and support. Second, they highlight that education alone, while necessary, is insufficient for sustained practice change. Ongoing reinforcement through visual reminders, regular case discussions and audit feedback is crucial for embedding evidence-based practices into routine care. Third, individualised feedback to surgeons, comparing their performance with departmental averages, appears to be a powerful motivator for behaviour change, consistent with audit and feedback literature.22

For institutions seeking to implement similar quality improvement initiatives, our experience suggests a phased approach: (1) establish baseline performance and identify specific barriers through surveys and focus groups; (2) implement education and develop consensus protocols to address knowledge gaps; (3) provide sustained reinforcement through visual aids, regular case-based learning and departmental presentations; (4) implement individualised audit feedback to clinicians with regular reporting and (5) establish ongoing monitoring and case review processes to sustain improvements. Adaptation to local context is critical for successful implementation. Institutions must first ensure that clinicians have access to the AAOS AUC web-based decision tool (available at www.orthoguidelines.org/auc) and are trained in its use, as this tool provides real-time guidance for clinical decision-making. In resource-constrained settings, we recommend prioritising low-cost interventions that integrate into the existing workflows, such as laminated pocket cards, visual flowcharts in clinical areas and incorporation of guideline training into routine educational activities rather than creating separate dedicated sessions. Leadership support for protected training time and establishment of formal governance structures with clearly defined roles enhance sustainability. Starting with small-scale pilot testing before broader implementation allows for iterative refinement based on local barriers and facilitators. Regular data collection with visual display methods (such as run charts) maintains team engagement and provides objective evidence of improvement.

Future directions

Based on our PDSA findings, future quality improvement efforts should focus on several areas. First, we plan to expand the educational interventions and visual decision aids to include other common paediatric fractures (such as forearm fractures and ankle fractures) where AAOS or other professional society guidelines exist. Second, we aim to link AUC adherence data with clinical outcomes to determine whether higher adherence rates are associated with improved patient outcomes, reduced complications or decreased healthcare costs. Third, we plan to conduct a multicentre implementation study to assess the generalisability of our approach to other institutions in Egypt and the broader Middle East and North Africa region. Finally, we will explore opportunities to develop institution-specific adaptations of the AUC that account for local resource availability while maintaining evidence-based principles.

Conclusion

This quality improvement project successfully implemented the AAOS AUC for paediatric SCHFs into routine clinical practice at a high-volume tertiary centre in Egypt. Through a structured, three-cycle PDSA approach, we demonstrated that combining education, departmental protocols, sustained reinforcement training and audit feedback can lead to significant and sustainable improvements in evidence-based care. The proportion of cases managed appropriately increased from 62.1% to 87.9%, while rarely appropriate management decreased from 19.0% to 3.4%.

The key lessons from this project are that organisational factors—particularly lack of awareness, absence of standardised protocols and insufficient reinforcement mechanisms—represent the primary modifiable barriers to guideline adherence. Sustained educational interventions with visual reminders, regular case-based discussions and individualised audit feedback are powerful strategies for bridging the gap between evidence and practice. This approach is feasible and effective even in resource-limited settings, provided that there is institutional support, multidisciplinary collaboration and a commitment to iterative learning through PDSA methodology.
