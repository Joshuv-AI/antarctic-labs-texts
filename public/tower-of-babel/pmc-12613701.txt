Preoperative serum magnesium level as a prognostic and predictive biomarker in locally advanced gastric cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy
Authors: Xiaohang Liu, Xiong Sun, Yao Lin, Chengguo Li, Qian Shen, Jianing Ding, Tianhao Li, Duofei Lu, Xuanfei Li, Shijun Zhao, Kaixiong Tao, Yuping Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global multicenter phase III MATTERHORN trial further validated the scientific rationale and clinical value of our earlier research, demonstrating that the combination therapy significantly improved patient survival outcomes and provided critical evidence for optimizing gastric cancer treatment strategies worldwide. Nevertheless, despite the overall effectiveness of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NICT), approximately half of the patients did not achieve optimal therapeutic responses. Moreover, only a few clinically useful biomarkers can predict the treatment outcomes. The relationship between pre-treatment serum electrolyte levels and treatment outcomes in patients with locally advanced gastric cancer (LAGC) after NICT remains unclear.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical data from 112 patients with LAGC who underwent radical surgery after NICT between 2020 and 2024. Serum electrolyte levels were measured prior to NICT. The associations between serum electrolyte levels and overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were evaluated.ResultsIn the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of pre-treatment serum electrolytes, only sodium showed a significant association; however, it was not an independent prognostic factor, according to the Cox regression analysis. Low pre-treatment serum magnesium levels were significantly associated with poorer OS (HR = 21.48, P < 0.001) and DFS (HR = 8.730, P < 0.001). Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that low serum magnesium was an independent prognostic factor for both OS (HR = 0.232, P = 0.007) and DFS (HR = 0.274, P = 0.004). Low magnesium levels were more prevalent in patients with signet ring cell carcinoma or positive perineural invasion. Furthermore, low serum magnesium levels were associated with a poor rate of major pathological response (P < 0.0001).ConclusionPre-treatment serum magnesium levels are independent prognostic factors and potential biomarkers in patients with LAGC undergoing NICT. However, prospective studies are required to validate these findings.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a common and fatal malignancy worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, GC causes over 700,000 deaths annually and ranks second in global cancer-related mortality [1]. Approximately 75% of new cases and deaths occur in Asia, and most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [2]. Although significant progress has been made in early detection and treatment strategies, the five-year survival rate for GC remains universally low, and the prognosis remains poor [2]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is a gold standard treatment option for locally advanced gastric cancer (LAGC). Although it has achieved some success in delaying disease progression, its efficacy remains limited, and many patients experience recurrence or metastasis after treatment [3, 4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more effective treatment strategies to improve prognosis.

Recently, the rise in immunotherapy has brought new hope for cancer treatment. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as PD-1 inhibitors, have shown significant efficacy in treating advanced GC and other malignancies. Studies have demonstrated that ICIs achieve favorable treatment responses in patients with LAGC after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NICT) [5, 6]. The recent global multicenter phase III MATTERHORN trial demonstrated that NICT was superior to previous standard therapies in patients with LAGC [7]. However, not all patients experience significant clinical benefits from NICT. This highlights the urgent need to identify new biomarkers to better predict treatment response and guide treatment decisions.

The role of serum electrolytes in cancer prognosis has garnered increasing attention in the search for novel biomarkers. Research has identified serum electrolytes such as sodium, calcium, and magnesium as critical components of the tumor microenvironment, which are closely related to tumor progression. Hyponatremia has been shown to correlate with poor prognosis in various cancers, including lung cancer and GC[8, 9]. Moreover, abnormal serum calcium levels have been associated with the invasiveness and metastatic potential of cancer cells. Patients with hypercalcemia tend to have more aggressive and metastatic cancers [10]. However, the role of magnesium as an immune regulator remains in the early stages of research. Studies have suggested that magnesium may regulate immune responses by affecting the activation and cytotoxicity of natural killer cells and CD8 + T cells [11, 12]. Despite these findings, the relationship between serum electrolyte levels and treatment outcomes in patients with LAGC remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between pre-treatment serum electrolyte levels and treatment outcomes in patients with LAGC receiving NICT, thereby laying the foundation for future clinical research on the use of serum electrolytes in LAGC treatment.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from patients with LAGC who underwent radical surgery after NICT at Wuhan Union Hospital, the Second Hospital of Shandong University, and Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University between January 2020 and July 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; pathologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma; clinical stage T3-4 N + M0; and prior treatment with NICT followed by radical surgery. The exclusion criteria included incomplete data for analysis, concurrent radiotherapy or targeted therapy, evidence of distant metastasis during NICT, history of other malignant conditions, and in-hospital mortality following surgery. In total, 112 patients who satisfied the inclusion criteria and showed complete data were included in the final analysis. Data were retrospectively collected from the electronic medical records (EMR) of the hospital. The study was approved by the ethics committee (approval No. 2024 − 0474), and it adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines.

Treatment and follow-up

Prior to radical surgery, all enrolled patients underwent NICT every 21 days. The chemotherapy regimen included SOX (S-1 and oxaliplatin), whereas the immunotherapy treatment involved tislelizumab.

After completing three or four cycles of NICT, patients underwent re-assessment to evaluate their condition and treatment response. Treatment efficacy was measured according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines, version 1.1. Patients with inadequate response discontinued NICT and were excluded from the study, whereas those showing effective response completed the remaining treatment cycles. Radical surgery was performed in patients who were eligible for R0 resection. Post-treatment, patients were followed up regularly, with the final follow-up for all patients conducted in December 2024.

Data collection

We collected clinical data including demographics, tumor characteristics, and serum electrolyte levels [sodium (Na), potassium (K), chloride (Cl), calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), and magnesium (Mg)]. Tumor staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines. Major pathological response (MPR) was defined as the percentage of viable residual tumor cells in a tumor specimen of no more than 10%. Serum electrolytes were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer with quality control measures to ensure accuracy. Patients were grouped according to serum electrolyte levels on the basis of optimal cutoff values derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States) and X-tile (version 3.6.1; Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA). Optimal cutoff values for serum electrolyte levels were determined using ROC curves, and diagnostic performance was assessed by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to evaluate overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to assess prognostic factors for OS and DFS. Chi-square tests and t-tests were used for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Determining the optimal cutoff values

The optimal cutoff values for serum electrolytes (Na, K, Cl, Ca, P, and Mg) and their associations with OS and DFS were determined using X-tile (Fig. 1). The optimal cutoff values for pre-treatment serum electrolytes were as follows: Na, 139.00 mmol/L; K, 4.17 mmol/L; Cl, 102.40 mmol/L; Ca, 2.22 mmol/L; P, 1.32 mmol/L; and Mg, 0.82 mmol/L. On the basis of these optimal cutoff values, the patients were categorized into low-level (electrolyte level ≤ cutoff value) and high-level (electrolyte level > cutoff value) groups for subsequent analysis.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between serum electrolyte levels and OS (Fig. 2). Pre-treatment serum Mg and Na levels were significantly associated with OS. Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated that low magnesium (HR = 21.48, P < 0.001) and sodium (HR = 4.498, P = 0.001) levels were significantly associated with poorer OS, suggesting that these two indicators might serve as prognostic biomarkers for patients with LAGC. Although P and Cl levels showed some correlation with OS (P = 0.063 and P = 0.052, respectively), they did not reach statistical significance, indicating that although they may play a potential role in LAGC prognosis, their role is not clearly defined. In contrast, the Ca and K levels were not significantly correlated with OS (P > 0.05).

According to Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Fig. 3), pre-treatment serum Mg (HR = 8.730, P < 0.001), Na (HR = 2.957, P = 0.032), and P (HR = 0.282, P = 0.045) levels were significantly associated with poorer DFS. Although K levels showed a trend towards significance (HR = 1.953, P = 0.081), they did not reach statistical significance. Cl and Ca levels were not significantly associated with DFS (P > 0.05). These results are consistent with those of the OS analysis, further highlighting the clinical value of serum Mg and Na levels as potential prognostic indicators for patients with LAGC.

Clinical characteristics and their association with serum Mg levels and MPR

To further investigate whether pre-treatment serum Mg levels are correlated with the clinical characteristics of patients, we compared the baseline clinical features of patients with LAGC grouped by low (Mg ≤ 0.82 mmol/L) and high (Mg > 0.82 mmol/L) serum Mg levels (Table 3). The analysis revealed that the proportion of signet ring cell carcinoma was significantly higher in the low-Mg group than in the high-Mg group (44.00% vs. 22.99%, P = 0.023), suggesting that lower serum Mg levels may be associated with more aggressive tumor subtypes. Interestingly, patients with a history of smoking were more common in the low-Mg group than in the high-Mg group (44.00% vs. 20.69%, P = 0.023). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, sex, BMI, tumor location, degree of tumor differentiation, cycle, alcohol consumption history, hypertension, or diabetes (P > 0.05). Perineural invasion was more common in the low-Mg group than in the high-Mg group (64.00% vs. 36.78%, P = 0.021); however, vascular invasion did not differ significantly between the two groups (36.00% vs. 28.74%, P = 0.622). More patients in the low-Na group were found in the low-Mg group than in the high-Mg group (36.00% vs. 14.94%, P = 0.026), and more patients in the high P group were found in the low-Mg group than in the high-Mg group (28.00% vs. 8.05%, P = 0.014). No significant differences in serum K, Cl, and Ca levels were observed between the two groups (P > 0.05). Patients in the low-Mg group exhibited significantly worse pathological responses (MPR = 1) than those in the high-Mg group (96.00% vs. 50.57%, P < 0.001), indicating that lower serum Mg levels may be associated with a poorer treatment response.

On the basis of these findings, we explored the direct relationship between pre-treatment serum Mg levels and MPR to further understand the impact of Mg on treatment outcomes (Fig. 4). Both scatter plots and box plots showed significant differences in Mg levels between the MPR = 0 group (better pathological response) and the MPR = 1 group (poorer pathological response) (P < 0.0001). Lower serum magnesium levels were associated with poorer pathological responses. The MPR = 1 group showed significantly lower serum Mg levels than the MPR = 0 group. The median Mg levels in the MPR = 0 group were higher than those in the MPR = 1 group, which were generally lower and more concentrated.

Discussion

LAGC remains a major global health challenge, particularly in East Asia, where the incidence and mortality rates are among the highest worldwide. Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management, prognosis remains unsatisfactory for many patients due to high recurrence rates and therapeutic resistance. The integration of immunotherapy into neoadjuvant treatment has transformed the therapeutic landscape, with NICT emerging as a promising strategy that combines cytotoxic agents with immune checkpoint inhibitors to induce tumor regression, increase R0 resection rates, and improve survival outcomes [5, 6]. However, not all patients benefit equally from NICT; significant heterogeneity exists in treatment response, reflecting underlying biological diversity and variations in host-tumor interactions. This variability underscores the urgent need for robust, reproducible biomarkers capable of predicting therapeutic efficacy, guiding patient selection, and optimizing personalized treatment strategies. Traditional indicators—such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, microsatellite instability (MSI), tumor mutational burden (TMB), and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) status—provide valuable insights into tumor immunogenicity but have shown inconsistent predictive performance across trials and patient subgroups [13]. Similarly, histopathologic systems such as the Lauren classification retain prognostic value but fail to account for host immune and metabolic status, thereby limiting their predictive utility in the context of NICT [14, 15]. Hence, there is growing interest in exploring novel, accessible, and cost-effective biomarkers that reflect both tumor biology and systemic physiological conditions to refine therapeutic decision-making.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the prognostic significance of pre-treatment serum electrolytes in LAGC patients treated with NICT and identified low serum magnesium as an independent predictor of inferior outcomes. Specifically, hypomagnesemia was significantly associated with reduced OS, DFS, and MPR [16]. Among all electrolytes examined, only magnesium exhibited a consistent and robust relationship with prognosis after multivariate adjustment, suggesting a unique pathophysiological role in modulating treatment response. Our study revealed that patients with lower serum magnesium levels were more likely to have signet ring cell carcinoma and positive perineural invasion—features indicative of aggressive tumor biology—consistent with previous reports [17]. These findings highlight serum magnesium as a simple, inexpensive, and clinically actionable biomarker for risk stratification. Our results align with prior studies across various malignancies—including colorectal, breast, and non-small cell lung cancers—where magnesium deficiency has been correlated with poor outcomes, higher recurrence rates, and diminished therapeutic response [18–20]. Collectively, this evidence underscores the broader relevance of magnesium as a determinant of cancer prognosis and supports its integration into pre-treatment evaluation frameworks. From a practical standpoint, magnesium monitoring could complement existing molecular markers to better identify patients who may require closer surveillance or adjunctive metabolic support to optimize outcomes.

The association between hypomagnesemia and adverse outcomes is biologically plausible and can be attributed to multiple immunological and metabolic mechanisms. Magnesium is indispensable for effective antitumor immunity, serving as a cofactor in T-cell receptor signaling, cytokine production, and effector cell activation. It facilitates the activation and cytotoxicity of natural killer (NK) cells and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which are critical mediators of tumor clearance [11, 12]. Deficiency impairs lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) activation, disrupts nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) signaling, and suppresses interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production, resulting in weakened immune surveillance and reduced tumoricidal capacity [21–25]. Moreover, magnesium plays a central role in metabolic reprogramming of immune cells, supporting glycolysis, mitochondrial respiration, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, all of which are essential for sustained effector function. At the tumor level, malignant cells frequently maintain elevated intracellular magnesium via upregulated transporters, enabling proliferation and survival even under systemic deficiency [26, 27]. Conversely, magnesium-deficient microenvironments favor oxidative stress, genomic instability, and immunosuppression, fostering tumor progression and therapy resistance. These findings suggest that hypomagnesemia not only reflects systemic nutritional imbalance but also actively contributes to an immunosuppressive milieu that undermines the efficacy of NICT. This mechanistic insight provides a plausible explanation for the observed association between low magnesium levels and inferior MPR and survival in our cohort.

Given these observations, proactive management of magnesium levels may represent a feasible adjunct to optimize NICT outcomes. Routine measurement of serum magnesium before and during treatment should be incorporated into standard perioperative protocols. Early detection and timely correction of hypomagnesemia could improve immune competence, enhance treatment tolerance, and mitigate chemotherapy-induced toxicity [28]. Magnesium deficiency is prevalent among cancer patients due to malnutrition, gastrointestinal losses, and medication effects, particularly from proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), which impair intestinal absorption and contribute to refractory hypomagnesemia [29, 30]. Conventional oral supplementation may be insufficient in such contexts; therefore, intravenous administration or combination strategies with microbiota-targeted interventions—such as inulin-based prebiotics—may be more effective in restoring homeostasis [31, 35]. Furthermore, magnesium repletion has been associated with improved metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes, offering potential systemic benefits beyond oncologic control [32–34]. However, supplementation should be personalized, as excessive magnesium can lead to hypermagnesemia and complications such as hypotension, arrhythmia, and respiratory depression [35]. Future clinical trials are warranted to determine optimal supplementation protocols, dosage thresholds, and timing to maximize therapeutic benefit. Integrating magnesium assessment into multidisciplinary management frameworks may enhance the precision of NICT delivery and overall treatment quality.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design introduces inherent selection bias and limits generalizability. Only baseline electrolyte levels were analyzed; dynamic changes during and after therapy were not captured and may hold additional prognostic value. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts and standardized cutoff values are needed to validate these findings. Mechanistic investigations integrating multi-omics approaches—including metabolomics, transcriptomics, and immune profiling—could elucidate the molecular pathways linking magnesium homeostasis to tumor-immune dynamics. Interventional studies exploring whether correcting hypomagnesemia improves NICT efficacy, modulates immune activation, and reduces resistance are also warranted. Incorporating magnesium status into comprehensive prognostic models alongside molecular markers, clinical parameters, and treatment variables may enhance predictive accuracy and facilitate truly personalized therapy for LAGC. Ultimately, understanding the interplay between systemic metabolism and immunotherapy response may open new avenues for metabolic-immunologic co-targeting strategies, improving survival outcomes for patients with gastric cancer [36–38].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that pre-treatment serum Mg levels were closely related to OS and DFS in patients with LAGC receiving NICT. Low serum Mg levels significantly predicted poor prognosis for patients with LAGC. Our findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the clinical application of serum magnesium as a potential prognostic and predictive biomarker in patients with LAGC receiving NICT. However, further validation through large-scale prospective clinical trials is necessary before serum magnesium assessment can be routinely recommended in clinical practice.
