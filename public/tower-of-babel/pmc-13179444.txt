A Real‐World Experience of Prognostic Factors of Survival Outcomes in a Taiwanese Population With Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma Treated With First‐Line Lenvatinib
Authors: Shou‐Wu Lee, Yi‐Jie Huang, Hsin‐Ju Tsai, Ying‐Cheng Lin, Chia‐Chang Chen, Teng‐Yu Lee, Yen Chun‐Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionLenvatinib is approved as a first‐line treatment for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but real‐world outcomes may vary due to patient heterogeneity. This study aimed to evaluate the real‐world efficacy of lenvatinib in Taiwanese patients and to identify prognostic factors influencing outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 111 patients with unresectable HCC treated with the first‐line lenvatinib between February 2020 and April 2023 at Taichung Veterans General Hospital. All patients were Child‐Pugh class A and had at least one measurable lesion per mRECIST. Radiological tumor response, overall survival (OS), and progression‐free survival (PFS) were recorded and analyzed.ResultsThe ORR and DCR were 36% and 81% respectively. The median OS of all patients was 17 months, and PFS was 10 months. Patients with ALBI grade 1 (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.24–0.84, p = 0.011) and receiving combined locoregional therapy (LRT) (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20–0.79, p = 0.003) had significantly longer OS. AFP ≤ 400 ng/mL was associated with longer PFS (HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.35–0.94, p = 0.028).ConclusionLenvatinib is effective in real‐world treatment of unresectable HCC, especially when combined with LRT. ALBI grade 1 and AFP ≤ 400 ng/mL are favorable prognostic indicators for survival and disease control. These findings support personalized treatment strategies and warrant further prospective validation.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, lenvatinib, survival outcome

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and represents a significant cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1]. Particularly in East Asia, includingTaiwan HCC prevalence remains high due to chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections. In Taiwan, HCC ranks among the top cancers in incidence and mortality [2]. Although surveillance programs have improved early detection rates, a significant proportion of patients are still diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages when curative treatments such as surgical resection, transplantation, or radiofrequency ablation (RFA) are no longer feasible [3].

Systemic therapies have become the cornerstone for the treatment of advanced or unresectable HCC. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including combinations of anti‐PD‐L1 and anti‐VEGF agents (e.g., atezolizumab and bevacizumab) or anti‐PD‐1 and anti‐CTLA (e.g., durvalumab and tremelimumab), were the treatment landscape for advanced‐stage HCC [4, 5, 6]. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) were still considered the first‐line treatment because a substantial proportion of patients are not optimal candidates for ICI‐based therapy owing to contraindications to immunotherapy, concerns regarding bevacizumab‐related bleeding risk, autoimmune disease, transplant status, or other comorbid conditions. Sorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor, was the first systemic agent approved for advanced HCC and remained the standard of care for over a decade [7, 8], but its limited efficacy and high toxicity profile led to the development of newer agents. Lenvatinib, another multikinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR1‐3, FGFR1‐4, PDGFRα, RET, and KIT, was approved following the REFLECT trial [9], which demonstrated its non‐inferiority to sorafenib in overall survival (OS). Consequently, lenvatinib has been approved as a first‐line therapy for patients with advanced HCC who meet specific inclusion criteria.

Despite promising results in controlled clinical trials, real‐world data often present a different picture due to broader patient heterogeneity. Furthermore, several clinical and laboratory parameters have been investigated to identify subgroups that might derive greater benefit from systemic therapy. Among these, liver function scores such as the Albumin‐Bilirubin (ALBI) grade have gained traction for their ability to objectively assess hepatic reserve without the subjective variables included in the Child‐Pugh score [10].

Alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP), a well‐known tumor marker in HCC, also plays a role in prognostication and treatment response evaluation. Elevated baseline AFP levels have been associated with poor outcomes in various settings, including ICI and TKI [11, 12]. Moreover, the integration of locoregional therapies (LRT), such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and RFA, with systemic therapy is increasingly explored as a strategy to improve survival outcomes [13, 14].

In the contemporary treatment landscape, atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and durvalumab plus tremelimumab are widely recommended as preferred first‐line options for patients with unresectable HCC and preserved liver function [4, 5, 6]. However, lenvatinib remains clinically relevant because a substantial proportion of patients are not optimal candidates for ICI‐based therapy owing to contraindications to immunotherapy, concerns regarding bevacizumab‐related bleeding risk, autoimmune disease, transplant status, or other comorbid conditions. In addition, for some patients requiring rapid tumor shrinkage or a planned multimodal strategy with locoregional therapy, lenvatinib may still represent a practical first‐line option. Therefore, real‐world data clarifying which patients derive favorable outcomes from lenvatinib remain important for treatment selection in the immunotherapy era.

Taiwan has a distinct epidemiologic profile with a high prevalence of hepatitis B virus related HCC and substantial proportion of patients different from the enrolled subjects of REFLECT‐trail [8], warranting localized real‐world data to guide treatment strategies. This study aimed to assess the real‐world efficacy of lenvatinib in patients with unresectable HCC treated at a tertiary referral center in Taiwan.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This was a retrospective, single‐center cohort study conducted at. Taichung Veterans General Hospital Patients diagnosed with unresectable HCC according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines [15] and who received lenvatinib as a first‐line therapy between February 2020 and April 2023 were eligible for inclusion. Inclusion criteria were confirmed diagnosis of unresectable HCC, initiation of lenvatinib as first‐line systemic therapy, classified as Child‐Pugh class A, at least one measurable lesion per modified RECIST (mRECIST) criteria [16]. Exclusion criteria included Child‐Pugh class B or C, ALBI grade 3, poor performance status (ECOG ≥ 2), life expectancy < 2 months, noncompliance to treatment, or use of ICIs. Patients were restricted to Child‐Pugh class A and ECOG 0–1 to ensure a relatively homogeneous population and to reduce confounding related to advanced hepatic dysfunction. During the study period, the selection of first‐line systemic therapy was determined by both treatment availability and patient‐specific clinical considerations. In the earlier phase of the study period, ICI‐based combination therapies, such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, were not yet widely available or reimbursed in Taiwan. Therefore, lenvatinib was commonly used as a first‐line treatment. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethics committees of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CE23139B), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Data Collection

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics were collected from medical records. Data included age, sex, HBV and HCV status, macrovascular invasion (MVI), extrahepatic spread (EHS), serum bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), AFP level, and ALBI grade. Baseline AFP was analyzed using a cutoff value of 400 ng/mL. This cutoff has been widely adopted in clinical practice and guideline‐based risk stratification. This cutoff has been widely used in prior clinical trials, including the REFLECT study, and is associated with tumor burden and prognosis [8]. Tumor staging was categorized using the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification. Patients with tumor characteristics that excluded them from the REFLECT trial (tumor burden > 50% liver volume, main portal vein invasion) were labeled as REFLECT‐out.

Treatment and Evaluation

Lenvatinib was administered orally once daily at a dose of 8 mg (< 60 kg) or 12 mg (≥ 60 kg). Additional LRT, including TACE or RFA, was defined as combined therapy. LRT was administered either concurrently with or after the initiation of lenvatinib, depending on clinical indications. Tumor response was assessed radiologically every 4 to 8 weeks using mRECIST criteria [16]. The best oval responses were recorded and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the sum of CR and PR. Disease control rate (DCR) included CR, PR, and SD. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as time from treatment initiation to radiologic progression or death. OS was defined as time from treatment initiation to death or last follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) and compared using independent t‐tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, compared using Pearson's chi‐square test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models identified prognostic factors for OS and PFS. Variables with a p‐value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariate Cox regression models. In addition, clinically relevant variables (e.g., ALBI grade, AFP level, and treatment‐related factors) were included regardless of their univariate significance. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and differences were evaluated using the log‐rank test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study provides real‐world evidence on the effectiveness of lenvatinib in Taiwanese patients with unresectable HCC, focusing on liver function reserve (ALBI grade), AFP levels, and combination with LRT. The median OS of 17 months and PFS of 10 months compare favorably with the REFLECT trial (median OS 13.6 months; PFS 7.3 months) [9]. Possible explanations include patient selection (high portion of HBV patients and Child‐Pugh A only) and LRT integration.

The ALBI score has emerged as a reliable tool for stratifying liver function and predicting treatment outcomes in HCC. In addition to its role as a prognostic indicator, hepatic reserve may also influence treatment outcomes through its impact on drug tolerability and treatment exposure. In clinical practice, adverse events associated with lenvatinib, such as hypertension, fatigue, anorexia, and hepatic dysfunction, frequently lead to dose reductions or treatment discontinuation, which may compromise therapeutic efficacy. Although detailed safety and dose intensity data were not comprehensively available in this study, it is plausible that patients with better preserved liver function (ALBI grade 1) are more likely to tolerate lenvatinib, maintain higher relative dose intensity, and sustain treatment for a longer duration. In contrast, patients with poorer hepatic reserve may require more frequent dose modifications, leading to reduced drug exposure. Our findings confirm that ALBI grade 1, better‐preserved liver function, is a strong predictor of improved OS. This aligns with previous literature, which supports the superiority of ALBI grade over traditional Child‐Pugh scoring in predicting prognosis and treatment tolerability [10, 17]. Therefore, the superior survival outcomes observed in patients with ALBI grade 1 may reflect not only more favorable tumor biology and liver function, but also improved treatment tolerability and continuity. Future studies incorporating detailed analyses of adverse events, dose intensity, and treatment duration are warranted to further clarify these relationships.

The integration of LRT within a multimodal approach also significantly enhanced survival, suggesting a potential synergistic effect when combined with lenvatinib. Recent studies have increasingly endorsed the concept of combination therapy, especially in patients with intermediate‐stage HCC or high tumor burden [17, 18, 19, 20]. The reason might be that the combination of systemic therapy with TACE or RFA could enhance tumor control by synergistically targeting both microvascular spread and residual viable tumor [21]. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. In real‐world practice, patients selected for combined LRT are often those with more favorable baseline characteristics, including lower tumor burden, less extensive vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread, and better preserved hepatic function. Therefore, the observed survival benefit may be influenced by confounding by indication. As such, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating an association rather than a causal effect of LRT on survival outcomes. Future prospective studies are warranted to define optimal timing and patient selection for such strategies.

The use of an AFP cutoff of 400 ng/mL in this study is consistent with prior clinical trials and commonly used prognostic stratification in HCC, further supporting its clinical relevance. AFP ≤ 400 ng/mL emerged as an independent predictor for longer PFS, supporting its role as a tumor burden biomarker. Past study indicates that baseline AFP, as well as AFP kinetics during treatment, may serve as a surrogate marker for tumor burden, treatment responsiveness, and disease progression [22]. Recent studies suggest AFP dynamics may also guide treatment response and prognosis, particularly in targeted and immunotherapy settings [23, 24]. In our study, baseline AFP stratification successfully predicted treatment durability, supporting its inclusion in prognostic models. The lack of statistical association between AFP and OS may reflect the influence of post‐progression therapies or variations in subsequent treatment strategies. Furthermore, nearly 40% of patients in our study were classified as REFLECT‐out, yet still showed median OS exceeding that of the REFLECT trial, suggesting lenvatinib may retain utility in broader real‐world populations. Prospective validation in this subgroup is warranted. In the current immunotherapy era, our findings should be interpreted within a treatment‐selection framework rather than as a direct challenge to ICI‐based first‐line standards. Recent guidelines recommend atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, durvalumab plus tremelimumab, or nivolumab plus ipilimumab as preferred first‐line regimens for most patients with advanced HCC [4, 5, 6], whereas lenvatinib is generally considered an important alternative when ICI‐based therapy is unsuitable or less desirable [15]. Such scenarios may include patients with contraindications to immune checkpoint inhibition, clinically relevant autoimmune disease, post‐transplant status, or concern for bevacizumab‐related bleeding risk, particularly in those with untreated or high‐risk varices. Although direct head‐to‐head randomized comparisons between lenvatinib and current ICI‐based combinations are lacking, available evidence suggests that treatment choice should not rely on cross‐trial numerical comparisons alone.

Real‐world studies have further suggested that outcome differences among first‐line regimens may be modest in some practice settings, and that patient selection and safety considerations are often decisive. Within this context, our study provides practical information for identifying patients in whom lenvatinib may remain a reasonable first‐line option. In particular, the favorable outcomes observed in patients with ALBI grade 1 suggest that preserved hepatic reserve remains a key prerequisite for successful lenvatinib therapy. In addition, the association between combined LRT and improved OS raises the possibility that lenvatinib may be particularly useful in selected patients for whom a multimodal strategy is planned. Therefore, rather than positioning lenvatinib against immunotherapy as a universal competitor, our results help refine patient selection for lenvatinib in contemporary routine practice.

Another important consideration in interpreting OS in this study is the potential impact of post‐progression therapy. In real‐world practice, patients with unresectable HCC often receive multiple lines of treatment, including ICI‐based regimens, alternative TKIs, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), or best supportive care after disease progression. Although detailed data on post‐progression treatment were not comprehensively available in our cohort, it is well recognized that subsequent therapies can substantially influence OS outcomes. In particular, patients with better preserved hepatic reserve are more likely to receive additional systemic or locoregional therapies after progression, which may prolong survival independently of first‐line treatment effects. This may partly explain the relatively modest association observed between baseline factors such as AFP or ALBI grade and OS in our study. In this context, hepatic reserve may influence prognosis not only through its impact on initial treatment response but also by determining access to and tolerance of subsequent therapies. Future studies incorporating detailed treatment sequencing and post‐progression survival (PPS) analyses are needed to more precisely delineate the contribution of first‐line therapy versus subsequent treatment strategies to overall survival outcomes.

Although the treatment landscape of advanced HCC has evolved with the introduction of multiple ICI‐based regimens [15], lenvatinib continues to play an important role in clinical practice. In particular, it remains a relevant option for patients who are not suitable candidates for immunotherapy or bevacizumab‐containing regimens, as well as in regions where access to newer therapies may be limited. Therefore, the present study should not be interpreted as challenging current standard‐of‐care therapies, but rather as providing real‐world insights into patient selection and prognostic factors in patients treated with lenvatinib. Our study focuses on a clinically relevant subgroup rather than aiming to represent the entire real‐world population.

Our study has several limitations. First, this is a retrospective study, and selection or reporting bias may have existed. Second, our sample size was one single‐center cohort and the follow‐up period was relatively short. These may restrict generalizability. However, its strength lies in the homogeneous patient population and robust follow‐up. Third, the use of combined LRT may be subject to selection bias, as patients selected for LRT are more likely to have favorable baseline characteristics. Therefore, the observed association between LRT and improved survival should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, detailed information on treatment‐related adverse events, dose modifications, and relative dose intensity was not consistently available, which may limit the ability to fully assess the impact of treatment tolerability on survival outcomes. Furthermore, the study population was limited to patients with preserved hepatic function (Child‐Pugh A) and good performance status, which may not fully reflect the broader real‐world population. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and refine therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, lenvatinib remains a clinically meaningful first‐line option for selected patients with unresectable HCC in the immunotherapy era, particularly those combined with LRT, or having ALBI grade 1 or AFP ≤ 400 ng/mL. Our findings highlight the need for refined clinical algorithms incorporating liver function, AFP, and treatment timing to optimize outcomes in unresectable HCC.
