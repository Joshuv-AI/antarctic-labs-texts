Gut microbiota: a new perspective for bioavailability of selenium and human health
Authors: Xuehua Wang, Yan Zhong, Zhenzhou Zhu, Na Zhang, Xu Chen, Feifei Wang, Li Wang, Cheng Chen, Jiangling He, Shuyi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota can metabolise and transform selenium, and even compete with the host for it. This provides new insights into the bioavailability of selenium and the mechanisms through which it exerts its beneficial effects. This review provides a comprehensive summary of selenium metabolism and bioavailability, along with the relationship between selenium, the gut microbiota and some diseases. This may lay the groundwork for its application as a functional food.

Introduction

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element for life. In the body, it is mainly active in the form of selenoproteins. To date, 25 selenoprotein genes encoding selenocysteine-containing selenoproteins have been identified in the human genome. Most of these selenoproteins contain selenocysteine in their active site, which exerts their antioxidant capacity through selenocysteine-catalyzed redox reactions1. Selenium has been demonstrated to exert crucial physiological functions, such as antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and immune regulation. Moreover, deficiencies in this element are associated with the development of specific pathologies, including Keshan and Kashin-Beck disease2. However, excessive selenium intake can cause adverse health effects, including hair loss, skin lesions, and nail abnormalities, and may ultimately prove fatal. Consequently, the World Health Organisation (WHO) has established recommended daily intake levels for adults of 50–200 µg of Se, with a maximum safe dose of 400 µg3.

The digestion, absorption, and subsequent utilization of selenium in biological systems are the physiological significance of Se. From a nutritional perspective, bioaccessibility refers to the conversion of ingested selenium into a soluble form within the intestine, which enables it to pass across mucous membranes into the bloodstream. Conversely, bioavailability measures the proportion of consumed selenium or its bioactive metabolites that is ultimately absorbed and utilised for physiological functions or storage4,5. Studies showed that the relative bioavailability of selenite, selenate, and SeMet was 55.5–100%, 34.7–94%, and 22–330%, respectively4. However, the traditional assessment of selenium bioavailability does not take into account its utilization by gut microbiota. A previous study demonstrated that gut microbiota metabolizes both semethylselenocysteine and selenocyanate into SeMet. Additionally, the major urinary selenosugar—identified as 1β-methylseleno-N-acetyl-D-galactosamine—was also shown to be converted by the gut microbiota in the rats6. Given the important role of the gut microbiota in the metabolism of undigested food in the colon, there is a growing consensus that the concept of bioavailability needs to be reconsidered7.

Numerous studies have indicated that the onset and remission of many diseases are closely related to the host’s gut microbiota. This association has been observed in a range of conditions, including intestinal bowel diseases (IBDs), obesity, type 2 diabetes, hepatic steatosis, and some types of cancers8. Dietary selenium intake is a significant modulator of the structure of the host’s gut microbiota9,10. The relationship between selenium, intestinal microflora, and diseases has received attention10,11. For example, people with Kashin-Beck disease, which may be caused by selenium deficiency, have a different composition of gut microbiota. This is characterised by increased levels of Fusobacteria and Bacteroidetes, as well as higher levels of the genera Alloprevotella, Robinsoniella, Megamonas, and Escherichia-Shigella compared to healthy individuals12. However, there is a lack of summaries of studies that report that selenium ameliorates specific diseases and at the same time modulates the gut bacteria of the host.

The present article provides a comprehensive synthesis of existing research on selenium metabolism in animal organisms. Beyond this fundamental aspect, the discussion extends to two critical areas: the bioavailability of selenium and the factors influencing its absorption from different food matrices. Next, the relationship between Se and the gut microbiota is introduced. Finally, selenium alleviates some diseases with regulating the structure of gut microbiota is summarised. This review may help to better understand the process by which selenium exerts its functional effects.

Metabolism

Selenium occurs in three basic chemical states: organic compounds, inorganic species, and the elemental form. Of these, organic selenium compounds are the most abundant and biologically important form, accounting for the majority of dietary selenium. Within this category, selenomethionine (SeMet) is the most prevalent form in plant-based foods, while both SeMet and selenocysteine (SeCys) are dominant in animal-derived foods13. Inorganic selenium encompasses compounds such as selenite [Na2SeO3, Se(IV)] and selenite [Na2SeO4, Se(VI)]. Elemental selenium includes gray-black non-biologically active substances as well as red biologically active substances, along with selenium nanoparticles.

The hypothetical model of selenium metabolism in animal organisms is visually summarised in Fig. 1. When animals consume adequate selenium or selenium-enriched food, it enters the gastrointestinal tract to be digested and absorbed. The small intestine serves as the primary site of absorption. However, the specific transport mechanisms involved vary significantly depending on the chemical form of selenium14. Current knowledge suggests different absorption mechanisms: selenate utilizes sulfate co-transporters, whereas selenite enters by passive diffusion. Notably, selenium-containing amino acids are transported by the same amino acid transporters as their sulfur-containing counterparts1. After absorption, the liver—as the primary metabolic organ—converts selenium to selenide, a universal intermediate that facilitates the biosynthesis of specialized selenoproteins. The liver produces selenoprotein P (SELENOP), a carrier protein that enters the bloodstream and facilitates the delivery of selenium (Se) to various tissues. This distribution enables the production of other Se proteins in these organs1,14. Some of the non-absorbable fraction, which is unable to cross the intestinal barrier, could be utilised by the gut microbiota. In vivo, studies have shown that the gut microbiota can metabolize Se into different metabolites depending on its form, including SeMet transformed from semethylselenocysteine and selenocyanate6, elemental selenium transformed from selenate and selenite15, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) transformed from selenium nanoparticles16. In addition, the gut microbiota present in normal rats can convert 1β-methylseleno-N-acetyl-D-galactosamine, which is the major selenosugar compound found in rat urine6. Studies have also shown that the gut microbial community can convert selenium into various forms, including SeMet, SCFA, and produce dimethyl diselenide (DMDSe) and nano-sized selenium particles (red amorphous elemental selenium) in vitro17–19. The other non-absorbable fraction is excreted directly by several routes. This fraction is exhaled as volatile dimethyl selenide, a product of selenium methylation, and is also excreted in the urine as water-soluble trimethyl selenium ions and selenium-containing sugar compounds20.Fig. 1The hypothetical model of selenium metabolism in body.

Bioavailability and bioaccessibility

Bioaccessibility specifically denotes the portion of a compound liberated from its food matrix during gastrointestinal digestion, becoming available for intestinal absorption into the circulatory system. In contrast, bioavailability represents a nutritional efficacy concept, indicating the proportion of food-derived nutrients and bioactive substances that an organism can effectively utilize. Consequently, the bioavailability concept encompasses two fundamental aspects: bioaccessibility and subsequent biological activity21,22 (Fig. 2). The biologically active forms of selenium include total selenium concent, specific chemical species (such as SeMet, SeCys, selenocysteine (SeCys2), Se-methylselenocysteine (SeMeSeCys), selenate, and selenite), as well as functional selenoproteins like glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3) and SELENOP glutathione peroxidase (GPx) enzymatic activity4,6. It is important to note that conventional bioavailability metrics often overlook the fraction of selenium utilized by the host’s intestinal microbiota. Therefore, a more inclusive definition of bioavailability has been proposed, consisting of two distinct parts: the portion that enters systemic circulation directly to exert physiological effects, and the portion metabolized by the gut microbiota into bioactive compounds (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic diagram showing the new definition of the bioavailability and bioaccessibility of selenium.

Schematic diagram showing the new definition of the bioavailability and bioaccessibility of selenium.

Selenium bioavailability can only be assessed through an in vivo approach, whereas selenium bioaccessibility can be assessed through both in vivo and in vitro approaches. While in vivo studies provide more realistic physiological conditions and allow extensive sampling for pharmacokinetic analysis, the resulting bioavailability and bioaccessibility measurements are influenced by the specific animal model selected and the physiological status of the test subjects4. In vitro methods provide an effective approximation to in vivo scenarios and offer the advantages of simplicity, ease of control, low cost, and good reproducibility. In vitro approaches to assess selenium bioaccessibility include several methodologies: artificial gastrointestinal digestion systems, cellular absorption models using Caco-2 cell monolayers, and laboratory-based simulations of colonic fermentation processes4,23. They are used to measure bioaccessibility in the small intestine, bioaccessibility through the enterocytes into the bloodstream, and bioaccessibility in the large intestine (colon) (used by gut microbes).

Several critical factors have been identified as influencing selenium bioaccessibility and bioavailability, including the specific animal species, the physiological state of the organism, the chemical speciation of selenium, its concentration levels, and the compositional characteristics of the food matrix4,21. Selenium bioavailability and bioaccessibility of chemically pure forms

The in vivo studies undertaken to evaluate bioavailability focused chiefly on selenate, selenite, and SeMet. Studies showed that the relative bioavailability of selenite, selenate, and SeMet was 55.5–100%, 34.7–94%, and 22–330%, respectively; selenite, selenate, and SeMet supplementation significantly increased plasma Se levels by 19–530%, 58–275%, 25–413%, and GPx activity by 16–300%, 30–200%, and 29–174%, respectively4. The large range of variation in bioavailability is due to differences in dosage, in vivo modeling, and differences in evaluation criteria. To assess the bioavailability of various selenium forms under identical experimental conditions, researchers administered nine different selenium compounds to rats. The results demonstrated that the levels of GPX3 and SELENOP obtained from selenocyanate(SeCN) and Se-methylseleno-L-cysteine(MeSeCys) were notably greater compared to those derived from SeMet6. In addition, except for trimethylselenonium ion(TMSe), all of the selenium compounds tested increased the recovery of GPX3 (20–40%) and SELENOP (40–80%)6,24.

Research conducted using the Caco-2 cell model demonstrated that the bioaccessibility of five selenium-containing compounds increased over time. Notably, the bioaccessibility of SeCys (39.4%) was substantially greater than that of MeSeCys, SeMet, Se(IV), and Se(VI) after 120 min25. Moreover, a separate investigation revealed that selenium is absorbed more efficiently by Caco-2 cells in this order: SeMet > MeSeCys > Se(VI) > Se(IV)26. In vitro studies using the digestion model showed that the bioaccessibility of selenium nanoparticles ranged from 33.57% to 56.93%27.

Selenium bioaccessibility in Se-enriched food

A recent study explored the bioaccessibility of Se in various commonly consumed foods using in vitro digestion models. The findings indicated that the Se bioaccessibility in the small intestine varied significantly across different food categories: nuts (26.2%), cereals (35.6%), fruits (45.1%), vegetables (26.5%), legumes (42.7%), oils (90.8%), beverages (64.5%), dairy products (67.8%), chicken (46.2%), beef (81.9%), salmon (98.9%), cod (60.2%), eggs (60.1%), lamb (40.8%), and pork (40.8%)23. Another in vitro study found that the bioaccessibility of Se-enriched food supplements varied between 19.31% and 66.10% when combined with a food matrix28. In vivo studies further demonstrated that the relative bioavailability of selenium-enriched yeast (272%) was substantially greater compared to that of sodium selenite (144%), and ~75.9–82.3% of the total Se was converted to selenoproteins in plasma after Se-enriched yeast supplementation29.

The bioaccessibility of Se in the colon has attracted considerable attention due to the growing emphasis on the role of gut microbiota in Se utilization and metabolism. An in vitro fermentation study was conducted to examine the Se bioaccessibility in the healthy adult colon across various food groups, including nuts (49.7%), cereals (34.9%), fruits (39.5%), vegetables (41.9%), legumes (35.5%), oils (0.51%), beverages (1.40%), dairy products (23.9%), chicken (41.2%), beef (10.5%), salmon (0.94%), cod (30.7%), eggs (31.8%), lamb (55.4%), and pork (48%)23. Another in vitro fermentation study found that the bioaccessibility in the colon of SelenoPrecise tablets (Se-enriched yeast) and SeMet decreased from ~80% to 20% and 100% to 40%, respectively, with fermentation time when incubated with colonic microbiota30. This suggested the colon microbiota utilized or metabolism the Se thus resulting in the reducing Se bioaccessibility in the fermention system.

Microbiota

Selenium is a vital trace element required by numerous organisms. In species such as Escherichia coli, the biological functions of Se are primarily mediated by selenocysteine (Sec). Research has been conducted to assess the presence of the Sec trait across over 700 bacterial and archaeal genomes, revealing that Deltaproteobacteria and Firmicutes/Clostridia are particularly abundant in selenoproteins31. In addition, Se and its associated key genes have been analyzed in more than 2300 bacterial and archaeal genomes, identifying several candidate genes linked to selenium metabolism. These include a predicted Se-related transporter (YedE), a redox protein (YedF), a Se-specific transcriptional regulator (LysR_Se) likely responsible for regulating Sec-decoding gene expression, and a potential chaperone protein (DUF3343) implicated in the intracellular transport of Se or sulfur32.

Utilization or metabolism of selenium by microbiota

A summary of the current knowledge on microbial metabolism and utilisation of selenium conversion is provided in Fig. 3. Some microorganisms can reduce the Se0 element to form selenides. For example, Desulfurispirillum indicum showed metabolic flexibility to convert SeO42- to SeO32- and then to elemental selenium33. Bacillus subtilus DA20 has been reported to be capable of converting SeO32- to SeNPs34. The conversion of SeO32- into elemental selenium or selenium nanoparticles is widely acknowledged as a microbial detoxification mechanism occurring in the cytoplasm35. As selenium is a vital trace element required for selenoprotein synthesis, some Se-enriched probiotics such as, yeast, lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria have been observed to accumulate and biotransform inorganic selenium into selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) and organic selenium36–38. In Se-enriched yeast, there are some highly active organic selenium including SeCys, SeMet, selenocystationine, SeMeSeCys, and γ-glutamyl-Se-methylselenocysteine38. In Se-enriched lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria, SeCys and SeMet are important representatives of selenoproteins38. Recently, gamma-glutamyl-selenomethionine (γ-Glu-SeMet) was also identified as the primary Se metabolite in Se-enriched Bifidobacterium longum DD939. Additionally, the novel probiotic Se-enriched Akkermansia muciniphila was found to contain ~81.9% organic selenium. The selenium was predominantly found to be protein-bound selenium (44.3%, primarily in the form of SeCys2 and SeMet), followed by nucleic acid-bound selenium (17.8%) and polysaccharide-bound selenium (17.4%)40. Engineer Bacillus subtilis 168 was designed to successfully produce Seleno-methyl-selenocysteine (SeMCys)41, while B. subtilis BSN313 was also proved to produce SeMet as the highest content of organic Se byproduct42.Fig. 3The different forms of selenium that are metabolised by the microbiota.

In vitro fermentation, selenite was metabolized by the gut microflora of rats to DMDSe and red amorphous elemental selenium, while SeMet was metabolized to DMDSe43. Another study found that gut microflora primarily converts MeSeCys, SeCys2, and selenite to SeMet during in vitro fecal incubation; and red selenium nanoparticles were also observed when SeCys2 and selenite were incubated with gut microflora18. SCFAs are common beneficial metabolites of gut microbiota. The production of acetate (46–68%), propionate (351–126%), and butyrate (350–400%) were found in vitro fermentation to be significantly increased with SeMet and MeSeCys incubation18. In addition, supplementation with 0.9 mg/kg nanoSe showed significantly higher total SCFA production than sodium selenite and selenium yeast in vivo16. A study conducted on mice in vivo revealed that selenium supplementation led to significant changes in 31 metabolites in the brains of conventional mice, whereas only 26 metabolites were altered in mice with depleted microbiota. This finding highlights the crucial role of gut microbiota in the metabolic processing of selenium44.

The gut microbiota can also utilize host dietary selenium to synthesize and express their selenoproteins. Following the administration of selenium supplements, there was a significant increase in the expression of selenoprotein genes (Dio3, TrxR3, Gpx1, Gpx2, Gpx4, and SelT) in the brains of conventional mice. Conversely, in mice with depleted microbiota, these selenoprotein genes either demonstrated reduced expression or remained unchanged44. Furthermore, selenium supplementation resulted in a 1.61-fold increase in SELENOP levels and a 1.30-fold rise in selenoalbumin (SeAlb) concentrations in the testes of conventional mice. Conversely, in microbiota-depleted mice, SELENOP levels decreased by 1.85-fold, and SeAlb levels dropped by 2.00-fold in the testes45. A separate study observed that, under selenium-sufficient conditions, selenoprotein expression was similar in germ-free and conventional mice. However, under conditions of selenium deficiency, germ-free mice exhibited elevated glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) activities in the gut and liver, along with higher selenium levels in plasma and liver tissues46. It indicated that bacteria may compete with the host for selenium when availability becomes limiting.

Selenium modulates the structure of gut microbiota in animals

The gut microbiota can metabolize and utilize dietary selenium, while selenium, in turn, can influence the colonization and composition of the gut microbial community. However, data from population-based experiments showed that the difference between the gut microbiota of populations in selenium-supplemented or selenium-enriched areas and the general population was not very significant47,48. An in vitro fermentation study using feces from healthy adults showed that SeMet, MeSeCys, and SeCys2 induced an increase in α-diversity and Bacteroidetes abundance, whereas selenite induced an increase in F/B ratio and a decrease in Bacteroidaceae abundance18. Sodium selenite is commonly used as an inorganic selenium supplement in animal experiments. Sodium selenite supplementation at concentrations of 0.10, 0.40, or 2.25 mg/kg within ten weeks has been observed to enhance microbial diversity and elevate the relative abundance of Bacteriodales, while concomitantly reducing the proportions of Alistipes and Parabacteroides in 4-week-old C57BL/6 male mice17. In addition, 0.65 mg /kg sodium selenite diet significantly increased α-diversity and the abundance of Christensenellaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Lactobacillus compared to normal (0.20 mg/kg Se) diet in BALB/c mice63,64. However, another study observed that a dose of 0.40 mg/kg sodium selenite supplementary for 8 weeks did not significantly affect the α-diversity of the gut microbiota in C57BL/6 male mice, but led to notable declines in Dorea levels and increases in Turicibacter and Akkermansia levels compared to a selenium-deficient diet (<0.01 mg/kg). While Se-adequate (0.15 mg Se/kg) also significantly decreased the abundance of Dorea but did not affect the abundance of Turicibacter and Akkermansia49. The studies indicated that the dose of sodium selenite, the duration of supplementation, the strain, and the physiological state of the mice could be the reason for the different effects on the regulation of gut microbiota structure. In addition, the administration of 6.28 g/kg of sodium selenite and supplementation at 0.14 ppm were found to regulate the abundance of Verrucomicrobia differently in male SD rats65 and Wistar rats52. A basal diet containing 0.06 mg Se/kg Na2SeO3 resulted in a decline in the relative abundance of Firmicutes and an increase in Eggerthellaceae compared to a diet with moderate selenium levels, while a high Se diet decreased the relative abundance of Campilobacterota, Terrisporobacter, Candidatus_Soleaferrea, Campylobacteraceae, Candidatus_Arthromitus and increased the proportion of Ruminococcus_torques_group and Merdibacte in roosters58. In fish, sodium selenite-treated grass carp (300 µg/L) exhibited an increase in Pseudomonas abundance and a decrease in Aeromonas, Flavobacterium, and Defluviimonas levels in comparison with the control group53. In earthworms, the study found sodium selenate or sodium selenite increased bacterial diversity in earthworm casts as well as the relative abundances of Pseudomonas while decreasing the relative abundances of Aeromonas and Bacillus15. In ruminant animals, sodium selenite supplementation has been observed to increase the abundance of bacteria that degrade cellulose, such as Ruminococcus albus, Ruminococcus flavefaciens, and Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens in cows and bulls60,61,66,67. In addition, sodium selenite supplementation has been shown to upregulate the production of volatile fatty acids in ruminate animals, which contributes to its unique role in promoting ruminant immunity and growth9.

Nano-selenium supplements are the main form of elemental selenium. The administration of Se-enriched Aspergillus oryzae A02 (Nano-Se) was found to increase the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota and Staphylococcus, and reduce the relative abundance of Bacteroidota and Lactobacillus in mice68. High dose of selenium-nanoparticle (1.4 ppm) supplementation increased the ration of F/B and the relative abundance of Verrucomicrobiota, while a low dose of selenium-nanoparticle (0.14 ppm) decreased the ratio of F/B and increased the abundance of Akkermansia and Muribaculaceae in male Wistar rats52. In addition, Se nanoparticles supplementation were found to upregulate the abundance of Eluaimicrobia, Firmicutes, and Clostridiales Incertae Sedis XIII and downregulate the abundance of Proteobacteria, Tenericutes, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia in male SD rats65. In fish, the medium and high doses of selenium nanoparticles had an opposite effect on the α diversity of the gut microbiota69,70. In broilers, the 0.9 mg/kg nano-Se group exhibited an increase in the relative abundance of Faecalibacterium and Lactobacillus, but a decrease in Turicibacter compared to the control group. Conversely, a decrease in the abundance of Lactobacillus was observed when the nano-selenium dose was increased to 1.5 mg/kg16. Another study highlighted that supplementation with selenium nanoparticles (60 mg/kg) led to a significant reduction in total bacterial counts, yeast and mold, E. coli, and Salmonella, while markedly increasing lactic acid bacteria34. This finding underscores the capacity of nano-selenium to not only suppress detrimental pathogens but also to foster the proliferation of beneficial bacteria. Similar results were found in quail supplemented with chemical nano-selenium56.

The study on organic selenium examined the following supplements: selenium-enriched bacteria, Se-rich royal jelly, C. hupingshanensis aqueous extract, yeast selenium, 2-hydroxy-4-methylselenobutanoic acid, Se-enriched macroalgae, and selenium-rich diet. Se-enriched P. kudriavzevii increased the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Faecalibaculum in mice71. Se-rich royal jelly effectively increased the F/B ratio and the relative abundance of the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, Prevotellaceae UCG 001, Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group, and Oscillibacter in mice72. Organic selenium extracted from C. hupingshanensis aqueous supplementation significantly increased the α-diversity, as well as the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroides under low selenium diet (0.01 ppm selenium) in rats55. In addition, the administration of yeast selenium at doses of 0.15, 0.30, and 0.45 mg/kg resulted in a significant increase in the relative abundance of Veillonella, Turicibacter, and Lactobacillus, while concomitantly reducing the proportion of Stenotrophomonas in hens57. In pigs, 2-hydroxy-4-methylselenobutanoic acid (HMSeBA) supplementation increased the abundance of Firmicutes, the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroides, Terrisporobacter, and Intestinibacter, while decreasing the proportions of Bacteroides, Melainabacteria, Spirobacteria, Prevotellaceae and Megasphaera54. In addition, a significant increase in the relative abundances of unclassified_Lachnospiraceae, Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, and Oscillospirales was observed in the pigs on the first day of lactation, which was induced by the administration of yeast selenium73. A higher abundance of Prevotella copri has been identified in pigs with elevated levels of selenium in the longissimus dorsi muscle. Furthermore, supplementation with P. copri has been shown to enhance selenium deposition and improve meat quality in the longissimus dorsi muscle of fattening pigs74. Rabbitfish supplemented with a medium dose of Se-enriched macroalgae exhibited an increase in α-diversity, whereas those supplemented with a high dose exhibited a decrease67. In Chinese mitten crabs, a 1.5 mg/kg bio-fermented selenium diet increased the bacterial diversity and the relative abundance of Fusobacteriota, a type of butyrate-producing anaerobic bacterium75. Yeast selenium administered showed a positive effect on the rumen microbial community and metabolic function in Tibetan sheep, although significant differences in bacterial abundance were observed in response to variations in yeast selenium doses59.

Additionally, studies have been conducted to compare the regulatory effects of various forms of selenium supplements on the structure of gut microbiota. Selenium supplementation did not change the α-diversity in the gut microbiota of puppies, but sodium selenite promoted the enrichment of Catenibacterium, Holdemanella, and Ruminococcaceae UCG-014 while selenium-enriched yeast increased the relative abundance of Actinobacteria, Lachnospiraceae, and Dubosiella in dogs51. Another study found that the addition of SeMet, selenium yeast, and selenium nanoparticles specifically altered the abundances of Rubrobacter and Rubritalea, Winogradskyella and Motilimonas, and Photobacterium in Pacific white shrimp, respectively50.

In conclusion, though two small human studies found no significant effect of selenium supplementation on the structure of the adult gut microbiota, most animal studies have shown that different forms of selenium supplements significantly regulate the structure of gut microbiota in animals. The structure of the gut microbiota in the host is significantly influenced by several key factors: chemical forms of selenium, the composition of the selenium-containing dietary matrix, the dosage of selenium, and the species and physiological state of the animal. Due to the difficulties involved in translating animal research findings into human clinical studies, there is a need to conduct more randomised controlled trials to evaluate the effects of different forms and dosages of selenium supplements on the composition and function of human gut microbiota.

Selenium, microbiota, and diseases

Deficiency or excess of Se supplementation has been reported to cause some diseases such as Keshan disease, thyroid disease, nail and hair loss, and gastrointestinal disorders2,10. On the other hand, selenium in the appropriate dose range plays a central role in the prevention and modulation of several diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), cancer, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular diseases, fertility disorders, inflammation, and infections, mainly due to the biological functions of selenoproteins exerted by essential enzymes in vivo2,11. Furthermore, insufficient selenium intake resulted in a gut microbiota profile that was more susceptible to dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis and Salmonella typhimurium infection, while adequate selenium levels optimized the gut microbiota to enhance resistance against such intestinal diseases49. This suggests that the preventive and palliative effects of selenium on a range of diseases may be modulated through two pathways: the biological function of selenoproteins and the modulation of the structure of the gut microbiota11. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

IBD is a group of chronic, recurrent autoimmune bowel diseases, such as Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), characterized by an imbalance in the gut microbiota and dysfunction of the immune system. SELENOP is a plasma protein that carries more than 50% plasma selenium. Epidemiological studies have shown a negative correlation between plasma levels of SELENOP and the incidence and severity of IBD76. In addition, a prospective cohort analysis showed that participants with the highest Se intake had a significantly lower risk of developing IBS than those with the lowest intake77. Animal models further suggested that dietary selenium deficiency exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice, whereas sodium selenite supplementation alleviated the colitis severity, and increased the relative abundance of Turicibacter and Akkermansia while decreasing the abundance of Dorea49. Another study showed that a low Se diet also induced IBS symptoms in normal mice, and increased the relative abundance of Faecalibaculum and Helicobacter while decreasing the abundance of Bifidobacterium and Akkermansia77.

In addition, other studies indicated that selenium-enriched or selenated polysaccharides, selenium nanoparticles, selenium-enriched bacteria, selenium yeast, Se-containing peptides, and selenium nanoparticles can both reverse the disrupted gut microbiota composition induced by DSS and attenuates DSS-induced colitis in mice.

For example, Se-enriched tea polysaccharides and low dosage of glucosamine selenium ameliorated DSS-induced UC and increase the α-diversity of the gut microbiota in mice78,79. Similarly, treatment with selenylated α-D-1,6-glucan or κ-selenocarrageenan oligosaccharides increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Firmicutes in DSS-induced mice80,81. Besides, both selenium-enriched polysaccharides promoted the production of SCFAs80,81. However, the administration of selenated soluble dietary fiber obtained from millet led to an increase in the proportional presence of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, Lactobacillus, while reducing the proportions of Proteobacteria, Ruminococcaceae, Porphyromonadaceae82. Different selenium-enriched or selenated polysaccharides influence the composition of the gut microbiota in mice with DSS-induced colitis, with the effects varying according to the type of polysaccharide supplementation administered. A similar phenomenon was also found in Se-enriched yeast, B. longum DD98, and Pichia kudriavzevii supplemented mice with DSS-induced colitis. Se-enriched yeast and B. longum DD98 both upregulated the relative abundance of Firmicutes while Se-enriched B. longum DD98 and P. kudriavzevii both upregulated the α-diversity71,83,84. Se-containing soybean peptide supplementation also increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes in DSS-induced colitis mice85. Selenium nanoparticles synthesized from Dumar polysaccharide and Lactobacillus reuteris both restored the structure of the gut microbiota near the control in DSS-induced mice86,87. Moreover, selenium nanoparticles also attenuated X-ray-induced colitis by regulation of selenoprotein expression, especially promoted GPX4 expression and reversed the decrease of Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, Actinobacteriota induced by radiation in colitis mice88.

Metabolic disease

Obesity and diabetes are prevalent metabolic disorders. The microbiota produces a wide range of metabolites, including SCFAs and lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which interact with host cell receptors. These interactions have the potential to either stimulate or suppress signaling pathways associated with metabolic disorders8.

Supplementation with selenium-enriched polysaccharides, including selenium-enriched chrysanthemum polysaccharide89 and selenium-enriched Ziyang green tea polysaccharide90, led to an increase in the relative abundance of Akkermansia in mice on a high-fat diet. Similarly, the addition of selenium-enriched kiwi fruit and chitosan-stabilized selenium nanoparticles was shown to increase the relative abundance of Akkermansia in mice with HFD diet91,92. Akkermansia is a mucus-degrading bacterium that produces acetic acid and butyric acid. Many studies have demonstrated the probiotic benefits of Akkermansia, including alleviating obesity and metabolic disorders caused by a high-fat diet93. In addition, the relative abundance of Bacteroides was increased after intervention with Se-enriched Ziyang green tea polysaccharide and Se-enriched kiwi fruit in mice on a high-fat diet90,91, and after intervention with nano-sized selenium in fish on a high-fat diet94. As glycan-degrading bacteria, Bacteroides spp. not only help to provide nutrients for microbial life in the gut but also help to reduce hydroperoxide95. Moreover, Nano-sized selenium supplementation promoted the production of SCFAs such as acetic acid, isobutyric acid, butyric acid, and isovaleric acid94. Compared with Panax notoginseng(PN), Se-enriched PN increased the relative abundance of Muribaculaceae, Rikenellaceae, and Erysipelotrichaceae in high-fat diet mice, as well as the activity of glutathione (GSH)96. Se contributed to weight gain and regulation of inflammation-related gene expression in mice with high fat-high sucrose diet, as well as to regulation the gut microbiota structure by increasing the relative abundance of Lactobacillaceae, Peptostreptococcaceae while decreasing the abundance of Eggerthellaceae, Oscillospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Lachnospiraceae97. However, Gao et al.98 found that the β-diversity of the gut microbiota in the Se-deficient black tea group was different from that in the HFD group, whereas little difference was detected between the HFD group and the Se-rich black-tea group, although both two kinds of black tea improved weight gain induced by high-fat diet.

A previous investigation revealed that SeMet and SeSer alleviate the metabolic abnormalities in db/db diabetic mice with an increase in the α-diversity and the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Lactobacillus murinus and a decrease in Corynebacterium glutamicum, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Aerococcus urinaeequi99. As a potential probiotic, L. murinus has been reported to play an important role in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis and anti-inflammatory properties100. SeMet supplementation also helps to alleviate transient middle cerebral artery occlusion in mice with an increase in the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae, Lactobacillaceae, Deferribacteraceae, and a decrease in Enterobacteriaceae101. The unclassified Lachnospiraceae was associated with a lower risk of T2D by reducing ketone body levels102. In addition, selenated polysaccharides derived from diverse polysaccharide sources demonstrate distinct regulatory impacts on the gut microbiota of diabetic mice, potentially attributable to the differing effects of the polysaccharides themselves. In contrast to the diabetic model group, the administration of selenized green tea polysaccharides resulted in a reduction in the relative abundance of Firmicutes and an increase in unclassified Oscillospiraceae103. Conversely, sweet corn cob selenium polysaccharide led to a decrease in the relative abundance of Verrucomicrobia and an increase in Firmicutes, Lactobacilluss_murinus, Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_539, Clostridium_CIEAF_017 and Oscillibacter_sp_1–3104. The intervention of selenated green tea polysaccharides reversed the decline in the α-diversity and relative abundance of unclassified_Oscillospiraceae in diabetic mice, which were reported to have the ability to synthesize butyrate103. In addition, both ordinary black tea and Se-enriched black tea showed the capacity to prevent hyperglycemia, while only the Se-enriched black could reverse the downregulation of the abundance of Bacteroides induced by STZ in diabetic mice105.

A high-salt diet-induced hypertension, excessive alcohol consumption, or reagent-induced liver injury can also lead to metabolic disorders in the body. The present study demonstrated that both selenium-enriched and ordinary green tea markedly increased the Chao index and reduced the F/B ratio in mice with hypertension caused by a high-salt diet. Se-enriched green tea increased the relative abundance of Allobaculum and Bifidobacterium compared to the green tea group106. A positive correlation was observed between the levels of Allobaculum and the expression of a pivotal regulator involved in lipid metabolism107. The present study investigated the efficacy of selenium-enriched Lactobacillus fermentum FZU3103 and selenium-enriched Lactobacillus rhamnosus GGG in attenuating alcoholic liver injury. The findings indicated that both strains exhibited superiority to L. fermentum FZU3103 and L. rhamnosus GGG, respectively, in attenuating alcoholic liver injury. In addition, both strains demonstrated greater efficacy compared to sodium selenite108,109. Se-enriched L. fermentum FZU3103 and L. rhamnosus GGG both increased the abundance of Clostridia_UCG-014, Ruminococcus, Lactobacillus, as well as SCFAs and GSH-Px levels and decreased the abundance of Enterorhabdusin in mice that had consumed alcohol in excess. Conversely, sodium selenite elicited an increase in the abundance of Eubacterium ruminantium group, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, Bacteroides, Alistipes while concurrently decreasing the abundance of Desulfovobrio, Lachnospiraceae UCG-006, Candidatus Saccharimonas, and Enterorhabdus108,109. In addition, both GSH and Se-enriched black garlic with SeMet and selenomethylcysteine (SeMC) meliorated acute liver failure induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and D-galactosamine with increasing α-diversity and the abundance of Lactobacillus and decreasing the abundance of Acidobacteria and Escherichia-Shigella110.

Heavy metal and toxic substance poisoning

As industrialization progresses, there is a concomitant increase in the variety and frequency of human exposure to and utilization of heavy metals and toxic substances. There is growing epidemiological evidence to indicate that heavy metals have a disruptive effect on the gut microbiota.

Sodium selenite supplementation restored the gut microbiota disturbances induced by heavy metal (As, Hg, Cd) exposure, such as the reversal of the increase in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus, Mucispirillum, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, Oscillospiraceae, Peptococcaceae, Muribaculaceae and Tannerellaceae, and the decrease in Roseburia induced by heavy metal (As, Hg, Cd) exposure111. Besides, Untargeted brain metabolomics revealed important impairments caused by heavy metals, such as increased N-acetyl aspartic acid levels, which were modulated by Se to control group levels111. Selenium treatment resulted in a partial restoration of the gut microbiota and enhanced catabolism and excretion of mercury (Hg). Specifically, sodium selenite reversed the decrease in the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, Oscillospira, Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Paraprevotella, Roseburia, and the increase in Firmicutes, Ruminococcus, Anaeroplasma, Lactobacillus, Clostridium and the F/B ratio in rats exposed to MeHg112. Se‑enriched B. subtilis reduces Hg toxicity in carp by decreasing the relative abundance of Aeromonas sobria113. A series of studies was conducted to investigate the regulatory impacts of various forms of selenium on the gut microbiota of mice subjected to lead acetate. The results demonstrated that selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) reduced the relative abundance of Desulfovibrio and elevated the abundance of Coprococcus in mice following lead acetate exposure. While selenium-enriched Lactobacillus rhamnosus SHA113 enhanced the abundance of Odoribacter and Desulfovibrio, while decreasing the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium in mice after lead acetate exposure114.

More studies pay attention to selenium helping to Cd exposure in animals. Cardamine hupingshanensis is a hyperpolymerised selenium plant. Research has demonstrated that this plant induces a decrease in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, Desulfovibrio, and Ruminococcus, and an increase in Verrucomicrobia, Bifidobacterium, and Akkermansia in mice compared with the CdCl₂ exposure group115. Se-enriched Lactobacillus plantarum provided significant protection to fish against the toxic effects of cadmium at subchronic levels with a decrease in the relative abundance of Aeromonas_caviae and Gemmobacter_sp. _yp3, Cetobacterium_somerae and an increase in the relative abundance of Burkholderia, Caballeronia, Paraburkholderia_unclassified, and Sphingopyxis_sp._DS68–4116. Moreover, compared to the Cd exposure model group, only Se-enriched Rosa roxburghii Tratt polysaccharide increased the relative abundance of Bifidobacteriales in mice, but not Rosa roxburghii Tratt polysaccharide117.

Benzo(a) pyrene, zearalenone, deoxynivalenol, patulin, ochratoxin A, and microplastics are common carcinogens from food or the environment, and diquat is a common pesticide. Se-enriched black soy protein (SeBSP) significantly prevented colonic toxicity of benzo(a) pyrene with higher levels of acetic, propionic, butyric acids, and an increase in the abundance of Bacteroides caecimuris, Lachnospiraceae bacterium, L. reuteri, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron while a decrease in Bacteroides, Alloprevotella, Parabacteroides118. Se-enriched Pediococcus acidilactici MRS‑7 helped to protect against patulin-induced jejunal injury in mice with a decrease in the abundance of Desulfovibrio, Alistipes, Clostridia, and an increase in Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Turicibacter, Aerococcus, and Akkermansia119. Selenium nanoparticles(SeNPs)-enriched Lactobacillus casei ATCC 393 mitigated intestinal barrier dysfunction caused by deoxynivalenol with an increase in the relative abundance of Firmicutes and total SCFAs levels, along with a concomitant decrease in Proteobacteria and Helicobacter120. SeNPs have been demonstrated to restore disrupted gut microbiota composition due to diquat exposure, resulting in an elevation in the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, Bacteroides, Verrucomicrobiota, Cyanobacteria, Patescibacteria, Akkermansia, Muribaculaceae, Parabacteroides, and a decline in Firmicutes, Campilobacterota, Helicobacter, and Ileibacterium121. Furthermore, the administration of Se nanoparticles has been shown to prevent or ameliorate the intestinal barrier damage induced by zearalenone in mice122, ochratoxin A in chickens123 and microplastics in fish124. It has also been observed to be concomitant with an increase in the abundance of Alloprevotella and Muribaculaceae in mice, Lactobacillus_johnsonii and Leuconostocaceae in chickens, and an increase in the α-diversity and a decrease in the abundance of Proteobacteria and Aeromonas in fish122–124. Alloprevotella and Muribaculaceae are beneficial bacteria that produce SCFAs122.

Bacterial infection

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC), Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis (S. Enteritidis), and Campylobacter jejuni are the most common bacterial foodborne pathogens worldwide, causing diarrhea and intestinal barrier dysfunction in humans and animals.

A previous study found that Lactobacilli strains effectively attenuated the immune response when mice were infected with E. coli O157:H7, and selenium nanoparticle-enriched Lactiplantibacillus plantarum elevated the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium spp. and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii compared to the model group125. The administration of selenium nanoparticle-enriched Lactococcus lactis NZ9000 led to the alleviation of ETEC K88-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in mice, resulting in a reduction in the relative abundance of norank_f_Muribaculaceae, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes_bacterium_M10-2, and an increase in Epsilonbacteraeota126. In addition, the administration of selenium-enriched mannan oligosaccharide led to a significant improvement in ETEC-induced diarrhea in weaned piglets. This was characterized by a decrease in the α-diversity, aerobic and gram-negative bacteria, and an increase in gram-positive bacteria127.

The impact of various selenium sources on egg quality in layers exposed to S. Enteritidis is a subject of interest. It has been observed that these sources exhibit distinct regulatory effects on the gut microbiota. Specifically, sodium selenite has been observed to elevate the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae and Christensenellaceae, while yeast selenium has been shown to enhance the abundance of Megamonas and Sphingomonas. In addition, selenium-enriched yeast cultures have been demonstrated to stimulate the abundance of Fusobacterium and Lactococcus128. In addition, supplementation with yeast selenium and selenium-enriched yeast cultures exhibited higher α-diversity compared to sodium selenite in hens, suggesting that organic selenium may be more effective than inorganic selenium in enhancing the richness of the gut microbiota in hens infected with S. Enteritidis128. However, the impact of Se yeast on the gut microbiota structure and its positive modulation in broilers colonized with C. jejuni was not significant129.

Neurodegenerative disease

The microbiota-gut-brain axis became apparent with the discovery that certain types of bacteria in the gut produce hormone- and neurotransmitter-like chemicals. Se-containing peptides showed anti-neuroinflammatory effects in mice injured by D-galactose (D-gal) or LPS, accompanied by modulation of the gut microbiota. For instance, selenium peptides derived from selenium-enriched Cordyceps militaris may serve as a potential dietary supplement for the prevention or treatment of Alzheimer’s disease induced by LPS. This is accompanied by an increase in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Alistipes, and a decrease in Akkermansia and Bacteroides130. In addition, selenopeptide demonstrated superior anti-neuroinflammatory efficacy compared to the native peptide in the experimental model of LPS-induced neuroinflammation in mice. This observation was accompanied by a significant increase in α-diversity and the relative abundance of Lactobacillus131,132. Se-containing peptides from Cardamine enshiensis (SeCPPs) were superior to Na2SeO3 and SeMet in alleviating the effect induced by D-gal. The administration of varying doses of SeCPPs was demonstrated to elicit disparate regulatory effects. The results demonstrated that low-dose SeCPPs induce an increase in the relative abundance of Akkermansia, middle-dose SeCPPs result in an increase in Firmicutes, while high-dose SeCPPs result in an increase in Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Proteobacteria133.

SeMet, Se-containing polysaccharides, chemically synthesized Se nanoparticles, Se-enriched B. subtilis, and Se-enriched egg and larvae powder also showed good regulation of gut microbiota with cognitive dysfunction. Supplementation with SeMet and selenium-containing α-D-1,6-glucan (Se-CPA) both have been demonstrated to enhance the α-diversity of intestinal flora and elevate the relative abundance of Akkermansia134,135. Moreover, Se-CPA has been shown to have more pronounced effects than α-D-1,6-glucan in addressing early cognitive dysfunction134. In addition, chemically synthesized lentinan-functionalized selenium nanoparticles have been shown to ameliorate surgery-induced cognitive dysfunction by increasing α-diversity and the abundance of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Lachnospiraceae, while concomitantly reducing the abundance of Proteobacteria136. Quercetin-decorated selenium nanoparticles have been shown to alleviate D-gal and HFD-induced cerebral impairment, accompanied by a substantial augmentation in α-diversity and the population of Akkermansia and Bacteroides137. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of selenium-enriched B. subtilis and selenium nanoparticle-enriched Lactobacillus casei ATCC 393 to mitigate cognitive dysfunction in fish and mice, respectively. These findings suggest a substantial modification of the intestinal microbiota composition following exposure to perfluorohexanoic acid or D-gal/aluminum chloride138,139. Se-enriched egg significantly maintained intestinal flora homeostasis in D-gal-induced aging mice with reversing the D-gal-induced decline in Blautia140. In vivo experiments revealed that Se-enriched Chrysomyia megacephala (Fabricius) larvae powder possessed antioxidant properties, effectively counteracting the rise in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and the decline in the activities of GPx and GSH, which were induced by D-gal141.

Immunological disorders, cancers, and others

Selenium and Se-enriched materials have also been found to be helpful in some immunological disorders, cancers, heat, cold or high alkalinity-induced stress, chronic fatigue syndrome, and antibiotic-induced disorders by regulating the structure of the gut microbiota.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Se-enriched S. cerevisiae supplements were both able to increase microbiota diversity in autoimmune encephalomyelitis mice, but only Se-enriched S. cerevisiae was able to reduce the increased absolute abundance associated with the disease142. The present study investigates the effects of selenium-enriched protein derived from Klebsiella pneumoniae (ADS18-Se) on the immune system of broilers. The results demonstrate that ADS18-Se effectively enhances the immune system in comparison to both the control group and the sodium selenite group. Furthermore, ADS18-Se has been shown to elevate the relative abundance of Lactobacilli spp. and Bifidobacteria spp., while reducing the abundance of E. coli and Salmonella spp143. In addition, Se-rich tea polysaccharide attenuated anaphylaxis with the regulation of gut microbiota by increasing the abundance of Lactobacillus and norank_f_Muribaculaceaek144.

Sodium selenite inhibited the occurrence of colorectal cancer (CRC) in mice with increasing the abundance of Akkermansia and Faecalibaculum145, SeMet inhibited the development of CRC Tumors with downregualted the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia_UCG-014, Helicobacter, Escherichia-Shigella, and upregualetd the abundance of Prevotellaceae_UCG-001146. Both Se-enriched microalgal protein and Se-enriched A. muciniphila were demonstrated to elevate the abundance of A. muciniphila in murine models of breast cancer under high-fat diet conditions and in colon tumour-bearing mice, concomitantly effectively attenuating disease progression147,148. The curcumin-modified selenium nanoparticles significantly inhibited S180 tumors in S180 tumor-bearing ICR mice, and enhanced the α-diversity and Limosilactobacillus abundance compared to curcumin149.

Nano-Se could counteract heat stress-induced intestinal damage in fish by upregulating the selenoproteins expression and restoring the increase in the relative abundance of Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Methylobacterium, Akkermansia, Deinococcus and the decrease in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria150. Se-enriched probiotics decreased diarrhea incidence rate in pigs and improved the production performance under high temperatures with the higher numerically changed fecal microbial flora, blood Se concentration, and GSH-Px activity than normal probiotics and sodium selenite group151. Se2NaO3 protects Yellow Catfish (Tachysurus fulvidraco) from low-temperature damage with an increase in the α-diversity, the relative abundance of Brevundimonas, Chryseobacterium, Sphingobium, Sphingomonas, and Flavobacterium, and a decrease in the relative abundance of Plesiomonas152. The administration of Se-enriched L. plantarum has been shown to alleviate the adverse effects of high alkalinity on the microbiota-gut-blood systems of fish. This process involves the promotion of Lactobacillus, Romboutsia, Dubosiella, Barnesiella, Thermomonas, and Akkermansia, while concomitantly inhibiting the growth of Bacteroidota, Providencia sp., and Vibrio. In addition to this, Se-enriched L. plantarum has been observed to reduce the levels of LPS in fish153. The study found that sweet corn cob polysaccharide selenium nanoparticles alleviated symptoms of chronic fatigue syndrome in mice with increasing the Chao1 and Shannon indices, as well as the abundance of Bacteroidota. The nanoparticles also enhanced GSH-Px levels154. Another study found that sodium selenite alleviated skeletal muscle atrophy induced by dexamethasone by regulating gut microbiota structure to Lactobacillus and Dubosiella enterotype155.

Se-deficient diet in mice after antibiotic administration resulted in a decrease in the GPx activity and the relative abundance of Lactobacillus reuteri156. Sodium selenite supplementation restored liver function in mice after antibiotic administration with an increase in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus spp, Ruminococcaceae, and Bacteroidetes157. Another study found that Se-enriched Bacillus longum DD98 has therapeutic potential in repairing antibiotic-induced dysbiosis of gut flora by increasing the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus and decreasing the relative abundance of Enterobacter to the control group level158. These studies showed that Se supplementation upregulated the relative abundance of Lactobacillus, which are commonly considered probiotics, in mice after antibiotic administration.

Conclusion and perspective

This review provides a comprehensive overview of the recent advancements in understanding the bioavailability of selenium in the small and large intestines, along with its impact on the composition of gut microbiota and its role in various diseases. Multiple factors modulate selenium bioavailability and gut microbial structure. These include the chemical form of selenium, dietary selenium concentration, dosage levels, and host-related variables such as species specificity and physiological status. Emerging evidence also indicates that selenium may ameliorate certain pathological conditions through dual mechanisms: by modulating gut microbiota ecology and by regulating selenoprotein expression pathways.

The absorption and utilization of selenium within the colon represent a compelling research focus, particularly given its role in modulating gut microbiota and potential to improve gut health through ameliorating certain disease symptoms. However, a deeper understanding of selenium’s bioavailability and its mechanistic impacts on intestinal health requires resolution of several fundamental questions: (1) The involvement of gut microbiota has complicated the concept of selenium bioavailability, necessitating a re-evaluation of its definition. This revised perspective should also extend to other specific diets that interact with gut flora; (2) Although abundant animal studies support a connection between dietary selenium and the modulation and utilization of gut microbiota, human in vivo evidence remains limited and warrants further validation; (3) While clinical data associate selenium deficiency with diseases such as Keshan and Kashin-Beck disease, no population-based or in vivo studies have explored gut microbiota regulation following selenium supplementation in these conditions; (4) The regulatory effect of selenium on gut microbiota is influenced by multiple factors. These complexities challenge the consistency of experimental outcomes, underscoring the need for large-scale data and advanced analytical systems to identify robust patterns; (5) Systematic investigation into how different selenium forms regulate key gut bacteria and their metabolites remains an ongoing task. Such research is essential to enable effective dietary selenium application and precise microbiota modulation. For instance, future studies should examine how specific probiotics—including A. muciniphila, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus—utilize dietary selenium and convert it into bioactive compounds such as SeMet and SeCys.
