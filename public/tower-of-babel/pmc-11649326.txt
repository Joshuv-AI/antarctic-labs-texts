Omelette and chocolate milk
Authors: Carol Lobo
Published: 2024

On a sunny morning, as I sat in the outpatient department of an HIV and palliative care unit in rural India, a little six-year-old soul with big brown eyes and beautiful long eyelashes arrived and immediately captured my attention. I’ll call her B. Her dishevelled, short, dull-brown hair framed her tiny face, and she wore a faded t-shirt and pants that were far too big for her 10-kg frame. Clutching the hands of her guardian tightly, she kept looking at the floor. The staff nurses and I were taken aback at her malnourished state.

B’s tragic journey had begun a month earlier, when she’d lost both her parents to HIV. She had no siblings. Her sole caretaker was her 80-year-old grandmother, who was unable to fully care for her. She had sent B with a distant relative to our hospital.

At first, B refused to acknowledge anyone. She seemed weary and had a high-grade fever with a severe cough, which led to a diagnosis of pneumonia. We initially admitted her to an isolation room as per our hospital protocols. She was unable to eat, crying out in pain with every swallow, a result of florid oropharyngeal candidiasis.

A desperate night unfolded for B as she writhed in pain, her cries piercing the silent corridors of the hospital. I was paged to have a look at her. Acetaminophen, antifungal mouth paint, and mouth rinses had been tried to no avail. I felt helpless and was desperate for anything to alleviate her suffering. At 3 am, in a spontaneous moment of brilliance, a nurse suggested using lidocaine spray. We sprayed and prayed for a respite from her pain. It helped and she was able to start on a liquid diet, although she was still unable to tolerate solid foods. I stayed with B through the night. Gradually, as the antifungals began to take effect, her pain began to fade.

Daily, after morning rounds, I would sit and try to talk with her. Initially, she would look down or turn away when spoken to, giving me the cold shoulder, and would respond only to her grandmother’s phone calls, but over the next two weeks, she began to warm up to me. I realized she was struggling to process the loss of her parents and was angry about being left alone in an unfamiliar place. Once her pneumonia resolved, we were able to move her to the common ward, where she found a new friend.

Mrs. A, a woman in her late forties who had recently received a diagnosis of HIV, was in hospital for treatment and rehabilitation. She had lost her husband to the same disease. Owing to the stigma around HIV, her family had ostracized her and forbade her from seeing her two children. Mrs. A was thrilled when she met B. Here was a child for her to love. She tended to B with utmost care, gently combing her hair every morning, feeding her meals, taking strolls in the evening, and rocking her to sleep at night. In B, she found the family she longed for. I was happy to see them bonding and that each had found a friend.

B’s recovery was not without incident. At one point, she refused to eat. Remembering she had told me how much she loved eggs and chocolate, I grabbed a bottle of Hershey’s chocolate syrup from my room and asked the kitchen staff to quickly prepare an omelette and chocolate milk. B’s spirits lifted visibly as the aroma of the omelette wafted through the hospital corridors.

But soon, B became increasingly selective about her eating habits, refusing regular meals and insisting on eating only omelettes. Mrs. A, determined to bring joy to her charge, indulged the child, occasionally sneaking in extras. After all, what could be the harm? However, I was worried that an unbalanced diet could worsen her pre-existing nutritional deficiencies. In a moment of frustration, I reprimanded Mrs. A for giving in to B all the time and admonished her and the staff, asking them to be stricter; the child needed other foods, especially vegetables. Watching me being harsh toward the person she adored most in the hospital, B’s face hardened. She stopped speaking to me. It broke my heart. It had taken me more than a month to establish her trust and I could sense the old rift opening. My choice to be firm and limit the things she loved didn’t change her picky eating. It made her feel alone. I had harmed the very relationship I was aiming to nurture.

It took coaxing and another meal of an omelette and chocolate milk as a peace offering to make amends. However, I was still unable to resolve her fussy food habits.

At my wit’s end, I went to the nearest tertiary care centre and consulted a dietitian, who provided me with an appropriate diet chart. With the right diet, B started gaining weight and turned out to be a chatterbox. She told us stories from her village, about the love of her grandmother, her friends, and her longing to return home. Listening to her chat brought me immense joy and a sense of accomplishment. She had come a long way from the withdrawn, shy child who first arrived at our hospital, and I was delighted to see her becoming healthier and more vibrant.

After her long rehabilitation, B was eventually discharged. Because her grandmother could no longer afford to look after her, she was sent to a school attached to our centre. Mrs. A was also discharged and went back to her home-town. I moved hospitals and lost touch with both.

Over the years I’ve often wondered whether Mrs. A was allowed to be reunited with her children and how B was coping in school. I have been curious to know if the little girl outgrew omelettes and chocolate milk, and whether she ever learned to like eating vegetables. Above all, I’ve wondered whether she missed Mrs. A’s motherly love.

My encounter with these two patients had a profound impact on my practice. I realized that a person who initially seems withdrawn may have some unmet need, and taking the time to figure that out has become a priority for me. I also learned the importance of collaboration with allied health care professionals in providing holistic care for patients, like the dietician who was instrumental in addressing B’s dietary issues.

But the biggest lesson came later, when I became a mother myself. I only really understood the depth of Mrs. A’s compassion when my own daughter started having food tantrums. In those moments, I would cook her an omelette, which would delight her. Her happiness brought me a profound sense of peace. I knew then that Mrs. A’s omelette and chocolate milk weren’t simply a pacifying indulgence. They were a gift of love, in the unlikeliest of places.
