A privacy-preserving distributed protocol for secure data sharing in untrusted supply chain
Authors: Maryam Shafiei, Alireza Hashemi Golpayegani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modern supply chains require effective information sharing and inter-organizational collaboration while safeguarding sensitive data to ensure operational efficiency. Despite significant progress, existing methods rarely provide a comprehensive approach that simultaneously ensures privacy and visibility, maintains owner control, and supports horizontal and vertical collaboration in distributed environment without reliance on centralized or globally trusted third parties. Most current works rely on centralized management or blockchain-based architectures that introduce issues of scalability, storage, and integration. To address these gaps, this paper presents a hierarchical model for secure data storage, access, and sharing, integrated with a secure protocol that manages authentication, authorization, and distributed access control. The proposed design enables private yet visible information exchange through metadata and hash propagation while preserving confidentiality, integrity, and anonymity. Protocol-level feasibility and security properties are established through formal and informal analysis, including AVISPA-based verification under standard symbolic adversary models. Simulation-based experimental evaluations across multiple network configurations demonstrate that, within the modeled topology and protocol assumptions, scalability is strongly influenced by balanced connectivity among supply chain nodes, resulting in stable throughput and low latency under increasing complexity. Overall, the proposed approach achieves favorable trade-offs between security, functionality, and computational and communication costs compared with existing methods, providing a well-founded architectural and protocol-level basis for secure information sharing in distributed supply chain environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50342-3.

Introduction

Supply chains are collaborative networks where competitive advantage depends on the timely, accurate, and secure sharing of information among participants. The growing adoption of digital technologies, advanced analytics, and automation within Industry 4.0 has intensified inter-organizational data exchange, enabling greater visibility and collaboration. However, as data becomes more widely shared across independent firms, concerns regarding privacy, security, and control have emerged as critical barriers to effective collaboration. Ensuring secure and privacy-preserving information sharing is therefore essential for achieving operational efficiency and resilience in modern supply chains1,2.

While information sharing improves visibility and collaboration, it also raises significant privacy and security concerns3–5. Autonomous participants often hesitate to share sensitive data that represent strategic assets or trade secrets. This creates a paradox: firms seek collaboration for efficiency gains yet fear exposure of proprietary information, eroding trust and impeding cooperation6–8. As past data breaches have shown, inadequate protection can result in financial loss, reputational damage, and legal liability9,10. Thus, achieving a balance between visibility and privacy remains a fundamental and unresolved challenge in the digital supply chain.

Recent advances in privacy-preserving computation have sought to reconcile collaboration with data protection through distributed analytics and selective disclosure. Approaches such as decentralized learning and cryptographic computation enable joint insights without centralizing raw data, while privacy-enhancing mechanisms limit unnecessary information exposure11–15. Despite their theoretical strength, these methods are often tailored to specific analytical tasks and lack adaptability to the broader, transaction-intensive processes of supply chain operations. In practice, computational overhead, synchronization demands, heterogeneity problems, and limited interoperability with access control or authentication systems constrain scalability and coordination across organizational boundaries16–19.

Moreover, despite their conceptual advances, existing architectures remain challenged by implicit reliance on centralized or globally trusted entities and performance bottlenecks. Centralized systems ensure coordination but rely on trusted intermediaries, creating single points of failure and discouraging participation when inter-organizational trust is limited20,21. Conversely, blockchain-based frameworks (BC) offer enhanced transparency and traceability but face challenges related to latency, storage, and scalability, limiting their applicability in dynamic supply chain environments22–24. Most importantly, current solutions often protect only isolated stages such as data access or sharing, without safeguarding the entire data lifecycle. Consequently, existing methods yield fragmented privacy assurance and limited interoperability, underscoring the need for a distributed framework that can efficiently achieve privacy, visibility, and collaboration. Given these challenges, this study addresses the following research questions:

RQ1: How can a distributed supply chain framework simultaneously achieve privacy, visibility, and operational efficiency without relying on a centralized authority?

RQ2: How can distributed authentication and access control mechanisms support secure and coordinated collaboration among supply chain participants throughout data access, storage, and sharing phases?

To address RQ1, this paper introduces a hierarchical distributed supply chain framework that integrates privacy-preserving information sharing with metadata-based visibility. Each participant retains full ownership of its data, storing it locally while transmitting metadata and hashes upstream to enable verification without revealing sensitive content. This mechanism supports both vertical and horizontal collaboration while ensuring visibility and privacy, without reliance on a globally trusted intermediary, and instead through localized, protocol-defined trust relationships.

In response to RQ2, a secure protocol is developed to manage authentication, authorization, and distributed access control. The protocol establishes mutual authentication among entities, access control based on attributes, and an anonymous identity mechanism to ensure secure collaboration across data access, storage, and sharing phases. Mutual authentication and access validation are established between vertically connected participants, using cryptographic verification and structurally defined upstream–downstream relationships. Certificate mediation and metadata propagation are explicitly bounded within the supply chain topology, ensuring that trust is localized, verifiable, and role-constrained, rather than globally assumed.

Through this integrated design, the proposed approach achieves privacy, integrity, and controlled visibility under full data ownership without dependence on centralized authorities or global trust assumptions, while operating through explicitly defined and cryptographically verifiable local relationships. Protocol-level feasibility and selected security properties are established through functional analysis and formal verification using the AVISPA tool under standard symbolic adversary models. This verification confirms resistance to modeled active and passive attacks within the assumed threat models, but does not constitute a comprehensive security guarantee beyond these formal abstractions or capture implementation-level, side-channel, or operational threats. Performance evaluation is conducted separately through controlled simulation experiments to assess scalability, latency, and throughput under fixed protocol, topology, and cryptographic assumptions. The simulation results indicate that authentication performance and overall throughput remain stable as network complexity increases, and that scalability is strongly influenced by balanced connectivity among nodes rather than network size alone. These findings characterize protocol behavior within the evaluated experimental scope and are not intended to represent general structural properties of all distributed supply chains or deployment-level performance under dynamic operational conditions.

Throughout this paper, the term no-trust is used strictly to denote the absence of global trust assumptions, centralized authorities, or universally trusted intermediaries. It does not imply an absence of trust at the protocol level; rather, the proposed framework relies on localized, structurally defined, and cryptographically verifiable trust relationships enforced through explicit protocol mechanisms within the modeled supply chain hierarchy.

Data privacy (P1) The necessity of information sharing in the supply chain to increase efficiency and facilitate optimal decision-making on the one hand, and the sensitivity of this information and the lack of trust between supply chain members make privacy preservation a vital feature for information sharing in the supply chain.

Data visibility (P2) Supply chain visibility provides critical information for stakeholders to collaborate effectively, ensuring that all parties act quickly and confidently. Enhanced visibility makes the supply chain more agile and resilient. However, there are privacy concerns associated with improved visibility. Achieving an appropriate balance between visibility and privacy can improve sustainability in supply chain operations.

No-Trust assumption (P3) This property evaluates whether an approach avoids reliance on global trust assumptions, trusted third parties, or centralized authorities for secure information sharing. Approaches satisfying this property achieve security through distributed or locally verifiable mechanisms rather than system-wide trust dependencies.

Distributed structure (P4) Centralized approaches face supply chains with many challenges, such as the single point of failure, increased risk exposure, inefficient access to data, and limited control of them. Exploring a distributed approach is essential to enhance secure and private information sharing in the supply chain.

Owner control (P5) This principle is vital for data sovereignty, trust, and secure collaboration in competitive, distributed settings. Centralized approaches compromise owner control and heighten misuse or unauthorized access risks by sharing data externally. Decentralized and cryptographic approaches help participants adopt fine-grained access policies while preserving control over their informational assets.

Full collaboration (P6) There are two types of collaboration in the supply chain: vertical and horizontal. In vertical collaboration, the participants have distinct roles in the supply chain, while in horizontal collaboration, they have the same role. In horizontal collaboration, the participants are usually competitors, so the sensitivity is higher. An approach must consider full collaboration between supply chain participants.

Data integrity (P7) Data integrity is critical for effective decision-making and forecasting, especially during significant disruptions. It provides reliable and accurate information for stakeholders, facilitatininformed decision-making, performance monitoring, and improved collaboration among participants.

Anonymity (P8) The importance of anonymity cannot be overstated in modern supply chain systems, especially in situations involving competitive stakeholders and sensitive trade relationships. Protecting participant anonymity safeguards proprietary strategies, reduces targeted attacks, and maintains business confidentiality during data sharing. Adopting an appropriate anonymity schema improves privacy across collaborative supply chain environments.

The application of information technology enhances interconnectivity across the supply chains. Nevertheless, despite its advantages, interconnectivity has challenges and increases cybersecurity risks. It is crucial to strike a balance between security in the IT-enabled supply chain and cooperation; this is an essential issue in supply chain management. As a first step, Smith et al.25 developed a model for information security risks in the supply chains by considering the origin point, threat type, possible effects on IT capital, and complications in the supply chains. Zhang et al.26 proposed a conceptual model for information leakage due to inference that permits a better understanding of this challenge by companies in a supply chain. The authors also provided a quantitative method for investigating the inference-based information leakage risks to help supply chain participants recognize confidential data and assess and mitigate the potential information leakage risk.

Lin et al.27 introduced cloud-based data storage and sharing framework incorporating authentication and ownership-transfer protocols, where encryption secures the owner’s data while external entities manage authorization. Although claimed to resist known attacks, subsequent analyses by Khor and Sidorov28 demonstrated susceptibility to denial-of-service and de-synchronization attacks, prompting an improved design. Nakasumi29 integrated blockchain with homomorphic encryption to enable encrypted data ownership and transparency in data usage, but Abidi et al.30 highlighted its vulnerability to hacking. Preuveneers, et al.31 introduced decentralized access control and a distributed trust model based on a private blockchain to provide integrity, transparency, and authorized information sharing in Industry 4.0. Yet, their approach suffered from scalability and privacy issues due to the growing blockchain size and resource constraints of participating devices. Gao et al.32 adopted a hybrid two-step block construction method combined with AES encryption to enhance supply-chain data protection, assuming secure communication and honest participants. While it improved throughput and reduced complexity, its practical performance remained limited.

To enhance decentralized security mechanisms, various studies have focused on authentication protocols and access control in supply-chain environments. Sidorov et al.33 proposed an ultra-lightweight protocol with mutual authentication that employs a permissioned blockchain network. Nevertheless, this protocol does not offer sufficient security measures because, due to bitwise rotation, an attacker can effortlessly acquire a sensitive credential by intercepting the transmitted messages. To address the limitation of33, Jangirala et al.34 demonstrated a secure authentication protocol named LBRAPS for supply chains in the 5G mobile edge computing environment. This method is secure, but its computational cost is high. Furthermore, centralized access authentication is done within the mobile core network in a cloud radio access network. Due to this particular circumstance, the network incurs extremely high operating and CapEx. To mitigate the computational cost and ensure adequate security, Kumar et al.35 developed a decentralized RFID Authentication protocol based on blockchain technology tailored for a supply chain. Supply chain members have guaranteed access permission in this protocol.

Dwivedi et al.36 employed smart contracts and consensus mechanisms for securely sharing information and key management, in the pharmaceutical supply chain, demonstrating resistance to common attacks with reasonable communication and computation costs. Bader, et al.20 advanced a scalable, multi-hop blockchain infrastructure enhancing accountability and data availability while balancing transparency and privacy through hybrid encryption integrated with decentralized attribute-based encryption. Their evaluation on real-world scenarios revealed strong privacy but also dependence on external storage and trusted intermediaries, which can lead to a loss of control and potential risks to data autonomy and verifiability. Also, the involvement of trusted third parties can compromise the decentralized characteristics inherent in blockchain technologies. Liu, et al.37 further improved fine-grained access control using multi-authority ABE to mitigate privacy leakage and anonymity gaps in blockchain-based supply chains. Through dual-system encryption and policy hiding, they achieved confidentiality and access flexibility. Li, et al.38 proposed Prochain, using blockchain and attribute-based encryption, and zero-knowledge proof to preserve privacy in the supply chain. Sarfaraz, et al.39 designed a multi-blockchain architecture offering layered scalability and fine-grained access control. Despite efficiency gains, their approach overlooked mining costs and ledger maintenance overhead inherent in Proof-of-Work (PoW) systems. Hu, et al.40 designed a blockchain-based access control framework that uses zero-knowledge proofs to resolve the tension between privacy and accountability, enabling fully anonymous access that can be revoked to reveal malicious users without a trusted third party. Hu, et al.41 tackled performance bottlenecks directly by introducing a specialized blockchain that minimizes redundant computations for ABAC policy decisions, significantly improving throughput.

A different class of approaches concentrates on preserving privacy through sanitization and restoration (S&R) techniques. Abidi, et al.30 introduced a three-stage method comprising data sanitization, key-based obfuscation, and restoration over a blockchain channel, providing provable security in information exchange. Viswanadham and Jayavel42 proposed a hybrid meta-heuristic framework combined with the Ethereum blockchain for enhanced supply chain data privacy, focusing on scalability, adaptability, and security in data governance. Unpredictability in finding optimal solutions is one limitation of Meta-heuristic algorithms. Gómez-Marín, et al.43 leveraged secure hardware elements for asset authentication, key storage, and digital signing to ensure integrity, authenticity, and resistance to counterfeiting or repudiation, effectively linking physical asset verification with cryptographic protection.

Recent advances in zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) have introduced new paradigms for privacy-preserving validation. ZKPs allow one entity to prove the validity of information to another without revealing underlying data, an essential property for multi-stakeholder environments such as supply chains. Gokulakrishnan and Sinha44 proposed a ZK-Rollup-based privacy-preserving identity and transaction verification system that leverages ZKPs to ensure trust while concealing sensitive supply chain information such as batch identifiers and manufacturer identities. Similarly, Samantray and Reddy45 introduced a Healthcare Supply Chain Management framework that integrates ZKPs and hashing within Ethereum smart contracts to ensure drug authenticity and secure real-time traceability. While Yakubu BM46 established a foundational blockchain framework for rice traceability, Yakubu, et al.47 enhances it significantly by integrating advanced cryptographic techniques like Zero-Knowledge Proofs and a hybrid RBAC/ABAC model to address critical gaps in security, privacy, and system efficiency. Lee, et al.48 developed PrivHChain, a hierarchical blockchain employing ZKP protocols to model complex supply-chain event dependencies, achieving private yet verifiable transactions. While the scheme enhanced throughput and privacy, scalability and generalizability to large supply networks were not fully addressed. Yakubu BM46 introduced a private Ethereum blockchain that uses Proof of Authority, Zero-Knowledge Proofs, and a hybrid access control model to balance privacy, security, and operational efficiency. Ravi, et al.49 employed ZKPs within Hyperledger Fabric to ensure transparent, privacy-preserving collaboration among authorized participants in permissioned blockchains. Nevertheless, computational demands and proof-generation complexity constrained applicability in resource-limited contexts, and proof-based access models potentially reduced data-query flexibility.

Differential privacy (DP) offers a rigorous mathematical framework for quantifiable privacy guarantees by injecting noise into data or query results, and protecting individual privacy while enabling statistical analysis. Keo, et al.11 integrated DP with blockchain, IoT, and IPFS in a Hyperledger Fabric–based agricultural traceability system to preserve privacy while improving transparency and fair pricing. Kumar and Bhatt50 proposed a framework that integrates Blockchain with Federated Learning, augmented by DP. This synergy creates a decentralized system where participants can collaboratively train ML models without exposing their raw data. Islam, et al.51 Applied DP in a permissioned blockchain in the supply chain in IIoT. It added noise to the actual data before sharing it with the requester, and considered privacy budget to manage accuracy and efficiency. Liu, et al.52 developed correlated DP mechanisms to protect sensitive supplier data in predictive models, such as logistic regression for supplier listing, effectively reducing privacy leakage from correlated datasets while maintaining high prediction accuracy. Li12 considered privacy-preserving in data processing, management, and analysis by DP, cryptography, and MPC for secure collaborative machine learning against inference attacks. Li, et al.53 employed DP for context-aware, real-time logistics data sharing by modulating noise intensity according to geographic sensitivity. Although effective, DP introduces computational overhead and potential accuracy loss, particularly in distributed blockchain or federated environments54.

Secure multiparty computation (MCP) allows multiple parties to jointly compute a function over their private inputs without revealing those inputs to each other55. This is crucial in supply chains where participants, such as manufacturers, suppliers, and distributors, need to collaborate on sensitive data without exposing proprietary information. Spini, et al.56 used MPC for privacy-preserving horizontal collaboration in transportation, while Din, et al.57 combined MPC with homomorphic encryption (HE) and blockchain for secure computation on encrypted IoT supply-chain data. Yakubu, et al.58 introduced HalalChain, a blockchain framework for the halal food supply chain that decentralizes certification using threshold cryptography and automates compliance with smart contracts, enhancing integrity through IoT and Merkle-tree validation, though scalability and privacy challenges remain. Research on Blockchain-based Internet of Medical Things (BIoMT) has evolved to address privacy at different layers. Li, et al.59 secured intra-chain data with a lightweight secret-sharing scheme, fragmenting data for secure storage on resource-constrained devices. To overcome interoperability, Li, et al.60 proposed a cross-chain model using a relay chain and a lattice-based Designated Verifier Proxy Signature (DVPS), enabling private data sharing across different blockchains. Further advancing cryptography for this setting, the same authors introduced a quantum-safe Identity-Based DVS, providing strong authentication and anonymity with built-in traceability Li, et al.61. Collectively, these works establish a foundation for secure data storage, cross-chain sharing, and quantum-resistant verification in BIoMT. Islam, et al.13 suggested a hybrid privacy protection method MPC, DP, and multimedia forensics for trustworthy data sharing and tampering prevention. Mukherjee, et al. developed an MPC-based multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that facilitates collaborative training without raw data exchange in a two-party supply-chain setup. Li, et al.63 introduced lightweight cloud-assisted homomorphic encryption, though it weakened end-to-end privacy strength under high data sensitivity. Despite strong privacy guarantees, MPC and HE often impose heavy computational and memory overhead, limiting real-time scalability.

Federated learning (FL) represents a complementary paradigm for privacy-preserving collaboration, enabling distributed entities to collectively train machine-learning models without directly sharing raw data. However, heterogeneity among participants and decentralized data distribution pose synchronization and efficiency challenges. Tang, et al.14 addressed the Federated Graph Neural Network which enables multiple enterprises to collaboratively train a model without sharing their raw data within a client–server architecture that mirrors the real-world supply chain topology without accessing the underlying data or the complete network structure. Wang, et al.64 proposed a privacy-preserving FL framework for AIoT-enabled supply chains that mitigates gradient inversion risks through the exchange of condensed meta-knowledge instead of full model parameters. Cai, et al.65 integrated FL with a custom blockchain consensus algorithm to address the privacy and efficiency, while Zhang, et al.15 combined federated multi-agent reinforcement learning with distributed simulation and surrogate modeling to optimize collaborative supply-chain performance under privacy constraints. Zheng, et al.19 enhanced FL-enables suppliers to collaboratively build a collective forecasting model for supply risk by sharing only model parameters. A key innovation is a clustering algorithm that allows suppliers to dynamically form optimal information-sharing teams based on data relevance, rather than contributing to a single global model. Zhu, et al.66 proposed a FL framework for IoT supply chains to address security vulnerabilities. It mentions that enhanced security brings system complexity, higher hardware costs, and potential performance overhead. Also FL utilized in zero-defect manufacturing in Industry 4.067, green medical supply chains68, securing healthcare logistics69, logistics and transportation efficiency70, smart manufacturing and product lifecycle management71. Most paper with federated learning approach focuses on selected issues in the supply chain and don’t consider overall supply chain data sharing. Nevertheless, FL continues to face unresolved challenges, including exposure to model-update inference attacks, susceptibility to data-poisoning, uneven contribution levels, and inefficiency caused by unbalanced data distributions14,19,66,72–74.

Although these approaches differ in scope and underlying mechanisms, they are often complementary rather than mutually exclusive. For instance, blockchain can provide immutable verification of outcomes generated through federated learning (FL) or secure multi-party computation (MPC), while differential privacy (DP) enhances confidentiality within such decentralized analytics frameworks. Nevertheless, these methods still face practical limitations in supply chain contexts. FL and MPC require continuous synchronization and high computational resources, which hinder scalability in large or heterogeneous networks. Moreover, they primarily focus on data analytics rather than secure data sharing or process-level collaboration, while DP may degrade data utility through excessive noise addition17–19.

Although blockchain and distributed ledger technologies have enhanced transparency and traceability in supply chain management, they continue to face challenges related to data storage inefficiency, network latency, and limited scalability, which restrict their effectiveness in large-scale or real-time environments22–24. Most existing studies also focus on partial collaboration models, horizontal or vertical, without addressing privacy-preserving mechanisms that enable secure and coordinated data exchange across the full supply chain ecosystem. Furthermore, achieving a balance between transparency, which is essential for visibility and decision-making, and privacy, which protects sensitive business information, remains an unresolved issue3,5. Current approaches often provide fragmented privacy protection, addressing only specific phases or purpose rather than offering an integrated and end-to-end solution.

These limitations highlight the need for a distributed and privacy-preserving framework that ensures visibility, confidentiality, integrity, and secure collaboration while maintaining efficiency. Building upon these gaps, the present study develops a privacy-preserving distributed protocol that enables secure data sharing and metadata-based visibility without exposing sensitive information among fully collaborative supply chain participants.

Modeling assumptions, security foundations, and scientific scope

This section formalizes the architectural assumptions, adversarial foundations, and analytical scope under which the proposed framework is designed and evaluated. All security, privacy, and performance claims in this work are conditioned on the assumptions stated herein. The purpose of this section is to explicitly bound the scope of the framework and clarify the conditions under which its guarantees and evaluation results hold, positioning the protocol’s guarantees, trade-offs, and evaluation methodology within contemporary research on secure and privacy-preserving supply chain systems.

Trust model and architectural scope

The proposed framework targets distributed supply chains that operate without reliance on centralized or globally trusted third parties. In this work, the term “no-trust” refers specifically to the absence of global trust anchors and does not imply absolute trustlessness among participants.

Instead, the framework introduces an architecturally enforced trust model based on localized and cryptographically verifiable interactions defined by explicit upstream–downstream relationships. Trust emerges through protocol execution, via mutual authentication, certificate issuance, and session key establishment, rather than through assumed institutional authority or implicit trust. Each interaction is independently verifiable and scoped to the participating entities.

This scoped trust model reflects realistic inter-organizational supply chain environments, where collaboration is necessary but unconditional trust is neither assumed nor desirable.

System model and operational assumptions

The framework is evaluated under a system model that captures essential characteristics of multi-tier supply chains while remaining analytically tractable. The adopted abstractions are introduced solely to bound the scope of analysis and evaluation; they do not imply deployment-level guarantees and do not claim completeness with respect to all operational or adversarial conditions encountered in real-world supply chain systems.

For evaluation purposes, the supply chain is represented as a directed hierarchical structure with explicitly defined upstream–downstream relationships. This abstraction reflects common organizational dependencies in multi-tier supply chains and enables systematic analysis of protocol behavior under structured coordination.

The framework relies on established cryptographic primitives, including elliptic-curve cryptography, symmetric encryption, and secure hash functions. These primitives are assumed to be secure and correctly implemented, consistent with prevailing cryptographic standards and common practice in protocol analysis.

Adversarial model and formal security validation scope

The security analysis adopts symbolic adversarial models commonly used in authentication research. Specifically, the Dolev–Yao (DY) model is employed to capture network-level threats in which the adversary has full control over the communication channel, including interception, modification, replay, and injection of messages. This aligns with the distributed messaging and metadata exchange setting of the proposed protocol76.

Because the protocol includes mutual authentication and session-key establishment, the analysis is further strengthened by adopting the Canetti–Krawczyk (CK) adversary model, which enables reasoning about session-oriented threats such as ephemeral key leakage, session-state exposure, and key-compromise impersonation77. This combination reflects common practice in contemporary authentication protocol analysis.

Formal security validation in this work is conducted within symbolic adversarial models using automated verification tools. The analysis confirms that the proposed protocol satisfies its intended authentication and secrecy properties under the Dolev–Yao and Canetti–Krawczyk assumptions, thereby establishing protocol-level correctness with respect to specified security goals. However, this validation is inherently limited to the abstract symbolic model and does not extend to implementation-level vulnerabilities, side-channel attacks, and real-world protocol interactions outside the modeled assumptions. Accordingly, all formal verification results presented in this study should be interpreted as symbolic, protocol-level validation rather than as comprehensive system or deployment-level security assurance.

Security and privacy scope

The framework ensures cryptographic confidentiality of original data during access, storage, and sharing, and satisfies core security properties under the modeled adversarial conditions. Unauthorized entities cannot recover protected data content within the defined model.

At the same time, the framework recognizes that privacy in distributed systems is multi-dimensional. While direct data disclosure is prevented, auxiliary information—such as metadata related to transaction timing, frequency, and structural flow—may remain visible. The protocol mitigates this exposure through per-order anonymization and controlled metadata propagation, but residual inference risks under long-term or correlated observation are not fully eliminated.

Accordingly, metadata visibility is treated as an architectural trade-off that enables operational observability, auditability, and coordination without centralized mediation. Mitigation mechanisms such as metadata minimization, adaptive identifier rotation, traffic shaping, or privacy-enhancing aggregation are not intrinsic guarantees of the current design and are therefore not implemented or evaluated in this work.

Evaluation and deployment scope

Experimental evaluation is conducted through controlled simulation across supply chain networks of varying size and structural complexity derived from real-world datasets. These experiments are designed to characterize protocol-level feasibility, scalability, and performance behavior under explicitly defined assumptions, hierarchical topologies and known cryptographic parameters.

Deployment-level factors, including certificate lifecycle management, participant churn, heterogeneous network conditions, and operational maintenance, are not explicitly modeled. Accordingly, the reported results reflect protocol-level feasibility and simulated performance trends rather than guarantees of real-world deployment.

Scope of claims

All security, privacy, and performance claims presented in this work are explicitly bounded by the assumptions and scope defined in this section. The proposed framework is intended as a protocol- and architecture-level solution whose guarantees hold under these conditions. Extensions to alternative threat models, topologies, cryptographic suites, and real-world deployment environments remain important directions for future research.

Accordingly, no security, performance, or structural observations reported in this work should be interpreted as universal properties of distributed supply chains, but rather as results that hold strictly within the assumptions, constraints, and controlled simulation environment considered in this study.

When an order is received, a node evaluates the order specifications and selects suitable collaborators from lower layers based on parameters such as available capacity, cost efficiency, and delivery time. After selection, all involved participants perform mutual authentication and establish a session key, ensuring secure communication through explicit cryptographic verification, without relying on implicit, global, or persistent trust assumptions. This mechanism strengthens both data privacy and collaborative visibility across the supply chain. Access control through mutual authentication enables supply chain members to interact and securely obtain authorized information from other participants. Data sharing

The data sharing phase enables controlled access to original data under the authority of its owner. When a participant (SCr) requires access to the data corresponding to specific metadata, it sends a request to the node maintaining that metadata (SCu). If the data owner and requester belong to a vertical collaboration, they perform mutual authentication directly. For horizontal collaboration, which often involves competitors, authentication is mediated through a superior node (SCu) that issues temporary certificates to both parties. Once mutual authentication is established, the data owner (SCc) evaluates the requester’s access permissions. If approved, the data is encrypted and securely transmitted. Upon receipt, SCr verifies data integrity by comparing the hash of the received data with the reference hash obtained from SCu.

Dynamic structural changes in the supply chain

Real-world supply chains evolve continuously, with participants joining or leaving the network and occasionally changing operational roles. In the proposed protocol, a newly added participant executes the same multi-hop registration procedure used by all existing nodes, establishing mutual authentication parameters with each of its upstream neighbors. Because this procedure is strictly local and structurally hierarchical, network reconfiguration is not required; thus, join operations incur only O(k) cost, where k is the number of upstream connections, and remain independent of the global network size. Furthermore, the proposed framework does not rely on predefined business roles. Instead, all security and verification steps are derived from structural attributes of the supply chain graph, specifically upstream–downstream relationships. Consequently, temporary or permanent role transitions do not affect correctness or security as long as these structural dependencies remain valid.

When a participant temporarily disconnects, its upstream nodes retain the associated metadata and hash values, allowing the participant to reconcile its data upon return, with updated metadata propagated as the new reference for verification. In the case of permanent removal, the original data become inaccessible, and all upstream nodes propagate the deletion of the corresponding metadata and hash values to maintain consistency. When a new participant takes its place, fresh metadata and hash records are generated and disseminated to initialize future operations.

These dynamic operations introduce additional overhead, including metadata propagation and records updating, but remain bounded and structurally contained due to the protocol’s decentralized design.

Failure management and metadata consistency mechanisms

In a distributed and hierarchical supply chain network, uninterrupted communication and metadata synchronization are critical for maintaining operational efficiency and visibility. However, practical environments often experience node failures, which can disrupt data flow and reduce system availability. To enhance system robustness, the proposed scheme introduces a lightweight failure management and metadata consistency, designed to maintain operational continuity and ensure eventual synchronization without compromising privacy or security. Similar to recent robustness-oriented studies that emphasize fast reconnection and reliable authentication in IoT environments78,79, the proposed design extends these principles to metadata-level consistency management in a privacy-preserving supply chain.

In the proposed architecture, each participant stores its original data locally, while metadata and corresponding hash values are transmitted to the upstream nodes. During node or network failures, the behavior of the system depends on the position of the affected node. When an upstream node experiences a failure, the downstream node that originally generated the data can retransmit the previously issued metadata and hash records once the connection is restored. This mechanism allows full recovery of metadata without compromising privacy or integrity. Conversely, when a data-generating node fails, the original data remain temporarily inaccessible because they are not duplicated elsewhere. However, the upstream node retains the corresponding metadata and hash, which serve as verifiable evidence of the data’s existence and integrity until the failed node recovers. Upon recovery, the node reauthenticates using mutual authentication protocol and resynchronizes its metadata with the upstream participant to restore consistency.

This design effectively balances privacy and resilience: it ensures metadata consistency and recoverability even under partial network failures, while the confidentiality of original data is fully preserved. The main management challenge, therefore, lies in maintaining synchronized and verifiable metadata under failure conditions without centralized control or data replication.

Functionality and security analysis

We analyze the security and functionality of the presented method in this section.

Proposition 1

The proposed method provides a mutual authentication property.

The SCu authenticates SCc by verifying Scu = Scu’ = h(Mu’||PIDSCc||xSCc||tSCc), where Mu’ = MPu⊕PPWSCc, only SCc with the correct password PPWSCc can compute a valid Mu′ and generate a matching Scu, confirming SCc’s authenticity. Computing Scu also relies on the correct computation of Mu = h(PIDSCc||IDSCu||PrSCu), which involves the private key of SCu. Conversely, SCc authenticates SCu by computing Yu = h(Mu||xSCu||tSCu) and checking it against the received Yu, which depends on the private-key-related value Mu generated by SCu. Successful verification on both sides establishes a shared session key K = h(Mu || Tk) and guarantees mutual authentication between the parties, relying solely on local credentials and cryptographic verification without the need for a global trusted entity.

Proposition 2

The proposed method supports supply chain data confidentiality.

Each participant controls its own data, which is never stored in a central repository. Only metadata and hashes are shared upstream. The attacker cannot obtain the original data by obtaining the hash due to one-way property of hash function. When a participant requests data, the owner encrypts it with a session-specific symmetric key after mutual authentication through the Cert signed by SCu. To calculate Certc = h(PIDSCr||Kuc||t||xn), knowing Kuc depends on the selected random number. Therefore, the attacker cannot get the session key and have unauthorized access to the data. Thus, this method provides data confidentiality in the data storage and sharing phases.

Proposition 3

The proposed method supports supply chain connection confidentiality.

In supply chain participant interactions, the identities of parties are never transmitted in plain text. Instead, pseudo-identities (PIDs) are used, which changes in each new order process. This design prevents adversaries from inferring supply chain relationships or tracking participants across sessions.

Proposition 4

The proposed method provides data integrity.

Data integrity is verified by comparing the hash of received data with the hash stored by the sender. Any modification or tampering is immediately detectable, and participants can verify the received information.

Proposition 5

The proposed method preserves supply chain participants anonymity.

We employ pseudo-identity to preserve anonymity in our scheme. Pseudo-identities conceal participant identities (PID’SCc = h(ID’SCc||rc)). Since parameters in exchanged messages are session-specific, adversaries cannot infer identities or link messages across sessions, preserving anonymity.

Proposition 6

The proposed method supports perfect forward security.

Since the agreed key K = h(Mu||Tk) depends on Tk, which is enclosed by random numbers chosen in each session, even with the private key SCu, it is not possible to calculate the session key. Therefore, even if the adversary obtains the session key, he cannot access the previous information due to the random session parameters.

Proposition 7

The proposed method preserves supply chain participants privacy.

Privacy is preserved through a combination of mutual authentication, data access control, anonymity, confidentiality, integrity, and traceability. Session-specific parameters and pseudo-identities prevent adversaries from gaining sensitive information, even if messages are intercepted.

The proposed method protects against man-in-the-middle Attack.

The proposed method resistant against replay attack.

Timestamps in all messages ensure freshness. Replayed messages are detected and rejected, preventing replay attacks.

The proposed method guards against electronic device stolen attack.

Therefore, this method is resistant to stolen verifiers and privileged insider attacks.

Simulation using AVISPA tool for formal security verification

To formally analyze the security properties of the proposed authentication protocol, we employ the Automated Validation of Internet Security Protocols and Applications (AVISPA) framework. AVISPA is a well-established symbolic verification tool for analyzing security protocols under adversarial network conditions and is widely used to assess resistance against both active and passive attacks within formally defined threat models. In this study, AVISPA is used to verify secrecy and authentication properties under the Dolev–Yao (DY) adversary assumptions.

The protocol is specified using the High-Level Protocol Specification Language (HLPSL), a role-based formal language that models protocol participants, message flows, and security goals. AVISPA provides multiple back-end analyzers—namely On-the-Fly Model Checker (OFMC), Constraint Logic-based Attack Searcher (CL-AtSe), SATMC, and TA4SP—to explore the symbolic state space of the protocol. In this work, verification is conducted using the OFMC and CL-AtSe back ends, which are commonly adopted for analyzing authentication and secrecy properties in distributed protocols.

The HLPSL specification of the proposed protocol consists of two basic roles and several composed roles. Specifically, SCu (upstream node) and SCc (counterpart node) are modeled as basic roles, representing the interacting supply chain participants, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The composed roles- session, goal, and environment-define the interaction context, security objectives, and adversary capabilities, as shown in Fig. 8. These roles collectively capture protocol execution over an untrusted communication network controlled by a symbolic adversary, while explicitly excluding reliance on any global or universally trusted third party. Trust is not assumed a priori between participants; instead, any trust relationship is strictly local, temporary, and conditionally established through successful cryptographic verification during protocol execution, rather than through assumed honesty, institutional authority, or out-of-band guarantees.Fig. 7SCc role specification (a) and SCu role specification (b) using HLPSL code.Fig. 8Session, goal, and environment role specification using HLPSL code.

SCc role specification (a) and SCu role specification (b) using HLPSL code.

Session, goal, and environment role specification using HLPSL code.

The formal security objectives verified in AVISPA are defined as follows:

Goal1: the secrecy of SCrt0 signifies that IDi and PWi are known only to SCci.

Goal2: secrecy of SCrt1 signifies that the random number rt used in the protocol is secret between SCc and SCu.

Goal3: the secrecy of SCrt2 signifies that the key k used for this session is secret between SCc and SCu.

Goal4: the secrecy of SCrt3 signifies that the random number ry used in the protocol is secret between SCc and SCu.

Auth1: authentication_on SCc_SCu means that when SCc generates a random number rt, if SCu receives it securely through the message, then SCu can authenticate SCc.

Auth2: authentication_on SCu_SCc means that when SCu generates a random number rt, if SCc receives it securely through the message, then SCc can authenticate SCu.

It is important to emphasize that these results establish protocol-level correctness strictly within the adopted symbolic adversarial models. The AVISPA-based analysis validates the logical consistency of the proposed authentication protocol and confirms resistance to attacks expressible under the Dolev–Yao and Canetti–Krawczyk abstractions. However, this form of verification is inherently limited to symbolic reasoning and does not address implementation-level vulnerabilities, side-channel leakage, performance constraints, protocol composition effects, or real-world operational interactions that fall outside the symbolic model. Accordingly, the AVISPA results should be interpreted as formal validation of protocol logic under abstract attacker assumptions, rather than as comprehensive security assurance for deployed systems.

Privacy limitations and metadata inference risks (design trade-off)

The protocol provides strong confidentiality guarantees for original data content under the assumptions of the proposed protocol and simulation environment. All sensitive data remain encrypted under owner-controlled keys and are disclosed only after successful mutual authentication and authorization. These guarantees hold independently of metadata visibility and define a clear cryptographic protection boundary.

At the same time, within the specific decentralized coordination model considered in this work, the framework intentionally exposes a limited set of auxiliary metadata—such as propagation timing, update frequency, upstream–downstream structural relationships, and cryptographic hash values—to participating supply-chain entities. Accordingly, the framework explicitly distinguishes between (i) cryptographically protected content confidentiality and (ii) controlled exposure of auxiliary information required for coordination and visibility in the assumed distributed topology.

In this analysis, we consider adversaries with network-level capabilities to intercept, replay, and inject protocol messages, as well as authorized participants with access to protocol metadata that may observe and correlate such information over time. Under standard cryptographic assumptions, including the Dolev–Yao and Canetti–Krawczyk models, protected content and attribute-controlled information remain confidential and inaccessible to unauthorized parties. In a realistic long-term observation setting, adversaries may perform inference attacks based on statistical correlations in observable metadata, rather than on direct disclosure of sensitive data.

Specifically, in the evaluated simulated scenarios, an adversary capable of passively collecting and correlating metadata over extended periods may perform inference attacks based solely on auxiliary information. For example, the frequency and timing of metadata records may reveal transaction volumes or periodic interaction patterns; the rate of metadata generation may indicate relative activity intensity at specific nodes; and persistent metadata-flow patterns may allow partial reconstruction of structural roles or identification of operational bottlenecks. These inferences arise from statistical correlation of observable metadata rather than from direct disclosure of sensitive data.

To limit inference across transactions within this architectural context, identity-related information is anonymized on a per-order basis and varies across executions. Pseudonymous identifiers, authentication credentials, and session-specific parameters are regenerated for each order, substantially complicating longitudinal tracking and correlation across multiple transactions. As a result, direct linkage of metadata to persistent participant identities is intentionally avoided at the protocol level.

Furthermore, metadata associated with completed orders is treated as a policy-managed architectural element. Depending on operational, contractual, or regulatory requirements, such metadata may be archived, restricted to the participants involved in the specific order, or rendered unobservable to other nodes after order completion. When these policies are enforced, adversarial visibility is confined to localized, order-scoped observations and does not extend to persistent global profiling.

Despite these mitigations, the framework does not claim to eliminate all forms of inference arising from long-term or correlated metadata observation. This residual risk is an inherent consequence of enabling metadata-based visibility and is therefore treated as an explicit design trade-off rather than a protocol flaw.

Importantly, metadata visibility in the proposed architecture is not fixed but configurable according to deployment-specific privacy and visibility requirements. Different deployments may choose stricter metadata retention policies, reduced visibility scopes, or aggregation mechanisms to balance observability and privacy. Future research may further address metadata inference through complementary techniques such as metadata minimization, adaptive identifier rotation, privacy-preserving aggregation, traffic shaping, or federated learning–based abstraction. These approaches introduce additional overhead and reduce real-time visibility but are orthogonal to the core protocol design.

In summary, under the assumptions of the simulated environment, threat model, and cryptographic primitives considered, the proposed framework guarantees strong content confidentiality while exposing limited metadata to enable collaboration and verification. The resulting inference risks are explicitly bounded, mitigated through protocol-level and architectural mechanisms, and acknowledged as a configurable design trade-off inherent to distributed, metadata-driven supply-chain systems.

Practical deployment considerations and evaluation scope

The proposed protocol is evaluated through controlled simulations to demonstrate protocol-level feasibility and to characterize relative performance trends under controlled conditions. These results are intended to validate correctness, scalability behavior, and interaction patterns within modeled supply-chain topologies, rather than to predict deployment-level performance in operational environments.

Dynamic node churn, including participant addition, temporary disconnection, and permanent removal, is supported by the protocol design through localized, hierarchical registration and metadata propagation mechanisms that avoid global reconfiguration. However, several practical deployment considerations are not explicitly captured within the simulation model.

In real-world deployments, certificate and credential lifecycle management—including issuance, renewal, revocation, and policy updates—may introduce additional administrative and computational overhead, particularly in large or multi-organizational supply chains. Network heterogeneity, such as variable latency, bandwidth constraints, intermittent connectivity, and asymmetric communication links, may further affect protocol timing and metadata propagation compared to the homogeneous assumptions used in simulation. Operational maintenance, including software updates, access-control policy evolution, failure recovery, and auditing, represents an additional layer of complexity beyond the scope of the current evaluation.

Accordingly, the reported simulation results should be interpreted as evidence of architectural soundness and protocol feasibility under controlled conditions, rather than as indicators of production readiness. Comprehensive field evaluation incorporating certificate management workflows, heterogeneous network environments, and long-term operational maintenance remains an important direction for future work.

Taken together, these considerations clarify the intended scope and interpretation of the reported results. The evaluation is designed to demonstrate protocol-level feasibility and to characterize relative performance trends within a controlled simulation environment. Formal verification and correctness analysis establish that the proposed authentication and data-sharing mechanisms operate securely and as specified, while simulation results reflect protocol behavior under modeled supply-chain topologies and interaction patterns. These results are inherently bounded by the evaluated scope, including the assumed hierarchical architecture, fixed cryptographic primitives, and controlled network conditions. Consequently, the observed performance trends should be interpreted as protocol and topology dependent within this defined parameter space, rather than as indicators of deployment-level performance or general properties of all distributed supply chain systems.

Summary

The experimental findings demonstrate that, under the evaluated protocol design and simulated supply chain configurations, the proposed authentication framework achieves security correctness, high reliability, and efficient scalability across diverse supply chain structures. The system attains sub-millisecond verification latency, while throughput and scalability adapt effectively to increasing network complexity. Within the evaluated simulation setting, the results indicate that balanced connectivity distribution, rather than network magnitude alone, is a primary determinant of communication efficiency for the proposed protocol.

Discussions and future works

This section discusses the implications of the proposed framework, interprets the experimental findings strictly within the evaluated simulation setting, and outlines key limitations and directions for future research.

This study introduces a distributed and collaborative model for secure and privacy-aware data access, storage, and sharing within fully collaborative supply chains that operate without reliance on centralized or globally trusted intermediaries. Modern supply chains face a critical paradox: while information sharing is fundamental to achieving efficiency and coordination, it inevitably raises privacy concerns that affect trust and visibility among participants. The proposed framework addresses this tension by enabling controlled information sharing through distributed metadata while preserving data ownership and access autonomy. This coexistence of visibility and privacy is achieved under explicitly defined architectural, cryptographic, and operational assumptions defined in this work, rather than as a universal property of all distributed supply chains.

For clarity, all security, performance, and scalability claims discussed in this section are conditioned on the following assumptions: the supply chain is modeled as a hierarchical, acyclic structure with explicitly defined upstream–downstream relationships; interactions occur through localized, protocol-enforced trust relationships established via mutual authentication rather than global trust; all participants execute the protocol as specified; message exchanges are subject to standard network adversaries but follow the modeled delivery semantics; and all cryptographic primitives (including elliptic-curve cryptography, symmetric encryption, and hash functions) are assumed to be secure and correctly implemented. Deviations from these assumptions may lead to different outcomes and are outside the scope of the present evaluation. Accordingly, the observed performance trends and structural effects should be interpreted within the scope of the modeled topology, protocol design, cryptographic assumptions, and simulation environment considered in this study, rather than as universally applicable properties of all distributed supply chains.

Unlike approaches that primarily focus on access control enforcement, the proposed framework addresses data access, storage, and sharing as a unified process within the defined protocol and topology model. By combining hierarchical organization with distributed authentication and attribute-constrained interactions, it enables both vertical and horizontal collaboration without reliance on centralized coordination or implicitly trusted global authorities. Importantly, all coordination and verification occur through localized interactions among directly connected participants, limiting trust exposure to explicitly defined relationships within the modeled topology.

From a performance perspective, the experimental evaluation confirms that these architectural and protocol choices translate into favorable behavior within the evaluated simulation conditions. As the size and complexity of the simulated supply chains increase, the authentication framework maintains high throughput and low latency, indicating efficient scaling behavior in the simulated environment. This behavior reflects the peer-to-peer nature of the protocol as instantiated in the simulation, which allows concurrent authentication sessions to proceed without dependence on centralized coordination. Only in the largest and most densely connected networks does synchronization overhead begin to marginally affect responsiveness, a result consistent with expectations for distributed systems operating under fixed protocol operations and controlled workloads.

An important insight from the experiments is that the structural properties of the network—rather than its size alone—determine overall communication efficiency within the modeled scenarios. Supply chains with more balanced connectivity patterns achieved better responsiveness, while those exhibiting strong centralization experienced localized congestion. In particular, moderate connectivity variability was associated with the most stable performance, suggesting that a certain level of hierarchy facilitates coordination, but excessive centralization reduces scalability. These observations are empirical findings specific to the adopted protocol, topology assumptions, and simulation environment, and should not be interpreted as universal structural properties of real-world distributed supply chains.

Taken together, the experimental results show that the proposed framework achieves a practical balance between privacy preservation, security enforcement, and operational efficiency within the defined experimental scope. The framework ensures protocol correctness under all tested scenarios, supports reliable metadata exchange, and maintains sub-millisecond-level responsiveness in simulation. By eliminating reliance on centralized intermediaries and embedding verifiable integrity mechanisms into the data-sharing process, the framework enables secure inter-organizational collaboration while preserving data ownership and confidentiality at the protocol level.

While the proposed framework prevents disclosure of original data and ensures cryptographic confidentiality, metadata-driven visibility inherently introduces residual inference risks that are not fully eliminated by encryption alone. Although the protocol partially mitigates inference by anonymizing direct linkages between metadata and specific supply chain participants on a per-order basis, long-term or correlated observation of metadata within the modeled system may still enable inference of transactional or structural patterns.

The proposed framework enforces strong cryptographic protection for all sensitive data content under the assumptions of the considered protocol design and evaluation setting, while permitting limited exposure of auxiliary metadata to support coordination and verification in a distributed supply-chain setting. This separation establishes a clear boundary between protected content and observable protocol information, enabling operation without reliance on centralized entities. The visibility of auxiliary metadata may reveal certain operational characteristics over time, but this exposure is intentionally bounded and managed at the architectural level. Importantly, metadata visibility is configurable and can be adjusted to align with deployment-specific privacy, operational, or regulatory requirements. Further refinement of these configuration choices remains a natural direction for future work.

Finally, while this study establishes protocol feasibility and favorable simulated behavior, real-world deployment introduces additional system-level considerations—including certificate lifecycle management, participant churn, network volatility, and organizational governance—that are not captured in the current evaluation and warrant further investigation.

Building upon these findings, future research will focus on developing an AI-enabled adaptive mechanism that dynamically balances privacy preservation and operational efficiency in distributed supply chains. One promising direction is to employ reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to continuously adjust access control policies, detect sensitive data, data visibility constraint and other related decision based on observed system performance, risk level, and contextual factors. RL-based policy agents could autonomously learn the optimal trade-off between strict privacy constraints and collaborative visibility to maximize global efficiency under varying operational conditions. Furthermore, federated anomaly detection can be incorporated to enhance proactive monitoring without compromising data confidentiality. In this setup, each node locally trains lightweight anomaly detection models on its own metadata and shares only model updates with a global aggregator. Such a mechanism would enable real-time identification of abnormal behaviors or potential privacy breaches while maintaining full data sovereignty. These AI-driven directions can extend the proposed framework toward a self-optimizing and privacy-aware ecosystem, improving scalability and resilience under complex, evolving supply chain dynamics.

Conclusions

This study proposed a distributed, privacy-preserving framework for secure data access, storage, and sharing across modeled distributed and highly interconnected supply chains operating without reliance on centralized or globally trusted third parties. By integrating a hierarchical data model with a collaborative mutual authentication and distributed access control protocol, the framework enables privacy, integrity, and controlled visibility through explicitly defined, localized, and cryptographically verifiable relationships among participating entities. Each participant maintains ownership of its data while securely exchanging metadata and cryptographic evidence, thereby establishing verifiable cooperation at the architectural and protocol levels.

The framework was evaluated through simulation across supply chain networks of varying size and connectivity to assess protocol-level behavior under controlled experimental conditions. The results demonstrate low latency, stable authentication behavior, and efficient throughput within the simulated environment. The experiments further indicate that performance is influenced not only by network scale but also by structural characteristics of the modeled topology, such as connectivity balance and interaction intensity. These observations are specific to the evaluated protocol, topology assumptions, and simulation environment and do not imply universal properties of distributed supply chains.

Overall, the proposed framework offers a methodologically sound and extensible approach to secure information sharing in collaborative supply chains at the architectural and protocol levels. All security and performance guarantees hold only under the stated assumptions regarding network structure, participant behavior, cryptographic security, and symbolic adversarial models. Advancing the framework toward real-world deployment will require addressing implementation-level security, performance constraints, governance, and operational dynamics, which remain important directions for future work.
