Prevalence, aetiology, and treatment needs of neonates with G6PD deficiency in Central Peninsular Malaysia
Authors: Mohamed Afiq Hidayat Zailani, Raja Zahratul Azma Raja Sabudin, Hafiza Alauddin, Nik Hafidzah Nik Mustapha, Faridah Hanim Zan, Azlin Ithnin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is the most common enzymopathy worldwide. Upon exposure to triggering substances, G6PD-deficient individuals can suffer severe complications, including acute haemolytic anaemia and neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia. This study aims to investigate the prevalence of G6PD deficiency among infants with neonatal jaundice (NNJ) in Malaysia and its association with sociodemographic characteristics, aetiology, and treatment needs. A cross-sectional study was conducted on 1,576 neonates who were admitted to the Special Care Nursery of Tuanku Jaafar Hospital, Seremban, Malaysia, between January and June 2018. Neonates were included if they had a total serum bilirubin (TSB) level of ≥ 5 mg/dL or 85 µmol/L. Consent was obtained and data were collected from the patients’ admission file. Statistical analyses were conducted using the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. Results demonstrated that 66.1% (n = 1,042) neonates had NNJ, of which 5% (n = 79) were G6PD-deficient. The prevalence of G6PD deficiency was higher in males (89.9%, n = 71) than in females (10.1%, n = 8). The disorder was found to be highest in Malay (6.4%, n = 67), followed by Chinese (0.9%, n = 9), Others (0.2%, n = 2), and Orang Asli (0.1%, n = 1). No G6PD-deficient NNJ case was detected among the Indian ethnic group. Three main aetiologies in this cohort were breastfeeding jaundice, followed by breast milk jaundice, and G6PD deficiency. A total of 35 G6PD-deficient neonates required phototherapy (PT) for more than 48 h, 39 cases required PT for 3 to 4 days, and 5 cases required PT for more than 5 days. G6PD-deficient neonates required a significantly longer duration (> 48 h) of phototherapy (55.7%, n = 44, P < 0.01) and had a higher proportion requiring ET (5%, n = 4, P < 0.01) compared to G6PD-normal neonates. This study highlights the high prevalence of G6PD deficiency among Malaysian neonates, which contributes to a significant burden on the national healthcare and economic systems through longer hospital stays for NNJ treatment. Effective screening, early identification among jaundiced neonates, targeted parental education, and strengthened clinical management are warranted to reduce associated risks and improve neonatal outcomes.Keywords: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, Neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia, Jaundice, Phototherapy, Exchange transfusion, HaemolysisSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is the most common enzymopathy worldwide that disrupts erythrocyte metabolism. The genetic disorder results in reduced G6PD enzyme activity, an essential component of the oxidative phosphogluconate pathway1,2. Globally, an estimated 400 million people are affected by the genetic condition, with studies demonstrating more than 200 different mutations cause the disorder3–5. It has a wide geographical distribution, reaching high frequencies in Africa (affecting up to 20% of the population), the Mediterranean (4% − 30%), and Asia (0.5–26%)6. In Malaysia, a Southeast Asian country, a high prevalence of G6PD deficiency has been reported among several ethnicities, ranging from 4.6% to 15.2%7–11. Malaysia is characterized by a complex and unique ethnic composition due to its historical development, including Malays, Chinese, Indians, Indigenous peoples (often called the Orang Asli), and others. Owing to this, previous studies have indicated that Malaysia has a significant G6PD genetic heterogeneity. In a recent study using DNA samples from 141 G6PD-deficient Malaysian neonates, a mutational analysis via reverse dot blot flow-through hybridization assay shows that 13 out of the 14 known G6PD mutations existed in the cohort, including 6PD Viangchan 871G > A (21%), G6PD Mahidol 487G > A(20%), G6PD Mediterranean 563 C > T (14%), G6PD Vanua Lava 383 T > C (12%), G6PD Canton 1376G > T (10%), G6PD Orissa 131 C > G (6.3%), and G6PD Coimbra 592 C > T (5.6%)12. In another study involving 662 blood samples of the Senoi Orang Asli, a high prevalence of G6PD deficiency (15.2%) was detected among the studied population, with seven mutations were identified by the investigators, including IVS11 T93C (47.1%), rs1050757 (3’UTR + 357 A > G)(39.1%), G6PD Viangchan 871G > A(25.3%), G6PD Union 1360 C > T (21.8%), c.1311 C > T(20.7%), G6PD Kaiping 1388G > A(8.0%), and G6PD Coimbra 592 C > T(2.3%)8.

G6PD deficiency follows an X-linked recessive pattern of inheritance, which indicates that the affected males will typically manifest the disorder. Females can be either homozygous or heterozygous for G6PD deficiency, but the majority are heterozygous carriers2. Although G6PD deficiency carriers are often asymptomatic, exposure to oxidative stresses such as fava beans (favism), certain antimalarial medications such as Primaquine, henna (a widely used cosmetic reddish dye), and several infections may result in an increased risk of developing life-threatening clinical complications. In adults, these G6PD-related clinical complications include acute hemolytic anemia, a severe condition where erythrocytes are destroyed more rapidly than they can be replaced by the body, in response to high oxidative burden and low RBCs’ G6PD activity. For example, researchers reported a case of acute hemolysis in a 26-year-old male with known G6PD deficiency after he was prescribed Primaquine for presumptive anti-relapse therapy against Plasmodium vivax malaria13. In another study, a case of henna-induced acute hemolysis with a hemoglobin level of 8.8 g/dL was reported in an 80-year-old female14.

In the children’s age group, many cases have also been reported regarding the G6PD complications. A 4-year-old child who ingested 40–60 g of fava beans suffered from severe hemolysis, for which he was later diagnosed with G6PD deficiency15. The case indicates that even a small amount of the known triggering substances, such as fava beans, can be harmful to G6PD-deficient patients. In another reported case, a 2-year-old child presented with signs and symptoms of acute hemolytic anemia, including jaundice, nausea, vomiting, and dark urine, following the consumption of a teaspoon of fava beans given by the mother. The child was also later diagnosed with the genetic condition16. Unlike adults and older children, the exposure of these oxidative triggers among G6PD-deficient neonates is less common. Previous studies have documented that G6PD-related complications in neonates may occur following the usage of mothballs (i.e., naphthalene and paradichlorobenzene) in the household and the consumption of fava beans (i.e., Vicia faba) by breastfeeding mothers17,18.

G6PD deficiency in neonates is more frequently manifested as rapid-onset, persistent neonatal jaundice (NNJ) and severe hemolytic anemia. Without early diagnosis and treatment, these G6PD-deficient neonates may suffer from severe neurological damage, including bilirubin-induced neurologic dysfunction (BIND) and kernicterus19. Studies reported that the frequency of kernicterus is particularly seen in G6PD-deficient neonates residing in the Mediterranean and Asian regions, compared to those in North America and Europe20. Although the overall incidence of kernicterus is infrequent, the neurological toxicity carries a significant mortality risk among neonates (up to 10%) and long-term morbidity (up to 70%)21,22. In Malaysia, however, the incidence of acute bilirubin encephalopathy and kernicterus among NNJ babies remains unknown as the data have not been routinely or systematically collected.

In addition to G6PD deficiency, various other causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia may also contribute to NNJ and should be considered during early diagnostic evaluation. These include cephalhematoma, early gestational age, exclusive breastfeeding, particularly when breastfeeding is unsuccessful or when there is notable weight loss (≥ 8–10%), isoimmune hemolysis, and other forms of hemolytic anemia23. Given the significant burden of G6PD deficiency in the Malaysian population, it remains a key factor to investigate in the context of NNJ. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the prevalence of G6PD deficiency among jaundiced neonates in Malaysia and the association between G6PD deficiency with sociodemographic characteristics, aetiological factors, and treatment needs.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted on 1,576 neonates who were diagnosed with NNJ and admitted to the Special Care Nursery (SCN) for phototherapy treatment in Hospital Tuanku Jaafar, Seremban, Central Peninsular Malaysia. The duration of this study was from January 2018 to June 2018. The inclusion criteria were neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia, defined as a total serum bilirubin (TSB) level of ≥ 5 mg/dL (85 µmol/L). Neonates were excluded if they had conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, defined as a direct (conjugated) bilirubin level greater than 20% of the TSB, or if the total serum bilirubin was ≤ 5.0 mg/dL with a direct bilirubin level > 1.0 mg/dL. Other exclusion criteria included neonatal sepsis, defined by systemic signs of infection (such as diminished spontaneous activity, reduced sucking, apnoea, bradycardia, temperature instability, respiratory distress, vomiting, or diarrhoea) along with the isolation of a bacterial pathogen from the bloodstream. Neonatal polycythaemia was also an exclusion criterion, defined as a haematocrit level > 65% or haemoglobin concentration > 22 g/dL from peripheral venous sampling in term infants.

All neonates who were admitted to the SCN, whether outborn or inborn, were initially assessed by the attending medical officer. A thorough history and physical examination were conducted, and relevant laboratory investigations were performed. Neonates who met the inclusion criteria based on physical examination and laboratory findings were provided with a Patient Information Sheet and a Consent Form. A detailed explanation of the study was given by the investigator to the parents, and informed consent was obtained from the parents after they had read the information sheet. The research protocol was fully registered with the National Medical Research Register (NMRR), Malaysia (NMRR-17–3034-39171). We confirm that all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We confirm that written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all neonates included in this study.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM) Research Ethics Committee (Reference Number: FF-2018-383).

Data collection

Data were collected from the Bed Head Ticket following the neonate’s admission. The data include demographic information such as gender and ethnicity, as well as clinical details related to jaundice, such as the causes of jaundice, age of onset, duration of double phototherapy, and whether ET was performed. Laboratory results were retrieved from the hospital’s Laboratory Information System, including total serum bilirubin levels, maternal blood group, neonatal blood group, Coomb’s test result, and G6PD status. The G6PD status was identified based on fluorescence spot test (FST) results that were used as the routine newborn screening method in our center (i.e., G6PD-deficient or G6PD-normal).

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and clinical variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and aetiologies of neonatal jaundice

A total of 1,042 out of 1,576 jaundiced neonates (66.1%) who were admitted for phototherapy fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were included in this study. Of these, 553 (53.1%) were males, 486 (46.6%) were females, and 3 (0.3%) had indeterminate gender due to ambiguous genitalia. Majority of the neonates (n = 599, 57.5%) were admitted for breastfeeding jaundice, followed by breast milk jaundice (n = 245, 23.5%), G6PD deficiency (n = 79, 7.6%), multifactorial causes of jaundice (n = 59, 5.7%), ABO incompatibility (n = 44, 4.2%), cephalohematoma (n = 13, 1.2%), and indeterminate causes (n = 2, 0.3%) (Fig. 1).

Distribution of aetiologies of unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia among neonates (n = 1,042).

Among G6PD-deficient neonates, 71 (89.9%) were males and 8 (10.1%) were females, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 9:1. A significant difference was found in the prevalence of G6PD deficiency between both genders (p < 0.001). According to the Malaysian ethnicities, G6PD deficiency was most prevalent among Malay neonates (n = 67, 6.4%), followed by Chinese (n = 9, 0.9%), other ethnicities (n = 2, 0.2%), and Orang Asli (n = 1, 0.1%). Meanwhile, no cases of G6PD deficiency were identified among Indian neonates. Statistical analysis showed a significantly higher prevalence of G6PD deficiency among Malays compared to other ethnicities (p < 0.005) (Table 1).

Clinical presentation and treatment needs

The age at presentation between G6PD-deficient and G6PD-normal neonates was significantly different (p < 0.01). Among G6PD-deficient neonates, the majority (n = 40, 50.6%) presented within the first day of life, followed by presentations between the second and fourth day (n = 27, 34.2%), and after the fourth day (n = 12, 15.2%). In contrast, among G6PD-normal neonates, a smaller number and percentage of cases (n = 129, 13.4%) presented within the first day, while the majority (n = 437, 45.4%) presented between the second and fourth day, and the remaining cases (n = 397, 41.2%) presented after the fourth day. Overall, G6PD-deficient neonates demonstrated a significantly earlier onset of jaundice compared to G6PD-normal neonates. With regards to the duration of phototherapy, it was significantly longer in G6PD-deficient compared to G6PD-normal neonates (p < 0.01). Among G6PD-deficient neonates, a total of 35 cases (44.3%) required phototherapy for less than 48 h, while 39 (49.4%) required phototherapy for three to four days. A remaining 5 (6.3%) G6PD-deficient neonates required phototherapy for more than 5 days. In contrast, the majority of G6PD-normal neonates (n = 752, 78.1%) completed phototherapy within less than 48 h. A smaller percentage of G6PD-normal neonates require phototherapy for three to four days (n = 200, 20.8%) and more than 5 days (n = 11, 1.1%)(Table 2).

Discussion

Globally, NNJ remains one of the leading causes of admission to neonatal intensive care units. In 2018, the Global Burden of Disease study ranked NNJ as the seventh leading cause of mortality in the first six days of the neonatal period24. Severe NNJ requires urgent attention by the attending physician, as it may lead to acute and chronic bilirubin encephalopathy. Hence, early identification and prompt management of NNJ are highly critical to prevent these life-threatening complications. During the six-month period of our study, a total of 1,576 NNJ cases were recorded in our health facility. This finding indicates a high burden of this clinical condition in our setting, as well as the continuing challenge faced by healthcare providers in managing NNJ effectively. Our findings are supported by a previous survey conducted in government hospitals and health centres under the Ministry of Health, Malaysia, which reported that 75% of newborns were jaundiced within the first week of life25. The high occurrence of NNJ in both local and global settings emphasizes the importance of continuous monitoring and effective public health interventions.

One of the well-established risk factors for NNJ is G6PD deficiency, a highly prevalent genetic disorder in Malaysia. In this study, G6PD deficiency was identified as the top 3 leading causes of NNJ, following breastfeeding jaundice and breast milk jaundice. Being an X-linked genetic condition, G6PD deficiency is more prevalent in males than in females. This is consistent with our findings, which showed that among G6PD-deficient neonates, the majority were males (89.9%, 71/79), compared to females (10.1%, 8/79), and the difference was statistically significant.

The overall prevalence of G6PD deficiency among the ethnic groups in this study was 6.4% in Malays, 0.9% in Chinese, 0.2% in other ethnicities, and 0.1% in the Orang Asli. Notably, none of the Indian neonates with NNJ in this cohort were diagnosed with G6PD deficiency. These findings differ from those reported in a previous study conducted by Ainoon et al., where the highest prevalence of G6PD deficiency was observed among Chinese neonates (6.0%), followed by Malays (4.6%) and Indians (1.3%)26. Similarly, a study by Alex et al. reported that the highest prevalence of G6PD deficiency was among Chinese (4.5%), followed by Malays (3.5%) and Indians (1.5%)27. The ethnic distribution discrepancy in our findings may be attributed to sampling bias, as the majority of NNJ admissions in our hospital were from the Malay ethnic group (86.3%, 899/1042), while Chinese and Indian neonates accounted for only 7.2% and 5.1% of the cases, respectively. However, this distribution reflects the demographic composition of our study setting, which was located in the Seremban district. In this regional area, Malays constitute more than 50% of the population. This demographic context may also explain the lower incidence of G6PD deficiency among Chinese neonates in our study compared to previous reports.

In addition, our findings demonstrated that G6PD deficiency accounts for 7.6% (79/1042) of the total selected NNJ admissions, of which more than half of these cases (55.3%, 44/79) require a prolonged duration of phototherapy of more than 48 h. In contrast, the majority of G6PD-normal neonates (78.1%) required phototherapy for less than 48 h. This signifies that there is a greater severity and persistence of jaundice among G6PD-deficient neonates compared to G6PD-normal neonates. The proportion of neonates requiring phototherapy beyond 5 days was also higher among G6PD-deficient babies (6.3%) compared to G6PD-normal neonates (1.1%). These findings suggest that ongoing haemolysis is present in G6PD-deficient neonates even in the absence of exposure to known haemolytic triggers. Spontaneous haemolysis in G6PD-deficient neonates without identifiable haemolytic stressors has also been documented in a previous study28. Our findings are supported by a study by Yang et al., which reported that G6PD deficiency accounted for 9.66% (42/435), of which 3 neonates of their cohort were complicated with acute bilirubin encephalopathy29. Hence, this neonatal group should be given closer monitoring and timely interventions to prevent G6PD-related complications.

We also observed that approximately half of the G6PD-deficient jaundiced neonates in our study (50.6%) were admitted for phototherapy within the first 24 h of life, whereas the majority of G6PD-normal jaundiced neonates (45.4%) were admitted for phototherapy between the second and fourth day of life. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.01). However, this finding differs from those reported by Ainoon et al., who found that 40% of G6PD-deficient neonates developed jaundice only after 48 h of life, and none developed jaundice within the first 24 hours26. One possible explanation for the earlier initiation of phototherapy in our setting is the current hospital practice of commencing prophylactic phototherapy, an approach of initiating phototherapy before the TSB level reaches a pre-specified threshold. Previous studies have demonstrated that prophylactic phototherapy in preterm and low birth weight neonates can reduce the need for ET by 78% and lower the risk of neurodevelopmental impairments, such as severe hearing loss and cerebral palsy, by 15% compared to controls30.

In this study, only 1.4% of G6PD-normal neonates required ET, compared to 5.0% among the G6PD-deficient neonates. This difference in ET requirement between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.01). Among G6PD-normal neonates, the primary aetiological cause of severe hyperbilirubinemia was breastfeeding jaundice. Maisels et al. reported that breastfeeding is associated with severe hyperbilirubinemia, particularly when linked to low caloric intake and dehydration31. Additionally, most of these neonates presented late to healthcare facilities, typically after the third day of life. This delay may have been due to late recognition, delayed referrals, or the common assumption that jaundice was physiological in origin. Early detection and timely initiation of phototherapy could have potentially prevented the need for ET in these cases. Although ET rapidly lowers circulating bilirubin levels by exchanging the infant’s blood with compatible donor blood, it carries inherent risks and potential complications.

Study limitations and recommendations

A noteworthy finding of this study was that the majority of G6PD-normal neonates who developed severe hyperbilirubinemia requiring ET were females (10 out of 14 cases). This suggests the possibility of undetected partial G6PD deficiency within this subgroup, as the qualitative FST was used in our study that may fail to detect heterozygous females with intermediate enzyme activity. The absence of quantitative enzyme assays in our protocol raise the possibility that some G6PD-deficient females were likely misclassified as G6PD-normal, potentially underestimating the true impact of G6PD deficiency on the requirement for ET. This finding is consistent with those reported by Reclos et al., who found that among 2,150 neonates initially classified as normal by FST, 24 female subjects were later identified as partially deficient using the quantitative enzyme assay32.

In addition, this study did not include a molecular analysis of G6PD genotypes. While identifying specific G6PD variants provides valuable epidemiological data, our research was designed to focus specifically on clinical outcomes and the practical management of neonatal jaundice, including the timing of presentation and the required duration of treatment. We acknowledge that genotyping would provide a more granular understanding of the aetiology and severity of neonatal jaundice in our cohort, which remains a target for future prospective research.

We also did not screen for other genetic mutations associated with hyperbilirubinemia, such as UGT1A1. Based on our clinical data, the primary identified cause of severe hyperbilirubinemia in the G6PD-normal neonates who required ET was breastfeeding jaundice, which was often exacerbated by late presentation to healthcare facilities. However, because UGT1A1 screening was not conducted, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that concurrent genetic factors contributed to the severity of the jaundice in these infants. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive molecular genotyping are warranted to better characterize the severity and underlying aetiologies of neonatal jaundice.

Lastly, in order to address the diagnostic limitation in future research, we recommend incorporating recent biosensor technology for quantitative point-of-care population screening, as demonstrated by multiple recent studies11,33,34. However, while these studies have demonstrated the efficacy of the biosensors in adult populations, their scientific evidence for application in neonates remains limited. This is particluarly important as higher reticulocyte counts in newborns can lead to higher baseline enzyme activity, and therefore, adult cutoff values are not directly applicable. Further research is required to establish standardized, universal cutoff values specific to the neonatal population to ensure diagnostic accuracy of these emerging technologies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the high prevalence of G6PD deficiency among Malaysian neonates, which contributes to a substantial burden on the national healthcare and economic systems. G6PD deficiency emerged as a critical aetiology in severe clinical presentations, characterized by a markedly earlier NNJ onset and a requirement for more prolonged phototherapy compared to G6PD-normal neonates. Ocnseuqently, effective screening strategies, early identification among jaundiced neonates, targeted parental education, and strengthened clinical management protocols are warranted to mitigate associated risks and improve neonatal outcomes.
