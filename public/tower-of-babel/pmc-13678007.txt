Structural basis for cysteine-linked redox-state plasticity in mammalian methionine sulfoxide reductase A
Authors: Byeongmin Shin, Hye-Jin Kim Hawkes, Seonha Park, Hosam Ki, Sulhee Kim, Fenil Shah, Giovanna Di Trapani, Kathryn F. Tonissen, Kwang Yeon Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methionine sulfoxide reductase A (MsrA) protects proteins from oxidative damage through cysteine-based redox cycling, but how mammalian MsrA is structurally regulated during oxidation and reductive recycling has remained unclear. We report three crystal structures of mouse MsrA (mMsrA) that capture distinct redox states — reduced (3.0 Å), BME-trapped intermediate-like (BTIL; 1.6 Å), and oxidized (1.9 Å) — and reveal an unrecognized layer of regulation centered on Cys107. The BTIL structure visualizes catalytic Cys72 as a mixed disulfide with β-mercaptoethanol (BME) alongside a sulfenic acid at Cys107, a configuration not previously observed. The oxidized structure resolves the canonical Cys72–Cys218 disulfide together with an unexpected Cys107–Cys227 pairing that locks a C-terminal loop into a cap over the active site. LC-MS/MS mapping, MD simulations, activity assays, and thioredoxin (Trx)-trapping experiments converge on a model in which Cys72 drives catalysis, while Cys107-mediated disulfide rearrangement and Cys227-directed Trx entry gate substrate access and enzymatic regeneration — defining a noncanonical oxidized state that expands the mechanistic repertoire of mammalian MsrA under oxidative stress.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can oxidize sulfur-containing amino acids and thereby alter protein structure, activity, and signaling [1]. Among these modifications, methionine oxidation is unusual because methionine sulfoxide can be enzymatically reduced back to methionine [2], [3]. This reversibility allows methionine residues to function not only as oxidation-sensitive sites but also as repairable redox switches in proteins involved in cellular homeostasis, stress resistance, and aging-associated pathways [4], [5], [6], [7].

Methionine sulfoxide reductases (Msrs) catalyze this repair reaction. MsrA reduces the S-epimer of methionine sulfoxide, whereas MsrB reduces the R-epimer [8], [9]. During the established MsrA catalytic cycle, the catalytic cysteine attacks methionine sulfoxide to generate methionine and a cysteine-sulfenic acid intermediate. This intermediate is then resolved through intramolecular disulfide exchange involving C-terminal resolving cysteines, and the enzyme is regenerated by thioredoxin (Trx) or a related reducing system [10], [11], [12], [13].

Previous structural and biochemical studies have established the conserved MsrA catalytic core, including the GCFWG active-site motif, as well as conformational variation in loops surrounding the active site, particularly the C-terminal resolving segment. However, this segment remains difficult to capture structurally because it is mobile and changes position dynamically during redox cycling. Most available structures therefore represent either reduced-like states or engineered oxidized states, leaving the native structural range of mammalian MsrA under oxidative conditions less clearly defined [12], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18].

Mammalian MsrA also has regulatory features, including N-terminal myristoylation and subcellular targeting, which distinguish it from many bacterial enzymes [19], [20]. Mouse MsrA (mMsrA) contains Cys107 near the active-site region, in addition to catalytic Cys72 and the C-terminal cysteines Cys218 and Cys227. Although Cys107 has not generally been assigned as a resolving cysteine in the established Cys72-Cys218-Cys227 relay, its position near the active-site region led us to examine whether it could contribute to redox-dependent conformational states under oxidizing or thiol-exchange conditions [10], [11], [21].

Here, we determine three crystal structures of mMsrA and combine them with LC-MS/MS peptide mapping, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, cysteine-mutagenesis activity assays, and Trx-trapping experiments. In particular, we show that Cys107 can be sulfenylated or incorporated into alternative disulfide arrangements and that the oxidized C-terminal loop can cap the active-site pocket, revealing an oxidation-associated conformation that may influence substrate access and Trx engagement. This study provides a structural framework for understanding how mMsrA responds to oxidative conditions through redox-dependent conformational remodeling.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

Full-length mMsrA was cloned into pET21a (Novagen). mMsrA was overexpressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta 1 cells (Novagen) at 37 °C for 4 h after induction with 1.0 mM isopropyl β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Cells were harvested, and each pellet from 1 L of culture was resuspended in 20 mL of 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME), followed by sonication on ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 28,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered and applied to Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. Bound protein was eluted at 5 mL/min with a linear imidazole gradient in 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM BME, and up to 500 mM imidazole. The protein was further purified by ion exchange chromatography using a linear NaCl gradient in 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and up to 1 M NaCl, and then concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore). Size exclusion chromatography was performed on a Superdex 75 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1% (v/v) glycerol, and 2 mM DTT. For preparation of the oxidized form, the same size exclusion step was performed without a reducing agent. Protein samples were concentrated to 25 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C.

Crystallization and X-ray data collection

Initial crystallization screening of mMsrA alone did not yield crystals suitable for structural analysis. To facilitate crystallization and explore potential complex formation with calmodulin or Trx, synthetic peptides derived from calmodulin, native Trx, and trapping Trx were used for co-crystallization screening. mMsrA was mixed with each peptide at a 1:1.3 M ratio before crystallization. Crystals were grown at 22 °C by sitting-drop or hanging-drop vapor diffusion, with 1 μL of protein solution mixed with 1 μL of reservoir solution. Reduced-form crystals were obtained in the presence of the calmodulin-derived MET6 peptide from 100 mM MES monohydrate, pH 5.9, and 19% (w/v) PEG 20,000. BTIL crystals were obtained in the presence of the trapping Trx peptide from 100 mM Tris, pH 9.0, 200 mM NaCl, 26% (w/v) PEG 3350, and 100 mM sodium iodide. Oxidized-form crystals were obtained without added peptide from 100 mM Tris, pH 9.0, 200 mM NaCl, 26% (w/v) PEG 3350, and 4% (v/v) formamide. The sequences of the synthetic peptides are summarized in Table S1. Reduced and BTIL crystals appeared within 7 days, whereas diffraction-quality crystals of oxidized form appeared only after incubation for more than 3 months. Crystals were transferred to the corresponding reservoir solution supplemented with 25% (v/v) glycerol and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory and processed with DENZO and SCALEPACK in the HKL-2000 package [22].

Structure determination

The mMsrA structures were solved by molecular replacement using MOLREP [23] and Phaser implemented in PHENIX [24], with bovine MsrA (PDB ID: 1FVA) as the search model. Matthews-coefficient analysis indicated one molecule in the asymmetric unit. Models were fitted with the electron-density maps using Coot [25] and refined iteratively with Refine module in PHENIX [26] and REFMAC5 in CCP4 [27]. Although calmodulin-derived and trapping Trx peptides were included in crystallization trials, no interpretable electron density corresponding to these peptides was observed in the final structures. Structural figures were generated using PyMOL [28]. Data-collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Table S2.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The oxidized (PDB ID: 44ND) and reduced (PDB ID: 44NI) structures of mMsrA were simulated using GROMACS 2021.4 [29] with the OPLS-AA/L force field [30] and TIP3P water model [31] in a cubic box with a 10 Å edge distance. Systems were neutralized with Na+/Cl− ions and energy-minimized with a force threshold of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1 before equilibration. Equilibration consisted of 1 ns under the NVT ensemble and 2 ns under the NPT ensembles at 300 K and 1 atm, followed by 100 ns production runs under NPT conditions. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the V-rescale thermostat [32] and Parrinello-Rahman coupling [33]. Periodic boundary conditions were applied, with a 1 nm cutoff for electrostatic and van der Waals interactions. Long-range electrostatics were computed using the particle-mesh Ewald method [34], and bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm [35]. Structural stability and dynamics were assessed by RMSD and RMSF calculations.

LC-MS/MS-based disulfide peptide mapping

Purified mMsrA at 380 μM in 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 9.0 was used as the untreated sample. For the oxidized sample, mMsrA was treated with 500 μM H2O2, corresponding to an H2O2: mMsrA molar ratio of approximately 1.3 : 1, for 3 min at room temperature and immediately buffer-exchanged into 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 9.0. Disulfide-linked peptides were analyzed by non-reducing LC–MS/MS peptide mapping [36]. Briefly, 100 μg of protein from each condition was prepared for digestion with trypsin/Lys-C or chymotrypsin. Samples were denatured with ammonium bicarbonate and 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol at 60 °C for 45 min. Reduced control samples were prepared in parallel by adding DTT to a final concentration of 5 mM. Free cysteines were alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After dilution with water and 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, samples were digested with trypsin/Lys-C or chymotrypsin at a protein-to-enzyme ratio of 20:1 (w/w) for 16 h at 37 °C. Digestion was quenched with formic acid, and peptides were desalted using Oasis HLB cartridges, dried by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. Peptides were analyzed using an EASY-nLC 1200 system coupled to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer. MS data were acquired in positive ion mode with full MS scans at 70,000 resolution over an m/z range of 350–2,000, followed by data-dependent MS/MS acquisition of the top 20 precursor ions. Disulfide-linked peptides were identified using BioPharma Finder 5.1 by comparing non-reduced and reduced peptide maps.

Activity assay for MsrA

Methionine sulfoxide reductase activity of wild-type and cysteine-to-serine mutant mMsrA proteins was measured using dabsyl-methionine-S-sulfoxide as substrate. Each 100 μL reaction contained 50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 200 μM dabsyl-methionine-S-sulfoxide, and 0.3–0.6 μM purified mMsrA. DTT-dependent activity was measured in the presence of 20 mM DTT. Thioredoxin-dependent activity was measured using 0.2 mM NADPH, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10 μM E. coli Trx1, and 1 μM human thioredoxin reductase 1 [37]. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and dabsyl-methionine formation was quantified by HPLC [38]. Specific activity was calculated as the amount of dabsyl-methionine formed per minute per milligram of protein and is reported as nmol min−1 mg−1.

Kinetic trapping of mMsrA by thioredoxin

The QuikChange II site-directed mutagenesis kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used to mutate one or more of four cysteine residues including Cys72, Cys107, Cys218, and Cys227 within the mMsrA sequence (UniProt Q9D6Y7, Gene ID 110265) to serine residues. Mutant constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, and protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG for 16 h at 37 °C. Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer at 10 mL/g wet weight and lysed by sonication. Cell debris was removed by ultracentrifugation at 117,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting extracts were used for trapping experiments. Prior to trapping experiments, expression of wild-type and mutant mMsrA proteins was assessed by immunoblot analysis of the corresponding cell extracts using the anti-mouse MsrA antibodies [39]. All variants were readily detectable and showed broadly comparable expression levels. For trapping experiments, the amounts of cell extract applied were adjusted, where necessary, to achieve approximately similar levels of recombinant mMsrA protein across samples. The Trx-trapping construct, kindly provided by Tobias Dick (Heidelberg, Germany) [40], encodes human Trx1 C35S fused to a hexahistidine tag and a streptavidin-binding peptide (SBP). In the CSAAA construct, the three additional cysteines Cys62, Cys69, and Cys73 were replaced with alanine to minimize nonspecific cysteine oxidation. The Trx-trapping construct in pQE-60 vector was transformed into E. coli XL1-Blue strain (Stratgene), expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG for 16 h at 37 °C, and the recombinant protein was purified using HisLink resin (Promega). Briefly, 100 μg of purified trapping Trx1 was incubated with 100 μL of streptavidin-Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare) overnight at 4 °C in streptavidin-binding buffer containing 40 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 20 mM DTT at pH 7.4. Thus, Trx1 was immobilized on the beads under reducing conditions. The following day, the Trx-loaded beads were washed five times with 1× PBS (pH 7.4) to remove residual DTT before addition of the target protein extracts. The washed Trx-loaded beads were then incubated with E. coli extracts expressing wild-type or mutant mMsrA for 1 h at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were washed and bound complexes were eluted. Samples were analyzed under reducing and nonreducing conditions by 15% SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting with anti-mouse MsrA rabbit polyclonal antibodies [39]. Anti-Trx rabbit polyclonal antibodies [41] confirmed Trx in the captured complexes.

Results

Overall structures of three redox-related states of mMsrA

Although numerous MsrA structures have been determined across diverse species, mammalian MsrA remains structurally underrepresented. Bovine MsrA has provided the principal published mammalian X-ray reference [12], and a myristoylated mMsrA structure has been determined by solution NMR [19]. mMsrA is highly conserved with human and bovine MsrA and contains the conserved GCFWG motif (Fig. S1A). It also contains four cysteine residues: catalytic Cys72, the C-terminal resolving cysteines Cys218 and Cys227, and the nearby Cys107, which is conserved in several mammalian MsrA homologs but has not been assigned as a classical resolving cysteine [10], [11], [12]. In the reduced structure, the catalytic pocket is well defined and contains Gly71, Cys72, Phe73, Trp74, and Gly75 in the GCFWG motif, together with Tyr103, Glu115, and Tyr155, which stabilize the pocket (Fig. S1B).

All three structures preserve the conserved catalytic core but differ markedly in terminal region and cysteine chemistry. In the reduced and BTIL structures, the N-terminal residues 1-25 and the C-terminal residues 215-235 were not visible (Fig. 1A and B). By contrast, in the oxidized structure, the N-terminal residues 1-44 remain unresolved, whereas the C-terminal residues 215-235 were clearly ordered and form a short helical segment stabilized by intramolecular disulfides (Fig. 1C).

Active-site cysteine oxidation and disulfide remodeling in mMsrA

In contrast to the reduced structure, the BTIL structure shows catalytic Cys72 forming a mixed disulfide with BME, whereas nearby Cys107 is observed in a sulfenic acid form (Cys107-SOH) (Fig. 2A). No interpretable density was observed for Cys218 or Cys227, indicating that the C-terminal resolving segment had not folded into the active-site region in this state. The oxidized structure contains the expected Cys72-Cys218 disulfide, consistent with the established MsrA resolving mechanism [10], [11], [12]. Unexpectedly, it also contains a second intramolecular disulfide between Cys107 and Cys227 (Fig. 2B). Following formation of the Cys72-Cys218 and Cys107-Cys227 disulfides, Tyr103, Glu115, and Tyr155 coordinate a bound water molecule rather than interacting with methionine sulfoxide or BME.

C-terminal capping and Cys107-linked alternative disulfide formation

In many MsrA structures from other species, the C-terminal loop carrying the resolving cysteine changes position substantially between redox states [12], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. In our oxidized structure, the C-terminal loop folds over the active site pocket and forms a cap-like conformation at the site where methionine sulfoxide or small thiol molecules would bind. To place this C-terminal conformation in a mammalian structural context, we compared oxidized mMsrA with reduced bovine MsrA (PDB ID: 1FVA), the closest published mammalian X-ray structure with a clearly resolved C-terminal segment [12]. Superposition of the two structures reveals that the largest difference occurs in the C-terminal loop (Fig. 3). In oxidized mMsrA, the Cys72-Cys218 and Cys107-Cys227 disulfides tether the normally flexible C-terminal segment to the protein core, rigidifying this region and effectively capping the active site. By contrast, the bovine structure lacks equivalent intramolecular disulfides among these cysteines, leaving the C-terminal loop oriented differently relative to the active site.

To test whether Cys107 can participate in alternative disulfide arrangements under oxidizing conditions, we performed LC-MS/MS-based disulfide peptide mapping. Cys72-Cys107 disulfide-linked peptides were detected in untreated mMsrA and remained detectable after H2O2 treatment, whereas H2O2 exposure additionally yielded Cys218-Cys227 and Cys107-Cys218 disulfide-linked peptides (Fig. S2). These findings indicate that Cys107 can participate in disulfide shuffling when mMsrA is exposed to oxidative stress.

Local flexibility in the terminal regions of mMsrA

The reduced, BTIL, and oxidized structures each contain regions with weak or missing electron density, particularly near the N- and C-terminal loops (Fig. 1). Together with the unusual disulfide pairings observed in the oxidized structure and LC-MS/MS peptide mapping, these unresolved regions suggest that local flexibility is an intrinsic feature of mMsrA.

B-factor analysis showed elevated values in the N-terminal region of the reduced structure and the C-terminal region of the oxidized structure, consistent with dynamic terminal-loop behavior (Fig. 4). To further assess structural dynamics, we performed 100 ns MD simulations of the reduced and oxidized forms. Per-residue RMSF analysis showed that the major differences between redox states were localized rather than globally distributed. Regions with elevated RMSF included the N-terminal loop/turn region including Thr45-His48 and His49-Gly52, the Asn100-Glu109 region, the Gln178-Phe186 region, and C-terminal segments including Leu210-Pro214 and Ser226-Glu235. Notably, the Asn100-Glu109 segment, which contains Cys107, showed higher RMSF values in the oxidized structure than in the reduced structure. When mapped onto the structures, these high-RMSF regions were located mainly in peripheral loops or terminal regions (Fig. 4). Interestingly, these flexible regions around Thr45-His48, His49-Gly52 and Leu210-Pro214 correspond to the boundaries of unresolved N- and C-terminal regions in the crystal structures, suggesting that terminal flexibility contributes to the lack of interpretable electron density in these regions. Because the oxidized and reduced structures contain different terminal residue ranges, RMSF values were plotted according to the original residue numbering and direct comparisons were restricted to residues present in both structures.

Comparison with MsrA homologs supports a distinct mammalian oxidized state

To date, no oxidized form of mammalian MsrA has been structurally characterized. Our oxidized mMsrA structure therefore provides a first structural example of a mammalian MsrA captured in an intramolecular oxidized state. To understand its unique features, we compared oxidized mMsrA with representative MsrA structures containing disulfides from other species (Fig. 5). The two E. coli MsrA structures (PDB IDs: 2IEM and 7OT4) capture disulfide states corresponding to the two-step resolving pathway in MsrA. The 2IEM structure shows a Cys51-Cys198 disulfide, corresponding to the Cys72-Cys218 pair in mMsrA, whereas the 7OT4 structure shows a Cys198-Cys206 disulfide, corresponding to the Cys218-Cys227 pair. Because these structures were obtained using C86S/C206S and C51S/C86A mutations, respectively, to block alternative disulfide formation, we interpret them as engineered catalytic-state mimics rather than fully native oxidized states.Fig. 5Structural comparison of oxidized mMsrA with representative MsrA homologs. We also examined the Neisseria meningitidis MsrA structure(PDB ID: 3BQH), which naturally contains only two cysteine residues, Cys51 and Cys198. As expected, these two residues form a disulfide bond in this oxidized catalytic state. In contrast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae Mxr1/MsrA (PDB ID: 3PIM) contains five cysteine residues and displays a different disulfide pairing between Cys68 and Cys176 in its unusual oxidized structure. Collectively, these comparisons show that oxidized mMsrA has a disulfide-bonding pattern and C-terminal-loop architecture distinct from previously reported MsrA structures [16], [17], [18].

Enzymatic activity of cysteine-to-serine mMsrA mutants

To examine the functional roles of the four cysteine residues in mMsrA, we measured the specific activities of wild-type and cysteine-to-serine mutant proteins under DTT-dependent and Trx-dependent reducing conditions (Table 1). Wild-type mMsrA showed robust activity under both conditions, whereas C72S was inactive, confirming Cys72 as the essential catalytic cysteine. C107S showed DTT-dependent activity comparable to that of the wild-type protein and retained substantial Trx-dependent activity, indicating that Cys107 is dispensable for the catalytic reductase cycle. In contrast, C218S and C227S retained DTT-dependent activity but showed markedly reduced or undetectable Trx-dependent activity. Similarly, the C218S/C227S and C107S/C218S/C227S mutants exhibited comparable or slightly reduced DTT-dependent activity relative to the wild type, whereas Trx-dependent activity was completely lost. C-terminal resolving cysteines provide Trx-accessible sites

Although Trx-dependent reduction of C-terminal resolving cysteines is well established for MsrA, the binding mode between mammalian MsrA and Trx has not been structurally defined. The yeast Mxr1-Trx2 complex provides one of the few structural examples of MsrA family enzyme bound to Trx [17]. When oxidized mMsrA was superposed with the yeast Mxr1-Trx2 complex, the C-terminal loop showed a noticeably different orientation (Fig. S3), suggesting that the oxidized mMsrA state is not preorganized in the same Trx-bound configuration. However, the Mxr1 construct used for complex formation also contains four cysteine-to-serine mutations, leaving Cys176 as the only remaining reactive cysteine. We therefore consider the possibility that alternative binding modes between mMsrA and Trx may still exist under native, non-mutated conditions.

To identify which mMsrA cysteines are directly engaged by Trx, we performed kinetic trapping experiments using 10 cysteine-to-serine mutants (Fig. 6). The trapping Trx protein contains a C35S substitution, allowing a stable mixed-disulfide complex to form in which the target protein remains covalently linked to Trx instead of being released after thiol-disulfide exchange [40]. Trx-dependent trapping was readily detected for wild-type mMsrA and mutants retaining Cys227, whereas markedly reduced trapping was visually observed for the C227S mutant. No Trx-bound mMsrA was detected when both Cys218 and Cys227 were mutated to serine. Trapping was also observed in triple mutants retaining only Cys227 or only Cys218, indicating that either residue could still participate in Trx engagement. In contrast, mutation of Cys107 did not abolish Trx trapping. Together, these results are consistent with Trx engagement with mMsrA through either Cys218 or Cys227 under the trapping conditions used, with the data suggesting a prominent role for Cys227. Furthermore, this interpretation is consistent with previous biochemical and mass-spectrometric analyses of bovine MsrA [10], [12]. Discussion

Reversible methionine oxidation provides a repairable redox modification, and MsrA is central to this process by reducing the S-epimer of methionine sulfoxide through cysteine-based chemistry [3], [11]. In this study, we captured three redox-related structural states of mMsrA and used them to examine how mammalian MsrA responds structurally to oxidation and reductive recycling. The established MsrA catalytic cycle depends on reversible oxidation of catalytic Cys72, formation of intramolecular disulfides with C-terminal resolving cysteines, and reduction by Trx or a related reducing system [10], [11], [12]. Our structures are consistent with this established Cys72-dependent pathway, but they also reveal a noncanonical oxidized state in which Cys107 can undergo sulfenylation and participate in alternative disulfide chemistry.

In the BTIL structure, catalytic Cys72 is capped by β-mercaptoethanol (BME), which was included as a reducing agent during purification. Sulfenylation of the catalytic cysteine is a canonical step in MsrA catalysis, but the BTIL state instead shows a sulfenic acid form at the nearby noncatalytic Cys107 (Fig. 2A). In an on-pathway catalytic intermediate, Cys72 would be expected to exist transiently as Cys72-SOH following reduction of methionine sulfoxide, before being resolved by the C-terminal resolving cysteine Cys218 to form the Cys72-Cys218 disulfide [13], [42]. Thus, the cysteine chemistry observed in the BTIL structure, with Cys72 trapped as a Cys72-BME mixed disulfide and Cys107 present as Cys107-SOH, differs from that expected for the canonical catalytic intermediate. This observation does not establish Cys107 as a second catalytic cysteine. Rather, it suggests that when Cys72 is functionally capped by a small thiol and the C-terminal resolving segment remains disordered, Cys107 can become a redox-sensitive cysteine within the local active-site environment. In our BTIL structure, Glu115 lies near both Cys72 and Cys107. Previous study of mMsrA proposed that the protonated form of this conserved glutamate can stabilize the neighboring cysteine thiolate through hydrogen bonding and thereby contribute to lowering its effective thiol pKa [21]. In addition, the BTIL crystal was obtained under a relatively high alkaline crystallization condition at pH 9.0, which would further increase the deprotonated cysteine thiolate and may have further favored the formation and oxidation of a reactive Cys107 thiolate. We also note that synchrotron X-ray exposure can promote site-selective oxidation of solvent-exposed cysteines during data collection, providing an additional route to sulfenic acid formation in crystallography. Radiolysis of solvent water by X-rays can generate reactive species, including hydroxyl radicals, which may contribute to oxidation of susceptible cysteine residues within the crystal environment [43], [44]. Therefore, we consider the possibility that X-ray exposure contributed to the Cys107-SOH modification observed in the BTIL structure. Taken together, the BTIL structure most reasonably reflects catalytic cysteine trapping at Cys72 together with selective oxidation of a nearby redox-sensitive Cys107 under the specific thiol, pH, and irradiation environment used for crystallography.

The oxidized mMsrA structure further supports an oxidation-associated role for Cys107 by showing the expected Cys72-Cys218 resolving disulfide together with an unusual Cys107-Cys227 disulfide. This double-disulfide state stabilizes the C-terminal loop over the active-site pocket and produces a cap-like conformation that could restrict substrate access and reposition the C-terminal redox module before Trx-mediated reduction. This localized structural remodeling is consistent with broader analyses of redox-paired protein structures, in which redox-active disulfides frequently accompany local conformational changes, order-disorder transitions, or altered interaction surfaces rather than simple global unfolding [45], [46], [47], [48]. In addition, structural comparisons with other MsrA homologs support the conclusion that oxidized mMsrA provides a previously unreported mammalian example of how an additional cysteine can reshape C-terminal loop geometry under oxidative conditions [16], [17], [18].

Based on the established MsrA catalytic framework and our cysteine-mutagenesis data, the results support the view that Cys72, Cys218, and Cys227 form the principal cysteine module for canonical mMsrA catalysis, whereas Cys107 is not required for this catalytic cycle. Cys72 acts as the essential catalytic cysteine that initiates MetO reduction and forms a Cys72-sulfenic acid intermediate. This intermediate is then resolved through the C-terminal cysteines, in which Cys218 and Cys227 support efficient Trx-dependent recycling(10-13, 23). When small-molecule reductants such as DTT or TCEP are present, these reducing agents have been reported to reduce the sulfenic acid state of catalytic cysteine in mMsrA, thereby bypassing the requirement for the resolving cysteines [39], [41]. Interestingly, this is consistent with our DTT-dependent activity assay with the very slightly reduced or retained activity of the resolving cysteine mutants in Table 1.

The catalytic Cys72 of mMsrA is expected to form a sulfenic acid intermediate during the canonical MetO reduction cycle. Because Cys72 has a low pKa and is readily oxidized by micromolar H2O2, oxidative conditions can also generate the same formal Cys72-SOH state. Under bulk oxidizing conditions, this sulfenic intermediate is likely to be transient and rapidly proceed to sulfenylamide formation, or hyperoxidation, depending on the redox environment [21], [42], [49]. However, the most direct kinetic evidence was obtained using a Cys72-focused mutant in which Cys107, Cys218, and Cys227 were replaced by serine, so the C-terminal resolving process could be diverted after formation of sulfenic intermediate, particularly under high pH, prolonged or stronger oxidative conditions, allowing Cys107 to participate in additional disulfides. Although the LC-MS/MS peptide mapping did not detect the exact Cys107-Cys227 disulfide observed in the oxidized crystal structure, it supports the possibility that Cys107 can form additional disulfides with other cysteines. In addition, we observed that Cys107 can exist as a redox-sensitive sulfenylated cysteine in the BTIL form of mMsrA and MD simulations further showed localized flexibility around the Asn100-Glu109 region, where Cys107 is located, as well as in the C-terminal segment (Fig. 4). Of these flexible regions, the Asn100-Glu109 segment may be particularly relevant in the oxidized state, as its increased flexibility coincides with the more extensive N-terminal disorder observed in the crystal structure. Although Cys107 forms a disulfide bond with Cys227 in the oxidized state, this local linkage does not necessarily rigidify the entire surrounding Asn100-Glu109 backbone segment, and the relatively elevated RMSF may therefore reflect local backbone mobility around the Cys107. This raises the possibility that changes in N-terminal conformation may be indirectly coupled to local dynamics within this segment, thereby contributing to redox-dependent conformational remodeling. Together, these results support the broader conclusion that Cys107 can enter alternative disulfide pairings under oxidative conditions.

The fact that oxidized mMsrA crystals appeared only after more than 3 months of crystallization also supports the possibility that the Cys107-Cys227 disulfide may have arisen not simply from the alkaline crystallization pH but also through slow air oxidation during long incubation in the crystallization solution. This process may have stabilized an oxidation-associated state rather than a rapidly formed catalytic intermediate. This interpretation is consistent with the unusual oxidized yeast Mxr1 structure (PDB ID: 3PIM), which contains five cysteine residues but requires only catalytic Cys25 and resolving Cys176 for canonical enzymatic turnover [17]. Moreover, structural comparison with the yeast Mxr1-Trx2 complex suggests that oxidized mMsrA is not preorganized for the same Trx-bound configuration. Together with our trapping data showing that Cys218 and Cys227 are the principal Trx-engaged resolving cysteines (Fig. 6), this comparison suggests that the Cys72-Cys218/Cys107-Cys227 double-disulfide state may be difficult for Trx to reduce efficiently and less likely to re-enter the catalytic cycle. Previous studies have also shown that mMsrA can access alternative oxidation chemistry when C-terminal resolving or Trx-dependent recycling is limited and that the enzyme is particularly susceptible to oxidation under micromolar H2O2 conditions [42], [49]. More generally, reversible intramolecular disulfide formation is a well-established mechanism by which oxidative conditions can stabilize alternative protein conformations and order–disorder transitions [45], [46], [48]. The cellular prevalence of the exact Cys72-Cys218/Cys107-Cys227 double-disulfide configuration remains to be established, but these findings provide a plausible context in which this oxidized state could accumulate when oxidative input exceeds reductive recycling capacity. We therefore view the oxidized form as a slowly accumulated oxidation-associated state that may persist until more favorable reducing conditions are restored.

Taken together, our findings provide new structural insight into the role of Cys107, a residue conserved in mammalian MsrA but previously considered nonessential for canonical catalysis, in redox-dependent conformational regulation. In addition, our results support the established mammalian MsrA catalytic cycle while also revealing new oxidation-associated branch states. Overall, this study places mMsrA within the broader context of methionine oxidation as a reversible redox modification and extends the structural framework for understanding how mammalian MsrA responds to oxidative conditions.
