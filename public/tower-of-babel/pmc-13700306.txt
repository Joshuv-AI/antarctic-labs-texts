Targeting MRPL37 disrupts the pro-tumorigenic mitochondrial cascade in bladder cancer: from LRPPRC deficiency to ROS/AKT axis inhibition
Authors: Xieyuan Leng, Wenhao Lu, Rongbin Zhou, Chengbang Wang, Zuheng Wang, Haodong Peng, Yitong Guo, Baoyu Huang, Dongwei Pan, Dianyu Wang, Zequn Su, Xuanfan Liu, Xuefang Liang, Tian Guo, Wen Liang, Wentin Chen, Zihang Huang, Zhe Chen, Yigan Zhang, Fubo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder cancer (BLCA) is a urinary system malignant tumor with high morbidity and mortality worldwide. There is an urgent need for new biomarkers and therapeutic targets. By integrating single-cell RNA sequencing, MitoCarta database and the Cancer Genome Atlas data, this study found that mitochondrial ribosomal protein L37 (MRPL37) was significantly up-regulated in BLCA, and its expression level was related to disease progression and poor prognosis. Functional experiments in vitro and in vivo confirmed that knockdown of MRPL37 could inhibit the proliferation, invasion, migration, metabolism and cisplatin resistance of BLCA cells by cell experiments, mouse subcutaneous tumor model, mouse orthotopic bladder cancer model and patient-derived organoid (PDO) model. A novel MRPL37-LRPPRC-ROS-AKT signaling axis was identified: loss of MRPL37 leads to the disorder of branched-chain amino acid metabolism, down-regulates the key mitochondrial protein LRPPRC, and then causes mitochondrial membrane potential damage and reactive oxygen species accumulation, which ultimately exert anti-cancer effect by inhibiting the AKT pathway. Introduction

Bladder cancer (BLCA) is a urothelial malignancy with high incidence and mortality, reported as the world’s 10th most common cancer, with more than 430,000 new cases annually [1, 2]. It is also the 14th leading cause of cancer deaths globally in both men and women [3], and in China BLCA has the highest incidence among tumors of the urinary and reproductive systems [4]. BLCA can occur at any age, but its incidence increases with age, peaking in the 50–70-year age group [2]. This high incidence is associated with smoking and long-term exposure to chemical products [5], while epigenetic diversity is also an indispensable component of BLCA development [6]. Because BLCA onset and progression is a slow, multifactorial, and covert process, traditional clinical tumor malignancy grading and prognosis assessment have substantial limitations for guiding treatment and formulating personalized therapeutic strategies. Epidemiological data indicate that, due to difficulties in early detection, some patients present with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) at diagnosis, and only about one-quarter can receive treatment and benefit from current standard care therapies [7]. In this context, clinicians face an urgent need to explore novel tumor biomarkers and identify effective targets for bladder cancer therapy.

Mitochondria, as one of the most important cellular substructures, are highly dynamic organelles enclosed by a double membrane and provide ATP via oxidative phosphorylation to support energy metabolism, while also regulating biosynthesis, Ca2 + homeostasis, redox balance, and other physiological processes [8]. Some studies have shown that tumor cell mitochondria differ markedly from those of normal cells, including mutations in mitochondrial DNA, altered mitochondrial morphology, and dysfunction. These changes may be related to tumor cell survival, proliferation, and anti-apoptotic capacity [9, 10]. Mitochondrial dysfunction further leads to the abnormal accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial DNA mutations, and energy metabolism dysregulation, thereby activating key signaling pathways such as mitophagy, apoptosis, or necrosis [11, 12]. Mitochondria play central and multifaceted roles in malignant tumor progression, and targeting mitochondria offers researchers new opportunities for cancer therapy [13, 14].

Eukaryotic mitochondrial ribosomes comprise a 28 S small subunit and a 39 S large subunit [15, 16], and they are key regulators of cellular respiration and energy metabolism. Evidence indicates that one of the shared molecular features across several tumor types is altered levels of nuclear-encoded proteins involved in mitochondrial ribosome biogenesis and function (including ribosomal proteins, assembly factors, and translation factors), which are linked to tumorigenesis [17, 18]. In recent years, studies on the mitochondrial ribosome protein-encoding gene family (MRPLs) have demonstrated that multiple targets are associated with tumor-specific cellular processes (e.g., proliferation, metabolism, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis). For example, MRPL13 has been shown to be involved in the proliferation cycle, migratory capacity, apoptosis, and autophagy in cancers including lung adenocarcinoma and breast cancer [19, 20]; MRPL12 acetylation at lysine 163 (K163) in clear cell renal cell carcinoma enhances mitochondrial metabolism and suppresses glycolysis, thereby inhibiting disease progression [21]. MRPL37 is likewise essential for mitochondrial function, but current studies are relatively scarce, having demonstrated its potential as a prognostic biomarker in gastric cancer and esophageal cancer [22, 23]. Its role in BLCA remains unclear, and no studies have deeply explored the mechanisms by which MRPL37 influences tumor initiation and progression.

In this study, we integrated analyses from multiple databases to identify a set of 11 mitochondrial genes that are concurrently enriched in tumor epithelial cells based on single-cell sequencing, significantly overexpressed in bladder cancer tissues compared to normal tissues in the TCGA database, and associated with poor prognosis in BLCA patients. LASSO regression analysis was then applied to refine this gene set, resulting in 8 key genes used to construct a prognostic model for bladder cancer. Subsequently, Mendelian randomization was employed to pinpoint MRPL37 as a critical gene. The functional impact of MRPL37 on bladder cancer was validated both in vitro using cell lines and in vivo with animal models. Further mechanistic investigation through sequencing and mass spectrometry techniques, complemented by experimental validation, revealed the MRPL37–LRPPRC–ROS–AKT molecular axis as a significant pathway involved in BLCA initiation and progression.

Methods

Human tissue specimens

Sections of bladder cancer tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. 2023-KY-0222).

Datasets acquisition

The RNA-sequencing data (401 BLCA tissues) for the TCGA-BLCA cohort were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data base (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The validation dataset comprises 165 BLCA tissue samples from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from the GSE222315 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Single-cell

The single-cell analysis pipeline was performed following previously established protocols [24, 25]. Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis was performed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R. During the quality control step, low-quality cells were excluded with the following thresholds: cells with fewer than 500 or more than 7000 detected genes, and those where the percentage of mitochondrial gene counts exceeded 25%. Normalization was performed using the LogNormalize method. The FindVariableFeatures function was employed to identify highly variable genes. To mitigate batch effects, data integration was conducted using the Harmony algorithm. Subsequently, the data were scaled using the ScaleData function, regressing out potential confounding factors, including cell cycle scores, the percentage of ribosomal and mitochondrial genes, and the total number of RNA molecules.Dimensionality reduction was performed sequentially using the RunPCA, RunUMAP, and RunTSNE functions. Cell clustering was conducted with the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Cell type annotation was accomplished by integrating automated annotation results from the SingleR package with a manual curation strategy. The FindAllMarkers function was utilized to identify cluster-specific marker genes, with the criteria that only positively expressed genes, a minimum expression fraction of 0.25, and a log2-fold change threshold greater than 0.75 were retained. The biological identity of each cell cluster was ultimately determined based on well-established marker genes from published literature.

Screening of mitochondrial genes

MitoCarta3.0 [26], which comprises 1136 protein-coding human genes with reliable mitochondrial localization, was utilized. Differential expression analysis of these mitochondrial genes between normal and BLCA tumor tissues was performed using the “limma” R package [27], with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. A co-expression network analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was subsequently constructed using the “igraph” R package [28].

Consensus clustering analysis

Consensus clustering based on the K-means algorithm was performed using the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” to categorize patients into distinct molecular subtypes [29]. The algorithm was repeated 50 times to achieve robust genotyping results. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating metrics including the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF), delta area, and cluster consensus score. Based on the consensus clustering of mitochondrial-localized genes, BLCA patients in the TCGA cohort were categorized into two distinct clusters. Overall survival (OS) between these two subgroups was subsequently compared using Kaplan-Meier analysis, implemented with the survival and survminer R packages.

Differences in clinicopathological characteristics and pathways between mitochondrial gene subgroups

Differences in clinicopathological variables between the two subgroups were analyzed and visualized using R packages including “limma”, “pheatmap”, and “ggpubr”. The prognostic outcomes of patients across different molecular subtypes were assessed with the “survival” and “survminer” packages. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two subgroups were identified using the “limma” package, with a screening threshold set at |log2FC| > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.001. To investigate the underlying biological processes and signaling pathways, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs using the “clusterProfiler” package [30].

Construction of a prognostic signature based on mitochondrial localization genes

Given the clinical importance of quantifying mitochondrial gene subtypes, this study analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two subclusters, using thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. The association of these DEGs with survival outcomes was then assessed via univariate Cox regression analysis using the “survival” R package [31]. Subsequently, a prognostic signature was constructed by applying the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis with the “glmnet” R package. The risk score for each patient was calculated using the following formula: Risk score = Σ(βi × Expi), where β represents the coefficient, and Exp denotes the RNA expression level. Finally, BLCA patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median value of the risk score.

Predictive performance of the BLCA prognostic model

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the “survivalROC” R package to evaluate the predictive performance of various clinicopathological factors and risk models for overall survival (OS). Using the concordance index (C-index) from the “pec” R package [32], the predictive accuracy between the prognostic model and clinicopathological characteristics was compared. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (using the “Survival” R package) were conducted to evaluate the predictive ability of the risk score calculated based on the prognostic model. Finally, the risk score and relevant clinical variables were integrated using the “rms” R package to construct a nomogram for predicting the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS of BLCA patients.

SMR analysis

In the present study, the SMR method was employed to investigate the potential causal relationship between gene expression and the risk of bladder cancer. The summary-level GWAS data for bladder cancer were obtained from the FinnGen project (https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r9/summary_stats/finngen_R9_C3_BLADDER_EXALLC.gz), comprising 2,053 bladder cancer cases and 287,137 controls of European ancestry. The cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTL) data were retrieved from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project version V8 (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr/#Over). The analysis was performed using SMR software (version 1.3.1) in command-line mode, with the significance threshold for SMR set at p_SMR < 0.05, alongside the HEIDI test (p_HEIDI > 0.05) to eliminate spurious associations caused by linkage disequilibrium (LD). Following false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple hypothesis testing, several candidate genes with significant causal associations with bladder cancer risk at the transcriptional level were ultimately identified.

Stemness analysis

We downloaded STAR-counts data and corresponding clinical information for BLCA from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). We then extracted data in TPM format and performed normalization using the log2(TPM + 1) transformation. Finally, the samples with both RNAseq data and clinical information will be retained for subsequent analysis.We utilized the OCLR algorithm developed by Malta et al. to calculate mRNAsi. This algorithm is based on features from the gene expression profiles of 11,774 genes. We processed the RNA expression data using Spearman correlation, and then mapped the dryness index to the range of [0,1] through a linear transformation, which involves subtracting the minimum value and dividing by the maximum value [33, 34]. All the above analysis methods and R package were implemented by R foundation for statistical computing (2020) version 4.0.3. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Functional enrichment analysis

We downloaded STAR-counts data and corresponding clinical information for BLCA from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). We then extracted data in TPM format and performed normalization using the log2(TPM + 1) transformation. We used the Limma package (version: 3.40.2) in R software to study the differential expression of mRNA. In the data from TCGA, we analyzed the adjusted P-values to correct for false positive results. We defined “Adjusted P < 0.05 and log2 (fold change) > 1 or log2 (fold change) < -1” as the threshold for screening differential mRNA expression [30]. To further explore the potential functions of the target, we performed a functional enrichment analysis on the data. Gene Ontology (GO) is a widely used tool for annotating the functions of genes, especially Molecular Function (MF), Biological Process (BP), and Cellular Component (CC). KEGG enrichment analysis is a practical tool for analyzing gene functions and related high-level genomic functional information. To gain a deeper understanding of the carcinogenic role of the target genes, we used the ClusterProfiler package in R to analyze the GO functions of potential mRNAs and enrich the KEGG pathways.Statistical analysis was conducted using R software, version v4.0.3. Results were considered statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Histological analysis

Paraffin sections were prepared from collected cancer tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues. The hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining procedure was performed as follows: dewaxed sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed, and then stained with eosin for 1 min. Paraffin sections were incubated with the MRPL37 primary antibody (biorbyt, orb628947) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. After rinsing, the samples were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol-xylene series, and finally mounted with neutral balsam (Beyotime, BL704A).

Cell culture and reagents

Bladder cancer cell lines T24 and J82 were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium and MEM medium supplemented with non-essential amino acids (NEAA) (Gibco, USA), respectively. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini, USA), penicillin G (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 µg/ml) (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. The antibodies used in this study were as follows: MRPL37 (biorbyt, orb628947), ACTB (MedChemExpress, AB-3102520), AKT (Servicebio, GB15689-100), p-AKT (Servicebio, GB150002-100), mTOR (Servicebio, GB11405-100), p-mTOR (Servicebio, GB114489-100), HSP60 (Servicebio, GB11243-100), and LRPPRC (Servicebio, GB115369-100).

A total of 5 µL transfection mixture was added to each well of the cell-cultured 6-well plate, followed by incubation for 24 h. The siRNA with the most significant knockdown effect was selected for subsequent cell function assays.

Cell counting Kit-8 assay

The initial step involved the seeding of the cells into 96-well culture plates, with each well containing a density of 5 × 103 cells. After that, the plates were placed in an incubator for a period of time ranging from 24 to 48 to 72 h. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (Beyotime; Cat: C0038) is currently available. For a duration of one hour, 10µL/well was added and cultured in an incubator. In order to have an accurate representation of the cell proliferation activity, the absorbance of each well was measured using a plate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Transwell assay

T24 and J82 cells that had been transfected were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber of each transwell. This process was repeated for each of the groups.Before the transwells were filled, the lower chamber was filled with fetal bovine serum at a concentration of 10%. In contrast to the migration experiment, invasion experiments were precoated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, United States of America). Methanol (Serang Biotechnology, Chengdu, China) was used to fix the cells, and Crystal Violet (Amresco, Solon, Ohio, United States of America) was used to dye the cells. Both processes ended at the stated time. For the purpose of determining the number of cells, a microscope with a magnification of 200×(Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) using five randomly selected fields was utilized.

Colony formation assay

A total of 2 × 10³ cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured at 37 °C for 7 days. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with a solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The number of colonies was observed and quantified using ImageJ software (minimum colony size threshold: ≥50 cells).

Western blot

Protein lysates (30 µg) from cells and tissues were separated by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked, incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature (RT) for 45 min. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system.

Flow cytometry

Cells were cent

Mice

Experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University. Animal experiments: 5-week-old male BALB/c mice (athymic) were provided by First Zheng Pharmaceutical Biotechnology Co., LTD. (Wuhan, China), housed and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions, with free access to food and water and a maximum tumor load of no more than 1500 mm³. Euthanasia was performed by CO₂ inhalation at the end of the study, all in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Animal experiments

Five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (athymic) were used to establish a subcutaneous xenograft model. The mice were randomly divided into experimental group and control group. Equal numbers of sh-NC and treated BLCA cells (2 × 10^7) were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of mice. The tumor mass was monitored every 2 days after injection, and the volume was calculated using the formula: V (mm ³) = width ² (mm ²) × length (mm) / 2. After 24 days, the mice were euthanized by inhalation of CO₂, the tumors were removed, and the body weight of mice in each group was measured.

Orthotopic bladder cancer model was established in 5-week-old male BALB/c nude mice. The mice were randomly divided into experimental group and control group. Anesthesia was induced with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (50 mg per kilogram), and a 1-cm longitudinal incision was made along the midline of the abdomen, extending upward from the genitalia. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscle were dissected, and the bladder was positioned and fixed. Equal numbers of sh-NC and treated BLCA cells (2 × 10^7) were injected into the vascular rich area of the bladder wall of mice using a syringe. After 24 days, tumor growth was detected by ultrasound. At the end of the study, the mice were euthanized by inhalation of CO₂, the tumors were removed, and the body weight of each group of mice was measured.

Branched-Chain Amino Acid (BCAA) quantitative detection

The concentration of BCAAs in tumor cells was determined using a branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) detection kit (ab83374, Abcam) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Color intensity was quantified using a microplate reader. Technical replicates were included for each sample in each plate.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP) assay

Changes in MMP were measured using a mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit containing JC-1 (Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, treated cells were harvested and washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in a mixture of 500 µl medium and 500 µl JC-1 staining solution, incubated at 37 °C protected from light, and observed under a fluorescence microscope. JC-1 exists either as cytoplasmic JC-1 monomers or mitochondrial J-aggregates, depending on the mitochondrial membrane potential. In healthy cells with high MMP, JC-1 spontaneously forms J-aggregates in mitochondria, emitting red fluorescence. In contrast, in unhealthy cells with decreased MMP, JC-1 is released from mitochondria and exists as monomers in the cytoplasm, producing green fluorescence. Carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP), which disrupts mitochondrial integrity and induces complete loss of MMP, was used as a positive control.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

Cells were treated with 10 µM 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescence intensity of 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein (DCF) was evaluated using a microplate reader.

Cellular ATP assay

Cellular ATP levels were measured using a luciferase-based ATP assay kit (Beyotime, S0026, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) with the significance level set at P < 0.05. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to screen key genes for constructing the diagnostic model, and genes with a P value < 0.05 were retained (based on the “glm” function in the R package ‘stats’ version 4.3.1). LASSO analysis with 5-fold cross-validation and 1000 repetitions was further employed to optimize the selection of the diagnostic gene set (based on the “cv.glmnet” function in the R package ‘glmnet’ version 4.1-8). Additional information regarding statistical tools, methods, and thresholds is provided in the Methods section for comprehensive clarification.

Establishment and culture of bladder cancer organoids

For surgically obtained bladder tumor tissues, they were first washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containingdouble antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin) to reduce the risk of contamination. After the tissues were minced, they were digested with a buffer containing 3000 U/mL collagenase and 1000 U/mL hyaluronidase at 37 °C for 30–60 min to dissociate the tissues into single cells or small cell clusters. The digested cell suspension was filtered through a cell strainer (100 μm pore size) to remove undigested tissue debris, followed by centrifugation to collect the cells. The isolated cells were gently mixed with Matrigel at low temperature to avoid bubble formation. The cell-Matrigel mixture was then seeded into well plates (20–50 µL per well generally) and incubated statically in a 37 °C incubator for 10–15 min to solidify into a “dome-shaped” structure. After Matrigel solidification, pre-warmed medium was slowly added along the well walls. When organoids grew to a certain size (e.g., diameter of 50–200 μm) or the medium turned significantly yellow, passage was required. The old medium was first aspirated, and the organoids were gently washed with PBS. Subsequently, digestion solution (TrypLE Express) was added, and digestion was performed at 37 °C for several minutes with pipetting during the process until the organoids were dissociated into single cells or smaller cell clusters. After terminating digestion, the cells were collected by centrifugation, re-mixed with Matrigel, seeded into new well plates, and fresh medium was added for further culture.

Results

Screening of differentially expressed genes in BLCA

By integrating results from multiple databases through intersection, we precisely identified a set of genes in BLCA tissues that are associated with bladder cancer initiation, progression, and malignancy. First, we analyzed the single-cell dataset GSE222315, comprising 13 single-cell samples (9 BLCA cases and 4 normal tissues) [35]. After stringent quality control, a total of 97,718 cells were retained. Using the Seurat package for data processing and batch effect removal, six major cell types were defined: epithelial cells (Epi); endothelial cells (Endo); smooth muscle cells (Smc); T cells; B cells; and monocytes (Mono) (Fig. 1A). Canonical markers for these six cell types and their cell-type proportion plots are shown (Fig. 1B). The UMAP features of the major cell clusters in the scRNA-seq dataset are presented (Fig. 1C). We then analyzed DEGs between tumor and normal tissues within each cell subpopulation; bar graphs (Fig. 1D, bottom panel) show the total numbers of upregulated and downregulated DEGs, while pie charts classify these DEGs into KEGG pathways (Fig. 1D, top panel), with the majority related to “Human Diseases.” Next, we validated 1,136 mitochondrial genes from MitoCarta3.0 in the TCGA-BLCA dataset to identify genes differentially expressed between normal and bladder cancer tissues (Fig. 1E), and through survival analysis pinpointed gene sets associated with BLCA prognosis (Fig. 1F). By integrating results across datasets, we intersected the DEGs enriched in epithelial cells from the scRNA-seq dataset, the mitochondrial DEGs upregulated in BLCA tissues versus normal tissues in TCGA, and the mitochondrial DEGs associated with poor prognosis in TCGA. This yielded 11 genes: MRPL37, HSPE1, NDUFA11, ACLY, PAM16, FASN, PET100, MCRIP2, UCP2, DCXR, CYB5R3 (Fig. 1G). A gene co-expression network revealed correlations among the expression levels of these 11 genes (Fig. 1H).

Consensus clustering analysis based on differentially expressed mitochondria-localized human protein-coding genes

Based on prognosis-related mitochondrial genes, consensus clustering identified K = 2 as the optimal partition (Fig.S1 A–G, Supporting Information), supported by maximized inter-cluster correlation and minimized dispersion (Fig.S1 H-I, Supporting Information). Tracking plots for K values ranging from 2 to 8 are provided (Fig.S1 J, Supporting Information). Consequently, BLCA patients were stratified into Cluster A (n = 294) and Cluster B (n = 137), with corresponding heatmaps detailed in (Fig.S2, Supporting Information). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a significant survival advantage for patients in Cluster B (P = 0.014, Fig.S1 K, Supporting Information), who also tended to present with earlier pathological grades (Fig. S1 L, Supporting Information). Expression profiles of 11 key mitochondrial genes across these clusters are shown (Fig.S1 M, Supporting Information). KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig.S3, Supporting Information) further suggested that the progression-driving Cluster A is significantly enriched in the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway and various metabolic processes, including butanoate, galactose, and branched-chain amino acid metabolism.

Construct a risk signature comprising 8 selected mitochondrial-related genes

An eight-gene prognostic signature was constructed using LASSO regression analysis based on the optimal λ value (Fig.S4 A-B, Supporting Information), with specific risk scores detailed in (Table S1, Supporting Information). In the TCGA training set, the model effectively stratified patients (P < 0.01, Fig.S4 C, Supporting Information) and demonstrated robust predictive performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates (AUC = 0.695, 0.649, and 0.655, respectively; Fig.S4 D, Supporting Information). These findings were confirmed through external validation using the GEO database (Fig.S4 E-F, Supporting Information). PCA and heatmap visualizations further demonstrated that the signature clearly distinguished high- and low-risk BLCA groups in both cohorts (Fig.S4 G–H, Supporting Information; Fig.S5, Supporting Information).Subsequent Cox regression analyses identified age, T stage, N stage, and risk score as independent prognostic factors (Fig.S4 I-J, Supporting Information). Specifically, multivariate analysis confirmed the independent predictive value of age (P < 0.001, HR = 1.035), T stage (P < 0.001, HR = 1.617), N stage (P < 0.001, HR = 1.488), and risk score (P < 0.001, HR = 2.82). These factors were integrated into a nomogram to predict individualized survival probabilities (Fig.S4 K, Supporting Information). The nomogram exhibited high reliability and accuracy, as evidenced by calibration curves showing high consistency with actual outcomes (Fig.S4 L, Supporting Information) and ROC analysis yielding 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUC values of 0.678, 0.642, and 0.672, respectively (Fig.S4 M, Supporting Information).

MRPL37 overexpression predicted poor survival in BLCA

We performed summary mendelian randomization (SMR) analysis to evaluate the strength of association between these eight genes and BLCA (with P < 0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance). The results showed that only MRPL37 was associated with BLCA (Fig.S6 A-B, Supporting Information). Consistent with SMR analyses, clinical analyses using the TCGA-BLCA dataset also revealed that MRPL37 was overexpressed in BLCA tissues in both unpaired samples as well as paired samples (Fig. 2A-B). The results of correlation analysis showed that the high expression of MRPL37 was significantly correlated with BLCA stemness (Fig. 2C). In addition, the expression level of MRPL37 was clinically correlated with BLCA pathological stage, degree of differentiation, and histological type (Fig. 2D-F). In addition, we also found that the expression level of MRPL37 in Asian patients was significantly lower than that in black/African American (P = 0.02) and white patients (P = 4.1 × 10− 5, Fig. 2G). Survival analysis showed that Patients with high MRPL37 expression had shorter Overall Survival (OS, P = 0.003, Fig. 2H) and Progression-Free Interval (PFI, P = 0.048, P = 0.048). 2I) and Disease Specific Survival (DSS, P = 0.005, Fig. 2J). Single cell analysis showed that MRPL37 was mainly expressed in epithelial cells (Epi), smooth muscle cells (SMC) and endothelial cells (Endo) (Fig. 2K). To further explore the potential function of MRPL37 in bladder cancer, the correlation between MRPL37 expression and the classical cell proliferation marker MKI67 was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2L, the log2(TPM) expression values of MRPL67 and MKI67 were significantly and positively correlated (Pearson R = 0.45, p < 0.001) based on the transcriptome data of bladder cancer samples from TCGA or our cohort. The high expression of MRPL37 may be accompanied by the up-regulation of MKI67, suggesting that MRPL37 may be involved in the occurrence and development of bladder cancer by promoting cell proliferation(Fig. 2L).The above results indicate that MRPL37 plays a carcinogenic role in BLCA and can be used as a prognostic marker.

Knockdown of MRPL37 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration and invasion of bladder cancer cells and induced apoptosis

We analyzed publicly available CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screening data in the DepMap database (Fig.S7, Supporting Information). The results showed that knockdown of MRPL37 exhibited an inhibitory effect on cell proliferation in pan-cancers including bladder cancer, suggesting that MRPL37 may be involved in cancer cell proliferation. Subsequently, our in vitro cell experiments further validated the DepMap analysis results. Firstly, Western Blot results showed that MRPL37 was highly expressed in T24 and J82 cell lines (Fig. 3A). Therefore, we selected these two cell lines for subsequent loss-of-function experiments. We used three different small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting MRPL37 for knockdown, and Western Blot and qPCR results jointly confirmed that the i#3 interfering sequence had the best knockdown efficiency in T24 and J82 cells (Fig. 3B, C). The results of CCK-8 and colony formation experiments showed that compared with the control group (NC), knockdown of MRPL37 significantly inhibited the proliferation and colony formation ability of T24 and J82 cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 3D, E). The results of flow cytometry showed that MRPL37 knockdown caused a significant G0/G1 phase arrest in T24 and J82 cells (Fig. 3F) and significantly promoted the apoptosis of T24 and J82 cells (Fig. 3G). Transwell results showed that knockdown of MRPL37 significantly inhibited the Migration and Invasion of T24 and J82 cells (Fig. 3H). Taken together, MRPL37 acts as an oncogene in bladder cancer cells.

Knockdown of MRPL37 significantly inhibited the growth and progression of bladder cancer in both in vivo tumor models

Subsequently, we further evaluated the oncogenic function of MRPL37 by in vivo animal experiments. Due to the poor tumorigenic ability of T24, we selected J82 with better tumorigenic ability to construct a cell line with stable knockdown of MRPL37. The results of RT-qPCR (Fig. 4A) and Western Blot (Fig. 4B) showed that we successfully constructed J82 cell line with stable knockdown of MRPL37. Subsequently, subcutaneous xenograft tumor models and orthotopic bladder cancer models were established in nude mice (Fig. 4C). In the subcutaneous xenograft tumor model, the results showed that the tumor volume generated in the sh-MRPL37 group was significantly smaller than that in the sh-NC group (Fig. 4D). Statistical analysis showed that both the mean tumor weight (Fig. 4E) and the final tumor volume (Fig. 4F) were significantly decreased in the MRPL37 knockdown group. In addition, the tumor growth curve showed that the growth rate of the sh-MRPL37 group was significantly slower than that of the sh-NC group since day 12 of inoculation, and the difference became more significant with time (Fig. 4G). In the orthotopic bladder cancer animal model, we also found that the orthotopic tumor volume of sh-MRPL37 group was significantly reduced, and the average tumor weight was significantly reduced compared with the control group (Fig. 4H-J). In addition, ultrasound imaging of the mouse bladder (Fig. 4K) was clearly shown. The bladder of mice in the sh-NC group was filled with huge solid masses, while the tumor load of mice in the sh-MRPL37 group was significantly reduced and the mass in the bladder cavity was smaller. All ultrasound images are provided in the supplementary file (Fig.S8, Supporting Information). These in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that MRPL37 knockdown significantly inhibited bladder cancer progression.

MRPL37 plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis by affecting LRPPRC synthesis

To investigate the molecular mechanism of MRPL37 affecting bladder cancer (BLCA) progression, we divided TCGA-BLCA samples into high and low expression groups according to the median expression level of MRPL37. Differential expression analysis was also performed (Fig.S9 A, B Supporting Information). Notably, cancer-promoting genes such as KRT6A and PI3 were highly expressed in the MRPL37 high expression group, while urothelial differentiation marker genes such as UPK3A and UPK1A were lowly expressed in the MRPL37 high expression group. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the up-regulated differentially expressed genes in the MRPL37 high expression group were significantly enriched in PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway and Citrate cycle (TCA cycle) pathways. GO functional enrichment analysis was enriched in G1/S transition of mitotic cell cycle, ATP generation from ADP and ATP hydrolysis activity and other core pathways of cell proliferation and energy metabolism (Fig.S7 C Supporting Information).

To validate the above analysis, we performed RNA-seq, DIA proteome and non-target metabolomics analysis on MRPL37 knockdown and control T24 cells. GO/KEGG analysis based on RNA-seq data showed that the genes significantly down-regulated by MRPL37 knockdown were significantly enriched in PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 5A, B). GO/KEGG analysis based on DIA proteomics data also showed that the significantly down-regulated proteins by MRPL37 knockdown were significantly enriched in mitochondrial translation, mitochondrial gene expression and PI3K-Akt/mTOR signaling pathways (Fig. 5C, D). Non-target metabolomic analysis showed that Citric acid, Succinic acid and Malic acid were significantly reduced after knockdown of MRPL37. In addition, branched-chain amino acids and their derivatives, antioxidants, and lipid metabolites were significantly reduced after MRPL37 knockdown (Fig. 5E).

Considering that ATP is a key product of energy metabolism, to assess the regulatory effect of MRPL37 on cellular energy status, we examined intracellular ATP levels in BLCA after MRPL37 knockdown. The results showed that intracellular ATP level was significantly decreased in BLCA cells after MRPL37 knockdown (Fig. 5E-G). In addition, we also found that MRPL37 knockdown significantly inhibited the intracellular branched-chain amino acid levels in BLCA (Fig. 5H). Branched-chain amino acid leucine is the substrate of LRPPRC (Leu-rich pentatricopeptide repeat-containing gene) protein synthesis, and its transmembrane transport requires ATP. DIA proteomics results showed that LRPPRC protein was significantly down-regulated after MRPL37 knockdown. Mitochondrial WB showed that knockdown of MRPL37 significantly inhibited the protein level of LRPPRC in mitochondria of BLCA cells (Fig. 5I). Previous studies have shown that LRPPRC is closely related to mitochondrial function and mitochondrial homeostasis, and its high expression usually predicts poor tumor prognosis [36, 37].We also demonstrated that MRPL37 knockdown indeed caused the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) by mitochondrial membrane potential assay with JC-1 probe and that MRPL37 knockdown resulted in impaired mitochondrial function (Fig. 5J).

MRPL37 knockdown elevates ROS levels and inhibits AKT/mTOR phosphorylation in an LRPPRC-dependent manner

Mitochondrial dysfunction usually leads to increased levels of oxidative stress and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [38], We also verified this in BLCA cells using the corresponding ROS detection kit (Fig. 6A, B). Elevated ROS levels inhibit the AKT pathway and its downstream targets [39], This is also consistent with the results of our previous multi-omics analysis (Fig. 5B, D). We also confirmed that MRPL37 knockdown inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR by Western blotting in BLCA cells (Fig. 6C, D). Since MRPL37 knockdown significantly inhibited LRPPRC protein expression, we hypothesized that MRPL37 knockdown activated ROS and inhibited AKT and mTOR phosphorylation by inhibiting LRPPRC protein. Salvage experiments showed that LRPPRC re-expression reversed the inhibitory phenotype induced by MRPL37 knockdown (Fig. 6E). In addition, exogenous overexpression of LRPPRC (oe-LRPPRC) partially restored mitochondrial homeostasis and removed excess ROS, and significantly increased the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR (Fig. 6F-H). To validate the role of ROS in MRPL37-mediated AKT/mTOR suppression, we treated sh-MRPL37 J82 cells with NAC (ROS scavenger). Functionally, NAC rescued the impaired proliferation (Fig. 6K), clonogenesis (Fig. 6I), and migration/invasion (Fig. 6J) of sh-MRPL37 cells. Moreover, NAC restored AKT phosphorylation in sh-MRPL37 cells (Fig. 6L). These results confirm that ROS accumulation (induced by MRPL37 knockdown) is the key mediator of AKT inhibition and malignant phenotype suppression, and MRPL37 promotes BLCA progression by upregulating LRPPRC to suppress ROS and sustain AKT signaling.

Finally, to verify the direct association between MRPL37 and LRPPRC, cell climbing section staining (immunofluorescence) was performed to visualize the spatial relationship between MRPL37 and LRPPRC in wild-type J82 cells. As shown in (Fig. 6M), our data show that although both MRPL37 and LRPPRC are abundantly distributed in the cytoplasm, they do not form tightly bound complexes. These imaging results suggest that while they may not interact stably in a large complex, their physical proximity within the same subcellular supports the regulatory axis proposed in our study. In summary, these findings delineate a novel MRPL37‑LRPPRC‑ROS‑AKT regulatory axis in bladder cancer. Loss of MRPL37 leads to insufficient cellular energy supply, which subsequently reduces LRPPRC expression and disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis. The resulting increase in mitochondrial ROS production inhibits the AKT signaling pathway, ultimately suppressing the proliferation, migration, and invasion of bladder cancer cells. Rescue experiments further confirm that this tumor‑suppressive effect is largely mediated by LRPPRC deficiency and the consequent ROS accumulation, as restoration of LRPPRC or ROS scavenging effectively reverses the observed phenotypes. Collectively, our results establish MRPL37 as a key upstream regulator of the LRPPRC‑ROS‑AKT cascade, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in bladder cancer.

Knockdown of MRPL37 enhances the sensitivity of bladder cancer organoids and drug-resistant cells to cisplatin chemotherapy by inhibiting PI3K-AKT pathway

Cisplatin is still the most important first-line chemotherapy drug for the treatment of BLCA [40]. However, the emergence of drug resistance seriously limits its clinical efficacy, leading to disease progression in most patients after initial treatment, which has become the main bottleneck leading to a grim prognosis [41, 42]. Several recent studies have shown that abnormal activation of PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is one of the core mechanisms mediating cisplatin resistance in BLCA [43, 44]. Based on this background, we further explored whether MRPL37 also participates in the drug resistance regulation process of BLCA by modulating this pathway.

To validate this hypothesis in a highly biomimetic model, we first constructed a patient-derived bladder cancer organoid (PDO) model. Immunofluorescence results confirmed that this PDO model stably expressed key markers such as CK7, GATA3, P63, and UPK2, demonstrating that it highly retained the epithelial characteristics and pathological fidelity of the parental tumor in vitro (Fig. 7A). This model exhibited stable growth capacity across different generations (the 3rd, 5th, and 7th generations) (Fig. 7B). In the PDO model, we confirmed the significant knockdown efficiency of MRPL37 by RT-qPCR (Fig. 7C), and observed that MRPL37 knockdown significantly inhibited the growth of organoids (Fig. 7D). The results of the drug sensitivity test indicated that the knockdown of MRPL37 significantly enhanced the sensitivity of BLCA organoids and T24 cells to cisplatin (Fig. 7E, F). To further verify the correlation between MRPL37 and cisplatin resistance, we established a cisplatin-resistant T24 cell line (T24R) and knocked down MRPL37 in this resistant cell line using siRNAs (T24R-KD, Fig. 7G). The CCK-8 assay results revealed that the knockdown of MRPL37 could reduce the cisplatin resistance of the drug-resistant cell line (Fig. 7G). These results collectively suggest that MRPL37 positively regulates cisplatin resistance in BLCA cell lines.

MRPL37 is upregulated in clinical tumour tissues, particularly in high-grade invasive and cisplatin-resistant bladder cancer

To further evaluate the expression of MRPL37 in the clinic, we analyzed the protein expression of MRPL37 in multiple independent cohorts of bladder cancer patients. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of five paired tumour and adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 8A and Fig.S10 A Supporting Information) showed that MRPL37 was markedly upregulated in tumour tissues compared with their normal counterparts (Fig. 8C). Next, we examined MRPL37 expression in clinical samples stratified by treatment response and tumor aggressiveness. Cases-CR1~3 were derived from patients with clinical cisplatin resistance (relapsed), while cases-CS1~3 were from cisplatin‑sensitive (non‑relapsed) patients (Fig. 8B). Discussion

Despite shifts in the burden of urological malignancies like bladder cancer over recent decades, their incidence remains a significant global health concern [45]. Recent statistics reveal a stark annual toll of around 549,000 new cases and 200,000 lives lost [46, 47]. There is an urgent need for effective therapies and targets to address the increasing risk of bladder cancer for both patients and clinicians.

Mitochondria are not only the center of energy production and metabolic regulation, but also the key hub of cell signaling and fate decisions. It relies on quality control (QC) mechanisms (such as dynamic deformation and mitophagy) to maintain its robustness, and then participates in the remodeling and maintenance of cell homeostasis by regulating programmed processes such as apoptosis [48]. In cancer, reprogramming of mitochondrial function is one of the key steps driving the malignant progression of tumors [49], it enables tumor cells to obtain sufficient energy for rapid proliferation and survival in harsh environments such as hypoxia [50]. This metabolic plasticity is coordinated through key signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, AMPK, mTOR and MYC [51–54]. Studying mitochondria-related genes and analyzing them and the abnormal changes of mitochondria in tumors has become a hot spot and direction of current tumor therapy [55]. In this study, we focused on the biological functions of mitochondria-related genes in BLCA. By integrating the results of single cell analysis, consensus cluster analysis, lasso, and SMR analysis, we finally identified a mitochondria-associated gene, MRPL37, which plays a key role in the malignant progression of BLCA.

As a member of the large subunit mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPLs) family, MRPL37 plays an important role in the construction of mitochondrial ribosome, and then participates in mitochondrial protein synthesis [56], the genes of this family have been confirmed to be closely related to the occurrence and development of a variety of cancers [57]. MRPL37 was mainly expressed in epithelial cells (Fig. 2), and its mRNA and protein levels were significantly up-regulated in BLCA. Clinical correlation and prognostic analysis confirmed that high expression of MRPL37 was clinically associated with malignant progression, stemness and poor prognosis of BLCA. Both in vitro cell experiments and in two in vivo animal models demonstrated that MRPL37 functions as an oncogene in BLCA (Figs. 3 and 4).

Because MRPL37 overexpression of drive BLCA progress of molecular mechanism is unclear. In this study, RNA-seq, proteomics, and metabolomics analyses were performed using MRPL37 knockdown cell lines to comprehensively reveal the differentially expressed genes, proteins, and metabolites caused by MRPL37 knockdown. In this study, we found that loss of MRPL37 leads to impaired synthesis of the mitochondria-associated protein LRPPRC, which has been shown to be associated with stabilizing mitochondrial electron transport chain complex (ETC) function, regulating REDOX homeostasis, and promoting the malignant progression of BLCA tumor cells [58, 59].Moreover, low levels of LRPPRC can affect mitochondrial homeostasis and significantly increase the level of intracellular ROS in BLCA [59]. Excessive ROS can affect the phosphorylation level of AKT pathway and lead to apoptosis [39, 60]. In the present study, we also found that loss of MRPL37 significantly increased ROS and downregulated AKT phosphorylation, leading to significant inhibition of cell growth.

Organoids are three-dimensional micro-organ models constructed by stem cells in vitro, which can highly reproduce the cellular composition, spatial structure and physiological function of the original tissue [61]. In tumor research, its core advantage lies in perfectly retaining the genetic variation, heterogeneity and microenvironment characteristics of primary tumors, and effectively overcoming the limitations of gene drift of traditional two-dimensional cell lines and high cost and long cycle of animal models. This makes organoids an ideal platform for the construction of patient “living biobank”, high-throughput drug screening and prediction of individualized treatment response, which strongly promotes the clinical transformation of precision medicine [62, 63]. In this study, we verified that knockdown of MRPL37 restored the sensitivity of BLCA cells to cisplatin by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in both organoid and cell models.

Tumor drug resistance is mainly caused by the high heterogeneity of tumor cells, genomic instability, EMT plasticity and tumor microenvironment, which leads to the easy failure of single-target therapy and the complex mechanism, which seriously restricts the long-term efficacy of clinical treatment [64–66]. In the present study, we also found that loss of MRPL37 significantly increased ROS and downregulated AKT phosphorylation, leading to significant inhibition of cell growth. The dysregulation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR is considered to be the classical pathway related to carcinogenesis, metabolic dysregulation, drug resistance and cancer progression [67–70]. Some PI3K-AKT pathway inhibitors, such as Alpelisib, have demonstrated efficacy in BLCA [71]. However, the precise mechanisms by which MRPL37 activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis to promote BLCA progression, and drug resistance remain to be fully elucidated.

In this study, our findings elucidate the oncogenic potential of MRPL37 in BLCA and underscore the role of the MRPL37-LRPPRC-ROS-AKT axis in driving its malignant progression and metabolic reprogramming. Results from our multi-omics analyses indicate that MRPL37 potentially activates the ROS/AKT signaling axis via LRPPRC. In our study, MRPL37 knockdown predominantly resulted in the downregulation of several acylcarnitines (including Octanoylcarnitine, Carnitine_C4:0, Carnitine_C6:0, and Carnitine_C18:1) and LPC(16:0/0:0). Accumulating evidence links these metabolites to PI3K-AKT signaling. For instance, Joo Ern Ang et al. demonstrated a significant correlation between acylcarnitines and PI3K pathway activation in murine models [72]. Similarly, Madonna R et al. confirmed a positive association between acylcarnitines and PI3K-AKT pathway activity in diabetes [73]. Furthermore, Zheqiong Tan et al. showed that LPC levels are closely associated with PI3K-AKT signaling in triple-negative breast cancer [74]. In the context of our study, MRPL37 deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS production, which are known to disrupt the balance of lipid biosynthesis and turnover. We hypothesize that this resulting lipid remodeling creates an unfavorable environment for AKT activation, potentially acting in concert with LRPPRC-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction to suppress PI3K-AKT signaling. Future studies are warranted to dissect the specific lipid mediators involved in this crosstalk.

This study also has several limitations. Establishing MRPL37‑overexpressing cell lines was a priority in the early phase of our research; however, because endogenous MRPL37 levels are constitutively high in bladder cancer cells, we were unable to generate cell lines with MRPL37 expression above baseline despite extensive optimisation using multiple vector systems and transfection strategies. Therefore, we relied on two distinct BLCA cell lines and patient‑derived organoid models, combined with multiple independent shRNAs and siRNAs, to ensure the specificity and consistency of the knockdown phenotype. Consequently, the present study lacks experimental validation using MRPL37‑overexpressing cells.Furthermore, the clinical correlation analysis between MRPL37 and BLCA patients should ideally be based on immunohistochemical results from tissue microarrays in a large clinical cohort; moreover, given the large number of differentially expressed genes and proteins identified by multi‑omics analysis, we strategically focused on the mitochondria‑associated protein LRPPRC to elucidate the pro‑tumorigenic mechanism of MRPL37. Notably, metabolomic analysis also revealed that MRPL37 knockdown significantly disturbed lipid metabolism pathways, although the specific molecular mechanisms underlying this observation require further investigation. Chemotherapy resistance is a multifactorial process. Nevertheless, our comprehensive data suggest that MRPL37 plays an important role in mediating cisplatin resistance, at least in part through the PI3K/AKT pathway.

In conclusion, this study reveals the molecular mechanism by which knockdown of MRPL37 inhibits the progression of bladder cancer, and provides an objective rationale and potential therapeutic target for the prognostic evaluation of BLCA and the overcoming of cisplatin resistance. Targeting MRPL37 may represent a promising strategy to enhance sensitivity to existing therapies.
