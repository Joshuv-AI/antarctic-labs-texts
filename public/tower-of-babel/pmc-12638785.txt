Predictive modeling & mechanistic validation of synergistic pimodivir combinations for anti-influenza therapy via PB2cap affinity boost
Authors: Peng Luo, Kexin Li, Yubin Xie, Kun Huang, Zhenzhi Qin, Jian-Piao Cai, Yu Fu, Jianli Cao, Sihang Cao, Ziyao Zhou, Zi-Wei Ye, Shuofeng Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces a machine learning framework to predict effective antiviral combinations for influenza A. It identifies Pimodivir with Epinephrine or L-Adrenaline as synergistic agents, confirmed by experiments demonstrating increased binding affinity and viral suppression. Multiple synergy scoring methods validate these drug combinations’ potential, offering a strategic pathway for designing rational combination therapies against influenza and other RNA viruses.Subject terms: Antimicrobials, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Main text

Influenza A virus (IAV), a segmented negative-sense RNA virus in the Orthomyxoviridae family, causes seasonal epidemics and pandemics with substantial public health burdens. Frequent antigenic drift and reassortment drive the emergence of novel strains, complicating prevention and treatment. Growing antiviral resistance further challenges disease management: Oseltamivir-resistant H1N1pdm09 strains, initially detected in immunocompromised patients, accounting for 2.14% in China1,2, while >99% of IAVs (including H3N2 and H1N1 subtypes) exhibit amantadine resistance3. Developing new antivirals and elucidating resistance mechanisms are critical to improving IAV control strategies. Antiviral drug development strategies include novel drug creation, drug repurposing, and synergistic combination therapies. Synergistic combinations are particularly valuable for addressing cross-resistance, as demonstrated by Oseltamivir-Zanamivir therapy effectively inhibiting drug-resistant H1N1 strains4. While developing such combinations for resistant viruses has clinical promise, challenges like reduced efficacy, toxicity risks, and labor-intensive experimental validation persist. This underscores the need for computational methods to efficiently predict synergistic effects and optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse impacts.

To this end, we selected eight clinically proven anti-IAV drugs (Neuraminidase inhibitor- Laninamivir, Oseltamivir, Peramivir, and Zanamivir; RNA-dependent RNA polymerase inhibitor- Bavipiravir, Baloxavir, and Pimodivir; and Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug- naproxen) and screened them against a curated library of 1612 investigational new drug (IND) compounds, which leverages their established safety and pharmacological profiles. We assessed cell viability in H1N1-infected cells under single/combination treatments before training machine learning models (Fig. 1). In total 70 machine learning models were developed using eight algorithms (Random Forest, Elastic Net, GBM, SVM, XGBoost, plsRglm, LASSO, Stepglm) trained on 1,612 single-drug features. Model performance was evaluated using Pearson correlation5–7, with the top five models selected: GBM (R = 0.406), Stepglm+GBM (R = 0.390), Lasso+GBM (R = 0.389), and Elastic Net (α = 0.1: R = 0.374; α = 0.2: R = 0.371) (test set R = 0.05–0.4; Fig. 2A). These models also showed robust predictive power when compared with previous reports8–10, with AUC values ranging from 0.701 to 0.752 (Fig. 2A). The GBM model achieved the highest AUC (0.752), demonstrating consistent performance across both metrics. The top five models jointly predicted 147 compounds (Supplementary Table 1) enhancing the baseline antiviral efficacy when paired with eight primary agents (Fig. 2B). Guided by these results, we further evaluated the selected machine learning models using AUC, F1 scores, accuracy, and precision to predict anti-H1N1 effects of two-drug combinations involving 8 antiviral drugs and 147 candidates (Supplementary Table 2). The GBM model outperformed others, accurately predicting 5/8 drug combinations and achieving exceptional performance for Pimodivir combinations (AUC = 0.993, F1 = 0.93, accuracy = 0.871) (Supplementary Fig. 1). While seven antivirals showed moderate synergy (AUC ~ 0.6), Pimodivir exhibited strong synergistic effects across both Bliss and HSA synergy metrics (Fig. 2C). We ranked combination indices (CIs) and prioritized 14 synergistic pairs for dose-response analysis (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrate the utility of single-drug-trained models—particularly GBM—for predicting combinatorial efficacy.

Expanding on Pimodivir’s combinatorial potential, we found that top-performing combinations—Pimodivir with SA00463894-A4 (Histamine), SA00463887-G11 (Hydroxyurea), or L2000-03-B10 (L-Adrenaline)—showed broad-spectrum synergy (median score >5), while SA00463886-B11 (Risedronate), L2000-05-B3 (Ironsucrose), and SA00463888-G10 (Isosorbide) exhibited concentration-dependent effects (Supplementary Fig. 2). In H1N1-infected MDCK cells, Pimodivir-Zanamivir combinations lacked cytopathic effect (CPE) inhibition, but SynergyFinder+ analysis of select pairs (e.g., Epinephrine bitartrate/L-Adrenaline + Pimodivir) revealed robust synergy across all four models (Fig. 2D, E). Notably, this synergy effect also applies to the H3N2 and recent bovine H5N1 virus circulating in North America (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results underscore Pimodivir’s versatility in synergistic regimens and nominate the best combinational pair for further development. Given Pimodivir’s mechanism of blocking PB2-mediated host mRNA cap binding, we investigated its synergy with Epinephrine bitartrate or L-Adrenaline using minigenome assays. While 20 µM Pimodivir alone suppressed viral polymerase activity (Fig. 2G, H), neither candidate compound showed standalone efficacy. Strikingly, both compounds dose-dependently enhanced Pimodivir’s inhibition when used in combination. Biacore analysis revealed Epinephrine bitartrate and L-Adrenaline strengthened Pimodivir-PB2cap binding, dramatically reducing KD values from 145.5 ± 10.1 nM (Pimodivir alone) to 17.1 ± 1.9 nM and 13.9714.5 ± 2.3 nM, respectively (Fig. 2I–K). This mechanistic synergy explains their potentiation of Pimodivir’s antiviral activity, offering a structural rationale for combinatorial therapy. To investigate the molecular basis underlying the conformational changes, we performed further molecular dynamics simulations and predicted that both drug compounds stabilize the pimodivir-PB2cap interaction by strengthening the interaction at the Phe323 position (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In summary, this study establishes and validates a machine learning framework predicting anti-influenza efficacy for combinational agents. We identified Epinephrine bitartrate/L-Adrenaline tartrate as potent tenfold enhancers of Pimodivir’s PB2cap binding affinity, driving synergistic inhibition of both pandemic H1N1 and recent bovine H5N1. Our approach bridges computational prediction with mechanistic validation—demonstrating ML’s power to accelerate antiviral discovery. Although the Pimodivir-Adrenaline combination still faces several challenges such as (i)in vitro-to-in vivo concordance, (ii) pharmacokinetic mismatch, (iii) potential off-target effects and a narrow therapeutic index, ongoing PK/PD optimization and formulation strategies are expected to translate this computational–experimental synergy into a safe and effective clinical regimen. The Pimodivir-Adrenaline synergy paradigm offers a template for rational combination therapy development, prioritizing both efficacy and target engagement for further optimization.

Methods

Drug data sources

We collected a total of 1369 compounds from MedChem Express and 676 compounds from Selleckchem, compiling a comprehensive collection of drug candidates. To evaluate the efficacy of these candidates, H1N1-infected cells were treated with the selected compounds, and cell viability data were subsequently obtained. To ensure the reliability of the data for training our model, we repeated the experiments to exclude compounds exhibiting a high standard deviation between the results. Ultimately, 1612 candidate compounds were identified for model training. Additionally, we selected eight drugs with established antiviral effects—Favipiravir, Laninamivir, Baloxavir, Neproxen, Oseltamivir, Peramivir, Pimodivir, and Zanamivir—to be used in combination with the candidate compounds. Favipiravir (Cat. No.: HY-14768), Laninamivir (Cat. No.: HY-14818), Baloxavir (Cat. No.: HY-109025A), Naproxen (Cat. No.: HY-15030), Oseltamivir (Cat. No.: HY-13317), Peramivir (Cat. No.: HY-17015A), Pimodivir (Cat. No.: HY-12353A), and Zanamivir (Cat. No.: HY-13210) were purchased from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA). Epinephrine bitartrate (Cat. No.:E4375) and L-Adrenaline (Cat. No.:E4250) were purchased from MERCK (New Jersey, USA). The highest DMSO concentration used in this study is 0.2%.

Preprocessing and characterization

We obtained the structures and SMILES representations of the candidate compounds from the MedChem Express and Selleckchem databases, and retrieved the corresponding SMILES for the antiviral drugs using PubChem. The RDKit package for Python was then employed to generate features for each drug. Molecular Fingerprints: We generated molecular fingerprints, including extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP) and feature connectivity fingerprints (FCFP), utilizing various bond lengths and radii (0/2/4/6) to characterize atoms in different ranges. These fingerprints consist of 1024 binary variables. Additionally, we prepared long extended-connectivity fingerprints (LECFP) and long feature connectivity fingerprints (LFCFP) using the same 1024-bit length. We also incorporated Avalon fingerprints, which are structurally similar to ECFPs but utilize a 512-bit length variable in this study. Furthermore, we included Molecular ACCess System (MACCS) fingerprints, comprising 167 binary variables that indicate the presence or absence of specific substructures in a compound, provided by Molecular Design Limited, Inc. Physicochemical Properties: We incorporated 20 dimensions of physicochemical properties, including molecular mass, number of hydrogen bond acceptors, number of hydrogen bond donors, aromaticity, and charge number, to describe the characteristics of the compounds(Supplementary Table 3).

Collectively, these molecular fingerprints and physicochemical property variables were expanded to form a 14,012-dimensional feature set for input into the machine learning model. To maintain an appropriate balance of variable influence and enhance the accuracy of predictions, we implemented correlation coefficient screening to limit the input variables to a range of 300–500.

For the combined characterization of candidate compounds and antiviral drugs, we processed the physicochemical properties and molecular fingerprints separately. The total physicochemical properties of drug combinations were obtained through direct summation. Given the binary nature of the molecular fingerprints, we employed binary arithmetic: if at least one of the compounds is represented as 1 in a certain bit, the drug combination is recorded as 1 for that bit; otherwise, it is recorded as 0.

Building machine learning models

We utilized the R (v 4.1.3) to train a variety of machine learning models for predicting the therapeutic effects of compounds. These models included several continuous variable approaches, such as random forest, elastic net, gradient boosting machine (GBM), ridge regression, lasso regression, and others. The dataset comprising features from 1612 compounds and their corresponding mean cell viability was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). All tests for model accuracy were performed in the testing set. Each model’s parameters were optimized to achieve the best predictive performance. Random Forest: Implemented using the randomForest package, we optimized the model by manually adjusting the mtry parameter, which determines the number of variables considered at each node of the binary tree. Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM): Utilized the gbm package, with model optimization achieved by tuning the n.trees parameter. Elastic Net and Lasso Regression: These models were implemented using the glmnet package. In the elastic net model, the alpha parameter was varied between 0.1 and 0.9, while the Lasso model utilized an alpha value of 1. Support Vector Machine (SVM): Implemented through the E1071 package. Generalized Linear Model Boosting (Glmboost): Provided by the caret package. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost): Implemented using the xgboost package. Partial Least Squares Regression Generalized Linear Model (plsRglm): Available through the plsRglm package. Stepwise Generalized Linear Model (Stepglm): Utilized the stats package for implementation. Additionally, we explored hierarchical model combinations to enhance predictive accuracy. By first employing one model to filter for high-weight variables, we subsequently applied another model to learn from these selected variables, thereby improving the overall prediction performance in certain instances.

Evaluation of model predictive effectiveness

Since the variables used in the models were continuous, we employed the Pearson correlation coefficient (R) and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) to assess model performance. The top five models, based on Pearson correlation coefficients, were utilized to predict the effectiveness of various two-drug combinations against H1N1 viral infections, and AUC values were computed for these five models. We separately counted the sum of the rankings of the efficacy of specific candidate compound combinations among all candidate combinations, resulting in the screening of 147 drugs that were effective and ranked within the top 200 across all five models.

Calculation of synergy scores

Based on the survival data of dual drugs acting separately or in combination on cell lines at a single concentration, we utilized both the Highest Single Agent (HSA) and Bliss methods to compute the combination index (CI) of drug combinations. This calculation was employed to determine the type of drug-drug interactions (DDI) and the strength of action of the candidate compounds and antiviral drug combinations.

Combinations with a CI > 1 and ranked in the top 15% of CIs for all drug combinations were labeled synergistic, whereas those with a CI < 1 and ranked in the bottom 15% of CIs were labeled antagonistic.

Where Ea and Eb represent the cell viability after the action of the drug candidate (antiviral drug), and Eab represents the cell viability after the action of the drug combination.

To assess drug synergy in relation to drug concentration, we implemented four synergy score calculations—Bliss, Loewe, HSA, and ZIP—using the SynergyFinder Plus11. A synergy score of less than −5 indicates antagonism between the two drugs, while a score greater than 5 implies synergy.

Virus culture and cell lines

Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, MA, USA), 50 µg/mL streptomycin, and 50 units/mL penicillin. The cells were cultured under conditions of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Mycoplasma contamination was assessed and confirmed negative using the PlasmoTest (InvivoGen).

The influenza A virus H1N1 strain A/HK/415742/2009 (H1N1)pdm09, a clinical isolate preserved by the Department of Microbiology at the University of Hong Kong, was utilized for infection studies. H1N1 was propagated in MDCK cells with the addition of 1 µg/mL N-Tosyl-L-phenylalanine chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin (Sigma). Upon reaching approximately 90% confluency, cells seeded in T75 flasks were passaged into new T175 flasks. The confluent T175 flasks of MDCK cells were inoculated with 20 µL of H1N1 virus stock in 20 mL of DMEM and subsequently incubated under 5% CO₂ at 37 °C for a duration of three days. Following the infection period, the culture supernatant was collected, aliquoted into 1.5 mL tubes, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent viral titer quantification via plaque assay.

Cytopathic effect inhibition assay

Cell viability was quantified utilizing the CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay kit (Promega, USA) following infection with H1N1 viruses. In brief, the culture medium was aspirated and replaced with 100 µL of DMEM supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (5000 U/mL). Subsequently, 100 µL of CellTiter-Glo reagent was introduced to each well, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After the incubation period, 100 µL of the resulting reaction mixture was transferred to an opaque-walled 96-well plate for luminescence measurement. This was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the EnSight Multimode Microplate Reader (Perkin Elmer, USA) at the specified time points.

The synergy of Pimodivir, Zanamivir, and their combinations were evaluated by a cytopathic effect (CPE) inhibition assay. In brief, MDCK cells were seeded at a density of 20,000 cells per well in a black transparent 96-well plate for 24 h incubation. Then 0.01 MOI H5N1(CH5, USA) was used to infect these cells, and the medium was replaced with Pimodivir, Zanamivir, and their combinations with indicated concentrations at 1hpi. The cell viability was detected at 48hpi using CellTiter-Glo and the effect of the synergy of these compounds was determined using SynergyFinder+.

Expression and purification of PB2cap

The PB2cap protein was purified with slight modification12. Briefly, the PB2 cap-binding domain (residues 318–483) of A/Vietnam/1194/2004(H5N1) influenza virus (GenBank accession no. AY651718) was cloned into pET-32a(+) expression vector (Novagen) using KpnI and NcoI. Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) was induced to overexpress PB2cap protein at 20 °C by adding 0.05 mM IPTG. HisTrap nickel affinity column (GE Healthcare) was loaded to purify protein from the cell lysate after sonication. The protein was finally eluted using a 50 mL linear gradient of imidazole (5–100% HisTrap buffer with 50 mM Na-HEPES pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole) and fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The purified PB2cap was stored at −80 °C for later use.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The synergy of Pimodivir with Epinephrine bitartrate or L-Adrenaline was determined by SPR. According to the standard operating manual, the PB2 cap-binding domain was immobilized to CM7 (Cytiva) using an amine coupling kit (acetate buffer, pH 4.0) in a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). HBS-EP + (10 mM HEPES pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.05% (v/v) Surfactant P20) with 5% DMSO was used to dilute Pimodivir from 19.5 nM to 1250 nM, with or without a final concentration of 10 μM Epinephrine bitartrate or L-Adrenaline, and 4.5–5.8% DMSO was used to correct the data. Finally, the binding parameters were fitted using kinetic (1:1).

Mini-replicon assay

The potential synergistic effect of combination drugs on polymerase activity was evaluated by Mini-replicon assay13. Briefly, 2 × 105 293 T cells were put into per well of 24-well plates. After overnight incubation, cells were transfected with plasmids encoding PB1 (50 ng), PB2 (50 ng), PA (50 ng), NP (50 ng), firefly luciferase (100 ng) and Nano-Luc (5 ng). Media were replaced with indicated compounds 6 h post transfection. After 30 h post transfection, cells were harvested, firefly luciferase activity was measured by using a Nano-Glo® Dual-Luciferase® Reporter (NanoDLR™)Assay System (Promega). The values were recorded on a GloMax EXPLORER machine (Promega).
