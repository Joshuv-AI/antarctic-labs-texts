Epstein curves and holography of the Schwarzian action
Franco Vargas Pallete, Yilin Wang, Catherine Wolfram

Abstract
We apply Epstein’s construction of hypersurfaces in the hyperbolic disk 𝔻 \mathbb{D} to prove identities between the Schwarzian action on PSL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \ Diff 3 ​ ( 𝕊 1 ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\backslash\mathrm{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1}) , the length of the corresponding Epstein curve in 𝔻 \mathbb{D} , and the area enclosed by the Epstein curve. These results are inspired by the holographic duality between Jackiw–Teitelboim gravity and Schwarzian field theory. We also show that the horocycle truncation used in the construction of the Epstein curve defines a renormalized length of hyperbolic geodesics in 𝔻 \mathbb{D} , which coincides with the logarithm of the bi-local observable of Schwarzian field theory. The construction of the Epstein curve also extends to the coadjoint orbits PSL 2 ( n ) ⁡ ( ℝ ) \ Diff 3 ​ ( 𝕊 1 ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}^{(n)}(\mathbb{R})\backslash\mathrm{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1}) , and we obtain the same identities for the analog of the Schwarzian action on these coadjoint orbits. Furthermore, we show that the Schwarzian action is the derivative of the Loewner energy of the welded Jordan curve. This energy is the action functional of Schramm–Loewner evolutions and holographically expressed as a renormalized volume in hyperbolic 3 3 -space. As a by-product of these relations, we obtain two immediate proofs of the non-negativity of the Schwarzian action using the isoperimetric inequality and the monotonicity of the Loewner energy.

Introduction

We write
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon|z|<1\}
,
\mathbb{D}^{*}=\{z\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\colon|z|>1\}
, and
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial\mathbb{D}=\partial\mathbb{D}^{*}
. We endow
\mathbb{D}
with the complete hyperbolic metric, and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is its boundary at infinity. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism.
The
Schwarzian action
of
\varphi
is defined as

I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{S}[\varphi]=(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime})^{\prime}-(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime})^{2}/2
is the Schwarzian derivative of
\varphi
. This is the action of the Schwarzian field theory, the same as considered in, e.g.,
[
3
,
43
]
, up to a multiplicative and an additive constant. See Remark
2.3
and Remark
3.21
.

In this paper, we relate the Schwarzian action of
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
to the following geometric quantities in 2D:

1.

it is the signed hyperbolic area enclosed by the Epstein curve associated with the pushforward metric
\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
;

2.

it is the asymptotic isoperimetric excess of the corresponding Epstein foliation of
\mathbb{D}
;

3.

it is the infinitesimal variation of the Loewner energy of the Jordan curve, whose welding homeomorphism is given by
\varphi
, along its equipotential foliation;

4.

it is the asymptotic change in hyperbolic area under a conformal distortion extending
\varphi
in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, when
\varphi
is analytic.

Using the second and third relations, we obtain two new proofs of the non-negativity of
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
(also shown in, e.g.,
[
3
,
27
]
) for circle diffeomorphisms, one using the isoperimetric inequality in the hyperbolic disk and the other using the monotonicity of the Loewner energy. Furthermore, we extend Relation 1 to the dual Epstein curve in the de Sitter space
\operatorname{dS}_{2}
, to the Schwarzian action associated with the Virasoro coadjoint orbits
\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\backslash\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, and to piecewise Möbius circle diffeomorphisms which are only
C^{1,1}
.

Although our viewpoint is purely geometric, our work is particularly motivated by the proposed holographic relationship between Schwarzian field theory
[
3
,
4
,
43
]
and Jackiw–Teitelboim (JT) gravity
[
21
,
48
,
29
,
19
,
17
,
22
]
. We comment on this further at the end of the introduction, see Section
1.4
.

Another key object in the Schwarzian field theory is the
bi-local observable

\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)
associated with the circle diffeomorphism
\varphi
and any two distinct points
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
Bi-local observables are predicted to correspond to boundary-anchored Wilson lines for JT gravity
[
9
]
.
It was also predicted in physics
[

]
and verified mathematically
[
28
,
5
]
that the correlation functions of bi-local observables characterize the Schwarzian field theory.
Here, we observe that bi-local observables can be expressed as the length of hyperbolic geodesics using the same truncation as in the construction of Epstein curves, and the bi-local observables along the edges of any ideal triangulation of
\mathbb{D}
fully determine the diffeomorphism up to Möbius transformations.

1.1
Relation to Epstein curve

We now give a conceptual description of the Epstein curve used in our first main result.
The construction of 1-dimensional Epstein curves in the disk is inspired by the analogous construction in higher dimensions.

The most studied setup is for hyperbolic
3
-manifolds. In fact, a complete convex co-compact hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
determines the conformal structure on its conformal boundary
\partial_{\infty}M
at infinity.
This property appeals to theoretical physicists, providing a framework for describing an
\operatorname{AdS}_{3}/\operatorname{CFT}_{2}
holographic correspondence
[
25
,
26
,
46
,
31
]
, and is also interesting from the point of view of hyperbolic geometry and Teichmüller theory
[
32
,
13
]
.

Epstein
[
18
]
proposed a way to further associate with each conformal metric on
\partial_{\infty}M
a truncation of
M
by horospheres, where the “size” of the horosphere
H_{z}
centered at each
z\in\partial_{\infty}M
is determined by the metric tensor at
z
. The Epstein hypersurface associated with the conformal metric is the hypersurface in
M
that is tangent to all horospheres and is called “the envelope” of the horospheres. See Section
2.3
for more details and Figure
1
for an illustration.
Moreover, for hyperbolic
3
-manifolds, the renormalized volume enclosed by the Epstein surface can be viewed as a renormalized Einstein–Hilbert action of
M
, and is shown to have the same conformal anomalies as the Liouville action defined for the conformal metrics on
\partial_{\infty}M
and has deep links to Teichmüller theory
[
46
,
25
]
.

-manifolds are by far the most studied, Epstein’s construction is valid in hyperbolic spaces of any dimension. Given that the Schwarzian field theory shall be obtained from a dimensional reduction of the Liouville theory
[

, it appears natural to us to consider the Epstein construction in the two-dimensional hyperbolic disk
\mathbb{D}
, whose conformal boundary is identified with

(See Theorem

)

More generally, the Epstein map

Lemma 1.2

(See Lemma

)
.

If

(a)

(b)

(c)

A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})=2\pi(\cosh(t)-1)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})

When

and the image of

.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism and
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
. We have

I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})-2\pi\sinh(t)\right)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(2\pi(\cosh(t)-1)-A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})\right).

The
isoperimetric profile
is the function
J:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

J(A)=2\pi\sinh\left(\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{A}{2\pi}+1\right)\right),

such that any domain in
\mathbb{D}
with hyperbolic area

. The equality is realized only by round disks.

Our next result expresses the Schwarzian action as an asymptotic isoperimetric excess along the Epstein equidistant foliation. We obtain the non-negativity of
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
using the isoperimetric inequality for the hyperbolic disk. We believe similar proofs are known in the physics literature.

Theorem 1.4

(See Theorem
3.18
and Corollary
3.19
)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism and
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
, then

\displaystyle 2I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\Big(L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})-J(A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}))\Big).

(1.3)

Moreover,
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\varphi
is Möbius.

We can also use the Epstein construction to give a geometric interpretation of bi-local observables, which are important observables in the Schwarzian field theory. See Section
4
for more background.
More precisely, for
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, we let
(H_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denote the horocycles associated with
\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
. For
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
distinct, we define the
renormalized length

\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v)
to be the signed hyperbolic distance from
H_{\varphi(u)}
to
H_{\varphi(v)}
along the geodesic
(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))
. The sign is positive if
H_{\varphi(u)},H_{\varphi(v)}
are disjoint and negative otherwise. The bi-local observables of Schwarzian field theory correspond to the exponential of the renormalized length.

Proposition 1.5

(See Proposition
4.4
)
.

Fix
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
distinct.
The bi-local observable and renormalized length are related by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)^{2}:=\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v)|}{|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}\exp(-\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v)).

Renormalized length (and bi-local observables) are naturally viewed as functions on the space of geodesics in
\mathbb{D}
, which are parametrized by their endpoints
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This is called the
kinematic space

\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\{(u,u):u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\}
. Furthermore, each
\varphi
gives a Lorentzian metric on
\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{4}\exp(-\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v))\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v

(1.4)

with constant curvature, hence solving the Liouville equation and isometric to the de Sitter space
\operatorname{dS}_{2}
.

In Section
4.2
, we show that it suffices to know
\{\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)\}
where
(u,v)
runs over the set of edges of any ideal triangulation of
\mathbb{D}
(see, e.g., Figure
5
) to fully recover
\varphi
up to Möbius transformations (Proposition
4.6
). This is closely related to the parametrization of the decorated universal Teichmüller space by log
\Lambda
-lengths by Penner
[
37
,
36
]
and the diamond shears coordinates studied by the last two authors
[
40
]
.

\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

Therefore,

is of particular interest in the study of the special Virasoro coadjoint orbits

[

We have the following identity:

1.2
Relation to Loewner energy

The Schwarzian action
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
is invariant under post-composition by Möbius transformations in
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
, hence, it is defined on the space
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\backslash\operatorname{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. This is a subspace of the
universal Teichmüller space

T(1):=\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\backslash\operatorname{QS}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
which is modeled on the space of quasisymmetric homeomorphisms of the circle. The space
T(1)
is further identified with the space of quasicircles via conformal welding:

\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})\backslash\{\text{Quasicircles in }\hat{\mathbb{C}}\}\xleftrightarrow[1:1]{\text{conformal welding}}\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\backslash\operatorname{QS}(\mathbb{S}^{1})/\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)

where
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
denotes the group of Möbius transformations of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
.

Another important quantity — the Loewner energy
I^{L}
which is also known as the universal Liouville action — arises as a Kähler potential for the unique homogeneous Kähler metric on
T(1)
and has been studied extensively
[
47
,
12
,
52
,
35
,
23
,
7
]
. The Loewner energy is finite on the space of Weil–Petersson quasisymmetric circle homeomorphisms
\operatorname{WP}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\subset\operatorname{QS}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, which contains all
C^{1.5+}
regular circle homeomorphisms. Moreover,
I^{L}
is bi-invariant under the action of
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
, and therefore is also considered a function on the space of Jordan curves modulo
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.
See Section
2.2
for more details.

Our next result shows that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
is equal to a derivative of
I^{L}
.
For this, let
\gamma
be a
C^{3,\alpha}
curve for some
\alpha>0
,
f
(resp.,
g
) be a conformal map
\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
(resp.,
\mathbb{D}^{*}\to\Omega^{*}
), where
\Omega
(resp.,
\Omega^{*}
) is the connected component of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\gamma
which does not contain
\infty
(resp., which contains
\infty
).
By Kellogg’s theorem,
f
and
g
extend to
C^{3,\alpha}
diffeomorphisms on the closures
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
\overline{\mathbb{D}^{*}}
. In particular, the welding homeomorphism of
\gamma
,
\varphi_{\gamma}:=g^{-1}\circ f|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
is a
C^{3,\alpha}
diffeomorphism.

Theorem 1.7

(See Theorem
5.3
)
.

Let
\gamma
be a
C^{3,\alpha}
Jordan curve for some
\alpha>0
. Using the notations above, we consider the family of
equipotentials

\Big(\gamma^{\varepsilon}:=f((1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{S}^{1})\Big)_{0\leq\varepsilon<1}
of
\Omega
.
We have

\frac{\mathrm{d}I^{L}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi_{\gamma}).

Note that the definition of equipotentials depends not only on
\gamma
, but also on the choice of the conformal map
f
. This is consistent with the fact that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
is only left-invariant by Möbius transformations of the circle.

Remark 1.8

(Second proof of non-negativity of
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
)
.

It was shown in
[
50
, Cor. 1.5]
that
\varepsilon\mapsto I^{L}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})
is strictly decreasing unless
\gamma
is a circle (then
I^{L}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})
is constantly zero). From this, we also see that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)\geq 0
for
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Inspired by the
\operatorname{AdS}_{3}/\operatorname{CFT}_{2}
correspondence and using the same Epstein construction in hyperbolic
3
-space, Bridgeman, Bromberg, and the first two authors
[
12
]
showed that
I^{L}(\gamma)
equals
4/\pi
times the
renormalized volume

V_{R}(\gamma):=\operatorname{Vol}(N_{\gamma})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial N_{\gamma}}H\mathrm{d}a

(1.5)

of the
3
-manifold
N_{\gamma}
bounded by the two Epstein surfaces associated with the hyperbolic metrics in
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
, where
H
is the mean curvature and
\mathrm{d}a
the area form induced by the hyperbolic metric of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
Given Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.7
, we obtain that a variation of a three-dimensional renormalized volume equals a two-dimensional renormalized area. However, we do not have a direct geometric proof of this fact, as the correspondence between Jordan curves and circle homeomorphisms via conformal welding is rather implicit. We also mention that, as shown in
[
53
]
, conformal welding is better viewed as a correspondence between curves on the conformal boundaries of the spaces
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\operatorname{AdS}^{2,1}
.

1.3
Relation to conformal distortion

We also show that the Schwarzian action appears asymptotically under conformal distortion of circles
\mathbb{S}_{r}=r\mathbb{S}^{1}
where
r
is close to
1
. Namely, fix a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
, and let
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\setminus K\to\mathbb{D}
be a locally conformal map such that the analytic function
\varphi\mid_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
maps
\mathbb{S}^{1}
to itself (we do not require
\varphi\mid_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
to be injective).
Let
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}
denote the hyperbolic metric on the disk.

Theorem 1.9

(See Theorem
6.1
)
.

Let
\rho=\varphi^{\ast}(\rho_{\mathbb{D}})
. Let
\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}
denote the length measure on
\mathbb{S}_{r}
for
\rho_{\mathbb{D}},\rho
respectively, and similarly let
k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}},k_{\rho}
denote the respective geodesic curvatures. Then

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}-\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg]=-\frac{2}{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\frac{2}{3}I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi).

(1.6)

Results of this nature are well-known in the physics literature, see, e.g.,
[
24
,
34
,
8
]
.

We include this result, which follows essentially from the Taylor expansion of
\varphi
, in part for comparison with the results for Epstein curves.
We note that the theorem above does not require that
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a homeomorphism.
However, even when
\varphi
is a homeomorphism,
the Epstein foliation considered in Section
1.1
and the conformal foliation formed by
\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r})
are, in general, not the same. Such conformal foliation was also considered in
[
51
]
, which observed the appearance of the Schwarzian action in the variation of Loewner–Kufarev energy of the foliation. By the above theorem and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, we again have a relationship between the Schwarzian action and hyperbolic area enclosed by
\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r})
.

Corollary 1.10

(See Corollary
6.2
)
.

Suppose that
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is quasiconformal with Beltrami coefficient supported in the compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
.
Then

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\bigg[A(\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r}))-A(\mathbb{S}_{r})\bigg]=-\frac{2}{3}I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi),

(1.7)

where
A(\gamma)
denotes the hyperbolic area of the region enclosed by the curve
\gamma
.

We may compare to Corollary
1.3
to see that the asymptotic behavior of the hyperbolic area bounded by the conformal foliation is different from that of the Epstein foliation.

1.4
Comments and further directions

The Schwarzian field theory has been studied extensively in physics and was recently formulated in a mathematically rigorous way using tools from probability theory
[
3
,
4
,
27
,
28
]
.
In physics, the Schwarzian field theory has also been linked to the Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) random matrix model
[
30
]
, low dimensional black holes
[
24
]
, a large
c
limit of two-dimensional Liouville CFT
[
33
]
, and Virasoro coadjoint orbits
[
1
,
43
]
.

While our viewpoint is geometric, this work is motivated by the proposed holographic relationship between Schwarzian field theory
[
3
,
4
,
43
]
and Jackiw–Teitelboim (JT) gravity on the disk with negative curvature
[
21
,
48
,
29
,
19
,
17
,
22
]
. See, e.g.,
[
34
]
and the references therein.

JT gravity on the disk is a model for random metrics of a fixed constant curvature, but unlike Schwarzian field theory, it does not yet have a rigorous mathematical formulation. The action of JT gravity is

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{JT}}(g,\Phi)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{D}\Phi(R+2)\sqrt{g}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\Phi_{b}\int_{\partial D}k\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
R
is the curvature of the metric
g
on
D
, and
k
is geodesic curvature. Fixing the boundary condition
\Phi
to be a constant
\Phi_{b}
and setting
R=-2
, the action reduces to
\int_{\partial D}k\,\mathrm{d}s
up to a multiplicative constant. By the Gauss–Bonnet formula, for the disk, this total curvature is (up to an additive constant) also equal to the total area of
D
under the metric
g
. A number of models in physics have been proposed to study random JT disks, many of which are defined as disks bounded by random curves. See, e.g.,
[
24
]
for an early work on JT random metrics on regions enclosed by Brownian loops,
[
44
]
on random embedded disks bounded by self-avoiding walk loops, and
[
19
]
for random disks given by immersions of the disk into the spaces of constant curvature and self-overlapping polygons. In mathematics, motivated by the model introduced in
[
19
]
, self-overlapping polygons immersed in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
were also studied in
[
14
]
.

The Epstein map on the circle has an extension to a differentiable map of the disk in into the hyperbolic plane (see Definition
3.9
), and provides a unified geometric framework to understand the Schwarzian action (Theorem
1.1
equates the action of Schwarzian field theory with the action of JT gravity for the metric on the immersed disk given by extending the Epstein map), its analogs for Virasoro coadjoint orbits (Theorem
1.6
), and the correlation functions of Schwarzian field theory (Proposition
1.5
).

However, the Epstein construction does not apply directly in the random setting, because it requires more regularity. As is typical in random models, the Schwarzian action itself is infinite for the random diffeomorphisms of Schwarzian field theory, which have regularity only
C^{3/2-}
. Correspondingly, the Epstein curve for the random diffeomorphism is not defined without regularization. In Section
7.2
, we extend the construction of the Epstein curve to
C^{1,1}
piecewise Möbius circle homeomorphisms. The regularization needed to extend classical constructions to the random setting often requires new ideas (e.g. as in the case of Liouville quantum gravity
[
16
]
), and the more substantial regularization needed to understand this construction in the random setting will be addressed in a subsequent work.

The relationship between the Schwarzian action and the Loewner energy derived in Theorem
1.7
also suggests that there is a link between SLE and Schwarzian field theory, which is under investigation but far from being clear. The Loewner energy is the action of SLE loop measures as shown in
[
15
]
. SLE loop measures are indexed by a parameter
\kappa\in(0,8)
and are rigorously constructed in
[
55
]
. They describe the interfaces appearing in the scaling limit of critical 2D lattice models, where the parameter depends on the specific model and encodes the central charge of the corresponding CFT.

Preliminaries

In this section, we collect previously known results about the Schwarzian action, define the Loewner energy, and describe Epstein maps in arbitrary dimension.

2.1
Schwarzian action

Let
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
be the unit circle centered at
0
.
Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism. The Schwarzian action of
\varphi
is given by

I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}-\mathfrak{i}z\,\mathcal{S}[\varphi](z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

where
\mathcal{S}[\varphi]=(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime})^{\prime}-(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime})^{2}/2
is the Schwarzian derivative,
\mathrm{d}z
the contour integral on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and for
z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,

\varphi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi(z)}{\mathrm{d}\theta}/\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}\theta}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{i}z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi(z)}{\mathrm{d}\theta}.

Remark 2.1
.

If
\varphi
extends to a holomorphic immersion in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, then the above convention of
\mathcal{S}[\varphi]
coincides with the Schwarzian derivative of a holomorphic immersion where
\varphi^{\prime}
is the usual complex derivative.

Remark 2.2
.

In Section
7.2
, we will discuss a generalization to
C^{1,1}
diffeomorphisms, where we interpret the Schwarzian derivative in the distributional sense.

The Schwarzian derivative satisfies a chain rule:

\mathcal{S}[f\circ g]=\mathcal{S}[f]\circ g(g^{\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{S}[g]

and
\mathcal{S}[f]\equiv 0
if and only if
f
is a Möbius map in
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, i.e.,
f(x)=(ax+b)/(cx+d)
for some
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
and
ad-bc\neq 0
.

Remark 2.3
.

If we conjugate
\varphi
by the map
[0,1]\to\mathbb{S}^{1}:\theta\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathfrak{i}\theta}
, we obtain a diffeomorphism
\tilde{\varphi}:[0,1]_{/0\sim 1}\to[0,1]_{/0\sim 1}
, such that
\varphi(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathfrak{i}\theta})=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathfrak{i}\tilde{\varphi}(\theta)}
. Then we have

\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{4\pi\mathfrak{i}\theta}=-\frac{\mathcal{S}[\tilde{\varphi}](\theta)}{4\pi^{2}}-\frac{[\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\theta)]^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

Hence,
(
1.1
) can be rewritten as

2\pi I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=-\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathcal{S}[\tilde{\varphi}](\theta)+2\pi^{2}[\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\theta)]^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta+2\pi^{2}=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\varphi})+2\pi^{2}

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{I}(\cdot)
is the Schwarzian action used in, e.g.,
[
3
]
, which is also shown to be larger or equal to
-2\pi^{2}
in
[
27
]
. See also Remark
3.21
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a Möbius map preserving
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, namely,

\alpha(x)=\frac{ax+b}{\bar{b}x+\bar{a}},\qquad\text{for some }a,b\in\mathbb{C},\quad|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1,

which we denote as

\alpha\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
. Then we have

\mathcal{S}[\varphi]=\mathcal{S}[\alpha\circ\varphi].

Summarizing the above, we obtain:

Lemma 2.4
.

The Schwarzian action
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
is non-negative on
\operatorname{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and invariant under the left action of
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
.

We will provide two different proofs of the non-negativity using our descriptions of the Schwarzian action.

The next lemma follows from the chain rule of the Schwarzian and expresses the Schwarzian action of a diffeomorphism in terms of its inverse, which will be used later.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism with inverse
\phi=\varphi^{-1}
. Then

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\phi^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{-1}\,\mathfrak{i}\zeta\,\mathcal{S}[\phi](\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(2.2)

2.2
Loewner energy and welding homeomorphisms

We recall the definition of Loewner energy.
Let
\gamma
be an oriented Jordan curve,
f
(resp.,
g
) be a conformal map
\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
(resp.,
\mathbb{D}^{*}\to\Omega^{*}
), where
\Omega
(resp.,
\Omega^{*}
) is the connected component of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\gamma
on the left of
\gamma
(resp., on the right of
\gamma
). The Carathéodory theorem implies that
f
and
g
extend to a homeomorphism on the closure of
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}^{*}
. We call the circle homomorphism
\varphi:=g^{-1}\circ f|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
a
welding
of
\gamma
.

Characterizing all the circle homeomorphisms that arise as welding is open and difficult
[
6
,
38
]
. However, if we restrict to the group of quasisymmetric circle homeomorphisms
\operatorname{QS}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, then we obtain a one-to-one correspondence:

\displaystyle\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})\backslash\{\text{Oriented quasicircles in }\hat{\mathbb{C}}\}

\displaystyle\xleftrightarrow[1:1]{\text{conformal welding}}\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\backslash\operatorname{QS}(\mathbb{S}^{1})/\operatorname{PSU}(1,1),

\displaystyle[\gamma]

\displaystyle\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 60.0pt}[\varphi=g^{-1}\circ f|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}].

Considering the equivalence classes above is natural. On the right-hand side, one may pre-compose
f
and
g
by Möbius transformations preserving
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
, this does not change the curve
\gamma
and gives an equivalent welding. On the left-hand side, replacing
(\gamma;f,g)
by
(\alpha\circ\gamma;\alpha\circ f,\alpha\circ g)
, where
\alpha\in\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is a conformal automorphism of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, shows that
\gamma
and
\alpha\circ\gamma
share the same welding.

The
Loewner energy
of a Jordan curve
\gamma
is first defined as the Dirichlet energy of its Loewner driving function in
[
39
]
. It was shown to be
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-invariant and independent of its orientation. The second author
[
52
]
also showed that it coincides with the universal Liouville action in
[
47
]
, which can be computed by choosing the orientation of
\gamma
such that
\Omega
does not contain
\infty
, and
g:\mathbb{D}^{*}\to\Omega^{*}
fixing
\infty
:

I^{L}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left\lvert\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right\rvert^{2}|\mathrm{d}z|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}^{*}}\left\lvert\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}\right\rvert^{2}|\mathrm{d}z|^{2}\right)+4\log\left\lvert\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{g^{\prime}(\infty)}\right\rvert\in[0,\infty],

(2.3)

where
|\mathrm{d}z|^{2}
denotes the Euclidean area measure.
Since
I^{L}(\gamma)
is
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-invariant, it defines a bi-invariant function on the space of weldings.

Theorem 2.6

(See
[
42
]
)
.

A Jordan curve
\gamma
satisfies
I^{L}(\gamma)<\infty
if and only if any welding of
\gamma
belongs to
\operatorname{WP}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
,
where

\operatorname{WP}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is the space of circle homeomorphisms
\varphi
that are absolutely continuous and

\log|\varphi^{\prime}|
is in the Sobolev
H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
space.

From this, we have the inclusions:

Lemma 2.7
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
,

C^{1.5+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\subset\operatorname{WP}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\subset\operatorname{QS}(\mathbb{S}^{1}).

In particular, the Loewner energy is well-defined, finite, and bi-invariant in the space of
C^{3}
circle homeomorphisms.

2.3
Epstein map in any dimension

We describe briefly the general construction of Epstein maps introduced in
[
18
]
. This construction will be used to define renormalized area and renormalized length.

Let
n\geq 1
. We use the ball model of the hyperbolic
n+1
space

\mathbb{H}^{n+1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\lVert x\rVert<1\right\},

whose conformal boundary is identified with the Euclidean unit ball
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.
Let
h
be a smooth Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
conformal to the round metric (induced by the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
). For
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
in the ball model, we let
\nu_{x}
be the hyperbolic visual metric on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\partial\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
seen from
x
(namely, the pull-back of the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
by any orientation-preserving isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
sending
x
to the origin
0
), then for
z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, we define

H_{z}=H_{z}(h)=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\,|\,\nu_{x}(z)=h(z)\}.

(2.4)

It is straightforward to check:

Lemma 2.8
.

The set
H_{z}(h)
is a horosphere centered at
z
and depends only on the metric tensor
h(z)
. Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
H_{z}(\mathrm{e}^{2t}h)
is the horosphere centered at
z
at signed distance
t
from
H_{z}(h)
. The sign is such that if
t>0
,
H_{z}(\mathrm{e}^{2t}h)
is contained in the horoball bounded by
H_{z}(h)
.

The
Epstein map

\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
is the solution to the envelope equation of these horospheres. More precisely, there exists a (unique) continuous map

\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{n+1},

(2.5)

where
T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
denotes the unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
,
such that
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(z)
is an outward pointing normal to the horoball bounded by
H_{z}
,

\operatorname{Ep}_{h}\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n+1}

is the composition of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
with the projection
T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, and the image of the differential of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
at
z
is orthogonal to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(z)
. We call the image of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
the
Epstein hypersurface associated with
h
and denote it by
\Sigma_{h}
. (We sometimes also do not distinguish between
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
.)
In particular,
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}(z)
is a tangent point between the horosphere
H_{z}
and the Epstein hypersurface.

Example 2.9
.

If
h
is the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, then for all
z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
,
H_{z}
is the horosphere passing through
0
and centered at
z
. The function
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
maps
z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
to
-z\in T^{1}_{0}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
and the Epstein hypersurface degenerates to the origin.

We record some basic facts about Epstein maps.

Lemma 2.10

(Naturality of Epstein map)
.

If
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
,
\alpha\in\operatorname{Isom}_{+}(\mathbb{H}^{n+1})
then
\nu_{\alpha(x)}=\alpha_{*}(\nu_{x})
, where
\alpha_{*}
is the pushforward by
\alpha
.
It follows that for
z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
,

\alpha(H_{z}(h))=H_{\alpha(z)}(\alpha_{*}h)\quad\text{ and }\quad\alpha\circ\operatorname{Ep}_{h}(z)=\operatorname{Ep}_{\alpha_{*}h}(\alpha(z)).

(2.6)

Theorem 2.11

(Scaling property of Epstein map
[
25
,
11
]
)
.

Let
h
be a
C^{2}
conformal metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Let
h_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{2t}h
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

1.

The value of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(z)
is determined by
h
and its first derivatives at
z
.

2.

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{t}:T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\rightarrow T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
be time
t
geodesic flow. Then
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h_{t}}=\mathfrak{g}_{-t}\circ\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
.

3.

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{-\infty}:T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}
be the hyperbolic Gauss map sending a tangent vector to the endpoint of the associated geodesic ray as
t\to-\infty
. Then
\mathfrak{g}_{-\infty}\left(\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(z)\right)=z
.

4.

For all
z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, there are at most two values of
t
where
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
is not an immersion at
z
.

5.

There exists
t_{0}
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
is an embedding. Let
\operatorname{I}_{t}
be the pullback metric by
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\Sigma_{h_{t}}
, then
4\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\operatorname{I}_{t}\to h
as
t\to\infty
.

Remark 2.12
.

In particular, the conformal metric
h
defines an equidistant foliation
(\Sigma_{h_{t}})_{t\geq t_{0}}
in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

The Epstein map is defined locally, therefore, we may extend its definition to a conformal metric defined on an open domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
. In Equation (
1.5
),
n=2
and
N_{\gamma}
is the domain bounded by two Epstein surfaces
\Sigma_{\rho_{\Omega}}
and
\Sigma_{\rho_{\Omega^{*}}}
, where
\rho_{\Omega}
and
\rho_{\Omega^{*}}
are respectively the hyperbolic metric on
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
.

Lemma 3.5

For a metric
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta

\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the signed arc-length
\mathrm{d}\ell
and geodesic curvature

of the Epstein curve

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma)\right)\mathrm{d}\theta,

(3.3)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\sigma}(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\sigma}(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)}.

Proof.

Observe that the tangent vector

\displaystyle T(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma_{\theta}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}+1)(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{2\sigma}-1)}{(\sigma_{\theta}^{2}+(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}+1)^{2})^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}+\frac{((\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}+1)^{2}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2})(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{2\sigma}-1)}{(\sigma_{\theta}^{2}+(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}+1)^{2})^{2}}\,\mathfrak{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{i}\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma)\right).

It follows that the signed arc-length
\,\mathrm{d}\ell
(according to the convention in Definition

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell=\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma)\right)\mathrm{d}\theta.

(3.4)

From the expression (

) for the normal vector to the Epstein curve, we can explicitly calculate

\displaystyle\langle-\nabla_{T}\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h},T\rangle=\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma)\right)\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}+\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\right)+\cosh(\sigma)\right),

(3.5)

where
\nabla,\langle.,.\rangle
denote the connection and metric of the hyperbolic plane. Hence it follows that the geodesic curvature

of the Epstein curve at

is given by

\displaystyle k=\frac{\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}+\sigma_{\theta}^{2}/2\right)+\cosh(\sigma)\right)}{\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\left(\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}/2\right)+\sinh(\sigma)\right)}=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\sigma}(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\sigma}(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)}

(3.6)

∎

We now extend the notion of enclosed area for Epstein curves.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta
be a
C^{2}
metric on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We define its
signed area

A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
as

A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h}):=\int_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Phi^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp})

where
\Phi:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
is any piecewise
C^{1}
extension of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
and
\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp}
denotes the area form of the hyperbolic disk.

Observe that since for any two piecewise
C^{1}
extensions
\Phi_{0}
,
\Phi_{1}
of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
there is a piecewise
C^{1}
homotopy
\{\Phi_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
satisfying
\Phi_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})=\Phi_{0}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})=\Phi_{1}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Phi_{1}^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp})-\int_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Phi_{0}^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp})=0.

Hence

is well-defined.

The following Lemma shows that the Gauss–Bonnet theorem extends to signed area
A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
and signed length
\mathrm{d}\ell
.

Lemma 3.10

(Gauss–Bonnet for signed length and area)
.

Let
\hat{h}_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{0}}\mathrm{d}\theta
,
\hat{h}_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{1}}\mathrm{d}\theta
be any
C^{2}
metrics in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then

\displaystyle A(\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{1}})-A(\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{0}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\hat{k}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{1}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\hat{k}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{0},

(3.8)

where
\hat{k}_{i},\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{i}
are the curvature and signed arc-length of
\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{i}}

(i=0,1)
.
In particular, it follows that Epstein curves satisfy the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. Namely, for any
C^{2}
metric
h
in
S^{1}
we have

\displaystyle A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell-2\pi.

(3.9)

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{0}+t\sigma
. Consider the
1
-parameter family of metrics
\hat{h}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{t}}\mathrm{d}\theta
. Their respective Epstein curves define a parametrized cylinder between
\hat{h}_{0},\hat{h}_{1}
by
\Psi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
given as

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta},t):=\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{t}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}).

As we can get an extension of
\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{1}}
by doing a domain reparametrization of a concatenating between
\Psi
and an extension of
\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{0}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle A(\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{1}})-A(\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{0}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]}\Psi^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp}).

Denote by
\hat{k}_{t},\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{t}
the curvature and signed arc-length of
\operatorname{Ep}_{\hat{h}_{t}}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp})=-\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{t}\wedge\mathrm{d}t=\sigma\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}}\left((\sigma_{t})_{\theta\theta}-\frac{((\sigma_{t})_{\theta})^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t\wedge\mathrm{d}\theta

which can be directly computed from (
3.1
) and Lemma
3.5
, or seen by observing that the component of
D_{t}\Psi
in
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
is given by the rate of change of distance between horospheres with respect to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
, namely
-\sigma
.

In
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
, define the
1
-form

\displaystyle\eta:

\displaystyle=\hat{k}_{t}\mathrm{d}\theta-\sigma_{\theta}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}}\left(-(\sigma_{t})_{\theta\theta}+\frac{((\sigma_{t})_{\theta})^{2}}{2}\right)+\cosh(\sigma_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}\theta-\sigma_{\theta}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}}\mathrm{d}t.

By direct computation, we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\eta=\sigma\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}}\left((\sigma_{t})_{\theta\theta}-\frac{((\sigma_{t})_{\theta})^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t\wedge\mathrm{d}\theta=\Psi^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp}).

Applying Stokes theorem and Lemma
3.5
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]}\Psi^{*}(\mathrm{d}a_{\rm hyp})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{1\}}\eta-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{0\}}\eta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{1}}\left((\sigma_{1})_{\theta\theta}-\frac{((\sigma_{1})_{\theta})^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma_{1})\right)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{0}}\left((\sigma_{0})_{\theta\theta}-\frac{((\sigma_{0})_{\theta})^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh(\sigma_{0})\right)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\hat{k}_{1}\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{1}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\hat{k}_{0}\mathrm{d}\hat{\ell}_{0},

so (
3.8
) follows.

In order to conclude (
3.9
), observe that by Example
3.1
this equation follows for
\tilde{h}=\mathrm{e}^{t}\mathrm{d}\theta
and
t>0
, as it can be computed directly while also being equivalent to the standard Gauss–Bonnet theorem for the associated Epstein curve. Now, for any given
h
, we can apply (
3.8
) for
\hat{h}_{1}=h
and
\hat{h}_{0}=\tilde{h}
. By adding this equation to (
3.9
) for
\hat{h}_{0}
we obtain (
3.9
) for
h
.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

Let
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta

metric on

. The term

of

.

By Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle k=\frac{1-k^{*}}{1+k^{*}}.

(3.10)

This also characterizes the points where

fails to be an immersion as the points where

. It follows from the formulae that if for constant

,

\displaystyle k^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-2t}k^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-k^{*}_{t}}{1+k^{*}_{t}}=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}k^{*}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}k^{*}}

We collect a few useful expressions for the arc-length and curvature of an equidistant Epstein foliation, both in terms of data at infinity for the initial Epstein curve.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta
be a metric on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
k^{*}
its curvature at infinity,

k
the geodesic curvature of the Epstein curve. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}h
. Denote by
\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}
and
k_{t}
the signed arc-length and geodesic curvature of the Epstein curve
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
.
The following identities hold:

1.

\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{t}+\mathrm{e}^{-t}k^{*})h

2.

k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{t}-\mathrm{e}^{-t}k^{*})h

3.

\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}=\cosh(t)\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\sinh(t)k\,\mathrm{d}\ell

4.

k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}=\sinh(t)\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\cosh(t)k\,\mathrm{d}\ell
.

Proof.

Let us start by verifying the first two items for
\mathrm{d}\ell
and
k\,\mathrm{d}\ell
. Recall that from the definition of
k^{*}
, Lemma
3.5
, and the equality
k=(1-k^{*})/(1+k^{*})
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+k^{*}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+k^{*}\right)h,

(3.11)

\displaystyle k\,\mathrm{d}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-k^{*}}{1+k^{*}}\right)(1+k^{*})h=\frac{1}{2}(1-k^{*})h.

(3.12)

Applying this for
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}h
, and recalling that its curvature at infinity is
k^{*}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-2t}k^{*}
, we now obtain the first two items

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+k^{*}_{t}\right)h_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{t}+\mathrm{e}^{-t}k^{*})h,

(3.13)

\displaystyle k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-k^{*}_{t}\right)h_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{t}-\mathrm{e}^{-t}k^{*})h.

In particular, the total signed length and total curvature of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
can be expressed as a function of
t
and the metric
h
by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}h+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}h-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h.

To express
\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}
and
k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}
as integrals in the Epstein curve it will be useful to have the following inversion formulas, which follow easily from the previous statements

\displaystyle k^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-k}{1+k},

(3.15)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{1+k^{*}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\ell=(1+k)\,\mathrm{d}\ell.

Combining then (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) we conclude the last two items as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}}{2}(1+k)\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{2}(1-k)\,\mathrm{d}\ell=\cosh(t)\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\sinh(t)k\,\mathrm{d}\ell

(3.16)

and analogously

\displaystyle k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}}{2}(1+k)\,\mathrm{d}\ell-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{2}(1-k)\,\mathrm{d}\ell=\sinh(t)\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\cosh(t)k\,\mathrm{d}\ell

(3.17)

which completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Epstein curves from circle diffeomorphisms

The previous results establish the basic properties of the Epstein curve for any
C^{2}
metric on the conformal boundary at infinity
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
, we now apply the results to metrics obtained by pushing forward
\mathrm{d}\theta
by a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism. Let
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
be the pushforward of
\mathrm{d}\theta
by
\varphi
. Let
A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
denote the signed hyperbolic area enclosed by the Epstein curve associated with
h
and
L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
the signed hyperbolic length of the Epstein curve, then we have

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=-A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h}).

Proof.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism with inverse
\phi=\varphi^{-1}
and

h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta=|\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|\mathrm{d}\theta
the corresponding
C^{2}
metric on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
of length
2\pi
. Writing
\sigma=\log|\phi^{\prime}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|
so that
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right),

(3.18)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left(-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}-\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)^{2}\right)-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right).

Recalling that
\phi
is a diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, one can verify that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left(-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)^{2}\right)=\Bigg|\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\Bigg|^{2}+4\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)+2-2|\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|^{2}.

(3.19)

We can then express
\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}/2
in terms of the first three derivatives of
\phi
using the relation
(\operatorname{Re}x)^{2}=(\operatorname{Re}(x^{2})+|x|^{2})/2
, (
3.18
), and (
3.19
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Re}\left(\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}-\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)^{2}\right)\right)-\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Re}\left(-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\Bigg|\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\Bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Re}\left(\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}{\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})}\right)^{2}\right)\right)+\frac{1-|\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{2\sigma}}{2}

where in the last step we used that
|\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}
.

In particular, by Lemma
3.5
and (
3.11
)
we have

\displaystyle-\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\mathrm{d}\ell=\frac{1}{2}(1+k^{*})\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta.

(3.20)

Using Lemma
2.5
to express the Schwarzian action in terms of the inverse diffeomorphism and
|\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|^{-1}=\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}(\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}))\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{\operatorname{Ep}_{h}}\mathrm{d}\ell

so it follows that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)
is equal to the signed length of the Epstein curve
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
induced by
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
.

As the total length of
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta
is equal to
2\pi
, we can rewrite the previous equation and use (
3.12
) to express the Schwarzian action as

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}(1+k^{*})\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}(1-k^{*})\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell.

It follows from Lemma
3.10
that the Schwarzian action is also equal to minus the signed area enclosed by the Epstein curve.
∎

As we prove later in two ways (Corollary
3.19
or Remark
1.8
),
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)\geq 0
if
\varphi
is a
C^{3}
circle diffeomorphism. This non-negativity can also be used to show that the Epstein curves of diffeomorphisms must have a non-immersed point.

Proposition 3.16
.

If
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism and
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
, then
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
has a non-immersed point.

Proof.

Let
\phi=\varphi^{-1}
. By Remark
3.6
and (
3.10
), the set of non-immersed points on
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
is the locus of points where
k^{*}=-1
. By (
3.20
), this is equivalent the set
\{\theta:\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})=0\}
.

Since both
\varphi
and
\phi
are diffeomorphisms,
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)\geq 0
and
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\phi)\geq 0
. In particular,
\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}
cannot be strictly negative (we already know it is real by Remark
2.3
). It also cannot be strictly positive as by Lemma
2.5
that would imply
\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}
is strictly negative. Therefore since
\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}
is continuous it must have a zero.
∎

3.3
Isoperimetric inequality

We note that a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism
\varphi
defines a
C^{2}
metric
h=\varphi_{*}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
with total length
2\pi
. Conversely, any
C^{2}
metric
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
with total length
2\pi
arises this way. In fact, let
\theta_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
, define

\phi(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})=\exp\left(\mathfrak{i}\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}\mathrm{e}^{\sigma(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

we have
\phi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\phi(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta_{0}})=1
. Let
\varphi=\phi^{-1}
, then we have
\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta=h
.
Choosing a different
\theta_{0}
amounts to precomposing
\varphi
by a rotation, and if
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
give rise to the same metric
h
, then
\varphi_{1}
is obtained from
\varphi_{2}
by a rotation.

In other words, we have a one-to-one correspondence between

\{C^{2}\text{ metrics in }\mathbb{S}^{1}\text{ with total length }2\pi\}\xleftrightarrow{1\,\colon 1}\operatorname{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1})/\mathbb{S}^{1}.

(3.21)

We also consider metrics of the form
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}h
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}
, which then encompasses metrics in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with arbitrary but finite total length.
Recall from Theorem
2.11
and Remark
2.12
that the family of Epstein curves
(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})_{t\geq t_{0}}
for some large
t_{0}
forms an equidistant foliation, which converges in
C^{1}
to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as
t\to\infty
(Remark
3.13
).

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
h
be a
C^{2}
metric on
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Let
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}h
, while
L(\cdot)
,
A(\cdot)
denote the signed length and signed area enclosed by an Epstein curve. Then, if
h
is a metric of total length equal to
2\pi
, we have

(a)

L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})+A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=0
,

(b)

L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})=2\pi\sinh(t)-\mathrm{e}^{-t}A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
,

(c)

A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})=2\pi(\cosh(t)-1)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
.

Proof.

The first item follows from Theorem
3.15
as a consequence of Gauss–Bonnet, (
3.11
), and (
3.12
):

\displaystyle L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})+A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell-2\pi=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}h-2\pi=0.

(3.22)

By (
3.16
), (
3.17
) and (
3.22
) we have that

\displaystyle L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\cosh(t)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\sinh(t)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell

(3.23)

\displaystyle=-\cosh(t)A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})+\sinh(t)(A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})+2\pi)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sinh(t)-\mathrm{e}^{-t}A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})

\displaystyle A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}-2\pi

\displaystyle=\sinh(t)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell+\cosh(t)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell-2\pi

\displaystyle=-\sinh(t)A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})+\cosh(t)(A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})+2\pi)-2\pi

\displaystyle=2\pi(\cosh(t)-1)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})

which concludes
(b)
and
(c)
.
∎

A simple consequence of the asymptotic growth for length and area in Lemma
3.17
is that for
t
sufficiently large, both
L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})
and
A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})
are positive. This is relevant for the following theorem, where we express the Schwarzian action as the asymptotic isoperimetric excess of the Epstein curve
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
as
t
goes to infinity. This should be compared to
[
49
, Thm. 1.2]
, where the renormalized volume of acylindrical convex co-compact manifolds was expressed as an asymptotic limit of the isoperimetric profile of the manifold.

The
isoperimetric profile
in the hyperbolic disk is the function
J:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

J(A)=2\pi\sinh\left(\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{A}{2\pi}+1\right)\right),

such that any (embedded) domain in
\mathbb{D}
with area
A
has boundary length larger or equal to
J(A)
where the equality is realized by disks. The disk with (hyperbolic) radius
t
has boundary length
2\pi\sinh(t)
and area
2\pi(\cosh(t)-1)
, which follows from, e.g., Lemma
3.17
.

Theorem 3.18

(Schwarzian action as an asymptotic isoperimetric excess)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism, and
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
, then

\displaystyle 2I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\Big(L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})-J(A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}))\Big).

(3.24)

Proof.

By items
(b)
,
(c)
of Lemma
3.17
, writing
A=A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
, the right-hand side of (
3.24
) can be calculated as

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(2\pi\sinh(t)-\mathrm{e}^{-t}A-2\pi\sinh\left(\cosh^{-1}\left(\cosh(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\frac{A}{2\pi}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-A+\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(2\pi\sinh(t)-2\pi\sinh\left(\cosh^{-1}\left(\cosh(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\frac{A}{2\pi}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-A+\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(2\pi\sinh(t)-2\pi\sqrt{\left(\cosh(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\frac{A}{2\pi}\right)^{2}-1}\right),

where we are using the well-known identity
\sinh(\cosh^{-1}(x))=\sqrt{x^{2}-1}
. By further simplifying the last expression, we obtain

\displaystyle=-A+\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(2\pi\sinh(t)-2\pi\sqrt{\sinh^{2}(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cosh(t)\frac{A}{\pi}+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{A^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-A+\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t}\left(\frac{-4\pi^{2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cosh(t)\frac{A}{\pi}+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{A^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\right)}{2\pi\sinh(t)+2\pi\sqrt{\sinh^{2}(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cosh(t)\frac{A}{\pi}+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{A^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-A+\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\left(\frac{-4\pi^{2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cosh(t)\frac{A}{\pi}+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{A^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\right)}{\mathrm{e}^{-t}\left(2\pi\sinh(t)+2\pi\sqrt{\sinh^{2}(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cosh(t)\frac{A}{\pi}+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{A^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=-A+\frac{-2\pi A}{2\pi}=-2A

which by Theorem
3.15
proves the desired identity.
∎

Corollary 3.19
.

The Schwarzian action of
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is non-negative and vanishes if and only if
\varphi\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
.

Proof.

Consider the associated metric
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and consider
t
sufficiently large so that the Epstein curve for
h_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t}h
is embedded and
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
corresponds to the inner normal (Remark
3.13
). Since by definition of the isoperimetric profile we have that
L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}})-J(A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}))\geq 0
, the non-negativity follows by Theorem
3.18
.

If we had equality, then by Theorem
3.15
, it follows that
A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=0
. Then for any
t
so that
\operatorname{Ep}(h_{t})
is embedded, we will have by items
(b)
and
(c)
of Lemma
3.17
that
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
is a solution of the isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{D}
. By the characterization of the solutions of the isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{D}
, we have then that
\operatorname{Ep}_{h_{t}}
is the boundary of a ball
B_{t}
of radius
t
in
\mathbb{D}
. This implies that
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
is constant and equals the center of the ball
B_{t}
by Theorem
2.11
.
Let
\alpha\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
be an Möbius transformation sending the center of the ball to the origin, the naturality of the Epstein map (Lemma
2.10
) shows that
\alpha_{*}h=\mathrm{d}\theta
by (
3.21
).
We obtain that
\alpha\circ\varphi
is a rotation.
Hence,
\varphi\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
. The converse follows by Example
3.1
and the naturality of the Epstein map (Lemma
2.10
).
∎

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism. Let
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta
be the pushforward of
\mathrm{d}\theta
by
\varphi
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h=2I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)-2\pi\geq-2\pi,

with equality if and only if
\varphi\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
.

Remark 3.21
.

The Schwarzian action
\mathcal{I}(\cdot)
in e.g.
[
3
,
43
]
is written with a different normalization, which by Remark
2.3
is related to ours by
\frac{1}{\pi}\mathcal{I}(\varphi)=2I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)-2\pi
. The above gives a geometric interpretation for this form of the action as the total curvature at infinity.

Proof.

We write
\phi=\varphi^{-1}
.
It follows from (
3.20
) that

\int_{S^{1}}k^{*}h=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}2\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})+\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}2|\phi^{\prime}|^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\phi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta-2\pi

where we used that the total length of
h
is
2\pi
. Lemma
2.5
then implies

\int_{S^{1}}k^{*}h=2I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)-2\pi

as claimed.
∎

Remark 3.22
.

Observe that for a
C^{2}
metric
h=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\theta
with curvature at infinity
k^{*}
, the integral
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h
can be reduce to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}(2\sigma_{\theta\theta}-\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}(\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)\mathrm{d}\theta

by an integration by parts.
Hence, we can extend the definition of
total curvature at infinity

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h
to
C^{1}
metrics in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k^{*}h=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}(\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1)\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.26)

which then also extends the definition of
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\cdot)
to
C^{2}
diffeomorphisms.

3.4
Schwarzian action for
n
-folds covers

We discuss the Epstein curve associated with a degree
n
local diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
(which we also call an
n
-fold cover of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
).

Definition 3.23
.

The group
\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\simeq\operatorname{PSL}^{(n)}(2,\mathbb{R})
is the group of circle diffeomorphisms obtained by conjugating
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
with the
n
-th power map
z\mapsto z^{n}
. That is,

B\in\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\iff\exists A\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1),\,(B(z))^{n}=A(z^{n}).

Observe that the Schwarzian action defined by (
1.1
) extends to any local diffeomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
. As these maps are
n
-fold covers of the circle, it is natural to study the Schwarzian action in
\operatorname{Diff}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1}):=\{\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}\,|\,\varphi\text{ is a $n$-fold cover}\}
. To describe this action, the following lemma will be useful.

Lemma 3.24
.

The map
{}^{n}:\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
given by

\phi\in\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\mapsto(^{n}\phi)(z):=(\phi(z))^{n}

is an
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
principal bundle with fiber action given multiplication by a
n
-root of unity.

Using this correspondence, we can express the Schwarzian action on
\operatorname{Diff}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
as a
\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
invariant functional on
\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Proposition 3.25
.

We define the map
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}:\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as the composition of
{}^{n}:\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}
. We have
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}
is invariant by the left action of
\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and is given by

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}(\phi)

\displaystyle:=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(^{n}\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}-\mathfrak{i}z\mathcal{S}[^{n}\phi](z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\phi)+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{n^{2}-1}{2}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime}(z)}{\phi(z)}\right)^{2}\mathfrak{i}z\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\phi)+\frac{1-n^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\phi^{\prime}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

In particular,
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}
is well defined on the coadjoint orbit
\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\backslash\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

[
54
,
1
]
.

Proof.

Let us prove the
\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
invariance first. Let
\phi\in\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
,
\varphi=\prescript{n}{}{\phi}
,
B\in\operatorname{M\ddot{o}b}_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
A\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
such that
(B(z))^{n}=A(z^{n})
. Then

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}(B\circ\phi)

\displaystyle=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\prescript{n}{}{(B\circ\phi)})=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(A(\prescript{n}{}{\phi}))

\displaystyle=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\prescript{n}{}{\phi})=I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}(\phi).

To get the formula for
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}
we can use the chain rule for the Schwarzian derivative and that the Schwarzian derivative for
z\mapsto z^{n}
is given by
(1-n^{2})/(2z^{2})
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[\varphi](z)=\frac{1-n^{2}}{2\phi^{2}(z)}\left(\phi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}+\mathcal{S}[\phi](z)

from which we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}-\mathfrak{i}z\,\mathcal{S}[\varphi](z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{n^{2}-1}{2\phi^{2}(z)}\left(\phi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}\mathfrak{i}z-\mathfrak{i}z\,\mathcal{S}[\phi](z)\,\mathrm{d}z

and the first identity follows. For the second identity it remains to observe that as
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
we have that
\phi^{\prime}(z)z=|\phi^{\prime}(z)|\phi(z)
.
∎

Recall that the definition of Epstein curve (
3.1
) and its normal (
3.2
) are given by the
1
-jet of the metric
h
on the circle. Hence we can extend these definitions to multi-valued metrics and define the Epstein curve associated to a
n
-fold cover of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Definition 3.26
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{2}

n
-fold cover. Then its associated metric in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is given by the multi-valued
C^{1}
metric
h=\varphi_{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)
. To this multi-valued metric we have its associated Epstein curve
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
and normal
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
, which are curves parametrized by

\operatorname{Ep}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}):=\operatorname{Ep}_{\varphi_{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}(\varphi(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}))

\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}):=\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{\varphi_{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}(\varphi(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}))

See Figure
3
and Figure
4
for examples.

Figure 4:
On the right are the Epstein curve and horocycles for the double cover corresponding to the metric
h^{a}
,
a=1/2
in Example
A.4
(i.e.,
h^{1/2}
pushed forward by
z^{2}
). On the left are twenty five evenly-space horocycles for only

Observe that for
n=1
this definition gives the associated Epstein curve as in the previous sections, but after a right composition by (in this case diffeomorphism)
\varphi
. While this changes the Epstein curve and its normal as maps, it does so only through a reparametrization, so geometric quantities such as the total length and total curvature remain the same.

Local geometric concepts associated with circle diffeomorphism generalize in a straightforward fashion to
n
-fold covers. Such is the case for the arc-length and geodesic curvature of Definition
3.4
(including their extensions by Lemma
3.5
to non-immersion points), the total length and total geodesic curvature of Definition

We can also extend Definition
3.9
for the signed area

3.10
needs some adjustment. Namely, (
3.8

) required that we verify it for one metric. In this case, we can compute it for

\displaystyle A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell-2\pi n.

(3.27)

We can also derive a geometric interpretation for

as follows.

Theorem 3.27
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism. Let
\varphi=\prescript{n}{}{\phi}
be the associated
n

can be computed as

I_{\operatorname{Sch}}^{n}(\phi)=L(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=-A(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})-2\pi(n-1).

Proof.

For the second equality, as in Lemma

∎

4
Bi-local observables

For
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, define a
C^{1}
function
\tilde{\varphi}:[0,2\pi]_{/0\sim 2\pi}\to[0,2\pi]_{/0\sim 2\pi}
by
\varphi(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\tilde{\varphi}(\theta)}
. Pick
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
distinct, and let
x,y\in[0,2\pi]
be their arguments. The
bi-local observable
of
\varphi
for
u,v
is the function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v):=\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(x)\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(y)}}{2\sin(\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\varphi}(x)-\tilde{\varphi}(y)))}=\frac{\;\;|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v)|^{1/2}}{|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|}.

(4.1)

It is straightforward to see that these are invariant under the left action
\varphi\mapsto\alpha\circ\varphi
for
\alpha\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
, and hence descend to functions on
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\backslash\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. In this section, we relate the bi-local observables to renormalized lengths of hyperbolic geodesics truncated by the horocycles in the construction of the Epstein curve (Proposition
4.4
). From this, we show that the bi-local observables along the edges of any ideal triangulation determine the circle diffeomorphism in
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\backslash\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
(Proposition
4.6
).

Although we do not deal directly with the Schwarzian field theory, let us comment on the role played by bi-local observables there. In field theories with a gauge symmetry (i.e. here the
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
action), correlation functions of gauge-invariant observables often play a central role in determining the field theory. A collection of bi-local observables
\{\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u_{j},v_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
is called
non-crossing
if the lines (hyperbolic geodesics) in the disk connecting
(u_{j},v_{j})
do not cross. Correlation functions for non-crossing bi-local observables were computed in the physics literature using a connection to two-dimensional CFT and predicted to determine the theory
[
33
]
; in
[
28
]
these correlation functions were computed mathematically rigorously and shown to determine the measure for the theory using the probabilistic framework for Schwarzian field theory. In the conjectured holography duality between Schwarzian field theory and JT gravity in the disk, bi-local observables in the Schwarzian theory are predicted to correspond to
boundary-anchored Wilson lines
for JT gravity
[
9
]
.

4.1
Bi-local observables as renormalized length

The Epstein curve for
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is the envelope of horocycles
(H_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
in
\mathbb{D}
, where the size of
H_{z}
is determined at each point by
\varphi^{\prime}(u)
and
\varphi(u)
if
z=\varphi(u)
. We saw in Theorem
3.15
that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)
can be computed as the hyperbolic area enclosed by the Epstein curve, loosely speaking as a “renormalized area” where we cut off the disk using horocycles at all points
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
The main result of this section is Proposition
4.4
where we show that the bi-local observable
\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)
is related to the
renormalized length
for
\varphi
of the hyperbolic geodesic from
\varphi(u)
to
\varphi(v)
, given by truncation with the same horocycles as the Epstein curve construction.

Let
d_{\mathbb{D}}
denote the distance function for the hyperbolic metric on
\mathbb{D}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Fix
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and let
(H_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denote the horocycles associated with the metric
\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
as in Section
3.1
. For
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
distinct, let
(u,v)
be the hyperbolic geodesic between them in
\mathbb{D}
.
We define the
renormalized length
for
\varphi
from
u
to
v
, denoted
\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v)
, to be the signed hyperbolic distance from
H_{\varphi(u)}
to
H_{\varphi(v)}
along
(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))
. The sign is positive if
H_{\varphi(u)}
and
H_{\varphi(v)}
are disjoint and negative otherwise.

Remark 4.2
.

Renormalized length is closely related to the
\log\Lambda
lengths of decorated Teichmüller theory, see e.g.
[
37
]
. These are also related to diamond shears studied by the last two authors
[
40
]
. The renormalized length corresponds to
\log\Lambda
length for the particular choice of horocycles given by the Epstein construction.

For any
\varphi
, renormalized length is invariant under post-composition by Möbius transformations by Lemma
2.10
. As the first step in understanding renormalized length, we can compute it for
\varphi=\mathrm{Id}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
distinct, the renormalized length for
\varphi=\mathrm{Id}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{RL}_{\mathrm{Id}}(u,v)=-\log\frac{4}{|u-v|^{2}}.

(4.2)

Proof.

Let
(H_{z}^{0})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denote the horocycles associated with
\mathrm{d}\theta
. By Example
2.9
, for each
z
,
H_{z}^{0}
is the horocycle based at
z
containing
0
. This is invariant under rotation, so it suffices to compute
\mathrm{RL}_{\mathrm{Id}}(-1,v)
for
v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

We compute the desired length from
H_{-1}\cap(-1,v)
to
H_{v}\cap(-1,v)
using the halfplane model
\mathbb{H}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta)>0\}
of the hyperbolic plane. Let
\mathfrak{c}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
be the Cayley map given by
\mathfrak{c}(z)=\mathfrak{i}(1-z)/(1+z)
. Since
\mathfrak{c}(0)=\mathfrak{i}
,
(H_{z}^{0})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
is sent by
\mathfrak{c}
to the collection of horocycles
(\tilde{H}_{x})_{x\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}}
all of which contain
\mathfrak{i}
. Note that
\mathfrak{c}(-1)=\infty
and let
\lambda=\mathfrak{c}(w)
.

Since
\tilde{H}_{\infty}
is the horocycle of Euclidean height
1
,
\tilde{H}_{\infty}
and
(\lambda,\infty)
intersect at
\lambda+\mathfrak{i}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}
must be centered at
\lambda+\mathfrak{i}r
for some
r>0
, and must contain
\lambda
and
\mathfrak{i}
. We use this to solve for
r
:

\displaystyle r^{2}=\lambda^{2}+(r-1)^{2}\implies r=\frac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2}).

Therefore the intersection of
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}
and
(\lambda,\infty)
is at
\lambda+(1+\lambda^{2})\mathfrak{i}
. Note that
1+\lambda^{2}\geq 1
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, corresponding to the fact that
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}
and
\tilde{H}_{\infty}
always intersect. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{RL}_{\mathrm{Id}}(-1,w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{RL}_{\mathrm{Id}}(\lambda,\infty)=\int_{1+\lambda^{2}}^{1}\frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y=-\log(1+\lambda^{2})

\displaystyle=-\log\frac{4v}{(1+v)^{2}}=-\log\frac{4}{|v+1|^{2}},

which completes the proof.
∎

One can observe directly from the lemma that
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Id};u,v)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\exp(-\mathrm{RL}_{\mathrm{Id}}(u,v))
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Fix
\varphi\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
distinct. The bi-local observable and renormalized length are related by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)^{2}=\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v)|}{|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}\exp(-\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v)).

(4.3)

The key observation to prove Proposition
4.4
is the following lemma coming from the naturality of the Epstein construction.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(H^{0}_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denote the horocycles associated with the metric
\mathrm{d}\theta
as in Example
2.9
and
(H_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
those associated with the metric
\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
. Then
H_{\varphi(z)}=\alpha_{z}(H^{0}_{z})
, where
\alpha_{z}\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)=\operatorname{Isom}_{+}(\mathbb{D})
is any osculating Möbius map at
z
such that
\alpha_{z}(z)=\varphi(z)
and
\alpha_{z}^{\prime}(z)=\varphi^{\prime}(z)
.

Proof.

We use the notation as in (
2.6
). Since
H_{\varphi(z)}:=H_{\varphi(z)}(\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta)
is a horocycle centered at
\varphi(z)
which only depends on the metric
\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
at
\varphi(z)
, we have

H_{\varphi(z)}=H_{\varphi(z)}(\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta)=H_{\alpha_{z}(z)}(\alpha_{z*}\mathrm{d}\theta)=\alpha_{z}(H_{z}^{0})

where the last equality follows from Lemma
2.10
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

Fix
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. As in Lemma
4.5
, let
(H_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
and
(H_{z}^{0})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denote the horocycles for
\varphi
and
\mathrm{Id}
respectively.

First consider the case where
\varphi
fixes
u,v
.
By Lemma
4.5
, for any
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
H_{\varphi(z)}=\alpha_{z}(H_{z}^{0})
, where
\alpha_{z}\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
has
\alpha_{z}(z)=\varphi(z)
and
\alpha_{z}^{\prime}(z)=|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|
. Since
\varphi(u)=u
,
H_{\varphi(u)}=H_{u}
and
H_{u}^{0}
are both horocycles at
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and further
H_{u}
and
H_{u}^{0}
are at signed distance
-\log|\varphi^{\prime}(u)|
, which is positive when
H_{u}
is contained in the horoball bounded by
H_{u}^{0}
. This also follows directly from Lemma
2.8
.
The same statement holds for
v
.
Hence, from the definition of renormalized length,

\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v)=\mathrm{RL}_{\mathrm{Id}}(u,v)-\log|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v)|=-\log\frac{4|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v)|}{|u-v|^{2}},

which proves (
4.3
) when
\varphi
fixes
u,v
.

If
\varphi
does not fix
u,v
, then we can find a Möbius transformation
\alpha:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
such that
\psi=\alpha\circ\varphi
fixes
u,v
. Then using
\alpha^{\prime}(z)\alpha^{\prime}(w)=\frac{(\alpha(z)-\alpha(w))^{2}}{(z-w)^{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(u)\psi^{\prime}(v)=\alpha^{\prime}(\varphi(u))\varphi^{\prime}(u)\,\alpha^{\prime}(\varphi(v))\varphi^{\prime}(v)=\frac{(u-v)^{2}}{(\varphi(u)-\varphi(v))^{2}}\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v).

As such,

\displaystyle\mathrm{RL}_{\varphi}(u,v)=\mathrm{RL}_{\psi}(u,v)=-\log\frac{4|\psi^{\prime}(u)\psi^{\prime}(v)|}{|u-v|^{2}}=-\log\frac{4|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{\prime}(v)|}{|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|^{2}}

(4.4)

which proves the result.
∎

4.2
From bi-local observables to a diffeomorphism

In this section, we explain how to reconstruct the diffeomorphism from its bi-local observables on any ideal triangulation. This is reminiscent of the coordinate system of
\log\Lambda
length for decorated Teichmüller space mentioned in Remark
4.2
.

A geodesic ideal triangle in
\mathbb{D}
is a triangle with vertices in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and hyperbolic geodesic edges. An
ideal triangulation
(or
ideal tessellation
)
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathbb{D}
is a locally-finite collection of geodesic ideal triangles that cover
\mathbb{D}
and have non-overlapping interiors. We denote the vertices and edges of
\mathcal{T}
by
(V,E)
. In particular,
V\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
is dense, and
E
is a collection of non-crossing geodesics in the disk. See Figure
5
for an example.

Figure 5:
Example of an ideal triangulation, where only finitely many edges are drawn.

Proposition 4.6
.

Fix
[\varphi]\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\setminus\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. For any ideal triangulation
\mathcal{T}=(V,E)
of
\mathbb{D}
,
\{\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)\}_{(u,v)\in E}
determines
[\varphi]
.

Remark 4.7
.

For any
u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the observable
\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)
is determined just by the equivalence class
[\varphi]\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)\setminus\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, as for any
\alpha\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
,
\mathcal{O}(\alpha\circ\varphi;u,v)=\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)
.

Remark 4.8
.

Since
V
is dense in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the data
\{\varphi(v)\}_{v\in V}
determines
\varphi
. As we will see in the proof,
\{\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)\}_{(u,v)\in E}
determines
\varphi(v)
and
H_{\varphi(v)}
(and hence
\varphi^{\prime}(v)
) for all
v\in V
. We require that
\{\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)\}_{(u,v)\in E}
come from a diffeomorphism in the statement since this over determination indicates that not all collections of numbers
(\mathcal{O}_{e})_{e\in E}
are the observables of a diffeomorphism.

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

Let
T
denote the set of triangles in
\mathcal{T}
. The dual graph of
\mathcal{T}
, denoted
\mathcal{T}^{*}=(V^{*},E^{*})
, has vertices
V^{*}=T
and edges
E^{*}
in correspondence with
E
(triangles
t_{1},t_{2}
are connected by an edge
e^{*}\in E^{*}
if and only if they share an edge
e\in E
). In particular, since
\mathcal{T}
is an ideal triangulation,
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is an infinite trivalent tree.
We use this tree structure to iteratively recover the values of
\varphi(u)
and the horocycle decoration
H_{\varphi(u)}
for all
u\in V
from
\{\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u,v)\}_{(u,v)\in E}
.

For this, fix a root edge
e_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0})\in E
and assume without loss of generality that
\varphi
fixes
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
, and moreover that
H_{\varphi(u_{0})}=H_{u_{0}}^{0}
. Since
\mathcal{O}(\varphi;u_{0},v_{0})
is the hyperbolic distance from
H_{u_{0}}
to
H_{v_{0}}
along
e_{0}
, this information determines
H_{v_{0}}
.

Now, take any ideal triangle
t=(u,v,w)\in T
. Let
a,b,c
denote the bi-local observables for
(u,v)
,
(v,w)
and
(w,u)
respectively. Given the data
\{\varphi(u),\varphi(v),H_{\varphi(u)},H_{\varphi(v)}\}
, the position of
\varphi(w)
is then determined by
\varphi(u)
,
\varphi(v)
, the relative distance
b-c
to
H_{\varphi(u)}
vs. to
H_{\varphi(v)}
,
and the order (clockwise or counterclockwise) of
u,v,w
, and is independent of the horocycle
H_{\varphi(w)}
. Once
\varphi(w)
is known, the value of
b
(or
c
) then determines
H_{\varphi(w)}
.

Therefore knowing
\{\varphi(u),\varphi(v),H_{\varphi(u)},H_{\varphi(v)}\}
for one edge
e=(u,v)
of a triangle
t=(u,v,w)\in T
, the bi-local observables then determine
\{\varphi(w),H_{\varphi(w)}\}
. Iterating this for all
t\in T
along the branches of the tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}
starting from
e_{0}
, we see that the observables determine
\varphi(v)
for all
v\in V
. Since
V\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
is dense, this determines
\varphi
.
∎

5
Schwarzian action and Loewner energy

In this section, we prove Theorem
5.3
relating the Loewner energy to the Schwarzian action. Therefore, although some results in this section hold for curves with lower regularity, we restrict our consideration to Jordan curves that are at least
C^{3,\alpha}
regular for some
\alpha>0
so that their welding homeomorphisms are at least
C^{3}
regular.

5.1
Variation of Loewner energy

We now recall briefly the variational formula of the Loewner energy proved in
[
47
]
.

Let
\gamma
be a
C^{3,\alpha}
curve,
f
(resp.,
g
) be a conformal map
\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
(resp.,
\mathbb{D}^{*}\to\Omega^{*}
), where
\Omega
(resp.,
\Omega^{*}
) is the connected component of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\gamma
which does not contain
\infty
(resp., which contains
\infty
). By Kellogg’s theorem,
f
and
g
extend to
C^{3,\alpha}
diffeomorphisms on the closures of
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}^{*}
respectively.
In particular, the
welding homeomorphism

\varphi:=g^{-1}\circ f|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
is a
C^{3,\alpha}
diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Let
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega^{*},\rho_{\Omega^{*}})\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega^{*})
be a Beltrami differential, where
\rho_{\Omega^{*}}(z)
is the density of the hyperbolic metric on
\Omega^{*}
.
For
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
small enough such that
\|\varepsilon\mu\|_{\infty}<1
, let
\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}:\hat{\mathbb{C}}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be any quasiconformal mapping solving the Beltrami equation

\bar{\partial}\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}={\varepsilon\mu}\,\partial\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}.

In particular,
\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}
is conformal in
\Omega
. By the measurable Riemann mapping theorem, another solution to the Beltrami equation is of the form
\alpha\circ\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}
where
\alpha\in\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is a Möbius map of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
.

If
\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}
is
normalized
such that
\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}
fixes
0
and
\infty
with
(\omega^{{\varepsilon\mu}})^{\prime}(0)=1
, then the map
\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}
depends analytically on
\varepsilon
in a neighborhood of
0
. The vector field of the infinitesimal variation

v=v_{\mu}:=\mathrm{d}\omega^{{\varepsilon\mu}}/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon|_{\varepsilon=0}=\dot{\omega}
satisfies

\mu=\bar{\partial}v
,
v(z)=O(z^{2})
as
z\to 0
, and
v(z)=O(z)
as
z\to\infty
.

The following result was proved in
[
47
]
(see also
[
45
]
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\gamma
be a
C^{3,\alpha}
curve for some
\alpha>0
and
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega^{*},\rho_{\Omega^{*}})\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega^{*})
.
With the notations above, let
\gamma^{{\varepsilon\mu}}=\omega^{\varepsilon\mu}(\gamma)
.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}I^{L}(\gamma^{{\varepsilon\mu}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{4}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{\Omega^{*}}\mu(z)\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}z\right)

(5.1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\int_{\partial\Omega^{*}}v(z)\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right],

(5.2)

where
g:\mathbb{D}^{*}\to\Omega^{*}
are any conformal maps,
\mathrm{d}^{2}z
is the Euclidean area measure,
\mathrm{d}z
is the contour integral.

We note that
\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)=O(z^{-4})
as
z\to\infty
and the right-hand side of (
5.1
) is convergent.

Remark 5.2
.

The second identity (
5.2
) follows from

\mu=\bar{\partial}v
and Stokes’ formula:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu(z)\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mu(z)\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{i}}\int_{\Omega}\bar{\partial}\left(v(z)\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{i}}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\left(v(z)\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{i}}\int_{\partial\Omega}v(z)\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

5.2
Derivation of the Schwarzian action

Using the same notation as in the previous section,
we consider the family of
equipotentials

\Big(\gamma^{\varepsilon}:=f((1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{S}^{1})\Big)_{0\leq\varepsilon<1}
in
\Omega
bounded by a
C^{3,\alpha}
Jordan curve
\gamma
for some
\alpha>0
.

Theorem 5.3
.

We have

\frac{\mathrm{d}I^{L}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi),

where
\varphi=g^{-1}\circ f|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
is the welding homeomorphism of
\gamma
.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
, let

f_{\varepsilon}(z)=f((1-\varepsilon)z)/(1-\varepsilon)
such that
\gamma^{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}(S^{1})
. The vector field
v:=\mathrm{d}f_{\varepsilon}/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon|_{\varepsilon=0}\circ f^{-1}
is holomorphic on
\Omega
. Since
f
is
C^{3,\alpha}
on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
,
v
is a holomorphic vector field on
\Omega
and is
C^{2,\alpha}
on
\overline{\Omega}
.

Since
f_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
,
f_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0)=1
, we have
v(z)=O(z^{2})
as
z\to 0
. We now extend
v
into a
C^{2}
vector field such that
v(z)=O(z)
as
z\to\infty
and that
\mu:=\bar{\partial}v
satisfies
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega^{*},\rho_{\Omega^{*}})\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega^{*})
. In other words,
v=v_{\mu}
as described in Section
5.1
.

Such an extension exists since we may extend the vector field
\tilde{v}(z):=\iota^{*}v(z):=v(1/z)z^{2}
to the bounded domain
\iota(\Omega^{*})
as a
C^{2}
vector field, which we also denote by
\tilde{v}
, such that
\tilde{v}(0)=0
. We define
v
in
\Omega^{*}
as
\iota^{*}\tilde{v}
, then it satisfies the conditions above.
In fact,
\tilde{\mu}=\bar{\partial}\tilde{v}=\iota^{*}\mu\in L^{2}(\iota(\Omega^{*}),\rho_{\iota(\Omega^{*})})\cap L^{\infty}(\iota(\Omega^{*}))
, where

\mu=\bar{\partial}v\qquad\text{and}\qquad\iota^{*}(\mu)(z)=\mu\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\frac{z^{2}}{\bar{z}^{2}}.

As
\mu\mapsto\iota^{*}\mu
preserves the
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
norms of Beltrami differentials, we also obtain this way that
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega^{*},\rho_{\Omega^{*}})\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega^{*})
.

Now, we apply Theorem
5.1
to the vector field
v
.
Using

\displaystyle v(z)

\displaystyle=-wf^{\prime}(w)+z\quad\text{ where }z=f(w)\text{ and }w\in\mathbb{S}^{1},

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(w)\mathrm{d}w,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{i}w\,\mathrm{d}\theta\quad\text{ where }w=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}[\varphi]\circ f^{-1}(z)(f^{-1})^{\prime}(z)^{2}+\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z),

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}I^{L}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}I^{L}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\int_{\partial\Omega^{*}}-wf^{\prime}(w)\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\int_{\partial\Omega}wf^{\prime}(w)\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\int_{\partial\Omega}wf^{\prime}(w)\mathcal{S}[\varphi]\circ f^{-1}(z)(f^{-1})^{\prime}(z)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right].

We used the Möbius invariance of
I^{L}
in the first equality above, Theorem
5.1
and that
z\mathcal{S}[g^{-1}](z)
is holomorphic in
\Omega^{*}
in the second, changed the orientation of
\gamma=\partial\Omega
in the third, used the fact that
z\mapsto wf^{\prime}(w)\mathcal{S}[f^{-1}](z)
is holomorphic in
\Omega
, and change of variables in the last equality.

Since we knew that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)
is real from Lemma
2.4
, the “real part” above is redundant and the proof of Theorem
5.3
is completed.
∎

6
Conformal distortion

Here, we show that the Schwarzian action is also related to the change of area bounded by asymptotical circles under a conformal distortion.
Let
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}=4(1-|z|^{2})^{-2}|\mathrm{d}z|^{2}
denote the hyperbolic metric on the disk and let
\mathbb{S}_{r}:=\{re^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}:\theta\in[0,2\pi]\}
denote the standard foliation of the disk by circles. Let
K\subset\mathbb{D}
be compact, and suppose that
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\setminus K\to\mathbb{D}
is locally conformal such that the analytic function
\varphi\mid_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
sends the circle to itself (note however that we do not require
\varphi\mid_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
to be injective).

Let
\rho=\varphi^{\ast}(\rho_{\mathbb{D}})
be the pullback of the hyperbolic metric by
\varphi
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}
denote the length measure on
\mathbb{S}_{r}
for
\rho_{\mathbb{D}},\rho
respectively, and similarly let
k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}},k_{\rho}
denote the respective geodesic curvatures. Then

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}-\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg]=-\frac{2}{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\frac{2}{3}I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi).

(6.1)

By Remark
2.1
, we may interpret the derivatives of
\varphi
in the action as the complex derivative with respect to
z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}
. Note that Theorem
6.1
applies even when
\varphi
is not a circle homeomorphism, e.g. it applies to
\varphi(z)=z^{n}
for any
n\geq 1
. However, when
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a homeomorphism, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 6.2
.

Suppose that
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is quasiconformal with Beltrami coefficient supported in the compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\bigg[A(\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r}))-A(\mathbb{S}_{r})\bigg]=-\frac{2}{3}I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi),

(6.2)

where
A(\gamma)
denotes the hyperbolic area of the region enclosed by the curve
\gamma
.

Proof.

Let
\rho=\varphi^{*}(\rho_{\mathbb{D}})
. By the Gauss–Bonnet theorem,

\displaystyle 2\pi

\displaystyle=-A(\mathbb{S}_{r})+\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle 2\pi

\displaystyle=-A(\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r}))+\int_{\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r})}k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=-A(\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{r}))+\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}

Rearranging and applying Theorem
6.1
gives the result.
∎

Combined with Theorem
5.3
, we also obtain another corollary.

Corollary 6.3
.

Suppose that
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is an analytic homeomorphism that admits a quasiconformal extension
\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
with Beltrami coefficient compactly supported in
\mathbb{D}
. Let
\gamma
be the solution of the welding problem for
\varphi
, and let
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
be the equipotential foliation for
\gamma
for some conformal map
\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
,
\partial\Omega=\gamma
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\bigg\lvert_{\varepsilon=0}I^{L}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})=\frac{3}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bigg[\mathrm{Area}(\varphi(\mathbb{S}_{1-\varepsilon}))-\mathrm{Area}(\mathbb{S}_{1-\varepsilon})\bigg].

(6.3)

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Since
\varphi
is conformal on
\mathbb{D}\setminus K
, in this region the perturbed metric
\rho
differs from
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}
by a Weyl scaling, i.e., there is
\sigma
such that
\rho=\mathrm{e}^{2\sigma}\rho_{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
\rho=\varphi^{*}(\rho_{\mathbb{D}})
,
\sigma
is given explicitly by

\displaystyle\sigma(z)=\log\bigg[|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{1-|\varphi(z)|^{2}}\bigg].

(6.4)

Further we have that
\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}s
and that the geodesic curvatures
k_{\rho},k_{\rho_{0}}
of a curve
\gamma
are related by:

\displaystyle k_{\rho}=\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}(k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}}+\partial_{n_{0}}\sigma),

(6.5)

where
\partial_{n_{0}}
denotes the outer normal directional derivative to the curve
\gamma
. When
\gamma=\mathbb{S}_{r}
,
\partial_{n_{0}}=\partial_{r}
is the radial derivative. Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s_{\rho}-\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}k_{\rho_{\mathbb{D}}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}\partial_{r}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}s.

(6.6)

For
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, let
z_{r}=rz
denote a point on
\mathbb{S}_{r}
. Noting that
\partial_{r}=r^{-1}(z\partial_{z}+\overline{z}\partial_{\overline{z}})
, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\sigma(z_{r})

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\log|\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})|+\partial_{r}\log(1-r^{2})-\partial_{r}\log(1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z_{r})}{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})}\frac{z_{r}}{r}\bigg)-\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}+\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(z_{r}\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})\overline{\varphi(z_{r})})}{r(1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2})}.

(6.7)

A simple calculation shows that for
\mathbb{S}_{r}
,
\mathrm{d}s=2r(1-r^{2})^{-1}\mathrm{d}\theta
, for
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. With
z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta},z_{r}=rz
, the left hand side of (
6.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z_{r})}{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})}{z}\bigg)-\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}+\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(z\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})\overline{\varphi(z_{r})})}{(1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(6.8)

For
\theta
fixed, we expand the integrand around
z
. Note that
z_{r}-z=-(1-r)z
. It is further important to note that all three terms in the integrand are real-valued. For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z_{r})}{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})}z\bigg)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)-\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)-\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}\bigg)(1-r)

\displaystyle\quad+O((1-r)^{2}).

For the third term, we expand the numerator and denominator and then their ratio, using that both are real. To simplify both expressions, we repeatedly use that

|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

(6.9)

which follows from the fact that
\varphi
fixes
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. For the denominator of the third term, we compute

\displaystyle 1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2}=

\displaystyle|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|\bigg[2(1-r)-\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z+\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)\bigg)(1-r)^{2}

\displaystyle+\bigg(\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)+\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)\bigg)(1-r)^{3}+O((1-r)^{4})\bigg].

For the numerator, we find

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}(z\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})\overline{\varphi(z_{r})})=

\displaystyle|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|\bigg[2-\bigg(2\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z+2\,\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)\bigg)(1-r)

\displaystyle+\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)+3\,\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)\bigg)(1-r)^{2}\bigg]+O((1-r)^{3}).

Let
x=(1-r)
,
a=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z+\operatorname{Re}(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z)
and
b=\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Re}(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2})+\operatorname{Re}(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2})
. With this notation, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(z\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})\overline{\varphi(z_{r})})}{1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2}}=\frac{2-2ax+3bx^{2}+o(x^{2})}{2x-ax^{2}+bx^{3}+o(x^{3})}.

As the coefficients
a,b
are real-valued for all
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(z\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})\overline{\varphi(z_{r})})}{1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2}}=\frac{1}{x}-\frac{a}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\bigg(4b-a^{2}\bigg)x+o(x).

Note that
a^{2}=\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}+\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)
. Hence Plugging in
x,a,b
gives

\displaystyle\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(z\varphi^{\prime}(z_{r})\overline{\varphi(z_{r})})}{1-|\varphi(z_{r})|^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z+\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle+\bigg(\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}\bigg)(1-r)

\displaystyle+O((1-r)^{2}).

Collecting terms by order in
(1-r)
, the integrand in (
6.8
) is equal to

\displaystyle-\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}+\frac{1}{1-r}

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)

\displaystyle+\bigg[\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)+\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\bigg](1-r)+O((1-r)^{2}).

For the first line, we note

\displaystyle-\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}+\frac{1}{1-r}=\frac{1}{1+r}.

By (
6.9
),
\log|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|
can be extended to a holomorphic function in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. As such
\partial_{\theta}=\mathfrak{i}z\partial_{z}
and we get the relation

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\log|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|=\mathfrak{i}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z+1-\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg).

(6.10)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\pi.

Hence, the first two lines together contribute
\frac{1}{2(1+r)}(1-r)
to the integrand, and all remaining terms are of order
(1-r)
. Collecting like terms again, up to higher order terms, the integrand in (
6.8
) is
(1-r)
times:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(1+r)}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{3}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}\bigg)+\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}\bigg).

We note that

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z^{2}+\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}=-\frac{2}{3}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](z)z^{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular, this is already real-valued. We make two observations to show that the remaining terms contribute zero to the integral. First, we note that

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)=\mathfrak{i}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}+\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z-\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}\bigg).

Combined with the expression above for
\partial_{\theta}\log|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|
in (
6.10
), we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\;\mathrm{d}\theta=0.

Therefore under the integral,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2(1+r)}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z^{2}\bigg)-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z\bigg)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\operatorname{Re}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z-\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)-1\bigg)+\frac{1}{4(1+r)}(1-r)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

And in sum we have reduced the right side of (
6.1
) to the
r\to 1^{-}
limit of:

\displaystyle\frac{2r}{1+r}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z-\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)-1\bigg)-\frac{2}{3}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](z)z^{2}+O(1-r)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

Finally we note that
\log|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|
is a function from
\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, hence,
\partial_{\theta}\log|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|\in\mathbb{R}
. As such by (
6.10
),

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z-\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z-1\in\mathfrak{i}\mathbb{R}\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{S}^{1},

and hence
\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}z-\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}z\bigg)-1=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Further discussions

7.1
Dual Epstein curves in de Sitter space

Using the duality between hyperbolic and de Sitter spaces (see, for instance,
[
2
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
41
]
), we can describe the dual Epstein map in de Sitter space, which we now summarize briefly.

In Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
we consider
q(x)=-x_{0}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n-1}^{2}+x_{n}^{2}
, the canonical quadratic form of signature
(-,+,\ldots,+)
. Here, hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and de Sitter space
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
are represented by the submanifolds
\{x=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\,|\,q(x)=-1,x_{0}>0\}
and
\{x=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\,|\,q(x)=1\}
(respectively) with the metrics induced by the Lorentzian ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. Totally geodesic submanifolds of dimension
k
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
are given by their intersections with
k+1
linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. Moreover, an orientation in a
(k+1)
-space
V^{k+1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
is in one-to-one correspondence with an orientation on its intersection
P
with
\mathbb{H}^{n}
or
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
. More precisely, for
x\in V
with
q(x)=\pm 1
, which is identified with a point in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
or
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
, an ordered basis
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
in its orthogonal complement
x^{\perp}\subset V
is positive if and only if
x,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
is positive in
V
. This choice considers the position vector as the outer-normal to
\mathbb{H}^{n}
or
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. In particular, this choice gives an orientation of the tangent space of
P
at
x
, and
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
their standard orientations.

The duality is a one-to-one correspondence between
k
-planes in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
(n-k-1)
-planes in
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
. In fact, given an unoriented
k
-plane
P=\mathbb{H}^{n}\cap V^{k+1}
we define its
dual

P^{\perp}
as the unoriented
(n-k-1)
-plane
P^{\perp}=\operatorname{dS}_{n}\cap(V^{k+1})^{\perp}
, where
(V^{k+1})^{\perp}
is the
(n-k)
-dimensional orthogonal complement of
V^{k+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. Observe that by the same process we can define the dual of unoriented
k
-planes in
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
as unoriented
(n-k-1)
-planes in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. It is easy to verify that this process is an involution, and if
P\subset Q
are totally geodesic planes in either
\mathbb{H}^{n}
or
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
, then
P^{\perp}\supset Q^{\perp}
. Moreover, we can also define duality between oriented planes, under the convention that
V\oplus V^{\perp}
is a positive basis in
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
, where
V
defines the plane in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
while
V^{\perp}
defines the dual in
\operatorname{dS}_{n}
.

We now come back to the
n=2
case. Observe that given
h
a
C^{2}
metric in
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
, the Epstein curve
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
defines a natural map into the set of oriented geodesics in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Indeed, we can consider the geodesic line at
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
orthogonal to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
(defined in (
2.5
) and (
3.2
)) oriented so that the pair
(v,\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h})
(where
v
is the velocity of the geodesic at
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
) is positively oriented in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
In this way,
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
can be viewed as the
(2-1-1)
-plane (namely, point) in
\operatorname{dS}_{2}
dual to the geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
spanned by
v
, and the Epstein map
\mathbb{S}^{1}\to T^{1}\mathbb{H}^{2}
is an immersion
\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\operatorname{dS}_{2}
.

By definition of the duality, the oriented geodesic spanned by
v
is the dual of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
and we denote it as
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp}
. Under our orientation conventions, the basis
\operatorname{Ep}_{h},\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp},\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
has the standard orientation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 7.1
.

Given
h
a
C^{2}
metric in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we define its
dual Epstein curve
in
\operatorname{dS}_{2}
as
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Given
h
a
C^{2}
metric in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and any
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the tangent to the dual Epstein curve at
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\in\operatorname{dS}_{2}
is parallel to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
.

Proof.

As the dual Epstein curve is given by
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
, it suffices to prove that
\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
is orthogonal with respect to the quadratic form
q
to
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
and
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
. Denote by
\langle,\rangle
the Lorentzian inner product associated to
q
.

As
q(\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h})\equiv 1
it follows that
\langle\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h},\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}\rangle=0
. On the other hand, as
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp}
is parallel to
\partial_{\theta}\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
it follows that

\langle\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h},\operatorname{Ep}_{h}\rangle=-\langle\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h},\partial_{\theta}\operatorname{Ep}_{h}\rangle=0

which concludes the proof.
∎

By the previous Lemma we have that the tangent and normal lines of the dual Epstein curve
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}
are given by
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp}
and
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
, as at every point of the dual curve the position, tangent and normal vectors should be mutually orthogonal vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
with norms equal to
\pm 1
. In particular it follows from Lemma
7.2
that the dual Epstein curve is space-like, as its tangent vector is parallel to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp}
with
q(\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp})=1
.

Given Lemma
7.2
, we make the following choice of unit tangent and unit normal for the dual Epstein curve:

Definition 7.3
.

Given
h
a
C^{2}
metric in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. At any point
\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
we denote by
-\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}^{\perp}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
and
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
as the unit tangent and unit normal of the curve, respectively.

Finally, we will explain how to relate the total signed length and the total geodesic curvature for the Epstein curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and its dual in
\operatorname{dS}_{2}
. Similar results are well-known in arbitrary dimension, we include them here as they are simple to describe explicitly in this special case.

For the Epstein curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
, denote
x(\theta):=\operatorname{Ep}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
,
T(\theta):=\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}^{\perp}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
,
N(\theta):=\widetilde{\operatorname{Ep}}_{h}(e^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})
, which are respectively the position, oriented tangent and unit normal. As the geometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is induced by the geometry of
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
, it follows that we can compute the signed length
\mathrm{d}\ell
and its product with its geodesic curvature
k\,\mathrm{d}\ell
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell

\displaystyle=\langle x^{\prime},T\rangle,

\displaystyle k\,\mathrm{d}\ell

\displaystyle=\langle-N^{\prime},T\rangle

where
′
is the standard derivative in
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Lorentzian metric defined by the quadratic form
q
.

For the dual Epstein curve in
\operatorname{dS}_{2}
, our conventions for the orientation of position, unit tangent, and unit normal are given by
N
,
-T
, and
x
, respectively. It also follows that the signed length
\mathrm{d}\ell^{\perp}
and its product with its geodesic curvature
k^{\perp}\mathrm{d}\ell^{\perp}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell^{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\langle N^{\prime},-T\rangle=k\,\mathrm{d}\ell,

\displaystyle k^{\perp}\mathrm{d}\ell^{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\langle-x^{\prime},-T\rangle=\mathrm{d}\ell.

Hence, by Theorem
3.15
we can compute the Schwarzian action in terms of the total length and total curvature of the dual Epstein curve.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism. Let
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
be the pushforward of
\mathrm{d}\theta
by
\varphi
. Let
k^{\perp}(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
denote the total geodesic curvature of the dual Epstein curve associated with
h
and
L^{\perp}(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})
the signed de Sitter length of the dual Epstein curve, then we have

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=k^{\perp}(\operatorname{Ep}_{h})=2\pi-L^{\perp}(\operatorname{Ep}_{h}).

7.2
Piecewise Möbius circle homeomorphisms

In this section, we show an extension of Theorem
1.1
to circle homeomorphisms of lower regularity, in the special case of piecewise Möbius circle diffeomorphisms.

Definition 7.5
.

A map
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a
C^{1}

piecewise Möbius
circle diffeomorphism, if
\varphi
is continuously differentiable and there exists
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
in counterclockwise order, such that the restriction of
\varphi
to the circular arc
[z_{j},z_{j+1}]
is in
\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
. We call the points
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
the breakpoints of
\varphi
.

We note that a
C^{1}
piecewise Möbius circle diffeomorphism is automatically
C^{1,1}
, that is, its derivative is Lipschitz.
This class of circle diffeomorphisms has been considered as a finite-dimensional approximation of the universal Teichmüller space, and can be described using finite shears and diamond shears
[
40
,
36
]
. They are also related to various optimization problems for the Loewner energy
[
39
,
10
,
53
]
.

We can extend the definition of the Schwarzian derivative to piecewise Möbius circle diffeomorphisms using the distributional derivative. For
z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, recall that
\varphi^{\prime}(z)
is defined so that
\mathfrak{i}z\varphi^{\prime}(z)=\varphi_{\theta}(z)
, where
\varphi_{\theta}
is the derivative of
\varphi
with respect to the argument of
z
. The Schwarzian is
\mathcal{S}[\varphi](z)=(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime}(z))^{\prime}-(1/2)(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime})^{2}
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose that
\varphi
is
C^{1}
piecewise Möbius with breakpoints
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
in counterclockwise order. In the sense of distributions,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\delta_{z_{j}},

where

\lambda_{j}=-\mathfrak{i}z_{j}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}(z_{j}+)-\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}(z_{j}-)\right)=\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(z_{j}-)-\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(z_{j}+)\in\mathbb{R}.

We therefore define the Schwarzian action of
\varphi
by

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{j}
denote the argument of
z_{j}
. First note that since
\partial_{\theta}=\mathfrak{i}z\partial_{z}
,

\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}(z)=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{i}z}\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}-\frac{1}{z}.

Since
\varphi
is piecewise Möbius, for any test function
g
and any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(\theta)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\theta_{j}-\varepsilon}^{\theta_{j}+\varepsilon}g(\theta)e^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\theta_{j}-\varepsilon}^{\theta_{j}+\varepsilon}g(\theta)\bigg(-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\bigg(\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

Integrating by parts, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\theta_{j}-\varepsilon}^{\theta_{j}+\varepsilon}g(\theta)\bigg(-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\bigg(\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g(\theta)\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\bigg\lvert_{\theta=\theta_{j}-\varepsilon}^{\theta=\theta_{j}+\varepsilon}+\int_{\theta_{j}-\varepsilon}^{\theta_{j}+\varepsilon}g_{\theta}(\theta)\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})+g(\theta)\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Since
\varphi_{\theta\theta}/\varphi_{\theta}=\mathfrak{i}z(\varphi^{\prime\prime}/\varphi^{\prime})
is piecewise smooth, taking
\varepsilon\to 0
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(\theta)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathfrak{i}\theta}\mathcal{S}[\varphi](\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g(\theta_{j})\bigg(\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta_{j}}-)-\frac{\varphi_{\theta\theta}}{\varphi_{\theta}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{i}\theta_{j}}+)\bigg).

This completes the proof since
g
is arbitrary.
∎

For
\varphi
which is
C^{1}
piecewise Möbius with breakpoints
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
, consider the metric
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
. The horocycles
(H_{\varphi(z)})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
corresponding to
h
are well-defined since
\varphi
is
C^{1}
, but since
\varphi
is not
C^{2}
, the image of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
in
\mathbb{D}
is disconnected. These both, however, have simple explicit descriptions, from which we can see that there is a natural way to construct an Epstein curve
\Sigma_{h}
which extends the image of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
.

Note that in previous sections, where we work with
C^{2}
metrics, we have interchangeably used
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
to denote both the map and its connected image curve
\Sigma_{h}
. In this section we use
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
to refer specifically to the map and its image, and
\Sigma_{h}
to refer to the curve we construct which extends the image of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
.

To construct
\Sigma_{h}
, let
(H_{z}^{0})_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denote the horocycles for the identity map and let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
be the Möbius transformations such that
\varphi\mid_{[z_{j},z_{j+1}]}=\alpha_{j}
. By Lemma
2.10
, we have

•

for any
z\in[z_{j},z_{j+1}]
,
H_{\varphi(z)}=\alpha_{j}(H_{z}^{0})
;

•

the image of
\operatorname{Ep}_{h}
is the collection of points
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}
, where
a_{j}=\alpha_{j}(0)
.

Since
\varphi
is
C^{1}
, for each breakpoint
z_{j}
, the horocycle
H_{\varphi(z_{j})}\ni\{a_{j-1},a_{j}\}
. We define
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
to be the arc of
H_{\varphi(z_{j})}
from
a_{j-1}
to
a_{j}
(where we identify
a_{-1}=a_{n}
), and define the Epstein curve for
\varphi
to be

\Sigma_{h}:=\Sigma_{h}^{1}\cup\dots\cup\Sigma_{h}^{n}.

See Figure
6
for an example. We parametrize
\Sigma_{j}
by arclength, oriented from
a_{j-1}
to
a_{j}
, and let
T_{j}
denote its unit tangent vector. Let
N_{j}
be the outward pointing unit normal vector to
H_{\varphi(z_{j})}\supset\Sigma_{h}^{j}
, and let
\beta_{j}
be the angle between
N_{j},N_{j+1}
at
a_{j}
(we describe these in Lemma
7.8
below). We define the signed arclength
\mathrm{d}\ell_{j}
along
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
to be standard arclength times the sign of the orientation of the basis
(T_{j},N_{j})
. In particular, if
a_{j}
is clockwise after
a_{j-1}
along
H_{\varphi(z)}
then the sign is positive (respectively if
a_{j}
is counterclockwise after
a_{j-1}
along
H_{\varphi(z)}
then the sign is negative).

Figure 6:
Let
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
,
\varphi
piecewise Möbius with breakpoints
1,\mathfrak{i},-1,-\mathfrak{i}
. Here are its horocycles in increments of
\pi/25
and completed Epstein curve
\Sigma_{h}
(left) and its horocycles at
z_{j}=1,\mathfrak{i},-1,-\mathfrak{i}
, the intersection points
a_{j}=\alpha_{j}(0)
, and the angles
\beta_{j}
between the outward normal vectors to the horocycles at
a_{j}
(right).

We now give a geometric interpretation of the “jumps”
\lambda_{j}
at the breakpoints of
\varphi
, which appear in Lemma
7.6
, as the signed length of the horocyclic arcs
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\ell_{j}
denote the signed arclength measure along
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
. The total signed length is

L(\Sigma_{h}^{j})=\int_{\Sigma_{h}^{j}}\mathrm{d}\ell_{j}=\lambda_{j}.

Proof.

We compute the signed hyperbolic distance along
H_{\varphi(z_{j})}
from
a_{j-1}=\alpha_{j-1}(0)
to
a_{j}=\alpha_{j}(0)
; note that this is Möbius invariant. Define
\alpha:=\alpha_{j-1}^{-1}\circ\alpha_{j}
. By Lemma
2.10
, it therefore suffices to compute the signed hyperbolic distance along
H_{z_{j}}^{0}
from
0
to
\alpha(0)
. By construction,
\alpha(z_{j})=z_{j}
,
\alpha^{\prime}(z_{j})=1
, and
-\mathfrak{i}z_{j}\alpha^{\prime\prime}(z_{j})=\lambda_{j}
. These three conditions imply that
\alpha
is determined uniquely by
z_{j},\lambda_{j}
and given by the formula:

\displaystyle\alpha(z)=\frac{(2\mathfrak{i}+\lambda_{j})z_{j}z-z_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j}z+(2\mathfrak{i}-\lambda_{j})z_{j}}.

Let
\mathfrak{c}_{j}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
be the Cayley map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{j}(z)=\mathfrak{i}\frac{1+z/z_{j}}{1-z/z_{j}}.

This Cayley map sends
H_{z_{j}}^{0}
to the horocycle at
\infty
through
\mathfrak{i}=\mathfrak{c}_{j}(0)
, i.e., the horizontal line
\{y=1\}
, and
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
to the segment of the horizontal line from
\mathfrak{i}
to
\mathfrak{c}_{j}(\alpha(0))
. Thus,

\displaystyle L(\Sigma_{j})=-\text{Re}(\mathfrak{c}_{j}(\alpha(0))).

The sign comes from considering orientation; the outward normal vector to the horocycle at
\infty
is parallel to
(0,-1)
, so the signed arclength is positive when the horocycle is oriented clockwise. We compute

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{j}\circ\alpha(z)=\frac{-z(\lambda_{j}+\mathfrak{i})-z_{j}(\mathfrak{i}-\lambda_{j})}{z-z_{j}}

from which we see that
\mathfrak{c}_{j}(\alpha(0))=\mathfrak{i}-\lambda_{j}
. Therefore,
L(\Sigma_{h}^{j})=\lambda_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
\varphi
be
C^{1}
piecewise Möbius with breakpoints
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
. The horocycles
H_{\varphi(z_{j})},H_{\varphi(z_{j+1})}
intersect at
a_{j}
, and the angle
\beta_{j}
between their outward pointing normal vectors
N_{j},N_{j+1}
at
a_{j}
(see Figure
6
) equals the Lebesgue measure of the circular arc
[z_{j},z_{j+1}]
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{j}=\varphi\mid_{[z_{j},z_{j+1}]}\in\operatorname{PSU}(1,1)
. Since
\varphi
is
C^{1}
,
\varphi^{\prime}(z_{j})=\alpha_{j}^{\prime}(z_{j})
and
\varphi^{\prime}(z_{j+1})=\alpha_{j}^{\prime}(z_{j+1})
. Hence, by Lemma
2.10
,
H_{\varphi(z_{j})}=\alpha_{j}(H_{z_{j}}^{0})
and
H_{\varphi(z_{j+1})}=\alpha_{j}(H_{z_{j+1}}^{0})
. Since
H_{z_{j}}^{0},H_{z_{j+1}}^{0}
intersect at
0
, these intersect at
a_{j}=\alpha_{j}(0)
. Since
\alpha_{j}
is conformal, the angle of intersection between
N_{j},N_{j+1}
at
a_{j}
is the same as the angle of between the normal vectors
N_{j}^{0},N_{j+1}^{0}
to
H_{z_{j}}^{0},H_{z_{j+1}}^{0}
respectively at
0
. These are the horocycles based at
z_{j},z_{j+1}
through
0
respectively, and hence the angle between their outward normal vectors at
0
is given by the arclength of
[z_{j},z_{j+1}]
.
∎

We combine the above with the Gauss–Bonnet theorem to obtain an analog of Theorem
1.1
for
C^{1}
piecewise Möbius circle diffeomorphisms.

Corollary 7.9
.

Let
\varphi
be a
C^{1}
piecewise Möbius circle diffeomorphism with breakpoints
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
in counterclockwise order, and define the metric
h=\varphi_{*}\mathrm{d}\theta
and its completed Epstein curve
\Sigma_{h}
as above. Then

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=L(\Sigma_{h})=-A(\Sigma_{h}).

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
7.7
that
I_{\operatorname{Sch}}(\varphi)=L(\Sigma_{h})
. For each
j
, let
\beta_{j}
be the angle between the outward normal vectors
N_{j},N_{j+1}
to
H_{\varphi(z_{j})}
,
H_{\varphi(z_{j+1})}
respectively as in Lemma
7.8
. By the Gauss–Bonnet theorem,

\displaystyle-A(\Sigma_{h})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Sigma_{h}^{j}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}=2\pi.

By Lemma
7.8
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}=2\pi
. For each
j
,
\Sigma_{j}
is a piece of a horocycle with outward pointing normal vector. In Example
3.7
we saw that the horocycle with
inward
pointing normal vector has
k\equiv 1
. Since
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
is oriented with the outward pointing normal vector, for all
j
,
\Sigma_{h}^{j}
has geodesic curvature
k\equiv-1
. Hence altogether,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Sigma_{h}^{j}}k\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{j}=-L(\Sigma_{h})
, and rearranging the equation completes the proof.
∎
