A note on the a.e. second-order differentiability of rank-one convex functions
Jonas Hirsch

Abstract
. In the Euclidean setting, the well-known Alexandrov theorem states that convex functions are twice differentiable almost everywhere. In this note, we extend this theorem to rank-one convex functions. Our approach is novel in that it draws more from viscosity techniques developed in the context of fully nonlinear elliptic equations. As a byproduct, the original Alexandrov theorem can essentially be reduced to the a.e. differentiability of one-dimensional monotone functions, as presented in the appendix.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this very short note is to show that rank-one convex functions are a.e. second order differentiable:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
f\colon B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, rank-one convex function, then
f
is second order differentiable for a.e.
x\in B_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

Let us shortly recall that a function
f\colon B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is called rank-one convex if its restrictions to line segments contained in
B_{1}
in rank-one directions are convex i.e.

t\mapsto f(x+t(a\otimes b))\text{ is convex }\forall x\in B_{1},a\in\mathbb{R}^{m},b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ on the interval of all $t$ with }|t||a||b|<\dist(x,\partial B_{1})\,.

Remark 1.1
.

With a few minor modifications to Lemma
2.1
, the same conclusion can be obtained for separate convex functions. This result may seem surprising at first due to the work of Conti, Faraco, Maggi and Müller
[
1
]
and Kirchheim and Kristensen
[
3
]
. This shows that almost everywhere second-order differentiability is not related to whether the Hessian is a measure.

2.
proof

Ingredient 1:
f
has a paraboloid tangent from above a.e. in
B_{1}

Since for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and any
i=1,\dotsc,m,j=1,\dotsc,n
one has
x_{ij}=(e_{i}\otimes e_{j})\colon x
, the rank-one convexity implies that in the weak and viscosity sense

\partial^{2}_{x_{ij}}f(x)=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}|_{t=0}f(x+t(e_{i}\otimes e_{j}))\geq 0\,.

Hence we deduce that
f
is sub-harmonic in the viscosity sense i.e.

\Delta f(x)\geq 0\,.

Remark 2.1
.

The proposition also holds true in the case of
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\operatorname{sym}}
. In this case, the rank-one directions are taken as
r_{ij}=(e_{i}+e_{j})\otimes(e_{i}+e_{j})
for
i,j=\{1,\dots,n\}
. These directions provide a basis since
2\operatorname{sym}(e_{i}\otimes e_{j})=r_{ij}-r_{ii}-r_{jj}
. We can now replace the Laplacian with the following two-homogeneous elliptic coefficient operator:

Lf(x)=\sum_{i,j}\partial^{2}_{r_{ij}}f(x)=\sum_{(kl),(mn)}a^{(kl)(mn)}\partial_{x_{kl}\,x_{mn}}f(x),

where
a=\sum_{i,j}r_{ij}\otimes r_{ij}
.

The statements concerning the viscosity can be verified by mollification since rank-one convexity is preserved under mollification and viscosity solutions are closed under uniform convergence.

Thus, we can apply Lin’s “classical” interior
W^{2,\epsilon}
estimate for viscosity subsolutions to fully nonlinear uniformly elliptic equations, e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
originally
[
4
]
,

(2.1)

\left|\left\{\overline{\Theta}_{f}>C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}t\right\}\cap B_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right|\lesssim t^{-\epsilon}\,.

To clarify the used notation in the above estimate: One says that a paraboloid
P
has opening
A
if
D^{2}P=A\,I
. For a continuous function
u\in C(\overline{\Omega})
one sets
\overline{\Theta}_{u}(x)
to be the smallest
a\geq 0
such that a paraboloid of opening
a
is tangent from above to
u
in
\Omega
at
x
i.e. there is
P
with
D^{2}P=aI
such that
P\geq u
in
\overline{\Omega}
and
u(x)=P(x)
. In the inequality above we have taken the domain to be
\overline{B}_{1}
.

Ingredient 2: upper bound implies lower bound

In this section we want to show the following Lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

There is a dimensional constant
C=C(n)
such that if a rank-one convex function
f
satisfies
f(x_{0})=0,Df(x_{0})=0
and there is a non-decreasing
g\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that
G(x)=g(|x-x_{0}|)
is tangent from above to
f
in
\Omega
at
x_{0}
then

(2.2)

f(x)\geq-C\,G(x)\quad\forall x\in\Omega\,.

Proof.

Passing to
x\mapsto f(x_{0}+x)
and
\Omega_{x_{0}}=\Omega-x_{0}
we may assume that
x_{0}=0
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be given, let
x_{i}
be the matrix of the first
i
th columns of
x
i.e.
x_{i}=(x_{\cdot 1},\dotsc,x_{\cdot i},0\dotsc 0)
. Furthermore we define the rank-one matrices build by the
i
th column
d_{i}=x_{\cdot i}\otimes e_{i}
. Hence we have
x_{i}=\frac{1}{2}x_{i+1}+\frac{1}{2}y_{i+1}
, where
y_{i}=x_{i}-2d_{i}
. Thus the rank-one convexity of
f
implies that

2f(x_{i})\leq f(x_{i+1})+f(y_{i})\leq f(x_{i+1})+G(x)\,,

using for the second inequality that
f\leq G
and the monotonicity of
g
together with
|y_{i}|=|x_{i}|\leq|x|
for all
i
.

Finally, one has
f(x_{1})\geq f(0)+Df(0)x_{1}\geq 0
since
x_{1}
is a rank-one matrix. Thus we can use the above inequality to deduce (
2.2
) inductively, since
x_{n}=x
.
∎

Proof of the Proposition.

Recall that every rank-one convex function is locally Lipschitz, with the quantitative estimate

(2.3)

\operatorname{Lip}(f,B_{r}(x))\leq n\frac{\operatorname{osc}(f,B_{2r}(x))}{r}\,.

Due to Rademacher’s theorem
f
is therefore a.e. differentiable i.e. there
N\subset B_{2}
with
|N|=0
and
f
is differentiable on
B_{2}\setminus N
with
|Df(x)|\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
for all
x\in(B_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\setminus N)
.

Step 1:
f_{x_{ij}}=\partial_{x_{ij}}f
can be touched from above and below by a cone with an opening of
CA
on the set
\Omega_{A}=\{\overline{\Theta}_{f}\leq A\}\cap(B_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\setminus N)

Given
x_{0}\in\Omega_{A}
we consider the rank-one convex function
\tilde{f}=f-f(x_{0})-Df(x_{0})(x-x_{0})
. By construction
\tilde{f}(x_{0})=0,D\tilde{f}(x_{0})=0

and since
x_{0}\in\Omega_{A}
we have
\tilde{f}(x)\leq\frac{A}{2}|x-x_{0}|^{2}
on
B_{1}
. In particular, this implies that the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
are satisfied with
g(t)=\frac{A}{2}t^{2}
.
Hence
\lVert\tilde{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\leq CAr^{2}
for all
r<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
Combining it with (
2.3
) we deduce for any
x\in B_{1}
with
r=|x-x_{0}|<\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
that

|Df(x)-Df(x_{0})|=|D\tilde{f}(x)|\leq\operatorname{Lip}(\tilde{f},\overline{B}_{r}(x_{0}))\leq C\frac{\lVert\tilde{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r}(x_{0}))}}{r}\leq CA\,r\,.

Step 2:
f_{x_{ij}}
is differentiable a.e. in
\Omega_{A}

The following argument is our version of Maly’s beautiful argument
[
5
]
, which unfortunately we only obtained afterwards. However, as his article is difficult to obtain, we have decided to present our version nonetheless. Our version uses a sub- and super-convolution that is closer to the one used by Jensen
[
2
]
.

We consider the sup- and inf-convolution with cones i.e. we define on
B_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
for
L=2C\max\{A,\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\}

\displaystyle w_{x_{ij}}^{-}(x)=\inf\{f_{x_{ij}}(y)+L|x-y|\colon y\in B_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}\setminus N\}

\displaystyle w_{x_{ij}}^{+}(x)=\sup\{f_{x_{ij}}(y)-L|x-y|\colon y\in B_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}\setminus N\}\,.

Firstly, we note that
\inf,\sup
are essentially taken over
|x-y|<\frac{1}{4}
, since
|y-x|\geq\frac{1}{4}
by our choice of
L
and the Lipschitz estimate, (
2.3
), we have for every
x\in B_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\setminus N
that

f_{x_{ij}}(x)\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}<f_{x_{ij}}(y)+L|x-y|\,.

Hence the infimum in
w_{x_{ij}}^{-}
can be taken only over
|y-x|<\frac{1}{4}
as claimed. Secondly, its not difficult to see that
w_{x_{ij}}^{-}\leq f_{x_{ij}}\leq w_{x_{ij}}^{+}
and that
w_{x_{ij}}^{\pm}
are
L
-Lipschitz continuous. Furthermore, Step 1 implies that
w_{x_{ij}}^{-}=f_{x_{ij}}=w_{x_{ij}}^{+}
on
\Omega_{A}
. Hence
w_{i}^{\pm}
for a.e. are differntiable
x_{0}\in\Omega_{A}
but since they are ordered their differential must agree, i.e.
Dw_{i}^{\pm}(x_{0})=M
for some
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. This implies the differentiablity of
f_{x_{ij}}
in
x_{0}
, again because they are ordered
w_{x_{ij}}^{-}\leq f_{x_{ij}}\leq w_{x_{ij}}^{+}
.

Step 3:
f
is second-order differentiable at a.e. point of
\Omega_{A}
.

Let
x_{0}\in\Omega_{A}
be a point in which all
f_{x_{ij}}
are differentiable, then for any
z
with
|z|\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
we have by the fundamental theorem of calculus, which is valid for Lipschitz functions: using Einstein summation we have

\displaystyle f(x_{0}+z)-(f(x_{0})+Df(x_{0})z+\frac{1}{2}Df_{x_{ij}}(x_{0})z_{ij}z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(f_{x_{ij}}(x_{0}+sz)-f_{x_{ij}}(x_{0})-Df_{x_{ij}}(x_{0})\right)z_{x_{ij}}(sz)\,ds

\displaystyle=o(|z|^{2})\,.

∎

Appendix A
A measure theoretic argument for the convex case

Alexandrov’s original theorem can essentially be reduced to two observations. Firstly, the one-dimensional situation can be reduced to the fact that monotone functions are differentiable almost everywhere (a result already discovered by Lebesgue). Secondly, the convex hull of the set
\{\pm he_{j}\colon j=1,\dotsc,n\}
contains the open ball
B_{\nicefrac{{h}}{{\sqrt{n}}}}
.

More precisely, we use a quantitative version of the almost everywhere differentiability of monotone functions — which was probably also known to Lebesgue — and the second observation to derive (
2.1
) with
\epsilon=1
. In the following,
Q_{r}(x)
denotes the cube
x+Q_{r}
, where
Q_{r}=[-r,r]^{n}
.

Measure theoretic ingredient:
Given a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
we consider the associated maximal function i.e.

M\mu(x)=\sup\left\{\frac{\mu(I)}{|I|}\colon x\in I=(a,b)\right\}\,.

The classical maximal-function estimate/ Lebesgue differentiation estimate states

|\{M\mu>t\}|\lesssim\frac{\mu(\mathbb{R})}{t}.

Applying the above to the truncated measure
\tilde{\mu}=\mu\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits[-2,2]
gives a localized version: for every
t>\mu[-2,2]
and any interval
I
with
\mu(I)>t|I|
we have
|I|<1
hence we deduce that

|\{M(\mu)>t\}\cap[-1,1]|\leq|\{M\tilde{\mu}>t\}|\lesssim\frac{\mu[-2,2]}{t}

Application to the Hessian of a convex function:

Let
f^{\prime\prime}
be the Radon measure provided by the second derivative of a convex function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
. then we have

f^{\prime\prime}[-2,2]=f^{\prime}(2)-f^{\prime}(-2)\leq 2\operatorname{osc}(f,[-3,3])\,.

Furthermore, if
f(0)
with
0\in\partial f(0)
for instance by approximation with mollification, one extends the classical Taylor approximation to

(A.1)

0\leq f(h)\leq f^{\prime\prime}[0,h]h\leq Mf^{\prime\prime}(0)\,h^{2}\text{ and }0\leq f(-h)\leq f^{\prime\prime}[-h,0]h\leq Mf^{\prime\prime}(0)\,h^{2}\quad\forall h>0\,.

Derivation of (
2.1
):
For a fixed direction
e_{i}
and
y\in e_{i}^{\perp}
we may consider the convex function
s\mapsto f_{y}(s)=f(y+se_{i})
and the associated set
E_{y}=\{Mf_{y}^{\prime\prime}>t\}\cap[-1,1]
. From the above, we have

|E_{y}|\lesssim t^{-1}\operatorname{osc}(f_{y},[-3,3])\,.

Hence, for each
t>2\operatorname{osc}(f,Q_{3})
we can combine them to
E_{t}=N\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\subset Q_{1}
, where
E_{i}=\bigcup_{y\in e_{i}^{\perp}\cap Q_{1}}E_{y}
and
N
is the set of measure zero where
f
is not differentiable. Using Fubini, we can estimate its measure from above by

|E\cup N\cap Q_{1}|\lesssim\frac{\operatorname{osc}(f,Q_{3})}{t}\,.

It remains to show that for
t>2\operatorname{osc}(f,Q_{3})

\{\overline{\Theta}_{f}>4nt\}\cap Q_{1}\subset E_{t}\,.

Since
f
is differentiable in
x_{0}
for any given
x_{0}\in E_{t}
we may consider

\tilde{f}(x)=f(x_{0}+x)-f(x_{0})-Df(x_{0})x\,.

Again, since
x_{0}\in E_{t}
we can apply (
A.1
) to
s\mapsto\tilde{f}(se_{i})
for a fixed direction
e_{i}
to deduce that

0\leq\tilde{f}(he_{i})\leq 2t\,h^{2}\quad\forall|h|<1\,.

Furthermore, for any
x\in K_{h}=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm he_{i}\colon i=1\dotsc n\}
we deduce appropriate
0\leq\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}\leq 1
that
0\leq\tilde{f}(x)\leq\sum_{i}\left(\lambda_{i}f(he_{i})+\mu_{i}f(-he_{i})\right)\leq 2t\,h^{2}
. But since
B_{\nicefrac{{h}}{{\sqrt{n}}}}\subset K_{h}
we deduce the claim.
