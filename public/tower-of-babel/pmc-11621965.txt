Long‐Term Success of Teeth Undergoing Intentional Replantation: A Report of Two Cases
Authors: Abdulaziz Bakhsh, Ghada Eisa, Hanen Hodhan, Emna Sakly, Fouad Abduljabbar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntentional replantation has been shown to be a viable treatment option for failed endodontic cases with long‐term success rates.Keywords: atraumatic extraction, endodontic treatment failures, intentional reimplantation, root‐end restoration

Introduction

Intentional tooth replantation (IR) is defined by Grossman as the purposeful removal of a tooth and its reinsertion into the socket almost immediately after sealing the apical foramina [1]. IR can be an alternative treatment option for teeth with poor prognosis, where nonsurgical retreatment is impractical and surgical treatment is not possible [2, 3].

Several studies advocated that IR should be considered as a “last resort” for the retreatment of very complex cases, where conventional surgical or nonsurgical retreatment are not recommended or have previously failed [4, 5]. Therefore, the primary outcome goal of IR is the retention of the tooth [1], when considered as an alternative to extraction and prosthetic replacement [6]. However, IR is contraindicated in teeth with flared and curved roots that have a high risk of fracture during extraction, nonrestorable teeth, and vertically fractured teeth [3].

IR is believed to be an economical and conventional technique, and also suitable for less complex cases, because it is an easier, more rapid to perform, and less costly procedure, with a good outcome [3].

The procedure of IR involves several clinical considerations. These considerations are divided into pretreatment, during treatment, and after treatment considerations [7]. According to the European Society of Endodontology position statement for teeth undergoing surgical extrusion, intentional replantation, and tooth autotransplantation, the clinician should consider taking a preoperative CBCT imaging to have full anatomical consideration. Furthermore, systemic antibiotics should be prescribed when indicated, and the oral cavity of the patient should be locally debrided from plaque or calculus [7]. During the IR procedure, the tooth should be extracted atraumatically to avoid fracture. Following the atraumatic extraction, the root‐end resection should be executed, and a retrograde filling should be placed. Finally, postoperative consideration of teeth which have undergone IR includes splinting of teeth, occlusal check, and prescription of medication if necessary [7].

Observational studies in IR reported low complication rates and high overall mid‐ to long‐term survival [8]. Recently, a clinical study on IR has reported that the long‐term survival rates of teeth which underwent IR is 77% [9]. However, according to recent systematic reviews and meta‐analyses of IR, the survival rate is around 90%. This rate decreases slightly in the first year but does not change within 3–4 years. At 4 years, the cumulative tooth survival rate is 82.8% [10].

Thus, the objective of this case series was to provide a scientific basis to perform IR, illustrating the possibility of IR to be an alternative option to extraction for teeth with poor prognosis.

Case History

A total of two patients were referred to the Department of Endodontics at King Abdulaziz medical city, National Guard Hospital, Jeddah (average age, 35.5 years). Both patients had no contributing medical history. The cases were premolars or molars with posttreatment disease diagnosed with apical periodontitis. Proper medical and dental history were reviewed. Subsequently, an extraoral examination, intraoral examination, and radiographic examination were carried out.

After diagnosis, all possible treatment options were discussed with the patients including, (i) retreatment and coronal restoration, (ii) extraction with/without replacement, (iii) periradicular surgery, (iv) intentional replantation. The patients chose IR with its associated risks and benefits. Written informed consents were signed, and the patients were instructed to rinse their mouth with chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% 1 h before the procedure.

Case 1

A healthy 33‐year‐old female patient presented to the endodontic clinic complaining of severe pain related to lower posterior left tooth pointing at tooth number 36 and a swelling with slight discomfort related to the last tooth in the same quadrant (tooth number 37). The patient reported that the swelling had started around 2 months ago. Medically, the patient was fit and healthy.

Regarding dental history, root canal treatment (RCT) for tooth number 37 had been done in the same hospital by an endodontist 4 years before and pulpectomy for tooth number 36 had been done 7 months before. Extraoral examination was carried out, showing no remarkable signs. Intraoral examination revealed a moderate swelling located coronally related to tooth number 37 with a deep, narrow, isolated periodontal pocket in the mid‐buccal surface of the tooth (10 mm) (Figure 1a). The tooth had a full porcelain fused to metal crown. The radiographic examination showed an apical radiolucency related to both teeth (number 36 and number 37) with furcal radiolucency related to tooth number 37 (Figure 1b). The diagnosis was determined as previously initiated tooth with chronic periapical abscess for tooth number 36, and previously treated tooth with chronic apical abscess for tooth number 37. However, based on Simon et al. (1972), the tooth is differentially diagnosed as primary endodontic secondary periodontal involvement [11].

The patient was informed that tooth number 37 had a poor prognosis of a conventional retreatment and was given the following alternative treatment options: extraction and a dental implant, extraction with no replacement, or intentional replantation. The patient rejected the first two treatment options because she wanted to save the tooth and was determined to maintain the tooth by any means. She ultimately opted for IR with its associated risks and benefits. Written informed consent was signed from the patient. The tooth was intentionally extracted and carefully inspected under an operating microscope (Proergo; ZEISS, Germany). No obvious cracks or fractures were noticed (Figure 1c–e). A strip perforation in the distal wall of the mesial root was identified (Figure 1d,f). The apices were resected, and root‐end cavities and perforation were sealed with EndoSequence Root Repair Material (BC RRM‐Fast Set Putty) (Figure 1g). The tooth was then replanted into its socket 10 min after extraction (Figure 1h).

Case 2

A 38‐year‐old healthy male patient was referred to the endodontic clinic. He suffered from pain when chewing related to the lower posterior right tooth (tooth number 47). Medical history was unremarkable. Dental history revealed that RCT for tooth number 47 had been previously done 3 years before. Extraoral examination was carried out, showing no remarkable signs. Intraoral examination revealed that the tooth was tilted and restored with composite restoration.

An orthodontist and a prosthodontist were consulted regarding the condition of the tooth and the treatment options. They concluded that extraction and implant is not preferred in this case due to complicated occlusion and overeruption of opposing first and second molars. The patient is adapted to this occlusion. Therefore, it is preferred to maintain the current situation. The radiographic examination showed fused roots with an apical radiolucency related to tooth number 47 (Figure 2a). The pulpal diagnosis for tooth number 47 was previously treated and the apical diagnosis was symptomatic apical periodontitis. However, based on Simon et al. (1972), the tooth is differentially diagnosed as primary endodontic secondary periodontal involvement [11].

Case 2; (a) preoperative radiograph; (b) tooth following extraction; (c) tooth following retrograde preparation; (d) tooth following retrograde filling; (e) postoperative radiograph.

Different treatment options were explained to the patient including endodontic retreatment, apical surgery, IR, and tooth extraction with/without replacement. After a risk and benefits discussion of each treatment option, the patient opted for the intentional atraumatic extraction and replantation. Written informed consent was signed from the patient.

Treatment protocol was followed as described below. The apex was resected and inspected under high magnification (×20, ×26). C‐shaped root canal configuration (isthmus) was evident under the microscope. Root‐end cavities were prepared and sealed with MTA (ProRoot MTA, White, Dentsply Sirona) (Figure 2b–d).

Methods

Clinical Procedure

Profound local anesthesia was achieved using 2% lidocaine containing 1:100,000 epinephrine (Xylocaine dental adrenaline 20 mg/mL, 1.8 mL/carpoule, DENTSPLY). The teeth were intentionally and atraumatically extracted with gentle luxation using forceps. The patients were asked to bite on a wet gauze while working on the tooth extraorally. The root surface was untouched to prevent periodontal damage. Root‐end resection was followed by root‐end preparation using ultrasonic tips to a depth of 3 mm and parallel to the long access of the root. Root‐end cavities were filled using bioceramic cement, EndoSequence Root Repair Material (BC RRM‐Fast Set Putty, Brasseler, GA, USA) or MTA (ProRoot MTA, White, Dentsply Sirona, NC, USA). The socket was irrigated with sterile saline solution, and the tooth was then replanted into its socket. Extraoral time was kept minimum (less than 15 min). All procedures were done under an operating microscope (Proergo; ZEISS, Germany). Clinical and radiographic follow‐up was done for all cases. The mean follow‐up time was 5.5 years.

Conclusion and Follow‐Up

Overall, IR presents an alternative treatment for the management of teeth that are periodontally involved. This conservative and economical procedure promotes tooth preservation by providing healthy gums, a significant reduction in pocket depth, and new bone formation. However, the prognosis of this treatment depends on several pre‐ and perioperative factors. When performed in accordance with contemporary apical microsurgery knowledge and the biological basis of dental trauma, this technique maximizes healing potential, reduces the risk of developing complications, and increases the functionality of affected teeth.

Case 1

The patient was recalled 1 week after the treatment. The tooth was asymptomatic with normal soft tissues. The patient was recalled again 3 months (Figure 1i), 6 months (Figure 1j), 18 months (Figure 1j), 3 years (Figure 1k), and 6 years after the procedure. 6 years after treatment, the patient was asymptomatic and the tooth was not tender to percussion, the probing depth was within normal limits (2–4 mm), tooth was grade zero mobile, and complete healing of the periapical lesion was evident (Figure 1l).

Case 2

Seven days after the procedure, the tooth was asymptomatic with normal soft tissues. The patient was recalled at 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and yearly up to 5 years after the procedure (Figure 2e). At those periods of follow‐up, clinical and radiographic examinations revealed complete healing, and the patient was asymptomatic. Clinically, the tooth was grade zero mobile, and probing was within normal limits (2–4 mm).

Discussion

Intentional replantation is a conservative treatment for the management of posttreatment apical periodontitis when both nonsurgical and surgical endodontic treatments are considered unreasonable [12]. The clinical decision‐making process implies discussion of all treatment options with the patient such as orthograde retreatment, apical microsurgery, IR, or extraction with/without replacement, for the patient to make an informed decision.

Considering the limitation of all these procedures, the patients have chosen IR which represents a conservative, favorable, and cost‐effective last resort option over tooth extraction. IR aims to preserve the natural tooth and consequent bone tissue maintenance. Therefore, this case series describes the treatment of premolars and molars with posttreatment disease diagnosed with apical periodontitis using IR.

In the literature, comparing the survival rates of single‐rooted and multirooted replanted teeth, no statistically significant difference between them was noticed. Both types of replanted teeth had an 86.7% overall survival rate [13]. In addition, in a 5–12‐year follow‐up study, Hou et al. [14] found that the mean accumulated survival rate of 17 case series was 88.2% [14]. However, the survival rate of intentionally replanted teeth with a C‐shaped canal was less than that shown in a recent systematic review [15]. The lower survival rate of intentionally replanted teeth with C‐shaped canals was associated with the use of ProRoot MTA as a retrofilling material and extraoral time exceeding 15 min [15]. In this case series, all the replanted teeth have survived following IR with a mean recall of 5 years.

IR consists of atraumatic tooth extraction, root‐end resection/filling with different calcium silicate materials, and repositioning of the extracted tooth. During extraoral handling, the preservation of periodontal ligament cells is a critical parameter. Moreover, it has been shown that the survival rate of IR decreases by 1.7‐fold with the increase of the extraoral time beyond 15 min [16].

In addition to traumatic manipulation during extraction and a long extraoral time, many studies suggest other prognostic factors for IR, that affect tooth survival negatively. These involve the periodontal involvement of teeth with two pockets ≥ 6 mm, existence of a sinus tract or abscess, and patients above 40 years old [17, 18]. Thus, this supports the importance of adequate surgical planning by the clinician and a proper case selection for successful prognosis. Conversely, teeth with divergent or curved roots and fractured radicular structures may contraindicate performing IR, hence the importance of a good clinical and radiological examination.

It has been reported that most complications following IR should appear within 1 year after replantation; therefore, it was proposed that the follow‐up period should extend for at least 3 years to identify late complications [9]. Most complications due to resorption or periodontal problems are seen after 1 year, although inflammatory resorption and ankylosis can be detected earlier, within 1–2 months [12]. According to Torabinejad et al. [15], the prevalence of root resorption in IR teeth was 11% [15]. For this reason, the present cases were regularly followed up and have shown neither complications nor evidence of resorption. Also, the teeth in this case series were clinically functional and asymptomatic.

Furthermore, the study by Cho et al. [9] has shown that most complications of IR arise during the first year after the procedure. The authors report a small increase in periapical radiolucency and external root resorption occurrence between the first and third postoperative years [9]. Thus, a minimum 3‐year follow‐up period has been suggested for all these cases.

It has been shown in this case series that IR can be a valid treatment option for prolonging the long‐term survival of root canal‐treated teeth. However, the heterogeneity of success criteria, inexistence of an established clinical protocol, achieving atraumatic tooth extraction, and performing all the extraoral procedures within 15 min may discourage clinicians from proposing IR to the patient.
