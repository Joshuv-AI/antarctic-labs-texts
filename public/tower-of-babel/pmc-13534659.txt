All-fiber random Kerr optical frequency combs
Authors: Da-Peng Zhou, Gerard Tatel, Yuan Wang, Paul S Westbrook, Liang Chen, Xiaoyi Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptical frequency combs (OFCs) unite the electromagnetic spectrum coherently, and they can be realized in a number of different techniques, e.g., OFCs based on mode-locked lasers, electro-optic modulation of continuous-wave (CW) lasers, CW-pumped ring cavities such as micro-resonators, soliton formation and compression through modulation instability induced by dual-laser pumping, etc. Here, we introduce a different approach, achieving the first random Kerr OFC, the spectral-synthesized analog of a random laser. Based on modulation instability for simultaneous parametric amplification and temporal amplitude compression assisted by the distributed random feedback through Rayleigh scattering within a half-open cavity, trains of solitons are formed and further compressed leading to OFCs with spectral bandwidth over ~ 100 nm, wide comb spacing of ~ 1 nm, and minimum soliton duration of ~ 390 fs. The random Kerr OFC can operate continuously as a robust and flexible device, offering adjustable comb spacing, tunable soliton repetition rate, and tunable gate rate.Subject terms: Frequency combs, Fibre lasers

Introduction

Optical frequency combs (OFCs), broad-spectrum light sources consisting of narrow, discrete, equally-spaced coherent laser lines, have been developed for more than two decades, enabling tremendous applications, including in atomic clocks, spectroscopy, light detection and ranging, microwave generations, and coherent optical communications1,2. Mode-locked lasers are the original OFCs, synchronizing the phases of a large number of frequency lines corresponding to the longitudinal modes of the laser cavity, and generating short optical pulses. These optical pulses result from the coherent addition of the resonant longitudinal optical cavity modes, spanning up to 100 nm in the optical domain, so that the evolution of the electric field, the phase dynamics of every frequency component in the OFC spectrum is deterministic. Alternatively, OFCs based on micro-resonator or microcombs, can serve as compact, chip-scale coherent sources with very broad optical spectrum, whose formation is enabled by the four-wave mixing (FWM) process inside the micro-resonators3,4. These OFCs exhibit low noise and smooth spectral envelopes when they operate in the dissipative soliton regime with the operation mechanism very different from mode-locked lasers5–7. Note that even though dissipative solitons exist in mode-locked lasers8, they can occur in systems sustained by parametric gain which are first studied in fiber cavities9,10. In micro-resonators, by properly tuning the pump laser frequency5, optical sidebands generated by means of the FWM undergo self-organization process leading to the emergence of a train of solitons, which are called dissipative Kerr solitons (DKSs). These soliton pulse trains can be described by the Lugiato-Lefever equation (LLE)11,12, that has the same form of a driven, damped and detuned nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLSE). Even though OFCs based on mode-locked lasers and microcombs are formed with very different mechanisms, they are both generated within high-quality optical cavities.

On the other hand, OFCs can also be generated without a cavity. Electro-optic (EO) comb generator utilizes an active modulator driven by an electronic synthesizer, imposing a grid of sidebands on a continuous-wave (CW) laser which offers OFCs a superior advantage of wideband and fast tuning of the line spacing13. Admittedly, placing a modulator inside an optical resonator and driving it with a signal whose frequency is consistent with the resonator mode spacing can effectively enhance the performance of the EO combs14–16, cascading multiple modulators without a cavity17, and combining them with proper optical filters18 and nanophotonic waveguides19, can lead to great performance of EO combs with noise that is comparable to that of the mode-locked lasers. Another way to generate OFCs without an optical cavity is to pump optical fibers with two CW lasers20–22. The beating of the two laser fields inside the fiber creates a periodic modulation pattern that can seed the formation of an OFC through modulation instability (MI), which also has the advantage of wideband, tunable comb spacing via the proper adjustment of wavelength difference between the two pump lasers. The use of dispersion-decreasing fiber (DDF) pumped with two lasers can create a train of ultrashort pulses with a comb-like spectrum23–25. Dual-laser pumped OFCs can also be generated in a normally dispersive optical fibers26; however, optical wave breaking (OWB) is responsible for the comb formation which is different from the DDF case where MI can lead to the spontaneous generation of solitons that can then be compressed.

So far, a random OFC with distributed random feedback has never been achieved, even though random lasers are studied intensively during the past two decades. Optical scattering was used to be considered detrimental to lasers because such scattering introduces loss into a conventional laser cavity27. However, random feedback due to optical scattering has been investigated in laser oscillators, and it can sustain random lasing within a random laser which refers to a non-conventional laser28,29. Optical-fiber-based random lasers show advantages due to simple configuration and high efficiency compared to dye- or powder-based counterparts as light is confined to nearly one dimension, where the random feedback is provided by Rayleigh scattering which naturally occurs in optical fibers due to small refractive index fluctuations that are introduced during fabrication process30,31. Even though a large variety of random lasers have been built, it is difficult to achieve a random OFC. Two challenges preclude the development of a random OFC up to now. First, random feedback due to Rayleigh scattering in optical fibers is rather weak, which cannot help form high-quality cavity that supports a large number of cavity resonances. Second, the soliton output of the OFC requires all the frequency components having stable phase relationship which apparently is inherently in contrast to the concept of random feedback.

Here, we demonstrate the first random Kerr OFC, an OFC formed within a half-open cavity constructed with a fiber Bragg grating (FBG) reflector and a random feedback provided through Rayleigh scattering in an optical fiber, with the parametric gain provided by MI. It could be regarded as the spectral-synthesized analog of a random laser. Note that a random optical parametric oscillator (OPO) is proposed recently32; however, it has to use long optical fibers to achieve laser output with low extinction ratio and limited tunability. In the random Kerr OFC, Rayleigh scattering of the spontaneous MI sidebands generated by a long pump pulse is filtered and reflected by the FBG, and the resultant light wave is synchronized with the next incoming pump pulse leading to a low-amplitude modulation on top of the pump pulse. This temporal amplitude modulation can be effectively compressed by the induced MI in the optical fiber resulting in a train of solitons which can be further compressed to form a random Kerr OFC with wideband spectrum. We explain the physical principle of the random Kerr OFC with random feedback, and characterize its main features, including its threshold behavior, spectrum, and temporal property. As we shall see, the random Kerr OFC turns out to be a highly versatile light source, allowing wide tunability of comb spacing, soliton repetition rate, and soliton train gate rate in an all-fiber configuration.

Results

Operation principle

MI can be interpreted in terms of an FWM process phase-matched by self-phase modulation (SPM)33, and it can be seeded through temporal amplitude modulation to enhance soliton formation from a CW input in optical fibers with anomalous dispersion34. For the random Kerr OFC, the amplitude modulation can be introduced by the coherent feedback of the spontaneous FWM noise at frequencies dictated by the phase-matching condition within a half-open cavity constructed through an FBG reflector and distributed random feedback provided by Rayleigh scattering. The Rayleigh scattering light wave of the spontaneous MI sidebands accompanying with a long pump pulse is filtered and reflected by the FBG, resulting in a very low-amplitude temporal modulation on the next incoming synchronized pump pulse. The compression of this weak amplitude modulation by the induced MI leads to the emergence of a train of solitons35, which can be further compressed to have the spectral broadening in the frequency domain36.

The operation principle of the random Kerr OFC is illustrated in Fig. 1. Two sections of optical fibers cascade together. The first-section fiber has anomalous dispersion with the input end inscribed with an FBG that has a high reflectivity and narrow bandwidth; while the second one serves as a soliton-compression fiber. Pump pulses, whose wavelength is adjusted to be outside of the reflection bandwidth of the FBG, are launched into the first-section fiber without attenuation as shown at Point A in Fig. 1. As a pump pulse propagates into the fiber, spontaneous MI sidebands are generated and co-propagate with the pump pulse serving as wideband parametric gain33. The pump pulse wavelength and peak power are fine tuned to have the FBG Bragg wavelength to fall within one of the spectral regions of the largest MI gain. In this way, the backward-propagating Rayleigh scattering light from the spontaneous MI sidebands is filtered and reflected at the FBG and re-injected into the fiber. Thus, a half-open cavity is formed for light waves whose wavelength are within the bandwidth of the FBG, between a fixed Bragg reflection and random distributed feedback. The Rayleigh scattering light will only undergo MI amplification if it arrives at the FBG at the same time as the next incoming pump pulse. The half-open cavity ensures coherent feedback and oscillation of the light waves that are in phase with the pump pulses, so that stable temporal amplitude modulation of the pump pulses can be introduced as indicated at Point B in Fig. 1. This only occurs for the light scattered at a specific fiber section, whose location and length are determined by the pump pulse repetition rate and pulse width. We refer to this section as an effective Rayleigh section (ERS) as indicated in Fig. 1, since the scattered light exclusively from this section oscillates coherently in the cavity. The pump pulse repetition rate is tunable, but it has to satisfy the condition that only one pulse exists within the first-section fiber at all time. If multiple pump pulses exist in the fiber simultaneously, light waves scattered by multiple ERSs interfere resulting in unstable temporal modulations of the pump pulse.

Operation principle of the random Kerr OFC.

Long pump pulses at Point A with tunable repetition rate are launched via a fiber Bragg grating (FBG), whose Bragg wavelength falls within the spectral regions of the largest modulation instability (MI) gain, into an anomalous-dispersion single-mode optical fiber, followed by a section of soliton-compression fiber. The repetition rate is set to guarantee that only one pump pulse exists in the first-section fiber at all time. The backward-propagating Rayleigh scattering radiation, shown at Point C, from the effective Rayleigh section (ERS) meets an incoming pump pulse when reflected and filtered at the FBG, introducing temporal amplitude modulation on top of the pump pulse as shown at Point B. The induced MI leads to compression of this coherent low-amplitude modulation, leading to a train of solitons as indicated at Point D. The solitons are further compressed within the soliton-compression fiber to have much shorter pulse duration forming a random Kerr OFC as shown at Point E. The upper insets show the OFC evolution in the time domain (t), while the lower insets indicate that in the wavelength domain (λ).

It must be noted that even when the pump wave propagates by itself, in the form of either a CW or a long pulse, MI can lead to spontaneous breakup of the input beam into a periodic pulse train. Further, since optical pulses with suitable peak powers have the tendency to re-adjust themselves inside the fiber with anomalous dispersion to form solitons, MI can also result in the spontaneous generation of fundamental solitons that can then be compressed25. An effective way to enhance the above process is to seed the MI through temporal amplitude modulation34. The coherent feedback from the half-open cavity shown in Fig. 1 turns out to be effective enough to provide this amplitude modulation as indicated at Point B, although the modulation depth is very low. Soliton evolution in optical fiber initiated by weakly-modulated field has been well-established35. As a result, trains of solitons can be generated due to the compression of this low-amplitude modulation by the induced MI. The solitons can be further compressed greatly within the soliton-compression fiber to form a random Kerr OFC with wideband spectrum. It must be highlighted that the two processes, compression of low-amplitude temporal modulation and parametric oscillation occurring in the same fiber which contains the half-open cavity, contribute collectively to the OFC formation, with an obvious threshold behavior. Above the threshold, a laser line with the wavelength falling within the FBG bandwidth can be observed at Point B as shown in Fig. 1, and a low-amplitude temporal modulation is developed on top of the pump pulse which can be compressed to form the short pulses and their comb spectra are indicated at Point D. At Point C, the ERS scatters back all the comb lines bearing typical random temporal fluctuations. The reason to choose an FBG as a reflector manifests itself that optical filtering is necessary to keep only one comb line to introduce coherent amplitude modulation which can be regarded as a dual-pumping case which is frequently used to generate soliton trains20–25. Admittedly, one can also build a half-open cavity with a mirror combined with the random distributed feedback, OFC can still be formed with less stability. The reason is that in this case, more than one comb lines may tend to oscillate within the half-open cavity which can disturb the stability of soliton formation. The pulse trains from the first-section fiber, shown at Point D in Fig. 1, can be further compressed by the second-section soliton-compression fiber to have shorter pulses and wider comb spectrum, as shown at Point E in Fig. 1. Note that compared to conventional fixed cavity built with two reflectors, e.g., two identical FBGs, the half-open cavity allows great flexibility for the pump pulse repetition rate which shows an advantage of tunable gate rate of the soliton trains. Additionally, due to the low reflectivity from the open end, almost all the output power from the solitons can be delivered to the next stage without the necessity of coupling the power out of the resonant cavity. It is worth mentioning that the random Kerr OFC converts a long pump pulse into a soliton train, and in this sense, it behaves like a modulation-instability laser (MIL)37–39, which is a new kind of OPO with the major difference compared to the conventional one being the ability to turn a CW pump into a train of short optical pulses33. However, the random OFC takes the advantage of half-open cavity, exhibiting a wideband spectrum with distinct comb lines that are different from the MIL constructed by a ring-cavity where limited amount of sidebands can be generated. Alternatively, the OFC also has the characteristics of a conventional OPO for the comb line whose wavelength coincides with the Bragg wavelength; however, the tuning range of this comb line is very much limited. It must be highlighted that the random Kerr OFC operates differently from the conventional OPOs or MILs which are built with fixed cavities, offering great flexibility and simplicity, providing widely tunable comb line spacing and soliton repetition rate without the necessity of precise cavity control and stabilization as normally required by the conventional OPOs for proper operation.

Spectrum

The experiment validation of the random Kerr OFC is performed as shown in Fig. 2a. We send long pump pulses with a wavelength of λlaser = 1548.78 nm and 50 ns duration into a Rayleigh-enhanced fiber (REF) with a length of 1 km via a high-reflectivity FBG with a central wavelength of λFBG = 1547.73 nm and a bandwidth of 0.1 nm. The REF, a G.657 compatible single-mode fiber (SMF), has an anomalous dispersion with a group velocity dispersion (GVD) parameter of β2 = − 26.6 ps2/km at 1550 nm and a nonlinear coefficient of γ = 1.4 (km⋅W)−1. It has been treated by ultraviolet (UV) exposure to enhance Rayleigh scattering within the wavelength bandwidth from 1537.3 nm to 1554.3 nm, and the scattering strength is approximately 13 dB greater than that of the standard SMF around 1550 nm. The repetition rate of the pump pulse is adjusted to be 110 kHz to ensure that there is only one pump pulse in the REF at all time. A 5.25 km-long DDF is connected to the REF after a variable optical attenuator (VOA) for input power optimization. The GVD parameter, β2, of the DDF varies from − 9.82 ps2/km to 0.38 ps2/km linearly along the fiber. It is well-known that the characteristics of the soliton compression can be controlled by tailoring fiber dispersion, and an efficient way to compress solitons is to use DDF with an anomalous dispersion that decreases to near zero along its length40–43. Solitons that propagate adiabatically are automatically compressed in proportion to the decrease of dispersion to maintain the balance between the nonlinear and dispersive effects. The GVD parameter of the DDF used in this work increases from negative to zero and then further increases to positive resulting in a fiber section with normal GVD; however, the solitons can remain in existence within this section without becoming completely dispersive at the output of the DDF, due to the presence of third-order dispersion (TOD) that introduces a spectral region of anomalous dispersion (see simulations in Supplementary Materials).

A 5.25 km-long dispersion-decreasing fiber (DDF) is connected to the REF with a variable optical attenuator (VOA). An isolator (ISO) at the end of the DDF prevents unwanted Fresnel reflections. Optical spectra are collected at three different locations as marked as Ports A, B, and C, and are shown in b, c, and d, respectively. At Port A, the spectrum is obtained with a 1% tap. Above threshold (TH), a laser emission with the wavelength coinciding with the Bragg wavelength of the FBG shows at Port A, and the OFC spectra are observed at Port B and Port C. For the results shown in Fig. 2, we keep the attenuation of the VOA to be 10 dB. The pump peak power can be adjusted to initiate the random Kerr OFC, and the spectra below and above threshold are shown in Fig. 2b, c, d, respectively, corresponding to the measurements carried out at Ports A, B, and C. At Port A, the spectrum is obtained with a 1% tap. It is obvious that below threshold, as shown by the red curves, there are no OFC generated. After the threshold is reached, when the pulse peak power reaches PA = 5 W, one can observe stable OFC spectra as shown by the black curves in Fig. 2c, d. At Port A, a laser emission whose wavelength falling within the FBG bandwidth can be observed as expected, with a 45 dB lower power than the pump power, which introduces low-amplitude temporal modulation to the pump pulse. The induced MI eventually transforms this temporal modulation into a train of solitons. The solitons can be further compressed within the DDF, leading to the formation of random Kerr OFC whose output spectrum has been observed to cover ~ 100 nm wide as indicated in Fig. 2d. There is a limited pump power range for obtaining the wide comb bandwidth above the threshold because the build up of the SPM of the spontaneous MI noises at sufficiently high pump power can lead to the disappearance of the clear comb spectra at the OFC output. We also estimate the maximum MI gain at the frequency shift of ~ 115 GHz from the pump in the REF with a pump power of 5 W. The comb spacing shown in Fig. 2c, d is ~ 131.25 GHz, and a close examination reveals that the first comb line with the wavelength longer than the pump indeed falls at the wavelength close to and slightly longer than the maximum MI gain wavelength. The comb spacing can be adjusted by tuning the pump wavelength within the MI gain bandwidth, as long as the gain is sufficient for parametric oscillations. It must be noted that when the comb spacing is sufficiently narrow to the extent that its frequency harmonics are also located within the MI gain bandwidth, high-order MI can occur. The phenomenon has been thoroughly studied in ref. 44, where the analysis developed provides potential techniques which could be used in the random Kerr OFC for generating pulse trains with higher repetition rate than the primary modulation frequency. This would be very interesting and worth exploring; however, we will only focus on the first-order MI at the moment.

We can vary the attenuation simply by adjusting the VOA before the pulse trains enter the DDF, and it can be observed through Fig. 3 that as the power of the pulses entering the DDF increases, the output comb spectrum becomes wider. In Fig. 3, different offsets are introduced for shifting the spectra vertically in order to display all the spectra in one figure. Note that the vertical axis has a scale of 20 dB per division. More than 100 nm wide comb spectrum can be observed with a comb spacing of around ~ 1 nm. It can also be observed that when the attenuation is lower, the input power to the DDF is higher, the comb spectrum shows a redshift which is known as Raman-induced frequency shift through intrapulse Raman scattering33. There is an optimized range of the attenuation, and if the input power to the DDF is too high, one can lose the comb structures in the output spectrum due to the build up of the SPM of the spontaneous MI noises at the REF output as shown in Fig. 2c. Wider OFC spectrum could in principle be achieved provided that Rayleigh scattering strength in the REF is further enhanced, so that the pulse trains out of the REF should have relatively lower spontaneous MI noises. Hence, one can inject higher power of the pulses into the DDF leading to wider OFC spectral output.

Threshold

We next provide more detailed experiment results regarding the threshold behavior of the random Kerr OFC. The results are shown in Fig. 4. We keep the attenuation of the VOA to be 10 dB, and measure the spectrum at the Port C. The pump pulse duration is set to 30 ns, and all the other parameters are kept the same as those used in producing Fig. 2. We show several OFC spectral output in Fig. 4a. It can be observed that after the threshold power is reached, wideband comb output can be generated; however, the available pump power range is limited as further increasing pump power results in the disappearance of the comb spectrum due to the build up of the SPM of the spontaneous MI noises even though the entire spectral envelope is further broadened.

Random Kerr OFC threshold behavior.

a Random Kerr OFC spectra for different average pump power with 30-ns pump pulse duration. All the other parameters used are the same as those in Fig. 2. As the pump power increases, there is a clear threshold behavior. The wideband random OFC spectra can be observed when the threshold is reached. After the threshold, the available pump power range is limited as further increasing pump power results in the disappearance of the comb spectrum. b shows the comb bandwidth versus average pump power. The bandwidth is determined by measuring spectral coverage of the comb lines that are clearly visible. c shows the output average power of several comb lines, #1, #2, #5, #8, and #10, as a function of average pump power.

In Fig. 4b, we plot the bandwidth of the random OFC as a function of average pump power for better quantitative clarification. The bandwidth is determined by measuring spectral coverage of the comb lines that are clearly visible. We also show average powers of several comb lines versus average pump power in Fig. 4c, indicating that when the pump power further increases over the threshold, the comb line power can decrease. We estimate the highest peak power for the first comb line to be ~ 9 mW and that for the tenth comb line to be ~ 0.8 mW. The efficiency of the random Kerr OFC is estimated to be ~ 1.2% by calculating the ratio of the total output power of all the comb lines (except the pump wavelength line) to the pump power. Note that the calculation includes all the insertion losses in the configuration. There are two main reasons responsible for the low conversion efficiency in our current experiment. The first one is due to the huge total loss of ~ 16 dB in the setup that includes the 10 dB attenuation introduced by the VOA. The second one is because that part of the pump power converts to the high spontaneous MI noise. The conversion efficiency and the available output power can be in principle increased greatly if Rayleigh scattering strength is further enhanced in the REF so that spontaneous MI noises can be suppressed, allowing more power delivered into the DDF. Also, the loss in the experiment setup can be reduced by properly coupling the power between different types of fibers to further optimize the insertion loss.

Stability

We perform further measurement demonstrating random Kerr OFC’s long-term stability which is critical for high-performance comb operation as shown in Fig. 5. The OFC output spectrum is recorded over 1-h period with all the parameters used the same as those in Fig. 2, indicating stable operation over long period. We repeat the same experiments for three times, and the results are very consistent. Even though the spectrum of the OFC is stable, when we look closely to the individual comb lines, we observe wavelength drifting and power variations. The results are shown in the upper and lower insets, respectively, for four different comb lines, #1, #2, #5, and #10. The standard deviations are calculated to be ~ 360 MHz for frequency drifting and ~ 8.5% for power fluctuations. The relatively large long-term frequency drift and power fluctuations are due to environmental changes. Especially, the spectra of FBGs and Rayleigh scattering are quite sensitive to temperature variations (1 K in temperature variation roughly changes ~ 1 GHz in their spectral frequency). In the experiment, we place the FBG and the REF in a styrofoam box to minimize the impact of environmental variations, but slight temperature variations or vibrations still result in comb frequency drifting which in turn lead to power fluctuations. However, due to the flexible tunability, the random OFC can still operate stably over long-time period.

As each comb line of the random Kerr OFC operates as optical pulses with the same duration as the pump pulse width, we filter out an individual comb line, and beat it with an external tunable CW laser whose linewidth is ~ 5 kHz (see Supplementary Materials for detailed experiment setup) in order to further investigate short-term frequency stability of the random OFC. For demonstration purpose, we show the results for comb line #1 and #3, respectively, in Fig. 6. The pump duration is selected to be 10 ns for these measurements, and the beat signals, whose frequencies are around 2–3 GHz by tuning the wavelength of the external CW laser close to that of the comb line, are detected with a photodetector of 12 GHz bandwidth and an oscilloscope of 25 GS/s sampling rate. The beat signal over ~ 200 μs for comb line #1 is displayed in Fig. 6a. We pick out three pulses, #1, #10, and #20, and display the enlarged traces in Fig. 6b. The consistent beat pattern demonstrates the interpulse coherence between pulses. The envelope fluctuations of the beat signals are primarily due to the polarization mismatch as all SMFs are used; while the beat frequency variations attribute to the mutual frequency drifting between the CW laser and the comb line. We further show the same measurement by filtering out comb line #3 in Fig. 6c, d. Similar results are observed, indicating constant phase relationship between comb lines #1 and #3. The beat measurements for other comb lines have similar results. Note that, these measurements cannot provide detailed frequency noise information for each comb line; however, they demonstrate the fixed phase relationship among random Kerr OFC lines and the coherence between the output soliton streams.

Tunability

The comb spacing of the random Kerr OFC and the soliton repetition rate are easily adjusted by either tuning the wavelength of the FBG or the pump laser wavelength. The tuning range is limited by the bandwidth of the parametric gain determined by the pump power and the characteristics of the optical fiber that is used. In the experiment, we adjust the wavelength of the pump laser over 0.3 nm range, so the corresponding comb spacing varies from 0.95 nm to 1.25 nm, which leads to the soliton repetition rate tuned from 118.75 GHz to 156.25 GHz. The limit of 0.3 nm tunable range of the comb spacing is due to the limited bandwidth of MI gain. When the wavelength is tuned outside this range, the gain is reduced to an extent that the parametric oscillations cease to occur. The spectra obtained from Port B are shown in Fig. 7a, and very similar comb spectra are observed for different comb spacing as expected. Their corresponding autocorrelation traces are displayed in Fig. 7b, where the pulse repetition rate variations are also observed, and the results are consistent with the spectral comb spacing measurement. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the autocorrelation pulse width is measured to be ~ 2.4 ps, corresponding to a pulse FWHM duration of ~ 1.6 ps. At Port C, we also measure the comb spectrum and the autocorrelation trace as shown in Fig. 7c and 7d, respectively. We set the VOA attenuation at 10 dB to obtain the results. The comb spectrum has been expanded to over 100 nm. It can be observed in Fig. 7c that for different comb spacing, the OFC output shows large spectral envelope variations, which is due to the different MI gain experienced for the parametric oscillations in the half-open cavity.

Tunability of comb spacing and soliton repetition rate.

a Optical frequency comb spectra and b autocorrelation traces measured at Port B with different pump wavelength (λlaser). The wavelength of the FBG (λFBG) is also indicated. c Optical frequency comb spectra and d autocorrelation traces measured at Port C with different pump wavelength. Clearly, both the comb spacing and soliton repetition rate are tunable by simply adjusting pump laser wavelength. The measured comb spacing and soliton repetition rate agree well with each other.

Therefore, different modulation frequencies experience different MI gain, and the modulation depth on the incoming pump also varies for different repetition rate, which result in OFC spectral envelope variations. The FWHM duration of the autocorrelation pulse width is compressed to about ~ 0.6 ps which corresponds to a ~ 390 fs soliton pulse duration as shown in Fig. 7d. The time-bandwidth product (TBP) is calculated to be ~ 0.49. Admittedly, the soliton can ideally be compressed to be much shorter with durations of a few optical cycles, we believe that there are two main reasons preventing the random Kerr OFC with the current setup from achieving it. The first reason is the presence of the TOD, which introduces a spectral region of normal dispersion that solitons cannot exist. As a very short pulses would require a rather broad spectrum, and since the pulse are solitons, the spectrum would have to maintain in anomalous dispersion regime. Therefore, as the pulse compresses, the tail of the pulse spectrum will eventually end up in the normal dispersion regime, thus limiting the spectral broadening and the pulse duration of the solitons. This also results in the TBP to be larger than the transform-limited TBP. The second one is the presence of large spontaneous MI noises at the output of the REF, which limits the input power of the pulses entering into the DDF, because less power results in limited spectral extent and, consequently, soliton duration. It is worth mentioning that the TOD makes the soliton last longer distance even though at the last section of the fiber where the GVD parameter becomes positive; however, it can introduce temporal asymmetry to the solitons and force them to give off some of their energies in the form of dispersive waves. Hence, in the autocorrelation measurement results shown in Fig. 7d, one can observe temporal oscillations between the solitons; however, the soliton characteristics is still largely preserved (see simulations in Supplementary Materials). Note that the autocorrelator used in the experiment performs background-free autocorrelation measurement which is designed for high repetition rate ultrashort pulse measurement, >1 MHz. However, our OFC output has the form of soliton stream, with the stream repetition rate of around 110 kHz. Therefore, we perform a large amount of measurements and average them to obtain the autocorrelator trace shown in the figures. As discussed in the last section, the random Kerr OFC has long-term comb spacing drifting due to external perturbations, which may result in the contrast variations of the autocorrelator trace due to the averaging over trace to trace. Note that one can use the DDF without normal GVD section to achieve shorter pulse and broader spectrum. Moreover, enhancing the Rayleigh scattering strength in the first-section REF could lead to reduced spontaneous MI noises; therefore, it is possible to have higher input pulse peak power to the DDF to achieve shorter output soliton pulses. In principle, we believe that sub-100 fs soliton pulses could be achieved with this approach25 by optimizing parameters of both the fibers.

The gate rate and gate duration of the random Kerr OFC can also be simply tuned by altering the input pump pulse repetition rate and pump pulse duration, respectively, so that the time duration between the streams of the soliton pulses as well as the time duration of each soliton stream can be varied. Here, we refer the gate rate to the frequency between the generation of successive soliton streams; while the gate duration to the time duration of each soliton stream. However, the gate rate cannot be altered arbitrarily, as one has to make sure that there is only one pump pulse in the REF, or only one ERS in the REF at all time; otherwise, the soliton formation is not stable because Rayleigh scattering from multiple sections interfere which is very sensitive to external perturbations. Hence, in the current setup, 1 km-long REF determines that the gate rate can be varied from 102 kHz to 204 kHz, within which only one pump pulse exists in the REF for stable comb formation. Note that longer REF may be used to extend the gate rate tunable range. As shown in Fig. 8 for example, we set up three different gate rates, and measure the corresponding spectra and autocorrelation traces at Port B and Port C, respectively. The wavelength of the pump pulse laser is kept at 1548.88 nm which results in the comb spacing of 1.15 nm as indicated in Fig. 8a, so that the pulse repetition rate is 143.75 GHz, which is consistent with the autocorrelation measurements as shown in Fig. 8b. As ERS at different locations has different Rayleigh scattering spectrum; therefore, when pump pulse repetition rate is tuned, the resultant random Kerr OFC comb spacing could be slightly different as well. However, as the difference is small, the resultant comb spectral envelope does not appear large variations as the case shown in Fig. 7c. The pump pulse duration is chosen to be 30 ns in obtaining Fig. 8. Since the pump pulse duration is reduced, the peak power has to be increased to supply higher gain to compensate the reduced strength of Rayleigh scattering. The peak powers for the 110 kHz, 135 kHz and 160 kHz cases are 6 W, 6.5 W, and 7 W, respectively, as higher gate rate corresponds to shorter cavity length with reduced parametric gain, so that higher pump power is required. Figure 8c, d shows the output spectra and the autocorrelation traces at Port C for the three different gate rates, with the corresponding VOA attenuation settings of 10.2 dB, 11.3 dB, and 12.7 dB, respectively. Wider comb bandwidth and shorter soliton duration are also observed. These experimental results confirm that both the gate rate and gate duration can be easily tuned for the random Kerr OFC offering great flexibility. In our experiment, pump pulse durations from 10 ns to 60 ns are tested, indicating a 50 ns gate duration tunability with our current setup. The limit for achieving longer gate duration is because of the power limit of the pump source. We believe longer gate duration should be realized; however, one has to make sure it does not reach the threshold of the stimulated Brillouin scattering.

Random OFC with normally dispersive fiber

The random Kerr OFC has an advantage of simple configuration without a closed cavity; therefore, it is very convenient to change the second-section soliton-compression fiber for comb spectral broadening based on practical requirements or fiber availability. For applications that do not require the output to be trains of solitons, one can generate OFCs with a different mechanism by exploring normally dispersive fibers. Even though anomalous dispersion is known to lead to a wider supercontinuum, significant spectral broadening from SPM can be observed in the normal dispersion regime of an optical fiber as well45,46. As an example, we switch the DDF to a 1 km-long normally dispersive high-nonlinearity fiber (HNF) with γ = 10 (km⋅W)−1 and β2 = 2.18 ps2/km. Solitons cannot exist in this fiber, so that the temporal characteristics of the random OFC output from this fiber is very different from the case using the DDF. The output spectra and autocorrelation trace are indicated in Fig. 9a, b, respectively, with all the parameters used are the same as those in Fig. 2 except that the attenuation between the REF and the HNF is adjusted differently as shown in Fig. 9. For higher input power to the HNF (lower attenuation), the output shows broader comb spectrum. As mentioned earlier, the formation of these comb spectra are very different from those in DDF. OWB plays a key role in producing spectral broadening of the frequency comb in this case. Figure 9b indicates the output autocorrelation traces with clear evidence of the OWB, showing large oscillatory structures near pulse edges. These oscillations are formed due to the OWB as a result of the beat of the light waves with multiple frequencies in both the leading and trailing regions of the pulses. These frequency components mix nonlinearly to produce, through FWM process, new frequency components, which forms broad OFC spectrum in the output as shown in Fig. 9a and develops intense temporal peaks as shown in Fig. 9b due to the simultaneous presence of these frequency components. For the HNF used in the experiment, as all the comb lines stay in the normal dispersion regime, neither soliton dynamics or MI contributes to the comb spectral broadening. Therefore, in principle, the resultant random OFC may offer increased robustness due to the absence of MI-amplified noise26. Therefore, the proposed OFC with normally dispersive fiber as the second stage may find many applications that do not require the frequency comb to be a train of solitons in the time domain.

Discussion

This work introduces a new paradigm for OFC generation, the random Kerr OFC, which takes use of MI as gain and Rayleigh scattering as distributed random feedback in an anomalous dispersion SMF followed by a soliton-compression fiber. The half-open cavity constructed by random distributed feedback and an FBG facilitates coherent and synchronized radiation that introduces low-amplitude temporal modulation on top of the incoming pump pulses. Both of the compression of this temporal modulation and the parametric oscillation collectively enable formation of the OFCs with tunable comb spacing as well as trains of solitons with tunable repetition rate. The random Kerr OFC has a broad comb spectrum, over 100 nm spectral bandwidth, within which a wide comb spacing, on the order of ~ 1 nm, with a tunable range of 0.3 nm has been demonstrated experimentally. The corresponding trains of solitons whose duration is a few hundred fs are obtained with the soliton repetition rate tunable from 118.75 GHz to 156.25 GHz. Taking the advantage of the distributed random feedback, the OFCs and soliton trains have tunable gate rate ranging from 102 kHz to 204 kHz, and gate duration tunable from 10 ns to 60 ns. Our work demonstrates a novel approach to generate a random OFC, which turns out to be a highly versatile light source with a simple all-fiber configuration. Admittedly, the stability of random Kerr OFC is subjected to external perturbations, such as temperature variations and vibrations which result in shift of the Bragg wavelength and Rayleigh scattering spectrum, leading to comb line frequency and comb spacing drifting as well as power fluctuations; however, the long-term stability can be improved by placing the FBG and REF in a better controlled environment. We compare the random Kerr OFC with other existing state-of-the-art OFC techniques, and summarize the key indicators in Table 1, where both the typical and the best achievable parameters are given to the best of our knowledge. It can be seen that the random Kerr OFC has the advantages of wide comb spacing tunability compared to other techniques except for the dual-pumping laser combs; however, only one laser is needed in the configuration. The spectral bandwidth of random Kerr OFC is limited to ~ 100 nm in its current form due to the limited power that can be delivered into the DDF, that also results in a low conversion efficiency. It must be highlighted that shorter soliton duration with broader comb spectrum and higher conversion efficiency can be achieved for the proposed random Kerr OFC. As discussed in the last section, the spontaneous MI noise limits the input power to the DDF, and at the same time, the presence of TOD introduces normal-dispersion spectral region, both of which are the major factors preventing the OFC from shorter soliton formation. Further optimization is feasible by properly designing the fiber parameters, e.g., enhancing Rayleigh scattering or tailoring fiber dispersion, etc., and we believe that soliton pulses with sub-100 fs duration with wider OFC spectra and a much higher conversion efficiency can be achieved. Moreover, we have not placed the tunable range of the entire OFC spectrum as an indicator in the comparison table as many technologies have already demonstrated great tunability over wide spectral range within one system47–49; however, the tunability of entire comb spectrum is limited for the random Kerr OFC even though the advantage of wide tunable range of comb spacing is demonstrated.

In summary, by bridging random feedback physics with Kerr nonlinearity, this work establishes a new class of OFC sources, operating continuously without feedback control loops, external active modulations or additional pump sources, resulting in a robust and flexible device offering turnkey operation capability. The random Kerr OFC can be used for applications that require wide tunable range of the comb line spacing, such as frequency synthesis, microwave photonics, high-repetition-rate pulse generation, etc. If higher soliton repetition rate is needed, high-nonlinearity REF could be used in the first stage to further increase the comb line spacing. Admittedly, the gated output of the soliton trains may be a drawback for applications that require continuous operation. However, the random Kerr OFC may be directly used in distributed fiber-optic sensing applications that streams of the soliton pulse trains are needed as the probe to enable spatial identification along the optical fiber50. Moreover, by taking the advantage of half-open cavity configuration, a random Kerr OFC can be constructed differently by simply replacing the soliton-compression fiber. For applications that do not require the output to be trains of solitons, a random OFC can be formed by using a normally dispersive HNF instead of the soliton-compression DDF. Instead of soliton compression, OWB plays the key role for broad comb spectrum formation. Similar comb characteristics have been achieved experimentally except that the resultant frequency comb is no longer a train of solitons. However, it offers better robustness due to the absence of MI-amplified noise.

Methods

Experiment setup with dispersion-decreasing fiber

The random Kerr OFC has been constructed with an FBG, a REF, a VOA, a DDF, and an optical isolator (ISO) cascaded in series as shown in Fig. 2a. The high-reflectivity FBG has a Bragg wavelength of 1547.73 nm and a bandwidth of 0.1 nm. The REF with a length of 1 km, is a G.657 compatible SMF whose Rayleigh scattering strength is enhanced by ~ 13 dB at the wavelength around 1550 nm through UV exposure. The DDF is a dispersion-decreasing fiber, with a GVD parameter, β2 varies from − 9.82 ps2/km to 0.38 ps2/km linearly along the fiber. The VOA is used to control the power delivered into the DDF. The FBG and REF are placed within a styrofoam box to reduce the influences from the external perturbations. The pump is a pulsed laser which is prepared by modulating a tunable CW laser followed by optical amplification and filtering (see Supplementary Materials for details), so the wavelength, power, duration and repetition rate of the pump pulses can be adjusted. The pump pulses are delivered into the FBG through a polarization controller (PC) for polarization optimization. The comb output is measured using an optical spectrum analyzer (Yokogawa AQ6370C) with a resolution of 0.05 nm and an autocorrelator (Femtochrome FR103-MN).

Experiment setup with normally dispersive fiber

The experiment setup is very similar to Fig. 2a. The only difference is that the DDF is replaced with a 1-km long normally dispersive HNF with a nonlinear coefficient of γ = 10 (km⋅W)−1 and GVD parameter of β2 = 2.18 ps2/km at 1550 nm. All the others, including the pulsed laser and the measurement equipment, are the same.

Coherence measurement

The output from the random Kerr OFC is first filtered by a narrow-band optical tunable filter to select individual comb line that needs to be measured. The resultant light beam after passing a PC combines with an external CW narrow-linewidth ( ~ 5 kHz) laser (Teraxion NLL-1550) beam through an optical coupler. The wavelength of the external laser can be fine tuned to close to that of the comb line to have the combined beat signals with frequencies around 2-3 GHz after the optical coupler, which are then delivered into a 12-GHz photodetector followed by an oscilloscope (Tektronix DPO71604SX) with a sampling rate of 25 GS/s (see Supplementary Materials for detailed schematic).
