Evaluation of Fibromyalgia Frequency in Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria Patients Referred to a Tertiary Dermatology Outpatient Clinic
Authors: Fatma Etgü, Derya Çırakoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) and fibromyalgia (FM) share overlapping features such as autoimmunity, psychological influence, and female predominance. Previous studies show conflicting results on FM prevalence in CSU patients.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the prevalence of concurrent FM in patients with CSU.MethodsA cross-sectional study including 257 CSU patients and age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Data collected included demographics, disease duration, severity (UAS-7), and quality of life (DLQI). FM was diagnosed using the 2016 ACR criteria.ResultsThe prevalence of FM was 18.98% in CSU patients and 4.43% for the control group (P<0.01). The mean WPI and SSS scores were higher in the CSU group; WPI was statistically significant (P < 0.01), whereas SSS showed no statistically significant difference (P = 0.05). CSU patients with FM had longer disease duration, higher disease severity, and more impairment in life. Furthermore, female CSU patients had more FM than did male CSU patients (P<0.001).ConclusionsFM was more prevalent among patients with CSU compared to the control group. Female sex, higher disease severity, and impaired quality of life were significantly associated with FM in CSU patients. Given the reduced quality of life observed in individuals with both conditions, clinicians should consider evaluating CSU patients, especially those with severe symptoms, for FM to ensure accurate diagnosis and comprehensive management. Further studies are needed to clarify the nature of this association.Keywords: Chronic spontaneous urticaria, Fibromyalgia, Comorbidity, Prevalence

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a common skin disorder characterized by recurrent wheals and/or angioedema lasting longer than six weeks without an identifiable trigger [1,3]. The prevalence of CSU in the general population is estimated to be between 0.5% and 5% [4,5]. Its course is unpredictable, with symptoms persisting up to two years in about two thirds of patients, five years in approximately one third, and more than 5–10 years in over 1% of cases [6,7].

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic pain syndrome marked by widespread musculoskeletal pain and various symptoms, including fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive difficulties, and psychiatric complaints [8,9]. Pain is persistent and can be triggered by physical or emotional stress. FM affects about 2% of the population, predominantly females aged 50 to 60 years [10,11].

Various dermatological conditions have been reported to be associated with FM, including xerosis, lichen simplex chronicus, neurotic excoriations, acneiform disorders, chronic urticaria (CU), hidradenitis suppurativa, contact allergy, psoriasis vulgaris, seborrheic dermatitis, vitiligo, hyperhidrosis, and burning sensations of the skin and mucous membranes [12]. The underlying mechanisms linking FM and these cutaneous disorders remain unclear. Emotional stress may increase the risk of skin diseases in FM patients. Additionally, the predominant symptom of FM, i.e., pain, is thought to be related to pruritus through neuroimmune and neuroglial interactions, loss of spinal cord inhibition, peripheral and central sensitization, increased opioid receptor activity, inflammatory mediators, and mast cell activation. Autoimmune processes might also contribute to the co-occurrence of skin diseases in FM [12].

Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the coexistence of FM and CSU. Identifying the prevalence and factors associated with this comorbidity is important for optimizing patient management and treatment.

Objectives

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of FM among CSU patients and to investigate the clinical factors influencing their co-occurrence.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

The study was conducted at Ordu University Training and Research Hospital between April 2023 and October 2024. The Ordu University Ethics Committee granted ethical permission prior to the study (approval number: 2024/188).

Patients aged 18 years and older diagnosed with CSU were included in the study. Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals without a history of urticaria or fibromyalgia were recruited as controls. Patients with any known autoimmune, inflammatory, or systemic disease were excluded. Individuals currently experiencing infection, pregnant or lactating women, and those using immunosuppressive or systemic corticosteroid therapy within the preceding three months were also excluded. Additionally, participants with psychiatric disorders that could interfere with study assessments were not included. These criteria were applied to minimize confounding factors and to ensure a homogeneous study population. CSU patients were diagnosed by dermatologists, while FM diagnoses were made by physical therapy and rehabilitation specialists, who are medical doctors with specialized training in musculoskeletal and pain disorders. Demographic data, including sex, age, and disease duration, were collected, and disease severity along with quality of life were evaluated.

Other dermatological, rheumatological, or systemic diseases were excluded based on a thorough review of the patients’ medical history, previous clinical examination, and relevant laboratory and radiological evaluations.

Patients with chronic inducible urticaria (CINDU) were excluded based on clinical evaluation and medical history, as the study focused on CSU.

Evaluation of the CSU Severity

Urticaria severity was assessed with the urticaria activity score-7 (UAS-7). UAS7 was determined by adding the scores for the number of hives each day and the degree of itching over a period of seven days. Increased scores indicate increased disease activity; UAS7 readings range from 0 to 42 [13].

Assessment of Quality of Life

The life quality of the patients with CSU was evaluated with The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), which was first developed in 1994 by Finlay et al. Total ratings range from 0 to 30, where worse quality of life is indicated by higher scores [14]. In 2006, Ozturkcan et al. carried out a validity and reliability assessment of the Turkish version of the DLQI [15].

Diagnosis of FM

The 2016 diagnostic criteria of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) were used to diagnose FM. Two measures made up these criteria: the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) and the Widespread Pain Index (WPI). The Fibromyalgia Score is the total of the WPI and SSS values. How many of the 19 subregions (upper left, upper right, lower left, lower right, and axial) experienced pain during the preceding week is indicated by the Widespread Pain Index. The overall score might be anything from 0 to 19. The SSS scale assigns a score of 0–3 points (0–1) to tiredness, restless waking, and cognitive symptoms based on the intensity of symptoms over the preceding week as well as the presence of headache, depression, and cramping in the abdomen during the previous six months. The total score is between 0 and 12 [8].

Sample Calculation

Sample size was calculated as 64 CSU patients and 64 control individuals for a 95% confidence interval (CI) and 0.8 power.

The number of cases was calculated based on the total number of patients who met the inclusion criteria for CSU and FM diagnosis, as outlined in the study’s methodology. Patients were selected from the clinical database, and their demographic and clinical data were reviewed to ensure eligibility. The final sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients who consented to participate in the study.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical tool MedCalc (version 20.009; Ostend, Belgium) was used to conduct the statistical analysis. The data are statistically described using numbers, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD). The groups were tested for conformance to a normal distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The patient and control groups were compared using an independent t-test. The groups’ category data were compared using the chi-square test. The groups’ numerical statistics are shown graphically as bar graphs. Graphs with stacked percentage columns were used to display categorical data. When interpreting the data, a significance level of P<0.05 was used.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the prevalence of FM in patients with CSU was significantly higher compared to the control group (18.98% vs. 4.43%, P<0.01). Additionally, CSU patients with FM had longer disease duration, higher disease severity, and greater impairment in quality of life. Female CSU patients exhibited a higher prevalence of FM than did their male counterparts (P<0.001). These findings suggest a significant association between CSU and FM, with disease severity, sex, and quality of life playing key roles. Further research is needed to explore the relationship between these two conditions.

FM prevalence in the general population ranges from 1.7% to 6% [12]. Our study found FM prevalence in CSU patients to be 18.98%, aligning with previous studies [16–23]. Variations in FM prevalence can be attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria, such as the ACR 2016 criteria used in this study, compared to the 1990 and 2010 criteria used in others [17–23]. Additionally, the failure to exclude other autoimmune and inflammatory diseases and smaller sample sizes in previous studies may affect FM prevalence estimates.

Mathkor et al. found that CU patients were more likely to experience widespread pain as well as FM symptoms, including morning stiffness, fatigue, headaches, psychiatric symptoms like anxiety and depression, sleep disturbances, and irritable bowel syndrome [20]. Our study aligned with these findings, showing a higher prevalence of FM in CSU patients [19–23]. However, some earlier studies found no significant difference in FM prevalence among CU patients [16,18]. Notably, Yener et al. reported that although FM prevalence was similar in both CU and control patients, FM-positive CU patients had a longer duration of FM symptoms, more tender points, worse quality of life scores, and higher pain scores. These factors were correlated with the severity and duration of chronic inducible urticaria (CIU) [16].

FM is more commonly observed in females [10, 11], and our study confirmed this trend. However, some studies found no significant sex differences between FM-positive CU patients [21]. The relationship between age and FM remains unclear, with studies showing varying results regarding age differences between urticaria patients with and without FM [17,18,21,22].

Pain assessment methods varied across studies. In our study, we used the Widespread Pain Index (WPI), and we found that widespread pain was more common in CSU patients compared to controls, which aligns with the results of Mathkor et al. and Oktayoğlu et al. [19,20]. In one study, chronic widespread pain was found in 52% of CU patients, although not all met FM diagnostic criteria [19]. In contrast, other studies used the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain assessment, with Yener et al. finding significantly higher VAS scores in the CU group, while Koca et al. and Acar et al. found similar scores between the urticaria and control groups [12,21,22]. Additionally, tender points were evaluated in several studies, with Torresani et al. and Yener et al. reporting more tender points in CU patients compared to controls [16,23].

In our study, the duration of CSU was associated with the prevalence of FM, consistent with previous studies [21]. However, some studies did not find a correlation between urticaria duration and FM prevalence [17,18,22]. Yener et al. noted that although FM prevalence was similar between CU and control patients, urticaria duration in FM-positive CU patients was correlated with the VAS score, tender points, and the overall duration of FM symptoms [16].

We also assessed the effect of urticaria severity on FM frequency and found that FM was associated with the UAS, consistent with previous literature [16,20,22]. Similarly, Yener et al. reported that UAS was correlated with the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ), VAS scores, tender points, and FM symptom duration in addition to its correlation with FM prevalence [16]. Koca et al. also found that UAS was correlated with FM severity, sleep disturbances, anxiety, and pain scores in FM-positive CU patients [22]. In contrast, Hapa et al. did not observe any effect of UAS on FM frequency in CU patients [18].

Both CSU and FM are known to significantly impair quality of life. In this study, we assessed how the quality of life of CSU patients influenced FM prevalence. We found that the DLQI was correlated with FM frequency, consistent with previous studies [16]. In contrast, Acar et al. did not find any link between DLQI and FM prevalence, though they reported a significant correlation between DLQI and FM severity [21]. Another study showed a correlation between the FIQ and DLQI [22]. Similarly, Oktayoğlu et al. found that while CSU patients with and without FM did not differ in anxiety and depression assessments, CSU patients with FM had worse health-related quality of life scores [19].

Previous studies have suggested that the coexistence of CSU and FM may be linked to the increased frequency of autoimmune disorders in both conditions [23]. Some studies have examined the role of autoimmunity by evaluating the autologous serum skin test (ASST) in CSU patients with FM, finding higher ASST positivity in this subgroup [17,20,23]. However, other studies did not find a significant association between FM and ASST positivity [18]. In our study, we excluded patients with known autoimmune, inflammatory, or systemic diseases to reduce potential confounding factors. Despite this exclusion, we found a significantly higher prevalence of FM in the CSU group compared to the controls, suggesting that subclinical immune dysregulation, rather than overt autoimmune disease, may contribute to the pathophysiological overlap between CSU and FM. The recent literature supports the idea that altered immune function can be present in both FM and CSU, even in the absence of diagnosed autoimmune conditions. This comorbidity may reflect shared immune mechanisms that warrant further investigation.

It is well known that FM is comorbid with a wide range of cutaneous diseases, including CSU [20]. In a casecontrol study, the authors compared skin findings in patients with and without FM and found that FM patients reported a higher frequency of xerosis, neurotic excoriations, lichen simplex chronicus, seborrheic dermatitis, dermographism, pruritus, burning, and stinging sensations. The study emphasized the psychocutaneous disorders observed in FM, highlighting the close relationship between the brain and skin, both of which share a common embryonic origin and are influenced by the same hormones and neurotransmitters, contributing to neurogenic inflammation [24].

Although no direct link between urticaria and FM was reported, several studies have demonstrated an increased risk of FM in patients with CSU [20,23]. The underlying pathogenesis between CSU and FM remains unclear, but several theories have been proposed. First, both conditions have been associated with an increased frequency of autoimmune disorders. Additionally, psychosocial factors play a significant role in the pathogenesis of both diseases [20]. In FM, alterations in mast cells, inflammatory cytokines, collagen mechanisms, skin circulation, the autonomic nervous system, and increased opioid receptors have been reported [12].

Growing evidence supports the contributory role of inflammation in FM. Proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, IL-1beta, TNF, and IL-10, released from monocytes, T cells, or macrophages, have been found elevated in FM patients [25]. These cytokines contribute to various FM symptoms, including fatigue, sleep disturbances, and depression. Inflammation is also well-established in dermatological diseases, including CSU [12]. The co-occurrence of CSU and FM may result from shared pathophysiological mechanisms involving peripheral nerves and neurotransmitters, such as substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), both of which play a role in both diseases. Mast cells may also contribute to the pathogenesis of both conditions [20].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample size of 257 CSU patients is larger than that of previous studies, it may still not fully represent the general population, limiting the robustness of the prevalence estimates. While CSU prevalence can reach up to 2% in the general population and FM prevalence is approximately 8% in the USA, our sample may not fully capture these rates. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader populations.

Second, important confounders such as ethnicity, depression, and anxiety, which could influence both CSU and FM, were not investigated. Psychological factors, in particular, are common in both conditions and may exacerbate symptoms or contribute to comorbidity. The lack of consideration of these factors may limit the interpretation of the results.

Moreover, the study does not propose a clinical model for risk stratification or management of CSU patients with comorbid FM. Developing such a model would be valuable to guiding diagnosis and treatment, helping to identify high-risk patients and personalize clinical strategies. Future studies should focus on creating such models to improve patient care.

Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents the establishment of a causal relationship between CSU and FM. The absence of provocation tests for CINDU also limits the ability to distinguish between CSU and CINDU, potentially affecting diagnostic accuracy in some patients.

Strengths

One strength of this study is the relatively larger sample size compared to previous studies, with 257 CSU patients, which enhances the reliability of the findings. Another strength is that all patients were evaluated by both dermatologists and physical therapy and rehabilitation specialists to minimize the risk of over- or underdiagnosis of FM. Additionally, patients with systemic or cutaneous comorbidities that could also influence FM prevalence were excluded, allowing for a more focused analysis of the effect of CSU on FM.

Recommendations

While this study highlights the prevalence of FM in patients with CSU, it does not provide specific recommendations for managing CSU patients with comorbid FM in daily clinical practice. Future research should aim to establish screening strategies to identify FM in CSU patients and to develop management guidelines for those with both conditions. Implementing these strategies could lead to more personalized and effective treatment plans, improving patient care and outcomes.

Generalizability

The results of this study are potentially generalizable to adult patients with CSU, particularly in similar clinical settings. However, as the study was conducted at a single center with a specific population, caution should be taken when applying the findings to broader or more diverse populations. Further multicenter studies are recommended to enhance external validity.

Conclusion

In this study, the prevalence of FM was higher in patients with CSU compared to the control group. Female CSU patients had a higher prevalence of FM than did male patients. In CSU patients with FM, urticaria severity, disease duration, and DLQI were associated with FM prevalence. Further studies are needed to investigate the relationship between CSU and FM. Given that patients with CSU may require additional therapy, clinicians should be aware of the potential coexistence of FM with CSU and consider consulting a rheumatologist. Since patients with both FM and CSU experience a significantly lower quality of life than those with only CSU, a thorough evaluation for FM diagnosis is essential.
