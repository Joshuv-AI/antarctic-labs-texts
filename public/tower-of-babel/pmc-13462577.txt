Microbial levan potentiates hepatic retention and antitumor activity of a PEGylated benzimidazole–curcumin nanocomplex through TLR2–FXR/FGF15-associated immunometabolic remodeling in experimental liver cancer
Authors: Heba Shawky, Noha E. Ibrahim, Mai N. Amer, Aisha A. K. Al-Ashmawy, Olfat A. Hammam, Dalia B. Fayed, Amany S. Maghraby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite major advances in nanomedicine, therapeutic efficacy remains constrained by unfavorable host responses that influence nanotherapeutic retention, biodistribution, and activity. Biological strategies capable of enhancing nanomedicine performance through modulation of host-response pathways, rather than additional nanoparticle engineering, remain largely unexplored. Herein, we investigated whether a microbial levan produced by Weissella paramesenteroides could potentiate the therapeutic activity of a PEGylated β-cyclodextrin-capped benzimidazole–curcumin nanocomplex (BMPE–Cur) in an Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC)-induced hepatic tumor model. Pharmacokinetic, biochemical, histopathological, angiogenic, immunological, molecular, and computational analyses were performed to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and explore underlying mechanisms. The co-administration of levan increased hepatic BMPE accumulation by 2.3-fold and significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of BMPE–Cur, improving survival to 95.45% compared with 60.66% mortality in untreated tumor-bearing mice. Combination therapy produced 86% inhibition of angiogenesis, markedly suppressed tumor-associated biomarkers, oxidative stress, inflammatory mediators, and immunosuppressive cell populations, and restored hepatic function. These effects were accompanied by reduced expression of components of the TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, including an 80.05% reduction in MyD88 and a 78.93% reduction in NF-κB, together with 6.59-fold and 7.45-fold increases in FXR and FGF15 expression, respectively. Molecular docking supported potential interactions of BMPE, curcumin, and levan-derived structural motifs with TLR2, while isobolographic analysis demonstrated predominantly synergistic interactions across multiple biological endpoints. These findings support the potential of microbial levan as a promising biological potentiator of nanotherapeutic efficacy, with concurrent enhancement of hepatic nanocomplex retention and coordinated immunometabolic remodeling. More broadly, microbial exopolysaccharides may represent a promising class of host-response modulators, supporting a therapeutic paradigm in which optimizing the biological environment may complement conventional nanocarrier engineering to improve the performance of cancer nanomedicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-64576-8.

Introduction

Liver cancer remains one of the most aggressive malignancies worldwide and is associated with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic responsiveness, accompanied by profound alterations in redox balance, immune regulation, and metabolic activity1. Despite tremendous advances in targeted and immune-based therapies, treatment options remain constrained by therapeutic resistance, dose-limiting toxicities, and disease heterogeneity, while curative interventions such as surgical resection and liver transplantation remain applicable only to a limited proportion of patients2. These challenges underscore the need for multimodal therapeutic strategies capable of simultaneously targeting oxidative, inflammatory, immune, and metabolic pathways involved in tumor progression.

Polymeric nanomedicines have emerged as promising platforms for improving the solubility, stability, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy of anticancer agents3. Through controlled drug release and improved pharmacokinetic behavior, nanotherapeutic systems can enhance drug exposure while reducing systemic toxicity4. In our previous study, we developed a PEGylated β-cyclodextrin-capped benzimidazole–curcumin nanocomplex (BMPE–Cur) that combined the complementary anticancer activities of benzimidazole and curcumin within a pH-responsive nano-delivery platform5. The nanocomplex demonstrated sustained drug release, potent antiproliferative activity against HepG2 cells, and favorable pharmacokinetic characteristics in healthy animals, supporting its potential as a nanotherapeutic platform for liver cancer5,6.

However, whether modulation of host biological responses could further influence the in vivo disposition and therapeutic performance of BMPE–Cur under pathological conditions remained unknown. This challenge is particularly relevant in metabolically active organs such as the liver, where hepatic clearance mechanisms, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation can substantially affect nanocarrier disposition, tissue retention, and therapeutic responsiveness, thereby limiting treatment efficacy7,8. Consequently, improving nanotherapeutic outcomes may require not only optimization of carrier design but also strategies capable of favorably modulating the host biological responses. In this context, increasing evidence suggests that biologically active macromolecules can influence therapeutic responsiveness by modulating host immune, inflammatory, and metabolic pathways9.

Among these, microbial exopolysaccharides have attracted growing attention owing to their ability to regulate oxidative homeostasis, inflammatory signaling, and innate immune responses10,11. Levan, a β-(2→6)-linked fructan synthesized by several bacterial species, has been associated with antioxidant, immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities in diverse experimental settings12,13. These biological properties suggest that microbial levans may function as adjunct modulators capable of influencing nanotherapeutic disposition and efficacy through favorable remodeling of the host biological environment. Consistent with this concept, our previous studies demonstrated the capacity of lactic acid bacteria-derived levans to attenuate inflammatory and oxidative pathology while modulating disease-associated molecular responses in multiple experimental models14–16. Nevertheless, whether bacterial levans can enhance nanotherapeutic performance by modulating host biological responses that influence nanocomplex disposition and antitumor efficacy remains largely unexplored.

One plausible biological framework through which microbial polysaccharides may influence therapeutic responsiveness involves the gut–liver axis, a bidirectional communication network linking intestinal microbial signals with hepatic immune and metabolic homeostasis17. Within this biological framework, Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), a key component of the innate immune system, recognizes pathogen-associated and damage-associated molecular patterns, thereby initiating immune responses18. In the liver, activation of TLR2 triggers the MyD88/NF-κB signaling cascade, promoting inflammatory cytokine production, angiogenesis, and tumor-supportive immune responses that contribute to hepatic carcinogenesis and disease progression19. Beyond immune regulation, the gut–liver axis also encompasses metabolic signaling pathways that govern hepatic homeostasis and xenobiotic metabolism20. A key component of this axis is the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and its downstream effector, fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15), which together form a central regulatory pathway linking intestinal and hepatic physiology via bile acid–mediated signaling21. In addition to maintaining metabolic homeostasis, FXR/FGF15 signaling regulates hepatic inflammatory responses, drug-metabolizing enzymes, and transporter systems involved in xenobiotic disposition22,23.

Given that dysregulation of FXR/FGF15 signaling has been associated with liver injury, metabolic dysfunction, and hepatocarcinogenesis, whereas restoration of this pathway has been linked to improved hepatic function and therapeutic responsiveness24,25, the modulation of this pathway may influence the pharmacokinetic behavior and hepatic retention of therapeutic agents.

Based on these insights, we sought to determine whether a Weissella paramesenteroides-derived levan could function as a biological potentiator of nanotherapeutic efficacy in experimental liver cancer. Given the established roles of the TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB pathway in tumor-associated inflammatory activation and the FXR/FGF15 pathway in hepatic metabolic homeostasis and xenobiotic disposition, these complementary signaling axes were investigated as candidate mechanisms through which microbial levan might influence nanotherapeutic performance. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the effects of levan co-administration on the pharmacokinetic behavior, therapeutic efficacy, and mechanistic determinants of BMPE–Cur activity in an EAC-induced hepatic tumor model. Although this model does not fully recapitulate the molecular heterogeneity of human hepatocellular carcinoma, it reproduces several hallmarks of tumor-associated hepatic pathology, including angiogenesis, oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and immune dysregulation26. These pathological features provide a biologically relevant context for investigating host-response pathways, including TLR2-associated inflammatory signaling. Through integrated biodistribution, biochemical, histopathological, immunological, and molecular analyses, we investigated whether microbial levan-mediated host modulation is associated with enhanced hepatic nanocomplex retention and antitumor efficacy without inducing additional hepatotoxicity.

Materials and methods

Chemicals, reagents, and drug synthesis

The parent benzimidazole compound, 1-(6-(1 H-benzo[d]imidazol-2-yl)−2-methylpyridin-3-yl) ethanone (BMPE), was synthesized as previously reported by Abdel-Karim et al.27, whereas the polymeric benzimidazole–curcumin nanocomplex (BMPE–Cur) was prepared according to our optimized protocol5. Unless otherwise stated, all chemicals and analytical-grade solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

Production and purification of the microbial levan

Levan was produced from Weissella paramesenteroides via submerged fermentation28. Briefly, 5 L of de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth were inoculated with 2% (v/v) actively growing bacterial culture (~ 10⁸ CFU/mL) and incubated under controlled conditions for 24–48 h. Following fermentation, cultures were centrifuged for 20 min at 4 °C to remove bacterial cells. The supernatant was adjusted to pH 6.8 and heat-treated at 100 °C for 30 min to inactivate enzymes. After clarification by centrifugation, levan was precipitated using an equal volume of ice-cold absolute ethanol and incubated at 4 °C for 18 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with distilled water, dialyzed against deionized water at 4 °C for 24 h, lyophilized, and stored at 4 °C until use.

Structural and physicochemical characterization

The structural features of the bacterial exopolysaccharide were evaluated using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Jasco 6100, Japan) over 400–4000 cm⁻¹, proton nuclear magnetic resonance (^1H NMR; Bruker 400 MHz, DMSO-d₆), and UV–Vis spectroscopy (T80+, PG Instruments, UK; 190–800 nm). These analyses were performed to confirm functional group composition and structural integrity before in vivo studies. The physicochemical characteristics of BMPE–Cur, including its average particle size (467.7 nm) and zeta potential (− 28.24 mV), as well as its pharmacokinetic profile in healthy animals, have been reported previously5,6. Therefore, the present study focused on evaluating the influence of microbial levan on the therapeutic performance and endpoint hepatic retention of BMPE–Cur in a pathological tumor model rather than re-characterizing the nanocomplex.

Animal model and housing conditions

Seventy female Swiss albino mice (22–25 g; 8–10 weeks old) were procured from the Animal House of the National Research Centre (NRC, Cairo, Egypt). The animals were housed in polypropylene cages under standardized environmental conditions, including a 12 h light/dark cycle, controlled ambient temperature (25 ± 2 °C), and relative humidity of 30–60%, within a well-ventilated facility. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum. Before the initiation of experimental procedures, all animals were acclimatized for one week. In compliance with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0, animal welfare was stringently monitored throughout the study. Health assessments were performed twice daily by a blinded veterinarian at the NRC Animal House, and clinical observations included monitoring for signs of pain or distress, fatigue, erythema of the skin or fur, abnormal ocular discharge, and mortality. Behavioral and neurological parameters, such as ataxia, depressive-like manifestations, hypoactivity or hyperactivity, and changes in locomotor function, were systematically documented to enable early detection of potential adverse effects.

Tumor induction

Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC) cells were obtained from the National Cancer Institute, Cairo University (Cairo, Egypt). Ascitic tumor cells were maintained via serial intraperitoneal (i.p.) passage in female Swiss albino mice as previously described29. For tumor induction, viable EAC cells (5.0 × 10⁶ cells/mouse in 250 µL PBS) were injected i.p according to the method described by Dos Santos et al.25, with minor modifications.

Dose selection and treatment regimen

Seven to ten days post-inoculation, mice exhibiting stable tumor development were randomly assigned to experimental groups (Table 1). Dose selection was based on previously reported safety and pharmacological data. BMPE–Cur (5 mg/kg/day) was selected within its established safe range27, and the parent BMPE compound was administered at a dose of 0.13 mg/kg/day to match its equivalent loading within the nanocomplex5, thereby enabling a direct comparison between the free drug and nanoformulated counterpart. Doxorubicin (DOX; 10 mg/kg/week) was used as a reference chemotherapeutic agent following established protocols30. For the bacterial levan, dose selection was guided by preliminary tolerability and feasibility assessments, given that this study represents its first in vivo evaluation. Initial dose-ranging experiments investigated daily levan doses between 0.05 and 1 mg/kg to define a tolerable exposure window. A dose of 100 µg/kg/day was selected as the highest well-tolerated dose for repeated administration over 15 consecutive days. During preliminary dose-optimization experiments (0.05–1 mg/kg/day), higher levan doses appeared to enhance the therapeutic effects of BMPE–Cur but were also associated with reduced survival. Therefore, 100 µg/kg/day was selected as the optimal dose providing the best balance between efficacy and tolerability (pilot data not shown). Different treatments were administered for 15 consecutive days according to previously established protocols31. At the end of the experimental period, body weight and abdominal circumference were recorded before sample collection.

Sampling

After the completion of the treatment protocol, blood samples were collected from the tail vein into heparinized tubes, and animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.). The collected blood was divided into two equal portions. One portion was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 10 min at room temperature to obtain plasma, which was subsequently stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. The second portion was used for the isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll® Paque Plus (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Liver tissues were excised, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and subdivided into three portions. The first portion was immediately snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C for molecular analyses. The second portion was fixed in 10% formaldehyde prepared in PBS for histopathological evaluation. The third portion was homogenized in ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4) to prepare 10% (w/v) tissue homogenates. These homogenates were centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent biochemical and immunological assays. For chromatographic quantification of BMPE and curcumin in liver tissue, an aliquot of the homogenate supernatant was mixed with acetonitrile at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v) and centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 15 min at room temperature. The clarified supernatants were then subjected to LC–MS analysis.

Pharmacokinetic and hepatic biodistribution analysis

The pharmacokinetic profile and hepatic distribution of the nanocomplex were assessed using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Quantitative determination of parent (unmetabolized) BMPE and curcumin in liver tissues was performed by liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–ESI–MS/MS). Chromatographic separation was conducted using an ExionLC AC system, coupled to a SCIEX Triple Quad 5500 + MS/MS instrument equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source (AB SCIEX, Redwood City, CA, USA)6. Milli-Q water and LC-grade acetonitrile were used throughout the analysis. Separation was achieved on a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) maintained at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of a binary mixture of 0.1% formic acid in water (40%, v/v; solvent A) and acetonitrile (60%, v/v; solvent B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The injection volume was 3 µL per sample. As BMPE is an in-house synthesized compound without a commercially available reference standard, calibration curves were generated by spiking blank liver homogenate supernatants with known concentrations (5–500 ng/mL) of purified BMPE and curcumin. The calibration equations obtained were as follows: BMPE, Y = 2896.11613X + 10.46878 (r = 0.99953, r² = 0.99906); curcumin, Y = 9737.89729X − 1771.52541 (r = 0.99921, r² = 0.99841) (Supplementary Data File 1). Mass spectrometric detection was performed in positive ionization mode under optimized conditions: curtain gas, 25 psi; ion spray voltage, 5500 V; source temperature, 400 °C; and ion source gases 1 and 2 set at 55 psi. Quantification was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM), with compound-specific transitions and optimized parameters summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Data acquisition and processing were performed using Analyst® software version 1.5 (AB SCIEX, Redwood City, CA, USA).

In silico metabolic predictions

The chemical structures and canonical MOL files of the principal pharmacophores examined in this study, namely BMPE, curcumin, and fructose (the predominant monosaccharide constituent of levan, ~ 80%28, were retrieved from the Cheminfo platform (http://www.cheminfo.org/Chemistry/Name_to_structure/index.html). These structural datasets were subsequently employed to predict Phase I and Phase II metabolic pathways, with particular focus on potential interactions with and inhibition of cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes, using the PreMetabo online prediction tool (https://premetabo.webservice.bmdrc.org)32.

Biochemical assessment of liver functions and redox status

Liver function parameters, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total and direct bilirubin (BiL), and alkaline phosphatase (AP), were measured in plasma using the corresponding commercial kits from Biodiagnostic (Cairo, Egypt) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Redox status markers, including nitric oxide (NO), malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), catalase, and superoxide dismutase activities, were assessed in liver homogenates using the corresponding Biodiagnostic (Cairo, Egypt) kits.

Evaluation of tumor-associated and angiogenic biomarkers

Peritoneal angiogenesis was qualitatively assessed by surgically exposing the peritoneal cavity of each mouse for macroscopic evaluation of vascularization, following the method described by Hajra et al.33. Evaluations were performed independently by two investigators blinded to treatment allocation, yielding high inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.903; > 90% reliability). Vascular density was quantified using ImageJ software26. To validate the automated analysis, a subset of samples was additionally subjected to manual planimetric assessment, demonstrating strong concordance between the two methods (R² = 0.8375). In parallel, plasma and liver tissue homogenates were analyzed for alpha-fetoprotein (AFP; Cat# SL0035Mo), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA; Cat# SL0120Mo), and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR; Cat# SL0561Mo) using the corresponding mouse ELISA kits (SunLong Biotech, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cut-off values were determined based on the minimum detection limits specified for each assay. Detailed performance characteristics, including assay sensitivity, dynamic range, and intra- and inter-assay variability, are provided in the respective manufacturer manuals and can be accessed using the cited catalog numbers.

Immunological studies

Immunological parameters were quantified in plasma, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and liver tissue homogenates using commercially available mouse ELISA kits (SunLong Biotech, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The analyzed cytokines and immune markers included interleukin (IL)−6 (Cat# SL0326Mo), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (Cat# SL0547Mo), IL-10 (Cat# SL0310Mo), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)−1 (Cat# SL0916Mo), CD4⁺ T helper (Th) lymphocytes (Cat# SL0716Mo), CD8⁺ cytotoxic T (Tc) lymphocytes (Cat# SL0717Mo), and CD25⁺ regulatory T (Treg) lymphocytes (Cat# SL0875Mo). Hepatic expression of Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) was determined using an in-house enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) adapted from the method originally described by Engvall and Perlman34 and later modified by Razin et al.35, with minor modifications. Briefly, flat-bottom polyvinyl microtiter plates (ALTO, Italy) were coated overnight at 4 °C with 50 µL of liver homogenate supernatants (1 µg protein) diluted in carbonate buffer (pH 9.6). Plates were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.05% Tween-20, followed by blocking with 200 µL of 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS–0.05% Tween-20 for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, 50 µL of rabbit polyclonal anti-TLR2 antibody (1:1000; Abcam, UK; Cat. No. ab213676) diluted in PBS–0.05% Tween-20 was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Bound primary antibodies were detected using 50 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H&L) (1:10,000; Abcam, UK; Cat# ab205718) prepared in blocking buffer. Color development was achieved by adding 100 µL of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. No. N301) and incubating the plates in the dark at room temperature. The reaction was terminated with 50 µL of 2 N sulfuric acid, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a Varioskan™ LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cut-off value for positivity was defined as twice the optical density at 490 nm (OD₄₉₀) of the negative control.

Molecular analyses

Hepatic mRNA expression levels of myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88), nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), farnesoid X receptor (FXR), and fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15) were quantified in control and treated Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC) mice using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues using the RNeasy Kit (Cat#74004; QIAGEN, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative amplification was carried out on a Rotor-Gene® Q real-time PCR system (QIAGEN, Germany) in a final reaction volume of 50 µL. Each reaction contained 1 µL of iScript reverse transcriptase for one-step RT-PCR (Cat. No. 345–0412; Bio-Rad, USA), 25 µL of 2× SYBR® Green RT-PCR reaction mix supplemented with magnesium chloride and 0.4 mM of each deoxynucleotide triphosphate (dATP, dCTP, dGTP, and dTTP), 200 ng of purified RNA template, and 10 µM of each gene-specific primer. The final volume was adjusted with nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions comprised an initial reverse transcription step at 50 °C for 10 min, followed by enzyme activation at 95 °C for 5 min. Amplification was performed over 45 cycles, each consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and primer-specific annealing at the designated temperature for 30 s, during which fluorescence signals were acquired. To confirm amplification specificity and exclude primer–dimer formation, a post-amplification melting curve analysis was conducted (95 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 1 min, followed by incremental increases of 0.5 °C per cycle for 80 cycles). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative threshold cycle (ΔΔCt) method and normalized to the geometric mean expression of the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Primer amplification efficiency was validated through standard curve analysis based on the linear relationship between Ct values and the logarithm of DNA copy number, assuming an amplification factor of 2. All primer sets exhibited amplification efficiencies ranging from 92.14% to 101.77%, with correlation coefficients (R²) exceeding 0.99. Primer sequences, annealing temperatures, and amplification efficiencies are provided in Supplementary Table 214,36–38.

Histopathological examination

Formalin-fixed liver tissues were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (70–100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm) were mounted on glass slides and stored at room temperature. Before staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through descending ethanol concentrations. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed following Suvarna and Layton39. Sections were examined under a light microscope, representative images captured digitally, and histopathological analysis was conducted by investigators blinded to the experimental groups.

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular docking analysis was performed to explore the potential capacity of nanocomplex and levan-derived constituents (benzimidazole, curcumin, and fructose units) to directly interact with the TLR2 ligand-binding domain. The synthetic phospholipid PE-DTPA (native ligand) and the lipopeptide Pam2CSK4 (TLR2 agonist; PDB ID: 3A7C) were included as reference controls40. The crystallographic structure of TLR2 was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) and subjected to docking using AutoDock Vina implemented in the PyRx platform (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/)41, following established protocols42,43. Protein preparation and energy minimization were performed using the YASARA server44, while ligand–protein interactions were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2024 (https://discover.3ds.com/). Docking reliability was confirmed by re-docking the native ligand into the TLR2 binding pocket, yielding an RMSD of 0.7 Å. Simulations targeted the hydrophobic cavity corresponding to the Pam2CSK4 binding site, a region known to be competitively occluded by staphylococcal superantigen-like proteins (SSLs)45. A grid box of 25 × 25 × 25 Å was centered at X = − 25.56, Y = − 7.64, and Z = − 9.68 to encompass the entire ligand-binding domain.

Mathematical evaluation of BMPE-Cur – levan interactions

The interaction between BMPE–Cur and levan was evaluated via isobolographic analysis to determine whether their combination was additive, synergistic, or antagonistic. The Combination Index (CI) was calculated using CompuSyn software46, which applies the median-effect principle to compare the observed combined effect with that expected if the agents acted independently. In this framework, CI = 1 denotes an additive effect, CI < 1 indicates synergism, and CI > 1 indicates antagonism. This approach provides a quantitative assessment of how the two agents modulate each other’s activity.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. A priori analysis comparing the vehicle control and untreated EAC groups revealed an 11.527-fold increase in serum AFP levels in the untreated group (Fig. S1). To detect this effect with a statistical power of 95% (1 − β) at a significance level of α = 0.05, the minimum required sample size was two mice per group; however, twelve mice were allocated to each group in accordance with Dos Santos et al.26. Numerical data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Survival was analyzed using the Mantel–Cox (log-rank) test. All other outcome measures were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons following a significant overall test to control the family-wise Type I error rate within each endpoint. Each experimental group included at least six independent animals (biological replicates). For biochemical, immunological, and molecular assays, each sample was measured in technical triplicate, and the mean value for each animal was used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Structural confirmation of microbial levan

Spectroscopic analyses confirmed that the purified polysaccharide is a levan-type fructan, consistent with previously reported microbial levans (Figs. S2 – S4). The obtained spectral signatures validated both its structural identity and high purity before biological evaluation. Comprehensive spectral characterization of the microbial levan is provided in Supplementary Data File 2.

Effect of therapeutic interventions on survival rate and EAC-associated morbidity

No mortality was observed in the sham or doxorubicin (DOX) groups, whereas the untreated EAC mice exhibited a 60.66% mortality rate by day 15 (Fig. 1A). All treatments significantly improved survival (P = 0.0114), reaching 78.57% (parent BMPE), 90.23% (BMPE–Cur), 95.45% (BMPE–Cur + Levan), 100% (DOX), and 68.69% (Levan). Untreated EAC mice developed progressive ascites, evidenced by marked increases in body weight (+ 63.9 ± 2.06%) and abdominal circumference (+ 40.0 ± 4.55%) vs. baseline, and by 33.18 ± 4.83% and 39.21 ± 3.71% relative to sham control (P < 0.0001 for all; Fig. 1B – D). All interventions significantly attenuated these parameters from day 7 onward (P < 0.0001). By day 15, body weight was reduced by 28.48 ± 3.82% (parent BMPE), 27.62 ± 2.98% (BMPE–Cur), 41.92 ± 3.61% (combination), 39.40 ± 4.85% (DOX), and 25.38 ± 3.16% (Levan), with corresponding decreases in abdominal circumference of 29.06 ± 3.16%, 33.34 ± 2.87%, 46.86 ± 4.11%, 42.29 ± 3.65%, and 12.86 ± 2.28%, respectively, compared to the untreated animals. Parent BMPE normalized both body weight and abdominal circumference relative to sham control, although a modest reduction in body weight change percentage remained evident (P < 0.0001). Likewise, BMPE–Cur and levan monotherapies produced terminal body weight changes comparable to those of sham animals. In contrast, DOX and BMPE–Cur + levan induced moderate but significant reductions in terminal body weight (–19.29 ± 3.04% and − 22.65 ± 2.94%, respectively) and abdominal circumference (–20.78 ± 2.74% and − 27.05 ± 3.33%, respectively) relative to sham controls (P < 0.0001 for all comparisons). However, whereas DOX-treated mice exhibited no significant difference between baseline and terminal body weight values, the combination treatment produced a significant reduction in body weight relative to baseline (P = 0.0214). Notably, despite comparable body weights, mice receiving BMPE–Cur + levan displayed a significantly smaller abdominal circumference than those treated with DOX alone (P = 0.0178).

Levan co-treatment enhances hepatic retention of BMPE–Cur nanocomplex

Quantitative LC–MS analysis demonstrated robust analytical performance, with linearity across 1–100 ppb and recovery values ranging from 95 to 109%. Curcumin was not detected in hepatic tissue in either nanocomplex-treated group. In contrast, the benzimidazole moiety (BMPE) was readily quantifiable following BMPE–Cur administration, whereas no corresponding signal was detected in mice receiving the parent BMPE formulation. Hepatic BMPE concentration increased from 2.352 ng/g in the BMPE–Cur group to 5.371 ng/g following co-administration with levan, representing a 2.3-fold increase in hepatic accumulation (P < 0.0001). To provide additional context for the observed biodistribution profile, in silico metabolism and transporter predictions were examined (Table 2). BMPE and curcumin were predicted to inhibit CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4, whereas fructose was predicted to inhibit CYP2C9 selectively. Curcumin was further predicted to act as a substrate for UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT), sulfotransferase (SULT), and P-glycoprotein, while BMPE lacked predicted Phase II liabilities and was classified as a non-substrate for P-glycoprotein. Collectively, these findings demonstrate enhanced hepatic accumulation of BMPE following levan co-administration and provide a metabolic framework for subsequent mechanistic analyses.

Levan co-treatment enhances the antitumor and anti-angiogenic activity of BMPE–Cur

The progression of EAC-induced hepatic tumorigenesis was associated with marked elevations in circulating and hepatic tumor-associated and angiogenic biomarkers compared with sham controls (Fig. 3A, B). Serum AFP, CEA, and VEGFR increased by 70.92 ± 2.22%, 97.17 ± 3.54%, and 93.18 ± 1.16%, respectively, while hepatic levels increased by 74.49 ± 1.41%, 77.32 ± 2.4%, and 84.26 ± 2.36% (P < 0.0001 for all). All therapeutic interventions significantly reduced these biomarkers relative to untreated EAC mice. Among the tested treatments, BMPE–Cur combined with levan produced the most pronounced effect, restoring AFP, CEA, and VEGFR to near-baseline levels and significantly outperforming free BMPE, DOX, and levan monotherapy. BMPE–Cur alone ranked second and consistently achieved greater suppression of tumor-associated biomarkers than free BMPE. Although DOX, free BMPE, and levan monotherapies also reduced biomarker levels, their effects were less pronounced and failed to normalize most parameters. Consistent with these findings, untreated EAC-bearing mice exhibited extensive peritoneal angiogenesis characterized by dense, tortuous, and highly branched neovasculature (Fig. 3D), whereas sham-operated animals displayed sparse and orderly vascular architecture (Fig. 3C). All treatments significantly inhibited angiogenesis compared with untreated EAC mice (P < 0.0001; Fig. 3J). Treatment with parent BMPE resulted in moderate inhibition of angiogenesis (–68.6 ± 2.7%), with a visible reduction in vessel density compared to the untreated group (Fig. 3E). Notably, the BMPE–Cur nanocomplex induced a more pronounced anti-angiogenic effect (–76.8 ± 2.39%), characterized by fewer and thinner blood vessels (Fig. 3F) than that in BMPE group (P = 0.0061). The combination of BMPE–Cur with levan produced the strongest suppression of angiogenesis (–86 ± 2.91%), outperforming both BMPE (P < 0.0001) and BMPE–Cur alone (P = 0.0005), where only sparse, fine vessels resembling normal vasculature were detected (Fig. 3G). Treatment with DOX (Fig. 3H) produced a significant reduction of neovascularization (–61.6 ± 2.7%), with fewer and thinner blood vessels compared to untreated EAC; however, this effect was weaker than that of parent BMPE (P = 0.009), BMPE–Cur alone, and in combination (P < 0.0001 for both). Levan monotherapy produced the least effective anti-angiogenic effect among all treatments (–53.8 ± 4.66%, P = 0.0033 – < 0.0001), showing visible but reduced vessel branching relative to untreated control (Fig. 3I).

Molecular docking supports direct interaction of BMPE and levan-derived ligands with TLR2

Consistent with the observed suppression of hepatic TLR2 signaling, molecular docking analysis revealed favorable interactions between TLR2 and the tested ligands, including BMPE, curcumin, and fructose (Fig. 7). BMPE and curcumin exhibited the highest binding affinities, each yielding a docking score of − 7.0 kcal/mol, compared with − 6.5 kcal/mol for the native ligand PE-DTPA and − 5.9 kcal/mol for the reference agonist Pam2CSK4. Fructose displayed a lower binding affinity of − 5.5 kcal/mol. As shown in Fig. 7A and B, BMPE occupied the hydrophobic ligand-binding cavity of TLR2 and closely overlapped one of the lipid chains of the native ligand. The benzimidazole NH group formed a hydrogen bond with Leu350 (2.37 Å), while additional interactions included van der Waals and π–π contacts with Phe322 and Phe355, together with alkyl and π–alkyl interactions involving Val343, Val348, Val351, and Pro352. Curcumin exhibited a comparable binding orientation within the same cavity (Fig. 7C, D). A hydrogen bond was formed with Ser346 (2.61 Å), accompanied by van der Waals and π–π interactions with Phe322, Phe349, and Phe355, as well as alkyl and π–alkyl contacts involving Ile319, Leu328, Val348, and Pro352. Structural superimposition demonstrated overlap of curcumin with approximately one and a half of the two lipid chains of the native ligand. Fructose adopted a distinct binding orientation relative to BMPE and curcumin (Fig. 7E, F). The molecule formed nine hydrogen bonds with Asp263, Asn267, Asp294, Phe295, Asp327, and Thr330 and occupied an adjacent polar cavity. Among these residues, Asp327 was shared with the reported TLR2 ligand-binding site. The docking poses of BMPE, curcumin, and fructose within TLR2 are summarized in Fig. 7G.

Histopathological confirmation of antitumor efficacy

As demonstrated in Fig. 8, histopathological examination of sham control liver tissue revealed preserved lobular architecture with well-arranged hepatocyte cords and intact sinusoids. In contrast, liver tissues revealed profound alterations in the untreated EAC control group, which exhibited complete disruption of hepatic architecture, dense cellular infiltration, pronounced nuclear pleomorphism, and necrotic foci, consistent with malignant progression. Treatment with the parent BMPE partially alleviated these alterations, restoring some hepatic cords and central veins, although residual degenerative changes and nuclear irregularities persisted. The BMPE–Cur nanocomplex produced a more pronounced improvement, characterized by preserved hepatocellular morphology, reduced necrosis, and well-defined sinusoidal spaces. Strikingly, the combination therapy demonstrated the most potent hepatoprotective effect, with near-normal lobular organization, intact vascular and biliary structures, and only minimal evidence of degeneration. Treatment with DOX significantly improved hepatic architecture; however, mild degenerative and congestive changes remained, reflecting its known hepatotoxic potential. In contrast, the levan monotherapy showed partial amelioration, with reduced but persistent degenerative changes and sinusoidal congestion, suggesting a supportive protective role rather than curative efficacy. Collectively, these findings indicate that the combination therapy exerts the strongest therapeutic effect, surpassing both single-drug regimens and probiotics alone. Mathematical evaluation of BMPE–Cur and levan interaction profiles

Isobolographic analysis was performed to quantitatively assess the interaction between BMPE–Cur and levan across multiple biological endpoints (Supplementary Table S3). Combination index values indicated synergistic interactions (CI < 1) for the majority of evaluated parameters, particularly those associated with tumor burden, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and metabolic regulation. The strongest synergistic interactions were observed for angiogenic markers (VEGFR), tumor-associated biomarkers (AFP and CEA), inflammatory mediators including IL-6 and TNF-α, components of the TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, and the FXR/FGF15 metabolic axis. Synergistic antioxidant effects were also evident, as reflected by coordinated reductions in NO and MDA together with enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defenses. Immune-related endpoints displayed variable interaction profiles. Synergism was observed for CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, CD25⁺ regulatory T cells, and tissue inflammatory mediators. In contrast, partial antagonism was detected for selected systemic immune parameters, including serum IL-10 and peripheral CD4⁺ T cells, whereas hepatic IL-10 retained a synergistic interaction profile. Overall, the calculated combination indices demonstrated predominantly synergistic interactions between BMPE–Cur and levan across the evaluated tumor-associated, immunological, oxidative, and immunometabolic endpoints.

Discussion

The development of targeted nanomedicines capable of overcoming rapid systemic clearance while simultaneously modulating the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) remains a major frontier in cancer therapeutics. The present study shows that microbial levan enhances the therapeutic efficacy of a PEGylated benzimidazole–curcumin (BMPE–Cur) nanocomplex in experimental liver cancer. Beyond improving antitumor activity, levan was associated with increased hepatic nanocomplex retention and coordinated immunometabolic remodeling, supporting the concept that modulation of host biological responses may complement conventional nanocarrier engineering to improve nanotherapeutic performance.

Consistent with the extensive tumor-associated hepatic injury characterized in this model26, the EAC-induced hepatic tumor model successfully recapitulated several hallmarks of advanced liver malignancy. These features included ascites accumulation, hepatic dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, angiogenesis, and profound immune dysregulation. The progressive increases in body weight and abdominal circumference, accompanied by elevated mortality, reflected a severe tumor burden47. Although all therapeutic interventions significantly improved survival and reduced EAC-associated morbidity, the combination of BMPE–Cur and levan consistently produced superior physiological outcomes. Interestingly, this combination achieved a greater reduction of abdominal circumference than the gold-standard chemotherapeutic doxorubicin (DOX) despite comparable body weights, suggesting more effective control of tumor-associated ascites and vascular permeability. Notably, the significant body-weight reduction observed in DOX-treated animals is consistent with previous reports describing DOX-induced systemic metabolic alterations associated with the depletion of adipose tissue and skeletal muscle mass48. In contrast, BMPE–Cur monotherapy did not induce significant body-weight loss despite its pronounced antitumor activity, while the co-administration of levan resulted in a moderate but significant reduction in body weight. This observation suggests that the microbial polysaccharide may have influenced the in vivo disposition of the nanocomplex, which in turn might have amplified BMPE-associated reduced food intake and body-weight loss upon its prolonged, sustained release49.

This pharmacodynamic hypothesis is supported by our pharmacokinetic findings, which revealed a 2.3-fold increase in hepatic BMPE accumulation with levan co-treatment. The limited residual hepatic concentrations of the parent BMPE compound likely reflect true metabolic clearance driven by tumor-induced oxidative stress and activation of conjugative enzymes, such as UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGTs) and sulfotransferase (SULTs), despite the predicted low physiological clearance of the compound49. In contrast, the absence of unmetabolized curcumin is consistent with its intrinsically rapid and extensive biotransformation through glucuronidation, sulfation, and reduction pathways, which is well documented even when nanoformulations improve its bioavailability50. Accordingly, while both compounds undergo metabolic clearance, the greater metabolic susceptibility of curcumin likely accounts for its complete absence at the terminal sampling point, whereas measurable hepatic BMPE concentrations remained detectable, particularly following levan co-administration. Importantly, the elevated hepatic BMPE levels resulting from the combination therapy suggests that levan may have altered the disposition of the nanocomplex and promoted the hepatic retention of the active payload. Our in silico metabolic predictions support this scenario, where fructose, the prominent monosaccharide unit of levan, was predicted to selectively inhibit cytochrome CYP2C9 while sparing CYP3A4 and CYP2C19, while concurrently attenuating P-glycoprotein efflux activity. Such selective modulation likely prolongs BMPE systemic exposure and enhances hepatic retention without inducing metabolic overload, while reduced drug efflux further consolidates local hepatic accumulation7.

The higher terminal hepatic BMPE concentration observed following levan co-administration was accompanied by substantial improvement in functional and structural indicators of tumor-associated liver injury. While BMPE–Cur alone significantly ameliorated the marked elevations in transaminases, bilirubin levels, cholestatic markers, tumor-associated biomarkers, and angiogenic mediators in EAC mice, co-administration with levan produced the most comprehensive restoration of hepatic function, nearly normalizing biochemical indices and markedly suppressing AFP, CEA, and VEGFR. These findings were further corroborated by the pronounced inhibition of peritoneal angiogenesis and the near-complete restoration of hepatic architecture observed histologically. While curcumin and benzimidazole scaffolds are known to inhibit VEGF/VEGFR signaling and promote endothelial apoptosis51,52, their combination with levan markedly amplified these effects, suggesting that the higher hepatic BMPE concentration observed with levan co-administration may have contributed to the superior antitumor efficacy observed at both biochemical and tissue levels. Interestingly, although DOX completely prevented mortality, it failed to restore hepatic function to the same extent as the combination treatment, consistent with its well-recognized hepatotoxic potential53.

Mechanistically, oxidative stress represents a major driver of hepatocarcinogenesis and tumor progression, contributing to genomic instability, chronic inflammation, angiogenesis, and hepatocellular injury54. The restoration of hepatic redox equilibrium by all treatments therefore likely contributed to the observed improvement in liver function and tumor-associated pathology54,55. Notably, the combination regimen produced the most profound antioxidant response, normalizing oxidative damage markers while elevating GSH, catalase, and SOD activities beyond baseline levels. These findings suggest activation of adaptive cytoprotective mechanisms, consistent with the potent antioxidant properties previously reported for BMPE49 and the well-established ability of curcumin to activate Nrf2-dependent antioxidant signaling56. The greater antioxidant response observed following levan co-administration is consistent with both the intrinsic antioxidant activity of levan observed in the present study and previous reports describing the antioxidant properties of microbial levans14–16. These findings suggest that levan contributed to the restoration of hepatic redox homeostasis. Given the close interplay between oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling57, the attenuation of oxidative injury may have also contributed to the downstream suppression of tumor-promoting pathways observed herein.

Given the established role of TLR2 signaling in promoting IL-10-producing Foxp3⁺CD25⁺ regulatory T-cell differentiation58, the observed reduction in Treg abundance is consistent with attenuation of TLR2-associated immunosuppressive signaling. At the signaling level, these immunological improvements were accompanied by pronounced suppression of the TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB signaling cascade, a key pathway linking gut-derived microbial signals to hepatic inflammation and tumor-promoting immune responses59. Interestingly, levan monotherapy exerted only modest suppression of hepatic TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB signaling, whereas its combination with BMPE–Cur produced the most pronounced inhibition of this pathway. This observation suggests that the principal contribution of levan is unlikely to involve direct inhibition of TLR2 signaling as the primary mechanism, despite previous reports suggesting inhibitory effects of β-(2→6)-type fructans on TLR2 signaling60. Instead, levan may exert an indirect modulatory effect through modulation of gut microbial communities that influence xenobiotic metabolism61, thereby influencing the pharmacokinetic disposition of the main pharmacophore (BMPE), as reflected by the higher terminal hepatic BMPE concentration observed following combination therapy. Although gut-derived microbial signals may contribute to this process, direct investigation of gut microbiota composition and function was beyond the scope of the present study.

Importantly, EAC-induced tumorigenesis was associated not only with activation of this inflammatory axis but also with concomitant suppression of FXR and FGF15 expression, indicating parallel disruption of metabolic regulatory pathways21. While the combination treatment produced the strongest inhibition of TLR2-associated signaling, it simultaneously restored FXR and FGF15 expression, suggesting coordinated immunometabolic remodeling62. Such dual regulation is particularly attractive from a therapeutic perspective because inflammatory and metabolic pathways are increasingly recognized as interconnected determinants of tumor progression, therapeutic responsiveness, and hepatic function62,63. Beyond restoring metabolic homeostasis, restoration of the FXR/FGF15 pathway may also influence drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporter systems involved in xenobiotic disposition22,23, potentially contributing to the altered pharmacokinetic behavior reflected by the higher terminal hepatic BMPE concentration observed following combination therapy. These findings support the concept that optimizing the host biological environment may complement nanocarrier engineering as a strategy to improve nanotherapeutic performance.

To further explore the observed suppression of TLR2 signaling, molecular docking studies were performed to evaluate whether components of the therapeutic system could directly interact with the receptor. Both BMPE and curcumin displayed favorable binding affinities exceeding those of the reference TLR2 agonist Pam2CSK4 and comparable to the native ligand. Importantly, both molecules occupied the hydrophobic ligand-binding cavity and exhibited substantial overlap with the lipid chains of the co-crystallized ligand, suggesting the potential to interfere with receptor–ligand recognition40,45. These observations provide a plausible structural rationale consistent with the attenuation of TLR2-associated signaling observed in vivo. Interestingly, fructose, used as a representative structural unit of levan, also demonstrated measurable affinity toward TLR2 despite adopting a distinct binding orientation. Unlike BMPE and curcumin, fructose occupied an adjacent polar cavity stabilized predominantly through hydrogen-bond interactions. Although the intact three-dimensional structure of levan differs substantially from its constituent monosaccharides, these findings suggest that levan-derived structural motifs may contribute to TLR2-associated modulation. However, experimental validation would be required to determine whether these predicted interactions occur under physiological conditions and contribute to the biological effects observed in vivo.

Histopathological observations corroborated the biochemical, immunological, and molecular findings, collectively supporting the superior therapeutic efficacy of the BMPE–Cur–levan combination. This integrated biological response was further substantiated by isobolographic analysis, which provided quantitative evidence that the enhanced efficacy of the combination treatment reflects genuine pharmacological synergy rather than a simple additive effect. Synergistic interactions predominated across tumor-associated biomarkers, angiogenesis, oxidative stress parameters, inflammatory mediators, components of the TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, and the FXR/FGF15 axis, whereas antagonistic interactions were infrequent. This pattern suggests selective modulation of immune and metabolic pathways rather than broad, nonspecific enhancement. The broad distribution of synergistic effects across multiple pathological domains suggests that the therapeutic advantage of levan cannot be explained by modulation of a single biological pathway. Instead, levan appears to function as a host-response modulator capable of amplifying nanotherapeutic activity through coordinated regulation of interconnected oxidative, immune, inflammatory, and metabolic pathways. Collectively, these findings support a multifaceted mode of action that is consistent with the enhanced hepatic retention of BMPE and the comprehensive biological improvements observed throughout the study. Limitations & future directions

Despite the strength and internal consistency of the present findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the study was designed to evaluate the biological consequences of levan co-administration, alterations in gut microbiota composition and microbial metabolite production were not directly assessed. Consequently, the contribution of microbiome remodeling to the observed immunometabolic effects remains inferential and warrants future investigation. Second, the pharmacokinetic evaluation was limited to hepatic tissue concentrations measured at a defined experimental endpoint. While these data clearly demonstrate enhanced hepatic accumulation of BMPE following levan co-administration, comprehensive pharmacokinetic characterization, including plasma exposure, systemic distribution, and temporal disposition profiles, was beyond the scope of the present study and should be addressed in future investigations. Third, although molecular docking and in silico metabolism analyses provided supportive mechanistic insight into potential TLR2 interactions and drug-metabolism pathways, these approaches remain predictive. Likewise, although restoration of FXR/FGF15 signaling was strongly associated with enhanced therapeutic efficacy and increased hepatic BMPE accumulation, a direct causal relationship between this pathway and nanocomplex disposition was not experimentally established. Fourth, although the EAC-induced hepatic tumor model reproduces key features of tumor-associated hepatic pathology, it does not fully capture the molecular heterogeneity of human hepatocellular carcinoma.

Future studies incorporating receptor-binding assays, transporter activity measurements, and direct characterization of drug-metabolizing enzyme expression and activity, as well as targeted manipulation of FXR/FGF15 signaling, will be required to confirm these mechanistic links. While the immune profiling captured the dominant immunological alterations in the EAC model, deeper characterization of innate immune populations, immune-cell polarization states, and functional T-cell responses would provide additional mechanistic resolution. Finally, future studies should define the therapeutic window of microbial levan through formal dose-escalation, mechanistic investigations into the reduced tolerability observed at higher doses, and long-term safety assessments. In addition, validation in complementary liver cancer models will be necessary before translational conclusions can be drawn.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings support the concept that microbial levan functions as more than a conventional prebiotic adjunct. Rather, it appears to function as a host-response modulator associated with enhanced nanotherapeutic retention and coordinated immunometabolic remodeling, characterized by suppression of TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB signaling and restoration of FXR/FGF15 activity. The convergence of these pharmacokinetic and host-response effects provides a plausible mechanistic framework for the superior antitumor efficacy observed with the BMPE–Cur–levan combination. More broadly, the present work supports a paradigm in which microbial biopolymers may serve not only as adjunct therapeutic agents, but also as host-response modulators capable of reshaping the biological environment to favor nanotherapeutic retention and therapeutic response.
