Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) for anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Moaz Safwan, Mariam Safwan Bourgleh, Hani AlShakaki, Khawaja Husnain Haider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Anthracyclines are widely used in cancer treatment and cause dose-dependent cardiotoxicity 2 different by increasing oxidative stress and RAS activation. Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) show promise in reducing this damage.OBJECTIVES AND DESIGN: This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy and safety of ACEIs in preserving left ventricular function and reducing cardiotoxicity associated with anthracycline therapy.METHODS: A comprehensive search of databases up to May 2024 included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that assessed ACEIs to prevent cardiotoxicity. Random-effects meta-analysis was applied.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: The primary outcome was changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Secondary outcomes included cardiac event incidence and adverse events.SAMPLE SIZE: Nine RCTs were included, encompassing 869 patients (440 ACEI group, 429 control group).RESULTS: ACEIs significantly improved LVEF at six months (mean difference of 7.93%; 95% CI 3.18–12.67%; P=.001) but not at 12 months. Moreover, ACEIs were associated with non-statistically significant lower rates of heart failure and arrhythmia development compared to the control, with no significant differences noted in adverse events.QUALITY OF EVIDENCE: Evidence quality was high for short-term LVEF improvement and moderate-to-low for other outcomes. Egger's regression test indicated a low risk of publication bias for LVEF.HETEROGENEITY: High (I²=97%) for LVEF at 6 months.CONCLUSION: ACEIs prevent cardiotoxicity in the short term without increasing adverse events. More extensive trials are needed to confirm long-term benefits.LIMITATIONS: The small number of RCTs and high heterogeneity limit the study. Inconsistent reporting of baseline cardiovascular factors and confounders also hindered accurate assessment of treatment effects.REGISTRATION: PROSPERO CRD42024555546.

INTRODUCTION

Anthracyclines are a class of chemotherapeutic agents widely used to treat both solid tumors and hematological malignancies.1 They exert their antitumor effects primarily through inhibition of topoisomerase II, resulting in DNA strand breaks and apoptosis.2 Doxorubicin (Adriamycin) is the prototypical agent of this group, which also includes daunorubicin, epirubicin, mitoxantrone, idarubicin, and valrubicin.3 Despite their proven efficacy, anthracyclines are well known for their dose-dependent cardiotoxicity, primarily mediated by the generation of reactive oxygen species that induce oxidative stress in cardiac tissues.4 This can lead to a wide spectrum of cardiac dysfunction, ranging from asymptomatic diastolic impairment to late-onset congestive heart failure (CHF).4 The incidence of heart failure increases substantially with cumulative doses of doxorubicin—rising from 3.5% at 400 mg/m² to 7–16% at 550 mg/m² and up to 48% at 700 mg/m².5 The mechanisms of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity are multifactorial. A key factor is the inhibition of topoisomerase 2β, which is expressed in quiescent, non-proliferating cells such as cardiomyocytes and can activate cell death pathways.6 Other contributing mechanisms include disruption of mitochondrial biogenesis, calcium dysregulation, and activation of apoptosis pathways.6

Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) are widely used in clinical practice for the treatment of hypertension, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease. Their cardioprotective effects are mediated by reduction of afterload, inhibition of angiotensin II-induced oxidative stress, and attenuation of adverse cardiac remodeling.7 In preclinical models, ACEIs have been shown to mitigate anthracycline-induced myocardial injury by suppressing cardiac renin-angiotensin system (RAS) overactivation and limiting the formation of free radicals.8 These findings support the hypothesis that ACEIs may play a protective role in preventing anthracycline-related cardiotoxicity.

Several previous meta-analyses have investigated the use of RAS inhibitors for this purpose; however, they often combined ACEIs and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) without distinguishing between their effects. For example, Gao et al reported a significant improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (SMD=0.915) without a significant increase in adverse events. Still, the analysis pooled both ACEIs and ARBs, limiting drug-specific conclusions.9 Similarly, Lewinter et al. also evaluated these agents collectively, making it difficult to isolate the unique contribution of ACEIs. This lack of distinction presents a methodological limitation, as ACEIs and ARBs differ in their mechanisms of action and potential benefits.10

We therefore conducted this study to address this critical gap by focusing exclusively on ACEIs, representing the first meta-analysis to systematically evaluate their isolated efficacy and safety in anthracycline-treated patients. This meta-analysis aims to assess the efficacy and safety of ACEIs in the prevention of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity. The primary outcome is the change in LVEF, while secondary outcomes include the incidence of major cardiac events and treatment-related adverse events.

METHODS

Protocol registration and search strategies

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines, we used an extensive search strategy to identify relevant studies (supplementary materials). This strategy involved systematic searches of multiple databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, EMBASE, and EBSCO, from their inception until May 2024. We used common text words and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, such as Anthracyclines, Heart Failure, Cardiomyopathy, Cardiotoxicity and ACE Inhibitors. These terms were combined using specific operators, such as ACE Inhibitors and Anthracyclines, to address Cardiotoxicity. A complete search strategy is presented in Table 1. Additionally, we manually searched the reference lists of included studies to ensure no eligible studies were missed. We did not apply any language restrictions. The study protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO with registration number CRD42024555546. This comprehensive search enhances the reliability of our findings.

Data extraction

Three independent authors (MH, HS and MB) assessed the studies' eligibility for the meta-analysis using a predefined data extraction Excel sheet. For each study included, the variables extracted included author name, year of publication, study design, sample size, gender distribution, mean sample age, mean BMI (Body Mass Index), cancer type, anthracycline type and dose, ACEI type and dose, control arm, follow-up duration in months, number of hypertension and diabetes mellitus cases, and myocardial assessment modality.

Quality assessment

The Cochrane Collaboration tool assessed the methodological quality of the RCTs included in the analysis.11 This tool evaluated the risk of bias (RoB) by considering various criteria, including sequence generation randomness, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each study was evaluated for RoB across various criteria, categorized as having a low, high, or unclear risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined by evaluating all the criteria together. The results were displayed in a risk of bias graph, offering an overview of the study's overall risk based on the assessed criteria.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan software. For continuous outcomes, data extracted as means and standard errors (SE) or confidence intervals (CI) were converted to means and standard deviations (SD) using the equations provided in the Cochrane Handbook.11 Due to the varying follow-up durations in the included trials, separate analyses were conducted for LVEF change from baseline at two follow-up points, six months and twelve months, to improve comparability between the studies. Weighted mean differences (WMDs) were calculated using a random-effects model for LVEF changes and presented with 95% CIs. For binary outcomes, including adverse events, heart failure incidence, and arrhythmia incidence, odds ratios (OR) were calculated and presented with 95% CIs. Subgroup analysis was performed based on the ACEIs used as an intervention: Enalapril, Perindopril, and Ramipril.

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and the following interpretations were followed: <25% indicating unimportant heterogeneity, 25–75% indicating moderate heterogeneity, and >75% indicating high heterogeneity. To assess the presence of publication bias for LVEF as the primary outcome, an Egger's regression test was performed using Jamovi software.12 A P value <.05 from Egger's test suggested significant evidence of publication bias. A P value <.05 was used to determine statistical significance in all analyses.

RESULTS

Literature search

The study inclusion process, outlined in the PRISMA chart (Figure 1), began with screening 650 records. From these, 27 studies were identified for full article review, excluding 18, as detailed in Figure 1. This resulted in the final selection of nine eligible RCTs comprising 869 patients (440 in the intervention arm and 429 in the control arm) for the meta-analysis.

Study selection flow diagram (PRISMA chart).

Characteristics of the RCTs included in the meta-analysis

Spanning from 2004 to 2021, the included RCTs involved patients with diverse malignant conditions, including breast cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, bone sarcomas, lung, and gastric cancers. The predominant types of anthracyclines administered were Doxorubicin and Epirubicin (five studies each), followed by Daunorubicin and Mitoxantrone (one study each), with dosages ranging from 272 to 643 mg/m2 in the intervention group and 263 to 643 mg/m2 in the control group. Among different ACEI types, Enalapril, with daily dosing ranging from 10 mg to 20 mg in adult patients and 0.1 mg/kg for pediatric patients, was the most commonly used (five RCTs), followed by Ramipril-daily dose ranging from 5 mg to 10 mg- and Perindopril-daily dose ranging from 8 mg to 10 mg- (two studies each). These RCTs were conducted in diverse locations, including the USA,13 Italy,14,15 Greece,16 Romania,17 India,18 Iran,19 Canada,20 and Poland.21 The age range of patients varied from 8 to 53 years in the intervention group and 8 to 51 years in the control group, with male patients comprising 0–70% and 0–68% of the intervention and control groups, respectively. Follow-up durations ranged from 3 to 24 months in the included RCTs. The baseline characteristics of the studies are summarized in Table 2.

Each included study underwent a rigorous RoB assessment using the Cochrane Collaboration risk-of-bias tool. All included RCTs were rated as high quality with low RoB (Figure 2).

Assessment of outcomes

LVEF

Four studies, encompassing 354 participants (intervention group: 179; control group: 175), reported changes in LVEF after a six-month follow-up. The ACEI group demonstrated a significant improvement of 7.93% (WMD [95% CI]=3.18%, 12.67%, P=.001, I²=97%) in LVEF compared to the control group. When analyzing the data by intervention type, the Enalapril subgroup showed a more significant improvement in LVEF of 9.76% (WMD [95% CI]=[5.05%, 14.47%], P< .0001, I² =95%) compared to the Ramipril subgroup, which showed an improvement of 2.5% (WMD [95% CI]=[1.34%, 3.66%], P<.0001) (Figure 3A).

Forest plot of the weighted mean difference in LVEF change from baseline to six months of follow-up (A) and twelve months of follow-up (B).

On the other hand, six studies involving 581 participants (intervention group: 293; control group: 288) reported changes in LVEF after a 12-month follow-up. The pooled analysis revealed an improvement of 4.08% in the intervention group compared to the placebo group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (WMD [95% CI]=-0.53%, 8.69%, P=.08, I²=98%). Subgroup analysis by drug type revealed that the Enalapril group showed the most significant improvement of 7.26% (WMD [95% CI]= [-7.52%, 22.06%], P=.34, I²=99%), followed by the Perindopril group with an improvement of 4.86% (WMD [95% CI]= [-0.64%, 10.37%], P=.08, I²= 94%). The Ramipril group showed the least improvement at 0.28% (WMD [95% CI]=[-3.23%, 3.79%], P=.88, I²=85%). However, none of these improvements were statistically significant (Figure 3B).

As indicated by Egger's regression test, the low risk of publication bias suggests that the results are less likely to be influenced by publication bias, thereby enhancing the study's validity.

Adverse events

Four RCTs reported the number of adverse events experienced during the follow-up period. Seventy-five adverse events (including hypotension, pulmonary edema, dizziness, fatigue, and dry cough) were recorded in the ACEI group (75 out of 202 participants, 37.13%), compared to 81 adverse events in the placebo group (81 out of 196 participants, 41.33%). The analysis did not reveal a statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups (OR 0.47 [95% CI 0.03, 7.64], P=.60, I²=92%) (Figure 4).

The forest plot shows the pooled odds ratio of the adverse events reported at the end of the follow-up.

Heart failure incidence

Three RCTs, comprising 283 participants (intervention group, 143; control group, 140), reported the incidence of heart failure. In the ACEIs group, two participants (1.4%) experienced heart failure, compared to eighteen participants (12.86%) in the control group. The pooled odds ratio revealed an 80% reduction in the incidence of heart failure in the ACEIs group compared to the control group. However, the difference was not statistically significant (OR 0.20 [95% CI] [0.03, 1.60], P=.13, I²=48%) (Figure 5).

Forest plot of the odds ratio of heart failure incidence.

Incidence of arrhythmia

Three RCTs involving 332 participants (168 in the experimental group and 164 in the control group) reported the incidence of new-onset arrhythmias. In the ACEIs group, five participants (2.98%) experienced arrhythmia, compared to thirteen participants (7.93%) in the placebo group. The pooled analysis revealed a 59% reduction in arrhythmia incidence in the ACEIs group compared to the control, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.41 [95% CI 0.05, 3.50], P=.42, I²=63%) (Figure 6).

Forest plot shows the pooled odds ratio of the incidence of cardiac arrhythmias compared to the control group.

Sensitivity analysis

Due to the high heterogeneity observed in LVEF at both six months and twelve months of follow-up, as well as in the adverse events OR, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to explore the effect of individual studies on the overall results. However, the analysis revealed that excluding individual studies did not significantly reduce the overall heterogeneity, suggesting that a single outlier study does not drive the observed high heterogeneity.

Grading of the evidence

To evaluate the quality of the evidence in our findings, we generated a summary of findings (SoF) table using the GRADE approach to assess the clinical applicability of the findings, thereby aiding clinicians in their decision-making in practice. The evidence was rated as high quality for the LVEF change at six months of follow-up, indicating that future research is unlikely to alter confidence in the estimated effect. In contrast, the evidence of LVEF change at twelve months of follow-up and the risk of heart failure was rated as moderate quality, indicating that future research may impact the estimate. Furthermore, the evidence of adverse events and arrhythmia was rated low quality, suggesting that future research is likely to significantly affect the estimated effect (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Anthracyclines have been widely used since their identification in the 1950s. These drugs have become the choice treatment for a diverse range of solid and hematological malignancies, including leukemia, lymphoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, and sarcoma.22 Their use has significantly improved the prognosis and survival outcomes for many cancer patients; however, it is not without severe complications, with cardiomyopathy and heart failure being among the most common side effects observed. Recent data suggest that the risk of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity can range from 3% to as high as 48%, depending on various risk factors, most notably the cumulative dose administered and the concomitant use of other chemotherapeutic agents.23 The precise mechanism underlying anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity remains unclear, but the formation of free radicals in the myocardium is believed to be a critical contributing factor.24 Anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity can be classified based on the timing of onset into three categories: acute, early-onset chronic, and late-onset chronic. Acute toxicity involves a reversible decline in myocardial contractility within two weeks of anthracycline administration. Early-onset chronic toxicity refers to the development of irreversible dilated hypokinetic cardiomyopathy within the first year of anthracycline treatment. In contrast, late-onset chronic toxicity is characterized by the development of irreversible dilated hypokinetic cardiomyopathy after the first year of anthracycline therapy.25

Chronic forms of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity, encompassing both early-onset and late-onset variants, are regarded as irreversible conditions and are linked to the onset of clinical heart failure. Consequently, various preventive strategies have been proposed to mitigate the cardiotoxic effects of anthracyclines, including the prophylactic administration of cardiovascular agents such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors. Research from experimental models suggests that levels of the cardiac RAS are elevated following exposure to anthracyclines, leading to increased production of free radicals and contributing to the development of cardiomyopathy.8

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of prophylactic ACEI use in patients exposed to anthracyclines to prevent the development of cardiotoxicity and reduce the risk of MACE. Anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy is typically diagnosed based on the clinical symptoms of heart failure and the evidence of a decrease in LVEF.26 We compared the change in LVEF between patients receiving ACEIs and the control group using data from available randomized controlled trials.

Our significant findings include 1) at six months of follow-up, high certainty evidence demonstrated that ACEIs significantly prevented the deterioration in LVEF by 7.93% compared to the control group, 2) at 12 months of follow-up, ACEIs resulted in 4.08% improvement in LVEF compared to control, although the difference was not statistically significant, 3) throughout the twelve months of follow up, there was no statically significant difference in the rate of adverse events between the ACEI intervention and control groups, 4) although the differences were not statistically significant, ACEIs exhibited lower rates in the incidence of heart failure and cardiac arrhythmia compared to control group.

A subgroup analysis examined the effects of different ACEIs tested across the included studies. The data suggest an advantage for enalapril, an ACEI, compared to other ACEI types. Specifically, we found that enalapril significantly prevented a 9.76% deterioration in LVEF at 6-month follow-up, compared to a 2.50% decline in LVEF prevented with ramipril.

Although none of the ACEI types demonstrated a statistically significant protective effect compared to control at 12-month follow-up, the LVEF results showed a preference for enalapril over ramipril and perindopril. These findings highlight the potential importance of ACEI selection in cardioprotection.

The transient cardioprotective effect observed with ACEIs in anthracycline-treated patients—evident at 6 months but diminished by 12 months—may arise from the interplay between their multifactorial mechanisms of action and the progressive pathophysiology of anthracycline cardiotoxicity. Mechanistically, both sulphydryl (i.e., captopril) and non-sulphydryl group (i.e., enalapril and ramipril) containing ACEIs have been shown to protect the myocardium against ischemia-reperfusion injury, stabilize the electrical activity, and prevent reperfusion-induced arrhythmias.27 At the molecular level, ACEIs exert additional cardioprotective effects by accumulating bradykinins. This bradykinin-mediated pathway leads to enhanced nitric oxide release, improved endothelial function, and reduced oxidative stress—mechanisms that contribute to myocardial preservation and are unique to ACEIs.28 Additionally, ACEIs mitigate cardiac remodeling by reducing angiotensin II-mediated vasoconstriction, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, which may initially counterbalance anthracycline-induced myocardial injury.29 However, anthracyclines cause cumulative, irreversible mitochondrial DNA damage and apoptosis through iron-mediated free radical generation, which may eventually counteract the antioxidative and antifibrotic benefits of ACEIs if therapy is discontinued prematurely. Furthermore, anthracyclines also impair cardiomyocyte autophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis, processes not directly targeted by ACEIs, which may explain why their benefits plateau despite early hemodynamic improvements. These findings underscore that while ACEIs modulate secondary injury pathways, their transient efficacy reflects the need for prolonged therapy to counteract the relentless toxicity of anthracyclines and the inherent limitations in addressing primary mitochondrial and metabolic insults.30

In addition to the development of heart failure, anthracyclines are also associated with the occurrence of fatal arrhythmias.31 A recent meta-analysis of thirteen studies involving 26 891 patients reported that patients receiving anthracycline treatment were at a 1.58-fold greater risk for the development of arrhythmias compared to the control group. The most commonly reported arrhythmias were ST-segment and T-wave abnormalities, as well as tachycardia. Notably, the risk of arrhythmia development was significantly higher even with exposure to low doses of anthracyclines compared to the control group.32 This heightened arrhythmia risk highlights the importance of proactive monitoring and implementing cardioprotective strategies for patients receiving chemotherapeutic agents.

Our study observed that the prophylactic administration of ACEIs to patients receiving anthracyclines resulted in non-statistically significant lower rates of arrhythmia during the 12-month follow-up period compared to the control. However, it is essential to note that only a few studies (three RCTs) have reported the incidence of arrhythmias. This highlights the need for future long-term RCTs to assess arrhythmia risk in patients receiving ACEIs and to validate these cardioprotective effects further.

Unlike a recently published meta-analysis by Gao et al., which consisted of fifteen RCTs involving 1977 breast cancer patients and evaluated the effects of ACEIs and ARBs, our current study represents the first investigation to test the protective efficacy of ACEIs alone in patients exposed to anthracycline therapy.9 The authors demonstrated that treatment with ACEIs and ARBs resulted in a higher LVEF compared to the control group, with a standardized mean difference of 0.915. Notably, similar to our findings, the study reported no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the intervention and control groups. 9 It is pertinent to note that previous meta-analyses have assessed the combined effect of ACEIs and ARBs without distinguishing between the two drug classes, a methodological limitation that may obscure class-specific effects. This methodological limitation has been typically addressed in our study by evaluating the efficacy of ACEIs alone in preventing cardiotoxicity, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of their potential role in cardio-oncology.

Another recent meta-analysis by Lewinter et al investigated the efficacy of ACEIs and ARBs in attenuating the cardiotoxicity of anthracyclines among female patients diagnosed with breast cancer. Their results reported a slight improvement in LVEF compared to the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant.10

The disparity between these findings and the results of our current study may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, our investigation did not focus solely on breast cancer patients; instead, it encompassed a broader population exposed to anthracyclines. Furthermore, our research is the first to conduct analyses that include both short-term and long-term follow-up. In contrast, the previous meta-analysis may not have reported outcomes across these distinct time frames.

Our meta-analysis did not include ARBs, as the primary objective was not to compare ACEIs and ARBs but to assess whether ACEIs—given their short-term beneficial effect established in our study—could serve as a reference for similar cardioprotective strategies. Identical to their interchangeable use in hypertension, our findings reinforce the efficacy of ACEIs in this setting. Emerging evidence suggests that their bradykinin-mediated effects, such as enhanced nitric oxide release, improved endothelial function, and reduced oxidative stress, may offer distinct advantages over ARBs.27,33 However, differentiating these classes mechanistically remains challenging due to their overlapping pathways, and clinically significant differences in outcomes are not well established. Therefore, focusing on ACEIs allowed for a more targeted evaluation of their specific benefits without conflating two pharmacologically distinct agents.

In addition to RAS inhibitors, several agents, such as beta-blockers and dexrazoxane, have been evaluated for preventing anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Sotiropoulou et al reported that patients prescribed β-blockers demonstrated a 74% lower likelihood of exhibiting cardiotoxicity symptoms. At the same time, dexrazoxane was linked to a threefold decrease in the risk of cardiac abnormalities. Notably, ACE inhibitor administration showed half the risk of cardiotoxicity compared with the control group, underscoring their superior efficacy compared to beta blockers. Notably, the study did not separately assess adverse events (e.g., hypotension, arrhythmias) or clinically defined heart failure, limiting insights into safety and long-term outcomes.34

Our study was limited by the relatively small number of RCTs that have assessed changes in LVEF and the incidence of MACE as outcomes. Specifically, only four RCTs involving 354 patients reported LVEF changes at the 6-month follow-up, while six RCTs with 581 patients provided LVEF changes data at the 12-month follow-up. Additionally, only three RCTs, which included 332 patients, reported the occurrence of new-onset arrhythmia. A high level of overall heterogeneity remains among these studies, and the underlying factors contributing to the variations have not been determined despite conducting subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses. Notably, cardiovascular baseline factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, as well as other potential confounders, were not consistently reported across the studies. Therefore, their effect on the treatment estimates could not be addressed.

Future RCTs with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up periods beyond 24 months, and comprehensive data on cardiovascular baseline factors are needed to better evaluate the cardioprotective effects of ACEI therapy in the setting of anthracycline exposure and to account for potential confounding factors.

CONCLUSION

Our research findings indicate that ACEIs significantly attenuated acute and early-onset cardiotoxicity compared to control groups without causing significant adverse outcomes. Importantly, ACEIs are well-tolerated, cost-effective, and easy to administer with minimal risks of serious side effects. Given these advantages, clinicians can consider ACEIs as a viable clinical option to improve outcomes in patients receiving anthracycline-based cancer treatment regimens.
