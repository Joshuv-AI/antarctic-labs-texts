Morus alba leaf tea consumption reduces hyperlipidaemia, adipocyte hypertrophy and inflammation in HFD-induced obese mice
Authors: Yen-Chun Koh, Jie-Yu Ruan, Cho-Hua Wan, Min-Hsiung Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimMorus alba (mulberry leaf) has long been used in East Asian medicine for its anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulatory properties. This study aimed to investigate the potential anti-obesity and anti-inflammatory effects of mulberry leaf tea (MAL) in C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD).Experimental procedureMale C57BL/6 mice were assigned to standard diet, HFD, or HFD with MAL supplementation via drinking water for 12 weeks. Body weight, fat mass, and adipocyte morphology were assessed. Serum adiponectin levels and hepatic AMPK activation were measured. Inflammatory protein markers in liver and adipose tissues were analyzed by Western blot.Results and conclusionMAL supplementation did not significantly reduce body weight or fat mass, possibly due to insufficient dosing via drinking water. However, MAL significantly reduced adipocyte size, suggesting a shift from hypertrophy to hyperplasia. Serum adiponectin levels were elevated, and hepatic AMPK activation was enhanced, indicating potential improvements in lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity. Moreover, expression of inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, MCP-1, and IL-6 was reduced in liver and adipose tissue. These findings suggest that while MAL had limited anti-obesity effects under current administration, it exerted notable anti-inflammatory benefits. Further studies using more concentrated supplementation forms are recommended.Keywords: Morus alba L., Mulberry leave, Tea, Obesity, Inflammation

Introduction

Obesity, affecting over 2 billion people globally, promotes excess lipid storage in white adipose tissue through both adipocyte hyperplasia and hypertrophy.1 While hyperplasia buffers nutrient overload, hypertrophy and ectopic triglyceride accumulation in organs such as the liver and muscle can trigger lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, contributing to metabolic disorders.2,3 Excess nutrient intake leads to adipocyte hypertrophy and ectopic lipid accumulation in organs such as the liver and skeletal muscle, triggering oxidative stress and inflammation.4

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a systemic metabolic disease driven by chronic nutrient excess, lipid accumulation, and inflammation.5 The “two hits hypothesis” proposes that hepatic steatosis is followed by oxidative stress and gut dysbiosis, promoting liver injury and fibrosis. Increasing evidence suggests that circulating free fatty acids,6 microbial-derived, lipopolysaccharides,7 adipokines,8 and immune cell-derived cytokines mediate inter-organ communication that contributes to NAFLD progression.9

In obesity, the levels of free fatty acids increase, and adipocytes become a key factor in the development of obesity-induced inflammation by altering the secretion of various chemokines and cytokines.10 For example, there is a decrease in the anti-inflammatory adipokine adiponectin, alongside an increase in pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1. Additionally, macrophages in adipose tissue shift from an anti-inflammatory (M2) to a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype. M2 macrophages are involved in adipose tissue remodeling, including the clearance of dead or dying adipocytes,11 while M1 macrophages have been shown to promote the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines.12 Free fatty acids and various inflammatory factors are transported through the bloodstream into metabolic tissues such as skeletal muscle and the liver, altering inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism, thereby contributing to the development of metabolic syndrome.13

Mulberry leaves (Morus alba L.), traditionally consumed in Asia, are rich in polyphenols and flavonoids such as quercetin and rutin. Their extracts have shown antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory properties. Studies have reported beneficial effects on insulin resistance, lipid metabolism, and hepatic steatosis in obesity and NAFLD models.14 Meng et al. (2020) found that mulberry leaf extracts can inhibit inflammation and oxidative stress, protect pancreatic β-cells, and regulate glucose metabolism, thereby improving symptoms of diabetes and insulin resistance.15 Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated that mulberry leaf powder and its extracts ameliorate obesity and NAFLD through various mechanisms and signaling pathways.16,17 In addition, Lown et al. (2017) reported that an aqueous mulberry leaf extract improved glucose tolerance and reduced insulin concentrations in normoglycemic adults, although the underlying mechanisms remained unclear.18 Interestingly, a more recent study found that evening intake of mulberry leaf extract was more effective in improving postprandial glucose levels compared to morning intake.19 In 2023, another study reported that mulberry leaf fu tea lowered blood glucose in diabetic rats, potentially by modulating gut microbiota composition and reducing inflammatory responses.20

While mulberry leaf extracts show promising metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects, most studies have used purified forms administered by diet or gavage. In contrast, the effects of mulberry leaf tea, prepared by steeping dried leaves in hot water, remain unclear. Given that AMPK is a key pathway linked to mulberry's bioactivity, this study investigates whether tea consumption can reduce lipid accumulation and inflammation in a high-fat diet–induced obesity model, supporting its potential as a functional beverage.

Materials and methods

Materials

The mulberry leaves used in this study were sourced from the Miaoli District Agricultural Research and Extension Station, Taiwan. Antibodies targeting anti-p-AMPK, AMPK, p-ACC, ACC, and C/EBP-α were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA), while antibodies against CPT-1A were acquired from Proteintech (IL, USA). Additionally, antibodies against PPAR-γ and Adiponectin were sourced from Abcam PLC (Cambridge, UK). The β-actin antibody was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MA, USA).

MAL preparation

Mulberry leaves were collected, sun-dried, and stored at room temperature until use. For tea preparation, dried mulberry leaves were infused in hot water (90–95 °C) for 5 min at leaf-to-water ratios of 1.5 g/150 mL (MAL-L) or 3.0 g/150 mL (MAL-H). After cooling to room temperature, the infusion was filtered to obtain mulberry leaf tea, which was freshly prepared daily for animal administration (Fig. S1A).

For in vitro experiments, freshly prepared mulberry leaf tea was freeze-dried and reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) prior to treatment. The freeze-dried extracts were analyzed for total phenolic and flavonoid contents. Total phenolic content was 100.97 ± 2.78 mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of extract, and total flavonoid content was 95.80 ± 1.14 mg rutin equivalents per gram of extract.

In vitro study-nitrite production in macrophages

The murine macrophage RAW264.7 cell line, obtained from ATCC, was used to evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of MAL. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. After 12 h, the cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h, with or without MAL at various concentrations, in DMEM medium without FBS supplementation. The culture medium was then mixed with Griess reagent, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 550 nm.

Oil Red O staining and quantification in HepG2 cells

HepG2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL and allowed to adhere for 12 h. After attachment, cells were co-treated with 300 μM BSA-conjugated oleic acid and MAL at concentrations of 400 or 600 μg/mL for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 1 h. After fixation, cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with freshly prepared Oil Red O solution for 10 min. Excess dye was removed by gentle washing with PBS and 70% ethanol. Cells were then maintained in PBS for microscopic imaging. Representative images were captured under a light microscope at 200 × magnification. For quantification, intracellular Oil Red O was eluted using isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 500 nm using a microplate reader.

Animal study and experimental design

The animal experiment was conducted using 32 male C57BL/6 mice aged 4-week-old, obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center (Taiwan). The mice were housed under the room temperature maintained at 23 ± 3 °C, relative humidity at 50 ± 10%, and a 12-h light-dark cycle. After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups, each consisting of 8 mice, and the experimental groups were as follows: ND (Normal diet): Powdered feed (Laboratory Rodent Diet 5001), ad libitum water, HFD (High fat diet): High-fat feed with 45% caloric content from fat (HFD), ad libitum water, MAL-L: HFD, with water containing 1.5 g/150 mL mulberry leaf tea, and MAL-H: HFD, with water containing 3.0 g/150 mL mulberry leaf tea. The experimental design is presented in Fig. S1B. Throughout the experiment, the mice were allowed ad libitum access to food and water.

Tea was replaced daily, and the feed was replaced every two days. Bedding was changed weekly, and food intake, water intake, and body weight were recorded twice a week. The animal study was conducted following ethical guidelines and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the National Taiwan University, with the approval number NTU-108-EL-00087. The experiment lasted for 14 weeks. The mice were not fasted prior to sacrifice. On the day of sacrifice, the mice were anesthetized with CO2 and immediately underwent cardiac blood collection. Following this, various organs such as the spleen, kidneys, liver, as well as white adipose tissues including epididymal adipose tissue, mesenteric adipose tissue, and perirenal adipose tissue were collected, photographed, and weighed. The collected blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was obtained as serum. The serum was then stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Dosage selection

Dosage levels were selected to approximate habitual and higher-range human tea consumption. According to Winiarska-Mieczan and Baranowska-Wójcik (2024), typical tea preparation involves steeping approximately 2 g of loose-leaf tea in 200 mL of hot water (70–100 °C).21 Based on this reference, the lower dosage was designed to reflect common human consumption, while the higher dosage represented an increased intake level.

The concentrations of the prepared infusions were estimated as approximately 10 mg/mL for MAL-L and 20 mg/mL for MAL-H based on the leaf-to-water ratios. Daily intake was calculated using average daily water consumption (4.98 ± 1.21 mL/day for MAL-L and 5.13 ± 1.10 mL/day for MAL-H) and normalized to body weight. The estimated intake was 1.46 ± 0.12 mg/g/day for MAL-L and 2.95 ± 0.26 mg/g/day for MAL-H, corresponding to mouse doses of approximately 1460 and 2950 mg/kg/day, respectively.

Human equivalent doses (HED) were calculated using the body surface area normalization method recommended by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. These mouse doses correspond to HEDs of approximately 118.4 and 239.2 mg/kg/day. For a 60 kg adult, this equates to an estimated daily intake of approximately 7.1 g and 14.4 g of dried mulberry leaf, respectively. Based on the infusion ratio used in this study, achieving the higher HED would require consumption of approximately 720 mL of mulberry leaf tea per day, a volume within the practical range of daily fluid intake. These estimates support the feasibility of translating the experimental dosages to future human dietary intervention studies.

Biochemical parameters

The experiment entrusted the National Laboratory Animal Center to measure biochemical indicators such as triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c).

H&E staining

After euthanizing the mice, liver and epididymal adipose tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h, then dehydrated through alcohol, xylene, and paraffin. The tissues were embedded in paraffin, cooled to solidify, and sectioned at 5 μm and 4 μm thicknesses, respectively. The sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. After staining, the sections were mounted on slides and observed under a microscope.

Western blot procedure

Liver and fat tissues were homogenized and lysed with ice-cold lysis buffer, followed by a minimum 1-h incubation on ice. After homogenization, samples were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 1 h at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C for future analysis. Protein concentrations were measured using a Bio-Rad protein assay. For electrophoresis, 25 μg of protein were loaded onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore Ltd., Tullagreen, County Cork, Ireland) and transferred. The membranes were blocked with a blocking solution and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the membranes were washed three times with 0.2% TPBS for 10 min before and after applying secondary antibodies to remove unbound antibodies. Protein bands were detected by chemiluminescence (ECL, Merck Millipore Ltd), and band intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. β-actin and vinculin were used as the internal control for Western blot analysis.

Proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines analysis

Proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and the chemokine MCP-1, in hepatic and adipose tissues were measured using ELISA kits from Invitrogen Inc, according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software. One-way ANOVA was conducted to assess differences, followed by Duncan's Multiple Range Test to compare variations between different groups. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), with at least three replicates for each test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of MAL on body weight in high-fat diet-induced obese C57BL/6J mice

The mice were sacrificed at week 14. Based on gross appearance, mice in the HFD group appeared larger and rounder than those in the ND group. In contrast, no obvious differences in body size or shape were observed between the MAL-L or MAL-H groups and the HFD group (Fig. 1A). The body weight change data also shows that by week 14, the average body weight of the HFD group was 33.01 ± 1.26 g, which was significantly higher than the ND group's average body weight of 28.35 ± 1.45 g, confirming that a high-fat diet successfully induced obesity in the mice. Meanwhile, the average body weights of the MAL-L and MAL-H groups at week 14 were 33.33 ± 2.50 g and 33.41 ± 3.33 g, respectively, which did not show a significant difference when compared to the HFD group (Fig. 1B–C). These results suggest that MAL in this experiment did not improve obesity by reducing body weight.

Weekly food intake was recorded throughout the experiment. The HFD group consumed significantly less food (2.86 ± 0.18 g/day) than the ND group (4.50 ± 0.48 g/day), which may be attributed to the higher caloric density of the high-fat diet (ND: 3.4 kcal/g; HFD: 4.4 kcal/g) and the effect of dietary fat on delaying gastric emptying and reducing gastrointestinal motility. The MAL-L and MAL-H groups showed food intakes of 3.02 ± 0.23 g/day and 3.16 ± 0.26 g/day, respectively, with no significant difference compared to the HFD group (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that mulberry leaf tea did not suppress food intake.

Water intake in the HFD group (3.59 ± 0.35 g/day) was significantly lower than that in the ND group (4.88 ± 0.39 g/day), consistent with previous reports that high-fat diets alter thirst regulation and reduce water intake. In contrast, water intake in the MAL-L and MAL-H groups increased to 5.10 ± 1.04 g/day and 5.10 ± 1.08 g/day, respectively, showing a significant increase compared with the HFD group but no significant difference compared with the ND group (Fig. 1E). Although caloric intake in the MAL-H group was significantly higher than that in the HFD group (Fig. 1F), no significant differences were observed among the HFD, MAL-L, and MAL-H groups in food conversion ratio or energy efficiency ratio (Fig. 1G and H). No overt adverse effects, such as diarrhea or abnormal bedding conditions, were observed during the experimental period. Collectively, these results indicate that mulberry leaf tea increased water intake without altering feeding efficiency or inducing observable adverse effects.

The effect of MAL on serum biochemical parameters in C57BL/6L mice induced by a high-fat diet

AST and ALT are indicators used to detect liver cell damage. ALT is primarily found in liver cells, making it more specific for liver cell injury, while AST is found not only in the liver but also in the brain, heart, and other organs. AST levels can also rise with muscle damage, potentially causing conditions like myocardial infarction or rhabdomyolysis.22 The results showed that the ALT concentration in the HFD group (78.62 ± 17.84 U/L) was significantly higher compared to the ND group (31.90 ± 3.20 U/L), while the MAL-L group (42.14 ± 6.13 U/L) and MAL-H group (35.94 ± 9.61 U/L) showed no significant difference from the ND group. This suggests that MAL could prevent liver cell damage caused by HFD, possibly by reducing lipid accumulation in the liver, providing a protective effect. However, no significant differences in AST levels were observed between the four groups (ND: 104.26 ± 19.83 U/L; HFD: 200.14 ± 98.80 U/L; MAL-L: 161.56 ± 61.34 U/L; MAL-H: 110.64 ± 29.51 U/L), which might be due to the large individual differences within the groups.

TG concentration can be used to assess the lipid metabolic state of patients. The results showed that compared to the ND group, the HFD group had significantly increased levels of TC, TG, HDL-c, and LDL-c. However, the TG levels in the MAL-L and MAL-H groups significantly decreased, returning to levels similar to the ND group, while the TC, HDL-c, and LDL-c levels showed no significant differences when compared to the HFD group (Table 1).

Effect of MAL on organ appearance and weight in high-fat diet-induced obesity in C57BL/6L mice

The gross appearance of organs was examined in the mice. Visually, an apparent difference in liver color was observed between the HFD and ND groups. Livers from the ND group appeared bright red, whereas those from the HFD group appeared paler, which may be associated with increased lipid accumulation. In contrast, no obvious differences in liver color were observed between the MAL-L or MAL-H groups and the HFD group. With respect to organ size, livers from the HFD group appeared larger than those from the ND group, while the liver size in the MAL-L and MAL-H groups appeared comparable to that of the HFD group. Further evaluation of hepatic morphology was conducted using histological staining and tissue sectioning. In addition, no apparent differences in the gross appearance or size of the kidneys and spleens were observed among the experimental groups (Fig. 2A). The liver weight results showed that the HFD group had a significantly increased liver weight (1.72 ± 0.05 g) compared to the ND group (1.34 ± 0.04 g), which corresponds to the appearance results. This increase in weight is likely due to fat accumulation in the liver. However, the liver weights of MAL-treated groups (MAL-L: 1.73 ± 0.15 g; MAL-H: 1.70 ± 0.16 g) did not show significant differences when compared to the HFD group (Fig. 2B). The kidney weight results indicated that the HFD group had a significantly increased kidney weight (0.45 ± 0.03 g) compared to the ND group (0.36 ± 0.01 g). The kidney weights in the MAL-treated groups (MAL-L: 0.41 ± 0.02 g; MAL-H: 0.40 ± 0.02 g) showed a decreasing trend compared to the HFD group, but the difference was not statistically significant, suggesting that MAL might have a certain alleviating effect on HFD-induced kidney toxicity (Fig. 2C). The spleen weight results revealed that the HFD group had a significantly increased spleen weight (0.088 ± 0.008 g) compared to the ND group (0.058 ± 0.004 g). The spleen weights in the MAL-treated groups (MAL-L: 0.073 ± 0.008 g; MAL-H: 0.078 ± 0.008 g) were slightly lower than that of the HFD group, but no significant difference was observed, suggesting that MAL may offer protective effects against HFD-induced damage (Fig. 2D).

Although previous analyses of the liver showed no significant differences in size, color, or weight between the two MAL-treated groups and the HFD group, further observation of the liver tissue through staining slices was conducted to confirm these findings. According to the liver H&E staining results, compared to the ND group, the liver cells in the HFD group displayed numerous prominent vacuoles, indicating that prolonged high-fat diet induction led to microvesicular fatty degeneration in the liver. However, whether this reaches the level of fatty liver disease requires further assessment through additional methods. In contrast, the MAL-treated groups showed a visible reduction in vacuoles, with the MAL-H group demonstrating better results than the MAL-L group, suggesting that MAL could mitigate the liver fat accumulation induced by a high-fat diet. Furthermore, the effect improved with the higher concentration of MAL, indicating a dose-dependent effect (Fig. 2E).

The effect of MAL on the hepatic AMPK pathway in C57BL/6L mice induced by a high-fat diet

The adiponectin levels in the HFD group were significantly lower compared to the ND group. However, the levels in both the MAL-L and MAL-H groups were significantly higher than those in the HFD group and showed no significant difference from the ND group. This suggests that MAL can counteract the reduction of adiponectin levels caused by a high-fat diet (Fig. 3A).

The activation status of AMPK in the liver was assessed through the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio. The results showed a significant decrease in the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio in the HFD group compared to the ND group, indicating a substantial reduction in AMPK activation in the liver of the HFD group. In contrast, the MAL-L group showed a significant increase in AMPK activation. While the MAL-H group did not show a significant difference, its p-AMPK/AMPK ratio was still markedly higher than that of the HFD group (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that MAL can enhance AMPK activation, which further implies that MAL may have a positive effect on liver damage and inflammation induced by obesity. However, there was no significant ameliorative effect in p-ACC/ACC (Fig. 3C).

The effects of MAL on the appearance and weight of adipose tissue in C57BL/6L mice induced by a high-fat diet

After sacrifice, epididymal, perirenal, and mesenteric adipose tissues were collected for observation and weighing. The appearance revealed that the epididymal, perirenal, and mesenteric adipose tissues in the HFD group were larger than those in the ND group. In contrast, the adipose tissue size in the MAL-treated groups appeared to have decreased compared to the HFD group (Fig. 4A). However, according to the weighing results, although the epididymal fat (1.25 ± 0.18 g), perirenal fat (0.59 ± 0.09 g), and mesenteric fat (0.38 ± 0.06 g) in the HFD group were significantly heavier than those in the ND group (epididymal: 0.38 ± 0.04 g; perirenal: 0.14 ± 0.02 g; mesenteric: 0.15 ± 0.05 g), no significant difference was found in the fat weight between the MAL-L group (epididymal: 0.98 ± 0.38 g; perirenal: 0.38 ± 0.15 g; mesenteric: 0.29 ± 0.10 g), the MAL-H group (epididymal: 1.13 ± 0.44 g; perirenal: 0.47 ± 0.20 g; mesenteric: 0.36 ± 0.16 g) and the HFD group (Fig. 4B–D).

Effects of MAL on adipocyte hyperplasia in C57BL/6L mice with high-fat diet-induced obesity

In the HFD group, C/EBPα levels showed a decreasing trend compared to the ND group, although the difference was not statistically significant, likely due to individual variability. However, the MAL-L and MAL-H groups exhibited significantly higher C/EBPα levels compared to the HFD group, with even higher levels than the ND group, indicating that MAL promoted C/EBPα expression (Fig. 5A). Similarly, PPARγ levels in the HFD group showed a decreasing trend but did not reach statistical significance. The MAL-L and MAL-H groups displayed a slight increase in PPARγ levels compared to the HFD group, reaching a level similar to that of the ND group, though the difference was not significant. These findings suggest that MAL may have a potential role in promoting PPARγ expression (Fig. 5B). Analysis of adiponectin levels in adipose tissue revealed that the HFD group exhibited significantly lower adiponectin expression compared to the ND group. In contrast, the MAL-L and MAL-H groups showed a significant increase in adiponectin levels relative to the HFD group, reaching levels comparable to the ND group (Fig. 5C). In addition, the result of p-AMPK/AMPK in adipose tissue showed that low dosage of mulberry leave tea could activate AMPK (Fig. 5D), but there was no effect on CPT1A expression (Fig. 5E). Based on these findings and the observed changes in adipocyte size, it can be inferred that MAL may promote adipogenesis, shifting the mode of adipocyte expansion from hypertrophy to hyperplasia. Furthermore, these results suggest that MAL may also play a role in reducing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

The effects of MAL on inflammation in HFD-induced obesity in C57BL/6L mice

Previous findings have demonstrated that MAL positively influences adiponectin levels, AMPK phosphorylation, and adipogenesis. Since these proteins and pathways play crucial roles in regulating inflammation, we further investigated the effects of MAL on inflammatory responses by measuring the expression of key cytokines, including TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-6, and IL-1β, in both liver and adipose tissues. In liver tissues, the levels of TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-6, and IL-1β were significantly elevated in the HFD group compared to the ND group. However, TNF-α and MCP-1 levels were significantly reduced in the MAL-L group relative to the HFD group, though they did not fully recover to ND levels. While the MAL-H group did not show statistically significant differences, a decreasing trend was observed. Additionally, IL-6 and IL-1β levels in both MAL-L and MAL-H groups showed a downward trend compared to the HFD group, but the differences were not statistically significant. These results suggest that MAL may help reduce the production of inflammatory cytokines, thereby exerting a protective effect on the liver (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of MAL on adipose accumulation, adipocyte size changes, adipogenesis, and inflammatory responses in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mice. In addition to reflecting typical human consumption patterns, the use of a low-dose tea preparation also considered potential safety concerns. Although previous studies have employed concentrated extracts or oral gavage, the long-term safety of high-dose mulberry leaf interventions remains uncertain. Given the 14-week duration of our model, we opted for a lower-dose, water-based administration to minimize toxicity risks while maintaining physiological relevance. The results showed that the weights of epididymal, perirenal, and mesenteric fat were significantly increased in the HFD group, accompanied by a marked enlargement of adipocytes, a reduction in adiponectin expression, and a significant elevation of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-6, IL-1β). However, both MAL-L and MAL-H groups exhibited a reduction in adipocyte hypertrophy and promoted adipogenesis, leading to an increase in adiponectin expression and a significant decrease in inflammatory cytokines. In particular, TNF-α and IL-1β levels in adipose tissue were reduced and approached levels comparable to the ND group, whereas IL-6 levels, although decreased, remained significantly higher than those in the ND group in the MAL-H group. Taken together, these findings suggest that MAL may regulate adipocyte formation pathways and inflammatory responses, thereby reducing HFD-induced fat accumulation and inflammation, demonstrating its potential anti-obesity and anti-inflammatory effects.

Our results showed that mulberry leaf tea supplementation, when provided as drinking water, did not significantly ameliorate obesity. In reference to other studies where mulberry leaves, after water or alcohol extraction and freeze-drying, were mixed into the feed at much higher concentrations,17,23 it is hypothesized that the lack of weight reduction in the mice in this study may be due to the insufficient concentration of the mulberry leaf tea used.

The results of H&E staining indicated that MAL-L and MAL-H could have a positive effect on lipid accumulation compared to the HFD group. To explore the potential translational relevance of MAL, we conducted a preliminary in vitro study using the human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2. HepG2 cells were co-treated with 300 μM oleic acid and either 400 or 600 μg/mL of MAL for 24 h. These concentrations were selected based on prior cytotoxicity screening, which indicated that doses above 600 μg/mL reduced cell viability. Oil Red O staining revealed that 600 μg/mL MAL significantly reduced lipid accumulation compared to the oleic acid-only group, whereas 400 μg/mL had no observable effect (Fig. S4). Although the reduction in lipid content at 600 μg/mL was statistically significant, the extent of improvement was relatively modest. Mulberry leaf extract has been shown to contain compounds such as rutin, which, according to previous studies, can reduce triglyceride levels in the liver and alleviate lipid-induced oxidative damage. Rutin plays an important role in combating cellular oxidative stress and regulating lipid metabolism,24 but the specific mechanisms require further investigation. Moreover, although there was no significant change in the weight of adipose tissue, it was observed that the size of adipocytes significantly decreased with the supplementation of mulberry leaf tea. Ye et al. (2021) investigated the effects of Pu-erh tea, which was brewed, cooled, filtered, and freeze-dried, on high-fat diet-induced C57BL/6 mice.25 Their results showed a reduction in fat size, which is consistent with the findings of our experiment. Liu et al. (2021) also reported that mulberry leaf polyphenols inhibited the enlargement of fat cells caused by a high-fat diet.26 Therefore, the reduction in fat size observed in this experiment may be attributed to the effects of mulberry leaf polyphenols. It has been reported that polyphenols in tea can increase the expression levels of PPARγ and C/EBPα, leading to the formation of smaller adipocytes to produce adiponectin.27 Based on the results regarding adipocyte size, it can be inferred that MAL may promote adipogenesis, shifting the increase in adipocytes from hypertrophy to hyperplasia. Furthermore, these findings suggest that MAL also has a positive effect on reducing the secretion of inflammatory factors. More importantly, the intake of mulberry leaf tea could reverse the elevation of serum TG, AST, and ALT. Literature has indicated that chlorogenic acid can inhibit adipocyte differentiation and the accumulation of TG.26 Therefore, it is speculated that the reduction in TG observed in MAL-treated groups of this experiment may be attributed to the effects of chlorogenic acid.

AMPK plays a crucial role in the liver, where it is activated by adiponectin, leading to phosphorylation and exerting its effects in physiological processes. Studies have shown that activation of AMPK can improve insulin resistance and glucose tolerance in high-fat diet-induced mice, and extend lifespan that is shortened due to obesity.28 AMPK is a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, controlling lipid metabolism in hepatocytes. As a therapeutic target for liver steatosis, AMPK can prevent liver fat accumulation by inhibiting fatty acid synthesis and enhancing fatty acid oxidation.29,30 While the primary source of adiponectin is not the liver, its effects on metabolism in the liver are well known, particularly through activation of the AMPK pathway by AdipoR1, which decreases the expression of lipogenesis genes. Additionally, adiponectin regulates glucose homeostasis and triglyceride production by increasing fatty acid oxidation and reducing fatty acid uptake. Its role in liver diseases is closely related to lipid degeneration, hepatomegaly, and inflammation.31 Our study showed that mulberry leaf tea supplementation could activate AMPK. Tung et al. (2021) found that rutin significantly increased the expression of p-AMPK in the liver of high-fat diet-induced obese mice, significantly enhancing fatty acid oxidation and thereby slowing the progression of NAFLD,32 which is consistent with the results of our experiment. Lastly, the mulberry extract exhibited a compelling anti-inflammatory effect in both liver and adipose tissue, suggesting its potential to mitigate inflammation associated with high-fat diet-induced obesity. Previous studies have indicated that mulberry leaf extract, when administered via gavage, can significantly reduce the expression of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β in white adipose tissue elevated by a high-fat diet. This suggests that mulberry leaves may help reduce obesity by modulating inflammatory responses.33 Although the study by Naowaboot et al. (2012) was conducted in 3T3-L1 adipocytes rather than in vivo, their findings showed that mulberry leaf extract increased adiponectin secretion independently of lipid accumulation.34 This supports the possibility that mulberry leaf-derived compounds may exert adipokine-modulating effects without directly altering adiposity. In our study, a similar pattern was observed, in which mulberry leaf tea increased adiponectin levels and reduced inflammatory markers without significantly affecting body weight or fat mass.

Although obesity is typically associated with metabolic dysfunction, epidemiological studies have identified a subset of individuals who are obese but metabolically healthy (MHO).35 The concept of MHO refers to individuals who, despite excess adiposity, maintain relatively normal metabolic profiles. The mechanisms underlying this phenotype remain poorly defined, but accumulating evidence suggests that systemic inflammation plays a central role in linking adiposity to metabolic disease. Therefore, interventions that reduce inflammation without necessarily reducing body weight may contribute to improved metabolic health. In our study, mulberry leaf tea improved adiponectin levels and attenuated inflammatory markers without significantly altering body weight or fat mass. These findings suggest a weight-independent mechanism of metabolic modulation that may align with the MHO phenotype. Nutritional compounds such as polyphenols, which are known to modulate inflammatory pathways, have been proposed as potential modulators of this phenotype. While the MHO concept offers a useful framework for interpreting our results, evidence directly linking natural dietary interventions to this phenotype remains limited. We therefore believe that our findings may provide a valuable reference point for future research exploring weight-independent strategies for improving metabolic health.

Several recent studies support the idea that mulberry leaf-derived preparations can improve metabolic health without necessarily reducing body weight. One study found that mulberry extract improved hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation in a NAFLD mouse model, possibly via the AMPK-PPARγ-NF-κB pathway.36 In another experiment, mice receiving 3% water-extracted mulberry leaf in their diet showed reductions in body weight and adiposity, while the ethanol extract, despite not affecting fat mass, still improved blood glucose control and liver lipid metabolism.37 These findings indicate that metabolic improvements from mulberry leaf compounds are not strictly tied to weight loss. In addition, several studies suggest that gut microbiota-mediated mechanisms may contribute to these effects. Ethanol extracts of mulberry leaf have been shown to shift microbial composition and inhibit intestinal FXR signaling by increasing fecal ω-muricholic acid, which enhances GLP-1 secretion and insulin sensitivity.38 Polysaccharide fractions improve lipid and glucose metabolism through changes in bacteria such as Prevotella, Lactobacillus, and Ruminococcus, along with bile acid-related gene expression (CYP7A1, TGR5, and FXR).39 Water and ethanol extracts have also been reported to elevate fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), stimulate GPR43 and GPR109A in the colon, and activate AMPK in the liver. These changes are accompanied by reduced inflammatory cytokines and enhanced intestinal barrier integrity through lower circulating LPS and higher levels of tight junction proteins.40 While our study did not directly measure gut microbial or bile acid parameters, the observed increase in adiponectin expression and reduction in adipose inflammatory markers, despite stable body weight, may reflect similar underlying mechanisms. Together, these data provide additional support for weight-independent benefits of mulberry leaf tea and offer a plausible explanation for its anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects.

In summary, our study demonstrated that mulberry leaf tea supplementation has beneficial effects on high-fat diet-induced obesity in C57BL/6 mice. The supplementation significantly reduced adipocyte size, improved lipid metabolism by lowering serum triglycerides, and activated AMPK, which is crucial for lipid and glucose metabolism. Additionally, mulberry leaf tea promoted adipogenesis through the regulation of adiponectin, PPARγ, and C/EBPα expression. Furthermore, the extract exhibited strong anti-inflammatory effects in both liver and adipose tissue, indicating its potential to reduce inflammation and improve metabolic health. These results suggest that mulberry leaf tea could be a promising natural intervention for managing obesity and related metabolic disorders. However, the oral administration via drinking water may have resulted in an insufficient dose of the mulberry leaf tea, which could explain the lack of significant anti-obesity effects observed in this experiment. Future studies could consider administering mulberry leaf tea in the form of a supplement to better evaluate its effectiveness. Additionally, further investigation into more inflammatory pathways could provide a deeper understanding of its potential mechanisms of action.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the specific bioactive constituents responsible for the observed effects of mulberry leaf tea remain unidentified. As the tea is composed of a complex mixture of flavonoids, alkaloids, and other phytochemicals, future research should aim to isolate and characterize the active compounds contributing to its metabolic effects. Second, although we observed reductions in inflammatory markers, the exact molecular pathways involved, particularly downstream targets of AMPK and related signaling cascades, were not fully explored. Given the modest physiological changes seen in this model, our focus was limited to systemic outcomes, and more detailed mechanistic investigations were beyond the scope of the current study.

In addition, while we found weight-independent improvements in metabolic and inflammatory profiles following long-term mulberry leaf tea intake, the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified. Recent evidence suggests that gut microbiota, bile acid metabolism, and SCFA-GPR signaling may play a role in modulating adipose tissue inflammation and insulin sensitivity. Therefore, future studies should consider incorporating gut microbiota profiling, including fecal SCFA analysis and intestinal expression of FXR and TGR5. These data would help determine whether the observed effects are mediated through microbiota–host interactions, and would also clarify whether tea-based interventions act via similar or distinct mechanisms compared to concentrated extracts used in previous studies.

Future perspective

Although the anti-inflammatory effects observed with mulberry leaf tea are encouraging, its relatively low dose and the limited duration of intervention may have constrained its influence on lipid metabolism and body weight. Future studies may consider increasing the dosage, extending the intervention period, or combining tea consumption with complementary strategies such as exercise or caloric restriction to assess potential synergistic effects.

In addition, further investigation into tissue-specific AMPK activation and immune modulation would be valuable for clarifying the underlying mechanisms and supporting the design of functional food products aimed at obesity-related inflammation. While the bioactivity of concentrated mulberry leaf extracts is well established, the current study highlights the physiological relevance of traditionally prepared tea. The anti-inflammatory effects observed, even at a modest dose, suggest a basis for developing more effective yet practical formulations, such as concentrated infusions or standardized water-based extracts.

While the dosage used in this study was relatively low, this choice was made to reflect real-world tea consumption and reduce potential toxicity associated with long-term administration of high-concentration extracts. Although the 14-week intervention period was appropriate for assessing mid-term responses, the possibility of more significant long-term effects cannot be ruled out and warrants further investigation.

Finally, the specific chemical composition of the mulberry leaf tea used in this study was not analyzed, and a full toxicological evaluation was not conducted. However, throughout the 14-week intervention, no signs of toxicity were observed. Liver enzyme levels (AST, ALT) remained within normal ranges, and no enlargement or pathological changes were detected in the liver, kidneys, or spleen. These findings suggest that the tea preparation was well tolerated at the given dose. Nonetheless, future studies should include detailed compositional profiling and toxicological assessments to further support its safety and reproducibility.
