Enfortumab vedotin induces immunogenic cell death and shows enhanced preclinical antitumor activity when combined with a PD-1 inhibitor
Authors: Devra J Olson, Bernard A Liu, Patrick Younan, Gabriele Blahnik-Fagan, R Greg Stacey, John Gosink, Katie Snead, Elena Tenn, Kelly Hensley, Disha Sahetya, Albina Nesterova, Margo Zaval, Anthony Cao, Christine O’Day, Ryan A Heiser, Timothy S Lewis, Shyra J Gardai, Taisuke Nakazawa, Masashi Shimazaki, Christopher Carosino, Gregory L Szeto, Sharsti Sandall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEnfortumab vedotin is a Nectin-4-directed antibody-drug conjugate designed to deliver the microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) to tumor cells. Using preclinical models of urothelial cancer (UC), we expand the understanding of the multifaceted mechanism of action for enfortumab vedotin that includes direct cytotoxicity on Nectin-4-positive tumor cells, indirect bystander effect on neighboring Nectin-4-negative tumor cells, and MMAE-mediated induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) and associated increase in activated immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. Importantly, vaccination with enfortumab vedotin-treated tumor cells results in protection against tumor rechallenge in mice, consistent with antitumor immunity. MMAE-mediated ICD induction modulates the tumor microenvironment in a complementary manner to immune checkpoint inhibition. Accordingly, enfortumab vedotin plus PD-1 inhibitor shows enhanced antitumor activity in vivo. These preclinical findings provide mechanistic hypotheses that may be relevant to the improved clinical outcomes observed for enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab relative to chemotherapy.Keywords: immunogenic cell death, ICD, enfortumab vedotin, EV, antibody-drug conjugate, ADC, MMAE, bystander-activity, bladder cancer, pembrolizumab, PD-1 inhibitor, nectin-4, microtubules

Introduction

Enfortumab vedotin is a member of the vedotin family of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), which are anticancer therapies composed of a monoclonal antibody, a protease cleavable maleimidocaproyl-valine-citrulline (mc-vc) linker, and the microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE).1,2,3 Enfortumab vedotin is directed to Nectin-4, a cell adhesion molecule involved in multiple cellular processes known to be associated with oncogenesis.3,4 Elevated Nectin-4 expression levels relative to normal tissue have been reported in multiple solid tumors, including urothelial cancer (UC) and head and neck cancer,3,4,5 and have been associated with poor prognosis and survival in various tumor types,6,7,8 making Nectin-4 an attractive molecular target for ADCs.

Enfortumab vedotin has demonstrated improved overall survival alone or in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with previously treated and untreated locally advanced or metastatic urothelial cancer (la/mUC), respectively, and in cisplatin-ineligible patients with muscle invasive bladder cancer (MIBC).9,10,11 Enfortumab vedotin, like other vedotin ADCs, is hypothesized to kill target cancer cells through a multifaceted mechanism of action (MoA). Vedotin ADCs are directed to molecular targets prevalent on the surface of target cancer cells, and once bound to the cell surface, are internalized and trafficked to the lysosome. Following vedotin ADC internalization, lysosomal proteases cleave the mc-vc linker and release MMAE into the cell, where it induces apoptosis by disrupting the microtubule network.1,3,12,13 In addition, intracellular MMAE can diffuse into adjacent tumor cells irrespective of molecular target expression, in a process known as the bystander effect.14,15,16 The bystander effect is hypothesized to contribute to the activity of vedotin ADCs in tumors with heterogeneous molecular target expression.14,15,16

Tumors circumvent adaptive immune surveillance through immunological tolerance of tumor-associated neoantigens or by forming an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).17,18 Therapies that activate adaptive and innate immune cells within the TME and promote T cell recognition of tumor neoantigens may overcome failures in immune surveillance.17 Apoptotic cell death is a normal aspect of cellular turnover, and therefore, is regarded as an immunologically silent process associated with a tolerogenic response.17,19 In contrast, immunogenic cell death (ICD) promotes the activation and recruitment of immune cells to tumors.17,19,20 Vedotin ADCs have been shown to induce ICD when MMAE causes microtubule disruption and subsequent endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress resulting in the exposure and release of immunostimulatory molecules such as calreticulin, non-histone chromatin-binding protein high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP).17,21 These extracellular hallmarks function as damage-associated molecular patterns, stimulating antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and reshaping the surrounding immune-suppressive microenvironment.17 Following induction of ICD, local APCs produce soluble cytokines and chemokines that recruit additional immune cells into the tumor.17 In addition to MMAE, ICD-inducing modalities include radiotherapy, photodynamic therapy, oncolytic viruses, and certain chemotherapies such as taxanes and anthracyclines, among others.19,22,23,24,25

Tumor cells also evade immunosurveillance by activating immune checkpoint pathways that suppress antitumor immune responses. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) block co-inhibitory signaling pathways and promote immune-mediated elimination of tumor cells.26 ICIs have shown promising therapeutic outcomes, and while immune-inflamed tumors have a high response rate to immunotherapy,26,27 the response rate is not uniformly high across all tumor types and patients, prompting efforts to improve efficacy.26 Combining ICIs with ICD-inducing therapies that create an inflammatory immune environment within the TME may be associated with clinical benefit in patients with cancer.

The capacity of vedotin ADCs to induce MMAE-mediated ICD in preclinical models supports the notion that combinations of vedotin ADCs with agents that increase antitumor immune activity, such as PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, may enhance the antitumor response. The complementary clinical activity of enfortumab vedotin combined with the anti-PD-1 ICI pembrolizumab has been observed in both the EV-302/KEYNOTE-A39 (NCT04223856) and EV-303/KEYNOTE-905 (NCT03924895) studies. In EV-302, the combination significantly prolonged overall survival with a 53% reduction in the risk of death (median overall survival; 31.5 vs. 16.1 months; hazard ratio 0.47 [95% CI: 0.38–0.54]; p < 0.00001) as compared to platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with previously untreated la/mUC.28 Based on these findings, enfortumab vedotin in combination with pembrolizumab was granted FDA approval in the US for the treatment of patients with la/mUC and is the first approved regimen to replace platinum-containing chemotherapy as the recommended preferred first-line therapy for la/mUC in US and European guidelines.29,30 The activity of enfortumab vedotin+pembrolizumab in MIBC was further substantiated in the EV-303 study, which showed significant improvement in event-free survival compared to surgery alone (74.7% vs. 39.4%; hazard ratio 0.40 [95% CI 0.28–0.57]; p < 0.001) in cisplatin-ineligible MIBC and led to FDA approval of the combination as perioperative treatment in cisplatin-ineligible MIBC.10 Similarly, in cisplatin-eligible MIBC, the global randomized phase III study EV-304/KEYNOTE-B15 recently demonstrated statistically significant improvement in event-free survival for enfortumab vedotin+pembrolizumab compared to gemcitabine+cisplatin (median not reached vs. 48.5 months; hazard ratio 0.53 [95% CI: 0.41–0.70]; p < 0.0001).31 Enfortumab vedotin is currently under active investigation in combination with durvalumab with or without tremelimumab in the VOLGA global randomized phase III study (cisplatin-ineligible MIBC) (Powles et al., ASCO GU Symposium, 2022, Abstract TPS579).

Here, we expand on previous observations3 by fully characterizing the multifaceted MoA of enfortumab vedotin using preclinical models of UC. We confirm that enfortumab vedotin exerts cytotoxicity through the release of MMAE and evaluate the induction of ICD hallmarks in Nectin-4-expressing cells. We further investigate how enfortumab vedotin impacts the TME and assess whether vaccination with enfortumab vedotin-treated cells protects against tumor rechallenge. Lastly, we evaluate the impacts of combining enfortumab vedotin with an anti-PD-1 inhibitor.

Results

Enfortumab vedotin mediates direct cytotoxicity in UC cell lines

It has recently been reported that only UC cell lines with the highest endogenous Nectin-4 expression (such as HT-1376) are partially sensitive to enfortumab vedotin in a long-duration (7–12 days) in vitro assay.32 However, in standard (4–6 days) cytotoxicity assays, we have not observed consistent enfortumab vedotin activity, highlighting the need for novel models of UC to better study the MoA of enfortumab vedotin. For this reason, engineered human (h)Nectin-4-expressing UC cell lines were utilized to explore in more detail the MoA of enfortumab vedotin. A comparison of Nectin-4 expression levels determined by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of bladder tumor sections from patients, available UC cell lines, and engineered cell lines used in these studies (Figures S1A–S1C) demonstrated the relevance of the study model compared to clinical samples. This finding was further corroborated by qFACS and RNA-seq data (Figures S1D and S1E). In mechanistic studies, we utilized indirect immunofluorescence to measure the internalization and trafficking of enfortumab vedotin to the lysosome, which is required for MMAE release from the ADC. T24-hNectin-4 cells were treated with enfortumab vedotin and incubated to allow for visualization. Following incubation, colocalization of enfortumab vedotin, and LAMP1 was observed within 2 h, indicating that enfortumab vedotin is quickly internalized and trafficked to lysosomes in these cells (Figure 1A).

To measure the intracellular accumulation of MMAE in cells treated with enfortumab vedotin, T24 parental cells lacking Nectin-4 expression and T24-hNectin-4 cells were treated with either 0.1 or 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin and incubated for 24 h. Mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was then used to assess intracellular MMAE levels. Enfortumab vedotin resulted in an accumulation of MMAE at 95 nM and 249 nM in only T24-hNectin-4 cells when treated at 0.1 μg/mL (IC50 concentration) and 1 μg/mL (IC90 concentration) dose levels, respectively (Figure 1B). MMAE accumulation occurred in Nectin-4-expressing cells, demonstrating that intracellular accumulation of MMAE is dependent on Nectin-4 expression in cells treated with enfortumab vedotin (Figure 1B). These findings are consistent with a previous study that explored comprehensively the relationship between Nectin-4 expression and sensitivity to enfortumab vedotin in cell lines.32

To assess microtubule disruption and mitotic arrest following enfortumab vedotin treatment, T24-hNectin-4 cells were treated with 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, incubated for 48 h, and stained with β-tubulin and DAPI. β-Tubulin staining showed that enfortumab vedotin treatment resulted in microtubule disruption in T24-hNectin-4 cells (Figure 1C). Furthermore, DAPI staining demonstrated that enfortumab vedotin treatment caused the formation of multinucleated T24-hNectin-4 cells, indicative of defective mitosis (Figure 1C).

Finally, we assessed the direct cytotoxic activity of enfortumab vedotin in both T24 parental and T24-hNectin-4 cells 120 h after treatment with enfortumab vedotin. Enfortumab vedotin treatment resulted in cytotoxicity in Nectin-4-expressing T24 cells but not in the parental T24 cell line, demonstrating that direct in vitro cytotoxicity induced by enfortumab vedotin requires Nectin-4 expression in UC cells (Figure 1D). These results are consistent with previous preclinical studies of enfortumab vedotin that have demonstrated cytotoxicity in breast and engineered prostate cell lines.3

Enfortumab vedotin mediates bystander effect on Nectin-4-negative cells

An in vitro bladder carcinoma cell line coculture assay system was designed to determine whether enfortumab vedotin can mediate bystander activity on Nectin-4-negative tumor cells (Figures 2A and S2). Enfortumab vedotin treatment produced a decrease in the viable UM-UC-3 GFP+/Nectin-4-negative population (Q3) relative to the non-binding ADC control (IgG-MMAE) (Figure 2B). Furthermore, enfortumab vedotin treatment showed a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect on GFP-positive bystander cells (UM-UC-3-GFP+), with a maximum of 80% reduction in cell viability at the highest tested enfortumab vedotin concentration of 1000 ng/mL (Figure 2C). Similar results were obtained when this experiment was repeated in T24 cells (Figure 2D).

Enfortumab vedotin induces ICD hallmarks and activates macrophages in vitro

MMAE released into tumor cells has the potential to induce microtubule disruption resulting in ER stress and ICD (Figure 3A). To determine whether enfortumab vedotin induces the ER stress response in vitro, T24-hNectin-4 bladder carcinoma cells were treated with 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, IgG-MMAE, or 1 nM free MMAE, and JNK phosphorylation levels, which increase in response to ER stress, were assessed by western blotting (Figures 3B and S3). A 2.2-fold increase in phospho-JNK levels was observed in cells treated with enfortumab vedotin compared with untreated cells at 48 h post exposure. Free MMAE resulted in a 1.7-fold phospho-JNK increase, while the non-binding ADC control led to a 1.5-fold increase (Figure 3B). A time-course study demonstrated that enfortumab vedotin increased phospho-JNK levels starting at 24 h post exposure and continuing through 48 h post exposure (Figure 3B). These data indicate that treating T24-hNectin-4 bladder carcinoma cells with MMAE, both free and enfortumab vedotin-bound, increases JNK phosphorylation in a manner consistent with induction of the ER stress response (Figure 3B).

Cells undergoing ICD exhibit defined hallmarks, which include increased translocation and exposure of calreticulin from the ER to the cell surface and extracellular release of ATP and HMGB1.17,20,33 A significant 10-fold increase in ATP and 6-fold increase in HMGB1 release was observed in T24-hNectin-4 cells treated with enfortumab vedotin compared with untreated cells (p < 0.001 based on multiple t tests with Bonferroni correction) (Figure 3C, top). Free MMAE resulted in an over 4-fold increase in ATP and HMGB1 release compared to untreated (p < 0.001) in both parental and target-expressing cells. This effect was specific to enfortumab vedotin and its MMAE payload since no effect was observed with the unconjugated Nectin-4-specific antibody lacking an MMAE payload (Nectin-4 Ab) or with IgG-MMAE. Consistent results were obtained with UM-UC-3-hNectin-4 cells (Figure 3C, bottom). To determine whether enfortumab vedotin increased the cell surface expression of calreticulin, T24-hNectin-4 cells were treated with 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, Nectin-4 Ab, or IgG-MMAE and analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 3D). Enfortumab vedotin treatment resulted in an increase in the proportion of cells co-expressing annexin V and calreticulin on the surface (39%) compared with untreated cells (5.1%), Nectin-4 Ab (8.1%) and IgG-MMAE (9.6%) treatments (Figure 3D). Treatment with 1 nM MMAE resulted in 8.6% calreticulin co-expression. To assess the consequence of ICD hallmark induction on innate immune cell function, T24-hNectin-4 cells were treated for 24 h with 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, Nectin-4 Ab, IgG-MMAE, or 1 nM MMAE and then cocultured for 24 h with purified monocytes that were isolated from PBMCs (Figure 3E). Enfortumab vedotin-treated tumor cells induced a significant increase in the cell surface levels of the activation markers HLA-DR (5-fold increase), CD86 (5-fold increase), and CD80 (4-fold increase) on monocytes compared to untreated control (Figures 3E and S4). Similar, although less pronounced, increase was observed after treatment with 1 nM MMAE (Figure 3E). This effect was specific to targeted delivery of MMAE by enfortumab vedotin since minimal increase was observed with either Nectin-4 Ab or IgG-MMAE treatment (Figure 3E). These results demonstrate that exposure to enfortumab vedotin-killed hNectin-4-expressing cancer cells activated CD14+ monocytes, which is consistent with the induction of ICD hallmarks by intracellular MMAE (Figure 3E).

Enfortumab vedotin-treated tumors activate macrophages and stimulate cytokine release in vivo

To further characterize the potential of enfortumab vedotin to induce ICD, we investigated whether enfortumab vedotin-treated tumors activate macrophages and stimulate cytokine release in vivo in Nectin-4-expressing human T24 urothelial xenograft mice. The antitumor activity of enfortumab vedotin was robust in this model compared with the parental T24 xenograft mice lacking Nectin-4, demonstrating that direct cytotoxicity induced by enfortumab vedotin is mediated through Nectin-4 expression, which is consistent with the results of the in vitro experiments and validates this model for use to investigate in vivo impacts of ICD (Figure S5). As such, mice implanted with T24-hNectin-4 tumor cells were treated with a single 3-mg/kg intraperitoneal (IP) injection of enfortumab vedotin or IgG-MMAE and compared with untreated mice. Tumors were harvested 5 days after treatment and processed for RNA-seq, IHC, and cytokine analyses. At the time of harvest, enfortumab vedotin-treated tumors showed tumor stasis compared with the growing untreated or IgG-MMAE-treated tumors (%TGI = 62% compared with untreated tumors) (Figure 4A). To determine if enfortumab vedotin treatment induced recruitment of mouse innate immune cells to the tumor site, tumor tissues were evaluated by IHC (Figure 4B). Quantification of stained tumor sections revealed an increase in the proportion of F4/80+ macrophages and CD11c+ APCs in enfortumab vedotin-treated tumors, indicating the recruitment of mouse innate immune cells (Figure 4B). The levels of cytokines released by macrophages and dendritic cells such as IL-1α and M-CSF (CSF1), and chemoattractants such as MIP1β and MCP1, were significantly increased in the enfortumab vedotin treatment group compared with the untreated or IgG-MMAE group (Figure 4C). Similarly, interferon-inducible T cell chemoattractants MIG and CXCL10 were significantly elevated in enfortumab vedotin-treated tumors compared to the IgG-MMAE group (Figure 4C). Additionally, enfortumab vedotin-treated tumor cells upregulated the expression of genes related to antigen processing and presentation, including human MHC class I (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C), MHC class II (HLA-DMA and HLA-DRB1), and a peptide transporter crucial to MHC class I loading (TAP2) (Figure 4D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that among the human gene signatures significantly upregulated by enfortumab vedotin-treated tumor cells were those associated with autophagy, type 1 interferon response, and ER stress (Table S1; full list of terms in Table S2). Consistent with enhanced innate immune activation of APCs, mouse RNA-seq analysis showed that GO terms related to myeloid DC activation and macrophage functional state were significantly upregulated after enfortumab vedotin treatment (Figure 4E). These results are consistent with the hypothesis that ICD hallmarks induced by MMAE promote a more inflammatory TME.21

Vaccination with enfortumab vedotin-treated cells protects against tumor rechallenge

A human Nectin-4-expressing MB49 syngeneic mouse model was generated to study the effects of enfortumab vedotin on immune responses in the absence of mouse Nectin-4 cross-reactivity and surrogate antibody. This model was used to determine if vaccination with enfortumab vedotin-treated cells protects against rechallenge, which is consistent with induction of ICD and antitumor immunity.20 MB49-hNectin-4 cells were treated in vitro with enfortumab vedotin in combination with a proton pump inhibitor, valspodar, to prevent the energy-dependent efflux of MMAE and enrich for dying cells (Figure 5). The treatment of MB49-hNectin-4 cells with enfortumab vedotin +10 μM valspodar or 50 nM MMAE resulted in an increase in the proportion of dying cells (annexin V-positive/PI-negative cells) when compared to untreated or freeze/thawed cells, which provide tumor-associated antigens in the absence of ICD. As an additional strategy to enrich for dying cells, enfortumab vedotin-treated cells were positively enriched with annexin V-conjugated microbeads. Similar to valspodar treatment, this approach resulted in a substantial increase in the percentage of annexin V+/PI− cells (from 21.6% pre-enrichment to 92.6% post-enrichment) (Figure 5A).

Immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice were inoculated with enfortumab vedotin-treated MB49-hNectin-4 cells and rechallenged 14 days later in the contralateral flank with untreated cells. This strategy resulted in improved protection against rechallenge compared to the unvaccinated control (Figure 5B). Notably, at the end of the observation period, 9/10 mice immunized with enfortumab vedotin + valspodar-treated cells and 4/5 mice immunized with enfortumab vedotin-treated annexin V-sorted cells had no tumor growth at the site of rechallenge, whereas all control mice exhibited tumor growth. At the end of the observation period, average tumor volume was calculated. Significantly lower average tumor volume was observed in mice vaccinated with enfortumab vedotin + valspodar-treated cells (12.11 mm3), enfortumab vedotin-treated annexin V-sorted cells (47.65 mm3), or MMAE-treated cells (65.56 mm3) compared to the unvaccinated control group (320.5 mm3) and the group immunized with freeze thawed cells (257.8 mm3) (Figure 5C).

Enfortumab vedotin in combination with anti-mPD-1 induces robust antitumor activity and changes in the TME in vivo

In the T24-hNectin-4 xenograft mouse model, enfortumab vedotin treatment resulted in antitumor activity and immunomodulatory effects including recruitment of macrophages and APCs to the tumor site, increased inflammatory mediators, and upregulation of gene signatures consistent with ICD. Next, we evaluated the antitumor activity of enfortumab vedotin as a single agent and in combination with an anti-mPD-1 antibody in 2 syngeneic murine models. The combination of enfortumab vedotin and anti-mPD-1 produced greater antitumor activity compared to either treatment alone in both an MB49-hNectin-4 bladder carcinoma model (Figures 6A, S6A, and S6B) and an EMT6-hNectin-4 breast cancer model (Figures 6B, S6C, and S6D). F4/80 is cell surface glycoprotein expressed at high levels on the surface of macrophages, therefore F4/80-positive staining can indicate the presence of infiltrating macrophages at the tumor site. IHC analysis of MB49-hNectin-4 tumors on day 5 post treatment demonstrated the combination of enfortumab vedotin and anti-mPD-1 showed greater F4/80 staining than either agent alone (Figure 6C). The improved antitumor activity of enfortumab vedotin and anti-mPD-1 combination therapy in the MB49-hNectin-4 model was confirmed in a repeat study (Figure 6D). To establish whether enfortumab vedotin treatment induces durable protection against rechallenge, tumor-free survivor mice at the end of the primary tumor study in the anti-mPD-1 group (n = 5) and combination group (n = 9) were rechallenged with parental MB49 cells in the contralateral flank on day 36 post treatment (Figure 6E). Two mice in the combination group developed tumors at the rechallenge site, and low tumor growth became detectable in one animal in the anti-mPD-1 group toward the end of the study. The rest of the animals, whose primary tumors stayed below the limit of detection for the duration of the study (four in the anti-mPD-1 group and seven in the combination group), rejected the rechallenge with tumor cells (defined as 0 mm3 tumor volume at the rechallenge site). Overall, at the end of the observation period (day 86 post treatment), 20% (4/20) of anti-mPD-1-treated animals and 35% (7/20) of enfortumab vedotin and anti-mPD-1 combination-treated animals achieved durable complete tumor regression at the primary tumor site and showed complete rejection of parental tumor cells, indicative of host immunity against tumor antigens. The rechallenge results with parental MB49 are consistent with durable antitumor immunity and suggest that protection was not solely directed against the human Nectin-4 antigen; however, antigen spreading, tumor-specific CD8 T cell priming, and antigen cross-presentation were not directly tested in this study.

Discussion

Immunosuppression within the TME or immunological tolerance of tumor-associated neoantigens are key barriers to protective anticancer immunity.17 Therapeutic approaches that modulate the immune microenvironment toward an immunogenic or inflammatory state may combine favorably with ICI therapies.

Here, we show that enfortumab vedotin elicits hallmarks of ICD in UC cells in vitro. Enfortumab vedotin-treated hNectin-4-expressing cells exhibited early markers of ICD and ER stress and a significant increase in the extracellular release of the immunostimulatory molecules ATP and HMGB1. Furthermore, exposure of monocytes to UC cells treated with enfortumab vedotin resulted in immune cell activation, as evidenced by the robust increase in cell surface expression of activation markers (HLA-DR, CD80, and CD86) and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines.

The ICD signature demonstrated in vitro was then confirmed in vivo using human Nectin-4-expressing UC xenograft models. RNA-seq analysis of enfortumab vedotin-treated T24-hNectin-4 mouse xenograft tumors 5 days post dosing revealed enrichment of ICD-associated genes involved in autophagy and type I interferon response and upregulation of HLA/MHC class I and class II genes in human tumor cells, as well as enrichment of gene sets for myeloid DC and macrophage activation and function in the mouse TME. In addition, enfortumab vedotin stimulated the recruitment of mouse macrophages and APCs to the tumor site, as measured by IHC, and increased the levels of mouse inflammatory mediators such as IL-1α, MIP1β, and MCP1. A similar increase in tumor macrophage infiltration was observed by IHC in the MB49-hNectin-4 mouse model, which is a model associated with higher IL-10 production and an immunosuppressive environment.34,35 We speculate that phagocytic uptake of enfortumab vedotin-killed cells leads to the presentation of tumor antigens and production of proinflammatory cytokines, which may prime the host immune system to mount a memory T cell response. This hypothesis is consistent with the tumor rechallenge study where immunomodulatory effects, such as recruitment of F4/80 macrophages to the tumor site, were further increased when enfortumab vedotin was dosed with anti-mPD-1. Animals able to control tumor growth after treatment with anti-mPD-1 alone or with the combination showed lasting antitumor immunity and protection from rechallenge with antigen-negative tumor cells, potentially indicative of antigen spreading. Higher incidence of durable tumor regressions was observed with the combination treatment.

The ability to elicit ICD is not unique to MMAE or enfortumab vedotin. Preclinical models demonstrate that certain classes of chemotherapy and gamma-radiation can elicit ICD in tumor cells, leading to the generation of immunologic memory.19,22,23,24 Although some chemotherapies elicit ICD, they can also hinder the expansion of antigen-specific lymphocytes, precluding antitumor immunity.36,37,38,39 An important property of MMAE is that it may spare normal T cells, while other chemotherapies such as topoisomerase I inhibitors and platinum-based drugs may be more immunotoxic.40,41,42,43,44 Preclinical studies of vedotin ADCs targeting proteins expressed on activated T cells (such as CD2545 and PD-L1 [Ramirez et al., AACR Annual Meeting, 2025, Abstract 322]) did not result in depletion of these cells, and lower incidence of neutropenia was observed with enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab than chemotherapy in the clinic.28,46 In addition to immune cell sparing, targeted delivery of potent ICD-inducing agents to the tumor may be required to avoid counterproductive lymphocyte depletion. Previous preclinical studies have demonstrated that the combination of the HER2-targeted ADC trastuzumab-emtansine with CPIs lead to improved PD-1 and CTLA4 blockade in an orthotopic breast cancer model.47 Other work has highlighted the ability of tubulysin and pyrrolobenzodiazepine dimer (PBD)-based ADCs to combine with immunotherapies to elicit antitumor immunity in preclinical murine syngeneic tumors; however, the cellular and molecular effects explaining how these ADC payloads can drive ICD were not well defined.48 Our results suggest that MMAE-mediated ICD following enfortumab vedotin treatment is associated with microtubule perturbation and induction of ER stress, unlike other known ICD-inducing agents, which affect DNA integrity.22,23 Combination of ICD-inducing MMAE-based ADCs with ICIs may drive complementary immune mechanisms while also eliciting direct cytotoxicity of tumor cells (Liu et al., AACR Annual Meeting, 2020, Abstract 5581; Klussman et al., SITC Annual Meeting, 2020, Abstract 618; Cao et al., AACR Annual Meeting, 2016, Abstract 4914).

In addition, there are data supporting the combination of anti-PD-1 inhibitors with other vedotin ADCs.49,50 To further the current understanding of the potent and broad ability of vedotin ADCs to induce ICD, preclinical studies have begun to compare the ICD potential of different classes of ADC payloads including other microtubule inhibitors (auristatins and maytansines) and DNA-damaging agents (DNA alkylators or topoisomerase inhibitors). Initial data indicate superior ICD induction by MMAE compared to other payloads, bolstering the rationale for using vedotin ADCs as preferred partners for ICI therapies (Klussman et al., SITC Annual Meeting, 2020, Abstract 618; Cao et al., AACR Annual Meeting, 2016, Abstract 4914). Whether this preclinical observation will translate clinically is still unclear. However, both sacituzumab govitecan and trastuzumab deruxtecan, which contain topoisomerase inhibitor payloads, are being tested in combination with ICI agents in mUC (TROPHY-U-01 cohort 3 and DS8201-A-U105, respectively), and early clinical data hint that these ADCs may not have the same combinatorial benefit in mUC as MMAE-based ADCs.51,52

In this preclinical study, the combination of enfortumab vedotin and an anti-PD-1 antibody produced greater antitumor activity compared to either treatment alone in two syngeneic mouse models. Furthermore, in a human urothelial CDX mouse model, enfortumab vedotin-treated tumor cells upregulated the expression of human MHC class I (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C), MHC class II (HLA-DMA and HLA-DRB1), and peptide transporter (TAP2) genes, which have been shown to be key for the activity of immunotherapies such as PD-1 inhibitors.53 These results are consistent with the significant clinical responses observed in the EV-302 study where the combination of enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab nearly doubled the overall survival and progression-free survival benefit compared to platinum-based chemotherapy in first-line treatment of la/mUC28 and with the positive results in the EV-303 study with significantly improved overall survival compared to surgery alone in cisplatin-ineligible MIBC, both of which led to FDA approval of the combination in these settings.10

The results presented here expand our understanding of the multifaceted MoA of enfortumab vedotin (Figure S7). Our results demonstrate that enfortumab vedotin exerts antitumor activity via direct cytotoxicity on Nectin-4-positive tumor cells and indirect bystander effect on neighboring Nectin-4-negative tumor cells. Enfortumab vedotin treatment resulted in MMAE-mediated ICD induction and associated increase in activated immune cells in the TME, and improved antitumor activity was observed in multiple mouse models when enfortumab vedotin was administered with an anti-PD-1 antibody. These data provide insight into the clinical activity observed with enfortumab vedotin as a single agent and in combination with an anti-PD-1 inhibitor. Moreover, these findings support the hypothesis that combinations of enfortumab vedotin with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, and possibly with other ICIs, may provide an enhanced antitumor effect greater than seen with either agent alone.

Limitations of the study

This study utilizes engineered Nectin-4-expressing cell lines, necessitated by the inconsistent endogenous expression of Nectin-4 in UC cell lines. The engineered UC cell lines showed physiologically relevant Nectin-4 expression levels compared to the levels observed in bladder tumor sections from patients with bladder cancer by IHC (Figure S1). However, these engineered lines may not fully recapitulate the biological complexity of endogenous Nectin-4 expressing cells. The lack of mouse Nectin-4 cross-reactivity and a surrogate antibody drove us to engineer the human gene into mouse tumor cells. While no experiments were performed in humanized mice receiving T cell adoptive transfer, the in vivo assessment of ICD in this study was conducted in syngeneic mouse models with an intact immune system, which is the recognized standard method to functionally evaluate ICD induction in vivo.20,54 Parental and human Nectin-4-expressing tumors showed similar growth rates in these mice, suggesting low antigenicity of the human Nectin-4. Human Nectin-4 expression was not explicitly tested for possible contribution to the observed immune response to the tumor or introduction of immunological artifacts. However, our rechallenge results with wild-type cells suggest the immune response is not fully dependent on the human Nectin-4 antigen. Finally, our rechallenge protection studies are consistent with enfortumab vedotin enhancing tumor-specific CD8 T cell priming by increasing antigen cross-presentation, but no direct evidence is provided. Future studies could be performed to further characterize the antitumor immune responses induced by enfortumab vedotin alone and in combination with an anti-PD-1 inhibitor in syngeneic mouse models, such as ex vivo profiling of tumor-specific T cells and targeted genetic or pharmacologic perturbations to functionally test cross-presentation and tumor-specific CD8+ T cell priming.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Engineered bladder cancer cell lines were developed to study the MoA of enfortumab vedotin in models which reflect a relevant range of Nectin-4 expression (Figures S1, S6A, and S6B). Human UC cell lines T24 (HTB-4, ATCC) and UM-UC-3 (CRL-1749, ATCC) and mouse bladder carcinoma cell lines MB49 (SCC148, Millipore Sigma) cultured per supplier instructions were transduced with commercial human Nectin-4 lentiviral particles (Cellecta). Individual isogenic clones were isolated from a stable pool under puromycin selection (1 or 2 μg/mL). EMT6 murine breast cancer cells expressing human Nectin-4 were generated by HD Biosciences, Ltd and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% HI FBS (Figures S6C and S6D). All cell lines used in this study were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 and were routinely authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Mice

All procedures performed on animals were in accordance with regulations and established guidelines and were reviewed and approved by an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Seagen/Pfizer protocol #SGE-029 or HD Biosciences) or through an ethical review process.

Athymic nude mice (BALB/cAnNHsd) and C57BL/6NHsd mice were obtained from Envigo/Inotiv. All mice used in the experiments were female, approximately 6–8 weeks old. Mice were maintained in a pathogen-controlled, AAALAC-accredited animal facility on a 12/12-h light/dark cycle with controlled temperature and humidity. Animals were housed in microisolator caging with environmental enrichment and provided with ad libitum access to irradiated pelleted food and water.

Method details

Fluorescent microscopy

Enfortumab vedotin, LAMP1, and Hoechst staining

T24-hNectin-4 cells were seeded at 30,000 cells per well in Ibidi μ-Plate 96-well microscopy plates and incubated overnight, after which an unconjugated Nectin-4–specific antibody lacking an MMAE payload (Nectin-4 Ab), a non-binding control ADC (IgG-MMAE) or enfortumab vedotin were added to each well and incubated for 30 min at 4°C. Cells were stained using standard methodology. Briefly, cells were then washed with cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and either fixed and permeabilized with BD Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Biosciences, Time 0), or fresh media. Fresh media was added, and cells were returned to incubate at 37°C and washed, fixed, and permeabilized as described. Detection antibodies (Goat anti-Human Alexa Fluor 488, diluted (1:1000); Mouse anti-Human CD107a-Alexa Fluor 647, diluted 1:50) were added together in BD Perm/Wash Buffer and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Cells were then washed with cold BD Perm/Wash Buffer and Hoechst (Sigma, diluted 1:10,000) was added to each well. Cells were visualized on the GE IN Cell Analyzer 2200 (20×). High-resolution images of select wells and fields were taken on the Olympus IX83 inverted fluorescent microscope with an oil immersion 60× objective.

β-Tubulin and DAPI staining

T24-hNectin-4 cells were seeded into coated glass bottom chamber slides and incubated overnight. Cells were then treated with enfortumab vedotin (1.0 μg/mL) for 48 h, washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were blocked in Odyssey Blocking Buffer (LICOR) for 1 h at 4°C. Microtubules were stained with a β-tubulin-specific antibody (Sigma clone SAP.4G5 [cat# T7816]) and nuclei were stained with DAPI (Invitrogen, diluted 1:1000). Stained cells were imaged using the IX83 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Measurement of intracellular MMAE by mass spectrometry

T24 parental and T24-hNectin-4 cells were plated, incubated overnight, and treated with enfortumab vedotin at 100 ng/mL and 1,000 ng/mL for 24 h. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS and harvested using TrypLE before being pelleted and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80°C. An MMAE standard curve was generated using untreated cell pellets from T24 parental and T24-hNectin-4 cell lines, containing approximately the same number of cells as enfortumab vedotin-treated samples. MMAE was analyzed by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a Waters Quattro Premier triple quadrupole mass spectrometer instrument using a 50 × 3.0 mm 5 μM BETASIL Silica reverse-phase C18 column. Cell pellets from experimental unknown samples were analyzed in parallel with the MMAE standards. MMAE concentrations were determined from peak area ratios by Waters Empower Software. Values were generated in triplicate and reported as a mean and standard deviation.

A stock solution of 0.25 ng/mL d8-MMAE in methanol was prepared. A 100 μM standard solution of MMAE in 50% methanol (methanol:water, both Optima™ LC/MS grade, Fisher Chemical) was prepared. Sub-stocks from the 100 μM MMAE standard solution at concentrations of 10 μM, 2 μM, 0.4 μM, 80 nM, 16 nM, 3.2 nM, 640 p.m. were prepared. Standards were prepared and were diluted 20× in the final pellet preparation.

Solvent A was water +0.1% formic acid and solvent B was acetonitrile +0.1% formic acid. The column was equilibrated at 95% solvent A for the first 0.25 min. The gradient consisted of varying solvent A from 5% to 20% from 0.25 to 1.0 min, holding at 20% solvent An until 1.7 min, and returning to initial conditions of 5% solvent A at 1.8 min. The column was equilibrated at 5% solvent An until the end of the method at 3.5 min. The mobile phase flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. The MMAE parent ion is 718.8 amu and the MS/MS fragment ion is 686.7 amu. The MMAE+8 internal standard used for the parent ion is 726.8 amu and the identical MS/MS fragment ion is 694.7 amu. The peak area of the MS/MS fragment ions were used for data processing. To derive an equation for the quantitation of free MMAE in the experimental unknown samples, the peak area for each MMAE standard was divided by the peak area obtained for the internal MMAE+8 standard. The resultant MMAE/MMAE+8 peak area ratio was subsequently plotted as a function of the total nanomoles (nmol) of MMAE per standard and the data points were fitted to a curve (weighted 1/x2, non-linear regression).

The results obtained from mass spectrometry were in total nanomoles of the submitted samples. These were converted to nM concentrations by dividing the total nanomoles by the total calculated volume of the cell pellet.

Cell viability assay to assess cytotoxicity of enfortumab vedotin

Cytotoxicity of enfortumab vedotin against T24 parental and T24-hNectin-4 cell lines was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (Promega). Enfortumab vedotin and IgG-MMAE were titrated onto cells as an 8-point, 5-fold dilution series (1000, 200, 40, 8.0, 1.6, 0.32, 0.064, 0.0128 ng/mL). Cells were treated for 96 h, then cytotoxic activity was measured with an EnVision Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer). IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 software.

Assessment of bystander effect by flow cytometry

Admixed Nectin-4–negative and Nectin-4–expressing cells were plated and incubated overnight. Admixed cells were then treated with ADCs in a 2-mL volume for 72 or 168 h, after which cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry. Data were collected using an Attune flow cytometer and processed using the Beckton Dickinson & Company (BD) software FlowJo version 10.6.1. Scatterplots were generated by visualizing the GFP channel in BL1-A (x axis) and AnnexinV-APC channel in RL-1A (y axis). To determine the viability of the Nectin-4–negative cells (GFP-positive), quadrant 3 (Q3) percentage was normalized to 100% viability and changes in percentage of cells were calculated based on the relative percentage change from untreated or those treated with IgG-MMAE.

ER stress determination by western blot

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 150,000 cells/mL and treated with 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, 1 μg/mL IgG-MMAE, or 1 nM MMAE for 48 h. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer, harvested, and centrifuged at ∼20,000×g for 20 min at 4°C. Following sample lysis, supernatant was saved as protein lysate and processed immediately or stored at −80°C. Samples were boiled in reducing sample loading buffer and separated on SDS-PAGE gels. Nitrocellulose membranes were incubated with primary antibodies phospho-JNK rabbit antibody (4668S, Cell Signaling), SAPK/JNK rabbit antibody (9252S, Cell Signaling), and anti–β-actin mouse monoclonal antibody (sc-47778, Santa Cruz) diluted (1:1000) in LiCOR Intercept T20 TBS Antibody Diluent and probed overnight at 4°C. Incubation was followed by secondary antibody staining for 1 h at ambient temperature. Blots were scanned and the density of each band measured and compared to the densities of the loading control bands using Adobe Photoshop.

ICD hallmark assessment

ATP release assay

Extracellular ATP levels were assessed using Promega’s CellTiter-Glo (CTG) Luminescent Cell Viability assay following the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence intensity per well was measured using the Envision plate reader. ATP release is reported as the fold over background of untreated samples.

HMGB1 release assay

HMGB1 release was measured with an ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Tecan, cat# ST51011). Optical density was measured with SpectraMax at 450 nm. HMGB1 release is reported as the fold over signal of untreated samples.

Calreticulin exposure

At approximately 40 h following treatment, cells were harvested using non-enzymatic dissociation buffer, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and stained with Live/Dead Zombie Aqua for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed and resuspended in staining buffer consisting of FACS buffer (2%FBS/PBS) + 1× Annexin V binding + Annexin V-FITC + Calreticulin-APC. Cells were stained for 20 min at room temperature and were analyzed on an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Invitrogen).

Immune cell activation

Cancer cell and PBMC coculture

A total of 20,000 cells per well of T24-hNectin-4 and UM-UC-3-hNectin-4 cells were plated in 96-well ultra-low attachment plates and treated with enfortumab vedotin, IgG-MMAE, and free MMAE at concentrations from 0 to 2000 ng/mL for 24 h. Cells were then washed with PBS and re-plated with purified human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from Bloodworks Donor 3260 (PBMCs were resuspended in either EMEM or McCoy’s Modified Medium at 1×106 cells/mL, and 200,000 cells were plated into the 96-well plate). PBMCs were cocultured with treated cancer cells for 24 or 48 h and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cancer cells/purified monocyte coculture

Activation of monocytes (Stem Cell Technologies, cat# 70034) following coculture of cancer cells treated with enfortumab vedotin or controls was monitored. Monocytes were counted and suspended at 1×107 cells/mL. hNectin-4–expressing cancer cells were exposed to 1 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, Nectin-4 Ab, IgG-MMAE, or 100 nM MMAE for 24 h. Cells were then washed and resuspended at 1×106 cells/mL, of which 100 μL was added to approximately 50,000 monocytes.

Assessment of cell activation by flow cytometry

After enfortumab vedotin treatment, cells were stained with Live/Dead Zombie Aqua (BioLegend) for 15 min at room temperature. Fc receptors were blocked by incubation with 2% Fc Receptor Blocking Solution for 10 min on ice. Next, samples were incubated with BV421-CD19, BV605-CD3, PV711-CD56, BV785-CD25, AF488-CD11c, PE/Dazzle-CD11b, PE-CD14, and PE/Cy7-HLA-DR and/or FITC-annexin V and AF647-calreticulin for 30 min in the dark on ice. Cells were then analyzed on an LSRII flow cytometer with DIVA software (BD Biosciences). HLA-DR geo mean fluorescence of the CD11b+ CD14+ cell population or the percent of CD14+ monocytes expressing activation markers on the cell surface was determined using FlowJo analysis software.

Assessment of cytokine production

After 72 h of treatment, tissue culture supernatants were processed for cytokine production using a Luminex Multiplex Kit (cat# HCYTA-60K-PX38) from Millipore as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mouse models

T24-hNectin-4 xenograft mouse model

T24-hNectin-4 cells were cultured as described and resuspended in 50% Matrigel before subcutaneous injection of 5 × 106 T24-hNectin-4 xenograft tumor cells (0.1 mL volume) in the flanks of athymic nude female mice (Envigo, BALB/cAnNHsd). Tumor pieces were passaged via trocar needle two times to establish consistent engraftment. On Day 15 post implantation, tumor volumes reached ∼200 mm3 (calculated using 0.5 × [length] × [width]2), and the mice were treated with a single intraperitoneal (IP) dose of 3 mg/kg enfortumab vedotin. As a control, mice were treated with 3 mg/kg IgG-MMAE. Tumors were harvested 5 days after treatment and processed for RNA-seq (Illumina HiSeq platform), fixed and paraffin embedded (FFPE) for immunohistochemical analysis, or processed for mouse cytokine analysis by Luminex MagPix system and associated Xponent software (Millipore Sigma).

EMT6-hNectin-4 syngeneic mouse model

Female BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with EMT6-hNectin-4 tumor cells (2×105) in 0.1 mL of DPBS at the right flank. Tumor size was measured 3 times a week as described above. Treatment was initiated when the mean tumor volume reached approximately 45 mm3. Mice (n = 12/group) were randomized to four treatment groups: 5% dextrose + control rat IgG2a (10 mg/kg), enfortumab vedotin (3 mg/kg) + control rat IgG2a (10 mg/kg), 5% dextrose + anti–mPD-1 antibody (10 mg/kg), or enfortumab vedotin (3 mg/kg) + anti–mPD-1 antibody (10 mg/kg). Tumor size was used for calculating tumor growth inhibition (TGI) values. TGI (%) = [1 − (Vtreat-t − Vtreat-1)/(Vcontrol-t − Vcontrol-1)] × 100, represents antitumor effectiveness, in which Vtreat-1 and Vcontrol-1 are the mean volumes of the treated and control groups on grouping day; Vtreat-t and Vcontrol-t are the mean volumes of the treated and control groups on a given day.

MB49-hNectin-4 syngeneic mouse model

MB49-hNectin-4 tumor cells were cultured as described. Tumors were established by subcutaneous injection of 5×105 tumor cells (0.2 mL volume) in the right flanks of female C57BL/6 mice. All mice were sorted into study groups based on caliper estimation of tumor burden, and treatment was initiated on Day 4 post implantation when the mean estimated tumor burden for all groups was 82 mm3. Mice (n = 12/group) were left untreated or were dosed with 5 mg/kg enfortumab vedotin (IV once/week for 2 weeks), 10 mg/kg anti-mPD-1 (IP twice/week for 2 weeks), or the combination of 5 mg/kg enfortumab vedotin (IV once/week for 2 weeks) + 10 mg/kg anti-mPD-1 (IP twice/week for 2 weeks), and tumor volumes were measured over time. Tumors were collected from additional 3 mice/group on Day 5 after the first treatment dose was administered and flash frozen at −80°C or FFPE processed for IHC analysis. In a repeat study, 20 mice/group were treated using the same regimen as above starting on Day 4 post implantation when the mean estimated tumor burden for all groups was 64 mm3. This primary efficacy study ended on Day 35 post inoculation, and on Day 36, mice with no measurable tumor burden (≤63 mm3) were rechallenged in the opposite flank with 5×105 untreated parental MB49 cells (0.2 mL volume), and tumor growth was measured up to Day 68 post inoculation. Naive mice (n = 10) were included as a control.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA was extracted from tumor fragments and subjected to polyA selection. Purified RNA was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform 2 × 150 bp configuration. RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by GENEWIZ, Inc. The FASTQ reads were aligned at Seagen to a digitally combined human and mouse genome (hg38mm10). Transcripts were quantified via RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM). Differential expression (DE) analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package. Gene-level count data were modeled using DESeq2’s negative binomial framework, and treatment-versus-control comparisons were evaluated using Wald tests. Differentially expressed genes were identified based on Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-values generated by DESeq2.

Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) enrichment analysis was performed using an over-representation analysis (ORA) approach. Lists of differentially expressed genes were tested for enrichment of GO-BP terms, and enriched biological processes were identified based on BH-adjusted p-values. Enrichment results were summarized using standard ORA metrics, including gene ratio and the number of differentially expressed genes associated with each GO term. Upregulated and downregulated genes were independently assessed.

Immunohistochemistry

FFPE blocks were sectioned at 4 μm, and sections were placed on charged slides. All samples were processed on a Bond-III or Bond Rx autostainer (Leica Microsystems Inc.) at ambient temperature following the manufacturer’s instructions. All antibodies were diluted to working concentration in Bond Primary Antibody Diluent (Leica Microsystems, cat# AR9352). Isotype-matched mouse IgG was used as a negative control for background staining. For automated IHC staining, we used the Leica Microsystems Bond Polymer Refine Detection (DAB) kit (cat# DS9800) or Bond Intense R Detection kit (cat# DS9263). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 7 min. Images were captured using a slide scanner (Leica Biosystems, Aperio AT2), and slides and/or images were evaluated and scored by a pathologist.

Assessment of mouse cytokine and chemokine production

Tumor samples were lysed, and supernatants were processed for mouse cytokine and chemokine production using a Luminex Multiplex Kit (cat# MCYTMAG-70K-PX32) from Millipore as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Vaccination study with enfortumab vedotin-treated bladder carcinoma cells

Cell culture and treatment

MB49-hNectin-4 cells were plated at 1×107 cells per 150 mm plate and treated with the drugs listed below. Cells were treated with 5 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin +10 μM valspodar, 5 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin, or 50 nM MMAE. Untreated cells and cells subjected to five freeze/thaw cycles were included as negative controls. Untreated MB49-hNectin-4 cells were used for rechallenge.

Isolation of annexin V-positive cells

MB49-hNectin-4 cells treated with 5 μg/mL enfortumab vedotin for 96 h were enriched for dying annexin V-positive cells. Cells that became detached, which included the cells of interest, were collected and pelleted at 1500 rpm for 5 min. Magnetic separation was conducted using annexin V Microbead Kit (Miltenyi, cat# 130-090-201). The bead-bound cells were pelleted, washed in annexin V binding buffer, and resuspended in 500 μL annexin V binding buffer. Magnetic separation of annexin V+ cells was performed using LS columns (Miltenyi, cat# 130-042-401) placed on a MACS Separator. The bead-bound cells were added to the column and washed 3 times with annexin V binding buffer to remove annexin V-negative cells. LS columns were removed from the magnetic separator, and the cells were passed through the column and pelleted as described. The magnetic beads were separated from the cells using Apoptotic Cell Releasing Buffer (Apoptotic Cell Isolation Kit, cat# K258; BioVision). An aliquot of the annexin V-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry following annexin V and PI staining.

Flow cytometry analysis

The proportions of dying and dead cells were determined using annexin V/PI staining and flow cytometry. Cells were washed and incubated with the following master mix: 1:20 annexin V, 1:1000 PI (1 μg/mL), and 1× annexin V binding buffer. The cells were then analyzed by dual parameter flow cytometry (Attune NxT Flow Cytometer) to quantitate dead cells in quadrant 2 (Q2; annexin V+/PI+), dying cells in Q3 (annexin V+/PI–), and live cells in Q4 (annexin V–/PI–).

Vaccination with treated bladder carcinoma cells and rechallenge

A total of 3×105 cells per condition were implanted into the right flanks of C57BL/6 mice (n = 5–15/group). On Day 14 post implant, mice were challenged on the contralateral (left) flank with untreated 3 × 105 MB49-hNectin-4 cells. Vaccinated mice were excluded if tumor growth on the vaccination side was ≥20 mm3. Mice in the unvaccinated group received untreated tumor cells during rechallenge. Tumor volume was measured twice a week as described above. Tumor growth was monitored until tumors reached a cumulative volume of 1000 mm3. Average tumor growth was calculated using the following formula: area under the curve (AUC)/number of observed days on study. Resistance to rechallenge was recorded if an animal had no evidence of tumor growth in the flank 14 days after inoculation with treated MB49-hNectin-4 cells. Data were originally collected and stored in Microsoft Excel and subsequently transferred to GraphPad Prism 8 for additional plotting.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantitative data from in vitro assays and in vivo studies are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) as indicated in each figure legend. For in vivo efficacy studies, tumor volumes calculated using the caliper method were plotted over time, and statistical comparisons were evaluated using the tumor volumes obtained at the designated study end day (Day 19 post treatment for MB49 and Day 20 for EMT6). For vaccination and rechallenge studies, the average daily tumor burden was calculated by dividing the longitudinal area under the curve (AUC) by the total observed days on study for each animal.

Statistical significance between two independent treatment groups was determined using unpaired (Student’s) t-tests. For experiments requiring multiple comparisons, significance was evaluated using multiple t-tests and applying the Bonferroni correction to determine adjusted p-values. For in vivo combination treatment studies, two comparisons between the combination group and each monotherapy group were conducted using an unpaired Independent-Samples t test. A statistically significant combination effect was defined a priori as achieving a significant difference (p < 0.05) in both independent comparisons. Detailed statistical methods, including specific post-hoc tests, and the number of independent biological replicates are provided in each figure legend. Data, excluding RNA-seq, were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8); p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. In the ATP and HMGB1 assays, adjusted ∗p = 0.03 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001 show significant difference from untreated determined using multiple t tests with Bonferroni correction. RNA-seq data were analyzed for differentially expressed genes by Wald test implementation in DESeq2, and GO-BP enrichment was analyzed using hypergeometric test. BH-adjusted p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant for genes and enriched GO-BP terms.
