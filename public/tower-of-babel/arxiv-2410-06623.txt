Microscopic theory of Mpemba effects and a no-Mpemba theorem for monotone many-body systems
Naruo Ohga, Hisao Hayakawa, Sosuke Ito

Abstract
Mpemba effects (MPEs), where a hotter system cools faster than a colder one, present intriguing anomalies in relaxation processes. Despite their universal observation and significant fundamental and practical implications, a comprehensive theoretical understanding based on microscopic properties remains elusive. In this Letter, we introduce two universal frameworks for classical systems to address this gap. Firstly, we reveal that MPEs, traditionally defined by macroscopic temperature comparisons, can be understood through microstate comparisons. This insight offers a straightforward and universal microscopic perspective on MPEs, relevant for experiments and numerical simulations to identify their microscopic origins. Secondly, we establish a “no-Mpemba theorem,” a rigorous sufficient condition for the absence of MPEs, thereby identifying specific classes of systems devoid of these effects. Our findings are exemplified using ferromagnetic Ising models and one-dimensional multiparticle systems, demonstrating the practical applicability of our theoretical advancements.

First, we show that MPEs are universally understood as a consequence of the accumulation of

This universal framework allows us to decompose the MPE into contributions from each microstate pair, facilitating the analysis of the microscopic origins of MPEs in experiments and numerical simulations.
Second, we propose a “no-Mpemba theorem” that provides a sufficient condition for the absence of ensemble MPEs in a broad class of Markovian discrete-state many-body systems.

The condition can be verified using the system’s microscopic description without solving the time evolution, making it applicable to a wide range of many-body systems, including those with quenched randomness.
We apply this theorem to demonstrate the absence of MPEs in the ferromagnetic Ising model under strong magnetic fields and in the exclusion process within a one-dimensional monotone potential.

Setup and definition of MPE

We consider a general classical system in contact with a thermal bath, undergoing a possibly underdamped stochastic time evolution. We write a microstate of the system as a vector
\bm{x}\equiv(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
, which may be high-dimensional for many-body systems and takes either discrete or continuous values. We write the set of all possible microstates as
\mathcal{X}\ni\bm{x}
.
The system at state
\bm{x}
has energy
\epsilon(\bm{x})
, which includes interaction energy for many-body systems.
The Gibbs distribution at temperature
T
is
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T}$}}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\exp\{-[\epsilon(\bm{x})-F_{T}]/k_{\mathrm{B}}T\}
, where
F_{T}=-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\exp[-\epsilon(\bm{x})/k_{\mathrm{B}}T]
, and
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant.
Here and hereafter, a probability distribution refers to a probability density function for continuous systems and a probability mass function for discrete systems, and the volume integral
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}
should always be replaced with
\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}
for discrete systems.

We put the system in a cold thermal bath at time
t=0
and let it relax. A single realization (trajectory) of this cooling process starts with one of the states in
\mathcal{X}
and continually moves over
\mathcal{X}
without ever stopping. We write the probability of being in
\bm{x}
at time
t
conditional to being in
\bm{y}
at time 0 as
P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
, which depends on the bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
.
The time evolution may be non-Markovian as long as the system holds no memory from
t<0
, and therefore
P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
is determined by the state
\bm{y}
at
t=0
.
The conditional distribution should converge to equilibrium,
\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})
, from any
\bm{y}
.

We consider two protocols for preparing the initial state at
t=0
before cooling the system and measuring an observable (a function of state)
a(\bm{x})
at time
t
. In the first protocol, we prepare the system in a single fixed state
\bm{y}
. After evolving the system, the average of
a(\bm{x})
at time
t
is given by
\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,a(\bm{x})P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
. In the second protocol, we equilibrate the system with a bath of temperature
T_{\mathrm{i}}
at
t<0
so that the initial state
\bm{y}
at
t=0
distributes according to
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})
. This second protocol is equivalent to averaging the first protocol over many initial states. Thus, the average of
a(\bm{x})
at time
t
is given by
\langle\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})
.

Let us consider the second protocol with two initial temperatures
T_{\mathrm{i}}=T_{\mathrm{c}},T_{\mathrm{h}}
and one bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
that satisfy
T_{\mathrm{h}}>T_{\mathrm{c}}>T_{\mathrm{b}}
. We define
the ensemble MPE with initial temperatures
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and
T_{\mathrm{h}}
at time
t
by the condition

\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}>\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{h}}}.

(1)

Noting that
\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}<\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{h}}}
at the initial time, this definition can be depicted as the crossing of two curves in Fig.
1
(a).
This comparison of two initial Gibbs distributions is a standard formulation in literature
[
31
,
30
,
43
,
34
,
36
,
32
,
33
,
26
]
, and here we call it the
ensemble MPE
for clarity. The average energy has served as a practical signature of MPEs
[
9
,
14
,
27
,
21
,
55
,
23
,
22
]
, especially for many-body systems.

Result 1: Universal microscopic framework of MPEs

Our first result relates the ensemble MPE to a microscopic phenomenon. To do so, we introduce a microscopic analog of MPE that compares two microstates, which we call the
microstate MPE
.
For a bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
, we say that
the microstate MPE between states
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
is present at time
t

if
\epsilon(\bm{x})-\epsilon(\bm{y})
and
\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})-\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})
have opposite signs, or equivalently,

c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\coloneqq\bigl[\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})-\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})\bigr][\epsilon(\bm{x})-\epsilon(\bm{y})]<0.

(2)

Since
\bar{\epsilon}_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=\epsilon(\bm{x})
and
\bar{\epsilon}_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})=\epsilon(\bm{y})
, this definition can be illustrated as the crossing of two curves in Fig.
1
(b).

Result 1 states that, for fixed bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
and time
t
, the following two statements are equivalent:
(a) The ensemble MPE occurs at time
t
for at least one pair of initial temperatures
T_{\mathrm{h}}>T_{\mathrm{c}}
(
>T_{\mathrm{b}}
); (b) The average of
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
,

C_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}})\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\!\!\!d\bm{x}d\bm{y}\,c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.53325pt}{${}_{T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}}$}}(\bm{x})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.53325pt}{${}_{T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}}$}}(\bm{y})

(3)

is negative for at least one temperature
T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}
(
>T_{\mathrm{b}}
)
. Moreover, the temperatures in these statements can be taken to satisfy
T_{\mathrm{c}}<T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}<T_{\mathrm{h}}
. More precisely, if (a) holds, we can take
T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}
of (b) between
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and
T_{\mathrm{h}}
, and if (b) holds, we can take
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and
T_{\mathrm{h}}
of (a) so that
T_{\mathrm{c}}<T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}<T_{\mathrm{h}}
. We prove this result in Supplemental Material (SM)
[
85
]
using the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

This result offers a comprehensive microscopic framework for understanding ensemble MPEs.
Specifically, the ensemble MPE manifests if and only if the microstate MPEs are prevalent across a significant number of state pairs.
The microstate MPE is characterized by higher-energy states dissipating energy more rapidly, while lower-energy states retain their energy for extended periods.
When this behavior is observed across numerous state pairs, the ensemble MPE emerges.
This conceptualization provides an intuitive understanding of ensembles MPE, which is not immediately apparent from the original definition in Eq. (
1
).

Furthermore, Result 1 helps analyze the origin of ensemble MPEs in experiments and numerical simulations. From Eq. (
3
), we can interpret
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}}$}}(\bm{x})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}}$}}(\bm{y})
as the contribution of the state pair
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
to the ensemble MPE. In particular, pairs with large negative values of
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
constitute major contributions to the ensemble MPE. Therefore, by calculating
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
from the average energy
\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})
over trajectories of the cooling process, one can identify pairs of states that have dominant contributions to the ensemble MPE. We demonstrate this application below.

Our approach can be combined with an existing theoretical framework
[
31
]
in two aspects, as detailed in SM
[
85
]
.
First, Result 1 can be extended for another definition of ensemble MPE
[
31
]
that uses the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence instead of the average energy.
Second, focusing on the long-time behavior of Markovian systems, we can apply the eigenmode expansion in Ref.
[
31
]
to
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. This enables us to evaluate the microscopic origin of ensemble MPEs based on the dominant eigenmodes instead of the average energy.

Result 2: No-Mpemba theorem

Our second main result is a “no-Mpemba theorem” that rigorously proves the absence of ensemble MPEs for a broad class of systems. In Result 2, we restrict ourselves to discrete-state Markovian systems with a finite number of states. The transition rate from state
\bm{y}
to
\bm{x}
with a bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
is written as
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
, which determines the conditional distribution by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{z}:\,\bm{z}\neq\bm{x}}\bigl[R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{z})P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{y})-R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{x})P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\bigr].

(4)

We assume that
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
is irreducible and has
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
as the stationary distribution so that
\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})
from any
\bm{y}

[
88
]
.
The main idea of Result 2, based on the theory of stochastic interacting systems
[
83
,
84
]
, is to introduce a partial ordering between states (
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
), which reflects the system’s inherent structures.

Result 2 is stated as follows: For a fixed range of temperature
[T_{1},T_{2}]
, the ensemble MPE is absent for any
T_{1}\leq T_{\mathrm{b}}<T_{\mathrm{c}}<T_{\mathrm{h}}\leq T_{2}
and any
t
if there exists a partial ordering
\preceq
with which the following conditions hold:
(a)
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
implies
\epsilon(\bm{x})\leq\epsilon(\bm{y})
;

(b)
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
is nonzero only if
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are comparable (i.e.,
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
or
\bm{x}\succeq\bm{y}
) for any
T_{\mathrm{b}}\in[T_{1},T_{2}]
; (c) There exists a monotone coupling of
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
with respect to
\preceq
for any
T_{\mathrm{b}}\in[T_{1},T_{2}]
.

A monotone coupling of
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
is a fictitious Markovian process over two copies of the system
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
that satisfies the following two conditions
[
83
,
84
]
.
First, letting
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})
be the transition rate from
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
to
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
of the monotone coupling,
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})
must be zero if
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
and
\bm{u}\not\preceq\bm{v}
.
Second, the marginal process of each system copy must be identical to the original process
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
. More precisely, we impose that
\sum_{\bm{v}}R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{u}|\bm{x})
holds independently of
\bm{y}
for every
\bm{u},\bm{x}
(
\bm{u}\neq\bm{x}
), and
\sum_{\bm{u}}R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{v}|\bm{y})
holds independently of
\bm{x}
for every
\bm{v},\bm{y}
(
\bm{v}\neq\bm{y}
).

Notably, the conditions (a)–(c) of the no-Mpemba theorem can be checked based on given
\epsilon(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
,
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
,
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt,\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt,\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
, and
\preceq
without explicitly solving the Markovian time evolution. Therefore, this theorem applies to systems whose time evolution is not solvable. Finding suitable monotone coupling and partial ordering is not trivial, but we can use known constructions for many important stochastic models, including the stochastic Ising model, exclusion process, and contact process
[
83
,
84
]
.

The proof of Result 2, found in SM
[
85
]
, considers the monotone coupling with an initial state in
\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mid\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}\}
.
Since
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})=0
for
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{K}
and
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\notin\mathcal{K}
, the process stays in
\mathcal{K}
indefinitely, meaning one copy does not overtake the other.
This property is akin to the absence of MPEs and, when combined with classical theorems
[
89
,
83
,
84
]
, leads to Result 2.

Example 1: Ferromagnetic Ising models

We demonstrate our two results with ferromagnetic Ising models, in which ensemble MPEs have been found by numerical calculations
[

.
Here, we consider the Ising model on an arbitrary lattice with
L
sites under an arbitrary boundary condition.
A state of the Ising model is specified as
\bm{x}\equiv(x_{1},\dots,x_{L})
, where
x_{i}=\pm 1
denotes the direction of the spin at site
i
. We use the symbols
\mathord{\uparrow}\coloneqq+1
and
\mathord{\downarrow}\coloneqq-1
.
The number of states is
2^{L}
.
The energy of the state
\bm{x}
is
\epsilon(\bm{x})=\sum_{i}H_{i}x_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}
, where the magnetic fields
H_{i}
and the coupling constants,
J_{ij}=J_{ji}\geq 0
and
J_{ii}=0
, may depend on site indices.
For any state
\bm{x}
,
\bm{x}^{i}\coloneqq(x_{1},\dots,-x_{i},\dots,x_{L})
denotes the state obtained by flipping the
i
th spin.
We assume that the transition rate from a state
\bm{x}
to
\bm{x}^{i}
is given by
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})=r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{x}))
, where
r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\Delta\epsilon)
is an arbitrary nonzero decreasing function of
\Delta\epsilon
that may depend on the site index
i
and satisfies the detailed balance
r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\Delta\epsilon)/r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(-\Delta\epsilon)=\exp(-\Delta\epsilon/k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})
.
We set
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=0
for any
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
whose spin configurations differ at two or more sites.

We use Result 2 to show that the ensemble MPE is absent when the magnetic field is strong,
H_{i}\geq\sum_{j}J_{ij}
for all
i
. We use the partial ordering,
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
if and only if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,L
[Fig.
2
(a)],
and a monotone coupling explained in SM
[
85
]
, both adopted from Ref.
[
83
]
.
Then, the prerequisites (a)–(c) of Result 2 are satisfied for any
T_{\mathrm{b}}
, and thus, the ensemble MPE is absent. We emphasize that this theorem applies to random parameters and random lattices.

On the other hand, ensemble MPEs may emerge when the magnetic field is weak, and their origins can be analyzed using Result 1. For concreteness, we consider a one-dimensional lattice with
L=6
under a periodic boundary condition.
We set
H_{i}=1.5
,
J_{i,i+1}=J_{i+1,i}=6
for all
i
(we use
L+1\equiv 1
) and any other coupling constants to zero. We use the transition rate
r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\Delta\epsilon)=\exp[-\Delta\epsilon/(2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})]
for all

. Figure
2
(b) shows that this system exhibits ensemble MPE.

To explore its microscopic origin, we plot the average energy
\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})
for each initial state
\bm{x}
in Fig.
2
(c). The average energy shows a complicated behavior: Some states with high energy lose their energy quickly, while other states with intermediate levels of energy do not relax until later times.
Thus, many pairs of states exhibit the microstate MPE.
In Fig.
2

for all pairs at
t=10^{-1}
, when the ensemble MPE is present. From the figure, we can identify three major regions of states that produce
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})<0
, marked with

\mathord{\star}

symbols.
The region (

\mathord{\star}

) consists of states with three isolated
\mathord{\uparrow}
spins and three
\mathord{\downarrow}
spins, and the region (

\mathord{\star}

\mathord{\star}

) consists of states with four isolated
\mathord{\uparrow}
’s and two
\mathord{\downarrow}
’s. These states have high energy, but they can relax fast by flipping the isolated

Example 2: One-dimensional particle system

Another important class of many-body systems is particle systems.
We consider an exclusion process on a one-dimensional lattice with reflective boundaries, a discretized model of particles with hard-core interactions. The lattice has
L
sites, labeled as
1,\dots,L
from left to right, and each site can accommodate up to one particle.
The system contains
N
(
<L
) indistinguishable particles.
A state of the system is specified by the set of the occupied sites,
\bm{x}\equiv(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
, in which
x_{i}
is the position of the
i
th particle from the left satisfying
1\leq x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}\leq L
.
The energy of state
\bm{x}
is
\epsilon(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{x_{i}}
, where
e_{k}
is the potential energy of site
k
.
The particle at site
k
jumps to the nearest neighbors
\ell=k\pm 1
with a nonzero rate
r_{\ell|k}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
if
1\leq\ell\leq L
and
\ell
is vacant.
The rate does not depend on the configuration of the other particles, and it satisfies
r_{\ell|k}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\exp(-e_{k}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})=r_{k|\ell}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\exp(-e_{\ell}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})
.

We use Result 2 to prove that this exclusion process does not show ensemble MPEs when the potential energy is monotone,
e_{1}\leq\cdots\leq e_{L}
.
We follow Ref.
[
84
]
to introduce a partial ordering,
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
if and only if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i=1,...,N
, and a monotone coupling explained in SM
[
85
]
.
We can then prove the prerequisites (a)–(c) of Result 2 for any
T_{\mathrm{b}}
and, thus, the absence of the ensemble MPE.

In the special case
N=1
, this result states that a single particle system in a one-dimensional lattice with a monotone potential does not exhibit the ensemble MPE. By taking an appropriate continuum limit, such a system converges to a single particle system in a one-dimensional continuous space governed by a Langevin equation
[
90
]
. While we skip the mathematical rigor, we can thus see that a one-dimensional Langevin equation with a monotone potential does not exhibit ensemble MPEs, which is consistent with previous numerical results
[
36
]
.
On the other hand, a single particle in a nonmonotonic potential can exhibit ensemble MPEs, and their microscopic origin can be analyzed using our Result 1 (see SM
[
85
]
).

Discussion

In this Letter, we have developed two universal microscopic theories of MPEs that rely on microstate comparisons.
The microstate MPE provides a universal microscopic framework for understanding and analyzing ensemble MPEs, and the no-Mpemba theorem gives a general criterion for excluding the possibility of ensemble MPEs based on the microscopic description of the system.

Future work will apply these findings to a broader range of systems. Observing microstate MPEs in experiments and numerical simulations will help uncover the microscopic origins of MPEs and harness MPEs. Applying the no-Mpemba theorem to a broader class of systems will lead to a comprehensive classification of systems into those with and without MPEs. More generally, our results show the validity of rigorous and universal approaches to MPEs. It is also an interesting direction to connect our frameworks to another rigorous approach based on majorization
[
91
]
.

The KL divergence and nonequilibrium free energy

Some literature defines the ensemble MPE using quantities other than the average energy. One of the often-used quantities is the KL divergence between the instantaneous probability distribution and the final Gibbs distribution, also called the
entropic distance

in Ref.
[
31
]
. Here, we define it in an equivalent but seemingly different way. We first introduce the instantaneous probability distribution of the relaxation process starting with a Gibbs distribution
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)

. We then define the stochastic entropy

and the nonequilibrium free energy
f_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})\coloneqq\epsilon(\bm{x})-T_{\mathrm{b}}s_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})

We consider the nonequilibrium free energy as an explicitly time-dependent observable. More precisely, we regard

as the value of the observable when the system is in state
\bm{x}
at time
t
. This formulation is standard in stochastic thermodynamics (see, e.g., Eqs. (60)–(63) of Ref.
[
86
]
). The average of the nonequilibrium free energy at time
t
over the relaxation process starting with a single fixed initial state

is given by

in general while

holds at time 0. Similarly, the average of the nonequilibrium free energy at time

over the relaxation process starting from the Gibbs distribution
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)

\langle\bar{f}_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,\bar{f}_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y};T_{\mathrm{i}})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})

The derivative of

reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}}D_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{i}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,f_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})p_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,f_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})\frac{\partial p_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}},

(S14)

where the second equality follows from

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,\frac{\partial f_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}}p_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})

\displaystyle\qquad=k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}}\frac{\partial}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,p_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})

\displaystyle\qquad=0.

(S15)

We then calculate the derivative of

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x};T_{\mathrm{i}})}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\frac{\partial\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})}{\partial T_{\mathrm{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{i}}^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})\left[\epsilon(\bm{y})-\langle\epsilon\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-T_{\mathrm{b}}/T_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{-1}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{i}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})\left[f_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y};T_{\mathrm{i}})-\langle f_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\right],

(S16)

where we define

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{b}}}{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\right)\epsilon(\bm{y})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{b}}}{T_{\mathrm{i}}}F_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}

and

\displaystyle\langle f_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{b}}}{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\right)\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,\epsilon(\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{b}}}{T_{\mathrm{i}}}F_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{b}}}{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\right)\langle\epsilon\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}+\frac{T_{\mathrm{b}}}{T_{\mathrm{i}}}F_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}.

(S18)

Inserting Eq. (

Appendix C
Relation to Markovian eigenmode analysis

Eigenmodes of Markovian time evolution

In this section, we focus on Markovian systems and relate our Result 1 to the eigenmode analysis of the Markovian generator of time evolution. Let
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
denote the generator of the Markovian time evolution of the probability distribution with the bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
. The generator is mathematically a linear map from a function on
\mathcal{X}
to another function on
\mathcal{X}
. For example,
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
may be a Fokker–Planck operator for continuous systems, for which
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\bm{y})
gives the right-hand side of the Fokker–Planck equation, such as the one in Eq. (
S53
). For discrete-state systems,
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
is the generator of a Markov jump process, and
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\bm{y})
gives the right-hand side of the master equation in Eq. (
4
). We define the adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\dagger}
through the identity
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,a(\bm{x})\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}b(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,b(\bm{x})\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\dagger}a(\bm{x})
for arbitrary real-valued functions
a(\bm{x})
and
b(\bm{x})
in the function space on
\mathcal{X}
.

We review the basic properties of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\dagger}
(e.g., Chapter 6 of Ref.
[
87
]
). The eigenvalue equations for
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\dagger}
read

\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=\lambda_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}),\quad\mathcal{L}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\dagger}\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=\lambda_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}),

(S22)

where
\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})
and
\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})
are the eigenfunctions. We assume that the eigenvalues are discrete, labeled by
\alpha=1,2\dots
. The eigenfunctions can be taken to be biorthonormal
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})\psi_{\beta}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
. For simplicity, we further assume that the sets of eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\}
and
\{\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\}
each form a complete set of basis of the function space on
\mathcal{X}
. Then, the conditional probability is expanded as

P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=\sum_{\alpha\geq 1}\exp(\lambda_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t)\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}).

(S23)

We also assume for simplicity that all the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are real, which is the case for overdamped systems in contact with a single reservoir, and sort the eigenvalues as
\lambda_{1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\geq\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\geq\cdots
. It is known that the first eigenvalue and eigenfunctions satisfy
\lambda_{1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}=0
,
\phi_{1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})
, and
\psi_{1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=1
. Thus, all eigenvalues are nonpositive, and
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})=0
holds for all
\alpha\geq 2
, as follows from the biorthonormality.

Eigenmode analysis of ensemble MPEs

Ensemble MPEs in Markovian systems have been commonly analyzed in terms of eigenmodes

.
While the analysis is valid for any Markovian system, it becomes the most relevant when a few modes are dominant for the time evolution in the focused time region. As has been done in literature, we assume
0=\lambda_{1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}>\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}>\lambda_{3}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
and consider a large enough
t
so that
\exp(\lambda_{3}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t),\exp(\lambda_{4}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t),\ldots
are exponentially smaller than
\exp(\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t)
. We can approximate Eq. (
S23
) as

P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\simeq\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})+\exp(\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t)\phi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})\psi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})

(S24)

in this asymptotic regime.

Reference
[
31
]
defined the ensemble MPEs in terms of the KL divergence [Eq. (
S10
)] and showed that the ensemble MPE is present in this asymptotic regime if

\bigl|a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})\bigr|<\bigl|a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})\bigr|.

(S25)

Here,
a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{i}})\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{y}\,\psi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\bm{y})
is the expansion coefficient corresponding to the second eigenfunction when we expand
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
in terms of
\{\phi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)\}
. Moreover, the condition in Eq. (
S25
) is valid for a broader range of definitions of ensemble MPEs
[
31
]
. However, it does not immediately apply to our definition of ensemble MPEs in Eq. (
1
).

Our definition of ensemble MPEs in terms of the average energy is related to the eigenfunctions in a different manner. Using Eq. (
S24
), we can expand the average energy in the asymptotic regime as

\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}\simeq\langle\epsilon\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}+\exp(\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t)\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{i}}),

(S26)

where we define
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,\epsilon(\bm{x})\phi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})
, which is the expansion coefficient on the second eigenfunction when we expand
\epsilon(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
in terms of
\{\psi_{\alpha}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)\}
. The difference of the average energies from two initial temperatures
T_{\mathrm{i}}=T_{\mathrm{h}},T_{\mathrm{c}}
reads

\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{h}}}-\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\simeq\exp(\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t)\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\left[a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})-a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})\right].

(S27)

Thus, the ensemble MPE is present in the asymptotic regime if the condition

\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})<\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})

(S28)

is satisfied.

The relation between the conditions (
S25
) and (
S28
) depends on the signs of
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})
and
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})
. The sign of the coefficient
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{i}})
is related to the behavior of the average energy in Eq. (
S26
). When this coefficient is positive, the average energy
\langle\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{i}}}
approaches the equilibrium value
\langle\epsilon\rangle_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
from above, which is an ordinary relaxation. When the coefficient is negative, the average energy approaches the equilibrium from below, exhibiting an anomalous undershooting. Below, let us assume for simplicity that
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})
and
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})
have the same sign, which is always the case if
T_{\mathrm{h}}
and
T_{\mathrm{c}}
are close enough to each other. When the average energies do not undershoot, i.e.,
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}}),\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})>0
, the condition in Eq. (
S28
) reduces to
|a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})|<|a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})|
. When the average energies undershoot, i.e.,
\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}}),\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})<0
, the condition is equivalent to
|a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{h}})|>|a_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})|
. Thus, unless the average energy undershoots, the condition in Eq. (
S25
) is still valid for the definition of ensemble MPEs in terms of the average energy.

Eigenmode analysis of microstate MPEs

While Eqs. (
S25
) and (
S28
) provide concise sufficient conditions for ensemble MPEs in the asymptotic regime, they do not reveal the microscopic origin of the ensemble MPEs. To get insight into the microscopic origin from the eigenmode analysis, we can combine the eigenmode expansion with our results on microstate MPEs.

For the definition of ensemble MPE in terms of the average energy in Eq. (
1

\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})

(S29)

and thus, the measure of microstate MPE

c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\simeq\exp(\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}t)\epsilon_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}[\psi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})-\psi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})][\epsilon(\bm{x})-\epsilon(\bm{y})].

(S30)

As discussed in the main text, our Result 1 implies that a pair of microstates

has a dominant contribution to the ensemble MPE if

Therefore, one can numerically evaluate

\lambda_{1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}>\lambda_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}>\lambda_{3}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}

. We first expand the nonequilibrium free energy as

where we use

, and we define a positive constant
A_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!d\bm{x}\,\bigl[\phi_{2}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})\bigr]^{2}/\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})
. The constant

is typically unity (e.g., Sec. 7, Chapter V of Ref.
[
90
]
), but this property is not needed below. The measure of the microstate MPE appearing in our Result S2,
\tilde{c}_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y};T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}})
in Eq. (

Appendix D
Proof of Result 2

Preliminaries

We prove Result 2 using classical results from the theory of stochastic interacting systems
[
83
,
84
]
. We introduce three definitions based on Chapter II of Ref.
[
83
]
and Chapter 22 of Ref.
[
84
]
. We fix a partial ordering
\preceq
between states
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}
. We say a real-valued function
a(\bm{x})
is
monotone
if
a(\bm{x})\leq a(\bm{y})
for all
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
. We say that a Markov process is
monotone
if, for any monotone function
a(\bm{x})
,
\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})
is a monotone function for all
t\geq 0
. We say that a probability distribution
q(\bm{x})
has
positive correlations
if

\displaystyle\sum_{x}a(\bm{x})b(\bm{x})q(\bm{x})-\biggl[\sum_{x}a(\bm{x})q(\bm{x})\biggr]\biggl[\sum_{x}b(\bm{x})q(\bm{x})\biggr]

\displaystyle\geq 0

(S34)

for all monotone functions
a(\bm{x})
and
b(\bm{x})
.

We use our notation from the main text for the proof. We always use
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
as a transition rate,
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})
as a monotone coupling, and
P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
as the conditional probability generated by
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
through the master equation in Eq. (
4
). As in the main text, we always assume that
P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
converges to
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})
as
t\to\infty
from any
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}
. We use the set
\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mid\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}\}
.

The proof of Result 2 proceeds in three steps. In Sec.
2
, we introduce a theorem to prove the monotonicity of a Markov process. In Sec.
3
, we introduce another theorem to show that a probability distribution has positive correlations. Finally, we combine these theorems with the assumptions of Result 2 to prove the no-Mpemba theorem in Sec.
4
. Note that the first and second steps are reviews of known results, but we provide proof of them for convenience.

Monotonicity of the process

We introduce a theorem to prove that a Markov process is monotone.

Theorem S4 (Proposition 22.7 of Ref.
[
84
]
)

If there exists a monotone coupling
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt,\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt,\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
of
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
, the Markov process generated by
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
is monotone.

Proof of Theorem S4

Let
a(\bm{x})
be any monotone function. We define three functions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
,
a^{(1)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\coloneqq a(\bm{u})
,
a^{(2)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\coloneqq a(\bm{v})
, and
a^{(3)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\coloneqq a(\bm{v})-a(\bm{u})
. We readily have
a^{(3)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\geq 0
for
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in\mathcal{K}
due to the definition of a monotone function. For
i=1,2,3
, we use
\overline{a^{(i)}}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
to denote the average of
a^{(i)}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt,\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
at time
t
over the coupled process starting with a fixed initial state
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
at time
0
. For any
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{K}
and any
t\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})-\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\overline{a^{(2)}}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})-\overline{a^{(1)}}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\overline{a^{(3)}}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\geq 0.

(S35)

Here, the first equality holds because each system copy in the monotone coupling has the same marginal transition rates as the original process, and thus, the statistics about each copy are the same as the statistics over the original process. The second equality follows from
a^{(3)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=a^{(2)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})-a^{(1)}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
. The last inequality holds because the coupled process starting with
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{K}
stays in
\mathcal{K}
, over which
a^{(3)}
is nonnegative.

In summary, we have proved that for any monotone function
a(\bm{x})
and for any
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
,
\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})\leq\bar{a}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})
for all
t\geq 0
. This statement is identical to the definition of a monotone process.
\blacksquare

If the partial order
\preceq
happens to be a total order, Result 2 directly follows from this Theorem S4. Indeed, if
\preceq
is a total order, any pair of states
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
are either
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
or
\bm{x}\succeq\bm{y}
. If
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
, the assumption (a) of Result 2 implies
\epsilon(\bm{x})\leq\epsilon(\bm{y})
, and combining assumption (c) of Result 2 and Theorem S4 gives
\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x})\leq\bar{\epsilon}_{\,t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y})
. Therefore, we have
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\geq 0
. If
\bm{x}\succeq\bm{y}
, we can similarly show
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\geq 0
by exchanging the roles of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
. Thus,
c_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\geq 0
for all pairs of states, i.e., the microstate MPE is absent. This implies
C_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}})\geq 0
for any
T_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}}
, namely the absence of ensemble MPEs.

For a general partial ordering
\preceq
, Theorem S4 alone is not sufficient to prove Result 2. We introduce another element of the proof below.

Positive correlations

We introduce a classical theorem to prove that a probability distribution has positive correlations.

Lemma S5 (Main theorem of Ref.
[
89
]
)

Suppose that the Markov process generated by
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
is monotone. Then, the following two statements are equivalent:
(a) The transition rate
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
is nonzero only if
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are comparable (i.e.,
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
or
\bm{y}\preceq\bm{x}
); (b) For any probability distribution
q(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
that has positive correlations, the probability distribution
\sum_{\bm{y}}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\bm{y})q(\bm{y})
has positive correlations.

We omit the proof of this Lemma. The proof in the original paper
[
89
]
is rather complicated. A simpler proof is found in Theorem 2.14, Chapter II of Ref.
[
83
]
. We then use this Lemma to deduce that the Gibbs distributions have positive correlations.

Theorem S6 (Theorem 22.16 of Ref.
[
84
]
)

Suppose that
(a) The Markov process generated by
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
is monotone, and (b)
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
is nonzero only if
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are comparable.
Then
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
has positive correlations for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof of Theorem S6

Under the assumptions of Theorem S6, we can use Lemma S5 to conclude that
\sum_{\bm{y}}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\bm{y})q(\bm{y})
has positive correlations for any
q(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
that has positive correlations.

Take an arbitrary state
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{X}
, and take the distribution concentrated on
\bm{z}
,
q(\bm{x})=\delta_{\bm{x}\bm{z}}
. This distribution has positive correlations because

\displaystyle\sum_{x}a(\bm{x})b(\bm{x})\delta_{\bm{x}\bm{z}}-\biggl[\sum_{x}a(\bm{x})\delta_{\bm{x}\bm{z}}\biggr]\biggl[\sum_{x}b(\bm{x})\delta_{\bm{x}\bm{z}}\biggr]

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt=a(\bm{z})b(\bm{z})-a(\bm{z})b(\bm{z})=0.

(S36)

Then,
\sum_{\bm{y}}P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\bm{y})\delta_{\bm{y}\bm{z}}=P_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt|\bm{z})

converges to

\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)

\blacksquare

Appendix E
Applications of the no-Mpemba theorem

Ferromagnetic Ising model

We provide the details of the application of the no-Mpemba theorem to the ferromagnetic Ising model under strong magnetic fields. Before doing so, we check that the transition rates of the Ising model are irreducible and have
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
as the stationary distribution, which we assumed in the main text. The irreducibility is confirmed by considering that, starting from any spin configuration, we can reach any other configuration by flipping spins one by one. The model has the stationary distribution
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
because it satisfies the detailed-balance condition,

\displaystyle\frac{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})}{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{x}^{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{x}))}{r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\epsilon(\bm{x})-\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i}))}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\frac{\epsilon(\bm{x})-\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right]=\frac{\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x}^{i})}{\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})},

(S38)

where we used the detailed-balance condition for
r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\Delta\epsilon)
in the main text.

We check the conditions (a)–(c) of Result 2 in order. To check condition (a) of Result 2, let
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
be two states that satisfy
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
. For every spin
i
such that
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
, it is guaranteed that
-1=x_{i}<y_{i}=+1
. Therefore,
\bm{y}
is obtained from
\bm{x}
by flipping those spins from
-1
to
+1
. By flipping a single spin
i
from
-1
to
+1
of an arbitrary state
\bm{z}
that has
z_{i}=-1
, the energy changes by

\displaystyle\epsilon(\bm{z}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=H_{i}[1-(-1)]-\sum_{j}J_{ij}z_{j}[1-(-1)]

\displaystyle=2H_{i}-2\sum_{j}J_{ij}z_{j}.

(S39)

This energy change is positive because

H_{i}-\sum_{j}J_{ij}z_{j}\geq H_{i}-\sum_{j}J_{ij}|z_{j}|=H_{i}-\sum_{j}J_{ij}\geq 0,

(S40)

where the first equality uses
J_{ij}\geq 0
, and the last inequality is the assumption of a strong magnetic field in the main text. Since
\bm{y}
is obtained from
\bm{x}
by consecutively flipping spins from
-1
to
+1
, the total energy change is positive, i.e.,
\epsilon(\bm{x})\leq\epsilon(\bm{y})
. This confirms condition (a) of Result 2.

Condition (b) of Result 2 can be checked by noting that, for any state
\bm{x}
and for every spin
i
,
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{x}^{i}
if
x_{i}=-1
and
\bm{x}\succeq\bm{x}^{i}
if
x_{i}=+1
. The transitions are only between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{i}
, thus between comparable states.

To check condition (c) of Result 2, we use a monotone coupling found in Ref.
[
83
]
(see Theorems 1.5 and 2.2 of Chapter III therein). For each coupled state
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
and for each spin
i
, we set the following transition rates: If
x_{i}=y_{i}
,

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\min\{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x}),\,R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})\},

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})-\min\{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x}),\,R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})\},

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})-\min\{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x}),\,R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})\},

(S41)

and if
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
,

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x}),

(S42)

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y}).

We set any other

with
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\neq(\bm{u},\bm{v})
to zero. With these coupled transition rates, the marginal transition rates of the first copy are the same as the original transition rates because

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{v}}R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{y})+R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})

(S43)

for every
\bm{x},\bm{y}
and
i
. The second copy satisfies a similar property. Moreover, the coupled process satisfies
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}^{\sharp}(\bm{u},\bm{v}|\bm{x},\bm{y})=0
if
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{K}
and
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\notin\mathcal{K}
, as proven in the following.

To prove this property, we first show that the transition rates of the original model satisfy the so-called
attractive
property (Sec. 2, Chap. III of Ref.
[
83
]
). The attractive property means that, for every
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
such that
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle\leq R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle\text{for all $i$ such that $x_{i}=y_{i}=-1$},

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle\geq R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle\text{for all $i$ such that $x_{i}=y_{i}=+1$}.

(S44)

This condition says that if
\bm{x}\preceq\bm{y}
, the state
\bm{y}
has a higher flip rate from
-1
to
+1
and a lower flip rate from
+1
to
-1
than
\bm{x}
. We check this attractive property for each

and
i

x_{i}=y_{i}=-1

S39

[\epsilon(\bm{y}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{y})]-[\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{x})]=-2\sum_{j}J_{ij}(y_{j}-x_{j})\leq 0

because the coupling is ferromagnetic

R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{y}^{i}|\bm{y})\geq R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})

S44

S44

We use this attractive property to show that, for every

-1=x_{i}<y_{i}=+1
, the possible transitions are
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\to(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{y})
and
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\to(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{i})
, whose destinations are in
\mathcal{K}

Note that the assumption about the transition rates,
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})=r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{x}))
with
r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
decreasing, is sufficient but not necessary for the attractive property. Since we did not directly use the form
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})=r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{x}))
to verify condition (c) of Result 2, we can apply our Result 2 to a slightly broader class of systems by removing the assumption
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{i}|\bm{x})=r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\epsilon(\bm{x}^{i})-\epsilon(\bm{x}))
and directly assuming the attractive property and the detailed balance condition.

Exclusion process

We discuss the details of the application of Result 2 to the one-dimensional exclusion process. We first define some notation. For a state
\bm{x}
and a site
k\in\{1,\dots,L\}

\bm{x}^{k+}
is valid if
1\leq k\leq L-1

\bm{x}

\bm{x}^{k+}
as the state obtained from
\bm{x}
by moving the particle from site
k
to
k+1
. Similarly, we say
\bm{x}^{k-}
is valid if
2\leq k\leq L
, site
k
is occupied, and site
k-1
is empty, and under these conditions, we define
\bm{x}^{k-}
similarly. The transition rates are
R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{k+}|\bm{x})=r_{k+1|k}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}
if

Before applying Result 2, we verify that the exclusion process is irreducible and has
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
as the stationary distribution. The process is irreducible because, starting with any configuration, the process can reach any other configuration by moving particles one at a time. The process has the stationary distribution
\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\hskip 1.00006pt\mathord{\cdot}\hskip 1.00006pt)
because the detailed-balance condition

\frac{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}^{k+}|\bm{x})}{R_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\bm{x}|\bm{x}^{k+})}=\frac{r_{k+1|k}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}}{r_{k|k+1}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}}=\exp\left[\frac{e_{k}-e_{k+1}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right]=\frac{\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x}^{k+})}{\pi\text{\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{${}_{T_{\mathrm{b}}}$}}(\bm{x})}

(S46)

is satisfied for any
\bm{x}
whose
\bm{x}^{k+}
is valid.

We check conditions (a)–(c) of Result 2 one by one. Condition (a) of Result 2 is satisfied because, for any

Condition (b) of Result 2 is verified by noting that

and
\bm{x}\succeq\bm{x}^{k-}

\bm{x}

\bm{x}^{k+}

\bm{x}

\bm{x}^{k-}

Numerical test of Result 2

We numerically verify the no-Mpemba theorem applied to the ferromagnetic Ising models and the one-dimensional exclusion processes introduced in the main text. The no-Mpemba theorem establishes the absence of ensemble MPEs under a very general setup, and it is thus impossible to fully verify the theorem by numerical methods. Nevertheless, numerical results can provide supporting evidence for the theorem.

For the ferromagnetic Ising models, we fix a one-dimensional lattice of six spins (
L=6
) with periodic boundary conditions, and we determine the coupling constants as follows. We sample the coupling constants
J_{12},J_{23},\ldots,J_{61}
from independent exponential distributions of mean 1.0.
We set
J_{21},\dots,J_{16}
by the symmetry
J_{ij}=J_{ji}
and any other coupling constants to zero. We set the magnetic fields to
H_{i}=(1+\alpha_{i})(J_{i,i+1}+J_{i,i-1})
with indices modulo 6, where
\alpha_{i}
are random numbers sampled from independent exponential distributions of mean 0.5 so that the constants satisfy
H_{i}\geq\sum_{j}J_{ij}
.
We use the transition rate
r_{i}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(\Delta\epsilon)=\exp[-\Delta\epsilon/(2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})]
.

For the exclusion process, we fix the number of sites to
L=8
and sample the number of particles
N
randomly from 1 to 7. We generate a random monotone potential energy by
e_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\alpha_{\ell}
for
k=1,\dots,8
, where
\alpha_{\ell}
’s are sampled from independent exponential distributions of mean 1.0. Since
\alpha_{\ell}\geq 0
, the potential energy satisfies
e_{1}\penalty\leq\penalty\cdots\penalty\leq\penalty e_{L}
. We set the transition rates to
r^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}_{k+1|k}=\gamma_{k}\exp[(e_{k}-e_{k+1})/(2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})]
and
r^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}_{k|k+1}=\gamma_{k}\exp[(e_{k+1}-e_{k})/(2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{b}})]
for
k=1,\dots,7
, where
\gamma_{k}
’s are sampled from independent exponential distributions of mean 1.0.

We numerically solve the time evolution of these random models and plot
C_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}})/C_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}})
for various values of
T_{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}}
in Fig.
S4
. All curves of
C_{t}^{T_{\mathrm{b}}}(T_{\mathchoice{\textstyle{*}}{\textstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}{\scriptstyle{*}}})
are above the horizontal line at zero up to a numerical error of

. This result provides evidence of the no-Mpemba theorem for ferromagnetic Ising models and exclusion processes.
