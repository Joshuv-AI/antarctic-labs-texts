Early pathological changes of coronary microvasculation in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction
Authors: Ziyu Guo, Yonghui Xu, Jingjing Xu, Wei Kong, Yanxiang Gao, Jingang Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe majority of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) patients present with coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD). However, it is uncertain whether CMD is a primary factor in ventricular remodeling/diastolic dysfunction and HFpEF, or whether CMD is secondary to myocardial remodeling in HFpEF.MethodsEight-week-old male C57BL/6N mice were simultaneously administered with high-fat diet (HFD) and N(ω)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) via drinking water for 5 weeks. Body weight, blood pressure and glucose tolerance, athletic endurance were observed. Cardiac systolic and diastolic function were detected using trans-thoracic Doppler echocardiography. Heart weight/tibial length and wheat germ agglutinin staining (WGA) were performed to evaluate cardiomyocytes. To detect the degree of cardiac fibrosis, picrosirius red (PSR) staining was performed. The coronary microvascular and pericytes were investigated by immunofluorescence staining. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) was measured by echocardiography to assess the coronary microvascular function.ResultsThe HFpEF mouse model was constructed using HFD and L-NAME. HFpEF was evident by preserved ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS) and significantly elevated E/e’, impaired glucose tolerance and reduced exercise capacity in mice treated with L-NAME and HFD. Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and cardiac fibrosis were observed in the HFpEF group. The density of capillary was decreased in HFpEF group, accompanied by increased capillary diameter and tortuosity. Moreover, the number and coverage rate of cardiac pericytes were reduced in HFpEF group. No significant differences were found in CFR after modeling 5 weeks. But CFR was decreased in HFpEF group when extending modeling time to 12 weeks.ConclusionsOur findings have demonstrated that coronary microvascular pathological changes appeared during the early stages of HFpEF and appeared before coronary functional indicator changed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05354-8.

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), previously known as diastolic heart failure, is a clinical syndrome where patients have symptoms and signs of heart failure, but their left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is ≥ 50% [1]. Approximately half of patients with heart failure have HFpEF [2]. HFpEF was previously considered to be solely a matter of myocardial stiffness caused by alterations in contractile proteins of cardiomyocytes or the impact of fibrosis on myocardial relaxation [3]. However, this paradigm has recently shifted with new insights into the role of coronary microcirculation in the progression of HFpEF [3]. In recent years, extensive research has shown that a high burden of comorbidities in HFpEF, such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, hypertension triggers a systemic inflammatory response that impairs microvascular endothelial cells (ECs) and leads to coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) [4]. Considering the complicated pathophysiology of HFpEF, coronary microcirculation has become an important area of research in HFpEF.

Coronary microcirculation is a complex network of arterioles, precapillary arteriole, and capillaries that allows nutrients and waste metabolites to be exchanged between blood vessels and the myocardium [5]. A high burden of multiple cardiovascular comorbidities, such as ageing, obesity, diabetes, and hypertension, often results in a state of systemic inflammatory that impairs the ECs in the microvasculature and contributes to CMD [6]. CMD including abnormalities in the function and structure of coronary microcirculation. HFpEF patients have been reported with a diminished coronary flow reserve, a surrogate marker for CMD associated with a more than five-fold increase in hospitalization risk. As shown by the PROMIS-HFpEF study, up to 75% of HFpEF patients had CMD [7]. Other studies showed that an increase over time in structural abnormalities of the coronary microcirculation resulted in ventricular filling problems caused by poor myocardial compliance, which can in turn contribute to the development of HFpEF [8, 9]. These findings indicated that changes in the microcirculation were part of HFpEF development. However, the hypothesis that these changes are causative factors in HFpEF has yet to be validated.

Although the ECs have been the primary focus of research into coronary microvascular dysfunction in HFpEF, the microvasculature includes not only ECs but also other cell types, such as pericytes [10]. Pericytes are currently defined as microcirculatory mural cells that wrap around ECs and share basement membrane with ECs [11]. They can be found in terminal arterioles, precapillary arterioles, and capillaries [11]. Close physical contact and biological interactions between pericytes and ECs play important roles in the formation, maturation, stabilization, maintenance and remodelling of vasculature [12]. Pericytes are widely distributed in the myocardial vasculature [13]. Currently, little research on cardiac pericytes were focused on acute myocardial infarction and no-reflow was reported to be characterized by a reduction of the vessel diameter due to pericytes contraction [14]. In addition, pericytes may act as a stem cell source, which are important for cardiac remodeling and recovery from heart ischemia [14]. However, not many studies have been conducted on pericytes in HFpEF.

To study the coronary microvascular changes during the development of HFpEF, we used a “two-hit” murine model of HFpEF coadministrating high-fat diet (HFD) with N(ω)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) and found the microcirculatory pathological changes in early HFpEF. Furthern more, we observed pericytes loss in HFpEF, which were crucial for maintaining vascular integrity, regulating permeability, and controlling vascular tone. These pathological changes appeared before prominent echocardiographic signs of CMD were present. To sum up, this research provided a new insights into the relationship between CMD and HFpEF.

Methods

Animal

Male adult (8–12 weeks old) C57BL/6 N mice were obtained from the Department of Laboratory Animal Science of Peking University Health Science Center. Mice were kept under a 12 h light/dark cycle room at a constant temperature of 22 ± 1 °C with controlled humidity (50–60%) and fed with a normal laboratory rodent diet. After 1 week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to control and HFpEF groups. We adopted “two-hit” mouse model of HFpEF: HFD (60% calories from lard, MD12033, Medicience Diets) and nitric oxide synthase inhibitor L-NAME (0.5 g/L in drinking water, N5751,Sigma Aldrich) exposed for five weeks. Animal welfare and experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance to the 1986 Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act and related guidelines. All experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a high-resolution ultrasound imaging system (Vevo®3100, FUJIFILM VisualSonics) and mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane inhalation and positioned in a supine position on the physiologic monitoring platform. The chest hair was removed using hair removing cream (NAR-255-1, Nair), and ultrasound gel was applied to the depilated skin. First, coronary microvascular function assessment was performed. The probe and platform positions were adjusted to form a modified parasternal long-axis (PLAX) view for examining the baseline blood flow velocity of left anterior descending artery (LAD) velocity. The isoflurane concentration was increased to 3% to induce LAD hyperemia and the highest blood flow velocity was recorded. CFR was calculated as the ratio of hyperemic blood flow and baseline coronary blood flow [15].

LV systolic and diastolic function were also evaluated. Echocardiography of the apical four-chamber view, parasternal left ventricle long-axis and short-axis views were performed and images were acquired. Stored images were used to analyze LVEF and fractional shortening (FS), ratio of peak early Doppler transmitral flow velocity (E) to peak late Doppler transmitral flow velocity (A) and to myocardial tissue Doppler velocity (e’), left ventricular anterior wall and posterior wall thickness of end-systole and end-diastole (LVAWs, LVAWd, LVPWs and LVPWd, respectively). All parameters were measured at least three times, and means were presented.

Measurement of blood pressure

Blood pressure was measured using a noninvasive tail-cuff monitoring system (BP2000, Visitech Systems Inc). The animals were trained for 5 days prior to measurements to minimize stress and were pre-warmed to 30 °C using a heater. To avoid variations in blood pressure owing to the time of day, all measurements were performed between 8 and 10 am. The mean values of ten readings were used for each animal.

Exercise exhaustion test

After 3 days of acclimation on the treadmill, an exhaustion test was administered to all the mice in the experimental groups. Animals ran on the treadmill at 20 degrees uphill, starting at a warm-up speed of 5 m/min for 5 min, after which speed was increased to 14 m/min. Exhaustion was defined as inability of the mouse to return to running after 10 s on the electric-stimulus grid. Both running time and running distance were recorded.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (ipGTT)

All animals were fasted for 8 h before glucose level measurement and ipGTT was conducted by injection of glucose (2 g/kg in saline). Blood samples were taken from the tail vein at 0, 15, 30, 45, 60 and 120 min after glucose administration, and glucose levels (mmol/L) were measured using a glucometer.

Picrosirius red staining (PSR)

The hearts were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 mm intervals. Prepared heart paraffin slices were heated at 65 °C for 20 min, dewaxed in xylene, and rehydrated in descending concentrations of ethanol. Then heart sections were performed antigen repair and block of nonspecific sites and blocked with goat serum for 1 h. After that, the sections were stained with Sirius red (G1018, Servicebio) to identify collagen fibers. Subsequently, the sections were conventionally dehydrated, cleared twice with xylene, and sealed with neutral resin and observed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence

The hearts were fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in 4% paraformaldehyde, 30% sucrose, and OCT, respectively, and then sectioned at 5-mm intervals. The frozen heart tissue slices were washed with PBS and incubated with an anti-neuroglial cell 2 (NG2) antibody (ab5320, Abcam, 1:200) overnight at 4°C. After washing by PBS for 3 times, heart slices were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab32733, Abcam, 1:200) for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. Following this incubation, the sections were washed again and incubated with 4’,6-diamidine-2’-phenylindole (DAPI) (S36938, Invitrogen, 1:1000) staining solution in the dark for 5 min. Then the slides were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium to prevent fluorescence quenching and then detected by confocal laser microscopy. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled isolectin B4 (IB4) (L2895, Merck Millipore, 1:50) staining and FITC-labeled wheat germ agglutinin (D20067, Biosharp) staining and were used to visualize the coronary capillaries and cardiomyocytes, respectively. Stained sections were imaged using confocal microscopy (TCS-SP8 STED 3X, Leica).

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used to examine the data normality. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± S.E.M, and an unpaired Student’s t-test was used to evaluate the difference between the two groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

The “two-hit” procedure induced in HFpEF phenotype in mice

To create an HFpEF-like state in mice, we induced comorbidities of hypertension and obesity/metabolic dysfunction by a combination of HFD (60% calories from lard) and L-NAME (0.5 g/L in drinking water) for five weeks [16]. The body weight increased significantly in the HFD + L-NAME group than in the control group (36.50 ± 1.18 g vs. 27.50 ± 0.43 g, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). Both systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were significantly higher in HFpEF mice than control mice (SBP: 121.91 ± 1.20 mmHg vs. 101.77 ± 1.79 mmHg, P < 0.05; DBP: 93.15 ± 1.43 mmHg vs. 79.16 ± 2.32 mmHg, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). LVEF and FS were generally preserved in HFpEF group, compared to control group (EF: 79.82 ± 1.67% vs. 80.34 ± 1.85%, P >0.05; FS: 48.12 ± 2.30% vs. 48.28 ± 1.94%, P >0.05) (Fig. 1C, D). Diastolic dysfunction of HFpEF mice was demonstrated by altered diastolic filling parameters. Specifically, the value of E/e’ in HFpEF group was increased compared to control group, showing impaired LV diastolic function (41.43 ± 1.23 vs. 23.97 ± 1.04, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1 C, D). The area under the glucose curve (AUC) were significantly higher in HFpEF mice than in control mice (AUC: 99.55 ± 6.18 vs. 78.03 ± 4.74, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E), indicating that glucose intolerance was induced. Also, exercise intolerance is a key symptom of HFpEF. To account for differences in body size, we can estimate the work performed by each mouse using the following formula: Work = body weight (g) × running distance (m). The “two-hit” procedure induced cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis

The heart weight to tibial length ratio (HW/TL) in HFD + L-NAME mice was greater than that in control mice (0.0094 ± 0.0004 g/mm vs. 0.0078 ± 0.0001 g/mm, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). To examine histopathological changes in the hearts of mice fed with HFD and L-NAME dietary, WGA staining of heart cross section were performed. Consistent with an increase in SBP, cardiacmyocyte hypertrophy was also observed in HFpEF mice (Fig. 2C). Pericytes loss in HFpEF

Pericytes are the second most abundant cell type in the coronary vasculature after ECs [13]. Pericytes interact with ECs through physical and paracrine mechanisms to maintain normal vascular homeostasis [17]. The number of pericytes was significantly lower in the HFpEF group (3.33 ± 0.33 vs. 6.17 ± 0.40, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A, B). Discussion

Due to high rates of readmission, morbidity, and mortality, HFpEF occupies substantial healthcare resources and has been a hot and challenging research topic in cardiovascular research field [2]. However, the pathological changes in the coronary microvasculature have not yet been elucidated. In this study we investigated the coronary microcirculatory alterations characteristic of HFpEF, including capillary rarefaction, increased vascular diameter and tortuosity, and decreased pericytes number and coverage. We revealed that capillary alterations in HFpEF may occur in the early stages of HFpEF, and before the change in the functional index, CFR.

Because HFpEF is often accompanied by multiple cardiovascular comorbidities, such as diabetes, hypertension, obesity and aging [18], functional and structural damage to coronary microvascular ECs leads to CMD [19]. CMD manifests in various ways, including increased coronary microvascular contractile response (such as coronary microvascular spasm), impaired endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation, and increased coronary microcirculatory resistance, resulting from structural alterations (such as vascular rarefaction, remodeling, luminal narrowing, and extramural compression) [20]. However, whether CMD is a primary factor in ventricular remodeling/diastolic dysfunction and HFpEF, or whether CMD is secondary to myocardial remodeling in HFpEF is uncertain. Morever, an autopsy study showed that coronary rarefaction in coronary microcirculation was more prevalent in HFpEF patients compared to age-matched controls [21]. However, this study was conducted post-mortem, and the patients included were most likely in late stages of HFpEF. Consequently, it remains unclear to what extent these structural abnormalities are present in earlier stages of HFpEF and whether they are a cause or a consequence of advanced stages of HFpEF [22]. Our study found that pathological changes such as reduced capillary number can occur in the early stages of HFpEF, suggesting that inflammation and oxidative stress in HFpEF may first cause changes in coronary microcirculation.

At the same time, we also discovered increased capillary tortuosity in HFpEF, which may affect the smooth flow of blood. Previous studies have revealed that vascular tortuosity is associated with various diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, aneurysms, and metastasis of tumors [23, 24]. Some studies suggested that increased vascular tortuosity exacerbates flow disturbances, potentially raising the risk of atherosclerosis [25, 26]. Tortuous blood vessels may cause the formation of microthrombi, changes in intravascular hemodynamics, and damage to distant areas due to slow blood flow [27].

In addition, we found a significant decrease in the number and coverage of pericytes in HFpEF. Pericytes were a sort of contractile cell that localized around capillaries and post-capillary venules [28]. Pericytes, sharing basement membrane with capillaries, have a branched morphology and play vital roles such as regulating blood flow, forming the blood-brain-barrier, immune cell trafficking and hemostasis, as well as recruiting and stabilizing ECs in neovascularization processes [10, 11]. Previous studies found that the higher the pericytes coverage, the higher the importance of pericytes for vascular integrity and tissue function [29–31]. In addition, pericytes actively interacted with vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), ECs, fibroblasts, and other cells, impacting the formation, stabilization, and remodeling, regenerative processes of vasculature [32]. For example, Chen et al. found that injected pericytes were able to replace missing cardiomyocytes in infarcted hearts, as well as cause reversal ventricular remodelling, cardiac fibrosis reduction, and angiogenesis stimulation [33]. However, the role of cardiac pericytes in common cardiac disease entities such as cardiac hypertrophy, cardiac remodelling and (diastolic) heart failure still remains unclear [34]. Our study revealed a significant reduction in both the number and coverage of pericytes in the hearts of HFpEF mice, which was coexisted with structural abnormalities of coronary microcirculation during the early stages of HFpEF, implicating the loss of pericytes may be a critical factor in HFpEF development.

This study had several limitations. First, this study was conducted solely on male mice and did not account for sex-related differences in HFpEF. Previous research suggested that there were likely molecular differences in how male and female hearts remodeled in response to pathologic conditions [18]. Second, although we assessed several fundamental pathophysiologic aspects in our HFpEF phenotyping, we did not test additional potentially causative comorbidities, such as the role of metabolic abnormalities in HFpEF pathophysiology [35, 36]. Third, this study is mainly descriptive, focusing on the comparison of pathological cardiac changes between HFpEF mice and healthy controls. An extended experiment may further clarify the relationship between CMD and abnormal cardiac function. Fourth, although existing literature has found that the phenotypes of HFPEF 12-week model mice are similar to those of 5-week model mice, the fact that we did not retain the 12-week model data of the mice remains a deficiency in the research design. This limitation should be taken into account for designing future studies [37–39]. In addition, our study did not explore the mechanistic basis of pericyte loss. Whether the reduction in pericyte density is primarily driven by enhanced cell death, impaired proliferation, or detachment from capillaries remains to be clarified. Future work incorporating assays such as TUNEL or caspase activity, as well as investigations into signaling pathways that regulate pericyte survival and function (e.g., Angiopoietin–Tie2 and PDGF-B/PDGFR-β), would provide important mechanistic insights. In vitro studies of pericyte biology may help to further delineate their role in the progression of HFpEF. Finally, pericytes share many markers with other cells, such as vascular smooth muscle cells and myofibroblasts. Multiple molecular markers, including PDGFR-β, α-SMA and NG2, are used to identify pericytes [29]. In our study, pericytes were labeled with NG2, one of the most widely used in heart. For this reason, we only studied pericytes around capillaries, where only pericytes were present. And pericytes can be identified based on their cell morphology.

Conclusions

In this study, we explored the characteristics of pathological changes in coronary microcirculation in the early stages of HFpEF, providing new insights for undersanding CMD in HFpEF. We found that abnormal coronary microvascular structure and function, decreased number and coverage of cardiac pericytes, and HFpEF were closely and complicatedly connected. Considering the importance microvasculation in HEpEF, more experiments are needed to explore the relationship between CMD and HFpEF.
