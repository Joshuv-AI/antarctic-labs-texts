Organoids in Precision Radiotherapy: Methodological Foundations, Tumor‐Specific Evidence, and Translational Roadmaps
Authors: Yixian Zhu, Yawei Jiao, Jiao Xue, Xiaoli Lv, Songbing Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRadiotherapy remains a cornerstone of cancer treatment, while its efficacy is often limited by tumor radioresistance and the risk of normal tissue toxicity. Conventional preclinical models, including two‐dimensional (2D) cell cultures and murine xenografts, exhibit significant limitations in recapitulating human tumor pathophysiology, thereby impeding the clinical translation of novel radiotherapeutic strategies. Patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as transformative three‐dimensional (3D) ex vivo models that recapitulate key aspects of original tumor heterogeneity and are increasingly applied in oncology research.MethodsThis article provides a comprehensive review of the literature on the application of PDOs in radiation oncology, with a focused analysis of their pathway toward clinical translation.ResultsPDOs demonstrate significant utility in predicting radiosensitivity, elucidating radioresistance mechanisms, optimizing combination therapies, modeling radiation injury, and screening targeted drugs. The integration of organoid technology with microfluidic organ‐on‐a‐chip (OoC) platforms also offers unprecedented capability to dynamically simulate the tumor microenvironment and conduct high‐throughput dose‐response studies. A translational roadmap is presented for leveraging these biomimetic systems to advance personalized radiotherapy, ultimately aiming to accelerate the clinical translational application of organoids.ConclusionsDespite challenges in standardization and immune component recapitulation, PDOs represent a powerful platform for advancing personalized radiotherapy.

Introduction

Transformative advances in targeted agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and precision radiotherapy [1] have collectively redefined cancer therapeutics. Key progress encompasses advanced radiotherapy techniques, optimized treatment sequencing, and synergistic integration with systemic therapies, demonstrating significantly improved survival outcomes [2]. Nevertheless, tumor radioresistance and normal tissue toxicity remain principal limitations to radiotherapy efficacy, demanding urgent mechanistic investigation to overcome these barriers.

Conventional preclinical models exhibit critical limitations that impede clinical translation in radiotherapy research. 2D cancer cell lines, while widely used, inherently fail to recapitulate native tissue architecture and intratumoral heterogeneity, fundamentally compromising their biological relevance [3]. Likewise, interspecies divergences in murine tumor models can distort therapeutic response predictions, resulting in significant discrepancies with clinical outcomes [4]. These persistent shortcomings underscore the need for more physiologically relevant model systems to bridge the gap between bench findings and clinical efficacy.

Organoids, derived from patient tumor tissues or stem cells, are three‐dimensional self‐assembling structures that recapitulate the spatial architecture and cellular hierarchy of native tumors [5]. By enabling 3D culture of patient‐derived cells, organoid systems preserve cell–cell interactions and signaling dynamics that closely mimic in vivo tumor pathophysiology, thereby overcoming many limitations of 2D monolayers and xenografts. This biomimetic fidelity positions PDOs as promising platforms for personalized therapeutic prediction. Emerging clinical studies using PDOs demonstrate the capacity to guide individualized radiotherapy dosing and combination regimens, with recent reports showing over 70% concordance between organoid‐predicted responses and actual patient outcomes [6]. However, correlations between organoid responses and patient outcomes should be interpreted cautiously because they support potential clinical validity but do not yet establish clinical utility or improved outcomes from organoid‐guided treatment.

This narrative review summarizes recent advances in organoid models in radiation oncology, with emphasis on tumor‐specific radiotherapy response, mechanisms of radioresistance, radiation‐induced normal tissue injury, radioprotection, and clinical translation. To improve transparency, relevant studies were identified through searches of PubMed and Web of Science using combinations of terms including “organoid,” “patient‐derived organoid,” “radiotherapy,” “radiation,” “radiosensitivity,” “radioresistance,” and “radiation injury.” Additional articles were identified from reference lists of relevant studies and reviews. Studies were selected based on relevance to the scope of this review and their contribution to tumor‐specific or translational understanding, rather than according to a formal systematic review protocol.

Methodological Foundations and Quality Control

To harness organoid models for precision radiotherapy research, robust methodological foundations and rigorous quality control are paramount. Core requirements span standardized organoid derivation timelines, irradiation modalities with traceable dosimetry, harmonized response assays, and principled reconstruction of tumor microenvironment elements. Adherence to these best practices underpins reproducibility and biological interpretability across studies. A concise minimum‐information checklist is summarized in Table 1. Recent consensus statements in radiobiology emphasize mandatory reporting items for preclinical irradiators and dosimetry [7], and broader standardization roadmaps highlight traceability to national primary standards [8].

Organoid Derivation and Timing

PDOs Establishment

PDOs are generated from resected tumors or biopsies by embedding dissociated cells in a basement membrane–based 3D matrix with lineage‐appropriate growth factors. Successful derivation and early expansion typically require ~1–4 weeks, after which early‐passage PDOs preserve the histopathology, genomic landscape, and lineage markers of their parental tumors, supporting high model fidelity for downstream radiobiology assays [9].

Timing Consideration

In radiotherapy experiments, irradiation is typically administered during the initial culture phase (commonly 24 h to 3–4 days post‐seeding) to standardize organoid geometry and cell‐cycle distribution, with responses assessed in the acute‐to‐subacute window of 7–14 days post‐irradiation [10]. For clinical application, the entire workflow, encompassing biopsy, PDO expansion, irradiation, and final readout, must align with clinical decision‐making timelines. Current functional precision medicine platforms achieve a turnaround of approximately 4–6 weeks for establishment and screening, a duration compatible with this time horizon [11]. Consequently, refining and accelerating derivation and assay protocols is paramount for successful translation.

Irradiation Modalities and Dosimetry Calibration

Radiation Sources for Organoid Experiments

Organoids are irradiated on cabinet X‐ray systems (∼160–320 kVp), γ sources, or clinical LINAC photon beams. Regardless of modality, careful calibration and transparent reporting are essential because multi‐well geometry, beam quality, source‐surface distance, scatter, and medium depth can decouple nominal settings from absorbed dose at the organoid. Cross‐facility surveys underscore the point that inter‐institutional variations up to 12%–42% have been documented, highlighting the need for traceable dosimetry and harmonized methods [12].

Dosimetry and Quality Assurance

Robust studies use calibrated reference dosimeters and plate‐mimicking phantoms to verify absorbed dose in the organoid compartment. Documenting beam quality, field size, dose rate, source‐surface distance, backscatter, medium depth, and fractionation details should be explicitly reported, with collaboration from medical physicists to institute routine QA [7]. For low‐energy X‐rays in particular, attenuation by overlying culture medium alters the delivered dose at the cellular layer. Protocolized measurement at the sample level is recommended to avoid mis‐specification of dose as mere machine settings [13]. Emerging minimum‐information guidance formalizes these expectations for preclinical literature, improving inter‐study comparability and aligning laboratories to primary standards traceability [14].

Unified Readouts and Quantification

Endpoints for Radiation Response

Methodological heterogeneity remains a key challenge in organoid radiobiology. Since the standard clonogenic survival assay is incompatible with 3D organoids, the field primarily utilizes ATP viability and imaging‐based size to quantify post‐irradiation effects [15].

Toward Unified Metrics

Growth‐rate–normalized metrics provide robustness against baseline proliferation differences, which is essential for cytostatic contexts like radiation. GR parameters correct key confounders and are recommended as primary summary measures when feasible [16]. Complementarily, longitudinal summaries such as relative area under the curve (rAUC) integrate multi‐timepoint viability to enable cross‐model comparisons [17].

Molecular Imaging Readouts

Whole‐mount immunofluorescence in organoids enables high‐content quantification of DDR kinetics, such as γ‐H2AX/53BP1/RAD51 foci, as mechanistic correlates of phenotype. Cerebral organoid work provides a clear template using 2 Gy reference exposures and early/late repair readout [18]. For early, label‐free functional surrogates, optical metabolic imaging captures shifts in NAD(P)H/FAD within hours and has been validated in PDOs to stratify therapeutic response [19]. High‐content live‐cell pipelines further increase throughput and objectivity for longitudinal viability/morphology calls in organoids, facilitating standardized analytics across studies [20]. In parallel with these technical advances, organoid‐based radiation response studies increasingly report a small set of comparable summary endpoints alongside transparent methods, enabling alignment to clinically meaningful predictions [21].

Tumor Microenvironment Reconstruction

A recognized limitation of conventional organoid culture is the absence of native TME components, such as immune infiltrates, cancer‐associated fibroblasts, and vasculature, that shape radiation response. Three complementary strategies are increasingly used to rebuild these cues in vitro.

Co‐Culture Systems

Co‐culturing PDOs with autologous immune or stromal elements provides a personalized system to dissect tumor microenvironment crosstalk following radiation. This strategy has been substantiated by platforms showing autologous T cells effectively eliminate matched tumor organoids, creating a validated methodology for immuno‐radiation studies [22]. Alternatively, air‐liquid interface (ALI) cultures maintain native tumor‐infiltrating immune populations, supporting clinically relevant assessments of checkpoint blockade [23]. Although immune cell persistence requires tailored assay conditions, these platforms demonstrate strong translational potential for guiding personalized radiotherapy–immunotherapy combinations [24].

Perfused Organoid‐On‐a‐Chip

Microfluidic chips introduce continuous flow and, in some designs, periorganoid microvessels, allowing control of nutrient/oxygen gradients and study of vascular injury after irradiation [25]. Human Gut‐on‐a‐Chip models have reproduced radiation‐induced epithelial injury and barrier dysfunction, illustrating how perfused systems can capture acute radiotoxicity and support countermeasure testing [26]. Importantly, chips permit physiologic or cycling hypoxia (∼1%–5% O2) rather than ambient 20% O2, aligning culture conditions with a major determinant of clinical radioresistance [27].

ECM and Stromal Cues

Matrix composition and stiffness modulate organoid architecture and signaling, with Matrigel alternatives now available using defined synthetic hydrogels that present tunable ligands and mechanics [28]. Hybrid 3D cultures embedding organoids within collagen or fibrin and co‐culturing with fibroblasts can model desmoplasia and TGF‐β–rich stroma after RT, enabling analysis of how irradiated fibroblast secretomes shape tumor DNA‐damage repair and survival pathways [23].

In summary, microenvironment reconstruction is an evolving frontier aiming to enhance the clinical concordance of organoid radiobiology models. Organoid‐on‐chip systems, in particular, allow inclusion of immune and stromal components in a controlled setting and are noted as a key trend for the field [29]. Brain Malignancies

Brain tumors, including high‐grade gliomas, pose a significant therapeutic challenge and have motivated the development of specialized organoid models. Cerebral organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells provide a platform to study normal brain tissue radiosensitivity, while PDOs enable personalized testing of treatments in an environment that preserves native tumor cytoarchitecture. Oyefeso et al. recently demonstrated the use of human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC)‐derived brain organoids to examine neural tissue responses to proton radiation [30]. By exposing cerebral organoids to low and moderate proton doses, they observed dose‐dependent gene expression changes and cellular stress responses that accurately recapitulated known tissue‐specific radiation effects in the brain. Such models are valuable for optimizing clinical dose constraints in the central nervous system and investigating mitigating agents for radiation‐induced cognitive injury.

In parallel, patient‐derived glioblastoma organoids (GBOs) have been established to model malignant brain tumor response. Jacob et al. developed a living biobank of GBOs and demonstrated rapid therapeutic screening on these organoids, closely mirroring clinical regimens [31]. Notably, when GBOs were treated with standard chemoradiotherapy (fractionated 10 Gy radiation with concurrent temozolomide) and assessed via proliferation (Ki‐67) and viability markers, the organoid responses showed a strong correlation with the corresponding patients' clinical outcomes. This suggests high clinical concordance, which underscores the potential of organoids as predictive assays for individualized radiotherapy planning.

Mechanistic insights have also emerged from brain tumor organoids. Sundar et al. reported that GBO models could reproduce therapy resistance observed in the clinic, particularly the notorious resistance of glioblastoma to combined radiation and temozolomide [32]. Their GBOs revealed a stratified resistance: a surviving outer proliferative layer and SOX2+ glioma stem cells exhibiting apoptosis resistance and therapy‐induced senescence, mirroring clinical recurrence from radioresistant cells. This model enables the interrogation of microenvironment‐targeted approaches, notably niche disruption and senescence‐associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs) modulation, as potential means to overcome radioresistance. In another study, Shard et al. used high‐grade glioma organoids to identify a neuron‐regulated gene module at the tumors' invasive edges [33]. Therapeutic targeting of paracrine neuronal signaling, which promotes glioma invasion, was achieved via GABA_A receptor antagonism (S44819), suppressing both invasion and proliferation. The anti‐infiltrative effect was markedly enhanced upon combination with radiation, proposing a novel combinatorial strategy that disrupts neuron‐glioma cross‐talk to augment radiotherapeutic efficacy.

While brain tumor organoids show tremendous promise, limitations include the current lack of integrated brain microenvironment components in most models, such as microglia and vasculature. Nonetheless, existing studies already demonstrate that brain organoid platforms can recapitulate key clinical phenomena and drive the discovery of combination therapies.

Head and Neck Cancers

Head and neck cancers (HNCs), arising from mucosal sites of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, nasopharynx, and associated glands. Given the abundance of critical structures in this region, precision radiotherapy is central for tumor control while preserving function. PDO models for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) have been successfully established and are providing new clinical insights. Li et al. recently grew HNSCC organoids under Wnt‐free conditions, simplifying the culture medium without compromising growth, in order to better reflect the tumor's native signaling context [34]. These organoids maintained the parental tumors' histopathological features, genetic profiles, and key biomarkers, and importantly, their in vitro treatment responses mirrored patient‐specific sensitivities. This demonstrates strong model fidelity and positions HNSCC organoids as preclinical tools for predicting personalized radiation responses.

Millen et al. further showed that the radiosensitivity of HNC organoids correlates with clinical outcomes in corresponding patients [35]. These data directly support the use of HNC organoids in guiding treatment selection, distinguishing patients likely to respond to standard chemoradiation from those requiring intensified therapy or novel radiosensitizers.

Beyond predictive use, HNC organoids have been instrumental in discovering mechanistic biomarkers and testing hypotheses for radioresistance. For example, Lucky et al. established nasopharyngeal carcinoma organoids under hypoxic conditions to mimic the often low‐oxygen microenvironment of these tumors [36]. They observed that hypoxic organoids had significantly decreased radiation sensitivity, mediated by HIF‐1α signaling and upregulation of DNA repair pathways. This mechanistic link suggests that hypoxia‐driven radioresistance might be overcome by altering fractionation or by pharmacologically targeting HIF‐1α. This finding has therapeutic implications, as HNC organoids may help identify hypoxic tumors that could benefit from hypoxia modification, altered fractionation, or dose escalation.

Issing et al. used an HNC organoid biobank to examine the interaction between TP53 mutation status, HPV infection, and radiation response [37]. Their data indicated that while HPV‐positive, TP53‐deficient tumors grew aggressively, the HPV16 E6/E7 oncoproteins also induced a sustained G2/M cell‐cycle arrest in organoids, which has implications for radiation sensitivity. This nuanced finding highlights how organoids can unravel the complex biology of virus‐associated cancers and suggests why HPV‐positive HNC, despite high proliferation, often responds well to radiation. In another study targeting DNA damage repair mechanisms, Shrivastava et al. showed that treating oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma organoids with a CDK4/6 inhibitor abrogated RAD51‐mediated homologous recombination repair, thereby markedly enhancing radiosensitivity [38]. This provides a strong rationale for combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with radiotherapy in select HNC patients to exploit a synthetic lethal interaction between cell cycle blockade and unrepaired DNA damage.

In summary, HNC organoids possess high clinical fidelity, positing them as powerful tools for radiosensitivity prediction and radiosensitizer discovery. While standard cultures lack durable immune components, ongoing research focused on integrating autologous immunity and vascularization is directly addressing this gap, promising enhanced physiological accuracy for future applications.

Thoracic Malignancies

Thoracic malignancies encompass cancers of the chest, notably lung cancer, breast cancer, and esophageal cancer, all of which commonly involve radiotherapy in management. Organoid models for these cancers are at varying stages of development, with especially promising work in breast and esophageal cancers, while lung cancer organoid radiation research has been comparatively limited to date.

Lung Cancer

As the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide, approximately 70% of cases receive RT at some point [39]. However, establishing lung tumor organoids has proven challenging, and current studies involving lung organoids and radiation have largely focused on normal lung injury models (see Section 5.2) rather than tumor organoids. One hurdle is the complex cellular composition of lung tumors and difficulties in culturing them long‐term. Nevertheless, a few groups have succeeded in creating non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) organoids and are beginning to test radiation responses. Given the paucity of data, lung cancer organoids remain an area in need of deeper exploration. Thus, ongoing efforts are justified to build larger lung organoid biobanks and incorporate them in radio‐oncology research.

Breast Cancer

Breast cancer organoids have been more extensively studied, particularly the aggressive triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtype that lacks targeted therapies [40]. TNBC organoids mirror the heterogeneous response seen clinically, where a subset of patients are refractory to standard chemo‐radiation. Using TNBC organoids, Parsyan et al. identified a potential combination therapy: adding a Polo‐like kinase 4 inhibitor (CFI‐400945) to radiation significantly increased tumor cell killing [41]. This synergistic effect suggests that centrosome duplication pathways (regulated by PLK4) might be an Achilles' heel in TNBC when combined with DNA damage from RT, offering a new strategy for radiosensitization. Another study by Drain et al. used breast tumor organoids to investigate how the biomechanical properties of the tumor microenvironment influence therapy response [42]. They found that residual TNBC cells after chemotherapy preferentially resided in a soft extracellular matrix niche within the organoids, which activated NF‐κB signaling and suppressed JNK‐mediated apoptosis, thereby promoting therapy resistance. By modifying matrix stiffness in organoid cultures and using pathway inhibitors, they demonstrated that targeting the NF‐κB–JNK axis can reverse this ECM‐induced radioresistance, pointing to a novel intervention to overcome microenvironment‐mediated treatment failure. These mechanistic insights, derived from organoid models, emphasize the interplay between physical tumor context and radiation response and highlight potential therapeutic targets for clinical testing in breast cancer.

Esophageal Cancer

Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) is another thoracic malignancy where organoids have shown high promise. Karakasheva et al. established EAC PDOs and reported that these models faithfully recapitulated patient‐specific responses to chemoradiation, mirroring both sensitivity and resistance patterns observed in the clinic [43]. The organoids also preserved inter‐patient heterogeneity in drug and radiation response, underscoring their value for personalized therapy screening. Significantly, Dings et al. leveraged EAC organoids to uncover a metabolic radioresistance mechanism involving estrogen‐related receptor alpha (ESRRA) [44]. They observed that radiation triggered an increase in mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation in EAC organoids, driven by ESRRA activation, which contributed to radioresistance. Treatment with an ESRRA inhibitor prior to irradiation abrogated this metabolic shift and restored radiosensitivity in the organoids. This finding positions ESRRA as a promising target for radiosensitization in esophageal cancer.

Collectively, thoracic cancer organoid studies illustrate the breadth of applications. A general limitation in this category is that many thoracic organoid studies to date have been retrospective, while prospective trials using these organoids to guide patient therapy remain to be done. Researchers are particularly interested in integrating lung and breast organoid models with immunotherapy studies, an area where co‐culture with immune cells or syngeneic organoid models could play a role in the near future.

Abdominal and Pelvic Cancers

Cancers of the abdominal and pelvic regions often rely on radiotherapy as a key treatment modality, either definitive or adjuvant. However, the therapeutic ratio in these diseases can be narrow due to the radiosensitivity of surrounding organs like the bowel. Organoid models derived from gastrointestinal (GI) tumors have been at the forefront of radiotherapy research, given the relative ease of culturing GI epithelium and the high clinical need to personalize treatment.

Pancreatic Cancer

Historically considered a relatively radioresistant malignancy, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma has recently become an important setting for organoid‐based radiobiology. Patient‐derived pancreatic cancer organoids have been used to quantify radiation response, revealing inter‐patient heterogeneity in radiosensitivity and supporting the feasibility of PDO‐based radiation‐response assessment. Van Goor et al. reported that pancreatic cancer PDOs could be irradiated and monitored using viability‐based and longitudinal response metrics, providing preliminary evidence that PDOs may capture heterogeneous radiation responses in vitro [17]. Naumann et al. further extended this concept by testing patient‐derived PDAC organoids treated with gemcitabine, 5‐fluorouracil, photon irradiation, proton irradiation, and combined radio‐chemotherapy, highlighting the potential of PDOs to compare radiation modalities and systemic radiosensitizing strategies [10]. Mechanistic studies have also begun to emerge. Shukla et al. showed that pancreatic cancer‐derived 3D organoids could be used to evaluate treatment response after radiation exposure, supporting their utility as a functional preclinical platform [45]. Roy et al. further demonstrated that radiation increased SOX2 and OCT4 expression in pancreatic tumor organoids, whereas combined 5‐fluorouracil and radiation suppressed these cancer stemness‐associated markers [46]. These findings suggest that pancreatic organoids may help dissect stemness‐related and metabolic mechanisms of radioresistance. Despite these advances, pancreatic cancer organoid studies focused on radiotherapy remain limited in number. Available studies suggest that pancreatic cancer PDOs can model heterogeneous radiation responses in vitro, but further prospective validation against matched clinical radiotherapy outcomes is still needed.

Cervical and Gynecologic Cancers

Organoid models for cervical and other gynecologic malignancies are emerging, although radiotherapy‐focused evidence is currently concentrated in cervical cancer. Hyung et al. developed cervical cancer organoids from various histologic subtypes [47]. Yu et al. further showed that NSUN6‐mediated m5C modification of NDRG1 mRNA promoted radioresistance in cervical cancer by enhancing DNA repair in patient‐derived 3D organoids [48]. These findings suggest that cervical cancer PDOs may connect functional radiation‐response testing with mechanism‐based biomarker discovery. Early indications are that cervical PDOs can reflect differences in HPV status and radiosensitivity, paralleling clinical observations that HPV‐positive cervical cancers respond well to radiation [49]. Nevertheless, radiotherapy‐focused organoid studies in cervical and other gynecologic malignancies remain at an early stage. Future work should incorporate clinically relevant treatment contexts, including concurrent chemoradiotherapy and brachytherapy, and stratify models by HPV status, histologic subtype, and molecular alterations.

Colorectal and Rectal Cancers

Colorectal cancer organoids are among the best‐established, and multiple studies have leveraged them to identify drivers of radioresistance in rectal cancer. Wanigasooriya et al. demonstrated that hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is a key driver of radioresistance in locally advanced rectal cancer [50]. Using rectal cancer organoids, they showed that radioresistant organoids often harbored activated AKT signaling, and treatment with a dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor (dactolisib) prior to irradiation significantly reversed this radioresistance. Another study by Endo et al. illustrated the concept of a regenerative niche after high‐dose irradiation. They observed that in colorectal organoids, a small subset of Wnt‐activated LGR5+ cancer stem cells survived high doses and drove regrowth post‐irradiation [51]. Inhibiting Wnt signaling after radiation significantly suppressed organoid regrowth, suggesting a therapeutic window to eliminate these surviving stem cells and improve tumor control. They also found that histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, known to affect chromatin structure, increased radiosensitivity of organoids by impairing DNA damage response and downregulating Wnt target genes. Notably, these findings were consistent with known in vivo behaviors, thereby validating the organoid model's relevance.

Across abdominal and pelvic cancers, organoid studies have largely confirmed that PDOs are highly faithful to patient tumors and can reveal both predictive markers and mechanistic targets to guide therapy. Many of these organoid‐derived hypotheses are now candidates for clinical translation.

Organoids in Radiation Injury and Protection

In addition to tumor‐focused research, organoids are increasingly used to investigate radiation‐induced normal tissue injury, screen radioprotectors or radiation mitigators, and develop frameworks for future patient‐specific toxicity prediction. Toxicity to normal tissues is often dose‐limiting in radiotherapy and can severely impact quality of life. Organoid models derived from these normal tissues offer an ex vivo system that recapitulates key aspects of tissue architecture and function, enabling controlled studies of radiation damage mechanisms and evaluation of protective strategies that would be difficult in vivo. This section reviews organoid‐based insights into three common radiation injuries: salivary gland damage, lung injury, and gastrointestinal syndrome. For each, we highlight how organoids reproduce key pathophysiology, what candidate therapeutic targets have emerged, and the interventions proposed. Figure 3 summarizes major molecular pathways implicated in these tissue‐specific injuries and points of potential intervention. Together, these models may complement tumor‐focused organoid studies by supporting radioprotector or radiation mitigator screening and, with further validation, informing future approaches to normal tissue toxicity assessment.

Mechanisms of radiation injury and intervention strategies in normal tissue organoids. This schematic delineates organoid‐revealed radiation injury pathways and corresponding therapeutic interventions across three critical normal tissues. In salivary glands, radiation triggers DNA damage and cellular senescence in stem/progenitor cells, leading to loss of regenerative capacity and xerostomia. Organoid studies have identified p53/p21‐mediated senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction (mitophagy deficits) as key pathways. Interventions like senolytics, mitophagy enhancers, or regenerative factors have shown efficacy in restoring function. In the lung, radiation causes an inflammatory cascade and fibrosis: Organoids reveal involvement of TGF‐β signaling, alveolar epithelial plasticity driven by pathways like PCLAF–DREAM–CLIC4, and radiation‐induced ferroptosis at higher doses. Antifibrotic drugs (e.g., phenelzine mimicking PCLAF axis inhibition) and ferroptosis inhibitors or antioxidants (Nrf2 pathway activators) appear promising. In the intestine, radiation damages Lgr5+ stem cells and epithelium, triggers microbial dysbiosis, and can induce ferroptosis. Radiation‐Induced Salivary Gland Injury

Radiation damage to the salivary glands is a prevalent toxicity in head‐and‐neck cancer, with long‐term xerostomia reported in roughly 63%–93% of patients despite modern techniques, leading to persistent dry mouth, dental morbidity, and impaired digestion [52]. Salivary gland organoids, derived from primary human or animal salivary tissue, recapitulate acinar–ductal architecture and stimulus‐evoked secretory functions, providing an ex vivo platform to interrogate how irradiation perturbs glandular physiology and repair [53].

Pathophysiology and Mechanism

Convergent in vivo and organoid evidence identifies stem/progenitor‐niche senescence as a central driver of chronic hypofunction after irradiation. In salivary tissue and SGOs, radiation provokes persistent DNA‐damage signaling with p21 induction, SA‐β‐gal activity, and a senescence‐associated secretory phenotype within ductal/acinar progenitors [54]. In parallel, mitochondrial quality‐control failure emerges as a tractable vulnerability: pharmacologic induction of mitophagy in irradiated salivary stem/progenitor cells reduces senescence and restores organoid growth and self‐renewal [55]. Together, these data support a model in which radiation‐evoked DNA damage and mitochondrial stress in the stem/progenitor compartment drive durable senescence and loss of regenerative capacity, providing mechanistic anchors for targeted radioprotection.

Targeting senescence and restoring stem‐cell function are convergent strategies emerging from SGO studies. While bona fide senolytics remain under evaluation, organoid‐based studies have suggested several indirect anti‐senescence strategies. Melatonin, a pleiotropic antioxidant with DNA repair–supporting activity, improved post‐irradiation viability and self‐renewal of salivary gland stem/progenitor cells and alleviated gland dysfunction in vivo, nominating a low‐toxicity radioprotector suitable for head‐and‐neck settings [56]. Complementing senescence control, mitochondrial quality‐control can be pharmacologically rescued: inducing mitophagy with urolithin A in irradiated SGOs reduced the senescent phenotype and restored organoid growth or self‐renewal [55]. Beyond epithelial‐intrinsic actions, HGF/c‐MET signaling provides paracrine regeneration, suggesting a co‐transplantation or trophic‐factor route to functional rescue [57].

Interventions

Together, SGO data converge on p53/p21‐linked senescence and mitochondrial injury as actionable liabilities, with melatonin, mitophagy enhancers, and HGF‐based strategies emerging as near‐term candidates for radioprotection and regeneration. Notably, melatonin also exhibits antitumor properties in head‐and‐neck models, supporting a favorable therapeutic ratio when normal‐tissue protection is pursued alongside oncologic control [58]. A GMP workflow for clinical‐grade SGO‐derived cell products has been established from patient biopsies, meeting predefined acceptance and stability criteria and underscoring the feasibility of cell‐based therapies for radiation xerostomia [59].

Radiation‐Induced Lung Injury (RILI)

RILI encompasses a spectrum from acute pneumonitis to chronic fibrosis and remains a major dose‐limiting toxicity in thoracic radiotherapy [60]. Clinically, it typically presents as an early inflammatory syndrome followed by fibrotic remodeling over 6–24 months [61]. Modeling RILI in vitro has been difficult given the lung's complex 3D architecture and cell diversity; however, next‐generation human systems now address parts of this gap. Matrix‐free iPSC‐derived human lung organoids (LuOrgs) that contain distal alveolar lineages (AT1/AT2) can be irradiated to elicit DNA damage responses and cell‐type‐specific senescence programs, providing tractable, human‐relevant readouts of normal tissue injury [62]. Complementing organoids, a microfluidic lung alveolus‐on‐a‐chip lined by primary human alveolar epithelium and pulmonary endothelium recapitulates hallmark features of acute RILI and has already been used to screen candidate mitigators [63].

Pathophysiology and Mechanism

Ferroptosis: At low exposures relevant to environmental/diagnostic contexts, human lung organoids show subtle metabolic stress without overt cytotoxicity, whereas higher exposures (2 Gy) shift the response toward oxidative injury phenotypes in multi‐omics space [64]. Although direct demonstrations of frank ferroptosis at exactly 2 Gy in human alveolar organoids are still emerging, convergent evidence indicates ferroptosis contributes to RILI biology. Several scRNA‐seq and transcriptome‐integrative studies of RILF lungs reveal increased ACSL4/NCOA4 signatures, reduced GPX4, and ferroptosis–ECM coupling, implicating ferroptotic stress in fibrogenic progression [65]. Moreover, independent work in intestinal radiation injury mechanistically ties ferroptosis to the STAT1–IRF1–ACSL4 axis, highlighting a tractable regulatory node that may generalize to thoracic tissues and merits testing in lung organoid systems [66].

Fibrosis and regeneration: Chronic injury reflects failed epithelial regeneration and excessive matrix deposition. In human alveolar models, Kim et al. uncovered a PCLAF–DREAM–CLIC4 regulatory axis governing AT2 plasticity, identified via transcriptional‐signature matching, which preserved regenerative programs and mitigated fibrosis in both lung organoids and mouse lungs [62]. These findings nominate epithelial plasticity control as a precision target in late RILI and illustrate how organoid‐anchored discovery can rapidly generate repurposing candidates.

Interventions

Current management of pneumonitis remains largely supportive with corticosteroids, and despite signals in small or historical studies, agents such as ACE inhibitors or pentoxifylline show heterogeneous, context‐dependent benefit and lack definitive prospective evidence in modern RT cohorts [67]. Building on organoid insights suggests three complementary avenues.

Anti‐ferroptosis strategies: Given the ferroptotic footprint in RILI/RILF biology [68], testing lipophilic radical‐trapping antioxidants, iron chelators, or GPX4/ACSL4‐pathway [69] modulators in irradiated lung organoids is rational.

Anti‐inflammatory and pro‐regenerative approaches: In the human alveolus‐on‐a‐chip, anti‐inflammatory agents suppressed early RILI‐like phenotypes, offering a screening path for cytokine‐targeted biologics.

Matrix modifiers and anti‐fibrotics: Lung organoid‐based fibrosis (LOF) models now enable direct testing of TGF‐β/lysyl‐oxidase pathway inhibitors and other anti‐fibrotics with single‐cell resolution [70]. In parallel, phenelzine's organoid‐anchored efficacy underscores a signature‐guided repurposing route for antifibrotics in RILI [71].

In summary, human lung organoids and advanced lung‐on‐chip platforms faithfully recapitulate the dose‐dependent progression of RILI, from subcellular metabolic perturbations to ferroptosis and fibrotic remodeling, furnishing platforms to test radioprotectors and mitigators before clinical translation.

Radiation‐Induced Intestinal Injury (RIII)

RIII is a frequent collateral effect of abdominal/pelvic radiotherapy and can progress from acute enteritis to the potentially lethal gastrointestinal syndrome. Hallmark features include profound loss of crypt stem cells, barrier failure, dysbiosis [72], and cascading inflammation. Intestinal organoids faithfully model crypt–villus organization and stem‐cell dynamics [73], enabling mechanistic dissection and drug testing in human‐relevant systems.

Pathophysiology and Mechanism

Stem cell depletion and regeneration: A defining lesion in gut radiation injury is depletion of Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells (ISCs) at the crypt base, with consequent regenerative failure. Using mouse models and rectal organoids, Tirado et al. showed that pelvic irradiation ablates Lgr5+ rectal stem cells, compromises crypt regeneration, and that organoid‐derived Lgr5+ cells can engraft and accelerate repair in vivo [74]. Beyond proof‐of‐concept, enema‐delivered colon organoids engrafted irradiated rectal mucosa and restored epithelial architecture in a mouse radiation proctitis model, underscoring the translational potential of epithelial stem‐cell transplantation [75].

Pharmacologic modulation of regeneration (the p53 axis): After severe radiation injury, a transient p53 “pulse” is required to induce fetal‐like revival stem cells (revSCs) that drive regeneration, while epithelial p53 loss abrogates this revSC program [76]. Conversely, excessive/sustained p53 activity enforces arrest and apoptosis. Accordingly, inhibiting CBP/p300‐mediated p53 acetylation mitigated GI‐ARS, preserved Lgr5+ ISCs, and improved survival in mouse and human intestinal organoid systems [77]. A complementary auranofin approach, which elicits a reversible p53/p21‐mediated arrest to permit DNA repair, conferred radioprotection in mouse and human colonic organoids [78]. Taken together, these data reconcile “pro‐p53” and “anti‐p53” strategies, which emphasize that timing dictates whether to transiently engage or temper p53 to optimize epithelial regeneration.

Microbiome influence: The gut microbiome has emerged as a key modifier of radiation injury, and intestinal organoids now enable reductionist dissection of host–microbe signaling. In a recent study, Xie et al. identified the tryptophan catabolite indole‐3‐carboxaldehyde (I3A) as a radioprotective metabolite [79]. By activating AhR signaling, I3A promoted intestinal stem cell expansion and epithelial IL‐10 expression, thereby sustaining organoid regenerative capacity post‐irradiation. Consistent with this concept, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) reduced epithelial apoptosis and preserved crypt architecture in preclinical irradiation models [80]. This underscores the crucial role of microbial metabolites in maintaining intestinal homeostasis after radiation injury and highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting the gut microbiome.

Ferroptosis in the gut: Multiple lines of evidence implicate ferroptosis in intestinal radiation injury. In mice, irradiation upregulates ACSL4 and suppresses GPX4 activity, driving lipid peroxidation and crypt loss [69]. Mechanistically, Kong et al. mapped a STAT1–IRF1–ACSL4 axis as a central driver of radiation‐induced ferroptosis in jejunal epithelium, with pathway inhibition mitigating injury [66]. These insights have catalyzed targeted interventions. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) preserved tight‐junction integrity and suppressed ferroptosis via mTOR activation in irradiated intestinal organoids and mice [81]. In parallel, the natural compound perillaldehyde robustly activated Nrf2 signaling in intestinal organoids and attenuated radiation‐induced ferroptosis, nominating a feasible path to radioprotection [82].

Interventions

Together, studies delineate a multi‐pronged mitigation strategy for radiation GI syndrome: preserve/restore ISCs, microbiome support with probiotics/AhR ligands, and inhibiting regulated cell death pathways such as ferroptosis. While most data remain preclinical, the use of human intestinal organoids and standardized host‐microbe co‐culture platforms provides a credible translational pathway toward initial human trials of these targeted radioprotective approaches.

Implementation Roadmap for Clinical Translation

Translating organoid‐based discoveries into routine clinical practice requires a concerted effort to standardize methodologies, validate findings across centers, and integrate organoid testing into the clinical decision‐making process. This section outlines a roadmap for clinical implementation, highlighting key steps and considerations. Minimal Information About Organoids and Their Use (MIAOU) and Data Reporting

As radiobiological studies increasingly utilize organoid models, standardized reporting frameworks become essential. The HYBRIDA consortium has established the MIAOU framework [83], defining core metadata requirements for culture conditions, irradiation parameters, and analytical methodologies. These guidelines are further supported by the ISSCR Standards [84]. Ethically, PDOs necessitate robust informed consent protocols, strict de‐identification procedures, and transparent data‐sharing policies. International biobanking initiatives that pair organoids with longitudinal clinical data now provide the infrastructure necessary for multicenter validation and meta‐analyses [85].

Dosimetry Traceability and Quality Assurance

Consistent, accurate dosing underpins any organoid‐based RT assay intended for clinical use. Traceability should link laboratory dose measurements to primary or secondary standards used clinically [86]. Implementation requires comprehensive documentation of the calibration hierarchy supplemented by regular output consistency checks. Particular attention should be directed toward developing appropriate formalisms for medium‐energy X‐rays, where standardized methodologies remain notably underdeveloped in preclinical radiobiology [8].

Assurance of delivered dose in organoid radiobiology entails routine QA and validation dosimetry [87]. For cabinet irradiators, this extends to detailed commissioning via dose modeling and periodic verification, which are essential practices for ensuring accuracy in organoid assays [88].

Multicenter translation necessitates rigorous cross‐institutional checks. Quality systems should also adopt clinical risk‐management principles by mapping workflows, identifying failure modes, and implementing traceable controls to maintain dosimetric integrity.

Inter‐Laboratory Ring Trials and Automation

To gain clinical acceptance, organoid RT assays must demonstrate robust reproducibility across centers. Ring trials represent a critical step (checklist served in Table 1) in validating organoid RT assays for clinical use, requiring multiple centers to execute identical irradiation protocols and analyze result concordance. Building upon successful multi‐center drug response platforms and systematic analyses of reproducibility factors [89], this methodology is now formally recommended in validation before clinical implementation.

Automated platforms overcome the fundamental limitations of manual organoid handling by delivering the reproducibility and scale essential for clinical applications. These systems implement end‐to‐end automation encompassing culture maintenance, precision irradiation, and high‐content phenotyping through coordinated microfluidics [90], robotics, and AI analytics [91]. The integration of bioprinting with advanced imaging techniques further augments experimental capabilities [92]. This convergence enables centralized organoid screening facilities where clinical samples undergo standardized processing to yield quantitative, clinically actionable reports. Validation trials should replicate the full clinical workflow from sample processing to reporting. High inter‐laboratory concordance will subsequently justify a unified protocol for prospective trials and eventual clinical adoption.

Ethical, Data Governance, and Clinical Interface

Ethical Governance

Integrating PDOs into clinical practice demands rigorous ethical frameworks. Informed consent should transparently cover biobanking scope, data usage, and result‐return policies, aligning with standards like the evolving ISSCR Guidelines [84]. Ethical analyses further emphasize sustained donor engagement and governance to preempt healthcare disparities [93]. To ensure equitable access, centralized biobanks with standardized, cost‐effective distribution are essential for broadening the availability of PDO‐guided decision‐making beyond specialized centers.

Data Governance

Organoid programs produce high‐dimensional data requiring linkage to clinical outcomes under strict privacy safeguards. Practical solutions include secure databases with role‐based access, robust de‐identification protocols for omics sharing, and federated learning to develop multi‐institutional predictive models without data transfer [94].

Clinical Integration

To fit tumor‐board workflows, organoid reports must deliver decision‐ready outputs in standardized formats. Following the prospective model demonstrated in colorectal cancer organoid studies [95], radiotherapy applications should define clear endpoints, incorporate reports into case reviews, and evaluate organoid‐informed treatment adaptation in prospective studies. If feasibility and clinical validity are established, randomized trials comparing organoid‐informed strategies with standard decision‐making would be needed to determine whether such approaches improve clinically meaningful outcomes.

Regulatory Pathway

For clinical adoption, organoid‐based radiosensitivity assays must satisfy three key criteria: analytical validity under CLIA/CAP standards [96], clinical validity through multi‐center outcome studies, and proven utility in prospective trials. This pathway is underscored by evolving U.S. regulatory discussions on LDTs [97], including the FDA's 2024 final rule and its ongoing legal challenges.

Operational Feasibility

After the roadmap components have been defined, clinical implementation also requires explicit consideration of practical feasibility, including assay failure, turnaround time, cost, and resource intensity [6].

Organoid establishment is not uniformly successful, and reported success rates vary across tumor types, sampling sites, and processing workflows. For example, Choi et al. reported an overall success rate of 40% when establishing patient‐derived organoids from malignant ascites or pleural fluid, with substantial variation across cancer types [98]. Establishment failure may be affected by pre‐analytical and biological factors, including low tumor‐cell content, poor sample viability, microbial contamination, prior treatment exposure, or slow growth kinetics [98, 99]. Therefore, these parameters should be reported together with culture success rates to allow comparison across institutions and tumor types. Turnaround time is another critical constraint. A functional precision medicine study in advanced colorectal cancer reported a 61.5% take‐on rate for PDOs derived from core needle biopsies and a median chemogram turnaround time of 6 weeks [11]. For organoid‐guided radiotherapy to be clinically actionable, the workflow should be completed within the radiotherapy planning window, particularly for diseases in which treatment initiation cannot be delayed. Cost and resource intensity may also limit broad implementation. Organoid culture and testing require specialized culture systems, automated imaging or viability platforms, calibrated irradiation systems, trained personnel, and dosimetry quality assurance [100]. Therefore, before organoid‐guided radiotherapy can be incorporated into routine clinical evaluation, its cost‐effectiveness and scalable workflow models need to be carefully assessed. Normal tissue organoids may also be integrated into this roadmap as complementary models to tumor PDO assays. While tumor PDOs primarily inform radiosensitivity and tumor‐control assessment, normal tissue organoids may support radioprotector screening and, with further validation, future toxicity assessment. This paired strategy could help extend organoid‐guided radiotherapy from tumor response prediction toward a more balanced evaluation of therapeutic ratio.

Challenges and Outlook

Key challenges in model reproducibility, immune and microenvironmental modeling, scalable culture, and clinical validation remain central to the translation of organoid‐based precision radiotherapy. Although ex vivo organoid responses have been correlated with pathological or clinical outcomes in selected tumor types, these associations should be interpreted as evidence of potential clinical validity rather than demonstrated clinical utility. Most available studies remain preclinical, retrospective, or observational, and mainly support feasibility assessment, biomarker development, and mechanistic discovery. Prospective studies comparing organoid‐informed treatment adaptation with standard clinical decision‐making using clinically meaningful endpoints, including tumor response, local control, survival, toxicity, and quality of life, are needed before routine clinical implementation. Operational barriers, including assay failure, cost, scalability, and access beyond specialized centers, are discussed in Section 5.5.

Variability

Robust assessment of organoid radio‐response demands rigorous standardization to dissect intrinsic heterogeneity from technical noise [101]. Key requirements include standardized protocols with synthetic matrices, appropriate replication, and rigorous batch controls [102]. Ultimately, data quality and cross‐laboratory comparability are anchored by the routine use of reference controls and harmonized analytics, a framework powerfully endorsed by multi‐center reproducibility studies [103].

Absence of Immune and Systemic Components

Conventional PDOs offer reductionist modeling of tumor‐intrinsic radiosensitivity but fail to recapitulate the immune, vascular, and stromal interactions that govern therapy response in vivo. This gap is being bridged by increasingly sophisticated immune‐competent organoid systems, including ALI cultures that preserve endogenous immune contexts for checkpoint studies [23], autologous T cell–organoid co‐cultures for functional cytotoxicity profiling [22], and microfluidic systems [104] that introduce perfusion and immune trafficking capabilities.

Throughput and Timing Constraints

The clinical implementation of organoid radiosensitivity testing faces operational constraints in both throughput and processing time. Recent technological developments are systematically addressing these limitations. Automated microfluidic systems permit parallel processing with precise environmental control [90]. Standardized miniaturization protocols enhance experimental scalability, while AI‐driven image analysis extracts quantitative endpoints from label‐free bright‐field data [91].

Multi‐Omics and Integrative Modeling

A key strength of organoids is the ability to pair functional RT phenotypes with multi‐omics layers before and after treatment to uncover biomarkers of response. In practice, organoid readouts should be integrated with tumor genomics from both the PDO and the patient specimen to generate mechanism‐anchored recommendations [102]. Beyond bulk genomics, emerging spatial and multiplexed techniques can add metabolomic dimensions that better resolve resistance programs and adaptive states.

Radiomics and Model Fusion

Non‐invasive imaging biomarkers can be refined using organoids as experimental ground truth. In endometrial cancer, MRI radiomics captured early treatment response in PDO models, illustrating how radiomic signatures can be trained and validated [105]. More broadly, radiogenomics frameworks are maturing to couple quantitative imaging with genomics and could naturally incorporate PDO functional phenotypes to improve predictive performance. Clinical trials of this nature directed at specific cancers are presently in progress, which will catalyze broader adoption and investment in organoid‐based precision radiotherapy.

Conclusion

PDOs provide biologically relevant platforms for investigating radiotherapy response, mechanisms of radioresistance, and normal tissue injury. Tumor PDOs may support ex vivo assessment of radiotherapy response by capturing selected features of patient‐specific tumor biology, whereas normal tissue organoids may be useful for radioprotector screening and future toxicity‐risk evaluation. Further clinical translation will depend on dosimetry‐traceable irradiation workflows, standardized culture conditions, harmonized response endpoints and reporting criteria, multicenter reproducibility studies, improved immune and microenvironmental modeling, and prospective validation against clinically meaningful endpoints. Together, these efforts will help define the role of organoid‐informed strategies in individualized radiotherapy decision‐making.
