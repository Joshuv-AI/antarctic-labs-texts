Predicting the abundance of Lepeophtheirus salmonis in the Bay of Fundy, New Brunswick
Authors: Marianne I Parent, Henrik Stryhn, K Larry Hammell, Mark D Fast, Raphaël Vanderstichel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe primary objective was to construct a time series model for the abundance of the adult female (AF) sea lice Lepeophtheirus salmonis in Atlantic Salmon Salmo salar farms in the Bay of Fundy, New Brunswick, Canada, for the period 2016–2021 and to illustrate its short‐term predictive capabilities.MethodsSea lice are routinely counted for monitoring purposes, and these data are recorded in the Fish‐iTrends database. A multivariable autoregressive linear mixed‐effects model (second‐order autoregressive structure) was generated with the outcome of the abundance of AF sea lice and included treatments, infestation pressures (a measure that represents the dose of exposure of sea louse parasitic stages to potential fish hosts) within sites (internal) and among sites (external), and other predictors. The treatments were categorized by duration and type.ResultThe effect of mechanical treatments decreased with increasing sea surface temperature. In‐sample predictions had good accuracy. A one‐standard‐deviation increase in the external infestation pressures (EIPAF) produced a significant relative increase in the abundance of AF sea lice by 5% when other model predictors were kept constant. Sites separated by short seaway distances had stronger EIPAF than sites with more considerable distances.ConclusionThis model may be helpful for managers and farmers in implementing sea lice mitigation strategies on salmon farms in the Bay of Fundy.Keywords: aquatic epidemiology, Atlantic Salmon, Fish‐iTrends, Lepeophtheirus salmonis, prediction, sea lice

INTRODUCTION

The Atlantic Salmon Salmo salar aquaculture industry in the Bay of Fundy, New Brunswick (NB), Canada, monitors the parasitic sea louse Lepeophtheirus salmonis. Since 2009, members of the industry have kept a record of their findings in the Fish‐iTrends data management system. The database holds information on L. salmonis counting events and delousing treatments, which are essential for management, monitoring, and regulatory purposes (for additional detail, see Gautam et al. 2017a). Counting events include multiple steps—namely the selection of cages on a farm or aquaculture site (hereafter, "site"), the capture and anesthesia of numerous fish, and the counting of three L. salmonis life stage categories (for additional detail, see Jones et al. 2012). The provincially regulated monitoring program sets minimum standards whereby each site must perform counts every week and before and after treatments (Jones et al. 2012; General Regulation 91‐158: Government of New Brunswick 2020); however, the frequency of counts is relaxed to once per month when sea temperatures fall below 5°C (Government of New Brunswick 2020). This results in a longitudinal repeated‐measures data set for sea louse abundance with uneven temporal patterns since the counting events occur sporadically (i.e., not every day for every cage at a site). These uneven gaps in the time series can be short interruptions in sampling events (e.g., adverse weather conditions or when air temperature is so cold that it could damage the fish to remove them from the water for sea louse counts) or lengthy interruptions during fallowing periods (ranging from 29 to 75 weeks; Elghafghuf et al. 2020), when a site is left empty of fish after harvest and before stocking the next group of smolts.

A mixed linear regression model of adult female (AF) sea louse abundance was published from the longitudinal repeated‐measures data set of cage‐level counts (Parent et al. 2021). We expanded on the previously published work to create a relatively simple model that is capable of short‐term predictions. A simple model that can generate accurate predictions may aid in planning decisions for sea louse mitigation strategies (Aldrin et al. 2017; Elghafghuf et al. 2018, 2021; St‐Hilaire et al. 2018). The previously published model could not be employed for prediction purposes because it had a complex organizational structure of count data, which were organized into segments (defined as counts after a topical treatment, followed by all additional counts until the next treatment within a site and production cycle; Parent et al. 2021). The segmented organization of the data was unsuitable for predictions due to the irregular arrangement of the segments, as they depended on the irregular temporal pattern of treatment applications.

Counts, which were performed in cages at least weekly but could occur on any given day, were submitted to Fish‐iTrends directly, producing a daily resolution to the data, with multiple irregular time gaps (Parent et al. 2021). Short‐term predictions were better suited to a weekly scale to represent management perspectives, as is more commonly applied in other predictive studies (e.g., 1 week: Elghafghuf et al. 2018, 2021; St‐Hilaire et al. 2018; 10 days: Myksvoll et al. 2018). The weekly scale of the predictions can be selected because the sea louse count data were submitted into the data management system as site‐level weekly mean abundances (e.g., the Norwegian BarentsWatch) or to complement the regulations for the minimum frequency of weekly sea louse counts (Elghafghuf et al. 2018, 2020). Additionally, few published papers have described the methods for calculating the site‐level weekly mean abundances, either because the information was not recorded in their database or simply because it was not included in the publication (e.g., the week's starting day was not defined by Elghafghuf et al. 2020). The daily cage‐level resolution of the Fish‐iTrends data offers the opportunity to describe the averaging methods to obtain site‐level weekly abundances of sea lice.

A state‐space model was previously published that produced accurate short‐term weekly predictions for the Atlantic Salmon aquaculture sites around Grand Manan Island, NB (Elghafghuf et al. 2021). State‐space models are powerful but complex, and a simpler model of NB's Atlantic Salmon industry would be beneficial. More specifically, a time series regression would be suitable for this task. One approach was the distributed lag model that is used to answer environmental epidemiological questions (Katsouyanni et al. 1996; Bhaskaran et al. 2013). This approach was deemed inadequate for the complex structure and hierarchy of sea louse data and the lack of an explicit intervention; multiple treatment applications were performed at numerous sites without spatiotemporal patterns. A mixed‐effects model, however, can handle the complexity of the sea louse data and can be accessible to the industry.

Both the state‐space model constructed for Grand Manan Island (Elghafghuf et al. 2020, 2021) and the mixed‐effects model with the segmented organization for the entire Bay of Fundy (Parent et al. 2021) employed predictive covariates of sea louse infestation pressures. The infestation pressures represent the dose of exposure of sea louse parasitic stages to potential fish hosts (Parent et al. 2021). The infestation pressure is organized for the site and production cycle (internal infestation pressure [IIP]) and among sites (external infestation pressure [EIP]). Inclusion of infestation pressures in the prediction models resulted in slightly better prediction accuracy than models without infestation pressures (Elghafghuf et al. 2021).

The primary objective of this work was to develop a predictive model that is useful for longitudinal data on AF L. salmonis abundance for the production cycles in the Bay of Fundy, NB, and to demonstrate its predictive capabilities with an illustrative example. We used recent data (2016–2021) and adjusted for the effects of infestation pressures and topical and in‐feed treatments. As the estimation of infestation pressures was necessary for the model, the secondary objective was to compare these infestation pressures with the previously published estimates for the same area, which differed in time, methodology, and data (Parent et al. 2021).

METHODS

Study area and data structure

Routine sea louse counts and treatment information from Atlantic Salmon aquaculture sites in the Bay of Fundy between January 2016 and July 2021 were extracted from the Fish‐iTrends data management system (n = 41,240 count events). Extracted data included two categories of L. salmonis life stages: (1) preadults (males and females) and adult males (PAAMs) as one category; and (2) AFs (with and without egg strings). Counts of sea lice were repeated measures within a cage; multiple cages were organized within sites, and numerous sites were included within bay management areas (BMAs). Three companies operated in the Bay of Fundy and were included in our analyses as a categorical predictor. Counts were labeled as weekly, pretreatment, or posttreatment depending on when they were applied to a cage. Production cycles further defined cohorts of fish at sites (i.e., site‐production cycles [hereafter, "site‐cycles"]). The production cycles were identified using the recorded variables of stocking and harvesting dates, such that the production cycle began when the first fish were stocked and ended with the last count at a site prior to fallowing and around the harvest date. In the Fish‐iTrends database, groups of fish within a cage had a unique identifier per site to follow the fish movement among cages that may have occurred during treatment events.

Preparation of the outcome

The observational unit for analysis was a site's weekly mean abundance of AF sea lice (site‐weeks; equivalent to the term “farm‐weeks” used by previous authors). The weekly time scale was a logical choice for prediction since it reduced the temporal variation of counts that occurred at the cage level and complemented the regulation requirement of weekly counts. The selection of AF abundance as the outcome was determined by the lower variance of AFs compared to PAAMs (Elmoslemany et al. 2013). The abundances were calculated as the natural logarithm of the sea louse count divided by the number of fish sampled, with an offset of 1 (additional details are given in the Supplemental Information). The average abundance of AF sea lice was calculated for a week (the week was defined as Monday–Sunday) and per site with cage‐weighting. The fish group identifier in Fish‐iTrends (used to follow groups of salmon that were split or moved between cages during the production cycle) was ignored during the averaging process, as the aggregation occurred at the site level.

Calculation of internal and external infestation pressures

The IIP and EIP were calculated using the previously described site‐week average abundance of sea lice. The IIP values for AFs (IIPAF) and for PAAMs (IIPPAAM) were calculated as 3‐ and 4‐week lagged abundances, respectively. The decision for the lags was based on a review of the literature and mean sea temperature for the study area (for additional detail, see Parent et al. 2021).

The EIPAF was calculated as the sum of the Gaussian kernel density estimate (KDE)‐weighted spatial average abundance of AFs with a 3‐week lag from other sites (i.e., neighbors). The spatial component was calculated as the seaway distance (least‐cost distance) at 100‐m resolution among the manually selected approximate center points (centroids) for sites using the package gdistance version 1.1‐3 (van Etten and de Sousa 2012) in R (R Core Team 2020). The weights were estimated from the seaway distances among sites using the Gaussian KDE with a kernel bandwidth of 10 km. The seaway distance is commonly used to estimate the dispersal of sea lice and was used to compare other estimates of dispersal (Parent et al. 2024). The 10‐km kernel bandwidth represented the optimal displacement distance for AF sea lice among sites and was selected from a previous sensitivity analysis comparing various kernel bandwidths (Parent et al. 2021). The Gaussian KDE weights were then multiplied by AF abundance with a 3‐week lag for other sites, and these values were summed to represent the EIPAF of a site. The equation for calculating the EIPAF using site‐level weekly abundances of AF sea lice is presented in the Supplemental Information.

Topical and in‐feed treatments

Treatments were considered as three separate predictors in the analysis. The variables were labeled as follows: Week†, where † represents the treatment type (C = short‐term chemical; M = mechanical; I = in‐feed chemical). The predictors were primarily created according to the duration of effect for topical (chemotherapeutic agents and nonmedicinal operations with a short duration of effect) and in‐feed/oral treatments (chemotherapeutic agents with a prolonged duration of effect [WeekI*]). The in‐feed treatments included emamectin benzoate (Slice; Merck Animal Health) and avermectins (Ivomec [Boehringer] and Noromectin [Norbrook Laboratories Ltd.]). The short‐duration treatment category was subdivided into chemical (WeekC*) and mechanical (WeekM*) treatments. The chemical treatments included hydrogen peroxide (combined Interox Paramove 50 [Solvay Chemicals Inc.] and Aquaparox 50 [Alpha Chemical Ltd.]) and azamethiphos (Salmosan Vet; Benchmark Animal Health). The mechanical treatments included thermal (also warmwater) treatment and water spray (Hydrolicer).

Each treatment was constructed to have four categories based on the temporal relation of a given week to a treatment (B = before; Tx = treatment; A = after; O = other). A treatment week (Week†Tx) may not have an associated Week†B and Week†A if no counts were performed the week before and/or after the treatment week. A treatment week was identified as having any treatment applied between Monday and Sunday. Treatment weeks could occur as consecutive weeks, and different types of treatments could occur during the same week.

Chemical and mechanical treatment predictors were categorized as a treatment week (WeekCTx and WeekMTx), a week before the treatment week (WeekCB and WeekMB), a week after the treatment week (WeekCA and WeekMA), and any other week that did not fit the previous criteria (WeekCO and WeekMO). WeekMTx was further subdivided into its components of thermal and Hydrolicer treatments to evaluate the impacts of each. An immediate effect (but measured over a 1‐week duration; i.e., reduction of sea louse abundance) was assumed for topical treatments, as applied in other publications (e.g., Elghafghuf et al. 2018).

The in‐feed treatment predictor was categorized similarly to the short‐term treatment predictors but with two critical differences. The week of treatment and the week before the treatment were combined (WeekITx). WeekIA encompassed 4 weeks, as the in‐feed treatments were believed to have residual effects (Elghafghuf et al. 2018, 2020); a laboratory trial demonstrated that the concentration of emamectin benzoate residues decreased until day 45 after oral administration, with a peak at day 5 (Whyte et al. 2011).

Time at sea

Atlantic Salmon growth was controlled in the model using the number of weeks since the salmon were stocked at a site (time at sea [TAS]). We note that salmon were stocked at a site often over several weeks, and the stocking date in the database was for the date that the initial cage at a site was stocked. The TAS represented salmon growth, as the age, size, and weight of the fish may be associated with variation in the abundance of sea lice (for review, see Frenzl 2014). However, information related to the age, size, and weight of the Atlantic Salmon was not recorded in Fish‐iTrends.

Company

Three companies owned the aquaculture sites in the Bay of Fundy over the study period. To examine for possible effects of company on the data, company identifiers were provided.

Modeling

Inclusion criteria

The data set was restricted to the BMAs where Atlantic Salmon were produced; BMAs 5 and 6 were not included (n = 349 counts [0.8%]). We required cages to have been counted at a frequency greater than five counting events for a site‐cycle (i.e., we excluded n = 369 counts [0.9%]). The observations were limited to production cycles starting in 2016–2020. Observations for production cycles that began in 2021 were not included (n = 150 counts).

Multivariable autoregressive linear mixed model

A multivariable autoregressive linear mixed‐effects model (Rosner et al. 1985) was used to predict the weekly average abundance of AF sea lice. Random effects were used for the site‐cycles. The BMAs were applied as fixed effects (Parent et al. 2021). The sea surface temperature (hereafter, "temperature") fixed effects were extracted from the UK Met Office (UKMO) Operational Sea Surface Temperature and Ice Analysis (OSTIA) temperature product (UK Met Office 2005) as previously described (Parent et al. 2021) and were centered at the median of 11.85°C. The reference values for treatment predictors (chemical, mechanical, and in‐feed) were set to the week before the treatment (WeekCB, WeekMB, and WeekITx) to examine the effects of before and after treatment. A fractional polynomial regression with default powers and two dimensions was used to examine potential transformations for improved fit of the TAS predictor. Random slopes for time were applied as dichotomized TAS (categorized as prior to and after 44 weeks at sea) to explore the possibility that the TAS was not constant between site‐cycles, and this model was compared to the model without random slopes to assess whether the fit was improved.

Autocorrelation functions (ACFs) and partial autocorrelation functions (PACFs) were utilized to evaluate autoregressive processes that may fit the residual errors. These ACF and PACF plots were assessed for each site‐cycle. The model was fitted with a second‐order autoregressive (AR[2]) process correlation structure to the errors, stratified by three temperature profiles for the site‐cycle hierarchical level. The temperature profiles were low (6–9°C), medium (10°C), and high (11–12°C), which were defined from the mean temperature for the site‐cycles (standard deviation [SD] = 0.7). To evaluate the potential effect of IIPAF, alternate residual structures were applied; first‐order autoregressive (AR[1]) and independent structures of the residual errors were used with stratification by temperature profiles.

An adjusted version of the time series “without winter gaps” was created for site‐cycles with intervals of 5 weeks or more during the winter (defined as December–March, inclusive). These gaps were replaced by a single week to evaluate the effect of correlation (exponential decay) in time since the model considered observations across a large interval to be independent.

Models were fitted with the restricted maximum likelihood estimation unless nested models were compared. Akaike's information criterion (AIC) was used to compare models with different residual correlation structures. The model assumptions of linearity, homogeneity of variance, and normality of residuals were assessed graphically for each level of the hierarchy.

Influential observations were evaluated and are described in the Supplemental Information (available in the online version of this article). Two observations were removed after evaluation, as they represented possible gross errors.

Unless otherwise specified, all analyses were conducted in Stata version 17 (StataCorp 2021), and statistical significance was assessed at p‐values less than 0.05.

Prediction

An illustration of the model's capability was produced by use of an in‐sample prediction with a horizon of 8 weeks. The prediction start date (week 36 of the year 2020, corresponding to August 31–September 6, 2020) was purposefully selected to make predictions during a period of high abundance of sea lice (late summer) and with a large training data set (≥2500 site‐weeks) to which the model was fitted.

Week 37 forward predictions were calculated using real‐life predictors (e.g., temperature for week 37) and the IIPAF, IIPPAAM, and EIPAF as calculated with observed (real‐life) AF and PAAM counts. The prediction for a site‐week was calculated as the sum of the linear and random effects and the predicted residuals for lag weeks 1 and 2 multiplied by the autoregressive coefficients for lag weeks 1 and 2 (Pinheiro and Bates 2000). The equation for the prediction is provided in the Supplemental Information. The highest temperature category of the random effects was selected to match the mean weekly temperature for the prediction period. After the first week of prediction, the predicted residual was replaced by a residual calculated from the difference between the observed abundance of AFs and the prediction from the first week. The predictions for weeks 1–8 were also calculated without predicted residuals or residuals calculated from the difference between observed and predicted values of AF abundance; the residuals for lag weeks 1 and 2 were set to zero. With this latter method, the predictions were entirely driven by the linear and random effects of linear prediction. Additional details are given in the Supplemental Information.

The accuracy of the predictions was assessed using the normalized prediction error. The normalized prediction error was calculated as the difference between the observed and residual AF abundance divided by the SD of the random effects squared (see the Supplemental Information for additional details).

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics of the observations modeled

There were 3137 site‐weeks modeled over 261 weeks from June 6, 2016, to June 7, 2021. There were 77 production cycles for 48 sites. On average, the site‐cycles lasted 40.7 weeks, with a range from 6 to 74 weeks. On 8.4% of occasions (274 of 3137 site‐weeks), cages at sites were counted multiple times per week (range = 1–5 counts.cage−1.site‐cycle−1.week−1).

The abundances of sea lice (AFs and PAAMs) were right‐skewed; therefore, these were transformed with a natural logarithmic function (additional details are included in the Supplemental Information). A descriptive summary of potential variables for the multivariable model of AF abundance is presented in Table 1. The AF abundances were highly correlated (0.82), with means of 2.05 (SD = 4.68) and 1.38 (SD = 4.91) for the outcome and IIPAF, respectively. The mean IIPPAAM was larger than the mean IIPAF, which in turn was larger than the mean EIPAF. The EIPAF was calculated from weights derived from seaway distances, with a mean of 29.07 km (SD = 19.14) among sites.

The mean weekly temperature was 11.21°C (SD = 2.31; Table 1), with a range of 3.85–16.16°C for 2016–2021. There was a larger number of observations for the 10°C temperature category of the random effects, with 1725 site‐weeks (55.0%). The 6–9°C category had 415 site‐weeks (13.2%), and the 11–12°C category had 997 site‐weeks (31.8%). The mean AF abundances for the temperature profiles were 1.52 (SD = 4.48) for 6–9°C, 1.93 (SD = 4.66) for 10°C, and 2.41 (SD = 4.62) for 11–12°C. The predicted period (weeks 37–40) had a mean temperature of 13.11°C (SD = 0.71; range = 11.32–15.42°C; n = 508 site‐weeks).

The Atlantic Salmon spent, on average, 44.0 site‐weeks (SD = 28.3) at sea (i.e., TAS), with a range of 4–123 site‐weeks. The fractional polynomial regression estimated a square‐root function as the best transformation for the TAS predictor.

A total of 353 treatment events occurred in the sample of counts modeled. Mechanical, chemical, and in‐feed treatments were applied on a site within 1 week (n = 173 of 1000 site‐weeks [17.3%]), but most extended beyond 1 week (n = 827 [82.7%]). There were 405 site‐weeks of chemical treatments (WeekCTx) and 382 site‐weeks of mechanical treatments (WeekMTx; Table 1). The Hydrolicer and thermal treatments were applied during 89 and 293 site‐weeks, respectively. The week before chemical treatments (WeekCB) had a mean AF abundance of 3.82 (SD = 4.79), which decreased to 3.66 (SD = 3.91) for the week after chemical treatments (WeekCA). The week before mechanical treatment (WeekMB) had a mean AF abundance of 3.87 (SD = 4.09), and the week after mechanical treatment (WeekMA) had a mean abundance of 2.18 (SD = 3.20). A total of 100 site‐weeks had both chemical and mechanical treatments applied. There were 1316 site‐weeks in which no treatments were applied (WeekMO, WeekCO, and WeekIO), and the mean AF abundance was 2.36 (SD = 5.21) during those weeks.

Final model

The final multivariable autoregressive linear mixed‐effects model (AR[2]) included the continuous predictors IIPAF, IIPPAAM, EIPAF, temperature, and TAS; and the categorical predictors of mechanical treatments, mechanical treatments × temperature interaction, chemical treatments, in‐feed treatments, and BMA (Table 2). The AR(2) had good fit according to the ACF and PACF descriptive plots (data not shown). The random effects were the site‐cycle, and the AR(2) structure of the errors was stratified by temperature. Temperature and BMA fixed‐effects results were similar to those of the previously published model (Parent et al. 2021). The company variable was not retained in the final model because it was nonsignificant (Wald chi‐square [χ2] test: p = 0.914).

Fixed effects

Internal and external infestation pressures

The IIPAF was not significantly different from zero in the final model (p = 0.926). The effect of the IIPAF was examined under varying orders of the autoregressive function: AR(2), AR(1), and the independent residual structures were specified for the random‐effects equation. The AR(1) and independent alternate residual structures did not improve the model (AIC difference [ΔAIC] = 66 and ΔAIC = 1170, respectively) but demonstrated that IIPAF was a significant predictor. The model with the AR(1) structure estimated an AF abundance that increased by a factor of 1.08 (i.e., a relative increase of 7.7%; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.04–1.12; p < 0.001) for a 1‐unit increase in the IIPAF when all other predictors were kept constant. The model with the independent structure to the errors estimated a relative increase of 35.3% in the abundance of AF sea lice (95% CI = 1.30–1.40; p < 0.001) for a 1‐unit change in the IIPAF.

The effect of IIPPAAM was a relative increase of 11.0% in AF abundance (p < 0.001) for a 1‐unit increase in IIPPAAM when other predictors were kept constant.

A 1‐unit increase in EIPAF resulted in a relative increase of 27.5% in AF abundance (p = 0.002) when other predictors were kept constant in the final model. The effects of applying mechanical treatments varied by temperature (Figure 4; Wald χ2 test: p = 0.005). Counts were performed at a median TAS of 46 weeks; the maximum TAS was 123 weeks. A fractional polynomial model suggested transformations with powers of −2.0, 0.0, and 0.5. DISCUSSION

Predicting the abundance of adult female sea lice

The objective was to illustrate the predictive capability of a model of AF sea louse abundance in the Bay of Fundy, NB. The constructed model was a multivariable autoregressive linear mixed‐effects model (AR[2]) of AF abundance. The illustration was accomplished with an in‐sample and short‐term weekly prediction for several sites during the summer of 2020. The predictions had good accuracy, which was expected with an in‐sample approach. This model could serve as a basis for managers and farmers to make decisions about managing their salmon, such as applying a treatment considering the predicted rise in the abundance of AF sea lice. The application of this and subsequent models would likely benefit from the input of farmers, managers, and veterinarians involved in sea louse management to ascertain their needs.

The predictions sometimes overestimated and sometimes underestimated the observed AF L. salmonis abundance at varying weeks in the prediction horizon and varied by site‐cycle. The overestimation of abundance was also observed in the model and predictions from Aldrin et al. (2017). Those authors proposed that the stochastic nature of infestation and other variables, such as salinity, affected the ability of the model to predict the abundance of sea lice accurately. For farmers, managers, and veterinarians, overestimation may result in unnecessary treatments, whereas underestimation may lead to a spike in abundance that impacts the management of sea lice in the long term.

The use of observed values for the calculation of residuals did not much improve the model's prediction accuracy compared to the method of calculating the residuals without the use of observed values (set at zero). The fixed and random effects of the predictions drove the prediction to a greater degree than the effects of the correlation parameters. The prediction with the largest accuracy was in the first week. There was no increase in the prediction error along the prediction horizon; this finding was likely due to the small data set for prediction across 8 weeks (n = 14 sites) with a high level of variability.

The evaluation of a site in BMA 1 demonstrated a situation with a much poorer prediction outcome (data not shown). The sea louse abundance at this site was high early into its production cycle; the stocking of the site was near in time to the date selected for the start of prediction (week 36 of the year 2020). The model's fixed effects could not predict this high AF abundance well because a more considerable value for the predictor TAS0.5 was required for a suitably high prediction. The random effects for site‐cycle increased the estimate of AF abundance, but the increase was too slight for it to be fully compensated. The high abundance at this site early in its production cycle may be of interest to farmers, managers, and veterinarians. The EIP may be one of the contributing factors, and further study is recommended to evaluate this occurrence.

Implementing this model for prediction purposes would require reliable prediction of the temperature used in the model (Elghafghuf et al. 2021). The prediction of infestation pressures would be necessary for using this model for a prediction horizon greater than 4 weeks, as IIPAF and EIPAF were dependent on AF sea louse abundance with a 3‐week lag and IIPPAAM was dependent on PAAM abundance with a 4‐week lag. Predictions could be produced for scenarios without treatment applications; alternatively, various scenarios could be produced according to topical and/or in‐feed treatments.

Contributions of internal and external infestation pressures

The infestation pressures were estimated for their use in the predictive model with the 2016–2021 data set. The infestation pressures among sites (EIPAF) and within sites (IIPAF and IIPPAAM) were important predictors for AF abundance. However, the effect of IIPAF had to be evaluated with alternate structures to the residual errors. The standardized estimated coefficient (hereafter, “standardized coefficient”) for IIPAF in the final model was null (0; 95% CI = −0.04 to 0.04), where the autoregressive structure absorbed the effect of IIPAF. The null IIPAF matches the null effect of IIP estimated in the state‐space model produced for Grand Manan Island (Elghafghuf et al. 2020). However, the effect was significant for IIPAF in the model with AR(1) and independent residual error structures, which highlights a technical aspect rather than a biological aspect of IIPAF significance in the model. The IIPAF standardized coefficient for the model with AR(1) was 0.08 (95% CI = 0.04–0.11), and it was more significant with the independent structure (standardized coefficient = 0.32; 95% CI = 0.28–0.35). The IIPAF standardized coefficient for the model with the Fish‐iTrends data for 2009–2018 was between the estimates for the AR(1) and independent structures of residual errors (Parent et al. 2021). In the region of Grand Manan Island, Elghafghuf et al. (2021) found that their predictive model had negligible effects from IIP on the outcome of PAAM abundance.

The standardized coefficient of EIPAF was 0.05 (95% CI = 0.02–0.08) for the final model. The effect of EIPAF was smaller than those of the IIPAF standardized coefficients with the AR(1) and independent structures of residual errors. The finding that the effect of IIPAF was greater than the effect of EIPAF duplicates the conclusion for the model produced with the Fish‐iTrends data for 2009–2018 (Parent et al. 2021). The larger effect of IIP compared to EIP was observed in other studies (Adams et al. 2012; Aldrin et al. 2013). This finding may be explained by a reproductive output of AF sea lice that increases over time at a site, resulting in self‐infestation (concept of positive feedback) that is greater than the infestation force produced by the dispersal of planktonic lice from neighboring farms. It is also possible that the larger effect of IIPAF was due to the duration of treatments, as it was noted that many cage‐level applications of treatments occur over 1 week at a site. The opposite relationship between EIPAF and IIPAF has been observed during other studies in Grand Manan Island (Elghafghuf et al. 2020) and Chile (Kristoffersen et al. 2013). The standardized coefficient of EIPAF was smaller than previously reported (Parent et al. 2021). The difference in the strength of EIP and IIP effects among studies may be due to the divergence in methodology for estimating these predictors. For instance, the mean temperature (11.2°C; SD = 2.3) from 2016 to 2021 increased compared to the mean temperature (10.9°C; SD = 3.0) from 2009 to 2018, and this difference highlights the fact that despite no evidence of an interaction, there may be unmeasured confounding factors that could have changed between the two timelines and inclusion criteria.

The standardized coefficient of IIPPAAM (0.11; 95% CI = 0.08–0.14) was larger than that of IIPAF in the final model and the model with the AR(1) structure of the residual errors but smaller than that in the model with the independent structure. Compared to the previously reported IIPPAAM for 2009–2018, the standardized coefficient was larger, but the 95% CI overlapped from 0.08 to 0.10 (Parent et al. 2021). The significance of this predictor in the current study and the previous study (Parent et al. 2021) suggests that the estimation of infestation pressures should consider both AFs and PAAMs within a site.

The larger contributions of IIP than EIP to AF sea louse abundance imply that the management of sea lice in the Bay of Fundy should primarily focus on the within‐site control of sea lice. The improvement of mitigation strategies—for example, the application of treatments to all active cages at a site over a shorter time interval—may decrease the IIP by decreasing the local planktonic sea louse burden and reducing self‐infestation (Aldrin et al. 2013). However, neighboring sites also contribute to site sea louse infestation. Mitigation strategies to reduce EIP may include synchronizing treatments and continued use of fallowing periods (Aldrin et al. 2013; Kristoffersen et al. 2013, 2014). The effect of synchronization of in‐feed treatments on the potential for the development of resistance is unknown (Arriagada et al. 2017). Synchronization of treatments may decrease the risk of resitance development, as the time to the next treatment may be extended (Arriagada et al. 2017). Sea lice would have reduced exposure to sublethal levels of the chemotherapeutant in this scenario; sublethal levels may be a risk factor for the development of resistance to chemotherapeutants (Lam et al. 2020).

Treatments and temperature

The treatments applied between 2016 and 2021 were included in the model to control for their potentially confounding effects, and the study was not explicitly designed to evaluate the efficacy of the treatments per se. Additionally, the model accounted for multiple other predictor variables that make it difficult to understand the treatment effects completely. Despite this, the mechanical, chemical, and in‐feed treatments significantly affected the estimated AF abundance in the model.

The effects of mechanical treatments varied by temperature. The ratio of predicted abundances for WeekMB to WeekMA demonstrated decreased effectiveness with increasing temperature (Figure 4). The relationship between thermal (i.e., warmwater) application and temperature was previously noted (Parent et al. 2021). The association between Hydrolicer application and temperature was weak in the previously published model (i.e., the 95% CI crossed zero in Parent et al. 2021). Parent et al. (2021) proposed that the diminished capacity of warmwater treatment to reduce the abundance of AFs was due to the acclimation of sea lice. In other words, thermal treatment during the spring and early summer (i.e., periods of lower temperatures) would represent a larger temperature difference applied to sea lice compared to treatment during late summer and early fall, when the sea lice are acclimated to higher temperatures. Borchel et al. (2018) demonstrated that the nauplii of L. salmonis have thermotolerance enabling their survival of rapid increases in temperature when exposed to an intermediate temperature prior to heat shock. The effect of thermal treatments for delousing was recently evaluated (Nilsson et al. 2023). The employed range was a 20.0–22.5°C difference between the seawater and the treatment water temperature per Norwegian industry guidelines (28–34°C; Nilsson et al. 2023). In the study area, the difference was 12–21°C at low ambient temperature and 18–27°C at high ambient temperature (based on the UKMO OSTIA temperature of 6.9–16.0°C during days when thermal treatments were applied). The effectiveness of the thermal treatments should be further evaluated in the study area.

Chemical treatments did not vary by temperature in this model, as was observed with hydrogen peroxide treatments in the Parent et al. (2021) study. The combination of hydrogen peroxide with azamethiphos might have masked these temperature variable effects. Bioassays for hydrogen peroxide exposure of L. salmonis at temperatures of 5°C and 10°C demonstrated a slight decrease in survival from 73% at 5°C to 67% at 10°C (VanIderstine et al. 2020). During the Parent et al. (2021) study, the effects of azamethiphos were categorized per method of application (tarpaulin, skirt, and wellboat) and demonstrated variable effects concerning temperature. There were minimal changes to the sensitivity of sea lice Caligus rogercresseyi to azamethiphos in the temperature range of 12–15°C, with a more effective concentration value at 9°C (González‐Gómez et al. 2023).

Temperature was a significant predictor for sea louse abundance and the developmental rate of the louse (Samsing et al. 2016; Hamre et al. 2019), and infestation success was dependent on temperature (Samsing et al. 2016). Additionally, temperature may direct mitigation strategies, such as treatment dosage and exposure time (Overton et al. 2017, 2019). Increasing temperatures have been associated with higher mortality after treatment (Overton et al. 2019).

The present study design improved the ability to observe the effects of in‐feed treatments compared to the 2009–2018 study with the segmented data organization (Parent et al. 2021). In‐feed treatments were typically applied early in the production cycle, and this predictor may capture the first period at sea. It is possible that the association observed between sea louse counts and the in‐feed treatments were confounded by TAS. The in‐feed treatments may also have been influenced by the application of lufenuron (Imvixa; Elanco Animal Health Inc.) to smolts at the hatcheries immediately prior to stocking. We have no records of such hatchery‐based treatments in the database, but it was reported that this treatment was applied at some hatcheries within the study period examined.

Considering these findings, mechanical and chemical treatments reduced AF abundance when the weeks before and after treatment were compared. The mechanical treatments' ability to reduce the abundance of AFs should be further evaluated at various temperatures. The efficacy of treatments should be evaluated with methods other than those presented in this study, such as the method of Gautam et al. (2017b) for the study area. This information would guide veterinarians in prescribing treatments that are more efficacious at the temperature measured at the time of application.

Time at sea

The AF abundance increased with TAS, as observed by others (e.g., Revie et al. 2002). The TAS was a continuous variable and may represent salmon growth, as the fish increased in size (weight and surface area) and age. The TAS was equivalent to the “count week” used by Kristoffersen et al. (2014), which was a significant predictor and EIP in the abundance of PAAMs. Other studies have mixed findings of an association between the abundance of sea lice and the size and age of fish (for review, see Frenzl 2014). Larger salmon present a greater surface area for attachment and decrease the competition for space (Frenzl 2014). Transport and smoltification (the period of morphological and physiological changes that occur in anadromous fish, allowing them to survive in salt water) increase the susceptibility of young smolts to stress and settlement of skin parasites (Madaro et al. 2016). The swimming depth of the fish may also predispose younger fish to infestation, as younger fish (0.5 kg) swam at shallower depths (2–5 m) compared to older fish (4.0 kg; 7–10 m) during the day (Korsøen et al. 2012). Smaller fish have higher densities of sea lice (Tucker et al. 2002), which is a function of the sea louse count per unit surface area of the fish; for a large fish and a small fish with equal counts of sea lice, the small fish will have a greater density of sea lice because its surface area is smaller.

Other predictors that may be used to represent the growth of salmon in the model are the individual weight of the salmon (Todd et al. 2000; Glover et al. 2004) and the average weight for the group (Kristoffersen et al. 2013). However, these data were not available in the Fish‐iTrends database. A predictor for salmon age/size, using an earlier extraction of the data set, was also not included in the Parent et al. (2021) study and other studies (e.g., Amundrud and Murray 2009; Elghafghuf et al. 2020, 2021).

Winter

The “without winter gaps” time series was applied to the final model to evaluate the effect of correlation in time. Where this time series did not improve the model fit and the coefficients did not considerably change the counts of sea lice before and after these large gaps in time, the observations before and after the winter period were deemed independent.

Study design and limitations

Study design

This study developed a predictive model using estimated infestation pressures in the Bay of Fundy using empirical AF sea louse abundances for the years 2016–2021 from the Fish‐iTrends database. The multivariable autoregressive linear mixed‐effects model was selected because it is well suited to the repeated measures of longitudinal data with a hierarchical structure. Moreover, the mixed‐effects model is relatively simple and could be employed for prediction purposes that may benefit salmon aquaculture veterinarians and managers. A single‐equation model was selected for simplicity; however, it could be argued that a model with equations for the first year at sea (defined as stocking to the first winter at sea) and the second to third years at sea (defined as after the first year at sea until harvesting) may be better suited. This avenue would have the advantage of separating the first year at sea from the other production years, as these periods may be different for several reasons, including higher AF abundance for populations in the second year at sea (Revie et al. 2002; Saksida et al. 2007), more in‐feed treatments and fewer topical treatments, and the possible in‐feed chemotherapeutic effects of lufenuron that may have been applied at the hatchery prior to smolt stocking (2017–2020). Other studies have utilized multiple‐equation models (e.g., Kristoffersen et al. 2013; Aldrin et al. 2019). Aldrin et al. (2019) selected a multivariate zero‐inflated negative binomial distribution that treated AFs and other mobile sea lice as separate time series because they have different parameter estimates. The disadvantage of the multiple‐equation models would be the added complexity during prediction and hence the decreased usefulness for the industry.

The time series utilized all counts and contained many time gaps. The mixed model was appropriate for managing time gaps without imputing sea louse counts, as Elghafghuf et al. (2020) did when using state‐space models in the Grand Manan Island region. Limiting the data to consecutive counts, as was employed by Kristoffersen et al. (2014), would not be possible with our data set because it would severely limit the sample size and potentially introduce a selection bias into the study.

The model's residual error structure was complex; it was an AR(2) structure with three temperature profiles. The null IIPAF in the AR(2) model reflected a lack of ability to pick up on the variation in explained terms. Other residual error structures were evaluated. The study with the Fish‐iTrends data for 2009–2018 utilized an exponential structure with three temperature profiles to its residual errors (Parent et al. 2021). The function of the exponential structure is similar to that of the AR(1) structure (Simpson et al. 2010). For the region of Grand Manan Island, an AR(1) function was found suitable for the development of AFs (Elghafghuf et al. 2020). Elghafghuf et al. (2018) remarked that high‐order autoregressive structures may be necessary to develop juvenile sea lice into adults and to produce offspring from the adults. The temperature categories for the structure of the residual errors were different in this model compared to the previous model, as the categories must be constructed from the average temperature at the lowest hierarchical level; the site‐cycle was the lowest level in the present case, whereas the segment was the lowest level in the Parent et al. (2021) study.

The repeated counts of sea lice were averaged weekly for all cages at a site, irrespective of the manager‐contributed label of a count occurring before and after treatment (i.e., pretreatment and posttreatment counts, respectively). The labels were effectively ignored, as an entire week (Monday–Sunday) was categorized as a treatment application. The consolidation of pretreatment, posttreatment, and regular weekly counts may result in reversion to the mean and may decrease the variability of the outcome and infestation pressures. Comparatively, Elghafghuf et al. (2020) were more temporally precise in identifying the treatments. They limited the mean abundance of sea lice to the counts labeled “posttreatment” if they occurred in the same week as a topical treatment.

Limitations

The present multivariable autoregressive linear mixed‐effects model was used to predict the abundance of AF L. salmonis with reasonable accuracy. This model did not account for multiple sources of variation (e.g., latent and observation errors) as was achieved with the state‐space model for Grand Manan Island (Elghafghuf et al. 2021). Like that model, our model did not include parameters for the larval stage (Elghafghuf et al. 2021). Adding predictors, such as seawater salinity and parameters that vary per larval stage, may improve prediction accuracy and may decrease prediction variability.

The estimates of the infestation pressures from this model for the years 2016–2021 differed from those previously published for 2009–2018 (Parent et al. 2021). These results highlighted the variability in the estimates of infestation pressures according to the different methodologies and data sets (e.g., Kristoffersen et al. 2014; Cantrell et al. 2019). The addition of an autocorrelation structure to the residual errors may have masked the actual effect of IIP versus EIP, making the comparison between them difficult to fully assess due to a combination of autocorrelation of counts and the “entangling” of life stages in the autocorrelation. The segmentation approach used by Parent et al. (2021) may better identify the effect of IIP versus EIP, as an estimate for IIPAF was observed from that model.

The evaluation of the time series for this study demonstrated a possible selection of cages for counts. For example, the same cage with a high sea louse abundance was counted twice, resulting in a large weekly average. This may have affected the averaging at the site and week levels and may have produced selection and information biases that diluted the observed treatment effects.

CONCLUSIONS

A mixed autoregressive model was constructed to predict the abundance of AF sea lice and estimate infestation pressures that affect the abundance of sea lice in NB, Canada. The model accounted for topical and in‐feed treatments, TAS, and BMA while modeling the abundance of AF sea lice. The model had a good level of accuracy for the prediction horizon of 4 weeks and could be considered for further development; this model may aid in decision‐making processes for the management of sea lice on salmon farms in the Bay of Fundy.

The AF abundance at a site is complex and impacted by multiple factors, including the contributions of IIP and EIP. Farmers and managers of aquaculture sites can utilize these results to better plan mitigation strategies at their sites and within BMAs. Mitigation strategies for sea lice should focus on the site while monitoring for trends in sea lice from neighboring sites to adjust and maximize the impact of mitigation.
