Iatrogenic Nerve Injuries Affecting Upper Limb Function: Medicolegal Implications
Authors: Dionysia Vasdeki, Nikolaos Stefanou, Asterios Gkougkoulias, Sokratis E. Varitimidis, Charalambos Chryssanthakis, Zoe H. Dailiana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeNerve injuries are among the commonest iatrogenic complications and most frequently are the result of direct intraoperative damage causing serious distress and long-term morbidity. Additionally, they can add a considerable financial burden to health care systems, while they constitute a well-known source of medicolegal implications. There are few published articles addressing the medicolegal consequences of the iatrogenic nerve injuries affecting upper limb function. Our aim was to identify malpractice claims related to iatrogenic nerve injuries affecting upper limb function and to define the reasons of successful litigations.MethodsThis is a retrospective study of all malpractice claims concerning iatrogenic nerve injuries related to upper limb function, which ended in a trial in a European country, within a 20-year period (2000−2019). The data were obtained from the archives of the Council of State of the country and were further analyzed to identify the number of claims related to iatrogenic nerve injuries affecting the function of the upper limb.ResultsIn total, there were 13 cases associated with the brachial plexus (6), spinal accessory (3), median (1), radial (1), and digital nerves (2). The injuries were related to upper limb or neck surgery and to vaginal deliveries, most complicated with shoulder dystocia. Eight cases closed in favor of the plaintiff, three in favor of the defendant, while two remained still open, at the time the study was conducted. The mean indemnity payment was €67.634 (€10.000−201.697), with the highest payments set for cases that required further operative reconstruction.ConclusionsNegligence claims are increasing worldwide, with a considerable impact on health systems and involved surgeons. Identifying the reasons for successful litigations will help surgeons take necessary preventive measures to minimize implications.Type of study/level of evidenceObservational IV.

Iatrogenic injuries are not rare and account for 17.4% of all surgically treated nerve injuries, while their exact incidence is difficult to be determined.1, 2, 3 Although a great percentage of these injuries are preventable, they do occur and they can result in serious distress and long-term morbidity, including neurological deficits and severe neurogenic pain.2,4,5

According to the intraoperative findings of procedures for the treatment of nerve injuries related to a previously performed surgical procedure, it is estimated that approximately 94% of these injuries are the result of direct intraoperative damage during the initial procedure. The median and the spinal accessory nerves are the most frequently affected nerves of the upper limb, with respective rates of 16% to 17% and 13%to 16%.1,5, 6, 7

Neonatal brachial plexus palsy (NBPP) was, for many years, considered a clear case of iatrogenic injury associated with medical negligence, since it was considered the result of excessive traction applied by the obstetricians or their assistants.8,9 NBBP incidence rate has been estimated between 0.05% and 0.26% of all deliveries. Most cases are transient and full recovery can be achieved in 70% to 95%, while 20% to 30% of neonates will experience long-term functional consequences.8,10, 11, 12

Iatrogenic nerve injuries add a considerable financial burden to the health care system, with the delay in their diagnosis and management contributing further to it. Additionally, they constitute a well-known source of medicolegal implications.2,4,13 Nerve injury has been described as a common reason for litigation, resulting in successful claims and in high amounts of payments to plaintiffs.14, 15, 16

Worldwide, there have been a few published articles presenting the medicolegal consequences of iatrogenic nerve injuries, while there are no published reports related to these issues in our country, located in southern Europe, with approximately 11 million inhabitants. The aim of this study was to seek the available data about malpractice claims related to iatrogenic nerve injuries, to define the reasons for filling a claim, and to evaluate the financial burden of the successful litigations in our country.

Materials and Methods

We requested data on all legal claims of negligence for hand and upper limb surgery that ended to a trial, attributed to all surgical specialties, within a 20-year period (2000−2019), from the archives of the Council of State. We further analyzed the data to identify the number of claims related to iatrogenic nerve injuries affecting the function of upper limb.

Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, School of Health Sciences, University of Thessaly (12-02-2019/16). All data were anonymized, as indicated by the General Data Protection Regulation.

The data sets used and/or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, School of Health Sciences, University of Thessaly (12-02-2019/16). No informed consent was required for the study. Data were already anonymized in the archives of the Council of State.

Results

Among all malpractice claims associated with hand and upper limb surgery that ended in a trial in the period 2000−2019 (31 cases), 13 were related to iatrogenic, nerve injuries affecting the function of the upper limb and often leading to paralysis. These injuries were related either to upper limb and neck surgery or to vaginal deliveries, complicated with shoulder dystocia (NBPP). The involved surgical specialties included orthopedic surgeons, general surgeons, and obstetricians. Six patients were neonates, and seven were adults. There were three cases related to injury of the spinal accessory nerve following a lymph node biopsy; six cases of NBPP; one case of radial nerve injury following fixation of a humeral fracture, one case of median nerve injury following carpal tunnel release, and two cases of digital nerve injuries following excision of Dupuytren disease and a giant cell tumor, respectively.

Spinal accessory nerve

There were three claims related to spinal accessory nerve injury following lymph node biopsy in the posterior triangle of the neck. All cases were performed by general surgeons. In two cases the injury of the nerve was managed conservatively, while in one case an additional surgical procedure was required for the reconstruction of the nerve, which was performed 7 months after the initial injury.

Two of the cases are still open as an expert witness’s opinion was required. Τhe third case was judged in a criminal court and a decision for 8 months’ imprisonment, suspended for 3 years, was set for the general surgeon who performed the lymph node biopsy, because of negligence (1) to identify and protect the nerve, while performing the procedure, and (2) to promptly identify the injury after surgery and refer the patient to a specialized unit for reconstruction.

Brachial plexus

There were six claims related to neonatal brachial plexus injury. Five cases were complicated by shoulder dystocia during labor, and in three cases the affected child underwent surgical procedures in specialized units.

In all cases, the plaintiffs accused the involved obstetrician of negligence, which resulted in functional and sensory impairment of the affected limb. The reasons for filing a claim were (1) failure of the obstetrician to predict the outcome of the delivery, despite the presence of risk factors, and to proceed to cesarean delivery, and (2) inappropriate or negligent employment of the maneuvers used to overcome shoulder dystocia during labor. All six cases were judged in administrative court, while in two cases the involved obstetricians were additionally judged in a criminal court, accused of causing bodily harm because of negligence.

There were four successful claims with a mean indemnity payment for the plaintiff of €104.184,19 (€12.000−€201.697), while two cases were settled for the defendant. The highest indemnity payments (€153.438 and €201.697) were set in the two cases in which further surgical procedures were performed in specialized units, to reconstruct the paralyzed limb.

The verdicts of the cases that were judged in the criminal court were in favor of the involved obstetrician in one case, and against him in the second case. In the latter case, the obstetrician was found guilty and a decision for 18 months’ imprisonment, suspended for 3 years, was set. The verdict was based on the lack of documentation to prove the presence or the participation of the accused obstetrician during the labor, which was performed by a resident, while negligence was defined as the absence during the labor and as the lack of interest by the obstetrician for such a difficult delivery.

Radial nerve

One case involved injury of the radial nerve during open fixation of a humeral fracture, which complicated an elective case of shoulder arthroplasty. The nerve injury was managed conservatively, without full recovery and resulted in patient’s long-term disability. The procedure was performed by an hand surgeon. The case was closed in favor of the plaintiff and an indemnity payment of €26.705 was set. The verdict defined negligence as the failure of the surgeon to protect the involved structures intraoperatively, which subsequently resulted in nerve injury.

Median nerve

There was one negligence claim regarding injury of the median nerve, following open carpal tunnel release. The release was performed by an hand surgeon. Because of the persistence of symptoms after surgery, a diagnosis of de Quervain’s tenosynovitis was made, and a new procedure was performed. Persistence of the symptoms can be attributed to several factors, as incomplete release, compression of the nerve at another, more proximal position or even incorrect first diagnosis.15 However, after failure of the second procedure to relieve the symptoms, an electromyographic study performed, which indicated injury of the median nerve. The patient who was a manual worker and retired prematurely because of disability, accused the hand surgeon of negligence leading to median nerve injury. The claim was successful and an indemnity payment of €20.000 was set for the patient.

Digital nerves

There were two claims related to injury of the digital nerves of the hand following excision of Dupuytren disease and excision of a giant cell tumor of a finger, respectively. Both procedures were performed by an hand surgeon. One case closed in favor of the defendant, while the second case was closed in favor of the plaintiff. Negligence was defined as failure of the surgeon to recognize and protect the involved digital nerve while performing the fasciectomy for Dupuytren disease, and an indemnity payment of €10.000 was set.

Discussion

In iatrogenic nerve injuries, direct surgical damage is described as the most common mechanism of injury, while less frequent causes involve injection/iv catheter placement, pressure, traction, compression, nerve damage from intraoperative positioning of the patient during different nonorthopedic procedures, improper supervision of residents/other staff, and radiation therapy.1,5,6 Apart from the notable morbidity for the patients and the financial burden for the health systems, these injuries constitute a considerable source of medicolegal implications.

The spinal accessory and the median nerves have been described as the most frequently affected nerves by iatrogenic damage, because of their superficial location at the posterior triangle of the neck and the carpal tunnel, respectively.1

The spinal accessory nerve innervates the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles and because of its superficial location in the posterior triangle of the neck, it is susceptible to injury during cervical lymph nodes biopsy and neck dissections.17,18 The incidence of its injury during lymph node biopsy ranges from 3% to 10%, while it can be even higher (50% to 60%) after radical neck dissection.6,19 However, up to 94% of spinal accessory nerve lesions that require treatment are iatrogenic.1,2,6

The spinal accessory nerve carries a high liability risk. Morris et al18 studied indemnity insurance cases and court trials involving surgical injury of the spinal accessory nerve. In most cases, the nerve was injured during a lymph node biopsy, while the surgeons performed the procedure were mainly general surgeons and ENT surgeons. In the majority (82%) of the indemnity insurance cases the plaintiff was compensated, with mean settlements ranging from $264.395 (pretrial settlement) to $443.538 (at trial). Surgical accident or error (98%), lack of informed consent (21%) and failure to diagnose the injury after surgery (20%) were the main reasons for filing a claim, in the cases ended to court.18 Delay in diagnosis of the lesion after surgery and subsequently limited possibility for repair was also described as a reason for litigation by Dubuisson et al2 and was also the reason for allegation in the successful claim related to the spinal accessory nerve injury in our study.

Iatrogenic injury of the median nerve has been mainly associated with carpal tunnel release (CTR), with this association being up to 80.8%.6,7 CTR is the commonest performed hand surgery procedure, constituting the gold standard for the management of carpal tunnel syndrome, with a rate of irreversible median nerve damage of 0.2% to 0.3%.20,21 The median nerve is vulnerable both in open and in endoscopic CTR.21,22 Injury of the median nerve following CTR has been described as a common cause of litigation.20,21,23,24 Ganesh Kumar et al20 reviewed 92 CTR litigation cases, of which 75% claimed surgeon’s negligence. Among the alleged injuries, injury of the median nerve was the most common (45%), which resulted in plaintiff’s indemnity in 41% of these claims.20 Similarly, Ajwani et al21 indicated median nerve injury as the main reason for litigation within 60 CTR claims in a 10-year period, with average paid damages of £55.460.

Neonatal brachial plexus palsy is the commonest neurologic injury seen in neonates.3,11,25 Its occurrence has been associated with risk factors such as shoulder dystocia, instrumental delivery, macrosomia, prolonged labor, and gestational diabetes.10,11,26 NBPP is considered an iatrogenic injury attributed to medical negligence. However, several studies suggest that some cases occurred despite the absence of difficulties during the time of delivery (uncomplicated deliveries or following a cesarean section), shifting the assumption that NBPP is a pure action of medical negligence.8,9

NBPP constitutes one of the commonest causes of medical malpractice lawsuits related to neurological birth injuries, following cerebral palsy.11 It has been estimated that for every 45,000 deliveries there is one case of litigation for NBPP.25 Factors associated with malpractice claims include parents’ perceived poor function and quality of life of their child, independent of the severity of injury, uncertainty about the affected child’s future, and parents’ perceived lack of information and support during the perinatal period.11,27 In a study of Chauhan et al. of 50 families with children affected by NBPP, 48% of them filed a claim, while the need of surgical reconstruction of the affected limb was a notable factor associated with litigation.25 A considerable percentage of these claims resulted in high monetary awards. McAbee et al. reported that 60% of lawsuits related to NBPP resulted in indemnities, which averaged $301.000, almost four times the average payout for all the other malpractice litigations during the study period.26 In our study, half of the claims referred to children who underwent further reconstructive operations in specialized units and these were the cases with the highest indemnity payments recorded. Furthermore, many allegations were supported on obstetrician’s failure to recognize preexisting risk factors predisposing to NBBP and necessitating a cesarean section. However, although there are well-known risk factors associated to NBPP, there are no models to safely predict the outcome of a delivery, while the question of how many unnecessary cesarean deliveries would be performed to prevent this risk also arises.8,12

In our study, 13 of 31 negligence claims related to hand and upper limb surgery were related to iatrogenic nerve injury affecting the function of upper limb. Negligence was defined as failure to identify and protect the nerve intraoperatively or failure to predict the outcome of a delivery and inappropriate or negligent employment of the maneuvers used to overcome shoulder dystocia at the time of delivery. Most of the claims were in favor of the plaintiff, with the highest indemnity payments set for cases which required further surgical reconstruction. The claims involved general and orthopedic surgeons, and obstetricians whose level of specialization was not always available; therefore, we cannot draw any conclusions relating the injuries with surgeon’s experience. These injuries are known complications of the respective surgical procedures and their presence does not always implicate that the surgeon was negligent. Provided that the doctor treats the patient with reasonable care, no negligence can be claimed, even if an iatrogenic injury occurs. Iatrogenic injuries are not equivalent to negligence and do not constitute an indication of incompetence.28

It is estimated that by the age of 45, 80% of surgeons will be included in at least one malpractice claim. Furthermore, most claims will be associated with common cases, as CTS release or management of hand fractures, while nerve injury has been the predominant cause of litigation.29 However, several measures can be taken to prevent the possibility of a claim related to iatrogenic nerve injury.

The final cases that ended up to court are few, but these are probably related to the population of the country in whose archives the study was conducted (approximately 11 million inhabitants) and the legal culture that characterizes it, which may be less aggressive in these claims compared to the United States, for example. However, medicolegal data sets that proceed all the way to trial are notoriously difficult to access, making even small cohorts valuable for deep-dive descriptive analysis. To strengthen the external validity of our findings, we compared our data with larger epidemiological series and national databases. We integrated a robust comparison with the massive USA national data registry as it is analyzed in the work of Krauss et al.30 We noted that similar to these large-scale series, orthopedic surgeries, management of upper limb fractures and NBPP comprise a major portion of our litigated cases. We also found that the number of incidents in relation to the time of recording and the population to which it refers is completely proportional with our study, something that confirms the frequency of incidents worldwide. It also appears from the literature that the majority of cases are related to the upper extremities (up to 85%), followed by the lower extremities and then the neck in terms of the frequency of these lesions per anatomical region. Ιt is remarkable that in our study, NBPP certainly occupies the majority among our records.5,30,31

Knowledge of surgical anatomy of the neck and upper limb and is crucial in preventing iatrogenic nerve injuries resulting to upper limb paralysis. Awareness of surgical procedures associated with a high risk for nerve injury, visual identification of these at-risk nerves before using sharp instruments or thermal devices nearby, their marking after identifying them with a vessel loop, selection of the safest proposed approaches and incisions and intraoperative nerve monitoring for high risk upper extremity revisions, providing clinical benefits and current nuanced standing in medicolegal defenses. Meticulous documentation of intraoperative findings will not prevent these injuries, but it will provide all the evidence in case of a malpractice claim.22,32 In more detail, we must specify exactly what a surgeon must document to mitigate liability—namely, explicitly noting the visual inspection and intact preservation of specific nerve structures before wound closure. The surgeon must emphasize that a primary driver of successful litigation is often delayed recognition and referral, by providing a concrete timeline recommending tertiary nerve specialist referral within 2−3 months if no clinical or electrodiagnostic recovery is evident.33, 34, 35

Communication with the patient is also of great importance. Explaining in detail to the patients their condition, its surgical management and all the possible complications and outcomes, no matter how unlike they are, can help surgeons build a strong and trustful relationship with the patients. Well-informed patients who have developed a strong relationship with their surgeons are less likely to sue for malpractice, when they will experience an outcome which was previously explained to them.29,36

One limitation of our study is that we present only the cases that ended in court, which cannot be interpreted as representative of all malpractice claims related to iatrogenic nerve injuries. In our country, there is no official authority for registration of all medical malpractice claims and therefore we are not able to present the exact number of claims filed, the number of claims rejected at an initial stage and the number of claims that were settled outside court. Moreover, a more detailed examination of the contributing factors to these injuries is necessary (technical or intraoperative errors, system-level failures, outcome potentially unavoidable) but reduced accessibility to the files was an inhibiting factor for this. All these factors introduce substantial selection bias and limit external validity.

Iatrogenic nerve injuries are common, and they result in paralysis and long-term disability. Additionally, they can add a significant financial burden to health systems, while they constitute a well-known reason for malpractice claims. Considering that there is an increasing tendency in negligence claims worldwide, with considerable impact on the health systems and the involved surgeons, it is important to identify the reasons why patients fill malpractice claims, as well the reasons of successful litigations, to improve everyday practice and minimize these implications.
