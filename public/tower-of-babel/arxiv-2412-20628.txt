Abstract
. A celebrated result of Bousquet-Mélou and Jehanne states that the bivariate power series solutions of so-called discrete differential equations , also known as polynomial equations with one catalytic variable , or simply catalytic equations , are algebraic series. We give a purely combinatorial derivation of this result in the case of order one catalytic equations , involving only one univariate unknown series. In particular, our approach provides a tool to produce context-free specifications or bijections with simple multi-type families of trees for the derivation trees of combinatorial structures that are directly governed by an order one catalytic decomposition. This provides a simple unified framework to deal with various combinatorial interpretation problems that were solved or raised over the last 50 years since the first such catalytic equation was written by W. T. Tutte in the late 60’s to enumerate rooted planar maps.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Order one catalytic equations.

We are interested in a family of functional equations that have surfaced in various places in the mathematical literature since the 60s: an
order one catalytic equation
is an equation of the form

(1)

F(t,u)=t\cdot Q\left(F(t,u),\frac{1}{u}(F(t,u)-F(t,0)),u\right),

with
Q(v,w,u)
a given series in the ring of formal power series
\mathbb{Q}[[v,w,u]]

and
F(t,u)
an unknown formal power series in

\mathbb{Q}[[t,u]]
.
Equation (
1
) is a special instance of the more general
family of
equations with one catalytic variable
thoroughly discussed in

[
4
]
, and also known as
discrete differential equations

as they are often written as above in terms of iterates of a divided difference
operator
\Delta F=\frac{1}{u}(F(t,u)-F(t,0))
in the so-called

catalytic variable

u
. These equations have gained over the
years the status of standard ingredients in enumerative combinatorics,
as discussed for instance in

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
15
,
25
]
.

The formal power series solutions of
order one catalytic equations are particularly well understood: as
discussed in
[
27
]
, it easily follows from the celebrated approach of
Bousquet-Mélou and Jehanne
[
4
]
that the univariate part

f\equiv f(t)=F(t,0)
of the solution of Equation (
1
)
admits generically the following simple univariate parametrization:

(2)

\displaystyle f\;=\;C_{\scriptstyle\square}-C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}\cdot C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},\quad\textrm{ or }\quad{\textstyle\frac{d}{dt}}f

\displaystyle=(1+C_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot Q(C_{\scriptstyle\square},C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}),

where
C_{\scriptstyle\square}\equiv C_{\scriptstyle\square}(t)
,
C_{\textstyle\bullet}\equiv C_{\textstyle\bullet}(t)
,
C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}\equiv C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}(t)
and
C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}\equiv C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}(t)
are the unique formal power series that satisfy the companion system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclcl}{C}_{\scriptstyle\square}&=&t\cdot Q({C}_{\scriptstyle\square},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}),\\
{C}_{\textstyle\bullet}&=&t\cdot(1+{C}_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot{Q}^{\prime}_{v}({C}_{\scriptstyle\square},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}),\\
{C}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}&=&t\cdot(1+{C}_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot{Q}^{\prime}_{w}({C}_{\scriptstyle\square},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}),\\
{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}&=&t\cdot(1+{C}_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot{Q}^{\prime}_{u}({C}_{\scriptstyle\square},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},{C}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}).\end{array}\right.

When
Q
is a polynomial, this parametrization immediately implies
that
f
is an algebraic series: the fact that solutions of polynomial
equations with one catalytic variable are algebraic series follows in
fact from earlier general algebraic results (
[
26
]
, see the
discussion in
[
1
, Sec. 5.3]
), but the explicit dependency
of Parametrization (
1.1
) in
Q
is remarkably appealing
from a combinatorial point of view. In particular, when
Q
is a
polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients, this
parametrization shows that the series
\frac{d}{dt}f
is

\mathbb{N}
-algebraic in the sense of
[
3
]
, and since the
first occurrences of catalytic equations in the literature, this fact
has called for combinatorial interpretations. Elegant interpretations
in terms of trees were indeed given for several special instances of
these equations (see
[
18
]
and references therein,
as well as the conclusion of the present article), based on remarkable
ad-hoc constructions building on specific properties of the underlying
combinatorial structures.

The purpose of this article, as presented in the next section, is
instead to give a generic combinatorial interpretation and derivation
of Equations (
2
) and (
1.1
) directly starting from
Equation (
1
). On the one hand this approach provides new
insights on algebraic combinatorial generating functions as discussed
by Bousquet-Mélou in
[
3
]
, and on the other hand it
yields a systematic approach to build bijections with simple
families of trees for combinatorial structures with order one
catalytic specification.

[

[

Under the same hypotheses on

2.2.
Plane maps and their spanning trees

Figure 4.
(i) A tree-rooted blossoming map with 2 incoming half-edges and 9 edges, (ii) its clockwise orientation, (iii) and its clockwise opening, which is a blossoming tree with 11 incoming and 9 outgoing half-edges.

It will prove convenient to describe our bijections graphically, in
terms of non-crossing arc systems built around plane trees, or of
planar maps endowed with spanning trees. These are standard
combinatorial concepts: the basic definitions and results we rely upon
can be found for instance in
[
24
, Chap.9]
or

[
28
, Sec. V.2 and V.3]
, but we recall here some standard definitions
and results that will be useful in the rest of the text.

A
planar map
is a proper embedding of a connected graph in the
sphere, considered up to orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of the
sphere. A
plane map
is a planar map with a distinguished face,
called the
unbounded face
. Equivalently a plane map can
be viewed as a proper embedding of a connected graph in the plane
considered up to orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of the plane,
with the distinguished face being literally the unbounded one. In
this context,
plane trees
can be described equivalently as
plane maps without cycles, plane maps with one face, or, plane maps
with one more vertices than edges. A
tree-rooted map
is a pair

(\mu,\tau)
where
\mu
is a plane map and
\tau
is a subset of the
edges of
\mu
that forms a spanning tree of its vertices, or
equivalently, such that between any two vertices of
\mu
there is a
unique simple path using only edges of
\tau
. Finally we extend
slightly the definition of plane map to allow for dangling oriented
half-edges in the unbounded face: the resulting decorated maps are
called
blossoming maps
, and in particular
blossoming
trees
when they have only one face. An example of tree-rooted
blossoming map is given in
Figure
4
(i).

The first ingredient we shall need is the
clockwise
(resp. counterclockwise) opening
of a tree-rooted map
(\mu,\tau)
,
which is the blossoming tree obtained from
(\mu,\tau)
along the following two steps:

•

First orient the edges of
\mu
that are not in

\tau
in clockwise (resp. counterclockwise) direction around
\tau

i.e.

each edge
e
is oriented so that the unique simple cycle it forms
with edges of
\tau
has the unbounded region of the plane map
\mu

on its left (resp. right) hand-side. This operation is illustrated by Figure
4
(ii).

•

Then break each (oriented) edge of
\mu\setminus\tau
into an
incoming half-edge and an outgoing half-edge. This operation is illustrated by Figure
4
(iii).

As illustrated by Figure
5
, the following
proposition is a reformulation in the language of maps of the well
known correspondence between arc diagrams and balanced parenthesis
strings, see
e.g.

[
29
, Prop. 6.1.1]
and
[
28
, Theorem V.6]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
p
,
q
and
\ell
be non-negative integers. Then clockwise (resp.
counterclockwise) opening is a vertex degree preserving bijection
between

•

tree-rooted blossoming maps with
p+1
vertices, and
p+q

edges, and
\ell
incoming half-edges in the unbounded face (but no
outgoing half-edges),

•

and blossoming trees with
p
edges, and
q
outgoing and

The inverse of clockwise (resp. counterclockwise) opening is called
the
clockwise (resp. counterclockwise) closure
. A standard way
to perform the closure, as illustrated by Figure
5
, is by iteration of local matchings: a

clockwise matching pair
of a blossoming map
\mu
is a pair

(x,y)
where
x
is an outgoing half-edge and
y
is an incoming
half-edge such that no other half-edge is met
while following the border of the unbounded face of
\mu
in clockwise
direction from
x
to

\displaystyle C_{\scriptstyle\square}=tC_{\scriptstyle\square}^{2}+\frac{2t^{2}}{1-2tC_{\scriptstyle\square}}=\frac{2t^{2}}{(1-tC_{\scriptstyle\square})(1-2tC_{\scriptstyle\square})}.

Rewiring is then a bijection between the class of closed planar

-companion trees (Theorems

As far as we know this is the first direct bijection between planar
\lambda

Rewiring also provides a bijection

\lambda

{\scriptstyle\square}
-rooted

-companion trees
(Corollary

). Equivalently, since a closed
\lambda

n

rephrased as a
1
-to-
(n+1)

\lambda

{\scriptstyle\square}
-rooted
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle f_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}[t^{3n-1}](f+f^{{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}})=\frac{1}{n+1}[t^{3n-1}]C_{\scriptstyle\square}=\frac{c_{n}}{n+1},

where the

To the best of our knowledge this is also the first direct

\lambda

\displaystyle F(u)

the necklace type generating function of

Non-negative
\mathcal{Q}

4.4.
Generic catalytic equations and weighted
\mathcal{Q}
-trees

4.4.1.
Weighted trees.

Our approach immediately extends to weighted
\mathcal{Q}
-trees:
Given a collection
(q_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{Q}}
of formal weights associated to
the vertex types in
\mathcal{Q}
, let the
weight
of a
\mathcal{Q}
-tree

\tau
be the product
q_{\tau}=\prod_{v\in\tau}q_{s(v)}
of the weights
of the types of its vertices. Then the weighted generating function

F(u)\equiv F(t,u)\in\mathbb{Q}[(q_{s})_{s}][[t,u]]

F(u)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{F}}q_{\tau}t^{|\tau|}u^{\mathrm{exc}(\tau)},

satisfies Equation (
1
)
with
Q(v,w,u)
the weighted vertex type generating function

Q(v,w,u)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{Q}}q_{s}v^{|s|_{\textstyle\bullet}}w^{|s|_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}}u^{|s|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}}.

Now all previously stated theorems and corollaries involve necklace-preserving bijections, hence the weights are taken into account in a
straightforward way and yield combinatorial versions of
Equations (
1
) and (
1.1
). The results also remain
true with non formal weights as soon as these weights are such that the
series
F(t,u)
is well defined.

4.4.2.
Generic catalytic equations.

The above weighted framework allows us to fit arbitrary catalytic
equations of the form (
1
) within our framework: assume
that

Q(v,w,u)=\sum_{i,j,k}q_{i,j,k}v^{i}w^{j}u^{k},

and that the weights
q_{i,j,k}
are such that Equation (
1
) has a formal power series solution
F(u)\equiv F(t,u)
in the variables
t
and
u
with coefficients in the same space as the weights. Then
F(u)
is the generating series of weighted non-negative
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{ord}}
-trees with
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{ord}}=\{{\textstyle\bullet}^{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}^{j}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}^{k}\mid i,j,k\geq 0\}

and weights
q_{{\textstyle\bullet}^{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}^{j}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}^{k}}=q_{i,j,k}
, and
Formulas (
1
) and (
1.1
) holds with their
interpretations in terms of weighted
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{ord}}
-companion trees.

4.4.3.
Inhomogeneous catalytic specifications.

Another application of the weighted framework is to allow to consider
more general notion of sizes, beyond the standard definition as number
of necklaces. Given a size function
\sigma:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{N}
on the
set of necklaces, let the size of a
\mathcal{Q}
-tree
\tau
be defined as the
sum of the sizes of its necklaces:

|\tau|_{\sigma}=\sum_{v\in\tau}\sigma(s(v))
. Then assuming that the
set of non-negative
\mathcal{Q}
-trees
\tau
with size
|\tau|_{\sigma}=n
is
finite for all
n\geq 0
, the corresponding gf of non-negative

\mathcal{Q}
-trees

F_{\sigma}(u)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Q}}}x^{|\tau|_{\sigma}}u^{\mathrm{exc}(\tau)}

is the unique formal series solution of the equation

(21)

\displaystyle F_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=Q_{\sigma}\left(F_{\sigma},\frac{1}{u}(F_{\sigma}-F_{\sigma}(0),u,x\right)

with

Q(v,w,u,x)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{Q}}v^{|s|_{\textstyle\bullet}}w^{|s|_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}}u^{|s|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}}x^{\sigma(s)}
,
and the gf
f
of non-negative
\mathcal{Q}
-trees with excess 0 with respect to the size is given by

(22)

\displaystyle F(0)

\displaystyle=C_{\scriptstyle\square}-C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}\cdot C{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},\qquad\textrm{or}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F(0)=(1+C_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot Q^{\prime}_{x}(C_{\scriptstyle\square},C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}},x),

where
C_{\scriptstyle\square}
,
C_{\textstyle\bullet}
,
C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}
and
C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}
are the unique solution of the equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{\scriptstyle\square}&=&Q(C_{\scriptstyle\square},C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}},x),\\
C_{\textstyle\bullet}&=&(1+C_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot Q^{\prime}_{v}(C_{\scriptstyle\square},C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}},C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}},x),\\

An example of inhomogeneous catalytic specification is the Tutte equation for rooted quasi-triangular planar maps counted
by non-root vertices and unbounded face degree

, whose gf satisfies

\displaystyle T(x,u)=xu(1+uT(x,u))^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(T(x,u)-T_{0}).

Identifying Formula (

21
) directly yields
Parametrization (
22
)-(
4.4.3

the detour of introducing an extra variable

to make the equation

4.5.
Linear catalytic equations

A catalytic equation of the form (
1
) is said to be linear
if the series
Q(v,w,u)
is linear in
v
and
w
:

Q(v,w,u)=P(u)+R(u)v+S(u)w
with
P(u)\neq 0
. Accordingly let

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
where

•

\mathcal{P}
is a non empty set of necklaces with a root
{\scriptstyle\square}
-pearl and possibly some
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}
-pearls,

•

\mathcal{R}
is a set of necklaces with a root
{\scriptstyle\square}
-pearl, a
{\textstyle\bullet}
-pearl and possibly some
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}
-pearls,

•

\mathcal{S}
is a set of necklaces with a root
{\scriptstyle\square}
-pearl, a
{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}
-pearl and possibly some
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\scriptstyle\blacktriangle}}
-pearls.

In this situation
\mathcal{Q}
-trees have a linear structure and can be
interpreted as words on the alphabet
\mathcal{Q}
. The non-negativity
condition is then similar to the standard non-negativity condition of
Dyck or more generally Łukasiewicz words, and rewiring is
essentially a variant of the standard prefix (de)coding of plane trees
by Łukasiewicz words
[
23
, Chapter 11]
or
[
29
, Chapter
6]
.

In particular, since a linear non-negative
\mathcal{Q}
-tree has exactly one necklace
v\in\mathcal{P}
, the associated unrooted
\mathcal{Q}
-companion tree can be rooted at this unique necklace
v
from
\mathcal{P}
. The root necklace decomposition then immediately yields a combinatorial derivation of the relation

(28)

\displaystyle f=t\cdot(1+C_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot P(C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}).

Moreover in view of the restricted type of
necklace occurring in
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
, the context-free
specification of Theorem
3
yields the independent subsystem
for the classes
\mathbf{C}_{\textstyle\bullet}
and
\mathbf{C}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}
,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{\textstyle\bullet}&=&t\cdot(1+C_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot R(C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}),\\
C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}&=&t\cdot(1+C_{\textstyle\bullet})\cdot S(C_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\scriptstyle\blacklozenge}}).\end{array}\right.

In particular it is noteworthy that when
P
,
R
and
S
are polynomials in
\mathbb{N}[u]
the series
f
is
\mathbb{N}
-algebraic, as opposed to only
f^{\prime}
being
\mathbb{N}
-algebraic in the non linear case.

5.
Conclusions

In many instances of enumeration result involving catalytic equations,
e.g.

[
6
,
7
,
8
,
14
,
17
,
20
,
30
,
31
]
,
Equation (
1
) arises from the direct translation for the gf

F(t,u)
of a catalytic specification
i.e.
, a combinatorial
recursive decomposition that can be put in the form
(
8
), for a bigraded combinatorial class
\mathcal{F}

whose objects
\gamma
are equipped with an additive size parameter

|\gamma|
with non-negative increments (marked by
t
), and an
additive catalytic parameter
c(\gamma)
with signed increments but a
non-negativity constraint (marked by
u
): the series
f=F(t,0)
is
the gf of the subclass
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{F}\mid c(\gamma)=0\}
and
\frac{d}{dt}f
is a gf for marked

\mathcal{F}_{0}
-structures.
Following the Schützenberger methodology as described for instance
in
[
3
]
, the fact that
\frac{d}{dt}f
can be expressed
positively in terms of the solutions of System (
1.1
) raises
the question of giving a context-free specification of the
form (
3
) directly for marked
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-structures.
To answer this question some knowledge of the actual recursive
decomposition of the
\mathcal{F}
-structures is needed, which is
typically encoded by a family of
derivation trees
describing
the way the recursion unfolds.

The strength of our model of non-negative

\mathcal{Q}
-trees is that it includes naturally many (most?) of the
derivation trees associated to first order catalytic decompositions in
the literature. As a consequence, our result can be considered as a generic recipe to
convert a catalytic specification governed by
Equation (
1
) into a bijection between the associated
derivation trees for
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and simple varieties of
multi-type trees governed by
Equations (
2
)–(
1.1
). Depending on the actual
relation between the underlying combinatorial structures and their
derivation trees, this can then also lead to a direct context-free
specification of the marked
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-structures counted by

\frac{d}{dt}f
.

A natural follow-up of this work is to make explicit these direct
context-free specifications and bijections with simple varieties of
trees that can be derived from our result for the various above
mentioned families of combinatorial structures governed by order-one
catalytic equations. The only results of this type we are aware of are
for planar maps, with the notable exception of
[
18
]
.
The search for context-free specifications for planar
maps can be traced back to early work of Cori prompted by
Schützenberger
[
12
,
13
]
and has
led to a long and rich series of work
[
28
]
with
ongoing offsprings
[
5
]
. Hopefully the
extension of these ideas to arbitrary structures governed by order one
catalytic equations that we propose here can lead to further
interesting developments.
