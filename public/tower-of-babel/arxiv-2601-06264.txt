Graph structure learning for stable processes
Florian Brück, Sebastian Engelke, Stanislav Volgushev

Abstract
We introduce Ising–Hüsler–Reiss processes, a new class of multivariate Lévy processes that allows for sparse modeling of the path-wise conditional independence structure between marginal stable processes with different stability indices. The underlying conditional independence graph is encoded as zeroes in a suitable precision matrix. An Ising-type parametrization of the weights for each orthant of the Lévy measure allows for data-driven modeling of asymmetry of the jumps while retaining an arbitrary sparse graph. We develop consistent estimators for the graphical structure and asymmetry parameters, relying on a new uniform small-time approximation for Lévy processes. The methodology is illustrated in simulations and a real data application to modeling dependence of stock returns.

Introduction

A Lévy process
\mathbf{L}=(\mathbf{L}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued stochastic process with independent and stationary increments that satisfies
\mathbf{L}(0)=\mathbf{0}
almost surely.
Besides being fundamental objects appearing naturally in applied probability theory, their statistical applications range from finance
(
39
)
over physics
(
28
)
and queuing theory
(
6
)
to ruin theory in insurance
(
2
)
; see
24
for details and more references.
By the famous Lévy–Itô decomposition, a Lévy process can be written as
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{J}
, the sum a Brownian motion
\mathbf{B}
with drift and a jump process
\mathbf{J}
. While the Brownian part is very well understood and can be modeled
parametrically through the covariance matrix, the jump part is
characterized by a potentially infinite Borel measure
\Lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
called the Lévy measure.
The latter describes the size distribution of the jumps of the Lévy process and whether
these jumps occur simultaneously in the different marginal processes.

The dependence structure of the jumps can be very complex,
especially since the Lévy measure is allowed to have mass on all

2^{d}
orthants of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We enumerate the orthants in a binary way where each orthant is associated to one element of
\{-1,1\}^{d}=:\mathcal{O}
, with the convention that
o_{i}=1
means that the
i
th component of vectors in orthant
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
is non-negative. Figure
1
shows the paths and the jumps of a two-dimensional Lévy process whose Lévy measure has mass on
the four orthants.

Fig 1:
Simulation of a two-dimensional Ising–Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process with symmetric (left) and asymmetric (center) orthant weights. The corresponding jumps (right) of the Lévy measure
are shown for both the symmetric (blue) and asymmetric (green) process.

Several works address the problem of
characterizing dependence in Lévy measures, including the
notion of Lévy copulas
(
21
)
and Pareto Lévy measures
(
8
)
.
Statistical models for jump processes resulting from these and other approaches in the literature are however either
simplistic with very few parameters, spectrally discrete

(
30
)
, or they are tractable only in very low dimensions
d
.

In classical statistics, moving to higher dimensions requires the concept of
sparsity to build parsimonious models
(
40
)
. Conditional independence and graphical models are the most commonly used tools for this purpose
(
25
)
.
For sparse modeling of data from higher-dimensional stochastic processes,
11
introduce Lévy graphical models as a Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
that satisfies the pairwise Markov property on a graph
G=(V,E)
with nodes
V=\{1,\dots,d\}
and edge set
E\subseteq V\times V
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(i,j)\notin E\quad\Rightarrow\quad L_{i}\perp L_{j}\mid\mathbf{L}_{V\setminus\{i,j\}},

(1.1)

where the conditional independence is to be understood on the level
of the sample paths.
They show that
the two components
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{J}
in the Lévy–Itô decomposition can be studied separately with regard
to conditional independence properties, and we focus
on the less-understood jump part. In the sequel, we therefore assume that there is no Brownian component and that
\mathbf{L}

is a pure jump process.
In this case, the conditional independence in (
1.1
) is characterized by the factorization
of the Lévy measure density into lower-dimensional terms—a property that is crucial for parsimonious models.

In this work, we introduce a Lévy process analog of Gaussian graphical models, the most widely used class of probabilistic graphical models
(
16
;
26
)
. Our model allows for flexible modeling of both, dependence within each orthant and asymmetry across different orthants.
Importantly, it turns out to be well-adapted to graphical structures
in the sense of (
1.1
).
The model is inspired by the popular Hüsler–Reiss
distribution in extreme value theory
(
19
)
, which can be parameterized by a variogram matrix
\Gamma
or, equivalently, by its positive semi-definite precision matrix
\Theta

(
18
)
.
While the classical Hüsler–Reiss exponent measure
\Lambda_{\text{HR}}
is a measure on the positive orthant only, we extend it
to a flexible Lévy measure on any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
by

\displaystyle\Lambda(A)=\sum_{{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}}\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}\Lambda_{\text{HR}}\left(\{\mathbf{x}\in(0,\infty)^{d}:(o_{i}x_{i})_{i\in V}\in A\}\right),

(1.2)

which can possibly have mass on all orthants; here
\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}\geq 0
is the weight for orthant
{\mathbf{o}}
.

In the symmetric case where all weights
\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}
are equal,
we can introduce sparsity on a graph
G
in this model class through the Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix: the conditional independence (
1.1
) holds if and only if
\Theta_{ij}=0

(
9
)
.
For more general, asymmetric models, the orthant weights must additionally satisfy certain constraints
in order to be compatible with the graphical structure
G
.
We propose a new approach to obtain such weights based on
the Ising model from statistical physics
(
20
)
.
In fact, any parameter vector
\Psi=(\psi_{i,j}:(i,j)\in E)
with
\psi_{i,j}=\psi_{j,i}\in\mathbb{R},(i,j)\in E
,
defines a valid set of orthant weights through

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}(\Psi):=\frac{2}{C(\Psi)}\exp\Bigg\{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\psi_{i,j}o_{i}o_{j}\Bigg\},

where
C(\Psi)>0
is a normalization constant.
The corresponding Lévy process with Lévy measure (
1.2
) is then indeed a graphical model on
G
.
This Ising model approach allows to model asymmetries in
the dependence structure of the jumps of the Lévy process in a
simple yet flexible way. Interestingly, it turns out that for
a tree, i.e., a connected graph without cycles, this
model class coincides with all possible orthant weights. For more general graphs, it is a large but strict sub-class of
all weight combinations.
The graphical model approach thus serves two purposes in this Ising–Hüsler–Reiss model class. On the one hand, it allows flexible and parsimonious modeling of the jump dependence encoded in
\Lambda_{\text{HR}}
. On the other hand, it provides a sparse and data-driven way to define valid orthant weights; indeed, note that we only have to specify the
|E|\leq{d(d-1)/2}
different parameters in
\Psi
instead of all
2^{d}
possible weights. While we apply the Ising–Hüsler–Reiss model in the context of Lévy processes, it can directly be used as a model for multivariate Pareto distributions in extreme value theory
(
32
;
23
)
, which allows for dependence between extreme positive and negative values as encountered in financial applications
(
29
)
.

Statistical inference for our model consists of two steps: estimation of the Hüsler–Reiss parameters including the graphical
structure; and estimation of the Ising parameters
\Psi
on the
estimated graph.
We first derive an estimator
\widehat{\Gamma}
of the Hüsler–Reiss parameter matrix
\Gamma
and show its consistency.
A crucial ingredient for this proof, and a key theoretical contribution of our work is a uniform
small-time approximation of the Lévy measure

\Big|P\{\mathbf{L}(t)\in R\}-t\Lambda(R)\Big|\leq Ct^{2},

for small enough
t>0
, a constant
C>0
and suitable rectangular sets
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
This approximation holds for general Lévy processes under certain regularity assumptions, and is therefore of independent interest for studying estimators for jump processes.
It is a strengthening and correction of a result in
4
,
where our assumptions allow for Lévy processes with mass on all orthants (as opposed to only the positive orthant), more general sets
A
and arbitrary dimensions
d
.
We show that this approximation holds
for the Ising–Hüsler–Reiss model with Lévy measure (
1.2
).
The consistent estimator
\widehat{\Gamma}
can directly
be used with known structure learning methods
(
12
)
to obtain
a consistent
estimator
\widehat{G}=(V,\widehat{E})
of the underlying graph structure.

In the second step, we study the weights
\Psi
of the Ising
parameters on the estimated graph.
While we do not have access to observations from the associated Ising model directly, we show that they can be substituted
using a moment matching method to obtain a consistent estimator
\widehat{\Psi}
. Putting both steps together, our methodology enables learning complex, sparse dependence structures for Lévy processes.
We illustrate the effectiveness of this approach in simulations and an application to modeling the dependence of stock returns of 16 American companies.

Background

We first introduce some notation.
Throughout, we let
V=\{1,\dots,d\}
denote
the index set of the
d
marginal processes. For integers
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the abbreviated notation
[m]:=\{1,\dots,m\}
. Vectors are always denoted in bold letters
\mathbf{x}
and random objects are capitalized
as
\mathbf{X}
. For a subset
I\subset V
we write for the
I
-th margin of a vector
\mathbf{x}_{I}:=\left(x_{I}\right)_{i\in I}
, and use the abbreviation
\mathbf{x}_{\setminus I}=\mathbf{x}_{V\setminus I}
with similar notation for matrices. For an orthant
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
as defined in the introduction and a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in a slight abuse of notation, we
write
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbf{o}}
if
\mathbf{x}\in\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})={\mathbf{o}}\}
, where the orthant function is
defined as
{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})=\left(1-2\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}<0\}}\right)_{i\in V}
, i.e., the vector of sign functions of the components.
The norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|
denotes the Euclidean
L_{2}
-norm if not otherwise stated. We define
B_{\infty}(\delta)=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}<\delta\}
. For a set
A
of finite cardinality we use
|A|
to denote the cardinality of
A
. For two real-valued sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1},(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
we write
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
if
b_{n}/a_{n}\to 0
and
a_{n}\ll b_{n}
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 0
.

2.1
Lévy processes

A
d
-dimensional Lévy process
\mathbf{L}:=(\mathbf{L}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a càdlàg stochastic process with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that possesses stationary and independent increments; see
35
for details.
The famous Lévy–Khintchine formula states that the characteristic functions of this process is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}\mathbf{L}(t)}\right]=\exp\left\{it\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}\bm{\tau}+t\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}\Sigma\mathbf{z}/2+t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}}e^{i\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}}-1-i\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{1}_{\{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq 1\}}\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\right\},

where
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrix, and the Lévy measure
\Lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
possibly explodes at the origin but always satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}}\min\{1,\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\}\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})<\infty.

The Lévy process is then said to have characteristic triplet
(\bm{\tau},\Sigma,\Lambda)
.
For a subset
I\subset V
, the marginal process
\mathbf{L}_{I}=\{(\mathbf{L}(t))_{I}\}_{t\geq 0}

is again a Lévy process with characteristic triplet
(\bm{\tau}^{\prime}_{I},\Sigma_{I},\Lambda_{I})
,
where
\bm{\tau}^{\prime}_{I}
is not simply the sub-vector of
\bm{\tau}
but can depend on other quantities. The marginal Lévy measure
\Lambda_{I}
is obtained in the usual way
by integrating out the components in
V\setminus I
and removing
\{0\}
from its support. Whenever the Lévy measure has a Lebesgue density
\lambda
, i.e.,
\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
, we call
\lambda
a Lévy density.

The Lévy–Itô decomposition shows that we can decompose the Lévy process as

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{J},

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{W}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion with covariance matrix
\Sigma
and drift
\bm{\tau}
, and
\mathbf{J}=(\mathbf{J}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a pure jump Lévy process with characteristic triplet
(0,0,\Lambda)
, which is independent of
\mathbf{W}
.
This decomposition often allows to analyze properties of
\mathbf{L}
separately for
\mathbf{J}
and
\mathbf{W}
. We say that
\mathbf{L}
is a non-Gaussian Lévy process whenever
\Sigma=0
, i.e., there is no Brownian part.

The behavior of a non-Gaussian Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
is fully encoded in
\Lambda
, which contains information about both the behavior of the marginal processes
L_{i}
,
i\in V
, and their dependence. Similarly to a copula approach in multivariate distributions, it is possible to separate the marginal and dependence structures via a so-called Pareto Lévy measure (PLM)
(
8
)
; a closely related concept are Lévy copulas introduced in
21
.
Formally, a PLM is any Lévy measure
\Lambda^{\star}
that satisfies the marginal constraints

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\star}(x_{i}>z)=\Lambda^{\star}(x_{i}<-z)=z^{-1},\quad z>0,~i\in V.

(2.2)

It can be seen as a standardized version of a Lévy measure whose marginal processes correspond to
1
-stable processes.

Every PLM
\Lambda^{\star}
together with a set of univariate tail integrals
(U_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
uniquely defines a Lévy measure, see
8
. Conversely, if the closure of the range of each univariate tail integral
U_{i}
of a Lévy measure
\Lambda
is
\mathbb{R}
, there is a unique PLM
\Lambda^{\star}
that corresponds to
\Lambda

(
8
, Theorem 1(i))
. This is an analog of Sklar’s copula theorem in the context of possibly infinite Lévy measures. In the sequel, we therefore often focus on the normalized measure
\Lambda^{\star}
to discuss dependence properties.

For the models in this paper, we exclude compound Poisson processes and assume that each marginal process
L_{i}
has infinite activity, i.e.,
\Lambda(\{\mathbf{x}\mid x_{i}\neq 0\})=\infty
for all
i\in V
. This implies that the marginal distribution function
F_{ti}
of
i
th process is continuous for all times
t>0

(
35
, Theorem 27.4)
.
Consequently, the copula
C_{t}
of the random vector
\mathbf{L}(t)
is unique.

21
show that the PLM can be approximated by small-time behaviour of the survival copulas by

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\star}(\mathbf{y}:y_{i}\geq x_{i}:i\in V)=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-1}P\{F_{ti}(L_{i}(t))>1-t/x_{i}:i\in V\},\quad\mathbf{x}\geq 0.

(2.3)

Similar approximation can be obtained for sets in other orthants.
This result enables statistical inference since it connects the dependence properties of the Lévy measure to observations of the process itself.

Example 2.1
.

A PLM
\Lambda^{\star}
is called stable if it satisfies

\Lambda^{\star}(tA)=t^{-1}\Lambda^{\star}(A),\quad t>0,A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

It is a Lévy measure of a multivariate
1
-stable process, i.e., a non-Gaussian Lévy process for which
\mathbf{L}(t)
has the same distribution as
t\mathbf{L}(1)+\mathbf{b}(t)
for some deterministic function
\mathbf{b}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
t>0
.

There are several parametric models for PLMs
that allow to fit flexible models to data.
The most relevant example for us is the Hüsler–Reiss
model, which was introduced in
19

in the context of extreme value modeling.

Example 2.2
.

The symmetric Hüsler–Reiss PLM
\Lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}

is defined by a symmetric, strictly
conditionally negative definite
d\times d
-matrix
\Gamma
. The model can alternatively be parameterized by its positive semi-definite precision
matrix
\Theta=(P(-\Gamma/2)P)^{+}
,
where
P=\text{Id}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/d
is the projection matrix onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{1}
, and
A^{+}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of a matrix
A

(
18
)
; note that the one-vector
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)
is in the kernel of
\Theta
.
The density of the Hüsler–Reiss Pareto Lévy measure on the standardized scale is

\displaystyle\lambda_{(\text{sym})}^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=c_{\Theta}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\log(|\mathbf{x}|)^{\intercal}\Theta\log(|\mathbf{x}|)+\log(|\mathbf{x}|)^{\intercal}r_{\Theta}\right\}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{|x|_{i}^{1+1/d}},\quad\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})^{d},

(2.4)

with
r_{\Theta}=-\Theta\Gamma\mathbf{1}/(2d)
and a normalizing constant
c_{\Theta}
such that
\int_{\{|x_{i}|>1\}}\lambda_{(\text{sym})}^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=2
for all
i\in V
. The subscript indicates that this is a symmetric Lévy measure with the same density on all orthants.
The corresponding PLM
\Lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}
is stable
as defined in Example
2.1
and satisfies the marginal constraint (
2.2
).

2.2
Lévy graphical models

A graph
G=(V,E)
is a collection of vertices
V=\{1,\ldots,d\}
and edges
E\subseteq V\times V
. We work with undirected models in which
(i,j)\in E
whenever
(j,i)\in E
. Moreover, we say that there is a path between
i
and
j
if there is a sequence of edges
(i_{k},j_{k})
such that
i_{1}=i
,
j_{n}=j
and
i_{k}=j_{k-1}
for all
2\leq k\leq n
. A graph is connected if there is a path between every two nodes.
A classical probabilistic graphical model for a random vector
\mathbf{X}
links a graph
G
to conditional independence statements through the pairwise Markov property, i.e., whenever
(i,j)\not\in E
then
X_{i}\perp X_{j}\mid\mathbf{X}_{V\setminus\{i,j\}}

(
25
)
.

The traditional notion of conditional independence of random vectors
can be extended to stochastic processes in several ways
(
7
;
31
;
27
)
.
For a Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
with characteristic triplet
(\bm{\tau},\Sigma,\Lambda)
, here we work with a notion on the level of sample-paths and write
for sets
A,B,C\subset V

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{A}\perp\mathbf{L}_{B}\mid\mathbf{L}_{C}

(2.5)

if the (path-valued) random variables
\mathbf{L}_{A}
and
\mathbf{L}_{B}
are independent given
\mathbf{L}_{C}
.

11
show that the Lévy–Ito decomposition in (
2.1
) naturally extends to this notion since

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{A}\perp\mathbf{L}_{B}\mid\mathbf{L}_{C}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{W}_{A}\perp\mathbf{W}_{B}\mid\mathbf{W}_{C}\text{ and }\mathbf{J}_{A}\perp\mathbf{J}_{B}\mid\mathbf{J}_{C},

which allows to separately analyze conditional interdependence statements for the Gaussian part and the jump part.
While the conditional independence statement for the Gaussian part is well-understood in terms of zeros on the precision matrix
\Sigma^{-1}

(
11
)
, the corresponding property of the jump part has only recently been studied.

Since the Lévy measure
\Lambda
characterizes the jump part
\mathbf{J}
,
it must also contain information about the conditional independence
structure. Since this measure is generally not finite and supported on the non-product space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, classical
notions of independence do not apply.
10
therefore define an abstract notion of conditional independence for the Lévy measure through classical conditional independence of random vectors
with distribution induced by
\Lambda
restricted to sets
with finite mass.
More precisely, for every product set
R=\times_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}
bounded away from the origin
\mathbf{0}
with Borel sets
R_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, associate a random vector
\mathbf{Z}^{R}\overset{d}{=}\Lambda(\cdot\cap R)/\Lambda(R)
.
Conditional independence for the Lévy measure
\Lambda
is then defined for any subsets
A,B,C\subset V
as

\displaystyle A\perp_{\Lambda}B\mid C~~:\Leftrightarrow~~\mathbf{Z}^{R}_{A}\perp\mathbf{Z}^{R}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Z}^{R}_{C}\text{ for all product sets }R\text{ bounded away from }\bm{0}\text{ s.t.\ }\Lambda(R)>0.

(2.6)

This conditional independence notion enjoys many natural properties and
is equivalent to density factorizations, whenever densities exist.
Most importantly for our work, it turns out that under mild regularity conditions, this
\Lambda
-conditional independence is equivalent to the corresponding statement

\mathbf{J}_{A}\perp\mathbf{J}_{B}\mid\mathbf{J}_{C}
on the sample-path level of the jump process.
This equivalence allows us to rely on simple properties of the Lévy measure
\Lambda
to study graphical models for Lévy processes.
Importantly, the conditional independence properties (
2.6
)
are independent of the marginal processes and only depend on the corresponding
PLM
\Lambda^{\star}

(
11
, Prop. 4.4)
.

Based on the conditional independence notion (
2.6
)
for the Lévy measure,
11

introduce Lévy graphical models as a tool to model stochastic processes with sparse dependence structures.
For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
with characteristic triplet
(\bm{\tau},\Sigma,\Lambda)
is a Lévy graphical model if it satisfies the pairwise Markov property on
G
with respect
respect to the notion (
2.6
), i.e.,

(i,j)\notin E\quad\Rightarrow\quad\{i\}\perp_{\Lambda}\{j\}\mid V\setminus\{i,j\},

or equivalently,
L_{i}\perp L_{j}\mid\mathbf{L}_{V\setminus\{i,j\}}
.

11
study the special case of tree
graphs
G
, which only have
d-1
edges and are the sparsest type of connected
graphs. While this allows very efficient inference and structure estimation, trees are often too simplistic for more complex dependence
structures. In the next section, we introduce a sparse Lévy graphical model for general graphs, which uses the following fundamental property of the Hüsler–Reiss model from Example
2.2
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a Hüsler–Reiss PLM with precision matrix
\Theta
whose Lévy measure
\Lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}
is defined through its density in (
2.4
).
We can then identify conditional independence statements
from zero entries in the precision matrix
(
9
)
, i.e.,

\{i\}\perp_{\Lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}}\{j\}\mid V\setminus\{i,j\}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Theta_{ij}=0.

Informally, this holds because factorization of the density
\lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}
is equivalent to its factorization into identical factors on each orthant. A formal proof is in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

3
Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process and Ising asymmetry

3.1
Hüsler–Reiss Lévy processes

The Hüsler–Reiss PLM introduced in Example
2.2
with precision matrix
\Theta
is symmetric around the origin and the jumps of the associated Lévy process inherit a symmetric dependence structure; see the left panel in Figure
1
. This property is too restrictive for most applications and we therefore introduce
an asymmetric version of this model that may have different weights
\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}\in[0,1]
for each orthant
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
. A higher weight implies that the jumps of the Lévy process lie more frequently in this orthant.
This yields an asymmetric Hüsler–Reiss PLM with density

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}(\mathbf{x}):=\lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}(\mathbf{x})\sum_{{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}}\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbf{o}}\}},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},

(3.1)

where
\lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}
is as in (
2.4
) and the weights must satisfy the constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O},o_{i}=a}\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}=1,\quad\text{for all }i\in V,a\in\{-1,1\},

(3.2)

so that the marginal constraint (
2.2
) of a PLM is satisfied. The symmetric case correspond to the choice
\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}=2^{-d+1}
for all
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda^{\star}
be an asymmetric Hüsler–Reiss PLM (
3.1
) with parameter matrix
\Gamma
, precision matrix
\Theta
and asymmetry parameters
{{\bm{\gamma}}}=(\gamma_{\mathbf{o}})_{{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}}
.
For parameters
c_{i}^{\pm}>0
and
\alpha_{i}\in(0,2)
, we set the marginal integrals to

\displaystyle U_{i}(x)=c_{i}^{+}x^{-\alpha_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x>0\}}-c_{i}^{-}|x|^{-\alpha_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x<0\}},\ x\neq 0.

This uniquely defines a Lévy measure
\Lambda=\Lambda^{({{\bm{\alpha}}},{{\mathbf{c}}},{{\bm{\gamma}}},\Theta)}
with marginal tail integrals
U_{i}
and
PLM
\Lambda^{\star}
. The corresponding Lévy process
\mathbf{L}

with Lévy triplet
(\bm{\tau},0,\Lambda)
is called the Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process with drift
\bm{\tau}
, dependence parameters
\Theta
and
{{\bm{\gamma}}}
and marginal parameters
{{\mathbf{c}}}=(c_{i}^{-},c_{i}^{+})_{1\leq i\leq d}
and
{{\bm{\alpha}}}=(\alpha_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
.

The next result shows that the class of Hüsler–Reiss Lévy processes is closed under scaling, translation and marginalization, showing that its definition is natural.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
denote an Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process as in Definition
3.1
with drift
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, parameter matrix
\Gamma
and precision
\Theta
, and asymmetry parameters
{{\bm{\gamma}}}
. Let
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathbf{a}\neq\bm{0}
and denote
I:=\{i\in V\mid a_{i}=0\}
. Then
\big((a_{i}L_{i}(t)+tb_{i})_{i\not\in I}\big)_{t\geq 0}
is a
(d-|I|)
-dimensional Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process with parameter matrix
\Gamma_{\setminus I,\setminus I}
.

In general, a Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
is not self-similar in the sense
(\mathbf{L}(at))_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}(a^{H}\mathbf{L}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. In fact, the latter only holds
in this model class if
{{\bm{\alpha}}}=(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
We can however show that all Hüsler–Reiss Lévy processes
satisfy a generalized version of self-similarity, which will be crucial for estimation of their parameters. Here, we present a simplified version of this statement, a more general result can be found in Theorem
A.1
in the Appendix. There, we also provide an explicit expression for
\mathbf{b}(\cdot)
which depends on the parameters
\Theta,\bm{\alpha},\bm{c}
of the process
\mathbf{L}
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
denote a Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process as in Definition
3.1
with drift
\bm{\tau}=\bm{0}
. Then, for any
t>0

\mathbf{L}(t)\overset{d}{=}\left(t^{1/\alpha_{i}}L_{i}(1)+b_{i}(t)\right)_{i\in V},

for some deterministic
\mathbf{b}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

An important implication of this result is that the copula of
\mathbf{L}(t)
does not depend on
t
, which we will later exploit in our estimation procedures.

3.2
Ising asymmetry

The Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
in Definition
3.1
is an ideal candidate to use the concepts of sparsity and graphical modeling in the framework of Lévy processes. Indeed, since conditional independence properties are fully
determined by the PLM regardless of the marginal processes, it follows from Example
2.3
that in the symmetric case

\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}=2^{-d+1}
,
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
, the graph
structure of a Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process is encoded
in the zero pattern of its precision matrix. More precisely, for an undirected graph
G:=(V,E)
, we have that

L_{i}\perp L_{j}\mid\mathbf{L}_{V\setminus\{i,j\}},\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Theta_{i,j}=0.

This property does no longer hold for
general, asymmetric Hüsler–Reiss Lévy processes.
In this case, a zero in the precision matrix
is no longer sufficient to imply a factorization of the Lévy density according to the corresponding graph as the following example shows.

Example 3.1
.

Let
d=3
and consider a Hüsler–Reiss PLM as in (
3.1
) with precision matrix
\Theta
;
assume that the only
0
-entry of
\Theta
is given by
\Theta_{1,3}=
0. By Example
2.2
, a possible graph
T=(V,E)
for the symmetric version
\lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}
as in (
2.4
) of this model is a tree with edge set
E=\{(1,2),(2,3)\}
. For
m_{1,3},m_{2,3}\in(0,1)
, define the weights

\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}:=[m_{1,3}\mathbf{1}_{\{o_{1}o_{3}>0\}}+(1-m_{1,3})\mathbf{1}_{\{o_{1}o_{3}<0\}}][m_{2,3}\mathbf{1}_{\{o_{2}o_{3}>0\}}+(1-m_{2,3})\mathbf{1}_{\{o_{2}o_{3}<0\}}],~{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O},

which can be checked to satisfy condition (
3.2
).
However, it is shown in Appendix
C.7
that the corresponding Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process in Definition
3.1
with those weights does not
satisfy the conditional independence
L_{1}\perp L_{3}\mid L_{2}

for any
m_{1,3}\neq 1/2
.

The issue in the above example (and more generally) arises from the fact that even though the Lévy density

\lambda^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda^{\star}_{(\text{sym})}{{\bm{\gamma}}}_{{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})}
factorizes according to
G
on every separate orthant
(
9
)
, we cannot extend this factorization to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. The reason is that the weights
\gamma_{{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})}
, seen as a function of
\mathbf{x}
, do not factorize according on
G
.
Thus, all we know in the case
\Theta_{i,j}=0
is that

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{V\setminus j})g(\mathbf{x}_{V\setminus i})\gamma_{{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})},

for some functions
f
and
g
.
In order to have conditional independence
L_{i}\perp L_{j}\mid\mathbf{L}_{V\setminus\{i,j\}}
it is necessary
that also
\gamma_{{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})}
factorizes according to
G
, i.e., that
\gamma_{{\mathbf{o}}(\mathbf{x})}=k(\mathbf{x}_{V\setminus j})l(\mathbf{x}_{V\setminus i})
for some functions
k
and
l
whenever
\Theta_{i,j}=0
.

The key idea to construct such graphical weight functions is to build a bridge to Rademacher distributions and Ising models.
A multivariate Rademacher vector
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{d})
takes values in
\{-1,1\}^{d}=\mathcal{O}
and the marginals satisfy
P(B_{i}=1)=P(B_{i}=-1)=1/2
. There is a one-to-one correspondence
between distributions for
\mathbf{B}
and valid orthant weights
that satisfy (
3.2
), which is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}=2P\left(\mathbf{B}={\mathbf{o}}\right).

(3.3)

Moreover, every distribution on
\mathbf{B}
that factorizes w.r.t. some
\Theta
results in a
{{\bm{\gamma}}}
which is compatible with the graph
G
.

Describing all possible distributions for
\mathbf{B}
that are compatible with
\Theta
is difficult. We therefore restrict our attention to a subclass of distributions that is tractable and yet flexible enough to model complex dependence between the univariate discrete margins. The Ising model originated in statistical mechanics
(
20
)
and is the most popular
probabilistic model for observations in
\{-1,1\}^{d}
.
It is a pairwise interaction model with probability mass function

P(\mathbf{B}={\mathbf{o}}):=\frac{1}{C(\Psi)}\exp\left(\sum_{i\in V}\psi_{i}o_{i}+\sum_{i,j\in V}\psi_{i,j}o_{i}o_{j}\right),\quad o\in\mathcal{O},

where
\Psi
contains all marginal and dependence parameters
\psi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\in V
, and
\psi_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i,j\in V
, respectively. The constant
C(\Psi)>0
normalizes the mass of the distribution to one.
In order to obtain a standardized distribution for
\mathbf{B}
that satisfies
P(B_{i}=1)=P(B_{i}=-1)=1/2,i\in V
it is well known that we
need to set
\psi_{i}=0
for all
i\in V
. Furthermore, a direct computation shows that a factorization
P(\mathbf{B}={\mathbf{o}})=p({\mathbf{o}}_{V\setminus j})q({\mathbf{o}}_{V\setminus j})

for suitable functions
p
and
q
is equivalent to
\psi_{i,j}=0
.

In the sequel, we assume that all marginal parameters satisfy
\psi_{i}=0
,
i\in V
, and then drop them in
\Psi
. We then always consider the parameters of an Ising model as symmetric matrix
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with entries
\psi_{i,j}
that satisfy
\psi_{i,i}=0
.
According to (
3.3
), we can thus define a vector of weights
{{\bm{\gamma}}}(\Psi)
that is compatible with a given
\Theta
and the corresponding graph
G=(V,E)
, by setting
\psi_{i,j}=0
for all
(i,j)\notin E
and

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}(\Psi):=\frac{2}{C(\Psi)}\exp\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\psi_{i,j}o_{i}o_{j}\right),\quad\psi_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R},(i,j)\in E

(3.4)

We refer to such weights as
Ising weights
and to the corresponding Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
constructed via Ising weights as
Ising–Hüsler–Reiss (IHR) process
. It can be checked that the class of IHR processes is closed under scaling and translation. However, their multivariate marginal distributions are generally only Hüsler–Reiss Lévy processes but not IHR processes, since the Ising models are not closed under marginalization.

We have the desirable property that conditional independence statements of the Hüsler–Reiss Lévy processes are entirely encoded in
\Theta
and
\Psi
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For an IHR process
\mathbf{L}
with Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix
\Theta
and Ising weights
\Psi
we have

L_{i}\perp L_{j}\mid\mathbf{L}_{V\setminus\{i,j\}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\big(\Theta_{i,j}=0~~~\mbox{and}~~~\psi_{i,j}=0\big).

Remark 3.1
.

Suppose that the graph
T=(V,E)
is
a tree, i.e., a connected graph without
cycles.
Further let
\mathbf{L}
be an IHR process
with Hüsler–Reiss parameter matrix
\Gamma
, precision
\Theta
and Ising weights
\Psi
satisfying
the global Markov property on the tree
T
.
As shown in
14
, the tree structure induces a particularly simple form of the
parameter matrix, namely it is a tree metric

\Gamma_{i,j}=\sum_{(k,l)\in\text{path}(i,j)}\Gamma_{k,l},

where
\text{path}(i,j)
denotes all edges on the undirected path from
i
to
j
.

The Ising weight of orthant
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
in (
3.4
) also takes a special form for trees, namely

\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}(\Psi)=\prod_{(i,j)\in E}{m}_{i,j}^{\mathbf{1}\{o_{i}o_{j}>0\}}(1-{m}_{i,j})^{\mathbf{1}\{o_{i}o_{j}<0\}},\quad{m}_{i,j}=\frac{\exp(2\psi_{i,j})}{1+\exp(2\psi_{i,j})}\in(0,1).

Interestingly, this form of weights represents
all

valid orthant weights in the sense of (
3.2
) in the case of tree graphical models; see
11
.
Therefore, Ising weights can be seen as a natural generalization of tree orthant weights for Lévy processes on general graphs; note that in the latter case, the Ising weights (
3.4
) are just a sub-class of all valid orthant weights.

4
Estimation of Ising–Hüsler–Reiss processes

In this section we develop estimation methods for the parameters of an IHR process, which can naturally be divided into the following three subproblems

(i)

estimation of the graph
G
;

(ii)

estimation of the parameters of
\Theta
that are compatible with the estimate of
G
;

(iii)

estimation of Ising parameters
\Psi
that are compatible with the estimate of
G
.

Steps (i) and (ii) will be treated jointly, whereas we devote a separate subsection to step (iii). Before discussing our suggested estimation methods we need an additional technical result, which is of independent interest and is essential to obtain the consistency of our estimators.

4.1
A concentration inequality for the small time behavior of Lévy processes

The basis for proving the consistency our estimation procedures is a fundamental result about the small time behavior of a Lévy process, which is an extension and correction of
4
; see Section
B.1
for additional details. Its proof can be found in Appendix
B
and is one of the main theoretical contributions of this paper. Define

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon}:=\{c_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]\mid c_{\epsilon}\text{ smooth and }\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in B_{\infty}(\epsilon/2)\}}\leq c_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in B_{\infty}(\epsilon)\}}\},

(4.1)

where we say that a function is smooth if it is infinitely differentiable. We need a regularity condition on the Lévy density to derive our bound on the small time behavior of the corresponding Lévy process.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
\lambda
denote the density of the Lévy measure
\Lambda
of a Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
. Assume that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a
c_{\epsilon}\in S_{\epsilon}
such that
h^{(\epsilon)}:=(1-c_{\epsilon})\lambda
satisfies the following conditions.

When
d=1
assume that
(1-c_{\epsilon})h^{(\epsilon)}
is bounded and continuously differentiable with bounded derivative. When
d\geq 2
assume that

(i)

h^{(\epsilon)}
has uniformly bounded univariate and bivariate margins
(h^{(\epsilon)}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}

(h^{(\epsilon)}_{i,j})_{1\leq i\not=j\leq d}
where
h^{(\epsilon)}_{i}(x_{i}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}h^{(\epsilon)}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{\backslash i}
and
h^{(\epsilon)}_{i,j}(x_{i},x_{j}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}h^{(\epsilon)}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{\backslash\{i,j\}}
.

(ii)

h^{(\epsilon)}
is continuous and
x_{i}\mapsto\partial_{i}h^{(\epsilon)}(\mathbf{x})
is continuous for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

(iii)

For every
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
j=1,\dots,d
there exists
\sigma(a,j)>0
and a function
M_{j,a,\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to[0,\infty)
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}M_{j,a,\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j}<\infty
and for all
1\leq i\not=j\leq d

y_{i}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{j,a,\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i,j}
is bounded on bounded sets and

\sup_{|x_{j}-a|\leq\sigma}\Big|\partial_{j}h^{(\epsilon)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\Big|\leq M_{j,a,\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{\backslash j}).

(iv)

For all
1\leq j\leq d

x_{j}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\left|\partial_{j}h^{(\epsilon)}(\mathbf{x})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j}
is uniformly bounded.

Essentially, we need Assumption
4.1
to ensure that for all
I\subset V
the functions of the type
\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\ni\mathbf{x}_{I}\mapsto\int_{\mathbf{x}_{I}\geq\mathbf{y}_{I}}\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})
are twice continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives outside a neighborhood of
\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
, which will be an essential ingredient to the proof of Theorem
4.1
below. While Assumption
4.1
may seem quite technical, it is surprisingly difficult to formulate simple regularity conditions directly in terms of
f
which ensure differentiability and boundedness of terms of the form
x_{i}\mapsto U_{i}(x_{i})=\sgn(x_{i})\int_{T(x_{i})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\lambda(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i}
for
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. When smoothing out the origin via
(1-c_{\epsilon})\lambda
, the resulting regularity conditions are still non-trivial, but can be summarized relatively concisely via Assumption
4.1
. To further illustrate the issue, Appendix
B
provides an example of a continuous bivariate Lévy density
\lambda
whose marginal Lévy densities are not continuous on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Lévy densities which have a singularity along the axes, i.e.,
\lim_{x_{i}\to 0}\lambda(\mathbf{x})=\infty
for
\mathbf{x}_{V\setminus i}\neq\bm{0}
, cannot satisfy Assumption
4.1
, since
x_{i}\mapsto\partial_{i}h^{(\epsilon)}(\mathbf{x})
cannot exist for small enough
\epsilon
. Thus, if
\lambda
satisfies Assumption
4.1
, it can only explode towards the origin. An example of a Lévy density that satisfies Assumption
4.1
is the Lévy density of a Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process, as is verified in the following Proposition. The proof of this result relies on subtle analytic bounds which are necessary due to the complicated form of the Hüsler–Reiss density and its partial derivatives.

Proposition 4.1
.

The Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process from Definition
3.1
satisfies Assumption
4.1
for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
{{\bm{\alpha}}}\in(0,2)^{d}
,
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

{{\mathbf{c}}}\in(0,\infty)^{2d}
,
{{\bm{\gamma}}}\in[0,\infty)^{2^{d}}
and any valid Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix
\Theta
.

We next show that that Assumption
4.1
is sufficient to uniformly control the small time behavior of a multivariate Lévy process on rectangles, i.e., sets of the form
R=\times_{i=1}^{m}J_{m}
where each
J_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval, which then allows us to uniformly control the small time behavior of the Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process by Proposition
4.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
denote a
d
-dimensional Lévy process with Lévy measure
\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
, drift
\mathbf{b}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Assume that
\lambda
satisfies Assumption
4.1
.
Then, for every
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C
which only depends on
\delta
but does not depend on
t
such that for all rectangles
R\subset B_{\infty}^{\complement}(\delta)
and
t>0
we have

\Big|P\left(\mathbf{L}(t)\in R\right)-t\Lambda\left(R\right)\Big|\leq Ct^{2}.

Note that Theorem
4.1
implies the same uniform bound for the small time behavior of all
I
-margins
\mathbf{L}_{I}
by setting
J_{i}=\mathbb{R}
for all
i\not\in I
.

Remark 4.1
.

It is well known, see e.g.
(
35
, Corollary 8.9)
, that the small time-behavior of a Lévy process
\bm{L}
“reveals” the Lévy measure
\nu
via

t^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[g\big(\mathbf{L}(t)\big)\right]\overset{t\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

for every bounded continuous function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
which vanishes in a neighborhood of
\bm{0}
. Theorem
4.1
above yields an analog of this result when
g
is the indicator function of a rectangular set
R
which is bounded away from
\bm{0}
, additionally quantifying the rate of the convergence to be of order
t
uniformly over all
R\subset B_{\infty}^{\complement}(\delta)
.

4.2
Estimation of the graph structure

Throughout this section, we will work with the following observation scheme.

Assumption 4.2
.

Assume that we observe a Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
with drift
\bm{\tau}
and parameters
(\Theta,{{\bm{\alpha}}},{{\mathbf{c}}},{{\bm{\gamma}}})
on
n
regularly spaced grid points
\Delta,2\Delta,\dots,n\Delta
with
\Delta>0
. Denote the corresponding variogram matrix by
\Gamma
and assume
\Gamma_{i,j}>0~\forall i\neq j
. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{s}:=\mathbf{L}(s\Delta)-\mathbf{L}(\{s-1\}\Delta),\quad s\in[n].

(4.2)

The stationary and independent increments property of Lévy processes implies that
\mathbf{D}_{s}
are i.i.d. across
s\in[n]
. The asymptotic regime that we will study in what follows is
n\to\infty
for fixed
\Delta>0
, that is, we observe the process on a growing time grid.

We will first discuss the estimation of the variogram matrix
\Gamma
based on the sample
\mathbf{D}_{s},s\in[n]
. To this end, we will rely on ideas from extreme value theory. The dependence structure of a random vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{j}:j\in V)
with continuous marginal distribution functions
F_{i}
is
multivariate regularly varying with exponent measure
\nu
if
\nu
is a Borel measure on
[0,\infty)^{d}
such that

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-1}P\big(F_{i}(X_{i})>1-t/x_{i}~\forall i\in V\big)=\nu(\{\mathbf{y}\mid y_{i}>x_{i}~\forall i\in V\})}

(4.3)

holds for all
\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
that are continuity points of
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\nu((\mathbf{x},\bm{\infty}))

(
37
, e.g.,)
; note the similarity to the small-time approximation of Lévy processes in (
2.3
). Our first step is to establish multivariate regular variation of certain random vectors constructed from
\mathbf{L}(\Delta)
and provide a quantification of the corresponding convergence rate for certain values of
\mathbf{x}
.

Fix
I\subseteq V
with
|I|\geq 2
. For every orthant

{\mathbf{o}}\in\{-1,1\}^{|I|}
, we define the random vector
\mathbf{L}_{I}^{\mathbf{o}}(\Delta)
with law

\mathbf{L}_{I}^{\mathbf{o}}(\Delta)\overset{d}{=}\mathbf{L}_{I}(\Delta)\mid\{\mathbf{L}_{I}(\Delta)\in{\mathbf{o}}\};

note that the probability of the conditioning event is positive by Lemma
A.1
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold for some
d\geq 3
. Define the marginal distribution functions of
|\mathbf{L}^{\mathbf{o}}(\Delta)|
(with absolute value interpreted component-wise) as
F_{i}^{+,{\mathbf{o}}}(z)=P\left(|L^{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|\leq z\right)
. Then, for any
0<\zeta<1\wedge\min_{i\in V}\alpha_{i}^{-1}
,
there exist
C>0
such that for all
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}

\mathcal{D}_{I}(\bm{x},q):=\Big|P\left(F_{i}^{+,{\mathbf{o}}}\left(|L^{\mathbf{o}}_{i}(\Delta)|\right)>1-qx_{i}\ \forall\ i\in I\right)\\
-q\Lambda_{\rm{HR}}^{(I)}\big(\{\mathbf{y}\in[0,\infty)^{|I|}\mid\mathbf{y}\geq 1/\mathbf{x}_{I}\}\big)\Big|\leq Cq^{1+\zeta}

for all
I\subseteq V
with
|I|\in\{2,3\}
, where
\Lambda_{\rm{HR}}^{(I)}

denotes the exponent measure of the Hüsler–Reiss distribution with variogram matrix
\Gamma_{I,I}:=(\Gamma_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
.

The bound above corresponds to a
second order condition
in extreme value theory
(
5
)
; this result is key for constructing an estimator for
\Gamma
and prove its consistency. Further, the result is of independent interest beyond the analysis of extremal variograms, since it allows one to study general M-estimators for parameters of a Hüsler–Reiss Lévy process that are based on ideas from extreme value theory. Note that conditioning on
\mathbf{L}_{I}(\Delta)\in{\mathbf{o}}
is crucial to obtain the correct exponent measure. The random vector
\mathbf{L}_{I}
itself is not multivariate regularly varying with the correct exponent measure, and taking absolute values does not solve this problem either.

Next, we utilize ideas from the estimation of
Hüsler–Reiss Pareto distributions
(
12
;
18
)
in order to construct estimators for
\Gamma
and subsequently
\Theta
. Consider a sequence
q_{n}\to 0
with
q_{n}\in(0,1)
for all
n
. For any fixed
i\neq j,m\in V
let
J=(i,j,m)
. For
\ell\in\{i,j,m\}
and an orthant
{\mathbf{o}}\in\{-1,1\}^{3}
, let
\widehat{F}_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}
denote the empirical distribution function of the sample
\{D_{s,\ell}\mid s\mbox{ such that }\mathbf{D}_{s,J}\in{\mathbf{o}}\}
, where we explicitly allow for
i=m
or
j=m
which results in
D_{s,J}
having repeated entries. Define
n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}:=|\{s\in[n]:\mathbf{D}_{s,J}\in{\mathbf{o}}\}|
and let

\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m}:=\Big\{\log\big[\tfrac{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}+1}{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}}-\widehat{F}_{i}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}(D_{s,i})\big]-\log\big[\tfrac{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}+1}{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}}-\widehat{F}_{j}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}(D_{s,j})\big]\ \Big|\ \widehat{F}_{m}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}(D_{s,m})>1-q_{n}\Big\}

denote the samples of
\log\big[\tfrac{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}+1}{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}}-\widehat{F}_{i}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}(D_{s,i})\big]-\log\big[\tfrac{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}+1}{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}}-\widehat{F}_{j}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}(D_{s,j})\big]
for which
\widehat{F}_{m}^{{\mathbf{o}},J}(D_{s,m})>1-q_{n}
.
An estimator for
\Gamma_{ij}
is now constructed as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}_{ij}^{(m,{\mathbf{o}})}:=\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m}),

(4.4)

where and
\widehat{\Var}(\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m})
is the empirical variance of the sample
\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m}
with scaling
(|\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m}|+1)^{-1}
where
|\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m}|
denotes the number of observations in the set
\mathcal{S}^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}_{m}
. This estimator is motivated by the empirical variogram of
14
. Note, however, that we have a possibly different sample for each orthant, so the matrix
(\widehat{\Gamma}_{ij}^{(m,{\mathbf{o}})})_{i,j\in V}
does
not
correspond to the empirical variogram matrix from
14
. As mentioned above, conditioning on orthants first is crucial to obtain valid estimators of
\Gamma_{i,j}
, and a naive application of the ideas from

Since for any fixed

the quantity
\widehat{\Gamma}_{ij}^{(m,{\mathbf{o}})}
is an estimator for the same population parameter
\Gamma_{i,j}
, we construct a combined estimator by considering all possible values of
m,{\mathbf{o}}
and define

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}_{i,j}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sum_{{\mathbf{o}}\in\{-1,1\}^{3}}\frac{n^{J,{\mathbf{o}}}}{n}\widehat{\Gamma}^{(m,{\mathbf{o}})}_{i,j},

(4.5)

implicitly setting
\widehat{\Gamma}^{(m,{\mathbf{o}})}_{i,j}=0
whenever there is no observation available to compute it, or when
i=j
. Note that
\widehat{\Gamma}^{(m,{\mathbf{o}})}_{i,j}
depends on
q_{n}
, and thus so does
\hat{\Gamma}
. Repeating this for all pairs
i,j\in V
yields and
estimator
\hat{\Gamma}
of the Hüsler–Reiss parameter matrix
\Gamma
of the IHR process
\mathbf{L}
. Our first fundamental result shows consistency of this estimator

Theorem 4.3
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold and consider the estimator
\widehat{\Gamma}
from (
4.5
). Assume that
q_{n}\to 0
with
1/2\geq q_{n}\gg n^{\xi-1}
for some
\xi\in(0,1)
. Then
\widehat{\Gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}\Gamma
.

Consistent estimation of the variogram
\Gamma
is the
fundamental building block for a data-driven
recovery of the graph structure.
Indeed, the
EGlearn
algorithm in
12
for estimation of extremal graphical models can be viewed as generic algorithm that takes as input an estimator of a variogram matrix and outputs an estimate of the sparsity pattern of the corresponding Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix
\Theta
. This directly yields an estimator
\widehat{G}=(V,\widehat{E})
of the graphical structure of the IHR process by Theorem
3.1
, provided that the Ising parameters
\Psi
satisfy
\psi_{i,j}=0
for all
i,j
such that
\Theta_{i,j}=0
.

Given the estimator of the graph structure,
18
construct an estimator
\widehat{\Gamma}^{\widehat{G}}

whose precision matrix
\widehat{\Theta}^{\widehat{G}}

is graph structured according to
\widehat{G}
.
To this end, they solve the matrix completion problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widehat{\Gamma}^{\widehat{G}}_{ij}&=\widehat{\Gamma}_{ij}&\quad&\forall(i,j)\in\widehat{E},\\
\widehat{\Theta}^{\widehat{G}}_{ij}&=0&\quad&\forall(i,j)\notin\widehat{E}.\end{aligned}

We will use the same approach in our framework of Lévy graphical models.

12
describe two base learners for the
EGlearn
algorithm: neighborhood selection
(
12
, Section 4.3.1)
and graphical lasso
(
12
, Section 4.3.2)
. In their simulations, neighborhood selection consistently outperforms graphical lasso in terms of graph recovery, and we observe the same in our simulations. For this reason, we only provide theory on consistency of neighborhood selection for graph estimation below and only consider graphical lasso in the simulations.
To state the next result, we need to introduce some additional notation. For
m\in V
, let
\Sigma^{(m)}:=[\Theta_{\setminus m,\setminus m}]^{-1}
denote the inverse of the Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix
\Theta
from which the
m
-th row and column have been removed. Let
S_{m,\ell}:=\{j\in V,j\neq m,\ell:\Theta_{\ell j}\neq 0\}
and define the neighborhood selection incoherence parameter through

\eta:=1-\max_{m,\ell\in V,m\neq\ell}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Sigma^{(m)}_{S_{m,\ell}^{\complement},S_{m,\ell}}[\Sigma^{(m)}_{S_{m,\ell},S_{m,\ell}}]^{-1}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}

where the complement of
S_{m,\ell}
is taken in
V\setminus\{m\}
and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|A\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}:=\max_{j\in V}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|A_{ij}|
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold and denote by
G
the graph corresponding to
\Gamma
. Denote by
\widehat{G}
the graph estimator from the
EGlearn
algorithm with neighbourhood selection with penalty parameter
\rho_{n}
based on the variogram estimator
\widehat{\Gamma}
from (

). Assume that
\eta>0
and that, for some
1>\xi>0
and
0<\zeta<1\wedge\min_{i\in V}
\alpha_{i}^{-1}

1\gg q_{n}\gg n^{\xi-1},\quad 1\gg\rho_{n}\gg q_{n}^{\zeta}(\log q_{n})^{2}+(nq_{n})^{-1/2}.

Then we have
(\widehat{G},\widehat{\Theta}^{\widehat{G}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}(G,\Theta)
.

4.3
Estimation of Ising weights

Given a Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix
\Theta
and the corresponding graph
G
, it remains to estimate compatible Ising weights. We first assume that we know
G
and later replace it with an estimated graph. Let
\mathbf{B}
denote the Rademacher random vector linked to the Ising weights through (
3.3
).
Recall that for a IHR Lévy process on graph
G
, the Ising parameters need to satisfy
\psi_{i,j}=0
if
(i,j)\not\in E
.
The Ising model is an an exponential family as its likelihood in (
3.4
) can be represented as

P(\mathbf{B}={\mathbf{o}})={\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}(\Psi)}/{2}=\exp\{\langle\Psi,S({\mathbf{o}})\rangle-A(\Psi)\},

where
S({\mathbf{o}})=\left(o_{i}o_{j}\right)_{(i,j)\in E}
is the sufficient statistic and
A(\Psi)=\log\{C(\Psi)\}
is a normalizing constant.
Since this only depends on the bivariate interactions
o_{i}o_{i}
, intuitively, one should be able to estimate
\psi_{i,j}
via the observations of
(L_{i},L_{j})
. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of the Ising model with given edge set
E
is unique when it exists and in the latter case can be found by setting the gradient of its log-likelihood to
0
. The MLE for a collection of i.i.d. samples
\mathbf{B}^{(s)},s\in[n]
exists iff the following system of equations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[B_{i}B_{j}\right]-n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{n}B_{i}^{(s)}B_{j}^{(s)}=0\quad(i,j)\in E,

(4.6)

has a solution where
\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}
denotes expectation with respect to an Ising model with parameter matrix
\Psi
; see
17
for a detailed derivation. Note that
n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{n}B_{i}^{(s)}B_{j}^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[B_{i}B_{j}\right]
is one half times the partial derivative of the Ising log-likelihood functions based on the observations
\mathbf{B}^{(s)},s\in[n]
, evaluated at the parameter
\Psi
with respect to
\Psi_{i,j}
.

This estimator only depends on the bivariate marginal distributions and one can show that the log-likelihood of the Ising model is concave in
\Psi
, which also implies that the MLE is unique when it exists. In our setting of IHR processes, we do not have access
to observations from the Ising model.
Instead, we note that the right-hand side of (
4.6
) is a consistent estimator of
\Cov_{\Psi}(B_{i},B_{j})=\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[B_{i}B_{j}\right]
since
\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[B_{i}\right]=0
in our model class.
We derive a different estimator for
the covariance that can be computed from
observations as in (
4.2
) of the Lévy process.
While a bivariate margin of an Ising model is generally
not an Ising model itself, we can express the covariance
in terms of the Lévy measure.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Lambda^{\star}
be the PLM of a IHR process with Ising weights
\Psi
. Then

\displaystyle\Cov_{\Psi}(B_{i},B_{j})=\frac{{\chi}_{i,j}^{(1,1)}+{\chi}_{i,j}^{(-1,-1)}-{\chi}_{i,j}^{(1,-1)}-{\chi}_{i,j}^{(-1,1)}}{{\chi}_{i,j}},

(4.7)

where
\chi^{(o_{1},o_{2})}_{i,j}:=\Lambda^{\star}\left(o_{1}x_{i}>1,o_{2}x_{j}>1\right)
,
which are related to the Lévy correlation
\chi_{i,j}
in

11
by

\chi_{i,j}:=2^{-1}\sum_{(o_{i},o_{j})\in\{-1,1\}^{2}}\chi^{(o_{i},o_{j})}_{i,j}.

A natural estimator for the four components of the
Lévy correlation is

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}^{(o_{1},o_{2})}_{ij}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{o_{1}\widehat{F}_{i}(\Delta_{i})-\mathbf{1}_{\{o_{1}=1\}}+\frac{k}{2n}>0,\ o_{2}\widehat{F}_{j}(\Delta_{i})-\mathbf{1}_{\{o_{2}=1\}}+\frac{k}{2n}>0\right\},

(4.8)

where
\widehat{F}_{i}
is the empirical distribution function of the increments
\Delta_{i}(s)=L_{i}(s\Delta)-L_{i}(\{s-1\}\Delta)
of the Lévy process, for any
i\in V
.

11
show consistency of this estimator for a suitable choice of intermediate sequence
k=k(n)
such that
k\to\infty
and
k/n\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. In order to obtain an estimator
\widehat{\Psi}{(E)}
of the Ising weights
\Psi
, we consider the system of equations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Psi{(E)}}\left[B_{i}B_{j}\right]-\widehat{\Cov}_{\Psi{(E)}}(B_{i},B_{j})=0,\quad(i,j)\in E,

(4.9)

where
\widehat{\Cov}_{\Psi{(E)}}(B_{i},B_{j})
denotes the plug-in estimator of (
4.7
) using (
4.8
) for
(o_{1},o_{2})\in\{-1,1\}^{2}
. This system of equations may not always have a solution in finite samples, but we will show in the proof of Theorem
4.4
that it has a solution with probability going to one. We further show that this provides a consistent estimator of the Ising weights, even when the graph is unknown and needs to be inferred from the data. For the purpose of the following theorem, let
\widehat{\Psi}{(\widehat{E})}
denote the solution of (
4.9
) when this solution exists and set
\widehat{\Psi}{(\widehat{E})}\equiv 0
otherwise.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold and denote by
G=(V,E)
the graph corresponding to
\Gamma
. Assume additionally that
\mathbf{L}
from Assumption
4.2
is an IHR process with Ising parameters
\Psi
and that
\psi_{i,j}=0
for all
(i,j)\not\in E
. Denote by
\widehat{G}=(\widehat{E},V)
the graph that is estimated by the
EGlearn
algorithm and assume that the conditions of Corollary
4.1
hold. Consider the estimator
\widehat{\Psi}{(\widehat{E})}
described before this theorem and assume
k\to\infty,k/n\to 0
. The Ising weights are consistent, i.e., for all
{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathcal{O}
we have

\hat{\gamma}_{\mathbf{o}}:=\frac{2}{C(\widehat{\Psi}{(\widehat{E})})}\exp\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\widehat{E}}\widehat{\Psi}_{i,j}{(\widehat{E})}o_{i}o_{j}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}\gamma_{\mathbf{o}}(\Psi),\quad n\to\infty.

Combined with the previous results, this shows that, under suitable assumptions, all parameters and the graph that govern the dependence structure of an IHR process can be estimated consistently. The practical performance of those estimators is illustrated in simulations and a data application in the following sections.

5
Simulation study

5.1
Setup

We conduct a simulation study to assess the finite sample properties of our estimators of the graph structure
G=(V,E)
and the Ising weights
\Psi
.

Across all experiments, the graphs are randomly drawn from the Barabasi–Albert model with attachment parameter
a\in\{1,2\}
, where
a=1
corresponds to a tree and
a=2
corresponds to a connected sparse graph with
2d-3
edges. Moreover, for a given graph, the parameters of
\Theta
on edges of
G
are drawn uniformly on
[2,5]
.

For the Ising model, we consider two regimes, the symmetric regime where all
\Psi_{i,j}=0
and the asymmetric regime where each entry of
\Psi_{i,j}
is drawn uniformly from
[-0.6,0.2]\cup[0.2,0.6]
for
(i,j)\in E
. Across all simulations, we set
{{\bm{\alpha}}}=\bm{3}/\bm{2}
and
{{\mathbf{c}}}=\mathbf{1}
. For every sample size
n
we set
q_{n}:=n^{-3/10}
, i.e., we use the
(100n^{-3/10})
-percent largest observations for the estimation of
\Gamma
. Since the entries of
\Gamma
are estimated separately, it can happen that our estimate
\hat{\Gamma}
is not conditionally negative definite,
in which case we project it onto the space of conditionally negative definite matrices.

Each experiment was repeated 50 times. The code to reproduce the simulation study can be found on
https://github.com/florianbrueck/graphicalmodelsforstableprocesses
.

5.2
Graph recovery

We compare several graph estimation methods on the simulated
data described above.
In order to assess graph recovery between true edges
E
and estimated edges
\hat{E}
, we use the
F_{1}
-score defined as

F_{1}(E,\hat{E}):=\frac{2\cdot|E\cap\hat{E}|}{2\cdot|E\cap\hat{E}|+|E^{\complement}\cap\hat{E}|+|E\cap\hat{E}^{\complement}|}.

As a first baseline method we use the minimum spanning tree based on
the Lévy correlation
\chi
from
11
.
We also use the same method but based on our estimator
of the Hüsler–Reiss parameter matrix
\Gamma
.
For estimation of general graphs we use our new method based on the
EGLearn
algorithm
(
12
)
, either with neighborhood selection (NS) and graphical lasso (Glasso),
which has a tuning parameter
\rho\geq 0
controlling the sparsity of the estimated graphs. We denote the corresponding graph estimates and presision matirx by

\hat{G}_{\rho}=(V,\hat{E}_{\rho})
and
\hat{\Theta}_{\rho}
, respectively.
To select the optimal
\rho
in a data-driven way, we propose using the pseudo Hüsler–Reiss log-likelihood
(
33
)
with
penalty for model complexity

-2\left\{\log(|\hat{\Theta}_{\rho}|_{+})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\Gamma}\hat{\Theta}_{\rho})\right\}+\text{IC}\{\hat{E}_{\rho}\}

as an approximation of AIC or BIC, where

\text{IC}(\hat{E}_{\rho}):=\begin{cases}2|\hat{E}_{\rho}|,&\text{IC}=\text{AIC},\\
\log\left(nq_{n}\right)|\hat{E}_{\rho}|,&\text{IC}=\text{BIC}.\end{cases}

Maximizing this pseudo likelihood over a range of
\rho
values yields a data-driven method of hyperparameter selection.

5.3
Estimation of Ising weights

In this subsection we focus on the estimation of the Ising parameters
\Psi
, which govern the asymmetry of the IHR model. We only consider the asymmetric regime as the parameters of
\Psi
on the edges of the graph are non-zero in this case. We further assume that the true underlying graph is known in order to disentangle the performance of our estimation method for
\Psi
from the graph estimation. For a given dimension
d
, we randomly generate one underlying graph from the Barabasi–Albert model with attachment parameter
a=2
with a fixed corresponding parameter matrix
\Psi
. Across the 50 repetitions we assess the variation of the estimates of
\Psi
around their true value.

Since there is no closed form solution to the system of equations (
4.9
) we suggest a gradient ascent based procedure to obtain an approximate solution. To deal with possibly non-existing solutions, we further add a penalty to the original problem. Specifically, the system of equations (
4.9
) gives the maximizer of the concave function

\hat{f}(\Psi):={-\log C(\Psi)+}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\widehat{\Cov}_{\Psi}(B_{i},B_{j})\psi_{i,j}

over all
\Psi
with
\psi_{i,j}=0
when
(i,j)\notin E
whenever the maximizer exists. To see this, recall that
C(\Psi)
denotes the normalizing constant in (
3.4
) and that
\partial\log C(\Psi)/\partial\psi_{i,j}=\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}[B_{i}B_{j}]
. To deal with possible non-existence, we propose to instead maximize

\hat{f}(\Psi)-v\sum_{(i,j)\in E}|\Psi_{i,j}|

(5.1)

where
v
is a penalty parameter that we set to
v=0.05
throughout all experiments. The corresponding sub-gradient is given by

\widehat{\Cov}_{\Psi}(B_{i},B_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}[B_{i}B_{j}]-v\sgn(\Psi_{i,j}),\quad(i,j)\in E.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[B_{i}B_{j}\right]
is not available in closed form, we replace it by an empirical estimate based on
5000
MCMC samples
of the Ising model. To perform the optimization of (
5.1
), we opt here for the ADAM optimizer
(
22
)
, with which we observe fast convergence and reliable estimates within less than
500
iterations. An exemplary traceplot of our gradient ascent procedure is provided in Figure
11
in the Appendix, which demonstrates quick convergence of the procedure.

Figure
4
shows boxplots of the estimates of
\Psi_{i,j}
for
(i,j)\in E
together with the corresponding true values
for different dimensions and sample sizes. The variation of the parameter estimates and the bias decreases as the sample sizes increases.

For lower sample sizes, we observe some outliers of extreme parameter estimates. This may be an artifact of the bias in the estimation of
\chi^{(o_{1},o_{2})}_{i,j}
. Overall, the estimation of the Ising weights seems to work well. In particular, the sign of the estimate of
\Psi_{i,j}
, and thus the direction of dependence of component
i
and
j
, is always estimated correctly. Moreover, the ordering of the estimates
\hat{\psi}_{i,j}
is largely in line with the true ordering of the
\psi_{i,j}
, which ensures that the ordering of the strengths of dependencies is reflected correctly.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a family of PLMs which encodes the conditional independence properties of the associated Lévy process in terms of zero-entries of the symmetric Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. This is an analog to the precision matrix
\Sigma^{-1}
of a Brownian motion, which also encodes conditional independencies in its zero-entries. Thus, our model may be viewed as a continuous time analog of the Gaussian graphical model for non-Gaussian Lévy processes. To be able to incorporate asymmetric dependencies for the jumps of the Lévy process we introduce a new model for an orthant-weighting scheme of a PLM based on the Ising model, which retains the underlying graph and allows to asymmetrize a given PLM in a sparse way. We propose sparsistent estimation methods for the underlying parameter matrices
\Theta
and
\Psi
, assuming that the marginal processes are stable with arbitrary stability indices. Importantly, the stability indices do not need to be identical across the margins and our proposed estimation procedure does not require any knowledge about these indices since it is solely based on the copula of the Lévy process at a fixed time point. Thus, our method models dependence and may be used in conjunction with any parameter estimation method for the marginal parameters of the stable processes to estimate the full data generating process.

causes no problems, so we focus on one term from the sum. By the choice of
\xi
and (

)

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}y_{k}^{-1-\alpha_{k}(1+\alpha_{j}^{-1}+\sum_{i=1,i\neq k}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{-1})}\prod_{i\neq k,j}\Big(\frac{|y_{i}|^{\alpha_{i}}}{|y_{k}|^{\alpha_{k}}}\wedge\frac{|y_{k}|^{\alpha_{k}}}{|y_{i}|^{\alpha_{i}}}\Big)^{\xi}d\mathbf{y}_{\setminus\{k,j\}}dy_{k}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle C_{\xi}(\bm{\alpha})^{d-2}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}y_{k}^{-1-2\alpha_{k}\alpha_{j}^{-1}}dy_{k}<\infty.

This proves that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}M_{j}(\mathbf{y}_{\backslash j})d\mathbf{y}_{\backslash j}<\infty
.
Boundedness of
x_{i}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{j}(\mathbf{y}_{\backslash j})d\mathbf{y}_{\backslash\{i,j\}}
on bounded sets (and, in fact, globally) follows by a similar computation, again noting that
\Big(\frac{|y_{j}|^{\alpha_{i}}}{|y_{k}|^{\alpha_{\ell}}}\wedge\frac{|y_{\ell}|^{\alpha_{k}}}{|y_{j}|^{\alpha_{i}}}\Big)^{\xi}\leq 1
.

B
A uniform bound for the small time behavior of a multivariate Lévy process

The goal of this section is to prove a general uniform (in
t
and
\mathbf{y}
) bound of the form

\Big|P\left(\mathbf{L}(t)\in[\mathbf{y},\bm{\infty})\right)-t\nu\left([\mathbf{y},\bm{\infty})\right)\Big|\leq Ct^{2}.

Our results can be viewed as an extension and correction of Lemma 4.3 in
4
, who derive a concentration inequality for the small time behavior of bivariate Lévy processes with only positive jumps, which is in turn a generalization of uni-variate results of
15
. We first provide some details on the issues that we found in the proof of Lemma 4.3
4
, which were confirmed in correspondence with the authors. Specifically,
4
consider the following regularity conditions.

Assumption B.1
(Assumption 4.1 of
4
).

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a bivariate Lévy process with Lévy measure
\nu
.
The following assumptions on
\nu
are in order:

(i)

\nu
has support
[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
.

(ii)

On this set it takes the form
\nu(d\mathbf{u})=s(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}
for a positive Lévy density
s

which satisfies

\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in M_{\eta}}\left(|s(\mathbf{u})|+\|\nabla s(\mathbf{u})\|\right)<\infty

for any
\eta\in(0,\infty)^{2}
, where

M_{\eta}=(\eta,\infty)^{2}\cup\big(\{0\}\times(\eta,\infty)\big)\cup\big((\eta,\infty)\times\{0\}\big),

and
\nabla s
denotes the gradient of
s
on
(\eta,\infty)^{2}
and the univariate derivative on the stripes through
0
, respectively.

Under these assumptions they claim the following result.

Lemma
(Lemma 4.3 of
4
).

Suppose that (i) and (ii) of Assumption
B.1
hold and let
\delta>0
be fixed.
Then there exist constants
K=K(\delta)
and
t_{0}=t_{0}(\delta)
such that the uniform bound

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{(1)}\geq x_{1},\,X_{t}^{(2)}\geq x_{2}\right)-t\,\nu\big([x_{1},\infty)\times[x_{2},\infty)\big)\right|<Kt^{2}

holds for all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in[\delta,\infty)^{2}\cup\big(\{-\infty\}\times[\delta,\infty)\big)\cup\big([\delta,\infty)\times\{-\infty\}\big)

and
0<t<t_{0}
.

However, it turns out that the proof of
4
is flawed. Specifically, Assumption 4.1 does not suffice to ensure that the function
g
in equation (A1) from
4
is twice continuously differentiable, which is needed to apply Ito’s lemma in the version that is used in (A1) in the latter paper. Further details are described in Section
B.1
below. In order to prove our Theorem
4.1
, which corrects and generalizes Lemma 4.3 in
4
, we needed to strengthen the requirements on the Lévy density to include twice continuous differentiability and impose the existence of certain majorants, which resulted in Assumption
4.1
.

B.1
Additional details on issues in the proof of Lemma 4.3 in
4

Let
\mathbf{L}
denote a bivariate Lévy process without drift and Gaussian component and Lévy measure concentrated on
[0,\infty)^{2}
. Define

f(x,y)=h(x)g(y)
,
where
g(y)
is a smooth and bounded probability density with bounded derivatives of a random variable on
[1,\infty)
. Note that we can choose
g
such that
\lim_{x\to 1}g(x)=0
. Further define

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{x\geq 1\}}\exp(-(x-1))(x-1)^{2}\sin(1/(x-1))^{2}=\mathbf{1}_{\{x\geq 1\}}\exp(-(x-1))\int_{1}^{x}q^{\prime}(z)\mathrm{d}z

where

q^{\prime}(z)=\begin{cases}2(z-1)\sin\left(1/(z-1)\right)^{2}-2\sin(1/(z-1))\cos\left(1/(z-1)\right)&\text{if }z>1\\
0&\text{if }z\leq 1\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
C:=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
,
\lim_{x\to 1}h(x)=0
and that
h(x)
is bounded and differentiable with bounded derivative
-h(x)+\exp(-(x-1))q^{\prime}(x)
everywhere. It is important to note that
h
has discontinuous derivative at
1
as
q^{\prime}
is not continuous at
1
.
This implies that
f
is continuous, bounded and its partial derivatives exist and are bounded. The same is true for the margins
f_{1}(x)=h(x)
and
f_{2}(y)=Cg(y)
obtained by integrating out the second and first components, respectively. Therefore, the gradients of
f
and of its marginal densities
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
exist and are bounded in the sense that

\displaystyle\forall\delta>0:\ \sup_{(x,y)\in[\delta,\infty)^{2}}(|f(x,y)|+\|\nabla f(x,y)\|)<\infty,

(B.1)

as required in Assumption 4.1(ii) in
4
.

Let
s
be a bivariate smooth Lévy density that satisfies the assumptions of
(
4
, Lemma 4.3)
, i.e.,
s
has support on
[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and its marginal Lévy densities
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
satisfy the bound (
B.1
). Assume further that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int_{v\geq 1-t}s(1-x,v)dv=\int_{v\geq 1-t}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}s(1-x,v)dv

for all
t,x<1/2
.

Then
s+f
is a Lévy density which satisfies these assumptions as well. But
s+f
has discontinuous partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}s(x,y)+h^{\prime}(x)g(y)
and therefore, for every
\epsilon<1/2
and every smooth
c_{\epsilon}
such that

\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in B^{\infty}(\epsilon/2)\}}\leq c_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in B^{\infty}(\epsilon)\}}

the function

G(x,y)=\int_{\mathbf{z}\geq\bm{1}-(x,y)}(1-c_{\epsilon}(z_{1},z_{2}))\left(s(z_{1},z_{2})+f(z_{1},z_{2})\right)\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}

has second order partial derivative for
x,y<1/2
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{(\partial x)^{2}}G(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\int_{z_{2}\geq 1-y}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}s(1-x,z_{2})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}h(1-x)g(z_{2})\mathrm{d}z_{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{z_{2}\geq 1-y}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}s(1-x,z_{2})\mathrm{d}z_{2}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}h(1-x)\int_{z_{2}\geq 1-y}g(z_{2})\mathrm{d}z_{2}

which is not continuous at
(x,y)=\bm{0}
because of the discontinuity of
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}h
at 1.

This precludes an application of Ito’s lemma to the function
G
(
g
with
\mathbf{x}=\bm{1}
in their notation) in Equation A.1 in the proof of Lemma 4.3 in
4
as all standard versions of Ito’s lemma require the continuity of the second order derivative, see e.g. Theorem 4.4.7 in
1
. This shows that the conditions stated in Lemma 4.3 in
4
do not suffice to prove their claim. The problem is not easily fixable by resorting to a variants of Ito’s lemma that does not require continuity of the derivative, since those formulas deviate from the standard Ito formula through the appearance of non-standard terms that would require additional arguments that we were unable to produce.

Thus, we have opted to strengthen the requirements on the Lévy density to be able to apply Ito’s formula in the proof of our Theorem
4.1
, which corrects and generalizes Lemma 4.3 in
4
.

B.2
A general regularity transfer from the density to the CDF

Here, we present a general criterion under which certain smoothness properties of the density transfer to its corresponding CDF and lower-dimensional densities.

Lemma B.1
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a Lebesgue integrable function such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}<\infty
. If
d=1
assume that
f
is bounded and continuously differentiable with bounded derivative. If
d\geq 2
assume that

(i)

f
has uniformly bounded univariate and bivariate margins
\left(f_{i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq d}

\left(f_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i\not=j\leq d}
where
f_{i}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{\backslash i}
and
f_{i,j}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{\backslash\{i,j\}}
.

(ii)

f
is continuous and
x_{i}\mapsto\partial_{i}f(\mathbf{x})
is continuous for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

(iii)

For every
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
j=1,\dots,d
there exists
\epsilon(a,j)>0
and a function
M_{j,a,\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to[0,\infty)
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}M_{j,a,\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j}<\infty
and for all
1\leq i\not=j\leq d

y_{i}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{j,a,\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i,j}
is bounded on bounded sets and

\sup_{|x_{j}-a|\leq\epsilon}\Big|\partial_{j}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\Big|\leq M_{j,a,\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{\backslash j}).

(iv)

For all
1\leq j\leq d

x_{j}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\left|\partial_{j}f(\mathbf{x})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus j}
is uniformly bounded.

Then for all
I\subset\{1,\ldots,d\}

F_{I}:\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\to[0,\infty);\quad\mathbf{x}\mapsto\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\mathbf{y}_{I}\leq\mathbf{x}\}}f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

is twice continuously differentiable with uniformly bounded first and second order partial derivatives.

The claim is trivial for
d=1
. Therefore, assume
d\geq 2
and observe that
F_{I}(\mathbf{x}_{I})=F_{V}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{I})=:F(\tilde{(}\mathbf{x})_{I})
, where
\tilde{x}_{I}:=\left(x_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{i\in I\}}+\infty\mathbf{1}_{\{i\not\in I\}}\right)_{1\leq i\leq d}
. Thus, we can prove the general claim by proving that

F(\mathbf{x}):(-\infty,\infty]^{d}\to[0,\infty);F(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{x}\}}f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

is twice continuously differentiable with bounded first and second order partial derivatives in its real-valued arguments. Since the assumptions and statements are equi-variant under permuting the entries of vectors, we will without loss of generality only consider partial derivatives w.r.t.
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, whereas the case that only one
x_{i}
is finite is also implicitly covered by only considering those parts of the proof that are relevant to this claim.

We start with proving the existence of the first order partial derivatives. For any fixed
\mathbf{z}\in(-\infty,\infty]^{d-1}
, the function

x\mapsto h_{\mathbf{z}}(x)=\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}f(x,\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

is uniformly bounded by
f_{1}(x)
since
f
is non-negative. It is also continuous since there exists an
\epsilon>0
such that for
|x-x^{\prime}|\leq\epsilon

|h_{\mathbf{z}}(x)-h_{\mathbf{z}}(x^{\prime})|\leq\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}|f(x,\mathbf{y})-f(x^{\prime},\mathbf{y})|d\mathbf{y}\leq|x-x^{\prime}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}M_{1,x,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y},

by the mean value theorem.
Since
F(x,\mathbf{z})=\int_{(-\infty,x]}h_{\mathbf{z}}(t)dt
, the existence of the first partial derivative follows by the (Lebesgue version of) the fundamental theorem of calculus and we have
\partial_{1}F(x,\mathbf{z})=\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}f(x,\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
.

We now prove that
x\mapsto\partial_{1}F(x,\mathbf{z})
is again differentiable for any fixed
\mathbf{z}
. This follows by the Leibniz rule for differentiating under the integral sign: by assumption, the function
x\mapsto f(x,\mathbf{y})
is differentiable for every
\mathbf{y}
and there exists some
\epsilon>0
such that the derivative satisfies

\sup_{|x^{\prime}-x|<\epsilon}\Big|\partial_{1}f(x,\mathbf{y})\Big|\leq M_{1,x,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})

which has finite integral by assumption. In particular, we obtain

\partial_{1,1}F(x,\mathbf{z})=\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}\partial_{1}f(x,\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}.

Moreover,

\left|\partial_{1,1}F(x,\mathbf{z})\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\left|\partial_{1}f(x,\mathbf{y})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

is uniformly bounded in
x
by assumption. Therefore,
\partial_{1,1}F(x,\mathbf{z})
is uniformly bounded.

To compute
\partial_{2,1}F(x,y,\mathbf{z})
, we again apply the Lebesgue version of the fundamental theorem of calculus. Fix
x\in\mathbb{R},\mathbf{z}\in(-\infty,\infty]^{d-2}
. Define
g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(t):=\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}f(x,t,\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
and observe that
\partial_{1}F(x,t,\mathbf{z})=\int_{(-\infty,t]}g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(s)ds
. If we can show that
s\mapsto g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(s)
is continuous everywhere, it will follow that
g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(t)
is equal to
\partial_{2,1}F(x,t,\mathbf{z})
. Since there exists some
\epsilon=\epsilon(s,2)
such that for all
|a_{n}|\leq\epsilon
and
\tilde{s}_{n}=\tilde{s}_{n}(x,\mathbf{y})
between
s
and
s+a_{n}
the mean-value theorem implies

\displaystyle|g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(s+a_{n})-g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(s)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}f(x,s+a_{n},\mathbf{y})-f(x,s,\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq|a_{n}|\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}\left|\partial_{2}f(x,\tilde{s}_{n}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\leq|a_{n}|\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}M_{2,s,\epsilon}(x,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\leq|a_{n}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{2,s,\epsilon}(x,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Since
x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{2,s,\epsilon}(x,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
is finite continuity of
s\mapsto g_{x,\mathbf{z}}(s)
follows.
This proves differentiability of
t\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial_{1}}F(x,t,\mathbf{z})
with respect to
t
and shows

\partial_{2,1}F(x,t,\mathbf{z})=\int_{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}}f(x,t,\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}\leq f_{1,2}(x,t)

is uniformly bounded

It remains to establish continuity of all second order partial derivatives, which then implies that
F
is twice continuously differentiable. Starting with second order partial derivatives with respect to the same component choose a sequence
(x_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})\to(x,\mathbf{z})
and observe

\displaystyle\partial_{1,2}F(x,\mathbf{z})-\partial_{1,1}F(x_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}_{n}\}}\partial_{1}f(x,\mathbf{y})-\partial_{1}f(x_{n},\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}\not\leq\mathbf{z}_{n}\}}\partial_{1}f(x,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}_{n},\mathbf{y}\not\leq\mathbf{z}\}}\partial_{1}f(x,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Assuming that
|x-x_{n}|\leq\epsilon(x,1)
, all integrands are dominated in absolute value by
2M_{1,x,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})
. Moreover, using that
x\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial_{1}}f(x,\mathbf{z})
is continuous, all integrands converge to zero Lebesgue almost-everywhere. Thus, the right-hand side tends to zero by dominated convergence and continuity follows.

For the continuity of mixed second order partial derivatives, note that

\displaystyle\partial_{2,1}F(x,t,\mathbf{z})-\partial_{2,1}F(x_{n},t_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}_{n}\}}(f(x,t,\mathbf{y})-f(x_{n},t_{n},\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}\not\leq\mathbf{z}_{n}\}}f(x,t,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}_{n},\mathbf{y}\not\leq\mathbf{z}\}}f(x,t,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

The integrand in the second integral is bounded by
f(x,t,\mathbf{y})
which is non-negative and integrates to
f_{1,2}(x,t)<\infty
. Since the indicator converges pointwise to the indicator of a Lebesgue null set when
\mathbf{z}_{n}\to\mathbf{z}
, the integral converges to zero by dominated convergence. The same argument applies to the third integral. For the first integral, note that by the multivariate mean-value theorem we have

f(x_{n},t_{n},\mathbf{y})-f(x,t,\mathbf{y})=(x_{n}-x)\partial_{1}f(\tilde{x}_{n},\tilde{t}_{n},\mathbf{y})+(t_{n}-t)\partial_{2}f(\tilde{x}_{n},\tilde{t}_{n},\mathbf{y})

where the point
(\tilde{x}_{n},\tilde{t}_{n})
lies on the line segment connecting
(x,t)
and
(x_{n},t_{n})
. Assuming that
|x_{n}-x|\leq\epsilon(1,x)=:\epsilon_{1},|t_{n}-t|\leq\epsilon(2,t)=:\epsilon_{2}
, this expression is upper bounded by
|x-x_{n}|M_{1,x,\epsilon_{1}}(\tilde{t}_{n},\mathbf{y})+|t-t_{n}|M_{2,t,\epsilon_{2}}(\tilde{x}_{n},\mathbf{y})
. Since
\tilde{x}_{n},\tilde{t}_{n}
stay in a bounded set, we obtain

\Big|\int_{\{\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}_{n}\}}(f(x,t,\mathbf{y})-f(x_{n},t_{n},\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\Big|\\
\leq|x-x_{n}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{1,x,\epsilon_{1}}(\tilde{t}_{n},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+|t-t_{n}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-2}}M_{2,t,\epsilon_{2}}(\tilde{x}_{n},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\to 0

since both integrals are bounded by assumption. This completes the proof of continuity of the mixed partial derivative.
∎

In particular, the smoothed version of the Hüsler–Reiss Lévy density
h_{\epsilon}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
B.1
.

B.3
Proof of Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
4.1

Finally, we are ready to prove the main results of this section.

The general structure of our proof follows the proof of
4
with the appropriate modifications to obtain the more general statements of Theorem
4.1
and corrections to account for the fact that their Lemma 4.3 was not proved as stated.

Let us denote the sets of rectangles consisting of solely unbounded open and closed intervals as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{d}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\times_{i=1}^{d}S_{i}(y_{i})\ \Big|\ S_{i}(y_{i})\in\{[y_{i},\infty),(y_{i},\infty),(-\infty,y_{i}),(-\infty,y_{i}],\mathbb{R}\}\Big\}

and the set of rectangles such that at least one half open interval does not intersect with
[-\delta,\delta]^{\complement}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{d,\delta}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\times_{i=1}^{d}S_{i}(y_{i})\in\mathcal{V}_{d}\ \Big|\ \exists i:S_{i}(y_{i})\subset[-\delta,\delta]^{\complement}\Big\}.

Rectangles
R\subset B_{\infty}^{\complement}(\delta)
of the form
W\times V

V\in\mathcal{V}_{d-1}
for an arbitrary interval
W
can be obtained from sets in
\mathcal{V}_{d,\delta}
by at most one operation of the form
A\setminus B

B\subset A

\mu(B)<\infty

\sup_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{d,\delta}}\Big|P\left(\mathbf{L}(t)\in V\right)-t\nu(V)\Big|\leq Ct^{2}.

All sets

for an arbitrary Lévy process
\mathbf{L}
which satisfies the assumption of Theorem
4.1

4.1
and there are only finitely many ways to permute and reflect the components. Therefore, from now on, w.l.o.g. we assume that
\mathbf{y}\in M_{1,\delta}
and set

.

Denote the triplet of

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{2}(1)}\left(g_{\mathbf{y}}(X(s-)+\mathbf{x})-g_{\mathbf{y}}(X(s-))-\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\nabla g_{\mathbf{y}}(X(s-))\right)c_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})\left(N_{X}-\mathcal{L}\otimes\Lambda\right)(\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{2}(1)}\left(g_{\mathbf{y}}(X(s-)+\mathbf{x})-g_{\mathbf{y}}(X(s-))-\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\nabla g_{\mathbf{y}}(X(s-))\right)c_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})\mathcal{L}\otimes\Lambda(\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x}))

where we recall that

First, observe that

\displaystyle T_{1}=g_{\mathbf{y}}(\bm{0})=\int_{\{\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{y}\}}(1-c_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}))\lambda(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}/K_{\epsilon}=\int_{\{\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{y}\}}\lambda(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}/K_{\epsilon}=\Lambda\left([\mathbf{y},\bm{\infty})\right)/K_{\epsilon},

(B.5)

since

Next, we bound

. Since

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}g_{\mathbf{y}}(X_{i}(s-))(b_{i}-b_{\epsilon,i})\mathrm{d}s\bigg|
