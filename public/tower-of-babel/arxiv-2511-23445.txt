Quantum Polymorphisms and Commutativity Gadgets
Lorenzo Ciardo, Gideo Joubert, Antoine Mottet

Abstract
. We introduce the concept of quantum polymorphisms to the complexity theory of non-local games. We use this notion to give a full characterisation of the existence of commutativity gadgets for relational structures, introduced by Ji as a method for achieving quantum soundness of classical CSP reductions. Prior to our work, a classification was only known in the Boolean case [Culf–Mastel, STOC’25]. As an application of our framework, we prove that the entangled CSP parameterised by odd cycles is undecidable. Furthermore, we establish a quantum version of Galois connection for entangled CSPs in the case of non-oracular quantum homomorphisms.



1.
Introduction

In 1971, Cook’s introduction of
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness
[
10
]
provided a way to mathematically assess the inherent hardness of computational problems.
Boolean satisfiability, graph colouring, linear equations over finite fields, and many other computatational problems of a seemingly distinct nature systematically began to fall into either the polynomial-time solvable or the
\mathsf{NP}
-complete buckets
[
23
]
, while intermediate complexity turned out to be a rarer phenomenon
[
24
]
. This fact soon sparked the hypothesis that the broad, natural class of computational problems involving satisfaction of constraints over finite alphabets—collectively known as (finite-domain) CSPs and encompassing the aforementioned problems and many others—could never exhibit the unnatural intermediate behaviour.
‘
\operatorname{P}
vs.
\mathsf{NP}
-complete’ dichotomies for fragments of CSPs started to emerge, primary examples being Schaefer’s
[
27
]
and Hell–Nešetřil’s
[
16
]
dichotomies for Boolean and graph CSPs, respectively.
However, a
unified
framework for approaching the
CSP Dichotomy Conjecture

[
15
]
only emerged at the turn of the century, with the foundational line of work initiated by Jeavons, Cohen, and Gyssens that became known as the
(universal-)algebraic approach to CSPs

[
18
,
19
]
.
By lifting CSP theory from the realm of Turing machines and reductions to that of algebra, the study of
polymorphism clones
—universal-algebraic objects capturing high-order invariance properties of CSP solutions—provided a clearer viewpoint on the CSP complexity landscape, and it eventually culminated with the positive resolution of the conjecture by Bulatov
[
7
]
and, independently, Zhuk
[
28
,
29
]
in 2017.

Seven years before the publication of Cook’s result, the work of Bell
[
5
]
originating from the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox
[
14
]
demonstrated that quantum systems can display correlations unattainable within classical physics, thus ruling out local hidden-variable theories as realistic explanations for
quantum mechanics. In its mathematical essence, Bell’s result can be formulated as a property of CSPs: There exist specific CSP languages for which the classical value and the entangled value can differ, where the latter is defined as the optimal success probability in a multiplayer interactive proof of the satisfiability of the CSP instance where the players have access to quantum resources.
A primary question arising from Bell’s theorem concerns the computational complexity of determining the entangled value of a CSP. In particular, it is known that determining whether an instance of SAT is perfectly quantum satisfiable is undecidable (in fact, even the
gap
version of the problem is known to be undecidable, as a consequence of the celebrated
\operatorname{MIP^{*}=RE}
theorem
[
20
]
). On the other hand, examples of tractable CSP languages for which quantum and classical satisfiability coincide, which implies polynomial-time solvability of the corresponding entangled CSPs, are known
[
2
,
9
,
6
]
.

Nevertheless, unlike in the classical case, the complexity landscape of entangled CSPs remains largely unexplored. A natural strategy to shed some light on it is to transfer the algebraic machinery that allowed proving the (classical) CSP Dichotomy to the quantum setting.
In particular, for classical CSPs, the concept of polymorphisms turned out to be the right tool for exploring CSP complexity at the algebraic level. The root of this phenomenon is a Galois connection between relational clones (expressing the CSP language) and operation clones (expressing the family of polymorphisms). Via this connection, interreducibility between CSPs associated with different languages can be explained via inclusion (or weaker forms of the latter, in particular, so called height-1 clone homomorphisms) between their polymorphism clones.

To what extent can this machinery by transferred to the setting of entangled CSPs, and thus provide some understanding of their complexity and interreducibility?
This question was first addressed at this level of generality by Ji in
[
22
]
. In particular, that work identified a substantial obstacle against the prospect of quantising
all
types of algebraic CSP reductions known as
gadgets
. Ultimately, the obstacle is caused by the non-classical phenomenon of
contextuality
: Replacing constraints by gadgets increases the size of the contexts—which, in general, destroys simultaneous measurability. In the same paper, Ji provided a method to solve this issue for specific CSP languages, via the new notion of
commutativity gadgets
. On a high level, these are specific types of gadgets having no classical counterpart, that can be plugged into classical gadgets to enforce simultaneous measurability of the final instance resulting from the reduction. For example, by showing that a specific
triangular prism
construction yields a commutativity gadgets for the 3-clique, Ji showed that the classical polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to 3-colouring can be lifted to a reduction between the corresponding entangled CSPs, thus proving undecidability of quantum 3-colouring.

Later works generalised this idea to more general types of CSPs. In particular, commutativity gadgets for certain Boolean CSPs were constructed in
[
2
]
. In a subsequent recent breakthrough
[
11
]
, Culf and Mastel gave a complete characterisation of Boolean CSPs admitting commutativity gadgets. In the same work, the authors identified a
sufficient
property of CSP languages—which they called
two-variable (non-)falsifiability
—guaranteeing the existence of commutativity gadgets for the corresponding CSPs, and thus allows quantising classical reductions. Very recently, Culf, de Bruyn, Vernooij, and Zeman
[
13
]
gave a different, necessary condition for the existence of commutativity gadgets, by showing that CSPs with non-classical quantum endomorphisms do not admit commutativity gadgets.

Despite this recent progress, a full characterisation of CSPs exhibiting commutativity gadgets is not available. In particular,
[
13
]
identified odd cycles as a challenging open case for which the existence or non-existence of commutativity gadgets cannot be established via available methods.

Contributions

We give a complete characterisation of CSPs exhibiting commutativity gadgets. To that end, we introduce the notion of
quantum polymorphisms
, which lift classical polymorphisms to the setting of entangled CSPs.
We show that quantum polymorphisms completely capture the existence of commutativity gadgets, by proving that a commutativity gadget exists if, and only if, the minion of quantum polymorphisms coincides with the
quantum closure
of the clone of standard polymorphisms.
Through this characterisation, we prove that all odd cycles admit commutativity gadgets and, thus, the corresponding entangled CSP is undecidable. This answers the question raised in
[
13
]
. In addition, we use quantum polymorphisms to build commutativity gadgets for Boolean structures in a unified manner, thus giving a different proof of the result of
[
12
]
.
Our characterisation works for both
oracular
and
non-oracular
CSPs. In the
non-oracular
setting, we additionally establish a quantum version of
Galois connection
that parallels the corresponding classical connection.

Structure of the paper

In
Section

2
, we give formal definitions of classical and entangled CSPs, and we list some basic results about quantum homomorphisms.
Section

3
presents the main definition of this work—quantum polymorphims—as well as their use to characterise the existence of oracular commutativity gadgets for entangled CSPs. In
Section

4
we show that the quantum polymorphisms of cliques are non-contextual, which gives a different proof of the undecidability of the corresponding entangled CSPs due to
[
22
,
13
]
. In
section

5
, we prove a general result on the consequence of contextuality in quantum homomorphisms, by showing that it causes the emergence of a specific
non-orthogonality
pattern in the underlying structures. We make use of this pattern in
section

6
, to prove that odd cycles admit commutativity gadgets and, thus, the corresponding entangled CSP is undecidable. In
Section

7
, we address the non-oracular case, by showing that a similar characterisation to the one in
Section

3
captures commutativity gadgets in the non-oracular setting. In addition, we establish therein a quantum version of Galois connection for the non-oracular quantum analogue of pp-definitions. Finally, in
Section

8

We obtain that structures that do not admit the ternary majority as a polymorphism must have a commutativity gadget, and that apart from trivial cases, structures that do have the majority operation as a polymorphism do not have a commutativity gadget.

3.
Quantum polymorphisms and reductions

In this section, we show that reductions between entangled CSPs can be obtained via a quantised notion of pp-definitions that we call
q-definitions
. In order to prove that q-definitions exist, we make use of the notion of
commutativity gadgets
as defined in
[

We begin by defining q-definitions.

Definition 8

Let

\sigma

and

\sigma

defined by

for all

\mathbb{R}\to_{q}\mathbb{A}_{S}

Corollary 52

If

Now for the when

Lemma 53

Assume

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that

is the tuple with

If

Let

We define

Assume we have tuples

(\neg x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})\notin R_{t}

Now let

be a non-oracular polymorphism of

Then we have

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}\left(\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}Q_{\mathbf{q},y}\right)Q_{\mathbf{s}_{2},x_{2}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{q},y}Q_{\mathbf{s}_{2},x_{2}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}(Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{q},y})(Q_{\mathbf{q},y}Q_{\mathbf{s}_{2},x_{2}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}})

\displaystyle=(Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{q},x_{1}})(Q_{\mathbf{q},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{s}_{2},x_{2}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}})+(Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{q},\neg x_{1}})(Q_{\mathbf{q},\neg x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{s}_{2},x_{2}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}})

\displaystyle=(0)(Q_{\mathbf{q},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{s}_{2},x_{2}}\dots Q_{\mathbf{s}_{k},x_{k}})+(Q_{\mathbf{s}_{1},x_{1}}Q_{\mathbf{q},\neg x_{1}})(0)=0

where the penultimate equality follows from (
(

QH
1

)
). This proves that every non-oracular quantum polymorphism of

preserves

and thus

\qnoPol(\mathbb{O}_{t})\subseteq\qnoPol(\mathbb{O}_{(1,0,\dots,0)})

Note that this theorem also covers the case when

(or

Next, we argue that the only real cases we need to consider in the full theorem is the
\mathbb{O}_{t}

Lemma 54
.

Every relational structure
\mathbb{A}
with a relation
R
that has any full binary projection has the property that
\qnoPol(\mathbb{A})=\qcPol(\mathbb{A})
.

Proof.

Consider a relational structure with a relation
R
that has a full binary projection on the components
\{i,j\}
. If this is the case, then in any power, any two elements appear together in some tuple of the direct power of
R
.∎

Theorem 55
.

For any relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{k}
the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

R
is not invariant under majority, majority preserves every proper projection of
R
, and every binary projection of
R
is not full.

(2)

R=R_{t}
for some tuple
t
.

Proof.

2.
implies
1.
Note that translating a relation by a tuple does not change the size of the binary projections and does not change preservation by majority, since majority is a self-dual operation.
It follows that it suffices to prove the implication in the case of
R_{1/k}
, for which all the properties in 1. are clear.

For
1.
implies
2.
suppose
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{k}
is not invariant under majority but majority does preserve every proper projection and no binary projection is full. Suppose
t\in\{0,1\}^{k}
is a witness to the fact that
R
is not invariant under majority, we first show that
\{x\oplus_{2}t\mid x\in R_{1/k}\}\subseteq R
.

For
i\in[k]
, let
x\in R_{1/k}
be the tuple with a 1 in the
i
th position.
We know that
\pi_{[k]\setminus\{i\}}(R)
is invariant under majority so there is an
r_{i}\in R
with
\pi_{[k]\setminus\{i\}}(r_{i})=\pi_{[k]\setminus\{i\}}(t)
.
Now, since
t\notin R
; we can conclude that
\pi_{\{i\}}(r_{i})\neq\pi_{\{i\}}(t)

and therefore
x\oplus_{2}t=r_{i}
, so
x\oplus_{2}t\in R
.

Finally, we use the non-fullness of binary projections to argue that any
r\in R
must match
\overline{t}=(1,\dots,1)\oplus_{2}t
in exactly one argument, placing it in
\{x\oplus_{2}t\mid x\in R_{1/k}\}
.

Assume that
r
matches
\overline{t}
in two arguments
1\leq i<j\leq k
. We know that
\pi_{i,j}(R)
contains
\pi_{i,j}(t)
,
(\pi_{i}(t),\pi_{j}(\overline{t}))
and
(\pi_{i}(\overline{t}),\pi_{j}(t))
; then
\pi_{i,j}(R)
is full, a contradiction. Additionally,
r
cannot match
\overline{t}
on zero arguments since then it would equal
t
, which is not in
R
. Thus every
r
is in
\{x\oplus_{2}t\mid x\in R_{1/k}\}
, completing the proof.
∎

We can now easily prove the general result.

Theorem 56
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is a Boolean relational structure such that
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
does not contain majority, then
\qnoPol(\mathbb{A})=\qcPol(\mathbb{A})
.

Proof.

Consider a relational structure
\mathbb{A}
; without loss of generality, every projection of a relation of
\mathbb{A}
is also a relation of
\mathbb{A}
, since this does not change the hypothesis or the conclusion of the statement.
Let
R
be a relation of
\mathbb{A}
that is not preserved by majority and of minimal arity.
By
Lemma

54
, if a binary projection of
R
is full then we are done, so assume that this is not the case. Then
Theorem

55

implies that
R=R_{t}
for some
k
-tuple
t
.

Finally, we know that
k\geq 3
since every binary relation of arity 2 is preserved under majority and so, most of the result follows from Corollary
52
and Lemma
53
. All that remains is for us to argue that Lemma
52
also covers the case where
\overline{t}\in R_{1/k}
.

This is since,
\mathbb{O}_{t}
has ‘essentially’ the same polymorphisms as

for any

. The formal fact we use is that

is a non-oracular quantum polymorphism of

∎

8.2.
The minimal contextual clone

In this section, we provide a clone with contextual polymorphisms that is contained within the quantum polymorphism clone of any Boolean relational structure with contextual polymorphisms.

In the previous section, we have seen that every boolean relational structure with contextual polymorphisms has non-full binary projections and contains majority in its (classical) polymorphism clone.
