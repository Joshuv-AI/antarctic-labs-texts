Glutathione-Responsive Disulfiram Delivery from Thiol-Functionalized N-Doped Carbon Nanodots: An in vitro Proof-of-Concept Study
Authors: Mohadese Panaei, Mohamad Mahani, Faten Divsar, Faeze Khakbaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and evaluate a glutathione (GSH)-responsive “Off–On” drug delivery system based on thiol-functionalized N-doped carbon dots (SNCDs) for controlled intracellular release of disulfiram (DSF).Materials and MethodsSNCDs were synthesized using a solvothermal technique and subsequently characterized using dynamic laser scattering, high-resolution electron imaging, UV–Vis spectrophotometry, and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). DSF was immobilized onto SNCDs through redox-cleavable disulfide linkages, and loading efficiency was determined by UV–Vis analysis. The in vitro drug release kinetics were examined under simulated physiological (pH 7.4) and tumor-relevant acidic (pH 5.4) conditions, evaluating the impact of glutathione (GSH) by performing experiments with and without its inclusion. Cytotoxicity and compatibility studies in MCF-7 breast tumor cells, evaluated via MTT test, were used to investigate SNCD biocompatibility and the anticancer performance of DSF-SNCDs.ResultsThe FTIR spectra confirmed effective thiol functionalization and revealed oxygenated and nitrogenous groups on the particle surface suitable for disulfide linkage formation. DSF loading efficiency reached approximately 75%. Under non-reducing conditions, DSF-SNCDs exhibited minimal premature release (≤30% over 24 h at pH 7.4 and 5.4), demonstrating an “Off” state. In contrast, the presence of 5% (w/v) GSH at pH 5.4 triggered rapid DSF release, achieving approximately 80% cumulative release within 24 h, corresponding to the “On” state through reductive disulfide bond cleavage. Bare SNCDs showed high biocompatibility (>85% cell viability at 50 µg/mL), whereas DSF-SNCDs produced significantly enhanced cytotoxicity under GSH conditions, confirming redox-activated drug release.ConclusionSNCDs provide an effective GSH-responsive nanoplatform for DSF delivery, combining high drug loading, excellent stability under non-reducing conditions, and efficient intracellular drug release under reductive environments. This strategy minimizes premature drug leakage while enhancing anticancer activity, demonstrating strong potential for cancer therapy.

Introduction

Globally, cancer remains a critical public health crisis, consistently ranking as a primary cause of mortality despite significant advancements in modern medicine.1 Traditional chemotherapy is frequently constrained by a lack of selectivity, hindering its broader clinical utility. To overcome these limitations, targeting the tumor microenvironment (TME) offers a promising alternative. Unlike conventional methods, TME-responsive strategies enable more precise, localized drug delivery. This approach leverages the distinct physiological hallmarks of malignant cells—such as elevated concentrations of reactive oxygen species (ROS), increased glutathione (GSH) levels, acidic pH, and unique enzyme overexpression—to trigger drug release only within the pathological site, thereby distinguishing them from healthy tissues.2,3 For instance, nanocarriers engineered with dual-responsive motifs can facilitate the controlled release of drug in response to specific redox potential (glutathione overexpression) and localized acidic environments.4,5 In contrast to low-micromolar extracellular/plasma GSH in humans (typically about 1–6 μM, with a representative value of 2.8 ± 0.91 μM reported in healthy adults), intracellular GSH in tumor cells is generally in the millimolar range, commonly about 1–10 mM, ie, roughly 103-fold higher than plasma and redox-sensitive advanced drug delivery systems were formulated by taking advantage of the elevated GSH concentration in tumor cells.6–8

Clinicians have utilized disulfiram (DSF) to treat alcohol use disorder for more than half a century.9 However, it has also shown promise as an anticancer agent due to its ability to inhibit multiple signaling pathways involved in cancer progression and metastasis. Numerous studies have demonstrated the strong anticancer potential of this compound against a broad spectrum of malignancies.10 Despite these potential benefits, DSF’s clinical translation has been limited by poor solubility and low bioavailability. To overcome these challenges and enhance the therapeutic efficacy of DSF in cancer treatment, researchers have explored nanoparticle-based delivery systems.11 Although encapsulating or conjugating DSF onto nanoparticles like polymer nanoparticles, liposomes, and nano-emulsions includes several advantages such as enhancing both the stability and solubility of DSF in physiological conditions, drug’s effectiveness, greater control over the release profile of the drug, prolonged circulation time within the body and provide opportunities for active targeting strategies by functionalizing their surfaces (this approach enables accumulation of DSF-loaded nanoparticles at tumor sites while minimizing off-target effects on healthy tissues) but many still suffer from premature leakage, poor aqueous dispersibility and….12,13 DSF can also encapsulated within carriers or nanoparticles through disulfide bridges. Hence, increased GSH levels inside cells can enhance the release of therapeutics from nanoparticles.14

Fluorescent carbon dots (CDs) loaded with therapeutic agents have been developed for various applications, including drug delivery and cellular imaging.15,16 This is mainly attributed to their biocompatibility and low toxicity, as well as distinctive optical properties, including remarkable photostability, and strong fluorescence emission that can be manipulated by adjusting particle size, surface chemistry, and excitation wavelength.17 In contrast to many conventional polymeric or inorganic nanocarriers, carbon dots can simultaneously serve as nanoscale drug-delivery platforms and intrinsically fluorescent components, enabling monitoring of their physicochemical behavior and cellular interactions. Their biological performance is strongly governed by surface chemistry; therefore, rational surface functionalization is essential to control drug loading, colloidal stability, cellular uptake, and stimulus-responsive release.18 Nitrogen doping was selected in the present study because incorporation of nitrogen-containing functionalities can modify the electronic structure, surface polarity, charge distribution, and density of reactive sites of carbon dots. These features may improve aqueous stability and provide additional hydrogen-bonding, electrostatic, and other noncovalent interactions with drug molecules. Nitrogen doping can also enhance the photoluminescence properties of carbon dots, thereby facilitating their characterization and potentially enabling optical tracking of the nanocarrier. Nevertheless, the contribution of nitrogen doping should be interpreted in the context of the whole surface structure, since the final properties of carbon dots depend on the introduction composition, reaction conditions, particle size, and distribution of surface groups.19,20 CDs coated with amino and carboxyl groups have been used as a carrier for doxorubicin (DOX) as a model drug.21 Pei et al22 designed a carbon dot-templated star-like copolymer prodrug system that achieves tumor-specific delivery via pH- and reduction-sensitive dual-release mechanisms. Nair et al23 reviewed the use of natural carbon-based quantum dots for drug delivery applications, highlighting their diverse functionality and biocompatibility. Wei et al24 reported the synthesis of green-emitting carbon quantum dots co-doped with nitrogen and sulfur, and showed their suitability for cellular imaging applications. Chung et al25 also designed nanoparticles composed of iron oxide and carbon dots that functioned as fluorescent agents for cancer imaging and drug delivery. Herein, the introduction of sulfur-containing or thiol-enriched surface functionalities was considered particularly relevant to the development of a redox-responsive disulfiram-delivery system. Thiol groups can alter the surface hydrophilicity, charge, and chemical reactivity of carbon dots and may provide additional interactions with disulfiram or with linker structures used for drug attachment. More importantly, thiol/disulfide chemistry used in intracellular redox-responsive delivery because the glutathione concentration is substantially higher in the cytosol than in the extracellular environment. In principle, GSH can participate in thiol–disulfide exchange reactions and destabilize disulfide-containing linkages, thereby promoting the release of a conjugated or associated payload. Thus, sulfur-containing surface modification was introduced to provide a chemically plausible basis for redox responsiveness, rather than merely to increase the loading capacity of the nanodots. Thus, thiol-functionalized nitrogen-doped carbon dots (SNCDs) were synthesized and conjugated with DSF. A GSH-triggered DSF-SNCD nano-delivery platform may improve the precision and release behavior of DSF in MCF-7 human tumor cells, ultimately enhancing its therapeutic potential. Under GSH-rich reducing conditions, the nanocarrier exhibited a stimulus-responsive release profile, with markedly accelerated DSF release compared with the GSH-free condition.

To our knowledge, this study presents the first GSH-responsive DSF-SNCD nanocarrier for targeted delivery of disulfiram to cells with increased GSH content. In addition, the anticancer toxicity of DSF-SNCDs was compared against the effect of unencapsulated DSF. This study highlights the versatility and potential of carbon dots as fluorescent probes for cell imaging and drug delivery applications.

Experimental

Materials

The citric acid (98% purity), urea, glutathione, and phosphate buffered saline (PBS) were obtained from Merck Company (Merck, Germany) and used without any further purification. Culture medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and trypsin-ethylenediamine tetra acetic acid X10 were prepared from Biosera Company (Biosera, England). Breast cancer cells MCF-7 cell were purchased from the National Cell Bank of Iran (Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran). Quinine hemisulfate (95% purity), disulfiram, dithiothreitol (DTT), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) (98% purity), and Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT, 98% purity) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany. All chemicals were analytical grade and used as supplied. Deionized water was employed for preparing all aqueous solutions.

Instrumentation

HRTEM images of the prepared SNCDs were obtained using a JEOL JEM-2100 instrument operated at 200 kV for structural characterization. The optical absorption spectra in the ultraviolet–visible region was recorded spectrophotometrically using an Agilent Cary 60 instrument (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Fluorescence spectra were measured by a spectrofluorometer (Carry Eclipse, Varian, Australia). FT-IR analysis was carried out on a TENSOR 27 instrument (Bruker, Germany) employing KBr pellets. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis by Zetasizer Nano S (Malvern Instruments Ltd., USA) was used to determine the average hydrodynamic particle size.

Synthesis of Nitrogen-Doped Carbon Dots (NCDs)

N-doped CDs were produced by dissolving citric acid (0.5 g) and urea (1.5 g) in 7 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide under constant stirring. The resulting uniform mixture was then poured into an autoclave lined with Teflon and subjected to heating at 160 °C for 4 h. The synthesis was accompanied by a pronounced color shift from colorless to dark brown, indicating the formation of nitrogen-doped carbon dots. Once cooling to ambient, the colloidal product was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 minutes to remove large aggregates. The supernatant was collected and dialyzed against distilled water for 24 hours using a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 3.5 kDa to remove unreacted precursors and small molecular impurities. The purified product was freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze-dryer, yielding a dark brown powder of NCDs, which was stored for further use. For characterization and subsequent functionalization, the NCDs were redispersed in distilled water at the desired concentration.

Preparation of Sulfur-Functionalized NCDs (SNCDs)

To sulfur functionalize the synthesized NCDs, 0.08 g of dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to 3 mL of an aqueous NCD colloid. The mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for 24 hours to ensure thorough functionalization. After the reaction, the sulfur-functionalized NCDs (SNCDs) were purified to remove unreacted DTT and other byproducts using a dialysis membrane (MWCO 3.5 kDa) against distilled water for 24 hours. After preparation, the SNCD colloid (10 mg/mL) was maintained at 4 °C for further studies.

Conjugation of Disulfiram (DSF) with SNCDs

The DSF-SNCDs were prepared by exploiting the formation of disulfide bonds between DSF and the thiol groups on the SNCDs. Specifically, 210 µL of the SNCDs colloid (10 mg/mL) was added dropwise to 3 mL of a DSF solution (250 µg/mL in distilled water) under vigorous stirring. The mixture was allowed to react for 24 hours at room temperature to ensure maximum loading of DSF onto the SNCDs. The colloid was then transferred into a dialysis membrane (MWCO 3.5 kDa) and dialyzed against distilled water overnight to remove unbound DSF. The concentration of free DSF in the dialysate was determined by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm spectrophotometrically, using a calibration curve constructed from standard DSF solutions in deionized water (Figure S1). The drug loading efficiency (DLE) was calculated based on the initial and final concentrations of DSF.

MTT Test

Cell viability was evaluated via the MTT reduction assay, following the established protocol.26 Initially, MCF-7 cell suspensions were aliquoted into 96-well culture plates at a seeding density of 5 × 103 cells/well, utilizing high-glucose DMEM fortified with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic reagents. The cultures were allowed to adhere for 24 h at 37 °C within a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Following this, the culture medium was exchanged for fresh medium incorporating varying concentrations of SNCDs, DSF-SNCDs, or pure DSF, and the plates were incubated for an additional 48 h. To quantify metabolic activity, the supernatant was removed, followed by a gentle PBS wash. Each well was then treated with 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL). Following a 4-hour incubation period at 37 °C, the MTT solution was aspirated, and 150 μL of DMSO was introduced to each well to facilitate the dissolution of the resulting formazan precipitates. After 10 minutes of mechanical agitation, the optical density was recorded at 570 nm utilizing a Wellscan Mk3 microplate reader (Labsystems Dragon Ltd, Finland).

Results and Discussion

The Mechanism of Redox-Responsive Drug Delivery System

It is well known that GSH plays a crucial role in the human body, where it can be oxidized to glutathione disulfide (GSSG) in a normal tissue microenvironment. In the presence of NADPH-dependent glutathione reductase, GSSG is regenerated to GSH, thereby supporting redox equilibrium essential for cellular function and survival.27 By comparison, tumor cells generally maintain much higher intracellular GSH levels (approximately 2–20 mM) because of their altered microenvironment, growth-related nutrient demands, and genetic abnormalities.4,28 The key goal of this work is to construct a nanocarrier that remains stable in non-reducing environments (“Off” state) while rapidly releasing DSF in reducing cancer cells media (“On” state). Scheme 1 clearly depicts the release behavior of the redox-responsive drug delivery system. Thiol-functionalized N-doped carbon quantum dots (SNCDs) provide a multifunctional surface suitable for DSF immobilization through disulfide chemistry. In healthy tissues media (low reducing power), disulfide linkages remain intact, limiting DSF leakage. The nanocarrier accumulates in tumor tissues through the enhanced permeability and retention effect during prolonged blood circulation in the presence of strong redox potential, the responsive linker decomposes, resulting in the collapse of the nanocarrier structure and prompt liberation of the encapsulated drugs. This mechanism allows for precise delivery, rapid drug release, and minimal side effects, leveraging the reduction potential gradient between cancerous and healthy cells. Characterization of SNCDs and DSF-SNCDs

We utilized a straightforward one-pot hydrothermal technique to produce NCDs, with citric acid serving as the primary carbon source, urea as the nitrogen source. For thiol functionalization of NCDs and facilitate their attachment to the drug via a covalent disulfide bond, DTT was utilized. NCDs typically exhibit significant ultraviolet absorption band with tails even reach into the visible spectrum.29 When the SNCDs are exposed to light from a 365 nm lamp, they exhibit a distinct and vibrant blue color. The particle size, surface engineering, and heteroatom doping of SNCDs strongly influence their optical properties, thereby expanding their applicability. Among these factors, nitrogen doping is particularly beneficial because it enhances aqueous solubility without the need for additional agents and simultaneously improves the photoluminescence efficiency of carbon dots.30 The UV-Vis spectrum of our SNCDs solution (Figure 1) exhibits absorption band at wavelength of 240 nm, which can be attributed to the π-π* transition of the aromatic C=C sp2 domains.20 The other strong absorption peak at 340 is assigned to the n–π* transition of possible C=O or C=N groups present in SNCDs structure, and the shoulder around 480 nm is given to multiple surface defect states.31 Similar to previous reports,32,33 the peak at 620 nm shows that our SNCDs are in an oxidized state. It was observed that the optical features of the carbon dots are significantly dictated by the functional groups present on their surface. In vitro Release: PH Stability vs GSH-Triggered “Off–On” Switching

To evaluate controlled release behavior and stimulus responsiveness, cumulative DSF release from DSF‑SNCDs was investigated (Figure 6). Free DSF exhibited a rapid burst release, with >90% diffusion into the medium within ~15 h, reflecting weak retention in the absence of a carrier. In contrast, DSF‑SNCDs displayed substantially suppressed release at both pH 7.4 and pH 5.4 in the absence of reductant, with cumulative release remaining ≤~30% after 24 h. The relatively small difference between pH 7.4 and 5.4 indicates that pH alone does not govern drug liberation, confirming that DSF is largely retained on the carrier (“Off” state) under non-reducing conditions.

Upon introduction of GSH (5% w/v in outside solution of dialysis bag) at pH 5.4, a pronounced increase in DSF release was observed, reaching ~80% at 24 h. This “On” state is attributed to GSH-mediated cleavage of disulfide linkages (reductive/thiol–disulfide exchange processes), enabling accelerated liberation of DSF in reducing environments. Collectively, these profiles demonstrate that DSF‑SNCDs behave primarily as a redox-responsive system rather than a purely pH-responsive carrier, thereby minimizing premature leakage while enabling intracellular activation.

In vitro Cytotoxicity and Biocompatibility

The biocompatibility of the carrier and the redox-triggered therapeutic action were assessed by MTT assay on MCF‑7 cells (Figure 7). Previous studies presented that nitrogen doped carbon dots were not cytotoxicity to MCF-7 cells.20 As shown in Figure 7, Bare SNCDs (CQD) showed excellent cytocompatibility, maintaining near-normal cell viability across the tested concentrations and remaining >~85% viable at 50 µg/mL, indicating low inherent toxicity of the nanocarrier. Free DSF exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity, as expected. Notably, DSF‑loaded SNCDs (CQD‑D) in the absence of GSH caused lower cytotoxicity than free DSF, consistent with restricted DSF availability in the “Off” state. However, in the presence of GSH (CQD‑D + G), cell viability decreased sharply in a concentration-dependent manner; importantly, viability dropped to ~50% at 6.25 µg/mL, confirming that reducing conditions unlock the drug activity. This behavior directly supports the “Off–On” release mechanism observed in the release studies (Figure 6) and indicates that SNCDs can effectively deliver DSF in a GSH-rich environment, potentially enhancing therapeutic selectivity.

Study Limitations

In the in vitro release experiments, a reduced glutathione (GSH) concentration of 5% w/v (~163 mM) was introduced into the external release medium (outside the dialysis bag). While the effective local concentration experienced by the nanocarrier is modulated by the dialysis membrane molecular weight cut-off (MWCO), diffusion kinetics, and compartment volume ratios, we acknowledge that this represents a supranormal, accelerated reducing challenge rather than a direct simulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME). The release data presented herein should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence demonstrating the intrinsic redox responsiveness and cleavage kinetics of the nanocarrier under strong reducing stress, rather than as a direct quantitative prediction of in vivo drug release behavior. Future studies will systematically evaluate drug release and stability across a physiologically relevant GSH gradient to comprehensively elucidate the in vivo-mimicking release kinetics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, thiol-functionalized nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (SNCDs) were synthesized and evaluated as a glutathione (GSH)-responsive nanocarrier for disulfiram (DSF) delivery based on redox-cleavable linkages. Spectrophotometric analysis demonstrated an efficient drug loading capacity of ~75%. In vitro release profiles exhibited comparatively low drug liberation under non-reducing physiological and mildly acidic conditions (pH 7.4 and 5.4; ≤30% at 24 h), whereas the presence of GSH markedly triggered DSF release (~80% at 24 h). Cell viability assays indicated the cytocompatibility of blank SNCDs and demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity of DSF-SNCDs against MCF-7 cells under redox-active conditions, which is consistent with the trigger-dependent release behavior observed in cell-free assays. Overall, these preliminary in vitro findings demonstrate the proof-of-concept potential of DSF-SNCDs for redox-responsive drug delivery with suppressed premature leakage. However, comprehensive cellular uptake studies, intracellular mechanistic analyses, and in vivo evaluations remain essential to fully determine their systemic biosafety, biodistribution, and therapeutic applicability.
