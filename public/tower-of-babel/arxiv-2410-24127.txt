More global randomness from less-random local gates
Ryotaro Suzuki, Hosho Katsura, Yosuke Mitsuhashi, Tomohiro Soejima, Jens Eisert, Nobuyuki Yoshioka

Abstract
Random circuits giving rise to unitary designs are key tools in quantum information science and many-body physics. In this work, we investigate a class of random quantum circuits with a specific gate structure. Within this framework, we prove that one-dimensional structured random circuits with non-Haar random local gates can exhibit substantially more global randomness compared to Haar random circuits with the same underlying circuit architecture. In particular, under a solvability condition, we exactly diagonalize the second-moment operators for these structured random circuits by establishing a link to free-fermionic models, and we show that their spectral gaps can exceed those of Haar-random circuits. While solvable structured random circuits exhibit free-fermionic behavior, the underlying fixed gate need not be a matchgate. Our findings have applications in improving circuit depth bounds for randomized benchmarking, in generating approximate unitary 2-designs from shallow random circuits, and in enabling exact analyses of quantum many-body dynamics.

II
Setup for structured random circuits.

We investigate how the precise structure of random local gates affects the global randomness of an entire system, and to this end we introduce models of random circuits with non-Haar random gates. Concretely, we consider a random two-qudit gate generated by fixing a two-qudit gate
u
and sandwiching it by single-qudit Haar random gates acting on the inputs and the outputs, as shown in Fig.
1
(a), which we call
u
-structured random gates
.
Formally, let
u\in\mathrm{U}(d^{2})
be a fixed two-qudit unitary operation and let
\nu_{\textrm{H}}
denote the Haar measure on
U(d)
.
A
u
-structured random gate is a random unitary
U\in\mathrm{U}(d^{2})
generated as follows:

U=(v_{1}\otimes v_{2})\,u\,(v_{3}\otimes v_{4}),\qquad v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\;{\sim}\;\nu_{\mathrm{H}}.

(1)

We consider a one-dimensional system of
n
qudits, where the local dimension is
d
.
A sequence of random gates specifies a probability distribution
\nu
in the unitary group, which we interchangeably refer to as the random circuit.
We consider the following circuit architectures of one-dimensional structured random circuits: (1)
u
-structured local random circuit

\nu_{u}^{L}
, where we pick a neighboring pair of qudits uniformly at random and apply a
u
-structured random gate, which is illustrated in
Fig.
1
(a),
(2)
u
-structured brick-wall random circuit

\nu_{u}^{B}
, where we apply staggered two layers of
u
-structured two-qudit gates on nearest neighbors, which is illustrated in
Fig.
1
(b).
For technical simplicity, we assume that the number of qudits

is even and the system satisfies the periodic boundary condition.
To exclude a trivial case, we also assume that
n>2
.
For a nearest-neighbour pair of sites
(i,i+1)
and a two-qudit unitary gate
G
, we write
G_{i,i+1}
for the two-qudit unitary acting nontrivially on qudits
i
and
i+1
and as the identity on all other sites.
Formally, we define structured random circuits as follows.

Definition 1

(
u
-structured random circuit)
.

Let us consider
n
-qudit one-dimensional systems

(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
with even
n
under periodic boundary conditions, where

.
Let
\mu_{u}
be the distribution of the two-qudit structured random gate with
u

\mathrm{U}(d^{2})
.
The local and brick-wall architectures are defined as follows.

1.

u
-structured local random circuit.
A random unitary
U\sim\nu_{u}^{L}
is generated by first drawing a site index

i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
uniformly at random and then sampling a two-qudit gate

G\sim\mu_{u}

U:=G_{i,i+1}
.

2.

u
-structured brick-wall random circuit.
The one-layer distribution
\nu_{u}^{B}
on

is defined by

U:=\left(\,\bigotimes_{i:\,\mathrm{odd}}G_{i,i+1}\right)\left(\,\bigotimes_{i:\,\mathrm{even}}G_{i,i+1}\right),

(2)

where all two-qudit gates

are drawn independently from

and the index

For local random circuits, we apply single-qudit Haar random unitaries on all qudits for technical convenience

We also define local and brick-wall Haar random circuits by

II.1
Characterization of randomness.

Here, we characterize randomness using the moment operator

[
34
,
42
]
.
Given a random circuit
\nu
, the second-moment operator is defined by

M_{\nu}:=\int U^{\otimes 2}\otimes{U^{*}}^{\otimes 2}d\nu(U),

(3)

where
{U}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
U
.
We denote moment operators for
u
-structured local and brick-wall random circuits in one step by
M_{L,u}
and
M_{B,u}
, respectively. For Haar random circuits, they are defined by replacing the subscript
u
by
\mathrm{H}
. The moment operators of these circuits with
t
steps, or
t
depth, are obtained by multiplying them
t
times, namely
\left(M_{a,u}\right)^{t}
for
a=L,B
.
We apply single-qudit Haar random unitaries on all qudits in both architectures, which become the projector onto the
2^{n}
-dimensional subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\ket{I},\ket{S}\}^{\otimes n}
by the fact that the second-moment twirling map is the projector onto the subspace spanned by the permutation operators
[
44
,
16
]
.
Here,
\ket{I}
and
\ket{S}
are the vectorization of the identity and SWAP operators acting on
\mathbb{C}^{d^{2}}
.
It is thus enough to consider
M_{a,u}
restricted to the subspace, for
a=L,B
.
Later, we show the diagonalization results in this subspace.
We write the eigenvalues of
M_{\nu}
in non-increasing order in absolute value
|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq\cdots
, where we have
\lambda_{i}=1
for
i=1,2
.
The largest eigenvalue is
1
, and the corresponding
1
-eigenspace includes the subspace
V_{1}
spanned by the
n
-fold vectorization of the identity and SWAP gate, that is,

V_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{I}^{\otimes n},\ket{S}^{\otimes n}\},

(4)

where
n
is the number of qudits.
This subspace
V_{1}
turns out to be the entire
1
-eigenspace if a fixed
u
is an entangling gate, because an entangling gate and the single-qubit gates form a universal gate set
[
17
]
, and then the
1
-eigenspace of
M_{\nu}
is equal to that of the second-moment operator of the
n
-qudit Haar random unitary
M_{\mathrm{H}}

[
16
]
, where the
1
-eigenspace of
M_{\mathrm{H}}
is exactly
V_{1}
.

We focus on structured random gates with an entangling gate
u
.
The gap between the largest and second-largest eigenvalues of
M_{\nu}
in absolute value, which is
1-|\lambda_{3}|
, is called the
spectral gap
, denoted by
\Delta_{\nu}
.
The eigenvalues of
M_{\nu}
determine the convergence rate to unitary 2-designs

[
42
,
16
,
22
,
54
]
, where unitary
k
-designs are ensembles whose
k
-th moment agrees with that of global Haar random unitaries
[
34
]
. Concretely, when the eigenvalues of
M_{\nu}
are small and hence its spectral gap
is large, we have fast convergence to unitary 2-designs.

We then formalize what “more randomness” means in two ways, where the former is based on the spectral gap, and the latter is rooted in the entire property of second-moment operators.

Definition 2

(More random with
respect to spectral gaps)
.

Let
\nu
and
\mu
be probability distributions of random circuits.
The distribution
\nu
is said to be more random with respect to spectral gap
than
\mu
if
\Delta_{\nu}>\Delta_{\mu}
.

Definition 3

(More random with respect to positivity)
.

When
M_{\nu}
and
M_{\mu}
are Hermitian operators,

\nu
is said to be more random with respect to positivity
than
\mu
if the restriction of the operator
\left.M_{\mu}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}}-\left.M_{\nu}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}}
is a positive operator,
where
{V_{1}^{\perp}}
is the orthogonal complement of
V_{1}
.

The spectral gaps of the ensembles forming exact unitary 2-designs are
1
, and, therefore, they are the most random ensembles by Definition
2
.
We note that
M_{L,u}
is always Hermitian, as discussed later.
More generally, when the circuit architecture of
\nu
is invariant under the time reflection,
M_{\nu}
is Hermitian.
We note if
\lambda_{3}\geq 0
for both random circuits
\nu
and
\mu
, and
\nu
is more random with respect to positivity than
\mu
, we have that
\nu
is also more random with respect to spectral gap than
\mu
,
where this follows from Weyl’s monotonicity theorem
[
13
]
.

We remark on the definition based on positivity.
One particular feature of Haar random unitaries is that an expectation value is highly concentrated around its mean value
[
62
]
. In this context, the bound on the second-moment implies the bound on the variance.
Specifically, more randomness of
\nu
than
\mu
with respect to positivity implies that, for a state vector
\ket{\psi}
, the probability distribution of
|\bra{\psi}U\ket{\psi}|^{2}
with
U
drawn from a structured random circuit
\nu
is more concentrated around its mean value than that of another structured random circuit
\mu
.
This kind of concentration bound has been
used, for example, to prove the equilibration of random
product states
[
36
]
and
lower-bound the circuit complexity of the random unitary ensemble
[
15
,
39
]
.

Entanglement and moment operators

1
]
, which is the linear entanglement entropy of a random product state applied by

, and its complementary quantity

, called the gate typicality
[

,
1
,
52
]
.

III.1
Entangling power and gate typicality

One of the common possible ways to define the entangling power of a unitary is as follows: The entangling power
e_{u}
of a two-qudit unitary
u
is quantified by the amount of entanglement of random product states applied by
u

\displaystyle e_{u}=\frac{d+1}{d-1}\int E_{\textrm{lin}}(u\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\phi})d\!\ket{\psi}d\!\ket{\phi},

(5)

where the integration is over the single-qudit Haar random state and
E_{\textrm{lin}}(\ket{\Psi})\coloneqq 1-\Tr\rho_{A}^{2}
, with
\rho_{A}=\Tr_{B}{\ket{\Psi}\!\bra{\Psi}}
, is the linear entanglement entropy. Here,
\ket{\Psi}
is a state in a two-qudit system, where we have defined the subsystems
A
and
B
by the individual qudits. A similar entanglement measure of a unitary
u
is the operator entanglement entropy.
Let
\ket{u}
be the normalized vectorization of a two-qudit unitary
u
,
where
\ket{u}=({1}/{d^{2}})(u_{A_{1},B_{1}}\otimes I_{A_{2},B_{2}})\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\ket{i,j,i,j}_{A_{1},B_{1},A_{2},B_{2}}
, and
\rho_{A}(u)=\Tr_{B_{1},B_{2}}{\ket{u}\bra{u}}
.
Then, the operator entanglement
[
77
]
of
u
is defined by

\displaystyle E(u)\coloneqq 1-\Tr\rho_{A}(u)^{2},

(6)

and this is equal to

\displaystyle E(u)=1-\frac{\bra{I}\otimes\bra{S}u^{\otimes 2,2}\ket{I}\otimes\ket{S}}{d^{4}}.

(7)

Since
\Tr\rho_{A}(u)^{2}
is the purity of
\rho_{A}(u)
, the operator entanglement is bounded as
0\leq E(u)\leq 1-{1}/{d^{2}}
.
As shown in Ref.
[
92
]
, the entangling power of a two-qudit unitary
u
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle e_{u}=\frac{1}{E(\text{SWAP})}\left(E(u)+E(u\cdot\text{SWAP})-E(\text{SWAP})\right),

(8)

where
SWAP
denotes the two-qudit swap gate, and it takes the maximum of the operator entanglement as

\displaystyle E(\text{SWAP})=1-\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

(9)

Throughout this work, we use
SWAP
for the two-qudit SWAP gate acting on one physical system and
S
for the swap operation acting on two copies of physical systems.
For two-qubit gate
u
, the entangling power can take all the value within
0\leq e_{u}\leq 2/3
, where, for example, the CNOT gate and the iSWAP gate have the maximum value
e_{u}=2/3
.
For two-qudit gate
u
with local dimension
d\geq 3
, the value takes
0\leq e_{u}\leq 1
.
For Haar random two-qudit unitary gates, the averaged entangling power is

\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{H}}\coloneq\int e_{u}d\mu_{\mathrm{H}}(u)=\frac{d^{2}-1}{d^{2}+1}.

(10)

For example, in the case of qubit-system, we have
e_{\mathrm{H}}=3/5
.

In addition, the
gate typicality

g_{u}

[
51
,
1
,
52
]
, which is a complementary quantity to
e_{u}
, is defined by

\displaystyle g_{u}=\frac{1}{2E(\text{SWAP})}\left(E(u)-E(u\cdot\text{SWAP})+E(\text{SWAP})\right).

(11)

For two-qudit gate
u
with local dimension
d\geq 2
, the value takes
0\leq g_{u}\leq 1
, where
g_{u}=1
and
g_{u}=0
if
u
is the two-qudit SWAP gate and the identity gate, respectively.
For Haar random two-qudit unitary gates, the averaged gate typicality is

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{H}}\coloneq\int g_{u}d\mu_{\mathrm{H}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}.

(12)

We finally remark that there are limitations of feasible
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
, i.e., there does not necessarily exist a unitary gate
u
for
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
satisfying the conditions above.
For a two-qubit unitary
u
, the values
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
are feasible if and only if they satisfy
2g_{u}(1-g_{u})\leq e_{u}\leq 2g_{u}
,
e_{u}\leq 2-2g_{u}
, and
e_{u}\leq\frac{2}{3}

[
52
]
.
For a two-qudit unitary with
d\geq 3
, the constraint
e_{u}\leq\frac{2}{3}
is replaced by
e_{u}\leq 1
, and the ranges of
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
are numerically shown in Ref.
[
52
]
, see the reference for more details.
In general, for an arbitrary two-qudit gate

, we have

\displaystyle e_{u}\leq 2-\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\textrm{H}}},

(13)

\displaystyle e_{u}\leq\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\textrm{H}}},

which follow from the definitions of
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
with the bound
0\leq E(u)\leq E(\textrm{SWAP})
.
The two-qudit unitary gates satisfying

are called dual-unitary gates
[

,

,

,

]
, and those satisfying

are called perfect tensors

]

III.2
Second-moment of structured random gates

Here, we relate the second-moment operators of
u
-structured random gates with the entangling power and the gate typicality.
The second moment operator of the
u
-structured random gate is

\displaystyle W^{u}\coloneq\int\left(\big(v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\big)u\big(v_{3}\otimes v_{4}\big)\right)^{\otimes 2,2}\prod_{i=1}^{4}d\nu_{\mathrm{H}}(v_{i}),

(14)

where
\nu_{\mathrm{H}}
is the Haar measure on the single-qudit unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
and we use the notation
U^{\otimes k,k}=U^{\otimes k}\otimes\left(U^{*}\right)^{\otimes k}
.
The second-moment operators of
u
-structured random circuits with the local and the brick-wall architectures are written as, respectively,

\displaystyle M^{(2)}_{L,u}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W^{u}_{i,i+1},

(15)

\displaystyle M^{(2)}_{B,u}

\displaystyle=\left(\,\bigotimes_{i:\,\mathrm{odd}}W^{u}_{i,i+1}\right)\left(\,\bigotimes_{i:\,\mathrm{even}}W^{u}_{i,i+1}\right),

(16)

where
W_{i,i+1}
is the operator acting nontrivially on sites
i
and
i+1
as
W
and as the identity on all other sites, and by periodic boundary conditions we have that
W^{u}_{n,n+1}=W^{u}_{n,1}
. They are direct consequences of the definition of the architectures: the local circuit architecture randomly picks up
i
and then applies
W_{i,i+1}
, which yields Eq. (
15
), and the brick-wall architecture applies
W_{i,i+1}
, for odd or even
i
, which yields Eq. (
16
).

We can integrate over the single-qudit unitaries in Eq. (
14
) using the Weingarten calculus
[
20
]
, to get

\displaystyle\int v^{\otimes 2,2}d\nu_{\mathrm{H}}(v)

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\big(\outerproduct{I}{I}+\outerproduct{S}{S}-\frac{1}{d}\big(\outerproduct{I}{S}+\outerproduct{S}{I}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\outerproduct*{\widetilde{I}}{I}+\outerproduct*{\widetilde{S}}{S}

where we have defined

\displaystyle\ket*{\widetilde{I}}\coloneq\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\left(\ket{I}-\frac{1}{d}\ket{S}\right),

(18)

\displaystyle\ket*{\widetilde{S}}\coloneq\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\left(\ket{S}-\frac{1}{d}\ket{I}\right).

The pair of the sets
\{\ket{I},\ket{S}\}
and
\{\ket*{\widetilde{I}},\ket*{\widetilde{S}}\}
is a biorthogonal system because they satisfy
\innerproduct*{\sigma}{\widetilde{\tau}}=\delta_{\sigma,\tau}
, where
\delta_{\sigma,\tau}=1
for
\sigma=\tau
and
\delta_{\sigma,\tau}=0
for
\sigma\neq\tau
.
We also introduce the orthonormal basis

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{0}}\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2d(d-1)}}\left(\ket{I}-\ket{S}\right),

(19)

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{1}}\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2d(d+1)}}\left(\ket{I}+\ket{S}\right).

In this basis representation, we have that

\displaystyle\int v^{\otimes 2,2}\,d\nu_{\mathrm{H}}(v)\,=\,\outerproduct{\overline{0}}{\overline{0}}\,+\,\outerproduct{\overline{1}}{\overline{1}}.

(20)

With the orthonormal basis
\{\ket{\overline{0}},\ket{\overline{1}}\}
, Eq. (
14
) becomes

\displaystyle W^{u}=\sum_{s_{i}\in\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}}W^{u}(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})\ket{{s}_1}\otimes\ket{{s}_2}\bra{s_3}\otimes\bra{s_4},

(21)

where we have defined

W^{u}(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})=\big(\bra{s_1}\otimes\bra{s_2}\big)u^{\otimes 2,2}\big(\ket{{s_3}}\otimes\ket{{s_4}}\big).

(22)

We now express the matrix elements of
W^{u}
in terms of the entangling power and the gate typicality. To this end, by Eqs. (
8
) and (
11
), we calculate that

\displaystyle E(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}-1}{2d^{2}}(e_{u}+2g_{u}),

(23)

\displaystyle E(u\cdot\text{SWAP})

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}-1}{2d^{2}}(e_{u}-2g_{u}+2),

where we have used
E(\text{SWAP})=(d^{2}-1)/d^{2}
. Then, we obtain by Eq. (
7
),

\displaystyle\bra{I}\otimes\bra{S}u^{\otimes 2,2}\ket{I}\otimes\ket{S}

\displaystyle=d^{4}\left(1-\frac{d^{2}-1}{2d^{2}}(e_{u}+2g_{u})\right),

(24)

\displaystyle\bra{I}\otimes\bra{S}u^{\otimes 2,2}\ket{S}\otimes\ket{I}

\displaystyle=d^{4}\left(1-\frac{d^{2}-1}{2d^{2}}(e_{u}-2g_{u}+2)\right).

We represent the operator
W^{u}
in the orthonormal basis
\bigl\{\ket{\overline{0},\overline{0}},\;\ket{\overline{0},\overline{1}},\;\ket{\overline{1},\overline{0}},\;\ket{\overline{1},\overline{1}}\bigr\}
,
where
\ket{s_1,s_2}=\ket{s_1}\otimes\ket{s_2}
with
s_{i}\in\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}
and
\ket{\overline{0}}
and
\ket{\overline{1}}
are defined in Eq. (
19
).
In this ordered basis, from Eq. (
24
), the matrix form of
W^{u}
is

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1-\dfrac{d+1}{2(d-1)}\,e_{u}&0&0&\dfrac{e_{u}}{2}\\
0&1-g_{u}&g_{u}&0\\
0&g_{u}&1-g_{u}&0\\
\dfrac{e_{u}}{2}&0&0&1-\dfrac{d-1}{2(d+1)}\,e_{u}\end{pmatrix}.

(25)

We give the derivation of
\bra{\overline{0},\overline{0}}W^{u}\ket{\overline{0},\overline{0}}
below. The other matrix elements are obtained in a similar way.
Let
U:=u^{\otimes 2,2}
and use the orthonormal basis vector

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{0}}=a\bigl(\ket{I}-\ket{S}\bigr),

(26)

where
a={1}/{\sqrt{2d(d-1)}}
is the scalar coefficient. We then have that

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{0},\overline{0}}=a^{2}\Bigl(\ket{I,I}-\ket{I,S}-\ket{S,I}+\ket{S,S}\Bigr),

(27)

and by defining
c_{X}\in\{1,-1\}
as the coefficient of
\ket{X}
in the expansion above for
X=\{II,IS,SI,SS\}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\bra{\overline{0},\overline{0}}W^{u}\ket{\overline{0},\overline{0}}=a^{4}\!\!\sum_{X,Y\in\{II,IS,SI,SS\}}\!\!c_{X}c_{Y}\bra{X}U\ket{Y}.

(28)

Using
U\ket{I,I}=\ket{I,I}
and
U\ket{S,S}=\ket{S,S}
, we have that

\displaystyle\bra{\overline{0},\overline{0}}W^{u}\ket{\overline{0},\overline{0}}

\displaystyle=a^{4}\Bigl[4d^{2}(d-1)^{2}+\bra{I,S}U\ket{I,S}+\bra{S,I}U\ket{S,I}

\displaystyle+\bra{I,S}U\ket{S,I}+\bra{S,I}U\ket{I,S}-2d^{2}(d^{2}-1)\Bigr].

(29)

Substituting Eq. (
24
) to it,
we find that all terms proportional to
g_{u}
cancel out, and we obtain

\displaystyle\bra{\overline{0},\overline{0}}W^{u}\ket{\overline{0},\overline{0}}=1-\frac{d+1}{2(d-1)}\,e_{u}.

(30)

III.3
Solvability condition

We identify the condition under which a second-moment operator becomes a free-fermionic model.
To this end, we begin by observing that the operator
W^{u}
in Eq. (
25
) admits an expansion in the Pauli basis of the form

\displaystyle W^{u}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{e_{u}}{4e_{\mathrm{H}}}-\frac{g_{u}}{4g_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)\mathbb{I}\mathbb{I}-\frac{de_{u}}{2(d^{2}-1)}\bigl(Z\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}Z\bigr)

(31)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{e_{u}}{4e_{\mathrm{H}}}-\frac{g_{u}}{4g_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)ZZ+\frac{e_{u}+2g_{u}}{4}XX-\frac{e_{u}-2g_{u}}{4}YY,

with the qubit-Pauli operators
\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}
.

We find that if
u
-structured random gates satisfy the condition

\frac{e_{u}}{g_{u}}=\frac{e_{\mathrm{H}}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}},

(32)

the coefficient of the
ZZ
interaction in Eq. (
31
) becomes zero, and the operator
W^{u}
can be regarded as an interaction term of the
XY
model.
The
XY
model in a one-dimensional system can be mapped to free fermions via Jordan-Wigner transformation
[
61
]
, and the Hamiltonian is exactly diagonalizable.
Therefore, we call the condition Eq. (
32
) the
solvability condition
.
Under the solvability condition, we treat
e_{u}
as a free parameter and take
g_{u}
as a function of
e_{u}
. Clearly, the Haar random case where
e_{u}=e_{H}
and
g_{u}=g_{H}
satisfies the condition.

We now turn to identifying the two-qubit gates that satisfy the condition:
The two-qubit gates take the
general form

u=\exp{-(i/2)(\theta_x XX+\theta_y YY+\theta_z ZZ)}
, up to single-qubit gates, where
\theta_{x}
,
\theta_{y}
, and
\theta_{z}
are real numbers
[
59
]
.
Here, the choice of single-qubit gates is irrelevant to the moment operator, since structured random gates are sandwiched by single-qudit Haar random gates. With this parametrization, the solvability condition on the parameters is

f(\theta_{x},\theta_{y})+f(\theta_{y},\theta_{z})+f(\theta_{z},\theta_{x})=0,

(33)

where
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sin^{2}(x_{1})[\cos^{2}(x_{2})-{3}/{5}]
. For example, when
\theta_{x}=\theta_{y}=\theta_{z}
, the solvability condition yields
u=\exp{-i (\theta/2)( XX+YY+ZZ)}
with
\cos{ \theta}=\pm\sqrt{{3}/{5}}
.
A derivation of Eq. (
33
) is written in Appendix
B
.
We remark that we can map the moment operators to free-fermion chains under the solvability condition, although the two-qubit gates are in general different from matchgates
[
87
,
86
,
53
]
.
Intuitively, structured random circuits under the solvability condition are free-fermionic “on average”, while the individual instances are rather chaotic.

IV
Structured local random circuits

In this section, we diagonalize the second-moment operator of a
u
-structured random quantum circuit under the solvability condition.
We also show that its spectral gap increases monotonically with both the entangling power and the gate typicality.
We summarize the results of the section below.

To state the theorem, we define two sets as

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{k=\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{n}\,\middle|\,m=-\frac{n}{2}+1,-\frac{n}{2},\dots,\frac{n}{2}\right\},

(34)

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{k=\frac{2m\pi}{n}\,\middle|\,m=-\frac{n}{2}+1,-\frac{n}{2},\dots,\frac{n}{2}\right\},

where
n
is the number of qudits.
The exact spectrum is obtained under the solvability condition.

Theorem 1

(Exact spectrum)
.

Let
u
be a two-qudit unitary gate satisfying the solvability condition (
32
).
Then, the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
are given by

\displaystyle E_{u}(\{n_{k}\})=1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\epsilon_{k}n_{k},

(35)

where
p\in\{0,1\}
,
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in K_{p}}

\in\{0,1\}^{\times n}
such that
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}
is even, and

\epsilon_{k}\coloneq\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos k\right).

(36)

In particular, the spectral gap of
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
is

\Delta_{\mathrm{L},u}=\frac{2e_{u}}{ne_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\right).

(37)

The proof for Theorem
1
is given in Section
IV.2
.

Beyond the solvability condition, we obtain that the monotonicity of
u
-structured local random circuits with respect to the positivity and spectral gap as the entangling power and the gate typicality of
u
increase.
Specifically, we show that structured local random circuits with large
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
are more random than the corresponding Haar local random circuits.

Theorem 2

(More global and less local randomness)
.

Suppose a two-qudit unitary gate
u
satisfies both
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}>g_{\mathrm{H}}
. Then,
u
-structured local random circuits
\nu_{u}^{L}
are more random than the corresponding Haar local random circuits
\nu_{\mathrm{H}}^{L}
with respect to both the spectral gap and positivity, in the sense of Definitions
2
and
3
.

The proof for the part of positivity is provided in Section
IV.3
. The proof for the part of the spectral gap combines the results of Theorem
1
and more randomness with respect to positivity, which is given in Section
IV.4
.

IV.1
Exact diagonalization of
XY
models

We briefly review the exact diagonalization of
XY
models by the Jordan-Wigner transformation
[
61
]
.
Readers familiar with free-fermion techniques may skip this subsection and proceed directly to Section
IV.2
.

Let us consider an
n
-qubit system with an even number
n
. The Hamiltonian
H_{XY}
of a one-dimensional
XY
model is given by

-J\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1+\gamma}{2}X_{j}X_{j+1}+\frac{1-\gamma}{2}Y_{j}Y_{j+1}\right)-h\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j},

(38)

where we consider the regime
J>0
,
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and
h
is a real number. We impose the periodic boundary condition on the system.
We map the spin operators to fermionic operators
c_{j}
and
c_{j}^{\dagger}
by the Jordan-Wigner transformation:

c_{j}=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{j-1}-Z_{l}\right)\sigma_{j}^{-},

(39)

where the lowering operator is
\sigma_{j}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}-iY_{j})

and the raising operator is
\sigma_{j}^{+}=(X_{j}+iY_{j})/2
.
The fermionic operators satisfy the relations
\{c_{i},c_{j}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{i,j}
.
The periodic boundary condition, namely
\sigma_{n+1}^{\pm}=\sigma_{1}^{\pm}
, implies the following boundary condition for the fermionic operators:

c_{n+1}=-(-1)^{N}c_{1},

(40)

where
N=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}
is the total fermion number operator.

By this mapping, the Hamiltonian
H_{XY}
is written in terms of fermionic operators as

\displaystyle H_{XY}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(J(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^{\dagger}c_{j})

(41)

\displaystyle+{J\gamma}(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}^{\dagger}+c_{j+1}c_{j})+2hc_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}-h\Big).

The Hamiltonian commutes with the parity operator
(-1)^{N}
, and the system decomposes into two parity sectors: the even and odd sectors, where
(-1)^{N}=1
and
(-1)^{N}=-1
, respectively.
When the parity is even (odd), the boundary condition becomes
antiperiodic
c_{n+1}=-c_{1}
(periodic
c_{n+1}=c_{1}
).

The Hamiltonian can be diagonalized in the momentum space via a Fourier transform:

c_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{-ikj}c_{j}.

(42)

We use the same notation of
c
-fermions for the real and momentum spaces, as we believe that there will be no confusion.
In each parity sector, the values that
k
can take depend on the parity so that the fermionic operators satisfy the boundary conditions.
Concretely, the sets of the wave numbers
k
, denoted by
K_{p}
for
p=0,1
are defined by Eq. (
34
).
Due to the assumption that
n
is even, the values of
m
are integers in Eq. (
34
).
Here
p=0
(
p=1
) refers to the even (odd) sector.
With these definitions, the inverse Fourier transform is given by

c_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in K_{p}}e^{ikj}c_{k},

(43)

where
K_{p}
depends on the parity sector on which
c_{j}
acts.
We note that the total fermion number operator transforms as
N=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}=\sum_{k\in K_{p}}c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{k}
.
We can then write the Hamiltonian Eq. (
41
) in the momentum space as

H_{XY}=\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\left[\xi_{k}c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{k}-\frac{i}{2}\Delta_{k}(c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{-k}^{\dagger}-c_{-k}c_{k})\right]+nh,

(44)

where
\xi_{k}=-2(J\cos k+h)
and
\Delta_{k}=2J\gamma\sin k
.
The Hamiltonian has the
Bogoliubov-de Gennes
(BdG) form
[
24
]
,

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{k}&-i\Delta_{k}\\
i\Delta_{k}&-\xi_{k}\end{pmatrix}\Psi_{k},

(45)

where
\Psi_{k}=(c_{k},c_{-k}^{\dagger})^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}
is the Nambu spinor. The Bogoliubov transformation to quasiparticles
\Phi_{k}=(\eta_{k},\eta_{-k}^{\dagger})^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}
is defined by
\Phi_{k}=U_{k}\Psi_{k}
, with the unitary matrix

U_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}&-i\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}\\
-i\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}\end{pmatrix},

(46)

where
\cos\theta_{k}=\xi_{k}/\epsilon_{k}
,
\sin\theta_{k}=\Delta_{k}/\epsilon_{k}
, and
\epsilon_{k}=\sqrt{\xi_{k}^{2}+\Delta_{k}^{2}}
.
This transformation diagonalizes the BdG form, as

\displaystyle H_{XY}=\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\epsilon_{k}\left(\eta_{k}^{\dagger}\eta_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right),

(47)

with the single-particle energy

\epsilon_{k}=2\sqrt{(J\cos k+h)^{2}+(J\gamma\sin k)^{2}}.

(48)

In
p
-parity sector, the ground state vector of the Hamiltonian is obtained by

\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{p}=\left(c_{0}^{\dagger}\right)^{p}\prod_{k\in K_{p}:0<k<\pi}\left(\cos\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}+i\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{-k}^{\dagger}\right)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}_{c},

(49)

where
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}_{c}
is the vacuum of the fermions
c_{k}
, namely, for any
k\in K_{p}
,
c_{k}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}_{c}=0
.
Here, for the purpose of the subsequent discussion, we have assumed that
J>\absolutevalue{h}
and hence
\xi_{0}<0
, so that the zero-momentum mode must be occupied in the ground state, yielding
c_{0}^{\dagger}
in the odd sector (
p=1
) in Eq. (
49
).
The occupation of the
k=0
mode guarantees that the ground state in the odd sector contains an odd number of fermionic operators
c_{k}
.
The ground states satisfies
\eta_{k}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{p}=0
for any
k\in K_{p}
, and the corresponding eigenenergy is

E_{\mathrm{GS}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\epsilon_{k}.

(50)

Moreover, for occupation numbers
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in K_{p}}\in\{0,1\}^{\times n}
such that
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}
is even, the eigenvectors of
H_{XY}
are

\ket{\{n_k\}}=\prod_{k\in K_{p}}(\eta_{k}^{\dagger})^{n_{k}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{p},

(51)

and the corresponding eigenvalues are

\displaystyle E_{\{n_{k}\}}=E_{\mathrm{GS}}+\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\epsilon_{k}n_{k}.

(52)

Requiring
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}
to be even guarantees that the parity of
\ket{\{n_k\}}
coincides with that of the ground state.

IV.2
Exact diagonalization of structured local random circuits

We apply the exact diagonalization of the
XY
models to the second-moment operators of structured local random circuits under the solvability condition.
As we discussed in Section
III.2
, the second-moment operator of a
u
-structured local random circuit is

M_{\mathrm{L},u}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W^{u}_{i,i+1},

(53)

where
W^{u}_{i,i+1}
is given by Eq. (
25
). From Eq. (
31
), we have that

\displaystyle M_{L,u}=I-\frac{1}{n}H_{u},

(54)

where we write the Hamiltonian
H_{u}
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &H_{u}=\,n\!\left(\frac{e_{u}}{4e_{\mathrm{H}}}+\frac{g_{u}}{4g_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)I-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl(\frac{e_{u}+2g_{u}}{4}X_{i}X_{i+1}\\
&-\frac{e_{u}-2g_{u}}{4}\,Y_{i}Y_{i+1}-\left(\frac{e_{u}}{4e_{\mathrm{H}}}-\frac{g_{u}}{4g_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)\!Z_{i}Z_{i+1}-\frac{e_{u}d}{d^{2}-1}Z_{i}\Biggl)\end{aligned}

(55)

When the solvability condition holds, it becomes

\displaystyle H_{u}=\frac{ne_{u}}{2e_{\mathrm{H}}}I-\frac{e_{u}}{2(d^{2}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d^{2}X_{i}X_{i+1}+Y_{i}Y_{i+1}-2dZ_{i}).

(56)

Up to an additive constant, we identify this as the Hamiltonian of the
XY
model with the parameters

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{u}}{2e_{\mathrm{H}}}

(57)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=e_{\mathrm{H}}

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=-\frac{e_{u}d}{d^{2}-1}.

The parameters in the BdG form Eq. (
45
) are given by

\displaystyle\xi_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(\cos k-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\right),

(58)

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=e_{u}\sin k.

Then the Bogoliubov transformation turns out to be

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{k}\\
\eta_{-k}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}&-i\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}\\
-i\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{k}\\
c_{-k}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

(59)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon_{k}}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}+\xi_{k}}&-i\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}-\xi_{k}}\\
-i\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}-\xi_{k}}&\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}+\xi_{k}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{k}\\
c_{-k}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}.

We also obtain the single-particle energy from Eq. (
48
),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{h^{2}+2hJ\cos k+J^{2}\cos^{2}k+J^{2}\gamma^{2}\sin^{2}k}

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos k\right).

In the basis of
\{\ket{\overline{0}},\ket{\overline{1}}\}^{\otimes n}
, the eigenvectors of
Z
are
\ket{\overline{0}}
and
\ket{\overline{1}}
with the eigenvalues
1
and
-1
, respectively, and the vacuum is given by
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}_{c}=\ket{\overline{1}}^{\otimes n}
. We note that in this basis,
c
-fermion is occupied for
\ket{\overline{0}}
and not occupied for
\ket{\overline{1}}
.
We derive the ground states
\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{p}
. Because we know that the second-moment operator
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
has two eigenvectors with eigenvalue
1
, namely
\ket{I}^{\otimes n}
and
\ket{S}^{\otimes n}
, the ground states are superpositions of them,

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{p}=a_{0p}\ket{I}^{\otimes n}+a_{1p}\ket{S}^{\otimes n}.

(61)

We expand as
\ket{I}=v_{0}\ket{\overline{0}}+v_{1}\ket{\overline{1}}
and
\ket{S}=-v_{0}\ket{\overline{0}}+v_{1}\ket{\overline{1}}
, where
v_{0}=\sqrt{d(d-1)/2}
and
v_{1}=\sqrt{d(d+1)/2}
. It implies that

\displaystyle\ket{I}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{n}\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}v_{i_{j}}\ket{\overline{i_1}, \overline{i_2} ,\cdots,\overline{i_n}},

(62)

\displaystyle\ket{S}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{n}\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{i_{j}}v_{i_{j}}\ket{\overline{i_1} , \overline{i_2} ,\cdots, \overline{i_n}},

and in the vector
\ket{S}^{\otimes n}
, the coefficient of the basis state is positive if and only if the number of occurrences of
\overline{1}
is even. Since
n
is even, the parity of the number of
\overline{1}
is equal to that of
\overline{0}
.
Therefore, the ground state vectors in even and odd sectors are constructed as

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2d^{n}(d^{n}+1)}}\left(\ket{I}^{\otimes n}+\ket{S}^{\otimes n}\right),

(63)

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2d^{n}(d^{n}-1)}}\left(\ket{I}^{\otimes n}-\ket{S}^{\otimes n}\right),

where we have normalized the states by using
\innerproduct{\sigma}{\tau}=d+\delta_{\sigma,\tau}(d^{2}-d)
, for
\sigma,\tau\in\{I,S\}
.

The other eigenvectors are obtained by adding
\eta
-fermions to the ground states.
Explicitly, for parity
p
and occupation numbers
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in K_{p}}\in\{0,1\}^{\times n}
satisfying the even-parity condition
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
, the eigenvectors are

\ket{\{n_k\}}=\prod_{k\in K_{p}}(\eta_{k}^{\dagger})^{n_{k}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}_{p},

(64)

where
\eta_{k}
is given by Eq. (
59
).
The corresponding eigenvalues of
H_{u}
are then given by Eq. (
52
): for parity
p
and
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in K_{p}}\in\{0,1\}^{\times n}
such that
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle E_{u}(\{n_{k}\})

\displaystyle=\frac{ne_{u}}{2e_{\mathrm{H}}}+\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\epsilon_{k}\left(n_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K_{p}}\epsilon_{k}n_{k}.

We derive the first excited energy. Since
\epsilon_{k}
is monotonically increasing with
|k|
, for
0\leq\absolutevalue{k}\leq\pi
, the first excited state in even (odd) sector is obtained by adding two quasi-particles with
k=\pm\pi/n\in K_{0}
(
k=0,2\pi/n\in K_{1}
) to the ground state. It implies that the first excited energies in the even sector
E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{even}}
and in the odd sector
E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{odd}}
are given by

\displaystyle E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{even}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\frac{\pi}{n}}+\epsilon_{-\frac{\pi}{n}}

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{2e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\right),

\displaystyle E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{odd}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\left[\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\right)+\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)\right],

where we have used Eq. (
60
).
Because
\cos{k}
is a strictly concave function, we have that
E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{odd}}>E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{even}}
for a finite
n
. The first excited energy is then given by
E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{even}}
.
On the other hand, the largest eigenvalue is obtained by filling all
\eta
-fermions, and it is

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos k\right)=\frac{ne_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}.

(68)

From
M_{L,u}=\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{n}H_{u}
, the corresponding eigenvalue of
M_{L,u}
is negative when
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
, and the absolute value of
1-e_{u}/e_{\textrm{H}}
for
e_{\mathrm{H}}<e_{u}\leq 1
is less than
1-(1/n)E^{(1)}_{u,\mathrm{even}}
for
n\geq 2
.
Therefore, we obtain the spectral gap
\Delta_{L,u}
of the second-moment operator
M_{L,u}
as

\Delta_{\mathrm{L},u}=\frac{2e_{u}}{ne_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\right).

(69)

IV.3
Beyond free fermions

We analyze the second-moment operator beyond the regime satisfying the solvability condition. The main goal in this subsection is to prove that
u
-structured random circuits are more random with respect to positivity than Haar local random circuits if
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}>g_{\mathrm{H}}
.

We begin by observing that more randomness with respect to positivity is trivially true under the solvability condition.
From Eq. (
56
), the operator
H_{u}
is proportional to
\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}
, which implies that the eigenvalues of
H_{u}
monotonically increase in
e_{u}
. Moreover, it is clear from Eq. (
59
) that the eigenvectors of
H_{u}
do not depend on
e_{u}
. Thus by
M_{\mathrm{L},u}=I-(1/n)H_{u}
, we have that, when
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
,

\displaystyle\left.M_{\mathrm{L},u}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}}<\left.M_{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{H}}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}},

(70)

where we have used the fact that
V_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{I}^{\otimes n},\ket{S}^{\otimes n}\}
is the ground eigenspace of
H_{u}
.

We now consider the general case, in which
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
do not necessarily satisfy the solvability condition.
From Eq. (
55
), we have that
H_{u}=({e_{u}}/{e_{\mathrm{H}}})H_{e}+({g_{u}}/{g_{\mathrm{H}}})H_{g}
, where
H_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{a,i}
, for
a=e,g
, with

\displaystyle h_{e,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}I+\frac{1}{4}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}+\frac{d}{2(d^{2}+1)}(Z_{i}+Z_{i+1})

(71)

\displaystyle-\frac{e_{\mathrm{H}}}{4}(X_{i}X_{i+1}-Y_{i}Y_{i+1}),

\displaystyle h_{g,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}I-\frac{1}{4}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}-\frac{1}{4}(X_{i}X_{i+1}+Y_{i}Y_{i+1}).

In the basis
\{\ket{\bar{0},\bar{0}},\ket{\bar{0},\bar{1}},\ket{\bar{1},\bar{0}},\ket{\bar{1},\bar{1}}\}
, the matrix representations are given by

h_{e}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{2(d^{2}+1)}&0&0&-\frac{e_{\mathrm{H}}}{2}\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
-\frac{e_{\mathrm{H}}}{2}&0&0&\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{2(d^{2}+1)}\end{pmatrix},

(72)

h_{g}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(73)

The eigenspectrum of
h_{e}
is
\{1,0,0,0\}
, and hence
h_{e}
is positive semidefinite. The matrix
h_{g}
is also positive semidefinite and the eigenspectrum of
h_{e}
is
\{1,0,0,0\}
.
The Hamiltonian
H_{u}
is a sum of
h_{e}
and
h_{g}
, and thus it is also positive semidefinite.

From the above discussion, the second-moment operator
M_{\mathrm{L},u}=\mathbb{I}-(1/n)H_{u}
monotonically decreases
in
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
, in the sense that

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{u}+e^{\prime},g_{u}+g^{\prime})\leq M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{u},g_{u}),

(74)

for
e^{\prime},g^{\prime}\geq 0
, where we explicitly write
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
as a function of
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
.
It implies that, if
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}>g_{\mathrm{H}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left.M_{\mathrm{L},u}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}}<\left.M_{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{H}}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}},

(75)

where we have used that
\Delta M_{\mathrm{L},u}\coloneq M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{u},g_{u})-M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{u}+e^{\prime},g_{u}+g^{\prime})
is strictly positive for
e^{\prime},g^{\prime}>0
, because the kernel of
H_{u}
is exactly
V_{1}
when
e_{u}>0
.

We remark that the above proof is independent of the circuit architecture, and therefore the operator inequality applies to structured local random circuits in arbitrary spatial dimensions and with arbitrary connectivity.
In addition, the moment operators
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
without the solvability condition are equivalent to frustration-free
XYZ
chains in a magnetic field, which are mapped to interacting Kitaev chains
[
55
,
56
,
88
]
.

IV.4
Monotonicity of gap in structured local random circuits

We now prove that
u
-structured random circuits exhibit a larger spectral gap than local Haar random circuits when
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}>g_{\mathrm{H}}
.
Given the monotonic operator inequality in Eq. (
75
), Weyl’s monotonicity theorem
[
13
]
guarantees that the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}
of
\left.M_{\mathrm{L},u}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}}
also monotonically decrease as
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
increase.
The spectral gap of
M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{u},g_{u})
then increases monotonically with
e_{u}
and
g_{u}
, when the eigenvalue with the second-largest absolute value is strictly positive.
In the following, we prove that the second-largest eigenvalue of
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
in absolute value is positive whenever
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}>g_{\mathrm{H}}
.

We write the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of
\left.M_{\mathrm{L},u}\right|_{V_{1}^{\perp}}
by
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(e_{u},g_{u})
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{u},g_{u})
, respectively.
We consider the regime
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}>g_{\mathrm{H}}
, and we aim to show that

|\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{u},g_{u})|\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(e_{u},g_{u}),

(76)

which implies that the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value is positive.
We can focus on the case where
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{u},g_{u})
is negative, since otherwise it is trivial that
|\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{u},g_{u})|=\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{u},g_{u})\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(e_{u},g_{u})
.
To prove Eq. (
76
), we derive a lower bound on the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
by using the exact results under the solvability condition.
We define the parameter

c_{u}\coloneq\max\left\{\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}},\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}}\right\}.

(77)

Then, from Eq. (
75
), it implies that

M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u},g_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u})\leq M_{\mathrm{L},u}(e_{u},g_{u}).

(78)

From this operator inequality, the eigenvalues are bounded as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(e_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u},g_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(e_{u},g_{u}),

(79)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u},g_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{u},g_{u}).

From Eq. (
69
), the maximum eigenvalue at the solvable point is given by

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(e_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u},g_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u})=1-\frac{2c_{u}}{n}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\right).

(80)

Moreover, from Eq. (
68
), the minimum eigenvalue is bounded by

1-c_{u}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(e_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u},g_{\mathrm{H}}c_{u}).

(81)

We derive upper bounds on
c_{u}
. Since the feasible values of
e_{u}
are
0\leq e_{u}\leq\frac{2}{3}
(
d=2
) and
0\leq e_{u}\leq 1
(
d\geq 3
), we have that

\displaystyle\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{10}{9}

\displaystyle(d=2),

(82)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{2}+1}{d^{2}-1}

\displaystyle(d\geq 3).

Furthermore, under the assumption
e_{u}\geq e_{\mathrm{H}}
, Eq. (
13
) implies that
\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}}\leq 2-e_{\mathrm{H}}
. This yields that

\displaystyle\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{5}

\displaystyle(d=2),

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2-\frac{d^{2}-1}{d^{2}+1}

\displaystyle(d\geq 3).

Combining them, we upper bound
c_{u}
as

\displaystyle c_{u}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{5}

\displaystyle(d=2),

(84)

\displaystyle c_{u}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{2}+1}{d^{2}-1}

\displaystyle(d\geq 3).

We use Eqs. (
80
) and (
81
) to have the difference

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-|\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}|

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}

(85)

\displaystyle\geq 2-c_{u}\left[\frac{2}{n}\left(1-\frac{2d}{d^{2}+1}\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\right)+1\right].

Since
{2}/{n}
and
1-({2d}\cos({\pi}/{n}))/({d^{2}+1})
are both positive and monotonically decreasing with
n
, their product is also monotonically decreasing.
The right-hand side of Eq. (
85
) then increases monotonically with
n
. By calculating it at the most stringent bound for
n=3
, we obtain

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-|\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}|\geq 2-c_{u}\left[\frac{2(d^{2}-d+1)}{3(d^{2}+1)}+1\right].

(86)

For
d=2
, substituting
c_{u}\leq\frac{7}{5}
into Eq. (
86
) yields

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-|\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}|\geq\frac{1}{25}>0.

(87)

For
d\geq 3
, substituting
c_{u}\leq\frac{d^{2}+1}{d^{2}-1}
into Eq. (
86
), we obtain

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-|\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}|\geq\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{d-2}{d^{2}-1}-\frac{2}{d^{2}-1}>0.

(88)

Therefore, we prove Eq. (
76
) for all
n\geq 3
and
d\geq 2
.

V
Structured brick-wall random circuits

We extend the analysis of the previous section to the brick-wall architecture and exactly diagonalize the second-moment operator of structured brick-wall random circuits under the solvability condition.

We summarize the results obtained in this section.
We obtain all the eigenvalues of the second-moment operator under the solvability condition (
32
) as follows.

Theorem 3

(Eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
)
.

Let
u
be a two-qudit unitary gate satisfying the solvability condition.
Then, the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
are given by

\prod_{k\in K_{p}}(\lambda_{k})^{n_{k}}

(89)

where
p\in\{0,1\}
,
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in K_{p}}
, for
n_{k}\in\{0,1\}
, satisfies that
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}
is even, and

\lambda_{k}=\left(a_{k}\widetilde{e}_{u}+\sqrt{a_{k}^{2}\widetilde{e}_{u}^{2}+1-\widetilde{e}_{u}}\right)^{2},

(90)

where
\widetilde{e}_{u}\coloneqq{e_{u}}/{e_{\mathrm{H}}}
and
a_{k}\coloneqq({d}/({d^{2}+1}))\cos{k}
.

A proof for Theorem
3
is given in Section
V.3
.

As a corollary, we obtain the following formula for the exact spectral gap.

Corollary 4

(Spectral gap)
.

The spectral gap of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
under the solvability condition is

\Delta_{\mathrm{B},u}=1-\max\left(|\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}|^{2},|\lambda_{0}\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}|\right),

(91)

where
\lambda_{k}
is defined in Eq. (
124
). Moreover, when
\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}
is a real number, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{B},u}=1-\left(\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}\right)^{2},

(92)

and when
\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}
is not a real number, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{B},u}=1-\lambda_{0}\left(\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}-1\right).

(93)

A proof for Corollary
4
is given in Section
V.4
.

Based on the formula, we show the monotonicity of the spectral gap.

Theorem 5

(Monotonicity of the spectral gap)
.

Suppose a two-qudit unitary gate
u
satisfies the solvability condition. Then,
u
-structured brick-wall random circuits
\nu_{u}^{B}
are more random than the corresponding Haar brick-wall random circuits
\nu_{\mathrm{H}}^{B}
with respect to the spectral gap if and only if
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
.

A proof for Theorem
5
is also given in Section
V.4
.

V.1
Fermion mapping of brick-wall circuits

We map the second-moment operators of brick-wall random circuits to fermionic chains via the Jordan-Wigner transformation, as with the previous section.
The moment operator that we consider is

M_{\mathrm{B},u}=\left(\,\bigotimes_{i\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}W^{u}_{i,i+1}\right)\left(\,\bigotimes_{i\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}W^{u}_{i,i+1}\right).

(94)

Here, without imposing the solvability condition, we have

\displaystyle W^{u}_{i,i+1}=I-\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}h_{e,i}-\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}}h_{g,i},

(95)

where the operators
h_{e,i}
and
h_{g,i}
are defined in Eq. (
71
).

We map the moment operator to a fermionic model.
To this end, we derive the logarithm
\log W^{u}_{i,i+1}
as

\displaystyle\log\left(W^{u}_{i,i+1}\right)=\log\!\left(1-\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)h_{e,i}+\log\!\left(1-\frac{g_{u}}{g_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)h_{g,i},

(96)

where we have used the fact that
\{I-h_{e,i}-h_{g,i},h_{e,i},h_{g,i}\}
are orthogonal projectors for each
i
.
Throughout this work,
\log
denotes the natural logarithm, namely,
\log\equiv\log_{e}
.
Here, to take the logarithm, we have assumed that
e_{u},g_{u}>0
,
e_{u}\neq e_{\mathrm{H}}
, and
g_{u}\neq g_{\mathrm{H}}
. For a negative number
z
, we take the value
\log(z)=\log(|z|)+i\pi
.
We note that
M_{B,u}
is continuous at
e_{u}=e_{\mathrm{H}}
, and thus the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
M_{B,\mathrm{H}}
are given by substituting
e_{\mathrm{H}}
for
e_{u}
.
We now impose the solvability condition (
32
), yielding

\displaystyle\log W^{u}_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(1-\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)\biggl(\mathbb{I}-\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}+1}X_{i}X_{i+1}

(97)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{d^{2}+1}Y_{i}Y_{i+1}+\frac{d}{d^{2}+1}(Z_{i}+Z_{i+1})\biggr).

We introduce the transfer matrices of the even and odd layers as
M_{\mathrm{B},u}=T_{1}T_{0}
, where we define

\displaystyle T_{a}\coloneq

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{i\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}W^{u}_{i,i+1},

(98)

for
a\in\{0,1\}
and
i\in\{0,1,...,n\}
.
Since the local operators
W^{u}_{i,i+1}
commute mutually, the logarithm of the tensor product becomes the sum

\displaystyle\log T_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}\log W^{u}_{i,i+1}

(99)

of the local logarithms.
To diagonalize the moment operator, we express
\log T_{a}
, for
a=0,1
, in terms of a Nambu spinor.
We consider the set of wave numbers
K^{+}_{p}\coloneq\{k\in K_{p}|k\in[0,\pi/2]\}
.
For technical convenience, we also set
\widetilde{K}^{+}_{p}\coloneq\{k\in K_{p}|k\in(0,\pi/2]\}
, which removes the term
k=0
from
K^{+}_{p}
if it exists, and likewise
\bar{K}_{p}^{+}\coloneq\{k\in K_{p}|k\in(0,\pi/2)\}
.
For
k\in\widetilde{K}^{+}_{p}
, we write the four-component Nambu spinor as

\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}=\left(c_{k},c_{-k}^{\dagger},c_{k+\pi},c_{-k+\pi}^{\dagger}\right)^{T}.

(100)

We also denote by
\Psi_{\mathrm{B},0}=(c_{0},c_{\pi}^{\dagger})^{T}
for
k=0
.
The operators
\log T_{a}
are then written in the BdG form with
4\times 4
Hamiltonians,

\displaystyle\log T_{a}=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}x_{u}+\sum_{k\in\widetilde{K}_{p}^{+}}w_{k}\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{a}(k)\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}

(101)

\displaystyle+\delta_{0}\left(\Psi_{\mathrm{B},0}^{\dagger}{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(0)\Psi_{\mathrm{B},0}+x_{u}\right),

where
x_{u}\coloneq({1}/{2})\log\!\left(1-{e_{u}}/{e_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)
, and for
k\in\widetilde{K}^{+}_{p}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{a}(k)\coloneqq x_{u}\Big[\cos\alpha\,\mathbb{I}Z-\cos k\,ZZ+\sin\alpha\sin k\,ZY

(102)

\displaystyle-(-1)^{a}\big(\sin k\,YZ+\sin\alpha\cos k\,YY\big)\Big],

with
\alpha
defined by
{2d}/{(d^{2}+1)}=\cos\alpha
, and for
k=0
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(0)

\displaystyle\coloneqq x_{u}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha-1&(-1)^{a}\sin\alpha\\
(-1)^{a}\sin\alpha&-\cos\alpha-1\end{pmatrix}.

(103)

where the factor
w_{k}
is defined by
w_{k}=1
for all
0<k<\pi/2
and
w_{\pi/2}=1/2
, and
\delta_{0}
is defined by
\delta_{0}=1
if
0\in K_{p}^{+}
and
\delta_{0}=0
otherwise.
The derivation for the BdG form in Eq. (
101
) is written in Appendix
C.2
.

Since the quadratic operators
\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{a}(k)\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}
commute for different wave numbers
k\neq k^{\prime}\in K_{p}^{+}
, the exponential of the sum factorizes into a product of exponentials:

\displaystyle T_{a}=e^{x_{u}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\delta_{0}\right)}\prod_{k\in K_{p}^{+}}\exp\left(w_{k}\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{a}(k)\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}\right).

(104)

It is important for the later analysis that we have the relation between
\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}(k)
as, for
k\in\widetilde{K}_{p}^{+}
,

\mathcal{H}_{1}(k)=Z_{1}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)Z_{1},

(105)

where we have defined
A_{1}\coloneqq A\otimes\mathbb{I}
for an operator
A
.

V.2
Exact diagonalization

We now diagonalize the second-moment operator
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
.
To this end, we diagonalize the BdG Hamiltonians
H_{a}(k)
.
Although the matrix of
k=0
is a
2\times 2
matrix and can be diagonalized easily, each wave number
k\in\widetilde{K}_{p}^{+}
gives a
4\times 4
matrix, which may not be straightforward to diagonalize. We can still obtain the exact diagonalization for these
4\times 4
matrices as follows.

The product of the two exponentials can be written as a single exponential by the following lemma.

Lemma 6

(Two exponentials as one)
.

Let
k\in K_{p}^{+}
such that
k\neq 0,\pi/2
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}
be the Nambu spinor defined in Eq. (
100
).
If
4\times 4
matrices
A
and
B
satisfy the relation
e^{A}e^{B}=e^{C}
for some
C
, then we have that

e^{\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}A\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}}e^{\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}B\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}}=e^{\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}C\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}}.

(106)

Lemma
6
is proven in Appendix
D
.

From the above lemma, for each
k
, we obtain

M_{\mathrm{B},u}=e^{x_{u}\left({n}+2\delta_{0}\right)}\prod_{k\in K_{p}^{+}}\exp\left(w_{k}\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}C_{k}\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}\right),

(107)

where
4\times 4
matrices
C_{k}
satisfy

\displaystyle\exp\left(C_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left({w_{k}Z_{1}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)Z_{1}}\right)\exp\left({w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)}\right)

(108)

\displaystyle=\left[Z_{1}\exp\left({w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)}\right)\right]^{2}

To diagonalize
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
, it is sufficient to diagonalize the non-Hermitian matrices
\exp(C_k)
for
k\in K^{+}_{p}
. This is because if we have
\exp(C_k)=V_{k}D_{k}V^{-1}_{k}
for some diagonal matrix
D_{k}
and invertible matrix
V_{k}
, it yields
C_{k}=V_{k}\log(D_k)V_{k}^{-1}
, and

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{B},u}

\displaystyle=e^{x_{u}\left({n}+2\delta_{0}\right)}\prod_{k\in K_{p}^{+}}\exp\left(w_{k}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}(\log D_{k}){\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}\right),

(109)

where we have defined the right and left vectors of quasi-particles
{\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}\coloneq V_{k}^{-1}\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}\coloneq\Psi_{\mathrm{B},k}^{\dagger}V_{k}
, respectively.

We write the elements of the right quasiparticle vector
\Phi_{\mathrm{B},k}
with annihilation-like operators
\eta
as
\Phi_{\mathrm{B},k}=(\eta_{k,1},\eta_{k,2},\eta_{k,3},\eta_{k,4})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
.
Because the diagonalizing matrix
V_{k}
is not unitary in general, the left quasiparticle vector
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}
is not simply the Hermitian conjugate of
\Phi_{\mathrm{B},k}
, and we write the elements as
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}=(\bar{\eta}_{k,1},\bar{\eta}_{k,2},\bar{\eta}_{k,3},\bar{\eta}_{k,4})
. It is straightforward to show that, for an invertible matrix
V_{k}
, they satisfy the canonical anticommutation relations, in the sense that for all
k\in\bar{K}_{p}^{+}
and
i,j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
,

\{\eta_{k,i},\bar{\eta}_{k,j}\}=\delta_{i,j},\qquad\{\eta_{k,i},\eta_{k,j}\}=\{\bar{\eta}_{k,i},\bar{\eta}_{k,j}\}=0.

(110)

With this relation, we have

\displaystyle w_{k}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}(\log D_{k}){\Phi}_{\mathrm{B},k}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}w_{k}(\log\lambda_{k,j})\bar{\eta}_{k,j}\eta_{k,j},

(111)

where
\lambda_{k,i}
are the eigenvalues of
D_{k}
.

We define the non-Hermitian fermionic number operators as
\hat{n}_{k,j}\coloneq\bar{\eta}_{k,j}\eta_{k,j}
.
From Eq. (
110
), they satisfy
[\hat{n}_{k,i},\hat{n}_{k,j}]=0
and
\hat{n}_{k,j}^{2}=\hat{n}_{k,j}
.
We thus have, for each
k\in\bar{K}_{p}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{4}(\log\lambda_{k,j})\hat{n}_{k,j}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{4}\left(\lambda_{k,j}\right)^{\hat{n}_{k,j}}.

(112)

Likewise, for
k=\pi/2
, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{1}{2}(\log\lambda_{k,j})\hat{n}_{k,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{2}\left(\lambda_{k,j}\right)^{\hat{n}_{k,j}},

(113)

where we have used the fact that only two wave numbers
k=\pi/2
and
k=-\pi/2
couple, and
\hat{n}_{k,1}
and
\hat{n}_{k,2}
are redefined by the corresponding occupation numbers. (see Appendix
C.3
for detail).

We define a right vacuum state vector
|R_{0}\rangle
and a left vacuum state
\langle L_{0}|
such that they are annihilated as follows: for all
k\in K^{+}_{p}
and
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
,

\eta_{k,j}\ket{R_0}=0,\quad\bra{L_0}\bar{\eta}_{k,j}=0.

(114)

The right eigenvectors are obtained by applying some of the creation-like operators
\bar{\eta}_{k,j}
to
\ket{R_0}
, and the corresponding eigenvalues of
M_{\textrm{B},u}
are the products of the eigenvalues
{\lambda}_{k,j}
of quasi-particle
\bar{\eta}_{k,j}
(see Appendix
C.4
for detail). The left eigenvectors are obtained similarly.
Their explicit eigenvalues are given later in Eq. (
180
).

V.3
Explicit formulas for the eigenvalues

It remains to diagonalize the
4\times 4
matrix
\exp(C_k)=\left[Z_{1}\exp\left(w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)\right)\right]^{2}
, which yields the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
.
To this end, we calculate

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}_{0}(k))^{2}=2x_{u}^{2}(I-\bm{n}(k)\cdot\bm{\sigma}),

(115)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\bm{n}(k)

\displaystyle\coloneq(\cos\alpha\cos k,\cos\alpha\sin k,\sin\alpha),

(116)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle\coloneq(Z_{1},Y_{1},XX).

Since
|\bm{n}|^{2}=1
and the pauli matrices in
\bm{\sigma}
are mutually anti-commuting, the operator

P(k)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(I-\bm{n}(k)\cdot\bm{\sigma})

(117)

is a projector.
By definition, we have that
(\mathcal{H}_{0}(k))^{2}=4x_{u}^{2}P(k)
, and thus
[\mathcal{H}_{0}(k),P(k)]=0
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)
is Hermitian, its kernel is equal to that of
(\mathcal{H}_{0}(k))^{2}
, and
P
is the projector onto the image of
\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)
.
Therefore, we have

(\mathcal{H}_{0}(k))^{3}=4x_{u}^{2}P(k)\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)=4x_{u}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k).

(118)

From the above relation, the higher power of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
becomes simply

(\mathcal{H}_{0})^{l}=\begin{cases}(2x_{u})^{l-1}\mathcal{H}_{0}&(\text{odd }l\geq 1),\\[4.0pt]
(2x_{u})^{l-2}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{2}&(\text{even }l\geq 2).\end{cases}

(119)

The exponential of an operator is defined by its Taylor series, and we obtain

\displaystyle e^{w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{l!}\left(w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)\right)^{l}

(120)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cosh(\tilde{x}_{k})+1}{2}I+\frac{\sinh(\tilde{x}_{k})}{2{x}_{u}}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)

\displaystyle-\frac{\cosh(\tilde{x}_{k})-1}{2}\bm{n}(k)\cdot\bm{\sigma},

where we have defined
\tilde{x}_{k}\coloneq 2w_{k}x_{u}
.

We derive the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{M}_{k}\coloneqq Z_{1}e^{w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)}
, which immediately yields the desired ones of
\exp(C_k)
as shown in Eq. (
108
).
To this end, we employ the following lemma, which is proven in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 7

(Eigenvalues as reciprocal pairs)
.

The eigenvalues of
4\times 4
matrix
\widetilde{M}_{k}=Z_{1}e^{w_{k}\mathcal{H}_{0}(k)}
, for
k\in\bar{K}^{+}_{p}
, form reciprocal pairs: if
\lambda\neq 0
is an eigenvalue,
\lambda^{-1}
is also an eigenvalue.

Let us consider
k\in\widetilde{K}_{p}^{+}
. From Lemma
7
, the matrices
\widetilde{M}_{k}
have four eigenvalues in reciprocal pairs
(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{-1},\mu_{1},\mu_{1}^{-1})
.
We define
\bar{\mu}_{0}\coloneq\mu_{0}+\mu_{0}^{-1}
and
\bar{\mu}_{1}\coloneqq\mu_{1}+\mu_{1}^{-1}
. The traces of the matrix are related by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{M}_{k})

\displaystyle=\bar{\mu}_{0}+\bar{\mu}_{1},

(121)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{M}_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\bar{\mu}_{0})^{2}+(\bar{\mu}_{1})^{2}-4.

On the other hand, from Eqs. (
102
), (
116
), and (
120
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{M}_{k})

\displaystyle=-2(\cosh(\tilde{x}_{k})-1)\cos\alpha\cos k,

(122)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{M}_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle=4\cosh(\tilde{x}_{k})\big(1+(\cosh(\tilde{x}_{k})-1)\cos^{2}\alpha\cos^{2}k\big).

We can solve these equations for
\mu_{i}
, which yield the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
.
These solutions yield the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
that are given by, for each
p\in\{0,1\}
,

\prod_{k\in K_{p}}(\lambda_{k})^{n_{k}}

(123)

where
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in K_{p}}
, for
n_{k}\in\{0,1\}
, satisfies that
\sum_{k\in K_{p}}n_{k}
is even, and

\lambda_{k}=\left(a_{k}\widetilde{e}_{u}+\sqrt{a_{k}^{2}\widetilde{e}_{u}^{2}+1-\widetilde{e}_{u}}\right)^{2},

(124)

where
\widetilde{e}_{u}={e_{u}}/{e_{\mathrm{H}}}
,
a_{k}=({d}/({d^{2}+1}))\cos{k}
, and we describe the derivations of Eqs. (
123
) and (
124
) in Appendix
C.5
.

From the above result, we find that while the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathrm{L},u}
are always real, those of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
can be non-real due to their non-Hermiticity.
We note that

\lambda_{\pi-k}=\left(a_{k}\widetilde{e}_{u}-\sqrt{a_{k}^{2}\widetilde{e}_{u}^{2}+1-\widetilde{e}_{u}}\right)^{2}=\lambda_{k}^{*}

(125)

when
a_{k}^{2}\widetilde{e}_{u}^{2}+1-\widetilde{e}_{u}<0
, where the eigenvalues come in complex conjugate pairs because
M_{a,u}
for
a=L,B
are real matrices.

V.4
Exact spectral gap

We finally derive the spectral gap of the second-moment operator
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
, for
u
-strucrtured brick-wall random circuits and prove that
u
-structured brick-wall circuits can be more random with respect to spectral gap than brick-wall Haar random circuits.

The absolute value of the eigenvalues
|\lambda_{k}|
satisfy
|\lambda_{k}|\leq 1
for all
k\in K_{p}
, and in particular
|\lambda_{k}|<1
for an entangling gate
u
. Then, the largest eigenvalue is obtained by choosing
n_{k}=0
for all
k\in K_{p}
in Eq. (
123
). It implies that the largest eigenvalue is
1
, as expected for a moment operator.

We next derive the second-largest eigenvalue.
From the expression for
\lambda_{k}
, we find that
|\lambda_{k}|
is monotonically decreasing in
k
for
0\leq k\leq{\pi}
.
Therefore, the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value in the even and odd parity sectors are
|\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}|^{2}
and
|\lambda_{0}\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}|
, respectively.
We note that
\lambda_{0}
is a real number for any integer
d\geq 2
and real number
0\leq e_{u}\leq{(d^{2}-1)}/{d^{2}}
.
We then obtain the spectral gap as

1-\max_{i,j\in K_{p}}\left(|\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}|\right)=1-\max\left(|\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}|^{2},|\lambda_{0}\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}|\right).

(126)

With Eqs. (
177
) and (
179
), Eq. (
126
) is the exact formula for the spectral gap.

We further compute the right-hand side in Eq. (
126
).
When
\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}
is a real number,
\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}
is also real.
In this case, we have that
(\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}})^{2}\geq\lambda_{0}\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}
due to the concavity of
\log\lambda_{k}
, and
the gap
1-(\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}})^{2}
monotonically increases with
e_{u}
.
We remark that
\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}
is real at least in the regime
0\leq e_{u}\leq e_{\mathrm{H}}
.
On the other hand,
\lambda_{k}
can be a complex number, for some
0<k\leq{\pi}/{2}
, if
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
.
When
\lambda_{k}
is not real, we have
|\lambda_{k}|=\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}-1
, which does not depend on
k
.
Additionally, when
\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}
is not a real number, so
\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}
is,
and in this case we have that

\max\left(|\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}|^{2},|\lambda_{0}\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}|\right)=\lambda_{0}|\lambda_{\frac{2\pi}{n}}|=\lambda_{0}\left({e_{u}}/{e_{\mathrm{H}}}-1\right)
.
Here, we have used that
\lambda_{0}
is a real number for any integer
d\geq 2
and
0\leq e_{u}\leq({d^{2}-1})/{d^{2}}
, which is true under the solvability condition and Eq. (
13
).
Therefore, if
\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}
is not real, the spectral gap of
M_{\mathrm{B},u}
is

1-\lambda_{0}\left(\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}-1\right),

(127)

which, in contrast, decreases monotonically with
e_{u}
.

We finally prove more randomness with respect to the spectral gap for structured brick-wall random circuits, as follows.
When
\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}
is real, the spectral gap also increases monotonically at least for
e_{u}\leq e_{\mathrm{H}}
.
As a special case corresponding to Haar-random gates, the spectral gap is obtained by setting
e_{\mathrm{H}}=e_{u}
in Eq. (
126
), yielding

1-\left(\frac{d}{d^{2}+1}\right)^{4}\left(2\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}+2\right)^{2},

(128)

which agrees with the spectral gap derived in Ref.
[
25
]
.
When
\lambda_{\frac{\pi}{n}}
is a non-real number, we have that

1-\lambda_{0}\left(\frac{e_{u}}{e_{\mathrm{H}}}-1\right)\geq 1-\left(\frac{d}{d^{2}+1}\right)^{4}\left(2\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}+2\right)^{2},

(129)

where we have used
e_{\mathrm{H}}<e_{u}\leq({d^{2}-1})/{d^{2}}
and
n\geq 4
. Therefore, the gap of
u
-structured brick-wall circuits is larger than that of brick-wall Haar random circuits if and only if
e_{u}>e_{\mathrm{H}}
.

|\lambda_{3}|=1-\Delta_{\nu}

and

The solvability condition is illustrated as the solvable

, where
e_{\mathrm{H}}={3}/{5}
for

and
e_{\mathrm{H}}={4}/{5}
for

We find an interesting difference between local random
circuits and brick-wall circuits as follows. In Figs.

VII
Applications

We suggest that our work may open up a number of compelling applications.
We can apply the enhancement of randomness in terms of the second moment to the followings:
(1)
Reduction of circuit depth for randomized benchmarking
.

Randomized benchmarking
(RB)
[
63
]
is a widely used technique for estimating the quality of quantum gates in quantum computers. Moreover, random unitary circuits consisting of random local gates serve as tools for assessing the error rate, known as the filtered RB, and the circuit depth for realizing it is upper-bounded by the inverse of the spectral gap of the second-moment operator in a sufficiently large depth
[
45
]
.
Combining it with our enhanced spectral
gaps, we can improve the required circuit depth for the filtered RB while maintaining a theoretical performance guarantee.
(2)
Improved bound on unitary 2-designs in shallow random circuits
.
Unitary
2
-designs have a range of applications, such as computational
advantage
[
40
,
21
]
, quantum channel fidelity estimation
[
22
]
, random coding
[
82
,
35
]
, and randomized measurement
[
3
,
28
]
. Recently,
Haar random
circuits have been shown
to form approximate unitary designs in depth
\log{n}
by the technique of gluing small random circuits
[
83
,
60
]
. Since the structured random circuits can have better upper bounds on the depth generating approximate unitary
2
-designs, combining it with the technique by gluing small structured random circuits, we can improve the approximate
2
-design time.
(3)
Exact result on quantum chaotic dynamics
.
More physically motivated applications of our results
manifest themselves by the connection between the second-moment operator and indicators of quantum chaos, such as

out-of-time ordered correlators

(OTOC) and the Rényi-2 operator entanglement entropy
[
81
,
46
,
94
,
31
]
. Because we derive the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the second-moment operators satisfying the solvability condition, in principle, one can
obtain closed formulas for these indicators in structured random
circuits satisfying the solvability condition.

VIII
Discussion and conclusion.

In our work, we have developed a theory of structured random circuits by the technique of exact diagonalization and have proven that random circuits with non-Haar random gates can be more random than those with Haar random gates. We expect that the solvable random gates introduced in this work will be of independent interest for the study of quantum many-body dynamics, where exact results on real-time evolution play a central role, as exemplified by the case of dual-unitary gates
[
10
]
.
Moreover, our work also applies beyond the solvable free-fermionic regime, since Theorem
2
does not rely on the solvability condition.
Also, more randomness with respect to positivity in structured local random circuits is true for an arbitrary spatial dimension, not only a one-dimensional system.

The additional structure improves the convergence rate to a unitary
2
-design ensemble, which will be particularly important when we use large-scale quantum computers. Since we have considered the second-moment, the Haar random single-qudit gates in the structured random gates can be replaced by any gates forming unitary 2-design locally, keeping our results the same.

While we have focused on the diagonalization of moment operators in the main text, we also derive the bounds on the frame potential in structured random circuits by the statistical mechanical mapping
[
71
,
49
]
, as shown in Appendix
E
.

We note that Ref.
[
25
]
has also derived the spectral gap of brick-wall Haar random circuits, and our result at the Haar point of
e_{u}=e_{\mathrm{H}}
and
g_{u}=g_{\mathrm{H}}
agrees with it.
Also, the spectra of matchgate brick-wall circuits have been considered in Ref.
[
79
]
, and we expect that our results on the diagonalization of brick-wall circuits are also applicable to matchgate
unitary circuits.
Within the context of entanglement dynamics, phenomena displaying a faster decay of purity in certain structured random circuits, which is similar to more randomness in our setting, have been observed in the early works
on random circuits Refs.
[
96
,
97
]

(see Appendix
A
for further details on related works).

We also note that, subsequent to our preprint of this work, a few works appeared that study a similar line of research from different perspectives
[
58
,
4
,
80
]
, which connect certain fixed gates, such as dual-unitary gates, with the fast generation of randomness measured by unitary or state designs.

Our results open up numerous opportunities for future work. It would be interesting to show more randomness of structured random circuits in terms of higher-moment operators, as well as other architectures, for example, higher dimensional brick-wall circuits. Other future directions are finer analyses of the applications we have discussed above.
Also, investigating the randomness in Brownian Hamiltonian dynamics
[
74
,
50
,
9
]
with an additional structure similar to this work would be a physically interesting direction. At the end of such a program stands an idea of how to best create randomness with the smallest possible circuit complexity. We hope that
this work helps to identify
the fundamental limits of
what notions of randomness one can achieve with reasonable constraints on circuit complexity.
