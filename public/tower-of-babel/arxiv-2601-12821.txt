Sub-wavelength resonances in two-dimensional multi-layer elastic media
Yan Jiang, Hongyu Liu, Fanbo Sun, Yajuan Wang

Abstract
. In this paper, we focus on the sub-wavelength resonances in two-dimensional elastic media characterized by high contrasts in both Lamé parameters and density. Our contributions are fourfold. First, it is proved that the operator 𝐒 ^ ∂ D ω \hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega} , which serves as a leading order approximation to 𝐒 ∂ D ω \mathbf{S}_{\partial D}^{\omega} as ω → 0 \omega\rightarrow 0 , is invertible in the space ℒ ⁡ ( L 2 ​ ( ∂ D ) 2 , H 1 ​ ( ∂ D ) 2 ) \mathcal{L}(L^{2}\left(\partial D)^{2},H^{1}(\partial D)^{2}\right) . Second, based on layer potential techniques in combination with asymptotic analysis, we derive an original formula for the leading-order terms of sub-wavelength resonance frequencies, which are controlled by the determinant of the 3 ​ N × 3 ​ N 3N\times 3N matrices. Mathematical setup

Focusing first mainly on the mathematics, but not the physical applications, we begin by describing the mathematical formulation for our study. Consider the total displacement field
\mathbf{u}

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mu}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})+\omega^{2}\tilde{\rho}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})=0,&\mathbf{x}\in{D},\\
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\mu}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})+\omega^{2}\rho\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})=0,&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\bar{D},\\
\left.\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\right|_{-}=\left.\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\right|_{+},&\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{j}^{\pm},1\leq j\leq N,\\

1.2.
Background motivation and technical developments

The recent development shows that metamaterials with negative or high contrast parameters offer new possibilities for imaging and for the control of waves at deep sub-wavelength scales. Sub-wavelength resonators, such as air bubbles exhibiting Minnaert resonance
[
36
,
6
]
, have become fundamental in the development of phononic crystals, where periodic arrangements of these high-contrast resonators give rise to distinctive wave propagation phenomena
[
37
,
8
,
38
]
, while also providing insights into the mechanisms of wave localization in disordered, finite systems
[
3
,
7
,
19
,
4
]
.

Mathematically, most existing research on metamaterials, which are designed by inducing specific resonances, has primarily concentrated on single- and double-layer structures. These include the surface plasmon resonance of metal nanoparticles, typically analyzed using the Drude model, and the core-shell geometry utilized in invisibility cloaks, which is achieved through anomalous local resonance
[
11
,
12
,
22
]
. However, these structures are often limited by poor filtering performance and narrow bandgap width. In contrast, multi-layer scatterers exhibit multiple vibration modes and greater degrees of freedom, positioning them as promising candidates for overcoming these limitations. Indeed, Ammari et al.
[
10
,
25
,
41
,
1
,
35
]
proposed vanishing structures with generalized polarization tensors (GPTs) and a scattering coefficient (SC), designing the structure using multi-coated concentric disks or balls and proving that near cloaking effects could be enhanced. To support multiband functionality and facilitate cloaking of larger objects, a multi-layer plasmon hybridization model was introduced, offering increased flexibility to suppress higher-order scattering modes
[
2
,
15
]
. Initial analyses focused on three- and four-layer structures
[
24
,
28
,
39
]
. Experimental and numerical studies
[
27
,
29
,
14
,
42
]
have shown that multi-layer concentric radial resonators can generate multiple local resonance bandgaps in the sub-wavelength regime. Specifically, plasmon resonances in multi-layered concentric spheres and confocal ellipses were investigated in
[
23
,
21
,
20
,
44
,
26
]
, revealing how plasmon modes interact and hybridize, generating localized surface plasmons at each interface. These studies demonstrate how mode splitting is influenced by geometry truncation and the breaking of rotational symmetry, thereby enabling cloaks with broader bandwidths and multiple operational frequencies. Despite substantial progress, mathematical modeling of sub-wavelength resonances and mode splitting mechanisms remains limited.

Inspired by the fact that plasmon modes in multi-layer systems interact and hybridize across interfaces, this paper
is to establish a sub-wavelength resonance theory for two-dimensional elastic media with high contrast. The coupling of longitudinal and transverse waves, along with the vectorial nature of the displacement field, renders the study of elastic sub-wavelength resonators notably more complex. The three-dimensional static single layer potential operator is always invertible and analytic with respect to the low-frequency regime, making it an important starting point for investigation. By using layer potential techniques and the variational method, papers
[
31
,
13
,
34
,
33
]
provide mathematical proofs of monopolar, dipole and quadrupole resonances using soft elastic materials (HISE structures) and dimers (THES structures). For phononic crystals composed of periodically arranged resonators, the authors in
[
40
]
demonstrate the existence of sub-wavelength band gaps. By contrast, in two-dimensional, similar to the derivations of Minnaert resonances given in Appendix B of
[
6
]
, elastic waves have the following four main challenges.

(1) the single layer potential
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}
may not be invertible (even for a disk) in two dimensions, while this property always holds in three dimensions;

(2) there is a logarithmic singularity in the asymptotic expansion of the single layer potential
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}^{\omega}
for small
\omega
, which means that it contains a double series comprising terms of the form
\omega^{2j}
and
\omega^{2j}\ln\omega
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
;

(3) even though
{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}
may not be invertible, it is necessary to prove the invertibility of leading term
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega}
in
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}\left(\partial D)^{2},H^{1}(\partial D)^{2}\right)
for sufficient small
\omega
;

(4) the spectral properties of the associated layer potential operators in two dimensions are more complex for radial geometry, involving not only the intrinsic coupling of all shear and compression waves, but also the corresponding Bessel and Hankel functions containing singular terms
\ln{k_{s}}
or
\ln{k_{p}}
.

To establish the primary conclusion of this paper, we first construct the low-frequency asymptotic expansions for layer potential operator. It is worth mentioning that the corresponding space for the static Lamé system with Neumann boundary conditions is three-dimensional, which affect the invertibility of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega}
. In fact, we have obtained the proof using the relationship matrix
{\mathbf{C}}
between the kernel spaces of operators
-\mathbf{I}_{2}/2+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}
and
-\mathbf{I}_{2}/2+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{*}
. Next, utilizing layer potential techniques and Gohberg-Sigal theory, we analyzed the resonance modes of a single resonator for simplicity. Furthermore, the strict extension of resonance theory to multi-layer elastic structures has advanced the existing theoretical framework. The results show that the leading-order terms of the resonance frequencies are controlled by the determinant of the
3N\times 3N
matrices, with the number of resonance frequencies increasing as the number of resonators grows. Specifically, there are
3N
resonance frequencies within an
N
-nested layer structure. However, in two dimensions, only an implicit expression for the leading term can be derived. Therefore, we focus on the resonators with radial geometry, where explicit expressions for the sub-wavelength resonance frequencies within a disk are obtained by solving the corresponding eigenvalue problem. Additionally, as the incident frequency
\omega
approaches the resonance frequency, the enhancement coefficient inside the resonator is on the order of
\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-2})
. Finally, our results are validated through several numerical examples.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews main results on layer potentials, and proves the invertibility of the operator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega}
in the space
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}\left(\partial D)^{2},H^{1}(\partial D)^{2}\right)
. In Section 3, we establish the primary conclusions, including the control equation for the sub-wavelength resonance frequency of multi-layer structures and the point scatterer approximation. Section 4 applies two methods to obtain a specific expression for the resonance frequency in radial geometry. Section 5 presents some numerical experiments to validate the theoretical results.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Layer potential theory

We first briefly review the potential theory of the Lamé system. The fundamental solution
\bm{\Gamma}^{\omega}
of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\mu}+\omega^{2}\rho
in two dimensions is given by
[
5
]

(2.1)

\bm{\Gamma}^{\omega}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\mu}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\mathbf{I}_{2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\omega^{2}\rho}\nabla\nabla\left(H_{0}^{(1)}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)-H_{0}^{(1)}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right),

where
\nabla\nabla
is the standard double differentiation,
\mathbf{I}_{2}
stands for the
2\times 2
identity matrix, and

(2.2)

k_{s}=\omega/c_{s},\quad k_{p}=\omega/c_{p},

with

(2.3)

c_{s}=\sqrt{\mu/\rho},\quad c_{p}=\sqrt{(\lambda+2\mu)/\rho}.

In particular, when
\omega=0
, we denote
\bm{\Gamma}^{0}
by
\bm{\Gamma}
for simplicity, and
\bm{\Gamma}
has the following expression

(2.4)

\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{1}\ln|\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{I}_{2}-\alpha_{2}\frac{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}}{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}},

where

(2.5)

\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{1}{2\mu+\lambda}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{2\mu+\lambda}\right).

Let
D
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with connected Lipschitz boundaries. Then the single-layer potential associated with the fundamental solution
\bm{\Gamma}^{\omega}
is defined by

(2.6)

\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}^{\omega}[\bm{\varphi}](\mathbf{x})=\int_{\partial D}\bm{\Gamma}^{\omega}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\varphi}(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

for
\bm{\varphi}\in L^{2}(\partial D)^{2}
. On the boundary
\partial D
, the conormal derivative of the single-layer potential satisfies the following jump formula

(2.7)

\left.\partial_{\bm{\nu}}\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}^{\omega}[\bm{\varphi}]\right|_{\pm}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{\omega,*}\right)[\bm{\varphi}](\mathbf{x}),

where

\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{\omega,*}[\bm{\varphi}](\mathbf{x})=\text{ p.v. }\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\bm{\nu}_{\mathbf{x}}}\bm{\Gamma}^{\omega}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\varphi}(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y}),

with p.v. standing for the Cauchy principal value. We would like to mention that the operator
\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{\omega,*}
in (
2.7
) is called the Neumann-Poincaré (N-P) operator, which is a critical operator in the investigation of metamaterials in elasticity. In what follows, we denote
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}^{0},\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{0,*}
by
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D},\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{*}
, respectively, for simplicity.

The elements of the matrices

and

where the constants

and

are given by (

) and (

Proof.

By using Green’s formula and Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\partial D,1}[\bm{\xi}_{j}](\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\bm{\nu}_{\mathbf{y}}}\bm{\Gamma_{1}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\xi}_{j}(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})=\int_{D}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\mu}\bm{\Gamma_{1}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\xi}_{j}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=b_{\lambda,\mu}\int_{D}\bm{\xi}_{j}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}-\int_{D}\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\xi}_{j}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}.

Therefore, we can get the proof from definition (

The existence of sub-wavelength resonance frequencies is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
\delta\ll 1
, and
\epsilon\ll 1
, there exists three sub-wavelength resonance frequencies, counted with their multiplicities. The leading order terms are given by the roots of the following equation

(3.14)

\det\Big(\rho\omega^{2}\ln\omega\mathbf{P}+\rho\omega^{2}\left(\ln{(\sqrt{\rho}\tau)}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{M}\right)-\epsilon\mathbf{Q}\Big)=0,

where the matrices
\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{Q}
are given in Proposition
3.1
and equation (
3.13
), respectively. In fact, since
\mathbf{Q}
is invertible when
\omega
is small enough, equation (
3.14
) is third-order with respect to
\epsilon
, meaning that it has three roots
\omega_{i}(\epsilon)(1\leq i\leq 3)
.

Proof.

We would like to find the characteristic values
\omega^{*}\ll 1
such that there exits a nontrivial function
\Phi_{\delta}
satisfying

(3.15)

\mathcal{A}(\omega^{*},\delta)[\Phi_{\delta}]=0.

Lemma
3.1
shows that

\mathcal{A}(0,0)[\Phi_{0}]=0,

where
\Phi_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{j}\hat{\Phi}_{j}
and the coefficients
c_{j}
are arbitrary. Thus one can treat
\Phi_{\delta}
as a perturbation of
\Phi_{0}
. We express the function
\Phi_{\delta}
by

\Phi_{\delta}=\Phi_{0}+\Phi_{1}\quad\text{with}\quad(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{0})_{\mathcal{H}}=0.

Using the definition of operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
given in (
3.12
), the equation (
3.15
) is equivalent to

(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{P})[\Phi_{0}+\Phi_{1}]=0.

By using the following fact
\mathcal{P}[\Phi_{0}]=\Psi_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{j}\hat{\Psi}_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{0})_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\left((\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\mathcal{B})^{-1}\mathcal{P}[\Phi_{0}]-\Phi_{0},\Phi_{0}\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\left((\mathbf{I}_{2}-\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{B}+(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{B})^{2}+\cdots){\Phi_{0}},{\Phi_{0}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}}-\Big(\Phi_{0},\Phi_{0}\Big)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\left((-\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{B}+(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{B})^{2}+\cdots)\Phi_{0},\Phi_{0}\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\left(-\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{B}[\Phi_{0}],\Phi_{0}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta\omega^{2}\ln{\omega}+\omega^{4}\ln{\omega}\right),

where
\mathcal{B}=\omega^{2}\ln\omega\mathcal{A}_{1,0}+\omega^{2}\mathcal{A}_{2,0}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{0,1}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln{\omega}+\omega^{4}\ln{\omega})
. Moreover, let
\mathbf{\Lambda}
be a diagonal matrix with
\Lambda_{ii}=d_{i}(1\leq i\leq 3)
and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})^{T}
. Then, we have

•

Calculation of
(\mathcal{A}_{1,0}[\Phi_{0}],\Psi_{0})
.

(3.17)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{1,0}[\Phi_{0}],\Psi_{0})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{i}c_{j}d_{j}\rho\tau^{2}\Big(\bm{\zeta}_{i},\mathbf{K}_{\partial D,1}[\bm{\xi}_{j}]\Big)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{i}c_{j}d_{j}\rho\tau^{2}P_{ij}=\rho\tau^{2}\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{c},

•

Calculation of
(\mathcal{A}_{2,0}[\Phi_{0}],\Psi_{0})

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{2,0}[\Phi_{0}],\Psi_{0})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{i}c_{j}d_{j}\rho\tau^{2}\Big[\ln{(\sqrt{\rho}\tau)}\Big(\bm{\zeta}_{i},\mathbf{K}_{\partial D,1}[\bm{\xi}_{j}]\Big)+\Big(\bm{\zeta}_{i},\mathbf{K}_{\partial D,2}[\bm{\xi}_{j}]\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{i}c_{j}d_{j}\rho\tau^{2}\Big(\ln{(\sqrt{\rho}\tau)}P_{ij}+M_{ij}\Big)=\rho\tau^{2}\Big(\ln{(\sqrt{\rho}\tau)}\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{c}\Big).

•

Calculation of

(3.19)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{0,1}[\Phi_{0}],\Psi_{0})=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{i}c_{j}d_{j}\left(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{{\tau\omega}}[\bm{\zeta}_{i}],(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{{\omega},*})^{-1}[\bm{\xi}_{j}]\right)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{i}c_{j}d_{j}Q_{ij}=-\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{c}.

Therefore, combining (

), (

3.1

The proof is completed.∎

\alpha_{i}(1\leq i\leq 3)
shall be determined later. Since the operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\mathcal{B}
is invertible, taking
(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\mathcal{B})^{-1}
on both sides of equation (
3.26
) yields that

\Phi_{k}+\Phi_{k}^{\bot}-\left(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{B}\right)^{-1}[\Phi_{k}]=(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\mathcal{B})^{-1}[\mathcal{F}],\quad\mathbf{x}\in\partial D.

Further calculation shows that for
\mathbf{x}\in\partial D
,

(3.27)

\Phi_{k}^{\bot}+\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\left(\omega^{2}\ln\omega\mathcal{A}_{1,0}+\omega^{2}\mathcal{A}_{2,0}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{0,1}\right)[\Phi_{k}]+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln{\omega}+\omega^{4}\ln{\omega})=(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\mathcal{B})^{-1}[\mathcal{F}].

In fact, we decompose the source term
\mathcal{F}
as
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\omega)
, where
\mathcal{F}_{0}=(\mathbf{d},0)^{T}
. Further assume that
\mathcal{F}_{0}
satisfies

\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{F}_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Upsilon_{i}\hat{\Psi}_{i}\quad\text{with}\quad\Upsilon_{i}=(\mathcal{F}_{0},\hat{\Psi}_{i}).

Subsequently, taking the inner product of both sides of (
3.27
) with
\hat{\Phi}_{j}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}\Big(\rho\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\ln\omega\mathbf{P}+\rho\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\left(\ln{(\sqrt{\rho}\tau)}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{M}\right)-\delta\mathbf{Q}\Big)\bm{\alpha}

(3.28)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln{\omega}+\omega^{4}\ln{\omega})=\mathbf{\Upsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\omega+\delta),

where we use the fact that
\Big({\hat{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}],\hat{\Phi}_{j}\Big)=0
, and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})^{T}
,
\mathbf{\Upsilon}=(\Upsilon_{1},\Upsilon_{2},\Upsilon_{3})^{T}
. From (
3.27
) one has

(3.29)

\Phi_{k}^{\bot}=(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0})^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{0}]-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\left(\omega^{2}\ln\omega\mathcal{A}_{1,0}+\omega^{2}\mathcal{A}_{2,0}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{0,1}\right)[\hat{\Phi}_{i}]+o(1).

Denote
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is the projection operator to
(\mathrm{ker}\mathcal{A}_{0})^{\bot}
, taking
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
on both sides of (
3.29
) we get

(3.30)

\Phi_{k}^{\bot}=(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0})^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}]-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}\left(\omega^{2}\ln\omega\mathcal{A}_{1,0}+\omega^{2}\mathcal{A}_{2,0}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{0,1}\right)[\hat{\Phi}_{i}]+o(1).

Since
\mathcal{F}_{1}\notin\mathrm{ker}\mathcal{A}_{0}
, which says that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\in\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A}_{0})
, thus
(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{0})^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}]={\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}]
. By the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
we can immediate get

(3.31)

({\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}])_{2}=-(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega})^{-1}[(\mathcal{F}_{1})_{1}].

where
({\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}])_{2}
signifying the second component of the vector
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}]
.
Indeed, the following formula holds

\int_{\partial D}({\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}])_{2}\cdot\bm{\xi}_{j}\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\partial D}(\mathcal{F}_{1})_{1}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega,*})^{-1}[\bm{\xi}_{j}]\mathrm{d}x=-(\mathcal{F}_{1},\hat{\Psi}_{j})_{\mathcal{H}}=0,

the derivation relies on the fact
(\mathcal{F}_{1})_{2}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\partial D)
. Similarly, one has

\int_{\partial D}\left(\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}}{2}+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{*}\right)(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega})^{-1}[(\mathcal{F}_{1})_{1}]\cdot\bm{\xi}_{j}\mathrm{d}x=0.

Thanks to the invertibility of
-\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}}{2}+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{*}
in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\partial D)
, then

({\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}])_{1}=\left(-\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}}{2}+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{*}\right)^{-1}\left[-\left(\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}}{2}+\mathbf{K}_{\partial D}^{*}\right)(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega})^{-1}[(\mathcal{F}_{1})_{1}]+(\mathcal{F}_{1})_{2}\right],

also belongs to
L^{2}_{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\partial D)
. At this moment, the displacement field inside the domain
D
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\delta{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{\partial D}^{\tau\omega}[\Phi]+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\delta\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\tau\omega}_{\partial D}[\hat{\Phi}_{i}]+\delta{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{\partial D}^{\tau\omega}[\Phi_{k}^{\bot}]+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\tau\omega}_{\partial D}[\hat{\Phi}_{i}](1+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{2}\ln\omega+\delta))+{{\mathbf{S}}}_{\partial D}^{\tau\omega}\Big[\mathcal{A}_{0}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}_{1}]\Big]\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln\omega).

Thus, when
\omega
satisfies (
3.14
), one has that
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(1/(\delta\omega^{2}\ln\omega))
from (
3.3
) and the displacement field inside the domain
D
takes the form

(3.32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\delta\alpha_{i}\left(\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}[\bm{\zeta}_{i}]+\int_{\partial D}\gamma_{\tau\omega}\bm{\zeta}_{i}+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{2}\ln\omega+\delta)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\varrho_{i}\bm{\xi}_{i}+\mathcal{O}(1)=\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-2}),

since
\gamma_{\tau\omega}=\mathcal{O}(\ln\omega)
. When
\omega^{2}\gg\delta
, one has that
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(1/(\omega^{2}\ln\omega))
, thus the displacement field inside the domain
D
satisfies

(3.33)

\mathbf{u}_{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\delta\alpha_{i}\left(\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}[\bm{\zeta}_{i}]+\int_{\partial D}\gamma_{\tau\omega}\bm{\zeta}_{i}+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{2}\ln\omega+\delta)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}(\delta/\omega^{2}).

The proof is completed by noting that
\omega\ll 1
, so that
\varrho_{i}=o(1)
and the term of
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
can be regarded as higher order term.
∎

4.
The sub-wavelength resonances for disk geometry

In this section, we use two methods to calculate the explicit expressions of the resonance frequencies within a disk. One is to utilize the conclusion derived from the formula (
3.14
) in Theorem
3.1
, the other is by directly solving (
3.9
). Although both analyses rely on asymptotic expansions in the frequency parameter
\omega
. To aid readers, we start by introducing the Bessel and Hankel functions, as well as some of the spectral properties of N-P operators in one disk geometry.

4.1.
The Bessel and Hankel functions

Let
J_{n}(t)
and
H_{n}(t),n\in\mathbb{Z}
, denote the Bessel and Hankel functions of the first kind of order
n
, respectively. For
f=J_{n}
or
H_{n}
, these functions satisfy the following Bessel differential equation

t^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(t)+tf^{\prime}(t)+\left(t^{2}-n^{2}\right)f(t)=0,

and the recursion formulas (cf.
[
16
]
)

f_{n+1}^{\prime}=f_{n}-(n+1)\frac{f_{n+1}}{t},\quad f_{n+1}=n\frac{f_{n}}{t}-f_{n}^{\prime},\quad n\geq 0,

f_{n-1}^{\prime}=-f_{n}+(n-1)\frac{f_{n-1}}{t},\quad f_{n-1}=n\frac{f_{n}}{t}+f_{n}^{\prime},\quad n\geq 1.

Moreover, there hold that
J_{n}(t)=(-1)^{n}J_{-n}(t)
and
H_{n}(t)=(-1)^{n}H_{-n}(t)
when
n
is negative. Therefore, for the argument
t\ll 1
, the functions
J_{n}
and
H_{n}
, enjoy the following asymptotic expansions(cf.
[
16
]
),

(4.1)

J_{0}(t)=1-\frac{t^{2}}{4}+\frac{t^{4}}{64}-\frac{t^{6}}{2^{8}\cdot 9}+\mathcal{O}(t^{8}),

(4.2)

J_{1}(t)=\frac{t}{2}-\frac{t^{3}}{16}+\frac{t^{5}}{2^{7}\cdot 3}+\mathcal{O}(t^{7}),

and

(4.3)

H_{0}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\left(E_{c}+2\ln t\right)-\frac{\mathrm{i}t^{2}}{4\pi}\left(-2+E_{c}+2\ln t\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}t^{4}}{64\pi}\left(-3+E_{c}+2\ln t\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{6}\ln t\right),

(4.4)

H_{1}(t)=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\pi t}+\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{2\pi}\left(-1+E_{c}+2\ln t\right)-\frac{\mathrm{i}t^{3}}{16\pi}\left(-\frac{5}{2}+E_{c}+2\ln t\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{5}\ln t\right).

Let
D_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the disk centered at the origin with radius
R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\mathbf{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the Euclidean coordinate, and
\theta
is the angle between
\mathbf{x}
and the
x_{1}
-axis,
r=|\mathbf{x}|
. In the following part, we can first derive the expressions for the single-layer potentials
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}
associated with the two densities
e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}
and
e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}
, where
\bm{v}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{T}
,
\bm{t}=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^{T}
represent the outward unit normal and tangential direction along a boundary
\partial D_{R}
, respectively. (cf.
[
32
]
).

Lemma 4.1
.

The single-layer potentials
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right]
and
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right]
have the following expressions for
\mathbf{x}\in\partial D_{R}

\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right](\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{3n}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}+\alpha_{4n}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\mathbf{t},

\alpha_{1n}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho R}\left(n^{2}J_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)H_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)+k_{p}^{2}R^{2}J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)\right),

\alpha_{3n}=-\frac{n\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho}\left(k_{s}J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}R\right)H_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)+k_{p}J_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)\right),

\alpha_{4n}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho R}\left(k_{s}^{2}R^{2}J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}R\right)H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}R\right)+n^{2}J_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)H_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)\right).

The single-layer potentials
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right]
and
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right]
have the following expressions for

\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right](\mathbf{x})=\frac{-\mathrm{i}\pi}{4\omega^{2}\rho R}\left(nk_{s}RJ_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{s,o}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)+k_{p}^{2}R^{2}J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{p,o}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right),

Besides, the single-layer potentials
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right]
and
\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right]
have the following expressions for
\mathbf{x}\in D_{R}
,

\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right](\mathbf{x})=\frac{-\mathrm{i}\pi}{4\omega^{2}\rho R}\left(nk_{s}RH_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{s,i}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)+k_{p}^{2}R^{2}H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{p,i}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right),

\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right](\mathbf{x})=\frac{-\pi}{4\omega^{2}\rho R}\left(k_{s}^{2}R^{2}H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}R\right)\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{s,i}\left(k_{s}|\bm{x}|\right)+nk_{p}RH_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{p,i}\left(k_{p}|\bm{x}|\right)\right),

where

\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{s,i}\left(k_{s}|\bm{x}|\right)=\frac{2nJ_{n}\left(k_{s}|\bm{x}|\right)}{k_{s}|\bm{x}|}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}+2\mathrm{i}J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}|\bm{x}|\right)e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t},

\bm{\Psi}_{n}^{p,i}\left(k_{p}|\bm{x}|\right)=2J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}|\bm{x}|\right)e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}+\frac{2\mathrm{i}nJ_{n}\left(k_{p}|\bm{x}|\right)}{k_{p}|\bm{x}|}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}.

Lemma 4.2
.

The N-P operator
\mathbf{K}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega,*}
have the following expressions with two densities
e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}
and
e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}

\mathbf{K}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega,*}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right]=a_{1n}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}+a_{2n}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t},

\mathbf{K}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega,*}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right]=b_{1n}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}+b_{2n}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t},

where

a_{1n}=-\frac{1}{2}+g_{1,n}(R),\quad a_{2n}=g_{2,n}(R),\quad b_{1n}=g_{3,n}(R),\quad b_{2n}=-\frac{1}{2}+g_{4,n}(R).

The coefficients
g_{i,n}(R)(1\leq i\leq 4)
can be derived from the tractions
\left.\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right]\right|_{+}
and

\left.\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right]\right|_{+}
evaluated on
\partial D_{R}
, as defined by the following expressions

\left.\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}\right]\right|_{+}=g_{1,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{v}+g_{2,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t},

\left.\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{S}_{\partial D_{R}}^{\omega}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\bm{t}\right]\right|_{+}=g_{3,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)e^{in\theta}\bm{v}+g_{4,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)e^{in\theta}\bm{t},

where

\displaystyle g_{1,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\left(2\mu n^{2}J_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)\left(H_{n}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)-k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\left.J_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)k_{p}R\left(H_{n}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\left(\omega^{2}\rho|\mathbf{x}|^{2}-2\mu n^{2}\right)+2k_{p}\mu|\mathbf{x}|H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right)\right),

\displaystyle g_{2,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)=

\displaystyle-\frac{n\mu\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\left(J_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)H_{n}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\left(k_{s}^{2}|\mathbf{x}|^{2}-2n^{2}\right)+2k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|J_{n}\left(k_{s}R\right)H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\left.2k_{p}RJ_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}R\right)\left(H_{n}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)-k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right)\right),

\displaystyle g_{3,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)=

\displaystyle\frac{n\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\left(J_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)H_{n}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\left(\omega^{2}\rho|\mathbf{x}|^{2}-2\mu n^{2}\right)+2\mu k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|J_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\left.2\mu k_{s}RJ_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}R\right)\left(H_{n}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)-k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right)\right),

\displaystyle g_{4,n}(|\mathbf{x}|)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}\mu\pi}{2\omega^{2}\rho|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\left(2n^{2}J_{n}\left(k_{p}R\right)\left(H_{n}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)-k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{p}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\left.k_{s}RJ_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}R\right)\left(k_{s}^{2}|\mathbf{x}|^{2}H_{n}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)+2k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|H_{n}^{\prime}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)-2n^{2}H_{n}\left(k_{s}|\mathbf{x}|\right)\right)\right).

4.2.
The derivation of resonance frequencies from Theorem
3.1

In this part, we obtain the specific expression for the eigenfrequency from Theorem
3.1
. Firstly, the elements of the kernel space are given by the following

\bm{\zeta}_{1}=\frac{1}{{2\pi R}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}1\\
0\end{array}\right),\quad\quad\bm{\zeta}_{2}=\frac{1}{{2\pi R}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}0\\
1\end{array}\right),\quad\quad\bm{\zeta}_{3}=\frac{1}{{2\pi R^{3}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}x_{2}\\
-x_{1}\end{array}\right).

These basis functions are orthonormal in
L^{2}(\partial D)^{2}
. Then, introducing parameters

\sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{1}{2\mu+\lambda}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\sigma_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{2\mu+\lambda}\right).

Finally, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
D
be a disk of radius
R
. For
\mathbf{x}\in D
, there holds

(4.5)

{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}[\bm{\zeta}_{i}](\mathbf{x})=\int_{\partial D}\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\zeta}_{i}(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})=\left(\sigma_{1}R\ln R-\frac{\sigma_{2}R}{2}\right)\bm{\zeta}_{i}(\mathbf{x}),\ i=1,2.

and

(4.6)

{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}[\bm{\zeta}_{3}](\mathbf{x})=\int_{\partial D}\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bm{\zeta}_{3}(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})={-\frac{R}{2\mu}}\bm{\zeta}_{3}(\mathbf{x}).

Proof.

Indeed, the first equation (
4.5
) can be proven by applying the fact that (cf.
[
11
]
)

{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}[c](\mathbf{x})=\left(\sigma_{1}R\ln R-\frac{\sigma_{2}R}{2}\right)c,\quad\mathbf{x}\in D,

for any constant vector
c=(c_{1},c_{2})^{T}
. In addition, by using polar coordinate calculations, it holds that

\int_{\partial D}\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\begin{pmatrix}y_{2}\\
-y_{1}\end{pmatrix}ds(\mathbf{y})=-\frac{R}{2}\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}\\
-x_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{x}\in D,

and

\int_{\partial D}\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}y_{2}\\
-y_{1}\end{pmatrix}ds(\mathbf{y})=\frac{R}{2}\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}\\
-x_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{x}\in D.

Hence, combining these with (
2.4
) yields the second equation (
4.6
).
∎

In fact, let

\mathbf{C}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big(\sigma_{1}\ln R-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2},\sigma_{1}\ln R-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2},-\frac{1}{2\mu R^{2}}\Big)\right),

it can verify the conclusion given in the previous result (Lemma
2.5
) that

\mathbf{S}_{\partial D}[\mathbf{G}]=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C},\quad\quad\int_{\partial D}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})^{T}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{I}_{3}.

Here, we need to emphasize that
\mathbf{C}
is singular matrix, i.e.
\det\mathbf{C}=0
if
\sigma_{1}\ln R=\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2}
. At this moment,
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}^{\omega}
is still invertible, but
{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}
is not.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
D
be a disk of radius
R
. For
\delta\ll 1
, and
\epsilon\ll 1
, there exist three sub-wavelength resonance frequencies,
counted with their multiplicities, whose leading-order terms denoted can be determined by the equations as follows

(4.7)

{\rho\omega^{2}\ln\omega p_{i}+\rho\omega^{2}\Big(\ln{(\sqrt{\rho}\tau)}p_{i}+m_{i}\Big)-\epsilon q_{i}=0,\quad i=1,2,3.}

where
p_{i}
,
m_{i}
and
q_{i}(1\leq i\leq 3)
are given by

p_{1}=p_{2}={a}_{\lambda,\mu}\pi R^{2},\quad\quad p_{3}=0,

Proof.

Consider first, that

Next, it follows from Proposition

that

,

and

\displaystyle M_{ii}

\displaystyle={b}_{\lambda,\mu}\int_{\partial D}\bm{\zeta}_{i}(\mathbf{x})ds(\mathbf{x})\cdot\int_{D}\bm{\xi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}-\int_{D}{\mathbf{S}}_{\partial D}[\bm{\zeta}_{i}](\mathbf{x})\cdot\bm{\xi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=m_{i},\quad i=1,2.
