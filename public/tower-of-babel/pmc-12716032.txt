Anaphylaxis after stings by Vespula spp. – preventions, safety profile of venom immunotherapy, data from the Anaphylaxis Registry for West Pomerania Voivodeship, Poland
Authors: Iwona Poziomkowska-Gęsicka, Marta Rudnicka, Artur Gęsicki, Małgorzata Zielska, Krzysztof Safranow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStings by Hymenoptera, especially by Vespula spp. are one of the most common causes of anaphylaxis among adults. Its efficacy, depending on the type of venom it is administered with, is very high at 77–84% for bee venom, and 91–96% for Vespula spp. venom. Retrospective, noninterventional study based on the analysis of medical records of 113 patients was conducted. The occurrence of severe complications, forcing the discontinuation of immunotherapy during the ultra rush regimen, was associated with higher baseline tryptase levels (p < .05) in the studied group and higher mean age (51 y.o.). Introduction

Stings by Hymenoptera, especially by Vespula spp., are one of the most common causes of anaphylaxis among adults as shown by previous publications from this region of Poland.1 Confirmation of the mechanism of the IgE-dependent reaction ensures effective therapy and prevents further episodes. It is the only cause of anaphylaxis for which effective prevention can be provided. The Anaphylaxis Registry for Western Pomerania has been in use since 2006, based on a questionnaire created and modified by the European Registry and NORA - (Network for Online – Registration of Anaphylaxis).2 The Local Registry analyzes triggers of anaphylaxis, clinical manifestations, occurrence of reaction cofactors, and possible causes of idiopathic anaphylaxis.1,3 This study analyzes the implemented prevention as tertiary prophylaxis of anaphylactic reactions induced by hymenopterous insects. The rate of systemic reactions to Hymenoptera stings in European epidemiological studies varies from 0.3% to 7.5% in adults.4 Anaphylaxis after those stings in the Local Registry was characterized by a slightly different clinical course, with cardiovascular symptoms being the predominant clinical manifestation,1 rather than skin and mucous membrane symptoms, and no deaths were reported after the sting. A UK report claimed 93 deaths caused by venom anaphylaxis for 20 y, accounting to 0.09 deaths per million inhabitants per year.5 As mentioned before, the only method of preventing further episodes of anaphylaxis, in people with allergy to Hymenoptera venom, is venom immunotherapy (VIT). Its efficacy, depending on the type of venom it is administered with, is very high at 77–84% for bee venom and 91–96% for Vespula spp. venom.6,7 Immunotherapy regimens, according to which they are carried out, can be divided into four main types, depending on the course (duration) of initial therapy:

Conventional – increasing the dose of venom is done slowly, every 7 d and lasts 12–16 weeks (about 3–4 months).

There are different variants of existing initial therapy regimens, but their end goals are the same: to achieve a tolerable dose of 100 µg of venom in one dose. Usually, a maintenance dose of 100 μg venom during VIT is sufficient for protection.6 During the ultra-rush therapy, the amount of 101.1 μg or even 111.111 μg of Vespula spp. or bee venom can be achieved in 1 d. All regimens can be changed and modified depending on the tolerance of the previously administered venom dose. The detailed protocol can be found on package inserts or in the guideline for venom immunotherapy.8 The described methods of conducting VIT are equally effective, with virtually no difference in the risk of complications,9–14 i.e., systemic and local reactions. The frequency of systemic adverse events during VIT ranges between 8% and 20% according to large multicenter studies.15,16 In a recent study by Stoevesandt et al., a systemic reaction (SR) rate of 11.7% (any reactions including the subjective ones) was reported during the build-up phase of VIT; however, the SR rate dropped to 3% when objective diagnostic criteria of anaphylaxis were used.17 Rapid dose increase during the build-up phase was a weaker but nonetheless established risk factor for sAEs.18,19 But there are also data showing that the ultra-rush protocol is the safest.20 Due to the short time needed to achieve tolerance to natural stings, in practice our center, the ultra-rush method is most commonly used or, if it fails, the cluster method is used, shortening the time of the entire procedure. In addition to all possible different regimens for conducting VIT, various medications containing Hymenoptera venom are available. Products for VIT are available as aqueous (or lyophilized) extracts or as depot medications adsorbed to aluminum hydroxide.8 Aqueous solutions of venom can be used for all available regimens for conducting immunotherapy, including rush and ultra rush methods (in these cases as the only ones). Slow-release vaccines, on the other hand, can only be used for extended regimens, i.e., conventional and clustered.6 After their use, local reactions are rarer and usually of lesser severity, which is related to the mechanism of action of the drug and slow release-depot form. Each method of immunotherapy has its advocates, and the choice of a particular treatment regimen depends on a number of factors: the time until the insect flight season starts, the availability of a particular VIT medication, the experience of the immunotherapy of the physician, as well as the concerns of the patient.

According to the WAO for sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) and subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT), complications of immunotherapy are usually mild, i.e., classified as grade I or II reactions, where grade I means any local reaction (e.g., skin symptoms or different forms of rhinitis or itchy throat or conjunctivitis or nausea or metallic taste or headache), grade II means symptoms of more than one organ system present: asthma symptoms or gastrointestinal symptoms or uterine cramps; reaction that resolves after symptomatic treatment, and severe-grade III asthma or upper respiratory edema; or IV respiratory failure or cardiovascular hypotension when a reaction intensity requires discontinuation of treatment.21 Despite fatal SCIT reactions being rare, they continue to occur at a rate of around 1 in 2–2.5 million injections in the United States.21,22

Objective

We analyze a group of patients undergoing Vespula spp. venom immunotherapy by ultra-rush method with regard to the safety profile of the procedure that was carried out with Venomenhal Wespe medicine.

Material and methods

Retrospective, noninterventional study based on the analysis of medical records of 113 patients was conducted. All patients gave written informed consent for immunotherapy with the appropriate Vespula spp. venom. The study protocol did not require the approval of a Bioethics Committee.

We analyzed the course of Vespula spp. venom-specific immunotherapy induction performed among adult patients of the Anaphylaxis Registry for West Pomerania, Poland. The records of patients (N = 113) who underwent immunotherapy induction (build-up phase) in the years (2008–2014) at the Department of Clinical Allergology of PMU in Szczecin were studied. Build-up phase of therapy was performed in two different schema (different no. of injection) (Table 1) in patients who had anaphylactic reactions in the past, at least grade II, according to Ring–Messmer, with a documented mechanism of IgE-dependent reactions, for the causative insect (positive sIgE for the venom and/or positive results in skin tests). sIgE for venom extract was tested with an Omega Diagnostics GmbH/Germany apparatus, and in selected cases, sIgE assays for Vespula spp. venom recombinants rVes v5, rVes v1 were used; with UniCap 100/Phadia/Sweden apparatus, Vespula spp. venom skin tests were performed according to the standards for VIT7: prick test concentration: 100 µg/mL, intradermal test concentration: 0.001 µg/mL, 0.01 µg/mL, 0.1 µg/mL, 1.0 µg/mL;23–25, preparation: Venomenhal Wespe (HAL Allergy/The Netherlands).

Statistical analysis

Obtained data were analyzed using the Statistica 12 software package, StatSoft, Inc. The results are presented as the mean ± SD, as a percentage or as the absolute value. The relationships were tested with the Mann–Whitney U test, using two-way tables (using Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s test). Statistical significance was adopted at a p value of <.05.

Results and discussion

Induction of Vespula spp. venom tolerance was carried out by an ultra rush with Venomenhal Wespe (Vespula spp. venom) – among 113 people from the Anaphylaxis Registry, with a history of anaphylaxis in the course of natural stinging (grade II according to Ring–Messmer and higher).

Women in the analyzed group constituted the majority, i.e., 56%. The average age at which the initial VIT therapy was performed was 45 y [SD ± 14.8], median 47 y, range 19–77 y. The percentage of individual degrees of severity of anaphylaxis according to Ring–Messmer among those undergoing VIT was as follows: II° – 56%; III° – 43% and IV° – 1%.

Results of additional tests in the study group: sIgE of Vespula spp. venom ranged from 0 to 61.6 IU with a mean value of 8.7IU. The mean tryptase level in the study group was 6.77 with a range of values from 2.1 to 26.1 ng/mL. In our data, patients had an intermediate-risk of VIT failure; the mean tryptase level was less than 7.5 mg/L.18 High basal tryptase levels in vespid allergy may be a risk factor for systemic adverse events in VIT,10 but an EAACI multicenter study found a slightly elevated risk in this case (OR 1.56; CI 1.15–2.10).6

Positive prick test results with Vespula spp. venom concentrations of 100 µg/mL were recorded in 69 cases – representing 62% of the analyzed patients, while a positive intradermal test showed a higher sensitivity, reaching 99% confirming the causal role of the insect in question. Importantly, an EAACI multicenter study showed that for severe initial sting reactions, positive skin tests at low test concentrations and high specific IgE levels are not regarded as risk factors for adverse events.6

In accordance with the start of the VIT procedure in our unit and different existing VIT regimens in place, immunotherapy was started at a concentration of 0.001 µg/mL in 14% of cases, while in 63% of cases, it was started at 0.01 µg/mL and in 23% of cases at 0.1 µg/mL.

Any complications (local or general) during therapy with Venomenhal Wespe occurred in 97 patients which is 86% of cases, of which mainly local complications − 91 cases (i.e., 81%), while systemic mild complications (did not affect the discontinuation of therapy) affected 22 people (i.e., 19.5%); 16 people had no complications (i.e., 14%) (Figures 1 and 2).

According to the literature, it appears that there is no correlation between gender, specific IgE values, and the number of adverse allergic reactions observed during VIT.7,21,26 The general opinion sometimes suggests that high specific IgE levels or skin test reactivity at lower venom doses poses a risk for severe sting reactions. However, clear evidence indicates that these factors do not predict the outcome of a future sting.18 Older age was not identified as a risk factor.27 Patients aged 40 y and older are more commonly affected by severe SSRs when compared with younger individuals (36.9% vs. 22.6%).18 In the case of the previously described mild generalized reactions, which occurred in 22 patients (19.5%), the most common were skin reactions (erythema, burning skin, pruritus, urticaria), from the gastrointestinal tract: nausea, allergic sore throat, from the cardiovascular system: headache, increased HR, tinnitus. The symptoms began to withdraw either spontaneously or after administration of an anti-histamine drug and fluid therapy. This is similar to the published data, describing the frequency of mild systemic reactions in 5–15% of the patients, undergoing VIT. Reactions requiring epinephrine treatment occur in <5% of patients.28

Due to the use of ultra-rush protocol, aqueous venom solution was applied, which resulted in intense local reactions (erythema, injection site wheal, and infiltration). These reactions were observed in 80.5% of patients during immunotherapy, which is typical for the method and this type of medication. In patients who developed severe allergic reactions during the procedure, resulting in discontinuation of VIT, immunotherapy was started with the lowest possible dose of venom, i.e., 0.001 µg/mL, given the severity of the initial anaphylactic reaction after the Vespula spp. sting. This, however, did not protect these patients from anaphylaxis during VIT.

The analysis shows that complications (systemic and local) occur more frequently when the procedure was initiated with lower contcetration of initial venom dose, and therefore the more doses (injections) had to be madeachieve the recommended protective dose of venom (p < .05; min 9, max 18 injections). However, it should be noted that systemic and allergic adverse reactions during the build-up phase of venom immunotherapy in rush and ultra-rush schedules are generally comparable to, or lower than, those seen with conventional protocols.11 This lower incidence is thought to result from delivering fewer injections and a smaller total venom dose, although the exact reaction rate varies with the chosen protocol.12,30 The correlation between systemic reactions, injection number, and cumulative venom dose has been demonstrated in several studies.31

Venom immunotherapy shows variable systemic reaction rates depending on the protocol, ranging from about 1–22% during quick build-up ultra rush, with higher rates of honeybee allergy.12,26,32–34 In our data, mild systemic complications occurred in patients with a mean age of 39 y. This indicates that the occurrence of these systemic complications depended on patients’ age (p < .05), the younger the patient, and the greater the risk of mild systemic complications. The occurrence of severe complications, forcing the discontinuation of immunotherapy during ultra-rush regimen, was associated with higher baseline tryptase levels (p < .05) in the studied group and higher age (mean 51 y.o.) which is corroborated in literature stating that severe systemic complications during VIT protocol occur twice as often in patients over 40 and older.18

Summary

Ultra rush performed with an aqueous solution of Vespula spp. venom is a very fast initial therapy, performed in a hospital setting. Within 3.5–4 h, the patients receive a dose of venom that protects them from anaphylaxis in the case of a natural sting. This protocol has proven safe for patients with Vespula spp. venom allergies. Local reactions confirm a well-chosen medication – for an appropriately sensitive patient. The percentage of pretreatment failures for Vespula spp. venom did not exceed literature data and was even slightly lower at 2.65% vs. 3%.13 Due to the lack of local reactions during initial injections (higher number of injections causes higher risk of increased local complications), the lowest initial doses were abandoned over time: 0.001 µg and 0.01 µg. The starting dose has traditionally been very low in all regimens (0.001 mcg), but clinical experience over many years has shown that 1 mcg is a safe starting dose.28 Shortening of the ultra-rush regimen resulted in faster procedure times and better patient acceptability of VIT. Our observational study focused on one type of vaccine – Venomenhal (Hal Allergy, Leiden, the Netherlands). This homogeneity resulted in greater reliability when comparing adverse reactions in patients. Additionally, this product was very safe (there were only three patients who had to stop treatment) in the ultra-rush protocol.

Conclusions

Before qualifying for VIT, skin prick tests with Vespula spp. venom should be performed, and if these tests prove to be negative, intradermal tests should be performed to identify the causative insect.

If diagnosis is to be started with allergen-specific IgE (sIgE) determinations, finding those determinations negative for insect venom does not preclude a positive skin prick test.

Aqueous solution of Vespula spp. venom is a valuable product that, depending on the capabilities of the center and current needs, can be used to conduct immunotherapy in an ultra-rush regimen, shortening the initial therapy to 1 d.

Very rapid immunotherapy with Vespula spp. venom aqueous solution, i.e. ultra-rush VIT, is a very safe and time-saving procedure for both patients and medical staff.

The current local reactions during ultra-rush schedule confirm that the preparation for VIT was a good choice.

However, due to the real risk of anaphylaxis during initial VIT, it is necessary to carry out the aforementioned procedure in a center with experience in conducting immunotherapy with this method and have the team trained and ready for rapid diagnosis and treatment of anaphylaxis.

Full-blown anaphylaxis is a rare and generally well-controlled complication of ultra-rush VIT procedure.

The future

The future goals are as follows: developing VIT products that cause fewer local reactions and improve tolerability of the ultra-rush VIT procedure; educating patients and staff on the use of Vespula spp. venom immunotherapy, especially during the initial phase; Exploring biomarkers and algorithms to predict the risk of VIT failure may contribute to improved patient care following anaphylaxis due to Vespula spp. stings.

Limitations

The main limitation of this study is the relatively small group of patients. Lack of representation of pediatric patients – we had only 11 children undergoing ultra-rush procedure with Vespula spp. venom. We did not have of analysis of patients undergoing bee venom immunotherapy. Another limitation is the lack of data, e.g., baseline tryptase in the entire study group, comorbidity, and chronic use of drugs. We observed typical adverse reactions, local reactions, mild systemic and severe systemic reactions. Other, nonallergic reaction events, such as hot flushes, headache, and rise in body temperature, were not analyzed. This observation is incomplete for a total evaluation risk factors to complications ultra rush regimen.
