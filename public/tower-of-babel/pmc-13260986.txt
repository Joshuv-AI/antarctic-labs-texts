Real-time AI-assisted quality control during nasopharyngolaryngoscopy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yun Li, Bin Ye, Yuanyuan Li, Cui Fan, Wenqing Chen, Yi Shuai, Bin Liu, Qiwei Liu, Kai Sun, Waner Zhang, Wujun Wang, Yalu Wang, Wenbin Lei, Mingliang Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nasopharyngolaryngoscopy (NPL) is widely used to examine the nasopharyngolaryngeal anatomical sites. The quality of NPL depends on the endoscopist’s performance, and incomplete examinations may contribute to missed findings in practice. Here, we developed ENDOVISTA-ENT, an intelligent quality control system trained on NPL videos from 3,630 patients. The system can monitor anatomical coverage in real time during NPL procedures. It is not designed to detect lesions. By integrating into the existing NPL workflow, it provides endoscopists with real-time feedback on anatomical coverage, examination progress, and procedure duration. To evaluate its effect, we conducted a prospective, double-centre, randomized controlled trial registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091245). A total of 318 patients were randomly assigned to undergo ENDOVISTA-ENT-assisted or conventional NPL examination. The primary outcome was coverage of predefined anatomical sites. Results showed that ENDOVISTA-ENT-assisted NPL examinations achieved signi6cantly higher mean anatomical coverage than conventional examinations (93.08% vs. 83.50%, P < 0.0001). Importantly, this improvement occurred without significantly increasing examination time. Subgroup analyses revealed benefits across all experience levels, particularly among junior endoscopists. These findings suggest that a real-time AI-assisted quality control system can support a more standardized NPL workflow and improve endoscopists’ procedural completeness during NPL.

Introduction

Nasopharyngolaryngoscopy (NPL) is a diagnostic endoscopic examination that plays a central role in the diagnosis of nasopharyngeal and laryngeal diseases1. With the use of digital flexible endoscopes, clinicians can examine the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, larynx, and hypopharynx in sequence through natural orifices2,3. However, NPL depends on the experience and proficiency of endoscopists, leading to variations in examination quality. Such variations could affect the completeness of key anatomical site inspections, and may further influence the early detection of nasopharyngolaryngeal malignancies, as well as their precursor lesions4–7. The 5-year survival rate for patients with early-stage nasopharyngolaryngeal cancers exceeds 90%8–10, while it is less than 50% for late-stage cases11,12. Notably, in many clinical settings, especially in low- and middle-income regions, the early diagnosis rate of nasopharyngolaryngeal cancers remains below 20%13,14. This low rate may be attributed to the endoscopists’ lack of NPL practical experiences and the poor quality of NPL examinations. Therefore, it is crucial to develop feasible tools to implement quality control during NPL examinations.

Artificial intelligence (AI) endoscopic devices represent a promising field for the development of intelligent medical equipment15. During endoscopic examinations, endoscopists often perform long periods of meticulous work. Such repetitive tasks may lead to fatigue and inconsistent decision-making16. AI has been shown to effectively assist doctors in making better decisions17. Several randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that computer-aided detection devices can standardize the quality of gastrointestinal endoscopy and improve the detection of gastrointestinal lesions18–20. However, to date, no AI endoscopic devices have been applied in real clinical practice specifically for monitoring the NPL examination process.

In this study, we developed an intelligent medical device, ENDOVISTA-ENT, to support NPL by monitoring procedural completeness, recording examination time, and saving standard images. The device integrates into the existing clinical workflow. In practice, ENDOVISTA-ENT operates in real time without changing the original video stream from conventional white-light endoscopes, and it does not require additional manual steps. To evaluate whether ENDOVISTA-ENT could improve endoscopists’ procedural completeness during NPL, we conducted a double-center randomized controlled trial. With real-time monitoring and anatomical site reminders, the device is designed to support more complete examinations and a more standardized NPL workflow.

Results

Figure 1 shows the overall study design, which was conducted in four stages.Fig. 1Flow chart for development and validation of ENDOVISTA-ENT.

Flow chart for development and validation of ENDOVISTA-ENT.

Stage 1: In vitro and in vivo classification. We retrospectively collected NPL videos from two hospitals and used a screenshot tool to automatically cut the video into images to form Dataset 1. Then we trained a classification model (Model 1) using Dataset 1 to distinguish the images between in vitro and in vivo situations.

Stage 2: Nasopharyngolaryngeal sites classification. Based on the in vivo images identified by Model 1, we further annotated them to form Dataset 2. And we trained a second model (Model 2) to identify specific nasopharyngolaryngeal anatomical sites using Dataset 2.

Stage 3: Model integration and system development. We integrated Model 1 and Model 2 to develop the ENDOVISTA-ENT system. To validate the system’s generalizability and enhance its clinical applicability, we tested NPL videos on the ENDOVISTA-ENT system and evaluated its performance.

Stage 4: Clinical application and trial. In a real-world clinical setting, we conducted a prospective, double-center randomized controlled trial to evaluate whether ENDOVISTA-ENT could improve endoscopists’ procedural completeness during NPL. In practice, ENDOVISTA-ENT obtains NPL video signals in real-time via the DVI video output interface of Olympus endoscopes, and classifies each frame to the predefined anatomical site. We compared the ENDOVISTA-ENT–assisted group with the conventional NPL group based on completeness-related performance during the examination.

We first collected data from 3630 patients who underwent NPL examinations between October 2021 and January 2024 at the Lu Wan Branch (RJLWH) and Zhoushan Branch (RJZSH) of Ruijin Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All examinations were performed using Olympus CV-170 and CV-190 endoscope processors. To further validate our findings, we also used external video test sets from 100 NPL patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (FAHSYSU) and 98 NPL patients at Ruijin Hospital (RJH), affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Performance of ENDOVISTA-ENT system in real NPL videos

We tested the monitoring of predefined anatomical coverage during NPL using 198 real-world NPL videos collected from FAHSYSU and RJH. The test results are shown in Table 2, Supplementary Table 3, and Fig. 2D, E. ENDOVISTA-ENT achieved a mean accuracy of 96.83% (95% CI, 96.10–97.56%) in predefined anatomical site monitoring, with accuracy ranging from 98.67 to 99.77% for each anatomical site across the 198 real NPL videos. The mean sensitivity and specificity were 97.10% (95% CI, 96.55–97.65%) and 99.77% (95% CI, 99.72–99.82%), with ranges from 94.95 to 98.99% for sensitivity and from 99.17 to 99.95% for specificity. Additionally, ENDOVISTA-ENT processed the videos at a high frame rate of 20 frames per second.

Prospective study in real world

Between 22 November 2024 and 29 May 2025, totally 321 patients were recruited for the clinical validation trial of the ENDOVISTA-ENT system. The trial flowchart is shown in Fig. 3. Three patients were excluded due to not meet with the inclusion criteria. The remaining 318 patients were randomly assigned to the ENDOVISTA-ENT group and the conventional NPL group, with 10 patients dropping out during the study. Ultimately, 318 patients were included in the full analysis set (FAS), with 159 in the ENDOVISTA-ENT group and 159 in the control group. A total of 308 patients met the protocol requirements and were included in the per protocol set (PPS), with 151 in the ENDOVISTA-ENT group and 157 in the control group. For cases with missing outcome data due to different reasons, the worst observed value within the same study group was used for imputation, and a FAS analysis was performed. In the FAS analysis, the ENDOVISTA-ENT group again showed higher anatomical coverage than the control group (91.14 vs. 82.91%, P < 0.0001; 95% CI, 5.28–11.17%). These findings remained consistent across different analysis sets and imputation strategies, supporting the robustness of the study conclusions in real clinical settings.

In terms of examination time, the PPS analysis showed no significant difference in the mean NPL duration between the two groups (120.4 vs. 113.0 s, P = 0.1028). In the FAS analysis, the mean NPL examination time in the ENDOVISTA-ENT group was also comparable to that in the control group (123.3 vs. 113.2 s, P = 0.0620).

To evaluate the examination performance of the ENDOVISTA-ENT system in real clinical settings, we analyzed 308 prospectively collected NPL videos. ENDOVISTA-ENT system achieved a mean accuracy of 97.49% (95% CI, 96.91–97.97%) in the recognition of predefined anatomical sites. The system demonstrated high sensitivity, with a mean value of 98.89% (95% CI, 97.47–98.47%), while maintaining good specificity, with a mean value of 93.50% (95% CI, 90.65–95.53%). Site-specific performance is presented in Fig. 5B. Recognition accuracy across anatomical sites ranged from 95.45 to 99.03%, sensitivity ranged from 95.19% to 100%, and specificity ranged from 85.71 to 97.37%. Supplementary Tables 16–18 summarize the recognition performance of the ENDOVISTA-ENT system across the predefined anatomical sites in the ENDOVISTA-ENT group, the control group, and the overall study population, respectively. In addition, agreement between ENDOVISTA-ENT and the expert reference was quantified using Cohen’s kappa (Supplementary Table 18). In both the FAS and PPS, κ was 0.85 (95% CI, 0.80–0.90) in the ENDOVISTA-ENT group and 0.89 (95% CI, 0.86–0.92) in the control group; the overall κ was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.90).

Based on PPS data, we further evaluated the effect of the ENDOVISTA-ENT system on the completeness of NPL examinations among endoscopists with different levels of experience. The analysis included six endoscopists, who were stratified according to years of practice and prior procedure volume. The distribution of examinations across groups is shown in Supplementary Table 19. As shown in Fig. 5C and Table 5, the use of ENDOVISTA-ENT was associated with a significant increase in predefined anatomical site coverage across all experience levels during NPL (all P < 0.001). A conservative sensitivity analysis was performed using a worst-case imputation approach. For patients with missing outcome data, values were replaced with the worst observed outcome within the same group. Under this assumption, the FAS analysis shown in Table 5 indicated that the ENDOVISTA-ENT group continued to achieve higher predefined anatomical coverage than the control group across all levels of endoscopist experience. These results were consistent with the PPS analysis and support the robustness of the study conclusions.

To further evaluate the effects of AI assistance and operator experience on anatomical coverage, we performed a two-way ANOVA (Table 6). In the FAS, AI assistance showed a significant main effect (F = 37.267, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.107). Operator experience also showed a main effect (F = 3.212, p = 0.042), although the effect size was small (partial η² = 0.020). No significant interaction between AI assistance and operator experience was observed (F = 0.282, p = 0.755). In the PPS, the main effect of AI assistance remained significant (p < 0.001), whereas the main effect of operator experience was not significant (p = 0.100), and no interaction was observed (p = 0.315). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to develop and validate an intelligent medical device, ENDOVISTA-ENT, that supports quality control during NPL. Unlike most existing endoscopy tools, the system monitors procedural completeness rather than detecting lesions. By recognizing predefined anatomical sites and providing real-time feedback on coverage, the system aims to standardize the NPL workflow and reduce operator experience-related variability. Our prospective randomized controlled trial showed that ENDOVISTA-ENT-assisted NPL examinations achieved significantly higher predefined anatomical coverage without significant increase in examination time. The system provided observable support across physicians with different skill levels, particularly among junior endoscopists. Together, these findings suggest that ENDOVISTA-ENT offers a practical approach to improving the accuracy, consistency, and overall quality of routine NPL examinations.

Recent studies have shown that deep convolutional neural networks perform well in medical image analysis21–24. Among commonly used architectures, including EfficientNet, ResNet50, ConvNeXt-Base, and DenseNet121, all of them demonstrate good performance in classification tasks. Prior work has reported that EfficientNet shows more stable advantages in accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, and specificity, especially under fixed decision thresholds25. This stability makes it suitable for clinical settings that require consistent and reliable predictions. The performance of EfficientNet is largely attributed to its compound scaling strategy, which balances network depth, width, and input resolution within a unified framework. This scaling strategy improves its feature representation in the condition of limited computational cost. By contrast, models based on ResNet, DenseNet, or Transformer architectures often depend on larger model sizes and higher computation. These requirements may restrict their inference efficiency and clinical deployment, even if high values of the area under the curve are achieved26–28. In this study, we compared ResNet50, ConvNeXt-Base, and DenseNet121 under the same classification tasks. EfficientNet-B0 showed the best overall performance, achieving higher accuracy with more stable predictions. These results indicate that EfficientNet-B0 offers a practical balance between performance, efficiency, and clinical applicability for intelligent endoscopic analysis.

AI is increasingly used in medical endoscopic imaging. Computer-aided detection has been shown to improve colorectal polyps’ detection in several randomized controlled trials29–32. Compared with the gastrointestinal tract, the nasopharyngolaryngeal airway is narrower, more anatomically complex, and containing more physiologically blind areas. These characteristics make NPL more technically challenging33. Most existing AI studies in NPL focus on lesion recognition and diagnosis, including models for detecting nasopharyngeal, laryngeal, and hypopharyngeal cancers using white-light endoscopic images, as well as smartphone-based screening tools34–37. However, the performance of these models depends highly on image quality and examination completeness. These factors vary in real-world practice, particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources. Given the persistently low early detection rates of nasopharyngolaryngeal cancers38, prioritizing examination completeness may offer greater clinical value than approaches relying solely on lesion-based image recognition.

Quality indicators in gastrointestinal endoscopy emphasize examination completeness39, and China also uses completeness as a national screening quality metric40. Several studies have tested AI quality-control tools for tracking coverage during gastrointestinal endoscopy. For example, WISENSE uses fixed upper gastrointestinal locations to estimate blind spots in real time, and a randomized trial reported a lower blind-spot rate with WISENSE assistance (5.86 vs. 22.46%)41. Similarly, Li and colleagues proposed cumulative colorectal mucosal exposure area (CCMEA) for colonoscopy, and they reported a higher adenoma detection rate in examinations that met the CCMEA criterion42. Even so, routine use of these systems remains limited. Designs built for gastrointestinal endoscopy also do not transfer well to NPL, because the anatomy, landmarks, and examination path are different. NPL quality also varies across operators, and practical tools to supervise completeness are still scarce. These realities make anatomical coverage a more basic process target in NPL. In our study, ENDOVISTA-ENT provided measurable coverage feedback and increased mean anatomical coverage (93.08 vs. 83.50%, P < 0.0001). We note that Cohen’s kappa is sensitive to the distribution of ratings. It may appear lower when “adequate visualization” is common across sites, even if the observed agreement is high. For this reason, we also report observed agreement (overall accuracy, i.e., the proportion of site-level judgments that match the expert reference) to support our conclusions.

To date, most AI studies focus on identifying a specific target in static images or short video segments. Dong et al. developed an explainable AI diagnostic system, ENDOANGEL-ED, using 4482 images and 296 focal gastroscopy lesion videos, and showed that it improved endoscopist diagnostic performance in gastroscopic images and videos43. He et al. collected 8816 frames from nasal endoscopy videos and developed NPC-SDNet for nasopharyngeal carcinoma segmentation and diagnosis, achieving a diagnostic accuracy of 95% on video frames44. Ma et al. retrospectively collected 12,302 endoscopic images of esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma and built an AI model for tumor staging with favorable diagnostic performance45. However, these studies are typically based on manually selected high-quality images or short video clips. These controlled conditions may overestimate real-world performance, for their validations are conducted under near-ideal conditions. In contrast, the ENDOVISTA-ENT system developed in this study provides real-time procedural guidance and quality-control feedback during NPL examinations. More importantly, the system was validated through a prospective randomized controlled trial. This study design allows a more accurate assessment of its performance and applicability in routine clinical practice.

For use in routine clinical practice, AI systems should integrate into existing workflows with minimal disruption31. ENDOVISTA-ENT was designed with this goal in mind. The system connects through a standard video interface (Fig. 4) and does not change current equipment or examination steps. This approach may contribute to breaking barriers of clinical use and wider adoption. During examinations, the system displays progress information alongside the conventional endoscopic image. The two interfaces are presented together to minimize distraction. This setup follows usual clinical habits and allows endoscopists to receive guidance while maintaining focused on the procedure. Moreover, the system applies video frame smoothing to reduce brief fluctuations in real-time recognition. This feature improves the continuity of system output and supports stable performance in clinical settings.

During conventional NPL examinations, endoscopists are often interrupted by the procedure to capture images on their own or relying on assistants41. ENDOVISTA-ENT excuses the endoscopists from the procedure by capturing images of predefined anatomical sites automatically and storing the complete NPL video at the same time. This design contributes to more complete and consistent documentations of examinations. The system also tracks examination time in real time and records the total duration automatically. This information gives endoscopists a clear view of how long each procedure takes and helps them manage the examination pace more effectively. By reducing dependence on personal time estimation, the system may minimize performance variations arising from individual habits or external pressures.

Another important finding was the consistent increase in predefined anatomical sites coverage during NPL examinations with ENDOVISTA-ENT assistance. Coverage improved across different experience levels, with the largest gain observed among junior physicians. In this subgroup, mean coverage increased from 80.30% to 92.61%. These results indicate ENDOVISTA-ENT can provide effective procedural guidance during NPL. This may support its role in promoting a more standardized NPL workflow and indicates its potential as a training tool to reduce operator experience-related variability during the learning phase.

In this study, we propose a paradigm that embeds AI into the clinical workflow to provide real-time process feedback. The system checks whether key anatomical sites are visited and whether required views are obtained, rather than attempting to make diagnoses. This paradigm may be applicable to other procedure- or imaging-based clinical settings that rely on standardized viewing and complete acquisition, such as gastroscopy, colonoscopy, bronchoscopy, cystoscopy, hysteroscopy, and ultrasound. In these contexts, real-time guidance could help clinicians follow a predefined protocol and improve examination completeness. At the same time, we acknowledge that our current anatomical definitions and implementation details are specific to the specialty and local practice. Consequently, direct transfer to other fields will require redefining target views and workflow standards. Field-specific validation will also be needed. In future studies, we plan to develop and validate more standardized quality indicators for NPL, such as a mucosal visibility score that accounts for secretions and blur. Furthermore, we plan to examine the relationship between procedural quality metrics and clinically relevant outcomes. We also intend to evaluate the impact of ENDOVISTA-ENT on learning curves and competency acquisition during training.

This study has several limitations that should be considered: (1) Our prespecified primary focus—procedural completeness can’t fully guarantee visualization quality in anatomically challenging regions. Although ENDOVISTA-ENT provides real-time feedback on coverage and prompts for unvisited regions, coverage alone does not guarantee adequate exposure or high-quality visualization of traditionally blind areas. These factors still depend on operator technique and patient anatomy. (2) ENDOVISTA-ENT was tested only with Olympus CV-170 and CV-190 processors and ENF-VH and ENF-V3 endoscopes. Its performance with other devices remains unknown and needs further study. (3) Because the system was used during the examination, endoscopists could not be blinded to group assignment. This might influence how examinations were performed, although we use video recording and blinded outcome to reduce this effect. (4) It remains uncertain whether better coverage leads to higher rates of early lesion detection. This correlation requires larger external validation cohorts with longer follow-up. (5) The external validation dataset was small and did not fully reflect differences across populations. Broader multicenter studies in different regions are needed to confirm the robustness and clinical use of the system.

In summary, this study shows that ENDOVISTA-ENT, a deep learning–based system, can support endoscopic quality control during NPL by tracking key nasopharyngeal and laryngeal anatomical sites in real time. The system records examination time and captures standard images without changing routine practice. In the prospective randomized controlled trial, NPL examinations assisted by ENDOVISTA-ENT achieved higher anatomical coverage without a significant increase in examination time. These findings suggest that real-time AI-assisted quality control system can support a more standardized NPL workflow and improve endoscopists’ procedural completeness during NPL.

Methods

This study comprised four main components: dataset collection, model development, construction of the ENDOVISTA-ENT system, and validation through a prospective clinical trial. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of Ruijin Hospital, Luwan Branch (RJLWH, approval no. 2023-013), Ruijin Hospital, Zhoushan Branch (RJZSH, approval no. 2023-039), Ruijin Hospital (RJH, approval no. 2024-133), and the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (FAHSYSU, approval no. 2024-117). Written informed consent was waived for the retrospective data.

Model development datasets and preprocessing

We retrospectively collected data from 3,630 NPL examinations performed at RJLWH and RJZSH from October 2021 to January 2024 for training and validation of ENDOVISTA-ENT. All examinations were conducted using Olympus CV-170 and CV-190 endoscopic processors. ENDOVISTA-ENT performs two sequential classification tasks. Model 1 removes unqualified frames, including extra-body views, blurred images, operation-related scenes, and clearly abnormal frames. Model 2 then classifies qualified frames into 11 standard nasopharyngolaryngeal anatomical sites. As there is currently no international consensus on standardized anatomical site coverage for NPL, we selected the 11 anatomical sites in this study based on expert consensus documents developed by Chinese otolaryngology specialists46,47.

Based on this workflow, we constructed two independent datasets for Model 1 and Model 2. Both datasets were split using a hold-out strategy and randomly divided into training, validation, and internal test sets at a 7:2:1 ratio. To prevent data leakage, images from the same subject were assigned to only one subset. We repeated random splits and experiments multiple times and reported the mean performance to ensure comparable data distributions across subsets. Dataset distributions are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Dataset 1 was used to develop Model 1, which distinguishes in vitro from in vivo endoscopic images. Initial annotations were performed by three otolaryngologists with more than 2 years of clinical experience and were reviewed by one otolaryngologist with over 5 years of experience. Representative images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A. Dataset 1 included 16,040 images, comprising 8032 in vivo images and 8008 in vitro images.

Dataset 2 was used to develop Model 2 for classification of 11 standard predefined anatomical sites. Two otolaryngologists with more than 6 years of experience independently annotated the images. Disagreements were resolved by a senior associate chief physician with over 15 years of experience. Representative images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2B. Dataset 2 included 43,540 images. The site-wise distribution is also reported in Supplementary Table 1.

After overall performance evaluation, EfficientNet showed the best results and was selected as the base model for this study. The comparison results are shown in Table 7 and Fig. 6. In this study, we used EfficientNet-B0 as the backbone model. We removed the original classification layer and built a new task-specific head, followed by end-to-end fine-tuning of all parameters. All input images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets. Data augmentation was applied only to the training set49, including random affine transformations, color jittering, and normalization using ImageNet statistics50.

Model training used the Adam optimizer51 with an initial learning rate of 5 × 10−4 and a MultiStepLR schedule52. We trained the model for 50 epochs with a batch size of 16 and used multiclass cross-entropy loss. We selected the final model based on the highest validation accuracy. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, sensitivity, macro-averaged F1-score, and specificity. All experiments were conducted at the Jingying Upper Airway Medical AI Laboratory in Shanghai using an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 3060 GPU (Table 7).

Development of the ENDOVISTA-ENT system and video testing

After model development, we integrated Model 1 and Model 2 to build the ENDOVISTA-ENT system. The system captures NPL video in real time through the DVI output of Olympus endoscopes and sends video frames to an image preprocessing module. After preprocessing, each frame is first classified as in vitro or in vivo. In vitro frames are discarded, while in vivo frames are used for recognition of predefined anatomical sites. To improve stability in real-time use, the system applies a frame-smoothing strategy based on random forest and k-nearest neighbor algorithms. A result is displayed only when at least four of five consecutive frames produce the same classification.

To assess its generalizability under real clinical conditions, we conducted video testing with ENDOVISTA-ENT. The test set included 198 NPL videos from two external centers, FAHSYSU and RJH. The videos were recorded using Olympus CV-170 and CV-190 processors with ENF-VH and ENF-V3 endoscopes. All videos were processed frame by frame, and recognition results were recorded to evaluate overall system performance in real-world video scenarios.

A prospective randomized controlled trial

This study was a prospective, double-center, single-blind, randomized, parallel-controlled trial. The trial was designed to evaluate whether ENDOVISTA-ENT could improve endoscopists’ procedural completeness during NPL. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of FAHSYSU and RJH. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091245).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using PASS 2023 for a superiority trial with 1:1 allocation. The primary endpoint was overall anatomical coverage, defined as the proportion of predefined anatomical sites adequately visualized during NPL for each participant (number of covered sites/total number of predefined sites). Because published data on NPL anatomical coverage are limited, the assumptions were based on a pilot dataset. We assumed a 10% absolute between-group difference in mean anatomical coverage, a common standard deviation of 0.15, and a superiority margin of 5%. These assumptions were derived from the pilot dataset and were considered reasonable for trial planning. The superiority margin was prespecified as the minimum relevant difference for superiority testing. With a one-sided α of 0.025 and 80% power, the required sample size was 143 participants per group. After allowing for a 10% dropout rate, the planned total sample size was 318 participants, with 159 participants in each group.

Participants

Patients who underwent NPL at FAHSYSU and RJH between 22 November 2024 and 29 May 2025 were enrolled. All participants were 18–80 years of age and provided written informed consents. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy; a history of allergy to local anesthetics such as tetracaine or lidocaine; language or communication barriers; inability to complete the examination in the supine position; and any condition judged by the investigators to make participation unsuitable.

Randomization

Eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either ENDOVISTA-ENT-assisted NPL (ENDOVISTA-ENT group) or conventional NPL without ENDOVISTA-ENT (control group). Randomization was performed using a center-stratified block design with a block size of 6. An independent statistician generated the allocation sequence in advance using SAS (version 9.4). After enrollment, group assignment was implemented using sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes that were opened in enrollment order. Participants were not blinded to group assignment because NPL was performed under topical anesthesia while they remained conscious. Endoscopists were unaware of allocation before assignment and were informed of group assignment only after allocation and before the procedure. Outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group assignment.

Procedures

Six endoscopists participated in the study. Each center, FAHSYSU and RJH, included one senior endoscopist with at least 5 years of NPL experience and more than 2000 procedures, one intermediate endoscopist with at least 3 years of experience and more than 1000 procedures, and one junior endoscopist with at least 1 year of experience and more than 200 procedures. Before trial initiation, all endoscopists received standardized training on the 11 predefined nasopharyngolaryngeal anatomical sites and on the ENDOVISTA-ENT interface.

Each eligible patient underwent NPL performed by the assigned endoscopist on the examination day. In the ENDOVISTA-ENT group, endoscopists performed routine NPL with the ENDOVISTA-ENT system assistance. Besides the conventional endoscopic view, the system displayed extra real-time information, including a schematic of covered anatomical sites, examination time, examination progress (showed as the proportion of covered sites), and the currently recognized anatomical site (Fig. 4A). In the control group, the ENDOVISTA-ENT display was covered to ensure a conventional NPL examination (Fig. 4B).

During each examination, an assistant physician recorded the start and end time and archived the whole video. An expert panel reviewed the original recordings later. The panel included three physicians: two senior reviewers with at least 10 years of NPL experience and one adjudicator with more than 15 years of experience. The two senior reviewers independently assessed anatomical site coverage. When their assessments differed, the adjudicator made the final decision, which served as the reference standard. A research assistant recorded the independent outputs of ENDOVISTA-ENT and compared them with the panel’s conclusions to assess system performance.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was procedural completeness during NPL, defined as overall anatomical coverage for each participant during the examination. Anatomical coverage was calculated as the proportion of adequately visualized sites among 11 prespecified anatomical sites. Reviewers determined whether each site was adequately visualized based on two criteria: (1) site-specific anatomical landmarks were identifiable to localize the region, and (2) the mucosal surface was clear and sufficiently interpretable for clinical inspection. At least two reviewers performed the assessments, and the expert panel’s final decision served as the reference standard.

Secondary endpoints included (1) total NPL examination time (seconds), recorded by an assistant and verified using video timestamps from endoscope insertion to withdrawal. We compared total procedure duration between groups and did not record site-level dwell time. (2) the independent recognition performance of ENDOVISTA-ENT system for anatomical site identification in real-world videos, reported as site-level accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.

Statistical analysis

All randomized participants who followed the intention-to-treat principle were included in the FAS, regardless of the intervention received. Participants who adhered to the protocol and completed all outcome assessments comprised the PPS. According to the intention-to-treat principle, all randomized participants were included whenever possible, with exclusions minimized through reasonable handling of the data. Baseline analyses were performed in the FAS, whereas analyses of the primary and secondary endpoints were performed in both the FAS and PPS.

The primary analysis was performed in both datasets. For the primary endpoint (overall anatomical coverage), between-group differences were quantified using the mean difference (MD) with 95% CI. We compared groups using an independent-samples t test when the distribution was approximately normal, and the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met; otherwise, we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U test). In addition to the primary comparisons between groups, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to further evaluate the effects of AI assistance and operator experience on anatomical coverage. The model included treatment group (AI vs. control), operator experience level (senior, intermediate, junior), and their interaction term. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (partial η²) for ANOVA analyses. For missing primary-endpoint values in the FAS, we applied a prespecified worst-case imputation approach using the worst observed value within the same treatment group and then performed the primary-endpoint analysis based on the imputed dataset. Missing values for outcomes other than the primary endpoint were not imputed, and analyses for these endpoints were based on observed data only.

For secondary endpoints (e.g., total examination time and the recognition performance of ENDOVISTA-ENT for anatomical site identification), we also reported MDs with 95% CIs and used an independent-samples t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. When the chi-square test was not applicable or expected cell counts were small (e.g., any expected count <5), Fisher’s exact test was used.

Superiority was concluded if the lower bound of the 95% CI for the between-group difference in anatomical coverage exceeded 5%. All hypothesis tests were two-sided, except that the sample size calculation used a one-sided α of 0.025. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4).
