Financial Distress among US Adults with Cardiovascular and Cerebrovascular Disease History: A Nationally Representative Analysis
Authors: Soonmyung A Hwang, Jaan K Nandwani, Laura K Stein, Parul Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases represent major causes of morbidity, mortality, and economic burden in the USA. Growing evidence demonstrates high rates of financial distress, defined as the negative impact of medical costs on a person’s financial wellbeing, among individuals with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. This study aimed to examine the association between cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease history and financial distress among US adults and to identify factors associated with financial distress in this high-risk population.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of pooled 2021–2023 National Health Interview Survey data. Financial distress was defined using self-reported measures of medical affordability and cost-related medication nonadherence. Propensity score matching was used to balance cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease and non-disease cohorts. Multivariable logistic regression models assessed the association between disease history and financial distress and identified factors associated with financial distress among the disease cohort.ResultsAfter matching, 20,246 respondents were included in each cohort. Financial distress was more prevalent among the disease cohort than the non-disease cohort (47.1% vs. 45.9%, p = 0.042), with higher odds of any financial distress (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.09, p = 0.014). The disease cohort had greater odds of problems paying medical bills (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.24–1.41, p < 0.001) and inability to afford prescription medications (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.11–1.31, p < 0.001). Among the disease cohort, female sex, Hispanic ethnicity, lower educational attainment, and lack of insurance were associated with higher odds of financial distress, whereas older age and Medicaid coverage were associated with lower odds.ConclusionsCardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease is associated with greater financial distress across multiple domains. Targeted strategies to reduce cost-related healthcare barriers in this population are urgently needed.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Cerebrovascular disease, Health disparities, Stroke, Financial toxicity

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases are leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the USA, resulting in substantial public health burden. Between 2017 and 2020, an estimated 127.9 million US adults (48.6%) had some form of cardiovascular disease, with cardiovascular disease accounting for 941,652 deaths in the USA in 2022 [1]. With the rising prevalence of key risk factors, such as obesity and hypertension, along with a growing aging population, the burden of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease conditions is poised to increase further in the coming decades [2–4].

The economic impact of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease on society is profound. Between 2020 and 2021, total costs associated with cardiovascular disease exceeded USD 417 billion, and projections indicate that expenditures related to cardiovascular risk factors like hypertension and stroke will grow rapidly through 2050 [1, 2]. Much of this burden is driven by the ongoing management required for chronic cardiovascular conditions, including long-term use of antihypertensives, statins, and antiplatelets, along with follow-up care and frequent monitoring [5, 6]. Further, the long-term recovery process can coincide with reduced income from work absence or disability, amplifying the financial strain on the patient. The cumulative impact of these factors can manifest as financial distress, which in this study will be defined as the material, psychological, or behavioral burden resulting from the inability to afford necessary medical care or to maintain financial stability during the course of illness [7, 8].

Growing evidence demonstrates that financial distress is common among individuals with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. Prior national studies have shown high levels of financial distress among adults with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including difficulty paying medical bills, medication nonadherence due to cost, and elevated rates of catastrophic health expenditures [9–11]. However, financial distress has yet been assessed comprehensively among patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. Importantly, for many patients, the financial burden of managing cardiovascular and/or cerebrovascular disease may become an overriding concern that rivals or evan surpasses the physical impact of the disease itself, fundamentally shaping their daily decision-making, treatment adherence, and overall quality of life. We sought to (1) estimate the prevalence of financial distress among US adults with a history of cardiovascular and/or cerebrovascular disease, (2) examine the association between cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease history and financial distress among US adults, and (3) identify sociodemographic and personal health factors associated with financial distress in individuals with a cardiovascular and/or cerebrovascular disease history.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

The National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), distributed by the National Center for Health Statistics, is a series of cross-sectional national surveys conducted annually [12]. The questionnaires collect self-reported information at the household, family, and personal level, including information on sociodemographic characteristics, diseases, health insurance coverage, access to healthcare services, and health-related behaviors of the US population of both adults and children. The 2021, 2022, and 2023 NHIS Adult sample datasets were pooled using the NHIS-recommended methods. Only individuals 18 years and older were included in the sample. Institutional Review Board approval was not required for this study, as NHIS data are publicly available and de-identified and all NHIS participants provided informed consent.

Cardiovascular/Cerebrovascular Disease History

The disease cohort consisted of adults with any cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease history, defined as answering “yes” to the question: “Have you EVER been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had… hypertension, angina, coronary heart disease, heart attack, or stroke?” Individuals who had any missing data or answered “did not know” to all of the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease entities of interest were excluded from the study sample. The non-disease cohort consisted of individuals who reported none of the cardiovascular or cerebrovascular conditions of interest and had complete data for all relevant variables (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/ced/accag003).

Study Outcomes

The main outcome of interest was any form of financial distress, defined based on responses to the following NHIS questions: “Worried about paying medical bills if sick or have an accident in the past 12 months,” “problems paying medical bills in the past 12 months,” “unable to pay medical bills in the past 12 months,” “couldn’t afford prescription medication in the past 12 months,” “skipped medication doses to save money in the past 12 months,” “delayed filling prescription to save money in the past 12 months,”, and “took less medication to save money in the past 12 months.” Most questions included the response options “yes,” “no,” “refused,” “not ascertained,” and “don’t know,” except the question regarding worry about paying medical bills offered the options “very worried,” “somewhat worried,” and “not at all worried.” In this case, financial distress was coded as present for respondents who answered “yes” to the “very worried” or “somewhat worried” options, while those who answered “not at all worried” were coded as not financial distress for this item. Financial distress was coded as present for respondents who either answered “yes,” “very worried,” or “somewhat worried” to the relevant items. Those who responded “refused,” “not ascertained,” or “don’t know” for any of the selected NHIS items were excluded from the study cohorts (online suppl. Table 2).

Study Measures

The following sociodemographic variables were included in our analyses: age (18–39, 40–64, 65+), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black/African-American, Hispanic, Asian, other), highest level of education attained (high school degree or less, some college or associate’s degree, bachelor’s degree and higher), and marital status (married, not married). The following personal health variables were also included in our analyses: health insurance status (private insurance, Medicaid, Medicare, other, no coverage), number of annual hospitalizations (0, 1, 2+), comorbidities (0, 1, 2, 3+), general health status (excellent, very good, good, fair, poor), and smoking status (never smoker, current smoker, former smoker). The frequency of comorbidities was determined from a list of self-reported chronic medical conditions: hyperlipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, asthma, cancer, and diabetes mellitus (online suppl. Table 3).

Statistical Analysis

In order to ensure comparable analyses between the two patient cohorts, we employed propensity score matching with 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement and a caliper of 0.05. Propensity scores were estimated utilizing a logistic regression model, with cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease history as the dependent variable. Independent variables utilized in the matching process include: age, race/ethnicity, highest level of education, marital status, general health status, smoking status, insurance status, annual hospitalizations, and number of comorbidities. Covariate balance after propensity score matching was assessed using standardized mean differences; an standardized mean difference below 0.10 was considered indicative of adequate balance (online suppl. Table 4).

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile ranges, as appropriate based on their distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regressions were conducted with clinically relevant covariates selected a priori based on existing literature and subject-matter expertise, regardless of statistical significance in univariable analyses, to assess the association between cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease status and financial distress, and also to identify associated factors of financial distress among those with history of cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease. All analyses were conducted using Statistical Analysis Software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The prevalence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease was 57.6% (n = 49,910) across the entire 2021–2023 NHIS dataset (Fig. 1). After propensity score matching and exclusion of survey participants with missing variables, we identified 20,246 adult respondents in the disease cohort and 20,246 respondents in the non-disease cohort (Fig. 1). The mean age of the disease cohort was 57.3 ± 15.9 years, while the mean age was 58.7 ± 15.4 years for the non-disease cohort (p < 0.001). Compared to the non-disease cohort, the disease cohort had a higher proportion of those aged 40–64 (50.8% vs. 48.9%, p = 0.020), those of non-Hispanic Black/African-American (12.4% vs. 10.6%, p < 0.001), Hispanic (13.0% vs. 11.5%, p < 0.001) and Asian (5.1% vs. 4.4%, p < 0.001) race/ethnicity, Medicaid coverage (8.0% vs. 7.1%, p < 0.001), no insurance coverage (5.7% vs. 5.2%, p < 0.001), and “poor” general health status (4.5% vs. 2.8%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Among the disease cohort, the mean age for those with any financial distress was 55.2 ± 14.7 years, while those with no financial distress were 61.9 ± 15.4 years (p < 0.001). Compared to the cohort of individuals with a history of cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease and no financial distress, those with any financial distress had a higher proportion of those aged 18–39 years (17.0% vs. 10.0%, p < 0.001), 40–64 years (58.7% vs. 43.8%, p < 0.001), females (56.4% vs. 51.8%, p < 0.001), non-Hispanic Black/African-American (13.5% vs. 11.3%, p < 0.001), Hispanic (17.0% vs. 9.5%, p < 0.001) race/ethnicity, high school degree or less (39.8% vs. 31.9%, p < 0.001), some college or associate’s degree (30.7% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.001), not married (55.3% vs. 51.4%, p < 0.001). The prevalence of any financial distress was 47.1% (n = 9,544) among the disease cohort and 45.9% (n = 9,298) among the non-disease cohort (p = 0.042). A greater proportion of the disease cohort had problems paying medical bills (11.7% vs. 9.2%, p < 0.001) and more reported that they could not afford prescription medicine (6.2% vs. 5.2%, p < 0.001). In a multivariable logistic regression model assessing the association between disease history and financial distress, there was a significantly greater odd of having any financial distress among the disease cohort compared to the non-disease cohort (aOR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.09, p = 0.014). In addition, there were greater odds of the following financial distress measures for the disease cohort: problems paying medical bills (aOR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.24–1.41, p < 0.001), inability to afford prescription medicine (aOR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.11–1.31, p < 0.001), and not getting care due to cost (aOR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19, p = 0.027) (Fig. 3, online suppl. Table 6).

Among the disease cohort, we conducted a multivariable logistic regression analysis assessing the associations between selected sociodemographic and personal health variables and any financial distress. Female sex (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.26–1.46, p < 0.001), Hispanic race/ethnicity (aOR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.45–1.79, p < 0.001), having a high school degree or less (aOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.26–1.52, p < 0.001), and some college or associate’s degree (aOR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.18–1.42, p < 0.001) were all associated with greater odds of financial distress. Further, individuals with no insurance coverage (aOR = 3.39, 95% CI: 2.81–4.09, p < 0.001), “very good” health status (aOR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.12–1.43, p < 0.001), “good” health status (aOR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.57–2.00, p < 0.001), “fair” health status (aOR = 2.55, 95% CI: 2.22–2.92, p < 0.001), “poor” health status (aOR = 2.83, 95% CI: 2.28–3.37, p < 0.001), one annual hospitalization (aOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05–1.27, p = 0.003), two or more annual hospitalizations (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.18–1.54, p < 0.001), and two medical comorbidities (aOR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.26, p = 0.009) had a significantly greater odds of having financial distress. In contrast, individuals aged 40–64 years (aOR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.74–0.91, p < 0.001), 65 years and older (OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.32–0.42, p < 0.001), those with Medicaid coverage (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.63–0.84, p < 0.001) and “other” insurance coverage (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.62–0.83, p < 0.001) were less likely to have financial distress (Table 3).

Among the disease cohort, we further examined associations between sociodemographic and personal health factors and each individual financial distress measure using multivariable logistic regression. Female sex, lack of insurance coverage, those with “good,” “fair,” or “poor” health status, current smoking status, and having two or more annual hospitalizations were each associated with significantly higher odds across all financial distress measures (p < 0.05). In contrast, adults aged 65 years or older were associated with lower odds across all financial distress measures (p < 0.001). Non-Hispanic Black or African-American race/ethnicity was associated with higher odds of problems paying medical bills (aOR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08–1.49, p = 0.003) and not being able to afford prescription medication (aOR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.11–1.66, p = 0.003) but was associated with lower odds of delaying care due to cost (aOR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.59–0.90, p = 0.003) and delaying counseling/therapy due to cost (aOR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.46–0.85, p = 0.003). Medicaid coverage was associated with lower odds of worrying about paying medical bills (aOR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.55–0.74, p < 0.001), skipping, delaying, or taking less medication to save money (aOR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.58–0.98, p = 0.034), problems paying medical bills (aOR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.54–0.82, p < 0.001), and delaying care due to cost (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.44–0.78, p < 0.001). Further, having one or more medical comorbidities were associated with higher odds of skipping, delaying, or taking less medication (p < 0.001) and not being able to afford prescription medication (p < 0.001). Attainment of a high school degree or less was associated with higher odds of worrying about paying medical bills (aOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.27–1.52, p < 0.001), problems paying medical bills (aOR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.19–1.59, p < 0.001), while it was associated with lowers odds of delaying counseling/therapy due to cost (aOR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.36–0.61, p < 0.001) (online suppl. Table 7; online suppl. Table 8).

Discussion

Across a nationally representative sample of US adults from 2021 to 2023, we found that adult respondents with cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease history reported a statistically significant but modest association with financial distress compared to those without disease history (OR = 1.05), with greater difficulty paying medical bills (OR = 1.32), affording prescription medications (OR = 1.20), and obtaining needed care (OR = 1.10). Relatedly, lack of insurance coverage (OR = 3.39) and poor self-rated health status (OR = 2.83) were even stronger determinants of financial distress. These findings suggest that while disease history contributes to financial distress, structural factors like insurance coverage gaps are likely powerful drivers as well.

Our results expand upon prior literature on financial distress in cardiovascular disease. An earlier study using 2013–2018 NHIS data demonstrated that atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease was associated with higher financial toxicity than cancer [7]. By incorporating multiple domains of financial distress and adjusting for sociodemographic and health factors, we demonstrate that financial distress remains pervasive. The ability to afford appropriate treatment is particularly important for patients with cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, as meta-analyses show that improved adherence to antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medications significantly reduces cardiovascular events, stroke, and all-cause mortality [13, 14]. When patients face financial barriers to essential medications, such as antiplatelet agents, statins, and anti-arrhythmics, adherence may decline, increasing the risk of disease progression, and adverse outcomes.

Sociodemographic and insurance-related disparities amplified financial vulnerability in our analysis. Female sex, Hispanic ethnicity, lower educational attainment, and unmarried status were associated with greater odds of financial distress. Conversely, older adults and those with Medicaid coverage were less likely to report hardship. The protective effects of Medicaid are notable, particularly given persistent coverage gaps in non-expansion states [15]. The lower prevalence of financial distress among older adults likely reflects reduced spending needs and access to safety net programs such as Medicare and Social Security, though this population remains vulnerable to catastrophic medical expenses [16, 17]. These results align with prior studies showing that financial distress in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease arises from systemic insurance gaps and sociodemographic inequities [7, 18].

Identifying characteristics associated with financial distress provides insight into the development of effective solutions and where to target efforts at the policy, health care system, population, and individual level. Financial navigation and counseling programs, cost-transparency tools, and proactive provider engagement in cost discussions – strategies proven effective in oncology – may help reduce distress and improve adherence among patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease [19]. The development of standardized tools, such as the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST) measure validated in oncology, may systematically identify patients at risk of financial distress [20]. Furthermore, there has been a recent shift toward the use of technology-enabled platforms, such as hybrid models integrating artificial intelligence-driven resource matching with human support. Such platforms are increasingly used to enhance accessibility and scalability of such financial navigation, especially in large or resource-limited settings [21]. Importantly, upstream interventions such as Medicaid expansion [22, 23], policies to reduce prescription drug costs [24], and programs addressing social determinants of health like food and housing security are necessary to mitigate the broader systemic contributors to financial hardship [25].

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths. The use of 3 years of nationally representative NHIS data allows for robust population-level estimates that are generalizable to US adults outside of institutional settings. The study also applied rigorous analytic methods, including propensity score matching to balance sociodemographic and health-related characteristics between comparison groups and multivariable regression modeling to adjust for a wide range of clinical and social determinants. Further, the inclusion of multiple complementary measures of financial distress strengthens construct validity and provides a nuanced assessment of cost-related barriers to care.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, this analysis relied on self-reported survey data, which are subject to recall bias and potential misclassification of both disease history and financial distress measures. Second, individuals with missing or indeterminate responses for key exposure or outcome variables were excluded, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, particularly if missingness was nonrandom. Third, while smoking status was included as a behavioral covariate, other lifestyle factors that may serve as confounders or mediators, such as physical activity, alcohol use, and diet, were not incorporated in the present analysis. The omission of these variables limits the ability to determine whether associations between disease history and financial distress reflect direct financial pathways or are partially mediated through behavioral mechanisms. Further, the NHIS does not capture detailed, objective financial information, precluding linkage to quantitative measures such as out-of-pocket expenditures, household income volatility, debt burden, or catastrophic health spending. As a result, financial distress in this study reflects perceived and reported affordability challenges rather than direct financial metrics.

Additionally, while several measures related to cost-related medication behaviors were included, the NHIS does not allow for direct assessment of medication adherence, dosing accuracy, or persistence over time, nor can it distinguish between intentional nonadherence due to cost and other adherence barriers. We were also unable to assess disease duration, clinical severity, or treatment intensity, all of which may influence both financial distress and healthcare utilization. Furthermore, the study period spans 2021–2023, encompassing distinct phases of COVID-19 pandemic recovery during which financial stress, healthcare utilization, and health outcomes may have varied due to pandemic-related disruptions, shifting insurance coverage policies, and the expiration of expanded financial relief programs. Without adjustment for survey year, residual temporal confounding cannot be excluded. To address this, we conducted a sensitivity analysis adjusting for survey year in the multivariable models (online suppl. Table 9); findings were robust to this adjustment and did not differ meaningfully from the primary analysis. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits evaluation of temporal relationships between cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, financial distress, and downstream clinical outcomes. Financial distress may precede and contribute to adverse health outcomes through pathways such as delayed care-seeking or medication nonadherence, or alternatively, managing a serious chronic illness may itself generate or worsen financial hardship. Longitudinal studies are needed to disentangle these directions of effect. Nonetheless, these analyses provide updated, nationally representative estimates of the economic burden faced by patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.

In this nationally representative analysis of US adults, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease were associated with a modest yet significantly greater level of financial distress, with nearly half of affected individuals reporting difficulty affording essential medical care or medications. Financial distress was most pronounced among younger adults, women, racial and ethnic minority groups, individuals with lower educational attainment, and those lacking adequate insurance coverage, highlighting persistent sociodemographic and structural inequities. Taken together, our findings suggest that financial distress among patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease conditions represents a substantial and clinically relevant public health concern, highlighting the need for multilevel interventions spanning patients, providers, and policy. Future efforts to increase access to financial navigation and counseling programs in the clinical setting are vital. Further research is needed to validate cardiovascular-specific tools for measuring financial distress and to evaluate whether early screening and intervention can mitigate financial hardship and improve long-term outcomes.
