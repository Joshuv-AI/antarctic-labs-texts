Association between school bullying type and the risk of eating disorders among Chinese college students: the mediating role of body dissatisfaction
Authors: Linlin Xie, Qingchen Da, Yichao Chen, Yueliang Chen, Feng Wu, Liping Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe link between school bullying and eating disorders is a complex issue that is poorly understood globally, and is further complicated by the distinct cultural nuances within China. This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the association between different types of bullying and the risk of eating disorders among college students in Shantou City, China. Additionally, this study explores the mediating role of body dissatisfaction in the relationships between bullying roles and the risk of eating disorders.MethodsThe present study employed a convenience sampling technique to recruit college students aged 16 years or older from a university in Shantou. The survey encompassed socio-demographic data, as well as measures of bullying and the risk of eating disorders. Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, logistic regression, chi-square test, and mediation analysis were used to analyze the results.ResultsA total of 1643 students were investigated in this study, revealing a prevalence of the risk of eating disorders at 29.21%. Additionally, the prevalence of bullying was found to be 5.78% for bullies, 24.83% for victims, and 28.36% for bully-victims. Furthermore, all types of bullying showed a significant positive correlation with the risk of eating disorders. Notably, gender differences were observed in the associations among social manipulation, cyber victimization, cyber perpetration, and the risk of eating disorders, with males exhibiting stronger correlations. Body dissatisfaction plays a mediating role between bullying-victims and the risk of eating disorders.ConclusionsA positive association has been observed between the type of school bullying and the risk of eating disorders among college students in Shantou. Additionally, gender and body dissatisfaction have been identified as significant factors that contribute to the relationship between school bullying and the risk of eating disorders.Keywords: Bullying, Risk of eating disorders, Body dissatisfaction, Observational study

Introduction

School bullying is an important public health problem that endangers the physical and mental health of children and adolescents. It is characterized by three typical characteristics: intentionality, some repetitiveness, and imbalance of power [1]. Depending on the specific type, school bullying can be classified as physical, verbal, social, and property bullying [2]. A new type of bullying, cyberbullying, has also emerged in recent years [3]. Cyberbullying refers to the act of intentionally using email, chat services, SNS, or other electronic communication media to harass or intimidate individuals [4]. School bullying is widespread around the world, and in 2019 UNESCO found that about one-third of students worldwide had been bullied at least once in the last month [5]. In China, the prevalence of bullying victimization is 22.7%, and the prevalence of bullying perpetration is 15.7% [6]. It is noteworthy that in China, cyberbullying is at a relatively high level compared to the global average. The average incidence of cyberbullying among adolescents is 23%, significantly higher than rates in countries like Germany, Australia, and Sweden [7]. School bullying also shows gender differences, with many studies indicating that boys have a higher rate than girls [8]. This may be due to boys’ greater propensity for impulsiveness and the fact that girls at this age are often more mature [9]. In various types of school bullying, male adolescents tend to engage more in physical bullying and cyberbullying, while female adolescents are more likely to engage in relational bullying [10]. In a bullying incident, common roles include bullies, victims, and bully-victims [11, 12]. The repercussions of school bullying are quite grave. Students who experience bullying as victims may develop psychological issues such as anxiety and depression, and could also suffer from eating disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder and other psychotic symptoms [13, 14]. Those who bully others may be at risk of developing antisocial personality traits and symptoms of mania [15, 16]. For individuals who are both bullies and victims, often referred to as bully-victims, the consequences could be doubly compounded [17].

Eating disorders are also one of the consequences of bullying. Eating disorders are a group of eating-related mental disorders including anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), binge eating disorders and other specified feeding or eating disorders [18]. A cohort study indicates that within childhood/adolescence, individuals experiencing bullying victimization were at increased risk for developing eating disorders as well as exhibiting associated features [19]. A Brazilian randomized controlled trial (RCT) study also confirmed that individuals with experiences of bullying exhibit more eating disorder behaviors, with female students being more prone to such behaviors than male students [20]. Eating disorders are also a serious issue in China, with a significant occurrence rate. The prevalence of AN and BN is high, with the prevalence of AN and BN per 100,000 population is 38.08 and 75.21, respectively [21]. However, the consequences of eating disorders are serious. Eating disorder has one of the highest suicide and mortality rates of all mental disorders [22].

Among the myriad factors influencing eating disorders, body dissatisfaction has been established as a significant influencing factor, with numerous studies indicating that it can predict future eating disordered behaviors [23]. Body dissatisfaction represents a negative evaluation of one’s physical attributes, and in an attempt to achieve a perceived satisfactory body shape, individuals may engage in dieting and extreme weight loss behaviors [24]. Adolescence is a critical period for the development of body image, as it is during these formative years that individuals begin to form a cohesive self-concept, which includes their physical appearance [25]. The significance of body image during this period cannot be understated, as it influences not only mental health outcomes but also social interactions and overall well-being [23]. Adolescents are highly attentive to both self-evaluation and the judgments of others, which can lead to lower satisfaction with their own body shape and emotional setbacks [26]. A cohort study conducted by Luis et al. on adolescents demonstrated that body dissatisfaction can predict the development of eating disorders two years later [27]. Dianne et al. found that high levels of body dissatisfaction can predict future dieting behaviors, extreme weight loss practices, and exercise behaviors [28].

Body dissatisfaction and school bullying are interrelated as both cause and effect. On one hand, body dissatisfaction may precipitate incidents of school bullying. Previous research has confirmed that adolescents with various body shapes, such as overweight, obese, and underweight individuals, are more likely to become victims of school bullying [29]. Studies have shown that dissatisfaction with body shape acts as a mediating factor between bullying victimization and overweight, thus potentially making them targets of others’ aggressive behavior or causing them to become victims themselves [30]. On the other hand, students who experience bullying are more likely to develop body dissatisfaction. Degradation and emotional abuse are integral components of bullying experiences and can significantly impact an individual’s perception of their body image. As noted in a recent study, such traumatic experiences are closely associated with the development of body dissatisfaction, highlighting the importance of addressing bullying as a potential antecedent to negative body image perceptions [31].

This connection between body image and bullying can further extend to the development of eating disorders, with the mechanisms varying significantly depending on the role one plays in bullying scenarios. For bullies, targeting others for their body shape may increase their sensitivity to their own body shape and lead to impulsive eating behaviors as a response to guilt. They often view eating as a means of self-control, which could contribute to a higher incidence of bulimic symptoms [19]. On the other hand, bullying often inflicts significant stress and trauma on its victims, which can manifest as various psychotic symptoms, including eating disorders [32]. It is noteworthy that conditions such as AN and BN are sometimes triggered by post-traumatic stress or are associated with risk factors for trauma [33].

In reviewing the existing literature on the relationship between school bullying and eating disorders, several gaps and disparities have become evident that warrant further investigation. Previous studies have focused more on Western countries and less on China. The unique cultural backdrop of China, with its emphasis on collectivist values and the concept of “face,” among other aspects, offers a distinct perspective on the dynamics of school bullying and eating disorders, which may differ from those observed in Western societies. In addition, gender may influence the association between school bullying and eating disorders. Males are more likely to experience school bullying, while eating disorders is more prevalent in young females, which may have an impact on the association [19]. What’s more, there is a lack of consensus on the role of body dissatisfaction in the association between school bullying and eating disorders. Some studies have suggested that bullying is not associated with body dissatisfaction, but does increase the prevalence of eating disorders [34]. However, another study has suggested that bullying is associated with body dissatisfaction and has a direct effect on bullies, while victims have an indirect effect [35]. Finally, the research on bullying and eating disorders is still incomplete. On the one hand, there is extensive research on the association between bullying victimization experiences and eating disorders, but the association between experience of bullying perpetration and eating disorder is under-researched. One study concluded that the experience of bullying perpetration was associated with eating disorders [19]. However, another meta-analysis showed that the experience of bullying perpetration was not associated with eating disorders [36]. On the other hand, relational bullying and cyberbullying have been linked to eating disorders behaviors in many studies, while the relationship between other bullying types and eating disorders behaviors has not been widely studied [37, 38].

Therefore, this study aims to examine the association between bullying experiences and the risk of eating disorders among Chinese college students, using Shantou City as an example, and to fill in the gaps in understanding the association between different bullying types and the risk of eating disorders, as well as to explore the mediating role of body dissatisfaction. To provide a theoretical basis for the prevention and control of future bullying and eating disorders.

Methods

Study design

Cross-sectional studies.

Participants

According to convenience sampling, college students aged 16 years or older were sampled from a university in Shantou. All participants voluntarily provided signed informed assent or consent. For participants under the age of 18, informed consent was obtained from their guardians. Participants were compensated for the time they dedicated to the study. Compensation was provided in the form of [cash/other forms of compensation] to acknowledge their contribution.

Procedure and settings

In October-December 2022, an online survey was conducted using the Questionnaire Star software. Twelve undergraduate students were recruited and trained as surveyors. The surveyors will be asked to distribute the questionnaires through their friends or organizations. Before the survey, the purpose of the survey and the principle of confidentiality were explained in the questionnaire and students were asked to sign an informed consent form. If students have questions about the questionnaire they can contact the surveyor for answers. After completing the questionnaire, the questionnaire will be checked. And if there are any omissions or missing information, the surveyors should be responsible for contacting the participant to make additions.

Measurement

Sociodemographic information

Sociodemographic information includes age, gender, ethnicity, boarding, home region, and only child status.

Bullying

Reference Huang, Jin-Yu, the Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS) and the Multidimensional Peer Bullying Scale (MPVS-RB) were adapted to measure the extent of past physical, relational, verbal, financial, and cyber bullying and victimization in the past year, using 20 items each [39]. Each of the 20 items on the MPVS and MPVS-RB scales was scored on a 5-point scale from 0 to 4, with 0 being “none” and 4 being “multiple times a week. The criteria for judging school bullying used in this study are as follows: in terms of bullying victimization, when the score of each dimension is > 1, it means that the student is being bullied or engaging in bullying perpetration in that dimension; when the total score of the five dimensions is > 1, it means that the student is being bullied or engaging in bullying perpetration at school. People who were both victims and perpetrators of bullying were defined as bully-victims. Cronbach’s α = 0.959 in MPVS and cronbach’s α = 0.973 in MPVS-RB.

Body dissatisfaction

The Stunkard Silhouette Scale was used, which provides nine body shapes from thin to obese for both men and women, numbered 1 to 9, and the subjects were asked to select their current and ideal body shapes [20]. The difference between the two scores was calculated from − 8 to + 8. And 0 represents satisfaction; negative values represent dissatisfaction with being thin; positive values represent dissatisfaction with being overweight; and higher scores represent more serious body dissatisfaction.

Risk of eating disorders

The Chinese version of the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire 6.0, EDE-Q 6.0 [40] was utilized to assess the risk of eating disorder in the past 28 days before investigation. The scale consists of four subscales evaluating psychopathological characteristics (restraint, eating concern, shape concern, and weight concern) and self-administered entries for eating disorder behaviors, with a total of 28 items. The subscales utilize a 7-point scoring system, ranging from 0 (indicating “not at all” or “not for a day”) to 6 (indicating “significantly” or “every day”). The total score was determined by taking the weighted average of the four subscale scores, which was determined by averaging all the items of each subscale. A total score of > 1.27 was considered indicative of a possibility of an eating disorder, according to the scale. The higher the subscale and total scale scores, the more severe the eating disorder of the subject. In this study, Cronbach’s α for the restraint subscale, the eating concern subscale, the shape concern subscale, and the weight concern subscale was 0.910, 0.840, 0.912, and 0.865, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using frequency and percentage, and continuous variables were described using means and standard deviation (SD) with normal distribution and median (range) without normal distribution. Differences in the association of bullying with baseline information were compared using chi-square tests. Differences in the distribution of different eating disorder scores across bullying roles were compared using Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. The association between different types of bullying and eating disorder were analyzed using logistic regression. A mediation model was constructed using bullying as the independent variable, eating disorder scores as the dependent variable, and body dissatisfaction scores as the mediating variable to test the mediating effects (Fig. 1). Gender was stratified to further assess the association by post stratification chi-square test. Mediational models were performed with SPSS PROCESS and Amos version 28. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 and R 4.2.1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Association between different bullying types and eating disorder

Statistical analysis indicated significant gender differences in the associations between social manipulation, cyber victimization, and the risk of eating disorders, with males being more susceptible to the effects of relational bullying and cyberbullying, which may increase the risk of developing eating disorders. Gender differences in the association between cyberbullying and the risk of eating disorders were statistically significant, showing a stronger correlation with a high risk of eating disorders in males compared to females. However, gender differences were not statistically significant in the association of the remaining bullying types with eating disorders. There were no statistically significant gender differences in bullying victimization and bullying perpetration (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Eating disorders and bullying are two types of adolescent health problems, and a growing body of research confirms an association between them. There are several reasons to explain the relationship between eating disorders and bullying. Studies have found that negative affect and functional impairment (e.g., difficulties in relationships with peers) are major predictors of eating disorders [41]. And bullying is among the common interpersonal problems. The present study confirmed that the experience of school bullying was associated with the risk of eating disorders among Chinese college students, filling a gap in Chinese research.

Compared to the 8–20.5% prevalence of eating disorders among college students found in previous studies, the present study was higher, at 29.21%. There are several potential reasons for this discrepancy. One possible explanation may be the advent of COVID-19. Our findings are in line with the COVID-19 study [42, 43]. Other studies have also concluded that the prevalence of eating disorders has increased after the occurrence of COVID-19 [44]. Another reason for the higher prevalence in our study may relate to the nature of the measurement used. Unlike clinical diagnoses of eating disorders, our assessment focused on the risk of developing eating disorders. This approach may capture a broader spectrum of behaviors and attitudes that could potentially lead to a full-blown clinical disorder, thus resulting in a higher percentage.

This study found a high prevalence of bullying among college students, with bullies constituting 5.78%, victims 24.83%, and bully-victims 28.36%. Notably, the bully-victims occupied a great proportion. Meanwhile, the present study also found that the bully-victims group had the highest scores in various types of the risk of eating disorders, which may be since that bully-victims was influenced by both bully and victim roles. Previous studies have also found more frequent binge eating and vomiting behaviors in bully-victims [19]. Bully-victims group were at-risk individuals in the bullying role who had more emotional and behavioral disorders, higher rates of emotional dysregulation, higher levels of behavioral problems, and lower self-esteem [45] Future research should focus on the bully-victims category.

Same as previous studies [19, 36], the present study concluded that school bullying was associated with the risk of eating disorders. In addition, different types of school bullying victimization and bullying perpetration were also associated with the risk of eating disorders, especially cyberbullying. From a psychological perspective, cyberbullying is analyzed as being associated with various psychological factors that may indirectly influence an individual’s dietary behaviors [46]. Cyberbullying may lead to compromised self-esteem in victims, thereby affecting their dietary behaviors. Moreover, peer influence within social networks can also impact the eating behaviors of adolescents. For instance, the emulation of others’ food intake through social media platforms [47].

This study found no gender differences in the associations of bullying victimization and bullying perpetration with the risk of eating disorders. However, specifically among the different types of bullying, there were statistically significant gender differences in the associations of social manipulation, cyber victimization, and the risk of eating disorders, and statistically significant gender differences in cyber perpetration and the risk of eating disorders. The gender differences for cyber perpetration and the risk of eating disorders were statistically significant. In contrast to previous studies, there were gender differences between cyber victimization and eating disorders [48]. In addition to this, we also found for the first time a gender difference between social manipulation, cyber perpetration, and the risk of eating disorders as well. The body image is the target of cyberbullying attacks [49]. Adolescents may be subjected to malicious comments or harassment for sharing their photos on the Internet, or ostracized on the Internet for being overweight, and these negative factors are associated with eating disorders [50]. The relationship between bullying and disordered eating is well-established. The association between relational bullying and eating disorders may be related to increased shame due to low self-esteem and/or negative evaluations from others [38]. The gender difference may be due to differences in social and biological factors between them or the proportions of different genders were unbalanced in this study, with more females than males, which remains a further exploration.

Body dissatisfaction exhibited a limited mediating role in the relationship between school bullying and the risk of eating disorders, with this effect being exclusive to the bully-victims. This may also be related to the fact that bully-victims group were at risk individuals in the bullying role. Specifically, students who experience bullying victimization may feel inferior and upset because of negative experiences such as being teased, ostracized, and discriminated against because of their body shape, which may lead them to eating disorders. Conversely, these individuals might also harbor dissatisfaction with their own body shape, prompting them to mock the body shapes of others, which could further escalate their risk of developing eating disorders.

Therefore, preventing school bullying and developing healthy body image concepts may be important ways to prevent eating disorders. Schools can promote students’ physical and mental health by providing anti-bullying education and psychological counseling, body image education, and nutritional counseling. At the same time, parents should also pay attention to their children’s body image, give them with warmth and support, and help them establish correct values and attitudes towards life.

Implications

Given the complex relationship between school bullying and the risk of eating disorders, compounded by factors such as gender and body dissatisfaction, a multifaceted approach is necessary to effectively prevent the occurrence of eating disorders and bullying behaviors. The following are some recommendations we propose to address this issue. First, schools should conduct regular anti-bullying education campaigns to educate students on how to recognize and respond to bullying. At the same time, a zero-tolerance bullying policy should be implemented to strengthen supervision and punitive measures to reduce the occurrence of bullying. Second, schools should carry out body image and eating disorders publicity and education to promote youth awareness about the importance of healthy eating and body image and help students develop a healthy mental and physical outlook in order to reduce the prevalence of eating disorders. Third, schools should provide counseling services to help students develop positive coping mechanisms and control their behavior, thus reducing the risk of problems such as bullying and eating disorders. Fourth, parents should pay attention to their children’s physical and mental health and teach them how to respect others, protect themselves, and eat sensibly. When they find that their children may have problems such as eating disorders or bullying, they should help them seek professional support and help as early as possible. Fifth, the government should strengthen the regulation of negative body image, eating disorders and bullying information in the media, and social networks to prevent children from being exposed to undesirable content. Develop anti-bullying and eating disorders policies and increase publicity to educate the public about the dangers of eating disorders and bullying. In conclusion, preventing bullying and eating disorders requires a collective effort. This involves educational, psychological, social, and family support and interventions to cultivate a healthy, safe school environment. Only through the joint efforts of the whole community can every child grow up in a healthy and safe environment.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several key strengths. It is the first to explore the relationship between various forms of bullying and the risk of eating disorders among Chinese college students. Additionally, it provides a comparative analysis of eating disorder prevalence across different roles involved in bullying scenarios and examines the impact of gender on these associations.

However, this study also has several limitations. Firstly, the research was conducted on students from only one university in Shantou, and cannot represent the whole country. Secondly, the reliance on self-reporting methods could introduce subjective biases into the data. Future studies should integrate a combination of self-reports with corroborated case files and reports from other sources, such as parents and teachers, to ensure a more objective and comprehensive analysis. Thirdly, Cross-sectional studies do not reveal cause and effect. Future prospective studies should be conducted to explore causality. Fourthly, This study solely measures the risk of developing eating disorders, rather than providing a clinical diagnosis of eating disorders. Future research could explore this area with a small sample size of clinically diagnosed cases. Fifthly, the study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality between bullying and the risk of eating disorders. Future research employing longitudinal or prospective study designs would help to elucidate the temporal and causal relationships. Fifthly, in this study, participants were compensated for their time with a modest cash reward. It is important to note that the use of monetary incentives may introduce certain biases into the data, as participants may feel compelled to respond in a particular way to justify their compensation. Finally, given that muscle dysmorphic disorder (MDD) may significantly influence the link between bullying and eating disorders, this variable should be closely monitored in studies, with particular attention to the moderating role of gender [51]. As a form of body image disorder, MDD has been shown to have a close association with both bullying behaviors and eating disorders [52]. Therefore, future research is needed to delve into how these complex factors interweave and interact, collectively driving the progression of the entire mediation process.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there is an association between school bullying experiences and the risk of eating disorders among college students in Shantou. Gender and body dissatisfaction also play important roles in the relationship between school bullying and the risk of eating disorders. Future research should explore the causal relationship between school bullying and eating disorders and adopt effective prevention and intervention strategies to reduce the occurrence of both problems in order to promote the physical and mental health of adolescents.
