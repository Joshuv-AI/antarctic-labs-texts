Emergence of capillary waves in miscible co-flowing fluids
Alessandro Carbonaro, Giovanni Savorana, Luca Cipelletti, Rama Govindarajan, Domenico Truzzolillo

Abstract
We show that capillary waves can exist at the the boundary between miscible co-flowing fluids. We unveil that the interplay between transient interfacial stresses and confinement drives the progressive transition from the well-known inertial regime, characterized by a frequency independent wavenumber, k ∼ ω 0 k\sim\omega^{0} , to a capillary wave scaling, k ∼ ω 2 / 3 k\sim\omega^{2/3} , unexpected for miscible fluids . This allows us to measure the effective interfacial tension between miscible fluids and its rapid decay on time scales never probed so far, which we rationalize with a model going beyond square-gradient theories. Our work potentially opens a new avenue to measure transient interfacial tensions at the millisecond scale in a controlled manner.

Capillary waves are small disturbances propagating at the interface between two fluids,
with interfacial tension acting as the only relevant restoring force

[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
They are very common in Nature
[
1
]
but can also be easily produced artificially
[
4
,
5
]
.
Notably, capillary waves have proved to be a valuable tool to measure interfacial or surface tension between immiscible fluids
[
6
]
, since their dispersion relation links the tension
\Gamma
to the wavenumber
k
and frequency
\omega
of the waves.
Recently, Hu and Cubaud
[
7
]
have shown that propagating modes can be excited in viscosity-stratified flows confined in microfluidic channels, and that some of the waves observed for coflowing immiscible liquids follow the capillary wave dispersion predicted for inviscid fluids,
k\sim\omega^{2/3}

[
1
]
.
Hence, even in confined flows, capillary waves can emerge when interfacial tension dominates over other stabilizing mechanisms such as viscous dissipation.
In the flow of
miscible
fluids, by contrast, interfacial stresses are typically negligible compared to viscous dissipation, such that their very existence is debated by an increasing number of research groups
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. An “inertial” regime characterizes the emerging instability, with
k\sim\omega^{0}

[
7
]
: the variation of
\omega
is solely dictated by a change in phase velocity, while the wavelength stays practically unaltered when the flow rate of either fluid is varied. This regime has also been observed for high Reynolds numbers in immiscible fluids, where it delimits the short wavelength limit
[
7
]
of the stability diagram, suggesting a maximum wavenumber dictated only by the rate of energy dissipation of the waves. Hence, dissipation, confinement, and interfacial tension are key to
understanding flow instabilities
and the transition from the inertial to the capillary regime.

To date, the presence of capillary waves in miscible fluids has only been inferred via light scattering in static conditions and for nearly critical fluids
[
16
]
, whose miscibility can be easily tuned by changing temperature, which however represent a limited subset of fluid pairs to study.

The direct observation of
capillary waves in fluids,
miscible in all proportions at any temperature below their boiling point,
has not been reported so far, most likely due to the rapidly decaying tension at their boundaries
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
9
]
.
Microfluidic devices provide an ideal playground to test whether interfacial stresses can transiently impact wave propagation, since the behavior of the interface between two fluids can be explored on time scales as short as a few tens of milliseconds since the fluids are brought in contact.
In this letter, we investigate the propagation of shear flow instabilities appearing when
two miscible fluids
, water and glycerol, flow side-by-side in rectangular microchannels. We unveil the existence of capillary waves,
with
two distinct branches of wave dispersion,
and we measure the effective interfacial tension
at the liquid-liquid boundary over time scales never probed so far,
opening a new avenue to measure transient interfacial tensions in a controlled manner at very short times
.

To summarize, we have shown that capillary waves, hitherto considered to be a distinctive phenomenon of interfaces between immiscible fluids, emerge also at diffuse boundaries between fully miscible liquids. Their wave propagation is dominated by a transient interfacial tension for high flow rates and large concentration gradients
, while dissipation and confinement dominate in the inertial regime, where one single wavenumber emerges
and capillary waves are suppressed
.
The transition from capillary waves to the inertial regime is generally smooth and occurs when the rate of the fastest fluid is decreased so that diffusion smears out interfaces, interfacial stresses become vanishingly small, and waves get concomitantly more confined.
We found very good agreement between the measured values of the effective tension and a model
[
9
]
that goes beyond the well-known second order gradient expansion of pressures and mixing free energy in systems far from equilibrium
[
14
,
26
]
. Our results indicate that interfacial tension in miscible molecular fluids
decays
much faster right after contact than the
t^{-1/2}
scaling predicted by square gradient models, as hinted at
by other results
based on the detection of capillary waves in miscible near-critical fluids
in static conditions
[
16
]
, while the square gradient approximation holds at later stages
[
23
]
.

The capillary wave patterns reported here for
microfluidic flows of miscible liquids are a novel and unexpected phenomenon, that we propose as a
measurement strategy for accessing transient interfacial tension down to the millisecond time scale.

Our work paves the way for a thorough understanding of capillary phenomena and flow stability in miscible fluids, which play a key role in a wide range
of research fields of both academic and practical interest,
from non-equilibrium thermodynamics
[
27
]
and geosciences
[
28
]
to oil recovery
[
29
]
, filtration and flow in porous media (e.g., in a chromatography column
[

), fluid removal
[
31
]
, and self-propulsion of droplets and colloids of biological interest
[
32
,
33
]
.
