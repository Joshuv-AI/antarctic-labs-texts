The Pattern Complexity of the 2-Dimensional Paperfolding Sequence


Abstract
We present an exact formula for the number of distinct crease patterns in a square shaped region of a given size that appear in the 2 dimensional paperfolding structure.

1
Introduction

The classical paperfolding sequence is a well known sequence studied in many areas, such as combinatorics, computer science, and crystallography. This sequence is obtain by repeated folding of a long strip of paper in the middle, then unfolding it and reading of the different types of creases seen. Reading the creases from the left yields the paperfolding sequences, see
A014577
in
[
16
]
. There are many versions and results around the paperfolding sequence, see
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
8
,
13
]
, to mention some references.

Ben-Abraham et al.
[
6
]
give a generalisation of the paperfolding sequence to higher dimensions via a recursive construction. Roughly descried, the 2-dimensional paperfolding sequence is obtained when a square shaped paper is folded in the middle, alternately between the axis parallel directions, and then reading of the crease structure seen when the paper is completely unfolded. In section
2
we give a detailed description of these folds, and state in detail the notions we use.

In this paper we focus on the complexity of the paperfoding structure in 2 dimensions. That is, we look at the number of crease patterns of a given size that occur in the paperfold structure. The complexity in the 1-dimensional case is given by Allouche in
[
1
]
. The main result of this paper is the following theorem, which was conjectured in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A_{n}
be the number of unique patterns of size
n\times n
that occur in the 2-dimensional paperfolding sequence of infinte order. Then
A_{1}=4
,
A_{2}=68
, and

A_{n}=12\cdot n^{2}+24\cdot n\cdot 2^{\alpha}-16\cdot 4^{\alpha}-4,

(1)

for
n\geq 3
, where
\alpha=\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\rfloor
.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In the next section we start by giving necessary definitions, and discuss how to generate the 2-dimensional paperfolding structure via substitution rules. Thereafter, in section
3
, we turn to the question of defining what we mean by a
pattern
, and look at the structure of sets of patterns. In the section thereafter we deduce a system of recursions describing the size of sets patterns. A solution to this system of recursion is presented in section
6
, which then leads to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The classical 1-dimensional paperfolding sequence is obtained, as briefly mention in the introduction, by repeatedly folding a long paper strip in the middle. 3
Sets of Patterns

In this section we turn our attention to defining what we mean by a pattern, and look at sets of patterns and the structure of such sets.

Recall the definition of the
T_{n}
, and
S_{n}
from (
4
) and (
5
). We can see these structures (the
T_{n}
s and
S_{n}
s) as matrices on their respective alphabet. According to the language used in the field of tilings, we say that submatrices of the
T_{n}
s and
S_{n}
s are called
patterns
or
subpatterns
. That is, a pattern is rectangular and without holes. (In the literature the term
patch
(see
[
5
,
11
]
) is also commonly used for this.) Clearly, any
T_{n}
or
S_{n}
is also a pattern. For completeness, we also say that
T
and
S
are also patterns (infinite ones). We also adopt the notations used for matrices, with rows and columns. This means we can describe a finite pattern
X
via its entries, that is,
X_{r,c}
is the entry in
X
at row
r
and column
c
.

For a pattern
X
(finite or infinite), we let
P(X,m\times n)
, where
m
and
n
are positive integers, be the set of all
m\times n
patterns that occur somewhere in
X
. In the case of
X
being a finite pattern, we use the notation
X[r,c,m\times n]
to denote the
m\times n
subpattern of
X
that has its upper left corner at row
r
and column
c
in
X
. The notation
|\cdot|
denotes the cardinality of a set. Note here that the quantity

A_{n}:=|P(S,n\times n)|

(6)

is what we mean by the number of patterns of size
n\times n
in Theorem
1.1
. With the help of the just introduced notations, we can now state a first result on the sets of patterns, implied by the block recursion given in (
4
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 0
. Then
T_{n}\in P(T_{n+1},2^{n}\times 2^{n})
.
\square

The Lemma
3.1
implies that the chain of nested sets of subpatterns,

P(T_{0},m\times m)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq P(T_{n},m\times m)\subseteq P(T_{n+1},m\times m)\subseteq\cdots,

is monotonic including in
n
, (if
m\leq 2^{n}
). Next, we show that this chain is strictly monotonic including until all possible subpatterns are contained.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
m\geq 1
. If there is an
n\geq 0
such that

P(T_{n},m\times m)=P(T_{n+1},m\times m),

(7)

with
m\leq 2^{n}
, then

P(T_{n},m\times m)=P(T_{n+k},m\times m)

(8)

for all integers
k\geq 1
, and in particular
P(T_{n},m\times m)=P(T,m\times m)
.

Proof.

We give a proof by induction on
k
in (
8
). The basis case,
k=1
, is direct from the assumption (
7
). Assume for induction that (
8
) holds for
1\leq k\leq p
.

For the induction step,
k=p+1
, consider a pattern
a\in P(T_{n+p+1},m\times m)
. Then there is a pattern
b\in P(T_{n+p},m\times m)
such that
a
is a subpattern of
\mu(b)
. By the induction assumption we have that
b\in P(T_{n+p-1},m\times m)
. This implies

a\in P(\mu(b),m\times m)\subseteq P(T_{n+p},m\times m).

Therefore
P(T_{n+p},m\times m)\supseteq P(T_{n+p+1},m\times m)
, and by Lemma
3.1
it follows that

P(T_{n+p},m\times m)=P(T_{n+p+1},m\times m),

which completes the induction.
∎

Example 3.3
.

By an enumeration we find

P(T_{5},2\times 2)=P(T_{6},2\times 2),

Let

The only non-clear part is the disjointedness. From the definition of

Lemma 4.2

Let

\begin{array}[]{rr@{\ }l}\mathit{1}.&a_{1,2}(2n)&=b_{1,2}(2n)=b_{2,2}(2n-1),\\

\mathit{4}.&a_{1,1}(2n+1)&=b_{1,1}(2n+1)=b_{2,1}(2n),\\
\mathit{5}.&a_{2,1}(2n+1)&=a_{1,1}(2n+2)=b_{2,1}(2n+1)=c_{1,1}(2n+1),\\
\mathit{6}.&a_{2,2}(2n+1)&=c_{1,2}(2n+1)=c_{2,2}(2n).\end{array}

a_{1,2}(2n)=b_{1,2}(2n)

u,v\in P_{1,2}(T,n\times(n+1))
such that
u[1,1,n\times n]=v[1,1,n\times n]
. Then we can find
s,t\in\mathbb{N}
such that

u[1+2s,2+2t,3\times 4]\neq v[1+2s,1+2t,3\times 4]

but with

u[1+2s,1+2t,3\times 3]=v[1+2s,1+2t,3\times 3].

That is, we have a contradiction to what we saw in Example
4.1
. The remaining equalities follow in the same way. See Figures

through Figure

∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then

\begin{array}[b]{rr@{\ }l}\mathit{1}.&a_{1,2}(4n)&=b_{1,2}(4n),\\
\mathit{2}.&a_{2,1}(4n)&=c_{1,1}(4n),\\
\mathit{3}.&a_{2,2}(4n)&=a_{1,2}(4n+1)=c_{1,2}(4n)=b_{2,2}(4n),\\
\mathit{4}.&a_{1,1}(4n+1)&=b_{1,1}(4n+1)=b_{2,1}(4n),\\
\mathit{5}.&a_{2,1}(4n+1)&=a_{1,1}(4n+2)=c_{1,1}(4n+1)=b_{2,1}(4n+1),\\
\mathit{6}.&a_{2,2}(4n+1)&=c_{1,2}(4n+1)=c_{2,2}(4n),\\
\mathit{7}.&a_{1,2}(4n+2)&=b_{1,2}(4n+2)=b_{2,2}(4n+1),\\
\mathit{8}.&a_{2,1}(4n+2)&=c_{1,1}(4n+2)=c_{2,1}(4n+1),\\
\mathit{9}.&a_{2,2}(4n+2)&=a_{1,2}(4n+3)=c_{1,2}(4n+2)=b_{2,2}(4n+2),\\
\mathit{10}.&a_{1,1}(4n+3)&=b_{1,1}(4n+3)=b_{2,1}(4n+2),\\
\mathit{11}.&a_{2,1}(4n+3)&=c_{1,1}(4n+3)=b_{2,1}(4n+3),\\
\mathit{12}.&a_{2,2}(4n+3)&=c_{1,2}(4n+3)=c_{2,2}(4n+2).\end{array}\hfill\square

4.3
, is that we can reduce the number of cases of the

Proof of Main Theorem

This section is devoted to give a simplified recursion for the number of patterns of given size, based on the system in (
39
) (
40
), and (
41
), and then use this simplification to prove Theorem

.

We start by expanding the list of equalities given in Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ }l}a_{1,2}(2n)&=a_{2,1}(2n)=a_{2,2}(2n),\\
a_{1,1}(2n+1)&=b_{1,2}(2n+1).\end{array}

(42)

Proof.

The initial cases,
n=1,2,3,4
are directly seen from Table
1
. To prove that the equalities in (
42
) hold, we consider two cases depending on the parity of
n
, that is,

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ }c@{\ }l@{\ }c@{\ }l}a_{1,2}(4k)&=&a_{2,1}(4k)&=&a_{2,2}(4k),\\
a_{1,2}(4k+2)&=&a_{2,1}(4k+2)&=&a_{2,2}(4k+2),\\
a_{1,1}(4k+1)&=&b_{1,2}(4k+1),\\
a_{1,1}(4k+3)&=&b_{1,2}(4k+3),\\
\end{array}

(43)

for
k\geq 1
. Here we give a proof by induction on
k
. The basis cases are, as just mentioned, clear. Assume for induction that the equalities in (
43
) hold for
1\leq k<p
. Then, for the induction step, Lemma
4.2
, the recursion from (
39
) (
40
), and (
41
), and the induction assumption imply

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,2}(4p)-a_{2,1}(4p)=2a_{1,2}(2p)-2a_{2,1}(2p)=0,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,2}(4p)-a_{2,2}(4p)=4a_{1,2}(2p)-4a_{1,2}(2p)=0,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,1}(4p+1)-b_{1,2}(4p+1)=2a_{1,1}(2p+1)-2b_{1,2}(2p+1)=0,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,2}(4p+2)-a_{2,1}(4p+2)=&\phantom{+}\ b_{1,2}(2p+1)+a_{1,2}(2p+2)\\
&-a_{1,1}(2p+1)-a_{2,1}(2p+2)=0,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,2}(4p+2)-a_{2,2}(4p+2)=a_{1,1}(2p)-a_{1,2}(2p)=0,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,1}(4p+3)-b_{1,2}(4p+3)=a_{1,1}(2p+1)-b_{1,2}(2p+1)=0,\end{aligned}

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then

\begin{split}a_{1,1}(2n)+4&=a_{1,2}(2n),\\
a_{2,1}(2n+1)+4&=b_{2,2}(2n+1).\end{split}

(44)

Proof.

We split the equalities in (
44
) into the cases depending on the parity of
n
. That is, we look at

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ }c@{\ }l@{\ }}a_{1,1}(4k)+4&=&a_{1,2}(4k),\\
a_{1,1}(4k+2)+4&=&a_{1,2}(4k+2),\\
a_{2,1}(4k+1)+4&=&b_{2,2}(4k+1),\\
a_{2,1}(4k+3)+4&=&b_{2,2}(4k+3),\\
\end{array}

(45)

for
k\geq 1
. The case
n=1
and
k=1,2
are direct from Table
1
.
We prove the equalities in (
45
) by induction on
k
. The basis cases are, as just mentioned clear. Assume for induction that the equalities in (
45
) hold for
1\leq k<p
. Then, for the induction step, Lemma
4.2
, Lemma
6.1
, and the induction assumption, give

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,2}(4p)-a_{1,1}(4p)&=a_{1,2}(2n)+a_{2,2}(2n)-a_{1,1}(2n)-a_{2,1}(2n)\\
&=a_{1,2}(2p)-a_{1,1}(2p)\\
&=4,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} a_{1,2}(4p+2)-a_{1,1}(4p+2)&=b_{1,2}(2p+1)+b_{2,2}(2p+1)\\
&\phantom{=}-a_{1,1}(2p+1)-a_{2,1}(2p+1)\\
&=b_{2,2}(2p+1)-a_{2,1}(2p+1)\\
&=4,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} b_{2,2}(4p+1)-a_{2,1}(4p+1)&=a_{1,2}(4p+2)-a_{1,1}(4p+2)=4,\end{aligned}

•

\begin{aligned} b_{2,2}(4p+3)-a_{2,1}(4p+3)&=a_{1,2}(2p+2)+a_{2,2}(2p+2)\\
&\phantom{=}-a_{2,1}(2p+1)-a_{2,1}(2p+2)\\
&=a_{1,2}(2p+2)-a_{2,1}(2p+1)\\
&=b_{2,2}(2p+1)-a_{2,1}(2p+1)\\
&=4,\end{aligned}

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.3

\displaystyle A_{4p+1}-2A_{2p}-2A_{2p+1}

\displaystyle=a_{1,1}(4p+2)-2a_{1,1}(2p)-2a_{1,1}(2p+2)

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For the even index case of (

1

\displaystyle A_{2n}

\displaystyle=12(2n)^{2}+24\cdot(2n)\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log_{2}(2n-1)\rfloor}-16\cdot 4^{\lfloor\log_{2}(2n-1)\rfloor}-4

\displaystyle=4\cdot 12\cdot n^{2}+2\cdot 24n\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log_{2}2(n-\frac{1}{2})\rfloor}-16\cdot 4^{\lfloor\log_{2}2(n-\frac{1}{2})\rfloor}-4

\displaystyle=4\cdot 12\cdot n^{2}+4\cdot 24n\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n-\frac{1}{2})\rfloor}-4\cdot 16\cdot 4^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n-\frac{1}{2})\rfloor}-4\cdot 4+12

\displaystyle=4\cdot A_{n}+12,

since

\displaystyle=48n\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor}-48n\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\rfloor}

\displaystyle\quad-32\cdot 4^{\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor}+32\cdot 4^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\rfloor}.

(49)

\displaystyle A_{2^{k+1}+1}

\displaystyle=48\cdot 2^{2k-1}\cdot(2-1)-32\cdot 4^{k-1}\cdot(4-1)

\displaystyle=24\cdot 4^{k}-3\cdot 8\cdot 4^{k}

7
Outlook

Our technique presented in this paper is fairly general and can be applied to find the pattern complexity of other tilings that are obtained via a substitution rule, such as the Table (or Domino) tiling (see
[
11
]
), the Squiral tiling
[
11
]
, and the structure generated by the Ulam-Warburton cellular automaton
[
4
,
15
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
, just to mention few examples. However, things are more subtle; it is easy to find substitution rules where the method of this paper does not seem to work, i.e the Chair tiling, see
[
11
]
. A different technique is applied by Galanov
[
10
]
who looks at the pattern complexity of the Robinson tiling
[
14
]
.

Our work here depends heavily on the extension properties, as discussed in section
4
. One question is to find necessary and sufficient conditions on the substitution rule in order to be able to calculate the pattern complexity in our way, and to classify different types of complexity functions that might appear.
