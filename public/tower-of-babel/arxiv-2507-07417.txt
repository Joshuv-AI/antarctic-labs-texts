May I have your Attention? Breaking Fine-Tuning based Prompt Injection Defenses using Architecture-Aware Attacks
Nishit V. Pandya, Andrey Labunets, Sicun Gao, Earlence Fernandes

Abstract
A popular class of defenses against prompt injection attacks on large language models (LLMs) relies on fine-tuning the model to separate instructions and data, so that the LLM does not follow instructions that might be present with data. There are several academic systems and production-level implementations of this idea. We evaluate the robustness of this class of prompt injection defenses in the whitebox setting by constructing strong optimization-based attacks and showing that the defenses do not provide the claimed security properties. Specifically, we construct a novel attention-based attack algorithm for text-based LLMs and apply it to two recent whitebox defenses SecAlign (CCS 2025) and StruQ (USENIX Security 2025), showing attacks with success rates of up to 70% with modest increase in attacker budget in terms of tokens. Our findings make fundamental progress towards understanding the robustness of prompt injection defenses in the whitebox setting. We release our code and attacks here .

Our algorithm finds adversarial input tokens that cause the LLM to focus its attention (measured by internal attention matrices) on the attacker-supplied instructions (i.e., the injected prompt). Intuitively, if the LLM’s context window only had the attacker’s instructions and the attacker’s instructions weren’t inherently malicious, then the LLM would follow them because there are no other instructions. Our attack generates tokens that tries to make the LLM ignore all other tokens in the context window, except the attacker-supplied ones. By contrast, the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) algorithm finds inputs that cause the LLM to generate attacker-desired output tokens, without taking the LLM’s architectural structure into consideration. That is, the attacker’s loss function is expressed in terms of output token probabilities. In practice, this represents a very difficult optimization objective, as evidenced by GCG’s general inability to convincingly break defenses like SecAlign. On the other hand,
ASTRA
gains its power from architectural awareness — by expressing the attacker’s loss function in terms of attention, it is able to compute better attacks than GCG alone.

Expressing a prompt injection loss function in terms of attention matrices requires addressing the challenge of determining which attention heads (out of thousands in modern LLMs) are most important for the specific task that is given to the model. Attention heads are not independent, but rather, a change in an earlier head results in downstream changes. In general, solving the problem requires extensive manual intervention and/or multiple expensive ablations that involve turning-off attention heads or patching activations and empirically observing their effects on the task in question
[
4
,
29
,
46
]
. Our key insight is that, in a prompt injection, the attacker wants specific output tokens to appear, and therefore, we do not need to explicitly find out which attention heads are critical for different types of tasks. Rather, since our goal is to find out-of-distribution inputs, we need to determine the attention heads for which small changes in the attention matrices cause large changes in the probability of the desired output tokens. We quantify this using a novel sensitivity metric that can be computed using gradient information, thus sidestepping the requirement for expensive ablations or manual interventions.

We evaluate
ASTRA
on SecAlign and StruQ, two recently-proposed techniques in the class of fine-tuning prompt injection defenses. We use the same evaluation methodology as outlined in these papers and demonstrate an attack success rate of
75\%
and
57\%
for the SecAlign defended versions of Mistral and Llama-3 respectively (obtained from the authors of these two systems). Our attacks require a modest increase in token budget — i.e., the original StruQ and SecAlign papers evaluate the setting where the attacker is given
20

25
tokens that can be prefixed or suffixed (or both) to the injected instruction.

Crucially, unlike the computer vision space where L-norms represented attacker threat models (because they captured image distortion) and had natural limits, the LLM space has no such inherent restriction. Context windows of modern LLMs are very large (often up to tens of thousands of tokens) and the trend is to make them even larger. Furthermore, when an LLM is used as part of a larger agentic setting, the attacker’s instructions can arrive from any source (e.g., an email or webpage), and thus, a larger attack string is likely to go un-noticed. Thus, on a fundamental level, the attacker is not restricted by the number of tokens they can inject.

We perform a scaling study on attacker token budgets, ranging from
20

35

II
Background

II-A

Transformer-based LLMs

A generative LLM can be modelled as a function
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}:V^{*}\to\mathbf{P}(V)
parameterized by some set of parameters
\Theta
that takes as input a sequence of tokens
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})\in V^{*}
, each coming from the fixed vocabulary
V
of size
|V|
, and outputs a probability distribution over the next token, i.e.
P(\cdot|x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n};\Theta)\in\mathbf{P}(V)
.

After computing
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
on the input
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
, the next token
x_{n+1}
is generated by sampling from the output distribution
P(\cdot|x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n};\Theta)
according to some sampling procedure. The LLM then autoregressively generates the token
x_{n+2}
by computing the distribution
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n},x_{n+1})
and sampling from it. This process typically continues until some stopping criterion is reached and the output tokens are decoded to get the final output string.

We use the notation
LLM_{\Theta}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
to refer to the actual text generated by the LLM on the input sequence of tokens
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
.

II-B

Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks

Indirect Prompt Injections are a class of inference-time attacks against LLMs that aim to subvert the expected output of an LLM in order to cause the LLM to output text of the attacker’s choice. That is, prompt injection attacks aim to override the developer or user intent and try to instead cause the LLM to generate some specific, malicious strings. Such attacks occur when an adversary controls a part of the input to an LLM. In real world applications, an attacker can achieve such control by inserting malicious strings into third-party content that is fetched and placed into the context window of an existing conversation.

Formally, a prompt injection attack happens when a malicious string crafted by the adversary (say
x_{Adv}
) is combined with the trusted instruction of the user (say
x_{Trusted}
) and is processed by the LLM. In practice, strings from untrusted contexts often undergo special preprocessing, such as filtering, before they are combined with the trusted strings. They can also be delimited by other special tokens to mark separation between the trusted and untrusted contexts. The goal of the adversary is then to craft an
x_{Adv}
which when combined with
x_{Trusted}
causes the LLM to output a string
y_{Target}
of the attacker’s choice. That is, the goal of the adversary is to craft an
x_{Adv}
such that

LLM_{\Theta}(\mathrm{Combine}(x_{Trusted},x_{Adv}))\approx y_{Target}

(1)

Prompt injection attacks are broadly of two classes - handcrafted and automated. Handcrafted prompt injections try to exploit linguistic quirks, role-playing and other similar tricks in order to fool LLMs into following the attacker’s instructions. Automated prompt injections rely on an optimization algorithm to generate adversarial inputs designed to mislead an LLM into generating attacker-chosen targets. Most optimization-based algorithms work by surrounding the attacker’s malicious instruction (which we call the payload) with tokens that are then optimized to lead to the desired output.

II-C

Defences against Indirect Prompt Injection

The primary goal of a defense is to make a model resist indirect prompt injection attacks.
This class of defense is separate from anti-jailbreak safety tuning approaches, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) which train to resist jailbreak attacks violating output content safety policy
[
2
]
.

One class of these defenses, detection-based
[
25
]
, work by predicting if an input contains a prompt injection. Examples of such defenses include Prompt Guard
[
28
]
, a known-answer detectior and its variants, like DataSentinel
[
24
]
. A model may then reject a query if a prompt injection is detected.

Another class, prevention-based, addresses the problem by making the target model more likely to correctly follow an original prompt instead of an injected instruction.
This can be achieved, for example, by paraphrasing fragile attack triggers
[
18
]
, by improving the model’s ability to recognize prompt structure, such as In-Context Defense
[
42
]
, or Spotlighting
[
17
]
, or by designing a system to make an attack unlikely to happen, such as with Quarantined LLMs
[
10
]
.

Notably, a special class of prevention-based defenses relies on fine-tuning the model itself to recognize the prompt structure and to separate instructions from data, which we denote as
fine-tuning based
prompt injection defenses.
Examples of such defenses include the open-source systems StruQ
[
8
]
and SecAlign
[
7
]
and closed-source implementations such as Instruction Hierarchy
[
40
]

Those defenses attempt to instill the correct instruction-following behavior directly into the model by appropriately training the model with the correct, expected behaviour. We describe the StruQ and SecAlign defenses in more detail in sections

III
Threat Model

Like prior work on indirect prompt injections, we assume that a (victim) user is using an LLM (target model) to interact with third-party content.

An attacker is an untrusted third-party who adversarially controls a part of the input to a target model and intends to steer the model away from following original, trusted instructions given by the user to a new set of instructions of attacker’s choice.
The delivery of adversarial instructions into a user’s context window can be carried out via several different methods such as poisoning a webpage to be summarized (as instructed by the user’s prompt to an AI assistant or as an operation of some agentic AI system), by poisoned documents, multimodal content, or code being processed
[
43
,
12
,
13
,
39
,
3
]
.
For the purposes of this paper, we treat the real-world delivery mechanism as an orthogonal problem to our work. Nonetheless, we note that the variety of delivery mechanisms and the increasing capabilities of LLMs have important consequences for the broader, general threat model of a prompt injection attack. That is, with the trend of increasing context windows in LLMs and improving capabilities such as multiple language understanding, multi-modality, and tool calling abilities, the attacker is not, in general, restricted by constraints in number of tokens or the size of the attack itself.

We assume a whitebox, adaptive adversary, whose goal is to create prompt injection attacks with high likelihood of success. That is, we assume a worst-case adversary who has complete access to the weights of the target model and has all the details of the surrounding components that are orchestrating the LLM-user interaction. Examples of such components can be filtering or sanitization components that can filter text out of third-party content. In addition, we also assume that the adversary has knowledge of the full context window. The adversary is allowed to craft a subset of the full context window, representing the malicious third-party data.
While previous works have studied prompt injections under weaker assumptions such as partial knowledge or via universality, we use the full prompt knowledge assumption to craft our attacks
[
36
]
,
[
37
]

This assumption, while often unrealistic, represents the strongest possible attack an adversary might be able to make against the user. This assumption has also been used by prior prompt injection attacks

IV
SecAlign and StruQ

SecAlign
[
7
]
and StruQ
[
8
]
are both examples of a class of defences which rely on fine-tuning the model itself to defend against prompt injection attacks. They aim to train the model directly to enforce the separation between instructions and data. That is, both of them aim to train an instruct tuned model directly to ignore the instructions present in data.

IV-A

StruQ

StruQ starts with a non-instruct-tuned base model and uses a version of adversarial training adapted for instruction tuning to obtain the final defended model.

Concretely, StruQ reserves special tokens which it uses to delimit data from instructions. The aim of the defense is to train the model to recognize these delimiters and ignore the instructions present in the part of the input delimited as data by these special tokens.

The fine-tuning itself is performed on a training dataset that contains of clean examples - demonstrating how the model should follow legitimate instructions - and dirty examples - demonstrating how the model should refuse to follow instructions coming from data delimited by the special delimiter tokens.

When deployed, the fine-tuned model is combined with a preprocessing filter that sanitizes input text to filter out the special tokens. This ensures that the model only ever sees the correctly delimited text as input and can’t be fooled by false delimiters inserted in the injected data.

The StruQ models show significant resistance to hand-crafted prompt injection techniques with success rate of known hand-crafted techniques close to
0\%
. Nonetheless, the StruQ models are, in fact, vulnerable to adversarial optimization methods like GCG, with the attackers being successful in approximately
60\%
of the cases when using GCG.

IV-B

SecAlign

SecAlign adopts a prima-facie similar approach to StruQ. It uses special delimiters which mark out the trust boundary between instructions and data. Like StruQ, it uses a preprocessing filter that removes special tokens from the untrusted data ingested by the model.

SecAlign differs significantly from StruQ in its fine-tuning procedure however. Instead of directly performing adversarial training, SecAlign phrases the task of defending against prompt injections in terms of preference optimization. That is, it aims to train the model to prefer to follow instructions coming from trusted sources over instructions coming from untrusted sources.

Implementationally, SecAlign creates a preference dataset from existing instruction-tuning datasets and performs preference optimization on an instruction tuned model using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

Like StruQ, SecAlign also reduces the attack success rate of hand-crafted attack techniques to close to
0\%
. More significantly however, SecAlign reduces the attack success rate of strong optimization-based attacks to
\sim 0\%
as well. SecAlign is robust against almost
100\%
of the prompt injections generated using GCG;

Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG)
[
50
]
is a state-of-the-art algorithm in white-box input optimization for an LLM. The algorithm was originally proposed in the context of jailbreaking. However, later work has modified and adapted the algorithm for generating prompt injection attacks under a variety of threat models and use cases
[
11

23

[
11

23
]
, here we focus on GCG as an optimization algorithm for the simplest objective function - that is, as an optimization algorithm to optimize a part of the input prompt (keeping the rest of the input prompt fixed) in order to maximize the probability of the LLM generating the attacker-chosen target string. We use this setting since this is the same setting as used in the evaluations for SecAlign and StruQ.

Furthermore, several recent works have also proposed several algorithmic enhancements to GCG
[
47
,
48
,
22
]
. These works either apply transferability or aid the search procedure performed by GCG via better exploiting the breadth/depth tradeoff during optimization or exploiting increased randomness at each step. In other words, these works don’t fundamentally change the nature of the information used by the optimization procedure. All these works still fundamentally rely on the gradients of the loss function as a signal to guide the optimization procedure. In this section, we aim to establish that the gradients for the loss function used by usual GCG don’t provide significant value to the optimization procedure.

Concretely, given an input prompt, represented as a sequence of tokens
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
, let
I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots n\}
represent the indices of the tokens which can be modified by the optimization algorithm (with the rest of the tokens corresponding to indices
\{1,2,\dots n\}\setminus I
kept fixed). Also, let
\mathbf{y}_{T}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{m})
represent an attacker-chosen target sequence of tokens. The attacker in this case wants the LLM to output the target tokens with high probability.
In practice, the optimizable tokens are appended to the attacker’s malicious payload.

The optimization problem to be solved in this simple case is -

\mathbf{x}_{I}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{x}_{I}\in V^{|I|}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\ \Pi_{j=1}^{m}P(y_{j}|x_{1},\dots x_{n},y_{1},\dots y_{j-1};\Theta)

(2)

That is, the GCG function tries to minimize the following loss function (using the notation
\|
to denote concatenation of sequences of tokens)

\mathrm{TargetLogprobs}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{T})=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{log}\ P(y_{j}\mid\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1};\Theta)

(3)

The above optimization problem is a hard discrete optimization problem. For an LLM with a vocabulary of size
|V|
and a perturbable subset of size
|I|
, the total search space consists of
|V|^{|I|}
discrete points. For a typical LLM,
|V|\approx 100,000
and for an attacker budget of
25
tokens, the size of the search space is
|V|^{|I|}=100,000^{25}\approx 2^{450}
sequences of tokens.

GCG addresses this with an iterative, guided, greedy search algorithm. That is, it starts with some initial sequence of tokens
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\in V^{|I|}
and iteratively obtains a sequence of points
\{\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots\}\subseteq V^{|I|}
until some stopping criterion is reached. At the
k+1
-th iteration, GCG performs the following two steps -

1.

(Guiding step)
- A coarse filtering step which uses the gradients of the objective function at
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
to generate a set of promising candidates
C^{(k)}\subseteq V^{n}
. The set of promising candidates
C^{(k)}
consists of candidate sequences of tokens each of which differs from the current point
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
at exactly one token at one of the
|I|
perturbable positions. That is, the candidates generated are all one token substitution away from the current point.

2.

(Greedy step)
A fine selection step which evaluates the objective function on the set of promising candidates
C^{(k)}
- consisting of single token substitutions of
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
- obtained from the guiding step. The best performing candidate from
C^{(k)}
is chosen as the next point
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}\in V^{n}
and the process is repeated.

Effectively, GCG uses gradients of the
\mathrm{TargetLogprobs}
function, which provide local information about the landscape of this loss function at the point
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
, to guess what is likely to be the actual function value at a far-away point, that is, at the point corresponding to the actual substituted token. A priori, this represents a sound strategy, that is, if the optimization function were a smooth enough function, such as a piecewise linear function, then on average gradients should be expected to provide a good guess for what happens at distant points. And if so, the guiding step should add significantly to the optimization procedure.

We establish empirically that when controlled for the attack hyperparameters of number of steps, candidate set size and initialization, GCG does not significantly outperform an unguided greedy search. That is, we perform the following two optimization algorithms which differ only in the guiding step.

1.

Guided version - We use the candidate set generation used by GCG that uses gradients (at the current point) to suggest promising single-token substitutions as candidates for the next point.

2.

Unguided version - We use a randomly generated set of single-token substitutions as candidates for the next point.

Crucially, for both the above, we do still evaluate the loss function exactly on the candidates generated. That is, the only difference lies in the candidate generation step.

For our empirical evaluation, we mimic the setting of the evaluation of SecAlign and StruQ. We perform our comparison study on
50

V
The
ASTRA
Attack

To address the above problems, we propose a new method that explicitly exploits the internal structure of an LLM. Concretely, we use the architectural properties of LLMs in order to craft a novel loss function that can be used to generate better prompt injection attacks. We use an optimized input generated by optimizing this loss function as a “warm start” initializer to “fine-tune” the adversarial input using standard algorithms like GCG.

Our key insight is that we can exploit an instruction-tuned model’s instruction-following ability to craft prompt injection attacks. Specifically, we note that if the attacker’s instruction payload was the only text in the input stream, the model would easily follow that instruction. This insight forms the basis for our loss function. To formalize the above insight mathematically, we rely on the attention mechanism of an LLM. The attention mechanism forms the key component of our new loss function.

V-A

Background on Attention

The attention mechanism in an LLM is a core component which assigns a weight to each previous token while trying to generate the next token.

Formally, when a sequence of tokens
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
is inputted into an LLM, it first maps each token
x_{i}
to an embedding vector
e_{i}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to obtain a sequence of initial representations
(e_{1}^{(0)},e_{2}^{(0)},\dots e_{n}^{(0)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. This sequence of representations is transformed by a series of decoder layers (say, total of
L
decoder layers) into a final transformed representation
(f_{1},f_{2},\dots f_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Finally, the “language modelling head”, represented by a matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times|V|}
is used to generate the probability distribution for the next token by the following calculation -

P(\cdot|x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n};\Theta)=\mathrm{softmax}(f_{n}\cdot W)\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}

where each coordinate lies between
0
and
1
and represents the probability of generation of that particular token given the context
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
.

The
l
-th decoder layer
D^{(l)}
is itself a complex computation that maps one sequence of representations in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
to another sequence of representations in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. It consists of an attention layer (typically prenormalized and postnormalized) and a fully connected layer that modify a residual stream that forms the internal representations that feed into the next decoder layer
D^{(l+1)}
. For the sake of brevity, here we only describe some relevant specifics of the attention mechanism. A more rigorous and complete treatment of transformers can be found in
[
32
]
.

The key architectural innovation at the core of a decoder layer is the Masked Multi-head Attention (MMHA) mechanism. The MMHA mechanism at the
l
-th decoder layer
D^{(l)}
takes in a sequence of representations obtained from the previous layer, say
E^{(l-1)}=(e_{1}^{(l-1)},e_{2}^{(l-1)},\dots,e_{n}^{(l-1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and computes a sequence of square matrices
(A_{1}^{(l)},A_{2}^{(l)},\dots A_{H}^{(l)})
called “attention matrices” where
A_{i}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The number
H
represents the number of “attention heads” of the LLM at layer
l
.
These matrices are computed as

A_{i}^{(l)}=\mathrm{softmax}(E^{(l-1)}W_{i}^{(l)}E^{(l-1)T}+\mathrm{CausalMask}_{n})

(4)

Here,
W_{i}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a pre-trained parameter of the LLM corresponding to the
i
-th attention head at the
l
-th layer. The matrix
\mathrm{CausalMask}_{n}
is an
n\times n
matrix with
-\infty
above the diagonal and
0
everywhere else, and the
\mathrm{softmax}
function is applied row-wise to the matrix.
The attention matrices
A_{i}^{(l)}
represent intermediate values computed within the decoder layer
D^{(l)}
that are ultimately used to further calculate the representations that can be used as input for the next layer
D^{(l+1)}
.

Due to the specific functional form above, an attention matrix
A
has significant structure to it. All attention matrices have the following properties. (We use the notation
A[j]
to refer to the
j
-th row of
A
and the notation
A[j][k]
to refer to the element in the
j
-th row and
k
-th column of
A
)

1.

The attention matrices are lower-triangular, that is, all elements above the diagonal are zero. That is,
A[j][k]=0
if
j<k
.

2.

Each element of the attention matrix lies between
0
and
1
(inclusive). That is,
0\leq A[j][k]\leq 1

3.

Each row of the attention matrix sums up to
1
. That is, for each
j
,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}A[j][k]=\sum_{k=1}^{j}A[j][k]=1

The above properties imply the following interpretation(s) for the rows of an attention matrix
A
.

1.

The
j
non-zero entries in the
j
-th row
A[j]
of an attention matrix represent the attention given (by that attention head) to the first
j
tokens when generating the
j+1
-th token.

2.

The
j
non-zero entries in the
j
-th row
A[j]
can be thought of as the weights of a convex combination over
j
dimensions

3.

The
j
non-zero entries in the
j
-th row
A[j]
can be interpreted as the probability masses of a discrete probability distribution over a set of
j
elements.

V-B

Crafting a loss function

We use the above interpretations to guide the design of our loss function.

When the LLM is processing the
n
tokens in the input
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
in order to generate the
n+1
-th token, the last (i.e.
n
-th) rows of all the attention matrices
A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})
represent the attention given to each of the
n
tokens in the input by that attention head. Since we want the model to attend only to the payload, we identify the indices of tokens corresonding to the attacker’s payload, say
J\subseteq\{1,2,\dots n\}
and try to maximize the sum of attentions given to this part of the input. As before, let
\mathbf{x}_{I}
represent the modifiable tokens which are to be optimized by the attacker. Let
y
be the target token that the attacker wants the LLM to generate as the
n+1
-th token.

Formally, for a single attention matrix
A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})
generated on input
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
of length
n
, we define

\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},y)=1-\sum_{j\in J}A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})[n][j]

(5)

We note that
\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},y)
doesn’t depend on the target token
y
to be generated in the next step and only depends on the input.

To account for the attention matrices over the entire LLM, we define

\mathrm{AttLoss}(\mathbf{x},y)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{H}w_{i}^{(l)}\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},y)

(6)

where
w_{i}^{(l)}
are some set of weights, assigned to each attention head in each layer.

For a longer sequence of target tokens
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{m})
, we just sum up the losses over the target tokens and define

\mathrm{AttLoss}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{AttLoss}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},y_{j})

(7)

This loss function becomes
0
when the entire attention of each attention matrix is concentrated on the attacker payload tokens. That is, this loss function attempts to capture how much of the attention of the LLM is being paid to the surrounding context. A larger value of this loss function implies that more of the attention across all layers is distributed over the entire context window, whereas a smaller value implies that a lesser amount of attention is distributed over the entire context.

In principle, we could simply add all contributions equally from each attention head
\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}
to craft the final loss function
\mathrm{AttLoss}
. However, this is likely to be suboptimal since

1.

Prior work from the field of mechanistic interpretability has shown that different attention heads carry out different functions in an LLM.
[
29
,
45
]

2.

Attention heads across layers are not independent from each other, since the value of the attention matrix in the
l+1
-th layer depends on the value of the attention matrix in the
l
-th layer. In particular, small changes in the values of the attention matrix in earlier layers can lead to large changes in the values of attention matrices in later layers.

In general, figuring out which attention head is important for which task requires extensive manual analysis and/or lengthy ablation experiments which turn off attention heads one-by-one to see the effect they have on the LLM’s output
[
45
]
. A typical LLM has more than a thousand attention heads (across all layers) which makes such an analysis impractical.

Our key insight is that since our eventual goal is finding adversarial perturbations in the token space that lead to an adversary chosen output, we don’t need to explicitly find out which attention heads are functionally important for certain abstract tasks. Instead, we only need to find those attention heads for which small changes in their corresponding attention matrices lead to large changes in the probability of the desired target token. This observation allows us to sidestep an expensive ablation experiment which would turn off attention heads one-by-one and analyze its impact on the output. Instead, since sensitivity to small perturbations (of the probability of the target token) is a local property of a function, sensitivity can be meaningfully quantified using gradients. We use these gradients as the key piece of information that allows us to quantify the importance of attention heads.

Formally, let
y
be a target token that the adversary wants the LLM to generate. Let
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots z_{n})
represent an input sequence of tokens which generate the attention matrices
\{A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z})\}
during the forward pass of the LLM.
Let

\mathrm{LogProb}(y|\mathbf{z})=\mathrm{log}P(y|\mathbf{z};\Theta)

represent the log of the probability of the target token
y
. Then,

\frac{\partial\ \mathrm{LogProb}(y|\mathbf{z})}{\partial\ A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

represents the gradients of this loss function with respect to the attention matrices at the input point
\mathbf{z}
. Since the
n+1
-th token prediction depends on the attention values in the
n
-th row, we look at the sum of the magnitude of the entries in the
n
-th row of the gradient matrix to get a measure for how sensitive the loss function is to changes in that row of the matrix.

That is, we define sensitivity as

\mathrm{Sen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z},y)=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathbf{z}|}\left|\frac{\partial\ \mathrm{LogProb}(y|\mathbf{z})}{\partial\ A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z})}[n][k]\right|

(8)

For a longer sequence of target tokens
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{m})
, we define

\mathrm{Sen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathbf{y}|}\mathrm{Sen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},y_{j})

(9)

Finally, to get an average sensitivity score for an attention head (with respect to a target sequence of tokens
\mathbf{y}
), we take a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of inputs
\mathbf{z}
and normalize the sensitivities by the total length
|\mathbf{z}|+|\mathbf{y}|
and average out over the dataset
\mathcal{D}

\overline{\mathrm{Sen}}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|+|\mathbf{y}|}\mathrm{Sen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

(10)

This provides a proxy measure for how sensitive, on average, the probability of a target token
y
is to changes in the value of the attention matrix at the
i
-th attention head at the
l
-th layer. We show the average sensitivities for two different SecAlign defended models for the target string
\verb|Hacked|
constructed from a training dataset consisting of examples from the Dolly fine-tuning dataset
[
9
]
in
Fig.

4
.

In practice, we find that a large subset of the sensitivities (across heads and layers) are very small, as can be seen in
Fig.

4
. Heuristically, small changes in these attention heads don’t lead to large changes in the probability of the target sequence of tokens and are likely to act as noise during optimization. We can therefore choose a threshold
\tau
and manually set the small contributions of heads less than
\tau
to
0
. That is, we define the clipped sensitivities as

\mathrm{ClipSen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})=\begin{cases}0&\mathrm{if}\ \overline{\mathrm{Sen}}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})\leq\tau\\
\overline{\mathrm{Sen}}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})&\mathrm{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

(11)

We use these clipped sensitivities as the final weights for our attention loss function.

V-D

The Final Attack

Using the weights from the previous section, we have the final loss function that the adversary has to optimize. For an input sequence of tokens
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n})
and a sequence of target tokens
\mathbf{y}
with modifiable indices of tokens
I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots n\}
and payload tokens
J\subseteq\{1,2,\dots n\}
, we have the loss function

\mathrm{AttLoss}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\mathrm{ClipSen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

(12)

With the above loss function, the goal of the adversary is to solve the following optimization problem, which we call attention loss minimization

VI-C

Dependence on Weights

The key novelty in the construction of our attack lies in the loss function, which is constructed in a series of steps. We perform a series of experiments to show that the clipped sensitivities used as weights in our loss function add meaningful value and outperform other intuitive and obvious choices for weights, in addition to outperforming the baseline itself.

Concretely, we compare five different loss functions each trying to maximize attention on the payload, on some subset of attention heads with the only difference being how the different attention heads are chosen and weighted.

1.

Only first - Where we only try to maximize attention on the first layer. In principle, this might be expected to work since deeper layers are all a function of the first layer. That is,

w_{i}^{(l)}=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{if}\ l=1\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

2.

Only last - Where we only try to maximize the attention on the last layer.

w_{i}^{(l)}=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{if}\ l=L-1\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

3.

Uniform - Each attention head in each layer is weighted uniformly. This is a naive weighting function that treats each attention head equally.

w_{i}^{(l)}=1

4.

Average Sensitivity - Weights are assigned as computed by the average sensitivities described in
Section

V
.

w_{i}^{(l)}=\overline{\mathrm{Sen}}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})

5.

Clipped Sensitivity - We drop the bottom
75\%
of all the weights from the average sensitivities

w_{i}^{(l)}=\mathrm{ClipSen}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{y})

We perform all the above experiments on the same set of
40
examples of the evaluation set and show the loss curves obtained over the entire attack in
Fig.

7
. Using clipped sensitivities as weights consistently outperforms other weighting functions.

VII
Discussion

VII-A

A more general framework

While we perform our attacks which directly maximize the attention on the payload string, we note that this attack can be generalized to more general distance functions between attention matrices.

That is, our attention loss function (for a single target token
y
and an input sequence
\mathbf{x}
of length
n
with payload indices
J
defined as (
Eq.

6
)

\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},y)=1-\sum_{j\in J}A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})[n][j]

can be rewritten as

\mathrm{AttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},y)=\sum_{j\in J}\frac{1}{|J|}-A_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})[n][j]

This can be interpreted as measuring the difference between an “ideal” attention pattern (which treats all tokens in the payload equally and ignores everything outside the payload) and the “true” attention patterns. Since rows of attention matrices have multiple interpretations as probability masses and coefficients of convex combinations, instead of just naively looking at the difference between attention values, we could potentially use more sophisticated distance or dissimilarity functions such as KL divergence or Wasserstein distances to craft attacks under different constraints.
The general form for such a loss function looks like

\mathrm{GenAttLoss}_{i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},y)=\mathrm{d}(A_{\mathrm{ideal},i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}),A_{\mathrm{real},i}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}))

where
\mathrm{d}
represents some distance or dissimilarity function that can be measured between attention matrices.
The rest of the construction of the complete attention loss function can be carried out as normal.

We leave the exploration of the utility of more sophisticated distance functions to future work.

VII-B

Evaluation of Prompt Injection Defenses

Scalability
Our scalability analysis shows that even existing white-box attacks can break prompt injection defenses significantly just with increased attacker budgets. Indeed, heuristically, in the worst case where the system or user instruction is short and the attacker controlled content is very long (relative to the instruction), it would be easier for the attacker to influence more of the conversation since it can control a much larger part of the context window.

We therefore propose that when evaluating prompt injection defenses against strong optimization-based adversaries, instead of evaluating the defense at one single attacker budget, the evaluation of the defense should show the behavior of a defense under scaling. With this premise, a strong defense is not required to just block attacks at a given (fixed) budget. Instead, a strong defense should show relatively small increases in attack success rate even with large increases in attacker budgets.

Randomness
Our results from
Section

IV
show that over a single evaluation run, randomness in the initialization can result in attack success. We therefore propose that evaluation of prompt injection defenses against automated algorithms should allow for randomized initializations instead of using fixed initializer strings. Further, while we do not explore the effect of randomization during the optimization process itself, we propose that a reasonable baseline evaluation should always allow for sufficient randomization to happen during the optimization process. That is, defense evaluations should consider evaluating on more aggressive randomization strategies that change more than one single token at any given iteration. Such aggressive randomization strategies were used, for instance, by Simple Adaptive Attacks
[
1
]
in combination with a prepared template to achieve high success rates.

VII-C

Limitations of ASTRA

Performance
The primary limitation of
ASTRA
lies in its memory footprint and performance. Concretely,
ASTRA
relies on extracting and computing on attention matrices during a model’s forward and backward passes. The size of an attention matrix grows quadratically with increase in the size of the context window. Therefore, for long context windows, the attention matrices take up a significant amount of memory. Since the amount of memory consumed is significant, in practice this leads to a reduction in the batch size that can be processed on a GPU in a single unit of time, and thus a slowdown in the entire attack as compared to attacks like GCG. For our implementation on a
48
GB GPU, we found
ASTRA
to be roughly
2\times
slower than GCG for the same number of forward passes. We leave the question of efficiency of our attacks to future work.

Dynamic Prompt Injections
We evaluated our attack for the simple case where the target string is a fixed string. In general, prompt injections can require dynamically generated target strings such as when the attacker wants to leak private data based on the conversation history. We leave the adaptation of our attack to dynamic target strings to future work.

VIII
Related Work

VIII-A

Jailbreaking

A related problem to prompt injection is jailbreaking. The threat model in jailbreaking requires causing the LLM to generate objectionable or harmful content that is forbidden by the developer’s policy.

Various automated methods have been proposed for the purposes of jailbreaking. These methods differ in terms of the access they assume to the target model, as well as along the approaches they take to automate the process of jailbreaking.

In the white-box setting, attackers can assume complete access to the model weights and parameters. In particular, attackers can compute gradients, thus leading to gradient-driven methods like GCG. Beyond GCG, AutoDAN
[
49
]
tries to construct interpretable jailbreaks by adding a regularization term to the usual GCG loss function that aims to minimize perplexity of the generated suffix. COLD
[
15
]
tries to add stealthiness and controllability using a special decoding procedure to generate the jailbreaks.

In the grey-box setting, attackers assume partial access to model — while an attacker might not be able to compute gradients, an attacker might stil be able to get information about the model. Different approaches differ in terms of the amount of information they need about the model. A major line of work relies on being able to compute target logprobs for a closed model. BEAST
[
33
]
, GCQ
[
16
]
and PAL
[
38
]
use these indirectly computed target logprobs to generate attacks.

Another class of algorithms tries to train a model to generate adversarial suffixes. Examples of model-based adversarial attack generation include AdvPrompter
[
31
]
and AmpleGCG
[
22
]
. AdvPrompter uses a BEAST-like algorithm to efficiently generate the training set for the model that generates the attacks, while AmpleGCG augments GCG in order to train the final model.

One important class of algorithms in the black-box setting uses another LLM in a feedback loop to guide the generation of jailbreaks. Examples of such attacks include TAP
[
27
]
and PAIR

Yet another class of attacks uses genetic algorithms and mutation and combination based methods to construct black-box jailbreaks. Rainbow Teaming

VIII-C

Attacks manipulating Attention

In the text domain, Attn-GCG
[
41
]
attempts to use attention matrices to craft better jailbreaking attacks. While there are a few superficial similarities to our work, we note several crucial and major differences between our attack and theirs.
We focus on the problem of breaking prompt injection defenses, while they focus on jailbreaking. Further, on the technical side, their loss function takes the form of a regularization term to the usual GCG loss, which is different from our approach which uses attention as a warm starter. In addition, they attempt to focus the attention on the adversarial suffix, which in practice is going to be gibberish, while we focus our attention on the payload which represents meaningful instructions. Further, they only consider the last decoder layer without appropriately chosen weights which as we show in
Section

VI
doesn’t work in practice. As a consequence, their attack shows only a marginal improvement to the success rate. In contrast, our attack shows significantly higher performance than GCG on prompt injection tasks.

IX
Conclusion

With prompt injections being a major risk against LLM-integrated systems, an emerging class of defenses attempt to rely on training the model to ignore the instructions given in the data part. With our novel attack that takes into consideration the actual architectural features of LLMs, we show that such defenses are fundamentally not robust to prompt injection attacks in the white-box setting against strong adversaries. We hope that our work begins a conversation around how to better evaluate prompt injection defenses against strong adversaries keeping in mind the concrete realities of the threat model and scalability constraints.
