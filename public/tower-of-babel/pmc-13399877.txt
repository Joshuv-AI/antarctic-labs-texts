Aortic root abscess complicating a unicuspid aortic valve in a 15-year-old boy: A case report on surgical management
Authors: Kanupriya Chaturvedi, Prerna Bhat, Sanjay Khatri, Gaurav Goyal, Sunil Kumar Kaushal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAortic root abscess, a dreaded and life-threatening complication of infective endocarditis (IE), is often challenging to deal with, both medically and surgically. If not treated in time, it can lead to cardiac fistula formation, rupture into a cardiac chamber, aneurysm formation or heart block, and arrhythmias. It is also a lesion that requires prompt recognition and early/urgent surgical intervention to minimize complications, morbidity, and mortality and improve outcomes. We present the challenging case of a 15-year-old boy, diagnosed with a unicuspid aortic valve during infancy, with progressively severe aortic stenosis, complicated by IE and aortic root abscess and the surgical decision-making and challenges encountered in his case.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of infective endocarditis (IE) is generally considered to be 0.05–0.12 cases/1000 pediatric hospital admissions,[1] with the overall mortality being about 25%.[2] The risk factors for IE primarily include structural heart disease, prosthetic valves, implanted cardiac devices, congenital heart disease, immunosuppressive diseases, and injection drug use. Among the congenital heart diseases, the lesions commonly associated with IE are ventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, and aortic stenosis.[3]

Bicuspid aortic valve is the most common congenital heart disease with a prevalence of approximately 0.5%–2%;[4] however, the true prevalence remains unknown due to undetected, asymptomatic cases in the community. Unicuspid aortic valve is considered a rare congenital heart anomaly with a 0.02% incidence the adult population.[5] Endocarditis of the aortic valve can be complicated by aortic root abscess, which is one of the most dreaded complications of this lesion with poor overall outcomes despite surgical intervention.[67] We describe the case of a child with unicuspid aortic valve endocarditis complicated by aortic root abscess and the challenges faced in the surgical decision-making of this case.

CASE REPORT

A 15-year-old boy was referred to our institution from an outside hospital with the diagnosis of IE. He had a long-standing fever for about 1 month, progressive weight loss, exertional dyspnea, and a history of unicuspid aortic valve which was diagnosed during infancy. The patient had progressive aortic stenosis and aortic regurgitation as documented in the initial echocardiography reports done at various hospitals. He was thereafter lost to follow up and presented with the above complaints. Review of his investigations done at the previous hospital showed microcytic hypochromic anemia and raised white blood cell count of 15,000/uL. His erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein (CRP) were 25 mm/h and 122 mg/dL, respectively, and the serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase and serum glutamic-pyruvic transaminase were marginally raised. The patient underwent 3 blood cultures on consecutive days, of which one sample demonstrated significant growth of Staphylococcus aureus while the other two samples were negative for any bacterial growth. The patient was started on intravenous antibiotics based on his culture report and was referred to our institution in view of persistent fever, history of unicuspid aortic valve, and growth of S. aureus on one of his blood cultures. An echocardiogram was done on admission which showed a unicuspid aortic valve [Figure 1] with severe aortic stenosis (peak instantaneous gradient ~65 mmHg) and severe aortic regurgitation. There were no evident vegetations seen on the aortic valve leaflet; however, an echo dense pocket was seen around the aortic root, raising the suspicion for an aortic root abscess. To further delineate the lesion, a computed tomography (CT) aortogram was done which demonstrated an ill-defined hypodense area in the periaortic region, predominantly extending to the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) and measuring approximately 10 mm in maximum thickness [Figure 2]. Two pseudoaneurysms were reported at the level of LVOT [Figure 3]. Given the persistent fever, elevated CRP, and the CT aortogram findings, it was decided to take the patient for urgent surgery.

The surgical decision-making and challenge

A standard median sternotomy was done and arterial cannulation was performed via the ascending aorta. Bicaval venous cannulation was established, and left atrium was vented through the right superior pulmonary vein. The surgery was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in moderate hypothermia. After cross-clamping, cardiac arrest and myocardial protection were achieved by direct coronary ostial administration of isothermic blood cardioplegic solution. Aortotomy was performed, and the peri-annular aortic abscess and its surroundings were delicately investigated. Aortic valve was unicuspid with verrucous vegetations around the valve [Figure 4]. There were two large cavities full of purulent necrotic tissues, below the right and noncoronary cusps each of which were burrowing into the subaortic myocardium [Figures 5 and 6]. The infected valve and all foreign materials were excised and were sent for culture; both cavities were thoroughly washed with antibiotic solution (gentamycin) and were covered with treated pericardial patch.

As the circular abscess spanned over more than one aortic cusp without aortoventricular dehiscence, the defect over the aortic annulus was reconstructed with use of a pericardial patch, and pledgeted sutures were placed on this patch during aortic valve replacement [Figures 5 and 6].

One fourth of the mechanical aortic valve (19 mm) ring was sutured onto the pericardial patch and reinforced further with interrupted 4-0 Prolene sutures on to the nearby aortic valve tissue [Figure 7]. Aortotomy was closed in two layers, and CPB was weaned off successfully with minimal inotropic support. Postoperative echocardiogram suggested no paravalvular leak, normal valve function, and good ventricular function [Figure 8]. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1 and was quickly weaned to room air, and ambulation was encouraged. He remained afebrile after surgery with inflammatory markers showing a downward trend and a sterile blood culture. The intraoperative pus culture was sterile, and the pathology was that of chronic inflammation. The patient was given antibiotics for 4 weeks after surgery, of which 2 weeks were inpatient and the rest as outpatient. At 6-month follow-up, the patient has not had any prolonged fever, has gained weight, and does not have any heart failure symptoms at present.

DISCUSSION

Aortic valve disease/prosthetic aortic valve is a nidus for IE and is associated with increased mortality and morbidity. Among the abnormal aortic valve lesions, unicuspid aortic valve is a rare entity which is primarily of two types: acommissural, which has no lateral attachment to the aorta, and unicommissural,[8] with one attachment and an eccentric orifice, as was seen in our patient.

Common surgical indications for IE include signs and symptoms of heart failure, IE caused by fungi or highly resistant organisms (e.g., vancomycin-resistant enterococcus), IE complicated by abscess, heart block, recurrent emboli, and persistent/enlarging vegetations despite optimum antibiotic therapy or mobile vegetations >10 mm.[9]

Aortic root abscess is a dreaded complication of IE of the aortic valve and requires urgent surgical intervention, without which fistulae to other cardiac chambers, false aneurysms, heart block of varying degrees, and involvement of mitral valve can occur.[71011] The surgical decision-making can be challenging in this cohort and should be decided on a case-to-case basis. Commonly practiced techniques for surgical management of aortic root abscess are aortic valve replacement with a prosthesis or complete aortic root replacement (ARR) with a biological or prosthetic composite graft.[12] In our patient, we decided for aortic valve replacement with patch repair of LVOT given that there was a good landing zone present for a prosthetic aortic valve. In a study conducted by Kirali et al., ARR was preferred over aortic valve replacement as ARR was shown to have lesser early and late adverse events.[12] However, in a study conducted by Croon et al., annular reconstruction was comparable to that of ARR.[13]

Although, aortic valve repair is the ideal surgery in young patients, but if the valve is not salvageable, the options of replacement include bioprosthetic valves, mechanical valves, aortic valve homografts, and a pulmonary autograft (Ross procedure). Ross procedure is considered superior for young patients as compared to a mechanical aortic valve but carries a higher rate of re-intervention. Mechanical valves, on the other hand, subject a patient to life-long anticoagulation. The decision regarding the choice of valve substitute should be done in consultation with the family after explaining the pros and cons of each valve to them.

Regardless of the technique adopted, aortic root abscess is a surgical emergency and needs to be dealt with at the earliest to avoid life-threatening complications and increased morbidity and mortality.

CONCLUSION

Our case highlights the importance of early diagnosis and intervention in aortic valve endocarditis and the need for urgent surgery in case on an aortic root abscess to avoid life threatening complications and mortality. The surgical technique and choice of valve substitute often becomes challenging based on the extent of spread of the abscess to the surrounding tissues, age of the patient, aortic annulus size, and the feasibility. Our patient underwent surgical intervention and could avoid a complete ARR.
