Clinical and toxicological trends in mad honey research
Authors: Abdul Samet Sahin, Emre Dilaver, Abdulkadir Gunduz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Honey derived from the nectar of Rhododendron species, known as “mad honey,” possesses unique pharmacological and toxicological properties due to its grayanotoxin content. Prior investigations have largely comprised case reports and experimental studies, lacking a comprehensive overview of broader scientific trends. The present study addresses this gap by providing the first bibliometric analysis of the historical development, thematic directions, and scientific structure of the “mad honey” literature published between 1954 and 2025.Methods:Data for this retrospective bibliometric analysis were collected from the Web of Science Core Collection database in January 2026. The search strategy used the keywords “mad honey,” “grayanotoxin,” “andromedotoxin,” “rhodotoxin,” and “asebotoxin,” encompassing publications from 1954 to 2025, with no language restrictions.Results:During the study period, 388 publications were identified across 214 journals, reflecting an annual growth rate of 1.65%. A total of 1253 authors contributed, with an average of 18.57 citations per publication. Country-level analysis revealed that Türkiye leads globally in both publication volume and citation impact, followed by the United States, China, and Japan.Conclusions:This analysis demonstrates that the mad honey literature has evolved from local case reports toward global clinical research and potential therapeutic applications. Research on mad honey has expanded globally since 2010, with Türkiye leading clinical data and diagnostic protocols. Future studies should shift focus from poisoning cases toward therapeutic potential, product standardization, and safe dosage parameters.

1. Introduction

Honey is a natural product produced by honeybees through the biochemical transformation of plant nectar and has been utilized for nutritional and therapeutic purposes in medical practice.[1,2] The botanical origin and environmental conditions of honey are key determinants of its chemical composition and biological characteristics.[3] Accordingly, specific honey types are highlighted in the scientific literature not only for their nutritional value but also for distinctive chemical constituents that confer pharmacological and toxicological properties.

One specific type of honey, referred to as “mad honey” in Türkiye, is derived from the nectar of Rhododendron species that grow in particular geographic regions, predominantly in the Black Sea region.[4,5] The distinctive properties of this honey are due to the presence of grayanotoxin compounds.[5] These diterpenoid compounds prevent sodium channel inactivation, prolonging membrane depolarization and producing significant biological effects, particularly in the cardiovascular and neurological systems.[6,7] Historically, these toxic components were extensively studied under the name andromedotoxin. Descriptions of the toxicity of various Rhododendron species – including their leaves, flowers, and honey – date back to ancient records.[8] This characteristic distinguishes mad honey from other honey types and has established it as a unique subject of scientific research.

The presence of mad honey in human history dates back to antiquity, with historical sources documenting that honey consumed along the Black Sea coast caused transient loss of consciousness and physical weakness among soldiers.[8] These historical records indicate that the effects of mad honey were recognized long before the emergence of modern toxicological knowledge.[9] Currently, mad honey is still used in some communities under the belief that low-dose consumption may confer benefits, and it is employed as a remedy for gastrointestinal disorders, metabolic disturbances, and circulatory system complaints.[4,10] As a result, a significant portion of consumers tends to obtain this product directly from local producers without undergoing standard industrial processing or regulatory inspection.[11] However, its use is limited by a narrow therapeutic window, necessitating careful scientific evaluation.

In the current scientific literature, mad honey is regarded as a multidisciplinary research topic situated at the intersection of botany, pharmacology, toxicology, and clinical medicine.[7,12,13] Accordingly, studies on mad honey cover a wide range of topics, from the biochemical mechanisms of grayanotoxins and experimental model outcomes to clinical findings and public health considerations. However, existing research has predominantly consisted of case reports, review articles, and experimental studies, while comprehensive and synthesizing analyses that quantitatively characterize the broader scientific trends of the field are scarce.

In recent years, bibliometric analyses of honey, bee products, and apitherapy have provided detailed assessments of global research trends, prominent themes, and collaborative networks in honey-related studies.[2,14-16] However, these studies generally address honey as a broad category and do not recognize mad honey as a distinct research domain. Therefore, the present study represents the first bibliometric analysis of mad honey research, with the aim of delineating the historical evolution, thematic orientations, and scientific structure of the literature published between 1954 and 2025.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and data source

This study is a retrospective bibliometric analysis providing a comprehensive evaluation of the global literature on “mad honey” and “grayanotoxin.” The research data were retrieved from the Web of Science (WoS) Core Collection database on January 19, 2026, due to its high data quality and comprehensive coverage for academic literature searches.

2.2. Search strategy and inclusion criteria

The data collection strategy employed the keywords “mad honey” OR “grayanotoxin” OR “andromedotoxin” OR “rhodotoxin” OR “asebotoxin,” with the “Topic” criterion selected in the WoS database. To maximize the analysis scope, no temporal or language restrictions were applied, and all publications from 1954 to 2025 were included.

Only publications classified as “Article” and “Review” were included in the analysis, while letters, meeting abstracts, book chapters, corrections, and editorial materials were excluded to maintain a focus on high-quality, peer-reviewed, full-text research. The document type was limited to academic journals, and duplicates and irrelevant literature were screened out. Two authors independently reviewed all literature for relevance, achieving 98% agreement; discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author.

2.3. Data analysis and visualization

The bibliographic data obtained from the WoS database (including authors, titles, publication years, citation counts, and institutional affiliations) were exported in “Plain Text File” format. Two different bibliometric software tools were used for data analysis and scientific mapping. Biblioshiny, the web interface for the R-based Bibliometrix package, was used to compute core bibliometric indicators, including annual scientific output, journal impact factors, h-index values, Bradford’s Law analysis, and citation bursts.[17] The other tool employed was VOSviewer, which was used to visualize keyword co-occurrence networks, co-authorship maps, and international collaboration networks.[18] This tool was preferred for generating network maps that facilitate a clearer understanding of conceptual clusters and the intellectual structure of the literature.

2.4. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was not required for this study, as it was based exclusively on publicly available bibliographic records retrieved from the WoS Core Collection and did not involve human participants, animal subjects, or identifiable personal data.

3. Results

3.1. Main bibliometric indicators

This bibliometric analysis covers the literature on mad honey published between 1954 and 2025. The scientific exploration of mad honey dates back to early records, with the earliest identified publication in this dataset dating to 1954. Historically, early research was primarily concentrated on experimental pharmacology and characterizing the toxic components, then known as andromedotoxin. The systematic search and selection process resulted in a final dataset of 388 publications, as detailed in the flow diagram (Fig. 1). Of the 388 publications, 29 were identified as review articles. During the study period, 388 publications were identified in 214 distinct scientific journals, with an annual publication growth rate of 1.65%. 3.4.1. Most relevant affiliations

Institutional analysis revealed that Karadeniz Technical University was the most productive institution in mad honey research, with 55 published articles. Following Duzce University (31 publications), Hiroshima University (26 publications), Erciyes University (23 publications), and Chinese Academy of Sciences (15 publications) were also identified as major contributors to the mad honey literature. 3.4.2. Institutional publication performance over time

When institutional publication trends over time are examined, it becomes evident that contributions were initially limited to a small number of institutions, followed by a pronounced rise in institutional output after the 2000s. A consistent growth in publication output was observed for Karadeniz Technical University over time, whereas Duzce University and Erciyes University exhibited a pronounced acceleration in publication activity in recent years. The contributions of institutions based in China and Japan were found to be concentrated in later periods.

3.6. Keyword analysis

3.6.1. Keyword distribution, frequency and trend topics

Keyword frequency analysis demonstrates that certain concepts are central within the mad honey literature. The most frequently used keyword was “mad honey,” appearing 116 times (13% of all keywords), followed by “Na” (121 occurrences; 13%) and “grayanotoxin” (91 occurrences; 10%). Additional high-frequency keywords included “bradycardia” (43; 5%), “intoxication” (33; 4%), “rhododendron” (33; 4%), “honey” (30; 3%), “grayanotoxins” (25; 3%), and “hypotension” (25; 3%). When examining the chronological flow, it is seen that fundamental physiological terms such as “mechanism” (1996), “skeletal-muscle” (1999), and “batrachotoxin” (2000) were dominant in the late 1990s. At the beginning of the 2000s, studies at the molecular level such as “channels” (2002), “d-ring” (2002), and “point-mutations” (2002) came to the fore, while terms such as “Turkey” and “grayanotoxin-i” (2009) entered the literature as of 2008. During the period between 2010 and 2020, terms related to clinical symptoms such as “poisoning” (2014), “syncope” (2014), “bradycardia” (2016), “intoxication” (2016), and “hypotension” (2016). Looking at the current period of the data (2020–2025), it is observed that the focus has shifted to biochemical and taxonomic terms such as “antioxidant” (2020), “diterpenoids” (2022), “biological-activities” (2023), and “ericaceae family” (2023). In the most recent data from 2024 and 2025, the terms “identification,” “nepal,” and “pollen” have entered the system as the median year. Three distinct main clusters were identified within the network structure. 3.8. Intercountry scientific collaboration analysis

An analysis of international scientific collaboration reveals the geographic distribution of international co-authorship relationships within the mad honey literature. Map-based network analysis demonstrates that, in addition to publication output, scientific collaborations are also concentrated around specific countries.

According to the analysis, Türkiye occupies a central position in the international collaboration network and exhibits direct connections with numerous countries. In particular, the collaborative links between Türkiye and the United States, Japan, and China are more pronounced. Collaboration links among these countries are represented by thicker and denser connections within the network.

4. Discussion

The bibliometric analysis of the “mad honey” literature covering the period from 1954 to 2025 demonstrates a clear increase in publication trends over time. The increasing trend in publication numbers, particularly in recent years, parallels the overall growth of scientific output in the literature. A similar pattern has been reported in other areas related to honey and health; for instance, studies on the antimicrobial and antioxidant properties of honey were limited in the early 2000s but accelerated markedly after the 2010s, exhibiting an exponential annual growth rate of approximately 17.5%.[14] Likewise, the global literature on honey and diabetes has shown a significant increasing trend during the last 10 years.[19] Similarly, publication activity in the field of mad honey remained limited during the mid-20th century but has accelerated since the 2000s, gaining notable momentum in recent years. This finding indicates that the topic has attracted increasing attention within the scientific community and has evolved in alignment with broader research trends.

Bibliometric data suggest that author collaboration networks in mad honey research are relatively limited in size but remain focused and specialized. When compared with similar bibliometric analyses, it is expected that the proportion of international collaborative studies remains relatively low in a niche field such as mad honey research. Indeed, global research on the health effects of honey has been reported to involve an average of approximately 5 authors per article, with nearly 27% of studies conducted through international collaboration.[14] This proportion may be more limited in the context of mad honey, as the topic is investigated by a geographically and scientifically specific research community. On the other hand, examination of the relevant literature highlights strong domestic collaborations and network structures centered around specific countries. For example, in bee venom research, South Korea has been reported as one of the main hubs, establishing dense collaborative links both among its domestic institutions and at the international level.[15] Similarly, in mad honey research, researchers in Türkiye appear to engage in dense domestic collaborations, while international networks remain relatively limited. Accordingly, the mad honey literature differs from honey and bee product research that has established extensive global collaboration networks; however, it reflects a collaboration dynamic similar to that observed in topics requiring region-specific expertise.

Country-based analysis indicates that mad honey research output is predominantly concentrated in Türkiye. This is an expected finding, as the mad honey phenomenon is historically and botanically associated with limited geographic areas, particularly the Black Sea region of Türkiye.[7] As noted by Santos-Buelga and González-Paramás, although Rhododendron species are distributed globally from Japan to the United States, the majority of poisoning cases continue to be reported from Turkey. This regional concentration is attributed not only to the local prevalence of toxic species like R ponticum and R luteum but also to specific beekeeping practices and local consumption habits.[20] For example, in global research on the medicinal properties of honey, numerous countries, including India, Italy, the United States, and Türkiye, have been identified among the most productive, each contributing approximately 25 to 30 publications.[14] Likewise, bibliometric evaluations of propolis research have shown that Brazil, China, and Türkiye collectively dominate the literature, reflecting a more balanced international distribution of research output.[16] In the context of mad honey, the predominance of Türkiye can be attributed to the regional specificity of the phenomenon, whereas in other bee products or apitherapy-related topics, scientific leadership appears to be shared among different countries. A similar comparison can also be made at the institutional level: The most productive institutions in the mad honey literature are predominantly universities in Türkiye, particularly Karadeniz Technical University, which also ranks among the leading contributors to the broader international honey research landscape.[14] This finding indicates that research on mad honey is largely concentrated in local centers of expertise, while these centers simultaneously constitute integral components of the global honey research network. Beyond publication counts, this concentration carries strategic implications. Indeed, bibliometric network analysis reveals that, despite Türkiye’s quantitative and citation-based dominance, the field exhibits a relatively inward-looking collaboration structure, with a multiple country publication (MCP) rate of only 5.38%. Whereas the international co-authorship rate in the broader apitherapy literature is approximately 27%,[14] in the mad honey literature it remains around 9%. Japan (12.85% of publications; 0% MCP) is oriented almost entirely toward domestic research, whereas the United States (11.83%; 8.70% MCP) and China (11.31%; 13.64% MCP) display markedly higher international integration. This geographic and methodological divergence carries an important secondary implication: researchers in endemic regions such as Türkiye and Nepal predominantly generate clinical emergency medicine, case-series, and epidemiological data, whereas centers in the United States, Japan, and China specialize in advanced organic synthesis, D-ring modifications, ion-channel electrophysiology, and metabolomics. To bridge this persistent gap between clinical field data and advanced molecular research, integrating the clinical data accumulated in endemic regions with global analytical and pharmacological laboratories through formal consortia represents a strategic necessity.

Content clustering based on keyword analysis demonstrated that the mad honey literature is divided into clearly defined subthemes. The findings suggest that publications are predominantly clustered around themes such as intoxication cases and toxicological effects, the cardiovascular consequences of grayanotoxin, and the potential medical applications of mad honey. This structure is consistent with patterns observed in other topics examined using similar bibliometric approaches. For example, bibliometric mapping of bee venom research has demonstrated that studies cluster around 3 main themes: the components of bee venom, allergic and immunological responses, and clinical therapeutic applications.[15] Similarly, publications on honey and diabetes have been reported to show keyword concentrations around clinical outcome–oriented themes such as human studies, glycemic control, and wound healing.[19] Furthermore, the recent European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) scientific opinion introduced a protective threshold of 0.05 mg/kg for grayanotoxins I and III in honey. This regulatory development highlights a shift in the literature from simple case reporting toward food safety and toxicological risk assessment. The fact that Turkey ranks first in citation impact suggests that Turkish researchers are providing the essential clinical and toxicological data required for such international safety standards.[21] In line with these observations, mad honey studies similarly form internal subclusters that shape the overall structure of the literature. In particular, the toxicological and public health dimension and the pharmacological potential dimension emerge as 2 prominent focal areas in the keyword analysis. This indicates that, although mad honey research represents a relatively limited field, it has matured into distinct subdisciplines and exhibits a developmental trajectory consistent with findings from other bibliometric analyses.

5. Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its status as the first bibliometric synthesis dedicated specifically to mad honey, its coverage of more than 7 decades without language restriction, and its use of 2 complementary tools (Bibliometrix/Biblioshiny and VOSviewer) for methodological triangulation. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. This analysis includes only journals indexed in the WoS database. Publications written in local languages, indexed exclusively in other databases, or not included in international indexing systems (e.g., studies published only in national databases in Turkish or Japanese) may therefore have been excluded from the analysis. Finally, as the search relied on predefined keywords and automated bibliometric extraction, records indexed under atypical terminology may have been missed, and citation-based indicators inevitably favor older publications that have had more time to accumulate citations.

6. Conclusion

This study provides the first bibliometric analysis of the “mad honey” literature from 1954 to 2025, demonstrating a significant acceleration in scientific output, especially after 2010. Türkiye, due to its geographic context and the contributions of local universities, occupies a leading position globally in both publication volume and citation impact. While existing research has primarily addressed intoxication cases and toxicological effects such as bradycardia and hypotension, further exploration of the therapeutic properties of grayanotoxins is essential for advancing the field. Future research should prioritize product standardization, determination of safe therapeutic dose ranges, assessment of pharmacological applicability, and broader international collaboration to translate region-specific clinical expertise into globally applicable evidence.
