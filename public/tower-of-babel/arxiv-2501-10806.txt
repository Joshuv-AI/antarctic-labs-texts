Non-Expansive Mappings in Two-Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation: Finite-Time Analysis
Siddharth Chandak

Abstract
Two-time-scale stochastic approximation algorithms are iterative methods used in applications such as optimization, reinforcement learning, and control. Finite-time analysis of these algorithms has primarily focused on fixed point iterations where both time-scales have contractive mappings. In this work, we broaden the scope of such analyses by considering settings where the slower time-scale has a non-expansive mapping. For such algorithms, the slower time-scale can be viewed as a stochastic inexact Krasnoselskii-Mann iteration. We also study a variant where the faster time-scale has a projection step which leads to non-expansiveness in the slower time-scale. We show that the last-iterate mean square residual error for such algorithms decays at a rate 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k 1 / 4 − ϵ ) \mathcal{O}(1/k^{1/4-\epsilon}) , where ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 is arbitrarily small. We further establish almost sure convergence of iterates to the set of fixed points. We demonstrate the applicability of our framework by applying our results to minimax optimization, linear stochastic approximation, and Lagrangian optimization.

Introduction

Stochastic Approximation (SA) is a class of iterative algorithms to find the fixed point of an operator given its noisy realizations
[
29
]
. These algorithms have found applications in various areas, including optimization, reinforcement learning
[
32
]
, communications and stochastic control
[
2
]
. Two-time-scale stochastic approximation is a variant of SA, where the iteration is as follows:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(f(x_{k},y_{k})-x_{k}+M_{k+1})

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\beta_{k}(g(x_{k},y_{k})-y_{k}+M^{\prime}_{k+1}).

Here
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are the coupled iterates updated on two separate time-scales, decided by the step sizes
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
.
M_{k+1}
and
M^{\prime}_{k+1}
are noise sequences. The iterate
x_{k}
updates on the faster time-scale, i.e., the step size
\alpha_{k}
decays slowly. While analyzing the behavior of iterate
x_{k}
, the iterates
y_{k}
are considered quasi-static or slowly moving. The iterate
y_{k}
updates on the slower time-scale, i.e., the step size
\beta_{k}
decays faster. The behavior of iterate
y_{k}
is often studied in the limit where
x_{k}
closely tracks
x^{*}(y_{k})
, where
x^{*}(y)
is the fixed point for
f(\cdot,y)
. The step sizes have to be appropriately chosen for the algorithm to converge.

The majority of analysis for two-time-scale algorithms has occurred under the assumption that the function
f(x,y)
is contractive in
x
, and the function
g(x^{*}(y),y)
is contractive
[
8
,
16
,
22
]
. Under these assumptions, the algorithm converges to
x^{*},y^{*}
, the unique solution to the equations:
f(x,y)=x
and
g(x,y)=y

[
2
]
. In recent years, there have been works that obtain finite-time bounds for iterates, but they also mainly focus on contractive mappings in both time-scales
[
8
,
16
]
.

In this paper, we consider a non-expansive mapping in the slower iteration, i.e., the mapping
g(x^{*}(y),y)
is non-expansive. Such two-time-scale iterations arise in applications including minimax optimization
[
24
]
, Lagrangian optimization
[
28
]
, and generalized Nash equilibrium problems
[
5
]
. The slower time-scale in these iterations can be considered a stochastic inexact Krasnoselskii-Mann (KM) algorithm (an iterative algorithm to find the fixed points of a non-expansive operator). Analysis of these iterations requires novel techniques to deal with the errors due to two-time-scale updates in a stochastic setting with non-expansive maps.

Our main contributions are as follows.

1.

We present a finite-time bound for our algorithm, which has contractive and non-expansive mappings in the faster and slower time-scales, respectively. We show that the last-iterate mean square residual error decays at a rate
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/4+\epsilon})
, where
\epsilon>0
can be arbitrarily small depending on the step size.

2.

We show that the iterates converge to the set of fixed points almost surely.

3.

We provide a similar result for a projected variant, where the iterates
x_{k}
, updated in the faster time-scale, are projected to a convex set after each iteration. We show that there exist scenarios where the projection in the faster time-scale leads to the non-expansive mapping (Subsection
5.3
).

4.

We adapt strongly concave-convex minimax optimization, linear SA, and Lagrangian optimization into our framework.

5.

Using similar techniques, we present an additional result where the slower time-scale iteration contains a gradient descent operator for a smooth function. The gradient descent operator is not necessarily non-expansive in this case. In this setting, we show that the minimum, from time
0
to
k
, of the expected norm of the gradient is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\log(k)/k^{2/5})
.

Related Work

Applications of SA to reinforcement learning (RL) and optimization have caused a growing interest in obtaining results which bound their finite-time performance. These include mean square error or moment bounds (e.g.,
[
10
,
31
]
) and high probability or concentration bounds (e.g.,
[
6
,
11
]
).

Two-time-scale algorithms with contractive mappings (in both time-scales) play an important role in RL
[
33
]
, and hence finite time bounds for such algorithms have also been studied in detail. In this setting, the bound is obtained on
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}+\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}]
. A major focus in this field is on linear two-time-scale SA, i.e., where the maps
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
are linear. In
[
22
]
, an
asymptotic
convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
is obtained, while in
[
13
,
14
,
20
,
21
]
the result is extended to a finite-time bound. We also study linear two-time-scale SA as a special case. But we study the case where the matrix relevant for the slower time-scale is negative semidefinite instead of Hurwitz or negative definite as assumed in the above works.

Bounds for contractive non-linear two-time-scale SA have also been studied in recent years. A rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2/3})
was previously established for this setting in
[
16
]
. This rate has since been improved to
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
in
[
8
]
. Rates for two-time-scale SA with contractions under arbitrary norms and Markovian noise were obtained in
[
7
]
.

Two-time-scale SA has also been studied in settings where the mappings are not contractive. In
[
5
]
, the slower time-scale has a non-expansive mapping, but the result is specific to control of strongly monotone games. In
[
34
,
36
]
, the slower time-scale contains a gradient descent operator
\nabla J(y_{k})
for a smooth function
J(\cdot)
. The operator
\nabla J(\cdot)
is not co-coercive in general and is different from our framework. Under this setting, bounds on
\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}]
are obtained. We give an additional result (Theorem
4.16
), where we match their bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log(k)/k^{2/5})
. We work under assumptions different from
[
36
,
34
]
where they consider Markovian noise but require boundedness of some operators. We also consider the setting where
J(\cdot)
is convex, which fits into the framework of Theorem
2.6
, and obtain a bound on
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla J(y_{k})\|^{2}]
.

Our slower time-scale can be written as a stochastic inexact KM iteration. Finite-time bounds for inexact KM iterations have been obtained under a variety of settings
[
15
,
17
,
23
,
25
]
. In recent years, there have also been works which give bounds for stochastic KM iterations in Banach spaces
[
4
,
3
]
. Our analysis requires an appropriate combination of errors due to two-time-scale updates with the techniques used for stochastic inexact KM iterations.

2
Non-Expansive Mappings in Slower Time-Scale

The primary focus of this paper is on two-time-scale iterations where the faster and slower time-scales are fixed point iterations with contractive and non-expansive mappings, respectively. We present the main results of this paper in this section after setting up the necessary notation and assumptions. Consider the following coupled iterations.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(f(x_{k},y_{k})-x_{k}+M_{k+1})

(1)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\beta_{k}(g(x_{k},y_{k})-y_{k}+M^{\prime}_{k+1}).

Here
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
is the iterate updating on the faster time-scale, and
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is the iterate updating on the slower time-scale.
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are the respective step sizes that dictate the update speed and are formally defined in Assumption
2.5
.
M_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
M^{\prime}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are martingale difference noise sequences (Assumption
2.4
).

We begin by assuming that the function
f(x,y)
is contractive with respect to
x
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
is contractive in
x
for each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Specifically, there exists a constant
0\leq\mu<1
, independent of
y
, such that

\|f(x_{1},y)-f(x_{2},y)\|\leq\mu\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

By the Banach contraction mapping theorem, the above assumption implies that
f(\cdot,y)
has a unique fixed point for each
y
. That is, for each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, there exists unique
x^{*}(y)
such that
f(x^{*}(y),y)=x^{*}(y)
. The following is an assumption on the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

The function
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is non-expansive, i.e.,

\|g(x^{*}(y_{1}),y_{1})-g(x^{*}(y_{2}),y_{2})\|\leq\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Let us denote the set of fixed points of
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
by
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}^{*}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mid g(x^{*}(y),y)=y\}
. We assume that the set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is non-empty.

Before discussing other required assumptions, we present alternate but equivalent formulations of the above assumptions. Define operator
T_{1}(x,y)=x-f(x,y)
. Then Assumption
2.1
is equivalent to the operator
T_{1}(x,y)
being strongly monotone in
x
:

\langle T_{1}(x_{1},y)-T_{1}(x_{2},y),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\geq(1-\mu)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}.

Similarly, define operator
T_{2}(y)=y-g(x^{*}(y),y)
. Then the non-expansiveness in Assumption
2.2
is equivalent to the operator
T_{2}
being co-coercive, i.e.,

\langle T_{2}(y_{1})-T_{2}(y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|T_{2}(y_{1})-T_{2}(y_{2})\|^{2}.

For completeness, the complete equivalence, including the proofs for the above statements and the converse directions, is presented in Appendix
E
.

Next, we present two assumptions required for our analysis. These are standard assumptions associated with analysis of SA iterations
[
2
,
10
]
. The first assumption states that the functions
f
and
g
are Lipschitz.

Assumption 2.3
.

Functions
f(\cdot,\cdot)
and
g(\cdot,\cdot)
are
L
-Lipschitz, i.e.,

\|f(x_{1},y_{1})-f(x_{2},y_{2})\|+\|g(x_{1},y_{1})-g(x_{2},y_{2})\|\leq L(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|).

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, we also assume that

\|f(x,y)\|+\|g(x,y)\|\leq\mathfrak{c}_{1}(1+\|x\|+\|y\|).

The next assumption is that
M_{k+1}
and
M^{\prime}_{k+1}
are martingale difference sequences.

Assumption 2.4
.

Define the family of
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(x_{0},y_{0},M_{i},M^{\prime}_{i},i\leq k)
. Then
\{M_{k+1}\}
and
\{M^{\prime}_{k+1}\}
are martingale difference sequences with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[M^{\prime}_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}[\|M_{k+1}\|^{2}+\|M^{\prime}_{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}(1+\|x_{k}\|^{2}+\|y_{k}\|^{2}),\;\forall k\geq 0.

We finally make an assumption about the step size sequences, which is necessary to ensure the time-scale separation between the two updates.

Assumption 2.5
.

The step sizes
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are of the form

\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{(k+K_{1})^{\mathfrak{a}}}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\beta_{k}=\frac{\beta}{(k+K_{1})^{\mathfrak{b}}},

where
0.5<\mathfrak{a}<\mathfrak{b}<1
,
\alpha,\beta\leq 0.5
and
K_{1}\geq 1
. This ensures that
(\alpha_{k})
is non-summable
(\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}=\infty)
, square-summable
(\sum_{k}\alpha^{2}_{k}<\infty)
and non-increasing
(\alpha_{k+1}\leq\alpha_{k})
, and analogously for
(\beta_{k})
. This also ensures that
\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k}=0
. Finally, we make the key assumption that

\beta_{k}^{2}/\alpha_{k}^{3}\leq 1.

We restrict our attention to step sizes of this form for simplicity but our results can be extended to other step size sequences. We do not include
\Theta(1/k)
in our set of step sizes because it is not optimal for non-expansive fixed point iterations. The assumptions that the step sizes are non-summable, square summable, and satisfy
\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k}
goes to zero as
k\uparrow\infty
are standard in stochastic approximation literature
[
2
]
. The assumption of
\beta_{k}^{2}/\alpha_{k}^{3}\leq 1
is the assumption that decides the time-scale separation between the updates. The assumption is key for our analysis as it is the deciding factor in the bound on the mean square error. It is required to show that the iterates are bounded in expectation (Lemma
3.11
) which is a key step in the proof. Similar assumptions have also been taken in papers such as
[
16
]
.

Define
\gamma_{1}\coloneqq(1-\mu)/(2L^{2})
and
\gamma_{2}\coloneqq\max\left\{\left(2\mathfrak{a}/(\mu\alpha)\right)^{1/1-\mathfrak{a}},\left(2\mathfrak{b}/\beta\right)^{1/1-\mathfrak{b}}\right\}
. The following is our main result. An outline of its proof is given in Section
3
, and the complete proof has been presented in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.5
hold. Let
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
be generated by (
1
) with
\beta/\alpha\leq\gamma_{1}
and
K_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}
. Then,

•

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{(k+1)^{\mathfrak{a}}}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\mathbb{E}\left[\|g(x^{*}(y_{k}),y_{k})-y_{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}}{(k+1)^{1-\mathfrak{b}}}.

•

Moreover,
\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|
converges to zero, and the iterates
y_{k}
converge to the set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
with probability
1
.

Best Rate and Step size Choice

The best rate for the algorithm based on the above analysis is
1/(k+1)^{0.25-\epsilon}
where
\epsilon>0
can be arbitrarily small. This can be achieved by choosing
\mathfrak{a}=0.5+(2/3)\epsilon
and
\mathfrak{b}=0.75+\epsilon
. Unlike contractive maps, in the analysis of fixed point iterations with non-expansive mappings, a slowly decreasing step size gives a better rate. Hence, a slowly decreasing
\beta_{k}
, or equivalently a smaller
\mathfrak{b}
, gives a better rate. This, along with the assumptions that
\mathfrak{a}>0.5
and
\beta_{k}^{2}/\alpha_{k}^{3}\leq 1
(equivalently,
2\mathfrak{b}\geq 3\mathfrak{a}
), dictates the optimal rate.

We also point out that
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]
and

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|g(x^{*}(y_{k}),y_{k})-y_{k}\|^{2}\right]
scale as

\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{k})
and
\mathcal{O}(1/(k\beta_{k}))
, respectively.
For the given stepsize sequences
\{\alpha_{k}\}
and
\{\beta_{k}\}
, these rates are the best known upper bounds for stochastic approximation with contractive and non-expansive operators, respectively, even in the single-time-scale setting
[
10
,
4
]
.
Consequently, when optimizing over all stepsize sequences admissible under Assumption
2.5
, the overall rate is fundamentally constrained by the stepsize requirements, and could potentially be improved only if these constraints were relaxed.

The assumptions on
\beta/\alpha
and
K_{1}
are required only for the bounds to hold for all
k\geq 0
. Instead, if we have that
\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k}\leq\gamma_{1}
for all
k\geq K_{0}
, then the bounds will hold for all
k\geq\max\{K_{0},\gamma_{2}\}
. The existence of such
K_{0}
is guaranteed by Assumption
2.5
.

2.1
Projected Variant

In this subsection, we consider a variant where the iterate in the faster time-scale is projected to a convex and bounded set at each step.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\big(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(f(x_{k},y_{k})-x_{k}+M_{k+1})\big)

(2)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\beta_{k}(g(x_{k},y_{k})-y_{k}+M^{\prime}_{k+1}).

Here
\mathcal{X}
is a convex and bounded set, and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the projection onto set
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)=\arg\min_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
.

We still assume that the function
f(\cdot,y)
is contractive for all
y
. But the relevant fixed point is now the fixed point for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}(f(\cdot,y))
, i.e., we now define
\hat{x}(y)
as the unique point that satisfies
\hat{x}(y)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}(f(\hat{x}(y),y))
. In this case, the function
g(\hat{x}(\cdot),\cdot)
is assumed to be non-expansive.

Assumption 2.7
.

The function
g(\hat{x}(\cdot),\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is non-expansive, i.e.,

\|g(\hat{x}(y_{1}),y_{1})-g(\hat{x}(y_{2}),y_{2})\|\leq\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Let us denote the set of fixed points of
g(\hat{x}(\cdot),\cdot)
by
\mathcal{\hat{Y}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{\hat{Y}}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mid g(\hat{x}(y),y)=y\}
. We assume that the set
\mathcal{\hat{Y}}
is non-empty.

This projected variant is studied as a special case because there exist scenarios where the projection of the faster time-scale updates is why the slower time-scale iteration has a non-expansive mapping (instead of a contractive mapping). Formally, there arise scenarios where the map
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
is contractive, but the map
g(\hat{x}(\cdot),\cdot)
is not guaranteed to be contractive, and is only non-expansive. We study one such scenario in Subsection
5.3
.

The main theorem for this projected variant is similar to Theorem
2.6
, and its proof is presented in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.7
and
2.3
-
2.5
hold. Let
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
be generated by (
2
) with
\beta/\alpha\leq\gamma_{1}
and
K_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}
. Then,

•

There exist constants
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}>0
such that for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-\hat{x}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{1}^{\prime}}{(k+1)^{\mathfrak{a}}},\;\;\text{and}\;\;\mathbb{E}\left[\|g(\hat{x}(y_{k}),y_{k})-y_{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}^{\prime}}{(k+1)^{1-\mathfrak{b}}}.

•

Moreover,
\|x_{k}-\hat{x}(y_{k})\|
converges to zero, and the iterates
y_{k}
converge to the set
\mathcal{\hat{Y}}
with probability
1
.

Proof Outline

In this section, we give a rough sketch for the proof for Theorem
2.6
. Appendix
A
presents the complete proof. The proof takes inspiration from analysis of two-time-scale iterations with contractive maps
[
16
]
and analysis of inexact Krasnoselskii-Mann (KM) iterations
[
15
,
23
]
, but requires novel analysis to deal with the additional errors of two-time-scale updates in a stochastic setting with non-expansive maps. For simplicity, we define
h(y)=g(x^{*}(y),y)
.

The first step in the proof is to show that
x^{*}(y)
is Lipschitz in
y
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
hold. Then
x^{*}(y)
, the unique fixed point of
f(\cdot,y)
, satisfies

\|x^{*}(y_{1})-x^{*}(y_{2})\|\leq L_{0}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Here
L_{0}\coloneqq L/(1-\mu)
is the Lipschitz parameter for the mapping
x^{*}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
.

This lemma ensures that the target points for the faster iteration
x^{*}(y_{k})
move slowly as the iteration
\{y_{k}\}
moves slowly. Apart from the noise sequence
M_{k+1}
, (
x^{*}(y_{k+1})-x^{*}(y_{k})
) acts as another ‘error term’ for the faster iteration. The Lipschitz nature of
x^{*}(\cdot)
, along with the slow changes in
y_{k}
, guarantees that this error term is small.

The next step of the proof is to obtain an intermediate bound for
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}]
. Here
y^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is some fixed point for the mapping
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
2.6
holds. Then for
y^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
, iterates
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
satisfy the following bounds.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}(y_{k+1})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mu^{\prime}\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;+\Gamma_{1}\alpha_{k}^{2}\left(1+\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}+\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|y_{k+1}-y^{*}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}+\mu^{\prime}\alpha_{k}\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;+\Gamma_{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}\left(1+\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}+\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;-\beta_{k}(1-\beta_{k})\|y_{k}-g(x_{k},y_{k})\|^{2}.

Here
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
are some constants and
\mu^{\prime}=1-\mu
.

When deriving the bound for the faster time-scale, we encounter an error term which has a dependence of
\beta_{k}^{2}/\alpha_{k}
on
k
. The assumption that
\beta_{k}^{2}\leq\alpha_{k}^{3}
helps us bound this term by
\alpha_{k}^{2}
. Since we have the assumption that
\alpha_{k}
is square-summable, we get that this error term is summable. Hence the assumptions
\beta_{k}^{2}\leq\alpha_{k}^{3}
and square-summability of
\alpha_{k}
are key to show that the iterates are bounded in expectation.

Lemma 3.11
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
2.6
holds. Then,

a)

The iterates are almost surely bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{k}(\|x_{k}\|+\|y_{k}\|)<\infty
a.s.

b)

The iterates
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
are bounded in expectation. Specifically, there exists
\Gamma_{3},\kappa_{1}>0
such that for all
k\geq 0
, and for
y^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}+\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}\right]\leq\Gamma_{3},

where
\Gamma_{3}\coloneqq\kappa_{1}\left(\kappa_{1}+\|x_{0}-x^{*}(y_{0})\|^{2}+\|y_{0}-y^{*}\|^{2}\right).

The above lemma is central to proving that the iterates converge to the set of fixed points w.p. 1. For this, we use the ODE approach for the analysis of SA algorithms. A key requirement for the approach is the almost sure boundedness of iterates, which is established in the above lemma using the Robbins-Siegmund Theorem
[
30
]
. The bound for
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}]
is also direct from the above lemma. We get the following recursive relation using Lemmas
3.10
and
3.11
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}(y_{k+1})\|^{2}\right]\leq(1-\mu^{\prime}\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]+\Gamma_{1}(1+\Gamma_{3})\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Solving this recursive relation gives us the bound that
\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{k})
. On analyzing the other bound, we get that the summation of
\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|y_{i}-h(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]
over all
i
is bounded by a constant. Recall that
h(y)=g(x^{*}(y),y)
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
2.6
holds. Then,

a)

\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|
converges to zero, and
y_{k}
converges to the set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
w.p. 1.

b)

There exist constants
C_{1},\Gamma_{4}>0
such that, for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{(k+1)^{\mathfrak{a}}}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(y_{i})-y_{i}\|^{2}\right]\leq\Gamma_{4},

where
C_{1}=\kappa_{2}\left(1+\|x_{0}-x^{*}(y_{0})\|^{2}+\|y_{0}-y^{*}\|^{2}\right)
for some
\kappa_{2}>0
.

The above lemma would be sufficient if we wanted to get a bound on the term
\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(y_{i})-y_{i}\|^{2}\right]
. But we wish to get a bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(y_{k})-y_{k}\|^{2}\right]
.

For this, we would like to get a bound on
\mathbb{E}[\|h(y_{i})-y_{i}\|^{2}]
in terms of
\mathbb{E}[\|h(y_{k})-y_{k}\|^{2}]
. But the martingale noise
M^{\prime}_{k+1}
does not allow us to obtain a useful bound. To solve this, we define an averaged error sequence
U_{k+1}=(1-\beta_{k})U_{k}+\beta_{k}M^{\prime}_{k+1}
, with
U_{0}=0
, and a modified iteration of
z_{k}=y_{k}-U_{k}
. We first show that
\mathbb{E}[\|y_{k}-z_{k}\|^{2}]
decays at a sufficiently fast rate, implying that we can analyze
\mathbb{E}[h(z_{k})-z_{k}\|^{2}]
instead of
\mathbb{E}[\|h(y_{k})-y_{k}\|^{2}].
Next, we study the iteration for
z_{k}
, showing that the error sequence for
z_{k}
satisfies a similar boundedness condition for the summation as in Lemma
3.12
b). Finally, we fulfill the purpose of studying
z_{k}
instead of
y_{k}
, by obtaining a bound on
\mathbb{E}[\|h(z_{i})-z_{i}\|^{2}]
in terms of
\mathbb{E}[\|h(z_{k})-z_{k}\|^{2}]
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem 2.6 holds. Then, for all
k\geq 0
,

a)

\mathbb{E}[\|y_{k}-z_{k}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|U_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\Gamma_{5}\beta_{k}
, for some
\Gamma_{5}>0
.

b)

\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(z_{i})-z_{i}\|^{2}\right]\leq\Gamma_{6}
, for some
\Gamma_{6}>0
.

c)

For all
i\leq k-1
, there exists
\Gamma_{7}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(z_{i})-z_{i}\|^{2}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(z_{k})-z_{k}\|^{2}\right]-\sum_{j=i}^{k-1}\Gamma_{7}\beta_{j}\alpha_{j}.

Lemma
3.13
implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|h(z_{k})-z_{k}\|^{2}\right]\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\leq\Gamma_{6}+\Gamma_{7}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\sum_{j=i}^{k-1}\beta_{j}\alpha_{j}.

We finally show that the summation on the right-hand side above is bounded. This requires the assumptions of square-summability of
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}^{2}\leq\alpha_{k}^{3}
and completes the proof for Theorem
2.6
.

4
Gradient Descent Operators in Slower Time-Scale

An important example of the iteration in (
1
) is one where the slower time-scale (iteration for
y_{k}
) is a gradient descent iteration in the limit
x_{k}\rightarrow x^{*}(y_{k})
. The following assumption formally states this.

Assumption 4.14
.

We assume that there exists differentiable function
J:\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
g(x^{*}(y),y)-y=-\nabla J(y)
. We further assume that
J(\cdot)
is bounded from below, i.e., there exists
J_{min}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
J(y)\geq J_{min}
for all
y
.

Depending on the assumptions on function
J(\cdot)
, different types of convergence rates can be obtained. The function
J(\cdot)
is smooth in all cases (i.e.,
\nabla J(\cdot)
is Lipschitz).

J(\cdot)
is strongly convex

In this case, the function
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
can be shown to be contractive, and hence we have a two-time-scale iteration where both time-scales have contractive mappings. In this case, the result from
[
8
]
can directly be applied, to obtain a bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
on
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}+\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|^{2}]
. Here
y^{*}
is the unique minimizer for
J(\cdot)
.

J(\cdot)
is convex

In this case, the function
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
is non-expansive. This gives us a two-time-scale iteration, which satisfies the assumptions for Theorem
2.6
. We can apply our result to get a bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{0.25-\epsilon})
on
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}+\|\nabla J(y_{k})\|^{2}]
. This case is discussed in more detail in Subsection
5.1
, when we discuss minimax optimization.

J(\cdot)
is non-convex

In this case, the function
g(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
is not necessarily non-expansive, and hence Theorem
2.6
can not be applied. But under Lipschitz assumptions on function
g(\cdot,\cdot)
, the function
J(\cdot)
is smooth, and hence bounds can be obtained in this case using a proof technique similar to the proof for Theorem
2.6
.
Consider the following assumption.

Assumption 4.15
.

We assume that the following statements hold.

a)

Function
f(\cdot,y)
is contractive with parameter
\mu
(Assumption
2.1
).

b)

Function
f
and
g
are Lipschitz with parameter
L
(Assumption
2.3
).

c)

M_{k+1}
and
M^{\prime}_{k+1}
are martingale difference sequences with bounded variances, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\|M_{k+1}\|^{2}+\|M^{\prime}_{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}
.

d)

The stepsize sequences satisfy
\mathfrak{a}<\mathfrak{b}<1
and
0.5<\mathfrak{b}
. We also have the key assumption that
\beta_{k}^{2}\leq\alpha_{k}^{3}
and
\beta_{k}\alpha_{k}\leq\beta\alpha/(k+K_{1})
.

The key difference in assumptions is that we
no longer require
\alpha_{k}
to be square summable
, allowing sequences such as
\alpha_{k}=\mathcal{O}(1/k^{0.4})
and
\beta_{k}=\mathcal{O}(1/k^{0.6})
. Then, we have the following theorem, with its proof presented in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 4.16
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.14
and
4.15
hold.
Then there exist constants
C_{3},C_{4}
such that if
\alpha\leq C_{4}
, then for all
k\geq 0
,

\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{3}(1+\log(k+1))}{(1+k)^{1-\mathfrak{b}}}.

The optimal rate in this case is obtained at
\mathfrak{a}=0.4
and
\mathfrak{b}=0.6
, with a bound of
\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(\log(k)/k^{0.4})
. In this case, we do not have a bound on the gradient at time
k
, but only have a bound on the minimum gradient attained before time
k
. Such non-convex two-time-scale algorithms arise in non-convex minimax optimization and reinforcement learning (actor-critic algorithms). The same upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log(k)/k^{0.4})
was obtained in
[
36
,
34
]
under different assumptions on the noise sequence, and remains the best-known upper bound for this setting.

5
Applications

In this section, we discuss three applications of the framework and results discussed in Section
2
.

5.1
Minimax Optimization

Consider the following minimax optimization problem:

\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}H(x,y),

where
H:\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the objective function. This formulation of finding saddle point solutions to minimax optimization problems arises in several areas, including two-player zero-sum games, generative adversarial networks, and robust statistics
[
24
]
. We consider the Two-Time-scale Stochastic Gradient Descent Ascent (TTSGDA) algorithm
[
24
]
for finding saddle points.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(\nabla_{x}H(x_{k},y_{k})+M_{k+1})\right)

(3)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\beta_{k}(-\nabla_{y}H(x_{k},y_{k})+M^{\prime}_{k+1}).

Here
M_{k+1}
and
M^{\prime}_{k+1}
denote the noise arising from taking gradient samples at the current iterate. These noise sequences are assumed to be unbiased and have bounded variance, i.e., they satisfy Assumption
2.4
.

We work under the setting of smooth strongly concave-convex minimax optimization. This is formally stated in the following assumption.

Assumption 5.17
.

Function
H(x,y)
is
L-
smooth,
\rho-
strongly concave in
x
and convex in
y
. The set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
is a convex set with a bounded diameter.

This assumption is sufficient to ensure that the faster time-scale (update of iterate
x_{k}
) has a contractive mapping and the slower time-scale (update of iterate
y_{k}
) has a non-expansive mapping. Define
\Phi(y)=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}H(x,y)
. Then we have the following result, with its proof presented in Appendix
B
.

Corollary 5.18
.

Suppose Assumption
5.17
holds for iterates
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
defined in (
3
), and let
\alpha_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/(k+1)^{0.5+0.66\epsilon}\right)
and
\beta_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/(k+1)^{0.75+\epsilon}\right)
. Then the iterates
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
converge to the set of saddle points of the function
H(x,y)
a.s., and
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\Phi(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(1/k^{1/4-\epsilon}).
Here
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small.

We note that stronger guarantees can be obtained for strongly convex-concave minimax optimization by using techniques such as extra-gradient, proximal best response, or just by considering averaged iterates
[
9
,
35
]
, but to the best of our knowledge, our result is the first last-iterate bound for
vanilla
TTSGDA.

5.2
Linear Stochastic Approximation

Consider the problem of finding solutions for the following set of linear equations:

A_{11}x+A_{12}y=b_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{21}x+A_{22}y=b_{2}.

(4)

At each time
k
, we obtain unbiased estimates
\tilde{A}_{ij}^{(k+1)}
and
\tilde{b}_{i}^{(k+1)}
of each
A_{ij}
and
b_{i}
,
i,j\in\{1,2\}
. This problem is solved using a linear two-time-scale algorithm.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(\tilde{b}_{1}^{(k+1)}-\tilde{A}_{11}^{(k+1)}x_{k}-\tilde{A}_{12}^{(k+1)}y_{k}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\beta_{k}\left(\tilde{b}_{2}^{(k+1)}-\tilde{A}_{21}^{(k+1)}x_{k}-\tilde{A}_{22}^{(k+1)}y_{k}\right).

This algorithm finds use in reinforcement learning
[
20
]
and linear SA with Polyak averaging
[
22
]
. Define matrix
\Delta=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}
. Under the assumption that matrices
-A_{11}
and
-\Delta
are Hurwitz, i.e., the real part of all their eigenvalues is negative, iteration (
5
) can be written as two-time-scale SA with both time-scales as fixed point iterations with contractive mappings.

In this paper, we consider the case where the matrix
-A_{11}
is negative definite and the matrix
-\Delta
is negative semidefinite. Formally, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 5.19
.

Iteration (
5
) satisfies the following.

a)

The matrix
-A_{11}
is negative definite and the matrix
-\Delta
is (non-zero) negative semidefinite.

b)

There exists a solution for the system of equations in (
4
).

c)

For
i,j\in\{1,2\}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{A}_{ij}^{(k+1)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=A_{ij},\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{b}_{i}^{(k+1)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=b_{i}.
Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{A}_{ij}^{(k+1)}-A_{ij}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq\mathfrak{c}_{3},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{b}_{i}^{(k+1)}-b_{i}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq\mathfrak{c}_{3}.

Part a) of this assumption ensures that iteration (
5
) satisfies Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. As Hurwitz matrices also correspond to contractive fixed point iterations
[
22
]
, we can also work with Hurwitz
-A_{11}
(instead of negative definite). Here is the result for this setting, with its proof presented in Appendix
B
.

Corollary 5.20
.

Suppose Assumption
5.19
holds for iterates
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
defined in (
5
), and let
\alpha_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/(k+1)^{0.5+0.66\epsilon}\right)
and
\beta_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/(k+1)^{0.75+\epsilon}\right)
. Then the iterates
\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
converge to the set of solutions of system of equations (
4
) a.s., and

\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{0.5}}\right)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{21}x_{k}+A_{22}y_{k}-b_{2}\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{0.25-\epsilon}}\right).

Here
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small, and
x^{*}(y)=A_{11}^{-1}(b_{1}-A_{12}y)
.

We note that the linear non-expansive map can also be treated as a linear contractive map under the seminorm
\|x\|_{\Delta}\coloneqq\sqrt{x^{\top}\Delta x}
, which vanishes on
\ker(\Delta)
. We believe that stronger guarantees can be obtained for iteration (
5
) using existing analyses for seminorm-contractive stochastic approximation
[
12
]
, although such analyses are currently available only for the single-time-scale setting and would need to be extended to the two-time-scale case. We include this application to show the broad applicability of our framework.

5.3
Constrained Optimization with Lagrangian Multipliers

Consider the following constrained optimization problem for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
:

\displaystyle\text{maximize}\;\;H_{0}(x)

subject to:

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;H_{i}(x)\leq 0,i=1,\ldots,m

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;Ax=b_{0}.

(6)

Here the constraints
H_{i}(x)\leq 0
together represent the set
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}=\{x\mid H_{i}(x)\leq 0,i=1,\ldots,m\}.
The equation
Ax=b_{0}
for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
and
b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
represents the additional linear constraint. This problem can be solved using Lagrange multipliers, specifically, a two-time-scale Lagrangian optimization method
[
28
]
.

Consider the following iteration.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\big(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(\nabla H_{0}(x_{k})-A^{T}\lambda_{k}+M_{k+1})\big)

(7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=\lambda_{k}+\beta_{k}(Ax_{k}-b_{0}).

Here
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
denotes the Lagrange multiplier.
M_{k+1}
denotes the noise arising from taking gradient samples at the current value of
x_{k}
, and satisfies Assumption
2.4
.

This problem can be viewed as a special case of strongly convex–concave minimax optimization. We treat it separately due to the interesting role played by projections (Lemma
5.22
), as well as its practical applications. The algorithm is particularly useful in distributed settings, where each node or agent updates some part of the variable
x
under some local convex constraints, and there is a global linear constraint. In that case, the local updates only require knowledge of the gradient, the Lagrange multiplier, and their local constraints. Another similar setting in which a similar algorithm can be used is generalized Nash equilibrium problems (or Nash equilibrium under constraints)
[
18
]
and game control for strongly monotone games
[
5
]
. In this setting, each player takes action in a convex set and updates their action using gradient ascent. The game controller has a global linear objective which they want the player’s actions to satisfy
[
5
]
.

We make the following assumption. The second part of the assumption is required for the existence of
\lambda
which satisfies
A\hat{x}(\lambda)=b_{0}
.

Assumption 5.21
.

Iteration (
7
) satisfies the following statements.

a)

Function
H_{0}(x)
is
\rho-
strongly concave and
L
-smooth.

b)

Functions
H_{i}(x)
are continuously differentiable and convex. Moreover, there exists
x\in\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
(i.e.,
H_{i}(x)<0,i=1,\ldots,m
) such that
Ax=b_{0}
.

This problem is an example of a setting where the projection in the faster time-scale gives rise to the non-expansiveness in the slower time-scale. Recall the definitions of
x^{*}(y)
and
\hat{x}(y)
. In this case,
x^{*}(\lambda)
is the unique solution to
\nabla H_{0}(x)-A^{T}\lambda=0
and
\hat{x}(\lambda)
is the unique solution to
x=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}(x+\nabla H_{0}(x_{k})-A^{T}\lambda)
. Then we have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.22
.

Under Assumption
5.21
and the assumption that the matrix
A
has full row rank:

a)

-Ax^{*}(\lambda)
is strongly monotone.

b)

-A\hat{x}(\lambda)
is co-coercive.

The above lemma implies that in the absence of the projection onto set
\mathcal{X}
, iteration (
7
) would have contractive mappings in both time-scales. On the other hand, the slower time-scale has a non-expansive mapping in the presence of the projection. The following is our main result for this setting.

Corollary 5.23
.

Suppose Assumption
5.21
holds for iterates
\{x_{k},\lambda_{k}\}
defined in (
7
), and let
\alpha_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/(k+1)^{0.5+0.66\epsilon}\right)
and
\beta_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/(k+1)^{0.75+\epsilon}\right)
. Then
x_{k}
converges to
\hat{x}(\lambda_{k})
and
Ax_{k}
converges to
b_{0}
a.s., and

\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-\hat{x}(y_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{0.5}}\right)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\mathbb{E}\left[\|Ax_{k}-b_{0}\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{0.25-\epsilon}}\right).

Here
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we simulate the three applications from the previous section. Each plot represents the average over 200 independent runs of the algorithm. The shaded region indicates one standard deviation around the mean. The stepsize sequences are of the form
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+100)^{\mathfrak{a}}
and
\beta_{k}=\beta/(k+100)^{\mathfrak{b}}
.

In Figure
1
, we simulate the linear stochastic approximation setting. We generate a random positive definite matrix
A_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{20\times 20}
and generate a positive semi-definite matrix
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{20\times 20}
of rank
5
. We set
A_{12}=A_{21}=I
and
A_{22}=A_{11}^{-1}+\Delta
. The vectors
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{20}
are obtained by randomly generating
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{20}
and computing
b_{1}=A_{11}x^{\prime}+A_{12}y^{\prime}
and
b_{2}=A_{21}x^{\prime}+A_{22}y^{\prime}
. At each timestep, i.i.d. normal noise is added to the vectors and the matrices, and the noisy residual error is used for updating the iterates
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
.

In Figure
1(a)
, we study the effect of the exponent in the stepsize (parameters
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
) on the residual error. We see that a slowly decreasing stepsize has a better rate of convergence. This matches our intuition and the bound obtained in Theorem
2.6
. In Figure

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied two-time-scale iterations where the slower time-scale has a non-expansive mapping. Our main contribution is the finite-time bound for this setting. We showed that the mean square residual error decays at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/4+\epsilon})
, where
\epsilon>0
is arbitrarily small depending on the step size. Our analysis takes inspiration from techniques in contractive SA and inexact KM iterations. Extensions of our work include studying the same algorithm under Markovian noise and extending the result to Banach spaces. Another extension could be to study non-expansive mappings in both time-scales, but the analysis would require further assumptions to ensure that the faster time-scale has a unique fixed point for each
y
.

i\geq 0
.

\displaystyle\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}[J(y_{i})]-4\mathbb{E}[J(y_{i+1})]+4(D_{7}D_{8}+1.5L_{J}\mathfrak{c}_{2})\beta_{i}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle\;\;+4D_{8}(\beta_{i}/\alpha_{i})\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i}-x^{*}(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i+1}-x^{*}(y_{i+1})\|^{2}\right]\right).

Summing over
i=0
to
k
gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4J(y_{0})-4\mathbb{E}[J(y_{k+1})]+4(D_{7}D_{8}+1.5L_{J}\mathfrak{c}_{2})\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle\;\;+4D_{8}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i}-x^{*}(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i+1}-x^{*}(y_{i+1})\|^{2}\right]\right).

(30)

We first need to bound the summation on the right-hand side to complete our bound.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i}-x^{*}(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i+1}-x^{*}(y_{i+1})\|^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq(\beta/\alpha)\|x_{0}^{2}-x^{*}(y_{0})\|^{2}-(\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}(y_{k+1})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\;\;-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{i}-x^{*}(y_{i})\|^{2}]\left(\frac{\beta_{i-1}}{\alpha_{i-1}}-\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)\leq(\beta/\alpha)\|x_{0}^{2}-x^{*}(y_{0})\|^{2}.

Here, the final inequality follows from the fact that
\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k}
is a decreasing sequence. Returning to (
30
), we have the assumption that the sequence
\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}
is
\mathcal{O}(1/(k+1))
. We also have the assumption that the function
J(\cdot)
is lower bounded. Hence the right-hand side of (
30
) is bounded by
D_{9}(1+\log(k+1))
for some constant
D_{9}>0
. Hence,

\displaystyle D_{9}(1+\log(k+1))\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]\right)k\beta_{k}.

This completes our proof with

\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla J(y_{i})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{3}(1+\log(k+1))}{(k+1)^{1-\mathfrak{b}}},

where
C_{3}=D_{9}/\beta
. The required
C_{4}
in the assumption
\alpha\leq C_{4}
for the result to hold is
C_{4}=\min\{1,(1-\mu)^{4}/(2+24L^{4}),(1-\mu)^{5}/(18(L^{6}+L^{5}+L^{4}+L^{2}+L))\}
.

Appendix E
Equivalence Between Formulations

We first show how a formulation using contractive and non-expansive maps implies the formulation using strongly monotone and co-coercive operators.

Lemma E.25
.

Consider function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

1.

If
h(\cdot)
is
\mu
-contractive for
\mu\in[0,1)
, then function
q(x)=x-h(x)
is
(1-\mu)
-strongly monotone and
(1+\mu)
-Lipschitz.

2.

If
h(\cdot)
is non-expansive, then function
q(x)=x-h(x)
is
0.5
-co-coercive.

Proof.

a).
For
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}-x_{2},q(x_{1})-q(x_{2})\rangle=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}-\langle x_{1}-x_{2},h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\rangle.

Now,
\langle x_{1}-x_{2},h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\rangle\leq\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\|\leq\mu\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}.

This implies that
\langle x_{1}-x_{2},q(x_{1})-q(x_{2})\rangle\geq(1-\mu)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}
. The function
q(x)
is trivially Lipschitz with constant
(1+\mu)
.

b).
For
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
s=x-y
and
t=h(x)-h(y)
. Then,
q(x)-q(y)=s-t
. We first note that
2\langle s-t,s\rangle=\|s-t\|^{2}+\|s\|^{2}-\|t\|^{2}
. Since
h(\cdot)
is non-expansive,
\|t\|\leq\|s\|
. This implies that
2\langle s-t,s\rangle\geq\|s-t\|^{2}
, and hence

\langle q(x)-q(y),x-y\rangle\geq 0.5\|q(x)-q(y)\|^{2}.

This completes the proof for Lemma
E.25
.

We now show the converse directions.

Lemma E.26
.

Consider function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose
\mathfrak{c},\ell>0
. Then,

a).

If
h(\cdot)
is
\mathfrak{c}
-strongly monotone and
\ell
-Lipschitz, then function
q(x)=x-(\mathfrak{c}/\ell^{2})h(x)
is
\sqrt{1-c^{2}/\ell^{2}}
-contractive.

b).

If
h(\cdot)
is
\mathfrak{c}
-co-coercive, then function
q(x)=x-2\mathfrak{c}h(x)
is non-expansive.

Proof.

a).
For
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|q(x_{1})-q(x_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}{\ell^{4}}\|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\|^{2}-2\frac{\mathfrak{c}}{\ell^{2}}\langle h(x_{1})-h(x_{2}),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}{\ell^{4}}\ell^{2}-2\frac{\mathfrak{c}}{\ell^{2}}\mathfrak{c}\right)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}=(1-\mathfrak{c}^{2}/\ell^{2})\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}.

The inequality follows from the strongly monotone and Lipschitz nature of
h(\cdot)
.

b).
For
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|q(x_{1})-q(x_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+4\mathfrak{c}^{2}\|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\|^{2}-4\mathfrak{c}\langle h(x_{1})-h(x_{2}),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+(4\mathfrak{c}^{2}-4\mathfrak{c}^{2})\|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\|^{2}=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}.

The inequality follows from the co-coercive nature of
h(\cdot)
.
