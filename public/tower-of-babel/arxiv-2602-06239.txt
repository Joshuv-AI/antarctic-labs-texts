Provably avoiding over-optimization in Direct Preference Optimization without knowing the data distribution
Adam Barla, Emanuele Nevali, Luca Viano, Volkan Cevher

Abstract
We introduce PEPO ( Pessimistic Ensemble based Preference Optimization ), a single-step Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)-like algorithm to mitigate the well-known over-optimization issue in preference learning without requiring the knowledge of the data-generating distribution π data \pi_{\mathrm{data}} or learning an explicit reward model. PEPO achieves pessimism via an ensemble of preference-optimized policies trained on disjoint data subsets and then aggregates them through a worst case construction that favors the agreement across models. In the tabular setting, PEPO achieves sample complexity guarantees depending only on a single-policy concentrability coefficient, thus avoiding the all-policy concentrability which affects the guarantees of algorithms prone to over-optimization, such as DPO. The theoretical findings are corroborated by a convincing practical performance, while retaining the simplicity and the practicality of DPO-style training.

Introduction

Learning from binary preferences is a fundamental technique for the post-training of large language models
(
10
;
44
)
. Among existing approaches,
Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO)
(
48
)
has gained widespread adoption due to its strong empirical performance and simplicity of implementation: indeed, DPO elegantly bypasses the intermediate step of learning a reward function from the preference dataset.

Despite these advantages, DPO is known to suffer from
over-optimization
: as training progresses, improvements in optimizing the training loss do not necessarily translate into better generation quality, and may even degrade performance relative to the initial model
(
18
;
47
;
64
)
.
Moreover, early stopping using the validation loss as a criterion is not reliable because it is only a loose proxy for what really matters, i.e. the quality of the responses that the model can generate in practice.

Several new DPO variants
mitigate over-optimization via
pessimistic regularization
. Notably, approaches such as
\chi^{2}
-regularized preference optimization (
\chi^{2}
PO)
(
23
)
and DPO augmented with supervised fine-tuning (DPO+SFT)
(
32
)
achieve guarantees that depend only on a
single-policy
concentrability coefficient, which is the theoretical certificate of robustness to over-optimization
(
23
)
. Indeed, these guarantees imply that the learned policy is competitive with any policy sufficiently covered by the data. In stark contrast, non-pessimistic approaches suffer from an
all-policy
concentrability coefficient, meaning that the output policy can be significantly suboptimal even compared to well-covered policies unless the dataset covers the whole policy class.

A notable merit of the guarantees in
(
23
;
32
)
is that they apply to general function classes, even to non-convex ones in the case of
\chi^{2}
PO. However, these guarantees rely on the assumption that we have access to the data-generating distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
, or to a reference/base policy that is well-covered by
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
. In practice, this assumption is often violated, for instance, when smaller models are post-trained using preference data generated by larger, proprietary systems whose logits can not be accessed when fine-tuning the smaller models. A common example is the UltraFeedback dataset
(
12
)
in which the responses are generated via a pool of LLMs, including proprietary ones such as GPT-4.

This gap motivates the following research question:

Open Question:
Can we design a DPO-like algorithm (i.e., which avoids learning an explicit reward) that is provably robust to over-optimization without knowledge of data-generating distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
?

Our work answers this question affirmatively by introducing
PEPO
(
Pessimistic Ensemble based Preference Optimization
). The central idea behind
PEPO
is to resort to
pessimism via ensembles
: a technique as simple as unexplored in the context of LLM alignment, especially in the DPO setting.

Concretely,
PEPO
trains an ensemble of lightweight DPO-style policies on disjoint subsets of the preference dataset, implemented via low-rank adapters
(
22
)
. To enable our guarantees, we train each model minimizing a pessimistic variant of the DPO loss derived from a tie-allowing variant of the Bradley-Terry (BT) model related to the classical preference models of
(
49
;
14
)
. Finally, at generation time, we use a certain notion of ensemble agreement to generate responses either via rejection sampling or approximating the (otherwise intractable) response-level normalization constant to the token-level counterpart.

Theoretical contribution

Our main theoretical result shows that, in the tabular setting,
PEPO
achieves sample complexity guarantees depending only on a single-policy concentrability coefficient, despite making no assumptions about
\pi_{\text{data}}
and not learning a reward model. This contrasts with standard DPO
(
48
)
, which inherently incurs in an all-policy concentrability dependence, with existing pessimistic DPO variants that require access to the data-generating distribution
(
23
;
32
)
and with purely theoretical works which are not DPO-like because they require a reward modeling step
(
69
;
68
;
51
;
30
)
. Moreover, we characterize the optimal
PEPO
ensemble size: it is the smallest value that is sufficiently high to guarantee pessimism.

PEPO
consistently improves over DPO in post-training the following 7/8B parameters models: Zephyr-7B

2
Preliminaries

In
learning from preferences
, we consider a prompt/state space
\mathcal{X}
and a response/action space
{\mathcal{A}}
, the learning algorithm receives as input a starting model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}
which is usually a model that exits a
Supervised Fine-Tuning
(SFT) routine, and a preference dataset of the form
\mathcal{D}=\left\{{X_{n},A^{+}_{n},A^{-}_{n}}\right\}^{N}_{n=1}
where
X_{n}\in\mathcal{X},A^{+}_{n},A^{-}_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
for all
n\in[N]
. In the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we observe the initial prompts sampled from an initial distribution
\nu_{0}
, i.e.
X_{n}\sim\nu_{0}
for each
n\in[N]
. Intuitively,
\nu_{0}(x)
is the probability with which a human presents prompt
x
to the language model.
Moreover, for each
X_{n}
,
\mathcal{D}
contains two responses
A^{+}_{n},A^{-}_{n}
sampled from an
unknown
distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
.
Moreover, the action
A^{+}_{n}
has been judged the winner over
A^{-}_{n}
by a Bradley-Terry preference model defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Bradley-Terry (BT) preference model

Given a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, two responses
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
and access to the
ground truth
reward function
r^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,R_{\max}]
for some
R_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}
, the Bradley-Terry preference model computes the probability of
a
being a winner over
b
in response to the prompt
x
(event denoted as
a\succ b|x
) as
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{true}}_{r^{\star}}(a\succ b|x)=\sigma(\Delta_{r^{\star}}(x,a,b))

where
\Delta_{\mathfrak{f}}(x,a,b)=\mathfrak{f}(x,a)-\mathfrak{f}(x,b)
for any function
\mathfrak{f}:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
denotes the sigmoid function.

Given the BT model above, the dataset is labeled sampling
Z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{true}}_{r^{\star}}(a\succ b|x))
for each
x,a,b
appearing in the dataset. If
Z=1
,
a
is declared as the winner; vice versa if
Z=0
,
b
wins the comparison.

The goal of the learning algorithm is to update the starting model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
to obtain a post-trained model
\pi_{\mathrm{out}}
such that it approximately equals

\mathop{\mbox{ arg\,max}}_{\pi\in\Pi}J_{\beta}(\pi):=\left\langle{\pi},{r^{\star}}\right\rangle-\beta KL(\pi,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})

(1)

where
\Pi
is the set of all mappings from prompts to distributions over responses
\Pi=\left\{{\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}}\right\}
. The inner product notation is defined for each policy
\pi\in\Pi
and for any function
r:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,R_{\max}]
as
\left\langle{\pi},{r}\right\rangle:=\sum_{x,a\in\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\nu_{0}(x)\pi(a|x)r(x,a)
and the average (over prompts) KL is defined for any policy pair
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi\times\Pi
as
KL(\pi,\pi^{\prime})=\sum_{x,a\in\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\nu_{0}(x)\pi(a|x)\log\nicefrac{{\pi(a|x)}}{{\pi^{\prime}(a|x)}}
.

The objective in (
1
) therefore prescribes to find a trade-off between two desiderata: (i) maximizing the
ground truth
reward
r^{\star}
used by the Bradley-Terry preference model to label the dataset and (ii) minimizing the shift as measured by the KL divergence from the initial model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. The value of
\beta\in(0,\infty)
must be set as high as much it is important to stay close to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
to make sure that
\pi_{\mathrm{out}}
inherits the useful properties learned by
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
from the data observed before entering the learning from preferences phase.

We now describe two fundamental ways to solve (
1
), which will serve as building blocks for our approach.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback: RLHF

In RLHF, the idea is to optimize (
1
) under two approximations. First, we approximate
r^{\star}
, which is unknown to the learner, with the maximum likelihood estimator, denoted by
\hat{r}
and learned on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
as follows:

\hat{r}=\mathop{\mbox{ arg\,max}}_{r:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,R_{\max}]}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\log\sigma(\Delta_{r}(X_{n},A^{+}_{n},A^{-}_{n})).

(2)

Secondly, in (
1
), the prompt distribution
\nu_{0}
is unknown; therefore, it must be approximated using, for example, the states observed in the dataset. All in all, RLHF methods output the policy maximizing the following empirical objective over
\pi\in\Pi
,

\sum^{N}_{n=1}\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi(a|X_{n})\left({\hat{r}(X_{n},a)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(a|X_{n})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|X_{n})}}\right).

(3)

RLHF has the merit of being the conceptual core of the large majority of, if not all, alignment algorithms. Sadly, it has the drawback of being split into two training steps represented by equations (
2
) and (
3
) respectively. This has been elegantly avoided by DPO
(
48
)
with a reparameterization trick explained next.

Direct Preference Optimization

The core observation is that (
1
), for any reward function
r
, admits the following closed form solution

\pi_{r}(a|x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)\exp(\frac{r(x,a)}{\beta})}{Z_{r}(x)},

where
Z_{r}(x):=\sum_{b}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(b|x)\exp(\frac{r(x,b)}{\beta})
. Albeit, being incomputable due to the hardness of computing
Z_{r}(x)
(unless the reward takes a very specific form
(
34
)
), the closed form expression for
\pi_{r}
allows to write the reward function as

r(x,a)=\beta\log\nicefrac{{\pi_{r}(a|x)}}{{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)}}+\beta\log Z_{r}(x).

Then, replacing this expression in (
2
), we can ensure that
\pi_{\hat{r}}
is in the solution set of the following single-step optimization problem, which is known as the DPO objective,

\pi_{\hat{r}}\in\mathop{\mbox{ arg\,max}}_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\log\sigma(\beta\Delta_{\log\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(X_{n},A^{+}_{n},A^{-}_{n}))).

(4)

Importantly, we have used that for each reward
r
,
\beta\Delta_{\log\pi_{r}/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(x,a,b):=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(a|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(b|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(b|x)}
equals
\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)
because the normalization constant
Z(x)
cancels out.

Limitations of RLHF and DPO (i.e., the lack of pessimism):

The missing ingredient above is that both method maximizes
\hat{r}
(directly or indirectly) blindly, i.e., treating it as if it were a perfect estimate of the ground truth
r^{\star}
. However, in practical situations where
N
is finite, this is not the case:
\hat{r}
might not be accurate, and taking into account its uncertainty becomes fundamentally important. As an example, think of a single state, two actions case in which
\hat{r}(a_{1})=\hat{r}(a_{2})+\epsilon
for some small
\epsilon
but the uncertainty
5
5

5

We keep uncertainty vague here for the sake of intuition.
of
\hat{r}(a_{1})
is much higher than
\hat{r}(a_{2})
. Then, choosing
a_{2}
is more reasonable while RHLF and DPO, which purely maximize
\hat{r}
would output the highly risky action
a_{1}
.
In a less toy example targeted to LLMs, DPO and RLHF might achieve high reward under
\hat{r}
exploiting hallucinations of
\hat{r}
, i.e., actions that have in fact small reward under the ground truth
r^{\star}
.

To mitigate these issues, the next section presents our techniques to incorporate pessimism in DPO. In contrast to previous pessimistic DPO versions
(
23
;
32
)
, we
assume no knowledge at all
of
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
whereas
(
23
)
assumed that
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and
(
32
)
leverages access to a policy
\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
such that
\left\|{\nicefrac{{\pi_{\mathrm{base}}}}{{\pi_{\mathrm{data}}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
is small.

3
The algorithm

Our algorithm,
PEPO
, applies three important changes on top of DPO, which will enable our guarantees featuring only the
single-policy
concentrability coefficient:
(1)
We derive a pessimistic DPO objective
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pessDPO}}
derived from a Bradley-Terry model variant which allows ties.

(2)
We split the dataset into
L
subsets of equal size and we maximize
L
different instances of the pessimistic DPO objective with respect to independent LoRA parameters over each subset.

(3)
Finally, our theory motivates outputting a policy obtained by aggregating the ensemble in a pessimistic manner. Unfortunately, computing this policy is intractable, but we present a rejection sampling method to sample exactly from it.

We now move to a detailed overview of these three components, whose interplay is key for the theoretical guarantees presented in the next section.

3.1
Bradley-Terry model with ties

In our analysis presented in
Section

4
, we need to build a
pessimistic
estimator for the winning probabilities probability under the ground truth Bradley-Terry model, i.e., we look for
\hat{\mathbb{P}}:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\in[0,1]
such that for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have that
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(a\succ b|x)\leq\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{true}}_{r^{\star}}(a\succ b|x)
.

Clearly,
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
cannot be a
proper
estimator, i.e., it cannot be a sigmoid function. Indeed, let us assume that for some reward
r
, we choose
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(a\succ b|x)=\sigma(\Delta_{r}(x,a,b))
. Then, if
\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)\leq\Delta_{r^{\star}}(x,a,b)
, we have that by the monotonicity of
\sigma(\cdot)
, it holds that
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(a\succ b|x)\leq\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{true}}_{r^{\star}}(a\succ b|x)
, satisfying the requirement for this ordering of
a
and
b
.

However, when the two actions are exchanged, recall that by definition
\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)=-\Delta_{r}(x,b,a)
and
\Delta_{r^{\star}}(x,a,b)=-\Delta_{r^{\star}}(x,b,a)
.
Moreover, since
\sigma(x)=1-\sigma(-x)
,
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(a\succ b|x)\leq\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{true}}_{r^{\star}}(a\succ b|x)
unfortunately implies that
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(b\succ a|x)\geq\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{true}}_{r^{\star}}(b\succ a|x)
. Therefore, for proper estimators, underestimating the winning probability of action
a
against
b
implies overestimating the win rate of
b
against
a
, whereby failing to underestimate both winning probabilities.

To overcome the issue, we need to look at
improper
estimators. In particular, we need to estimate
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
within a class of models which does not force
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(b\succ a|x)
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(a\succ b|x)

to sum up to
1
. Our idea is, therefore, to predict
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
within the class of Bradley-Terry models with ties introduced in the sequel. Having a third event in the distribution’s support allows now the sum of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(b\succ a|x)
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(a\succ b|x)
to be equal to some value
c\leq 1
. Then the comparison between
a
and
b
is declared a tie with probability
1-c
. We formally introduce the Bradley-Terry models with ties below.

Definition 3.1
.

Bradley-Terry model with ties

The Bradley-Terry preference model with ties, associated to a reward function
r
, computes the probability of
a
being a winner over
b
in response to the prompt
x
as

\mathbb{P}_{r}(a\succ b|x)=\frac{\exp\left({\beta\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)/2}\right)}{Z(x,a,b)},

where given a tie weight
\lambda:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
which satisfies
\lambda(x,a,b)=\lambda(x,b,a)
, we defined

Z(x,a,b):=\exp\left({\beta\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)/2}\right)+\exp\left({\beta\Delta_{r}(x,b,a)/2}\right)+\lambda(x,a,b).

Moreover, the comparison is declared a tie with probability

\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}(x,a,b)=\frac{\lambda(x,a,b)}{Z(x,a,b)}
.

While modification of the Bradley-Terry models to allow ties already appeared in classical statistics
(
49
;
14
)
and have been used recently in the RLHF/DPO literature
(
7
;
31
)
, we did not find the model described above to already exist in the exact same form. In particular, it is important for our theoretical analysis that the tie probability weight
\lambda
can depend on the triplet
x,a,b
involved in the comparison, while in
(
49
;
14
)
the ties probability weights are prompt and answers independent. Moreover, notice that
\lambda(x,a,b)=0
for all
x,a,b
recovers the standard BT model.

Pessimistic interpretation of shifted sigmoid functions

An important observation about
Definition

3.1
is that we can rewrite
\mathbb{P}_{r}(a\succ b|x)
as

\displaystyle\sigma(\beta\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)-\underbrace{\log(1+\lambda(x,a,b)e^{\beta\Delta_{r}(x,a,b)/2})}_{\mathrm{Shift}})=\sigma_{\mathrm{pess}}(\beta\Delta_{r}(x,a,b),\lambda(x,a,b)),

(5)

where we define the pessimistic sigmoid function as
\sigma_{\mathrm{pess}}(x,\lambda):=\sigma(x-\log(1+\lambda e^{x/2}))
. Notice that
\sigma_{\mathrm{pess}}
is a sigmoid function translated on the right.

This observation surprisingly allows us to connect our pessimistic technique to the extensive literature on DPO with margins. Indeed, we highlight in passing that right-shifted sigmoid functions have already been used in literature with the motivation of ensuring a margin, at least as large as
\mathrm{Shift}
, between the winner and the loser response
(
57
;
35
;
62
;
55
;
67
)
. On the opposite side,
(
21
)
shifts the sigmoid on the left which can be interpreted as an optimistic exploration strategy that becomes useful when the Bradley Terry model can be queried online and not exclusively on an offline dataset. We offer an additional discussion on this point in
Appendix

B
and we now move to the second important component of our algorithm.

3.2
Creating the models ensemble

We build on the pessimistic sigmoid function to train
L
pessimistic language models over a partition of the preference dataset. To this end, we partition the data
\mathcal{D}=\left\{{X_{n},A^{+}_{n},A^{-}_{n}}\right\}^{N}_{n=1}
in
L
subsets, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}=\cup^{L}_{\ell=1}\mathcal{D}^{\ell}
,
where we denote the data in the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
chunk as
\mathcal{D}^{\ell}=\left\{{X_{n,\ell},A^{+}_{n,\ell},A^{-}_{n,\ell}}\right\}
.
At this point, we compute
\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}
for each
\ell\in[L]
as the solution of the following pessimistic DPO objective which modifies the original DPO objective in (
4
) by using only the subset
\mathcal{D}^{\ell}
instead of using the full dataset
\mathcal{D}
and replacing the standard sigmoid with
\sigma_{\mathrm{pess}}
in (
5
):

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\mbox{ arg\,max}}_{\pi\in\bar{\Pi}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pessDPO}}(\pi;\mathcal{D}^{\ell}):=\sum_{x,a,b\in\mathcal{D}^{\ell}}\log\left({\sigma_{\mathrm{pess}}(\beta\Delta_{\log\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(x,a,b),\lambda^{\ell}(x,a,b))}\right)

(6)

Notice that the tie weight
\lambda^{\ell}
parameter is allowed to depend on the ensemble index and we denote the tie probability associated with parameters
\lambda^{\ell}
as
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}^{\ell}
. In our analysis,
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}^{\ell}(x,a,b)
should be set closer to
1
, whenever the triplet
x,a,b
feels very uncertain for the model
\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}
. Having a dependence on the ensemble index thus allows us to instantiate models with different levels of confidence for different regions of the state action space.
Moreover, we maximize (
6
) over a subset of
\Pi
denoted by
\bar{\Pi}
, defined as
\bar{\Pi}:=\left\{{\pi\in\Pi:\left|{\beta\Delta_{\log\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}\right|\leq 2R_{\max}}\right\}
. This restriction is important in the analysis because it ensures that
\beta\Delta_{\log\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
is bounded for each
\ell\in[L]
.

3.3
Pessimism via ensemble

We now aim at aggregating the
L
models in the ensemble in a pessimistic manner. Towards this goal, recall that by the correspondence between policy and reward space given by (
2
), each implicit reward learned obtained as
\beta\log\frac{\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(a|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)}
can be considered as an independent estimate of the ground truth reward.
6
6

6

For the whole solution set of (
6
), we can guarantee that
\Delta_{\log\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
is a good estimate of
\Delta_{r^{\star}}
but assuming for simplicity of presentation that we pick the specific solution which ensures that
\sum_{b}\pi_{\mathrm{data}}(b|x)\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(b|x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(b|x))=\sum_{b}\pi_{\mathrm{data}}(b|x)r^{\star}(x,b)
, then we can consider
\log\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
to be an accurate pointwise estimate of
r^{\star}
. As explained in detail in
Section

E.1
, this assumption is only for clarity in the main text, while it is not required for our analysis in
Appendix

E
.
Therefore, we can consider the set
\left\{{\beta\log\frac{\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(a|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)}}\right\}^{L}_{\ell=1}
to be a DPO-induced reward ensemble.
At this point, similarly to
(
11
)
, we aim at optimizing a notion of worst case reward over the ensemble. In our case, we choose the worst case reward as

r^{-}(x,a)=\beta\min_{\ell\in[L]}\log\frac{\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(a|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)}-B\overline{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}}(x,a)

where
B=6e^{3R_{\max}}
and
\overline{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}}(x,a)
is a high probability upper bound on the probability of observing a tie playing action
a
against a response sampled from
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
, i.e.
\overline{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}}(x,a)\geq\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi_{\mathrm{data}}(a|x)\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}^{\ell}(x,a,b)
for all

We present here the theoretical guarantees for
PEPO

Our theoretical goal is to quantify the required dataset size

J_{\beta}(\pi^{\star})-J_{\beta}(\pi_{\mathrm{out}})\leq\varepsilon

\pi^{\star}\in\Pi

In particular, as motivated by

In stark contrast, methods prone to overoptimization such as DPO incur an

coefficient

C^{\mathrm{all}}:=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\nu_{0}(x)\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\frac{(\pi(a|x))^{2}}{\pi_{\mathrm{data}}(a|x)}.

We highlight that

and
C^{\mathrm{all}}
have strongly different qualitative behaviors. On the one hand,

is reasonably small as soon as sampling from
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
allows to observe with high enough probability all the actions which are often chosen by
\pi^{\star}
. On the other hand,
C^{\mathrm{all}}
imposes basically to observe with positive probability all possible responses in the dataset

If

contains the uniform policy over responses.

\pi^{\star}

4.1
Analysis sketch

For clarity, we still operate under the benign assumption that

Step 1: Establishing pessimism

A fundamental quantity of the analysis is the implicitly estimated reward gap

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}(x,a,b):=\beta\left({\min_{\ell\in[L]}\log\frac{\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(a|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|x)}-\max_{\ell\in[L]}\log\frac{\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(b|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(b|x)}}\right)-B\overline{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}}(x,a)

Lemma 4.2

Let us denote

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced
PEPO
, the first DPO-like algorithm that provably and practically avoids over-optimization without knowing the data distribution of the responses recorded in the dataset. This situation is very common in practice when post-training models by leveraging responses that are either human or proprietary models generated. This contribution opens several exciting directions, the most important of all being to establish the same result beyond the tabular setup. Indeed, while the good experimental results suggest that the tabular setting might be an accurate enough model for practical post-training, there are theoretical results
(
23
;
32
)
which go beyond it but at the cost of knowing
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
. We consider getting the best of both worlds an extremely exciting open problem.

Another interesting direction inspired by
(
65
)
is to investigate if the notion of ensemble disagreement can be used to detect the risk of hallucinations and intervene, forcing the model to abstain from answering. In particular, we could force the model to respond “
I do not know
.” if
Algorithm

2
does not terminate within a maximum number of attempts.

We expand on those and other exciting directions in

C
.

A.5.1
Models and Fine-Tuning Setup

We evaluate

on four instruction-tuned language models spanning a range of sizes. At the 7–8B scale, we consider Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B-SFT, which serves as a strong open-source baseline, along with Zephyr-7B-SFT and Mistral-7B-SFT. To demonstrate scalability, we also include Yi-34B-Chat, a 34B parameter model. All backbone models have undergone supervised fine-tuning (SFT) prior to preference optimization.

A.5.2
Dataset and Preprocessing

For all large-scale experiments, we utilize the UltraFeedback Binarized dataset
[
12
]
. This version is derived from the original UltraFeedback corpus by "binarizing" multi-model outputs into discrete preference pairs. Specifically, for each prompt, four responses were generated by diverse language models and scored by GPT-4 across multiple dimensions (e.g., helpfulness and honesty). The binarized version identifies the highest-scoring response as the
chosen
sample and selects one of the remaining lower-scoring responses as the
rejected
sample. We use the standard
train_sft
split for training and
test_sft
for validation. However, we note that the validation loss is a poor proxy for downstream evaluation performance (e.g., AlpacaEval win rate), making early stopping based on this metric unreliable. This further underscores the need for methods like PEPO that are inherently robust to overoptimization.

During preprocessing, we truncate sequences to a maximum length of 1024 tokens, with prompts limited to 512 tokens to ensure sufficient space for generated responses.

Data Partitioning.

For
PEPO
, we partition the training data into
L
disjoint subsets of approximately equal size, where
L
is the ensemble size. This partitioning ensures statistical independence across ensemble members, as required by our theoretical analysis (
Lemma

4.2
). We note that in practice, data efficiency could potentially be improved by using overlapping subsets, though we leave this exploration to future work.

A.5.3
Training Procedure

We train all models using the AdamW optimizer with
bfloat16
mixed precision for computational efficiency. Training is carried out with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
and a weight decay of
0.01
. We maintain a consistent effective batch size of 64 across all experiments, using gradient accumulation where necessary. To accelerate training, we pre-compute and cache the reference model’s log-probabilities for all training examples, avoiding redundant forward passes through the frozen backbone.

For
PEPO
, the
\beta
parameter controlling KL regularization is set to 0.1, matching the DPO baseline. We set the pessimism parameter
B\overline{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}}
(denoted as
\alpha
in our codebase) to 0.1. While our theory suggests this term should be state-dependent, we treat it as a constant prompt-response independent hyperparameter in practice for simplicity. We conducted a preliminary ablation on the value of this parameter, but observed no substantial difference in downstream performance within this range. We ablate the ensemble size
L\in\{2,3,4\}
to understand the trade-off between pessimism strength and data efficiency. Note that
L=1
corresponds to a single model trained with the pessimistic DPO loss, while
L=1
with the pessimism parameter
\alpha
set to 0 recovers the standard DPO.

For the baselines,
\chi^{2}
PO uses
\beta=0.1
,
\gamma=1.0
, and
R_{\max}=10.0
with internal gradient clipping, following the original paper. SFT+DPO combines the DPO objective with a supervised fine-tuning regularizer weighted by
\lambda_{\text{SFT}}=0.005
. In SFT+DPO we use chosen as well as rejected answers from the UltraFeedback as an estimate for
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
. All experiments use seed 42 for reproducibility.

Ensemble Training and Inference

The
PEPO
ensemble is trained by assigning each LoRA adapter to its corresponding data partition. Ensemble members can be trained sequentially or in parallel across multiple GPUs
without any required communication
between the members of the ensemble. Therefore, the training of all
L

While we introduced a rejection sampling scheme in

During inference of

This allows us to efficiently switch the adapters on and off to run the forward pass with different members of the ensemble without reloading the model to and from device each time. We make use of multiple GPUS by distributing multiple copies of this shared backbone model on each available device. This is shown in

A.5.5
Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate all models on AlpacaEval
[
29

, a benchmark that measures pairwise win rate against a reference model across 805 diverse prompts spanning open-ended generation, reasoning, and instruction-following tasks. We report the win rate against the initial (epoch 0) SFT model, which measures the improvement from preference optimization. To ensure reproducibility and manage costs, we use Llama-3-70B-Instruct as the preference judge (run locally), utilizing the

All responses are generated using a maximum of 1024 new tokens. We evaluate model checkpoints at every training epoch to capture learning dynamics and potential over-optimization. The win rates reported in the

A.5.6
Computational Resources

Experiments were conducted on NVIDIA GH200 120GB and NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs. We allocated one GPU per ensemble member for training (e.g., 4 GPUs for a
PEPO
model with
L=4
, trained in parallel) and two GPUs for evaluation and generation (mainly to accommodate the Llama-3-70B-Instruct judge). Since we parallelize the training of the ensemble across devices and partition the dataset such that each member sees only
1/L
of the data, the wall-clock time required for training is significantly reduced (roughly proportional to
1/L
) compared to training a single model on the full dataset.

For training, we observed that the average wall-clock time per epoch for the Llama-3-8B model dropped from 3.61 hours (
L=1
) to 0.90 hours (
L=4
). Similarly, for Yi-34B, training took approximately 4.0 hours per epoch with
L=4
.

For evaluation on AlpacaEval, generating responses and computing win rates using the token-level sampling detailed in
Section

A.4
for the 8B model takes approximately 30 minutes on a single NVIDIA GH200 GPU and approximately 1.25 hours on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. In contrast, full rejection sampling using the minimum probability criterion is prohibitively expensive. While this method remains efficient in early training updates—achieving a strong 67.77% win rate on Llama-3-8B, we observe that the acceptance probability quickly collapses (
\alpha\to 0
) as the ensemble diverges. This causes the generator to saturate the trial limit (
\sim 16
attempts per answer), increasing the evaluation time to approximately 80 hours on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. However, an optimized variant using mean and standard deviation criteria mitigates this collapse, maintaining a high acceptance rate (
\alpha\approx 0.8
) and low overhead (
\sim 1.2
attempts per answer). While this optimized variant reduces the runtime to around 4 hours, it remains significantly slower (
8\times
) than the greedy approach. This vast difference in computational cost—without a corresponding gain in performance—justifies our choice of the efficient token-level approximation for large-scale benchmarks.

Discussion: the pessimistic sigmoid

In this subsection, we want to briefly discuss in more detail the effect of shifting the sigmoid function on the right along the horizontal axis as depicted in
Figure

6
.
The standard sigmoid is plotted in blue and
\sigma(-x)=1-\sigma(x)
is plotted with the dotted blue line. It is easy to notice that for any value along the horizontal axis the two values sum up to
1
.
In addition, we show with a solid red line the sigmoid shifted on the right, i.e.
\sigma(x-\gamma)
by some margin
\gamma
and in dotted red the reflected version, i.e.
\sigma(-x+\gamma)
, which is shifted on the left compared to
\sigma(-x)
.
The effect is that for the shifted sigmoid the sum of
\sigma(-x+\gamma)
and
\sigma(x-\gamma)
does not equal
1
. In particular, we plot with the gray shaded area the amount of missing mass to reach
1
, i.e.
1-\sigma(-x+\gamma)-\sigma(x-\gamma)
and we interpreted this value in the main text as the probability of observing a tie. We notice that while the probability of tie vanishes for large positive or negative values along the
x
axis, it reaches its maximum value when the reward gap on the horizontal axis is
0
. That is, the tie probability reaches the maximum when the two response under comparison have equal reward function.

The important viewpoint for our analysis is that for any fixed value along the horizontal axis, the shifted sigmoid (red line) lower bounds the original sigmoid (in blue). This is key to develop our pessimistic method. As mentioned, the use of the shifted sigmoid in the preference optimization literature is not new. For example, it appeared in
[
57
,
35
,
62
,
55
,
67
,
60
]
. However, in those works, the motivation is different. Their viewpoint is that for a fixed value of winning probability
p
, i.e. a fixed level on the vertical axis, the inverse shifted sigmoid evaluated at
p
is larger than
\sigma^{-1}(p)
. As a result, the model learn a reward function which separates the winner and looser response by a larger margin.

We think that these two viewpoints are complementary and interestingly, we did not find this analogy to be previously mentioned in the literature, therefore we felt it important to highlight here this connection.

Open Question

Optimism via Ensemble

In the context of LLM post-training there are applications in which at training time it is possible to generate sentences from the model which is currently learning and getting feedback for this response under the form of a reward signal or a preference bit. Examples of the first kind are the emerging family of algorithms for reasoning such as GRPO
53
,
15
, GRPO Done Right
33
and TBRM
66
while for online algorithms learning from preference signals we highlight Online DPO
20
and Nash Learning from Human Feedback
37
.
While promising at an empirical level, they all miss an exploration mechanism that should be included to ensure that the learning model queries the reward or preference oracle for response which are uncertain, i.e. for which new information should really be acquired. Such lack of exploration has been also observed in certain experiments
4
,
16
.
While recently some exploration mechanism have been proposed for improving GRPO, Best of N and Online DPO
63
,
58
none of this works leverages the ensemble mechanism which we show to be effective for pessimism. Therefore, it remains open to investigate optimistic ensemble based versions of the above algorithms and see if they can lead to any theoretical and practical advantage over the existing exploration methods.

Outputting a parametric policy

Our work successfully avoids over-optimization without knowledge of
\pi_{\mathrm{data}}
at the cost of outputting a non-parametric policy from which we can sample efficiently but that we can not compute in closed form. Therefore, our method is ideal for being the last step of an LLM training pipeline (which is expected to be most often the case for DPO-like algorithms). However,
PEPO
is not suitable as an intermediate step of the training pipeline because it does not output a parametric policy, i.e. a training checkpoint in a more applied jargon. It is therefore interesting for future work to solve the exact same question this work solves but using parametric policies.

Lemma F.4

and differentiation yields

f^{\prime}(y)=2\left(\frac{\lambda+\frac{D^{\prime}(y)}{2\sqrt{D(y)}}}{\lambda y+\sqrt{D(y)}}+\frac{1}{1-y}\right),\qquad D^{\prime}(y)=2y(\lambda^{2}-4)+4.

For
y\in I
, we have
y\geq a
and
1-y\geq a
, hence

D(y)\geq 4y(1-y)\geq 4a^{2},\qquad\sqrt{D(y)}\geq 2a,\qquad\lambda y+\sqrt{D(y)}\geq 2a.

Moreover,

|D^{\prime}(y)|\leq 2(\lambda^{2}+4)+4=2\lambda^{2}+12.

Therefore,

\frac{\lambda+\frac{|D^{\prime}(y)|}{2\sqrt{D(y)}}}{\lambda y+\sqrt{D(y)}}\leq\frac{\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}+6}{2a}}{2a},\qquad\frac{1}{1-y}\leq\frac{1}{a}.

Combining these estimates, we obtain

|f^{\prime}(y)|\leq 2\left[\frac{\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}+6}{2a}}{2a}+\frac{1}{a}\right]=\frac{\lambda+2}{a}+\frac{\lambda^{2}+6}{2a^{2}}.

Since
a^{-1}=e^{2R_{\max}}+1
, the stated bound follows.
∎

Proof.

Let us denote

At each round,

where we defined
Z=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\min_{\ell\in[L]}\tilde{\pi}^{\ell}(a|x)\exp(-B\overline{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathrm{tie}}}(x,a)/\beta)
with
B=6e^{3R_{\max}}
. We omitted the dependence on the prompt
x
for simplicity.
Therefore, the random variable
X
representing the number of trials until the first successful acceptance follows a Geometric distribution:
X\sim\text{Geometric}(Z)

We want to find
N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)
such that the probability of failing to get a sample after
N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)
trials is at most
\delta
:

P(X>N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x))\leq\delta

The event
X>N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)
occurs if and only if all
N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)
independent trials result in a rejection. The probability of rejection in a single trial is
(1-Z)
. Therefore:

P(X>N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x))=(1-Z)^{N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)}

Therefore, our goal can be rewritten as

(1-Z)^{N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)}\leq\delta

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides (and noting that
\ln(1-Z)
is negative, which flips the inequality):

N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)\ln(1-Z)\leq\ln(\delta)

which implies

N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)\geq\frac{\ln(\delta)}{\ln(1-Z)}

To simplify this into a more interpretable bound, we use the fact that for
Z\in(0,1)
,
\ln(1-Z)\leq-Z
. This implies that:

\frac{1}{\ln(1-Z)}\geq-\frac{1}{Z}

Substituting this into our expression for
N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)
:

N_{\mathrm{RS}}(x)\frac{\ln(\delta)}{-Z}=\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{Z}

.
∎
