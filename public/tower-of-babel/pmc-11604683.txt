Awareness of Jordanian Researchers About Predatory Journals: A Need for Training
Authors: Omar F. Khabour, Karem H. Alzoubi, Wesal M. Aldarabseh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The use of the open publishing is expected to be the dominant model in the future. However, along with the use of this model, predatory journals are increasingly appearing. In the current study, the awareness of researchers in Jordan about predatory journals and the strategies utilized to avoid them was investigated. The study included 558 researchers from Jordan. A total of 34.0% of the participants reported a high ability to identify predatory journals, while 27.0% reported a low ability to identify predatory journals. Most participants (64.0%) apply “Think. Check. Submit.” strategy to avoid predatory journals. However, 11.9% of the sample reported being a victim of a predatory journal. Multinomial regression analysis showed gender, number of publications, using Beall’s list of predatory journals, and applying “Think. Check. Submit.” strategy were predictors of the high ability to identify predatory journals. Participants reported using databases such as Scopus, Clarivate, membership in the publishing ethics committee, and DOAJ to validate the journal before publication. Finally, most participants (88.4%) agreed to attend a training module on how to identify predatory journals. In conclusion, Jordanian researchers use valid strategies to avoid predatory journals. Implementing a training module may enhance researchers’ ability to identify predatory journals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11948-024-00519-8.

Introduction

Reporting of research results is an integral part of the scientific research process. This can be achieved by publishing in scientific journals, conferences, symposia, and others (Ashcraft et al., 2020; Cunningham-Erves et al., 2021). However, publication in scholarly journals remains the main avenue for dissemination of research findings (Collins et al., 2015). Publishing in journals is expressed in two models: the open-access publishing model and the subscription-based publishing model (Sukhov et al., 2016; Gasparyan et al., 2019; Montecucco & Nathoe, 2022). The use of the open publishing model is increasing over time and is expected to be the dominant model in the future (Eysenbach, 2006; Kerig, 2018). This increase is mainly due to the increase in the likelihood of article being cited and the removal of barriers to knowledge (Eysenbach, 2006).

In parallel with the increase in the use of the open-access publishing model, predatory journals are increasingly emerging (Oermann et al., 2016; Mudry & Ruben, 2019; Duc et al., 2020; Fadel et al., 2024; Martinino et al., 2024). Predatory journals use the open-access publishing model to profit from scholarly publishing in a dishonest way (Sharma & Verma, 2018; Cortegiani et al., 2020; Cukier et al., 2020; Panda, 2020; Fox, 2021; Barker et al., 2023). Common characteristics of predatory journals are dishonesty, insufficient transparency, poor quality, focus on making profits, invalid peer-review process, and fake indexing information (Laine & Winker, 2017; Richtig et al., 2018; Tulandi & Balayla, 2019; Gabrielsson et al., 2021; Tang & Jia, 2022; Amsen, 2024). Therefore, predatory journals are considered fake/trash journals (Sorokowski et al., 2017; Samuel & Aranha, 2018; Kert & Švab, 2021; Chandra & Dasgupta, 2024).

Publication in predatory journals from developing countries is common (Demir, 2018; Memon & Azim, 2018; Memon & Waqas, 2018). medical students’ knowledge regarding scientific journals including predatory journals was assessed as insufficient (Ashour & Funjan, 2022). However, knowledge regarding predatory journals among researchers in Jordan and how to avoid such journals still needs to be investigated. Therefore, the aim of the current study is to examine the awareness of researchers in Jordan about predatory journals and the strategies utilized to avoid them.

Methods

Study Design

The study is cross-sectional, self-reported, and survey-based. Recruitment of subjects was conducted during the second half of the 2022 year. Jordanian researchers from academic institutions and health sectors were invited to participate in the study via institutional e-mail lists or respective colleges’ WhatsApp groups. The target sample was calculated using the G * Power 3.1.9.7. with α = 0.05, power = 0.95, and an effect size = 0.15. The required sample size was 472 subjects. A sample of Jordanian researchers (N = 997) were invited to participate in the study. A total of 558 researchers filled out the questionnaire. This indicates a response rate of 56%. The study instruments: The “Google Forms” application was used to prepare the study instrument, which included the following domains. The first domain collected general information about the study participants. This included the highest academic degree, academic rank, number of articles published, current job, years of experience, work sector, gender, and others. The second domain asked questions regarding the ability of researchers to identify predatory journals and the strategies used to avoid them. The last domain asked questions regarding the need for training to enhance awareness of predatory journals among Jordanian researchers. The study questions included choices such as “ yes” or “no “, and a 3-point Likert scale “ agree, neutral, disagree”. The study instrument was designed based on previous studies (Christopher & Young, 2015; Richtig et al., 2019a; Bakri & Shah, 2021; Maurer et al., 2021). The research team created a preliminary version of the study. Subsequently, the questionnaire underwent evaluation for face and content validity by two research ethics experts in order to verify the clarity and comprehensibility of the questions. The opinions of experts were considered, and the questionnaire was adjusted accordingly. Subsequently, a pilot test was conducted involving 10 researchers, who were requested to provide feedback regarding the clarity and comprehensibility of the questions incorporated in the questionnaires. The data obtained during this pilot testing was excluded from the final analysis of the results. The study instrument is available as a supplementary file (see supplement 1).

Ethical Issues

The study followed international ethical guidelines and those adopted by the Institutional Review Board of Jordan University of Science and Technology (approval identification number: 2022/149/30). The questionnaire was anonymous, and no identifying information was collected from the participants. Access to the collected data was restricted to the research team.

Statistical Analysis

The SPSS software was used to analyze the data. Data were expressed mainly as percentages. Appropriate statistics such as cross-tabulations and regression models were used to identify factors associated with Jordanian researchers’ knowledge about predatory journals. A P-value less than 0.05 was used as a threshold for inferring statistical significance.

General characteristics of the participants

Table 2 reported the self-reported ability of the participants to identify predatory journals. A total of 199 participants (34.0%) reported a high ability to identify predatory journals, while 156 participants (27.0%) reported a low ability to identify predatory journals. Factors such as age, gender, academic rank, academic degree, research area, use of Beall’s list of predatory journals, and use of “Think. Check. Submit.” strategy when publishing were found to be associated with the ability to identify predatory journals by researchers. Multinomial regression analysis showed that the ability to identify predatory journals was high among researchers with high number of publications (more than 20 publications, P < 0.05), using Beall’s list of predatory journals (P < 0.01) and applying “Think. Check. Submit.” strategy when publishing (P < 0.001). A lower ability to identify predatory journals was found to be related to young age (P < 0.05), female gender (P < 0.01), and lower number of publications (P < 0.01).

Discussion

In the current study, the awareness of Jordanian researchers about predatory journals was investigated. In addition, the strategies used by the researchers to avoid predatory journals were examined.

Predatory journals are illegitimate journals that use the open-access publishing model to make profit from scholarly publishing (Sharma & Verma, 2018; Cortegiani et al., 2020; Panda, 2020; Fox, 2021). In the recent years, predatory journals have increasingly appeared (Oermann et al., 2016; Mudry & Ruben, 2019; Duc et al., 2020). For example, in nursing, several predatory journals were identified in 2016 (Oermann et al., 2016). Predatory journals usually attract their victims by sending unsolicited emails asking for manuscript submissions and offering quick review and rapid publication in few days (Wilkinson et al., 2019).

According to the current study, about a third (34%) of the participants reported a high ability to identify predatory journals. In a previous study from Austria of dermatological researchers, only 29.4% of the study participants were aware of predatory journals (Richtig et al., 2019b). A similar percentage of awareness about predatory journals was reported among German orthopaedic and trauma surgeons (39.9%) (Maurer et al., 2021). In the United States, about 33.0% of the authors surveyed were somewhat familiar with predatory journals (Cohen et al., 2019). Low awareness (9.1%) about predatory journals was reported among fifth year medical students in Saudi Arabia (Alamri et al., 2020). On the other hand, relatively high awareness (69.7%) about predatory journals was reported among oncologist in Austria (Richtig et al., 2019a), researchers from North Eastern Hill University’s (58.75%) and Oakland University (87%) (Swanberg et al., 2020; Kharumnuid & Singh Deo, 2022), and nursing faculty members (68.6%) in Saudi Arabia (Hashish et al., 2024).

Publication in predatory journals from developing countries including Jordan is common (Demir, 2018; Memon & Azim, 2018; Memon & Waqas, 2018).

According to the current study, about 11.9% of the sample reported being a victim of a predatory journal. In studies from the region, publication in predatory journals was reported by 15.2% of medical residents in the UAE (Ibrahim et al., 2022), and by 26% of fifth year medical students in Saudi Arabia (Alamri et al., 2020). In a study that was conducted on 101 neurosurgeons from Africa, 13% reported have published at least one article in a predatory journal, which was associated with the respondent age and total scholarly output (Kabulo et al., 2022). According to a study of researchers from Turkey, India, and Ukraine, publication in predatory journals was reported to be 5.5% (Gupta et al., 2020). Finally, in recent studies from USA, 14–30% of authors surveyed authors reported the likelihood that one of their articles would be published in a (possibly) predatory journal (Cohen et al., 2019; Conlogue et al., 2022). Thus, students and medical residents appear to be more prone to publish their work in predatory journals than faculty members. In addition, publication in predatory journals seems to be a global issue that needs immediate action. Publication in predatory journals has been attributed to inadequate mentorship and support, lack of awareness of such journals, limited resources including access to articles published in legitimate journal, and publishing pressure imposed by promotions (Tulandi & Balayla, 2019; Sotomayor-Beltran, 2020).

Among the proposed strategies for avoiding predatory journals is to apply “Think-Check-Submit” principle and Beall’s list of predatory journals (Pearson, 2017; Cortegiani & Shafer, 2018; Richtig et al., 2024). The checking process includes adherence to publishing ethics, transparency of the peer review process, commitment to academic values, and indexing/abstracting (Danevska et al., 2016). About two-thirds of the participants (64.0%) reported that they applied “Think. Check. Submit.” strategy to avoid predatory journals. The study also showed gender, number of publications, use of Beall’s list of predatory journals, and applying “Think. Check. Submit.” strategy were predictors of a high ability to identify predatory journals. In a study of an African neurosurgeons, the “Think-Check-Submit” principle and Beall’s list were familiar to 19% and 13% of the respondents, respectively (Kabulo et al., 2022). In Austria between 7 and 26% of researchers were familiar with Beall’s list (Richtig et al., 2019a; Richtig et al., 2019b). In addition, awareness of predatory journals was associated with education level, scientific experience, and number of publications but not with gender (Richtig et al., 2019a; Richtig et al., 2019b; Maurer et al., 2021). In the United States, about 26.2% of the authors were aware of Beall’s list of predatory journals (Cohen et al., 2019). In the UAE, only 5% of medical resident were aware of Beall’s list (Ibrahim et al., 2022). Finally, a recent study showed enhanced awareness about predatory journals after implementing an educational intervention (Al-Ani et al., 2023). Thus, expanding awareness about of how to avoid predatory journals is needed.

In the current study, participants reported using databases such as Scopus, Clarivate, membership in the publishing ethics committee, and DOAJ to validate the journal prior to publication. In a study from Germany, 21.0% of orthopaedic and trauma surgeons knew about the DOAJ as a register for non-predatory open access journals (Maurer et al., 2021). In the United States, the use of index databases such as Medline and journal scope has been reported to be utilized to select a journal for publications (Conlogue et al., 2022). Researchers from different countries also use PubMed to select journals for publications (Gupta et al., 2020). Thus, using legitimate databases can be a useful strategy to select a valid journal for publication.

Most of the participants (88.4%) agreed to attend a training module on how to identify predatory journals. Training on scientific writing and selection of journals can significantly improve knowledge and awareness of how to avoid predatory journals (Al-Khatib, 2016; Koroulakis et al., 2020). Continuing education of postgraduates on scholarly publishing has also been suggested as a strategy to improve the quality of publications (Goyal et al., 2020). Training can significantly enhance awareness about predatory journals and how to avoid them (Hansoti et al., 2016; Johal et al., 2017). Training can also enhance the awareness of authors of publisher websites or emails that are unprofessional and suggestive of predatory journals (Beall, 2016).

A limitation of the current study is that the study is cross-sectional and self-reported. Future studies using more potent approaches such as interviews and focus groups could provide a deep insight regarding publication in predatory journals and how to avoid them.

In conclusion, Jordanian researchers use accepted strategies to avoid predatory journals. Implementation of a training module may enhance their ability to identify such journals.
