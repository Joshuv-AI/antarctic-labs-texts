Effect of acidic surface treatment on shear bond strength of composite cement to zirconia: a comparative study
Authors: Noor Raed Al Imam, Abdul Rahman Mohammed Saleh, Aylin Baysan, Ahmed Sleibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research assessed the effect of acidic surface treatment in two different approaches involving airborne-particle abrasion (APA) surface treatment, relative to APA by itself, on the shear bond strength (SBS) of composite cement adhered to zirconia. Sixty cubes of 5 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (10 × 10 × 8 mm) were created and categorized into three groups (n = 20 each): APA Group, treated with 50 µm Al2O3 APA; APA + ZE 30 Group, underwent the same treatment as APA Group, followed by a 30 min ultrasonic application of Zircos-E etchant (ZE); and APA + ZE 120 Group, which received the same treatment as APA Group, followed by ZE for 120 min. SEM analyses were performed on three samples for each surface treatment. Each group was subdivided (n = 10 each) to receive either self-adhesive composite cement (RelyX U200) or MDP-containing primer (G-Multi primer) combined with BisGMA-based composite cement (G-CEM LinkForce). Composite cement cylinders were cast onto zirconia samples utilizing a mold (Ø4 × 3 mm). Samples were kept in an incubator at 37ºC with purified water for 24 h. An SBS test was subsequently performed using a universal testing machine (0.5 mm/min crosshead speed). Both ZE treatment groups demonstrated a significant level of difference compared to APA (p < 0.001), while there was no notable variation between the ZE treatment groups (p = 0.841). Moreover, there was no meaningful difference observed between the two varieties of composite cements (p > 0.05). Ultimately, SEM examinations of both ZE groups showed pits and porosities. Using acidic surface etchant is promising as an additional to APA for increasing SBS of composite cement to zirconia.Keywords: Zirconia retention, Etching, Resin cement, Shear bond, Translucent zirconia

Introduction

In the last ten years, the application of yttrium-stabilized zirconia for indirect restorations has surged significantly due to the introduction of highly translucent monolithic zirconia with elevated ytteria levels, exhibiting translucency values that fall between traditional zirconia and lithium disilicates (Alammar and Blatz 2022). The flexural strength of yttria-stabilized zirconia (5Y-PSZ) is said to be lower than that of conventional zirconia, yet it surpasses the strength of lithium disilicate glass ceramics (De Angelis et al. 2020; Findakly and Jasim 2019; Harada et al. 2016). Zirconia’s polycrystalline structure, glassy phase of less than 1%, and low silicon dioxide content, make it resistant to conventional bonding strategies including acid etching and the application of silane (Dérand and Derand 2000; Özcan and Vallittu 2003). Kim and others Kim, et al., (2011) assessed surface chemical reactivity of zirconia to different resin cements. Their results showed different levels of surface energy of luting cement to zirconia surface, and there was positive correlation between cement surface energy and shear bond strength. Therefore, increasing the surface roughness of zirconia would achieve micromechanical interlock of zirconia to various resin cements, regardless to surface reactivity, perhaps, increase bond durability and restoration longevity in clinical setting (Attia 2011; Kim et al. 2011).

Different mechanical, chemical, and combined chemical–mechanical techniques such as airborne-particle abrasion (APA) with Al2O3, selective infiltration etching, silicon coatings such as tribochemical silica coating and plasma surface modification, laser irradiation, and chemical etching solutions (Asadzadeh et al. 2019; Usumez et al. 2013; Komine et al. 2010; Papia et al. 2014; El-Shrkawy et al. 2016) have been employed to enhance the bonding of composite cement to zirconia. In this respect, Zhang et al. 2021 reported that APA with 50 μm alumina particles could create reliable resin bonds. However, this protocol failed to achieve sufficient surface roughness in another study (Zhao et al. 2019). Some studies reported that a combinational surface treatment of APA followed by the application of primers containing phosphate monomers resulted in long-term durable high translucent composite cement-to-zirconia bonds (Alammar and Blatz 2022; Quigley et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2018). There is still no optimal cementation protocol for oxide-based ceramics, however (Cuevas-Suárez et al. 2020; Comino-Garayoa et al. 2021).

Zircos-E etchant (BIO DEN Co, Ltd, Geumcheon-gu, Seoul, South Korea) (ZE), consisting of various acids including hydrofluoric acid, hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, nitric acid, and phosphoric acid, was introduced to improve zirconia bond strength as a means of etching and modifying zirconia’s surface topography (Ansari et al. 2018; Cho et al. 2017; Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021; Nasr et al. 2021; Sales et al. 2022). There is no consensus regarding ZE’s effect on composite cement-to-zirconia shear bond strength (SBS). The application of 2 h of ZE alone without sandblast was reported to increase SBS for monolithic zirconia crown compared to non-treated samples, however, it was significantly lower than samples received sandblast only (Al-Qaraghuli and Gholam 2025). In a different study, applying ZE on 5Y-PSZ significantly enhanced the shear bond of composite cement to zirconia; however, no impact was observed on 3 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (Ansari et al. 2018). Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021 showed comparable bond strengths of etched zirconia disks and 50 µm Al2O3 airborne-particle abraded disks. However, the above study had only one ZE application strategy in which the immersion in the etching solution was performed for 2 h at room temperature which is in accordance to manufacture instruction. Nevertheless, the etching time could be reduced to 75% or even more by using ultrasonic bath, achieving comparable results (Xie et al. 2017). The above study applied different etching solution, hydrofluoric acid, and they reported the process ultrasonic bath assist in creation micro and nano porosity in the zirconia surface, and thereby increase SBS to resin cement. Accordingly, 30 min exposure to ZE with aid of ultrasonic effect could achieve comparable roughness effect to 2 h exposure to the same acidic solution.

This research sought to evaluate the SBS of a 50-µm Al2O3, APA surface treatment, APA with ZE etchant (30 min in ultrasonic equipment), and APA with ZE etchant (120 min without ultrasonic equipment) on 5Y-PSZ utilizing a universal testing machine. The null hypothesis stated that the use of ZE etchant on the zirconia surface would have no impact on the SBS of the composite cement.

Materials and methods

Samples preparation

5Y-PSZ zirconia blocks (DD cubeX2® ML, Dental Direkt, Spenge, Germany), with dimensions Ø98.5 * 14 mm, were machined to create 60 cubes (10 mm × 10 mm × 8 mm) employing a CAD/CAM milling machine (Imes-Icore, Hessen, Germany). The sample size was determined using data from earlier research (Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021; Sales et al. 2022) with G power 3.1.9.7 (software developed by Franz-Faul, University of Kiel, Germany), study power = 85%, alpha error of probability = 0.05 two-tailed, and the effect size of F was 0.45 (considered large effect size). Consequently, the overall sample size consisted of 60 specimens, with each group containing 20 specimens pertaining to surface treatment. Every group was additionally divided into two subgroups (n = 10 each) based on the type of resin cement used. The test cubes were sintered in a furnace (VITA ZYRCOMAT© 6000 MS, Bad Säckingen, Germany) at a temperature of 1,450 °C for 568 min, following the manufacturer's guidelines, using a scaling factor of 1.23. The cubes were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 20 each) as follows: the APA Group underwent treatment with APA using 50 µm Al2O3 (Al2O3 Cobra, Renfert, Hilzingen, Germany) at 0.3 MPa for 20 s while maintaining a 10 mm distance between the sandblast nozzle and the zirconia cube; the APA + ZE 30 Group received APA treatment followed by immersion in ZE etchant for 30 min in an ultrasonic device at 40 kHz (Sunshine, GuangZhou, China); and the APA + ZE 120 Group was treated with APA and then immersed in ZE etchant for 120 min (Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021), situated inside the ZE transparent workbox at a stable room temperature of 25ºC. All samples underwent ultrasonic cleaning in isopropyl alcohol for 5 min.

Study design

Each group was divided into two subgroups (n = 10 each) to receive either self-adhesive dual-cure composite cement (SADCC) (RelyX U200) or MDP-containing primer (G-Multi primer) in addition to a BisGMA-based dual-cure composite cement (BDCC) (G-CEM LinkForce) (Table 1). Initially, adhesive double-sided tape with a Ø4-mm hole was placed in the middle of a zirconia sample to allocate the area of primer and/or resin cement application. The SADCC (RelyX U200) cylinders were applied on the top of zirconia samples (N = 10) from each group using a mold of Ø4 mm* 3 mm height.

A mylar strip and glass slab were placed on top of the cylindrical mold already filled with composite cement. Subsequently, a static vertical load of 5.0 kg was applied over the cylindrical mold for 6 min to simulate the applied vertical pressure in a clinical setting (Cho et al. 2017). Excess cement was removed with a micro-brush and each sample was polymerized using the light curing system (1500 mW/cm2, SDI Radii Plus, Victoria, Australia) from top for 40 s (Zhao et al. 2019; Tsuka et al. 2017). The other subgroup received a BDCC (G-CEM LinkForce). Initially, a uniform layer of MDP-containing primer (G-Multi primer) was applied to the allocated area of the zirconia sample using a micro-brush, after which it was dried using an air syringe. The G-CEM LinkForce composite cement was injected inside the mold on top of the dried layer of the G-Multi primer. Subsequently, the previous steps were repeated, a static vertical load of 5.0 kg for 6 min was also applied, excess cement was removed with a micro-brush, and each sample was polymerized from top for 40 s using the same light curing device (Fig. 1) (Tsuka et al. 2017). Finally, the applied load, glass slab, mylar strip, and the mold with the masking tape were all removed, and the samples were immersed in 37 °C purified water for 24 h until they were subjected to an SBS test (Fig. 2).

The SBS test was performed with the universal testing machine (Laryee Technology Co., Ltd.) at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min until it failed. The samples were positioned and secured in the lower section of the machine horizontally, and a notched blade was subsequently set vertical to the samples’ horizontal plane, creating a 90° angle between the blade and the composite-zirconia inter-phase. The SBS values were measured in MPa, calculated by dividing the peak load in Newtons (N) at fracture by the adhesion area (mm2); the resultant value was 12.57 mm2 for each sample. The failure mode was examined with a digital microscope (DM09 LCD 1200.Tomlov, Shenzhen, China) at different magnifications (40x–75x). The modes of failure were classified as follows: adhesive failure, where over 50% of the failure results from joint rupture; cohesive failure, in which more than 50% of the failure stems from rupture within the substrates, either in the composite cement or the zirconia; and mixed failure, characterized by 50% adhesive and 50% cohesive failure (Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis

Three zirconia samples from each group were evaluated using an SEM (Inspect F50, FEI, USA). The top surface was gold sputter-coated prior to the assessment at different magnifications (× 20,000 magnification and less, HV 30.00 kV, WD 5.5 mm).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20, Chicago, USA) with 95% confidence interval (α = 0.05). The data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. SBS test results were evaluated by One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and the post hoc Tukey HSD test. A student t-test was performed to compare the SBS of the SADCC (RelyX U200) and the MDP-containing primer (G-Multi primer) plus BDCC (G-CEM LinkForce) within each surface treatment group. A Fisher’s Exact test was performed to analyse failure modes of composite cement and zirconia de-bonded samples.

SEM Analysis

The SEM analysis using × 20,000 magnification (Inspect F50, FEI, USA) for three samples from each group highlighted changes in the zirconia surfaces with disruption of its typical poly-crystals. Airborne-particle abraded surfaces (APA) showed a cloudy appearance and the presence of small micro-pits (Fig. 3a-c). The SEM analysis of APA with both ZE treatment groups demonstrated more porous surfaces with evidence of deep pits in addition to an erosive appearance (Fig. 3d-f and g-i).

Discussion

This laboratory study assessed the impact of ZE alongside 50 µm Al2O3 APA, with or without utilizing an ultrasonic device. The null hypothesis was dismissed since the SBS of the composite cement to zirconia significantly improved with the addition of ZE in both application methods, irrespective of the type of composite cement used. In this research, the bond strength was improved by applying the ZE surface treatment together with 50 µm Al2O3 APA. The APA surface treatment by itself (control group) produced the lowest average SBS. The enhanced bond strength could be linked to the greater porosity in the zirconia's top-surface layer resulting from the etching solution. With this technique, the zirconia top surface layer is ionized creating a micro-porous surface. SEM images of the APA + ZE 30 and APA + ZE 120 zirconia samples revealed porosity with irregularities and disruption of the typical polycrystalline structure (Fig. 3). In this respect, Sales et al. 2022 reported that µSBS of translucent zirconia after being subjected to the combination of 110 µm APA and 2 h immersion in ZE significantly improved in comparison to no treatment (p = 0.001) or 110 µm Al2O3 APA (p = 0.018) with a mean µSBS of 32.0 MPa. The findings of this research align with those of the study mentioned earlier. Although the mean SBS values in this study differ from those from Sales et al., this may be due to the different test conditions and/or Al2O3 particle size. In the current study, 50 µm Al2O3 particles were used and a macro SBS was performed. Another reason for the increased bond strength could be the enlarged surface area that was created by the etching solution (Sokolowski et al. 2023). These results also align with the previous study conducted on zirconia-zirconia samples luted with a dual-polymerizing hydrophilic resin cement (URC; Bioloren) that reported that SBS of 5 mol% yttria stabilized zirconia had been greatly enhanced by the use of ZE after 50 µm APA (p < 0.05) (Ansari et al. 2018).

In another study, it was reported that the use of this acidic solution increased the bond strength of 3 mol% yttria stabilized zirconia to both self-adhesive (Rely X Unicem) and self-cure (Superbond) cements (Cho et al. 2017). Lee et al. 2019 determined that the shear bond strength of composite cement to zirconia was more robust when zirconia underwent etching with a potent acid, composed of 70% nitric acid (HNO3) and 48% hydrofluoric acid, in contrast to APA alone. That strong etching of zirconia resulted in considerable surface modifications that improved the SBS of composite cement. However, Nasr et al. 2021 and Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021 found comparable bond strengths regarding APA and ZE treatments (p > 0.05), possibly due to differences in methodology, as the authors carried out the thermocycling after the storage of samples for 24 h, or differences in sample design, since the composite cylinders were cemented to zirconia samples utilizing self-bonding composite cement (SpeedCEM Plus, Ivoclar Vivadent, AG) (Sadid-Zadeh et al. 2021), while enamel of central incisors was cemented to zirconia samples using MDP-containing composite cement (Panvania V5, Kurary Noritake Dental Inc, Japan) (Nasr et al. 2021).

Ultrasonic exposure was believed to increase the rate of the zirconia etching. A shorter treatment time of 30 min (APA + ZE 30) resulted in comparable bond strength to the longer duration of 120 min (APA + ZE 120) with (p = 0.839) and (p = 0.841) for two different composite cements. Ultrasonic waves are sent to the ZE etchant via longitudinal acoustic transmission, featuring a high-frequency fluctuation in density. When tensile stress occurs, the vacuum nuclear clusters of bubbles develop in the liquid (cavitation effect) (Xie et al. 2017). The produced bubbles are compressed under force, resulting in a vigorous effect that accelerates the etching of grain boundaries and allows the ZE etchant to penetrate. This increases the permeability of the ZE etchant to the zirconia particles beneath the surface layer, leading to the creation of cracks that penetrate further along the edges of the grains. Consequently, the ultrasonic bath can enhance surface roughness and accelerate the zirconia etching process, possibly providing clinical benefits.

Concerning the type of cement, the SBS values (MPa) for samples bonded with the MDP-containing primer (G-Multi primer) combined with BDCC (G-CEM LinkForce) versus SADCC (RelyX U200) showed no statistical significance (p > 0.05). It is important to mention, however, that G Multi primer includes MDP in its formulation; incorporating the phosphate ester monomer 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10MDP) into bonding agents (primers or cements) seems to enhance the adhesion to zirconia due to the P-O-Zr chemical bonds formed between 10MDP and zirconia (Chen et al. 2012). These functional monomers have demonstrated the ability to create strong chemical bonds with the metal oxides in zirconia. Moreover, the inclusion of silane in the primer offers ideal wettability properties to the material, which may improve the initial bond strength between the composite cement and zirconia (Lima et al. 2019). It was additionally suggested that merging agents with MDP and APA surface treatment could create a stronger bond to zirconia via chemical bonding and micro-mechanical interlocking (Shahin and Kern 2010).

In this study, surface topography was assessed using SEM analyses of three samples per surface treatment group. However, roughness measurements with confocal microscopy and analyses of surface wetting were not performed which could reveal whether the increased bond strength is attributable to a wettability change or not. Another limitation of this study is that the concentration of different acids that formulate ZE etchant was not included. In addition, the effect of thermo-cycling was not evaluated. This would simulate the best scenario condition regardless of other co-variants since limited data are available in the literature to assess the effect of the highly acidic solution on zirconia SBS. Further laboratory-based and clinical studies are recommended in challenging conditions such as with different thermocycling protocols, with or without surface wetting, to extend the current findings (Fig. 4).

Conclusion

Surface etching improved adhesion, regardless of the bonding strategy used. The shear bond strength of composite cement to zirconia may be enhanced by using surface etchant in addition to airborne-particle abrasion rather than the composite cement bonding strategies.
