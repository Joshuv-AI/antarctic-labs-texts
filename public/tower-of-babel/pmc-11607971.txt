Network integration and segregation changes in schizophrenia: impact of electroconvulsive therapy
Authors: Ningning Ding, Entu Zhang, Yangyang Liu, Shuaiqi Zhang, Pei Lu, Haisan Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies have confirmed brain network topology disruption in schizophrenia (SZ). Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) rapidly improves acute psychiatric symptoms, yet the exact mechanism by which it impacts brain network topology in SZ patients remains unclear. This study aims to explore topological changes in SZ patients' whole-brain functional networks during ECT, ultimately elucidating implicated neurological mechanisms.MethodsThis study collected resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data from 53 patients with schizophrenia before and after ECT, as well as data from 46 age-, gender-, and education-matched healthy control participants (HC). Using the Brainnetome Atlas, brain functional networks were constructed for each participant. Graph theory methods were applied to measure global and nodal topological properties. Clinical symptoms of patients were assessed using the Positive And Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). Independent sample t-tests were employed to compare topological properties between patients and healthy controls, while paired t-tests were used to assess before and after ECT differences within the patient group. Finally, partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between changes in topological properties and changes in PANSS scores among patients before and after ECT.ResultsBefore ECT, compared to the HC group, the patient group demonstrated reduced local efficiency (Eloc) and clustering coefficient (Cp). In the right superior temporal gyrus, degree centrality (Dc) and nodal global efficiency (Ne) were lower, whereas in the left cingulate gyrus, Ne and Dc were higher. Following ECT, Eloc and Cp normalized in the patient group. Additionally, nodal local efficiency (NLe) and nodal clustering coefficient (NCp) increased in the bilateral superior frontal gyrus. Conversely, in the left inferior parietal lobule, Ne and Dc decreased, and nodal shortest path length (NLp) increased. Both NLe and NCp were lower in the bilateral lateral occipital cortex, both before and after ECT. However, no significant correlation was observed between changes in PANSS scores and alterations in global and nodal topological properties before and after ECT treatment.ConclusionsOur study suggests that ECT may improve psychiatric symptoms by modulating the integration and dissociation functions within damaged brain networks in SZ patients. Specifically, the balance between the integration and dissociation functions of the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and auditory networks (AN) may play a crucial role in the improvement of psychiatric symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06331-9.

Background

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a complex disorder of brain connectivity [1, 2]. The primary clinical manifestations observed in patients are characterized by positive symptoms, including delusions, hallucinations, and formal thought disorder, as well as negative symptoms, such as avolition, reduced verbal output, and diminished emotional expression [3]. These symptoms may be associated with a variety of genetic, developmental, and molecular mechanisms, and this complex etiology has hindered the development of widely effective treatments [4–6]. In clinical settings, it is observed that 20–30% of patients with schizophrenia demonstrate no significant response or show poor response to standard antipsychotic medications [7]. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a safe and effective treatment that swiftly ameliorates psychiatric symptoms in patients with SZ and prevents psychosis relapse when used in conjunction with suitable antipsychotics [8].

With the relentless advancement of examination techniques, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has become a pivotal tool for elucidating psychiatric disorder mechanisms, including schizophrenia, due to its non-invasive nature and other advantageous features [9, 10]. Previous fMRI studies on ECT treatment in SZ patients have mostly anchored on functional connectivity in individual brain regions or specific brain regions [11–13]. The human brain is one of the most complex networks of multiple brain regions working in concert to accomplish complex thinking and behavioral activities [14]. The structure and function of the brain presents a small-world network, an architecture that facilitates effective information integration and separation at low cost and high efficiency [15]. This graph-based frame work allows researchers to explore the intricate relationships and patterns within brain networks in the context of brain diseases, providing new clues for quantifying how effective treatments can repair patients' topological network configurations [16].

Previous research in functional networks has discovered that schizophrenia patients exhibit disruptions in the topology of ideal small-world networks [17, 18]. Concurrently, alterations in the characteristic path length and clustering coefficient of patients' network features have been identified in structural networks [19]. For instance, both white matter and gray matter networks show a reduction in local efficiency and clustering coefficient among patients, with white matter network topological abnormalities also negatively correlating with negative symptoms [20]. The investigations conducted by Hu et al. indicate that schizophrenia patients exhibit deviations in the nodal efficiency, degree centrality, and betweenness centrality within the right inferior occipital cortex, alongside abnormalities in the nodal efficiency specifically of the right fusiform gyrus [21]. These findings indicate that alterations in network topology may play a crucial role in the neuropathological mechanisms of SZ, potentially leading to the emergence of abnormal and uncontrollable clinical symptoms in SZ patients [22, 23]. Therefore, complex brain network analyses may provide new clues to the underlying mechanisms of electroconvulsive therapy for the rapid improvement of psychiatric symptoms in SZ patients.

Geng et al. [24], by applying graph theory attributes, discovered that the efficacy of the eighth ECT in patients with SZ could be predicted based on the response to the initial treatment. Li et al. [25] pioneered the integration of graph theory with fMRI, revealing that ECT increases the shortest path length in patients with major depressive disorder while concurrently decreasing the overall efficiency of the functional network. These studies suggest that utilizing graph theory methods to investigate the potential pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of ECT on mental health patients is a viable approach. However, research examining the relationship between ECT efficacy and the topological structure of brain functional networks in SZ patients remains limited. There is an urgent need for more evidence to validate these findings.

This study employs resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and graph theoretical methods to investigate the topological changes in both global and nodal properties of brain networks among patients with SZ undergoing ECT. Additionally, it examines the relationship between these neural changes and clinical symptomatology. We aim to uncover the neuroimaging basis of ECT in schizophrenia through studying its impact on brain network changes, providing a scientific rationale and fresh perspective for its therapeutic use.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Fifty-three SZ hospitalized patients were enrolled according to the following criteria: (1) The diagnosis aligns with the criteria for schizophrenia as specified in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), and has been confirmed by two experienced psychiatrists through a concordance assessment; (2) The condition is consistent with the indications for ECT as prescribed by the attending psychiatrist; (3) PANSS Total score ≥ 60; (4) Age 18–50, right-handed. The exclusion criteria are listed below: Other psychiatric and organic disorders, history of ECT and MRI-related contraindications. Forty-six right-handed health volunteers were recruited from the surrounding community. All health volunteers were matched to patients in terms of age, gender and years of education. Exclusion criteria for the healthy control group were personal or family history of psychiatric and organic brain diseases (including first-, second-, and third-degree relatives) and MRI-related contraindications. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University, and written informed consent was secured from the subjects or their families before participation.

All patients who met the inclusion criteria had general data information, clinical evaluation and MRI scanning collected before the first ECT (pre-ECT), and clinical evaluation and MRI scanning within 10–12 h after the eighth ECT treatment (post-ECT). In addition, general information and MRI data were collected at enrollment in the healthy control group. Patients' clinical symptoms were assessed using PANSS, which consists of three scores: positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and general psychopathology. All patients in this study had a ≥ 20% decrease in total PANSS score after final ECT treatment, and all met the response criteria.

ECT treatment

The ECT course consists of 8 sessions, administered three times per week. Before ECT treatment, chest X-ray, electrocardiogram, complete blood count, and blood biochemistry are examined, weight is measured, and routine fasting for 6 h is observed. The improved bilateral ECT protocol using Thymatron TM DGx IV (American Igel Medical Technology Company) from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University is adopted. General anesthesia is administered intravenously. Intravenous injection includes atropine 0.01 mg/kg, propofol 2 mg/kg, and succinylcholine chloride 1 mg/kg. For further ECT details, please refer to our previous studies [24, 28].

During the entire treatment period, mood stabilizers and hypnotic sedative drugs were discontinued, while the antipsychotic treatment regimen remained unchanged, including the type of medication, dosage, and timing of administration.

Resting-state fMRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Imaging data were obtained using a MAGNETOM Verio 3.0 T scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The gap between the coil and head was fixed with foam padding to reduce head movement artifacts, and rubber earplugs and special nonmagnetic earphones were used to reduce noise interference. Participants were instructed to close their eyes and remain awake. Resting-state functional images were collected using a gradient-recalled echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: repetition time/echo time = 2000ms/30ms; slice thickness/gap = 4 mm/0.8 mm; field of view = 220 × 220 mm2; image matrix = 64 × 64; and flip angle = 90°.

The rs-fMRI data underwent preprocessed via the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI DPARSF (Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI). The steps are as follows: (1) Converting DICOM format to NIFTI format; (2) The first 10 images were discarded from the dataset; (3) The remaining 230 images underwent head motion correction, slice timing correction, and normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute's standard template, with a resampling resolution of 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm; (4) The image was smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6mm × 6mm × 6mm FWHM to improve the image signal-to-noise ratio and make the image closer to the random Gaussian field model; (5) Linear and quadratic trends were removed, and nuisance covariates such as Friston 24 motion parameters, white matter signals, and cerebrospinal fluid signals regressed out; (6) Temporal data were also band-pass filtered (0.01–0.08 Hz); (7) Participants were excluded if the fMRI scan showed more than 3 mm maximum linear displacement in x, y, or z directions or 3° of angular displacement.

Definition of the functional network

GRETNA2.0 (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna), was used to construct network nodes and edges. (1) Brain network nodes were defined using the Brainnetome atlas, which divides the whole brain into 246 subregions, including 210 cortical regions and 36 subcortical regions, with each subregion representing a network node; (2) Brain network edges were defined by extracting the mean time series from each node and calculating the Pearson correlation between the mean time series of all node pairs as the brain connection (edge). This resulted in a correlation matrix of 246 × 246 for each participant; (3) Each individual correlation matrix was binarized within a certain range of connectivity density thresholds, resulting in a set of binary networks. Based on previous studies [24, 29], a sparsity threshold range of 0.05 to 0.50 with a step size of 0.05 was used, ensuring that the functional network nodes of the subjects were in a fully connected state and the small-world properties could be maximally estimated. Calculate the area under the curve (AUC) of global and nodal topological properties across the brain network over the full range of thresholds.

Statistical analysis

Differences in gender between SZ and HC groups were evaluated with a chi-square test. Age and education disparities were analyzed using independent t-tests. Topological attribute comparisons between groups were adjusted for age, gender, years of education, head motion and site as covariates. Independent t-tests assessed topological differences between patient and HC groups. Paired t-tests examined changes in topological attributes pre-ECT and post-ECT. FDR corrected global properties, and Bonferroni corrected nodal properties, with p < 0.05. Partial correlation analysis combining covariates explored the relationship between changes in topography and changes in PANSS scores before and after ECT treatment.

Comparison between post-ECT and pre-ECT

Compared with before ECT, the topological properties of the brain network of SZ patients were significantly changed after ECT.

At the global attribute level, both Eloc (p = 0.0016, FDR corrected) and Cp (p = 0.0026, FDR corrected) were significantly increased in SZ patients after ECT (Fig. 1, Table 2). At the nodal attribute level, NCp and NLe were elevated in the bilateral superior frontal gyrus of SZ patients after treatment (p < 0.001, Bonferroni corrected) (Table 2, Figs. 2 and Figs. 3). The differences in other nodal topological attributes were not statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the changes in the topological properties of functional brain networks in SZ patients before and after ECT. There were two main findings: first, the Eloc and Cp increased significantly in postECT compared to preECT. Second, the nodal properties of SZ patients, including Dc, Ne, NLe, NCp, and NLp, were profoundly affected by ECT, and the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and auditory networks (AN) played important roles in ECT treatment. Our results provide preliminary evidence for the effects of ECT on the topology of brain networks in SZ patients, suggesting that changes in the functional integration and separation among DMN, CEN, and AN have important roles in antipsychotic treatment.

Global attribute characterization changes influenced by ECT

As part of our investigation, we found that ECT can improve overall information separation by increasing the Eloc and Cp in the global attributes of SZ patients. Our findings are also supported by the results of previous studies, which found that SZ patients had reduced Eloc and lower Cp compared to healthy controls [30–32]. Both Eloc and Cp are separation metrics in graph-theoretic characterization, and reduced separation functionality is a manifestation of failures in specialized modules, which reduces robustness to nodal damage but increases wiring costs [33]. Reduced information separation function may lack information encapsulation and proper information transformation, leading to some psychiatric symptoms [33]. For example, some studies have shown that reduced dissociative functioning of brain networks is associated with positive or negative symptoms in psychopathology [34, 35]. However, existing literature reports inconsistent results regarding Cp and Eloc of SZ patient networks [20, 22]. Inconsistency may be due to different ways of constructing brain networks, differences in sample size, and so on. The specific and consistent effects of ECT on the topological organization of brain networks in SZ patients await further study in the future with a large number of homogeneous samples.

Characteristic changes in node attributes affected by ECT

In the present research, we found that compared with HC, patients with SZ had lower Ne and Dc values in the right superior temporal gyrus and higher Ne and Dc values in the left cingulate gyrus before ECT, but the differences were not significant after ECT treatment. The left Inferior parietal lobule showed a significant decrease in Ne and Dc after ECT treatment. Ne denotes the efficiency of this node in transmitting information to other nodal, and Dc reflects the importance of this nodal in the network, both of which reflect the role of functional integration and represent the efficiency of information transmission over long distances in the network of nodes [36]. The decrease of Ne and Dc indicates that the integration function of this nodal is reduced and the speed of information transfer between distant nodes in the network is affected. In contrast, Ne and NLp responded to the degree of functional integration of the brain network [37].

Previous studies have observed by structural neuroimaging that patients with schizophrenia have significantly lower gray matter volume in the right superior temporal gyrus than controls [38, 39], and the severity of the patient's auditory verbal hallucinations correlated with a reduction in the volume of the superior temporal gyrus [40], Whereas ECT combined with antipsychotic medication significantly increased the volume of gray matter in the superior temporal gyrus in patients with schizophrenia [41]. Functional neuroimaging studies have also suggested enhanced functional connectivity of the superior temporal gyrus in schizophrenia patients after ECT treatment and found that baseline categorical scores predicted final ECT outcome by constructing classifiers [42]. This is consistent with the results of the present study, both of which suggest that ECT works by modulating the structure or function of the superior temporal gyrus in patients with schizophrenia to achieve an improvement in the patients' clinical symptoms.

The cingulate gyrus is the core brain area of the DMN [43], Plays a role in executive functions such as learning, processing and memory [44]. The potential relationship between ECT-induced hyperconnectivity within the DMN and improvement in schizophrenia symptomatology has been explored, confirming that ECT controls psychiatric symptoms by modulating temporoparietal connectivity within the DMN [11]. A recent study combining MRS and fMRI techniques found that the more severe the impairment of sensory integration, the higher the cingulate glutamate level in SZ patients [45]. This is similar to the present study, i.e., Ne and Dc of cingulate gyrus were higher in the SZ patients than in the HC group before ECT, and Ne and Dc of cingulate gyrus of the patients returned to normal after treatment. This suggests that ECT may be able to achieve improvements in patients' clinical symptoms by modulating the efficiency of information integration in the default network of SZ patients.

In addition, this study found that NLe and NCp in the bilateral superior frontal gyrus were significantly elevated after ECT in SZ patients, while NLe and NCp in the bilateral extra occipital cortex were consistently lower than those in the HC group before and after treatment. In these results, NLe and NCp represent the degree of functional separation of brain networks [46], The frontal gyrus is an important hub of the CEN and is involved in many cognitive and motor control tasks. [47]. Impairment of CEN and related executive functions has been identified as an important feature of schizophrenia and may be involved in the pathology of this disorder [48, 49]. Previous functional imaging studies have found that occipital regions and superior frontal gyrus exhibit lower nodal efficiency in patients with schizophrenia by constructing dynamic brain networks [50] [51]. Jo YT et al. further showed that the lateral occipital cortex is an important region for categorizing patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls [52]. On the other hand, the occipital cortex is strongly associated with cognitive function and may be the neurobiological basis for early visual processing deficits in schizophrenia [53] [54]. These suggest that patients with schizophrenia continue to have deficits in the integration and separation of information between the CEN and the lateral occipital cortex after ECT, and that this deficit may be the very reason why patients with schizophrenia do not fully recover cognitive functioning at the end of ECT with the Inferior parietal lobule belonging to the default network [55].

Clinical research evidence of increased functional connectivity between the Inferior parietal lobule and visual and sensorimotor areas in patients with schizophrenia [56]. Liu et al. demonstrated for the first time the presence of disruptions in topological properties in schizophrenic patients by means of a cerebral blood flow covariance network, which included abnormalities in the centrality of the Dc of the inferior parietal lobule [57]. Dysfunction of the Inferior parietal lobule is thought to contribute to working memory deficits in patients with schizophrenia, which are considered central to the cognitive deficits associated with schizophrenia [58]. Previous studies have noted that ECT may cause transient cognitive impairment in patients, especially in memory [59]. This may explain the side effect of temporary memory loss in schizophrenic patients after ECT.

However, this study has several potential limitations. Firstly, there was no significant correlation observed between the clinical symptoms and brain network topology properties of the patients before and after ECT, which may indicate a time lag effect between these two factors. Secondly, the relatively small sample size in this study may elevate the risk of heterogeneity in the results. Additionally, the study did not include a comparison with a pure pharmacotherapy group, thereby hindering the ability to exclude the potential influence of medication on the outcomes. Lastly, this study solely analyzed the impact of ECT on brain network function in schizophrenic patients at a static level, neglecting changes in dynamic brain networks. Future studies should assess at various time points, expand the sample size, and incorporate a control group to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the effects of ECT on brain network function.

Conclusions

In this study, we applied graph theoretic methods to explore how ECT influences brain network topology in SZ patients. We found that ECT enhances the overall dissociative function by increasing the Eloc and Cp values in SZ patients. Furthermore, ECT modulates the integration and separation functions within the DMN, CEN, and AN, thereby improving psychotic symptoms in SZ patients. This suggests that maintaining a balance between the integration and separation states of these networks is crucial for preventing the recurrence of psychotic symptoms.
