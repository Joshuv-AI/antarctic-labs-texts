Axisymmetric stationary surfaces for the moment of inertia
Ulrich Dierkes, Rafael López

Abstract
. We investigate axisymmetric surfaces in Euclidean space that are stationary for the energy E α = ∫ Σ | p | α ​ 𝑑 Σ E_{\alpha}=\int_{\Sigma}|p|^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma . By using a phase plane analysis, we classify these surfaces when they intersect orthogonally the rotation axis. We also give some applications of the maximum principle characterizing the closed stationary surfaces and the compact stationary surfaces with boundary a circle when α = − 2 \alpha=-2 . Finally, we prove that helicoidal stationary surfaces must be rotational surfaces.

1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Euler investigated planar curves of constant density with the least powers of the moment of inertia with respect to the origin
0

[
10
]
. In polar coordinates
r=r(s)
, these curves
\gamma
are minimum of the energy
\int_{\gamma}r^{\alpha}\,ds
where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Explicit parametrizations of the extremals can be found in
[
4
,
18

\alpha=2
, the energy represents the moment of inertia with respect to
0
and the extremals are given by
ar^{3}=\mbox{sec}(3\theta+b)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. For this value of

, Mason found the minimizers joining two given points of the plane
[
14

Recently, the first author and G. Huisken have generalized the Euler’s problem in arbitrary dimensions
[
8
]
. For surfaces in Euclidean
3
-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
\Sigma
be a connected, oriented surface and consider a smooth immersion
\Phi\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, define the energy

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Sigma}|p|^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma,

where
d\Sigma
denotes the area element induced on
\Sigma
by the Euclidean metric
\langle,\rangle
and we are identifying
p\in\Sigma
with its image
\Phi(p)
by the immersion. In order to use the techniques of calculus of variations, we need to assume that the origin

\mathbb{R}^{3}
does not belong to
\Sigma

E_{\alpha}
under compactly supported variations of
\Sigma

(1)

E_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\alpha\frac{\langle\nu(p),p\rangle}{|p|^{2}}-H(p)\right)|p|^{\alpha}\xi(p)\,d\Sigma,

where
H
and

are the mean curvature and the unit normal vector of
\Sigma

\xi
is the normal component of the variational vector field of the variation. The convention for the mean curvature is that

is the sum of the principal curvatures. Thus a sphere of radius
r>0
has
H=2/r
with respect to the inward orientation. From (
1

E_{\alpha}
giving the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

A surface
\Sigma
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-\{0\}
is said to be a
stationary surface
of
E_{\alpha}
if

(2)

H(p)=\alpha\frac{\langle\nu(p),p\rangle}{|p|^{2}},\quad p\in\Sigma.

If
\alpha=0
, the energy
E_{0}
is the area functional, where stationary surfaces are minimal surfaces. We will discard the case
\alpha=0
.

In
[
8
]
the authors have studied the stability of spheres and minimal cones as well as minimizers of
E_{\alpha}
. See also
[
6
]
and generalizations to other energies in
[

]

can be viewed as critical points of a weighted area defined in

where the density is

. This implies that stationary surfaces (

The purpose of this paper is the study and classification of all axisymmetric stationary surfaces of the energy
E_{\alpha}
. By the way, we revisit the results of
[
8
]
for closed stationary surfaces, we investigate problems on compact stationary surfaces with boundary and finally we classify all helicoidal stationary surfaces.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sect.

Sections

Section

(1)

If

(2)

2.
Examples of stationary surfaces and the tangency principle

In the first part of this section, we see the type of transformations of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that preserve stationary surfaces and then we show examples of stationary surfaces.

In general, rigid motions of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
do not preserve solutions of Eq. (
2
) because of the term
|p|^{2}
. This occurs, for example, with translations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or symmetries about a plane which does not contain
0
. The following result says that stationary surfaces are preserved by vector isometries and by dilations from the origin.

Proposition 2.1
.

(1)

Let
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a vector isometry. If
\Sigma
satisfies (
2
), then
A(\Sigma)
satisfies (
2
) for the same constant
\alpha
.

(2)

Let
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a dilation from
0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
\Sigma
satisfies (
2
), then
h(\Sigma)
satisfies (
2
) for the same constant
\alpha
.

We give explicit examples of stationary surfaces, focusing on isoparametric surfaces, that is, planes, spheres and cylinders. These surfaces are the only ones with constant Gauss curvature and constant mean curvature. Among these surfaces, we find those ones that are stationary surfaces of the energy
E_{\alpha}
. Since a plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has zero mean curvature, a plane
\Sigma
is stationary for (
2
) if and only if
\langle\nu(p),p\rangle=0
for all
p\in\Sigma
. This implies that
0
is a point of the plane hence, the plane is a vector plane.

Proposition 2.2
.

A plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an
\alpha
-stationary surface if and only if it is a vector plane. This occurs for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

We now study which spheres are stationary surfaces.

Proposition 2.3
.

The only stationary spheres are the following:

(1)

any sphere centered at
0
(
\alpha=-2
);

(2)

any sphere containing
0
(
\alpha=-4
).

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a sphere of radius
r>0
centered at
q=(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A parametrization of
\Sigma
is

\Phi(s,t)=(q_{1}+r\cos s\cos t,q_{2}+r\cos s\sin t,q_{3}+r\sin s),\quad s,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
H=2/r
, a substitution in (
2
) yields a polynomial of type

A_{0}(s)+\left((4+\alpha)rq_{1}\cos s\right)\cos t+\left((4+\alpha)rq_{2}\cos s\right)\sin t=0.

for some function
A_{0}
. Consequently,

(3)

(4+\alpha)q_{1}\cos s=(4+\alpha)q_{2}\cos s=0

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We have the following discussion of cases.

(1)

Case
\alpha=-4
. Now Eq. (
2
) is simply
|q|^{2}-r^{2}=0
. This is equivalent to say that the sphere contains the origin.

(2)

Case
\alpha\not=-4
. Then (
3
) implies
q_{1}=q_{2}=0
. Now Eq. (
2
) is

2q_{3}^{2}+(2+\alpha)r^{2}+rq_{3}(4+\alpha)\sin s=0.

Since this holds for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
2q_{3}^{2}+(2+\alpha)r^{2}=0
and
rq_{3}(4+\alpha)=0
. It follows that
q_{3}=0
and
\alpha=-2
. Thus the center
q
of the sphere is the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The converse is straightforward, that is, the spheres of items (1) and (2) are stationary surfaces.
∎

Let us point out that it is implicitly assumed that the origin is not included in the vector planes of Prop.
2.2
neither in the spheres of Prop.
2.3
. Finally we see that there are no stationary circular cylinders.

Proposition 2.4
.

No circular cylinders are stationary surfaces.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a circular cylinder of radius

and suppose that
\Sigma
also is an
\alpha
-stationary surface. Using (1) of Prop.
2.3
, and after a vector isometry of

, we can assume that the rotation axis of the cylinder is parallel to the

-axis and that the axis is included in the

\Sigma

\Phi(s,t)=(q_{1}+r\cos t,q_{2}+r\sin t,s),\quad s,t\in\mathbb{R}.

We know that

and substituting in (

q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}+r(\alpha+2)\left(q_{1}\cos(t)+q_{2}r\sin(t)\right)+(1+\alpha)r^{2}+s^{2}=0.

This is a polynomial equation on

, whose leader coefficient is

∎

We focus on closed
\alpha
-stationary surfaces. In
[
8

the authors proved the following result:

(1)

If
\alpha>-2
, then there are no closed stationary surfaces.

(2)

If
\alpha=-2
, then the only stable closed stationary surfaces are spheres centered at the origin.

(3)

Let

. If

For the proof of (1) and (3), the authors used the maximum principle for the Laplacian of the function

. If

. We now revisit these results using a suitable statement of the maximum principle and proving that the condition on stability on (2) can be dropped.

We view the class of stationary surfaces of the energy
E_{\alpha}
in the context of the theory of manifolds with density. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, consider a positive density
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
used to weight volume and surface area. The new weighted volume and weighted area are given by
dV_{\phi}=\phi dV_{0}
and
dA_{\phi}=\phi dA_{0}
, where
dV_{0}
and
dA_{0}
are the Euclidean volume and area of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let us observe that this is not equivalent to scaling the metric conformally by the factor
\phi
because the exponent of
\phi
in
dV_{\phi}
and
dA_{\phi}
is the same. A surface
\Sigma
is a critical point of
A_{\phi}
for any volume-preserving variation of
\Sigma
if and only if its
weighted mean curvature

H_{\phi}
is constant, where
H_{\phi}
is defined by

(4)

H_{\phi}=H-\langle\nu,\overline{\nabla}\phi\rangle,

where
\overline{\nabla}
is the gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(
[
2
,
15
]
). By choosing
\phi(p)=|p|^{\alpha}
, then
H_{\phi}=0
coincides with Eq. (
2

E_{\alpha}
are weighted minimal surfaces (
H_{\phi}=0
) for the density
|p|^{\alpha}
. Standard theory of elliptic equations can be applied Eq. (
4
): see the general reference
[
11
]
. In the following result, we use the maximum principle to compare two surfaces that are tangent at some point
[
16
]
. Denote by
\partial\Sigma
the boundary of
\Sigma
and let
\mbox{int}(\Sigma)=\Sigma-\partial\Sigma
be the set of interior points of
\Sigma
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
be two oriented surfaces tangent at some interior point
p\in\Sigma_{1}\cap\Sigma_{2}
such that
\nu_{1}(p)=\nu_{2}(p)
. If
\Sigma_{1}
lies above
\Sigma_{2}
around
p
according to
\nu_{i}(p)
, denoted by
\Sigma_{1}\geq\Sigma_{2}
, then
H_{\phi}^{1}(p)\geq H_{\phi}^{2}(p)
(comparison principle). In addition, if
H_{\phi}^{1}=H_{\phi}^{2}=\mbox{constant}
, then
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
coincide in an open set around

(tangency principle). The same statement holds if

\partial\Sigma_{1}
and
\partial\Sigma_{2}
are tangent at
p
.

If both surfaces satisfy
H_{\phi}=0
, that is, they are stationary surfaces for the same value of
\alpha
, and
\Sigma_{1}\geq\Sigma_{2}
around a point, no matter what the orientation is, because reversing the orientation, the equation

is preserved. We show some applications of the tangency principle.

In the theory of surfaces with constant mean curvature, the tangency principle is employed in the reflection technique. Alexandrov proved that spheres are the only embedded closed surfaces with constant mean curvature
[
1
]
. However, this technique fails for stationary surfaces because reflections about planes do not preserve the solutions of (

2.1

[
8
]
, and improve the case
\alpha=-2
, where we drop the stability condition assumed there.

Theorem 2.6
.

Spheres centered at the origin are only closed
\alpha
-stationary surfaces.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed
\alpha
-stationary surface. Let
r>0
be sufficiently big so
\Sigma
is included in the round ball determined by the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r)
of radius
r>0
centered at the origin. Letting
r\searrow 0
, we arrive until the first touching point with
\Sigma
. Suppose that this occurs for
r=r_{0}
and let
p\in\Sigma\cap\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{0})
. Consider the weighted mean curvature

H_{\phi}
given in (
4
). With respect to the inward orientation on
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r)
, we have

H_{\phi}^{\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{0})}(p)=\frac{2}{r_{0}}-\alpha\frac{\langle\nu(p),p\rangle}{|p|^{2}}=\frac{2+\alpha}{r_{0}}.

For
\Sigma
, we have
H_{\phi}^{\Sigma}=0
regardless the orientation on
\Sigma
. Since
\Sigma\geq\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{0})
around
p
, the comparison principle gives

0\geq\frac{2+\alpha}{r_{0}}.

This yields
\alpha\leq-2
. If
\alpha=-2
, the tangency principle implies that
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{0})
and
\Sigma
coincide in an open set around
p
. By connectedness, we conclude
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{0})=\Sigma
.

Finally, we prove that there are no closed
\alpha
-stationary surfaces if
\alpha<-2
. Let
r>0
be sufficiently small such that
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r)\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
. Let
r\nearrow\infty
until the first touching point with
\Sigma
at
r=r_{1}
. With the inward orientation on
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{1})
, we have
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{1})\geq\Sigma
around the contact point and the comparison principle yields

\frac{2+\alpha}{r_{1}}\geq 0.

This implies
\alpha\geq-2
, which it is a contradiction.

∎

Corollary 2.7

Let

Applications of the tangency principle

such that

\alpha
) and spherical caps in spheres (
\alpha=-2,-4
). As we will prove in Sect.

\alpha
. This is obtained by taking suitable pieces of rotational stationary surfaces that intersect orthogonally the rotation axis: see Fig.
1

Proposition 3.1

Let
\Sigma
be a compact
\alpha
-stationary surface whose boundary is a circle centered at the

\alpha>-1
, then
\Sigma
contained in the vertical cylinder

Proof.

Let

whose rotation axis is the

H_{\phi}^{C_{r}}(p)=\frac{1}{r}-\alpha\frac{\langle\nu(p),p\rangle}{|p|^{2}}=\frac{|p|^{2}+\alpha r^{2}}{r|p|^{2}}=\frac{(1+\alpha)r^{2}+z^{2}}{r|p|^{2}}.

If

, then

. Let

be sufficiently big so
\Sigma
is included in the domain bounded by

, consider the first cylinder

\Sigma

. Suppose that

is an interior point of
\Sigma

around

when
\alpha\geq-2
. We give a proof that it only uses the divergence theorem, which it is weaker than the tangency principle

Proposition 3.4
.

(1)

If
\alpha>-2
, there are not closed stationary surfaces.

(2)

If
\alpha=-2
, spheres centered at
0
are the only closed stationary surfaces.

Proof.

For any surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Laplacian of the restriction of the norm
|p|^{2}
on
\Sigma
satisfies

\Delta|p|^{2}=4+2H\langle\nu(p),p\rangle.

Suppose that
\Sigma
is an
\alpha
-stationary surface. By using Eq. (
2
), we obtain

(5)

\Delta|p|^{2}=4+2\alpha\frac{\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}}{|p|^{2}}.

(1)

Suppose that
\Sigma
is a closed surface and that
\alpha>-2
. Using the divergence theorem in (
5
) and because
\partial\Sigma=\emptyset
, we have

0=\int_{\Sigma}\left(4+2\alpha\frac{\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}}{|p|^{2}}\right)\,d\Sigma=2\int_{\Sigma}\frac{2|p|^{2}+\alpha\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}}{|p|^{2}}\,d\Sigma.

If
\alpha\geq 0
, this is not possible. If
-2<\alpha<0
and because
\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}\leq|p|^{2}
and
\alpha<0
, we have
\alpha\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}\geq\alpha|p|^{2}
for all
p\in\Sigma
. This yields

0=2\int_{\Sigma}\left(\frac{2|p|^{2}+\alpha\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}}{|p|^{2}}\right)\,d\Sigma\geq 2(2+\alpha)\mbox{area}(\Sigma)>0,

obtaining a contradiction.

(2)

If
\alpha-2
, the same computation yields

0=2\int_{\Sigma}\frac{2|p|^{2}-2\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}}{|p|^{2}}\,d\Sigma\geq 0.

This implies
\langle\nu(p),p\rangle^{2}=|p|^{2}
for all
p\in\Sigma
, hence
\Sigma
is a sphere centered at
0
.

∎

4.
The rotation axis of a stationary rotational surface

In the next sections, we investigate axisymmetric stationary surfaces, that is, stationary surfaces invariant by a one-parametric group of rotations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In the definition of the energy
E_{\alpha}
, the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a privileged point because the moment of inertial is calculated with respect to
0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In principle there is not
a priori
a relation between the rotation axis of an axisymmetric stationary surface and the origin. For example, if
\alpha=-4
, we know that any sphere containing the origin is stationary and this sphere is a surface of revolution with respect to any line through its center which may not cross
0
. However, in the next result we prove that this the only exception.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be an axisymmetric surface about an axis
L
. If
\Sigma
is stationary, then either
0\in L
or
\Sigma
is a sphere containing
0
.

Proof.

Using Prop.
2.1
and after a vector isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we can assume that the rotation axis
L
is parallel to the
z
-axis and contained in the
xz
-plane coordinate. Thus the equations of
L
are
\{x=q_{1},y=0\}
, with
q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. A parametrization of
\Sigma
is

\Phi(s,t)=(q_{1}+x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)),\quad s\in I,t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\gamma(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
,
s\in I\subset\mathbb{R}
, is the generating curve of
\Sigma
. We need to prove that
q_{1}=0
(and thus
0\in L
) or that
\Sigma
is a sphere with
0\in\Sigma
.

Without loss of generality, we suppose that
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length. Since
x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+z^{\prime}(s)^{2}=1
, there is a smooth function
\psi
such that

\begin{split}x^{\prime}(s)&=\cos\psi(s),\\
z^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\psi(s).\end{split}

We calculate all terms of the stationary surface equation (
2
). The unit normal vector of
\Sigma
is

\nu=(-\sin\psi\cos t,-\sin\psi\sin t,\cos\psi).

The principal curvatures of
\Sigma
are

(6)

\kappa_{1}=\psi^{\prime},\quad\kappa_{2}=\frac{\sin\psi}{x},

and thus the mean curvature is

H=\psi^{\prime}+\frac{\sin\psi}{x}.

Equation (
2
) becomes

A_{0}(s)+A_{1}(s)\cos t=0,

where

(7)

\begin{split}A_{1}&=xq_{1}(2x\psi^{\prime}+(2+\alpha)\sin\psi)\\
A_{0}&=\sin\psi\left(q_{1}^{2}+(\alpha+1)x^{2}+z^{2}\right)+x\psi^{\prime}(q_{1}^{2}+x^{2}+z^{2})-\alpha xz\cos\psi.\end{split}

Therefore we have
A_{0}(s)=A_{1}(s)=0
for all
s\in I
. From the equation
A_{1}=0
, we have two possibilities. If
q_{1}=0
, then the rotation axis
L
is the
z
-axis which contains the origin. This proves the result in the first case.

Suppose
q_{1}\not=0
. Then
A_{1}=0
implies
2x\psi^{\prime}+(2+\alpha)\sin\psi=0
identically in
I
. This gives

(8)

\psi^{\prime}=-\frac{(2+\alpha)\sin\psi}{2x}.

Moreover, by substituting the value of
\psi^{\prime}
in the expression of
A_{0}
, we have

(9)

\sin\psi(q_{1}^{2}-x^{2}+z^{2})+2xz\cos\psi=0.

We solve this equation. Since our study is local, let us write
\gamma
as a graph on the
z
-axis. First we need to ensure that
\sin\psi\not=0
. In case that
\sin\psi=0
identically, then
z=z(s)
is a constant function. This implies that
\gamma
is a horizontal line and thus
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane. Since
\Sigma
is stationary, this plane is the plane of equation
z=0
. In particular, this surface is rotational about the
z
-axis, proving the result in this particular case.

Finally, suppose
\sin\psi\not=0
. Then we write
\gamma
as the graph of a function
u
on the
x
-axis by letting
r=x(s)
,
u=z(s)
and
u=u(r)
. Then we have
u^{\prime}=\cot\psi
. We will prove that
\Sigma
is a sphere with
0\in\Sigma
. Equation (
9
) is

2ruu^{\prime}-u^{2}+r^{2}+q_{1}^{2}=0.

The solution of this ODE is

(10)

u(r)=\sqrt{q_{1}^{2}-r^{2}+rc},\quad c\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, the generating curve of
\Sigma
is
\gamma(r)=(q_{1}+u(r),0,r)
. It follows that
\gamma
is a circle contained in the
(x,z)
-plane of center
(q_{1},\frac{c}{2})
of radius
\frac{\sqrt{c^{2}+4q_{1}^{2}}}{2}
. In consequence,
\Sigma
is a sphere containing
0
. This proves the result. If we follow further, we have

u^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{\psi^{\prime}}{\sin^{3}\psi}.

Since
\sin^{2}\psi=\frac{1}{1+u^{\prime 2}}
, equation (
8
) writes as

\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}=\frac{2+\alpha}{2u}.

Substituting the value of
u(r)
given in (
10
), we arrive to

(4+\alpha)(4q_{1}^{2}+c^{2})=0
. This implies
\alpha=-4
because
4q_{1}^{2}+c^{2}>0
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In case that the rotation axis does not cross the origin, then
\alpha=-4
and
\Sigma
is a sphere. We will see in Sect.
6
that there are non-spherical stationary surfaces for
\alpha=-4
which are axisymmetric about the
z
-axis.

5.
Axisymmetric stationary surfaces intersecting the rotation axis

In this section we prove the existence of axisymmetric stationary surfaces intersecting orthogonally the rotation axis.
Let
\Sigma
be an axisymmetric stationary surface. By Prop.
4.1
, we know that the rotation axis crosses
0
. After a linear isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(Prop.
2.1
) we can assume that the rotation axis is the
z
-axis.
A parametrization of
\Sigma
is

\Phi(s,t)=(x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\gamma(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
is the generating curve and
x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}=1
. Computing again (
2
) or equivalently, considering the equation
A_{0}=0
in (
7
), we have

(11)

\psi^{\prime}+\frac{\sin\psi}{x}=\alpha\frac{z\cos\psi-x\sin\psi}{x^{2}+z^{2}}.

It is natural to expect the existence of solutions intersecting orthogonally the rotation axis. However, equation (
11
) is singular at
x=0
because of the left hand-side, hence standard arguments do not assure existence of solutions.

The method that we use to prove the existence of these solutions is the fixed point theorem. Since we require that the intersection of
\gamma
is orthogonal to the
z
-axis, we can assume that
\gamma
is a graph on the
x
-axis. Thus we change the parametrization of the generating curve
\gamma
by
\gamma(r)=(r,0,u(r))
, where
u=u(r)
is a smooth function defined in an subinterval of
(0,\infty)
. Now Eq. (
11
) writes as

(12)

\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{(1+u^{\prime 2})^{3/2}}+\frac{u^{\prime}}{r\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}}}=\frac{\alpha(u-ru^{\prime})}{(r^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}}}.

Multiplying by
r
, this equation becomes

\left(\dfrac{ru^{\prime}(r)}{\sqrt{1+u^{\prime}(r)^{2}}}\right)^{\prime}=\alpha\frac{r(u-ru^{\prime})}{(r^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}}}.

This equation is singular at
r=0
. Let
r_{0}\geq 0
and consider the initial value problem

(13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\dfrac{ru^{\prime}(r)}{\sqrt{1+u^{\prime}(r)^{2}}}\right)^{\prime}=\alpha\dfrac{r(u-ru^{\prime})}{(r^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}}},&\mbox{ in $(r_{0},r_{0}+\delta)$}\\
u(r_{0})=u_{0}&\\
u^{\prime}(r_{0})=0,&\end{array}\right.

where
u_{0}>0
. The following theorem proves the existence of axisymmetric stationary surfaces intersecting orthogonally the rotation axis.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
u_{0}>0
the initial value problem (
13
) with
r_{0}=0
has a solution
u\in C^{2}([0,R])
for some
R>0
. Moreover, the solution depends continuously on the parameters
\alpha
and
u_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}=\{r\in\mathbb{R}\colon r\geq 0\}
. Define the functions
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

f(y)=\frac{y}{\sqrt{1+y^{2}}},\qquad g(x,y,z)=\frac{\alpha(y-xz)}{(x^{2}+y^{2})\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}.

It is clear that a function
u\in C^{2}([0,R])
, for some
R>0
, is a solution of (
13
) if and only if

\left\{\begin{split}(rf(u^{\prime}))^{\prime}&=rg(r,u,u^{\prime})\\
u(0)&=u_{0}\\
u^{\prime}(0)&=0.\end{split}\right.

By means of the inverse
f^{-1}
and integrating the first equation, the function
u
is given by

u(r)=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}tg(t,u,u^{\prime})\,dt\right)\,ds.

Fix
R>0
to be determined later. For
u\in C^{1}([0,R])
, define the operator
\mathsf{T}
by

(\mathsf{T}u)(r)=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{t}{s}g(t,u,u^{\prime})\,dt\right)\,ds.

It is clear that a fixed point
u
of
\mathsf{T}
,
\mathsf{T}u=u
, is a solution of the initial value problem (
13
). The existence of the fixed point will be proved by using the Banach fixed point theorem. Let
C^{1}([0,R])
endowed with the norm
\|u\|=\|u\|_{\infty}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. We will prove that
\mathsf{T}
is a contraction mapping in a closed ball about
u_{0}
,
\overline{\mathcal{B}(u_{0},\epsilon)}\subset C^{1}([0,R])
for some
\epsilon>0
.

We consider the next three steps.

(1)

Prove that
\mathsf{T}
is well-defined. Since

f^{-1}\colon(-1,1)\to\mathbb{R},\quad f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

we need to check

\left|\int_{0}^{s}\frac{t}{s}g(t,u,u^{\prime})\,dt\right|<1,\mbox{ for all $r\in[0,R]$}.

Let
\epsilon<\{1,u_{0}\}
. Consider the functions
f^{-1}
and
g
defined in
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
and
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\times[u_{0}-\epsilon,u_{0}+\epsilon]\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
, respectively. Let
M>0
denote an upper bound of
g
obtained as follows:

|g|\leq\frac{|\alpha|}{(u_{0}-\epsilon)^{2}}(u_{0}+\epsilon+\epsilon^{2})\leq\frac{|\alpha|(u_{0}+2)}{(u_{0}-\epsilon)^{2}}:=M.

Let
R
be a positive number such that
R<\frac{1}{M}
. For all
s\in[0,R]
, we have

\left|\int_{0}^{s}\frac{t}{s}g(t,u,u^{\prime})\,dt\right|\leq\frac{Ms}{2}\leq\frac{MR}{2}<\frac{1}{2},

which leads that
T
is well-defined.

(2)

Prove the existence of
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathsf{T}(\overline{\mathcal{B}(u_{0},\epsilon)})\subset\overline{\mathcal{B}(u_{0},\epsilon)}
. First, we change the value of
R
by another one satisfying

(14)

R<\min\{\frac{1}{M},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\epsilon,\frac{2\epsilon}{M\sqrt{4+\epsilon^{2}}}\}.

Since
f^{-1}
is an increasing function

|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)-u_{0}|\leq\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{t}{s}M\,dt\right)\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\frac{Ms}{2}\right)\,ds\leq\frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}.

By using (
14
), we have

|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)-u_{0}|\leq\epsilon/2
. Thus
\|\mathsf{T}u-u_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. Similarly, we have

\begin{split}|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}-(u_{0})^{\prime}(r)|&\leq f^{-1}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t}{r}g(t,u,u^{\prime})\,dt\right|\right)\leq\left|f^{-1}\left(\frac{M}{2}r\right)\right|\\
&\leq\frac{MR}{\sqrt{4-M^{2}R^{2}}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\end{split}

because (
14
) again. Then
\|(\mathsf{T}u-u_{0})^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon/2
.
Definitively, we have proved
\|(\mathsf{T}u-u_{0})\|\leq\epsilon
.

(3)

Prove that
\mathsf{T}\colon\overline{\mathcal{B}(u_{0},\epsilon)}\to\overline{\mathcal{B}(u_{0},\epsilon)}
is a contraction.
Denote
L_{f^{-1}}
and
L_{g}
the Lipschitz constants of
f^{-1}
and
g
in their respective domains
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
and
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\times[u_{0}-\epsilon,u_{0}+\epsilon]\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
, respectively. For all
u,w\in C^{1}([0,R])
, we have

\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|=\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|_{\infty}+\|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}-(\mathsf{T}w)^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

Consider the first term
\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|_{\infty}
. Given
u,w\in\overline{\mathcal{B}(u_{0},\epsilon)}
and
r\in[0,R]
, we have

(15)

\begin{split}|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)-(\mathsf{T}w)(r)|&\leq L_{f^{-1}}\left|\int_{0}^{r}\left(\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t}{s}(g(t,u,u^{\prime})-g(t,w,w^{\prime}))\,dt\right)\,ds\right|\\
&\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}(\|u-w\|_{\infty}+\|u^{\prime}-w^{\prime}\|_{\infty})\int_{0}^{r}(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{t}{s}\,dt)\,ds\\
&=L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\frac{r^{2}}{4}\|u-w\|\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\frac{R^{2}}{4}\|u-w\|.\end{split}

For the term
\|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}-(\mathsf{T}w)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
, the argument is similar. Indeed,

(16)

\begin{split}|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}(r)-(\mathsf{T}w)^{\prime}(r)|&\leq\left|f^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t}{r}(g(u,u^{\prime})-g(w,w^{\prime}))\,dt\right)\right|\\
&\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t}{r}\,dt=L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\frac{r}{2}\|u-w\|\\
&\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\frac{R}{2}\|u-w\|.\end{split}

Let us change
R
by the condition (
14
) together

R\leq\min\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}}},\frac{1}{2L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}}\}.

Then we find from (
15
) and (
16
),
\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|u-w\|
and
\|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}-(\mathsf{T}w)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|u-w\|
, respectively. This gives

\|Tu-Tw\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u-w\|.

This proves that
\mathsf{T}
is a contraction.

It remains to prove that the solution
u
extends with
C^{2}
-regularity at
r=0
. Let
r\to 0
in Eq. (
12
). By L’Hôpital rule, we have

(17)

2u^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{\alpha}{u_{0}},

hence
u^{\prime\prime}(0)=\alpha/(2u_{0})
.
∎

In Thm.
5.1
, we have proved existence of radial solutions of (
13
) in discs
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
for sufficiently small radius
R
. By the way, we have proved regularity of solutions of (
13
) in case that the solution
u=u(r)
arrives until
r=0
under the condition
u^{\prime}(0)=0
. Notice that the solutions of (
13
) with initial conditions at
r_{0}>0
may not extend until
r=0
. This is the case for
\alpha>-2
. In Fig.
2
, we show the solutions of (
13
) when
r_{0}=1
. The initial conditions are
u(1)=1
and
u^{\prime}(1)=0
and the solutions cannot extend until
r=0
.

Figure 2.
Two solutions of (
13
) which cannot extend to
r=0
. The initial conditions are
r_{0}=u_{0}=1
and
\alpha=1
(left) and
\alpha=-1
(right).

In case that a solution of (
13
) with
r_{0}>0
can be extended to
r=0
, regularity at
r=0
is not assured. In the following theorem we address the problem of removable singularities for Eq. (
2
). Here we follow the general theory for solutions of mean curvature equation type. The typical example is the minimal surface equation when the singularity consists in a single point. It is well known that under this situation, the singular point can be removed
[
3
,
17
]
.

We use the notation
q=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
for points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
(\cdot)
for the Euclidean product of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. First, we write Eq. (
2
) in nonparametric way

(18)

\mbox{div}\left(\frac{Du}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\right)=\alpha\frac{u-(q\cdot Du)}{(x^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}},

where the graph of
u=u(x,y)
is an
\alpha
-stationary surface.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
u
be a
C^{2}
-solution of (
18
) defined in a punctured disk
B_{r}(0)-\{0\}
which is Lipschitz continuous on
B_{r}(0)
. Then
u
is analytic on
B_{r}(0)
.

Proof.

We prove that
u
is a weak Lipschitz-solution of (
18
) on
B_{r}(0)
. Choose a sequence
\{\eta_{n}\}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{r}(0))
with the following properties:

(1)

\mbox{supp}(\eta_{n})\subset B_{r}(0)-\{0\}
, in particular,
\eta_{n}=0
identically in a small neighborhood around
0
;

(2)

0\leq\eta_{n}\leq 1
and
\eta_{n}\to 1
a.e. on
B_{r}(0)
;

(3)

||D\eta_{n}||_{1,B_{r}}=\int_{B_{r}(0)}|D\eta_{n}|\,dxdy\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{r}(0))
be arbitrary and put
\phi_{n}=\varphi\eta_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\phi_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{r}(0)-\{0\})

and (
18
) yields the relation

(19)

\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{Du\cdot D\phi_{n}}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\,dxdy=\alpha\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{((q\cdot Du)-u)\phi_{n}}{(x^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\,dxdy.

Observing that
D\phi_{n}=\eta_{n}D\varphi+\varphi D\eta_{n}
, we conclude from (
19
) and (2), (3) the relation

\int_{B_{r}}\frac{Du\cdot D\varphi}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\,dxdy=\alpha\int_{B_{r}}\frac{(q\cdot Du)-u}{(x^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\varphi\,dxdy.

This means that
u
is a Lipschitz continuous solution of (
18
) which, in addition, is of class
C^{2}
on the punctured disc
B_{r}(0)-\{0\}
. By elliptic regularity theory, this implies that
u
is of class
C^{2}
in
B_{r}(0)
and hence analytic in
B_{r}(0)
.

∎

As a consequence of this theorem, if a radial solution
u
of (
11
) can be extended until
r=0
and intersects the
z
-axis non-tangentially, then this solution is analytic in the disk
B_{r_{0}}(0)
and consequently, the intersection with the rotation axis must be orthogonal. In case that
\gamma
intersects the
z
-axis tangentially, one can show that the rotational graph
u
of
\gamma
is a weak solution of (
18
) which is of class
C^{2}(B_{r}(0)-\{0\})\cap H_{1}^{1}(B_{r}(0))

[
7
]
.

6.
Geometry properties of axisymmetric stationary surfaces

In this section we give a classification of the axisymmetric solutions of Eq. (
2
) when the solution intersects orthogonally the rotation axis, obtaining their main geometric properties. Suppose that the generating curve
\gamma
of the surface is parametrized by arc-length,
\gamma(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
,
x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}=1
. By using (
11
), the ODE system that determines the generating curve
\gamma
is

(20)

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}(s)&=\cos\psi(s),\\
z^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\psi(s),\\
\psi^{\prime}(s)&=\alpha\frac{z(s)\cos\psi(s)-x(s)\sin\psi(s)}{x(s)^{2}+z(s)^{2}}-\frac{\sin\psi(s)}{x(s)}.\end{split}\right.

The initial conditions are
x(0)=0
and
\psi(0)=0
(orthogonal condition). By Prop.
2.1
, after a vector isometry we can suppose that
z(0)>0
, and after a dilation, let
z(0)=1
. Definitively, consider initial values at
s=0

(21)

\left\{\begin{split}(x(0),z(0))&=(0,1),\\
\psi(0)&=0.\end{split}\right.

In many of the next arguments, we will analyze the phase plane of an autonomous system. For this, it is convenient to introduce the polar angle
\theta
determined by the condition

(22)

\tan\theta=\frac{z}{x}.

By using the first two equations of (
20
), we have

\theta^{\prime}=\cos^{2}\theta\frac{\sin\psi-\tan\theta\cos\psi}{x}=\frac{\cos\theta}{x}\sin(\psi-\theta).

On the other hand, the third equation in (
20
) can be written as

\psi^{\prime}=\alpha\frac{\tan\theta\cos\psi-\sin\psi}{x(1+\tan^{2}\theta)}-\frac{\sin\psi}{x}=-\frac{\alpha\cos\theta\sin(\psi-\theta)+\sin\psi}{x}.

Then

\frac{d\psi}{d\theta}=\frac{\psi^{\prime}(s)}{\theta^{\prime}(s)}=-\frac{\alpha\cos\theta\sin(\psi-\theta)+\sin\psi}{\cos\theta\sin(\psi-\theta)}.

This gives the planar autonomous ordinary system

(23)

\left\{\begin{split}\frac{d\psi}{dt}&:=h_{1}(\psi,\theta)=-\sin\psi-\alpha\cos\theta\sin(\psi-\theta),\\
\frac{d\theta}{dt}&:=h_{2}(\psi,\theta)=\cos\theta\sin(\psi-\theta).\end{split}\right.

We study this system according to the Bendixson-Poincaré theory. From the equation

\begin{split}P_{1}&=(2n\pi,k\pi),\\

P_{3}&=(n\pi,\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi),\\
\end{split}

where

. We study the linearized system at a neighbourhood of the equilibrium points. At the points

\frac{\partial(h_{1},h_{2})}{\partial(\psi,\theta)}(P_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}-1-\alpha&\alpha\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

According to the eigenvalues, we have the following possibilites.

(1)

are stable nodes.

(2)

If

For the points

\frac{\partial(h_{1},h_{2})}{\partial(\psi,\theta)}(P_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}1+\alpha&-\alpha\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

The type of equilibrium points are:

(1)

If
\alpha>0

(2)

7.
Helicoidal stationary surfaces

In this section we study helicoidal stationary surfaces. A helicoidal motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a rotation about some line followed by a translation parallel to that line. A helicoidal surface
\Sigma
in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a surface invariant under a one-parameter group of helicoidal motions. The helicoidal group is determined by the twist axis and its pitch
h\in\mathbb{R}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that the twist axis is the
z
-axis. Then this group is
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
where
\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is given by

(28)

\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y,z)=(x\cos t-y\sin t,x\sin t+y\cos t,z+ht).

If
h=0
, then
\Sigma
is simply a surface of revolution about the
z
-axis. In the following result we see that the only helicoidal stationary surfaces are rotational surfaces. As in the case of rotational stationary surfaces, there is not an a priori relation between the twist axis of a helicoidal stationary surface and the origin
0
.

Theorem 7.1
.

If
\Sigma
is a helicoidal stationary surface, then
h=0
, that is,
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution.

Proof.

The proof is by contradiction. Let
\Sigma
be a helicoidal stationary surface such that
h\not=0
. Thanks to Prop.
2.1
, and after a vector isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we can assume that the twist axis
L
is parallel to the
z
-axis and contained in the
xz
-plane. If the equations of
L
are
\{x=q_{1},y=0\}
,
q_{1}\geq 0
, the helicoidal motions are as in (
28
) but moving the twist axis to
L
. Then

\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y,z)=(q_{1}+(x-q_{1})\cos t-y\sin t,(x-q_{1})x\sin t+y\cos t,z+ht).

The generating curve
\gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a planar curve contained in the
xz
-plane. Let
\gamma(s)=(q_{1}+x(s),0,z(s))
, where
x,z
are smooth functions and
x(s)>q_{1}
. Then a parametrization of
\Sigma
is

(29)

\Phi(s,t)=(q_{1}+x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)+ht).

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length. Then
x^{\prime}(s)=\cos\psi(s)
and

z^{\prime}(s)=\sin\psi(s)
for some smooth function
\psi
. Regularity of
\Sigma
is given by the condition
W:=x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\psi>0
. The unit normal vector
\nu
is

(30)

\nu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}\left(h\cos\psi\sin t-x\sin\psi\cos t,-h\cos\psi\cos t-x\sin\psi\sin t,x\cos\psi\right),

and the mean curvature
H
is

(31)

H=\frac{x(x^{2}+h^{2})\psi^{\prime}+\sin\psi\left(x^{2}+2h^{2}\cos^{2}\psi\right)}{W^{3/2}}.

After some computations, equation (
2
) becomes

(32)

A_{0}(s)+A_{1}(s)t+A_{2}(s)t^{2}+A_{3}(s)\sin t+A_{4}(s)\cos t=0,

where
A_{n}=A_{n}(s)
are smooth functions on the variable
s\in I
. Since the functions
\{1,t,t^{2},\sin t,\cos t\}
are linearly independent, then all coefficients
A_{n}
must vanish. A computation gives

A_{3}=\alpha hq_{1}\cos\psi W.

Equation
A_{3}=0
gives two cases to discuss.

(1)

Case
q_{1}=0
. Then the twist axis is the
z
-axis. With this value of
q_{1}
, we have

A_{2}=h^{2}\left(x(x^{2}+h^{2})\psi^{\prime}+\sin\psi\left(x^{2}+2h^{2}\cos^{2}\psi\right)\right).

Since
h\not=0
, we can get
\psi^{\prime}
from
A_{2}=0
, obtaining

\psi^{\prime}=-\frac{\sin\psi(x^{2}+2h^{2}\cos^{2}\psi)}{x(h^{2}+x^{2})}.

Substituting in
A_{1}
, we have

A_{1}=\alpha hx\cos\psi W.

Equation
A_{1}=0
yields
\cos\psi=0
identically. Since
x^{\prime}(s)=\cos\psi(s)
, we deduce that the function
x=x(s)
is constant. If
x(s)=x_{0}
, then
A_{2}=x_{0}^{2}h^{2}
and
A_{2}=0
gives a contradiction because
x_{0},h\not=0
.

(2)

Case
q_{1}\not=0
. Then
A_{3}=0
implies
\cos\psi=0
identically and thus
x=x(s)
is a constant function,
x(s)=x_{0}
. Now we have
A_{2}=x_{0}^{2}h^{2}
and
A_{2}=0
gives a contradiction again.

∎

Equation (
2
) for stationary surfaces is similar to the equation of the shrinkers of the mean curvature flow (MCF), which are obtained with a similar density. To be precise, a surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be a
shrinker
of the MCF if
\Sigma
is a weighted minimal surface for the density
\phi(p)=e^{\alpha|p|^{2}/2}
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Equation
H_{\phi}=0
writes as
H=\alpha\langle\nu,p\rangle
. If
\alpha<0
, the surface is a called a self-shrinker while if
\alpha>0
, the surface is called a self-expander (
[
9
]
).

Shrinkers of helicoidal type form a rich family of surfaces (
[
12
,
13
]
). This contrasts with the situation of stationary surfaces for
E_{\alpha}
where the only helicoidal stationary surfaces are rotational surfaces (Thm.
7.1
). As in the situation of stationary surfaces, there is not an a priori relation between the twist axis of a helicoidal shrinker and the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. However, as far as the authors know, there is not a discussion on it in the literature, and usually is assumed that the twist axis of a helicoidal shrinker contains the origin. The following results clarifies this situation.

Theorem 7.2
.

If
\Sigma
is helicoidal shrinker, then the twist axis crosses the origin of coordinates.

Proof.

The proof is similar to Thm.
7.1
and we will follow the same notation. The solutions of
H=\alpha\langle\nu,p\rangle
are also invariant by vector isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus we can assume that the twist axis is
L=\{x=q_{1},y=0\}
. The proof consists in proving that
q_{1}=0
. Now the equation
H=\alpha\langle\nu,z\rangle
becomes as (
32
) with
A_{2}
trivially
0
. We distinguish if
h=0
or
h\not=0
.

(1)

Case
h\not=0
. We have

A_{1}=\alpha hx\cos\psi W.

Then
A_{1}=0
implies
\cos\psi=0
identically and thus
x(s)=x_{0}
is a constant function,
x_{0}>0
. Now
A_{3}
is trivially
0
and
A_{4}=\alpha q_{1}x_{0}^{3}
which implies
q_{1}=0
.

(2)

Case
h=0
. Now the surface is rotational and the equation
H=\alpha\langle\nu,p\rangle
is
A_{0}(s)+A_{4}(s)\cos t=0
, where

A_{4}=\alpha q_{1}x^{3}\sin\psi
. Equation
A_{4}=0
gives two cases to discuss, namely,
q_{1}=0
and
\sin\psi=0
