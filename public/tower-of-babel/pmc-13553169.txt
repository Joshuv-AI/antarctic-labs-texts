Beyond Amyloid: Hippocampal Atrophy as a Prognostic Marker in Amyloid-Negative Subjective Cognitive Decline
Authors: Hyuk Sung Kwon, Seong-Ho Koh
Published: 2026

The emergence of blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) has transformed the diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).12 Plasma phosphorylated tau, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL) have demonstrated remarkable accuracy in detecting amyloid pathology and predicting disease progression across the AD spectrum.123 An important question that arises as these biomarkers increasingly enter clinical practice is whether they retain prognostic value in individuals who lack cerebral amyloid pathology.

In this issue of the Journal of Clinical Neurology, Yoon et al.4 address this important knowledge gap by examining the comparative prognostic performance of plasma biomarkers and structural MRI markers in amyloid-negative subjective cognitive decline (A-SCD). Their study provides valuable insights into a clinically common but biologically heterogeneous population that remains poorly understood.4

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) is widely recognized as one of the earliest clinical manifestations of cognitive impairment.5 However, most biomarker research has focused on amyloid-positive individuals who are presumed to represent preclinical AD,6 which contrasts with approximately three-quarters of individuals with SCD being amyloid-negative. These patients likely encompass a broad spectrum of pathological processes, including primary age-related tauopathy (PART), limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE), cerebrovascular disease, age-related neurodegeneration, psychiatric conditions, and other non-AD etiologies. This means that prognostic markers identified in amyloid-positive populations might not be directly applicable to A-SCD.4

The following principal finding of the study is noteworthy4: while plasma biomarkers including pTau181, GFAP, NfL, and the pTau181/Aβ42 ratio showed poor and inconsistent predictive performance, the hippocampal volume showed consistent associations with both cognitive decline and the conversion to mild cognitive impairment over a 4-year follow-up. The white-matter hyperintensity burden also exhibited significant prognostic value, although its performance was generally inferior to that of hippocampal atrophy.4

These findings suggest that the relative prognostic performance of BBMs and imaging biomarkers depends on the biological context of the population being studied. In amyloid-negative SCD, structural MRI markers may provide complementary—or, in this cohort, potentially greater—prognostic information than currently available AD-specific plasma biomarkers.12 Instead, the findings suggest that the prognostic accuracy is heavily dependent on the underlying biological substrate of the population being studied. Neurodegeneration itself may represent the dominant driver of future decline in amyloid-negative individuals, whereas AD-specific molecular biomarkers may have low sensitivity in detecting heterogeneous nonamyloid pathologies.

The biological plausibility of these observations is supported by the currently available evidence. Hippocampal atrophy is a final common pathway of multiple neurodegenerative processes. Beyond AD, hippocampal volume loss may reflect neuronal injury associated with PART, LATE, hippocampal sclerosis, vascular pathology, and age-related synaptic degeneration. Consequently, structural MRI may capture the cumulative neurodegenerative burden more effectively than biomarkers designed primarily to detect amyloid-related tau phosphorylation.47

Of particular interest is the study’s relevance to the evolving ATN framework and the recent recognition of non-AD neurodegenerative entities.8 There is increasing evidence that individuals classified as A-T+N+ may experience meaningful cognitive decline despite lacking amyloid pathology. In this context, hippocampal atrophy serves not merely as a marker of AD but as a broader indicator of neurodegenerative vulnerability.4

The findings also raise important questions regarding current plasma biomarker strategies. The study utilized plasma pTau181, which remains an established marker of AD pathology. However, recent evidence supports that pTau217, plasma MTBR-tau species, and other emerging biomarkers offer higher sensitivity in detecting subtle neurodegenerative changes. Whether these newer assays would demonstrate improved prognostic performance in A-SCD remains an important area for future investigation.

Several strengths increase the significance of this work.4 The longitudinal design, amyloid-confirmed cohort, standardized biomarker assessment, and direct comparison between BBMs and imaging markers provided a thorough framework for evaluating prognostic utility. Furthermore, the inclusion of both clinical-conversion and cognitive-decline outcomes provided a comprehensive view of disease progression.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged.4 First, the study involved a relatively small sample, and relatively few of the participants progressed to mild cognitive impairment during the follow-up period. The predictive estimates should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution, since small numbers of events will reduce the statistical power and increase the uncertainty around effect estimates. Second, the reported predictive performance was derived from the study cohort itself without external validation, and so the generalizability of these findings to other amyloid-negative SCD populations remains uncertain and hence requires confirmation in larger independent cohorts. Third, attrition during the 4-year follow-up may have introduced selection bias, which is an inherent challenge of longitudinal studies. Fourth, the absence of longitudinal biomarker measurements precluded assessments of temporal biomarker trajectories, which may have provided greater prognostic information than the baseline values alone. Fifth, the lack of tau positron-emission-tomography markers, α-synuclein biomarkers, TDP-43 biomarkers, and pathological confirmation restricted the ability to identify the biological mechanisms underlying hippocampal atrophy in this heterogeneous population.

Despite these limitations, the study has provided valuable preliminary evidence that in A-SCD, structural MRI markers of neurodegeneration may currently provide greater clinically useful prognostic information than AD-focused plasma biomarkers.4 This observation should not be interpreted as reducing the value of BBMs. Rather, it highlights the importance of matching biomarker selection to the biological context of the population under investigation.

As dementia research increasingly moves beyond an amyloid-centric perspective, studies such as that by Yoon et al.4 remind us that cognitive decline is not synonymous with AD. The future of precision medicine in cognitive disorders will likely require multimodal approaches integrating molecular biomarkers, neuroimaging, genetics, and clinical phenotyping.9 Until more-sensitive biomarkers of non-AD neurodegeneration become available, hippocampal atrophy remains a clinically accessible marker that can provide useful prognostic information pending further validation.

In the era of BBMs, the study of Yoon et al.4 serves as an important reminder that sometimes the established imaging markers can continue to provide valuable prognostic information alongside emerging BBMs. For A-SCD, the hippocampus continues to tell a story that blood alone might not yet fully reveal.
