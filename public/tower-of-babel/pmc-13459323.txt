Theoretical impact of a mobile team dedicated to patients with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias on medication exposure in community-dwelling older patients (MEMORIAS study)
Authors: Victor El-Jammal, Frederic Gervais, Gaelle Richard, Alexis Lepetit, Christelle Mouchoux, Teddy Novais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) are frequently associated with behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), which complicate care, increase medication exposure and cognitive iatrogenic risk. The mobile team for ADRD (MTAD) provides therapeutic recommendations for acute cases following home interventions.ObjectivesThis study evaluated the theoretical impact of MTAD medication recommendations on medication exposure in community-dwelling older patients with neurocognitive disorders (NCD) and BPSD.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included older patients with NCDs visited by MTAD. Medication exposure was evaluated theoretically, assuming full implementation of MTAD medication recommendations, based on medication number, prevalence of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) identified using the EU(7)-PIM list, and sedative/anticholinergic burden quantified by the Drug Burden Index (DBI). MTAD medication recommendations were classified.ResultsAmong 246 patients (mean age 83.9 years), MTAD recommendations would result in a significant reduction in PIMs per patient (0.91 versus 0.79, p < 0.001), with a significant increase in the overall and sedative DBI (0.55 versus 0.74, p < 0.001; 0.46 versus 0.65, p < 0.001, respectively), and number of prescribed medications (6.5 versus 6.9, p < 0.001), without change in the anticholinergic DBI (0.25 versus 0.26, p = 0.206). A total of 337 recommendations were issued, mainly additions (51%) and discontinuations (23%), primarily targeting psychotropics.ConclusionsThe theoretical implementation of MTAD recommendations for older patients with BPSD could reduce exposure to PIMs and may lead to an increased use of sedatives. However, further efforts are needed to optimize somatic treatments and minimize iatrogenic risks through comprehensive medication reviews.

Background

In 2019, Alzheimer’s Disease International estimated that 50 million individuals were affected by dementia worldwide. This figure is projected to reach 152 million by 2050 [1]. In individuals aged at least 65 years, Alzheimer’s disease is the leading cause of dementia, followed by other neurodegenerative disorders, grouped under the term Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD). These conditions result in a progressive cognitive and functional decline [2], which may significantly affect caregiver burden and healthcare costs [3, 4]. As ADRD progresses, behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) become more frequent and severe, further worsening patient prognosis. Consequently, the management of BPSD through non-pharmacological and pharmacological therapies represents a significant challenge for non-specialized healthcare professionals [5]. To address this issue at Hospices Civils de Lyon, France, a mobile team dedicated to the management of BPSD in patients with ADRD (MTAD) visiting patients’ homes or nursing homes has been implemented [6].

Polypharmacy is highly prevalent in older patients with dementia (62%) given the need to treat both comorbidities and BPSD [7, 8]. Medication exposure – defined here as polypharmacy, the use of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), and the use of medications with anticholinergic and/or sedative properties - is a potentially modifiable factor associated with cognitive iatrogenic risk and dementia in several studies [9–13]. A cognitive iatrogenic risk is an adverse drug event defined as a decrease in the cognitive reserve as well as an onset or worsening of cognitive disorders [11]. It results from a complex interaction between predisposing and precipitating risk factors that must be taken into account in older patients’ care pathway. Therefore, identifying potentially inappropriate medications and considering their dose reduction or discontinuation when appropriate is essential to optimize pharmacological therapy in older patients with neurocognitive disorders (NCD).

The MEMORIAS (Medication Exposure of older community-dwelling patients with neurocognitive disorders treated by a MObile team dedicated to behavioRal dIsorders linked to Alzheimer’s disease or related dementiaS) study was designed to better understand the medication exposure of older patients with BPSD living at home. In this study, patients were examined at home by the MTAD as part of support for the management of BPSD. Thus, the main objective of this study was to assess the theoretical impact of the MTAD medication recommendations on the medication exposure of older patients with BPSD living at home in the context of neurocognitive disorders. The secondary objectives were to describe the MTAD medication recommendations and the BPSD course during a 60-day follow-up period.

Methods

Study design and participants

The MEMORIAS study is a single center and observational study based on retrospective data collected between January 2021 and December 2022 in patients with BPSD visited by the MTAD. The study protocol has been reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of the Hospices Civils de Lyon, France (N°25-5122, 12/12/2022). All patients aged 65 years and over, visited at home or in an autonomous residence by the MTAD during the study period for the management of BPSD in the context of documented NCD, were included. Patients living in institutional settings such as nursing homes or long-term care units, as well as those without medication data in the electronic medical record, were excluded.

Mobile team dedicated to patients with Alzheimer’s disease

The MTAD is affiliated to the Clinical and Research Memory Center of Lyon (Hospices Civils de Lyon, France); it was created in 2011 and has been described in a previous publication [6]. Although initially created within the framework of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD), the MTAD intervenes in routine clinical practice for patients with any documented neurocognitive disorder (major or minor) presenting with BPSD, regardless of etiology, particularly in complex situations where primary care management has reached its limits and specialized multidisciplinary support is required. MTAD is composed of a multidisciplinary team comprising a geriatrician, a psychiatrist, a nurse, and psychologists. MTAD visits take place after request from a general practitioner (GP), a specialist such as a neurologist or geriatrician, or by other healthcare professionals involved in the patient’s care. During the MTAD intervention, a clinical evaluation including BPSD inventory was conducted, as well as a systemic evaluation of the factors contributing to BPSD (e.g., environmental changes, caregiver burden and iatrogenic factors). Subsequently, the MTAD provides recommendations in three fields: non-pharmacological interventions, pharmacological therapies (medication recommendations), and medico-social interventions. In the present study, only medication recommendations were analyzed, as data on non-pharmacological and medico-social recommendations were not collected.

Data collection

Patients’ data were collected from MTAD reports. For each patient, demographic data (age, sex), clinical data such as etiology and severity of the NCD, comorbidities, medication data and recommendations were collected. Neuropsychiatric comorbidities were defined as coexisting neurological or psychiatric disorders.

Theoretical impact of the MTAD therapeutic recommendations on medication exposure

The present analysis was conducted under a theoretical full implementation scenario and did not assess the actual implementation of MTAD medication recommendations by the referring physician. Medication exposure was evaluated by comparing the patient’s usual treatment, as identified during the MTAD visit, with the treatment that would theoretically result from full implementation of the medication recommendations issued by the MTAD.

In this study, medication exposure comprised the number of prescribed medications per patient, as well as polypharmacy, potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), and medications with sedative and/or anticholinergic properties. Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of 5 to 9 medications per day, while excessive polypharmacy was defined as the use of at least 10 medications per day [14]. The PIMs are medications that should not be prescribed in the geriatric population due to a risk of adverse events outweighing the clinical benefits, especially when there is evidence supporting a safer or more effective alternative therapy for the same medical condition. PIMs were identified using the EU(7)-PIM list [15]. In addition, treatment duration-dependent PIMs as well as dose-related PIMs were taken into account. Regarding the anticholinergic and sedative drug exposure, it was measured using the Drug Burden Index (DBI) [16]. The list of medication with anticholinergic or sedative properties was obtained from the literature and adapted to the French practices [17, 18]. A DBI score under 1 was considered as a low exposure to sedative and anticholinergic drugs whereas DBI score equal or superior to 1 was considered as a higher exposure [19].

Description of MTAD medication recommendations

Herein, only medication recommendations were considered. They could be classified into 6 different types: medication discontinuation, medication addition, dosage reduction, dosage increase, medication substitution, and modification in the dosing regimen.

Description of changes in BPSD at 60 days

In the MTAD program, an evaluation of the BPSD severity was performed during the MTAD visit and 60 days after, using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) [20]. A higher overall NPI score at 60 days (maximum 144) indicated more severe BPSD. During the visit, NPI was evaluated with the caregiver by a psychologist or a nurse of the MTAD. At 60 days, the NPI was evaluated by phone with the same caregiver. This follow-up assessment focused on BPSD severity and did not include verification of the implementation of MTAD medication recommendations.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables were expressed as count and percentages. Comparative analyses were conducted for paired samples. Categorical variables were compared using the McNemar test and continuous variables using Student t-test for paired series or Wilcoxon signed-rank test (in case of non-normal distribution). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2012. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0. Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Overall, 248 patients were eligible, of whom 246 were included since the data regarding medications were not documented for 2 patients. The mean age of the patients was 83.9 ± 6.8 years. The etiologies of NCDs were heterogeneous; the predominant etiology was Alzheimer’s disease (22.8%), followed by vascular dementia (10.2%), Parkinson’s disease (5.3%), multiple etiologies (4.5%), and Lewy body dementia (4.1%). However, 46.7% had an unspecified NCD. Among all patients, 64.2% had major NCD, 1.6% had minor NCD, and in 34.2% severity was not documented (Table 1). The mean number of comorbidities per patient was 6.5 ± 3.3, including 2.2 ± 1.0 neuropsychiatric comorbidities per patient.

Primary outcome: theoretical impact of MTAD medication recommendations on medication exposure

Exposure to polypharmacy

At the time of the MTAD visits, patients were taking a mean of 6.5 ± 3.8 medications. Polypharmacy was observed in 53% of patients, while excessive polypharmacy concerned 17% of them (Table 2). When considering the medication recommendations from the MTAD, the mean number of medications increased to 6.9 ± 3.8, with 55% of patients presenting polypharmacy and 20% excessive polypharmacy. These increases were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for both).

Among the identified PIMs, the most represented were those from the ATC class N (nervous system) accounting for 98 PIMs (43.9%) at the time of the MTAD visits, which were reduced to 72 (36.9%) in MTAD recommendations. Overall, the MTAD predominantly recommended reduction in antipsychotics (e.g., cyamemazine, risperidone, and periciazine), anxiolytics (e.g., alprazolam, bromazepam, clotiazepam, and prazepam), hypnotics (e.g., zopiclone and lormetazepam), and antidepressants (e.g., paroxetine, amitriptyline, and fluoxetine). However, in detail, the MTAD also suggested the initiation of clozapine and aripiprazole among antipsychotics, lorazepam among anxiolytics, and venlafaxine among antidepressants. The MTAD recommendations did not suggest modifications in other ATC classes involved in PIMs, except for two oxybutynin discontinuations, suggested given these anticholinergic properties, and one tropsium addition (ATC class G; Table 3).

Exposure to medications with anticholinergic and/or sedative properties

Among the 1615 medications prescribed at the time of the MTAD visits, 135 (8.4%) had exclusively sedative properties, 45 (2.8%) had exclusively anticholinergic properties, and 221 (13.7%) had both sedative and anticholinergic properties. Among the 1707 medications found in the MTAD recommendations, 199 (11.7%) had exclusively sedatives properties, 45 (2.6%) had exclusively anticholinergic properties, and 249 (14.5%) had both sedatives and anticholinergic properties. The mean DBI at the time of the MTAD visits was 0.55 ± 0.50. When considering the MTAD medication recommendations, a significant increase (p < 0.001) in the DBI was found (DBI = 0.74 ± 0.49). Regarding the DBI classes, 55 (23%) patients were not exposed to anticholinergic and/or sedative medications, and 38 (15%) had a high exposure. When considering the MTAD recommendations, 25 (10%) and 69 (28%) patients were not exposed or highly exposed, respectively. A significant increase was found in sedative exposure (DBIS at the time of the MTAD visits = 0.46 ± 0.43 versus DBIS with MTAD recommendations = 0.65 ± 0.43, p < 0.001; Table 2). However, no significant difference was observed in anticholinergic exposure (p = 0.206; Table 2). Overall, the MTAD recommendations suggested the addition of 92 medications with anticholinergic and/or sedatives properties, leading to an increase from 344 (at the time of the MTAD visits) to 438 (in the MTAD recommendations) psychotropic medications: addition of 66 antidepressants, 11 antipsychotics, 10 hypnotics, 6 antiepileptics, and 1 anxiolytic. Secondary outcomes

Description of the MTAD medication recommendations

The MTAD made a total of 337 medication recommendations (Table 5); 171 (50.8%) were additions, 77 (22.9%) were discontinuations, 54 (16.1%) were dose increases, 21 (6.3%) were substitutions, 8 (2.4%) were changes in the dosing regimen, and 5 (1.5%) were dose reductions. A total of 332 medication recommendations (98.5%) involved an ATC class N; 158 (47.5%) antidepressants, 89 (26.8%) antipsychotics, 42 (12.6%) anxiolytics, 25 (7.5%) hypnotics and sedatives, 14 (4.2%) antiepileptics, 3 (0.9%) other anti-dementia drugs, and 1 (0.3%) analgesic. The most frequently found medications in the MTAD recommendations were sertraline (n = 75), risperidone (n = 49), mirtazapine (n = 31), and oxazepam (n = 29). In addition, among the 337 medication recommendations, 141 (41.8%) involved a PIM: 55 discontinuations (39.0%), 52 additions (37.0%), 16 dose increases (11.3%), 5 dose reductions (3.5%), 8 medication substitutions (5.7%), and 5 dosing regimen modifications (3.5%).

Change in BPSD during the 60-day follow-up

Regarding BPSD, 167 patients were evaluated using the NPI at the time of the MTAD visit and 60 days after the visit. A significant decrease in the mean NPI was observed; the mean NPI at the time of the MTAD visits was 41.04 ± 15.05 while it was 29.13 ± 16.61 60 days after the visits (p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study suggests that the theoretical implementation of MTAD medication recommendations may be associated with significant reductions in PIMs and NPI, which highlights the potential value of tailored medication recommendations. However, the findings also reveal an increase in polypharmacy and sedative exposure. This increase is mainly related to the recommendations involving the initiation of psychotropic medications in the context of acute BPSD crises, such as agitation, aggression, severe anxiety, or sleep disturbances, when non-pharmacological measures were considered insufficient.

A high prevalence of polypharmacy was highlighted in the present study population, which is in line with previous studies that included patients with dementia and reported a polypharmacy prevalence of 72% in the USA and 55% in Denmark [21, 22]. While MTAD recommendations led to a significant increase in the mean number of medications, the reduction in PIM prevalence demonstrates the MTAD potential to refine medication regimens. Of note, the strategic discontinuation of specific PIMs was balanced by introducing alternatives that may better address specific symptomatology. This is in accordance with the broader purpose of minimizing cognitive iatrogenic risks in older patients with NCD. A previous study reported that among 1.6 million older person-year observations, 32.7% used at least one PIM during a calendar year, according to the Beers criteria [23]. Another study conducted in eight European countries using the EU (7)-PIM list [24] reported that 60% of patients with dementia had at least one PIM. In that study, antipsychotics, antithrombotics, and anxiolytics were the most commonly used PIM classes. In contrast, in our cohort, the most frequently identified PIM categories included antithrombotics, anxiolytics, antidepressants, antipsychotics, antiarrhythmics, and antidiabetics. However, comparison of PIM prevalence between studies is difficult due to the criteria used to identify these PIMs.

Despite improvements in PIM management, we observed an increase in DBI, mainly due to the introduction of psychotropic medications to manage severe BPSD, resulting in higher sedative exposure. The reduction in PIM prevalence alongside the increase in DBI scores can be explained by the fact that these indicators capture different dimensions of medication appropriateness. While the EU(7)-PIM list identifies medications considered inappropriate in older adults, the DBI quantifies cumulative exposure to drugs with sedative and anticholinergic properties, regardless of their appropriateness. Consequently, replacing certain PIMs with alternative psychotropic agents may reduce PIM prevalence while still increasing overall sedative burden. Notably, 17 of the PIMs targeted by MTAD interventions contributed directly to the DBI, indicating a possible overlap between these two indicators. However, the added sedative burden of psychotropic agents illustrates the complexity of medication optimization, where reducing inappropriate prescriptions does not necessarily lower the overall pharmacological risk. The ATC N class was predominant in the MTAD medication recommendations (98.5%) and the details of the additions or medication substitutions suggest that these recommendations are related to the management of BPSD resistant to non-pharmacological measures. These recommendations were formulated by a multidisciplinary team including geriatric and psychiatric specialists, which may contribute to the selection of medications considered appropriate for older patients with neurocognitive disorders. However, the MTAD targeted the discontinuation of inappropriate medications with anticholinergic properties such as tricyclic antidepressants, first-generation antipsychotics, paroxetine, or oxybutynin. Few previous studies have used the DBI to assess exposure to anticholinergic and sedative medications in community-dwelling patients with NCDs. In subjective cognitive decline, Margolis et al. reported that 24.3% of community-dwelling patients had a low exposure (0 < DBI < 1) and 12.6% had high exposure (DBI ≥ 1) [25]. In dementia, Katipoglu et al. reported that 34.1% of community-dwelling patients had a low exposure, and 38.1% had a high exposure [26]. In the present study, 62% had a low exposure and 15% had high exposure. The differences in DBI values across studies may be explained by variations in population characteristics, calculation methods, and prescribing practices. The younger, at-risk population in Margolis et al. had a lower DBI, while the older, cognitively impaired populations in Katipoglu et al. and our study may have had higher DBI values due to greater polypharmacy and differences in dosage references (Food and Drug Administration, European guidelines).

An encouraging finding is the significant decrease in NPI score observed 60 days after the intervention. This result could highlight the clinical value of the MTAD interventions and is similar to an analysis published in 2016, which also highlighted a significant decrease in the NPI score among 111 patients with a significant impact on four BPSD: agitation/aggression, depression/dysphoria, sleep disturbance, and aberrant motor behavior [6]. However, the present study did not allow for a direct association between this change in score and the medication recommendations of the MTAD; the analysis did not account for the actual implementation of these interventions as well as that of the non-pharmacological recommendations. Future studies could investigate whether there is a relationship between these interventions and the change in the NPI, reflecting the BPSD, and even to understand the role of pharmacological interventions in this course.

During the study period, the MTAD composition leaned toward psychiatric expertise, shaping its optimization framework. While the MTAD medication recommendations primarily focus on psychotropic drugs, the substantial prevalence of non-psychotropic drugs contributing to PIMs and DBI scores suggests that broader pharmacological expertise is needed to ensure comprehensive medication management. This focus may partly explain the limited optimization of non-psychotropic PIMs, despite the presence of a geriatrician, as the primary objective of the intervention was the urgent management of BPSD rather than a comprehensive somatic medication review. These findings further support the potential value of conducting systematic medication reviews, even in the context of acute BPSD crises, to ensure comprehensive identification of both psychotropic and non-psychotropic medications that may contribute to cognitive or behavioral vulnerability, particularly those with anticholinergic or sedative properties, such as certain urological drugs. Evidence shows that untreated somatic conditions can worsen NCD, highlighting the need for optimal treatment strategies [27, 28]. In addition, although MTAD currently lacks direct involvement from pharmacists, research underscores the value of collaborative medication reviews. Studies demonstrated that pharmacists can identify PIMs, recommend changes, and significantly reduce anticholinergic burden, whether working independently or within multidisciplinary teams [29–31]. Tools such as the DBI have proven effective in optimizing prescriptions by deprescribing anticholinergic and sedative medications through pharmacist-physician collaboration [32, 33]. A study by Vergnaud et al. illustrated the role of mobile geriatric teams, including pharmacists, in optimizing medication use [34]. In 276 patients, the pharmacist identified 304 medication-related problems and 169 PIMs. Pharmacists’ involvement in MTAD could enhance the holistic patient assessment, optimize therapeutic management, and prevent medication-related cognitive decline.

This study has several limitations. First, as previously mentioned, the analyses did not account for the actual implementation of the MTAD medication recommendations by the referring physician, as the evaluation was based on a theoretical full implementation scenario. Additionally, the temporal scope of the present study is limited to the critical period surrounding the MTAD intervention, without long-term follow-up. Furthermore, nearly half of the cohort had an unspecified NCD etiology and severity was not documented for a substantial proportion of patients. These missing clinical details may limit the interpretability of disease-specific findings and reflect the retrospective nature of the data collection.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the theoretical implementation of MTAD medication recommendations in patients with BPSD could reduce exposure to PIMs while increasing sedative burden associated with BPSD management. These findings highlight the need for broader therapeutic optimization to minimize iatrogenic risks and improve patient care pathways. Future research should assess the actual implementation of the MTAD recommendations and explore the integration of systematic medication reviews involving pharmacists.
