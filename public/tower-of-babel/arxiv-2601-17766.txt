Computing L-functions of λ \lambda -adic representations of global function fields
David Kurniadi Angdinata

Abstract
. The L-function L ⁡ ( ρ λ , s ) L(\rho_{\lambda},s) of an almost everywhere unramified λ \lambda -adic representation ρ λ \rho_{\lambda} of a global function field 𝔽 q ​ ( C ) \mathbb{F}_{q}(C) is known to be a rational function in q − s q^{-s} satisfying a functional equation up to some complex sign ϵ ⁡ ( ρ λ ) \epsilon(\rho_{\lambda}) . This paper presents a systematic framework to compute the coefficients of L ⁡ ( ρ λ , s ) L(\rho_{\lambda},s) and its sign ϵ ⁡ ( ρ λ ) \epsilon(\rho_{\lambda}) with some explicit examples. Keywords: L-functions, λ \lambda -adic representations, global function fields Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 11Y40, 11R59, 11M38; Secondary 11G40, 14G10, 11M41



1.
Introduction

Given a global field, there are L-functions
L(\rho,s)
in the complex variable
s\in\mathbb{C}
associated to suitable continuous representations
\rho
of its absolute Galois group, which are conjecturally meromophic over
\mathbb{C}
and conjecturally satisfy functional equations. These L-functions play the role of half of the modern Langlands philosophy, and there are many crucial algebraic and analytic questions that remain unanswered. For instance, when
\rho=\chi
is a Dirichlet character, the generalised Riemann hypothesis describes where its
\zeta
-function
\zeta(s)=L(\chi,s)
vanishes, and when
\rho=\rho_{A,\ell}
arises from an abelian variety
A
, the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture describes the arithmetic of
A
in terms of its L-function
L(A,s)=L(\rho_{A,\ell},s)
. The importance of L-functions in arithmetic algebraic geometry is so much so that thousands of CPU years have been spent on computing their data for various representations in the massive L-functions and modular forms database (LMFDB)
[
43
]
, which has proven to be an immensely useful resource for number theorists.

At a cursory glance, most of the data in the LMFDB are of objects over various number fields. In this setting, their L-functions are transcendental functions of
s
that can be written as Dirichlet series, with the aforementioned conjectures still open for most objects. Much of their data were computed from Platt’s code for degree one L-functions
[
32
]
based on Booker’s algorithm
[
4
]
, from the
lcalc
library in C++ based on Rubinstein’s algorithm
[
36
]
for Maass forms, and from the work of Booker–Sijsling–Sutherland–Voight–Yasaki
[
3
]
for genus two curves. In a much larger generality, T Dokchitser wrote an efficient algorithm to compute the special value of a motivic L-function, assuming a conjectural functional equation that can be numerically verified
[
16
]
. This was originally implemented as the PARI
[
31
]
package
ComputeL
, but has since been ported to the core libraries of Magma
[
5
]
as the
LSeries
function and SageMath
[
41
]
as the
dokchitser
function.

In a stark contrast, the arithmetic of analogous objects over
K\coloneqq\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
is very well-understood
[
27
]
. In this setting, their L-functions are rational functions of
q^{-s}
, satisfying a known functional equation and even the Riemann hypothesis. Yet, the computational ecosystem over
K
is severely limited, with little to no support for explicit computations in the core libraries of most computer algebra systems, including PARI and SageMath. Magma is better in this regard, including multiple constructors for
K
, explicit class field theory computations with
WittRing
and
CarlitzModule
, and even an
LFunction
for non-constant elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
. Using this interface, Comeau–Lapointe–David–Lalín–Li computed
L(E,\chi,s)=L(\rho_{E,\ell}\otimes\chi,s)
for a fixed Legendre curve
E
over
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
twisted by Dirichlet characters
\chi
to investigate their vanishing
[
6
]
. In private communication, Maistret–Wiersema also computed
L(E,\chi,s)
for many constant elliptic curves
E
by hand to formulate an analogue over
K
of the twisted BSD-type formula of V Dokchitser–Evans–Wiersema
[
17
]
. This seems to be the extent to which L-functions of
K
have been computed explicitly in the literature, which renders the creation of an analogue of the LMFDB over
K
difficult.

This paper presents an analogue of T Dokchitser’s algorithm for
K
, in the language of
\lambda
-adic representations
\rho_{\lambda}
for some prime
\lambda\nmid q
of a fixed number field
F
. These are the natural Galois representations associated to L-functions of motivic origin, including Riemann/Dedekind
\zeta
-functions, Dirichlet/Weber/Hecke/Artin L-functions, and Hasse–Weil L-functions of varieties. Then there is a rational function
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)\in F_{\lambda}(T)
, called the
formal L-function
of
\rho_{\lambda}
, such that
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},q^{-s})=L(\rho_{\lambda},s)
. Under the condition that
\rho_{\lambda}
is
self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})\in\mathbb{N}
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})\in\{\operatorname{id},\operatorname{cc}\}
, where
\operatorname{id}
is the identity and
\operatorname{cc}
is complex conjugation, there is a field element
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})\in F_{\lambda}^{\times}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
satisfies a functional equation

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)=\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})\cdot T^{n(\rho_{\lambda})-d(\rho_{\lambda})}\cdot\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},(q^{w(\rho_{\lambda})+1}T)^{-1})^{c(\rho_{\lambda})},

where
n(\rho_{\lambda}),d(\rho_{\lambda})\in\mathbb{N}
are the degrees of the numerator and denominator of
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
respectively. If the genus of
C
and the dimension of
\rho_{\lambda}
are small, then
n(\rho_{\lambda})-d(\rho_{\lambda})
is essentially the degree of the
Artin conductor
of
\rho_{\lambda}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Corollary
3.13
, Corollary
3.15
, Corollary
3.16
)
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K
.

(1)

There is an algorithm to compute
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
with a running time that is roughly exponential in
n(\rho_{\lambda})
.

Assume further that
\rho_{\lambda}
is self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})\in\{\operatorname{id},\operatorname{cc}\}
.

(2)

If
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
is known, then there is an algorithm to compute
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
with a running time that is roughly exponential in
\lfloor n(\rho_{\lambda})/2\rfloor
.

(3)

Otherwise, there is an algorithm to compute
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
with a running time that is typically exponential in
\lfloor n(\rho_{\lambda})/2\rfloor
, and at worst exponential in
n(\rho_{\lambda})
.

The algorithms in Theorem
1.1
have been implemented in Magma
[
1
]
and bug-tested against existing implementations of
L(E,s)
and
L(E,\chi,s)
. The proof of (1) is elementary and only uses properties of formal power series. The proofs of (2) and (3) use a generalisation of (1), and are heavily inspired by the algorithm of Comeau-Lapointe–David–Lalín–Li for
L(E,\chi,s)

[
6
, Section 5.1]
, although their analogue of (1) is an ad-hoc computation using Legendre symbols.

Section
2
reviews some useful background on invariants associated to
\rho_{\lambda}
, including
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
and
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
, which are sometimes implicit in the literature without good references. Section
3
details Theorem
1.1
and briefly justifies their running time complexities. Section
4
works them out by hand for explicit examples of elliptic curves, Dirichlet characters, and other motivic objects.

2.
\lambda
-adic representations

In this section,
K
will be a non-archimedean local field or a global function field, which will be clear from context, of residue size
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, let
\lambda\nmid q
be a fixed prime of a coefficient number field
F
, and let
V
and
V_{\lambda}
be fixed finite-dimensional vector spaces over
F
and
F_{\lambda}
respectively. If
F=\mathbb{Q}
, write
\lambda=\ell
instead.

2.1.
Local invariants

In this subsection, let
K
be a non-archimedean local field, let
\mathcal{O}_{K}
be its valuation ring with normalised valuation
|\cdot|_{K}
, normalised uniformiser
\varpi_{K}
, and residue field size
q_{K}
such that
|\varpi_{K}|_{K}=q_{K}^{-1}
, and let
W_{K}
be its Weil group with inertia subgroup
I_{K}
and a fixed choice of geometric Frobenius
\phi_{K}
.

A
\lambda
-adic representation
of
K
is a continuous homomorphism
\rho_{\lambda}:W_{K}\to\operatorname{GL}(V_{\lambda})
, where
\operatorname{GL}(V_{\lambda})
is endowed with the
\lambda
-adic topology.

Example 2.1
.

A smooth proper variety
X
over
K
has a natural
\lambda
-adic representation
\rho_{X,\ell}
given by the continuous action of
W_{K}
on the first
\ell
-adic cohomology group
H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{1}(\overline{X},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
of
X
induced by functoriality. Then
\rho_{X,\ell}
is
independent of
\ell
, in the weak sense that the characteristic polynomial of
\rho_{X,\ell}(\phi_{K})
has coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
, since it is true when
X
is abelian
[
20
, Theorem 4.3]
and
H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{1}(\overline{X},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})\cong H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{1}(\operatorname{Alb}(\overline{X}),\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})

[
25
, Theorem 2A9(6)]
, where
\operatorname{Alb}(X)
is the Albanese abelian variety of
X
.

Example 2.2
.

An
Artin representation
of
K
is a continuous homomorphism
\varrho:W_{K}\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
, where
\operatorname{GL}(V)
is endowed with the discrete topology. This can be viewed as a
\lambda
-adic representation of
K
via a fixed embedding
V\hookrightarrow V_{\lambda}
.

The invariants of
\rho_{\lambda}
are best defined in terms of
Weil–Deligne representations

(\rho,\nu)
. This is the data of a
Weil representation

\rho
, which is a continuous homomorphism from
W_{K}
to
\operatorname{GL}(V^{\prime})
, where
V^{\prime}
is a finite-dimensional vector space over an arbitrary field
F^{\prime}
with
\operatorname{char}(F^{\prime})=0
endowed with the discrete topology, and a nilpotent endomorphism
\nu:F^{\prime}\to F^{\prime}
such that for any
c\in W_{K}
,

\rho(c)\cdot\nu\cdot\rho(c)^{-1}=|c|_{K}\cdot\nu.

It turns out that there is an explicit equivalence between the symmetric monoidal categories of
\lambda
-adic representations
\rho_{\lambda}
and of Weil–Deligne representations
(\rho,\nu)

[
11
, Section 8]
, where the tensor product is given by

(\rho,\nu)\otimes(\sigma,\upsilon)\coloneqq(\rho\otimes\sigma,\nu\otimes 1+1\otimes\upsilon).

Then
\rho_{\lambda}
is said to be
unramified
if
\rho
is unramified and
\nu=0
, and
indecomposable
if
(\rho,\nu)
is not a direct sum of subspaces invariant under both
\rho
and
\nu
.

For any subrepresentation
\sigma:W_{K}\to\operatorname{GL}(V^{\prime})
of
\rho
, let
\sigma^{G}
denote the subrepresentation of
\sigma
invariant under a subgroup
G
of
W_{K}
, namely

\sigma^{G}\coloneqq\{\xi\in V^{\prime}:\forall c\in G,\ \xi^{\sigma(c)}=\xi\}.

The
Artin conductor exponent

\alpha(\rho_{\lambda})
of
\rho_{\lambda}
is the sum of

\alpha(\rho)\coloneqq\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\dim\rho-\dim\rho^{G_{K,r}}}{[G_{K,r}:I_{K}]},\qquad\beta(\rho_{\lambda})\coloneqq\dim\rho^{I_{K}}-\dim\ker(\nu)^{I_{K}},

where
G_{K,r}
is the
r
-th higher ramification group in lower numbering of
K
for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
G_{K,0}=I_{K}
. Then
\alpha(\rho_{\lambda})=0
precisely when
\rho_{\lambda}
is unramified, since
G_{K,r}=0
for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu=0
. Note that this one of many equivalent definitions
[
46
, Section 4 to Section 8]
. When
\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{E,\ell}
arises from an elliptic curve
E
over
K
, this is equivalent to the usual conductor exponent of
E
.

Now let
\psi
be a non-trivial additive character of
K
, let
\mu
be an additive Haar measure on
K
, and let
\gamma(\psi)
be the smallest integer
r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\psi(\varpi_{K}^{r}\mathcal{O}_{K})=1
. Langlands
[
28
]
and Deligne
[
11
]
independently gave non-constructive proofs on the existence of a constant
\epsilon(\rho,\psi,\mu)\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
unique under the following properties.

•

Additivity: if
\sigma
and
\tau
are Weil representations of
K
in a short exact sequence
0\to\rho\to\sigma\to\tau\to 0
, then

\epsilon(\sigma,\psi,\mu)=\epsilon(\rho,\psi,\mu)\cdot\epsilon(\tau,\psi,\mu).

•

Inductivity in degree zero: if
\rho^{\prime}
is a virtual Weil representation of degree zero over a finite separable extension
K^{\prime}
of
K
, and
\mu^{\prime}
is an additive Haar measure on
K^{\prime}
, then

\epsilon(\operatorname{Ind}_{K}^{K^{\prime}}\rho,\psi,\mu)=\epsilon(\rho^{\prime},\psi\circ\operatorname{tr}_{K^{\prime}/K},\mu^{\prime}).

•

Quasi-character formula: if
\rho
is one-dimensional and corresponds to a quasi-character
\chi:W_{K}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
via local class field theory, then

\epsilon(\rho,\psi,\mu)=\begin{cases}\chi(\phi_{K})^{\gamma(\psi)}\cdot q_{K}^{\gamma(\psi)}\cdot\mu(\mathcal{O}_{K})&\text{if}\ \rho\ \text{is unramified},\\
\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\varpi_{K}^{r}\mathcal{O}_{K}}\chi^{-1}(x)\psi(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This depends on
\psi
and
\mu
in general, but Langlands and Deligne made specific choices whose differences in convention are summarised by Tate
[
42
, Section 3.6]
. The
local
\epsilon
-factor
of
\rho_{\lambda}
is then
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda},\psi,\mu)\coloneqq\epsilon(\rho,\psi,\mu)\cdot\delta(\rho_{\lambda})
, where

\delta(\rho_{\lambda})\coloneqq\dfrac{\det(-\rho^{I_{K}}(\phi_{K}))}{\det(-\ker(\nu)^{I_{K}}(\phi_{K}))}.

The following generalises a well-known formula for tensor products of Weil representations with one unramified factor to that of
\lambda
-adic representations.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
and
\sigma_{\lambda}
be
\lambda
-adic representations of a non-archimedean local field
K
such that
\sigma_{\lambda}
is unramified. Then

\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda}\otimes\sigma_{\lambda},\psi,\mu)=\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda},\psi,\mu)^{\dim\sigma_{\lambda}}\cdot\det\sigma_{\lambda}(\phi_{K})^{\alpha(\rho_{\lambda})+\gamma(\psi)\dim\rho_{\lambda}}.

Proof.

Let
(\rho,\nu)
and
(\sigma,\upsilon)
be the Weil–Deligne representations associated to
\rho_{\lambda}
and
\sigma_{\lambda}
respectively. Then
[
11
, Equation 5.5.3]

\epsilon(\rho\otimes\sigma,\psi,\mu)=\epsilon(\rho,\psi,\mu)^{\dim\sigma}\cdot\det\sigma(\phi_{K})^{\alpha(\rho)+\gamma(\psi)\dim\rho}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\delta(\rho_{\lambda}\otimes\sigma_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\det(-\rho^{I_{K}}(\phi_{K})\otimes\sigma(\phi_{K}))}{\det(-\ker(\nu)^{I_{K}}(\phi_{K})\otimes\sigma(\phi_{K}))}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\det(-\rho^{I_{K}}(\phi_{K}))^{\dim\sigma}\cdot\det\sigma(\phi_{K})^{\dim\rho^{I_{K}}}}{\det(-\ker(\nu)^{I_{K}}(\phi_{K}))^{\dim\sigma}\cdot\det\sigma(\phi_{K})^{\dim\ker(\nu)^{I_{K}}}}

\displaystyle=\delta(\rho_{\lambda})^{\dim\sigma}\cdot\det\sigma(\phi_{K})^{\beta(\rho_{\lambda})}.

The result follows by multiplying both expressions.
∎

In particular, by Proposition
2.3
with
\rho_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{1}
and the quasi-character formula,

\epsilon(\sigma_{\lambda},\psi,\mu)=(q_{K}^{\gamma(\psi)}\cdot\mu(\mathcal{O}_{K}))^{\dim\sigma_{\lambda}}\cdot\det\sigma_{\lambda}(\phi_{K})^{\gamma(\psi)},

so that
\epsilon(\sigma_{\lambda},\psi,\mu)=1
whenever
\gamma(\psi)=0
and
\mu(\mathcal{O}_{K})=1
.

2.2.
Global invariants

Now let
K
be a
global function field
, namely the function field
\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
of a smooth proper geometrically irreducible curve
C
of genus
g(C)\in\mathbb{N}
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of prime power size
q\in\mathbb{N}
. The valuative criterion for properness gives a bijective correspondence between the set of closed points of
C
and the set of places
V_{K}
. In particular, an open affine subset
C_{0}\subseteq C
corresponds to a ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq K
whose fraction field is
K
, and its complement
C\setminus C_{0}
corresponds to finitely many infinite places. For each place
v\in V_{K}
, write

\mathcal{O}_{v}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}_{K_{v}},\ |\cdot|_{v}\coloneqq|\cdot|_{K_{v}},\ q_{v}\coloneqq q_{K_{v}},\ W_{v}\coloneqq W_{K_{v}},\ I_{v}\coloneqq I_{K_{v}},\ \phi_{v}\coloneqq\phi_{K_{v}}.

Furthermore, let
G_{K}
be the absolute Galois group of
K
, and let
\overline{G_{K}}\coloneqq G_{\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(C)}
be its quotient by the smallest closed subgroup containing
I_{v}
for all places
v\in V_{K}
.

As in the local case, a
\lambda
-adic representation
of
K
is a continuous homomorphism
\rho_{\lambda}:G_{K}\to\operatorname{GL}(V_{\lambda})
, where
\operatorname{GL}(V_{\lambda})
is endowed with the
\lambda
-adic topology. Assume throughout that
\rho_{\lambda}
is
almost everywhere unramified
, namely that its restrictions
\rho_{\lambda,v}\coloneqq\rho_{\lambda}|_{W_{v}}:W_{v}\to\operatorname{GL}(V_{\lambda})
are unramified as
\lambda
-adic representations of
K_{v}
at all places
v
in an open subset
C_{0}\subseteq C
. It turns out that there is an explicit equivalence between the categories of
\lambda
-adic representations unramified on
C_{0}
and of lisse
\lambda
-adic sheaves on
C_{0}

[
24
, Proposition 1.2.5]
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\rho_{\lambda}}
denote the push-forward of this lisse sheaf to
C
, which is a constructible sheaf on
C
.

A
\lambda
-adic representation
\rho_{\lambda}
is said to be
self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})
in the sense of Ulmer
[
44
, Section 3.1.4]
if

•

it is
\iota
-pure of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
for some fixed embedding
\iota:F_{\lambda}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, namely that there is some integer
w(\rho_{\lambda})\in\mathbb{Z}
such that each eigenvalue of
\rho_{\lambda}(\phi_{v})
has modulus
q_{v}^{w(\rho_{\lambda})/2}
under
\iota
at all unramified places
v\in V_{K}
, and

•

there is some field endomorphism
c(\rho_{\lambda}):F_{\lambda}\to F_{\lambda}
such that

\rho_{\lambda}^{\vee}\cong\rho_{\lambda}^{c(\rho_{\lambda})}\otimes F_{\lambda}(w(\rho_{\lambda})),

where
\rho_{\lambda}^{\vee}
is the contragredient of
\rho_{\lambda}
and
F_{\lambda}(w(\rho_{\lambda}))
is the Tate twist by
w(\rho_{\lambda})
, so that
F_{\lambda}(w(\rho_{\lambda}))|_{W_{v}}=|\cdot|_{v}^{w(\rho_{\lambda})}
at all unramified places
v\in V_{K}
.

By Lafforgue’s theorem, if
\rho_{\lambda}|_{\overline{G_{K}}}
is absolutely irreducible, then it corresponds to a unique cuspidal automorphic representation of
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{A}_{K})
in the sense of Langlands, and hence is
\iota
-pure of some weight and independent of
\lambda

[
27
, Theorem VII.6]
.

In many cases,
c(\rho_{\lambda})
is either the identity
\operatorname{id}
or complex conjugation
\operatorname{cc}
.

Example 2.4
.

As in the local case, an abelian variety
A
over
K
has a natural
\lambda
-adic representation
\rho_{A,\ell}
, which is self-dual of weight
1
and sign
\operatorname{id}
.

Example 2.5
.

As in the local case, an
Artin representation
of
K
is a continuous homomorphism
\varrho:G_{K}\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
, which is self-dual of weight
0
and some sign. When
\varrho
is one-dimensional, these correspond to Dirichlet characters with sign
\operatorname{cc}
. While they are often of special interest, Artin representations with sign
\operatorname{id}
are expected to be rare in higher dimensions
[
34
]
.

The
Artin conductor
of
\rho_{\lambda}
is the effective multiplicative divisor

\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{\lambda})\coloneqq\prod_{v\in V_{K}}v^{\alpha(\rho_{\lambda,v})},

which is a finite product since
\rho_{\lambda}
is almost everywhere unramified by assumption. When
\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{E,\ell}
arises from an elliptic curve
E
over
K
, this is the analogue for
K
of the usual conductor of
E
as seen in the LMFDB.

For each place
v\in V_{K}
, let
\psi
be a non-trivial additive character of
\mathbb{A}_{K}/K
, and let
\mu
be an additive Haar measure on
\mathbb{A}_{K}
that is trivial on
\mathbb{A}_{K}/K
and on
\mathcal{O}_{v}
for all but finitely many places
v\in V_{K}
. The
global
\epsilon
-factor
of
\rho_{\lambda}
is then

\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})\coloneqq\prod_{v\in V_{K}}\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda,v},\psi_{v},\mu_{v}),

where
\psi_{v}\coloneqq\psi|_{K_{v}}
and
\mu_{v}\coloneqq\mu|_{K_{v}}
. This is a finite product since
\gamma(\psi_{v})=0
and
\mu_{v}(\mathcal{O}_{v})=1
for all but finitely many places
v\in V_{K}
, and is independent of the choices of
\{\psi_{v}\}_{v\in V_{K}}

[
11
, Equation 5.4]
and of
\{\mu_{v}\}_{v\in V_{K}}

[
11
, Equation 5.3]
. Thus it suffices to choose
\mu_{v}
to be self-dual with respect to the Fourier inversion formula on
\psi_{v}
for each place
v\in V_{K}
, so that
\mu_{v}(\mathcal{O}_{v})=q_{v}^{-\gamma(\psi_{v})/2}

[
48
, Corollary 3 of Proposition VII.2.2]
, which defines an additive Haar measure
\mu\coloneqq\bigotimes_{v\in V_{K}}\mu_{v}
on
\mathbb{A}_{K}
that is trivial on
\mathbb{A}_{K}/K

[
48
, Corollary 2 of Theorem VII.2.1]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be a
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K
that is self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})
. Then

|\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})|=q^{(\deg\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{\lambda})+(2g(C)-2)\dim\rho_{\lambda})(w(\rho_{\lambda})+1)/2}.

Proof.

For each place
v\in V_{K}
,
[
33
, Proposition (i) of Section 12]

|\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda},\psi_{v},\mu_{v})|=q_{v}^{(\gamma(\psi_{v})\dim\rho_{\lambda}+\alpha(\rho_{\lambda,v}))(w(\rho_{\lambda})+1)/2},

so the result follows from
\prod_{v\in V_{K}}q_{v}^{\gamma(\psi_{v})}=q^{2g(C)-2}

[
48
, Proposition VII.2.6]
.
∎

2.3.
L-functions

For each place
v\in V_{K}
, the
formal local Euler factor
of
\rho_{\lambda}
at
v
is the inverse characteristic polynomial of
\rho_{\lambda,v}^{I_{v}}(\phi_{v})\in\operatorname{GL}(V_{\lambda})
, namely

\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{\lambda},T)\coloneqq\det(1-\rho_{\lambda,v}^{I_{v}}(\phi_{v})\cdot T^{\deg v})\in 1+T\cdot F_{\lambda}[T].

The
formal L-function
of
\rho_{\lambda}
is then the formal power series

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)\coloneqq\prod_{v\in V_{K}}\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{\lambda},T)^{-1}\in 1+T\cdot F_{\lambda}[[T]],

which recovers the usual L-function of
\rho_{\lambda}
by
L(\rho_{\lambda},s)\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},q^{-s})
. As a consequence of the proof of the Weil conjectures,
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
is known to be a rational function satisfying a functional equation and the Riemann hypothesis.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
.

(1)

Rationality: there are unique coprime polynomials
N(\rho_{\lambda},T),D(\rho_{\lambda},T)\in 1+T\cdot F_{\lambda}[T]
of degrees
n(\rho_{\lambda}),d(\rho_{\lambda})\in\mathbb{N}
respectively such that

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)=\dfrac{N(\rho_{\lambda},T)}{D(\rho_{\lambda},T)}.

(2)

Integrality: if
\rho_{\lambda}
has no
\overline{G_{K}}
-invariants, then
d(\rho_{\lambda})=0
.

(3)

Degree formula:

n(\rho_{\lambda})-d(\rho_{\lambda})=\deg\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{\lambda})+(2g(C)-2)\dim\rho_{\lambda}.

(4)

Functional equation:

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)=\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})\cdot T^{n(\rho_{\lambda})-d(\rho_{\lambda})}\cdot\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda}^{\vee},(qT)^{-1}).

(5)

Riemann hypothesis: if
\rho_{\lambda}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
, then the roots of
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)
have modulus
q^{-(w(\rho_{\lambda})+1)/2}
.

Proof.

Let
H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{r}(\overline{C},\mathcal{F}_{\rho_{\lambda}})
be the
r
-th
\lambda
-adic cohomology groups of
\mathcal{F}_{\rho_{\lambda}}
for each
r\in\{0,1,2\}
, and let
P_{r}(\rho_{\lambda},T)\coloneqq\det(1-H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{r}(\overline{C},\mathcal{F}_{\rho_{\lambda}})(\varphi_{q})\cdot T)
be the characteristic polynomials of
q
-Frobenius
\varphi_{q}\in\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})
acting on
H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{r}(\overline{C},\mathcal{F}_{\rho_{\lambda}})
. Then set

N(\rho_{\lambda},T)\coloneqq P_{1}(\rho_{\lambda},T),\qquad D(\rho_{\lambda},T)\coloneqq P_{0}(\rho_{\lambda},T)\cdot P_{2}(\rho_{\lambda},T).

The first statement follows from the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
. The second statement follows from Poincaré duality
[
10
, Equation 3.2.6.2]
. The third statement is the Grothendieck–Ogg–Shafarevich formula
[
22
, Theorem 7.1]
. The fourth statement follows from Deligne’s functional equation
[
11
, Theorem 9.3]
. The fifth statement follows from Deligne’s purity theorem
[
12
, Theorem 3.3.1]
. The main arguments with the required level of generality are also sketched by Ulmer
[
45
, Theorem 1.3.3 and Theorem 1.4.1]
.
∎

Theorem
2.7
is central in the computation of
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
, which will be explained now. Part (1) reduces its computation to that of
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)
and
D(\rho_{\lambda},T)
. Now
D(\rho_{\lambda},T)
is typically known, and in fact often trivial by part (2), so it remains to compute
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)
. Part (3) reduces the computation of its degree
n(\rho_{\lambda})
to that of
\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{\lambda})
, which is simply a local calculation. This is sufficient to determine the coefficients of
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)
by computing
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
for sufficiently many places
v\in V_{K}
. Parts (4) and (5) are auxiliary in the computation of
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
. Assuming that
\rho_{\lambda}
is self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})\in\{\operatorname{id},\operatorname{cc}\}
, then

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda}^{\vee},(qT)^{-1})=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda}^{c(\rho_{\lambda})}\otimes F_{\lambda}(w(\rho_{\lambda})),(qT)^{-1})=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},(q^{w(\rho_{\lambda})+1}T)^{-1})^{c(\rho_{\lambda})},

so part (4) says that

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)=\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})\cdot T^{n(\rho_{\lambda})-d(\rho_{\lambda})}\cdot\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},(q^{w(\rho_{\lambda})+1}T)^{-1})^{c(\rho_{\lambda})},

which gives a relation on the coefficients of
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)
. In particular, if
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
is known, then this significantly reduces the number of places
v\in V_{K}
necessary in the previous computation. Otherwise, it enforces relations between
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
and the coefficients of
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)
that allows for an independent computation of
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
. Both parts (4) and (5) also serve as sanity checks to test for implementation bugs.

Remark 2.8
.

The condition that
\rho_{\lambda}
has no
\overline{G_{K}}
-invariants, and hence Theorem
2.7
(2), applies in many situations. Notably, if
\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{E,\ell}
arises from a non-constant elliptic curve
E
over
K
, then
E(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(C))
is finitely generated by the Lang–Néron theorem, so
E(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(C))[\ell^{r}]
eventually stabilises for increasing
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and hence
\rho_{E,\ell}
has no
\overline{G_{K}}
-invariants. Note that this argument fails for an abelian variety
A
over
K
, since its
(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(C)/\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
-trace is not trivial in general
[
8
, Section 2]
.

Since
I_{v}\subseteq\overline{G_{K}}
for any place
v\in V_{K}
, this also applies whenever
\rho_{\lambda}
has no
I_{v}
-invariants for some place
v\in V_{K}
. For instance, this applies when
A
has purely additive reduction at some place
v\in V_{K}
, where the
I_{v}
-invariant subgroup of
\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{A,\ell}
is the identity component of the special fibre of its Néron model at
v

[
39
, Lemma 2]
, which is trivial since the Tate module of the additive group is trivial.

More generally, a tensor product of
\lambda
-adic representations, one of which with no
I_{v}
-invariants at some place
v\in V_{K}
, has no
\overline{G_{K}}
-invariants if their Artin conductors are coprime. This is the case for twists of
A
by irreducible Artin representations
\varrho
such that
(\mathfrak{f}(A),\mathfrak{f}(\varrho))=1
and
\mathfrak{f}(\varrho)\neq 1
. The latter condition applies to twists of
A
by primitive Dirichlet characters that factor through separable geometric extensions of the genus zero global function field
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
, which cannot be everywhere unramified by the Riemann–Hurwitz formula
[
35
, Theorem 7.16]
.

3.
Computing L-functions

With the application of Theorem
2.7
in mind, the setup in this section can be made completely abstract, albeit with suggestive notations. In what follows, let
F
be a field, and let
V
be a possibly infinite indexing set.

Notation 3.1
.

For any polynomial or power series
P(T)\in F[[T]]
and any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P_{i}
be the
i
-th coefficient of
P(T)
, and let
[P(T)]_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{i}P_{k}T^{k}\in 1+T\cdot F[[T]]
.

Definition 3.2
.

A
stratification
is a function
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
such that

V_{i}\coloneqq\{v\in V:\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)_{i}\neq 0,\ [\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)]_{i-1}=1\}

is finite for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case, define the formal product

\mathcal{L}(T)\coloneqq\prod_{v\in V}\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)^{-1}\in 1+T\cdot F[[T]],

which is well-defined as each coefficient of
\mathcal{L}(T)
is a finite combination of coefficients of
\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)
for finitely many indices
v\in V
. Note that the data of
F
and
V
are implicit.

In what follows, functions with values in
F
or
F[T]
should be
computable
, in the naïve sense that they can be evaluated within reasonable time. For a stratification
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
, the running time of its evaluation at any
v\in V
should preferably be polynomial in the number of bits representing
v
.

Example 3.3
.

If
\rho_{\lambda}
is an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K
, then its formal local Euler factor
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T)=\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
is a stratification of
V_{K}
, in which case
\mathcal{L}(T)=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
is its formal L-function.

3.1.
Rationality

Assume that
\mathcal{L}(T)
is the quotient of some polynomial
N(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of known degree
n\in\mathbb{N}
by some known polynomial
D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the coefficients of
N(T)=\mathcal{L}(T)\cdot D(T)
can be determined completely by taking the product of
\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)^{-1}
for sufficiently many indices
v\in V
.

Algorithm 3.4
.

Input:
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n_{0},n_{1},n^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a computable stratification,
D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a polynomial, and
n_{0},n_{1},n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
are integers with
n_{0}\leq n_{1}\leq n^{\prime}
.

(1)

Initialise
P(T)\coloneqq 1
.

(2)

For
i\in\{n_{0},\dots,n_{1}\}
and for
v\in V_{i}
, replace
P(T)\coloneqq[P(T)\cdot[\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)]_{n^{\prime}}]_{n^{\prime}}
.

(3)

Replace
P(T)\coloneqq[P(T)^{-1}]_{n^{\prime}}
.

(4)

Return
P(T)\coloneqq[P(T)\cdot[D(T)]_{n^{\prime}}]_{n^{\prime}}
.

Output: a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
be a stratification with

\mathcal{L}(T)=\dfrac{N(T)}{D(T)},

for some polynomials
N(T),D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degrees
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
respectively, and let
n_{0},n_{1},n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\{0,1\}\ni n_{0}\leq n_{1}\leq n^{\prime}\leq n
.

(1)

Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n_{0},n_{1},n^{\prime})
outputs a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
n^{\prime}
such that
[P(T)]_{n_{1}}=[N(T)]_{n_{1}}
.

(2)

If
n_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
n_{1}<n_{1}^{\prime}\leq n^{\prime}
, then Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),1,n_{1}+1,n_{1}^{\prime},n^{\prime})
outputs a polynomial
Q(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
n^{\prime}
such that
[P(T)\cdot Q(T)]_{n_{1}^{\prime}}=[N(T)]_{n_{1}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Since
V_{0}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)^{-1}\equiv\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)\equiv 1\mod T^{n_{1}+1}
for any
v\in V_{i}
whenever
i>n_{1}
, Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n_{0},n_{1},n^{\prime})
computes

P(T)=[D(T)]_{n^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{i=n_{0}}^{n_{1}}\prod_{v\in V_{i}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)^{-1}]_{n^{\prime}}\equiv D(T)\cdot\mathcal{L}(T)\mod T^{n_{1}+1},

so the first statement follows immediately. Multiplying
P(T)
with the output of Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),1,n_{1}+1,n_{1}^{\prime},n^{\prime})
increases the range of the product to
i\in\{n_{0},\dots,n_{1}^{\prime}\}
, so the second statement also follows.
∎

In particular, Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),1,n,n)
outputs
N(T)
, but the extra degrees of freedom on the last three integers will be useful later.

3.2.
Functional equation

Now in addition to rationality, assume further that
\mathcal{L}(T)
satisfies a functional equation of the form
\mathcal{L}(T)=\epsilon\cdot T^{n-d}\cdot\mathcal{L}(f/T)^{c}
, for some known field elements
\epsilon,f\in F
and some known field endomorphism
c:F\to F
.

Algorithm 3.6
.

Input:
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),m,n_{1},n^{\prime},f,c)
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a computable stratification,
D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a polynomial,
m,n_{1},n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
are integers with
m\leq n_{1}\leq n^{\prime}
,
f\in F
is a field element, and
c:F\to F
is a computable field endomorphism.

(1)

Run Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),1,n_{1},n^{\prime})
and output
P(T)
.

(2)

Set
d\coloneqq\deg D(T)
.

(3)

For
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
:

(a)

Set
h\coloneqq 1
and
k^{\prime}\coloneqq\min(d,k)
, and initialise
M_{k}\coloneqq 0
.

(b)

For
i\in\{0,\dots,k\}
:

(i)

If
0\leq k-i\leq k^{\prime}
, then replace
M_{k}\coloneqq M_{k}+P_{i}^{c}\cdot D_{d-(k-i)}\cdot h
.

(ii)

If
1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}
, then replace
M_{k}\coloneqq M_{k}-M_{k-i}\cdot D_{i}^{c}\cdot h
.

(iii)

Replace
h\coloneqq h\cdot f
.

(4)

Return
(P(T),M_{0},\dots,M_{m})
.

Output: a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
and field elements
M_{0},\dots,M_{m}\in F
.

Remark 3.7
.

The number of additions in Algorithm
3.6
can be optimised when
k\leq d
, in which case the ranges of both conditionals overlap for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. In any case, it might be interesting to obtain a closed form expression for all
M_{k}
, in contrast to a recursive procedure. Note that when
D(T)=1
, which is often the case, Algorithm
3.6
computes the simple relation
M_{k}=P_{k}^{c}f^{k}
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
be a stratification with

\mathcal{L}(T)=\dfrac{N(T)}{D(T)},

for some polynomials
N(T),D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degrees
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
respectively, satisfying a functional equation

\mathcal{L}(T)=\epsilon\cdot T^{n-d}\cdot\mathcal{L}(f/T)^{c},

for some field elements
\epsilon,f\in F
and some field endomorphism
c:F\to F
, and let
m,n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
m\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\leq n^{\prime}\leq n
. Then Algorithm
3.6
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),m,\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n^{\prime},f,c)
outputs a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
n^{\prime}
such that
[P(T)]_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=[N(T)]_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
and field elements
M_{k}\in F
such that
N_{n-k}=\epsilon M_{k}
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
.

Proof.

The functional equation says for each
r\in\{-d,\dots,n\}
,

\underset{i-j=r}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{d}}N_{i}D_{j}^{c}f^{j}=\underset{(n-i)-(d-j)=r}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{d}}\epsilon N_{i}^{c}D_{j}f^{i}.

By expressing
j\coloneqq i-r
in the left hand side, with a further replacement
i-r\mapsto i
, and by expressing
j\coloneqq d-((n-r)-i)
in the right hand side,

\sum_{i=-\min(0,r)}^{\min(d,n-r)}N_{r+i}D_{i}^{c}f^{i}=\sum_{(n-r)-i=-\min(0,r)}^{\min(d,n-r)}\epsilon N_{i}^{c}D_{d-((n-r)-i)}f^{i}.

In particular, setting
r\coloneqq n-k
for each
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, a rearrangement gives

N_{n-k}=\sum_{k-i=0}^{\min(d,k)}\epsilon N_{i}^{c}D_{d-(k-i)}f^{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\min(d,k)}N_{n-(k-i)}D_{i}^{c}f^{i}.

By Proposition
3.5
(1), step (1) of Algorithm
3.6
computes a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
n
such that
P_{i}=N_{i}
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
, and in particular for all
i\in\{0,\dots,k\}
and all
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
. The remaining steps then compute

M_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{k-i=0}^{\min(d,k)}P_{i}^{c}D_{d-(k-i)}f^{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\min(d,k)}M_{k-i}D_{i}^{c}f^{i},

so the result follows by induction.
∎

Algorithm
3.6
removes the need to compute
V_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1},\dots,V_{n}
.

Algorithm 3.9
.

Input:
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n,\epsilon,f,c)
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a computable stratification,
D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a polynomial,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is an integer,
e,f\in F
are field elements, and
c:F\to F
is a computable field endomorphism.

(1)

Set
n_{1}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and
m\coloneqq n-n_{1}-1
.

(2)

Run Algorithm
3.6
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),m,n_{1},n_{1},f,c)
and output
P(T)
and
(M_{0},\dots,M_{m})
.

(3)

For
k\in\{n-m,\dots,n\}
, replace
P(T)\coloneqq P(T)+\epsilon M_{n-k}T^{k}
.

(4)

Return
P(T)
.

Output: a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
be a stratification with

\mathcal{L}(T)=\dfrac{N(T)}{D(T)},

for some polynomials
N(T),D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degrees
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
respectively, satisfying a functional equation

\mathcal{L}(T)=\epsilon\cdot T^{n-d}\cdot\mathcal{L}(f/T)^{c},

for some field elements
\epsilon,f\in F
and some field endomorphism
c:F\to F
. Then Algorithm
3.9
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n,\epsilon,f,c)
outputs
N(T)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.8
, step (2) of Algorithm
3.9
computes the polynomial
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}N_{i}T^{i}
and field elements
M_{k}\in F
such that
N_{k}=\epsilon M_{n-k}
for all
k\in\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1,\dots,n\}
. The remaining steps then compute
N(T)=P(T)+\sum_{k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}^{n}\epsilon M_{n-k}T^{k}
, so the result follows.
∎

3.3.
\epsilon
-factors

In some cases, the field element
\epsilon
cannot be provided as an input. Algorithm
3.6
gives an independent way to compute it.

Algorithm 3.11
.

Input:
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n,f,c)
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a computable stratification,
D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
is a polynomial,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is an integer,
f\in F
is a field element, and
c:F\to F
is a computable field endomorphism.

(1)

Set
m\coloneqq n_{1}\coloneqq\lceil n/2\rceil
.

(2)

Run Algorithm
3.6
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),m,n_{1},n,f,c)
and output
P(T)
and
(M_{0},\dots,M_{m})
.

(3)

Choose
k\in\{n-m,\dots,n\}
minimal such that
M_{n-k}\neq 0
.

(4)

If
n_{1}<k
, then run Algorithm
3.4
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),1,n_{1}+1,k,k)
and output
Q(T)
.

(5)

Set
R(T)\coloneqq[P(T)\cdot Q(T)]_{k}
.

(6)

Return
\epsilon\coloneqq R_{k}/M_{n-k}
.

Output: a field element
\epsilon\in F
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T):V\to 1+T\cdot F[T]
be a stratification with

\mathcal{L}(T)=\dfrac{N(T)}{D(T)},

for some polynomials
N(T),D(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degrees
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
respectively, satisfying a functional equation

\mathcal{L}(T)=\epsilon\cdot T^{n-d}\cdot\mathcal{L}(f/T)^{c},

for some field elements
\epsilon,f\in F
and some field endomorphism
c:F\to F
. Then Algorithm
3.9
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(T),D(T),n,f,c)
outputs
\epsilon
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.8
, step (2) of Algorithm
3.11
computes a polynomial
P(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
n
such that
P_{i}=N_{i}
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,\lceil n/2\rceil\}
and field elements
M_{k}\in F
such that
N_{k}=\epsilon M_{n-k}
for all
k\in\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\dots,n\}
, and step (3) terminates for some
k\in\{n-m,\dots,n\}
since
M_{0}=D_{d}\neq 0
. By Proposition
3.5
(2), the remaining steps compute a polynomial
R(T)\in 1+T\cdot F[T]
of degree
k
such that
R_{k}=N_{k}=\epsilon M_{n-k}
, so the result follows.
∎

3.4.
\lambda
-adic representations

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
, where
\lambda\nmid q
is a fixed prime of a coefficient number field
F
. Then
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
is a rational function in
F_{\lambda}(T)
by Theorem
2.7
(1), so the following is immediate from Proposition
3.5
(1).

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K
. Then Algorithm
3.4
outputs
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)\in 1+T\cdot F_{\lambda}[T]
with inputs

(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(\rho_{\lambda},T),\ D(\rho_{\lambda},T),\ 1,\ n(\rho_{\lambda}),\ n(\rho_{\lambda})).

For many explicit
\lambda
-adic representations
\rho_{\lambda}
of a global function field
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
, the polynomial
D(\rho_{\lambda},T)
and the integer
n(\rho_{\lambda})
are easily computable, and
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
should be computable with a running time of
O(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(\deg v))
for some function
b_{\rho_{\lambda}}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
that depends only on
\deg v
rather than
v
itself. For instance, when
\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{E,\ell}
arises from an elliptic curve
E
over
K
, computing the only non-trivial coefficient of
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{E,\ell},T)
reduces to computing
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q^{\deg v}})
via Schoof’s algorithm, which has a running time of
\widetilde{O}((\deg v\cdot\log q)^{4})
in the number of operations on
F=\mathbb{Q}

[
38
]
, while
D(\rho_{\lambda},T)
and
n(\rho_{\lambda})
can be determined by Tate’s algorithm, which is very efficient.

Let
n_{1}=n^{\prime}=n(\rho_{\lambda})
. The running time of Algorithm
3.4
with the inputs of Corollary
3.13
is clearly dominated by step (2), which involves computing
\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)
and a multiplication modulo
T^{n^{\prime}}
. The latter can be done via the Schönhage–Strassen multiplication algorithm with a running time of
O(n^{\prime}\cdot\log n^{\prime}\cdot\log\log n^{\prime})=\widetilde{O}(n^{\prime})
in the number of operations on
F=F_{\lambda}

[
37
]
. By the prime number theorem for global function fields
[
35
, Theorem 5.12]
, there are
O(q^{i}/i)
places
v\in V_{K}
of degree
\deg v=i
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n_{1}\}
, so the total running time would be

O\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\dfrac{q^{i}}{i}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(i)+n^{\prime}\cdot\log n^{\prime}\cdot\log\log n^{\prime})\right)=\widetilde{O}(q^{n_{1}}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n_{1})+n^{\prime})).

Remark 3.14
.

While
b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n_{1})
usually dominates
n^{\prime}
, this is not always the case, especially if
\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)
can be obtained from a lookup table. In practice, both are dominated by
q^{n_{1}}
, so the total running time is roughly exponential in
n_{1}
. In other words, the running time is typically dominated by the enumeration of
V_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n_{1}\}
rather than the actual multiplications of
\mathcal{L}_{v}(T)
modulo
T^{n^{\prime}}
.

Assuming that
\rho_{\lambda}
is self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})\in\{\operatorname{id},\operatorname{cc}\}
, the following is immediate from Theorem
2.7
(4) and Proposition
3.10
.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
that is self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})
. Then Algorithm
3.9
outputs
N(\rho_{\lambda},T)\in 1+T\cdot F_{\lambda}[T]
with inputs

(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(\rho_{\lambda},T),\ D(\rho_{\lambda},T),\ n(\rho_{\lambda}),\ \epsilon(\rho_{\lambda}),\ 1/q^{w(\rho_{\lambda})+1},\ c(\rho_{\lambda})).

Let
n=n(\rho_{\lambda})
and
n_{1}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Step (2) of Algorithm
3.9
with the inputs of Corollary
3.15
reduces to Algorithm
3.6
with inputs
(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(\rho_{\lambda},T),D(\rho_{\lambda},T),n-n_{1}-1,n_{1},n_{1},1/q^{w(\rho_{\lambda})+1},c(\rho_{\lambda}))
. The running time of Algorithm
3.6
is in turn dominated by its step (1), which has a running time of
\widetilde{O}(q^{n_{1}}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n_{1})+n_{1}))
as in the previous discussion, since the remaining steps have a total running time of
O((n-n_{1}-1)^{2})=O(n_{1}^{2})
. Thus Algorithm
3.9
also has a total running time of
\widetilde{O}(q^{n_{1}}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n_{1})+n_{1}))
, since it is clearly dominated by its step (2).

As with
D(\rho_{\lambda})
and
n(\rho_{\lambda})
, both
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and
c(\rho_{\lambda})
are typically known, but
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
may be difficult to compute given its elusive non-constructive definition. The following is immediate from Theorem
2.7
(4) and Proposition
3.12
.

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
\rho_{\lambda}
be an almost everywhere unramified
\lambda
-adic representation of a global function field
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(C)
that is self-dual of weight
w(\rho_{\lambda})
and sign
c(\rho_{\lambda})
. Then Algorithm
3.11
outputs
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})\in F_{\lambda}^{\times}
with inputs

(\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(\rho_{\lambda},T),\ D(\rho_{\lambda},T),\ n(\rho_{\lambda}),\ 1/q^{w(\rho_{\lambda})+1},\ c(\rho_{\lambda})).

Let
n=n(\rho_{\lambda})
and
n_{1}\coloneqq\lceil n/2\rceil
. Steps (1) and (2) of Algorithm
3.11
subsume that of Algorithm
3.9
, and have the dominant running time of
\widetilde{O}(q^{n_{1}}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n_{1})+n))
as in the previous discussion, so this computation should not be repeated. In the best case scenario where
M_{m}\neq 0
, step (3) sets
k=n-m
and step (4) becomes vacuous, so its total running time is
\widetilde{O}(q^{n_{1}}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n_{1})+n))
. In the worst case scenario where
M_{1}=\dots=M_{m}=0
, step (3) sets
k=n
and step (4) has a running time of
\widetilde{O}(q^{n}\cdot(b_{\rho_{\lambda}}(n)+n))
, which is equivalent to that of Algorithm
3.4
.

Remark 3.17
.

Heuristically, this worst case scenario occurs very rarely, while the best case scenario almost always occurs. This was also observed in a footnote of Comeau-Lapointe–David–Lalín–Li for
L(E,\chi,s)

[
6
, Footnote 5]
.

4.2.
Elliptic curves

Let
\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{E,\ell}
be the
\ell
-adic representation associated to an elliptic curve
E
over
K
. Then

\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{E,\ell},T)=\begin{cases}1-a_{v}(E)T^{\deg v}+q^{\deg v}T^{2\deg v}&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is good},\\
1-T^{\deg v}&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is split multiplicative},\\
1+T^{\deg v}&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is non-split multiplicative},\\
1&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is additive},\end{cases}

where
a_{v}(E)\coloneqq 1+p-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q^{\deg v}})
is the trace of
\rho_{E,\ell}(\phi_{v})
. When
\operatorname{char}(K)>3
, there is an efficient lookup table to determine
\epsilon(\rho_{E,\ell})
in terms of Jacobi symbols
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
. If
v
is potentially good, then

\epsilon(\rho_{E,\ell})=q^{n(\rho_{E,\ell})-d(\rho_{E,\ell})}\cdot\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ \operatorname{ord}(\Delta_{v}(E))\equiv 0\mod 12,\\
(\tfrac{-3}{q})^{\deg v}&\text{if}\ \operatorname{ord}(\Delta_{v}(E))\equiv 4,8\mod 12,\\
(\tfrac{-2}{q})^{\deg v}&\text{if}\ \operatorname{ord}(\Delta_{v}(E))\equiv 3,9\mod 12,\\
(\tfrac{-1}{q})^{\deg v}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\Delta_{v}(E)
is the minimal discriminant of
E
at
v
. Otherwise

\epsilon(\rho_{E,\ell})=q^{n(\rho_{E,\ell})-d(\rho_{E,\ell})}\cdot\begin{cases}-1&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is split multiplicative},\\
1&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is non-split multiplicative},\\
(\tfrac{-1}{q})^{\deg v}&\text{if}\ v\ \text{is additive}.\end{cases}

Remark 4.2
.

When
\operatorname{char}(K)\in\{2,3\}
, it may be possible to generalise the root number results by Kobayashi
[
26
]
and Dokchitser–Dokchitser
[
14
]
to
K
.

If
E
is
constant
, in the sense that it arises as the base change to
K
of an elliptic curve
E_{0}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then the remaining are

D(\rho_{E,\ell},T)=N(E_{0},T)\cdot N(E_{0},qT),\ n(\rho_{E,\ell})=4g(C),\ w(\rho_{E,\ell})=1,\ c(\rho_{E,\ell})=\operatorname{id},

where
N(E_{0},T)
is the numerator of the
\zeta
-function of
E_{0}
. Otherwise,

D(\rho_{E,\ell},T)=1,\ n(\rho_{E,\ell})=\deg\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{E,\ell})+4g(C)-4,\ w(\rho_{E,\ell})=1,\ c(\rho_{E,\ell})=\operatorname{id},

where
\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{E,\ell})
can be determined from Tate’s algorithm.

Remark 4.3
.

When
E
is non-constant, its associated elliptic surface
\mathcal{E}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has at least one singular fibre. Then
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{E,\ell},T)=N(\rho_{E,\ell},T)
is the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi_{q}
acting on the orthogonal complement of the trivial sublattice of the Néron–Severi group
\operatorname{NS}(\mathcal{E})
under its cycle class map to the second
\ell
-adic cohomology group
H_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{E}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}(1))

[
40
, Theorem 4]
. This is the idea behind Magma’s implementation of
LFunction
for non-constant elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
.

For simplicity, the following example considers
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
, but the same algorithm will work for general
K
assuming a uniform algorithm to compute
a_{v}(E)
.

4.3.
Dirichlet characters

Let
\rho_{\lambda}=\chi
be a primitive Dirichlet character of
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
of some monic non-constant square-free modulus
P(t)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. This is a homomorphism
\chi:(\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/P(t))^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
that factors through a separable geometric extension of
K
, so it makes sense to evaluate
\chi
at a finite place
v\in V_{K}
by considering any generator of the ideal of
v
. By viewing
\chi
as a character of
G_{K}
, it also makes sense to evaluate
\chi
at the unique degree one infinite place
1/t\in V_{K}
and compute
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)=P(t)\cdot(1/t)^{\alpha(\chi_{1/t})}
, with
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]

\chi(1/t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ \chi|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\alpha(\chi_{1/t})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \chi|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}=1,\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 4.5
.

These statements can be generalised to primitive Dirichlet characters of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
of arbitrary modulus by studying the ramification behaviour of
1/t
over Carlitz extensions, which will be explored in a future work.

Then
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\chi,T)=1-\chi(v)T^{\deg v}
for any place
v\in V_{K}
, and the invariants are

D(\chi,T)=1,\ n(\chi)=\deg\mathfrak{f}(\chi)-2,\ w(\chi)=0,\ c(\chi)=\operatorname{cc}.

Now
\epsilon(\chi)
can be computed via Gauss sums
[
9
, Corollary 2.4]
or the canonical divisor class
[
47
, Theorem 6.4.6]
, but this is not necessary given Corollary
3.16
.

, so Theorem
2.7
(5) is verified.

4.4.
Other examples

The range of examples of
\rho_{\lambda}
illustrated here is only limited by the computations of
\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{\lambda})
and
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
that are easily done by hand or currently implemented in Magma. With a bit more work, many other examples of
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
and
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
can be computed from well-known results in the existing literature.

An Artin representation of
K
can be given as an explicit matrix representation
\varrho:\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
for some finite extension
L
of
K
, in which case
\mathfrak{f}(\varrho)
is immediate. Computing
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\varrho,T)
then reduces to identifying
\phi_{v}
as elements of
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
, which are effectively decidable thanks to the work of Dokchitser–Dokchitser
[
15
]
. Furthermore
w(\varrho)=0
, and if
\varrho
is irreducible and ramified somewhere, then
D(\varrho,T)=1
and
n(\varrho)=\deg\mathfrak{f}(\varrho)+4g(C)-4
. In many cases
c(\varrho)\in\{\operatorname{id},\operatorname{cc}\}
, but this is not a necessary restriction, and the case when
c(\varrho):F\to F
is an arbitrary field endomorphism will be explored in a future work.

The Jacobian
J
of a curve
X
over
K
has
w(\rho_{J,\ell})=1
and
c(\rho_{J,\ell})=\operatorname{id}
, and
n(\rho_{J,\ell})-d(\rho_{J,\ell})=\deg\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{J,\ell})+2g(X)(2g(C)-2)
. If
X
is given by an explicit hyperelliptic equation with
\operatorname{char}(K)\neq 2
, then
\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{J,\ell})
can be determined completely from the machinery of cluster pictures of Dokchitser–Dokchitser–Maistret–Morgan
[
13
]
, with examples of families over number fields worked out explicitly
[
2
]
. Computing
\mathcal{L}_{v}(\rho_{J,\ell},T)
could involve decomposing
\rho_{J,\ell}
into simpler components whose local Euler factors are computable, similar to the work of Maistret–Sutherland for many genus two curves over
\mathbb{Q}

[
29
]
. Note that when
X
is superelliptic, there are analogous decompositions of
\rho_{J,\ell}
in terms of cluster pictures
[
30
]
.

By Lafforgue’s theorem, the L-function
L(\rho,s)
of a cuspidal automorphic representation
\rho
of
K
corresponding to some
\lambda
-adic representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
K
would naturally inherit the rationality and functional equation of
L(\rho_{\lambda},s)
, so its computation can be done similarly. This may include L-functions of automorphic origin, such as those of Drinfeld modular forms arising from non-constant elliptic curves
[
23
]
, which are certain rigid-analytic functions on the Drinfeld upper half plane constructed from Drinfeld modules
[
18
]
. It may be interesting to see if the positive characteristic Goss L-functions of Drinfeld modules, and more generally of
t
-motives
[
19
]
, can fit in the abstract framework of the previous section.

Finally, let
\rho_{\lambda}
and
\sigma_{\lambda}
be two
\lambda
-adic representations of
K
. In many cases, including when
(\mathfrak{f}(\rho_{\lambda}),\mathfrak{f}(\sigma_{\lambda}))=1
, the invariants of
\rho_{\lambda}\otimes\sigma_{\lambda}
can be expressed in terms of those of
\rho_{\lambda}
and
\sigma_{\lambda}
. In particular, having an explicit expression of
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda}\otimes\sigma_{\lambda})
in terms of
\epsilon(\rho_{\lambda})
and
\epsilon(\sigma_{\lambda})
via a global version of Proposition
2.3
caps the worst running time in the computation of
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda}\otimes\sigma_{\lambda},T)
in terms of those for
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\lambda},T)
and for
\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{\lambda},T)
. This will be explored in a future work.
