Job satisfaction among Saudi and Non-Saudi critical care and emergency nurses: associations with psychological distress, turnover intention, and perceived retention strategies
Authors: Zyad T. Saleh, Majed S. Al-Za’areer, Rami A. Elshatarat, Amal Ali Alasmari, Wesam T. Almagharbeh, Faroq Abdulghani Alshameri, Hala Zaiton, Abdullah R. Allugmani, Bader Hameed Aljuhani, Rami Saleem Aloufi, Khaled M. Al-Sayaghi, Ghaida Shujayyi Alsulami, Abdallah Hussein Amoush, Maha Salah Ismail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCritical care and emergency nurses work in complex and demanding environments that may affect their occupational well-being, psychological health, and job satisfaction. Although previous studies have examined nurse retention challenges, limited evidence exists regarding factors associated with job satisfaction among Saudi and non-Saudi nurses working in high-acuity clinical settings in Saudi Arabia.PurposeThis study aimed to assess job satisfaction, psychological distress, turnover intention, and perceived organizational retention strategies among critical care and emergency nurses in Saudi Arabia and to identify factors statistically associated with higher job satisfaction.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional correlational study was conducted among 494 critical care and emergency nurses recruited through convenience sampling from hospitals across five cities in northwestern Saudi Arabia. Data were collected using the McCloskey/Mueller Satisfaction Scale, Turnover Intention Scale, Patient Health Questionnaire-2, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2, and a structured retention strategy questionnaire. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, Pearson correlation analysis, and multiple logistic regression analysis were performed.ResultsOverall, 57.9% of nurses reported moderate job satisfaction and 51.8% reported moderate turnover intention. Non-Saudi nurses reported higher perceived job distress and turnover intention compared with Saudi nurses. Job satisfaction was negatively associated with turnover intention (r = − 0.435, p = 0.021). Multiple logistic regression analysis showed that salary ≥ 10,000 Saudi Riyals (OR = 2.68), employment in governmental hospitals (OR = 3.14), working in emergency departments (OR = 1.92), lower perceived job distress (OR = 2.62), absence of anxiety symptoms (OR = 2.47), lower back pain severity (OR = 1.86), and absence of depressive symptoms (OR = 1.58) were statistically associated with higher job satisfaction.ConclusionJob satisfaction among critical care and emergency nurses was associated with workplace characteristics, physical health status, and psychological well-being. Although turnover intention was inversely correlated with job satisfaction, the cross-sectional design limits interpretation of temporal relationships. Organizational strategies focusing on supportive work environments, psychological well-being, equitable employment conditions, and professional development may contribute to improved nurse satisfaction and workforce stability.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Critical care and emergency nursing environments are widely recognized as among the most demanding healthcare settings due to high patient acuity, rapid decision-making, emotional burden, and continuous exposure to life-threatening conditions [1, 2]. These occupational demands have been consistently associated with psychological distress, burnout, reduced job satisfaction, and increased turnover intention, contributing to workforce instability in high-acuity settings [3–6]. In Saudi Arabia, these challenges are further compounded by nursing shortages, reliance on expatriate nurses, increasing healthcare demands, and organizational diversity across governmental and private healthcare sectors [4, 7].

Previous studies conducted in Saudi Arabia have demonstrated that nurses working in intensive care units and emergency departments frequently experience moderate-to-high levels of burnout, emotional exhaustion, and only moderate levels of job satisfaction [8, 9]. Psychological distress, including anxiety and depressive symptoms, has also been associated with poorer occupational well-being and higher turnover intention among nurses [10, 11]. Similarly, physical health problems such as musculoskeletal pain, sleep disturbances, fatigue, and back pain are prevalent among nurses working in high-acuity environments and may further compromise their work performance and occupational well-being [2, 9].

Organizational retention strategies represent important workplace approaches intended to improve nurses’ professional experiences and support workforce stability. Previous research has identified several organizational practices that may contribute to nurses’ perceptions of supportive work environments, including effective leadership, flexible scheduling, adequate staffing, professional development opportunities, recognition, financial incentives, and workplace safety initiatives [12, 13]. Although these strategies are increasingly emphasized in nursing workforce planning, their implementation and perceived importance may vary according to nurses’ employment circumstances and organizational contexts.

Turnover intention is another important workforce indicator frequently examined alongside job satisfaction. Previous studies have demonstrated that nurses’ intention to leave is associated with occupational stress, psychological distress, workload, organizational support, and workplace conditions [12, 14, 15]. However, turnover intention is a complex workforce outcome influenced by multiple interacting factors, and understanding its relationship with job satisfaction provides important descriptive information regarding nurses’ occupational experiences.

Despite increasing research on the nursing workforce in Saudi Arabia, several important gaps remain. Several previous studies have examined burnout, job satisfaction, or turnover intention independently or have focused on specific nursing specialties rather than integrating critical care and emergency nurses within the same study population [16–18]. Furthermore, limited studies have simultaneously examined demographic characteristics, occupational factors, physical health problems, psychological distress, job satisfaction, turnover intention, and perceived organizational retention strategies among nurses working in high-acuity clinical settings [4, 8, 16].

Additionally, evidence comparing Saudi and non-Saudi nurses remains limited despite the multinational composition of the Saudi nursing workforce [4, 18]. Differences in employment conditions, compensation, career opportunities, organizational support, and cultural adaptation may contribute to variations in nurses’ workplace experiences and satisfaction. Understanding these differences is essential for developing equitable workforce strategies that address the needs of both Saudi and expatriate nurses [4, 16, 18].

Therefore, the present study aimed to examine job satisfaction as the primary outcome among Saudi and non-Saudi critical care and emergency nurses in Saudi Arabia. Specifically, the study aimed to describe nurses’ job satisfaction, turnover intention, physical and psychological health problems, and perceived organizational retention strategies; compare these characteristics between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses; examine the association between job satisfaction and turnover intention; and identify demographic, occupational, physical, and psychological factors independently associated with high job satisfaction using multiple logistic regression analysis.

Study hypotheses

Based on previous literature and the study objectives, the following hypotheses were examined: (1) Job satisfaction is significantly associated with turnover intention among critical care and emergency nurses; (2) Demographic, employment, physical health, and psychological characteristics are significantly associated with nurses’ job satisfaction; and (3) Saudi and non-Saudi critical care and emergency nurses differ in job satisfaction, turnover intention, selected physical and psychological health characteristics, and perceived organizational retention strategies.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional correlational design was used to examine job satisfaction, turnover intention, physical and psychological health problems, and perceived organizational retention strategies among Saudi and non-Saudi critical care and emergency nurses in northwestern Saudi Arabia. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies [19, 20].

The study was designed to examine associations among occupational, psychological, physical, and organizational characteristics rather than to evaluate causal relationships. Job satisfaction was considered the primary outcome for multivariable analysis, whereas turnover intention was examined descriptively and through its bivariate association with job satisfaction.

Setting and sample

The study was conducted in five major cities in the northwestern region of Saudi Arabia, including Medina, Tabuk, Jeddah, Makkah, and Yanbu. Participants were recruited from governmental and private hospitals providing critical and emergency healthcare services.

The target population consisted of registered nurses currently working in intensive care units, coronary care units, and emergency departments. Nurses were eligible to participate if they were currently employed in hospital-based critical care or emergency settings, had at least six months of clinical experience, and were able to read and understand English. Nurses working exclusively in outpatient clinics, educational settings, or administrative departments, as well as nurses on leave during the data collection period, were excluded.

A non-probability convenience sampling technique [19, 20] was used because no comprehensive sampling frame of critical care and emergency nurses was available across the participating hospitals, and an online survey enabled recruitment of nurses working different shifts and geographical locations [19, 20]. Participants were recruited through verified professional nursing groups, hospital communication networks, and nursing associations to maximize access to the target population.

Eligibility was verified through self-report questions requiring participants to confirm that they were registered nurses currently working in an intensive care unit, coronary care unit, or emergency department before they were permitted to continue the survey.

Although convenience online sampling facilitated broad geographical recruitment, it may have introduced selection bias because nurses with greater interest in workplace issues or stronger opinions regarding job satisfaction or turnover intention may have been more likely to participate [19, 20]. Furthermore, because the survey link was distributed electronically through multiple professional platforms, the total number of eligible nurses who received the invitation could not be determined; therefore, a response rate could not be calculated. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution and may not be fully generalizable to all critical care and emergency nurses in Saudi Arabia.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1 [21]. An a priori power analysis was performed to estimate the minimum sample size required for the planned statistical analyses, including Chi-square tests (χ²), Pearson correlation analyses (r), and multiple logistic regression analysis. The calculation was based on a two-tailed significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 80% (1–β = 0.80), and a moderate effect size of 0.30 [21, 22]. For the multiple logistic regression analysis, the sample size estimation considered approximately 10 independent variables, including demographic, occupational, psychological, and organizational factors potentially associated with job satisfaction and turnover intention.

Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be approximately 420 nurses. To compensate for potential incomplete responses, non-response, and to enhance the reliability and precision of the multivariable regression estimates, additional participants were recruited. Ultimately, 494 nurses completed the survey and were included in the final analysis, exceeding the calculated minimum sample size and ensuring adequate statistical power for the planned analyses.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted between November 2025 and January 2026 using a secure online survey platform. The survey link was disseminated through hospital communication systems, professional nursing forums, and social media groups commonly used by nurses working in critical care and emergency departments.

The first page of the online survey included a detailed participant information sheet describing the study purpose, procedures, confidentiality, voluntary participation, estimated completion time, and participants’ rights. Participants were required to click the “Next” button before accessing the questionnaire, which served as their electronic informed consent.

All questionnaire items were programmed as mandatory fields; therefore, no missing data were identified in the final dataset. The survey required approximately 15–20 min to complete.

To minimize duplicate participation, participants were asked to provide either an email address or telephone number solely for verification purposes. These identifiers were reviewed only to identify duplicate submissions and to confirm that each individual participated once. The identifiers were not linked to questionnaire responses, were not included in the analytical dataset, and were permanently removed immediately after verification.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Al-Rayan Colleges (Approval No. N-2025-023) before data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, including voluntary participation, informed consent, confidentiality, privacy protection, and minimization of potential risks to participants.

Before accessing the online questionnaire, participants received a study information sheet describing the study purpose, procedures, confidentiality measures, and their rights as research participants, and provided electronic informed consent. To prevent duplicate survey submissions, participants were informed that a temporary contact identifier (telephone number or email address) would be collected solely for verification purposes. This procedure was approved by the IRB and included in the informed consent process.

Following duplicate verification, all contact identifiers were permanently deleted before data analysis, and only fully de-identified data were retained. All study data were securely stored and used exclusively for research purposes in accordance with institutional and international ethical standards.

Instruments

Demographic and work-related questionnaire

A structured questionnaire developed by the researchers was used to collect demographic and occupational characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, educational level, nationality, monthly salary, years of nursing experience, hospital type, and clinical work area. The questionnaire items were adapted from recent studies investigating job satisfaction, turnover intention, and workforce characteristics among nurses in Saudi Arabia and similar healthcare settings [4, 18], ensuring that the collected variables reflected factors consistently associated with nursing workforce outcomes.

Physical and psychological disorders/problems

Participants were asked to report common physical and psychological symptoms experienced while working in critical care and emergency settings. Physical symptoms included gastrointestinal problems, cardiovascular symptoms, musculoskeletal pain, headaches, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and back pain. These symptoms were assessed as self-reported occupational health problems commonly associated with high workload, psychological stress, shift work, and prolonged exposure to critical clinical situations among emergency and critical care nurses. The symptom checklist was adapted from recent occupational health studies among hospital nurses [23–25], supporting the continued relevance of these indicators for evaluating nurses’ physical health in contemporary clinical environments. Previous studies have identified these physical health problems as important occupational concerns among nurses working in high-acuity settings because of their potential associations with work-related stress, reduced well-being, and impaired occupational functioning [23–26].

Psychological symptoms were assessed using the validated ultra-brief screening instruments, the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2) [27, 28]. The PHQ-2 is a two-item screening instrument derived from the PHQ-9 and is commonly used to identify individuals with possible depressive symptoms during the previous two weeks. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 6. Scores of ≥ 3 indicate possible depressive symptoms requiring further clinical assessment rather than a diagnosis of depression [27, 29, 30].

Similarly, anxiety symptoms were assessed using the GAD-2, a two-item screening instrument derived from the GAD-7 [31]. Each item is rated using the same four-point response scale, yielding total scores from 0 to 6. Scores of ≥ 3 indicate possible clinically relevant anxiety symptoms warranting further assessment rather than a confirmed anxiety disorder [27, 30, 32].

Although Arabic versions of the PHQ-2 and GAD-2 have demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Middle Eastern populations [33], no validation study specific to critical care and emergency nurses in Saudi Arabia was identified. Therefore, the internationally recommended screening cut-off score (≥ 3) was adopted to facilitate comparison with previous nursing studies [29, 32]. The findings derived from these instruments should therefore be interpreted as reflecting possible depressive and anxiety symptoms rather than clinical diagnoses.

In addition, emotional exhaustion and perceived job-related distress were assessed using two self-reported numerical rating scales (0–10). Although these single-item measures are not intended to replace comprehensive multidimensional instruments, they have been widely used in occupational and clinical research to provide rapid assessment of symptom severity while minimizing respondent burden in busy clinical environments [34]. Recent nursing workforce studies have continued to employ brief self-report measures to assess occupational strain when survey burden is a concern [3, 13, 35]. These measures were included to capture important indicators of psychological burden and occupational stress frequently reported among critical care and emergency nurses, rather than to provide clinical diagnoses of burnout or psychological disorders.

McCloskey/Mueller Satisfaction Scale (MMSS)

Job satisfaction was assessed using the McCloskey/Mueller Satisfaction Scale developed by Mueller and McCloskey [36]. The MMSS contains 31 items assessing nurses’ satisfaction across several domains, including extrinsic rewards, scheduling and work-family balance, professional opportunities, co-worker relationships, interaction opportunities, praise and recognition, and control and responsibility [36–38].

Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Very dissatisfied”) to 5 (“Very satisfied”) [37, 38]. Total scores ranged from 31 to 155, with higher scores indicating greater job satisfaction. Mean item scores were interpreted as follows: <3.0 indicated low satisfaction, 3.0–3.9 indicated moderate satisfaction, and ≥ 4.0 indicated high satisfaction [37]. The MMSS continues to be widely used in contemporary nursing workforce research across diverse healthcare settings, including studies conducted in Asia, Europe, and the Middle East [38–40], supporting its continued relevance for assessing nurses’ job satisfaction.

For descriptive analyses, the MMSS was analyzed as a continuous measure and also categorized into low, moderate, and high satisfaction to facilitate interpretation. For logistic regression analyses, job satisfaction was dichotomized (high versus low/moderate satisfaction) because the primary objective was to identify characteristics associated with membership in the highly satisfied group, thereby allowing estimation of adjusted odds ratios (aORs). Although dichotomization may reduce statistical power and result in some loss of information, this approach was selected to enhance the clinical interpretability of the regression findings and is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Turnover intention scale

Turnover intention was assessed using the four-item turnover intention scale developed by Mobley et al. (1978) [41]. The instrument measures nurses’ intention to leave their current hospital employment through questions regarding thoughts of leaving, job searching, intention to resign, and willingness to leave if another opportunity became available. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating greater turnover intention [41]. The scale has remained one of the most frequently applied instruments for evaluating turnover intention among nurses and has been successfully used in recent workforce studies conducted in hospital settings [26, 42], demonstrating its continued applicability.

Retention strategy questionnaire

Nurses’ perceptions of organizational retention strategies were assessed using a structured questionnaire developed by the researchers based on an extensive review of international and Saudi nursing workforce literature and previous studies examining nurse retention, organizational support, and workforce sustainability [43–47].

The questionnaire consisted of 27 items organized into eight domains: staffing and workload management, work environment and safety, leadership and organizational support, professional development and career advancement, compensation and recognition, communication and participation, work-life balance and scheduling flexibility, and technological and workplace resources. Rather than measuring a latent psychometric construct, the questionnaire was developed as a structured descriptive checklist to identify organizational strategies perceived by nurses as important for retaining the nursing workforce. Accordingly, each strategy represented an independent organizational practice rather than an indicator of an underlying dimension. Consequently, exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was not considered appropriate because the questionnaire was not intended to produce a summed scale score or latent factor structure.

Participants were asked to identify organizational strategies that they perceived would encourage them to remain in their current employment. Items addressed organizational practices related to staffing adequacy, workplace safety, leadership support, professional development opportunities, career advancement, financial and non-financial recognition, communication, work-life balance, technological support, and organizational resources. An additional open-ended response option was provided to allow participants to report other retention strategies that were not included among the predefined items.

To establish content validity [48], the initial item pool was reviewed by a panel of five experts with expertise in nursing administration, critical care nursing, emergency nursing, and nursing research methodology. The experts evaluated each item for relevance, clarity, comprehensiveness, and cultural appropriateness. Based on expert feedback, several items were revised for wording clarity and contextual relevance, and the final questionnaire included 27 items across eight retention strategy domains [48]. The content and structure of the questionnaire were informed by previous investigations examining nurse retention and workforce sustainability [46, 49], supporting the relevance of the included organizational domains.

Tool validity, reliability, and pilot study

The selection and adaptation of all study instruments were guided by the study objectives and supported by previous nursing workforce research. Previously validated instruments were used whenever available, including the MMSS, Turnover Intention Scale, PHQ-2, and GAD-2.

For the researcher-developed Retention Strategy Questionnaire, content validity was established through a systematic development process that included an extensive review of the nursing workforce and retention literature, expert evaluation, and pilot testing [48]. The questionnaire comprised 27 items across eight domains representing organizational retention strategies, including work environment, leadership support, professional development, career advancement, compensation and recognition, communication, work-life balance, and workplace safety.

An expert panel consisting of five specialists in nursing administration, critical care nursing, emergency nursing, and research methodology independently evaluated the questionnaire for item relevance, clarity, comprehensiveness, and cultural appropriateness [48]. Based on their recommendations, minor modifications were made to improve item wording and content coverage.

A pilot study was conducted among 30 critical care and emergency nurses who met the study inclusion criteria to evaluate questionnaire clarity, readability, feasibility, cultural appropriateness, and estimated completion time. Minor wording and formatting modifications were implemented based on participant feedback. Data obtained from the pilot study were excluded from the final analyses. Internal consistency reliability of the researcher-developed Retention Strategy Questionnaire, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha [48, 50] during the pilot study, was 0.81, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Because the questionnaire was designed primarily as a structured descriptive instrument to assess nurses’ perceptions of individual organizational retention strategies rather than a latent psychometric construct, exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was not performed, and responses were analyzed at the individual-item level rather than as a composite scale.

Internal consistency reliability was also assessed for the validated psychometric instruments using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients [48, 50]. The MMSS demonstrated excellent reliability (α = 0.91), the Turnover Intention Scale demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.87), and the PHQ-2 and GAD-2 demonstrated acceptable reliability coefficients of α = 0.81 and α = 0.84, respectively.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 [51]. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize participants’ demographic and work-related characteristics, physical and psychological symptoms, job satisfaction, turnover intention, and perceived retention strategies. In keeping with the cross-sectional correlational design, all analyses were conducted to examine associations between variables rather than to infer causal relationships. Pearson’s chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses, while Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the continuous job satisfaction and turnover intention scores.

Job satisfaction was analyzed both as a continuous and a categorical variable. The continuous MMSS score was used for descriptive and correlation analyses, whereas for logistic regression analyses, job satisfaction was dichotomized into high (mean score ≥ 4.0) and low/moderate (mean score < 4.0) categories according to the established MMSS scoring guidelines. This approach was used to identify factors associated with a high level of current job satisfaction.

To identify factors associated with current job satisfaction, a two-step logistic regression procedure was performed [20, 52]. First, separate univariate simple logistic regression analyses were conducted for the demographic and employment characteristics and for the physical and psychological disorders/problems. The demographic and employment variables included age, educational level, gender, marital status, monthly salary, job position, type of hospital, area of work, total nursing experience, and experience in the current hospital. The physical and psychological variables included gastrointestinal problems, cardiovascular symptoms, musculoskeletal pain, back pain, headache/migraine, fatigue/exhaustion, sleep disturbances, depressive symptoms measured using the PHQ-2, anxiety symptoms measured using the GAD-2, emotional exhaustion, and perceived job distress. Second, the variables that demonstrated statistically significant associations with current job satisfaction in the initial univariate logistic regression analyses (p < 0.05) were selected and entered into the multiple logistic regression analysis simultaneously. Thus, the final multiple logistic regression model was constructed from the statistically significant variables identified in the preceding univariate analyses [51]. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate the strength and precision of the associations. Model assumptions were evaluated by assessing multicollinearity, goodness-of-fit, and influential observations, and no major violations were identified. Because the PHQ-2 and GAD-2 are screening rather than diagnostic instruments, findings were interpreted as reflecting possible depressive and anxiety symptoms rather than confirmed clinical diagnoses. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and work-related characteristics

The study included 494 nurses, of whom 153 (31.0%) were Saudi nurses and 341 (69.0%) were non-Saudi nurses. As presented in Table 1, approximately half of the participants were younger than 30 years (50.2%), and the majority held a bachelor’s degree (69.2%), were female (77.3%), married (55.1%), and worked as staff nurses (80.6%). Participants were mainly employed in intensive care units (36.8%), coronary care units (33.0%), and emergency departments (30.2%).

Comparison of demographic and work-related characteristics between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses showed that the groups were comparable across most variables, including age, education level, gender, marital status, job position, hospital type, clinical area, and years of nursing experience. Monthly salary was the only characteristic demonstrating a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.004), with Saudi nurses more frequently reporting salaries ≥ 10,000 Saudi Riyals compared with non-Saudi nurses.

No statistically significant differences were observed between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses for hospital type (p = 0.082) or any other demographic and work-related characteristics except monthly salary.

Physical and psychological health problems among nurses

The prevalence of reported physical and psychological health problems according to nationality is presented in Table 2. Cardiovascular symptoms were reported by 28.7% of nurses and differed significantly between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses, with a higher proportion among Saudi nurses (35.9% vs. 25.5%, p = 0.025). Back pain also differed significantly between the two groups, with moderate-to-severe back pain reported more frequently among non-Saudi nurses than Saudi nurses (38.4% vs. 29.4%, p = 0.046).

After adjustment for the variables included in the model, salary ≥ 10,000 Saudi Riyals (aOR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.21–3.29, p = 0.018), employment in governmental hospitals (aOR = 3.14, 95% CI: 1.27–3.41, p = 0.003), and working in emergency departments (aOR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.09–2.53, p = 0.029) were significantly associated with higher odds of reporting high job satisfaction.

Among physical and psychological variables, lower back pain severity (aOR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.22–2.74, p = 0.004), absence of depressive symptoms (aOR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.02–2.36, p = 0.041), absence of anxiety symptoms (aOR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.61–3.58, p < 0.001), and lower perceived job distress (aOR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.73–3.79, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with higher odds of high job satisfaction.

Overall, the multiple logistic regression analysis identified employment characteristics, physical health, and psychological well-being as factors associated with current job satisfaction among the participating nurses. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings represent statistical associations and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships.

Discussion

This study examined job satisfaction, psychological distress, turnover intention, and perceived retention strategies among critical care and emergency nurses in Saudi Arabia, with a particular focus on differences between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses. The findings extend current evidence by examining these workforce outcomes within the context of Saudi Arabia’s multinational nursing workforce.

Overall, nurses reported moderate job satisfaction, considerable psychological distress, and moderate turnover intention. The observed relationships among these variables represent statistical associations and should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the cross-sectional design.

An important contribution of this study is the comparison between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses, providing insight into how employment conditions, workplace experiences, and psychological well-being may be associated with nurse retention in the Saudi healthcare system.

Workforce context and Saudi–non-Saudi differences

The findings reflect the current composition of the Saudi nursing workforce, where non-Saudi nurses represent a substantial proportion of nurses working in specialized settings [4, 8]. Similar workforce patterns have been reported in Saudi Arabia, where expatriate nurses frequently experience unique occupational challenges related to career development, employment conditions, and organizational support [4, 8].

Although Saudi and non-Saudi nurses were comparable across most demographic and professional characteristics, salary differences and higher turnover intention among non-Saudi nurses highlight the potential influence of employment circumstances, perceived organizational fairness, and career opportunities. Nationality should not be considered a direct cause of turnover intention but rather a marker of different social and occupational experiences.

These findings support organizational justice theory, which suggests that perceptions of fairness in resource distribution, recognition, and career opportunities influence employees’ attitudes toward their workplace and intention to remain [1, 8, 53].

Psychological distress and occupational health challenges

The study identified substantial psychological and physical health concerns among nurses, including depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, emotional exhaustion, and job distress. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that nurses working in intensive care and emergency environments experience high occupational demands due to workload, emotional stress, and exposure to critical events [9, 10].

Importantly, emotional exhaustion should not be considered equivalent to burnout. Burnout is a multidimensional construct involving emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced professional efficacy; therefore, the current findings indicate psychological strain rather than burnout as a complete syndrome [9, 10, 16].

The Job Demands–Resources model provides a useful explanation, suggesting that excessive workload and emotional demands may increase psychological strain, whereas organizational resources such as leadership support, teamwork, and professional development opportunities may protect nurses from adverse outcomes [3, 11, 13, 35].

Non-Saudi nurses reported higher perceived job distress than Saudi nurses. This difference may reflect additional challenges experienced by expatriate nurses, including cultural adaptation, employment uncertainty, reduced social support, and perceived inequities [4, 17, 54]. However, longitudinal studies are required to determine the mechanisms underlying these differences.

Job satisfaction, turnover intention, and retention

Nurses reported the highest satisfaction with interpersonal relationships, physician collaboration, and social interaction. These findings emphasize the importance of supportive professional relationships as organizational resources that enhance teamwork and psychological safety. Conversely, dissatisfaction was greatest regarding professional advancement, research opportunities, benefits, and career development. These findings suggest that retention strategies should address not only interpersonal relationships but also organizational policies that support professional growth and recognition [16, 43, 55].

The significant negative association between job satisfaction and turnover intention is consistent with previous research [3, 53]. However, the cross-sectional design prevents determining whether reduced satisfaction increases turnover intention or whether nurses intending to leave perceive their workplace more negatively.

Recent evidence supports the importance of examining organizational mechanisms underlying turnover. Luo et al. (2024) found that workplace psychological violence influenced turnover intention through job satisfaction [14], while Lu et al. (2023) demonstrated that psychological contract fulfillment was associated with lower turnover intention among nurses [55]. These findings highlight that retention depends on broader organizational experiences rather than satisfaction alone.

Factors associated with job satisfaction

The regression analysis identified salary, governmental hospital employment, emergency department employment, lower psychological distress, and fewer physical health problems as factors associated with higher job satisfaction. However, these findings indicate associations rather than causal effects, as the cross-sectional design does not allow determination of the direction or timing of these relationships [4, 16].

Higher salary was associated with greater satisfaction, supporting previous evidence that compensation influences perceptions of recognition, fairness, and organizational value among nurses [4, 56]. However, financial incentives alone are unlikely to address retention challenges without concurrent improvements in psychological support, leadership practices, and opportunities for professional growth. The strong relationship observed between psychological well-being and job satisfaction highlights the importance of incorporating mental health support and strategies to reduce workplace distress as essential components of nursing workforce planning [9, 15, 44, 57, 58].

Overall, the findings indicate that nurses’ retention outcomes are associated with multiple interacting factors, including psychological well-being, job satisfaction, organizational conditions, and employment experiences. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that turnover intention among nurses is not determined by a single factor but reflects the combined effects of workplace stress, organizational support, and perceptions of fairness [4, 9, 15, 44]. The present study contributes to the literature by integrating psychological distress, job satisfaction, turnover intention, and perceived retention strategies while comparing Saudi and non-Saudi nurses within the same healthcare context.

Comparison with similar recent studies

Recent international studies support the current findings that occupational stress, health problems, and organizational factors are associated with nurses’ retention outcomes. Zhang et al. (2025) reported that occupational health problems, income, and work intensity were associated with turnover intention among nurses in China [24]. Importantly, Zhang et al. (2025) focused specifically on head nurses, who occupy managerial or supervisory roles, whereas the present study focused primarily on frontline critical care and emergency nurses directly involved in patient care. Wang et al. (2022) identified workload, occupational stress, and workplace relationships as important factors related to nurses’ intention to leave [59]. These findings align with the current study’s observation that psychological distress, physical health concerns, and employment conditions are important aspects of nurses’ workplace experiences.

Studies among emergency and critical care nurses also demonstrate similar patterns. Ma et al. (2022) found that work stress and organizational factors were associated with turnover intention, whereas job satisfaction and organizational support were associated with better retention outcomes [15]. Ji et al. (2026) further highlighted the interconnected relationships among work stress, job satisfaction, and turnover intention among intensive care nurses [60]. Luo et al. (2024) emphasized the role of psychological safety and job satisfaction [14], while McIntyre et al. (2024) identified workplace environment, violence, and organizational factors as important contributors to emergency nurse turnover [47]. Collectively, these studies support the multidimensional approach of the present study, which extends previous evidence by examining Saudi and non-Saudi nurses within Saudi Arabia’s multinational nursing workforce.

Study implications and recommendations

The findings of this study highlight the need for comprehensive retention strategies that address both organizational and psychological determinants of nurses’ workplace experiences. Healthcare organizations should strengthen supportive leadership, effective communication, teamwork, shared decision-making, professional development opportunities, and equitable workplace practices to enhance nurses’ organizational commitment and job satisfaction. Previous research has demonstrated that healthy work environments, leadership support, and perceived organizational fairness are important factors associated with improved nurse retention and reduced turnover intention [8, 13, 55].

Given the considerable prevalence of psychological distress, anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and emotional exhaustion observed among nurses, healthcare institutions should integrate psychological support within workforce management strategies [2, 8, 53]. Accessible counseling services, peer-support programs, stress-management interventions, and early identification of psychological concerns may help promote nurses’ well-being. However, these individual-level interventions should be accompanied by organizational changes addressing underlying workplace stressors, including excessive workload, staffing challenges, inadequate work–life balance, and insufficient professional support [2, 9].

The higher turnover intention and greater perceived job distress reported among non-Saudi nurses indicate the importance of developing culturally sensitive and inclusive retention approaches [16, 35, 56]. Healthcare organizations should promote equitable access to career advancement opportunities, professional recognition, organizational resources, and supportive employment policies for both Saudi and expatriate nurses. Previous studies in Saudi Arabia have emphasized that fairness, leadership support, and opportunities for professional growth are essential for improving retention among a diverse nursing workforce [4, 8].

Future research should prioritize longitudinal, multicenter, and intervention-based designs to clarify the temporal relationships among psychological distress, job satisfaction, organizational factors, and turnover intention. Evaluating the effectiveness of targeted retention interventions, including leadership development programs, psychological support initiatives, and workplace improvement strategies, will provide stronger evidence for nursing workforce policies and sustainable retention planning [14, 15, 55].

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cross-sectional design allowed the identification of associations among psychological distress, job satisfaction, turnover intention, and perceived retention strategies; however, it does not establish temporal relationships or causality because exposures and outcomes were measured simultaneously [20, 52]. Therefore, it remains unclear whether psychological distress contributes to reduced job satisfaction and increased turnover intention or whether unfavorable workplace experiences contribute to psychological distress. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the sample [19, 20]. Although this approach is frequently used in nursing workforce research, future studies using probability-based sampling methods are recommended to improve the generalizability of findings across different healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia [4, 20].

The reliance on self-reported questionnaires may have introduced reporting bias, recall bias, and subjective interpretation of workplace experiences. Moreover, collecting all variables from the same participants at a single time point may increase the potential for common method bias and social desirability bias [20, 52], particularly when evaluating sensitive outcomes such as psychological distress, job dissatisfaction, and turnover intention [52]. Furthermore, job satisfaction was dichotomized into high versus low/moderate satisfaction categories for multivariable logistic regression analysis. This approach was selected to facilitate the clinical interpretation of factors associated with highly satisfied nurses and to estimate aORs; however, dichotomization may reduce variability, decrease statistical power, and result in some loss of information compared with analyzing job satisfaction as a continuous outcome. Additionally, multiple statistical comparisons were conducted without adjustment for multiple testing; therefore, findings with p-values close to the conventional significance threshold should be interpreted cautiously, as the possibility of type I error cannot be completely excluded [20, 52]. The study was also conducted among nurses from selected hospitals in northwestern Saudi Arabia, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare sectors [20, 52]. Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into factors associated with job satisfaction and workforce retention challenges among critical care and emergency nurses. Future longitudinal, multicenter, and mixed-methods studies are recommended to further examine the temporal relationships among organizational factors, psychological well-being, job satisfaction, and retention outcomes while incorporating broader factors such as leadership style, organizational justice, resilience, psychological empowerment, and work–family conflict.

Conclusion

This study hypothesized that job satisfaction would be significantly associated with turnover intention, that demographic, employment, physical health, and psychological characteristics would be associated with job satisfaction, and that Saudi and non-Saudi nurses would differ in selected job-related and health-related characteristics. The findings largely corroborated these hypotheses: job satisfaction was significantly and negatively associated with turnover intention, several employment, physical health, and psychological factors were independently associated with higher job satisfaction, and significant differences between Saudi and non-Saudi nurses were observed in perceived job distress and turnover intention.

Overall, job satisfaction among critical care and emergency nurses was associated with workplace characteristics, physical health status, and psychological well-being. Although the findings support the proposed hypotheses, the cross-sectional design limits causal and temporal interpretations of these associations. Organizational strategies focusing on supportive work environments, psychological well-being, equitable employment conditions, adequate staffing, and professional development may help enhance nurses’ job satisfaction and workforce stability. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are recommended to clarify temporal relationships and evaluate the effectiveness of specific organizational retention strategies.
