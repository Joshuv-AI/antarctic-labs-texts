Hemangioma of the Mitral Valve and Aortomitral Curtain in an Adolescent
Authors: Aditya Sengupta, Gina LaRocca, Martin E. Goldman, David H. Adams, Anelechi C. Anyanwu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A hemangioma of the mitral valve and aortomitral curtain was incidentally discovered in an adolescent. The lesion was surgically excised, and the mitral valve was reconstructed with complete preservation of valvular function. Pertinent principles of multimodality imaging-based diagnosis and nuances of operative management of this rare, vascular tumor are discussed.

Cardiac hemangiomas are very rare and are not typically considered in the differential diagnosis of cardiac masses.1 We describe management of a patient with a mitral valve hemangioma, emphasizing a multimodality imaging-based approach.Take-Home Message•Although rare, mitral valve hemangiomas can be successfully managed without valve replacement. This requires a multidisciplinary team with expertise in valve imaging and surgical repair.

A 19-year-old young woman presented with palpitations and was found on echocardiography to have an incidental 1.0- × 1.5-cm sessile, oval mass on the anterior mitral leaflet (AML) that perfused with ultrasound enhancement (Figure 1A). Precise anatomic location of the mass on the atrial side of the aortomitral curtain was subsequently delineated with transesophageal echocardiography (Supplemental Figure 1). The mass was also highlighted on computed tomography (Supplemental Figure 2), which showed no extracardiac lesions. Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed an immobile AML mass attached to the atrial side of the aortomitral curtain. On both T1- and T2-weighted images, the mass was isointense with surrounding myocardium (Figures 1B and 1C). Perfusion and complete enhancement of the mass were observed on contrast perfusion and delayed enhancement imaging, respectively (Figure 1D, Video 1). Surgical exploration confirmed the imaging findings (Figure 1F). We excised the mass en bloc and reconstructed the valve with bovine pericardium, applying cryotherapy to the aortomitral curtain (Video 2). The completed repair is shown in Figure 1G. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. Final histopathology revealed hemangioma (Figure 1H, Supplemental Figure 4).

Mitral valve hemangiomas are extremely uncommon, with few reported cases.1 Resection is indicated to prevent thromboembolism or valvular compromise. Often, as in the present case, imaging-based diagnosis of cardiac tumors is challenging, and resection is required to establish a diagnosis and exclude malignancy. A presumptive diagnosis of hemangioma can sometimes be made using cardiac magnetic resonance signal intensity characteristics, including heterogeneous prolonged enhancement after contrast injection, but such findings are nonspecific and can sometimes be observed with myxoma, angiosarcoma, and pheochromocytoma.2 The diagnosis of hemangioma can only be established after surgical extirpation.

Complete resection of cardiac tumors is critical in preventing recurrence, irrespective of their malignant potential. Given proximity to the aortic valve, AML tumors that extend to the aortomitral curtain are challenging to manage because of risk of injury to the aortic valve. In our case, at the aortomitral curtain, the resection margin was adjacent to the aortic valve. Because we could not guarantee a clear margin in this area, we applied cryoablation to destroy any residual tumor cells. As demonstrated in Video 2, given adequate leaflet substrate, valve reconstruction can be performed with complete preservation of function, despite restricted exposure due to a normal-sized left atrium and a minimal-access approach used for surgery. Valve repair is desirable in adolescents because there are no good mitral valve replacement strategies for these patients. This is particularly true for women of childbearing age. Given reports of late recurrence after cardiac hemangioma resection,3 meticulous long-term surveillance is warranted.
