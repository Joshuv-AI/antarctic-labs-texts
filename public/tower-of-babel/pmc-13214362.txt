Cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome stages 0–3 and cognitive decline in Chinese adults: a longitudinal analysis from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Zhihao Chen, Ruixue Li, Juan Liang, Bingsong Li, Qingxin Meng, Xinyan Li, Runqing Yang, Ran Tan, Xuejun Wang, Jingxi Li, Mei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome is increasingly linked to cognitive impairment, yet its association with cognitive trajectories at early stages remains unclear in Chinese adults.MethodsWe analyzed 6,982 adults (≥ 45 years) without baseline clinical cardiovascular disease from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Participants were classified into early CKM stages (0–3). Cognitive trajectories (standardized z-scores) over 7 years were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models. Cox proportional hazards models assessed the risk of substantial cognitive decline (≥ 1 SD decrease from baseline).ResultsIndividuals in stage 3 showed significantly lower baseline cognitive scores than those in stage 0. All groups experienced cognitive decline during follow-up, but decline was faster in stage 3, whereas stages 1–2 showed trajectories similar to stage 0. Stage 3 was also associated with a higher, borderline significant risk of substantial cognitive decline after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors.ConclusionsCKM stage 3 was associated with poorer cognitive performance and accelerated cognitive decline. Early identification and comprehensive management of CKM syndrome may help preserve cognitive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05835-4.Keywords: Cognition, Memory, Aging, Cardiovascular health, Metabolic health, Kidney function, Risk factors

Background

With population aging accelerating, cognitive decline and dementia have become major global public health challenges, increasingly driven by the clustering of cardiometabolic disorders [1, 2]. While hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are established risk factors for cognitive impairment [3], prior research has largely examined them in isolation. This approach overlooks the realities of multimorbidity and the shared pathophysiological pathways, such as endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress, that jointly impair brain health [4–8].

To address this, the American Heart Association proposed the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework, staging risk from adiposity (Stage 0) to clinical cardiovascular disease (CVD) (Stage 4) [9]. This integrated construct provides a conceptual bridge linking the heart–kidney–metabolic axis to cognitive outcomes [10]. Recently, several large cohort studies have consistently demonstrated a graded relationship between advanced CKM stages and a higher risk of incident dementia or cognitive decline [11–14].

Despite these findings, three important knowledge gaps remain. First, most studies focus on clinical dementia as an endpoint, rather than longitudinal trajectories of subclinical cognitive decline. Second, evidence is predominantly derived from Western populations, leaving it unclear if these associations generalize to Chinese adults with distinct lifestyle and genetic profiles. Third, prior research has largely emphasized advanced CKM stages. The impact of earlier stages (stages 0–3)—ranging from abnormal adiposity to subclinical target-organ damage without overt CVD—on cognitive function is less understood. This preclinical phase represents a critical window for early intervention, as it is during this period that subclinical pathologies, such as cerebral small vessel disease, chronic neuroinflammation, and silent brain atrophy, gradually accumulate before the onset of overt cognitive decline [6, 15].

Addressing these gaps, we used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to evaluate middle-aged and older Chinese adults free of clinical CVD at baseline. We aimed to systematically investigate: (1) the association between baseline early CKM stages (0–3) and long-term trajectories of global cognitive scores; and (2) the relationship between these stages and the risk of incident mild cognitive impairment. Ultimately, this study seeks to provide epidemiologic evidence supporting early, comprehensive CKM management to prevent cognitive decline. The primary innovation of this study lies in its longitudinal evaluation of the early, subclinical CKM continuum (Stages 0–3) in a Chinese population, moving beyond the traditional focus on late-stage dementia in Western cohorts. Furthermore, by pinpointing the specific threshold where cognitive decline accelerates, this study offers critical clinical guiding significance: it underscores the urgent need to shift clinical practice from merely preventing overt cardiovascular events toward proactive cognitive screening and aggressive risk factor management during early CKM stages, thereby providing a vital therapeutic window to preserve brain health.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study used data from 4 waves (2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). CHARLS is an ongoing nationwide cohort designed to collect high-quality microdata on Chinese middle-aged and older adults (aged ≥ 45 years) to investigate population aging and related public health issues. In the 2011 national baseline survey, approximately 17 705 respondents from about 10 000 households were enrolled, covering 150 county-level units and 450 village-level units across China. CHARLS was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent. Interviewers received standardized training and administered face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires [16].

For the present analyses, we first excluded individuals who were younger than 45 years at baseline, lacked follow-up data on cognitive function, or had missing values for key variables. We then excluded participants classified as CKM stage 4 (i.e., those with prevalent clinical cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events, specifically including a history of physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke) and those with implausible BMI values (< 10 or > 45 kg/m²). The final analytic sample included 6 982 participants, who were followed for up to 7 years. The flowchart of participant selection is presented in Fig. 1.

Estimation of eGFR and 10-year CVD risk

eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation [17], based on age, gender, and standardized serum creatinine levels.

10-year CVD risk was estimated using the gender-specific PREVENT risk equations [18], which incorporate age, blood pressure, lipid levels, smoking status, diabetes status, eGFR, and the use of antihypertensive and statin medications. The detailed formulas and parameter specifications for both eGFR and CVD risk calculations are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Definition of CKM stages

Following the staging framework proposed by the American Heart Association for CKM health, CKM syndrome was categorized into 5 stages as follows: Stage 0: No CKM risk factors, defined as ideal metabolic health (BMI < 23 kg/m2, waist circumference < 80 cm in women / <90 cm in men, fasting glucose < 100 mg/dL, HbA1c < 5.7%, BP < 130/80 mmHg, no diabetes, eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 10-year CVD risk < 20%). Stage 1: Excess or dysfunctional adiposity without overt metabolic disease, defined by BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2, increased waist circumference, impaired fasting glucose (100–124 mg/dL), or HbA1c 5.7–6.4%. Stage 2: Presence of metabolic risk factors or early CKD, defined by hypertriglyceridemia (TG ≥ 135 mg/dL), hypertension, diabetes, or CKD with eGFR ≥ 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. Stage 3: Subclinical cardiovascular damage or high predicted risk, defined by moderate-to-severe CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or a high 10-year CVD risk (≥ 20%). Stage 4: Clinical CVD attributable to CKM syndrome, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, or atrial fibrillation. The detailed algorithms and combinations of these clinical cutoffs are comprehensively provided in Supplementary Table S1.

In the present study, participants were staged according to the AHA CKM framework using baseline (2011) information on physical measurements, laboratory biomarkers, chronic disease diagnoses, and estimated CVD risk. Individuals with CKM stage 4 were considered to have prevalent disease and were excluded from the analyses.

Definition of cognitive decline

Cognitive function was assessed across 2 domains: episodic memory and executive function. Episodic memory was measured using immediate and delayed word recall tests. Interviewers read a list of 10 unrelated Chinese nouns aloud, and participants were first asked to recall as many words as possible immediately (immediate recall), and then again after approximately 5 min (delayed recall). The episodic memory score was defined as the average number of correctly recalled words across the 2 trials, ranging from 0 to 10.

Executive function comprised 3 components: figure drawing, serial subtraction, and time orientation. Participants received 1 point for correctly copying a figure; 0 to 5 points for correctly performing five successive subtractions of 7 from 100 (serial sevens); and 0 to 5 points for correctly identifying the year, month, day, season, and day of the week (time orientation). The executive function score was the sum of these components, ranging from 0 to 11.

The global cognitive score was calculated as the sum of the episodic memory and executive function scores, yielding a total range of 0 to 21. To enhance comparability of cognitive performance across survey waves, we standardized the global cognitive score using the baseline mean and standard deviation (SD) to obtain a cognitive z score, which was used as a continuous outcome throughout follow-up.

The primary outcome was substantial cognitive decline, defined as a decrease in cognitive z score of at least 1 SD from baseline at any follow-up visit. We used a custom R function to automatically identify the first wave at which each participant met this threshold and then derived an event indicator (1 = event, 0 = censored) and corresponding event time for time-to-event analyses.

These cognitive assessment tools were adapted from the American Health and Retirement Study (HRS). They have been well-validated and widely utilized in Chinese populations to reliably capture early trajectories of cognitive decline and detect mild cognitive impairment in large-scale epidemiological settings [19].

Covariates and data integration

Baseline covariates (2011) were extracted from the original CHARLS database and included:

Sociodemographic characteristics: age, gender, educational, marital, and residence. Lifestyle and behavioral factors: smoking, drinking, sleep hours, and social activity. Physical measurements: height, weight, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. Laboratory markers: fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, blood lipids, serum creatinine, and cystatin C. Functional status: limitations in activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL). Psychological status: depressive symptoms assessed by the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CESD-10). Chronic conditions: diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart disease, and stroke. Medication use: antihypertensive agents and statins.

Social activity was derived from responses to the question, ‘In the last month, have you engaged in any of the following social activities?’, which listed 12 options (e.g., visiting friends, playing mahjong, dancing, participating in community organizations). Participants who selected ‘none of the above’ were classified as having no social activity; all others were classified as having social activity.

ADL limitations were defined based on 6 items assessing difficulty with dressing, bathing, eating, getting in or out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling urination and defecation. If a participant reported ‘I have difficulty and need help’ or ‘I cannot do it’ for any item, they were classified as having an ADL limitation. IADL limitations were defined analogously using 5 items on doing household chores, cooking, shopping, managing finances, and taking medications on schedule; any such difficulty was considered an IADL limitation.

Depressive symptoms were evaluated with the CESD-10 scale, which has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese populations. The scale records the frequency of depressive symptoms during the past week, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30; a score ≥ 10 was taken to indicate the presence of clinically relevant depressive symptoms.

Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, or current use of antidiabetic medication. Heart disease, stroke, and chronic kidney disease were ascertained based on responses to the question, ‘Have you been diagnosed with the following conditions by a doctor?’. Participants who reported taking either Chinese or Western medications for hypertension were considered to be using antihypertensive drugs; those reporting use of Western medications for dyslipidemia were classified as statin users.

All variables were cleaned and harmonized according to official CHARLS coding guidelines. Binary variables were recoded to 0/1 indicators. For continuous variables, implausible values were identified and corrected or set to missing based on reasonable physiological ranges. Missing values for laboratory markers were imputed using the overall median to improve consistency and comparability of variables across survey waves.

Statistical analysis

We first described baseline characteristics of the study population across CKM stages 0 to 3. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between-group differences were assessed using analysis of variance, Kruskal–Wallis tests, or χ² tests, according to the distribution and type of each variable.

To examine trajectories of cognitive function over time, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and random slopes at the individual level. Time (in years since baseline) was centered, and an interaction term between centered time and CKM stage was included to test whether the rate of cognitive decline differed by CKM stage. We constructed 4 models with progressive adjustment: Model 0: Basic model including CKM stage, time, and their interaction (CKM stage × time). Model 1: Model 0 plus sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education, residence, marital status). Model 2: Model 1 plus lifestyle and functional factors (smoking, drinking, ADL and IADL limitations, social activity, and depressive symptoms). Model 3: Model 2 plus chronic conditions and medication use (diabetes, chronic kidney disease, antihypertensive use, and statin use). Predicted trajectories of cognitive decline by CKM stage were derived from the fully adjusted model using the ggpredict function.

For the time-to-event outcome of substantial cognitive decline (≥ 1 SD decrease in cognitive z score from baseline), we used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for each CKM stage, with CKM stage 0 as the reference. To account for intra-individual correlation across repeated measures, we applied robust variance estimation clustered on participant ID. Four models with incremental adjustment were fitted: Model 0: CKM stage only. Model 1: Model 0 plus age modeled with a natural cubic spline (3 degrees of freedom), gender, education, marital status, and residence. Model 2: Model 1 plus smoking, drinking, ADL and IADL limitations, social activity, and depressive symptoms. Model 3: Model 2 plus diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and use of antihypertensive drugs and statins.

We additionally performed subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, education level, residence, marital status, social activity, and depressive status to assess whether the association between CKM stage 3 and substantial cognitive decline was consistent across these strata. Interaction terms between CKM stage 3 and each stratifying variable were entered into the Cox models, and P values for interaction were used to test for effect modification.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1), and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 6 982 participants were included in the analysis and classified into 4 CKM stages: 813 in stage 0, 1671 in stage 1, 3859 in stage 2, and 639 in stage 3. Baseline characteristics differed substantially across CKM stages (Table 1). With higher CKM stages, participants tended to be older, with the highest mean age observed in stage 3. The proportion of men was also greatest in stage 3 (67.8%). Cardiometabolic and kidney-related indicators showed a clear gradient of worsening with advancing CKM stage. BMI, waist circumference, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, and blood pressure were progressively higher in stages 1 and 2, whereas eGFR was markedly lower in stage 3 (mean ≈ 80.7 mL/min/1.73 m²). The prevalences of diabetes and chronic kidney disease increased substantially at higher CKM stages. With respect to functional and psychological status, limitations in ADL and IADL were most frequent in stage 3 (9.7% and 22.2%, respectively). Although depressive symptom scores differed statistically across CKM stages, the absolute differences were modest. The proportion of participants reporting social activity was broadly similar across groups. Cognitively, participants in CKM stage 3 had the lowest baseline global cognitive score (7.73 ± 4.51), clearly lower than that in the other CKM stages.

Taken together, individuals in higher CKM stages were older, had more adverse metabolic and renal profiles, more functional impairment, and lower baseline cognitive performance.

Association between CKM stages and cognitive decline

Results from the linear mixed-effects models indicated significant differences across CKM stages in both baseline cognitive status and subsequent rate of cognitive decline (Table 2). At baseline, compared with CKM stage 0, stages 1 and 2 had slightly higher cognitive z scores, whereas stage 3 had a significantly lower cognitive level (fully adjusted Model 3: β = −0.19; 95% CI − 0.28 to − 0.09; P < 0.001).

Over follow-up, global cognitive function declined in all CKM groups, but the rate of decline showed a marked gradient. In CKM stage 0, the annual decline in cognitive z score was approximately − 0.05 (95% CI − 0.06 to − 0.04; P < 0.001). Relative to stage 0, the annual rates of decline in stages 1 and 2 were similar (interaction terms, both P > 0.05), whereas stage 3 showed a significantly faster decline (Model 3 interaction: β = −0.08; 95% CI − 0.09 to − 0.06; P < 0.001), suggesting that CKM stage 3 represents a critical phase for accelerated cognitive deterioration.

Predicted trajectories derived from the fully adjusted model further illustrated these patterns (Fig. 2). From baseline through 7 years of follow-up, participants in CKM stage 3 experienced the steepest decline in cognitive z scores, with the gap between stage 3 and the other stages widening over time. In contrast, trajectories for stages 1 and 2 remained largely parallel to that for stage 0. This consistent pattern indicates that greater CKM severity is associated with more pronounced long-term cognitive decline, with stage 3 in particular showing an accelerated decline profile.

Overall, CKM staging was associated not only with baseline cognitive performance but also with the subsequent rate of cognitive decline, and these associations remained robust after extensive adjustment for potential confounders.

Association between CKM stages and risk of substantial cognitive decline

Cox proportional hazards models showed a positive association between CKM stage and the risk of substantial cognitive decline (defined as a ≥ 1 SD decrease in cognitive z score from baseline), with risk generally increasing across CKM stages (Table 3). In the unadjusted model (Model 0), CKM stages 1 and 2 did not differ significantly from stage 0, whereas stage 3 was associated with a markedly higher risk (HR = 1.56; 95% CI 1.32–1.84; P < 0.001). After stepwise adjustment for sociodemographic factors (Model 1) and additional lifestyle and functional variables (Model 2), the magnitude of the association was attenuated but the overall pattern persisted. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), CKM stage 3 still showed a higher risk of substantial cognitive decline compared with stage 0, although the association reached borderline statistical significance (HR = 1.24; 95% CI 0.99–1.56; p = 0.06), indicating a relatively modest effect size after accounting for comprehensive confounders. This finding suggests that advanced CKM status may be an important risk phase for pronounced cognitive deterioration.

Subgroup analyses

To examine potential effect modification of the association between CKM stage 3 and substantial cognitive decline, we conducted stratified analyses across multiple demographic and behavioral characteristics (Fig. 4). Age, gender, educational attainment, marital status, place of residence, social activity, and depressive status did not significantly modify the association between CKM stage 3 and risk of substantial cognitive decline; all interaction p values exceeded 0.05. Across all subgroups, HRs for CKM stage 3 were directionally consistent, indicating an elevated risk of substantial cognitive decline relative to lower CKM stages, although the magnitude of the association varied slightly by subgroup, likely reflecting differences in sample size and underlying characteristics. These subgroup analyses support the robustness of the main findings and suggest that the association between CKM stage 3 and increased risk of substantial cognitive decline is broadly consistent across diverse demographic and health-related subgroups.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the relationship between early CKM stages (0–3) and cognitive outcomes in a nationally representative Chinese cohort. We found a graded, dose–response pattern where poorer CKM health correlates with worsening cognitive trajectories, highlighting CKM stage 3 as a critical transition point where cognitive aging accelerates into a pathological descent.

To contextualize these findings, while recent large cohort studies in Western populations have established that poorer overall CKM health confers higher late-life dementia risk, they have predominantly focused on clinical endpoints in advanced disease stages. Building upon and advancing this existing literature, our study possesses several distinct strengths that highlight its innovative value. First, by utilizing a nationally representative Chinese cohort, we provide critical evidence from a non-Western population with distinct genetic and lifestyle profiles. Second, rather than relying on a single cross-sectional time point or late-stage dementia diagnoses, our longitudinal design (spanning up to 7 years) uniquely captures the dynamic trajectories of cognitive change during the early, subclinical phases of CKM syndrome (Stages 0–3). Furthermore, our robust statistical approach, which thoroughly accounts for a wide array of functional, psychosocial, and clinical confounders, ensures a highly realistic and rigorous evaluation of how early multisystem morbidity impacts brain health.

Our longitudinal and Cox models demonstrated that while early metabolic abnormalities (stages 1 and 2) exert a relatively modest impact on cognition, progression to stage 3 (subclinical cardiovascular damage) is marked by the fastest rate of cognitive decline and the highest risk of substantial deterioration. Although extensive adjustment for covariates, such as depression and functional limitations, attenuated the hazard ratio (HR = 1.24), these factors likely partially mediate the CKM-cognition relationship [13, 20, 21]. The consistent directional trend underscores the profound neurocognitive vulnerability at stage 3.

Interestingly, participants in stages 1 and 2 exhibited slightly higher adjusted baseline cognitive scores than those in stage 0. This paradox may be explained by higher baseline cognitive reserve among younger, more educated individuals with early metabolic traits [22], or enhanced health consciousness and social engagement following early medical diagnoses [23]. Subgroup analyses confirmed these trends were robust across diverse sociodemographic strata.

The accelerated cognitive decline observed at higher CKM stages is likely driven by several interconnected pathways. Systemic vascular aging, arterial stiffening, and microvascular hypoperfusion increase white matter hyperintensities and brain atrophy, directly impairing global cognition [15, 24]. A hallmark of CKM, systemic inflammation disrupts the blood–brain barrier, activates microglia, and promotes neuronal apoptosis [25, 26]. Insulin resistance, lipotoxicity, and reduced eGFR induce neuronal oxidative stress, downregulate protective regulators like SIRT1 [27], and lead to the accumulation of neurotoxic uremic byproducts [28, 29].

While our focus is on stages 0–3, the detrimental effects of inflammation and metabolic imbalance extend into advanced disease (e.g., heart failure), where elevated inflammatory and metabolic markers (such as the triglyceride-glucose index, prognostic nutritional index, and C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio) strongly predict long-term mortality and arrhythmic events [30–32]. Furthermore, clinical cardiovascular disease introduces severe neurodegenerative risks, notably through incident atrial fibrillation (AF). AF accelerates cognitive decline via cerebral microembolism and hypoperfusion, making aggressive rhythm control (e.g., catheter ablation) a critical therapeutic target for mitigating cognitive consequences in vulnerable populations [33, 34]. This highlights the urgency of managing CKM in its early stages (0–3) to prevent irreversible downstream events.

Extensive evidence links individual cardiometabolic factors [35–37] and advanced CKM syndrome [11–14] to dementia risk, primarily in Western populations. Our study extends this literature by applying the comprehensive AHA CKM framework to a non-Western cohort, utilizing a longitudinal design to capture early, subclinical dynamic changes.

Clinically, these findings emphasize that stages 1 and 2 represent a crucial window for cognitive preservation through aggressive lifestyle and metabolic interventions. Furthermore, establishing stage 3 as a threshold for accelerated decline necessitates an update to routine care: clinicians must integrate proactive cognitive screening for patients with subclinical cardiovascular or renal damage. A holistic, multidisciplinary approach is essential to prioritize cognitive health across the CKM continuum.

Limitations

Our study’s strengths lie in its nationally representative longitudinal design and robust statistical adjustments for a wide array of confounders. However, several limitations exist: first, self-reported medical history may introduce recall bias, and the lack of baseline neurological evaluations means early-stage neurodegenerative conditions cannot be entirely ruled out. Second, the absence of neuroimaging data precluded direct examination of structural brain changes like cerebral small vessel disease. Third, the modest sample size in stage 3 resulted in wider confidence intervals. Fourth, the cognitive tests in CHARLS may lack sensitivity for detecting very subtle early changes. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., diet, genetics) remains possible. Future large-scale, prospective studies across diverse populations are warranted to externally validate these findings and further establish the clinical utility of early CKM staging.

Conclusion

CKM stage 3 was consistently associated with lower cognitive performance and accelerated long-term cognitive decline, whereas earlier CKM stages showed limited impact on cognition. The transition to subclinical cardiovascular damage may represent a critical tipping point for pathological cognitive aging. Early identification and comprehensive management of CKM syndrome before or at stage 3 may help preserve cognitive health and delay substantial cognitive decline.
