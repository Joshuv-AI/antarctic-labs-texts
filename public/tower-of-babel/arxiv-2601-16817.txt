Discrete FEM-BEM coupling with the Generalized Optimized Schwarz Method
Antonin Boisneault [0000-0001-7986-9048], Marcella Bonazzoli [0000-0002-0284-5643], Xavier Claeys [0000-0003-0826-6244], Pierre Marchand [0000-0002-2522-6837]

Abstract
The present contribution aims at developing a non-overlapping Domain Decomposition (DD) approach to the solution of acoustic wave propagation boundary value problems based on the Helmholtz equation, on both bounded and unbounded domains. This DD solver, called Generalized Optimized Schwarz Method (GOSM), is a substructuring method, that is, the unknowns of an iteration are associated with the subdomains interfaces. We extend the analysis presented in [ 22 ] to a fully discrete setting. We do not consider only a specific set of boundary conditions, but a whole class including, e.g., Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin conditions. Our analysis will also cover interface conditions corresponding to a Finite Element Method - Boundary Element Method (FEM-BEM) coupling. In particular, we shall focus on three classical FEM-BEM couplings, namely the Costabel, Johnson-Nédélec and Bielak-MacCamy couplings. As a remarkable outcome, the present contribution yields well-posed substructured formulations of these classical FEM-BEM couplings for wavenumbers different from classical spurious resonances. We also establish an explicit relation between the dimensions of the kernels of the initial variational formulation, the local problems and the substructured formulation. That relation especially holds for any wavenumber for the substructured formulation of Costabel FEM-BEM coupling, which allows us to prove that the latter formulation is well-posed even at spurious resonances. Besides, we introduce a systematically geometrically convergent iterative method for the Costabel FEM-BEM coupling, with estimates on the convergence speed.

Introduction

In this work, our aim is to design an efficient solver for acoustic wave
propagation modelled by the Helmholtz equation. The objective is to develop
a numerical method that is versatile enough to accommodate heterogeneous
and complex environments, while remaining efficient and tailored to high
performance computing. Two classes of methods are often used for the
numerical simulation of time-harmonic wave propagation: the finite element
method (FEM), which is versatile and suited to heterogeneous materials, and
the boundary element method (BEM), which performs best on piecewise
homogeneous materials and/or unbounded domains. In order to optimally handle
different types of materials, we seek a numerical algorithm to
systematically couple and benefit from these two methods.

To this end, we introduce a new FEM-BEM coupling strategy based on a substructuring domain decomposition method called
Generalized Optimized Schwarz Method
(GOSM). Substructuring DD methods involve

•

a non-overlapping partition of the domain of interest,

•

resolution of local problems with optimized boundary conditions in each subdomain that can be performed in parallel,

•

an iterative resolution of a substructured problem, that is, a problem on the skeleton (the union of the boundaries of the subdomains).

The novelty of our approach lies in the extension to FEM-BEM coupling of recent developments from
[
21
,
22
]
. One of the strengths of this current work is a clear well-posedness and convergence theory.

Research on domain decomposition methods for time-harmonic wave propagation started with a proper choice of transmission condition at the interface between subdomains. In

Another approach studied in

A strong requirement for such a fast convergence is the absence of cross-points, i.e. points shared by more than two subdomains in the domain partition. ), and thus (
19
), is well-posed
1
, or it is not. The latter can happen for a cavity problem when
\kappa
is a resonance. In both cases, (
19
) will be rewritten as a substructured form after decomposing the domain
\Omega
.

In the next section we exhibit other choices of
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}

to reformulate also Problem (
11b
) into the generic form (
19
). This can be achieved by means of FEM-BEM coupling
schemes, which model the Dirichlet and Neumann transmission conditions
on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
, the Helmholtz equation with constant
wavenumber in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
, and Sommerfeld’s radiation
condition at infinity.

3
FEM-BEM coupling

We are particularly interested in FEM-BEM coupling and one of the goal
of the present contribution is to design a numerical strategy that
systematically converges, with proven convergence bounds, toward the
solution to a FEM-BEM formulation. In the present section, we
introduce a few notations related to layer potentials and boundary
integral operators, in order to then define
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
for
FEM-BEM coupling formulations.

Denote
\bm{x}\mapsto\mathscr{G}_{k}(\bm{x})
the outgoing
Green kernel
for the Helmholtz operator with constant wavenumber
k>0
. For
d=3
, this is

\mathscr{G}_{k}(\bm{x})\coloneq\exp(\imath k|\bm{x}|)/(4\pi|\bm{x}|)

and, for
d=2
, this is
\mathscr{G}_{k}(\bm{x})\coloneq iH^{(1)}_{0}(k|\bm{x}|)/(4\pi)
, where
z\mapsto H^{(1)}_{0}(z)
is the
0
-th
order Hankel function of the first kind, see e.g.
[
45
, Chap.10]
. For any sufficiently
smooth pair of Dirichlet and Neumann traces
(v,p)
, we define the

total layer potential operator
by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}(v,p)(\bm{x})\coloneq\int_{\Gamma}\bigl(\bm{n}(\bm{y})\cdot(\nabla\mathscr{G}_{k})(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,v(\bm{y})

(20)

\displaystyle+\;\mathscr{G}_{k}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,p(\bm{y})\bigr)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s(\bm{y}),\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma,

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
refers to the Lebesgue surface measure on
\Gamma
and

\bm{n}\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unit vector field normal to
\Gamma

directed toward the exterior of
\Omega
. The map
(v,p)\mapsto\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}(v,p)|_{\Omega}
can be extended by density as
a bounded linear operator
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Delta,\overline{\Omega})\coloneq\{v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega),\;\Delta v\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\}
, see
e.g.
[
51
, Thm.3.1.16]
or
[
42
, Thm.6.11]
.
Similarly,
(v,p)\mapsto\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}(v,p)|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega}
can be extended
by density as a bounded linear operator
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Delta,\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)
.

We stress that, because of the convolutional form of the potential
operator (
20
) and the properties of the outgoing
Green kernel, for any pair
(v,p)\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
,
the function
u(\bm{x})\coloneq\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}(v,p)(\bm{x})
satisfies
Sommerfeld’s condition at infinity (
12
), see
[
54
,
55
,
58
,
44
]
, as well as the Helmholtz equation with wavenumber
k>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
, see e.g.
[
27
, §2.4]
.

Next, we define the
Dirichlet-Neumann
interior trace map

\gamma_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Delta,\overline{\Omega})\to\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
as the unique bounded linear
operator satisfying, for all
\varphi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\coloneq\{\varphi|_{\Omega},\;\varphi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
,
the formula

\gamma_{\Gamma}(\varphi)\coloneq(\varphi|_{\Gamma},\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi|_{\Gamma}).

We emphasize that traces are taken from the interior of
\Omega
here.
Similarly, we define the
Dirichlet-Neumann exterior trace map

\gamma_{\Gamma,c}\colon\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Delta,\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\Omega})\to\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
as the unique bounded
linear map satisfying
\gamma_{\Gamma,c}(\varphi)\coloneq(\varphi|_{\Gamma},\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi|_{\Gamma})
for all

\varphi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)
where, this
time, traces are taken from the exterior of
\Omega
(with
\bm{n}
still
directed toward the exterior of
\Omega
).

With Dirichlet-Neumann trace maps, we can form

\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{\Gamma}+\gamma_{\Gamma,c})\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
, which is commonly decomposed into a
2\times 2
matrix
of
boundary integral operators

\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{\Gamma}+\gamma_{\Gamma,c})\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma}&\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}\\
\mathcal{W}_{\Gamma}&\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right]

(21)

where the bounded operators
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{+1/2}(\Gamma)
(
single-layer operator
),

\mathcal{W}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{H}^{+1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
(
hypersingular operator
),

\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{H}^{+1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{+1/2}(\Gamma)
(
double layer operator
), and

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
(
adjoint double layer operator
) can simply
be defined so as to comply with (
21
). Both single-layer and
hypersingular operators are symmetric, while
\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma}^{*}=-\Kprime
.

FEM-BEM coupling can be achieved by certain choices of

\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
involving the operators in (
21
). These
choices model the Dirichlet and Neumann transmission conditions on

\Gamma
, the Helmholtz equation with wavenumber
k>0
in

\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
, and Sommerfeld’s
condition (
12
). Several FEM-BEM couplings exist, and here we shall concentrate on
three of them, which were reviewed in e.g.
[
4
]
.

Example 3.1

(Symmetric Costabel coupling
[
29
,
28
]
)
.

It is classically written as two equations:
find
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
p\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}(u),v\rangle+\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{W}_{\Gamma}(u|_{\Gamma})\,v|_{\Gamma}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{Id}/2+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\Gamma})(p)\,v|_{\Gamma}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\langle\ell_{\Omega},v\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{Id}/2-\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})(u|_{\Gamma})\,q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(p)\,q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=0

\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\forall q\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
,
with
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
and
\ell_{\Omega}
defined in (
13
).
After summing the two equations, it fits formulation (
19
) by taking
\ell_{\Gamma}=0
and

\textbf{Costabel:}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{W}_{\Gamma}&\mathrm{Id}/2+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\Gamma}\\
\mathrm{Id}/2-\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma}&-\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right].

(22)

Note that
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}^{*}=\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
, hence the name
symmetric
for the Costabel coupling.

Example 3.2

(Johnson-Nédélec coupling
[
40
,
52
,
56
]
)
.

It is classically written as two equations:
find
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
p\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}(u),v\rangle+\int_{\Gamma}p\,v|_{\Gamma}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\langle\ell_{\Omega},v\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{Id}/2-\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})(u|_{\Gamma})\,q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(p)\,q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=0

\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\forall q\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
,
with
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
and
\ell_{\Omega}
defined in (
13
).
After summing the two equations, it fits formulation (
19
) by taking
\ell_{\Gamma}=0
and

\textbf{Johnson-N\'{e}d\'{e}lec:}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mathrm{Id}\\
\mathrm{Id}/2-\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma}&-\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right].

(23)

Example 3.3

(Bielak-MacCamy coupling
[
8
]
)
.

It is classically written as two equations:
find
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
p\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}(u),v\rangle+\int_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{Id}/2+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\Gamma})(p)\,v|_{\Gamma}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\langle\ell_{\Omega},v\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}u|_{\Gamma}\,q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(p)\,q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=0

\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\forall q\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
,
with
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
and
\ell_{\Omega}
defined in (
13
).
After summing the two equations, it fits formulation (
19
) by taking
\ell_{\Gamma}=0
and

\textbf{Bielak-MacCamy:}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mathrm{Id}/2+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\Gamma}\\
\mathrm{Id}&-\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right].

(24)

It is natural to ask whether the FEM-BEM couplings above comply with the imaginary part sign assumption (
18
). As a matter of fact, only
the Costabel coupling fits this hypothesis.

Proposition 3.4
.

Operator (
22
) for the Costabel coupling
fulfills Assumption (
18
).

Proof.

For the sake of brevity, set
[(u,p),(v,q)]_{\Gamma}\coloneq\langle u,q\rangle-\langle v,p\rangle
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\Gamma}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{\Gamma}+\gamma_{\Gamma,c})\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}
,
see (
21
).
Then, with
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
defined by (
22
),
observe that
\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(u,p),(v,q)\rangle=-[\mathfrak{A}_{\Gamma}(u,p),(v,q)]_{\Gamma}+(\langle u,q\rangle+\langle v,p\rangle)/2
. This implies

\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(u,p),(\overline{u},\overline{p})\rangle\}=-\mathfrak{Im}\{[\mathfrak{A}_{\Gamma}(u,p),(\overline{u},\overline{p})]_{\Gamma}\}
because
\langle u,\overline{p}\rangle+\langle\overline{u},p\rangle=\langle u,\overline{p}\rangle+\overline{\langle u,\overline{p}\rangle}=2\mathfrak{Re}\{\langle u,\overline{p}\rangle\}
.
Finally, according to
[
12
, Proposition A.2]
or
[
44
, Lemma 3.2.1]
,

\mathfrak{Im}\{[\mathfrak{A}_{\Gamma}(u,p),(\overline{u},\overline{p})]_{\Gamma}\}\geq 0
for all
(u,p)\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Operators (
23
) and (
24
) for the Johnson-Nédélec and
Bielak-MacCamy couplings do
not
fulfill
Assumption (
18
).

Proof.

We construct a pair
(\Phi,P)\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)

such that
\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(\Phi,P),(\overline{\Phi,P})\rangle\}>0
in the case of the
Johnson-Nédélec coupling. A similar construction can be made for the
Bielak-MacCamy coupling. For all
\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
,
p\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(\phi,p),\overline{(\phi,p)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle p,\overline{\phi}\rangle+\langle(\mathrm{Id}/2-\K)\phi-\V p,\overline{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle p,\overline{\phi}\rangle+\langle\phi,\overline{p}\rangle-\langle(\mathrm{Id}/2+\K)\phi,\overline{p}\rangle-\langle\V p,\overline{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle p,\overline{\phi}\rangle+\overline{\langle\overline{\phi},p\rangle}-\langle(\mathrm{Id}/2+\K)\phi,\overline{p}\rangle-\langle\V p,\overline{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{Re}\{\langle p,\overline{\phi}\rangle\}-\langle(\mathrm{Id}/2+\K)\phi,\overline{p}\rangle-\langle\V p,\overline{p}\rangle.

According to
[
34
, Lemma 3.1]
,

\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\V p,\overline{p}\rangle\}\geq 0
. Our goal is
then to find a pair
(\phi,p)
such that
-\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle(\mathrm{Id}/2+\K)\phi,\overline{p}\rangle\}>\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\V p,\overline{p}\rangle\}
.
Consider any invertible real positive definite operator
\T\colon\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
. According to the Fredholm alternative,
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}/2+\K)<+\infty
, so
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}/2+\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})\neq\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and there exists
\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
satisfying

\lVert(\mathrm{Id}/2+\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{T}}>0
.
Let
\alpha>0
be some real parameter whose value will be
chosen appropriately below, and set
p_{\alpha}\coloneqq\alpha p

with
p\coloneqq\imath\T(\mathrm{Id}/2+\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})\phi
. We obtain

\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(\phi,p_{\alpha}),(\overline{\phi,p_{\alpha}})\rangle=2\mathfrak{Re}\{\langle p_{\alpha},\overline{\phi}\rangle\}+\imath\,\alpha\lVert(\mathrm{Id}/2+\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}-\alpha^{2}\langle\V p,\overline{p}\rangle.

Hence,
\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(\phi,p_{\alpha}),(\overline{\phi,p_{\alpha}})\rangle\}=\alpha\lVert(\mathrm{Id}/2+\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}-\alpha^{2}\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\overline{p},\V p\rangle\}
. If
\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\overline{p},\V p\rangle\}=0
, we can choose
\alpha=1
. Otherwise,
\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\overline{p},\V p\rangle\}>0
and we can choose

\alpha>0
satisfying
\alpha<\lVert(\mathrm{Id}/2+\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma})\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}/\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\overline{p},\V p\rangle\}
. Then
(\Phi,P)\coloneqq(\phi,p_{\alpha})

yields a counterexample of Assumption (
18
).
∎

In the following, we are going to develop a discrete counterpart of the
theory in
[
22
]
that will cover in particular the case where

\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
yields one of the previous FEM-BEM couplings, to
reformulate Problem (
11b
). However, even
when (
11b
) is well-posed, the corresponding variational
formulation (
19
) can be ill-posed at some wavenumbers, called

spurious resonances
, due to the introduction of boundary integral
operators, see e.g.
[
53
]
. Thus, several situations can
occur:

•

(
19
) is well-posed, then (
19
) will be
rewritten as a substructured form with a trivial kernel. It can be the
case of the three
couplings (
22
), (
23
)
and (
24
) if the wavenumber
\tilde{k}
does
not match a spurious resonance.

•

Assuming (
18
) and using Moore-Penrose
pseudo-inverses, (
19
) will be rewritten as a
substructured form with a trivial kernel, even when the operator
attached to (
19
) admits a non-trivial kernel because of a
spurious resonance. In this situation, the substructured formulation
takes the form of “identity + contraction”. This is the case of the
Costabel coupling (
22
).

These two situations are not mutually exclusive: for instance, the Costabel coupling (
22
) falls into both when the wavenumber is not a spurious resonance.

Remark 3.6
.

If the Costabel FEM-BEM coupling is applied, then for any value of

\tilde{k}
we can reconstruct a solution of Problem (
11b
)
from a solution of the variational form (
19
). If the
Johnson-Nédélec or Bielak-MacCamy FEM-BEM couplings are applied, then we
can reconstruct a solution of Problem (
11b
) from a solution
of the variational form (
19
) only when
\tilde{k}
does
not match a spurious resonance.

4
Discretization

Now we introduce notations related to the discretization of problems
of the form (
19
). We shall assume that the polyhedral
domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is covered by a
d
-dimensional regular
simplicial mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Omega)
, i.e. a
collection of
d
-dimensional simplices:

\overline{\Omega}=\cup\{\;\overline{\tau},\;\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Omega)\;\}.

Any polyhedral
d
-dimensional subdomain
\Theta\subset\Omega
will
be said to
conform
to this mesh if there exists a
sub-collection
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Theta)\subset\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Omega)
such that
\overline{\Theta}=\cup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Theta)}\overline{\tau}
. Let us denote

\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d-1}(\Omega)
the collection of
(d-1)
-dimensional
simplices that are faces of elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Omega)
.
Any
(d-1)
-dimensional set
\Upsilon\subset\overline{\Omega}
is
said to
conform
to
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Omega)
if there exists a
subcollection of faces
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d-1}(\Upsilon)\subset\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d-1}(\Omega)
such that
\overline{\Upsilon}=\cup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d-1}(\Upsilon)}\overline{\tau}
. In
particular, the boundary
\partial\Theta
of any conforming

d
-dimensional subset
\Theta\subset\Omega
is conforming. Next we
consider the space of
\mathbb{P}_{k}
-Lagrange finite element functions

\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\coloneq\{v\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(\overline{\Omega}),\;v|_{\tau}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(\tau),\;\forall\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{d}(\Omega)\}.

We have
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\subset\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
. For any

d
-dimensional or
(d-1)
-dimensional conforming subset

\Theta\subset\Omega
, we consider the corresponding

\mathbb{P}_{k}
-Lagrange finite element space
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Theta)\coloneq\{v|_{\Theta},\;v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\}
. We will also need to consider
the spaces

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma)\subset\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma),\;\;\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma)<+\infty,

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\coloneq\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma).

The subsequent analysis does not need any further assumption on

\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma)
. The finite dimensional space
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)
yields a Galerkin approximation of

\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
, which leads to the operator

\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\to\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)^{*}
defined by

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}(u,p),(v,q)\rangle\coloneq\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}(u),v\rangle+\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(u|_{\Gamma},p),(v|_{\Gamma},q)\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall(u,p),(v,q)\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma).

The operator
\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}
is simply the matrix stemming
from the Galerkin discretization of (
19
). Similarly, we
consider
\ell_{\Omega\times\Gamma}\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)^{*}
defined
by
\langle\ell_{\Omega\times\Gamma},(v,q)\rangle\coloneq\langle\ell_{\Omega},v\rangle+\langle\ell_{\Gamma},(v|_{\Gamma},q)\rangle
for all
v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)
,

q\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma)
. Leaving (
19
) apart, from now on we
shall focus on the discrete problem

\displaystyle(u,p)\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\;\text{and}

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}(u,p)=\ell_{\Omega\times\Gamma}.

One of our goals is to develop a discrete
counterpart of the theory presented in
[
22
]
, and the
starting point of this analysis will be (
25
).

We do not discard the possibility of a non-trivial kernel

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\neq\{0\}
. If the wavenumber

\kappa(\bm{x})
is real-valued, this may occur for example when
considering (
13
)-(
2
)-(
25
)
or (
13
)-(
2
)-(
25
). This can
also occur with FEM-BEM coupling,
i.e. (
13
)-(
22
)-(
25
)
and (
13
)-(
23
)-(
25
),
when the wavenumber matches a spurious resonance. See the discussion at the end
of Sections
2
and
3
.

5
Domain decomposition

We now subdivide the domain
\Omega
into a finite
collection of non-overlapping polyhedra
\overline{\Omega}=\overline{\Omega}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\overline{\Omega}_{J}
with

\Omega_{j}\cap\Omega_{k}=\emptyset
for
j\neq k
, and assume that
each
\Omega_{j}
conforms with the mesh

\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)
.
In practice, the
generation of such a partition can easily be automated with the help
of a graph partitioner.
In the context of FEM-BEM coupling, already
the case
J=1
appears relevant.
We shall denote
\Gamma_{j}\coloneq\partial\Omega_{j}

and
\Sigma\coloneq\Gamma\cup\Gamma_{1}\cup\dots\cup\Gamma_{J}
the
skeleton
of the subdomain partition. In line with the notation of Section
4
, we recall that
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma)\coloneq\{v|_{\Gamma},\,v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\}
,
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\{v|_{\Sigma},\,v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\}
,
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{j})\coloneq\{v|_{\Gamma_{j}},\,v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\}
, and
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{j})\coloneq\{v|_{\Omega_{j}},\,v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega)\}
.
Next, we introduce function spaces
adapted to the multi-domain setting:

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{h}^{\textsc{b}}(\Gamma)\coloneq\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\coloneq\mathrm{V}_{h}^{\textsc{b}}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{1})\times\cdots\times\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{J})

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{1})\times\cdots\times\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{J}).

The space
\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
consists of independent tuples of Dirichlet traces on the boundary
of each subdomain, and we shall refer to it as
Dirichlet multi-trace space
.
Then, we introduce trace operators, beginning with

\mathcal{B}\colon\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\to\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
defined by
\mathcal{B}(v,q)\coloneq v|_{\Sigma}
.
We also consider a multi-domain boundary trace
operator
\mathrm{B}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
that acts
subdomain-wise and is block-diagonal:

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}\coloneq\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{B}_{\Gamma},\mathrm{B}_{\Omega_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{B}_{\Omega_{J}})

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{\Gamma}(v,q)\coloneq v\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{B}_{\Omega_{j}}(u)\coloneq u|_{\Gamma_{j}}.

In practice, the trace operators
\mathcal{B},\mathrm{B}
are made of Boolean matrices that
keep track of the correspondence between volume degrees of freedom and
boundary degrees of freedom. We also need restriction operators

\mathcal{R}\colon\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
and

\mathrm{R}\colon\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v,q)\coloneq((v|_{\Gamma},q),v|_{\Omega_{1}},\dots,v|_{\Omega_{J}})

\displaystyle v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega),q\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}(\Gamma)

(26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(u)\coloneq(u|_{\Gamma},u|_{\Gamma_{1}},\dots,u|_{\Gamma_{J}})

\displaystyle u\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Sigma).

Here again, the restriction operators
\mathcal{R},\mathrm{R}
reduce to
Boolean matrices encoding adjacency relation between subdomains or
boundaries. They play a pivotal role in the expression of
transmission conditions. Note the commutativity relation

\mathrm{R}\cdot\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{B}\cdot\mathcal{R}
. Next we need to introduce
a notation for the range of the restriction operators

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)\coloneq\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)),

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Sigma)).

By construction,
\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)\subset\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
and

\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\subset\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
. We call
\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
the

Dirichlet single trace space
: it consists of tuples of Dirichlet
traces that match across subdomain interfaces. Because this space encodes
Dirichlet transmission conditions, it will play an important role in the
subsequent analysis. Neumann transmission conditions are encoded by its
annihilator
\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}\subset\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
. The
following two properties hold.

Lemma 5.1
.

i)

\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)=\mathrm{B}^{-1}(\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma))
,

ii)

\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)^{\circ}=\mathrm{B}^{*}(\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ})
.

Here
\mathrm{B}^{-1}
refers to a preimage, as
\mathrm{B}
is not invertible. We do not provide the proof of this lemma as it follows exactly the
same path as the one of
[
22
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Let us now discuss in detail how we impose transmission
conditions through interfaces. Our strategy requires a
scalar product on the trace space, induced by an operator
\mathrm{T}
called
impedance
(or
transmission
)
operator
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\quad\text{such that}

(28)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{T}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle>0,\ \forall\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\setminus\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{T}^{*}=\mathrm{T}=\overline{\mathrm{T}}.

The above properties transfer to the inverse map

\mathrm{T}^{-1}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
which itself induces a
scalar product on
\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
. We shall abundantly use the
corresponding norms defined as follows:

\|\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}\coloneq\langle\mathrm{T}(\bm{v}),\overline{\bm{v}}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{p}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}\coloneq\langle\mathrm{T}^{-1}(\bm{p}),\overline{\bm{p}}\rangle.

The following result, whose proof can be found in
[
23
, Lemma 3.4]
, establishes a connection between the
orthogonal complement of the single trace space and its annihilator.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any impedance operator
\mathrm{T}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
we have
the two orthogonal decompositions

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\phantom{*}}=\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\phantom{\circ}}\oplus\mathrm{T}^{-1}(\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}=\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}\oplus\mathrm{T}(\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)).

The space
\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}
is a subspace
of
\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
that provides a
characterization of both Dirichlet and Neumann transmission conditions (note that they are satisfied by functions of the space
\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)

in the initial discrete problem (
25
)). The next result follows from Lemma
5.2
and
is a further characterization of this subspace, established e.g. in
[
23
, Lemma
3.5 and 3.7]
. It reformulates Dirichlet/Neumann transmission conditions as
generalized impedance transmission conditions
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Define
\Pi\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
by
\Pi\coloneq 2\mathrm{T}\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{R}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{R})^{-1}\mathrm{R}^{*}-\mathrm{Id}
. Then
\Pi
is an involutive isometry, that is,

\Pi^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
and
\|\Pi(\bm{q})\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}=\|\bm{q}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}},\ \forall\bm{q}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
. Moreover, for
any pair
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
, we have

(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}\quad\iff\quad-\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})=\Pi(\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})).

(29)

This characterization of transmission conditions is non-trivial,
even in the case of a single subdomain
J=1
, i.e., if the
computational domain
\Omega
is not subdivided. We name
\Pi
the

exchange operator
. The exact form taken by this operator
intrinsically depends on the choice of the impedance operator
\mathrm{T}
. The
operator
\Pi
a priori couples distant subdomains, although, for
certain choices of
\mathrm{T}
, the exchange operator becomes local, that is, it couples only adjacent subdomain, see the
discussion in
[
23
, Section 9]
.

6
Substructured reformulation of the problem

We wish to reformulate Problem (
25
) considering the domain decomposition introduced in the previous section. Define the Galerkin discretization

\mathrm{A}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathrm{V}_{h}^{\textsc{b}}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{V}_{h}^{\textsc{b}}(\Gamma)^{*}

of the boundary operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
, that is,
\langle\mathrm{A}_{\Gamma}(\bm{u}),\bm{v}\rangle\coloneq\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(\bm{u}),\bm{v}\rangle
for all
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathrm{V}_{h}^{\textsc{b}}(\Gamma)
, and define the block-diagonal operator

\mathrm{A}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}\coloneq\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{A}_{\Gamma},\mathrm{A}_{\Omega_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{A}_{\Omega_{J}})

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{A}_{\Omega_{j}}(u),v\rangle\coloneq\int_{\Omega_{j}}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-\kappa^{2}uv)\,d\bm{x}\quad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{j}).

Due to Assumption (
10
) on the sign of the imaginary part of the problem under study,
if Assumption (
18
) is also satisfied, then the block diagonal operator

\mathrm{A}
itself satisfies a similar property

\eqref{Assumption1},\eqref{Assumption2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{v}),\overline{\bm{v}}\rangle\}\leq 0,\ \forall\bm{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega).

(30)

The source term of (
25
) can be decomposed similarly to
\mathrm{A}
. We
define
\ell_{\Omega_{j}}\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{j})^{*}
by
\langle\ell_{\Omega_{j}},v\rangle\coloneq\int_{\Omega_{j}}fv\,d\bm{x}
for all
v\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{j})
, and then

\bm{\ell}\coloneq(\ell_{\Gamma},\ell_{\Omega_{1}},\dots,\ell_{\Omega_{J}})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}
.
By definition of
\mathcal{R}
in (
26
), we have the factorizations

\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\mathrm{A}\mathcal{R}\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{\Omega\times\Gamma}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\bm{\ell}.

(31)

The block-diagonal operator
\mathrm{A}
can then be used to decompose Problem (
25
) subdomain-wise, and express the following first reformulation of Problem (
25
).

Lemma 6.1
.

If
(u,p)\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)
satisfies Problem (
25
), then
there exists
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
such that, for
\bm{u}=\mathcal{R}(u,p)
, the following equations hold

\displaystyle\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega),\bm{p}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}

(32)

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}\bm{u}-\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}=\bm{\ell}

\displaystyle-\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}=\Pi(\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}).

Reciprocally if (
32
) holds for a pair

(\bm{u},\bm{p})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
, then
there exists
(u,p)\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)
solution to Problem (
25
) such that
\bm{u}=\mathcal{R}(u,p)
.

Proof.

Since by definition
\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)\coloneq\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma))
, Equation (
25
) can be rewritten
[\bm{u}\coloneq\mathcal{R}(u,p)\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)
and
\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{u})-\bm{\ell},\bm{v}\rangle=0,\ \forall\bm{v}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)]
, which is equivalent to
[\bm{u}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)
and

\mathrm{A}(\bm{u})-\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)^{\circ}]
. Next we know from
i)
of Lemma
5.1
that
[\bm{u}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)]\iff[\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\;\text{and}\;\mathrm{B}(\bm{u})\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)]
, and
ii)
of Lemma
5.1
yields
[\mathrm{A}(\bm{u})-\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)^{\circ}]\iff[\exists\bm{p}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}\;\text{such that}\;\mathrm{A}(\bm{u})-\bm{\ell}=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}]
. Thanks to the characterization of
\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)=\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}
provided by Lemma
5.3
, we finally obtain (
32
).
∎

We wish to further rearrange Problem (
32
), so as to
eliminate the volume unknown
\bm{u}
, and reduce the problem to an
equation with a substructured unknown on the skeleton
\Sigma
only. Following the theory in
[
23
,
21
]
, we rewrite the first equation of (
32
) as
(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\bm{u}=\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u})+\bm{\ell}
. However, in the present case the
operator
\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
may be singular, contrary to
the situation in
[
23
,
21
]
.
Here are two fundamental
properties that we will use to circumvent this state of affairs.

Lemma 6.2
.

If either
\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
is invertible, or Assumption (
18
) holds, then

\displaystyle\textit{i)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})

\displaystyle\textit{ii)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A})+\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{B}^{*})

Proof.

The result is clear if
\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
is invertible. Indeed,
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
implies
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
, and
\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}=\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\subset\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A})+\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{B}^{*})
implies
\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A})+\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{B}^{*})=\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}
.

Let us assume now that
\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
has a non-trivial kernel, but Assumption (
18
) holds, which implies (
30
):
\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{v}),\overline{\bm{v}}\rangle\}\leq 0,\ \forall\bm{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
.
On the one hand,
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})
. On the other hand,
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\Rightarrow 0=\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle-i\langle\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}=\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}-\|\mathrm{B}(\bm{u})\|_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}
, so
that
0\leq\|\mathrm{B}(\bm{u})\|_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}=\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}\leq 0\Rightarrow\mathrm{B}(\bm{u})=0\Rightarrow\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})
. Finally,
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})
. This establishes
i)
.

Since
\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}^{*}(\overline{\bm{v}}),{\bm{v}}\rangle\}=\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{v}),\overline{\bm{v}}\rangle\}\leq 0,\ \forall\bm{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
, we prove in the same manner that

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}^{*}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}^{*})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})
.
Hence, using (
6
) and (
4
),

\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A})+\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{B}^{*})=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}^{*})^{\circ}+\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})^{\circ}=(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}^{*})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B}))^{\circ}=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}^{*}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})^{\circ}=\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Under one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
, if (
32
) holds, then

\bm{\ell}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})
necessarily.

Remark 6.4
.

In the following, we will use the relations i) and ii) of Lemma
6.2
. Thus, our discrete theory will cover both the case when
\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
has a non-trivial kernel, and when it is invertible. In the latter case, proofs could be simplified using the stronger result that
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
and
\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})
is the whole discrete space.

Like in
[
22
,
23
,
21
]
, the rearrangement of Problem (
32
)
will rely on pairs of
Dirichlet-Neumann traces.
This leads us to introduce the multi-trace space

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)\coloneq\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}

\displaystyle\|(\bm{v},\bm{p})\|_{\mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}\coloneq\|\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}+\|\bm{p}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}.

Two subspaces of
\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)
will play a pivotal
role. They are related to local problems and transmission conditions,
and defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})

\displaystyle\coloneq\{(\mathrm{B}(\bm{u}),\bm{p})\in\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma),\;\mathrm{A}\bm{u}=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}\},

(33)

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ},

where
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
is often called the
Cauchy data
set. The single-trace space
\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
was already introduced in Lemma
5.3
and characterized using the exchange operator
\Pi
.
We have the following fundamental results involving the space
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Under one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
, the application

(\bm{v},\bm{p})\mapsto\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})
isomorphically maps

\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
onto
\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
, with the estimate

\|(\bm{v},\bm{p})\|_{\mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\|\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\,\|(\bm{v},\bm{p})\|_{\mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}
for all

(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
.

Proof.

Pick
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
arbitrary. According to

ii)
of Lemma
6.2
, there exists

\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that
(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\bm{u}=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{q}
, which rewrites
\mathrm{A}\bm{u}=\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{q}+i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u})
.
As a consequence
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\coloneq(\mathrm{B}\bm{u},\bm{q}+i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
, and
\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}=\bm{q}
by
construction, hence the surjectivity.

Next, for any
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
, there exists

\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that
\bm{v}=\mathrm{B}\bm{u}
and
\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}=\mathrm{A}\bm{u}
. Thus,
\langle\bm{p},\overline{\bm{v}}\rangle=\langle\bm{p},\mathrm{B}(\overline{\bm{u}})\rangle=\langle\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle=\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle
.
Then, (
30
) yields
0\leq-2\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\bm{p},\overline{\bm{v}}\rangle\}\leq\|(\bm{v},\bm{p})\|_{\mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}
, hence
0\leq\|\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}-\|(\bm{v},\bm{p})\|_{\mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\|(\bm{v},\bm{p})\|_{\mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}
, which establishes both
injectivity and the estimates.
∎

Proposition 6.6
.

Assume one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
. Define the graph space of
\,i\mathrm{T}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
:
\mathscr{G}(i\mathrm{T})\coloneq\{(\bm{v},i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})),\bm{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)=\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})\oplus\mathscr{G}(i\mathrm{T}).

Proof.

Let
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathscr{G}(i\mathrm{T})
. There exists
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that
\bm{v}=\mathrm{B}(\bm{u})
,
\mathrm{A}\bm{u}=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}
, and
\bm{p}=i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}
. Combining these relations with Lemma
6.2
, we deduce that
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})
, and then
(\bm{v},\bm{p})=(0,0)
.

Now take an arbitrary
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)
. By surjectivity of
\mathrm{B}
, there exists
\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that
\mathrm{B}(\tilde{\bm{u}})=\bm{v}
. Using
ii)
of Lemma
6.2
, we deduce that there exists
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that
(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\bm{w}=\mathrm{A}\tilde{\bm{u}}-\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}
. Set

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}\coloneq\mathrm{B}(\bm{w}),

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{1}\coloneq i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}_{1}=i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}(\bm{w}),

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{2}\coloneq\mathrm{B}(\tilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{w}),

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{2}\coloneq\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}(\bm{w}).

By construction,
(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{p}_{1})\in\mathscr{G}(i\mathrm{T})
. Besides,
\mathrm{A}(\tilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{w})=\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{w})=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}_{2}
, so that
(\bm{v}_{2},\bm{p}_{2})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
. To end the proof, note that
\bm{v}_{1}+\bm{v}_{2}=\mathrm{B}(\tilde{\bm{u}})=\bm{v}
and
\bm{p}_{1}+\bm{p}_{2}=\bm{p}
so that
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})+\mathscr{G}(i\mathrm{T})=\mathscr{H}_{h}(\Sigma)
.
∎

The space
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
introduced above “models” the wave
equation local to each subdomain. In the next Proposition, we introduce the so-called

scattering operator
, which will play the role of a local solver
in our domain decomposition method. Recall the
definition of the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse mentioned in
Section
1
and denoted with
†
.

Proposition 6.7
.

Assume one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
. There exists a unique
linear map
\mathrm{S}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
, later
referred to as
scattering operator
, that satisfies

\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}=\mathrm{S}(\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}),\quad\forall(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A}).

(34)

This operator is also given by the formula
\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{Id}+2i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})^{\dagger}\mathrm{B}^{*}
.

Besides, assuming (
18
),
\mathrm{S}
is

\mathrm{T}^{-1}
-contractive
2
, i.e. for any
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
,
\|\mathrm{S}(\bm{q})\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}\leq\|\bm{q}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}
.
More precisely,

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{S}(\bm{q})\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}-4\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}(\bm{u}),\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}=\|\bm{q}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\text{where}\;\;\bm{u}=(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})^{\dagger}\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{q}.

Proof.

Proposition
6.5
shows that Equation
(
34
) unequivocally defines the operator

\mathrm{S}
. Next, pick
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
arbitrary. According to

ii)
of Lemma
6.2
, there exists

\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that

(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\tilde{\bm{u}}=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{q}
. Let us set
\bm{u}\coloneq(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})^{\dagger}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\tilde{\bm{u}}
. Then,
according to the standard properties of Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse

\mathrm{M}\cdot\mathrm{M}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathrm{M}=\mathrm{M}
, with
\mathrm{M}=\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
.
So, we conclude
(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\bm{u}=(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\tilde{\bm{u}}=\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{q}
.

Now set
\bm{p}=\bm{q}+i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}
. We have
\mathrm{A}\bm{u}-\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}=0
hence
(\mathrm{B}\bm{u},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
. Since
\bm{q}=\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}
, Equation (
34
) gives
\mathrm{S}(\bm{q})=\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}=\bm{q}+2i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}=(\mathrm{Id}+2i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})^{\dagger}\mathrm{B}^{*})\bm{q}
for all

\bm{q}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
, which confirms the alternative
expression of
\mathrm{S}
stated above. The contractivity identity (
35
) is established as follows

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{S}(\bm{q})\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{q}+2i\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{q}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}+4\|\mathrm{B}\bm{u}\|_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}+4\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\bm{q},\mathrm{B}\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{q}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}+4\|\mathrm{B}\bm{u}\|_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}+4\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}\bm{u},\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}-4\mathfrak{Im}\{i\langle\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}\bm{u},\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{q}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}+4\mathfrak{Im}\{\langle\mathrm{A}\bm{u},\overline{\bm{u}}\rangle\}.

∎

The scattering operator actually gives a characterization of the space of Cauchy data
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
.

Proposition 6.9

7
Analysis of resonances

As already pointed out at the end of Section
4
, the operator
\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}
of Formulation (
25
), that
describes our boundary value problem, is not necessarily
invertible. This may occur when modelling physical resonance
phenomena, but may also occur in the case of spurious resonances
stemming from a FEM-BEM coupling, as discussed at the end of
Section
3
. In the present section, we shall
investigate how this situation transfers to our domain decomposition skeleton formulation (
37
). Our analysis is based on an intermediary map
\Phi
that we study in detail.

The next result involves
\mathrm{B}^{\dagger}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
, which is called
harmonic lifting map
in the literature on domain decomposition (see e.g.
[
57
, §4.4]
or
[
47
, §1.2.6]
). We remind that
\mathrm{B}^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the (surjective) trace map

\mathrm{B}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)
, see Section
1
and in particular (
9
)
for a characterization.

Proposition 7.1
.

Assume one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
. Define the operator
\Phi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
by the formula

\Phi(\bm{u})\coloneq(\mathrm{B}^{\dagger})^{*}\cdot(\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B})\cdot\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})
.
Then
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\iff\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})
,
and
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B}))
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\bm{u}=(u,p)\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
is arbitrary.
We have
\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{R})=\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)
by (
27
) and
\mathrm{A}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathcal{R}^{*})=\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)^{\circ}
by (
31
) and (
5
). According to
ii)
of Lemma
5.1
, there exists
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}
such that
\mathrm{A}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})=\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p})
.
Then, setting
\bm{v}=\mathrm{B}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega))
by
i)
of Lemma
5.1
,
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
— see (
33
). Besides,

(\mathrm{B}^{\dagger})^{*}\mathrm{A}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})=(\mathrm{B}^{\dagger})^{*}\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p})=(\mathrm{B}\cdot\mathrm{B}^{\dagger})^{*}(\bm{p})=\bm{p}
, since
\mathrm{B}
is surjective. This leads to

\Phi(\bm{u})=\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v}).

(38)

Since
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
and Proposition
6.5
holds, we derive
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\iff\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})=0\iff(\bm{v},\bm{p})=0
. By the very definition of
\bm{v}
and

\bm{p}
above, and since
\mathrm{B}^{*}\colon\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}
is
one-to-one, we have
(\bm{v},\bm{p})=0\iff[\;\bm{v}=0=\mathrm{B}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})
and
\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p})=0=\mathrm{A}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})\;]\iff\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})
.

We finish the proof by noting that
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
is one-to-one according to its definition (
26
), hence
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)=\mathrm{dim}\,\mathcal{R}(\,\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\,)=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B}))
.
∎

We underline that, in the previous result, the map
\Phi
is taken restricted to
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
. After having studied the kernel
of this map, we now study its range.

Proposition 7.2
.

Assume one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
.
Let
\Phi
be the operator defined in Proposition
7.1
.
Then
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)=\Phi(\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\,)=\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})
.

Proof.

Recall the description of the map
\Phi
from the beginning of the
proof of Proposition
7.1
and (
38
). What precedes shows that, if
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
, then
\Phi(\bm{u})=\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})

for some
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
.
Applying the definition of the scattering operator
\mathrm{S}
, see (
34
),
\mathrm{S}\cdot\Phi(\bm{u})=\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})
. Then, applying the characterization of
\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
with the exchange operator

\Pi
, see (
29
), we obtain

\Pi\cdot\mathrm{S}\cdot\Phi(\bm{u})=\Pi(\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v}))=-\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})=-\Phi(\bm{u})
. Hence,
(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})\Phi(\bm{u})=0
for all
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
, which rewrites

\Phi(\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\,)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S}).

There only remains to show that

\Phi(\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\,)\supset\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})
. Pick an arbitrary
\bm{q}_{-}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})
and
set
\bm{q}_{+}=\mathrm{S}(\bm{q}_{-})
. Since
(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})\bm{q}_{-}=0
we
conclude that
\bm{q}_{-}+\Pi(\bm{q}_{+})=0
. Next define
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}
by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}=\bm{q}_{-}\\
\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}=\bm{q}_{+}\end{array}\right.\quad\iff\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{v}=\mathrm{T}^{-1}(\bm{q}_{+}-\bm{q}_{-})/(2i)\\
\bm{p}=(\bm{q}_{+}+\bm{q}_{-})/2\end{array}\right..

According to the equations satisfied by
(\bm{q}_{-},\bm{q}_{+})
, we
have
\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}=\mathrm{S}(\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}\bm{v})
and
-\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\bm{v}=\Pi(\bm{p}+i\mathrm{T}\bm{v})
, which rewrites

(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathscr{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
due to (
29
) and Lemma
6.8
.
Since
(\bm{v},\bm{p})\in\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathrm{A})
, there exists
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)
such that
\mathrm{B}(\bm{w})=\bm{v}
and
\mathrm{A}\bm{w}=\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p})
. On the other hand, as
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)
, we derive

\bm{w}\in\mathrm{B}^{-1}(\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma))=\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Omega)=\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma))
, see
i)
of Lemma
5.1
and (
27
). So
\bm{w}=\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})
for
some
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)
, and

\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}(\bm{u})=\mathcal{R}^{*}\mathrm{A}\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})=\mathcal{R}^{*}\mathrm{A}\bm{w}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\mathrm{B}^{*}\bm{p}=\mathcal{B}^{*}R^{*}\bm{p}=0
since
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}(\Sigma)^{\circ}=(\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Sigma)))^{\circ}=\mathrm{Ker}\,\mathrm{R}^{*}
by (
5
). As a consequence,
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
and
\Phi(\bm{u})=\bm{p}-i\mathrm{T}(\bm{v})=\bm{q}_{-}
.
∎

Theorem 7.3
.

Assume one of the hypotheses of Lemma
6.2
. Then

\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B}))+\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})
.

Proof.

Using the rank-nullity theorem,
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})=\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)+\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)
. Then

\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B}))
by Proposition
7.1
, and
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)=\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})
by Proposition
7.2
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Note that Theorem
7.3
holds
no matter the specific choice of the impedance operator
\mathrm{T}
, as soon as it induces a scalar
product, i.e. it complies with (
28
). Theorem
7.3
adapts
[
22
, Propositions 8.2 and 8.4]
to the discrete setting and, at
the same time, provides an extension since in the present case we do
not assume that
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
. The
case
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})\neq\{0\}
covers situations
where FEM-BEM couplings satisfying Assumption (
18
) suffer from a spurious resonance phenomenon.

An interesting consequence of the formula of Theorem
7.3
is that, if

\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}
is invertible, then, systematically, both

\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
(see Lemma
6.2
) and

\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S}
are invertible. On the other hand, if
\mathrm{A}-i\mathrm{B}^{*}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{B}
is invertible, then
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
, see Lemma
6.2
. This occurs for
example when imposing local boundary conditions such as Dirichlet,
Neumann or Robin conditions on
\Gamma
. In that case the map
\Phi

establishes an isomorphism between

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
and

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})
.
Finally,
[
11
]
establishes that
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Gamma}-i\mathrm{B}_{\Gamma}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}\mathrm{B}_{\Gamma})
and
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})
are either simultaneously trivial or non-trivial for the Costabel coupling, i.e., the dimensions are equal. Thus, we deduce from Theorem
7.3
that the substructured problem (
37
) is well-posed even at spurious resonances for the Costabel FEM-BEM coupling!

8
Inf-sup estimates

We have just seen that, under the hypothesis

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
, the operators

\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}
and
\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S}
are simultaneously
both invertible or both non-invertible. Keeping the hypothesis

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
, we wish to discuss how the
inf-sup constants of these two operators compare. For this we need to
introduce a few constants. First, we define two constants related
to the boundary trace operator

\displaystyle t_{h}^{-}\coloneq\inf_{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\setminus\{0\}}\|\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p})\|_{\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}}/\|\bm{p}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}

\displaystyle t_{h}^{+}\coloneq\sup_{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\setminus\{0\}}\|\mathrm{B}^{*}(\bm{p})\|_{\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)^{*}}/\|\bm{p}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}

These are the extremal singular values of the operator
\mathrm{B}^{*}
.
These two constants were thoroughly discussed in
[
23
, Section
7]
and
[
21
]
, where it was shown that an
appropriate choice of impedance operator
\mathrm{T}
leads to
t_{h}^{\pm}

being
h
-uniformly bounded from below and above. We also need to
consider the continuity modulus

\|\mathrm{A}\|\coloneq\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|\langle\bm{v},\mathrm{A}(\bm{u})\rangle|}{\|\bm{v}\|_{\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)}\|\bm{u}\|_{\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Omega)}}.

The next theorem compares the inf-sup constants (see definition (
1
)) of the
undecomposed problem (
25
) and of the skeleton formulation (
37
). We do not provide the proof of this result: for

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\neq\{0\}
, this inequality is a
direct consequence of Theorem
7.3
, and,
for
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})=\{0\}
, the proof closely
parallels the one of
[
23
, Propositions 8.1 and 8.4]
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Assume
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
.
Then the following estimate holds

\mathop{\mathrm{infsup}}_{\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\to\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)^{*}}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma})\leq((t_{h}^{+})^{2}+(2\|\mathrm{A}\|/t_{h}^{-})^{2})\mathop{\mathrm{infsup}}_{\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}}(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S}).

Recall that both
\Pi
and
\mathrm{S}
are contractive
2
in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}
(see Lemma
5.3
and Proposition
6.7
), so following again the same
argumentation as in
[
23
, Corollary 6.2]
, the previous
estimate combined with the contractivity of
\Pi\mathrm{S}
implies the
strong coercivity of the skeleton operator
\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S}
.

Corollary 8.2
.

Consider that
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{A})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{B})=\{0\}
and the imaginary sign assumption (
30
) holds.
Then we have the estimate

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\Sigma)^{*}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\mathfrak{Re}\{\langle(\mathrm{Id}+\Pi\mathrm{S})\bm{p},\mathrm{T}^{-1}\overline{\bm{p}}\rangle\}}{\|\bm{p}\|_{\mathrm{T}^{-1}}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\;\frac{\alpha_{h}}{(t_{h}^{+})^{2}+(2\|\mathrm{A}\|/t_{h}^{-})^{2}}\;\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\alpha_{h}\coloneq\mathop{\mathrm{infsup}}_{\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)\to\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Omega,\Gamma)^{*}}(\mathrm{A}_{\Omega\times\Gamma}).

Remark 8.3
.

Assume that (
19
) admits a unique solution and no
physical resonance phenomenon is occurring. The impedance operator

\mathrm{T}
can be chosen appropriately so as to guarantee that the
constants
t_{h}^{\pm}
are
h
-uniformly bounded from below and above,
see e.g.
[
21
]
. With such a choice of impedance, since
the discretization can reasonably be assumed stable,
i.e.
\liminf_{h\to 0}\alpha_{h}>0
, the inequality in Corollary
8.2
provides
h
-uniform coercivity
estimate. Such an estimate guarantees
h
-robust convergence of classical
linear iterative solvers such as GMRes.

On the other hand, the present analysis covers FEM-BEM
coupling schemes such as (
22
) for which we are
able to prove a convergence bound, see Theorem
10.1
. To our knowledge, this yields the
first
h
-robust iterative algorithm for FEM-BEM coupling with
guaranteed convergence bounds.

9
Extension to more general problems

The theory developed so far can be generalized to boundary value problems that combine both Problems (
11a
) and (
11b
). In addition to
\Omega
, let
\Omega_{\text{O}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a polyhedral open set (not necessarily bounded nor connected) with bounded boundary and
\Omega_{\text{O}}\cap\Omega=\emptyset
. The wavenumber is constant in the BEM subdomain

and can vary arbitrarily in the FEM subdomain

\Omega_{\text{O}}
represents for instance an impenetrable obstacle (or a cavity) with a sound-soft or sound-hard boundary. Note that this configuration can include cross-points, that is, points where the three domains

,
\Omega_{\text{O}}
and
\Omega_{\text{B}}
are adjacent, like in Figure

. Acoustic wave propagation is then modelled by the following general boundary value problem:

\displaystyle u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\text{O}}})\;\;\text{and}\;\;-\Delta u-\kappa^{2}u=f\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\text{O}}}

(39)

\displaystyle+\;\text{boundary condition on }\partial\Omega_{\text{O}}

\displaystyle+\;\text{Sommerfeld's radiation condition if }\Omega_{\text{B}}\neq\emptyset.

Note that Equation (

) when

We wish to give a generalization of variational formulation (

), which we remind is the starting point of our theory.
Let
{\Gamma}_{\text{B}}=\partial\Omega_{\text{B}}
and
{\Gamma}_{\text{O}}=\partial\Omega_{\text{O}}
, and define the space

\begin{split}\mathscr{X}(\Omega,{\Gamma}_{\text{B}},{\Gamma}_{\text{O}})\coloneq\{&(v,(\psi_{\text{B}},q_{\text{B}}),(\psi_{\text{O}},q_{\text{O}}))\in\\

As an analogue to (

), it can be shown (see

) that Problem (

\displaystyle\text{Find}\;(u,(\phi_{\text{B}},p_{\text{B}}),(\phi_{\text{O}},p_{\text{O}}))\in\mathscr{X}(\Omega,{\Gamma}_{\text{B}},{\Gamma}_{\text{O}})\;\text{such that}

(40)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}(u),v\rangle+\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma_{\text{B}}}(\phi_{\text{B}},p_{\text{B}}),(\psi_{\text{B}},q_{\text{B}})\rangle+\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma_{\text{O}}}(\phi_{\text{O}},p_{\text{O}}),(\psi_{\text{O}},q_{\text{O}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\ell_{\Omega},v\rangle+\langle\ell_{\Gamma_{\text{O}}},(\psi_{\text{O}},q_{\text{O}})\rangle,\quad\forall\,(v,(\psi_{\text{B}},q_{\text{B}}),(\psi_{\text{O}},q_{\text{O}}))\in\mathscr{X}(\Omega,{\Gamma}_{\text{B}},{\Gamma}_{\text{O}}),

where

represents a FEM-BEM coupling (see (

), (
23
) or (

)),
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega},\ell_{\Omega}
are defined in (

), and

) or (

), together with

).
Then, the whole theory for the domain decomposition method can be seamlessly extended by replacing, in the definition of
\Sigma

Remark 9.1

(Strongly and weakly imposed boundary conditions)
.

When the boundary condition on
{\Gamma}_{\text{O}}
is enforced thanks to an operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma_{\text{O}}}
as above, we say that the boundary condition is

imposed.
Alternatively, the boundary condition on
\Gamma_{\text{O}}
can be

,
\ell_{\Omega}
in (

21
]
for more details, in which the theory is established for Helmholtz equation in a bounded domain with strongly imposed impedance boundary condition.

10.2
Choosing transmission operators

We now describe the three types of transmission operators used in our
numerical tests for a block of
\mathrm{T}
associated with a subdomain
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The transmission conditions are applied on

\Gamma_{\omega}\coloneqq\partial\omega
.

Transmission operator 1: local Després operator

The first one is the
local
Després operator
\mathrm{T}_{D,\Gamma_{\omega}}
, given by

\langle\mathrm{T}_{D,\Gamma_{\omega}}(u),v\rangle_{\Gamma_{\omega}}=\kappa\int_{\Gamma_{\omega}}uv\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
, of classical impedance
transmission conditions, so called because it was used
in
[
32
]
for the first optimized Schwarz method for the
Helmholtz equation. It defines a scalar product on the local trace space,
and thus can be used to define an operator
\mathrm{T}

satisfying (
28
) on
\Sigma
. But it does not satisfy
Assumption (
42
), i.e., it is
not

h
-uniformly
stable (see for instance
[
21
, Section 5]
).

Transmission operator 2: Yukawa hypersingular operator

The second one, already used for instance
in
[
25
, §3.1.2]
, is
\mathrm{T}_{Y,\Gamma_{\omega}}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}_{i\kappa,\Gamma_{\omega}}
, the hypersingular
operator, defined in (
21
), this time taking the Green kernel of a
“positive Helmholtz” operator, also known as the
Yukawa operator
,
that is,
-\Delta+\kappa^{2}\,\mathrm{Id}
. By definition

\mathrm{T}_{Y,\Gamma_{\omega}}
is
non-local
, can be used to define a
transmission operator (
28
) and satisfies
Assumption (
42
), see
again
[
21
, Section 5]
. Since an explicit expression of the
Green kernel, available only for constant
\kappa
, is needed to implement
such operators, in practice
\mathrm{T}_{Y,\Gamma_{\omega}}
can only be defined
for homogeneous subdomains.

Transmission operator 3: Schur complement

The last transmission operator
\mathrm{T}_{S,\omega}
is a Schur complement
based operator, relying on the discretization of a positive
Dirichlet-to-Neumann (
\DtN
) map for the Yukawa operator. This

non-local
operator has been first introduced
in
[
46
, Chapter 8]
, and then used
in
[
26
]
,
[
20
, Section 4.2]

and
[
3
, Section 6]
. To derive it we consider

\omega_{s}\subset\omega
, a thin layer along
\Gamma_{\omega}
, as
shown in Figure
1
. The boundary of
\omega_{s}

is the union of two disjoint parts, namely
\Gamma_{\omega}
and

\Gamma_{s}\coloneqq\partial\omega_{s}\setminus\Gamma_{\omega}
. As
in
[
3
, Section 6]
, the map
\mathrm{T}_{S,\omega}:\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{\omega})\rightarrow\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{\omega})^{*}
is then defined
as the unique Hermitian positive definite linear map satisfying the
minimization property

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{T}_{S,\omega}(v),\overline{v}\rangle\coloneqq\min\{\lVert\nabla v_{s}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega_{s})}^{2}+\kappa^{2}\lVert v_{s}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega_{s})}^{2}+\kappa\lVert v_{s}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{s})}^{2}\mid v_{s}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}(\omega_{s}),v_{s}|_{\Gamma_{\omega}}=v\}.

The third term in the sum above expresses the choice of imposing
homogeneous Robin boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{s}
, as
in
[
46
, Section 8.3]
. The thickness
\delta
of the

\omega_{s}
is a numerical parameter, which is chosen

)
(see
[
46

). From now on we shall take

\delta=\lambda/10
as a rule of thumb, i.e. ten percent of the

In practice, to evaluate the action
\mathrm{T}_{S,\omega}
, we work
with the following characterization,
suitable for matrix-vector products: for
g_{D}\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{\omega})

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}_{S,\omega}(g_{D})=q\;\text{ with }(v,q)\in\mathrm{V}_{h}(\omega_{s})\times\mathrm{V}_{h}(\Gamma_{\omega})^{*}\text{ such that }

(43)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{A}_{\omega_{s}}&-(\mathrm{B}^{{}^{\prime}})^{*}\\
\mathrm{B}^{{}^{\prime}}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v\\
q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
g_{D}\end{pmatrix},

The configuration names refer to the subdomains, from the outside to the inside, that is, the first letter refers to the BEM subdomain, and the second one to the FEM subdomain.
The thin layer needed to define

is considered inside

. The operator

is defined by taking

as the

).
Finally, note that the last three configurations only involve

transmission operators.

For the
D-D

S-S
and
Y-Y
configurations,
the same transmission operator is chosen for both domains.
Then,
\tilde{\Pi}
becomes the simple swap operator

(p,q)\mapsto(q,p)
and we recover the setup of classical OSMs.
For the
S-S
,
Y-Y
and
Y-S
configurations, all transmission operators satisfy the

-uniform stability Assumption (
42
). Then, according to
10.1
, the convergence rate

is

This result does not hold for the
D-D
configuration, for which an estimate similar to the one of Theorem

exists, but with
\tau_{h}
asymptotically behaving like
1-\mathcal{O}(h^{2})(h\to 0)
, as shown in
[

, Remark 12.3]
. We emphasize that the geometric convergence arises for the
D-D
configuration because we study a discrete problem, whereas it is known that at the continuous level the convergence is not geometric, see
[
24
]
.

Matrix properties

Once discretized, classical FEM-BEM couplings (
19
) for the Helmholtz equation lead to block matrices where some blocks are sparse and others are dense. Mixing sparse and dense blocks can be particularly difficult to deal with for numerical solver, the choice of a software to perform an efficient LU factorization being not natural.
In what follows, we highlight that depending on the choice of the transmission operators, the GOSM can also suffer from this issue, or not, when solving local problems.

S-S

The local BEM problem
\mathrm{A}_{\Gamma}-i\mathrm{B}_{\Gamma}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}\mathrm{B}_{\Gamma}
mixes a sparse block and dense blocks.

Y-Y

The local FEM problem mixes a dense block from the transmission operator and sparse blocks.

Y-S

Transmission operators associated with each subdomain are non-local, and of the “type of their local subproblem”,
i.e.

\mathrm{A}_{j}
and
\mathcal{\mathrm{T}}_{j}
are both either sparse or dense. Thus, local problems do not mix dense and sparse blocks. Yet, transmission operators are distinct, meaning that even for a single interface the exchange operator
\tilde{\Pi}
is not just the swap operator, and mixes dense and sparse blocks. The difference with the previous configurations is that applying
\tilde{\Pi}
means solving a positive definite problem. So we can use a CG solver and only use matrix vector product of the
block diagonal
operator
\mathrm{T}
, without mixing in practice dense and sparse blocks.

Remark 10.4
.

Avoiding mixing dense and sparse blocks also mean that in practice, one can use different optimized code for local FEM and BEM problems. For instance, one could use one-level or two-level preconditioners well-suited for FEM problems (see, e.g.,
[
41
]
), while using analytic preconditioners well-suited for BEM problems (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
).
This is one of the motivations in using “weak”/substructured FEM-BEM couplings, see
[
18
,
17
]
.

10.3.3
Conclusions

Overall, for the two material settings the worst number of iterations is obtained with
D-D
configuration, whether when refining the mesh or for increasing
\kappa
, and the
Y-S
configuration is the one for which

10.3
.

Besides, note that configurations such as

and
Y-Y
, which involve the same transmission operator on a boundary shared by two subdomains

and

, can only be considered when either

is defined independently of its neighbouring domains.
