Irreversible electroporation in hepatocellular carcinoma: from an anatomical niche to an immunologic platform
Authors: Xiaokun Chen, Xiao Liu, Shunda Du, Kai-Wen Huang
Published: 2026

Local ablation has become an important curative-intent treatment for patients with early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who are unsuitable for surgical resection or liver transplantation (1). Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (MWA) remain the dominant techniques (2). However, their safety and efficacy can be compromised when tumors are located adjacent to major vessels, central bile ducts, or other critical structures because of the heat-sink effect and the risk of thermal injury. Irreversible electroporation (IRE) offers a distinct solution to this anatomical challenge. By delivering short, high-voltage electrical pulses that disrupt cell membrane integrity, IRE induces predominantly non-thermal cell death while relatively preserving the extracellular matrix, blood vessels, and bile ducts. Beyond HCC, IRE has been explored in other anatomically challenging hepatopancreatobiliary malignancies, including unresectable hilar cholangiocarcinoma (3) and locally advanced pancreatic cancer (4). Early clinical studies have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach for HCCs in challenging anatomical locations. Sutter et al. reported that among 58 patients with 75 HCC nodules, complete ablation was achieved in 92% of tumors after up to three IRE procedures, with a 12-month local tumor progression-free survival rate of 70% (5). Recently, Peddu et al. reported a radiological complete response rate of 83.9% and a 2-year local tumor progression-free survival rate of 73.7% in 54 patients treated at a tertiary liver center (6). Together, these findings support IRE as an extension of curative local therapy for HCCs that are unsuitable for conventional thermal ablation, rather than as a replacement for RFA or MWA. However, the current evidence remains largely based on small, single-center, and retrospective studies, highlighting the need for prospective multicenter validation, standardized procedural protocols, and more precise patient selection.

More intriguing than local tumor destruction itself is the potential immunomodulatory effect of IRE. In patients with HCC, Guo et al. found that IRE increased activated T cells and natural killer cells, while reducing regulatory T cells. In murine HCC models, IRE also increased intratumoral Granzyme B-positive CD8+ T cells and circulating IFN-γ, suggesting both local and systemic antitumor immune activation (7). Experimental comparisons also suggest that this immune response may differ from that induced by conventional ablative modalities. IRE has been associated with more pronounced inflammatory responses and deeper immune-cell infiltration than RFA (8,9), while early intratumoral CD3+ T-cell infiltration after IRE was approximately twice that observed after cryoablation in a pancreatic cancer model (10). Several complementary mechanisms may underlie these observations. IRE can induce immunogenic cell death, promote tumor-antigen release, and enhance effector and memory T cell responses (11). The relative preservation and remodeling of the microvasculature and extracellular matrix may alleviate tumor hypoxia and reduce stromal barriers to immune cell trafficking (12). Although much of this mechanistic evidence originates from pancreatic cancer models and requires validation specifically in HCC, it raises the possibility that IRE may function not only as a local ablative procedure but also as an in situ tumor vaccine and sensitizer to systemic immunotherapy.

The recently reported NIVOLEP trial provides an important prospective clinical test of this concept in HCC (13). In this multicenter phase II study, 43 patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) A HCC who were unsuitable for surgical resection or thermal ablation received two neoadjuvant doses of nivolumab, followed by curative-intent IRE and, when appropriate, 12 monthly doses of adjuvant nivolumab therapy. Thirty-five patients ultimately underwent curative IRE, and the 1-year local recurrence-free survival rate was 70.6%. Importantly, sequential tumor biopsies demonstrated that pathological response following neoadjuvant nivolumab was associated with enrichment of pathways related to leukocyte migration and T-cell activation, together with increased CD8+ T-cell and B-cell infiltration (13). These findings do not yet prove a synergistic interaction between IRE and immune checkpoint blockade, but they move the field beyond viewing IRE simply as a technique for destroying anatomically challenging tumors and toward considering it as a potential platform connecting local tumor ablation with systemic immune modulation.

Important questions nevertheless remain. NIVOLEP was a small, single-arm trial and therefore could not distinguish how much of the observed local control resulted from IRE, peri-procedural nivolumab, patient selection, or a genuine interaction between the two modalities. Moreover, whether programmed death 1 (PD-1) blockade alone is sufficient to exploit the immune changes induced by IRE in the highly immunosuppressive HCC microenvironment remains uncertain. Future studies should therefore focus not merely on expanding the indications for IRE, but on defining which patients benefit most, whether IRE-induced immune activation translates into durable tumor control, and how contemporary systemic therapies can amplify this effect. In particular, combining IRE with immune checkpoint inhibitors and antiangiogenic agents or tyrosine kinase inhibitors is biologically attractive, because these agents may complement local antigen release and microenvironmental remodeling induced by IRE. This strategy may be particularly relevant for patients with locally advanced, initially unresectable but liver-confined HCC, in whom enhanced local control could potentially facilitate conversion to surgical resection (14). Prospective controlled studies incorporating longitudinal tumor and blood sampling are essential to distinguish between additive and truly synergistic effects and to identify biomarkers of the immune response.

The future role of IRE in HCC is unlikely to depend on direct competition with RFA or MWA, but rather on its unique combination of anatomical safety and immunomodulatory potential. Further studies are needed to refine patient selection, standardize technical procedures, identify relevant immune biomarkers, and clarify its potential synergy with systemic therapies. With stronger clinical evidence, IRE may expand beyond its current use for anatomically challenging tumors and become an important component of multimodal treatment for HCC.
