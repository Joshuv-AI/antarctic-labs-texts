Do LLMs have a Gender (Entropy) Bias?
Sonal Prabhune, Balaji Padmanabhan, Kaushik Dutta

Abstract
With the growing popularity of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), and their widespread adoption in powering content generation and recommendations, it is increasingly vital to rigorously and continuously assess these models for potential biases. In this paper, we investigate the existence and persistence of a specific type of gender bias in some of the popular LLMs. Unlike other contemporary work, our research focuses on evaluating gender bias in these models using a business-centric approach based on datasets that we created from real-world questions asked by people on various discussion forums. We contribute this new benchmark dataset RealWorldQuestioning (released on HuggingFace [ 1 ] ). We focus on four specific domains relevant to businesses, in which we studied recommendations made by LLMs for real user queries in education, jobs, personal financial management, and general health. We define and study entropy bias , which we define as a discrepancy in the amount of information generated by an LLM in response to real questions users have asked. We tested this using four different LLMs and evaluated the generated responses both qualitatively and quantitatively by using ChatGPT-4o (as "LLM-as-judge"). Our analyses (metric-based comparisons and "LLM-as-judge" evaluation) suggest that there is no significant bias in LLM responses for men and women at a category level. However, at a finer granularity (the individual question level), there are substantial differences in LLM responses for men and women in the majority of cases, which "cancel" each other out often due to some responses being better for males and vice versa. This is still a concern since typical users of these tools often ask a specific question (only) as opposed to several varied ones in each of these common yet important areas of life. We suggest a simple debiasing approach that iteratively merges the responses for the two genders to produce a final result. Our approach demonstrates that a simple, prompt-based debiasing strategy can effectively debias LLM outputs, thus producing responses with higher information content than both gendered variants in 78% of the cases, and consistently achieving a balanced integration in the remaining cases.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are widely used today, but there are concerns about the presence of bias
[
2

Given the significance and nuances of the question and the dynamic nature and breadth of the domain, no single paper can, indeed, do justice to this space. There is a need for substantial research that carefully explores different biases to gain a deeper understanding of this space. This includes identifying different types of bias, developing methods to detect them, and establishing guardrails to mitigate them. As expected, there is a wealth of ongoing work actively addressing these issues, which we highlight in Section
2
of this paper.

However, many of the current datasets designed for testing and debiasing the LLMs are based on broad categories of social science, such as the association of occupation to gender (for example, WinoBias, etc.
[
4
]
and do not fully reflect actual consumer or business use cases, as much. While such work is necessary to highlight general concerns, the literature can benefit from studies that explore these from the point of view of real use cases and specific business contexts, and there are few currently doing this. This is important because business applications of LLMs are quite varied and diverse, such as automated chatbots and virtual assistants to handle customer inquiries, streamlining ticketing systems by categorizing and routing support tickets, creating marketing or social media content, generating product descriptions and recommendations, analyzing large datasets to extract insights and trends, managing HR inquiries and recruitment screening, supporting medical professionals by summarizing patient records and medical literature, developing interactive and personalized learning experiences, or assisting with content creation for educational materials. Each such problem area indeed has its nuances and may need to be explored carefully through these lenses in order to ensure proper use, given our understanding of how pervasive gender bias can be in AI.

Our paper is one of the first to investigate the presence and persistence of gender biases in specific consumer/business use cases using real-world questions posted by real people for recommendations. We focused on four use cases in the areas of (i) education recommendations made by LLMs for training, courses, and universities, (ii) job recommendations of LLMs in response to user queries, (iii) recommendations for personal financial management and investment options in response to real user stories, and (iv) general health queries made by individuals and recommendations offered by LLMs in such cases. In this paper, we explore gender bias in the responses generated by three LLM models to these real-world questions for these four use cases. We curated datasets of questions posted by real users on discussion forums such as Reddit, Quora, and Market Watch. These questions were manually scrutinized, and gender information was appropriately added to create a dataset of around
870
binary gender questions to evaluate gender bias in LLMs. While we recognize and acknowledge all the diversity in gender identities, for the scope of this research, we test with reference to only binary genders of male and female and leave the non-binary analyses to future work (the nuances there warrant separate and careful consideration in and of itself and can be another important contribution in this space).

While gender bias can show up in several different ways, here we mainly focus on one type - specifically, we explore gender-based biases with respect to the amount of information present in the generated responses, which we define as
entropy bias
.
Unless stated otherwise, this paper uses the term gender bias to refer to gender entropy bias. We recognize that gender biases can show up in many other ways, beyond just the amount of information produced, and leave other types of gender bias treatments to future work. That said, the amount and nature of information provided are critical for recommendations such as jobs, health, financial advice, and education. Typically, these responses require LLMs to provide various options to users, and considering the amount of information provided, it is therefore an important angle to consider.

Overall, our findings reveal that while common quantitative metrics show no significant gender bias in LLM responses at the category level, deeper qualitative analysis and LLM-as-judge evaluations uncover meaningful question-level disparities. These biases often offset when aggregated, masking localized differences. To address this, we propose a simple, model-agnostic debiasing method that effectively reduces such biases. We also discuss practical implications and offer directions for future research.

This paper makes the following contributions. First, we present and define
gender entropy bias
, a novel construct that captures bias in the amount of information provided by LLMs to specific genders. Second, we curate and provide a real-world dataset on HuggingFace datasets named "RealWorldQuestioning" (
[
1
]
) relevant to business and health contexts that can be used to study gender bias in LLMs. Third, we present results investigating gender entropy bias across popular LLMs. Finally, we present a simple debiasing approach that can readily be integrated into any LLM to mitigate gender entropy bias.

Literature Review

The emergence of pre-trained transformer-based generative models with over a hundred billion parameters has revolutionized artificial intelligence. These large language models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable capabilities in reasoning, understanding, and text generation. However, the training processes of LLMs, reliant on massive and diverse data corpora that are largely unmoderated, have inherited historical biases embedded in human-generated content. As society evolves, the biases preserved in these archived data sources persist, inadvertently permeating the LLMs. Consequently, the biases observed in LLMs are often reflections of the societal prejudices embedded in their training data.

Entropy Bias

Entropy in information theory refers to the uncertainty or randomness in a set of data
[
24
]
.
Various approaches have been proposed in the context of measuring the information content of data
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
24
]
. We build on these to derive measures for the information content in the text. When this variation in the information content of the text is due to sensitive attributes in a GenAI prompt, we posit the presence of Entropy Bias in generative AI.

3.1
Proxy Measures for Information Content

In our context, we define entropy bias as the variation in the information content of the generated text under different values of specific sensitive attributes in a prompt that is accessible to the model. Since there isn’t a single established measure to compute the information content in text documents, we assess this variation using different measures, as noted below.

Shannon Entropy

The method of entropy measurement is adopted from the seminal paper by Shannon to measure entropy with N-grams
[
27
]
.
For text, this typically involves first calculating the frequency of each symbol (word) and then using it to calculate the probability of each symbol (word) appearing in the text, which is then used to calculate the entropy as:

H=-\Sigma p(x)logp(x)

(1)

where H is the entropy, and p(x) is the probability that the symbol (word) x will appear in the text. p(x) is calculated as

p(x)=N_{x}/N

(2)

where
Nx
is the number of times the word
x
occurs in the text, and
N
is the total number of words in the text

A higher Shannon entropy generally indicates a more diverse vocabulary and a more uniform distribution of word usage and, therefore, captures both the variety and distributional patterns. For these reasons, it is a common measure of lexical diversity.

In addition to Shannon Entropy, we also use the Lexical Richness package by PyPI
[
28
]
to assess the lexical diversity of the generated text.
[
29
]

[
30
]
.
We choose to use two metrics from the Lexical Richness to measure the diversity of the textual lexicon, the Corrected Type Token Ratio (CTTR)
[
22
]
and Maas
[
23
]
.

Corrected type-token ratio (CTTR)

The corrected type-token ratio (CTTR) is calculated as

CTTR=t/\sqrt{2*w}

(3)

The corrected type-token ratio (CTTR)
[
22
]
measures the proportion of unique words (
t
) while correcting for text length (
w
) bias. Since Shannon Entropy is affected by the length of the text, we chose this measure in addition to Shannon Entropy since this measure is said to reduce the dependency on text length.

Maas

M=(log(w)-log(t))/(log(w)^{2})

(4)

Since Maas has been shown
[
23
]
to have a low impact due to text length
[
31
]
, we also used this to measure the lexical diversity of the generated text. Maas is a measure that uses a logarithmic transformation of types (t) and tokens (w) and balances lexical diversity and vocabulary size. The Maas index evaluates the rate of increase in unique words (types) as the number of total words (tokens) grows. A lower Maas measure indicates higher lexical diversity or, in other words, better information content.

More recently, significant research has also been done on measuring the entropy of a text, some of which involves accounting for the semantic similarity between words in the text.
[
32
]
,
[

[

3.2
Definition of Entropy Bias

Definition.
We define an LLM as having a gender entropy bias with respect to a query (prompt)
q
if:

1.

The information content in the output of the LLM for
q
is not expected to be different for different genders,

2.

There exist semantically equivalent queries of
q
as
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, where
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are identical queries semantically but adjusted for different (binary) genders and

3.

The expected information content of the output is statistically different for
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
.

We note the following:

•

There may be some queries where the output is expected to depend on gender (e.g., “Give me a list of places to shop for women”). We note that this is a subjective call that can depend on social and cultural norms, too (in our experiments, we focus on business-centric practical queries for jobs, education, and health information, where such variation is undesirable).

•

The semantically equivalent queries of “Give Jane a list of resources to learn about RAG” might be the original query (for the female version) and “Give John a list of resources to learn about RAG” (for the male version). We assume that the construction of such queries is also a task for users trained in bias to ensure proper (equivalent) formulation (as counterintuitive as it may seem for a paper like this, we do note that LLMs can be used as a starting point to generate such semantically equivalent queries before they are modified/altered by the human expert). In our experiments, we manually modified queries, as noted later, to ensure such equivalence.

•

Due to the inherent randomness in how outputs are generated, rather than comparing a single pair of outcomes from an LLM to conclude bias (or not), we suggest, through this definition, that such a comparison needs to be done in expectation. Further, the expected information content of the output can be computed using any of the measures noted earlier (or others as well that capture similar notions) by tests of statistical significance across many runs of the LLM for these queries.

4.1
Dataset Curation and Benchmarking

In this study, we introduce the RealWorldQuestioning Benchmark
[
1
]
, a novel dataset curated and uploaded on Hugging Face to facilitate the evaluation of gender bias and informational disparity in Large Language Models (LLMs). We collected and curated 870 real-world questions by hand from publicly available discussion forums such as Quora, Reddit, and, in the case of investments, MarketWatch, focusing on authentic information-seeking behavior across four business-relevant domains: Education, Jobs, Investment, and Health.

We initially collected 150 real-world questions for each category and then manually reviewed and filtered them according to a defined set of criteria, especially focusing on the fourth criterion of our Gender Entropy Bias definition. This resulted in a refined dataset of 79 questions for Education, 129 for Jobs, 137 for Investment, and 89 for Health. These questions reflect the kind of complex, personal, and sometimes ambiguous queries people pose online, often containing both relevant and irrelevant information, which poses a realistic challenge for LLMs that must attend to the most pertinent aspects of a query.

For example, in the category of job or investment recommendations, we included realistic queries such as:

For longer questions with gender information present, we used ChatGPT 4o to change the gender to get a version of the question with the opposite gender, using the prompt "please change the gender for the following text," and then manually checked the generated response. Each question, modified by this process to add male and female attributes, was manually read and verified by the authors. To explicitly examine question-level variability, we execute the same query multiple times per LLM (as supported by Algorithm

The evaluation procedure, detailed in Algorithm

5.3
Summary Results

Quantitative metrics (Entropy, CTTR, Maas) showed no consistent system-wide gender bias, with similar average information content across genders. However, LLM-as-judge evaluations occasionally favored one gender, typically male, depending on the category. While such effects may cancel out in aggregate, individual queries can still show meaningful disparities. Deeper analysis revealed localized biases not visible at the category level. To address this, we propose and test a real-time, model-agnostic debiasing method that merges gender-specific responses, improving both fairness and information richness.

6
Debiasing

Building strategies to systematically minimize gender bias in real-world contexts will be important and is a promising direction for future work. However, we share a simple model-agnostic debiasing strategy here.
We use the same LLM for debiasing as the one that was used for generating the initial responses. For this paper, we have used only the ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo for demonstrating the debiasing approaches because we saw higher variations between male and female responses with that LLM.

To demonstrate this (refer to algorithm
4
), we first prompt the LLM to generate gender-specific variants of a user-provided question, one framed from a female perspective and the other from a male perspective. These reformulated prompts are then independently submitted to the same language model (ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo), which returns corresponding gendered responses. To quantify potential disparities in content richness or informativeness, we compute the Shannon entropy of each response as a proxy for lexical and informational diversity. Next, we iteratively combine and refine these two responses using a three-level prompt chain designed to preserve, enrich, and restructure the information across both responses. Each refinement step is guided by an increasingly detailed prompt, and the resulting outputs are re-evaluated based on their Shannon entropy. The final debiased response is selected as the output with the highest entropy, or one that surpasses the entropy of the original gendered responses, if such early improvement occurs. This ensures that the final answer retains the most diverse and informative elements from both perspectives.

This simple yet effective strategy is model-agnostic and relies only on prompting and entropy-based selection, making it easy to apply across various deployment settings. Importantly, by forcing the model to reconcile gendered perspectives into a unified, high-entropy output, it helps mitigate information asymmetry that may arise from biased or incomplete responses tailored to one gender. As such, the method offers a lightweight and scalable approach to reducing disparities in language model outputs without requiring retraining or access to model architectures, thus proving useful on closed-source models.

Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated the potential for Gender Entropy Bias, the possibility that large language models (LLMs) may generate responses with differing information content depending on the gendered framing of the input question. In general, unlike what we expected going into this work, we did not find large-scale and systematic bias favoring one gender over the other in many practical, real-life contexts when we used real questions asked by users. Quantitative metrics such as Shannon entropy, CTTR, and Maas consistently indicated minimal systematic bias, suggesting that contemporary LLMs may already include guardrails to mitigate such disparities, particularly in practical scenarios. Our analysis through varied approaches for measuring this in practical, real-life scenarios presents mixed and nuanced findings. We presented detailed results across many approaches to gain insights into this issue and conclude, from these analyses, that while there is still some gender bias in these models in important areas, overall, it does appear as if the guardrails put in place by the LLMs may be working better than we may have expected.
We speculate that developers can more easily implement safeguards using well-defined, quantitative measures like entropy, whereas preventing more nuanced forms of bias, those grounded in social norms or subjective interpretations, requires extensive labeled data, which is more costly and time-consuming to generate. Nonetheless, it does appear that efforts are on the way to generate good unbiased training data

, and with time, we anticipate that bias in practical scenarios such as the ones we studied will continue to be less in large-scale commercial LLMs.

We hope that ideas such as generating multiple gender-specific options in real-time and combining them can be explored in more detail in future work as possible solutions that can be easily implementable by end-users of AI models, even if the foundation models do not do this for inference cost reasons. Regarding entropy bias specifically, we also believe that developing better metrics to measure information content in real-life contexts can be particularly useful since such quantitative metrics are likely more straightforward (i.e., less expensive) to use when developing guardrails for large AI models.

Based on our experiments, analysis, and findings, we also recommend using real-world questions to investigate biases in AI as opposed to more stylistic questions that may not reflect actual end-user scenarios. Our research also demonstrates that the bias in LLM-generated content may not always be quantifiable through metrics but may need human analysis as well. For this reason, we advocate incorporating human-in-the-loop evaluations, especially for high-stakes applications. Incorporating such human intervention will be key to ensuring more fair outcomes for users of all genders. Clarity in defining what constitutes gender bias in textual responses is critical; specifically, as stated earlier, our findings differed substantially based on whether we focused on a specific quantitative metric or assessments from LLM-as-a-judge.

There are several limitations of our work as well, which we note. First, as mentioned earlier, the domain as well as the question is broad and nuanced, and no single study can indeed conclusively answer this question. We need a large body of work, with different experiments in varied contexts, to help provide a more conclusive picture. Second, one specific limitation of our work could be the choice of questions we used (i.e., based on the sources we gathered the real data/questions from). Another limitation could be the metrics chosen for quantitative evaluation - Shannon Entropy, CTTR, and Maas. As noted earlier, future research might suggest a better choice of metrics for measuring information content in LLM-generated responses. Finally, due to cost considerations, many of our analyses are fairly small in size, i.e., the number of questions considered was in the hundreds and may not, therefore, reflect the true scale of some of these problems. Despite these limitations, we hope the datasets curated and the range of analyses conducted here help present a rich perspective that can guide future work.

Appendix B
Dataset

In this study, we introduce the RealWorldQuestioning Benchmark
[
1
]
, a novel dataset curated and uploaded to Hugging Face to facilitate the evaluation of gender bias and informational disparity in Large Language Models (LLMs). Comprising over 400 authentic user questions sourced from public forums such as Reddit and Quora, the dataset is categorized into four high-impact domains—Education, Jobs, Investment, and Health—reflecting real-world decision-making contexts. Each question is framed from both male and female user perspectives to assess demographic sensitivity and includes metadata such as forum source, domain category, and group index. Four anonymized LLMs were used to generate responses for each question, with Shannon entropy computed per response to capture the information content. This enables the identification of entropy bias, a phenomenon wherein models may produce more or less informative outputs depending on user framing. By combining qualitative and quantitative annotations, the dataset supports comprehensive benchmarking of LLM fairness, robustness, and information asymmetry, making it a valuable resource for researchers studying bias in generative AI.
