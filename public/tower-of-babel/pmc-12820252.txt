Study on the spatiotemporal variation mechanisms of soil moisture in maize fields
Authors: Lihua Lan, Tingting Zhang, Baolin Wang, Fei He, Xiaoyong Wu, Junwei Bao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soil Moisture Content (SMC) is crucial for sustaining agricultural productivity, ecosystem health, and climate feedback processes. This study investigates the spatiotemporal variation of SMC during two key maize growth stages using a combined physically-based and data-driven approach, which synergizes Water Cloud vegetation correction, Dubois–Dobson dielectric retrieval. The developed SMC inversion method achieving high accuracy with determination coefficients (R2) of 0.75 in the maturity stage and 0.78 in the filling stage, yielding high-resolution SMC product. Machine learning methods, enhanced by Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), were employed to analyze the impacts of environmental factors on SMC based on the high-resolution SMC product. Land surface temperature (LST) was identified as the primary driver of spatial variation during maturity, while elevation dominated during the filling stage. The different levels of SMC in two stages were largely dictated by meteorological factors, but the role of maize was deemed inconsequential on SMC’s temporal variation. Furthermore, the relationships between SMC and environmental factors were quantified. SMC exhibits a gradual decrease trend with rising LST, yet this trend escalates sharply when LST surpasses 15 °C. An optimal range of Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) value, between 0.2 and 0.5, was discovered to be most effective for preserving SMC. This research offers a comprehensive perspective on the drivers of SMC variation, which is pivotal for informed agricultural practices and the enhancement of climate models.

Introduction

Soil moisture content (SMC) plays a significant role in understanding crop yield dynamics, drought conditions, and agricultural forecasting1–4. Therefore, it is essential to apply a high-resolution SMC product5,6. However, currently, obtaining high-resolution SMC data across various spatial and temporal scales remains a daunting task, and there are challenges posed by the limitations of SMC inversion methods7–9. Traditional monitoring methods, which rely on fixed-point probes and physical model simulations, are primarily applicable to point-scale SMC value acquisition10,11. However, these methods have inherent temporal and spatial constraints12. Additionally, the presence of vegetation canopy complicates remote sensing of SMC across various spectral domains. In visible and infrared wavelengths, vegetation can obscure the soil surface, while in thermal bands, it alters the energy balance and temperature signals13,14. Microwave remote sensing emerges as a highly promising approach for addressing these issues in SMC monitoring, as it is capable of delivering high-resolution SMC products15. Also, given the high aggregation and density of agricultural fields, along with their relatively small distribution areas and frequent vegetation cover16–18, there is an urgent need to develop algorithms for high-resolution SMC data inversion for agricultural water monitoring.

Spatiotemporal variation of SMC is shaped by a complex array of factors that exhibit significant variations with the scale of observation19–22. At the field scale, local factors are the primary drivers of SMC variation, encompassing soil properties such as texture and porosity, soil layer depth, vegetation cover and its seasonal fluctuations, irrigation, and topographical elements that influence water flow and retention23–25. Meteorological factors, notably precipitation and radiation, are suggested to be the principal controllers of SMC spatial variation at larger scales26,27. Increasingly, observational evidence supports the correlation between long-term spatial patterns of SMC and soil characteristics28–31. Research by Perry and Niemann32, Duan et al.33, and Biswas et al.34 have consistently shown that SMC variation are driven by the interplay of topographical features and soil attributes across both hillslope and landscape scales. Several studies have indicated that SMC exhibits scale effects in its spatiotemporal variation. Cheng et al.35 highlighted that SMC is subject to a variety of influences depending on the climatic region. Deng et al.21 observed that, globally, rising temperatures are associated with reduced SMC. Despite numerous regional studies, field-scale SMC patterns exhibit complex spatial and temporal variability. While internal and external factors driving SMC dynamics have been extensively investigated (e.g., soil properties, topography, and meteorological conditions), their relative contributions and interactions at the field scale remain an area of ongoing exploration, particularly in the context of specific crop growth stages36.

Researchers have employed a multitude of approaches to decipher the SMC variation in recent years. Brocca et al.37 analyzed surface processes’ effects on SMC by decomposing spatial variance into temporal components. Wang et al.38 combined structural equation modeling with neural networks to explore SMC mechanisms. Wang et al.31 used variance decomposition to link SMC with sandy soil. The Geo Detector model have been instrumental in SMC variation analysis39,40. Furthermore, interpretable machine learning has gradually emerged as a powerful tool for explaining the spatiotemporal variation of SMC41–44. However, the current methodologies employed to investigate the spatiotemporal variations in SMC are limited by the scarcity of soil measurement data45–47. Moreover, the findings are challenging to standardize for comparison, as they are predicated on a variety of scales and climatic contexts across different studies. These research efforts have also fallen short in thoroughly dissecting the critical factors that impact on SMC across both temporal and spatial dimensions. Consequently, they have been unable to furnish actionable insights and directives for the management of agricultural SMC48,49.

Black soil, renowned for its high organic matter content and exceptional moisture retention capacity, plays a vital role in agricultural productivity, particularly in Northeast China—a key grain-producing region. However, despite extensive research on SMC dynamics in black soils, critical gaps persist in understanding the fine-scale spatiotemporal variability of SMC and its environmental drivers across different phenological stages of maize. Previous studies have primarily relied on coarse-resolution data or generic empirical models, limiting their applicability for precision agriculture in uniquely fertile yet climate-sensitive black soil ecosystems. To bridge this gap, a semi-empirical model was developed to generate high-resolution SMC estimates across maize phenological stages, addressing the limitations of earlier approaches. Further, a machine learning-driven attribution analysis was conducted to quantify the contributions of environmental factors to SMC variations, advancing beyond conventional statistical correlations. Specifically, three machine-learning models were tested to identify the most robust predictor of SMC dynamics, with additional interpretability achieved through SHAP analysis. This multiscale methodology not only enhances the resolution and accuracy of SMC mapping but also deciphers the nonlinear interactions between environmental thresholds (e.g., soil properties, climate, and crop phenology) and moisture variability—a dimension previously underexplored in black soil agroecosystems. By integrating spatially continuous SMC products with environmental covariates, this study offers novel insights into the mechanistic drivers of SMC variability, thereby supporting targeted soil conservation strategies and adaptable water management in black soil regions under climatic stressors.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is located in Lishu County, Jilin Province, China. The central coordinates were 43°23′N, 123°49’ E. It is located in a semi-humid continental monsoon climate region characterized by four seasons with sufficient sunshine and precipitation during crop growth. Lishu County experiences an average annual precipitation of 500–650 mm, which aligns with the climatic conditions typical of semi-humid regions in Northeast China, with a southeast to northwest precipitation gradient and homogeneous water resource distribution. The region receives an average annual sunshine of 2644 h, with the crop-growing season lasting from April to September. Ample sunshine and abundant light energy make it an ideal location for agricultural activities. Data

Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data

Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data for two stages to examine the consequences of the SMC variation were collected (Fig. 2). Two SAR images were acquired in the same area on October 2022 (mature stage), and September 2023 (filling stage). The mature stage signifies the time for harvesting, while the filling stage, characterized by the swift accumulation of starch and other nutrients in the kernels, plays a crucial role in determining yield. This study utilized RADARSAT-2 data (Canadian synthetic aperture radar satellite-2) in the C-band (5.3 GHz) with an incidence angle of 29°, spatial resolution of 8 m, and area coverage of 25 km × 25 km. The dataset encompassed four polarization modes: HH (horizontal-horizontal polarization), VV (vertical–vertical polarization), HV (horizontal-vertical), and VH (vertical-horizontal). Ground sampling campaign

Field measurement experiments, including SMC, vegetation water content (VWC), and dielectric constant (DC), were conducted within the study area during two distinct phenological stages of corn growth: the maturity stage in October 2022 and the grain filling stage in September 2023. These stages were selected to capture contrasting soil and vegetation conditions associated with different physiological processes. A total of 55 and 50 soil samples were collected in 2022 and 2023, respectively, coinciding with the acquisition of RADARSAT-2 images. All field sampling was conducted after ≥ 72 rain-free hours, eliminating confounding effects of transient rainfall. This ensures observed soil moisture patterns reflect sustained climatic and topographic influences rather than ephemeral weather events. The geographical locations of these samples were precisely recorded using a Global Positioning System (GPS). Topsoil samples (0–5 cm depth) were collected using a coring device, as this depth aligns with the penetration depth of RADARSAT-2 C-band signals and is most sensitive to surface SMC dynamics, which are critical for vegetation water uptake13,15. The bulk density of each sample was determined to enable accurate conversion of gravimetric measurements to volumetric values.

The weight water content of soil samples was measured using the standard oven-drying method at 105 °C for 24 h, ensuring complete evaporation of free water; VWC was then calculated by multiplying the weight water content by the bulk density. To minimize spatial variability, the final VWC value for each sampling point was averaged from three adjacent samples. Simultaneously, corn stover (stalks and leaves) from the immediate vicinity of each soil sampling location was collected to quantify the VWC of vegetation. Stover samples were dried at 70 °C until a constant weight was achieved, and VWC was calculated as the difference between fresh and dry weights relative to the dry weight. This method ensures consistent and comparable VWC measurements across different sampling sites, even under varying plant health conditions. The DC data was measured using an Agilent handheld vector network analyzer, which provides precise dielectric properties of the soil for radar signal interpretation.

To address potential concerns regarding sampling conditions, all soil samples were collected under clear sky conditions and within a 2-h window around solar noon to minimize diurnal variations in soil temperature and moisture. Additionally, the sampling sites were selected to represent shaded (under canopy) conditions. This approach ensures that the measured SMC and temperature data accurately represent the field conditions and are relevant to vegetation growth dynamics. In this study, all sampled soils were uniform in texture, classified as typical black soil (chernozem) with consistent physical properties (e.g., clay loam dominant). While natural micro-variability exists, spatial homogeneity was prioritized during sampling to minimize confounding effects from texture variations.

Environmental indices data

Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), and Grey Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) were derived from Sentinel-2A and Sentinel-2B imagery. NDVI and EVI were calculated using Sentinel-2B data. GLCM was generated from the panchromatic (Band 8, 842 nm) band using texture analysis methods through Sentinel-2B. Land surface temperature (LST) was estimated using Landsat 8 imagery by applying the Statistical Mono-Window (SMW) method. Albedo data were obtained from the MCD43A3 products. The evapotranspiration (ET) data provided by the MOD16A2 product, and represent the actual amount of water released into the atmosphere by vegetation and soil. Precipitation data were sourced from the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Station data (CHIRPS) dataset. Terrain attributes, including slope and aspect, were assessed using the THORPEX Interactive Grand Global Ensemble (TIGGE) repository on Google Earth Engine. The 30-m resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data were sourced from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) dataset, which is publicly available via the US Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer platform. The processed DEM was then resampled to a resolution of 20 m using bilinear interpolation to match the spatial resolution of other datasets in the study.

All datasets were processed to match the acquisition dates of RADARSAT-2 imagery, and two weekly averages were calculated to synchronize the temporal resolution. To harmonize the spatial resolution and ensure comparability of the datasets, we implemented a bilinear resampling strategy. All datasets were resampled to a consistent spatial resolution of 20 m, which served as the standard scale for our analysis (Tables 2, 3). Methods

The research methodology in this paper consists of two main components: the SMC inversion methods and the SMC variations attribution analysis. The SMC inversion methods utilizes both empirical models and machine learning techniques, while the SMC variations attribution analysis is conducted using machine learning methods and SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis (Table 6).

SMC inversion methods

A lookup table for the DC computation is obtained using the Dobson model. Then the lookup table method was used to invert the SMC. In this study, the temperature for SMC measurements was 15 °C. The sand volume fraction was 39.84, the clay volume fraction was 10.66, and the bulk density (bd) was 1.4, aligning with the soil texture specific to the study area. The SMC value ranged from 0 to 0.6, with increments of 0.001.

During the development of the empirical model for SMC retrieval, all key parameters (A, B, and soil electrical conductivity) were fitted using the least squares method. For this analysis, measured samples were collected during two distinct phenological stages of vegetation growth, with 55 samples in the maturity stage and 50 samples in the filling stage. The model’s fitting accuracy was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean square error (RMSE), as presented in the Results.

Three machine learning models (XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost) were also applied for SMC inversion. The input variables for the ML models were the environmental parameters listed in section "Environmental indices data", and the target variable was the in-situ measured SMC samples. The samples were partitioned into training and testing subsets in a 7:3 ratio randomly. Subsequently, the tenfold cross-validation to pinpoint the most effective parameters were utilized. The optimal hyperparameters including learning rate, max_depth, n_estimators, were selected based on the best performance in cross-validation. The hyperparameter ranges were set as 0.001–0.1 for learning rate, 3–10 for max_depth. The optimal hyperparameters were selected based on the best performance in cross-validation using RMSE.

Methods for analyzing factors’ impacts to spatial variations and SMC temporal variations

Machines learning models accompanied with SHAP method were used in this study for attributing SMC variations (temporal and spatial variation) in maize fields (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Flowchart of studying the mechanisms of spatiotemporal variation in SMC.

Flowchart of studying the mechanisms of spatiotemporal variation in SMC.

The XGBoost model is a powerful and efficient gradient boosting framework designed to optimize both speed and performance, making it particularly suitable for complex datasets57. It integrates basis functions and weights to achieve high predictive accuracy and includes regularization techniques to prevent overfitting.

The LightBoost model is designed for high efficiency, utilizing techniques such as gradient-based one-sided sampling and exclusive feature bundling to significantly reduce computation time and memory usage while maintaining high accuracy58. It supports efficient parallel training and uses leaf-wise tree growth, making it particularly effective for large-scale machine learning tasks.

The CatBoost model excels in managing both continuous and categorical variables59. It excels at attributing both the temporal and spatial variations of SMC. Consequently, when the input SMC samples consist of spatially continuous data, CatBoost effectively addresses the attribution of SMC spatial variations. Likewise, when the input samples are categorical levels of SMC at different time (i.e., categorical variables), CatBoost is also capable of handling the attribution of SMC temporal variations.

The SHAP method was employed here for in-depth analysis of the degree of factors’ impacts to SMC variations, and was mainly applied to machine models with the highest accuracy for quantitative interpretation. The SHAP framework offers a theoretical approach to interpreting the model, establishing itself as an indispensable tool in the domain of machine learning model interpretation60. SHAP values quantify the extent and direction (positive or negative) of a feature’s impacts on the SMC. Simultaneously, we use partial dependence on variables to evaluate the relationship between individual predictor factors and the SMC. This method allows us to isolate the effect of a specific predictor while accounting for the average effects of other predictors in the model.

Prior to attribute the variations of SMC using three machine learning methods, comprehensive data preparation was conducted. Different types of indicators were selected. The indicators encompass vegetation cover, surface structure, topography, soil properties, and meteorological data, first principal components of soil properties and vegetation cover were calculated and standardized. The definitions and abbreviations of these indicators are displayed in Tables 2, 3. High-resolution SMC products which were simulated in section "SMC inversion methods" and the selected indicators were both resampled to a 20-m resolution. The values of each pixel from the high-resolution SMC product were extracted using a systematic sampling approach. The extraction process involved loading the SMC product into a geospatial analysis software (e.g., Google Earth Engine or QGIS) and overlaying the study area boundary to ensure spatial alignment. Subsequently, the pixel values within the study area were extracted programmatically using spatial query functions, resulting in a total of 1,562,500 pixel values. These pixel values were then preprocessed to eliminate any invalid entries, such as null values or outliers, by applying thresholding techniques based on the valid range of the SMC product. Finally, the cleaned pixel samples were divided into training and testing sets in a 7:3 ratio, serving as input for the SMC variation attribution model.

For the attribution of spatial variation in SMC, we input continuous values of SMC from 1, 562, 500 pixels for training and testing using XGBoost, LightBoost and CatBoost methods. In contrast, for the attribution of temporal variation in SMC, the samples we input are categorical samples of SMC from different periods, CatBoost is employed to attribute the temporal variation of SMC.

During the model training phase for attribution both SMC temporal variations and spatial variations, the selected indicators were used as independent variables, and SMC values for each pixel served as the dependent variable. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid search with fivefold cross-validation, evaluating all combinations of: (1) learning rate (0.001, 0.01, 0.1), (2) max_depth (3, 5, 7, 9), and (3) n_estimators (50, 100, 200). The optimal set was selected by minimizing RMSE (root mean square error), with concurrent monitoring of training/validation curves to ensure generalization. Final parameters achieving the lowest validation error were subsequently used for full model training. During testing, the machine learning algorithm with the highest performance was selected to determine the significance of contributing factors using SHAP method.

Validation methods

To assess the performance of the SMC inversion and attribution models in this study, we utilized five performance statistics: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), R-squared (R2), Accuracy (Overall correct prediction ratio), Precision (Correctness of positive predictions), and Recall (Ability to find all positives).

Discussion

Mechanisms of SMC spatiotemporal variations

This study analyzed the mechanisms affecting the spatial variation (different locations at the same time) and temporal variation (same location at different times) of SMC, and explore the impacts of various indicators on SMC and the mechanisms behind.

As for mechanisms of SMC spatial variations, research has shown a generally positive correlation between vegetation cover and SMC across different regions. Areas with higher vegetation cover tend to have greater surface SMC. This phenomenon may be attributed to the water uptake by plant roots and increased transpiration, which enhance precipitation, thereby providing more moisture to the surface soil61. Our study reinforces this perspective (Fig. 8), demonstrating that areas with higher vegetation cover are more likely to be accompanied with greater SMC, which aligns with findings made by Jiang et al.62. Secondly, topographical factors significantly influence the spatial distribution of SMC, as noted by Zhang et al.63. Our research reveals that in areas with steeper slopes, rapid surface runoff is likely, which impedes the effective infiltration of precipitation into the soil, thereby reducing the soil’s water-holding capacity. These results are corroborated by other studies (Fig. 8)64,65. In line with our findings, Henninger et al.66 also observed that regions with higher values in the digital elevation model (DEM) correspond to lower SMC levels. Our research findings further reveal that higher surface albedo is often associated with lower SMC. This correlation is primarily due to the fact that lower albedo may indicate a higher organic matter content and better soil structure, both of which contribute to the soil’s enhanced water retention capacity67. Additionally, Sun et al.68 confirmed our conclusions in their study. Meteorological factors also significantly influence the regional variations in SMC. Studies have shown that an increase in LST is typically accompanied by a decrease in SMC, while in regions with increased precipitation, the opposite is observed. This is mainly because rising temperatures accelerate the rapid evaporation of SMC, whereas precipitation directly replenishes the SMC69.

As for mechanisms of SMC temporal variations, SMC levels vary across different phenological stages due to the interaction of multiple environmental factors70. The results revealed that vegetation cover indicators were significantly higher during filling stage than during mature stage. Studies have shown that during periods of high vegetation cover, the albedo, or reflectivity, of the soil surface is typically diminished71,72 (Fig. 9a,c). Concurrently, at a certain period, LST is also reduced due to the cooling effects caused by increased ET resulting from higher vegetation cover73,74 (Fig. 9h,d), which is consistent with some studies75,76.

However, the study showed that meteorological factors were the primary contributors to SMC temporal variations, which can be confirmed in some studies77,78. Additionally, several studies have highlighted the influence of meteorological factors on SMC temporal variations , and Feng79 demonstrated that meteorological factors are the primary reasons for SMC temporal variations. Li et al.80 further proved that precipitation, WVP, ET, and LST are the predominant factors affecting SMC over time. Precipitation exerts a direct influence on SMC temporal variations, whereas other factors modulate SMC temporal variations through processes such as ET and LST81,82.

The impact of topographical factors on the temporal variability of SMC is second only to that of meteorological factors., which has been confirmed64,83. However, aligning with related findings, we also found that vegetation cover exert a minimal influence on the SMC temporal variations when compared to meteorological and topographical factors (Fig. 10), this is because vegetations primarily affect SMC temporal variations through altering the canopy’s rainfall interception capacity of canopy and soil organic matter content84,85.

Therefore, this study reveals the dominant factors and mechanisms influencing the spatial and temporal variations of SMC in maize-growing regions, providing valuable reference for the management of farmland SMC.

Limitations

While this research provides meaningful insights into soil moisture dynamics during critical phenological stages, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied on field measurements from only two distinct phenological phases (maturity and filling stages), which, although strategically selected to capture moisture-sensitive periods, may not fully represent year-round soil moisture behavior. Additionally, the practical constraints associated with manual field sampling limited the temporal continuity of data collection. Although rigorous validation demonstrated the reliability of SAR-based inversion within the sampled periods, the absence of long-term continuous monitoring remains a limitation. Future studies would benefit from extended temporal coverage and higher-resolution observational data to further validate and generalize the findings.

Conclusions

Our results revealed that as for SMC spatial variations, LST was the primary factor influencing the spatial variation of SMC during the mature stage, while elevation played a more significant role during the filling stage. However, for the temporal variation of SMC, we found that meteorological factors were the main contributors to the temporal variation of SMC across different vegetation growth periods, with vegetation growth having a relatively minor impact on the temporal variation of SMC. Through partial dependence analysis, we were able to quantify the impact effects of environmental variables on SMC. Our findings not only enhance the scientific community’s knowledge of SMC dynamics but also offer practical implications for agricultural water management, emphasizing the importance of considering both spatial and temporal factors in water resource planning and policy-making.
