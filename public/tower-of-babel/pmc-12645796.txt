HMGA2 links morphological evolution and microenvironment dynamics to systemic therapy response in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Takahiro Nakamoto, Takashi Yoshida, Chisato Ohe, Yoshiki Yasukochi, Naho Atsumi, Takeshi Sano, Koichiro Higasa, Katsunori Uchida, Koji Tsuta, Hidefumi Kinoshita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) exhibits significant heterogeneity due to morphological changes and tumor microenvironment dynamics, influencing systemic therapy responses. While the role of high-mobility group AT-hook 2 (HMGA2) in tumor progression has been implicated in other cancers, its significance in ccRCC remains unclear. This study investigates the role of HMGA2 in these processes and its clinical impact.MethodsSpatial transcriptomics (ST) was performed on primary ccRCC samples to investigate expression trajectories associated with HMGA2 expression and morphological evolution. In metastatic ccRCC cohorts treated with systemic therapy, immunohistochemistry and bulk RNA sequencing data were analyzed to evaluate molecular and clinical features in relation to HMGA2. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were used to explore immune cell populations and their interactions. Based on these findings, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) assessed spatial distribution, cell–cell interactions, and pathological responses of key immune populations.ResultsHMGA2 expression was associated with aggressive morphological patterns, such as solid sheets and rhabdoid/sarcomatoid. ST revealed a progressive increase in HMGA2 expression along the morphological trajectory, marked by a shift from clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm, with eccentric nuclei and prominent nucleoli, and loss of vascular architecture. HMGA2-high tumors exhibited aggressive phenotypes driven by cell cycle, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and inflammatory signaling pathways. Clinically, patients with high HMGA2 had worse progression-free survival but responded better to immune checkpoint inhibitor combination (Combo-ICI) therapy than to tyrosine kinase inhibitor monotherapy. To assess the immune landscape, scRNA-seq data revealed that HMGA2-high tumors were enriched with progenitor exhausted CD8+ T cells (Tpex), along with increased frequencies of conventional dendritic cell type 1 (cDC1) and inflammatory cDC type 2, which were found to interact with Tpex via ICAM-1. mIHC confirmed that Tpex were enriched among Combo-ICI responders in HMGA2-high tumors, with higher densities and closer proximity to ICAM-1+ cDC1.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that dynamic HMGA2 expression contributes to morphological evolution and modulates immune responses through enhanced Tpex–cDCs engagement, serving as a potential marker for systemic therapy response in ccRCC. However, additional experimental studies are required to validate these mechanisms.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Kidney Cancer, Pathology

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study shows that HMGA2 expression increases along with morphological progression in ccRCC and correlates with a poor prognosis. Despite this link to aggressive features, patients with high HMGA2 expression responded favorably to ICI combination therapy, as shown by subgroup analysis. This response involves the presence of progenitor exhausted CD8+ T cells and immunostimulatory dendritic cells (conventional dendritic cell type 1 and inflammatory conventional dendritic cell type 2), which interact via ICAM-1 and are known contributors to clinical benefit from ICI therapy.

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounting for approximately 75% of cases.1 Approximately 10–30% of patients present with metastatic disease, and the median overall survival is approximately 40 months.2 3 Although tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have traditionally been the standard treatment for metastatic ccRCC, immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) combination therapies, such as TKI+ICI or ICI+ICI, have emerged as promising alternatives.4,8 Despite these advancements, many patients remain refractory and experience primary and acquired resistance. A comprehensive understanding of tumor evolution and immune interactions is essential to refine treatment strategies and elucidate mechanisms of treatment response.

ccRCC exhibits significant heterogeneity, characterized by myriad morphological patterns that correlate with the tumor microenvironment (TME) and clinical outcomes.9,11 These patterns—compact/small nested, macrocyst/microcystic, tubular/acinar, alveolar/large nested, thick trabecular/insular, papillary/pseudopapillary, solid sheets, and rhabdoid/sarcomatoid types—reflect underlying molecular changes.9 10 These alterations are integral to the evolutionary process of ccRCC, linking molecular genetic and morphological changes with prognostic and therapeutic implications.9 However, the mechanisms driving these changes are poorly understood, necessitating further research.

High-mobility group AT-hook 2 (HMGA2) is a chromatin-associated protein crucial for cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, cancer progression, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).12 13 Elevated HMGA2 expression is associated with poor prognosis in various cancers, contributing to tumor progression, reduced survival, and therapy resistance.14,16 In ccRCC, HMGA2 also contributes to tumor invasiveness and metastasis through mechanisms such as transcriptional activation by NUF2 and enhancement of the TGF-β/SMAD2 signaling pathway.17 18 However, its potential role in driving morphological evolution and modulating the TME remains of significant interest.

This study aims to investigate the expression of HMGA2 in ccRCC and evaluate its association with morphological alterations, the TME, and clinical outcomes. By exploring the role of HMGA2 in histological progression and immune regulation, this study seeks to elucidate how HMGA2 may impact treatment responses and contribute to therapeutic resistance in ccRCC.

Methods

Study design and participant details

The overview of the study cohorts and analysis workflow is summarized in online supplemental figure S1. This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. After obtaining Institutional Review Board approval from Kansai Medical University (approval number: 2018109), 90 patients with metastatic ccRCC who were treated with TKI or ICI combinations and had available pretreatment samples for analysis (KMU-01 cohort) were recruited. Four additional patients who underwent radical nephrectomy for localized ccRCC (KMU-02 cohort)19 and five with ccRCC who received ICI combination therapy before nephrectomy (KMU-03 cohort) were also enrolled.

Exclusion criteria applied across all cohorts, except for the KMU-02 and KMU-03 cohorts, were as follows: (1) presence of a non-clear cell histological component; (2) no TKI and/or ICI administered as first-line therapy; (3) lack of response evaluation data; (4) insufficient tissue samples for analysis; and (5) lack of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) or DNA mutation data (applicable only for the IMmotion151 (IM151) and Javelin Renal101 (JR101) cohorts).

In the KMU-01 cohort, 20 patients were excluded: 9 had a non-clear cell histological component, 2 did not receive TKI or ICI as first-line therapy, 1 lacked imaging data for response evaluation, and tissue microarray (TMA) construction was not feasible for 8 patients due to insufficient tissue samples (online supplemental figure S1A).

RNA-seq and clinical data were obtained from publicly available datasets for two separate phase 3 clinical trials. In the IM151 cohort, 915 patients were randomized to receive atezolizumab plus bevacizumab or sunitinib (Sun) alone20 21; in the JR101 cohort, 886 received avelumab plus axitinib or Sun alone.7 22 The current study included 823 patients (13 excluded from the 836 patients with clinical data available due to missing RNA-seq data) from the IM151 cohort and 726 (160 patients excluded due to missing RNA-seq data) from the JR101 cohort. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) immunohistochemistry (IHC) and tumor mutation burden data were available for 670 IM151 and 691 JR101 patients. These data were used to analyze HMGA2 expression in relation to clinical outcomes and molecular characteristics. The IM151 and JR101 cohorts were stratified into high and low HMGA2 expression groups using the median expression value as the cut-off.23 Molecular pathway analysis was performed using GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) V.4.3.3 and the Hallmark gene collection.

The single-cell RNA (scRNA) cohort comprised single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from two public repositories: the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive (SRZ190804) and Broad Institute Single Cell Portal (SCP1288).24 25 A total of 12 samples from six patients diagnosed with ccRCC were collected from two datasets, with samples collected after treatment with ICI. Of the originally identified nine patients, three were excluded due to the presence of a non-clear cell component (n=1), the absence of tumor tissue due to complete response (n=1), and lack of treatment response data (n=1; online supplemental figure S1A). Analysis of the scRNA cohort focused on exploring associations between HMGA2 expression and immune cell populations, including their interactions.

Treatment responses were evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1 criteria (complete response: CR; partial response: PR; stable disease: SD; and progression disease: PD). Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from the start of ICI until disease progression or death from any cause.

Pathological evaluation and immunohistochemistry

Pathological evaluation was performed by an expert uropathologist (CO) who prepared H&E slides. TMAs were constructed from 2 mm cores of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from the KMU-01 and KMU-03 cohorts. For the TMA of the KMU-01 cohort, three representative cores were sampled from each case. Cores were excluded from analysis if they contained insufficient tissue or had extensive tissue loss during processing procedures. The remaining cores and the corresponding whole-mount sections were evaluated for morphological patterns and other histological features, following the protocol described in online supplemental method S1.9 10 26 In the TMA of the KMU-03 cohort, multiple areas with different pathological response patterns to ICI combination (Combo-ICI) were selected from one patient using H&E staining following a previous study.27

IHC analysis was performed by incubating TMA sections (4 µm thick) with a primary antibody against HMGA2 (rabbit monoclonal, 1:800; Cell Signaling Technology) at 20–25°C for 60 min. Subsequently, the sections were treated with a detection reagent (N-Histofine, Simple Stain MAX PO; Nichirei Biosciences) for 30 min at 20–25°C. HMGA2 protein expression was evaluated using the H-score method in each cohort, which combines the staining intensity and percentage of positively stained cells to generate a semi-quantitative score ranging from 0 to 300. The H-score for each case was calculated as the average score across evaluable TMA cores. Using the median H-score within each cohort as the cut-off, cores or patients in the KMU-01 and KMU-03 cohorts were classified into HMGA2-high and HMGA2-low groups.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) was performed manually on TMA sections from the KMU-03 cohort using the SignalStar mIHC Panel (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Eight targets were simultaneously stained: PD-1, ICAM-1, XCR1, TCF7, CD11c, TIM-3, CD8α, PanCK in FFPE tissue. A network of complementary oligonucleotides with fluorescent labels enhanced signals for these eight targets over two imaging rounds within the same FFPE tissue. The mIHC panels were validated by the manufacturer for each antibody. The mIHC panel was designed using the SignalStar mIHC Panel Builder. Detailed information regarding markers, clone numbers, fluorophores, and imaging rounds is provided in online supplemental table S1. The stained slides in each imaging round were aligned using QuPath28 and analyzed with HALO software and SPIAT29 for fluorescence intensity, cell density, and spatial relationships. Technical details were provided in online supplemental method S2.

Spatial transcriptomics analysis

RNA quality-controlled paraffin sections were obtained for patients in the KMU-02 cohort.29 Following the manufacturer’s protocol (CG000407, 10x Genomics), probe hybridization and library preparation were performed using the Visium Spatial for FFPE Gene Expression Kit (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, California, USA). H&E-stained Visium Gene Expression slides were imaged using a NanoZoomer (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). Libraries were sequenced using an MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA).

Spatial transcriptomics analysis was performed using the Seurat R package, followed by dimensionality reduction, clustering, and integration using Harmony. Spots were filtered for gene and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, as well as mitochondrial content. Trajectory analysis was conducted with Monocle3,30 defining the root cluster based on morphological features.31 Gene set scoring was performed using Escape32 with Hallmark gene sets, and HMGA2 expression levels were used to stratify spots into high and low groups (online supplemental method S3 for details).

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Standard scRNA-seq analyses, such as dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering, were performed using Seurat. For the SRZ190804 dataset, count data were extracted from the Seurat file. According to the original study, quality control filtering had already been applied, including removing cells with >20% counts from mitochondrial genes and genes detected in fewer than three cells across all samples. The dataset was filtered to retain only tissue-derived samples based on annotation information, generating a new dataset.

For the SCP1288 dataset, raw data generated on the 10x Genomics platform were obtained and processed into a Seurat object. To ensure data quality and consistency across datasets, cells with <200 detected genes, >20% counts from mitochondrial genes, and genes detected in fewer than three cells across all samples were excluded before integration.

Preprocessed scRNA-seq datasets were normalized and integrated using Seurat with canonical correlation analysis, followed by dimensionality reduction with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and clustering with the Louvain algorithm. Broad immune lineages, including “T cell and NK cell” and “myeloid cell”, were identified using marker genes and clustermole, then re-clustered for refined annotation with CellTypist.33 Gene signature scores were calculated using Escape, and GSEA was conducted using ClusterProfiler. Cell–cell interactions between dendritic and CD8 T-cell clusters were inferred using CellChat,34 stratified by HMGA2 expression levels based on pseudo-bulk RNA-seq analysis. Detailed parameter settings are provided in online supplemental method S4. Clinical information and pseudo-bulk RNA-seq results for each sample are available in online supplemental table S2. The gene signature sets used in scRNA-seq analyses are summarized in online supplemental table S3.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For continuous variables, a two-sided t-test was used for parametric distributions, and the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-parametric distributions. The Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg methods were used for multiple comparisons. The χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, or Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test was used for categorical variables. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank tests, and HRs with 95% CIs were calculated using Cox proportional hazard models. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted to adjust for potential confounders and to explore factors associated with survival outcomes. Statistical analyses and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0 (RRID: SCR_002798), R V.4.3.3, and EZR V.1.63. Statistical significance was set at two-sided p<0.05.

Results

Dynamic HMGA2 expression reflects ccRCC morphological evolution

Representative H&E staining and HMGA2 IHC images for each major morphological pattern observed in ccRCC9 10 are presented in figure 1A. TMA scores within the KMU-01 cohort (n=175) revealed that HMGA2-high scores were not detected in compact/small nested, macrocyst/microcystic, tubular/acinar, or alveolar/large nested patterns (figure 1B). The frequency of HMGA2 progressively increased across morphological patterns, with rates of 2.8% in thick trabecular/insular, 25.0% in papillary/pseudopapillary, 32.6% in solid sheets, and 45.5% in sarcomatoid/rhabdoid patterns.

To investigate the association between morphological evolution and HMGA2 expression, spatial transcriptomics analysis was performed in the KMU-02 cohort. First, a pathologist (CO) annotated major morphological patterns on five H&E slides, two of which (KMU-2A and KMU-2B) were from the same patient (figure 1C). Subsequent clustering analysis identified 20 subclusters, which generally aligned with the boundaries between non-tumor and tumor regions (online supplemental figure S2A,B). HMGA2 expression was predominantly observed in thick trabecular/insular, solid sheets, and rhabdoid patterns (figure 1C), consistent with the protein expression patterns (figure 1B).

We focused on tumor regions by excluding stroma, necrosis, and normal kidney tissue (online supplemental figure S2B). UMAP visualizations of the integrated data and H&E staining revealed that while certain clusters were predominantly associated with specific morphological patterns, others overlapped (figure 1C and online supplemental figure S2C). For example, Cluster 1 predominantly appeared in compact/small nests but also at lower frequencies in alveolar/large nests and thick trabecular/insular; Cluster 5 was similarly represented in solid sheets and alveolar/large nests; meanwhile, Cluster 6 predominantly reflected rhabdoid but was also commonly detected in alveolar/large nests and solid sheets, and infrequently in compact/small nests. Thus, transcriptomic clusters broadly associate with morphological patterns but are not uniquely associated with any one of them.

Pseudotime analysis using UMAP revealed HMGA2 expression on tissue slides and in the UMAP projection (figure 1C,D, and online supplemental figure S2C). HMGA2 expression was most concentrated in the regions corresponding to the later pseudotime stages, suggesting an increase in HMGA2 expression as the morphological patterns progressed.

To demonstrate the relationship between HMGA2 expression and the dynamic evolution of morphological patterns, we focused on Clusters 0, 1, 4, and 6 using trajectory analysis (figure 1E). HMGA2 expression progressively increased along the trajectory, peaking at Cluster 6. Cellular transformations, including eccentric nuclei with prominent nucleoli, a shift from clear cell to spherical eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions, loss of vascular architecture, and loosening cellular junctions, correlated with increased HMGA2 expression. Hence, HMGA2 may be involved in the morphological evolution of ccRCC.

HMGA2 expression is associated with adverse prognostic pathways and pathological features

Using integrated spatial transcriptomics data from the KMU-02 cohort, the spatial distribution of HMGA2-related hallmark gene signatures was analyzed across H&E-stained sections. These notably elevated signature scores were predominantly found in regions with solid sheet morphology, corresponding to areas with higher HMGA2 expression (figure 2A). By stratifying the spots into HMGA2-high and HMGA2-low groups based on median expression, significantly higher scores were observed for E2F (p<0.001), G2M checkpoint (p<0.001), mitotic spindle (p<0.001), inflammatory response (p<0.001), and EMT (p=0.024) pathways in HMGA2-high spots. Meanwhile, angiogenesis-related signatures showed no significant difference (p=1.00; figure 2A). This was consistent with the findings of previous reports linking HMGA2 expression to cell cycle regulation, inflammation, and EMT.1218 35,37

GSEA was performed using bulk RNA-seq data from the IM151 and JR101 cohorts to validate these findings. The analysis revealed enrichment of mitotic spindle, cell cycle-related pathways (G2M checkpoint, E2F targets), MYC target gene sets V2, EMT, inflammatory response, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-⍺ signaling in HMGA2-high patients. Meanwhile, angiogenesis-related signatures were not statistically significant in either cohort (false discovery rate (FDR) q value≥0.1). In contrast, metabolic pathways, including bile acid metabolism, adipogenesis, and fatty acid metabolism, were depleted (figure 2B).

The association between HMGA2 expression and ICI response markers38 was assessed using the IM151 cohort. HMGA2-high patients exhibited significantly higher PD-L1 expression (56.1% vs 40.2%, p<0.001) and tumor mutation burden (median 4.39 vs 3.51 mutations/Mb, p=0.022) than HMGA2-low patients, with similar trends in the JR101 cohort (figures 2C and online supplemental figure S3).

The clinicopathological significance of HMGA2 expression was evaluated in the KMU-01 cohort (figure 2D). HMGA2-high patients had a significantly higher proportion of high-grade tumors (Grade 3–4: 92.9% vs 48.2%, p<0.001) and more frequent necrosis (64.3% vs 8.9%, p<0.001) than HMGA2-low patients. Stratification by immune phenotypes revealed that all HMGA2-high patients exhibited excluded or inflamed types, compared with 71.4% of HMGA2-low patients (p=0.063). Additionally, 92.9% of HMGA2-high patients were classified as category 3 based on a vascularity-based architectural classification,10 compared with 37.5% of HMGA2-low patients (p=0.001).

Overall, these findings suggest that high HMGA2 expression is associated with cell cycle activation, EMT, inflammatory pathways, and aggressive pathological features, which are expected to worsen oncological outcomes in ccRCC.

High HMGA2 expression in patients with ccRCC is associated with improved ICI response, despite overall poor prognosis

The clinical impact of HMGA2 expression (low vs high) was evaluated across the KMU-01, IM151, and JR101 cohorts, with baseline characteristics summarized in online supplemental table S4. High HMGA2 expression in the KMU-01 and IM151 cohorts was significantly associated with worse International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium risk classification (p<0.001), while in the JR101 cohort, it correlated with younger age (p=0.002), male sex (p=0.003), and the Sun arm (p=0.009). After adjusting for these baseline biases, HMGA2-high patients in KMU-01 had significantly lower PFS rates (HR=5.75, p<0.001), with similar trends in the IM151 (HR=1.24, p<0.001) and JR101 (HR=1.75, p<0.001) cohorts (figure 3A). Subgroup analyses further suggested that this unfavorable impact was generally consistent across clinical subgroups (online supplemental figure S4). Furthermore, multivariate analysis confirmed high HMGA2 expression as an independent predictor of worsened PFS in the KMU-01 and IM151 cohorts (figure 3B). For the JR101 cohort, subgroup and multivariate analyses could not be performed because only limited clinical outcome data were available.

To investigate the association between HMGA2 and systemic therapeutic response, we compared the distribution of best response categories (CR+PR, SD, PD) between TKI alone and Combo-ICI therapy. In HMGA2-high patients, Combo-ICI therapy showed a higher proportion of CR+PR than TKI alone in the KMU-01 cohort (50.0% vs 33.3%; Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, p=0.800), with a significantly higher proportion observed in the IM151 cohort (45.4% vs 31.0%; χ2 test, p=0.016; figure 3C). Furthermore, PFS was significantly higher in HMGA2-high patients following Combo-ICI across all cohorts (HRs: KMU-01, 8.50, p=0.026; IM151, 1.40, p=0.004; JR101, 1.78, p<0.001). Meanwhile, no significant differences in PFS were observed for HMGA2-low patients between treatment arms (figure 3D).

High HMGA2 expression is linked to poor prognosis in patients with ccRCC. However, these patients might respond better to ICI-containing regimens than TKI alone, suggesting that HMGA2-high tumors may have a more favorable tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).

HMGA2-high tumors exhibit enrichment of progenitor exhausted CD8+ T cells and correspond with improved ICI responses

Given the differential responses to ICI combination therapy based on HMGA2 expression levels, HMGA2 was hypothesized to influence CD8+ T-cell infiltration within the TIME in ccRCC. To investigate CD8+ T-cell dynamics, 85,287 single cells from publicly available scRNA-seq datasets were evaluated (online supplemental figure 5A). T and natural killer cell subclusters were annotated using CellTypist and major gene expression patterns (figure 4A,B and online supplemental figure 5B,C). CD8+ T cells were extracted and reclustered to refine their transcriptional landscape (figure 4C). CD8+ T cells in the HMGA2-high group were more highly distributed in CD8 T cell-1, CD8 T cell-2, and CD8 T cell-3 clusters than the HMGA2-low group (figure 4C and online supplemental table S2).

Given the growing interest in T-cell exhaustion, CD8+ T-cell clusters were characterized by analyzing progenitor (TCF7, IL7R, NR4A1) and exhaustion markers (HAVCR2, PDCD1, and TOX). Progenitor markers were highly expressed in CD8 T-cell-1 and CD8 T-cell-2 clusters, with TCF7 and IL7R enriched in both clusters, and NR4A1 particularly elevated in the CD8 T-cell-2 cluster. Exhaustion markers were expressed at lower levels in CD8 T-cell-1 but were elevated in other clusters (figure 4D and online supplemental figure S6A).

To further characterize CD8+ T-cell exhaustion states, progenitor exhausted CD8+ T cell (Tpex) and terminally exhausted CD8+ T cell (Ttex) signature scores were computed using established gene signatures.32 39 A portion of the CD8 T-cell-1 and whole CD8 T-cell-2 clusters exhibited higher Tpex scores, while Ttex scores were significantly lower in the CD8 T-cell-1 cluster (figure 4E,F).

Next, the relationship between HMGA2 expression and CD8+ T-cell clusters was examined, focusing on the CD8 T-cell-1 and CD8 T-cell-2 clusters. In the CD8 T-cell-1 cluster, progenitor markers (TCF7, NR4A1) were significantly enriched in the HMGA2-high group, whereas exhaustion markers (TIGIT, TOX, PRDM1) and cytolytic molecules (PRF1, GZMA) were more abundant in the HMGA2-low group. In the CD8 T-cell-2 cluster, an exhaustion marker (TOX) and progenitor markers (NR4A1, ZFP36L2) were predominantly upregulated in the HMGA2-high group (figure 4G and online supplemental figure S6B). GSEA confirmed significant enrichment of Tpex gene signatures in the CD8 T-cell-1 and CD8 T-cell-2 clusters within the HMGA2-high group (adjusted p=0.023; figure 4H), whereas no significant difference was detected in the enrichment of Ttex signatures between the groups (adjusted p=0.432; online supplemental figure S6C). Analysis of bulk RNA-seq data from the IM151 and JR101 cohorts confirmed the consistent upregulation of Tpex-related genes, such as TCF7, IL7R, and ZFP36L2, in the HMGA2-high group (figure 4I).

Furthermore, the effect of Tpex on ICI efficacy within the HMGA2-high group was assessed. Patients with high HMGA2 and TCF7 expression exhibited significantly improved PFS with Combo-ICI compared with TKI alone (IM151: HR 1.78, p<0.001; JR101: HR 2.15, p<0.001). However, in the TCF7-low subgroup, no significant PFS difference was observed between Combo-ICI and TKI (IM151: HR 1.05, p=0.789; JR101: HR 1.37, p=0.191; figure 4J).

These findings highlight the potential role of high HMGA2 expression in improving ICI outcomes, particularly through Tpex within the HMGA2-high group.

ICAM-1-mediated dendritic cell–CD8+ T-cell interactions are enhanced in HMGA2-high tumors

Intercellular communication between T cells and myeloid cells is crucial in tumor progression and ICI efficacy in ccRCC.24 40 Myeloid cell subcluster annotations using CellTypist highlighted major gene expression patterns (online supplemental figure S7A) and identified four macrophage clusters, three conventional dendritic cell clusters (cDC1, cDC2-1, cDC2-2), and one monocyte cluster (figure 5A,B).

Next, the myeloid subclusters with differential proportions based on HMGA2 expression were identified. cDC1, cDC2-1, and monocyte clusters were more abundant in the HMGA2-high group (p=0.043, p=0.043, and p<0.001, respectively; figure 5C).

Previous studies suggest that cDC1 supports ICI responsiveness by maintaining Tpex in the immune niche, while inflammatory cDC2 (inf-cDC2)—a recently identified subset within cDC2—presents antigens to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.41 42 Gene expression was compared with better understand DC cluster characteristics, revealing that the cDC1 cluster expressed key markers like XCR1, BATF3, and IRF8, typical of classical cDC1. In contrast, the cDC2-1 cluster exhibited upregulated SIRPA, FCGR1A, SETMAR, ITGAM, IL12A, IL23A, STAT4, and inflammatory cytokines (CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11), indicating a potential role in recruiting CD8+ T cells to inflammatory sites (figure 5D). To further characterize the cDC2-1 cluster, pathway analysis was performed against cDC2-2, revealing significant enrichment of pathways related to TNF-⍺ signaling via NF-κB, inflammatory response, EMT, and complement activation, consistent with an inf-cDC2-like profile (figure 5E).

After characterizing cDC1 and cDC2-1, their interactions with CD8 T-cell-1 and T-cell-2 clusters, containing Tpex-like cells, were investigated using CellChat. Since ICAM-1 plays a key role in DC-T-cell adhesion,43 the mechanism through which HMGA2 expression influences ICAM1-mediated interactions between these cell populations was investigated. ICAM-mediated interactions between cDC1 and CD8 T-cell-1+2 clusters were more pronounced in the HMGA2-high group (ICAM-1–ITGAL+ITGB2: 67.0%; ICAM-1–ITGAL: 33.0%; figure 5F and online supplemental figure S7B). Similarly, cDC2-1 exhibited strong ICAM-dependent interactions with these CD8 T cell clusters (ICAM-1–ITGAL+ITGB2: 50.9%; ICAM-1–ITGAL: 25.0%), with additional contributions from ICAM-2 (ICAM-2–ITGAL+ITGB2: 24.1%). In contrast, the HMGA2-low group displayed weaker ICAM-mediated interactions, with minimal connectivity between myeloid cells and CD8 T cells.

These results suggest that in the HMGA2-high state, cDC1 and inf-cDC2-like populations establish robust ICAM-1-dependent interactions with Tpex-like cells.

HMGA2-associated spatial immune landscape supports ICI combination therapy response

mIHC was performed on TMA samples from the KMU-03 cohort to examine the spatial relationship between HMGA2 expression, immune cell distribution, and response to ICI. Baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in online supplemental table S5. To evaluate the heterogeneous response to ICI within tumors, pathological responders were classified based on previously reported criteria for each TMA core: (1) immune activation with lymphoid infiltrates, (2) cell death with foamy macrophages, and (3) tissue repair with proliferative fibrosis on H&E staining.27 Tumors lacking these features were categorized as non-responders (figure 6A). HMGA2 IHC was performed on each TMA core to assess its relationship with ICI response, revealing that responders had significantly higher HMGA2 H-scores than non-responders (p=0.009; figure 6B).

Next, TMA cores were stratified into HMGA2-high and HMGA2-low groups based on the median H-score, and mIHC was performed to identify tumor cells (PanCK+), Tpex-like cells (CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+), Tex-like cells (CD8α+PD-1+TIM-3+), cDC1-like cells (CD11c+XCR1+), and ICAM-1+ cDC1-like cells (ICAM-1+CD11c+XCR1+) (figure 6C). A trend of higher cell densities (CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+, CD8α+PD-1+TIM-3+, and ICAM-1+CD11c+XCR1+ cells) was observed in the HMGA2-high group compared with the HMGA2-low group (figure 6D).

The relationship between CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+ cells, CD8α+PD-1+TIM-3+ cells, and HMGA2 expression was also evaluated, revealing higher infiltration densities of both cell types in the HMGA2-high group compared with the HMGA2-low group (p=0.008 and p=0.015, respectively; figure 6E). Further spatial cell–cell interactions between tumors classified by HMGA2 expression and CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+ cells revealed that these cells were more frequently located near tumor cells in the HMGA2-high group than in the HMGA2-low group (eg, distance=30 µm: 3.43×10³ vs 0.77×10³; figure 6F). Moreover, CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+ cells were significantly more abundant in responder cores than non-responder cores in the overall KMU-03 cohort (p=0.014) and HMGA2-high subgroup (p=0.036). In contrast, no significant difference in CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+ cell infiltration was observed in the HMGA2-low group (p=0.295; figure 6G).

Regarding cDC1-like cells, the proportions of CD11c+XCR1+ and ICAM-1+CD11c+XCR1+ cells were significantly higher in the HMGA2-high group than in the HMGA2-low group (p=0.034 and p=0.046, respectively; figure 6H), while no significant difference was observed for ICAM-1⁻CD11c+XCR1+ cells (p=0.182).

Finally, based on scRNA analysis, the spatial proximity and interaction between CD8α+PD-1+TCF7+ T cells and ICAM-1+CD11c+XCR1+ cells were investigated in the context of HMGA2 expression. A strong positive correlation was observed between the densities of these immune cells (figure 6I: r=0.94, p<0.001), indicating their tendency to increase together in the TME. Additionally, in the HMGA2-high group, these immune cells were in closer spatial proximity and at higher frequencies than in the HMGA2-low group, suggesting a potential spatial association in HMGA2-rich TMEs (eg, distance=30 µm: 9.40×10³ vs 5.44×10³; figure 6J).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated tumor characteristics according to HMGA2 expression in relation to tumor morphological evolution, TIME, and responses to ICI. Our findings highlight that HMGA2 expression correlates with high-malignancy tumor morphology, specific immune cell compositions, and differential responses to ICI.

HMGA2 expression progressively increases along the tumor’s morphological trajectory, localizing to high-malignancy patterns, such as solid sheets and rhabdoid patterns. Notably, H&E staining revealed spherical eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in HMGA2-high regions. This may be linked to EMT-associated cellular remodeling, as EMT induces mechanical changes that lead to mitotic rounding.37 Spatial transcriptomics and bulk RNA-seq data revealed enrichment of EMT and mitotic spindle pathways in HMGA2-high tumors, further supporting HMGA2’s role in tumor plasticity. This finding is consistent with those in other cancers, where spatially resolved profiling has provided valuable insights into disease evolution.44

TIME plays a critical role in shaping responses to ICI in ccRCC.21 45 46 Specifically, histological features like sarcomatoid and rhabdoid differentiation influence ICI efficacy by modulating the TIME.47 Meanwhile, our study analyzed TIME at the single-cell level in ccRCC, focusing on CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and its impact on ICI response. A correlation was observed between Tpex, HMGA2 expression, and ICI efficacy, suggesting that HMGA2 expression may be associated with tumor morphological changes and immune modulation through Tpex enrichment.

The importance of Tpex cells in ICI response has been recognized in esophageal cancer, where SPRY1+ Tpex cells predict a favorable outcome.48 Our findings extend the role of Tpex cells in ICI response to ccRCC, demonstrating how tumor-intrinsic factors may help shape the immune landscape and influence immunotherapy efficacy. Furthermore, these Tpex cells interact with dendritic cells through ICAM-1. A previous report showed that in patients with RCC enrolled in a phase 1 trial of atezolizumab, those with a high dendritic cell gene set had a higher survival rate than those with a low dendritic cell gene set.49 This report also suggests that inhibition of PD-L1 on dendritic cells releases PD-L1-B7.1 binding, thereby enhancing T-cell priming through B7.1/CD28 interactions. While we focused on ICAM-1-mediated interactions, multiple pathways may contribute to the complex mechanisms underlying dendritic cell function.

A strength of this study is the assessment of drug efficacy using pathological response. In clinical practice, responsive and non-responsive areas can exist within the same patient, reflecting intratumoral heterogeneity. To explore this relationship, we evaluated the pathological response and performed mIHC to analyze immune cell interactions. The significance of pathological response has been highlighted in lung cancer, where its depth correlates with improved event-free survival in neoadjuvant therapy.50 Here, we found that pathological response was associated with HMGA2 expression and Tpex cells, underscoring their role in shaping the TIME and influencing therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, the spatial resolution provided by mIHC revealed frequent proximity between Tpex cells and DCs in the HMGA2-high group, suggesting a potential mechanism by which immune cell dynamics contribute to differential treatment responses.

Our study has certain limitations. The retrospective design of this study and the relatively small cohorts treated with different types of TKI and ICI may impact the generalizability of the results. Additionally, while our findings provide valuable insights, further validation through experimental models, such as in vitro cell-based assays or in vivo mouse models, will strengthen the evidence and deepen our understanding.

In conclusion, this study suggests a role for HMGA2 in promoting tumor morphological evolution, shaping the immune microenvironment, and influencing ICI response in ccRCC. As HMGA2 expression increases, morphological evolution progresses, accompanied by higher Tpex, cDC1, and inf-cDC2 abundance, which may underlie better ICI responses. Further functional studies are needed to validate the mechanistic insights suggested by these findings.
