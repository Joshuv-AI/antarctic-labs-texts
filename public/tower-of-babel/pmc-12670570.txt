Unmasking heterogeneity in metabolic syndrome in a national sample of disaggregated Asian American subgroups
Authors: Lin Zhu, Wei Jenny Yang, Crystal Tse, Grace X Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOur previous research has shown that Asian Americans with a body mass index (BMI) above 23 have a higher risk of metabolic syndrome (MetS) than non-Hispanic whites of the same BMI. This exploratory study aimed to characterize patterns of metabolic syndrome heterogeneity across Asian American subgroups.MethodThis is a cross-sectional epidemiological study. Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, we computed age-adjusted, gender-specific prevalence for MetS and its individual components for three BMI categories among five major Asian American ethnic groups: Chinese (652), Filipino (262), Asian Indian (409), Vietnamese (243), and Korean (215).ResultsHigh MetS prevalence was observed in Asian Americans with a BMI above 23 (overweight or obesity). There were notable variations in MetS prevalence rates across Asian subgroups. Among the five Asian subgroups, excessive MetS risks were observed in Asian Indians, Filipinos, Vietnamese, and Koreans in one or more sex/BMI level categories. In particular, among men who had overweight (BMI 23–27.4), Asian Indians had statistically higher rates of MetS than their Vietnamese counterparts (50.80 % vs. 22.66 %). We also found elevated MetS risks in Asian Indian women who had overweight and Korean women who had obesity (BMI ≥27.5).ConclusionOur findings highlight an urgent need for further research on cardiometabolic risks and mechanisms driving these risks among disaggregated Asian ethnic groups. Greater understanding of these factors is essential for the development of culturally tailored intervention efforts to reduce MetS disparities among diverse Asian ethnic populations in the United States.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Body mass index, Asian American, Data disaggregation

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic dysregulations associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and all-cause mortality. Its components consist of abdominal obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia [1,2]. Several different diagnostic criteria have been proposed [3], including that of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [4], which defines MetS as central obesity plus two out of the other four conditions. The prevalence of various cardiometabolic risk factors varies markedly amongst different ethnic groups, which is in part a reflection of rapidly changing demographics and social/behavioral factors unique to each population.

In the United States, the Asian American population affords a valuable model to understand the roles of biological, sociocultural, and behavioral factors in cardiometabolic disease etiology. Previous research has found that Asian Americans have a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome compared to non-Hispanic Whites (NHWs) at lower BMI levels [5,6]. One possible explanation for this is that certain Asian subgroups have increased fat deposition in visceral tissue (e.g., abdominal adiposity) rather than peripheral tissue (e.g., fat accumulated around the hip and thigh region), which may result in metabolic complications at lower body weights [7]. Yet, the mechanism underlying higher rates of metabolic disturbances at lower BMI levels among Asian Americans remains unclear.

Previous studies are limited by aggregate reporting of MetS prevalence data. Prior research shows that reporting health risk factors for aggregated Asian American racial groups masks heterogeneity in the Asian American population [8,9]. This becomes especially important when evaluating the variation of individual MetS components, such as insulin resistance, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, among Asian American ethnic groups. For example, one study using electronic health data from Kaiser Permanente Northern California health plan members found a higher prevalence of diabetes, cardiovascular conditions, and obesity in Filipinos and South Asians than East Asian subgroups [10]. Other studies have reported that Vietnamese and Filipino men have a higher likelihood of being diagnosed with high blood pressure [11,12]. Research has also demonstrated that dyslipidemia subtypes differ among Asian American ethnic groups, with Filipino women having the highest prevalence of triglycerides (TG) and Asian Indian men having the highest prevalence of low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [13]. For Asian Indians, it appears that hyperglycemia is the primary driving force for MetS development to a greater extent than in other US ethnic groups [14,15]. Together, these studies indicate that specific MetS components are markedly different for Asian American subgroups.

Given the cultural and sociodemographic heterogeneity of the Asian American population [16], it is crucial to comprehensively examine different etiologic factors that contribute to increased MetS risk in specific Asian American subgroups. Such findings could help advance efforts to identify high risk groups and guide the development of culturally tailored strategies for improving MetS detection and prevention in Asian American subgroups.

The present study aimed to compare MetS across Asian American subgroups. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use a nationally representative sample of Asian Americans to compare MetS prevalence in the five largest Asian subgroups in the United States, including Chinese, Filipinos, Asian Indians, Vietnamese, and Koreans.

Methods

Study sample

This cross-sectional epidemiology study included data on non-Hispanic Asian (hereafter referred to as Asian) adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2016. NHANES is one of a series of health-related programs conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Center for Health Statistics. The objective of NHANES is to monitor trends in the prevalence of selected diseases and to study the relationship between diet, nutrition, and health [17]. NHANES uses a multistage, stratified design to produce a study sample that is representative of the noninstitutionalized civilian resident population in the 50 states and the District of Columbia [17]. The survey consists of two parts. First, survey questionnaires were administered to eligible participants at home, where person-level demographics, health, and nutrition information were collected. Then, participants were invited to visit specially equipped mobile examination centers for a standardized health examination. The survey procedures are detailed elsewhere [17].

The 2011–2016 NHANES oversampled Asians and several other subpopulations to increase the precision of estimates for these groups. To facilitate the oversampling of the Asian population, NHANES provided survey materials and a promotional video in traditional and simplified Mandarin, Korean, and Vietnamese [18]. This study included both public use data and restricted access data of NHANES. Specifically, the detailed Asian ethnicity variable was included in the restricted access data. The sample used in this study consisted of 652 Chinese Americans, 409 Asian Indians, 262 Filipino Americans, 243 Vietnamese Americans, 215 Korean Americans, and 6,318 NHWs.

Measures

Metabolic syndrome. To define MetS, we used the 2005 definition from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [4]. Under the IDF definition (Table 1), an individual is deemed to have MetS if they have central obesity (waist circumference ≥90 cm for South and East Asian men and ≥80 cm for South and East Asian women, with ethnicity specific values assumed if BMI is > 30 kg/m2), plus any two of the following four factors: 1) raised triglycerides (≥150 mg/dL) or specific treatment for this lipid abnormality; 2) reduced HDL-C (<40 mg/dL in males, <50 mg/dL in females) or specific treatment for this lipid abnormality; 3) raised blood pressure (blood pressure ≥130/85 mm Hg) or treatment of previously identified hypertension; and 4) raised fasting plasma glucose (≥100 mg/dL) or previously diagnosed T2DM.

Body mass index (BMI). BMI is a measure of body fat based on height and weight that applies to adult men and women. It is defined as body mass in kilograms (kg) divided by the square root of body height in meters (m), expressed in units of kg/m2. We used the Asian-specific cut-off points of BMI for overweight and obesity (23.0 and 27.5 kg/m2) recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) [19]. BMI was categorized as: <23 kg/m2 (underweight or normal weight), 23–27.4 kg/m2 (overweight), and ≥27.5 kg/m2 (obesity). Importantly, we applied these Asian-specific BMI cutoffs to both Asian American subgroups and the NHW comparison group. This methodological approach allows for direct comparison of metabolic syndrome prevalence at identical BMI levels across ethnic groups. While standard WHO cutoffs for NHW define overweight as BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and obesity as ≥30 kg/m2, using different cutoffs would preclude direct comparison at the same body weight categories.

Demographic characteristics. We conducted the analysis separately by sex (man or woman). We also accounted for age (in years), marital status (currently married or not), education (high school or below; college or some college; or graduate degree), and poverty level (ratio of annual family income to the federal poverty line).

Modifiable lifestyle behaviors. To quantify the energy expenditure of physical activity, we calculated metabolic equivalent task (MET) scores by multiplying the frequency, duration, and NHANES-recommended MET values (8.0 for vigorous-intensity and 4.0 for moderate-intensity) [20]. We then developed four categories based on the cut-off points from the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans [21]: sedentary (those reporting no regular physical activities), insufficient (those performing 1–499 MET-minutes of activities per week), moderate (those performing 500–1,000 MET-minutes of activities per week), and high (those performing greater than 1,000 MET-minutes of activities per week) [22]. Tobacco use was measured in two categories: whether an individual currently uses any tobacco products or not. In addition, we measured the amount and frequency of alcohol use in four categories [23]. Lifetime abstainers reported having consumed fewer than 12 drinks in their lifetime. Former drinkers reported drinking more than 12 drinks in their lifetime but none in the past year. Non-excessive current drinkers reported 14 or fewer drinks per week on average for men or 7 or fewer drinks per week on average for women. Excessive current drinkers reported more than 14 drinks per week on average for men or more than 7 drinks per week on average for women, or 5 or more drinks in a single day at least once in the past year for either sex.

Statistical analysis

We applied the appropriate sample weights according to the National Center for Health Statistics guidelines to account for the complex survey design and the oversampling of Asian Americans. Data on fasting glucose, which was collected on a subsample of the population, was also taken into account [24]. We used the svy command in Stata to apply the weights. A p-value of .05 or below was considered statistically significant. We computed age-adjusted, sex-specific MetS prevalence and each MetS component separately for each of the three BMI levels. Specifically, we used the age distribution in the 2010 US Census to standardize the prevalence and means of MetS and its five components. All age-adjusted estimated prevalence rates were presented as a percentage with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). We computed the weighted prevalence and 95 % CIs of MetS for the Asian American subgroups and NHWs separately at different levels of BMI and separately for men and women. We also presented the weighted prevalence and 95 % CIs for each component of MetS for the Asian American subgroups and NHWs separately at different levels of BMI and separately for men and women. All data analyses were conducted in Stata 16 [25].

Results

Some significant subgroup and gender differences in MetS prevalence were observed at each BMI level. First, among those with a BMI below 23, the MetS prevalence rates ranged from 3.89 % in the Korean subgroup to 8.58 % in the Asian Indian subgroup. Interestingly, even the highest rate within Asians (represented by the Asian Indian subgroup) were lower than rates among NHWs, though the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The MetS rates were much higher in women, ranging from 13.77 % in the Korean subgroup to 31.70 % in the Filipino subgroup to 33.44 % in the Asian Indian subgroup. It was noteworthy that the Filipino subgroup (31.70 %, 95 % CI: 19.92 %, 43.47 %) had significantly higher rates of MetS than did the Chinese subgroup (14.45 %, 95 % CI: 19.08 %, 18.82 %). Although the Filipino and Asian Indian subgroups had higher MetS prevalence rates than NHWs, the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Significant subgroup differences in MetS prevalence were observed among those with a BMI between 23 and 27.4. Among men, the MetS prevalence in the Asian Indian (50.80 %, 95 % CI: 42.64 %, 58.96 %) subgroup was significantly higher than that in the Vietnamese subgroup (22.66 %, 95 % CI: 13.60, 31.71) and the NHW group (42/72 %, 95 % CI: 38.07 %, 46.38 %). Similarly, among women at the same BMI level (23–27.4), the MetS prevalence in the Asian Indian subgroup (58.26 %, 95 % CI: 48.92 %, 69.61 %) was significantly higher than that in NHWs (34.44 %, 95 % CI: 30.01 %, 38.87 %).

Among those with a BMI level of 27.5 or above, MetS prevalence rates were generally high (60 % or above), regardless of ethnicity. Among men, all Asian subgroups except the Chinese subgroup had higher MetS rates than did NHWs, by at least 5 % points, although the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Among women, the Korean subgroup observed the highest rate of MetS (89.63 %, 95 % CI: 72.92 %–106.35 %), which was significantly higher than that in NHWs (62.95 %, 95 % CI: 59.83 %, 66.07 %). Additionally, Filipino and Vietnamese subgroups also had MetS rates that were notably higher, albeit statistically non-significant compared to NHWs.

Regarding central obesity rates, we found that prevalence was similar between Asian Indian men and NHW men at all three BMI levels; rates for both groups were notably higher than the rates of other Asian ethnic subgroups. Among men with a BMI below 23, Chinese (4.52 %, 95 % CI: 0.64 %, 9.67 %), Filipino (5.48, 95 % CI: 3.34 %, 14.29 %), Vietnamese (8.07 %, 95 % CI: 0.99 %, 17.12 %), and Korean American men (3.89 %, 95 % CI: 2.00 %, 9.78 %) had significantly lower rates of central obesity than NHW men (17.15 %, 95 % CI: 13.37 %, 20.94 %). Among men whose BMI was between 23 and 27.4, Asian Indians (68.15 %, 95 % CI: 59.51 %, 76.79 %) showed significantly higher rates of central obesity than Filipinos (40.59 %, 95 % CI: 30.42 %, 50.76 %), Vietnamese (31.37 %, 95 % CI: 21.79 %, 40.94 %), and Korean American men (58.60 %, 95 % CI: 45.85 %–71.34 %). For all five subgroups, among those with a BMI of 27.5 or higher, central obesity rates were close to 100 %. Similar ethnic disparities in central obesity rates were observed in women, although the rates were notably higher in women than in men in the same ethnic group and same BMI level. Among women with a BMI below 23, Asian Indians (55.61 %, 95 % CI: 43.21 %, 68.00 %) had significantly higher rates of central obesity than their Chinese counterparts (30.54 %, 95 % CI: 24.09 %, 36.98 %). Among women with a BMI between 23 and 27.4, Asian Indian (95.22 %, 95 % CI: 90.14 %, 100.30 %) and Korean women (96.57 %, 95 % CI: 89.91 %, 103.24 %) had much higher rates of central obesity than Vietnamese women (71.48 %, 95 % CI: 61.70 %, 81.25 %). Similar to men, women with a BMI of 27.5 or higher had a 100 % central obesity rate, regardless of race/ethnicity.

Regarding the prevalence of raised triglycerides, we found that among men with a BMI between 23 and 27.4, Asian Indians (62.08 %, 95 % CI: 52.45 %, 68.81 %) and Koreans (64.98 %, 95 % CI: 52.30 %, 77.67 %) had significantly higher rates of raised triglyceride levels than NHW men (44.30 %, 95 % CI: 41.14 %, 47.46 %). Among men with a BMI of 27.5 or higher, the Vietnamese (82.75 %, 95 % CI: 66.32 %, 99.8 %) and Filipino (80.40 %, 95 % CI: 65.77 %, 95.02 %) subgroups had significantly higher rates of raised triglyceride levels than NHW men (62.50 %, 95 % CI: 59.50 %, 65.51 %). Additionally, Filipino men with a BMI below 23 (50.12 %, 95 % CI: 21.40 %, 78.84 %) had notably higher rates of raised triglycerides than other Asian subgroups, but the differences were not statistically significant. Among women with a BMI between 23 and 27.4, Asian Indian (55.64 %, 95 % CI: 44.32 %, 66.95 %) and Korean women (57.61 %, 95 % CI: 39.51 %, 75.71 %) had significantly higher rates of raised triglycerides than NHW women (31.67 %, 95 % CI: 27.65 %%, 35.69 %). Among women with a BMI of 27.5 or higher, Korean women (85.40 %, 95 % CI: 65.50 %, 105.28 %) had a significantly higher rate of raised triglycerides than their NHW counterparts (52.65 %, 95 % CI: 49.55 %, 55.76 %).

Among men with a BMI between 23 and 27.5, Asian Indians (47.11 %, 95 % CI: 36.24 %, 57.98 %) had significantly higher rates of reduced HDL cholesterol than Vietnamese men (37.44 %, 95 % CI: 26.46 %, 34.38 %). While Filipino men had the highest rates of reduced HDL cholesterol among all six subsamples by at least 10 % points, the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Among women with a BMI below 23, we found that Asian Indians (47.38 %, 95 % CI: 31.36 %, 63.40 %) had a significantly higher rate of reduced HDL cholesterol than Chinese (24.73 %, 95 % CI: 18.31 %, 31.16 %) and Korean (18.81 %, 95 % CI: 6.97 %, 30.66 %) women, as well as NHWs (20.68 %, 95 % CI: 16.80 %, 24.56 %). It is noteworthy that among women with a BMI of 27.5 or higher, Korean women (74.31 %, 95 % CI: 54.86 %, 93.75 %) had a higher rate of reduced HDL cholesterol than NHW women (53.26 %, 50.22 %, 56.31 %), although the difference was not statistically significant.

While we did not detect any significant differences in raised blood pressure by ethnic subgroups at any BMI level, we do note that for women with a BMI between 23 and 27.4, all five Asian ethnic subgroups had higher rates of raised blood pressure, ranging from 32.81 % to 39.26 %, than NHW women (29.24 %). Similar elevated rates in Asian subgroups were observed among men at the same BMI level (23–27.4), except for Asian Indians.

For men, all five Asian ethnic subgroups at all three BMI levels had notably higher rates of raised fasting plasma glucose levels than NHW men. In particular, Filipino men with a BMI between 23 and 27.4 (70.82 %, 95 % CI: 55.96 %, 85.27 %) had a significantly higher rate of raised fasting plasma glucose than NHW men at the same BMI level (49.51 %, 95 % CI: 44.20 %, 54.82 %). Similar patterns of elevated risks of raised fasting plasma glucose for Asian ethnic subgroups were also observed among women, with the exception of Asian Indian women with a BMI of 27.5 or higher, who had rates similar to those of NHW women. It was particularly noteworthy that Vietnamese women with a BMI between 23 and 27.4 (53.89 %, 95 % CI: 34.10 %, 73.68 %) had a significantly higher rate of elevated fasting plasma glucose levels than NHW women at the same BMI level (33.77 %, 95 % CI: 29.25 %, 38.29 %).

Discussion

Using a nationally representative sample of Asian American subgroups, we found significant heterogeneity in MetS prevalence across Asian subgroups. Specifically, our findings revealed that Asian Indian and Filipino Americans had a greater prevalence of MetS than other Asian ethnic subgroups examined in this study, especially among women. Specifically, Asian Indian men with a BMI between 23 and 27.4 (overweight) had significantly higher MetS rates than their Vietnamese counterparts. Asian Indian women with a BMI below 27.5 (normal or overweight) also had the highest MetS rates among all subgroups examined in this study. Filipino American men had elevated rates of abnormalities in triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose, while Filipino women showed significantly higher risks for MetS even when their BMI level was normal (<23). Previous studies have shown that at any given body mass index or waist circumference, these two subgroups experience higher cardiovascular disease burdens and greater metabolic abnormalities (e.g., insulin resistance, lower HDL, and type 2 diabetes) than other Asian ethnic subgroups and NHW groups [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. Higher total body fat percentage [[32], [33], [34], [35]] and larger abdominal subcutaneous and visceral adipocyte size [34,[36], [37], [38]], both observed in South Asian and Filipino populations, have been linked to cardiometabolic risks and elevated heart disease burden in the two groups. In addition, tobacco use, physical inactivity, and dietary patterns, such as high fat, carbohydrate, and sugar intake, as well as psychosocial stress related to migration, were also cited as important contributors to the development of cardiometabolic abnormalities among South Asian and Filipino populations [27,[39], [40], [41], [42], [43]]. While the promotion of weight loss, tobacco cessation, increased physical activity, and an anti-atherogenic diet is clearly warranted for the prevention and treatment of cardiometabolic conditions, clinical and public health measures need to take into account social and cultural factors that are unique to these two groups. Adopting culturally tailored measures and implementation strategies is key to achieving efficacy in promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors and utilization of appropriate healthcare services. Future research should also examine how culturally specific dietary patterns and circadian health behaviors (e.g., shift work, meal timing, sleep patterns) may contribute to ethnic differences in adipose tissue distribution and metabolic risk. Migration-related disruptions to traditional eating patterns and sleep-wake cycles may be particularly relevant for understanding subcutaneous and visceral fat accumulation in Asian American subgroups.

In addition, this study reviewed unique patterns of excess risks for MetS and several MetS biomarker components in Vietnamese and Korean Americans. For these two subgroups, risks for cardiometabolic abnormalities and chronic diseases are poorly understood. Because of their generally lower BMI levels, Vietnamese and Korean Americans have been largely overlooked in cardiometabolic health research [43], which has resulted in a lack of sufficient baseline epidemiological information on the burden of cardiometabolic conditions in these two populations. Our findings shed light on the unique patterns of excess risk of cardiometabolic abnormalities in the two groups. Specifically, we found that Vietnamese men with a BMI of 27.5 or above (obesity) had the highest rate of raised triglycerides—significantly higher than the rate in NHW men in the same BMI category. Hyperlipidemia also has been poorly studied in the Vietnamese American population. One study using the National Health Interview Survey found that Southeast Asians (including Vietnamese) had higher hyperlipidemia rates than NHW and other Asian subgroups [44], while another study found that the hypercholesterolemia rate in Vietnamese Americans was similar to that in NHW adults [45]. In addition, we found that Vietnamese women with a BMI of 23 or above (overweight or obesity) had the highest rate of raised fasting plasma glucose levels among all Asian subgroups and NHWs, consistent with the findings from two previous studies in California [46,47]. It was particularly noteworthy that Vietnamese American women of a lower BMI level had higher or at least comparable rates of raised fasting plasma glucose than NHW women of a higher BMI level. For example, 36.12 % of Vietnamese Americans who were normal or underweight (BMI <23) had raised fasting plasma glucose, whereas only 33.77 % of NHW women who had overweight (BMI between 23 and 27.4) had the same condition. This, combined with the lower rates of obesity found in this study and in previous research [48,49], points to an excess of diabetes risk for Vietnamese women, which warrants future research to examine potential genetic and behavioral mechanisms.

We also found elevated risks of MetS and dyslipidemia in Korean Americans, especially Korean women with a BMI of 23 or above, who had significantly higher risks for raised triglycerides than their NHW counterparts. A similar racial/ethnic pattern in triglyceride levels was observed for Korean men with a BMI between 23 and 27.4. Such excessive risks for MetS conditions for Korean Americans were not previously well examined. One systematic review of cardiovascular disease in Korean Americans reported that the prevalence of an abnormal total cholesterol level ranged from 33.5 % to 52.7 % in Korean Americans. Yet, Korean American men are less likely than NHWs or African Americans to control or lose weight to lower their high cholesterol [50]. Together, these findings highlight the need for lifestyle behavioral interventions in both community and clinical settings, to promote awareness and lifestyle modifications in Korean American communities.

Our findings underscore the critical importance of waist circumference measurement in both clinical practice and public health surveillance, particularly for Asian American populations. Despite being a key component of metabolic syndrome diagnosis, waist circumference is frequently overlooked in routine physical examinations and public health awareness campaigns. Given that certain Asian American subgroups such as Filipinos and Asian Indians demonstrated elevated central obesity rates at lower BMI levels, routine waist circumference measurement should be standard practice in primary care settings serving these communities. Additionally, enhanced public health surveillance systems that capture waist circumference data for disaggregated Asian American subgroups are needed to monitor cardiometabolic risk trends and inform targeted prevention efforts. Community-based education programs in Asian American communities should also raise awareness that a "normal" BMI does not preclude metabolic risk, particularly regarding central adiposity.

Limitation

These results should be interpreted in light of several limitations. This is a cross-sectional study, which limited our ability to draw causal inferences from our findings. Longitudinal data is needed to determine how socio-behavioral factors influence the metabolic outcomes of Asian Americans in comparison to their NHW counterparts. Additionally, while NHANES provides a large sample size for Asian Americans, it is likely that undocumented immigrants, those with low socioeconomic status, and those with limited English proficiency were underrepresented in our sample [51]. These medically underserved populations face multilevel barriers to preventive healthcare services and warrant the attention of public health researchers and healthcare providers. Furthermore, only the five largest Asian ethnic subgroups were included in this study; other subgroups, such as Cambodian, Hmong, Bangladeshi, Laotian, and Malaysian populations, were not examined because of the small number of participants who self-identified as these ethnicities in NHANES. However, these groups are particularly understudied, and their cardiovascular burden and healthcare service needs are not well understood. With growing evidence of cardiovascular disease and mortality disparities across Asian American subgroups, it is ever more urgent that research focus on disaggregated Asian American data to fully appreciate the magnitude and nature of health disparities in the diverse Asian American populations [52,53]. Combining data from multiple time points of population-based surveys such as NHANES would allow researchers to highlight the unique health needs of specific subgroups and to uncover the underlying causes of health disparities in chronic diseases.

Some confidence intervals for prevalence estimates exceed 100 %, reflecting small sample sizes in certain subgroups and the statistical uncertainty inherent in age-adjusted estimates. These findings should be interpreted with particular caution, and they underscore the need for larger studies with adequate representation of diverse Asian American subgroups.

As with any study involving multiple comparisons across ethnic groups and metabolic outcomes, there is potential for Type I errors. We did not apply multiple comparison corrections because this study is descriptive and exploratory, aimed at characterizing heterogeneity rather than testing predetermined hypotheses. Readers should interpret findings as hypothesis-generating, with confirmation in independent cohorts warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, we found significant heterogeneity of MetS risk across Asian subgroups and a higher predicted probability of MetS among normal/underweight Asian women compared to Asian men of the same weight category. These findings have important real-world implications. In particular, identifying Asian American subgroups at highest risk of developing MetS can help advance efforts to develop targeted strategies for improving MetS prevention in Asian American communities.
