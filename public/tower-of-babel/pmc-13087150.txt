New large pterosaur tracks from Korea and their implications on terrestrial behavior
Authors: Jongyun Jung, Kyung Soo Kim, Lida Xing, Byung-Do Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPterosaurs were important components of Mesozoic ecosystems, occupying diverse ecological niches from the Late Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous. Among them, neoazhdarchians have been hypothesized as terrestrial carnivorous based on anatomical and functional evidence, yet direct indications of predation on land have remained elusive. Here we report Jinjuichnus procerus ichnogen. et ichnosp. nov., a new pterosaur track discovered in the Jinju Formation of South Korea. The track, characterized by a compact and long metatarsus in its pedal configuration, is consistent with neoazhdarchian pterosaurs. The trackway is preserved in close association with a small tetrapod trackway that exhibits abrupt changes in direction and increased stride length. This association provides the potential ichnological evidence of terrestrial vertebrate interaction by a pterosaur. Nonetheless, an alternative interpretation of the two trackways remains possible, making it difficult to confirm any direct interaction between the trackmakers. The paired trackways offer insight into the factors to consider when evaluating potential interactions with the trackmaker. While scenarios such as predation remain ambiguous, they nonetheless highlight the interpretive complexity inherent in assessing behavioral associations preserved in trackway assemblages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48019-y.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Zoology

Introduction

Pterosaurs were key components of Mesozoic ecosystems, thriving from the Late Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous1–3. They occupied a wide range of ecological niches and exhibited diverse dietary adaptations4,5. In particular, Neoazhdarchia, which includes thalassodromids, chaoyangopterids, and azhdarchids, has been inferred to represent predominantly terrestrial carnivorous hunters, based on anatomical features, functional morphology, and other associated fossil records4,6–8. These interpretations have suggested that some groups may have employed hunting strategies comparable to extant terrestrial stalkers, such as storks or cranes6,7.

Nevertheless, the fossil record has so far lacked direct evidence of terrestrial predation in pterosaurs. Although the diets of many pterosaurs have been inferred from gut contents (e.g.,9–12), emetolite (e.g.,13), and coprolite (e.g.,14,15), there are no content fossils directly associated with terrestrial vertebrates. Therefore, alternative lines of evidence are required to evaluate these hypotheses.

In this study, we describe Jinjuichnus procerus ichnogen. et ichnosp. nov., a large pterosaur track from the Lower Cretaceous Jinju Formation of South Korea. The morphology of this track, together with its preservation in close association with a small vertebrate trackway, provides rare ichnological insight into the terrestrial ecology of neoazhdarchian pterosaurs. We evaluate whether the various features preserved in these trackways provide evidence for potential interaction between the two trackmakers.

Geological setting

The Jinju Innovation City, where the specimen was discovered, belongs to the Gyeongsang Basin (Fig. 1a). The Gyeongsang Basin, located in the southeastern Korean Peninsula, represents the most extensive Cretaceous non-marine basin16. This basin is further categorized into the Milyang, Uiseong, and Yeongyang subbasins17. In ascending order, the Milyang Subbasin is divided into the Sindong, Hayang, and Yucheon groups17 (Fig. 1b). The fossil tracksite in this study is located at the Jinju Formation, at the uppermost part of the Sindong Group, and this formation is approximately 1000 to 1800 m thick. The Jinju Formation consists of gray to black shale with packets of sandstone and is interpreted to have been formed in a fluvial-lacustrine environment under a balanced-fill type basin17,18. The age dating results from the Jinju Formation indicate that it ranges from the latest Aptian to middle Albian19. The U-Pb zircon age dating of the fossil tracksite yields an age of 106.5 Ma, corresponding to the middle Albian20.

A wide range of vertebrate ichnofossils, including dinosaurs, birds, and pterosaurs, have been reported from the Gyeongsang Basin21. Pterosaur tracks have been documented from the Hasandong, Jinju, Haman, and Jindong formations22. Among them, the Jinju Formation has been a prolific source of various vertebrate ichnofossils, leading to its consideration as a Konservat-Lagerstätte. Among the significant discoveries, the Jinju Innovation City tracksite yielded rich vertebrate ichnofossils, including dinosaur tracks such as Corpulentapus, Asianopodus, Grallator, Minisauripus, and Dromaeosauriformipes rarus23–25. Additionally, crocodilian tracks Crocodylopodus26, lepidosaurian tracks Neosauroides innovates27, and anuran tracks Ranipes28 have been unearthed in this tracksite. Furthermore, the Excavation Projects in the Jinju Innovation City have revealed an impressive assemblage of approximately 2500 pterosaur tracks, including the presence of the diminutive pterosaur tracks Pteraichnus gracilis29.

Diagnosis. Jinjuichnus procerus is differentiated by the following combination of characters: large and asymmetrical tridactyl digitigrade manus with claw marks (length = 185 to 227 mm) (Table S1); high length-to-width ratio of manus (manus length/manus width = 3.9 to 4.6); anteriorly oriented and blunt-shaped digit Ⅰ (30 to 53 mm); laterally or posterolaterally oriented and short digit Ⅱ (21 to 45 mm); posteriorly oriented with elongated, slender and outwardly curved manus digit Ⅲ (136 to 154 mm); the distal part of digit Ⅲ is slightly outward curved; extremely wide divarication between digits Ⅰ and Ⅲ (170 to 184 º); triangular symmetrical tetradactyl plantigrade pes; triangular form with narrow V-shape pes heel; the ratio between manus digits is about 1:0.9:3.8 (DI: DII: DIII); elongated and slender pes (pes length/pes width = 2.5 to 2.9); prominent pedal claw marks; relatively long and compact metatarsus and short spreading digits (digit length/metatarsal length = 0.52 to 0.70); similar length of the digits.

Description

Jinjuichnus procerus, preserved as a negative epirelief on a medium-grained sandstone slab, comprises four successive manus-pes couplets (Fig. 2). Four manus and three pes track impressions are well-preserved, but the single pes impression (Rp2) shows partial preservation, with the anterolateral part broken. The sharply defined track margins indicate that these are fidelitous tracks rather than undertracks.

The manus impressions are asymmetric, tridactyl, and fully digitigrade (Fig. 2d–g). The deepest impressions occur at the metacarpophalangeal joint, gradually becoming shallower distally. Digit I is directed anteriorly, blunt, and lacks curvature, though occasional claw marks may be present (e.g., Rm1). Digit II is similar in length to or shorter than Digit I and is oriented laterally to posterolaterally. Digit III is directed posteriorly and is extremely elongated and slightly curved outward. Its depth diminishes distally, and in some instances, the distal portion resembles a drag mark rather than an anatomical impression, as abrupt changes in depth and width may mark the boundary between the accurate digit trace and the dragged portion. The manus length ranges from 185 to 227 mm (mean = 208 mm), and widths range from 40 to 57 mm (mean = 50 mm). Divarication angles between digits I and II range from 81° to 126° (mean = 108°), and between digits II and III from 44° to 103° (mean = 70°).

The pes impressions are tetradactyl and plantigrade (Fig. 2d–g), oriented slightly outward from the midline. Pes lengths range from 100 to 123 mm (mean = 114 mm), and widths from 41 to 48 mm (mean = 45 mm). The metatarsophalangeal region is more deeply impressed than the digit tips and heel. Digit I angles slightly inward, while digit IV is less distinct than the others. Digits I and IV are consistently shorter than digits II and III. The pedal digit region (approximated by the length of digit III) ranges from 42 to 49 mm (mean = 45.5 mm), while the metatarsal region (approximated by metatarsal III) ranges from 70 to 81 mm (mean = 75.5 mm). In Rp1, an anteriorly extended impression may represent a dragged trace. The relatively short digits and elongated metatarsus result in a digit-to-metatarsus (D/Mt) ratio of 0.51 to 0.70.

Description of the small vertebrate trackway

The trackway of a small vertebrate is preserved on the same surface as negative epirelief, where the pterosaur trackway is preserved (Fig. 2h, i). The total number of manus-pes couplets is about 24, but it is hard to determine the number of individual manus and pes prints because the outline of the tracks is blunt, and many tracks merge the manus and pes (Fig. 2i). Based on the non-merged manus and pes tracks, the manus is approximately 12 mm and the pes is approximately 27 mm long. The pes has an elongated shape, rotated outward with respect to the midline. The manus has an oval shape, positioned parallel to the midline. However, the detailed outline of the tracks is not visible, including the digit shape or number.

The total length of the trackway is approximately 1,130 mm. It continues for about 350 mm before making a 25º turn to the left, where only a faint tail trace appears for roughly 420 mm. It then extends again for about 360 mm. The stride and pace of the footprints tend to change depending on the direction of the trackway. VT01 to VT11 maintain a consistent stride length of around 55 to 60 mm. After the transitional area, where the direction and preservation conditions change, the terrain becomes unstable and exhibits a wide range thereafter.

Probable trackmakers of quadrupedal vertebrate taxa that exhibit small size, large pes relative to manus, and tail trace in the small vertebrate trackway include salamanders, lizards, and crocodilians30–32. Among these, the shape of the blunt footprints and the distinct tail trace are more similar to tracks of salamanders32. Although no Cretaceous salamander bodies and trace fossils have been reported from Korea, large numbers of salamanders have been reported from the Early Cretaceous of China (e.g.,33–36). In general, salamander footprints are distinguished from those of lizards by shorter digits, a blunt footprint outline, and the number of manus digits (quadradactyl in salamanders and pentadactyl in lizards). The small vertebrate trackway in this study shows a similar morphology to that of a neoichnological experiment with newts under certain substrate conditions, such as moisture, grain size, and slope angle37,38. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that they were made by a lizard, which has prominent digits, as these digit prints may not have been left under some substrate conditions39.

Comparison with other pterosaur ichnotaxa

To date, ichnofamilies widely accepted as pterosaur ichnofossils are Pteraichnidae40 and Agadirichnidae41. Pteraichnidae was first proposed by Lockley et al.40 and includes Pteraichnus42, which contains seventeen ichnospecies, and Purbeckopus43, which contains a single ichnospecies. The original diagnosis of Pteraichnidae is “wide quadrupedal trackway; elongated, symmetrical, tetradactyl, plantigrade pes impression; asymmetric tridactyl manus”, and it is not amended, and this diagnosis is quite similar to the diagnosis of Pteraichnus40,42,44. This issue arose as new specimens with greater morphological diversity than those proposed and revised in the early studies, causing most of the newly reported pterosaur footprint fossils to be classified as Pteraichnus. Based on this study of the morphological diversity of pterosaur footprint fossils45,46, it is necessary to emend the diagnosis of Pteraichnidae and Pteraichnus further, but this was not addressed in this study as it falls outside the scope of this study.

Among them, the ichnofamily Agadirichnidae41 includes Haenamichnus47 and Agadirichnus41,48, each with a single ichnospecies, H. uhangriensis47 and A. elegans41,48, respectively. The diagnosis of Agadirichnidae is as follows: large-sized tracks (77–340 mm) with elongated, slender pes prints and massive manus prints41. The size and morphology of J. procerus falls within this range, comparable to Haenamichnus ichnosp.47 and Agadirichnus elegans41. The pes is about 114 mm long, with a slender, elongated form. Also, its large manus (~ 200 mm) is consistent with features diagnostic of Agadirichnidae.

Nevertheless, J. procerus can be differentiated from both Agadirichnus and Haenamichnus by several unique features. Agadirichnus exhibits a rounded heel and subparallel edges in the pes41, whereas J. procerus presents a pointed, subtriangular heel morphology. The manus digits of J. procerus are more slender with extremely elongated digit III, contrasting with the more robust digits of Agadirichnus. However, the elongated appearance of digit III in J. procerus is unlikely to represent its actual anatomical manus anatomy but instead appears to include drag traces produced by dynamic limb movement. This interpretation is supported by (1) the absence of such extremely elongated digit III morphology in the corresponding body fossil record, (2) substantial variation in the length of digit III impressions along the trackway, and (3) abrupt changes in both depth and width along the digit III impressions. In Haenamichnus, the pes displays undifferentiated digits with only pad impressions47, while in J. procerus, the digits are pointed and distinct. Additionally, although manus morphology is broader than that observed in J. procerus, which exhibits a more gracile digit configuration, these morphological distinctions differentiate J. procerus from other known Agadirichnidae ichnotaxa.

Discussion

Possible trackmaker

Deciphering the identity of vertebrate trackmakers based on trace fossils remains inherently challenging. Nevertheless, numerous studies have sought to associate footprints with plausible producers. Over the past three decades, research on pterosaur ichnology has increased substantially, with many investigations attempting to infer trackmaker identity. Commonly employed criteria for such inferences include the co-occurrence of body fossil records in the same stratigraphic units or localities49–55, the absolute and relative size of the footprints47,49,51,56, and their morphometric characteristics47,49,52,54,56,57. These parameters have been widely used to constrain the taxonomic identity of pterosaur trackmakers. Smyth et al.45 for example, linked three distinct pterosaur footprint morphotypes to specific pterodactyloid clades by identifying diagnostic features preserved in the pedal anatomy. The inferred associations, ctenochasmatoids, dsungaripterids, and neoazhdarchids, are congruent with broader macroevolutionary and paleoecological hypotheses derived from the body fossil records58.

Based on the distribution of body fossils, footprint size, and morphometric characteristics, J. procerus is most plausibly attributed to a neoazhdarchid trackmaker (Fig. 3). The narrow, elongated pes and prominent claw marks are consistent with a neoazhdarchian type45. In addition, J. procerus exhibits relatively similar digit length, which distinguishes it from the Dsungaripterid type, in which digit I is notably shorter45. Although the relatively long metatarsals resemble those of the Ctenochasmatoid type, the compact arrangement of the metatarsals in J. procerus differs from the splayed metatarsals characteristic of Ctenochasmatoid45. Furthermore, Ctenochasmatoid-type tracks typically show manus imprints that are proportionally smaller than the pes, a condition inconsistent with J. procerus45. Neoazhdarchians are characterized by relatively short, compact feet in proportion to their body size, with elongate and slender metatarsals arranged in a parallel configuration and accompanied by abbreviated pedal digits that are interpreted as indicative of terrestrial adaptation6,59–62. In most neoazhdarchians (e.g., Shenzhoupterus, Chaoyangopterus, Jidapterus, Meilifeilong), the metatarsals are longer than the digits and maintain a parallel orientation (Fig. 3g, S2), whereas Zhejiangopterus exhibits slightly splayed metatarsals from proximal to distal ends63,64 (Fig. 3a). In addition, chaoyangopterids possess reduced and gently curved pedal unguals, further supporting interpretations of terrestrial locomotor adaptations62.

One of the most frequently cited ichnological records potentially attributed to neoazhdarchians is Haenamichnus, interpreted as the footprint of giant azhdarchids47. This ichnotaxon exhibits a slender and compact pedal morphology, subequal digit lengths, and a parasagittal gait, all traits congruent with the neoazhdarchian bauplan6,47,61. J. procerus similarly displays elongate metatarsals with reduced digits, a compact pedal outline, and a slender manus, even accounting for potential drag traces associated with digit III. The digit-to-metatarsus ratios observed in neoazhdarchian include 0.66 (Zhejiangopterus), 0.79 (Jidapterus), 0.80 (Meilifeilong sanyainus), and 0.87 (Meilifeilong youhao), all of which fall within the range observed for J. procerus (0.52 to 0.70).

According to Smyth et al.58, pes prints attributed to neoazhdarchians are typified by a narrow and elongated morphology, faint digit impressions, and a deep, sub-circular heel impression. In the case of J. procerus, the pes exhibits a relatively narrow heel impression that, while not prominently deep or rounded, is comparable in general morphology to putative neoazhdarchian tracks reported from Haenam and Daegu formations58. Therefore, neoazhdarchian pterosaurs are the most probable trackmakers of J. procerus.

Correlation between pterosaur and small vertebrate trackways

Identifying intraspecific interactions from ichnofossil evidence remains a significant challenge in paleontology. Nonetheless, several studies have applied various methods to infer behavioral interactions between trackmakers, particularly in the context of predation scenarios65–67. Hunt and Lucas68 proposed six predictive models under which ichnological evidence may support interpretations of behavioral interaction each other. However, they emphasized the need for careful application of Occam’s razor, as trackways fulfilling multiple criteria may still require alternative, more parsimonious explanations. Although the short length of pterosaur trackways described herein limits precise behavioral reconstructions, we explore the possibility of a predatory interaction based on track morphology and spatial association.

The surface preserving the tracks reveals at least four identifiable elements, including the basal substrate, pterosaur tracks, tetrapod tracks, and microbially induced sedimentary structures (MISS) (Fig. 2h, I). MISS are known to form through the interaction of microbial mats with physical sedimentary dynamics69. These structures are also considered important contributors to the exceptional preservation of vertebrate ichnofossils, as they form a thin film that helps retain fine morphological details of the tracks (e.g.,70–72). On the specimen surface, wrinkled-shaped MISS are pervasively developed but show clear evidence of disruption caused by the overlying pterosaur and tetrapod tracks, both of which show a comparable degree of preservation quality.

In terms of footprint depth, both trackways exhibit a consistent pattern: the imprints are relatively shallow at the beginning of the trackways (Fig. 2f, g, i), whereas those near the end of the trackway are noticeably deeper (Fig. 2d, e, h). This asymmetry suggests a subtle spatial difference in substrate consistency at the time of track formation. The fact that both trackways display this same depth gradient further implies that they were produced within a relatively short temporal window during which the substrate conditions remained relatively stable. It might be possible that both the pterosaur and small vertebrate trackways remained exposed for a considerable interval. However, environments in which MISS form typically reflect the interaction of microbial activity with hydrodynamic, physical, and sedimentary processes, producing a wide suite of structures73,74. Such structures vary according to local conditions and, importantly, additional features such as shrinkage cracks, mat chips, and mat curls are known to develop sequentially over time even in modern settings (e.g.,75–77). The persistence of a delicate substrate capable of preserving both pterosaur and small vertebrate tracs without generating any additional sedimentary structures and ichnofossils is therefore unlikely. Consequently, although the precise duration of the interval cannot be determined, it was most likely shorter than a multi-day timescale.

Although neither the preservation condition, including MISS, nor the observed depth variation alone provides definitive evidence of synchronicity of direct interaction between the trackmakers, these features together support the interpretation that the tracks were likely formed under similar substrate conditions and within a brief interval during which the surface remained moister and undisturbed.

Beyond taxonomic inference, the morphology and configurations of trackways offer valuable insight into trackmaker behavior (Fig. 4). The small vertebrate trackway progresses in a consistent direction over approximately 350 mm (Fig. 4b), after which it undergoes an abrupt leftward deviation at an angle of ~ 25 °. Following this directional shift, the trackway continues for at least 360 mm, though the clarity of gait diminishes around the 420 mm mark (Fig. 4d). Notably, the anterior orientation of the trackway remains consistent before and after the directional change. Furthermore, the tail trace of the small vertebrate trackway aligns closely with that of J. procerus beyond the infection point, suggesting a spatial correlation.

Morphological differences between the pre- and post-deviation segments of the small vertebrate trackway are apparent. Initially, manus and pes prints appear merged, with a relatively sinuous tail trace (Fig. 4b). After the transition, manus and pes impressions are more clearly separated, and the tail trace mark becomes straighter (Fig. 4d). This shift is also reflected in stride length (Fig. 4c; Table S2): prior to the directional change, stride lengths are relatively constant (55 to 60 mm), while afterward, they increase to ~ 69 mm before subsequently decreasing. Given that stride length correlates with locomotor speed78, this pattern implies a phase of rapid acceleration following a change in direction. In the absence of notable variation in substrate grain size or moisture content across the slab, such footprint transformations are likely attributed to changes in gait or velocity79,80.

However, it remains unclear whether the magnitude of stride variation observed here has been sufficient for effective escape from a predator. In the small vertebrate trackway, the range of stride length variation is approximately 22 to 33% when calculated relative to the average or minimum stride length. The result of a neoichnological experiment on tiger salamanders indicates that stride length is influenced more by environmental factors, such as substrate conditions, than by speed81. In lizards, the range is considerably broader, from about 31 to 94%82. This variability likely reflects the inclusion of bipedal running mechanisms in this dataset82 and the fact that both stride length and stride frequency contribute to acceleration, with their relative importance differing among taxa83,84. Furthermore, the observed decrease in speed following acceleration may represent buster-and-stop locomotion, a common pattern in lizards85–88.

In general, speed estimated from vertebrate trackways relies on the calculation of hip height inferred from pes length and stride length78,89. Among pterosaurs, functional hip height and speed estimation based on track morphology have only been quantified for Pteraichnus junggarensis90. In this study, we applied the correlation between pes length and functional hip height for pterodactyloids proposed by Li et al.90 to estimate the speed of the Jinjuichnus trackmaker (Fig. 5). The calculated speed of approximately 0.8 m/s is slower than the 2.75 m/s estimated for Pteraichnus palacieisaenzi yet appears relatively high compared with most other reported pterosaur speeds (Figure S3). Because P. palacieisaenzi exhibits outlier stride lengths and Froude Numbers, analyses including this ichnospecies are presented in the Supplementary Information. The P. procerus trackway also shows higher speeds and Froude Numbers than similarly sized trackways from Ulsan Pteraichnus and P. junggarensis (Fig. 5). However, functional hip height in walking or running postures remains controversial in pterosaurs, and variation in standing or locomotor posture likely existed within Pterodactyloidea91–94. Additional data are therefore required to refine relative speed estimates among pterosaur taxa. Finally, the ecological adaptations of the trackmaker pterosaur, likely a neoazhdarchian, provide critical context for interpreting the behavioral scenario inferred from the trackways. Neoazhdarchians exhibit numerous morphological and ecological traits indicative of terrestrial adaptation. These include pedal morphology as preserved in trace fossils and body fossils3,6,47,61,62, limb and cranial proportions3,6,61 a fossil record biased toward continental deposits6, and an enlarged sacral neural canal95. Witton and Naish6 suggested that azhdarchids were adept terrestrial foragers employing a “terrestrial stalking” strategy, ecologically comparable to extant storks. Chaoyangopterids, which share many of these anatomical features with azhdarchids, may have had similar foraging strategies3,62. Thalassodromids had likewise been proposed to exhibit a terrestrial predation ecology3. These anatomical and ichnological indicators of terrestrial agility and stalking capacity lend support to the interpretation that the two trackmakers may have been engaged in a predator-prey interaction.

Considering the following factors: (1) the probable temporal proximality of the two trackways; (2) the abrupt change in both speed and direction of the small vertebrate trackway; (3) Apparent same direction progression of two trackways; (4) the inferred high speed of the pterosaur trackmaker; and (5) the paleoecological context of neoazhdarchians, an interaction between the two trackmakers appears more parsimonious than coincidental co-occurrence without behavioral correlation. While trackway association alone does not constitute direct evidence of predation, the convergence of these lines of evidence, particularly in light of the size and ecological inference of the trackmaker, could suggest an interaction scenario (Fig. 6). Nevertheless, an alternative scenario in which the apparent association between the two trackways arose coincidentally cannot be excluded. However, the multiple lines of evidence explored in this study to assess potential relationships between the two trackways may serve as a useful framework for interpreting the association between different trackmakers.

Conclusion

A new pterosaur ichnotaxon, Jinjuichnus procerus et ichnogen. et ichnosp. nov., is described from the Lower Cretaceous Jinju Formation of South Korea. This trackway is characterized by a large size, highly elongated digit III, a blunt digit I, and a wide manus divarication angle, features that distinguish it from previously reported pterosaur ichnotaxa. Based on pedal morphology and comparative analysis, the trackmaker is most plausibly attributed to a neoazhdarchian pterosaur that inhabited the Korean Peninsula during the Early Cretaceous.

The trackway was discovered in close association with a small tetrapod trackway, which exhibits abrupt directional changes, tail-trace changes, and increased stride length. Multiple lines of evidence support the correlation between two trackways: (1) their temporal proximality, indicated by preservation quality and interaction with MISS; (2) the abrupt phase change in the trackway; (3) the apparent same direction progression of both trackways; (4) the inferred high speed of the trackmaker; and (5) the broader paleoecological context of trackmaker. This is consistent with the hypothesis that neoazhdarchians were well-adapted terrestrial predators capable of active vertebrate predation. However, a coincidental association between the two trackways cannot be ruled out, but the evidentiary approach applied here provides a useful framework for evaluating potential relationship between tracks made by different trackmakers.

Materials and methods

The holotype specimen of Jinjuichnus procerus was found in 2010 during “The Fossil Heritage Survey of the Jinju Innovation City Development Area”. The specimen is stored and exhibited in the Jinju Pterosaur Track Museum, Jinju City, Gyeongsangnam-do, Republic of Korea, under the collection number JPTM-1-4.

Numerical measurements were obtained both in the field and from digital images using ImageJ (v.1.53k). Graphs were generated with RStudio96 (v.2024.12.1), and all figures were prepared with Adobe Illustrator 2025 (v.29.7.1). To obtain photographs of the sample, we used a digital camera, the Sony Alpha 7 Mark III, with a digital lens, SEL2470Z. A total of 287 photographs were used to create the photogrammetric model. To create photogrammetric 3D images, we followed the guidelines provided by Falkingham et al.97 and Romilio98 and converted the photographs into 3D models using Agisoft Metashape Professional (v.2.0.2) and visualized them with Paraview (v.5.11.1).
