Exploiting Constraint Reasoning to Build Graphical Explanations for Mixed-Integer Linear Programming
Roger X. Lera-Leri, Filippo Bistaffa, Athina Georgara, Juan A. Rodríguez-Aguilar

Abstract
Following the recent push for trustworthy AI , there has been an increasing interest in developing contrastive explanation techniques for optimisation, especially concerning the solution of specific decision-making processes formalised as MILP s. Along these lines, we propose X-MILP , a domain-agnostic approach for building contrastive explanations for MILP s based on constraint reasoning techniques. First, we show how to encode the queries a user makes about the solution of an MILP problem as additional constraints. Then, we determine the reasons that constitute the answer to the user’s query by computing the Irreducible Infeasible Subsystem (IIS) of the newly obtained set of constraints. Finally, we represent our explanation as a “graph of reasons” constructed from the IIS , which helps the user understand the structure among the reasons that answer their query. We test our method on instances of well-known optimisation problems to evaluate the empirical hardness of computing explanations.

Introduction

In recent years,
Artificial Intelligence
(AI)
has achieved notable momentum, enabling impressive results in many application domains.
Alongside such improvements, there has been an increasing interest in developing trustworthy, human-centred AI that aligns with ethical values. A key requirement is
explainability
, namely, providing explanations to humans who use or are affected by AI systems
[
12
]
.
Consequently, there has been a resurgence in the area of
eXplainable Artificial Intelligence
(XAI)
,
which is concerned with designing
AI
systems whose decisions can be explained and understood by humans
[
38
]
.

XAI
has been widely active in
Machine Learning
(ML)
to provide insights into the functionalities of models that operate as “black boxes”.
Following this push on
XAI
, there have been recent works on explainability for optimisation in domain-specific applications (e.g., scheduling
[
37
]
or team formation
[
17
]
).

Here we consider a domain-independent approach where the optimal solution of an optimisation problem formalised as a
Mixed-Integer Linear Programming
(MILP)
is presented to a user, who then asks for an explanation of such a solution.
Based on the user query, the literature typically computes an alternative solution compliant with such a query, the so-called user-desired solution
[
17
,
46
]
.
Then, it provides a contrastive explanation comparing the optimal solution with the user-desired solution.
In contrast, our goal is to provide a
contrastive explanation
to the user, presenting the
reasons
for which their query leads to worse decisions in terms of optimality.

To achieve this goal, we propose
Explainable MILP
(X-MILP)
, a general method that aims to provide contrastive explanations for
MILP
problems (see Figure
1
).

X-MILP
is a process that follows three main steps.
First, it casts a user-desired optimisation scenario compliant with a user’s query as a
Constraint Satisfaction Problem
(CSP)
. Second, it finds the constraints in the
CSP
that make it infeasible. The infeasibility of such
CSP
proves that the user-desired optimisation scenario cannot lead to a better solution than the challenged optimal solution of the
MILP
. With this goal, our approach exploits constraint reasoning techniques to calculate the minimal subset of constraints that make the
CSP
infeasible, i.e., the
IIS

[
10
]
. Third,
X-MILP
builds a structured, human-readable explanation from the unstructured collection of mathematical constraints in the
IIS
.
Such explanation, the so-called
graph of reasons
, graphically captures the relationships between constraints and assigns them a
natural language
interpretation, which we call “
reason
”.

To the best of our knowledge, the novelty of our work stems from the following contributions:

•

We cast the problem of generating a contrastive explanation for the result of an optimisation as a
CSP
, which encodes a user-desired scenario compliant with a query posed by a user.

•

We exploit constraint reasoning techniques to find why a user-desired optimisation scenario cannot lead to a better solution than the optimal solution. We do so by computing the minimal set of constraints, i.e., an
IIS
, in the
CSP
encoding the user-desired scenario.

•

We build a graphical explanation based on the information in the
IIS
, whose structure allows to capture the relationships between constraints that an unstructured set of constraints cannot.

•

We evaluate
X-MILP
on different
MILP
problems to showcase its generality. We test our approach on a scheduling problem, the so-called
Resource-Constraint Project Scheduling Problem
(RCPSP)
, and a particular instance of the
Weighted Set Packing
(WSP)
problem.
Our results show that despite the hardness of solving these optimisation problems,
X-MILP
computes explanations in less than
60
seconds in most cases. Moreover,
X-MILP
does not recompute the solution to an optimisation problem, in contrast with previous works
[
46
,
17
]
.

Background

2.1
Mixed-Integer Linear Programming

An
MILP
is an optimisation problem in the form

\mbox{minimise}\quad\textbf{c}^{T}\cdot\textbf{x}+\textbf{h}^{T}\cdot\textbf{y},\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad A\cdot\textbf{x}+G\cdot\textbf{y}\leq\textbf{b},

(1)

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a vector of integer variables,
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a vector of variables,
\textbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\textbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\text{ and }\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are column vectors, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\text{ and }G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
are matrices.
In general,
MILP
problems are NP-Hard
[
44
]
and are expensive to solve.
On the other hand, available solvers such as CPLEX optimally solve
MILPs
.

2.2
Benchmark Optimisation Problems

We describe two well-known optimisation problems that can be formalised as
MILPs
, which we employ as benchmarks in Section
5
to evaluate our approach. We choose them because they are examples of a scheduling problem (the
RCPSP
), and a
WSP
problem (the
Winner Determination Problem
(WDP)
for
Combinatorial Auction
(CA)
).
Our choice of employing a
WSP
formalisation is also motivated by the fact that such a problem has been used in real-world application domains such as
shared mobility

[
4
]
,
cooperative learning

[
2
]
, and, according to
[
15
]
, any scenario involving the formation of collectives of agents.
The details of their formalisation are in the supplementary material.

RCPSP

RCPSP
is a problem consisting of activities that must be scheduled to meet precedence and resource constraints while minimising a project’s makespan
[
20
]
.
Each activity requires a processing time and certain resources to be completed.
Moreover, each activity has preceding activities, and hence it can only begin once they finish.
The goal of an
RCPSP
is to find a feasible schedule that minimises the total project duration.
Therefore, the decision variables of the
RCPSP
are
x_{j,t}\in\{0,1\}
, which indicate whether activity
j
is completed at time
t
. Then, an
RCPSP
s’ constraints are: (i) every activity must be completed, (ii) every activity must start after their predecessors have been completed, and (iii) resource availability has to be respected every time unit.

WDP

Given a combinatorial auction that receives a set of bids over a set of goods, the
WDP
is that of selecting the subset of bids that maximise revenue
[
13
]
.
We follow the standard approach to solve a
WDP
by casting it as a
WSP
problem modelled as an
MILP
, where decision variable
x_{b}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether bid
b
is selected.
The
WDP
’s constraints force that every good is allocated up to one winning bid.

2.3
Irreducible Infeasible Subsystem

The
Irreducible Infeasible Subsystem
(IIS)
is a minimal set of inconsistent constraints.
However, to properly define the notion of
IIS
, we must first define the notions of constraint and system of constraints. We borrow the definition of constraint from
[
33
]
:

Definition 1

Given a sequence of distinct variables
V=\langle x_{1},\dots,

x_{k}\rangle
and their associated finite domains
\mathcal{D}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{k}
, a constraint
c
is a subset of
\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{D}_{k}
. Thus, a constraint
c
specifies the assignments allowed by the variables of
V
.

In other words, a constraint is a condition that variables must satisfy.
Then, a constraint system is a set of constraints
C
.
Hence, we define an infeasible system as follows.

Definition 2

An Infeasible System of constraints is a set of constraints
C
such that no assignment of
V
exists that satisfies all the constraints in
C
.

Having defined an infeasible system of constraints, we define an
IIS
borrowing the definition from
[
10
]
:

Definition 3

An
IIS
is a subset of constraints within an infeasible system that is in itself infeasible, but any proper subset of the
IIS
is feasible.

Finding an
IIS
is crucial in
CSP
because it identifies the constraints preventing us from obtaining a feasible solution. Thus, many algorithms have been developed to find the
IIS
for
Linear Programming
(LP)

[
43
,
18
,
36
,
9
]
,
MILP

[
19
]
or Non-Linear Integer Programming
[
8
]
. Finding the
IIS
involves iteratively analysing its infeasible system and identifying the minimal subset of constraints responsible for infeasibility. Some examples of algorithms that find
IISs
are: the Deletion Filter
[
11
]
, which iteratively deletes constraints and checks the feasibility of the system; the Additive Method
[
42
]
, which iteratively adds constraints to an initially empty set and tests whether the set is feasible; or the combination of both methods
[
19
]
.
Although computing
IISs
can be costly, state-of-the-art solvers efficiently compute them.

3
Related Work

Here, we discuss recent literature in the
eXplainable Artificial Intelligence
field and explainability for optimisation.
Notably, most
XAI
works focus on
ML
models.
For instance,
[
3
,
1
]
provide an overview of the
XAI
current proposals in
ML
.
These works categorise
XAI
techniques based on their explanations’ scope, the methodologies behind the algorithms, and their explanation level or usage. Besides,
[
40
]
point out future
XAI
challenges, such as formalising a theory of
XAI
or providing high-level explanations.

Furthermore, it is important to characterise what constitutes a
good
explanation.
[
34
]
reviews the social sciences literature that studies how humans define, generate, and evaluate explanations.
[
34
]
extracts important findings and provides insights on how to apply such findings to
XAI
, concluding that giving explanations is a non-trivial task since such explanations have to be: (i)
contrastive
(in response to particular counterfactual cases); (ii)
social
(to transfer knowledge as a part of a conversation); (iii)
selected
(humans select one or two reasons as an explanation); and (iv)
causal
(explanations with causal relationships are more important than referring to probabilities or statistical relations).

Even though many AI systems use optimisation-based approaches, there is limited research on providing explanations for such systems.

[
35
]
find justifications for the outcome of collective decision algorithms.

[
5
]
give step-wise explanations for
CSP
.
Moreover,
[
24
,
37
,
17
]
explain the outcome of different classical combinatorial problems. However, the explanations generated by such approaches lack some key properties enumerated by
[
34
]
. Although
[
24
,
17
]
provide contrastive explanations, they refer to numerical reasons, instead of referring to causal relationships. On the other hand,
[
37
]
refer to reasons, but their explanations are not contrastive. Moreover, these approaches are not general; each one addresses a particular application domain.
Against this background, here we propose
X-MILP
, a domain-independent explanatory approach that generates contrastive, social, and selected explanations that refer to reasons indicating why the optimal solution is better than the one posed by the user.

Although we employ
IISs
in
X-MILP
,
Minimal Unsatisfiable Subset
s (MUSs)
are an analogous concept that has been studied in the Boolean satisfiability (SAT) literature
[
41
]
.
Several works discuss how to extract multiple
MUSs

[
32
]
,
since an infeasible system of constraints may possess an exponential number of
MUSs

[
7
]
.
Along these lines,
[
22
,
16
]
propose methods to select the
MUS
whose constraints are the most relevant according to user preferences.
Furthermore, there has been an effort in computing the
MUS
with minimal cardinality, i.e., the
MUS
with a minimum number of constraints
[
31
,
21
]
.
Crucially, the above-mentioned works on
MUS
focus on providing explanations for the
infeasibility
of a given
SAT Problem
by extracting a
MUS
.
In this paper, we pursue a
different

goal. We aim to provide explanations for
MILPs
by answering a user’s query on the optimal solution.
We do so by computing the
IIS
of a specially-defined
CSP
(see Section
4.2
) and identifying the
reasons
that constitute such an explanation, which are then represented as a human-readable graph (see Section
4.4
).

Building Explanations for
MILPs

We aim to provide
contrastive explanations to users for
MILP
problems.
2
2

2

In this paper we only focus on providing explanations for optimisation problems formalised as
MILPs
. Explaining problems formalised as
Mixed-Integer Programming
s (MIPs)
, (Max)SATs, etc., is outside the scope of this paper.

In such problems, constraints encode restrictions that the solution must satisfy, while the objective function encodes some preferences or quantities to be optimised
[
6
]
. Thus, a MILP solver output is affected by the constraints and the objective function.
Hence, we propose to exploit the information provided by the constraints and the objective function in an
MILP
to build contrastive explanations.
The following running example illustrates the concepts of our approach.

Example 1

Consider an
RCPSP
instance with 30 activities and 4 different resources.
As mentioned in Section
2.2
, solving an
RCPSP
amounts to minimising a project duration objective function
f
,
while satisfying a constraint set
C
.
We partially show the optimal solution
\textbf{x}^{*}
in Table
1
. The optimal (minimum) project duration would be 43 (
f^{*}=43
).

Some
XAI
approaches in the literature build a contrastive explanation by comparing an optimal solution with a user-desired solution
[
17
,
46
]
. The latter results from translating a user “
query
”, i.e., a question for which a user expects an explanation, into
addditional constraints
that a solution must satisfy.

4.1
Query Translation

Here, we characterise the types of queries that a user can pose and how to encode them into query constraints.
More precisely, a user asks “
why
” questions about value assignments that did occur in an optimal solution
\textbf{x}^{*}
of an
MILP
.
For example, regarding a scheduling problem, a user might ask “why is job j scheduled at a given time t?”.
Hence, a contrastive explanation should tell the user why a solution complying with the query constraints is not as good as the optimal solution.
For that, we assume that the query a user poses refers to a
MILP
solver output
[
45
]
.
Therefore, a user query is related to the decision variables of the
MILP
at hand.
In combinatorial optimisation problems, decision variables are related to objects, activities, agents, etc.
For instance, in the
RCPSP
, the decision variables indicate whether an activity is completed at a given time. Hence, there is a decision variable for every pair of activities and time units.

Here we follow the query constraint classification proposed in
[
17
]
:

•

enforce
constraints to express that some value assignments that do not hold in an optimal solution are required to hold in the user-desired solution; and

•

veto
constraints to express that some value assignments that do hold in an optimal solution must not hold in the user-desired solution.

Following such classification, Table
2
lists possible queries for the
RCPSP
. The table also shows the translations of queries into (linear) query constraints.
Note that such a methodology can be applied to other
MILP
problems
[
17
]
. We show the list of possible queries that we identify for the
WDP
in the supplementary material. Here, we discuss some examples for the
RCPSP
.

In the case of the
RCPSP
, for example, the query “
Why is activity
j
scheduled to be completed at time
t
?
” translates into
vetoing
the completion of activity
j
at time
t
(i.e.,
x_{j,t}=0
) to enforce an alternative solution to the optimal solution. Notice that this query refers to a
single assignment
occurring in the optimal solution.
Additionally, to comply with the query “
Why is the group of activities
A
scheduled to be completed at time
t
?
” we must
veto
the completion of all activities in
A
at
t
(i.e.,
\sum_{j\in A}x_{j,t}=0
). In this case, the query refers to the schedule of multiple activities, i.e.,
multiple assignments
, in the optimal solution.
Furthermore, when addressing the query “
Why is activity
j

not
scheduled to be completed at time
t
?
” we must
enforce
the hypothetical scheduling of activity
j
, which the current scheduling disregards.
Moreover, to answer the query “
Why is activity
j
scheduled to be completed at time
t
instead of activity
j^{\prime}
?
”, we must: (1) enforce activity
j^{\prime}
; and (2) veto
j
at time
t
. Thus, we consider combinations of
enforce
and
veto
constraints to answer more specific queries about value assignments that did and did not occur.

4.2
The User-Desired Satisfiability Problem

Based on a user’s query, the literature typically considers user-desired optimisation scenarios that accommodate such a query,
aiming at quantifying how worse a user-desired solution would be in terms of optimality.
In this context, the literature typically defines the following extended problem
[
46
,
17
]
.

Definition 4

Given a
MILP
problem
M=\langle f,C\rangle
of the form of (
1
) (we refer to
M
as
main problem
, where
f
is the objective function and
C
are the constraints), and a set of query constraints
C_{Q}
associated with a query, we obtain the
extended problem

M^{\prime}=\langle f,C\cup C_{Q}\rangle
by adding
C_{Q}
.

Assuming
M
is a feasible problem, solving an extended problem
M^{\prime}
would result in one of the following three cases.

1.

Infeasibility.
There is no guarantee that the extended problem is feasible. Hence, adding the query constraints makes the new problem
M^{\prime}
infeasible.

2.

Optimality.
We obtain a solution whose value is the same as the optimal solution for
M
. This is possible when the main problem has multiple optimal solutions.

3.

Suboptimality.
This occurs when the optimal solution to
M^{\prime}
is worse than the optimal solution to
M
.

However, in this paper, we neither solve an extended
MILP
problem that accommodates a user’s query (as in
[
46
]
) nor quantify how worse the user-desired solution is
(as in
[
17
]
).
Since numerical comparisons are not desirable in explanations
[
34
]
, we propose instead to find the reasons why the user-desired scenario cannot lead to a better solution than the optimal solution.
To do so, we cast the problem of obtaining the user-desired solution as a
CSP
. Such a
CSP
contains the problem constraints, the query constraints and an additional constraint enforcing a solution at least as good as the optimal solution to the “
main
” problem.
This formulation will serve to extract the constraints that restrict us from obtaining a user-desired solution with optimal value.

Definition 5

Let
M
be a
MILP
,
C_{Q}
a set of query constraints, and
f^{*}=f(\textbf{x}^{*})
the value of the optimal solution
\textbf{x}^{*}
for
M
.The
User-Desired Satisfiability Problem
(UDSP)
for
M
is a
CSP
composed of the following constraints:

(i)\;f(\textbf{x})\leq f^{*},\quad(ii)\;C,\quad(iii)\;C_{Q},

(3)

where
x
are variables and (i) is a minimality constraint.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
M
is a minimisation problem, and hence the value of a better solution must be lower than
f^{*}
.

Example 3

We define a
UDSP
for example
2
as the following
CSP
with variables
x
:

(i)\;f(\textbf{x})\leq f^{*}=43,\quad(ii)\;C,\quad(iii)\;\sum_{t=1}^{40}x_{24,t}=1,

(4)

where
(i)
is the minimality constraint,
(ii)
are the main problem constraints, and
(iii)
is the query constraint.

4.3
Computing the Infeasible Constraints

Having defined the
UDSP
, we extract the minimal set of constraints that prevent us from finding a feasible solution to the
UDSP
, i.e., we extract the
IIS
.
Notice that there might be multiple
IISs
corresponding to the same set of constraints since the number of
IISs
increases exponentially with the size of a constraint set
[
7
]
.
More precisely, different
IISs
(despite being all “irreducible”) could contain different numbers of constraints (i.e., some
IISs
might be smaller than others).
Hence, this would subsequently lead us to explanations of varying sizes, since we build our explanations based on the constraints of an
IIS
.
Following
[
39
]
,
3
3

3

The number of “rules” (i.e., constraints in our case) in an explanation is a commonly used metric to evaluate its simplicity.
we should aim to compute the smallest possible
IIS
because the more concise an explanation, the easier to understand.

On the one hand, some algorithms in the literature can compute the “smallest”
IIS

[
31
,
21
]
. However, computing the “smallest”
IIS
is very costly.
On the other hand, off-the-shelf solvers such as CPLEX use specialised heuristics to efficiently compute a single
IIS
that is not guaranteed to be the smallest one.
In Section
5.2
, we experimentally compare the solutions computed by an algorithm for computing the smallest
IIS
, adapted from
[
21
]
, and CPLEX.
We observe that, in practice, CPLEX computes the smallest
IIS
for most instances with a significantly lower computational cost.

4.4
Constructing the
Graph of Reasons

When considering a complex, large-scale optimisation problem (such as our benchmark problems in Section
2.2
), its
IIS
can involve many constraints.
Henceforth, the challenge lies in organising such constraints to assemble an understandable explanation for a user.
With that aim, in what follows, we show that, by exploiting the structural relations among constraints in the form of a graph, we can build a “graph-based”, structured explanation for a user whose structure can be better understood (in contrast with the unstructured list of constraints in the
IIS
).

To this end, we rely on well-known concepts from the Constraint Reasoning literature as follows.
Hypergraphs
[
14
]
represent how a set of constraints relates to decision variables.
A
hypergraph

\mathcal{H}=(V,S)
is a pair where
V
is a set of vertices, and
S=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{l}\}
,
S_{i}\subseteq V
, is a set of subsets of
V
called hyperedges. Notice that the vertices represent the set of decision variables of our model, and a hyperedge
S_{i}
is the scope of constraint
c_{i}
, which is the subset of decision variables involved in the constraint.
However, hypergraphs do not directly represent the relations among constraints, which is, instead, the purpose of the concept of dual graphs.
Formally, a
dual graph

[
14
]

D=\{S,I\}
is an undirected graph whose vertices
S
are the hyperedges of
\mathcal{H}
and
(S_{i},S_{j})\in I
iff
S_{i}\cap S_{j}\neq\emptyset
.
Intuitively, each node of a dual graph corresponds to a constraint, and each edge indicates that two constraints share some variables.
Therefore, the dual graph of an
IIS
captures the relations between its constraints. Hence, we will use it as the basis for building an explanation.

Figure
2
showcases our approach for building explanations from an
IIS
in the context of our running example. First, we relate the constraints in an
IIS
to yield its dual graph.
Despite capturing the structural relations among constraints, the dual graph of an
IIS
might still be challenging to understand for a user since it does not contain any
natural language
(NL)
interpretation of the mathematical constraints.
To overcome this limitation, following the approach by
[
37
]
, we label each constraint in the dual graph with an
NL
template.
4
4

4

Notice that translating queries and labelling constraints using
NL
templates are the only problem-dependent steps that must be carried out by an expert, in contrast with the rest of our approach, which is domain-independent.
As an example, Table
3
shows the
NL
templates involved in our running example for each type of
RCPSP
constraints in Section
2.2
.

Figure
2
shows the constraint labelling for our running example.
Therefore, we can finally see that a “
reason
” takes the form of the
NL
interpretation of a constraint.
Therefore, we refer to our graph-based explanation as a
graph of reasons
.
A graph of reasons captures the structural relations among constraints inherent in the dual graph while providing human-readable semantics for each constraint.

A desirable property that characterises our explanations is that they are not “disconnected”, i.e., they involve reasons that are always related to each other. This property can be directly expressed in terms of the graph-theory concept of
connectedness
, as formally discussed in Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.1

Given an
IIS
, the
IIS
dual graph
D
is connected.

Proof

We prove by contradiction that a disconnected
IIS
dual graph, i.e., a graph with multiple disconnected components, is not possible to obtain due to the infeasible and irreducible properties of an
IIS
(Definition
3
).

Consider an
IIS
graph that is disconnected in two subgraphs,
A=(C_{A},E_{A})
and
B=(C_{B},E_{B})
such that

\displaystyle C_{A}\cup C_{B}

\displaystyle=IIS,

\displaystyle C_{A}\cap C_{B}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle S_{A}\cap S_{B}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

where
S_{A},S_{B}
are the scopes of the set of constraints
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
respectively. Notice that, since the graph is disconnected,
A
and
B
subgraphs do not share variables, i.e.,
S_{A}\cap S_{B}=\emptyset
.
Then, since the subset of constraints
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
are (disjoint) subsets of the
IIS
, they are feasible (see Definition
3
).
However, since the both subsets of constraints have different variables (different domains), the union of both constraint sets would not affect the feasibility status of the resulting system of constraints.
As a result, we would have a feasible
IIS
,
which is a contradiction in itself.
Therefore, it is not possible to have a disconnected
IIS
graph.

5
Experimental Evaluation

The main goal of this section is to evaluate the empirical hardness
[
29
]
of computing explanations.
As a benefit, this allows us to characterise problem instances for which we can compute explanations in real time for users.
Our study will measure (i) the times needed to compute
IIS
s to build explanations, and (ii) the
time overheads
of building explanations with respect to solving optimisation problems. Thus, given a problem and a query, we define the
time overhead
to build an explanation for the query as the ratio between the time to compute the
IIS
and the time to compute the optimal solution for the problem. Therefore, our study compares the hardness of solving optimisation problems with respect to the hardness of computing their explanations.

An additional goal is to empirically learn whether the computational effort required to compute the smallest
IIS
with state-of-the-art techniques pays off. This would be the case if we do obtain
IISs
that are significantly smaller than those computed by CPLEX, our off-the-shelf solver of choice. With this aim, we compare the
IIS
sizes (number of constraints) obtained and the runtimes required by both methods.

6
Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we aim to build fundamental algorithmic tools for generating explanations for optimisation. We introduced
Explainable MILP
, an approach to building contrastive explanations to answer queries posed by a user. Our goal is to provide them explanations presenting the reasons for which their query leads to a worse solution.
Such reasons are then presented in natural language as a
graph of reasons
, whose structure helps a user to understand the relations between reasons.
We evaluate our approach with two well-known optimisation problems: the
RCPSP
and the
WDP
. Our results indicate that for the
RCPSP
, our method computes explanations much faster than solving an
MILP
problem. This is a key aspect of
X-MILP
since state-of-the-art approaches require solving additional
MILP
problems to generate explanations
[
17
,
46
]
. For the
WDP
, we can compute explanations in less than one minute for small and medium instances, though computing explanations for large instances can be more challenging.

As future work, we plan to predict the expected runtime required to compute an explanation for a given problem instance.
In addition, we plan to apply
X-MILP
to other optimisation-based application domains such as
Bachelor’s Degree Planning

[
26
]
,
Research Project Planning

[
28
]
or
Value Systems Aggregation

[
27
]
.
