Novel bispecific T-cell engagers overcoming acquired EGFR resistance
Authors: Lennart Kühl, Ann-Kathrin Löffler, Oliver Seifert, Dennis Michler, Miriam Kuhlmann, Giulio Russo, Philipp Kuhn, Helena Nowack, André Frenzel, Thomas Schirrmann, Monilola A Olayioye, Roland E Kontermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a validated therapeutic target in several human cancers harboring wild-type KRAS. However, intrinsic and acquired resistance to EGFR-targeted antibody therapies such as cetuximab remains a major limitation to achieving broad and durable treatment responses. Extensive clinical and translational studies have shown that resistance frequently arises from missense mutations within the extracellular domain (ECD) of EGFR. In this study, we developed novel antagonistic EGFR antibodies that bind epitopes overlapping but distinct from the cetuximab-binding site while retaining high affinity for all major EGFR ECD escape variants. Antibody binding effectively inhibited EGFR phosphorylation, downstream signaling, and tumor cell proliferation. The antibodies were further engineered into bispecific EGFR × CD3 T-cell engagers (TCEs) with either 1 + 1 or 2 + 1 stoichiometries. In contrast to cetuximab-based TCEs, the newly developed EGFR-directed TCEs efficiently induced T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumor cells expressing wild-type EGFR as well as the clinically relevant ECD escape variants S492R and G465R. Anti-tumor activity was additionally demonstrated in an EGFR-expressing CT-26 syngeneic tumor model in vivo. Collectively, these findings define a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome resistance to current EGFR-targeted therapies and provide a strong rationale for the development of next-generation T-cell – redirecting therapies in patients with EGFR-positive malignancies.KEYWORDS: EGFR, CD3, resistance mechanisms, bispecific antibody, T-cell retargeting

Introduction

The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a member of the ErbB family of receptor tyrosine kinases, is a validated therapeutic target in multiple solid tumors.1 EGFR expression on tumor cells is associated with enhanced survival, proliferation, and migration.2 Approved EGFR-targeted therapies include ligand-competitive inhibitory antibodies as well as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).3 To date, four monospecific antibodies (cetuximab, panitumumab, necitumumab, nimotuzumab) and one bispecific antibody (amivantamab; EGFR × c-Met) have been approved for the treatment of colorectal cancer (CRC), head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and glioma.4 In addition, several TKIs targeting EGFR kinase activity such as gefitinib, erlotinib, and osimertinib are approved for the treatment of NSCLC.3,5

The clinical benefit of EGFR-blocking therapies is limited by intrinsic (primary) or acquired resistance. Both forms of resistance frequently arise through oncogenic activation of downstream MAPK or PI3K signaling pathways, which confer a survival advantage to mutant tumor cells. For example, approximately 30% of CRC patients carry activating KRAS mutations at positions G12, G13, or Q61, rendering them ineligible for anti-EGFR therapy. Similarly, in patients treated with cetuximab or panitumumab, acquired gain-of-function mutations can arise in KRAS/NRAS (40%), BRAF (7%), or PIK3CA (11%).6 Upregulation of alternative receptor tyrosine kinases including HER2, HER3, c-Met, Axl, and the insulin-like growth factor receptor (IGF-1 R) can also drive compensatory signaling.7–10 Additionally, acquired resistance may result from mutations in the EGFR extracellular domain (ECD) that impair therapeutic antibody binding or from mutations in the kinase domain that reduce sensitivity to TKIs.

For cetuximab, necitumumab, and panitumumab, escape mutations predominantly cluster within a shared epitope in ECD III.11–13 Among the most prevalent ECD III mutations are S464L (22%), G465R/G465E (9–22%), and S492R (11–42%).14–21 These EGFR ECD mutations typically arise in subclones and co-occur with other resistance alterations, most commonly in KRAS, as shown by sequencing analyses of liquid biopsies.19,21–23 Given the diversity of resistance mechanisms and substantial intratumoral heterogeneity within individual patients, therapeutic strategies that operate largely independently of mutational status are expected to produce broader and more durable clinical responses. In this context, bispecific T-cell engagers (TCEs), which redirect T-cells to tumor cells for MHC-independent cytotoxicity, represent a promising alternative to monospecific ligand-blocking antibodies.24 Importantly, this mode of action is inherently less dependent on the mutational landscape of the tumor cell.

In this study, we developed novel antagonistic EGFR antibodies that bind epitopes overlapping the cetuximab epitope while maintaining affinity to all major ECD escape variants. These antibodies were subsequently used to generate EGFR × CD3 TCEs by applying the eIg platform.25,26 This technology enables the generation of bispecific Ig-like antibodies with correct heavy- and light-chain pairing and flexible dual specificity. Unlike cetuximab-based TCEs, our newly engineered EGFR-directed TCEs efficiently induced T cell-mediated killing not only of tumor cells expressing wild-type EGFR, but also of those expressing clinically relevant ECD S492R and G465R escape variants. Anti-tumor activity of these TCEs was further demonstrated in an EGFR-expressing CT-26 syngeneic tumor model. Together, our findings establish a promising strategy to eliminate cancer cells that have acquired resistance to current EGFR-blocking therapies.

Results

Generation of novel EGFR-neutralizing antibodies

Novel antibodies were selected from a human antibody phage display library using the extracellular region (domains 1–4) of EGFR. An initial screening of 17 selected antibodies with different VH and VL sequences for effects on EGFR phosphorylation using EGFR-expressing DiFi and FaDu cells identified one antibody, MKU011-A7 (A7), with partial blocking activity (data not shown). To further improve the activity of A7, the antibody was subjected to affinity maturation by VL-shuffling and phage display selection. Two derivatives, YU250-A02 (A02) and YU250-H10 (H10), with improved binding properties were identified. The increased binding compared to A7 was confirmed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) (Figure 1(a)). Here, H10 showed 6-fold stronger binding to EGFR compared with A7 and bound with a comparable EC50 value to that of cetuximab (0.33 nM vs 0.43 nM). The affinity of the Fabs for recombinant EGFR was further analyzed by biolayer interferometry (BLI). Here, A7 Fab bound with a KD of 32.0 nM, while A02 and H10 Fab bound with 2.5-fold and 7.7-fold higher affinities, respectively (KD = 12.9 nM for A02 and 4.17 nM for H10) (Table 1; Suppl. Figure S1). Thus, H10 reached a similar affinity to that measured for cetuximab (1.69 nM). Of note, A02 and H10 were capable of binding to cynomolgus and mouse EGFR in ELISA with similar EC50 values between 0.3–0.6 nM, while cetuximab recognized only human and cynomolgus EGFR (Figure 1(b)). The affinity-improved antibodies were further analyzed as IgGs for their potential to inhibit EGFR phosphorylation. Compared with A7, A02 and H10 showed greater inhibition of EGF-induced receptor phosphorylation of FaDu cells, an effect similar to that achieved by cetuximab (Figure 1(c)).

A02 and H10 antibodies efficiently block EGFR activation and cell proliferation

The inhibitory effects of the affinity-improved EGFR-targeting antibodies A02 and H10 were further investigated for four cancer cell lines that express varying amounts of wild-type EGFR: LIM1215 (~55,000 EGFR/cell), FaDu (~200,000 EGFR/cell), A431 (~106 EGFR/cell) and DiFi (~106 EGFR/cell). Cell binding analyzed by flow cytometry showed that A02 and H10 bound with EC50 values in the range of 0.1 to 1.6 nM, comparable to that of cetuximab (Figure 1(d), Table 2). In unstimulated cells, very weak phosphorylation of EGFR was observed in LIM1215 and FaDu cells, while EGFR was strongly phosphorylated in DiFi and A431 cells. For all four cell lines, very weak pERK levels were detected in the absence of EGF stimulation. Under these conditions, all three tested antibodies reduced/abolished EGFR and ERK phosphorylation. Furthermore, these findings indicate that the IgGs themselves do not trigger receptor phosphorylation, i.e., are not agonistic (Figure 1(e)).

Much stronger inhibitory effects of the antibodies were observed for cells stimulated with EGF. Here, strongest inhibitory effects on EGFR and ERK phosphorylation were observed for FaDu and LIM1215 cells, followed by DiFi and A431 cells (Figure 1(e)). Thus, A02 and H10 inhibited EGF-mediated EGFR phosphorylation and activation of the MAPK signaling pathway (as determined by phosphorylation of ERK) of unstimulated and EGF-stimulated cells to a similar extent as cetuximab.

Importantly, A02, H10, and cetuximab significantly inhibited the basal proliferation of all four cell lines in the absence of exogenous EGFR ligands (Figure 1(f)). Upon stimulation with EGF or HB-EGF, the proliferation of DiFi and LIM1215 cells was increased by approximately 2-fold. A02 and H10 reduced the ligand-stimulated proliferative activity of the cells in the range between 64.6 and 94.9%, i.e., below that of unstimulated cells (Figure 1(g)). In summary, the novel EGFR antibodies efficiently inhibit EGFR phosphorylation, downstream signaling, and cell proliferation. In general, treatment with H10 or A02 was as effective as cetuximab.

A02 and H10 epitopes are distinct from the epitopes of clinically approved antibodies

We next performed epitope mapping studies to further characterize the novel EGFR-binding antibodies. In ELISA, A7 and its affinity-matured derivatives A02 and H10 bound to ECD III of the EGFR (data not shown). Preincubation of EGFR with H10 Fab blocked the binding of A02 IgG and H10 IgG (self-blockage), as well as the binding of cetuximab, necitumumab and panitumumab (Figure 2(a)). Furthermore, preincubation of EGFR with IgG of the different antibodies resulted in complete blockage of Fab H10 (data not shown). Thus, the novel antibodies bind to a region similar to or overlapping with that of the three approved antibodies, which all share a common epitope within ECD III (Figure 2(b)) (Li et al., 2005,12,13).

To further characterize the binding properties, ECD III variants carrying clinically relevant mutations known to impair the binding of the approved antibodies (V441D/G, S442R, S464L, G465R, K489E, S492R),14–21 as well as a proline substitution identified in one patient pre-treated with a therapeutic EGFR-targeting antibody (Q435P)23 were generated. H10 was found to bind to all ECD III variants except for the variants carrying a Q435P mutation (Figure 2(c)). A7 showed a similar pattern, although binding to the V441D and S464L variants was slightly reduced. In the case of cetuximab, binding was abolished by the V441D, S464L, G465R, S492R, and Q435P mutations, and reduced by the S442R and K489E mutations. For panitumumab, binding was abolished by the S442R, S464L and G465R mutations, and reduced by the V441D/G, and K489E mutations. Necitumumab did not bind to the ECD III harboring V441D and S442R mutations and showed reduced binding to ECD III with V441G, G465R, K489E as well as G435P mutations. Thus, while the binding of cetuximab, panitumumab and necitumumab was affected by several of the tested escape mutations, the A02 and H10 antibodies retained binding to the respective ECD III variants, except for the one harboring the rare Q435P mutation.

To further map the epitope of H10, we generated a first panel of EGFR ECDIII variants with single or double alanine substitutions at selected positions within the cetuximab and EGF binding region (G434A, F436A/I462A, F381A, H383A, F381A/H383A, S484A, K487A).27 These variants were tested for binding to cetuximab and the new EGFR antibodies A07 and H10. Binding to the ECD III variant carrying the G434A substitution was slightly affected for A7 and H10, but not for cetuximab. The double mutation F383A/H383A also impaired the binding of A7 and H10. All other mutations neither reduced the binding of cetuximab nor that of the new EGFR antibodies A7 and H10.

The study was then extended with a second panel of ECDIII variants comprising 3 or 4 alanine substitutions located adjacent to the cetuximab-binding site (v1 = K346A, D347A, L349A; v2 = D368A, H370A, L372A, Q408A; v3 = L406A, Q432A, H433A; v4 = R429A, T430A, K431A). Cetuximab was capable of binding the four variants v1-v4 similarly to wild-type EGFR. In contrast, binding of H10 to the ECD III variant 4 was completely abolished and reduced for the variants v1 and v2, but only slightly affected for v3 (Figure 2(c)). A7 showed a similar binding pattern, with v4 mutations completely abolishing binding. However, in addition and distinct from H10, binding was also completely abolished for the variant v3, which had no or only weak effects on H10 binding. These data confirm that the epitopes of A7 and H10 are different from that of cetuximab. Furthermore, these findings indicate that affinity maturation by chain shuffling resulted in the selection of partially altered residues involved in the interaction. Residues involved in H10 binding are shown in Figure 2(d).

To further investigate the binding of H10 to DIII, we performed in silico docking of a model structure of the H10 Fv fragment with a model structure of EGFR DIII containing the six escape mutations V441D, S442R, S464L, G465R, K489R, and S492R (DIIImut). The selected HADDOCK cluster exhibited a HADDOCK score of −63.3 and a Z-score of −1.4. The negative HADDOCK score indicates an energetically favorable complex, whereas the Z-score reflects a moderate deviation from the mean of all clusters. In the resulting model, the Fv is positioned adjacent to the escape mutations and overlaps with the cetuximab binding site (Figure 2(e)). Within DIIImut, the following residues identified in the mutational study lie within 4 Å of Fv residues: K346, D347, L349, H370, T430, K431, Q432, H433, G434, Q435, and F336. In addition, the model suggests that several DIII residues, which were not included in the mutational study, may also contribute to binding, including I340, G341, S348, F404, and D458. Furthermore, the docking results indicate that some residues within variants 1–4 may not contribute to antigen binding, such as R429 of variant 4. Within H10-VH, residues S30, S31, Y32, R52, Y52a, G96, N97, S98, G99, and Y100a were identified as potential contact residues, and residues Y48, Y49, D50, F52, and K65 were identified within H10-VL.

In summary, epitope mapping identified residues within the ECD III that are recognized by the novel EGFR antibodies A7 and H10 and are distinct from those interacting with other EGFR antibodies. Our results further demonstrate binding of the A7 and H10 antibodies to several clinically relevant EGFR escape mutants. Of note, A02 and H10 bound EGFR-wt and the various EGFR escape mutations in ELISA with similar EC50 values ranging from 0.20 to 0.25 nM, except EGFR variant V441D, which resulted in slightly higher EC50 values, and EGFR variant Q435P, which showed no binding (Suppl. Figure S2).

A02 and H10 antibodies inhibit wild-type and mutant EGFR activation

To investigate the effects of A02 and H10 on the signaling of clinically relevant EGFR escape mutants, murine NIH-3T3 fibroblast cell lines expressing wild-type, G465R or S492R EGFR variants were established as test systems. Across the three cell lines, expression of similar amounts of human EGFR with the expected molecular weight was confirmed by Western Blot (Figure 3(a)). Flow cytometry measurements revealed binding of A02 and H10 antibodies to both wild-type and mutant EGFR-expressing cells. In contrast, cetuximab only bound to wild-type EGFR-expressing cells, necitumumab to wild-type and S492R EGFR-expressing cells, and with reduced efficacy to those harboring the G465R variant, while panitumumab bound to the cell lines expressing wild-type and S492R EGFR, but not to those expressing the G465 EGFR variant, in accordance with ELISA data (Figure 4(b)). To analyze effects on EGFR activation, cells were treated with the antibodies prior to stimulation with EGF. A02 and H10 effectively inhibited EGF-induced EGFR and downstream ERK phosphorylation in all three cell lines (Figure 3(c)). In contrast, cetuximab failed to inhibit the activation of the G465R and S492R EGFR variants. While necitumumab blocked the phosphorylation of wild-type and S492R EGFR and slightly that of the G465R variant, panitumumab inhibited wild-type and S492R EGFR phosphorylation but not that of the G465R variant, in accordance with the binding pattern. No wild-type EGFR phosphorylation was observed in the absence of EGF stimulation (Figure 3(c), left panel), confirming that the antibodies do not have any agonistic activity.

Generation of EGFR-directed T-cell engagers

Cetuximab and the novel anti-EGFR antibody A7 and its affinity-improved variant H10 were then used to generate bispecific EGFRxCD3 TCEs. Here, two bispecific formats, a 1 + 1 bivalent bispecific eIg (e11-1–1) and a 2 + 1 trivalent bispecific Fab-eIg (e21-1–1) format were selected based on previous studies 26 (Figure 4(a)). A humanized variant of UCHT1 (huU3) was used as a CD3 binding moiety. The TCEs (A7-TCE1 + 1, A7-TCE2 + 1, H10-TCE1 + 1, H10-TCE2 + 1, Cet-TCE1 + 1, Cet-TCE2 + 1) were expressed in transiently transfected HEK293-6E cells, purified by protein A affinity chromatography followed by a preparative SEC-FPLC. Protein purity was greater than 98% as determined by analytical SEC, and SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions confirmed the presence of all four chains of the respective TCE and their integrity under non-reducing conditions (Suppl. Figure S3).

In a first set of experiments, we compared the cetuximab-based TCEs with H10-derived TCEs. All TCEs were capable of binding to CD3-expressing Jurkat cells as well as to EGFR-expressing FaDu, DiFi, and HCT-116 cells (~17,000 EGFR/cell) (Figure 4(b,c)). Binding to Jurkat cells was approx. 10-fold lower for the 2 + 1 TCEs when compared to the 1 + 1 TCEs. Presumably, this is due to a steric hindrance of the inner CD3 binding site by the outer EGFR Fab domain, as observed previously 26 (Figure 4(b)). Binding to EGFR-expressing tumor cells was slightly increased for H10-TCE 2 + 1 when compared to H10-TCE1 + 1, indicative of an avidity effect. This effect was less pronounced for the cetuximab derivates, Cet-TCE1 + 1 and Cet-TCE2 + 1.

Next, T-cell mediated cytotoxicity was evaluated in vitro using human peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMCs) at an effector to target cell ratio of 10:1. For FaDu and HCT-116 cells, the 2 + 1 molecules H10-TCE2 + 1 and Cet-TCE2 + 1 were more potent than the matching 1 + 1 molecules (Figure 5(c)) (Table 3). Thus, for HCT116 cells the difference between the 2 + 1 and 1 + 1 molecules was 14.9- to 21.6-fold, and for FaDu cells 6.9- to 10.0-fold. For DiFi cells, which express high levels of EGFR, the differences between 1 + 1 and 2 + 1 formats were less pronounced (1.6- and 2.4-fold).

We further analyzed 1 + 1 and 2 + 1 TCEs derived from an irrelevant antibody, CB6, directed against the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. CB6-TCE1 + 1 and CB6-TCE2 + 1 showed binding to CD3-expressing Jurkat cells but no binding to HCT116 cells. No or only marginal killing (6–20%) at the highest concentrations of 10 nM was observed on HCT116 cells in co-culture experiments with human PBMCs, confirming the target-dependent activity of the EGFR-targeting TCEs (Suppl. Figure S4).

To evaluate possible effects of the affinity for the EGFR on T-cell mediated target cell killing, we next compared 1 + 1 and 2 + 1 TCEs derived from A7 and H10 (A7-TCE1 + 1, A7-TCE2 + 1, H10-TCE1 + 1, H10-TCE2 + 1). For SW620 cells expressing very low EGFR levels (~1,000/cell) a strongly increased binding was observed for both 2 + 1 TCEs compared to their 1 + 1 counterparts. For FaDu and HCT116 cells, this effect was mainly seen for the A7-derived TCEs and was less pronounced for the H10-derived TCEs, indicative of an avidity driven increase in binding for the lower-affinity EGFR binder A7 (Figure 4(d)).

Regarding cell killing activity, A7-TCE1 + 1 showed the lowest activity on all three cell lines. On FaDu and HCT-116 cells, H10-TCE1 + 1 was 2.9- and 4.9-fold more potent than A7-TCE1 + 1, while for A7-TCE2 + 1 and H10-TCE2 + 1 no differences in cell killing activity were observed (Figure 4(d)). Similar effects were observed for SW620 cells, although maximal killing reached only approximately 50%.

Taken together, all TCEs showed effective T-cell mediated cytotoxicity which was dependent on their valency for EGFR and the EGFR expression levels on the target cells. Of note, the TCEs were also capable of mediating the killing of HCT-116 and SW620 cells carrying a mutated KRAS gene.

TCE-mediated killing of cancer cells with EGFR mutations

To next evaluate the in vitro and in vivo efficacy of the TCEs, we utilized the murine CT-26 cancer cell line as a model system. These cells lack endogenous EGFR expression and bear an activating G12D mutation in the KRAS gene.28 Successful stable expression of wildtype human EGFR, EGFR-G465R or EGFR-S492 in the CT-26 cell line was confirmed by Western blot analyses (Figure 5(a)). A quantitative flow cytometry analysis revealed surface expression levels ranging from 98,000 to 135,000 receptors per cell. H10 IgG was capable of binding to CT-26 cells expressing wild-type EGFR, EGFR-G465R or EGFR-S492, while cetuximab only bound to wild-type EGFR-expressing CT-26 cells (Figure 5(b)).

Using these cell lines, we then assessed the functional activity of the cetuximab and H10 based TCEs. We observed efficient killing of wild-type EGFR-expressing cells in all cases, with the 2 + 1 formats being more potent than the 1 + 1 formats (Figure 5(c)). In the absence of EGFR expression (parental CT-26), none of the TCEs showed cytotoxicity. Importantly, for cells expressing the G465R and S492R mutants, only the TCE based on H10 showed cytotoxicity. Here, the trivalent, H10-TCE2 + 1 was 3- and 12-fold more potent compared to the bivalent, bispecific molecule H10-TCE1 + 1. TCEs based on cetuximab did not induce any killing of cells expressing the mutated EGFR variants (Figure 5(c)). This demonstrates that the bispecific TCE based on H10 effectively mediates T-cell dependent cytotoxicity in the presence of both ECD III mutations and mutations in intracellular signaling pathways downstream of the EGFR (mutated KRAS).

The novel EGFR-directed TCE suppresses tumor growth in vivo

To investigate tumor growth inhibition of H10-based TCEs in vivo, we used a syngeneic mouse model implanted with CT-26 cells expressing wild-type EGFR. As the CD3-binding arm of the bispecific TCE investigated in vitro is not cross-reactive with murine CD3 (humanized variant of UCHT1), a set of surrogate molecules comprising the anti-CD3 moiety 2C11,29 and an adapted composition were generated (H10-sTCE1 + 1, H10-sTCE2 + 1) (Figure 6(a)). The surrogates were successfully purified by protein A affinity chromatography from the supernatant of transiently transfected HEK293-6E cells followed by a preparative size exclusion chromatography-fast protein liquid chromatography (SEC-FPLC). Purities >98% were observed by analytical SEC-high performance LC (HPLC) and sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) revealed the presence of the individual chains under reducing conditions, as well as protein integrity under non-reducing conditions.

T-cell activation and antitumor activity of surrogate 1 + 1 and 2 + 1 H10 TCEs. a) Binding of H10-sTCE1 + 1 and H10-sTCE2 + 1 to isolated mouse splenocytes. b) Activation of CD4+ and CD8+ splenocytes by TCEs in co-culture with CT-26 EGFRwt cells. c) Tumor growth inhibition mediated by different doses of H10-sTCE1 + 1 and H10-sTCE2 + 1 in a CT-26 EGFRwt syngeneic model in mice. d) Comparison of tumor volumes at day 17.

The surrogate TCEs bound to human wild-type EGFR as analyzed by ELISA (data not shown) as well as to CD3 on murine splenocytes (Figure 6(a)). Binding of the bivalent, bispecific TCE (H10-sTCE1 + 1) to EGFR was 43-fold reduced compared to the trivalent, bispecific TCE (H10-sTCE2 + 1). In contrast, binding to CD3 increased 10-fold for H10-sTCE1 + 1 compared to H10-sTCE2 + 1 in accordance with the binding properties of H10-TCE1 + 1 and H10-TCE2 + 1. Functional assessment of the surrogates in co-culture experiments with CT-26 cells expressing human EGFRwt and murine splenocytes revealed potent early activation (CD69 expression) of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells for H10-sTCE2 + 1 (Figure 6(b)), while H10-sTCE1 + 1 was less active. Taken together, the surrogates were capable of binding to murine T-cells and activation in vitro.

For the analysis of therapeutic activity, CT-26-EGFRwt cells were injected into the left and right flanks of BALB/c mice. After reaching a medium tumor volume of 100 mm3, mice were treated with different doses of the bispecific TCE twice per week (H10-sTCE1 + 1: 25 µg, 2.5 µg, 0.25 µg / H10-sTCE2 + 1 30 µg, 3 µg, 0.3 µg). In general, reduced tumor growth was observed for all treatments compared to mice treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Figure 6(c); Suppl. Figure S5). After two treatments with 30 µg or 3 µg H10-sTCE2 + 1, tumor growth was significantly inhibited in comparison to the PBS control group, although no tumor regression was observed (Figure 6(d)). No weight loss was observed, indicating that repeated treatment with the highest dose was also well tolerated (data not shown). However, several animals developed an anti-drug antibody response, especially in the groups with the highest dose, which limited long-term analysis of tumor growth. Nevertheless, this first study demonstrates that TCE surrogates based on the novel EGFR-specific H10 antibody can suppress tumor growth in vivo.

Discussion

Acquired resistance to therapeutic EGFR blockade remains a central limitation of cetuximab- and panitumumab-based regimens in metastatic CRC and other EGFR-driven malignancies. A substantial body of clinical and translational evidence has established that a major mechanism underlying this resistance is the emergence of missense mutations in the EGFR ECD, particularly within domain III, which disrupt antibody binding while largely preserving ligand-induced signaling competence.14,18 Among these, S492R and G465R have been repeatedly identified in post-treatment tumor biopsies and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) following cetuximab or panitumumab exposure. Their presence correlates strongly with renewed EGFR-MAPK pathway activation and clinical progression, highlighting the need for next-generation therapeutics capable of overcoming this escape route.30

In this context, our study provides compelling evidence that the novel anti-EGFR antibodies we developed effectively bind most of the clinically observed EGFR ECD escape mutations, in contrast to currently approved anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies displaying non-overlapping and incomplete binding profiles toward these variants. For example, cetuximab binding is disrupted by S492R and G465R mutations, whereas panitumumab is affected by a partially overlapping but distinct spectrum of alterations.12,13,31,23 These differential binding properties highlight how minor residue substitutions at the antibody/epitope interface can selectively abrogate antibody binding without impairing ligand interactions and receptor activation.

Our antibodies show binding across this landscape of escape mutations. Epitope mapping and in silico docking confirmed that the epitope is distinct from the shared epitope of the approved EGFR-targeting antibodies cetuximab, panitumumab and necitumumab, which all bind to a very similar region adjacent to the epitope of the new antibodies. Thus, the novel EGFR-targeting antibodies, especially the affinity-improved variants A02 and H10, are promising candidates to treat tumors of patients with acquired resistance to EGFR-targeting therapies resulting from EGFR ECD mutations.

Beyond EGFR inhibition, several approaches have been proposed using EGFR as a target for therapeutic interventions independently of downstream receptor signaling.32 This includes mono- and bispecific EGFR-targeting ADCs for delivery of potent cytotoxic payloads into tumor cells, antibody fusion proteins and bispecific antibodies to provide immuno-modulatory activities within the tumor, and bispecific T-cell engagers and CAR-Ts for recruitment of effector T-cells to EGFR-expressing tumor cells.33–38

In this study, we explored T-cell engagement for our novel EGFR antibodies, comparing the parental A7 antibody with the affinity improved H10, employing 1 + 1 and 2 + 1 formats. The bispecific 2 + 1 TCE derived from H10 mediated superior killing of EGFR-expressing cancer cell lines, which was especially pronounced for cells expressing low or medium receptor levels. This difference was even stronger for A7-derived TCEs, indicative of an avidity-driven increase in target cell binding.39 The 2 + 1 TCEs mediated an increased killing of EGFR-expressing cell lines compared to their 1 + 1 counterparts. This is in accordance with previous findings comparing TCEs of varying valency and geometry using cetuximab-derived EGFR binding arms.26 Similar effects were also described for CEAxCD3 TCEs.40

In vitro, the cetuximab-based TCEs demonstrated comparable potency to the H10-derived TCEs across multiple cell lines expressing wild-type EGFR, indicating that the distinct yet partially overlapping epitopes recognized by cetuximab and H10 do not substantially influence T-cell engagement. In vivo, the H10-based TCE exhibited clear anti-tumor activity in a syngeneic tumor model, although tumor regression was not observed. These findings are consistent with previous studies evaluating EGFR-targeting T-cell engagers. For example, anti-tumor activity of hEx3-Db was reported in a xenograft tumor model in SCID mice in the presence of lymphokine-activated T cells (T-LAK), with efficacy comparable to that of cetuximab.41 Similarly, T cells armed with a bispecific EGFR × CD3 antibody conjugate (EGFRBi-ATC) incorporating cetuximab as the EGFR-binding moiety demonstrated anti-tumor activity in a COLO356 xenograft model comparable to cetuximab, whereas cetuximab showed superior activity in LS174 tumors.42 This difference was proposed to result from reduced binding of EGFRBi-ATC to LS174 cells compared with COLO356 cells, which might potentially be compensated by higher dosing. In a Phase 1/2 clinical trial in patients with locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, these bispecific antibody – armed activated T cells were well tolerated and induced endogenous adaptive anti-tumor immune responses.43

Furthermore, TCEs based on H10 were capable of killing target cells expressing wild-type EGFR as well as the G465R and S492R variants. This finding clearly demonstrates that EGFR-targeting with our novel antibodies is not affected by the escape mutations. Of note, the TCEs were also capable of killing cancer cells harboring mutated KRAS, such as the human cancer cell line HCT-116 and the murine colorectal carcinoma cell line CT-26 stably expressing human EGFR.28 Thus, the TCEs should be suited for the treatment of tumors with oncogenic alterations that confer resistance to EGFR antibodies such as cetuximab or panitumumab. In line with this, a recombinant EGFR x CD3 TCE, SMET12, was recently reported to be safe and efficacious in combination with PD-1 blockade and chemotherapy in EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs.44

Although EGFR-targeting TCEs represent a promising immunotherapeutic strategy, the clinical application of conventional CD3-engaging TCEs might be limited by the expression of EGFR in normal tissues, including the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal epithelia. Expression levels varying from 40,000 to 100,000 receptors/cell have been described for normal cells,45 which can lead to on-target, off-tumor toxicities and cytokine release. Thus, in the reported SMET12 Phase 2 study in NSCLC patients more than 50% of patients developed CRS.44 Novel approaches aim to address these safety challenges, for example, with TCEs such as SGN-EGFRd2, engaging and activating γδ T-cells specifically in the presence of EGFR-expressing tumor cells, and exploiting the phospho-antigen-enriched environment of stressed or malignant tissues to enhance tumor selectivity.46 Alternatively, conditionally active EGFR-targeting TCEs such as TAK-186 and JANX008 use protease-cleavable masking strategies.47 Preclinical studies demonstrated that these conditional TCEs maintain potent T cell-mediated cytotoxicity while markedly reducing systemic toxicity. Furthermore, we recently showed that the format as well as the arrangement and valency of EGFR-binding sites can affect not only TCE-mediated cytotoxicity, but also T-cell activation and cytokine release.26 Thus, the EGFR-targeting TCEs developed in this study could benefit from further format evaluation and additional safety measures to minimize on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Such strategies would confine T-cell engagement and cytotoxicity to the tumor microenvironment, improving the therapeutic window.

Taken together, novel EGFR-targeting and neutralizing antibodies, as well as bispecific T-cell engagers, have been developed with the potential to overcome acquired EGFR resistance. These antibodies demonstrate activity against both wild-type EGFR and therapy-emergent resistance variants, suggesting their broad applicability for EGFR-positive tumors, either as a first-line treatment or in cases of primary or acquired resistance to conventional EGFR-targeted therapies. Future studies will aim to determine whether these properties translate into enhanced antitumor efficacy in vivo and, ultimately, into meaningful clinical benefit for patients.

Materials and methods

Materials and cell lines

For analysis of species cross-reactivity of anti-EGFR antibodies, His-tagged mouse EGFR (Sino Biological #1091-M08H) and cynomolgus monkey EGFR (Sino Biological #90285-C08H) was used for coating. The sequence of anti-SARS-Cov-2 spike protein antibody CB6 was derived from published sequences.48 Human cell lines A431, DiFi, FaDu, HCT-116, Jurkat, LIM1215, SW620 and Platinum-E as well as murine fibroblast cell lines NIH-3T3 and CT-26 were cultivated at 37°C/5% CO2. Cells were cultivated in their suitable medium and split every two to four days using trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Cells were stimulated with EGFR-ligands EGF (PeproTech #100–15) and HB-EGF (Sino Biological #10325-HNAB). For detection of Fab- or IgG-binding by ELISA, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled antibodies directed against human Fab (Sigma-Aldrich #A0293) or human Fc (Sigma-Aldrich #A0170) were used, respectively. For detection of cell binding for IgG or TCE by flow cytometry, R-PE-coupled antibody directed against human Fc (Jackson ImmunoResearch #109–115-098) was used. For detection of EGFR expression by cancer cells lines, cells were first stained with anti-EGFR antibody (clone AY13, BioLegend #352902) followed by secondary, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-coupled antibody directed against murine IgG (Agilent #D0479). For Western Blot, primary antibodies directed against EGFR (Santa Cruz #sc-03), pEGFR Y1068 (Cell Signaling #3777), ERK (Cell Signaling #9107), pERK T202/Y204 (Cell Signaling #9101), α-tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich #05–829) and secondary, HRP-conjugated antibodies directed against rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch #111–035-144) or mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch #115–035-062) were used. For analysis of TCE-binding to CD3+ splenocytes isolated from BALB/c mice, cells were stained with FITC-coupled anti-mouse-CD3 antibody (Miltenyi Biotec #130–119-758). For analysis of early T-cell activation for co-cultures of CT-26 cells expressing human EGFRwt and splenocytes isolated from BALB/c mice, T cells were stained with antibodies directed against mouse CD3 (Miltenyi Biotec #130–119-758), CD4 (Miltenyi Biotec #130–118-568), CD8 (Miltenyi Biotec #130–123-781) and CD69 (Miltenyi Biotec #130–115-461). Buffy coats for isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were from anonymous donors and obtained and certified by DRK Blutspendedienst Baden-Württemberg/Hessen, Institut für Klinische Transfusionsmedizin und Immungenetik Ulm gemeinnützige GmbH, Germany (an ethical votum is not required). PBMCs from buffy coats were purified by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (Lymphocyte Separation Medium 1077, Promocell, C-44010) and cryopreserved in FCS containing 10% DMSO at −80°C. For functional assays, PBMCs were thawed and rested overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2 to allow monocyte adherence, and non-adherent lymphocytes were used as effector cells.

Recombinant protein production

Both antigens and antibodies were produced by transient transfection of HEK293-6E cells as described previously.49 This included the ECD of human EGFR fused to a His-tag or a mouse Fc as well as Fabs, IgGs and TCEs used in this study. Therefore, encoding sequences were cloned into pSecTagA-based expression vectors. Mutations in the ECD of human EGFR were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis (New England Biolabs #E0554S). Recombinant protein was isolated from supernatants using protein A resin (Fc-comprising molecules; Cytiva #17–1279-03), CH1-XL resin (Fabs; Thermo Scientific #1943462010) or Ni-NTA resin (His-tagged antigens; Macherey-Nagel; 745,400.100). Protein was eluted using 100 mM glycine pH 3.5 (protein A and CH1-XL resin) or 250 mM imidazole (Ni-NTA resin). Eluted proteins were dialyzed against PBS overnight. Proteins were further purified with a preparative SEC by FPLC. Isolated proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and SEC as described previously.26

Library generation

Previously, a VL gene library was generated from the naïve B-cell repertoire of 62 human donors and cloned into YUMAB’s phage-display vector. The final VL library contained a theoretical diversity of ~7×108 lambda and ~1×109 kappa VL genes, respectively (data not shown). In a second cloning step, the isolated VH gene of the EGFR-specific antibodies was inserted into these vectors, generating a functional single-chain variable fragment (scFv)-pIII fusion protein. The final vectors were transformed into E. coli and packaged into antibody-phage, achieving final library sizes (# of transformants) of 3.0 × 108 (lambda) and 0.8 × 108 (kappa) clones with approximately 75% of clones comprising full-length scFv sequences.

Antibody-phage selection and antibody characterization

For the selection of VL-shuffled antibodies with improved binding properties, an affinity-driven biopanning was performed. First, cross-reactive antibody-phage were removed from the library by incubation with M-280 Dynabeads™ (25 µl, ThermoScientific, #11206D) and mouse IgG (10 µg) in a 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA)-PBS with Tween 20 solution for 1 h at room temperature (RT). The cleared library was subsequently challenged with a limiting amount of soluble biotinylated EGFR ECD in solution. In panning round (PR) one, 20 nM antigen was used for positive selection. After 1 h of incubation, target-specific antibody-phage were isolated by magnetic pull-down using the M-280 Dynabeads. After removal of nonspecific and weakly-bound phage by washing, target-specific phages were eluted by trypsin treatment. The phages were rescued by E. coli infection, propagated via M13K07 co-infection, and used for the next panning cycle. In PR two, the amount of target antigen for positive selection was reduced to 2 and 0.2 nM. After 1 h incubation, the non-biotinylated target antigen was added in excess (75 nM) to the selection mix, thereby preventing antibodies with a fast off-rate to rebind to the biotinylated antigen. The antibody-phage with best affinity that remained bound to the biotinylated antigen were then isolated by magnetic pull-down and rescued as before.

Individual clones from the second PR were isolated and cultivated in 96-well plates before scFv production was induced by addition of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside. The culture supernatants were then used for ELISA screening on the target (Streptavidin-captured EGFR ECD) and negative antigens (streptavidin only, mouse IgG, BSA). 88 antibody clones with an ELISA signal-to-noise ratio >100 were selected for DNA-sequence analysis. Twelve sequence-unique clones were identified.

The identified scFv genes were subcloned in mammalian expression vectors and produced as scFv-Fc fusion proteins in HEK cells by transient transfection. After Protein A affinity chromatography of the culture supernatants, the binding characteristics of the purified antibodies were validated by ELISA. Two lead candidates were identified (YU250-A02, YU250-H10), which were then produced as human IgG as described above.

ELISA

Binding of Fabs, IgGs and TCEs to EGFR ECD (aa 25–645) and mutated variants thereof fused to a murine Fc-part or a His-tag was analyzed by ELISA as described previously.25 For epitope-blocking ELISA, plates coated with EGFR were first incubated with 200 nM Fab H10 (primary saturating antibody) followed by an incubation step where 1 nM IgG (secondary blocking antibody) is spiked into 200 nM Fab. Binding of the IgG upon epitope blocking by the Fab was detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-human-Fc antibody.

Determination of KD using BLI

The binding affinity was determined by BLI using FAB2G sensors on an Octet® QKe system (ForteBio). First, a baseline measurement was performed for 120 sec at 1,000 rpm and RT in assay buffer (PBS, supplemented with 2% BSA and 0.05% Tween 20). Then, Fab antibodies (2.5 µg/mL) were loaded onto the sensors for 180 sec. After another baseline measurement, the association of an antigen concentration series (500 nM, 158 nM, 50 nM, 15.8 nM, 5 nM, 1.58 nM, 0.5 nM, and 0 nM) was measured for 300 sec. The dissociation of the antigen was measured for 300 sec in assay buffer only. The KD was calculated with a 1:1 binding model using the Data Analysis HT software (version 11.1.2.48). Traces with a significant signal (>0.05 nm) and an antigen concentration ranging from 0.1–10x of the KD were used for analysis.

Structure prediction and docking

The structures of the H10 Fv and the antigen EGFR-ECDIIImut (DIIImut) were predicted using AlphaFold3.50 The models with the highest confidence scores were used for subsequent docking experiments. Protein – protein docking was performed using the HADDOCK 2.4 web server 51,52 using the H10 Fv and EGFR-ECDIIImut models as input molecules. For the Fv, a solvent-exposed residue (S98) located within the complementarity-determining region (CDR) H3 loop was defined as the active residue. Passive residues were defined as residues located within the CDRs with a solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) greater than 20 Å2, calculated in PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0 Schrödinger, LLC). For the antigen DIIImut, residues experimentally identified to be involved in binding were defined as active residues. Passive residues were defined as residues with a SASA greater than 20 Å2 that were located within 4 Å of the active residues. Docking was performed using the standard HADDOCK protocol, consisting of rigid-body energy minimization (it0), semi-flexible refinement in torsion angle space (it1), and final refinement in explicit solvent. Docked models were clustered based on the fraction of common contacts (FCC). The best cluster was selected based on the HADDOCK score, and the top-ranked model from this cluster was used for subsequent interface analysis using PyMOL.

Flow cytometry

Binding of antibodies to cells was analyzed by flow cytometry as described previously 49 using a MACSQuant VYB (Miltenyi Biotec). Data were analyzed using FlowJo (Tree Star), and relative MFI was calculated as follows: relative MFI = ((MFIsample - (MFIdetection - MFIcells)) / MFIcells) (MFIcells = median fluorescence of cells alone, MFIdetection = median fluorescence of cells incubated with secondary detection antibody, MFIsample = median fluorescence of cells incubated with primary antibody followed by detection antibody). The unspecific binding of the detection antibody (MFIdetection-MFIcells) was subtracted from the value of the sample (MFIsample). The relative MFI was then calculated by dividing this value by the MFI of cells (MFIcells).

Isolation of splenocytes

Splenocytes were isolated from BALB/c mice by dissociating spleen into DPBS using a 40 µm cell strainer. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 5 min at RT before discarding the supernatant and resuspending cells in 5 mL erythrocyte lysis buffer (55 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.4 mM NaOH / pH 8.0) and incubation on ice for 5 min. Afterward, 45 mL RPMI +10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) were added and cells were harvested by centrifugation as described before. Isolated cells were resuspended in RPMI +10% FBS +50 µM β-mercaptoethanol + Penicillin-Streptomycin.

Signaling inhibition analysis by Western Blot

Cells were seeded in cultivation medium (10% FBS) and incubated for 24 h at 37°C/5% CO2. Next, medium was replaced to starvation medium (0.2% FBS) and cells were incubated for another 24 h. For treatment, IgGs were diluted to 100 nM in starvation medium and cells were incubated for 1 h prior to adding EGF to a final concentration of 50 ng/mL. After stimulation for 15 min, cells were analyzed for activation of the EGFR signaling pathway by Western blot as described previously.53

Inhibition of cell proliferation

Cells were seeded in cultivation medium (10% FBS) and incubated for 24 h at 37°C/5% CO2. Next, medium was replaced to starvation medium (0.2% FBS) and cells were incubated for another 24 h. For treatment, IgG were diluted to 10 nM (without stimulation) or 100 nM (with stimulation) in starvation medium (0.2% FBS) and cells were incubated for 1 h prior to adding ligands to a final concentration of 1 ng/µL (EGF) or of 3 ng/µL (HB-EGF). After incubation for 120 h, proliferation was analyzed using CellTiter-Glo™ 2.0 (PROMEGA) as described previously.53

Generation of stable transfected cell lines

Stable CT-26 and NIH-3T3 cell lines expressing EGFR wild-type or variants thereof were generated as described previously.54 Therefore, the full-length receptor sequence was cloned into a retroviral vector containing a Puromycin resistance gene (pMSCV-LTR-Puro) for production of murine stem cell virus using the retroviral packaging cell line Platinum-E. NIH-3T3 or CT-26 cells were infected three times with supernatant harvested from the retroviral packaging cell line and one day after the last infection, 10 µg/mL (CT-26 cell lines) or 1 µg/mL (NIH-3T3 cell lines) Puromycin (Sigma Aldrich) was added for selection. To obtain cell lines with uniform receptor expression, cells were stained with 100 nM antibodies binding to EGFR (EGFR wild-type: Cetuximab/EGFR G465R or S492R: GC1118) and an R-PE-conjugated antibody binding to the human Fc-part. Uniform populations were sorted using a FACSAria™ III Cell Sorter. Generated cell lines were maintained in RPMI +10% FBS +10 mM HEPES +1 mM sodium pyruvate +1% non-essential amino acids +10 µg/mL Puromycin (CT-26) or DMEM +10% FBS +1 µg/mL Puromycin (NIH-3T3).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay of TCEs

Cytotoxicity assays for investigation of T cell-mediated killing of cultured cells were performed as described previously49 using an effector-to-target ratio of 10:1.

In vivo tumor model

Animal studies were approved by the University of Stuttgart and governmental authorities. 106 CT-26 cells expressing EGFR wild-type in 100 µL DPBS were injected into the left and right flank of female BALB/c mice. Treatment with surrogates of TCE targeting EGFR was initiated upon reaching a mean tumor volume of ~100 mm3 (V[mm3] = max(length, width) [mm] x min(length, width)2 / 2, length and width were measured with a caliper). Mice were treated twice per week with indicated doses in 100 µL DPBS by intravenous injection into the tail vein. Mice were sacrificed when reaching a tumor volume >1,300 mm3, the observation of necrotic tumor, or a weight loss by ≥15%.

Statistics

If not stated elsewhere, all experiments were performed three times. For co-culture experiments with PBMCs from healthy donors (cytotoxicity, T-cell activation), three different donors were analyzed. Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7. In vivo tumor growth inhibition studies are shown as mean ± SEM. All other data is shown as mean ± S.D. Significance between multiple groups was analyzed by One-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc test for multiple comparison. p values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant (p < 0.05: * / p < 0.01: ** / p < 0.001: *** / p < 0.0001: **** / p ≥ 0.05: n.s. [not significant]).
