Barriers and Capacity for Prosthetic Dental Care in Myanmar: A Cross-sectional Survey
Authors: Tun Min Bo, Duangporn Duangthip, Nay Aung, Palinee Detsomboonrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and AimsLimited evidence exists on prosthetic dental care in Myanmar. This study aimed to assess prosthodontic service capacity, practitioner-perceived barriers, teledentistry use, and training needs.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among dentists providing prosthodontic treatment in Myanmar (November-December 2025), using convenience sampling. The questionnaire covered demographics, service capacity, barriers, teledentistry, and training needs. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests with Bonferroni correction, and logistic regression were used.ResultsAmong 367 dentists, most practiced in urban areas (71.9%) and private clinics (79.0%). The majority of respondents reported providing crowns, bridges, and dentures, while 29.4% offered implants. High-volume delivery (>50 units/month) was reported by 36.2%, associated with male sex (adjusted odds ratio, 2.88) and ≥10 years’ experience (adjusted odds ratio, 3.60). Leading barriers to providing prosthetics were patients’ financial constraints (87.5%), low patient awareness regarding prosthetic treatment options (71.4%), and high prosthetic material costs (70.8%). Barriers related to material and equipment availability, patient fear, and technician access varied significantly across geographic regions (P < .05). Teledentistry use was low (30.8%), and top training needs were digital dentistry (88.6%) and implant prosthodontics (82.0%).ConclusionsProsthodontic services in Myanmar remain predominantly urban-centred and privately delivered, with significant structural, geographic, and economic barriers affecting equitable access to care. Workforce development, laboratory support, and digital dentistry training may strengthen service delivery. Teledentistry remains underutilised; while early adopters view it positively, poor internet and training gaps are major barriers to wider implementation.Clinical RelevanceThis study provides preliminary practitioner-based evidence regarding prosthetic dental service capacity and barriers to care in Myanmar and may help inform workforce planning and digital oral health strategies in similar low-resource settings.

Introduction

Oral health is a key determinant of overall health and well-being, with established links to nutrition, communication, psychosocial functioning, and economic participation.1,2 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) including Myanmar, tooth loss and untreated partial tooth loss remain common due to dental caries and periodontal diseases.3,4 Limited access to tooth replacement services results in avoidable functional impairment and ongoing oral health disparities.5,6 Prosthodontic care, which includes removable, fixed, and implant-supported rehabilitation, is central to restoring masticatory efficiency and quality of life, yet its delivery in fragile health systems is constrained by significant structural and economic barriers.7,8

Myanmar, the largest country in mainland Southeast Asia with a population of approximately 54 million, exemplifies such a setting, characterised by pronounced urban-rural disparities, an uneven distribution of the dental workforce, and constrained investment in specialised services.9 The country faces a critical oral health crisis where tooth extraction remains the standard solution for dental caries, leading to an average loss of 9 to 10 teeth among the elderly population. National survey data reveal a caries prevalence of 85.3% in 6-year-olds and nearly universal decay (93.6%) in adults aged 60 to 74 years, with the majority of decayed teeth left untreated.3 These figures indicate a massive potential demand for prosthetic and restorative care that remains unquantified in current research. However, no comprehensive national data are currently available regarding whether crowns, bridges, removable dentures, or other prosthetic treatments are more commonly provided in routine dental practice in Myanmar.

This burden is exacerbated by a severe workforce shortage. According to the Myanmar Dental Association, approximately 4000 dentists are currently practicing in Myanmar, giving the country only 6.8 dentists per 100,000 population. There are no dental hygienist services and only a few dental nurses. Comprehensive national data regarding workforce composition, including the number of prosthodontists and other dental professionals involved in prosthetic care, or the presence of alternative providers such as denturists, remain limited. Furthermore, the oral health care system, overseen by the Ministry of Health, consists of a public-private combination where public facilities are often underequipped and human resources are insufficient to reach the entire population, particularly in rural and conflict-affected regions.3,9

While Myanmar is transitioning towards universal health coverage through its National Health Plan, oral health remains a low political priority.10 The health budget, despite recent increases to 3.4% of total government expenditure, remains the lowest in the Asia-Pacific region, and oral health receives only a small share of this allocation. Access to dental care may be further constrained by multiple types of disruptions. In some regions, natural disasters such as floods and cyclones can damage infrastructure, interrupt service delivery, and disrupt supply chains. In contrast, ongoing conflict and political instability in several rural areas may lead to prolonged health care disruptions, including reduced workforce availability, facility closures, and restricted access to services, particularly in underserved communities.11 Similar challenges, including disrupted infrastructure, fragile supply chains, and reduced household purchasing power, have been reported to limit access to prosthodontic care in other low-resource and conflict-affected settings as well.12,13

Digital health strategies, including teledentistry, are proposed as potential tools to extend access to specialised care in geographically dispersed and resource-constrained settings.14, 15, 16 Teledentistry involves the use of information and communication technologies for remote consultation, triage, and professional collaboration.17, 18, 19 Despite the potential relevance of digital approaches in underserved settings, evidence regarding prosthetic service capacity, geographic disparities in service provision, practitioner-perceived barriers, and readiness for digital prosthodontic care in Myanmar remains limited. Consequently, there is an absence of evidence regarding how dental practitioners in Myanmar perceive the balance between patient need and clinical capacity, the relative salience of different barriers to care, or their training priorities in both conventional and digital prosthodontics. Addressing this evidence gap is essential for informing effective, context-sensitive strategies to enhance prosthetic service delivery.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) assess prosthetic service capacity and related factors among dental practitioners providing prosthetic care in Myanmar, (2) compare perceived barriers to prosthetic care across different geographical areas (urban, rural nonconflict, and rural conflict-affected), (3) identify practitioner characteristics associated with higher prosthetic service delivery, and (4) examine dentists’ current use of and perceptions towards teledentistry. By providing practitioner-reported data, this study aims to generate evidence to inform policy development, workforce planning, and strategies to improve equitable access to prosthetic services in Myanmar.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive cross-sectional online survey of licensed dental practitioners in Myanmar was conducted between November and December 2025.

Participants and sampling

Dentists who were actively engaged in clinical practice in Myanmar at the time of data collection were invited to join the study. A nonprobability convenience sampling approach was used. Because a probability-based national sampling frame was not available, convenience sampling was considered the most feasible approach for this exploratory nationwide survey. The survey was disseminated via professional networks (Myanmar Dental Association [MDA], regional societies), social media groups for dental professionals, and direct messaging (eg, Viber, Facebook, email). The survey link was openly distributed within professional dental networks and social media communities. An online recruitment approach was used because dental practitioners are geographically dispersed across Myanmar, and access to some regions remains challenging due to infrastructure and security constraints. Because rural and public-sector dentists are scarce in Myanmar, targeted efforts were made to recruit practitioners from these settings to improve representation in the sample. This was done by sending the survey link through MDA regional organisations. Participation was voluntary, and responses were anonymous. Dentists who were not in active practice or who submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded. As the survey was distributed through multiple professional networks and social media platforms, the exact number of dentists who received or viewed the survey invitation could not be determined; therefore, a response rate could not be calculated.

The sample size was calculated to estimate the proportion of dentists with key indicators of prosthodontic service capacity (eg, availability of services and resources). Based on an estimated national dental workforce of approximately 4000 practitioners (MDA, 2024), the minimum required sample size for estimating a proportion with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error was 351, assuming a conservative proportion of 50% due to the lack of prior national data. In addition, the achieved sample size was considered adequate for exploratory analyses of associations, as it exceeded the minimum requirement for proportion estimation and provided sufficient observations across subgroups to support regression analyses.

The questionnaire comprised 5 sections:1.Demographic and practice characteristics, including sex, age group, professional designation, years of experience, practice location, and practice setting. Practice location was categorised as urban, rural nonconflict, or rural conflict-affected. Rural conflict-affected areas were defined as regions experiencing ongoing security instability and service disruptions during the study period, while rural nonconflict areas referred to rural regions without such reported disruptions.11 Practice setting was categorised as private, public, or university-based.2.Prosthetic service capacity and perceived patient demand (types of prosthetic services provided, monthly prosthetic treatment volume, proportion of patients requiring prosthodontic treatment, and laboratory support availability); for this study, a prosthetic unit referred to a single prosthodontic restoration or appliance delivered to a patient (eg, crown, bridge unit, denture case, or implant-supported prosthesis).3.Potential perceived barriers to providing prosthetic care, including lack of patient awareness about prosthetic options, low patient demand due to financial constraints, unpleasant experience (eg, anxiety or fear), high costs of prosthetic materials, difficulty accessing trained dental technicians, and limited availability of prosthetic materials/equipment). Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (never, rarely, sometimes, often, very often).4.Teledentistry use, challenges, and attitudes, covering prior adoption, communication tools used, implementation challenges, 8 perception statements (rated on a 5-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”), and willingness to adopt teledentistry in the future.5.Perceived importance of continuous prosthodontic training, training needs in specific areas of prosthodontics (eg, complete dentures, removable partial dentures, fixed prosthodontics, implant prosthodontics, maxillofacial prosthetics, digital dentistry, and occlusion/Temporomandibular disorder (TMD)), and preferred training formats for improving prosthodontic competence (eg, hands-on, simulation-based, and mentorship-based training).

The questionnaire was developed through a structured review of the relevant literature on dental prosthetic services, barriers to care, and teledentistry, followed by refinement of the questionnaire items based on expert feedback to ensure content relevance and contextual appropriateness for Myanmar.14,16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 The questionnaire was developed using a structured and theory-informed approach. Item generation was guided by Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use24 and the World Health Organization Health Systems Framework25 to ensure coverage of key domains, including service capacity, workforce characteristics, access-related barriers, and system-level factors. Relevant items were identified through a review of the literature on prosthodontic service provision, access to care, and teledentistry and were adapted to the context of Myanmar. The initial item pool was subsequently evaluated by a panel of 3 prosthodontists and 2 public health researchers to establish content validity. Face validity and clarity were further assessed through pilot testing with 15 dental practitioners, and minor revisions were made before the final survey was administered. The instrument was piloted with 15 practitioners (data excluded from analysis), confirming clarity, comprehension, and an average completion time of 10 to 12 minutes.

Because the barrier items (6 items) and teledentistry perception items (8 items) were analysed as individual items rather than composite scales, internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) was not calculated. This approach was considered because the study aims to explore item-specific practitioner perceptions and service-related factors rather than develop or validate a psychometric measurement scale. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Appendix S1.

Data collection

The survey was administered electronically via Google Forms. The survey link was openly distributed through professional dental networks and social media platforms commonly used by dental practitioners in Myanmar. To reduce duplicate submissions, the “Limit to 1 response” setting was enabled, requiring respondents to sign in with their Google account and permitting only 1 submission per account. A total of 410 responses were received. After excluding incomplete questionnaires (n = 43), complete responses from 367 dentists were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure S1). Mandatory response settings were enabled to minimise incomplete submissions. Participation was voluntary, and responses were collected without recording personal identifiers. Although IP tracking was not used due to privacy considerations, restricting responses to 1 Google account minimised duplicate entries. No duplicate response patterns were identified during data cleaning. No financial or material incentives were provided for participation. Survey completion was considered to imply informed consent. The questionnaire was administered via Google Forms with mandatory response settings enabled to prevent missing data. Participants could review and revise their responses before submission. The participant flow is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Reporting followed the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies and the CHERRIES checklist for internet-based surveys (Supplementary Checklist S1).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University (Approval No. HREC-DCU 2025-146), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed of the study’s voluntary nature and their right to withdraw.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 30 (SPSS, Inc). Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarise demographic characteristics, prosthetic service capacity, perceived barriers, teledentistry use, and training needs. For barrier analysis, Likert scale responses were dichotomised (sometimes/often/very often = “barrier present”) to facilitate interpretation of barrier prevalence and comparison across practice locations in this exploratory analysis. Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between perceived barriers and practice location, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0167) where appropriate. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify practitioner characteristics associated with high-volume prosthetic delivery (>50 units/month). Variables entered into the regression model included sex, years of experience, practice setting, and practice location based on their potential relevance to prosthetic service provision. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the robustness of the dichotomisation approach, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a stricter cutoff (ie, only responses of “often” or “very often” were classified as barrier present). The results are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

A sensitivity analysis using an alternative cutoff (“often/very often” vs all other responses) yielded lower overall prevalence estimates but confirmed that financial constraints, lack of patient awareness, and high material costs remained the 3 most frequently reported barriers, consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

In contrast, significant geographic differences were observed for the remaining 3 barriers. Limited availability of prosthetic materials/equipment was reported by 61.0% of urban practitioners, compared to 78.6% in rural nonconflict areas and 87.9% in rural conflict-affected areas (overall P < .001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0167) revealed that both rural nonconflict (P = .004) and rural conflict-affected (P < .001) areas had significantly higher rates than urban areas, while the 2 rural groups did not differ significantly from each other (P = .239). Unpleasant patient experiences or fear were reported by 57.2% of urban practitioners, 74.3% of rural nonconflict practitioners, and 84.8% of rural conflict-affected practitioners (overall P < .001). Pairwise comparisons showed that both rural groups differed significantly from the urban group (P = .007 for rural nonconflict; P < .001 for rural conflict-affected) but not from each other (P = .228). For difficulty accessing trained dental technicians, reported rates were 60.2% (urban), 65.7% (rural nonconflict), and 84.8% (rural conflict-affected) (overall P = .015). Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction indicated that only rural conflict-affected areas differed significantly from urban areas (P = .003). The difference between rural nonconflict and urban areas (P = .381) and between the 2 rural groups (P = .037) did not reach statistical significance after correction.

Teledentistry adoption, perceptions, and training needs

A minority of respondents (30.8%, n = 113) reported using teledentistry for prosthetic care. Among these users, the most common communication tools were phone calls (93.8%) and Viber (92.0%). The principal barriers to using teledentistry were poor internet connectivity (85.0%) and lack of professional training in teledentistry (69.0%). Despite these challenges, 93.8% of current users expressed willingness to adopt teledentistry more broadly in future practice.

Among respondents with prior experience, perceptions of teledentistry were generally positive. Most agreed that it improves communication (92.9%), supports patient education (90.3%) and follow-up (90.3%), and improves access to care (88.5%). Moderate agreement was observed for its role in reducing in-person visits (69.0%), providing reliable diagnostics (61.9%), supporting remote treatment planning (60.2%), and decreasing workload (57.5%), with a notable proportion of neutral responses for these items.

Respondents also reported substantial training needs. The highest demand was for digital dentistry (Computer-Aided Design and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAD/CAM), 3-dimensional printing) (88.6%), followed by implant prosthodontics (82.0%) and occlusion/temporomandibular disorders (55.6%). Other areas included maxillofacial prosthetics (50.4%), complete dentures (43.6%), fixed prosthodontics (33.5%), and removable partial dentures (33.5%). Hands-on practical workshops were the most preferred training format (93.7%), followed by mentorship or clinical observation (51.2%), short-term certificate courses (50.4%), formal advanced degree programs (44.4%), simulation-based training (41.4%), online theoretical courses (39.5%), and interactive seminars or conferences (36.0%).

Discussion

This study provides one of the first practitioner-based assessments of prosthetic dental service delivery in Myanmar and highlights several structural barriers affecting access to prosthodontic care. The findings suggest that although many dental practitioners provide basic prosthetic treatments such as removable dentures and simple fixed restorations, more advanced procedures remain limited. This distribution pattern may reflect the concentration of specialised services, trained personnel, laboratory support, and advanced prosthodontic resources within urban private-sector settings, where access to complex restorative care is generally greater. This pattern reflects broader trends observed in many LMICs, where oral health systems often prioritise emergency and basic dental care while more complex restorative treatments are less accessible due to limitations in infrastructure, workforce capacity, and financial resources.26 In Myanmar, where out-of-pocket expenditure remains a major component of health care access, financial and geographic barriers may further restrict access to advanced prosthodontic rehabilitation, particularly for patients in rural and underserved communities.

Geographic disparities also emerged as a major factor affecting prosthetic service delivery. Practitioners working in conflict-affected or remote regions reported greater challenges related to shortages of materials, equipment, and laboratory services. Similar inequalities in oral health service distribution have been widely reported in LMICs, where dental professionals and health care resources are often concentrated in urban areas while rural populations remain underserved.27 In Myanmar, these disparities may be further compounded by limitations in transportation infrastructure, supply-chain instability, and restricted access to specialised prosthodontic services and trained dental technicians in underserved regions. These disparities likely reflect structural inequalities in the health system, including uneven infrastructure and workforce distribution. Subgroup analysis showed that gaps in material availability and patient-related barriers (eg, prior negative experiences and fear) were more pronounced in rural than urban areas, while limited access to dental technicians was a distinct challenge in conflict-affected settings. These findings underscore the need for context-specific strategies, including strengthening supply chains, improving patient engagement, and expanding technical workforce support in underserved areas. Targeted continuing professional education and improved distribution of prosthodontic resources may also help reduce disparities in access to restorative dental care across different geographic settings.

Financial barriers were a major challenge identified by practitioners in this study, with many reporting that patients were unable to afford prosthetic treatment due to high costs. In many LMICs, dental care is largely financed through out-of-pocket payments, creating substantial barriers to access. Previous studies have also highlighted the significant economic burden of dental diseases on both health care systems and households.28, 29, 30 In Myanmar, where out-of-pocket expenditure remains a major component of health care access, the financial burden associated with prosthodontic rehabilitation may be particularly substantial for patients requiring complex or laboratory-dependent treatments. Consistent with these findings, low patient demand driven by financial constraints emerged as the most commonly reported barrier, alongside limited patient awareness and the high cost of prosthetic materials. The lack of significant variation across practice locations suggests that these challenges are systemic rather than geographically specific. These findings may indicate that economic constraints affect not only patient access to prosthodontic care but also the capacity of practitioners to provide more advanced restorative services in resource-constrained settings.

In addition to financial constraints, workforce and laboratory capacity also play a critical role in prosthetic service provision. Shortages of dental professionals, dental technicians, and laboratory resources have been reported in many developing health systems; these shortages can restrict the availability of prosthodontic services.31, 32, 33, 34, 35 In this study, most practitioners relied on external dental laboratories for prosthetic fabrication. While common in many health care systems, reliance on centralised laboratory services can create logistical challenges and treatment delays, particularly in geographically remote areas. In Myanmar, where specialised prosthodontic services and laboratory resources are concentrated primarily in urban settings, these limitations may further affect the availability of advanced restorative care in rural and underserved communities. Together, these findings highlight the interplay of demand-side constraints (eg, affordability and low oral health literacy) and supply-side limitations (eg, workforce shortages and laboratory capacity), underscoring the need for integrated, system-level strategies. Strengthening technical workforce capacity and improving access to laboratory-supported prosthodontic services may therefore be important for reducing disparities in prosthetic care delivery.

Another notable finding of this study was the growing interest in digital technologies and remote communication tools among dental practitioners. Some respondents reported using digital platforms to communicate with patients or colleagues, suggesting a gradual adoption of teledentistry practices. Evidence suggests that teledentistry can improve access to care in underserved areas through remote consultation, screening, and follow-up, although its implementation depends on adequate infrastructure, digital literacy, and system integration.36,37 In Myanmar, ongoing challenges related to internet connectivity, digital infrastructure, and unequal access to technology may influence the feasibility of wider teledentistry implementation, particularly in rural and conflict-affected settings. Reported training needs further highlight priorities for workforce development. Practitioners expressed interest in advanced techniques (eg, digital dentistry and implant-supported prostheses) alongside strengthening foundational skills. Similar increases in demand for digital prosthodontic training and CAD/CAM-related competencies have been reported in other developing health care settings. For example, a recent cross-sectional study among dentists in the Dominican Republic also identified high demand for CAD/CAM training, with cost and lack of formal education as major barriers to adoption.38 These findings underscore the importance of continuing professional education to enhance treatment capacity and maintain clinical competence. Expanding short-term training, supporting capacity-building in underserved areas, and strengthening dental technician training, laboratory infrastructure, and material supply chains may further improve service availability and reduce delays. Targeted workforce development strategies may therefore play an important role in improving equitable access to advanced prosthodontic care in resource-constrained settings. Recent modelling from Saudi Arabia similarly highlights the need for proactive workforce planning to meet rising demand for dental prostheses, particularly as populations age and treatment expectations rise.39

This study has several limitations. The use of nonprobability sampling and online recruitment through professional networks and social media platforms may limit representativeness and generalisability to the wider dental workforce in Myanmar. Because a probability-based national sampling frame was not available, the study relied on voluntary participation, which may have introduced selection bias. Practitioners with greater interest, experience, or involvement in prosthodontic care may have been more likely to participate, potentially leading to overrepresentation of dentists actively engaged in prosthetic service delivery. In addition, dentists practicing in rural or underserved areas with limited internet access or lower participation in professional online networks may have been underrepresented. The relatively small number of respondents from some regions may reduce the power of regional comparisons.

The study was based on self-reported questionnaire data, and the reported prosthetic service volumes, treatment estimates, and practice characteristics could not be independently verified. As a result, responses may have been influenced by recall bias or reporting bias. Furthermore, the findings primarily reflect practitioner perceptions and may not fully represent patient experiences, population-level treatment needs, or health care policy perspectives. Because the survey focused on an exploratory assessment of practitioner-reported barriers and service capacity, some measures were analysed at the item level rather than as validated psychometric scales. Likert-scale responses were dichotomised for exploratory comparative analysis, which may have reduced response granularity and limited detection of more subtle variations in practitioner perceptions. In addition, teledentistry perception findings were derived primarily from respondents with prior teledentistry experience, as perceptions from nonusers were not collected. Consequently, these findings may be subject to selective reporting bias and should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between practitioner characteristics and prosthetic service delivery patterns. Despite these limitations, the study provides important preliminary evidence regarding structural, geographic, workforce-related, and economic barriers affecting prosthetic dental service delivery in Myanmar and may help inform future workforce planning, infrastructure development, and digital oral health strategies in similar resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights systemic structural and economic barriers to prosthetic service delivery in Myanmar. While basic services are widely available, access to advanced care remains limited by workforce, laboratory, and affordability constraints, with disparities more pronounced in conflict-affected areas. Improving access will require strengthening supply chains, targeted workforce training, and expansion of digital dentistry, alongside broader health system efforts to reduce inequalities.
