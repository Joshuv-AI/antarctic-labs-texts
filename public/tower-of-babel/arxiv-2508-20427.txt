Rethinking Purity and Diversity in Multi-Behavior Sequential Recommendation from the Frequency Perspective
Yongqiang Han, Kai Cheng, Kefan Wang, Enhong Chen (✉)

Abstract
In recommendation systems, users often exhibit multiple behaviors, such as browsing, clicking, and purchasing. Multi-behavior sequential recommendation (MBSR) aims to consider these different behaviors in an integrated manner to improve the recommendation performance of the target behavior. However, some behavior data will also bring inevitable noise to the modeling of user interests. Some research efforts focus on data denoising from the frequency domain perspective to improve the accuracy of user preference prediction. These studies indicate that low-frequency information tends to be valuable and reliable, while high-frequency information is often associated with noise. In this paper, we argue that high-frequency information is by no means insignificant. Further experimental results highlight that low frequency corresponds to the purity of user interests, while high frequency corresponds to the diversity of user interests. Building upon this finding, we proposed our model PDB4Rec, which efficiently extracts information across various frequency bands and their relationships, and introduces Boostrapping Balancer mechanism to balance their contributions for improved recommendation performance. Sufficient experiments on real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our model.

INTRODUCTION

With the rapid development of the Internet, recommendation systems are extensively used across online services. Within this domain, sequential recommendation(SR), which involves forecasting a user’s next item by treating their past interactions as sequences in time, has garnered significant interest from both academia and industry
[
13
,
3
]
. In reality, users exhibit multiple behaviors when interacting with items, which reflect their multidimensional preferences. For instance, on e-commerce platforms, users can engage with items through various behaviors such as clicking, tagging as favorites, and making purchases. These diverse behaviors represent users’ preferences across different dimensions and can serve as auxiliary knowledge to enrich information and enhance the accuracy of recommendation for the target behavior. Recent studies
[
30
,
26
,
28
]
have explored the field of multi-behavior sequential recommendation which incorporate multi-behavior information into user representations by utilizing transformer or graph neural networks to model different user behaviors.

The impact of different frequency components in user behavior sequences on recommendation results.

Although the incorporation of multi-behavior information can further effectively explore user behavior patterns and multidimensional user interest, some behavior data will also bring inevitable noise to the modeling of user interests. Some of the user’s clicks may be accidental clicks or unintentional browsing. For example, a woman may have mistakenly clicked on a man’s shoe. These noise behaviors bring challenges to the representation of users’ interests. Some studies have focused on denoising techniques
[
31
,
5
]
to improve the accuracy of user preference prediction from the frequency domain perspective. These studies tend to transform user behavior sequences into the frequency domain, such as through the Fast Fourier Transform
[
14
]
, to obtain a frequency domain representation of user behaviors. Their findings indicate that low-frequency information tends to be valuable while high-frequency information is noise. Therefore, these studies designed filter modules to denoise the user behavior sequences.

In this paper, we argue high-frequency information is by no means insignificant. As illustrated in Figure
1
,
low frequency corresponds to the purity of user interests, and high frequency corresponds to the diversity of user interests.
To validate our thoughts, we perform extensive ablation studies of different frequency components. The experimental results highlight the importance of high-frequency signals, which are crucial for capturing the subtle and dynamic aspects of user interests. These signals are not only essential for accurately reflecting the diversity of user preferences but also key to ensuring the diversity of recommendation results.

In light of the analysis of the efficacy of high-frequency information, we model user sequences within the frequency domain. Instead of ignoring the high frequency, we consider both low and high frequency. However, in the context of multi-behavior sequential recommendation, the interrelationships between user behaviors are complex. Conventional global spectrum methods may lead to the mixing of different frequency components
[
6
,
5
]
, making it challenging to effectively distinguish and decouple these components. As a result, such methods often fail to fully explore and characterize user behavior patterns and interest points at various time granularities. Moreover, the inadequate decoupling of different components further leads to insufficient modeling of their relationships, thus struggling to accurately capture the diversity of user interests across different time scales and the complex relationships among multiple interests.

To address the aforementioned issues, we propose our model, PDB4Rec. Initially, we mine local insights from distinct frequency bands, ensuring comprehensive integration of data within each band. Subsequently, we explore the interconnections among these bands by employing the frequency-domain attention and the Boostrapping Balancer mechanism, which dynamically balance the contribution of high-frequency and low-frequency information. Moreover, to enhance the link between the target behavior and various other user behaviors, we apply contrastive learning to both multi-behavior sequence representations and those of the target behavior. This process is then co-optimized with the task of prediction, ensuring an end-to-end approach to user sequence modeling. To summarize, the contributions of this article are as follows:

•

We investigated the limitations of frequency-domain-based information denoising methods in the context of multi-behavior sequential recommendation, with a focus on the usefulness of the high-frequency information that is often overlooked.

•

From a frequency domain perspective, we elucidate the impact of both high and low-frequency information on recommendation outcomes. Specifically, we find that low-frequency information correlates with the purity of interests, while high-frequency information signals the diversity of interests.

•

We propose PDB4Rec, a model that dynamically balances the contributions of high-frequency and low-frequency information, capable of reconciling the purity and diversity of user interests during the denoising process.

•

We conducted comprehensive experiments on three real-world datasets. The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of PDB4Rec, and complexity analysis proves its efficiency.

Multi-behavior sequential recommendation models complex user interactions (clicks, favorites, purchases) to predict and recommend the next item accurately.

DEFINITION 2.
(

\widetilde{S}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\widetilde{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}

3.2
Results and Findings

From our experiments, several conclusions can be drawn:
Low-High Frequency Signals Reflecting Interest Purity.
Recommendations based on medium and low-frequency information exhibit higher accuracy, while those based on high-frequency information show lower accuracy. Medium and low-frequency components are effective signals, while high-frequency information is often regarded as noise, which can be denoised through learnable filters
[
31
]
.

Highlighting the Importance of High-Frequency Information.
High-frequency information is not insignificant. Recommendations based on high-frequency data demonstrate diversity, capturing nuanced and dynamic aspects of user interests. This ensures recommendation diversity and accurately reflects user preferences.

Low-Pass Filter Trait in Many Models.
The low-pass filter characteristic of attention modules in models like SASRec results in lower diversity compared to GRU4Rec. Similarly, GCNs also exhibit low-pass filter properties, inadequately mining high-frequency information. This can lead to suboptimal local solutions and loss of diverse user interest.
To address these issues, we propose a frequency-based multi-behavior sequential recommendation technique that unifies user interest purity and diversity from a frequency-domain perspective, using various frequency-domain modeling strategies to enhance recommendation effectiveness.

4
METHODOLOGY

Following the analysis of the effectiveness of high-frequency information, in this paper, we introduce a new model named PDB4Rec, designed to model user sequences in the frequency domain. Instead of ignoring the high frequency, we consider both low and high frequency. To address the aforementioned issues, we propose our model, PDB4Rec. Initially, we mine local insights from distinct frequency bands, ensuring comprehensive integration of data within each band. Subsequently, we explore the interconnections among these bands by employing a frequency-domain attention mechanism, which dynamically balances the contribution of high-frequency and low-frequency information. Moreover, to enhance the link between the targeted behavior and various other user behaviors, we apply contrastive learning to both multi-behavior sequence representations and those of the target behavior. This process is then co-optimized with the task of prediction, ensuring an end-to-end approach to user sequence modeling.

4.1
Local Spectral Modeling

Firstly, according to the
Definition 2
, we obtain the frequency domain representation of the user sequence,

X=\mathcal{F}(S)\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times d},\quad M=\lceil L/2\rceil+1

(1)

In the context of Multi-Behavior Sequential Recommendation (MBSR), the interplay between user behaviors is complex. Traditional global spectrum analysis methods often blend various frequency components, making it difficult to differentiate and decouple them. As a result, these methods fail to fully explore and characterize user behavior patterns and interests at different temporal granularities.
To overcome the issues above, we divide the spectrum into different frequency bands to enhance the extraction of local details. Specifically, we divide the spectral representation
X
into
N
parts, with each part serving as a frequency band, and mine the local information within each frequency band.

In addition, past studies
[
31
]
often employed a strategy of element-wise multiplication by a matrix to amplify or attenuate each element in the spectrum as
\widetilde{{X}}={W}\odot{X}
. However, this approach has several limitations. First, the dot product operation cannot fully integrate the information of spectral bands (manifested in the time domain as the user’s interest information in multiple time scales). Second, the number of parameters of
W
grows with the increase of the length of the input sequences, and it is difficult for the model to flexibly adapt to the change of the input length. Third, in our behavior-modeling situation, user behavior is easily concentrated in a certain frequency band, and the model can easily overfit local information.

To this end, we propose the Efficient Spectrum Miner (ESM) and enhance three key aspects of the dot-product operation involving the
W
-matrix within it. Specifically, we utilize matrix multiplication instead of dot product to achieve better frequency domain fusion however, this will further increase the number of model parameters. So we further propose the Blockwise Diagonal Strategy, which enables model parameters to be shared among different frequency bands so that the model can adaptively handle sequences of different lengths. Finally, considering that user behavior information may cluster in certain frequency bands, we design a Compactness Regularization method for sparsifying the frequency tokens in the frequency domain. The specific process is as follows:

Spectrum-Aware Fusion.

In ESM, the first improvement involves utilizing matrix multiplication instead of the traditional dot product operation, i.e.,
H\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times d\times d}
instead of
W\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times d}
. This approach allows for the effective fusion of user behavioral information across different frequency bands. By considering long and short-term user interests in multiple time scales, ESM allows for more efficient mining of user behavior patterns.

Blockwise Diagonal Strategy.

To address the issue of increasing matrix parameters as user sequences grow, ESM introduces a blockwise diagonal mechanism for complex weight matrices. The weight matrix
H\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times d\times d}
is decomposed into
k
shared weight matrices
H^{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{d/k\times d/k}
(
n=1,\ldots,k
), each with reduced dimensions. This decomposition into
k
smaller diagonal weight matrices denoted as
H^{n}
, is somewhat interpretable, similar to
k
-head attention while enabling computational parallelization. So we get
\tilde{x}_{i}^{n}=H^{n}x_{i}^{n}
, where
{x}_{i}^{n}
represents the
n
-th block of the
i
-th frequency token (
i\in[1,M//(d/k)]
). Specifically, we employ a double-layer MLP structure as
W^{n}
. The formula is as follows:

\tilde{x}_{i}^{n}=\operatorname{MLP}\left(x_{i}^{n}\right)=H_{2}^{n}\sigma\left(H_{1}^{n}x_{i}^{n}+b_{1}^{n}\right)+b_{2}^{n},

(2)

where the weights
H^{n}
and
b^{n}
are shared are all frequency bands, and thus the parameter count can be significantly reduced.

Frequency Uniformity Constraint.

User behavior is easily concentrated in a certain frequency band
[
29
]
, resulting in behavioral features not being adequately fused in the frequency domain, and the model can easily overfit local information, so ESM incorporates a regularization loss that promotes the sparsity of frequency band in the frequency domain. Compactness is a desired trait of intra-factor representations and its opposite is what we expect for inter-factor representations. ReduNet
[
1
]
proposed to measure compactness of representation with rate-distortion
R(z,\epsilon)
, which determines the minimal number of bits to encode a random variable
z
subject to a decoding error upper bounded by
\epsilon
. Inspired by this, we design a compactness regularization loss function to control the diversity of the token space. The specific formula is as follows:

R(\mathcal{X},\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\log\mathrm{det}\left(I+\frac{d}{L\epsilon^{2}}\mathcal{X}\mathcal{X}^{T}\right),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\text{compactness }}=\sum_{u_{i}\in U}R\left(\mathcal{X}_{i},\epsilon\right)\vskip-3.0pt

(3)

where
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{M//(d/k)\times d}
is the token matrix and the rest are hyperparameters.
\log\mathrm{det}
means the logarithm of the determinant of a matrix and
I
is the identity matrix. By introducing a representation compactness metric and a corresponding regularized loss, frequency bands are sparsified during the training process to control their spatial diversity for better fusion of behavioral features.

In conclusion, our proposed Efficient Spectrum Miner can both capture complex user patterns adequately and maintain model simplicity through the utilization of Spectrum-Aware Fusion, the Blockwise Diagonal Strategy, and Frequency Uniformity Constraint. With ESM, noise in the data is effectively reduced, yielding purer item embeddings. Similar to SASRec
[
11
]
, we incorporate skip connections, layer normalization, and dropout to alleviate gradient vanishing and unstable training issues.

4.2
Global Spectral Modeling

We have conducted an efficient and thorough exploration of the information in each frequency band through Local Spectral Modeling. To accurately capture the diversity of user interests across different time scales and the complex relationships among multiple interests. In this section, we proceed to model the relationships among the various frequency bands for Global Spectral Modeling.

Measuring the Importance of Frequency Features.

After thoroughly mining the local aspects of the spectrum, we proceed to capture the relational information between different frequency bands. In particular, we employ frequency domain self-attention to capture the information within each band. The specific formulas are as follows:

Q=W_{Q}\cdot\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde{Z}),\quad K=W_{K}\cdot\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde{Z}),\quad V=W_{V}\cdot(\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde{Z})+i\cdot\operatorname{Im}(\widetilde{Z})),

(4)

where
\widetilde{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times d}
is a complex matrix representing the locally mined information from each frequency band after mean pooling along the length dimension.
W_{Q}
,
W_{K}
, and
W_{V}
are the learned weight matrices for transforming the real and imaginary parts of
\widetilde{X}
into Q, K, and V, respectively. This method captures the complex relationships within the frequency domain, with the real part being more reflective of user behavior and interest, resulting in a parameter-efficient attention mechanism.

\vskip-15.0ptA=\operatorname{Softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

(5)

Boostrapping Balancer.

Due to the observation in our experiments that the model tends to discard too many high-frequency components at the early stages of training, potentially leading to local optima and resulting in homogenized modeling of user interests, we introduce the Bootstrapping Balancer. This mechanism tunes the model to amplify the weight of high-frequency information during the initial training phase.

O=(A+I)V,\quad I_{n}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(n-N/2)}},\vskip-5.0pt

(6)

where
\alpha
is a hyperparameter that governs the distribution of weights between high and low frequencies, and it decreases with each training epoch, similar to how the learning rate decays. Based on the analysis of the diversity of user interests as indicated by high-frequency information, this module enhances the model’s ability to capture the diversity of user interests.

4.3
Target-behavior Contrastive learning

Due to the large number of auxiliary behaviors and the significant noise in the data. To strengthen the connection between target behaviors and users’ other behaviors and further enhance the robustness of the model, we adopt Target-behavior Contrastive learning. Specifically, we obtain representations of multi-behavioral sequences
\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a}
and target behavioral sequences
\widetilde{S}_{i}^{t}
, respectively, through local spectral modeling and global spectral modeling. Subsequently, we use a contrastive learning strategy, defining the two representations from the same user as positive samples, and those from different users as negative samples. In addition, we use the InfoNCE Loss
[
4
]
to distinguish the augmented representations of the same item from others. The specific formula is as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{CL}=-\sum_{u_{i}\in U}\left[\log\frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{sim}\left(\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a},\widetilde{S}^{t}_{i}\right)/\tau\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{neg}}\exp\left(\mathbf{sim}\left(\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a},\widetilde{S}_{j}^{a}\right)\right/\tau)}\right],

(7)

Here,
\textbf{sim}(\cdot)
is the cosine similarity function,
\left(\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a},\widetilde{S}^{t}_{i}\right)
is a positive sample pair and
\left(\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a},\widetilde{S}_{j}^{a}\right)
is a negative sample pair. In particular, temperature coefficient
\tau
is a hyper-parameter, which controls the impact of the diversity of negative samples on the model. By performing contrastive learning with the target behavior sequence, the model can more effectively identify the underlying associations between auxiliary behaviors and target behaviors, enhancing the model’s robustness under high levels of data noise, and thus more accurately capturing users’ behavioral preferences.

4.4
Multi-Task Learning

With the main prediction task and contrastive learning task, we jointly optimize them in this section. The Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss is implemented for the prediction task. These two losses are formulated as:

\hat{y}_{u_{i},v}=\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a}*e_{v},\vskip-10.0pt

(8)

\mathcal{L}_{Pred}=-\sum_{u,v}\left[y_{u,v}\ln\left(\hat{y}_{u,v}\right)+\left(1-y_{u,v}\right)\ln\left(1-\hat{y}_{u,v}\right)\right].\vskip-5.0pt

(9)

where
\widetilde{S}_{i}^{a}
is the representation of the multi-behavior sequence for user
u_{i}
after spectrum modeling,
\hat{y}_{u_{i},v}
represents the prediction for user
u_{i}
and item
v
and
e_{v}
is the embedding of item
v
.

Therefore, the final objective function of PDB4Rec is:

\vskip-5.0pt\mathcal{L}_{Total}=\theta_{1}\mathcal{L}_{Pred}+\theta_{2}\mathcal{L}_{camp}+\theta_{3}\mathcal{L}_{CL}+\theta_{4}\|\Theta\|_{2},

(10)

where
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{2}
,
\theta_{3}
are hyper-parameters,
L_{camp}
is compactness regularization loss is ESM, and
\theta_{4}
is the
L2
regularization parameter to prevent over-fitting. The symbol
\Theta
denotes all learnable parameters in the model and they are computed by gradient descent with back-propagation. The integration of the main prediction task with contrastive learning allows for a more robust and accurate model by filtering out noise and strengthening the link between target behaviors and other user behaviors, resulting in improved recommendation effectiveness.

4
, we can conclude as follows:
(1) Hyperparameter that governs the distribution of weights between high and low frequencies
\alpha
.

\alpha

Conversely, if the coefficients are too high, it can introduce noise into the model and prevent the achievement of a balance between low-frequency and high-frequency information.

(2) Number of frequency bands
N
.

we find that
N

\theta_{2}
.

We observe that an appropriate range of values for
\theta_{2}
, between 0.01 and 0.001, achieves better performance. It suggests that excessive regularization loss can negatively impact the normal representation of vectors, while insufficient regularization may not adequately constrain the sparse representation of frequency domain tokens.

RELATED WORK

Multi-Behavior Sequential Recommendation

In the domain of recommendation systems, two primary approaches have emerged for processing multi-behavior data. The first approach uses multi-task frameworks to improve performance. For example, the NMTR model
[
7
]
models the cascade relationship among behaviors, while the DIPN model
[
8
]
classifies behaviors into distinct tasks using hierarchical attention mechanisms. The second approach integrates multiple behaviors to enhance recommendations for target behavior. These techniques use additional behaviors as auxiliary signals to better understand user patterns and interests. Methods like attention mechanisms
[
21
]
, graph neural networks
[
2
]
, and others are employed. For instance, MATN
[
23
]
captures behavior interplay using transformers and gated networks, while CML
[
20
]
uses multi-behavior contrastive learning to refine behavior representations. KMCLR
[
26
]
combines comparative learning tasks with functional modules to leverage diverse user behavior signals. However, including certain behaviors, such as random clicks, can introduce noise, challenging accurate user interest modeling.

Denoising in Recommendation

Earlier studies used explicit user feedback to bridge the gap between implicit feedback and user preferences
[
12
]
. However, the scarcity of explicit feedback has led to the adoption of unsupervised denoising methods, which can be categorized into hard and soft denoising. Hard denoising removes discrete noise by eliminating specific items from user behavior sequences. For example, CLEA
[
15
]
separates items into positive and negative sub-baskets, while RAP
[
18
]
uses a policy network to decide item removal. ADT
[
19
]
reduces noise adaptively during early training stages.
Soft denoising removes continuous noise at the embedding or feature level. FMLP-Rec
[
31
]
applies FFT and learnable filters to improve sequence representations, and DPT
[
29
]
uses a three-stage paradigm for denoising and fine-tuning.
However, current denoising research often overlooks the trade-off between preference accuracy and diversity, which brings the challenge of balancing personalized relevance and the diversity of their interests.

CONCLUSION

In this paper, we explored the importance of high-frequency information in multi-behavior sequential recommendation (MBSR). Unlike traditional views that treat high-frequency signals as noise, our research shows that these signals are essential for capturing user preference diversity. Based on this insight, we introduced PDB4Rec, a novel MBSR model that efficiently extracts information from various frequency bands and their relationships, while balancing their contributions through a Bootstrapping Balancer mechanism. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our approach. Future work will focus on advancing techniques for modeling user interests in the frequency domain.
