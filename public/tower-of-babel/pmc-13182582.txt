Adult presentation of a vein of Galen malformation as cognitive decline in a 47-year-old multiparous female: illustrative case

Vein of Galen malformations (VOGMs) are the most common form of cerebral arteriovenous shunt in neonates and infants. They are characterized by a persistent embryonic median prosencephalic vein of Markowski (MPVM), which serves as a venous collector, resulting in a substantial arteriovenous shunt.1,2 There are three subtypes of VOGM: choroidal, mural, and mixed. Choroidal VOGMs are characterized by multiple high-flow arteriovenous fistulas originating from the anterior choroidal, posterior choroidal, anterior cerebral, quadrigeminal, and thalamoperforating arteries, into the anterior aspect of the MPVM. Mural VOGMs involve fewer, low-flow arteriovenous fistulas, typically from the quadrigeminal arteries and posterior choroidal arteries, draining into the inferolateral margin of the MPVM. Choroidal VOGMs tend to be more severe, presenting with refractory cardiac failure and multiorgan dysfunction. In contrast, mural VOGMs generally have a more indolent course and may present later in life with hydrocephalus, venous hypertension, sinus occlusion, or hemorrhage. Mixed-type VOGMs exhibit features of both choroidal and mural subtypes.3,4

Initial diagnosis of VOGM may be made prenatally via ultrasonography and confirmed with fetal MRI, which additionally aids in the identification of secondary complications such as hemorrhagic injury in cerebral white matter.5,6 Despite timely diagnosis, across the largest reported series roughly one-half to two-thirds of neonates with VOGMs experienced heart failure refractory to medical therapy necessitating urgent embolization.7,8 Despite endovascular embolization and dedicated care at specialized centers, mortality for patients remains high at an early age.6

Few cases of VOGMs in adult patients have been reported in the literature. However, among those reported, endovascular and microsurgical interventions have shown favorable outcomes.9 In this report, we present a case with accompanying multimodal imaging of a patient with a history of progressive neurological decline and new-onset seizures who was diagnosed with a VOGM at 47 years of age.

Illustrative Case

Initial Presentation

A 47-year-old Puerto Rican mother of 2 arrived at the emergency department with new-onset seizures and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 6 (eyes 1, verbal 1, motor 4). Physical examination was remarkable for exophthalmos and markedly pronounced facial veins.

Prior History

Further investigation revealed a history of chronically compensated heart failure as well as apparent early-onset dementia of unclear etiology. The latter compromised her professional role as a pharmaceutical assistant to the point where she was forced to retire, despite having completed all her academic pursuits in the past. Soon after retiring, her condition rapidly deteriorated, and workup at an outside institution led to the diagnosis of a cerebral arteriovenous malformation that was initially deemed untreatable.

Further Workup

On presentation to our institution, initial noncontrast CT imaging (Fig. 1) revealed an extensive intracerebral hemorrhage, an intraventricular hemorrhage, widespread parenchymal calcifications, and hydrocephalus. After emergency placement of an external ventricular drain, multimodal angiographic images were obtained for further evaluation.

Axial noncontrast CT image obtained on admission demonstrating hydrocephalus, intraventricular hemorrhage, and extensive parenchymal calcifications.

CTA (Fig. 2A) showed an extensive arteriovenous shunt with arterial supply from the pericallosal and callosomarginal branches of the anterior cerebral arteries. Additional contributions were noted from the middle and posterior cerebral arteries. The vein of Galen and straight sinus were markedly dilated with calcifications along their walls. Significant filling of intraorbital and facial veins was noted (Fig. 2B). Given the size of the malformation and the neurological state of the patient, she was determined to have a poor prognosis; however, the endovascular neurosurgery team was consulted for consideration of further treatment options.

Lateral (A) and anteroposterior (B) views of a 3D-reconstructed CT angiogram demonstrating dilated vein of Galen and straight sinus with associated calcifications.

The patient underwent cerebral angiography (Figs. 3 and 4) to better characterize the shunt, determine interventional alternatives, and assess prognosis. Angiography demonstrated a brisk arteriovenous shunt into the anterior aspect of the MPVM as seen in choroidal VOGMs, with retrograde venous drainage into facial vessels and poor visualization of additional parenchymal vessels. The diagnosis of a choroidal VOGM was made.

Lateral views of digital subtraction angiograms of the right internal carotid artery (A) and left internal carotid artery (B) demonstrating brisk arteriovenous shunting into the vein of Galen and straight sinus.

Lateral view of a 3D-reconstructed digital subtraction angiogram of the right internal carotid artery confirming diagnosis of a VOGM.

Progression

On evaluation the following day, the patient had a GCS score of 5T (eyes 1, verbal 1, motor 3). Electroencephalography showed a background of low-voltage, poorly modulated, generalized 3- to 4-Hz generalized delta activity compatible with an encephalopathic process, but no evidence of seizure activity.

On hospitalization day 5, the patient showed an improved GCS score of 8T (eyes 2, verbal 1, motor 5). The family was provided education regarding treatment alternatives, including endovascular embolization, as well as the patient’s prognosis and recovery expectations. However, the family expressed their wishes to explore withdrawal of care. Over the following days, the patient’s GCS score fluctuated, eventually reaching 4T, and the patient’s family opted for withdrawal of care.

Discussion

Observations

The diagnosis of a choroidal VOGM in an adult is extremely rare. We present the case of a 47-year-old female patient who presented with a history of heart failure and progressive cognitive decline at a young age. It is of particular interest to us how, despite being born with this progressive and devastating congenital condition, the patient was able to have two successful pregnancies, complete professional studies, and live independently until the age of 45 years.

There are multiple observations worth highlighting from this interesting case. First, several potential key interventions could have altered the disease course at a young age. The patient grew up in a system with reliable access to adequate medical care, raising the question of whether the failure to diagnose this patient’s condition stemmed from a failure to recognize systemic associations or from a failure to understand the condition itself. Naturally, the concern of improper prenatal care in this patient’s mother arises, as this pathology could have been identified during prenatal ultrasonography screening.8 Second, we highlight this patient’s earlier misdiagnosis of a cerebral arteriovenous malformation. Of particular concern is the lack of consideration of potential treatments, even though earlier endovascular or microsurgical intervention may have altered this patient’s disease course.9,–,11

VOGMs have been reported to present with different symptoms in adults than in neonates, with previous cases reporting symptoms of headaches, vomiting, dizziness, vertigo, seizures, loss of consciousness, and focal neurological deficits.9,12,13 To our knowledge, slowly progressing cognitive decline and early-onset dementia have not been previously described secondary to adult-presenting choroidal VOGM. More common etiologies of dementia, such as Alzheimer’s disease, should remain high on the list of differentials of patients presenting with dementia at an early age.14 However, this case sheds light on an atypical cause of cognitive decline: VOGMs. It has been previously described that vascular intracranial malformations cause cerebral hypoperfusion and may present with global cerebral dysfunction and dementia.15 This underscores the importance of a comprehensive neurological workup, including neurovascular imaging, in patients presenting with cognitive decline, particularly those with early-onset dementia symptoms.

Despite this case being an anomaly, we believe recent advances in endovascular intervention techniques would have allowed for adequate treatment, which may have prevented this patient’s eventually lethal intracerebral hemorrhage. A systematic review of VOGMs by Udine et al. demonstrated that interventions in VOGM in adult cases tend to have a very favorable outcome, with only 2 of their identified patients having died after undergoing treatment.9 We understand that, regardless of the age of presentation, this is a complex lesion that signifies a risk for the patient’s life, and treatment should be discussed with the patient and considered whenever feasible. Additionally, early intervention should be favored as the timing of hemorrhagic events in these cases is unpredictable, and delays in treatment may lead to catastrophic and irreversible sequelae, as was the case in our patient.16

Our case is limited by its single-case nature and by the family’s decision not to pursue further treatment, which limits our evaluation of additional interventions. Additionally, there was no record of neuropsychological testing prior to the patient’s decline, limiting our ability to provide objective measurements of the patient’s cognitive function. Similarly, prior imaging was unavailable, and we are therefore unable to report on the radiographic evolution of this atypical pathology. This case, however, is a valuable contribution to the limited number of reported adult VOGM cases in the literature, with a unique presentation mimicking neurodegenerative disease rather than classic symptoms. We also present multimodal imaging studies that contribute to the diagnostic clarity and clinical severity of the malformation. Finally, we highlight a critical gap in early recognition of the pathology and decades of missed diagnosis, which critically impacted the patient’s life and outcome.

Lessons

We report the rare case of a 47-year-old Puerto Rican female who, despite having a history of congestive heart failure and a recent diagnosis of early-onset dementia, never received care for the likely underlying cause of her symptoms, a VOGM, which eventually led to her death. Multiple key lessons can be drawn from this unfortunate case. First, although VOGMs have a poor prognosis and are typically detected on antenatal ultrasonography, they may still present in adult patients. Second, while newborns primarily present with congestive heart failure, adult patients with VOGM may have atypical presentations, such as this patient, who experienced progressive cognitive decline likely due to global cerebral hypoperfusion. Additionally, the diagnosis of a VOGM in adults is challenging and requires advanced imaging modalities such as CTA and DSA, and expert opinions from specialists in highly specialized fields such as pediatric neuroradiology should be sought. Finally, when a VOGM is diagnosed in an adult patient, early endovascular or open microsurgical intervention should be strongly considered to avoid potentially lethal sequelae.
