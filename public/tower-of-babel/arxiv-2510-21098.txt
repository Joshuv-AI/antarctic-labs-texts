On Non-KAM Invariant Circles for Area-Preserving Twist Maps
Jiashen Guo, Yi Liu, Lin Wang

Abstract
. In this note, we investigate the dynamics of invariant circles in area-preserving twist maps. The invariant circles under consideration lie beyond the applicability of classical KAM theory, as the perturbations involved exceed the scope of standard KAM methods. By integrating both constructive and non-constructive techniques, we establish several results on the existence and breakdown of such non-KAM invariant circles. These findings provide new evidence in support of affirmative answers to two open questions posed by Mather in 1998.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
37J40, 37E40

1.
Introduction

The existence of invariant circles (i.e., homotopically non-trivial invariant curves) for area-preserving twist maps was first established by Moser in his seminal work
[
Mos62
]
, commonly referred to as
Moser’s twist theorem
. This groundbreaking result is also recognized as one of the foundational contributions to the KAM (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) theory, where the map was initially required to be of class
C^{333}
. The regularity condition was significantly relaxed to
C^{4+\varepsilon}
by Rüssmann
[
Rüs70
]
. Subsequently, Moser
[
Mos73
, Page 53]
asserted that
C^{3+\varepsilon}
regularity suffices for the existence of invariant circles with constant type rotation numbers by employing more refined estimates within the KAM framework. The full proof of this assertion was independently provided by Rüssmann
[
Rüs83
]
and Herman
[
Her83
]
, each utilizing distinct methodologies. In
[
Rüs83
]
, Rüssmann introduced a novel iteration process that yields invariant circles which are merely continuous. In contrast, Herman’s approach in
[
Her83
]
leverages the Schauder fixed point theorem, building upon his celebrated work on circle diffeomorphisms (
[
Her79
]
). Furthermore, Herman
[
Her86
]
showed that an invariant circle with a constant type rotation number persists under small perturbations in the
C^{3}
topology. By a geometrical approach, Herman
[
Her83
]
constructed an example demonstrating that each invariant circle can be destroyed by a small
C^{3-\varepsilon}
perturbation. This implies that
C^{3}
regularity is optimal.

The preceding analysis reveals that for a given rotation numbers, one cannot generally expect invariant circles to persist under
C^{r}
-small perturbations when
r<3
. Such persistence questions lie beyond the reach of classical KAM theory and its modern extensions. Indeed, even under favorable conditions, constructing explicit examples where such non-KAM invariant circles either persist or are destroyed remains a challenge. We begin by formalizing the central object of study:

Definition 1.1
.

A
non-KAM invariant circle
is an invariant circle of an integrable area-preserving twist map that persists under
C^{r}
-small perturbations for some
r<3
, but
C^{3}
-large.

As a preliminary approach to identifying such invariant circles, we assume for simplicity that the perturbations themselves are either
C^{\infty}
or real-analytic (
C^{\omega}
). By the celebrated Birkhoff graph theorem
[
Bir20
]
(see also
[
Her83
, Chapter I]
), if the area-preserving twist map is of class
C^{1}
, then any invariant circle must be a Lipschitz graph. Therefore, in what follows, we refer to
invariant circles
as
invariant graphs
.

Under the above definition, we focus primarily on the following questions:

(I)

Existence:

Can one construct examples of area-preserving twist maps that admit such non-KAM invariant graphs?

(II)

Dynamics:

What is the dynamics of the map restricted to such non-KAM invariant graphs? In particular, are there constraints on the rotation number as a circle homeomorphism?

(III)

Fragility:

How fragile are these invariant graphs? For instance, can they be destroyed by
C^{\infty}
-small perturbations?

(IV)

Smoothness:

How pathological can the regularity of these invariant graphs as functions be?

Herman and Mather made fundamental contributions to the development of twist map theory during the 1980s and 1990s. With the aid of the powerful tools they developed, the study of area-preserving twist maps has achieved significant progress. However, certain questions of a similar nature, which remain not fully resolved to this day, were explicitly raised by both of them and others in various contexts; see, for example,
[
FK18
,
Her98
,
MY02
]
. In this note, we aim to contribute to the study of these questions by refining and extending the tools introduced by Herman and Mather.

1.1.
Basic Setting

To state the main results, let us recall some notions and notations. Denote
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
the flat circle. Let
|\cdot|
denote the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}
. The induced flat metric on
\mathbb{T}
is defined as

\|\cdot\|:=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}|\cdot+p|.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number, expressed via its continued fraction expansion as
\alpha=[a_{0},a_{1},\ldots]
. The convergents
\left\{{p_{n}}/{q_{n}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\alpha
are defined by
{p_{n}}/{q_{n}}=[a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}]
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\alpha\in[0,1)
and
q_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

An irrational number
\alpha
is said to be of
constant type
if the coefficients
a_{i}
in its continued fraction expansion are uniformly bounded. We write
\mathcal{C}
for the set of constant type irrational numbers in
(0,1)
. It is well known that
\mathcal{C}
has zero Lebesgue measure but full Hausdorff dimension. More generally, we say that
\alpha
is a Diophantine number if there exist constants
D>0
and
\tau\geq 0
such that for all integers
p,q
with
q\neq 0
,

(1)

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{D}{|q|^{2+\tau}}.

To emphasize the dependence on the parameters
D
and
\tau
, we also refer to such
\alpha
as
(D,\tau)
-
Diophantine numbers
. Hence, any number of constant type is a
(D,0)
-Diophantine number, and vice versa. Irrational numbers that are not Diophantine are called
Liouville numbers
. The set of Brjuno numbers is a larger class of irrationals than the Diophantine numbers. The Diophantine condition can be expressed in terms of this expansion as

(2)

\ln q_{n+1}\leq C\ln q_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

The Brjuno condition can be stated as

(3)

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\ln q_{n+1}}{q_{n}}<+\infty.

We denote by
\mathrm{Diff}_{+}^{r}(\mathbb{R})
(resp.
\mathrm{Diff}_{+}^{r}(\mathbb{T})
) the group of
C^{r}
orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms on
\mathbb{R}
(resp.
\mathbb{T}
), where
0\leq r\leq\omega
. Define

D^{r}(\mathbb{T}):=\left\{f\in\mathrm{Diff}_{+}^{r}(\mathbb{R})\ \middle|\ f-\mathrm{Id}\in C^{r}(\mathbb{T})\right\},

which is the universal covering space of
\mathrm{Diff}_{+}^{r}(\mathbb{T})
. For
f\in D^{r}(\mathbb{T})
, the rotation number
\rho(f)
is well defined. Define the following sets:

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{r}:=\left\{f\in D^{r}(\mathbb{T})\ \middle|\ \rho(f)=\alpha\right\},\quad\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{r}:=\left\{h^{-1}\circ R_{\alpha}\circ h\ \middle|\ h\in D^{r}(\mathbb{T})\right\},

{H}^{r}:=\left\{\phi\in C^{r}(\mathbb{T})\ \middle|\ \int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi(\theta)\,d\theta=0\right\}.

A classical result by Herman and Yoccoz
[
Her79
,
Yoc84
]
shows that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\infty}=\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{\infty}
when
\alpha
is a Diophantine number.

Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the family of maps
f_{\beta}^{\phi}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
defined by

(4)

f_{\beta}^{\phi}(x,y)=\bigl(x+\beta+y+\phi(x),\ y+\phi(x)\bigr),

where
\phi
is a
1
-periodic smooth function with zero mean:
\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\,dx=0
. Then
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
is an exact area-preserving twist map. In particular,
f_{\beta}^{0}(x,y)=(x+\beta+y,\,y)
is integrable. Let
F_{\beta}^{\phi}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
denote the lift of
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
. If it exists, we denote an invariant graph of
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
by

\Gamma:=\{(x,\Psi(x))\mid x\in\mathbb{T}\}.

For simplicity, we use the same notations for the following objects and their lifts:
\phi,\Gamma,\Psi,g
.

1.2.
Related work by Herman

A key observation of Herman
[
Her83
]
(also noted by Mather
[
Mat84
]
) is that
F_{\beta}^{\phi}
admits an invariant graph
\Psi
over
\mathbb{T}
if and only if there exists
g_{\beta}\in D^{r}(\mathbb{T})
such that

(5)

\frac{g_{\beta}+g_{\beta}^{-1}}{2}=\mathrm{Id}+\frac{1}{2}\phi.

Moreover, the circle diffeomorphism
g_{\beta}
can be written as

g_{\beta}(x)=x+\beta+\Psi(x)+\phi(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

For
0\leq r\leq\omega
, define the map
\Phi:\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{r}\to{H}^{r}
by

\Phi(g):=\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2}-\mathrm{Id}.

Remark 1.2
.

Herman
[
Her83
]
originally established formula (
5
) for maps of the form

F(x,y)=(x+y,\,y+\phi(x+y)).

Through a straightforward modification (see Lemma
2.1
), one can show that (
5
) remains valid for the parameter-dependent family
F_{\beta}^{\phi}
, and the map
\Phi
is independent of
\beta
; that is, for any
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\Phi(g_{\beta_{1}})=\Phi(g_{\beta_{2}}).

This elementary fact plays a crucial role in the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
.

Remark 1.3
.

Let
\mathrm{Lip}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
denote the group of bi-Lipschitz orientation-preserving maps on
\mathbb{R}
. Define

D^{\mathrm{Lip}}(\mathbb{T}):=\left\{f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\ \middle|\ f-\mathrm{Id}\text{ is Lipschitz on }\mathbb{T}\right\},

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{Lip}}:=\left\{f\in D^{\mathrm{Lip}}(\mathbb{T})\ \middle|\ \rho(f)=\alpha\right\}.

The Birkhoff graph theorem implies
\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{0})=\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{Lip}})
.

By Herman
[
Her83
, Chapter II]
, we know

Proposition 1.4
.

(i)

\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\infty})
is open in
{H}^{\infty}
.

(ii)

\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{0})\cap H^{\infty}
is closed in
H^{\infty}
under the
C^{1}
topology.

(iii)

\Phi
is injective for irrational
\alpha
.

Let

U^{r}_{\delta}:=\left\{\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\ \middle|\ \|\phi\|_{C^{r}}<\delta\right\}.

By
[
Her86
, Corollary 7.10]
, we know that for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(6)

U_{\delta}^{3}\subseteq\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\infty}).

On the other hand, Herman
[
Her83
, Theorem 3.3]
constructed a
C^{\infty}
perturbation
\phi
that is arbitrarily small in the
C^{2-\varepsilon}
topology, such that all invariant graphs of the twist map
F^{\phi}
are destroyed. Let

\mathcal{F}^{0}:=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{0}.

Then Herman’s result can be reformulated as follows: for each
\delta>0
, we have

(7)

(U_{\delta}^{2-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{2})\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{0})\subsetneqq U_{\delta}^{2-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{2}.

A natural question concerns the structure of the set
(U_{\delta}^{2-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{2})\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{0})
. In
[
Her83
, Chapter III]
, Herman proved that

Proposition 1.5
.

•

the set
\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{0})\cap U_{\delta}^{1}
is non-convex;

•

for each irrational
\alpha
, the set
\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{\alpha})\cap U_{\delta}^{2-\varepsilon}
is non-convex;

•

for each irrational
\alpha
, the set
\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{\alpha})\cap U_{\delta}^{1}
is non star-shaped with respect to
\phi\equiv 0\in U_{\delta}^{1}
.

This suggests that the set of perturbations in
U_{\delta}^{r}
(for
r<2
) that guarantee the existence of invariant graphs has a poor geometric structure, which poses further difficulties for addressing questions (I)-(IV).

1.3.
On Questions (I) and (II)

By using certain modifications of the Arnold family (see Section
2.2
), we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and
\iota\in\mathbb{N}
with
\iota\geq 2
. For any rotation number
\alpha\in(0,1)
and any
\delta>0
,

\left(U_{\delta}^{\iota-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{\iota}\right)\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\omega})\neq\emptyset.

Theorem
1
is proved by explicitly constructing an element in the corresponding set. More precisely, we show that there exists a perturbation
\phi\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T})
whose
C^{\iota-\varepsilon}
-norm is arbitrarily small but whose
C^{\iota}
-norm is large, such that for every rotation number
\alpha
, one can find
\beta
(depending on
\alpha
) for which the twist map
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
defined by (
4
) admits a
C^{\omega}
invariant graph with rotation number
\alpha
.

By (
5
), the circle map
g_{\beta}
corresponding to
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
is also real-analytic. According to the Denjoy-Herman-Yoccoz theory of circle maps
[
Den32
,
Her79
,
Yoc84
,
Yoc95
,
Yoc02
]
, we have the following:

•

If
\alpha
is rational, then
g_{\beta}
is not conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
.

•

If
\alpha
is irrational, then
g_{\beta}
is at least
C^{0}
-conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
.

•

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{H}
, then
g_{\beta}
is
C^{\omega}
-conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
(i.e.,
C^{\omega}
-linearizable); see Remark
1.6
for further details on the set
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 1.6
.

The set
\mathcal{H}
is larger than the Diophantine numbers but smaller than the Brjuno numbers; see
[
Yoc02
]
for its precise definition. In terms of the continued fraction expansion, let
\{p_{n}/q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of convergents of
\alpha
. Then
\alpha\in\mathcal{H}
if there exists
\mu>0
such that

\ln(q_{n+1})\leq C(\ln q_{n})^{\mu}\quad\text{for all }n.

If
\{a_{n}\}
are the partial quotients in the continued fraction expansion of
\alpha
, then any
\alpha
satisfying

e^{(a_{n})^{\mu}}\leq a_{n+1}\leq e^{a_{n}}\quad\text{for all }n,

for some
0<\mu<1
, is a Brjuno number but does not belong to
\mathcal{H}
.

For
\alpha\notin\mathcal{H}
, Yoccoz constructed examples of real-analytic diffeomorphisms with rotation number
\alpha
that are not analytically linearizable. Theorem
1
implies that under perturbations satisfying
\phi\in U_{\delta}^{\iota-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{\iota}
, invariant graphs with rotation number
\alpha\notin\mathcal{H}
can still be preserved. This leads to a natural question: how pathological is the dynamics on these preserved invariant graphs? Building on Herman’s work, we provide a partial answer in the generic case.

We now recall the notion of singular conjugation.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
f\in D^{1}(\mathbb{T})
with
\rho(f)=\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
such that
f
is
C^{0}
-conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
; write
f=h^{-1}\circ R_{\alpha}\circ h
. We say that
f
is singular conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
if
h
is not absolutely continuous (i.e., there exists a Borel set
\mathcal{B}
such that
m(\mathcal{B})=0
but
m(h(\mathcal{B}))\neq 0
).

Define
C^{a.c.}(\mathbb{T})
as the space of absolutely continuous,
\mathbb{Z}
-periodic functions.
Define the space

\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{k,r}:=\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{r}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{k}.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and
\iota\in\mathbb{N}
with
\iota\geq 2
. There exists a residual set
\mathcal{R}\subset[0,1]
such that for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
and every
\delta>0
,

\left(U_{\delta}^{\iota-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{\iota}\right)\cap\left(\Phi(\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{\omega,0})\setminus\Phi(\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{\omega,\mathrm{a.c.}})\right)\neq\emptyset.

By the Baire category theorem, the set
\mathcal{R}
is dense in
[0,1]
. To prove Theorem
2
, we construct a perturbation
\phi\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T})
with the required properties such that for every rotation number
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
, there exists
\beta
(depending on
\alpha
) for which the twist map
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
defined by (
4
) admits a
C^{\omega}
invariant graph on which the dynamics is singular conjugate to the rigid rotation
R_{\alpha}
.

•

For
\iota=2
, Theorem
1
implies that the preimage under
\Phi
of the set
(U_{\delta}^{2-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{2})\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{0})
can contain invariant graphs with arbitrary rotation numbers, whether rational or irrational. (Note that if
\alpha
is rational, the restriction of
\Phi
to
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{\alpha}
is not necessarily invertible.)

•

For
\iota=3
, Theorems
1
and
2
provide answers to Questions (I) and (II) above. Specifically, there are no restrictions on the rotation numbers of the invariant graphs, and the dynamics on these graphs exhibit diversity related to the arithmetic properties of the rotation numbers.

Remark 1.8
.

According to
[
Yoc02
]
, the set
\mathcal{H}
is of type
F_{\sigma\delta}
but not
F_{\sigma}
, which implies that

\mathcal{Y}:=[0,1]\setminus(\mathbb{Q}\cup\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{R})\neq\emptyset.

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{Y}
, we only know that there exists
\beta
(depending on
\alpha
) such that the twist map
f_{\beta}^{\phi}
defined by (
4
) admits a
C^{\omega}
invariant graph on which the dynamics is not
C^{\omega}
-conjugate to the rigid rotation
R_{\alpha}
, while it remains undetermined whether the conjugacy is singular.

Remark 1.9
.

Based on Herman’s formula (
5
), we can transform the construction of twist maps into the construction of circle maps. For certain Liouville rotation numbers, examples of
C^{\omega}
circle maps that are singularly conjugate to rigid rotations are known (see, e.g.,
[
KH95
, Theorem 12.5.1]
). However, to the best of our knowledge, the perturbation in these known examples has large
C^{0}
-norm. Alternatively, using the Anosov-Katok method (i.e., approximation by conjugation), one can construct
C^{\infty}
circle maps that are singularly conjugate to rigid rotations for certain Liouville numbers, with perturbations arbitrarily small in the
C^{\infty}
topology (see, e.g.,
[
KH95
, Theorem 12.6.1]
). Nevertheless, this method is problematic to apply to
C^{\omega}
constructions.

In the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
, the constructions based on the modified Arnold family yield invariant graphs that are themselves
C^{\omega}
. In fact, for the construction given by Herman’s formula (
5
), the regularity of the invariant graph coincides with that of the circle map
g
. The existence and breakdown of invariant graphs with lower regularity (e.g., non-
C^{\infty}
) have attracted considerable interest, corresponding precisely to Questions (III) and (IV). In the next section, we address these two questions.

1.4.
On Questions (III) and (IV)

These questions are related to two problems posed by Mather in 1998. Specifically, Mather asked the following questions (
[
MY02
, Page 181-182]
):

•

Question 1:

Given a
C^{\infty}
twist diffeomorphism and an invariant graph which is not
C^{\infty}
, is it possible to destroy it by an arbitrarily small
C^{\infty}
perturbation?

•

Question 2:

Does there exist an example of a
C^{r}
area-preserving twist map with an invariant graph which is not
C^{1}
and that contains no periodic point? (separate the
question for each
r\in[1,+\infty]\cup\{\omega\}
)

1.4.1.
A Positive Evidence for Question 1

The first question concerns the
C^{\infty}
destruction of invariant graphs. Mather
[
Mat88
]
showed that any invariant graph with a Liouville rotation number can be destroyed by arbitrarily small
C^{\infty}
perturbations. Later, Forni
[
For94
]
proved that even real-analytic (
C^{\omega}
) perturbations can destroy such graphs for a certain subclass of frequencies (a proper subset of non-Brjuno numbers). Here, we provide a positive evidence to this question for invariant graphs with Diophantine rotation numbers.

Theorem 3
.

Given
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and a
(D,\tau)
-Diophantine number
\alpha
, there exists a sequence of
C^{\infty}
perturbations
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\|\phi_{n}\|_{C^{3+\tau-\varepsilon}}\to 0,\quad\text{but}\quad\|\phi_{n}\|_{C^{3+\tau+\varepsilon}}\nrightarrow 0,

and the following properties hold:

•

The perturbed map
F^{\phi_{n}}
admits an invariant graph
{\Gamma}_{n}
with rotation number
\alpha
;

•

The graph
{\Gamma}_{n}
is not of class
C^{\infty}
, and it can be destroyed by an arbitrarily small
C^{\infty}
perturbation.

Remark 1.10
.

Theorem
3
only offers a partial resolution to Mather’s question, demonstrating the existence of a class of
C^{\infty}
twist diffeomorphisms for which certain non-
C^{\infty}
invariant graphs are not stable under arbitrarily small
C^{\infty}
perturbations. Whether this phenomenon holds for all such diffeomorphisms remains an open question.

1.4.2.
Partial Support for Question 2

The second question concerns the minimal attainable regularity of invariant graphs in higher regularity systems. Recent contributions to this problem appear in
[
Arn11
,
Arn14
,
AF22
]
. Arnaud, in
[
Arn11
,
Arn14
]
, constructed explicit
C^{1}
and
C^{2}
maps possessing invariant graphs that include non-differentiable points, with the minimal invariant set forming a Cantor set. In a different direction, Avila and Fayad exhibited a
C^{1}
map for which the minimal invariant set is the entire circle. The question of whether invariant graphs containing non-differentiable points can arise in maps of higher regularity was subsequently emphasized by Fayad and Krikorian at the ICM 2018 (see
[
FK18
, Question 26]
). Inspired by Mather’s problem from 1998, one can pose the following variant:

•

Question 2’:

Given a
C^{\infty}
area-preserving twist map that admits an invariant curve with an irrational rotation number, which is represented as the graph of a function
\psi
, what is the minimal regularity of
\psi
?

According to
[
Sal04
]
, if an invariant graph with constant type rotation number is of class
C^{r}
with
r>4
, then it must be
C^{\infty}
. In
[
KO95
]
, Katznelson and Ornstein showed that if
f
is a
C^{3+\gamma}
area-preserving surface diffeomorphism that admits a
C^{2+\varepsilon}
invariant graph
\psi
with a “good” rotation number, then
\psi
is in fact
C^{2+\gamma^{\prime}}
for all
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
. Here, a “good” rotation number
\alpha
refers to one whose continued fraction expansion
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
satisfies
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, which is a broader condition than being of constant type.

Combining these results, (
6
) and Theorem
3
, we obtain the following:

Theorem 4
.

Given
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and a constant type number
\alpha
, there exists a sequence of
C^{\infty}
perturbations
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\|\phi_{n}\|_{C^{3-\varepsilon}}\to 0,\quad\text{but}\quad\|\phi_{n}\|_{C^{3}}\nrightarrow 0,

and the following holds:

•

The perturbed map
F^{\phi_{n}}
admits an invariant graph
{\Gamma}_{n}
with rotation number
\alpha
;

•

The graph
{\Gamma}_{n}
is not of class
C^{2+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

In light of Theorems
1
and
2
, Theorem
4
can be reformulated as follows: for any
\nu>0
,

(8)

\left(U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{3}\right)\cap\left(\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{0})\setminus\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{2+\nu})\right)\neq\emptyset.

Remark 1.11
.

Whether Theorem
4
remains valid under real-analytic (
C^{\omega}
) perturbations is still unknown. The major difficulty lies in the fact that
C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T})
is not a Fréchet space, preventing the direct application of the inverse function theorem (see
[
Her83
, Theorem 2.6.1]
) to verify openness properties.

Remark 1.12
.

Numerical evidence provided by Olvera and Petrov
[
OP08
]
suggests that for certain area-preserving twist maps (such as the standard map), the critical invariant circle (with rotation number equal to the golden mean
(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
) is a
C^{1+\eta}
graph, with
\eta\in(0.7,1)
. The dynamics on this graph is conjugate to the rigid rotation by a
C^{\eta^{\prime}}
function, where
\eta^{\prime}\in(0.7,1)
. This numerical result underscores the subtlety in attempting to further lower the regularity threshold in Theorem
4
.

1.5.
More on the Set
U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{3}

Herman
[
Her83
, Theorem 4.9]
proved that for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists a
C^{\infty}
perturbation
\phi
that is arbitrarily small in the
C^{3-\varepsilon}
topology such that the invariant graph with rotation number
\alpha
of the corresponding twist map
F^{\phi}
is destroyed. In the notation of (
7
), this result can be stated as: for each
\delta>0
,

(9)

(U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{3})\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{\alpha})\subsetneqq U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\setminus U_{\delta}^{3}.

Combining the preceding results, we obtain the following:

Theorem 5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}
. For each
\delta>0
, the following strict inclusions hold:

U_{\delta}^{3}\subsetneqq U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\cap\Phi(\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{\infty})\subsetneqq U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{0})\subsetneqq U_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}.

The first strict inclusion follows from (
6
) and Theorem
1
; the second from (
8
); and the last from (
9
). If the rotation number is not required to be of Diophantine type, then by Theorem
2
, one obtains invariant graphs exhibiting a wider variety of dynamical behaviors.

Remark 1.13
.

Analogously to the set
U_{\delta}^{r}
, we define

V^{r}_{\delta}:=\left\{\phi\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T})\ \middle|\ \|\phi\|_{C^{r}}<\delta\right\}.

It is known
[
Her79
,
Her83
]
that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\omega}=\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{\omega}
, and for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(10)

V_{\delta}^{3}\subseteq\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\omega}).

Moreover, Herman’s result
[
Her83
, Theorem 4.9]
also applies in the
C^{\omega}
category (see
[
Wan22
]
). Consequently, we have

V_{\delta}^{3}\subsetneqq V_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\cap\Phi(\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}^{\omega}),\quad V_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}\cap\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{0})\subsetneqq V_{\delta}^{3-\varepsilon}.

However, for the reasons outlined in Remark
1.11
, it remains unclear whether the two intermediate sets coincide.

In addition to the open problems mentioned above, the authors believe that answering the following question is crucial for deepening our understanding of non-KAM invariant graphs:

•

Question
\diamondsuit
:

Does there exist a
C^{\omega}
area-preserving twist map, which is a
C^{r}
-small perturbation (
r<3
) of an integrable system, that admits an invariant graph with a constant type rotation number
\alpha
, and on which the dynamics is singularly conjugate to the rigid rotation
R_{\alpha}
?

By the correspondence between twist maps and circle maps, if such a map exists in Question
\diamondsuit
, then the regularity of the preserved invariant graph cannot exceed
C^{2}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The Herman Formula

Inspired by Herman
[
Her83
]
, the proof proceeds by exploiting the relationship between twist maps and orientation-preserving circle diffeomorphisms. We first construct a suitable circle map and then derive the corresponding twist map.

To build the required example, we consider a family of area-preserving twist maps defined by:

(11)

F_{\beta}^{\phi}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R},\quad\mathrm{via}\ \ (x,y)\mapsto\left(x+\beta+y+\phi(x),\ y+\phi(x)\right),

where
\phi:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{1}
function with zero mean, i.e.,
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi(\theta)\,d\theta=0
. Recall the convention that we use the same notation for the following objects and their lifts:
\phi
,
\Gamma
,
\Psi
, and
g
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The graph
\Gamma=\{(x,\Psi(x))\mid x\in\mathbb{T}\}
is an invariant circle of
F_{\beta}^{\phi}
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

The map
g_{\beta}:=\mathrm{Id}+\beta+\Psi+\phi
belongs to
D^{0}(\mathbb{T})
;

(2)

The identity
\mathrm{Id}+\frac{1}{2}\phi=\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{\beta}+g_{\beta}^{-1}\right)
holds.

Proof.

We denote
F:=F_{\beta}^{\phi}
and
g:=g_{\beta}
for simplicity.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
\Gamma
is invariant under
F
.

(1)

Let
\pi
be the projection onto the first coordinate. Then
g(x)=\pi\circ F(x,\Psi(x))=x+\beta+\Psi(x)+\phi(x)
, which implies
g_{\beta}\in D^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.

(2)

From (
11
), we have:

F(x,\Psi(x))=(x+\beta+\Psi(x)+\phi(x),\ \Psi(x)+\phi(x)).

Note that
g(x)=x+\beta+\Psi(x)+\phi(x)
. The invariance of
\Gamma
implies
\Psi(x)+\phi(x)=\Psi(g(x))
, or equivalently,

g(x)-x-\beta=\Psi(g(x))\quad\text{and}\quad x-g^{-1}(x)-\beta=\Psi(x).

Using
\Psi(x)=g(x)-x-\beta-\phi(x)
, we obtain:

x-g^{-1}(x)-\beta=g(x)-x-\beta-\phi(x),

which rearranges to:

\mathrm{Id}+\frac{1}{2}{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\left({g}+{g}^{-1}\right).

(
\Leftarrow
) Conversely, assume (1) and (2) hold. Then:

F(x,\Psi(x))=(g(x),\ \Psi(x)+\phi(x)).

From condition (2), we have
x-g^{-1}(x)-\beta=\Psi(x)
. Hence,

\Psi(x)+\phi(x)=\Psi(g(x)).

Since
g\in D^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, it follows that
\Gamma
is an invariant graph of
F
.
∎

2.2.
The Arnold Family

The Arnold family (or standard family) is a two-parameter family of circle maps defined by:

(12)

f_{\lambda,\sigma}(x):=x+\lambda+\frac{\sigma}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi x)\pmod{1},\quad\lambda,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}.

This family was first studied by V. Arnold
[
Arn61
]
. In this work, we consider the parameter range
\sigma\in[0,1)
, which ensures that each map is a diffeomorphism.

•

When
\sigma=0
, the map reduces to a rigid rotation:
f_{\lambda,0}(\theta)=R_{\lambda}(\theta)
, so
\rho(f_{\lambda,0})=\lambda
.

•

For
\lambda=0
, the point
\theta=0
is a fixed point for all
\sigma
, so
\rho(f_{0,\sigma})=0
.

•

The rotation number
\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})
is continuous in both
\lambda
and
\sigma
, and
\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})
takes all values in
[0,1]
as
\lambda
varies.

Let us recall a key fact about the Arnold family (see
[
dMvS93
, Chapter I, Lemma 4.2]
for instance).

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
0<\sigma<1
. Suppose
\lambda_{0}
is such that
\rho(f_{\lambda_{0},\sigma})=p/q
is rational. Then there exists an interval
I
containing
\lambda_{0}
with nonempty interior such that
\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})=p/q
for all
\lambda\in I
. Moreover, the function
\lambda\mapsto\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})
is increasing, locally constant at rational rotation numbers, and strictly increasing at irrational values.

Figure 1.
Arnold tongues: level sets
T_{\rho}
of the rotation number
\rho
for
w,\sigma\in[0,1]
. Tongues of every rational number are connected and have nonempty interior. Besides, if
\theta
is an irrational number,
T_{\theta}
may not have an interior point.

The following result was proved by Herman
[
Her79
, Page 169, Prop. 1.13]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
0<\sigma<1
. Define

K_{\sigma}:=[0,1]\setminus\mathrm{Int}\{\lambda\mid\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\}.

There exists a residual set in
K_{\sigma}
such that for each
\lambda\in K_{\sigma}
, the map
f_{\lambda,\sigma}
is singular conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})
.

We now consider a modification of the classical Arnold family. For
\kappa>0
, choose
n
sufficiently large such that
n^{\kappa}>2\pi
, and define

(13)

f_{\beta,n}(x):=x+n\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\kappa}}\sin(2\pi x).

Let

h_{n}(\beta):=\rho(f_{\beta,n}).

For
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and a set
U
, define

aU:=\{au\mid u\in U\},\quad a+U:=\{a+u\mid u\in U\}.

Note that affine maps are homeomorphisms. The following is immediate:

Lemma 2.4
.

For each
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, if
U
is a residual set in
V
, then
aU
(resp.
a+U
) is also a residual set in
aV
(resp.
a+V
).

By Lemma
2.3
, a direct translation yields:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
N^{\kappa}>2\pi
. Define

K_{N}:=[0,1]\setminus\mathrm{Int}\{N\beta\mid h_{N}(\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\}.

There exists a residual set
G_{N}
in
K_{N}
such that for each
\beta\in\frac{1}{N}G_{N}
, the map
f_{\beta,N}
is singular conjugate to
R_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=h_{N}(\beta)
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N^{\kappa}>2\pi
, define

\mathcal{K}_{N}:=[0,N]\setminus\mathrm{Int}\{N\beta\mid h_{N}(\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,N]\},

\mathcal{D}_{N}:=\mathcal{K}_{N}\cap\{N\beta\mid h_{N}(\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,N]\}.

Then the following hold:

•

h_{N}:\mathcal{K}_{N}\to[0,N]
is surjective;

•

h_{N}:\mathcal{K}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{N}\to[0,N]\setminus\mathbb{Q}
is injective.

By
[
Her79
, Page 38, Prop. 5.3(b)]
, we have:

Lemma 2.6
.

For each residual set
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{K}_{N}
, the image
h_{N}(\mathcal{R})
is also a residual set in
[0,N]
.

To prove Theorem
1
, we require a further modification of the Arnold family. The following lemma relates the rotation numbers of the modified system to those of
f_{\beta,n}
. See
[
Her79
, Chapter XI.2]
for more general argument.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix
\kappa>0
. Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, let

g_{n}(x):=x+\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\kappa+1}}\sin(2\pi nx).

Then
\rho(g_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\rho(f_{\beta,n})
, where
\rho(f_{\beta,n})
is defined by (
13
).

Proof.

Fix
x_{0}\in(0,1)
and consider the orbit
\{x_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
where
x_{m}=g_{n}^{m}(x_{0})
. Then

x_{m+1}=x_{m}+\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\kappa+1}}\sin(2\pi nx_{m}),

which implies

nx_{m+1}=nx_{m}+n\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\kappa}}\sin(2\pi nx_{m}).

Let
y_{m}=nx_{m}
. Then
\rho(g_{n})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{x_{m}}{m}=\frac{1}{n}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{y_{m}}{m}
, where
\{y_{m}\}
satisfies

y_{m+1}=y_{m}+n\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\kappa}}\sin(2\pi y_{m}).

Define

(14)

f_{n}(x):=x+n\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\kappa}}\sin(2\pi x),

which implies

\rho(f_{n})=n\rho(g_{n})
. ∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

We prove Theorem
1
by establishing the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\iota\in\mathbb{N}
with
\iota\geq 2
. Fix
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. For each
\delta>0
,
M>0
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a twist map
F_{\beta}^{\phi}(x,y)=(x+\beta+y+\phi(x),y+\phi(x))
such that
\|\phi\|_{C^{\iota-\varepsilon}}<\delta
,
\|\phi\|_{C^{\iota}}>M
, and
F_{\beta}^{\phi}
admits an invariant circle with rotation number
\alpha
.

According to
Faà di Bruno’s formula
, the
n
-th derivative of a composite function is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}f(g(x))=\sum_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}km_{k}=n}\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\,m_{2}!\,\cdots\,m_{n}!}\cdot f^{(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n})}(g(x))\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{g^{(k)}(x)}{k!}\right)^{m_{k}}.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
and choose
0<\nu<\varepsilon
. Define

g_{\beta,n}(x):=x+\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu}}\sin(2\pi nx),

\phi_{\beta,n}(x):=g_{\beta,n}(x)+g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)-2x.

Then we obtain:

\phi_{\beta,n}(x)=\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu}}\left[\sin(2\pi nx)-\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right)\right].

We will prove the estimate

\|\phi_{\beta,n}\|_{C^{\iota-\varepsilon}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\varepsilon-\nu}}\right),

so that for sufficiently large
n
, we have
\|\phi_{\beta,n}\|_{C^{\iota-\varepsilon}}<\delta
.

Furthermore, we will show that
\|\phi_{\beta,n}\|_{C^{\iota}}
admits a positive lower bound. This allows us to construct a twist map
F_{\beta}^{\phi_{\beta,n}}
with an invariant circle of any prescribed rotation number, such that
\|F_{\beta}^{\phi_{\beta,n}}-F_{\beta}^{0}\|_{C^{\iota}}
is not small, where
F_{\beta}^{0}
is the integrable system
F_{\beta}^{0}(x,y)=(x+y+\beta,y)
.

We denote
D^{n}:=\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}
for simplicity. Differentiating
g_{\beta,n}
for
m
times yields:

\displaystyle Dg_{\beta,n}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2\pi}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\cos(2\pi nx)=O(1),

\displaystyle D^{m}g_{\beta,n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{m}}{n^{\iota-\nu-m}}\sin\left(2\pi nx+\frac{m\pi}{2}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu-m}}\right),\quad\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}.

Since
g_{\beta,n}
is invertible and differentiable, and

x=g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu}}\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right),

we differentiate
g_{\beta,n}^{-1}
twice:

\displaystyle Dg_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\frac{2\pi}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\cos\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right)}=O(1),

\displaystyle D^{2}g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{n^{\iota-\nu-2}}\cdot\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right)\cdot\left(Dg_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right)^{3}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu-2}}\right).

Then we compute:

\displaystyle D\phi_{\beta,n}(x)

\displaystyle=Dg_{\beta,n}(x)+Dg_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)-2

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\cos(2\pi nx)+\frac{1}{1+\frac{2\pi}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\cos\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right)}-1

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\right).

For
m\geq 2
, differentiating
\phi_{\beta,n}

m
times gives:

\displaystyle D^{m}\phi_{\beta,n}(x)=\frac{(2\pi)^{m}}{n^{\iota-\nu-m}}\sin\left(2\pi nx+\frac{m\pi}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu}}\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right).

By
Faà di Bruno’s formula
,

D^{m}\sin\bigl(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\bigr)=\sum_{\sum kb_{k}=m}\frac{m!}{\prod b_{k}!}(2\pi n)^{B}\sin\Bigl(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\frac{B\pi}{2}\Bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{D^{i}g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)}{i!}\right)^{b_{i}},

where
B=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{m}
.

From
g_{\beta,n}(g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))=x
, for
m\geq 2
:

0=D^{m}g_{\beta,n}(g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))=\sum_{\sum kb_{k}=m}\frac{m!}{\prod b_{k}!}D^{B}g_{\beta,n}(g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{D^{i}g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)}{i!}\right)^{b_{i}}.

Isolating the
b_{m}=1
term:

D^{m}g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)=-\frac{\sum_{\sum kb_{k}=m}^{b_{m}\neq 1}\frac{m!}{\prod b_{k}!}D^{B}g_{\beta,n}(g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{D^{i}g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)}{i!}\right)^{b_{i}}}{Dg_{\beta,n}(g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))}.

By induction, for
m\geq 2
:

\displaystyle D^{m}g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(2\pi)^{m}}{n^{\iota-\nu-m}}\sin\Bigl(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\frac{m\pi}{2}\Bigr)(Dg_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))^{m+1}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2\iota-2\nu-m-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu-m}}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle D^{m}\phi_{\beta,n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{m}}{n^{\iota-\nu-m}}\left[\sin\Bigl(2\pi nx+\frac{m\pi}{2}\Bigr)-\sin\Bigl(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\frac{m\pi}{2}\Bigr)(Dg_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))^{m}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\iota-m+1-2\nu}}\right).

Using the Hölder estimate
[f]_{\theta}\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}^{1-\theta}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{\theta}
for
\theta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\beta,n}\|_{C^{\iota-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\iota-1}\|\phi_{\beta,n}^{(i)}\|_{\infty}+[\phi_{\beta,n}^{(\iota-1)}]_{1-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-\nu}}\right)+2\|\phi_{\beta,n}^{(\iota-1)}\|_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}\|\phi_{\beta,n}^{(\iota)}\|_{\infty}^{1-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-\nu}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{(1-\nu)\varepsilon}}\right)O\left(\frac{1}{n^{-\nu(1-\varepsilon)}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\varepsilon-\nu}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\quad(\varepsilon>\nu).

Define

\Delta:=\frac{\cos\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right)}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\right).

Then

\displaystyle D^{\iota}\phi_{\beta,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\left[\sin\left(2\pi nx+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right)-\frac{\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right)}{(1+\Delta)^{\iota}}\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-2\nu}}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\left[\sin\left(2\pi nx+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right)-\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right)\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-2\nu}}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\cdot 2\cos\left[\pi n(x+g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right]\sin\left[\pi n(x-g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x))\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-2\nu}}\right).

Let
y:=2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)
. Since

x=g_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)+\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu}}\sin\left(2\pi ng_{\beta,n}^{-1}(x)\right),

we have

\displaystyle D^{\iota}\phi_{\beta,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\cdot 2\cos\left(y+\pi n\beta+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right)\sin\left(\pi n\beta\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-2\nu}}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{\beta,n}\right\|_{C^{\iota}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\iota}\left\|\phi_{\beta,n}^{(i)}\right\|_{\infty}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-\nu}}\right)+\left\|(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\cdot 2\cos\left(y+\pi n\beta+\frac{\iota\pi}{2}\right)\sin\left(\pi n\beta\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-2\nu}}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=2(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\left|\sin(\pi n\beta)\right|+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-2\nu}}\right)\succcurlyeq(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}\left|\sin(\pi n\beta)\right|.

For
M>0
, define

\displaystyle\Lambda_{M}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\beta,n)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}:\|\phi_{\beta,n}\|_{C^{\iota}}>M\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{M}

\displaystyle=\left\{\rho(g_{\beta,n})\bmod 1:(\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{M}\right\}.

where we introduce the asymptotic notation:

A_{n}(x)\succcurlyeq B_{n}(x)
if for every
\delta>0
, there exists
N=N(\delta)>0
such that for all
n>N
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
|A_{n}(x)|>|B_{n}(x)|-\delta
.

The following result is well known.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, the sequence
\{\{n\gamma\}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is dense in
(0,1)
, where
\{x\}=x\bmod 1
.

We now show that for every

, the set

contains all numbers in

Consider

g_{\beta,n}(x)=x+\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu}}\sin(2\pi nx).

We analyze the rotation number

and relate

to it. Let

f_{\beta,n}(x)=x+n\beta+\frac{1}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\sin(2\pi x).

By Lemma
2.7
,
\rho(f_{\beta,n})=n\rho(g_{\beta,n})
. Note that (
15
) resembles the Arnold family (

) with parameters
(\lambda,\sigma)=\left(n\beta,\frac{2\pi}{n^{\iota-\nu-1}}\right)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For every
M>0
, we have
\mathcal{C}_{M}=(0,1)
.

Proof.

For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, define

S_{\gamma}=\left\{(\lambda,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,2\pi]:\rho(f_{\lambda,\sigma})=\gamma\right\},\quad d_{\gamma}=\inf_{(\lambda,\sigma)\in S_{\gamma}}|\lambda|,\quad d=\inf_{\gamma\in I_{0}}d_{\gamma},

.

Recall the induced flat metric on

is defined as

\|\cdot\|:=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}|\cdot+p|.

For any

, choose

such that

. Such an

- If

Let

Then
\rho(f_{\beta,n})=\gamma
, so

\rho(g_{\beta,n})=\frac{\rho(f_{\beta,n})}{n}=\frac{\gamma}{n}=\alpha.

Moreover,

\|D^{\iota}\phi_{\beta,n}\|_{C^{0}}\succcurlyeq(2\pi)^{\iota}n^{\nu}|\sin(n\beta\pi)|>(2\pi)^{\iota}\cdot\frac{2M}{(2\pi)^{\iota}|\sin(d\pi)|}\cdot|\sin(d\pi)|=2M.

Hence,

\alpha\in\mathcal{C}_{M}
. Since
\alpha\in(0,1)
was arbitrary, we conclude

.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
