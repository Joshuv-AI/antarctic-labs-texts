Ultrasound Biomicroscopic Evaluation of Postoperative Anterior Segment Changes in Canine Intumescent Cataracts
Authors: Jiyi Hwang, Donghee Kim, Ji Seung Jung, Sooyeon Lee, Mirae Lee, Haemi Seol, Soohyun Kim, Kyung‐Mee Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate pre‐ and postoperative changes in the iridocorneal angle, ciliary cleft (CC), and ciliary muscle (CM) in canine eyes with intumescent cataracts using ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), with comparative reference to normal and non‐intumescent cataractous eyes.MethodsUBM was performed preoperatively on 61 eyes from 40 dogs, classified into 3 groups: (a) normal (n = 18), (b) cataract (n = 23), and (c) intumescent (n = 20) groups. Following surgery, UBM measurements were conducted on 51 eyes from 37 dogs: (d) postoperative (PO)‐cataract (n = 21) and (e) PO‐intumescent (n = 29) groups. Pre‐ and postoperative measurements were obtained from independent groups of dogs. Parameters analyzed included angle opening distance (AOD), iridocorneal angle (ICA), ciliary cleft width (CCW), ciliary cleft length (CCL), ciliary cleft area (CCA), ciliary body axial length (CBAXL), ciliary process‐sclera angle (CPSA), and ciliary body muscle thickness (CBMT).ResultsThe intumescent cataract group showed significant narrowing of CC collapse. Postoperatively, ICA and CC were significantly wider in the intumescent group, while no significant postoperative difference was observed in CM thickness.ConclusionIntumescent cataracts were characterized by narrowing of the anterior chamber angle and collapse of CC, whereas phacoemulsification was associated with partial widening of CC parameters. These findings suggest that anterior segment structural changes are associated with postoperative prognosis in dogs with intumescent cataracts.

Introduction

Intumescent cataracts are characterized by lens swelling due to intralenticular fluid absorption in both humans and dogs. They are frequently associated with systemic diseases, particularly diabetes mellitus (DM), which can induce changes in lens volume and increase lens thickness [1, 2, 3]. Eyes with intumescent cataract exhibit an increased anteroposterior lens thickness [4]. In some eyes with intumescent cataracts, the swollen lens may mechanically appose the pupillary margin of the iris, potentially interfering with aqueous humor (AH) flow and contributing to pupillary block [5, 6]. If present, this mechanism may promote the accumulation of AH within the posterior chamber and predispose the eye to ocular hypertension or secondary glaucoma [7, 8, 9]. However, the clinical presentation may vary, as concurrent lens‐induced or phacoclastic uveitis associated with leakage of lens material may initially result in reduced intraocular pressure (IOP) in some eyes [10, 11].

The pathophysiology of intumescent cataracts is therefore closely associated with the AH outflow pathway [10], highlighting the importance of evaluating structures involved in AH dynamics using non‐invasive imaging modalities such as ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) or optical coherence tomography (OCT) [9, 12]. In human ophthalmology, several studies have examined changes in anterior chamber parameters in eyes with intumescent cataracts, as well as structural alterations following phacoemulsification [13, 14]. In dogs, several studies have reported postoperative anterior segment changes with typical cataracts [15, 16]; however, to date, no research has characterized the preoperative and postoperative anterior segment features of eyes affected by intumescent cataracts. UBM enables more accurate assessment by providing detailed cross‐sectional visualization of anterior segment structures [17]. The ciliary cleft (CC) is a triangular or trapezoid space within the AH pathway, adjacent to the trabecular meshwork (TM) and located between the cornea and part of the ciliary muscle (CM) [18, 19]. The CM, located within the ciliary body (CB), plays a crucial role in regulating AH outflow through the uveoscleral pathway by contracting and relaxing, thereby influencing AH flow through the extracellular matrix [20]. The CB, a part of the uvea, is a highly specialized structure with multiple functions, including AH production in the ciliary processes, regulation of its outflow through the uveoscleral pathway, and accommodation [21, 22, 23].

Therefore, evaluating CC‐related parameters, including CC area, length, and width, along with iridocorneal angle (ICA) configuration using UBM, may provide clinically relevant information regarding AH outflow dynamics and potential glaucoma risk in dogs with intumescent cataracts. Because structural changes in the ICA, CC, and adjacent CB may affect conventional AH outflow, such alterations may have clinical relevance to IOP‐related complications, including pupillary block in eyes with intumescent cataracts, as well as ocular hypertension or possible glaucoma development before or after cataract surgery. Accordingly, the present study was designed to compare the anatomical characteristics of the ICA, CC, and CM between eyes with intumescent and non‐intumescent cataracts and to evaluate postoperative anatomical differences following phacoemulsification.

Materials and Methods

Collection of Clinical Information

The clinical data of dogs included in this study were collected both retrospectively and prospectively from the Veterinary Teaching Hospital of Chungbuk National University, South Korea, between March 2018 and October 2025. A total of 112 eyes from 71 dogs were evaluated. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (CBNUA‐24‐0090‐01). All enrolled dogs underwent a comprehensive ophthalmological examination performed by a Veterinary Ophthalmology faculty member (KMP) or ophthalmology‐trained veterinarians (JYH, JSJ, DHK, MRL, SYL, and HMS). The examination included slit‐lamp biomicroscopy (MW50D, SHIGIYA, Hiroshima, Japan), Schirmer tear testing (Schirmer Tear Flow Strips, Gulden Ophthalmics, Elkins Park, PA), menace response, pupillary light reflex, dazzle reflex, rebound tonometry (TonoVet Plus, icare, Vantaa, Finland), gonioscopy (Ocular Koeppe Diagnostic Lenses, Ocular Instruments Inc., Bellevue, WA), indirect ophthalmoscopy (Pan Retinal 2.2, VOLK, Mentor, OH), electroretinography (RETevet, LKC Technologies, Germantown, MD), and 50‐MHz UBM (VuPAD, Sonomed Escalon, Lake Success, NY).

Cataract Criteria

The cataract stages in this study were classified as incipient, immature, mature, and hypermature [24]. Cataract stage was determined based on the extent of lens opacity, preservation of tapetal reflection, and associated visual impairment, according to previously described veterinary ophthalmic criteria [24]. Incipient cataracts involved < 15% of the lens volume with preserved tapetal reflection and no significant visual impairment; immature cataracts involved > 15% of the lens with reduced but present tapetal reflection and partial visual impairment; mature cataracts were characterized by complete opacification of the visual axis with absent tapetal reflection and functional blindness; and hypermature cataracts showed lens protein liquefaction and resorption with capsular wrinkling and variable return of tapetal reflection, typically accompanied by lens‐induced uveitis.

Patient Groups

The dogs consisted of five groups: (a) normal (n = 18), (b) non‐intumescent cataract (n = 23), (c) intumescent cataract (n = 20), (d) postoperative (PO)‐non‐intumescent cataract (n = 21), and (e) PO‐intumescent cataract (n = 29). All sample sizes are reported per eye. The non‐intumescent cataract and intumescent cataract groups included cases of immature and mature cataracts, with the intumescent group further characterized by evident lens swelling. Pre‐ and postoperative data were collected from independent cohorts. Not all intumescent cataracts were associated with DM; both diabetic and non‐diabetic dogs were included. In the normal group, no eyes were from diabetic dogs. In the cataract group (3/16 dogs), 4 of 23 eyes were from diabetic dogs, whereas in the intumescent group (4/14 dogs), 8 of 20 eyes were from diabetic dogs. Thus, although DM was more common in the intumescent group, not all intumescent cataracts were associated with DM.

Inclusion Criteria

Patients with senile cataract, hereditary cataract, and diabetic cataract were eligible for inclusion in this study. The etiology of cataracts was classified based on clinical history, signalments, and medical records. Diabetic cataracts were diagnosed in dogs with confirmed DM. Senile cataracts were defined in older dogs without evidence of systemic disease or hereditary predisposition. Hereditary cataracts were presumptively diagnosed in young dogs of predisposed breeds in the absence of systemic disease. The normal group included clinically healthy dogs that visited the hospital for routine dental scaling, whose owners voluntarily consented to UBM examination. Dogs with normal findings on slit‐lamp biomicroscopy, gonioscopy (open angle), and funduscopy were included. Also, only eyes with normal IOP (10–25 mmHg) were included [25]. Lens thickness was measured using a 10 MHz B‐scan ultrasonography. Based on the previous studies, this study defined intumescent cataracts as those with a lens thickness of 7.7 mm or more [26, 27, 28].

Exclusion Criteria

Patients presenting ocular abnormalities other than cataract on comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation were excluded. Patients with hypermature cataract were excluded, as lens‐induced uveitis can lead to ocular hypotony [28]. Furthermore, patients with abnormalities like glaucoma and intraocular hemorrhage, as well as those with a history of previous ocular surgery except phacoemulsification, were excluded. Cases with peripheral iris synechiae were also excluded. Eyes exhibiting abnormal findings on fundoscopic examination or electroretinogram (ERG) were excluded from analysis, and exclusion criteria were applied uniformly across all groups. Eyes that developed postoperative hypertension, underwent glaucoma surgery, or presented with lens luxation were excluded to avoid confounding effects on anterior segment morphology.

UBM Methods

UBM was performed during the ophthalmic examination in both normal and cataractous eyes in this study. UBM imaging was performed in the same location under identical lighting conditions. The pupil was dilated by topical administration of 1% tropicamide (Mydriacyl; Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland). In addition, topical anesthesia was administered using one drop of 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride (Alcaine; Alcon). Before the UBM examination, 2% hypromellose (Hycell; Samil Pharmaceutical, Seoul, South Korea) is instilled onto the cornea. The dogs were positioned in a sitting posture, and the palpebral fissure was manually opened. The UBM was performed by placing a transducer perpendicularly to the corneoscleral limbus in the dorsal quadrant.

In retrospective cases, the postoperative timing of UBM examination was not standardized. In contrast, in the prospective cohort, UBM imaging was performed at least 4 weeks after surgery, taking into consideration the time required for postoperative inflammation to subside.

All measurements were performed using the built‐in measurement tools of the UBM system. Measurements were conducted by a single observer in a mask fashion, with the observer mask to the clinical group assignment; therefore, interobserver agreement could not be assessed. To minimize measurement variability, three separate images were obtained and stored for each eye, and all parameters were measured on each image, with the mean values used for subsequent analyses.

Surgical Methods

The phacoemulsification was performed by a veterinary faculty member (KMP) using two different phacoemulsification systems: The Stellaris PC (Bausch & Lomb Inc., Bridgewater, NJ) and the Pulsar Minimal Stress (OPTIKON, Rome, Italy). Foldable acrylic intraocular lenses (IOLs) (Loki, CRISTALENS, Lannion, France) with a power of +41.0 diopters and a diameter ranging from 12.00 to 14.00 mm were implanted through a 3.0 mm clear corneal incision.

Prior to surgery, the irrigation fluid was prepared by supplementing a balanced salt solution (BSS) or Opticosol solution (Reyon Pharm, Seoul, South Korea) with epinephrine (1 mg/mL) and heparin (5 000 units/mL). During the procedure, an ophthalmic viscoelastic device (Kukje Hyaluronate‐I Inj; KUKJE, Gyeonggi, South Korea) was applied and subsequently removed through irrigation/aspiration (I/A) at the end of the surgery. To ensure complete elimination of the viscoelastic material, I/A was continued for an additional 30 s after complete removal of visualized viscoelastic material.

The corneal incision was closed with 8–0 polyglactin (Vicryl) or 8–0 nylon (Usiol) sutures using a simple interrupted pattern. After surgery, dexamethasone (2 mg/eye) and antibiotics were administered subconjunctivally. Antibiotic treatment consisted of either cefazoline (40 mg/eye, prepared from intravenous formulation) or moxifloxacin (1 mg/eye, topical formulation).

Measurement Parameters

Anterior segment parameters included the angle opening distance (AOD), ICA, and CC‐related measurements. AOD was defined as the perpendicular distance from the inner corneal surface at the Descemet's membrane endpoint to the iris surface [15]. The ICA was measured as the angle formed by the Descemet's membrane, TM, and iris [29, 30] (Figure 1A). Ciliary cleft width (CCW) was measured as the perpendicular distance from the anterior surface of the iris root to the inner sclera [18, 31], while ciliary cleft length (CCL) was defined as the straight‐line distance from the apex of the TM to the midpoint of CCW [19] (Figure 1B). Ciliary cleft area (CCA) was calculated as the total area of the TM using the built‐in free‐form measurement tool of the UBM system [32].

ICA (iridocorneal angle): Angle formed by Descemet's membrane/the TM complex and the iris. (B) Ciliary cleft width and length. CCW (ciliary cleft width): Perpendicular distance from the anterior surface of the iris root to the inner sclera. CCL (ciliary cleft length): Straight‐line distance from the TM corner to the midpoint along CCW. (C) Ciliary cleft area and ciliary body geometry. CCA (ciliary cleft area): Planimetric area encompassing the TM/cleft region. CBAXL (ciliary body axial length): Distance from the midpoint of the ciliary body base to its apex. CB parameters included ciliary body axial length (CBAXL), ciliary process–scleral angle (CPSA), and ciliary body muscle thickness (CBMT). CBAXL was measured as the distance from the midpoint of the CB base to its apex [16, 33]. CPSA was defined as the angle between the long axis of the CB and the extension line of the sclera [34] (Figure 1C). CBMT was measured as the distance from the base of CM to the scleral line along the inner sclera and the angle recess, terminating at the point where the outer iris root meets the dome‐shaped CB [35] (Figure 1D).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). To assess baseline group comparability, categorical variables including sex and breed were analyzed using the Chi‐square test. Because some dogs contributed both eyes to the dataset, linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) were used for all group comparisons to account for inter‐eye correlation. Model assumptions were evaluated by visual inspection of residual plots and Q‐Q plots. Dog identity was included as a random effect. Group was included as a fixed effect, and age and body weight were incorporated as covariates to control for potential confounding effects. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction when significant group effects were detected. Because preoperative and postoperative datasets were derived from independent cohorts, all comparisons between these groups were performed as cross‐sectional analyses. Variable distributions were additionally assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Correlations between lens thickness and UBM parameters were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation analysis. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Canine Characteristics

There were no differences in breed diversity, body weight, and age among the groups. UBM was performed on the control group, comprising a total of 18 eyes from 10 dogs. Most dogs in this group were purebred, with the most common breeds being Poodle (n = 3) and Bichon frise (n = 3). The average age and weight of dogs in this group were 7.3 ± 3.4 years and 6.1 ± 1.7 kg. The mean lens thickness in this group was 6.6 ± 0.5 mm.

The non‐intumescent cataract group consisted of 23 eyes from 16 dogs. Most dogs in this group were purebred, with the most common breeds being Poodle (n = 5), Maltese (n = 3), and Bichon frise (n = 4). UBM measurements were performed on dogs diagnosed with immature or mature cataracts through preoperative examinations, with no concurrent ophthalmic abnormalities other than the cataract itself. The average age and weight of dogs in this group were 9.2 ± 2.5 years and 5.4 ± 2.6 kg. The mean lens thickness in this group was 6.7 ± 0.5 mm.

The intumescent group consisted of 20 eyes from 14 dogs. Most dogs in this group were purebred, with the most common breeds being Maltese (n = 4) and Bichon Frise (n = 2). The average age and weight of dogs in this group were 10.9 ± 2.5 years and 5.1 ± 1.9 kg. The mean lens thickness in this group was 8.5 ± 0.6 mm.

Lens thickness was compared only among the preoperative groups. No statistically significant difference was observed between the normal and non‐intumescent cataract groups (p = 0.43), while both groups exhibited significant differences when compared with the intumescent group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2).

Correlation Between Lens Thickness and Anterior Segment Parameters

Spearman correlation analysis was performed across the pooled baseline dataset, including normal, non‐intumescent cataract, and intumescent cataract eyes, to evaluate the relationship between axial lens thickness and anterior segment parameters. Greater lens thickness was significantly associated with lower values of several UBM parameters, including AOD (ρ = −0.347, p = 0.006), ICA (ρ = −0.474, p < 0.001), CCW (ρ = −0.637, p < 0.001), CCL (ρ = −0.416, p < 0.001), CCA (ρ = −0.612, p < 0.001), and CPSA (ρ = −0.486, p < 0.001). No significant associations were observed between lens thickness and CBAXL (p = 0.996) or CBMT (p = 0.099).

Comparison of Iridocorneal Angle Parameter

Among the preoperative groups, AOD values did not differ significantly among the normal (0.88 ± 0.11 mm), non‐intumescent cataract (0.91 ± 0.18 mm), and intumescent cataract groups (0.79 ± 0.14 mm). No significant difference in preoperative angle width was observed among the groups. Within each cataract category, postoperative AOD values did not differ significantly from their respective preoperative groups (PO‐non‐intumescent cataract: 0.97 ± 0.13 mm; PO‐intumescent cataract: 0.97 ± 0.31 mm) (Figure 3A). Similarly, ICA values did not differ significantly among the normal (14.58° ± 2.24°), non‐intumescent cataract (14.92° ± 2.72°), and intumescent cataract groups (11.82° ± 1.84°). For ICA, the PO‐non‐intumescent cataract group (14.83° ± 3.73°) did not differ significantly from the corresponding preoperative group, whereas the PO‐intumescent cataract group (16.91° ± 4.83°) showed significantly higher ICA values compared with the preoperative intumescent cataract group (p < 0.05) (Figure 3B).

In contrast, in the intumescent cataract group, the PO‐intumescent group (0.41 ± 0.08 mm) showed significantly higher values compared with the preoperative intumescent cataract group (0.27 ± 0.04 mm) (p < 0.05) (Figure 4A).

Comparison of Ciliary Muscle Parameter

For CBAXL, no significant difference was observed among the groups. The mean CBAXL values were 1.64 ± 0.24 mm in the normal group, 1.58 ± 0.24 mm in the cataract group, 1.62 ± 0.23 mm in the intumescent group, 1.60 ± 0.22 mm in the PO‐non‐intumescent cataract group, and 1.65 ± 0.27 mm in the PO‐intumescent group (Figure 5A). For CBMT, only the PO‐intumescent group (0.63 ± 0.11 mm) demonstrated significantly lower values compared with the normal group (p < 0.05) (Figure 5C). Discussion

This study aimed to determine how intumescent cataracts affect anterior segment structures in dogs and characterize the postoperative anterior segment changes observed following surgical management of intumescent cataracts. By comparing normal, non‐intumescent cataract and intumescent cataract eyes using UBM, we sought to assess structural differences associated with lens swelling and its subsequent surgical removal. To our knowledge, this is the first UBM‐based study to characterize the effects of an intumescent lens on the ICA, CC, and CM and to compare these structural features with those observed in postoperative eyes.

Intumescent cataracts are recognized as a major precipitating factor for angle closure, as the swollen lens can displace the iris and CB anteriorly, thereby promoting crowding of the anterior chamber angle [13]. In humans, clinically important angle‐closure mechanisms have been described in association with intumescent cataracts, including pupil‐block, peripheral iris crowding, lens‐related, and retro‐lenticular causes, which often coexist and exert combined vector forces on the iris, CB, and lens [13]. Eyes with primary angle closure glaucoma typically demonstrate reduced CBMT, an anteriorly positioned ciliary process, anterior displacement of the lens, increased lens thickness, and reduced anterior chamber depth [5, 36, 37].

In the present study, lens thickness showed significant negative correlations with several anterior segment parameters, including AOD, ICA, CCW, CCL, CCA, and CPSA. These findings suggest that increasing lens thickness may be associated with a narrower anterior chamber angle and CC structures. This relationship supports the concept that enlargement of the lens can promote anterior segment crowding by displacing adjacent structures anteriorly. In contrast, no significant correlations were observed between lens thickness and CBAXL or CBMT. These results suggest that changes in lens thickness may exert a greater influence on anterior chamber angle and CC configuration than on CB thickness or axial morphology. Notably, the strongest correlations were observed with CCW and CCA, indicating that CC morphology may be particularly sensitive to variations in lens thickness. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously because the correlation analysis was performed using a pooled baseline dataset comprising normal eyes and preoperative non‐intumescent and intumescent cataract eyes. Therefore, the observed associations may partly reflect between‐group differences, particularly the greater axial lens thickness in intumescent cataract eyes, rather than a consistent within‐group relationship.

Regarding the anterior chamber angle parameters, AOD values did not differ significantly among the five groups, suggesting that AOD alone may not be a sufficiently sensitive indicator for distinguishing the structural effects of lens intumescence. This finding differed from observations in humans, where intumescent cataracts and primary angle closure are associated with anterior chamber angle narrowing, and AOD 500 has been reported to increase significantly following phacoemulsification [38, 39, 40]. In contrast, previous canine studies have reported that AOD measurements may not show significant differences as cataracts progress to the mature stage [15], and the present results were more consistent with these previously reported canine findings. In this study, AOD was defined as the perpendicular distance from the inner corneal surface at the Descemet's membrane endpoint to the iris surface [15, 41, 42], as the scleral spur is not consistently identifiable in dogs, and the Descemet's membrane endpoint provides a more reproducible reference in UBM. Similarly, ICA values did not differ significantly among the preoperative groups. However, ICA was significantly wider in the postoperative intumescent eyes compared with preoperative intumescent eyes, whereas no significant difference was observed between the non‐intumescent cataract groups.

In the present study, eyes with intumescent cataracts exhibited marked narrowing of the CC, as reflected by significantly lower CCW, CCL, and CCA compared with both normal and non‐intumescent cataract groups, demonstrating a distinct pattern of CC collapse. These findings suggest that swelling of the lens may lead to anterior displacement and compression of anterior segment structures, resulting in CC narrowing. No significant differences were observed between the normal and non‐intumescent cataract groups, and lens thickness did not differ significantly between these groups. The observed CC narrowing may therefore be interpreted as a structural feature specifically associated with the presence of the intumescent lens. Following phacoemulsification, the mean value of CCW, CCL, and CCA was all significantly greater in the postoperative intumescent cataract group than in the preoperative intumescent cataract group, and the overall CC configuration in the postoperative intumescent group was comparable to that observed in normal eyes. These results indicate that the CC was markedly collapsed in eyes with intumescent cataracts but showed partial widening following removal of the swollen lens. This less collapsed postoperative configuration suggests that lens intumescence contributes to CC narrowing in affected eyes. These findings are broadly consistent with reports in humans describing widening of the anterior chamber angle following lens extraction [14, 43].

In contrast, in the non‐intumescent cataract group, CCW and CCA tended to decrease following surgery compared with preoperative values. This pattern differed from that observed in the intumescent cataract group, suggesting that the structural effects of lens removal on anterior segment configuration may not be identical between these two cataract types. However, in the present study, no significant difference in axial lens thickness was observed between normal and non‐intumescent cataract groups, and additional morphometric parameters such as lens diameter or lens area were not evaluated. Further studies incorporating detailed lens morphometric assessment are needed to more clearly elucidate the underlying mechanisms. The potential influence of surgery‐related factors, including phacoemulsification energy, irrigation/aspiration time, intraoperative fluid dynamics, and residual viscoelastic material, should also be evaluated in future studies.

No significant differences in CBAXL were observed among the groups; however, significant differences were identified in CPSA. The intumescent cataract group exhibited a significantly narrower CPSA compared with the non‐intumescent cataract group, suggesting that anterior displacement of anterior segment structures caused by the swollen lens may influence the orientation of the ciliary process. Similar findings have been reported in human studies, in which increased lens thickness or anterior displacement of the lens is associated with alterations in CB configuration, including anterior rotation of the ciliary process and thinning of the CM [37, 44].

For CBMT, only the postoperative intumescent cataract group demonstrated significantly lower values compared with the normal group, whereas no significant differences were observed among the other groups. These findings indicate that while overall CB structural changes were limited, a relative reduction in CBMT was observed in the postoperative intumescent group. Previous reports have suggested that surgery‐related factors, including intraoperative tissue manipulation and postoperative tissue responses, may affect CB morphology [16, 45]. Intraocular inflammation is typically associated with CB edema and thickening rather than thinning, and such features were not observed in the present study. Therefore, the lower CBMT in the postoperative intumescent group is unlikely to be explained by inflammation alone. Instead, it may reflect residual structural differences associated with preoperative anterior segment crowding, surgery‐related remodeling, or a combination of both. In human studies, narrowing of the anterior chamber angle has been associated with thinning and anterior displacement of the CM in eyes with primary angle closure [36, 46]. Although consistent changes in CM thickness were not identified across all groups in the present study, these reports support the possibility that anterior segment crowding may contribute to alterations in CB configuration.

Based on these structural observations, eyes with intumescent cataract may exhibit a more crowded anterior segment and a narrower CC than eyes with non‐intumescent cataracts, potentially reflecting an increased susceptibility to glaucoma. For this reason, assessment of glaucoma risk both before and after surgery is clinically important in this population. Previous reports have shown that postoperative narrowing of CC may increase the likelihood of secondary glaucoma or ocular hypertension following cataract surgery [15, 47]. In contrast, the present study demonstrated that CC parameters in postoperative intumescent eyes were not significantly different from the mean values observed in normal eyes. These findings suggest that phacoemulsification may be associated with partial postoperative expansion of the CC in eyes with intumescent cataracts.

CBMT was significantly lower in the PO‐intumescent group than in the normal group (p = 0.023). However, no significant difference in CBMT was observed between preoperative and postoperative intumescent eyes (p = 1.00). In contrast, CC parameters in postoperative intumescent eyes showed no significant differences from those in normal eyes. These findings indicate that, although CC dimensions in postoperative intumescent eyes were not significantly different from normal values, CBMT was still lower than that in normal eyes. Given the role of the CM in AH dynamics, these structural differences may be relevant to glaucoma susceptibility. However, because the present findings are based on intergroup comparisons, the functional implications of lower CBMT should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, postoperative changes in these UBM variables may not solely reflect cataract‐related structural differences but may also be influenced by surgery‐related effects. Nevertheless, these observations support the importance of continued postoperative monitoring in eyes undergoing cataract surgery.

In humans, intumescent cataracts commonly induce pupillary block and anterior chamber angle closure due to marked lens swelling. As a definitive treatment, the swollen lens is removed via phacoemulsification and replaced with an intraocular lens implant, thereby relieving the obstruction and reducing the elevated IOP [14, 48]. As noted previously, the angle opening distance and the iris, which had been displaced anteriorly by the swollen lens, were positioned more posteriorly in postoperative eyes compared with preoperative intumescent eyes [49]. In the present study, dogs with intumescent cataracts similarly demonstrated postoperative expansion of both the anterior chamber angle and the CC after phacoemulsification, consistent with the postoperative findings in humans. Although anatomical differences exist between humans and dogs in the anterior segment, the restoration of the angle observed in canine intumescent cataracts suggests that similar mechanisms may underlie the postoperative response.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly within each subgroup, should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, because the pre‐ and postoperative datasets were not fully matched, the main analyses were conducted as cross‐sectional comparisons between independent groups, and interpretations of postoperative structural changes should therefore be made with appropriate caution. Although all postoperative UBM examinations were performed at least one month after surgery to minimize the influence of acute postoperative inflammation, the follow‐up interval varied widely, ranging from one to 44 months, which limited the ability to consistently evaluate the temporal pattern of chronic structural changes. Second, gonioscopy was routinely performed preoperatively; however, retrospective gonioscopic findings were not consistently recorded using a standardized grading system, which precluded objective quantitative comparison between gonioscopic findings and UBM measurements. Furthermore, UBM evaluation was restricted to the dorsal quadrant and therefore did not capture the full 360‐degree anterior segment anatomy. In addition, although tropicamide‐induced mydriasis may have affected iris configuration to some extent, its use was necessary for routine perioperative management of cataract surgery, and pre‐ and postoperative UBM examinations were performed using the same protocol. Therefore, this potential effect should be considered when interpreting UBM‐derived anterior segment parameters, particularly those associated with iris position and ciliary cleft morphology. Third, because only normotensive eyes were included, it was not possible to directly assess how the structural features identified in the present study relate to IOP elevation or the development of postoperative ocular hypertension. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized postoperative imaging intervals, long‐term follow‐up, and inclusion of cases with elevated IOP or postoperative glaucoma will therefore be necessary.

In summary, eyes with intumescent cataracts exhibited a narrower CC than eyes with non‐intumescent cataracts, suggesting distinct anterior segment structural features that may be relevant to secondary glaucoma. In postoperative intumescent eyes, anterior chamber angle and CC parameters were close to those observed in normal eyes, whereas CBMT was relatively lower. These findings provide foundational data for understanding the anterior segment structural characteristics of intumescent cataracts and the postoperative morphological response following phacoemulsification and highlight the need for further investigation into the relationship between these structural features, AH dynamics, and glaucoma risk in affected dogs.
