Partitions in quantum theory
Augustin Vanrietvelde, Octave Mestoudjian, Pablo Arrighi

Abstract
Decompositional theories describe the ways in which a global physical system can be split into subsystems, facilitating the study of how different possible partitions of a same system interplay, e.g. in terms of inclusions or signalling. In quantum theory, subsystems are usually framed as sub-C* algebras of the algebra of operators on the global system. However, most decompositional approaches have so far restricted their scope to the case of systems corresponding to factor algebras. We argue that this is a mistake: one should cater for the possibility for non-factor subsystems, arising for instance from symmetry considerations. Building on simple examples, we motivate and present a definition of partitions into an arbitrary number of parts, each of which is a possibly non-factor sub-C* algebra. We discuss its physical interpretation and study its properties, in particular with regards to the structure of algebras’ centres. We prove that partitions, defined at the C*-algebraic level, can be represented in terms of a splitting of Hilbert spaces, using the framework of routed quantum circuits. For some partitions, however, such a representation necessarily retains a residual pseudo-nonlocality. We provide an example of this behaviour, given by the partition of a fermionic system into local modes.



1
Introduction

The ability to consider a whole as made out of parts – a foundational tenet of any physical theory – can be conceptually framed in two opposite ways. In the
bottom-up
or
compositional
approach, one starts with individual systems, taken to be primitive, and then composes them together to form bigger, joint systems. The
top-down
or
decompositional
approach goes the other way: starting with a large ‘supersystem’
\Omega
, one asks about the ways in which it can be carved up into a bunch of smaller parts. In most of physics – and especially in quantum information – the bottom-up approach is the standard, to the point that it usually goes without saying.

There are, however, many situations in which it proves advantageous to think top-down. This is because, in contrast with the compositional one, the decompositional approach does not posit a system’s partition as written in stone: it allows one to consider scenarios in which this partition depends on the features of the situation at hand, and/or on the perspective one decides to adopt on it. Consequently, and even more crucially, it is the appropriate setting for
comparing
different ways of partitioning a same system. In other words, not only does it permit us to state that
\Omega
can be partitioned either into
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
or into
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, but it then allows us to study their interplay – e.g., whether
A_{1}
is included in
B_{1}
, whether the information in
A_{1}
corresponds to correlations between
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, whether one can find a common fine-graining of these two bipartitions into a quadripartition, etc.

Research directions that require to compare different partitions are, in fact, ubiquitous; let us just briefly mention a few examples from currently active research subjects in the field of quantum foundations. A first instance is quantum causal modelling
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
: there, it can be seen that a unitary channel’s causal structure comes down to how it relates the partition of its input into subsystems to the partition of its output. Quantum reference frames
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
provide another example from a completely different direction: it has been demonstrated
[
9
]
that they essentially correspond to a relativity of subsystems, i.e. to the possibility to carve the world in different ways, with quantum reference frame transformations precisely embodying how these partitions relate.

See especially Ref.

]
for an approach to quantum reference frames that resonates particularly closely with our considerations in this paper.
Finally, in the controversy

on whether an architecture called the Quantum Switch

; in that perspective, the move from one notion of events to another is yet another case of a repartition.

In the field of (finite-dimensional) quantum information, past works adopting the top-down perspective have put forward the idea that subsystems of a system
\Omega
are best framed as corresponding to
sub-C* algebras
of the C* algebra of operators on

(an idea that first arose, in another context, in algebraic quantum field theory

non-factor
C* algebras (the mathematical and physical meanings of which we will present in detail in a moment). As we shall argue, this is a mistake: non-factor subsystems appear naturally in physics, typically from of the imposition of symmetries and superselection rules. More than that, their peculiar features often prove critical at the structural level: in each of the examples we just mentioned of research fields involving partitions, non-factor algebras have been revealed to play a pivotal role – see e.g. Refs.
[
1
,

]
for quantum causal models, Ref.
[

[

Therefore, it is an important task to pin down what partitions of quantum systems are, and analyse their features, in the presence of non-factor subsystems. This is what this paper achieves. In a sense, our first and most important result might well be the realisation that, as opposed to the standard case of factorisations, even defining partitions in this general setting is a non-trivial task. More specifically, while
bi
partitions (partitions into two parts) of factors admit a natural definition even when the parts are not factors (as in Refs.
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
), it is with partitions into three parts or more (which, to our knowledge, we are the first to consider) that most subtleties arise. As we shall explain, these complications are essentially due to a congenital defect of non-factor partitions, which we call ‘Failure Of Local Tomography’ or FOLT: in such partitions, there exists genuinely global information that is not accessible even in the correlations between measurements on the individual parts.

In a nutshell, our contributions here are the following. First, we provide a self-contained presentation of the conceptual and mathematical foundations for using sub-C* algebras (including the non-factor ones) to model subsystems in quantum theory. Second, we motivate and propose a mathematical definition of partitions of a quantum system into an arbitrary number of parts. Third, we prove some of its properties, showing in particular that
centres
(the parts of C* algebras that encapsulate their non-factorness) possess a remarkably rigid structure: more specifically, we display a theorem showing how the centre of a conjunction of subsystems can be deduced from the centres of each subsystem. Fourth, we study
representations
of a partition, by which we mean ways to see them as corresponding to splittings of an underlying Hilbert space structure. We show that, using the framework of
routed quantum circuits

[
32
,
33
]
, representations can be found in which an action on any individual part acts locally on a corresponding Hilbert space; but that, very surprisingly, some partitions are
not fully representable
, in the sense that, for these, there are, in any representation, actions on the conjunction of several parts that take a non-local form. We show that the partition of a fermionic system into local modes is an example of a non-fully representable partition.

Given the pervasiveness of non-factor subsystems in physics, and the rich structure of partitions involving those, we expect that the general theory presented here will find much practical use in the framing and analysis of concrete situations involving different partitions of a same system. A first concrete instance, in the field of quantum causal modelling, is provided by the proof of the causal decomposability of 1D quantum cellular automata
[
34
]
, which makes heavy use of the present framework and results.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we present the conceptual foundations for framing subsystems as sub-C* algebras and the mathematical structure of the latter, and we discuss in particular the physical status of subsystems corresponding to non-factor sub-C* algebras and the significance of FOLT. In Section
3
, we present a simple example of a tripartition, in order to build intuition as to its properties and to unveil some requirements for a sound definition of partitions in general. In Section
4
, we motivate and propose such a definition and study its properties, showing in particular how centres of joint parts can be deduced from centres of individual ones. In Section
5
, we demonstrate how partitions at the C* algebraic level can be represented in terms of operators over a split Hilbert space, using the framework of routed circuits. In Section
6
, we investigate the partition of a fermionic system into local modes, and example of a non-fully representable partition. We conclude in Section
7
.

2
Conceptual and mathematical preliminaries

In this section we introduce, in a pedagogical way, the C*-algebraic perspective on subsystems and some of its properties. We first present the conceptual foundations of the algebraic perspective and discuss its operational meaning. Then, we introduce some of the mathematical toolkit for analysing the structure of C* algebras. Finally, we discuss the standard case of
bipartitions of factors
in the C*-algebraic framework, and illustrate it on a concrete physical scenario, superposition of trajectories.
Note that this section is written for non-familiar readers; those accustomed to seeing things through the algebraic lens can safely skip part or all of it.

2.1
(Sub)systems as (sub-)C* algebras

The standard framework for finite-dimensional quantum theory is that of Hilbert spaces. However, a decompositional approach is hard to formalise in this picture: typically, given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}\cong\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}
, there is no sharp way to characterise
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}
as corresponding to a substructure of
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
.
2
2

2

In particular, it is worth reminding that
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}
is not a subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
. Subspaces of Hilbert spaces appear in sectorisations such as
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}=\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}
, and thus correspond not to parts but to
alternatives
.

The decompositional approach fits more nicely within the algebraic picture
[
35
]
. The shift to the algebraic picture is given by moving from the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
to
{{{\omega}}}:=\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})
, the space of linear operators on it. In other words, a quantum system is then to be seen as a
C* algebra
, which in finite dimension is essentially a complex vector space equipped with an inner multiplication and an adjunction. (We leave the mathematical definition and presentation of C* algebras to the next section, in order to first stress conceptual ideas.) From this perspective, subsystems arise naturally as corresponding to
sub-
C* algebras, i.e. sub-vector spaces closed under multiplication and under the adjoint: typically, in the example of
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}=\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}
, one can define system
A_{1}
as corresponding to
\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\left\{f_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{2}}\,|\,f\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}})\right\}\subseteq{{{\omega}}}
, which can easily be verified to be a sub-C* algebra. As for
A_{2}
, it corresponds to the symmetrically defined
\mathcal{A}_{2}
– which, as we shall see in Section
2.3
, arises naturally as the complement of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
through the use of commutants.

The idea of framing subsystems as corresponding to sub-C* algebras of operators is of course hardly new. It is in particular one of the core ingredients of algebraic quantum field theory (AQFT)
[
25
,
26
]
, where algebras are associated to regions of spacetime. The same idea has also been investigated in the context of finite-dimensional quantum information, in particular in order to pin down ‘virtual subsytems’ immune to noise
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
3
3

3

This has laid the foundation for a rich literature discussing how a
preferred
partition into subsystems – and therefore a preferred notion of locality – can arise, e.g. from the spectrum of the global system’s Hamiltonian
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
or from correlation measures
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
(see also Ref.
[
40
]
, which argues that this preferred partition should be evolving in time). Our considerations here are agnostic with respect to that question: we focus on defining what a partition is and what its features are, not on what might make a certain choice of a partition more natural than others.
We can also mention other approaches to subsystems, in which C* algebras are replaced with Lie algebras
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
, convex cones of states
[
44
]
, groupoids
[
45
]
, sets of transformations in general physical theories
[
46
,
28
,
47
]
, or restrictions with respect to a canonical basis
[
48
,
49
]
.

In this paper, the claim that subsystems correspond to sub-C* algebras is accepted as a starting point. Justifying that claim, e.g. from operational considerations, is of course a task of great importance (which has, at least partially, been undertaken in past work on the subject). However, a proper study of that question would require an article of its own. We therefore leave it out of the scope of our present work, so we can focus on examining the consequences of upholding it, especially in the case of multipartitions.

The operational interpretation of associating a sub-C* algebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{{{\omega}}}
to a subsystem
A
of
\Omega
is that
\mathcal{A}
contains the actions on
\Omega
that only affect
A
. Depending on the kind of physical data that one takes as primordial, this can take slightly different forms. If one emphasises the role of observables (as is typical of AQFT, for instance), the interpretation is that observables on
A
are the Hermitian elements of
\mathcal{A}
. If one emphasises unitary evolutions, it is that evolutions of
A
are the unitary elements of
\mathcal{A}
. If one emphasises quantum transformations described as completely positive maps (as is typical in quantum-informational approaches), it is that the transformations of
A
are maps that admit a Kraus decomposition whose Kraus operators are in
\mathcal{A}
. Here, we shall assume that these slightly varying flavours are different sides of the same coin.
4
4

4

However, rigorously proving their equivalence is a subtle issue, which relates to the previously mentioned problem of operationally justifying the use of sub-C* algebras to describe subsystems. We also leave this important question for future work.

This interpretation can be illustrated by resorting to agents. To
A
, we can associate an agent Alice that ‘only has access to
A
’;
\mathcal{A}
then contains the observables (resp. unitary evolutions or Kraus operators of quantum transformations) that Alice can measure (resp. apply) on
\Omega
through that access. We will make much use of this picture since it greatly helps intuition. Note, however, that it does not commit us to upholding agents as a fundamental ingredient of physics; it can very well be taken as nothing more than an illustrative tool.

2.2
A classification of C* algebras

2.2.1
Wedderburn-Artin

Let us recall the definition of a C* algebra in finite dimension. Note that we could equivalently talk about Von Neumann algebras, which in finite dimension are the same notion.

Definition 2.1

(* algebras)
.

A (finite-dimensional)
* algebra

{{{\omega}}}
is a finite-dimensional algebra over the field of the complex numbers, equipped with an involution
{\dagger}
satisfying:

•

\forall x,y\in{{{\omega}}},\,(x+y)^{\dagger}=x^{\dagger}+y^{\dagger}
;

•

\forall x,y\in{{{\omega}}},\,(xy)^{\dagger}=y^{\dagger}x^{\dagger}
;

•

\forall x\in{{{\omega}}},\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{C},(\lambda x)^{\dagger}=\lambda^{*}x^{\dagger}
.

A sub-* algebra of
{{{\omega}}}
is a subalgebra of
{{{\omega}}}
closed under the dagger.

Definition 2.2

(*-algebra homomorphisms)
.

A linear map
h
between two * algebras is a
homomorphism of * algebras
if it preserves multiplication (
h(xy)=h(x)h(y)
) and the dagger (
h(x^{\dagger})=h(x)^{\dagger}
). If
h
is bijective, then it is an
isomorphism of * algebras
.

Definition 2.3

(C* algebras)
.

In finite dimension, a
C* algebra

{{{\omega}}}
is a * algebra that is isomorphic to a sub-* algebra of the *-algebra
\Lin(\mathcal{H})
of linear operators over some (finite-dimensional) Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, equipped with the standard adjoint as its dagger.

Any sub-* algebra of
{{{\omega}}}
is then a C* algebra, and can thus be called a sub-C* algebra of
{{{\omega}}}
.
5
5

5

The reader more used to infinite dimension will notice that we picked a definition of C* algebras that makes no reference to the requirement of featuring a norm that plays well with the dagger and yields a Banach space structure. We did this deliberately, in order to stress the point that in finite dimension, and as far as one is interested in
sub
-C* algebras, it is not necessary to care about norms and the topologies they induce. This is essentially due to the fact that in finite dimension, all norms over any complex vector space induce equivalent topologies, and all complex vector spaces are complete.
Similarly, * algebra homomorphisms and isomorphisms between C* algebras are called C* homomorphisms and isomorphisms.

A natural example of a finite-dimensional C* algebra is that of an algebra
{{{\omega}}}=\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})
of operators over a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
. C* algebras that are isomorphic to such algebras of operators are called
factors
. Importantly, not all finite-dimensional C* algebras are factors; in general, they are direct sums of factors. The idea that such
non-factor
C* algebras should be taken seriously is a central tenet of this work.

Theorem 2.1

(Wedderburn-Artin
[
50
]
)
.

Given a finite-dimensional C* algebra
{{{\omega}}}
, there exists a list of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k})_{k\in K}
, indexed by a finite set
K
, such that

{{{\omega}}}\cong\bigoplus_{k\in K}\Lin\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}\right)\,.

(1)

This can be restated in an even more concrete way, which allows us to see
{{{\omega}}}
as an algebra of
block-diagonal
operators over a single Hilbert space with a preferred sectorisation.

Corollary 2.1
.

Writing
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}:=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}
in the previous theorem,

{{{\omega}}}\cong\left\{f\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall k,f\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}\right\}\,.

(2)

In this representation,
{{{\omega}}}
becomes the set of operators on
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}:=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}
that map each of the
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}
’s within itself, i.e. the ones whose matrices in an adapted basis are block-diagonal.

2.2.2
A representational tool: routes

This block-diagonalness finds a natural formalisation using
routes

[
32
]
, which will be an important tool through this paper. Let us provide a basic introduction. The idea is that if we have two sectorised Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{A}^{k}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}=\bigoplus_{l\in L}\mathcal{H}_{B}^{l}
and a relation
6
6

6

Relations are generalisations of functions. A relation maps each element of its domain to a (possibly empty) set of elements of its output domain.

\eta:K\to L
– which we can equivalently represent as a Boolean matrix
(\eta_{k}^{l})_{k\in K,l\in L}
–, then we can interpret
\eta
as encoding sectorial constraints on how linear maps
\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{B}
are allowed to connect input sectors to output sectors, with its zeroes stipulating the disallowed connections. The set of linear maps satisfying these constraints is

\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}):=\left\{f\in\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall k,f\left(\mathcal{H}_{A^{k}}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{l\textrm{ such that }\eta_{k}^{l}=1}\mathcal{H}_{B^{l}}\right\}\,.

(3)

A relation
\eta
seen in this way is called a route, and we say that
f
follows the route
\eta
if it is in
\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
. If
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\mathcal{H}_{B}
7
7

7

By this we mean that their sectorisations are also the same, and are labelled by the same set.
, we simply write
\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{A})
. Note that
\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
forms a C* algebra if and only if
\eta
is a partial equivalence relation.

Using the identity relation
\delta
(corresponding to the Kronecker-delta Boolean matrix
(\delta_{k}^{l})_{k,l}
), (
2
) can be rewritten as

{{{\omega}}}\cong\Lin_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})\,.

(4)

This means that one can make use of the diagrammatic representation of
routed circuits

[
32
]
to deal with non-factor C*-algebras. In this representation, sectorised Hilbert spaces are represented by wires whose names feature superscripts; for instance,
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{A}^{k}
will be denoted
A^{k}
.
8
8

8

The
k
here should be thought of as a free variable, denoting the existence of different possible values for
k
, and not a single specific value of it. This is similar to the Einstein notation convention for linear tensors, in which
g_{\mu\nu}
refers to a whole tensor and not to one of its coefficients.

f:A^{k}\to B^{l}

\eta_{k,l}

2.2.3
Centres

It is important for our purposes to emphasise the critical aspects of C* algebras, which yield crucial structural handles on them. First among these is the notion of
centre
. The centre of
{{{\omega}}}
,
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
, is the set of elements of
{{{\omega}}}
that commute with everything in it:

\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}}):=\left\{f\in{{{\omega}}}\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall g\in{{{\omega}}},fg=gf\right\}\,.

(8)

The centre plays a key role in characterising an algebra. It is easy to see that it itself forms a commutative C* algebra, i.e. one whose elements all commute pairwise. This allows us to characterise it very nicely using the following slogan.

Slogan 1
.

A commutative C* algebra is just a bunch of orthogonal projectors.

Let us make this more formal. We say that
\pi\in{{{\omega}}}
is an orthogonal projector if
\pi\pi=\pi
and
\pi^{\dagger}=\pi
, and that the projectors
\pi^{1}
and
\pi^{2}
are orthogonal if
\pi^{1}\pi^{2}=0
. Our slogan then takes its roots in the following result, proven in Appendix
A.1
.

Theorem 2.2

(Commutative C* algebras)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be a commutative (finite-dimensional) C* algebra. Then there exists a unique (finite) set of orthogonal projectors
\{\pi^{k}\}_{k\in K}
, pairwise orthogonal and non-null, forming a basis of
\mathcal{Z}
.

We call this unique set
\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z})
, the set of
\mathcal{Z}
’s
atomic projectors
. They are the second important structural handle that we will be using.

Atomic projectors have a direct connection to the direct sum structure displayed in Theorem
2.1
. Indeed, if, for a C* algebra
{{{\omega}}}
, we take
\{\pi^{k}\}_{k\in K}
to be the atomic projectors of its centre
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
, and consider the

\forall k\in K,\quad\pi^{k}{{{\omega}}}:=\{\pi^{k}f\,\,|\,\,f\in{{{\omega}}}\}\,,

(9)

then it is easy to see that these form factor sub-C* algebras of
{{{\omega}}}
, and that

{{{\omega}}}\cong\bigoplus_{k\in K}\pi^{k}{{{\omega}}}\,.

(10)

As is evident from the way the
\pi^{k}
’s are defined in Appendix
A.1
, the
\pi^{k}{{{\omega}}}
’s are then precisely
{{{\omega}}}
’s blocks, i.e., the direct sum in (
10
) corresponds to that in (
1
).

Thus, the atomic projectors of an algebra’s centre precisely designate its blocks. In particular, a C* algebra is a factor if and only if its centre is trivial, i.e. only contains multiples of the identity.

2.3
Bipartitions of factors

2.3.1
Definition

We can now apply our newfound tools to a first discussion of bipartitions. Taking a system
\Omega
corresponding to a factor C* algebra
{{{\omega}}}
, our question is: what would it mean to partition it into two complementary parts
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
? As we already argued, these should first correspond to two sub-C* algebras
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
of
{{{\omega}}}
. In addition, a natural way to express their complementarity is to employ
commutants
.

Given
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{{{\omega}}}
,
\mathcal{A}
’s commutant within
{{{\omega}}}
is the set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
of elements of
{{{\omega}}}
that commute with all elements of
\mathcal{A}
,

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\{f\in{{{\omega}}}\,\,|\,\,\forall a\in\mathcal{A},fa=af\}\,.

(11)

It is natural, from any of the different conceptual perspectives on subsystems enunciated earlier, to argue that the algebras corresponding to the two subsystems in a bipartition should be each other’s commutants. For instance, in the perspective where subsystems are defined by the observables on them, it is natural to say that the subsystem complementary to
A_{1}
corresponds to the observables that commute with the observables on
A_{1}
; and a similar reasoning can be made in a perspective that emphasises the role of unitary evolutions. This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 2.4

(Bipartitions of factors)
.

Let
{{{\omega}}}
be a factor. We say that a pair of two sub-C* algebras
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})
of it forms a
bipartition
, denoted as
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
, if

\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}\,.

(12)

Note that the symmetry of this definition relies on the important fact that any sub-C* algebra
\mathcal{A}
of a factor
{{{\omega}}}
is
closed under the bicommutant
, i.e.

\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{A}\,.

(13)

This ensures that ‘the complementary of the complementary of
A
is indeed
A
.’ This will not be the case for
{{{\omega}}}
non-factor. Note that this is also a trademark of finite dimension.
9
9

9

This is a direct consequence of Von Neumann’s bicommutant theorem
[
51
]
, which states that in a factor, any subalgebra’s bicommutant is equal to its closure in the weak and strong topologies; in finite dimension, every sub-C* algebra is closed with respect to any norm, and thus is equal to its bicommutant.

A basic and important fact about bipartitions of factors is that they share a common centre.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
be a bipartition of a factor. Then
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
.

Proof.

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1}):=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}=:\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
.
∎

In particular,
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is a factor if and only if
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is a factor; in that case, we call the partition a
factorisation
.

2.3.2
Representation

We now turn to finding a representation theorem for such bipartitions. By this, we mean finding a way of seeing
{{{\omega}}}
as a concrete algebra of operators on a Hilbert space, such that this Hilbert space itself is ‘made of parts’ corresponding to
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
.
10
10

10

This use of the word ‘representation’ should of course not be confused with the study of representation theory.

Theorem 2.3

(Representations of bipartitions of a factor

11

11

Theorem
2.3
can be obtained as a special case of the more general Theorem
5.2
which we will prove later on.
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
be a bipartition of a factor. Then there exist a finite set
K
, two lists of Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}})_{k\in K}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k}})_{k\in K}

\iota\colon{{{\omega}}}\to\bigoplus_{k\in K}\Lin\left(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k}}\right)

(14)

such that

\iota\left(\mathcal{A}_{1}\right)=\left\{\bigoplus_{k\in K}f^{k}_{{A_{1}^{k}}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{{A_{2}^{k}}}\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall k,f^{k}\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}})\right\}\,;

(15a)

\iota\left(\mathcal{A}_{2}\right)=\left\{\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathbb{1}_{{A_{1}^{k}}}\otimes g^{k}_{{A_{2}^{k}}}\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall k,g^{k}\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k}})\right\}\,.

(15b)

We see that we find a representation of
{{{\omega}}}
as a space of operators on

\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k}}\,.

(16)

This structure of ‘sectorisation then factorisation’ is typical of partitions. We can see that the general recipe for getting two complementary systems is to first slice up a Hilbert space into sectors, then to factorise each sector in two. Operators on the first (second) part are then those that are acting on the first (second) factors of each sectors.

Note that the sectors here are precisely those designated by the atomic projectors of the common centre (by Proposition
2.1
)
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
. In particular, in the case of a factorisation, that common centre is trivial and thus so is the direct sum, and we recover a standard representation as a tensor product.

In the general case where the direct sum is not trivial, one practical issue with this representation is that it blends together tensor products and direct sums, which is impractical to work with. A trick around this is to rather see the operators as acting on an extended space

\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{\textrm{ext}}:=\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}}\right)}_{=:\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}}\otimes\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{l\in K}\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{l}}\right)}_{=:\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}}\,,

(17)

but to require them to be null outside of the
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{l}}

k\neq l

where
\mathbb{\Pi}_{A_{1}}^{k}
(resp.
\mathbb{\Pi}_{A_{2}^{k}}

\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k}}
(resp. of
\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}

\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k}}

12

2.3.3
FOLT

It is important to discuss a seemingly disturbing feature, which will be central to what follows: if
\mathcal{A}_{1}

\mathcal{A}_{2}

\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}

not

{{{\omega}}}

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq{{{\omega}}}

\mathcal{A}\vee\mathcal{B}
to be the closure of
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
under C*-algebraic operations (dagger, multiplication and addition), we have

\mathcal{A}_{1}\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})^{\prime}=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})^{\prime}\subsetneq{{{\omega}}}\,.

(25)

In other words,
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
taken together are unable to recover the non-block diagonal operators in the sectorisation given by their common centre. This disturbing fact that ‘
{{{\omega}}}
is more than the sum of its parts’

14

14

Note that this goes beyond the well-known idea that in quantum theory, entanglement means that ‘the whole is more than the sum of its parts.’ Indeed, in the standard case of factorisations, the possibility for entanglement does not prevent the fact that one has
\mathcal{A}_{1}\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}={{{\omega}}}

when the parts are allowed to be non-factors will be important to keep in mind; we will call it ‘Failure Of Local Tomography’, or FOLT.
15
15

15

The operational meaning of FOLT, its fundamental or merely circumstantial status, and its ontological implications are a subject of active discussion (see e.g. Ref.
[
52
]

In particular, we are being deliberately loose as to how the algebraic property we call FOLT relates to FOLT as defined at the strictly operational level, in order to stress the algebraic structure at hand, in the spirit of our resolution about operational considerations described in Section
2.1

One might very well find FOLT unpalatable, and want to forbid the cases in which it appears. This is an understandable stance – FOLT will indeed, down the line, lead to numerous complications and counter-intuitive effects –, but we need to stress that
adopting this view is exactly the same as requiring that subsystems only correspond to factor algebras

\mathcal{A}_{1}

\mathcal{A}_{2}

redundancy
in our bipartitions, i.e. the fact that algebras corresponding to complementary subsystems have a non-trivial intersection (given by
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})

This is an apt moment to discuss how our approach to subsystems compares to previous C* algebra-based proposals. Indeed, a significant fraction of those precisely impose local tomography as an axiom in order to forbid non-factor algebras. Refs.
[
23

24
]

\mathcal{A}_{1}\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}={{{\omega}}}

[
24

), thereby restricting themselves to considering factorisations (which they call ‘tensor product structures’). This assumption remains prevalent in subsequent literature, e.g. Refs.
[
36

37

38

39

40

[
29

30

31

[
28

The case of AQFT is more subtle. There, non-factor algebras are allowed, but an extra requirement, the
split property

[
26

Without entering the details of the framework, the broad idea is that the non-factorness of the algebra associated to a spacetime region should always be ‘screened’ at an arbitrarily close distance, using a factor algebra. This has the effect of essentially banning redundancies, i.e. non-trivial intersections, between algebras associated to independent regions of spacetime. Indeed, these redundancies describe correlations between the observables of these regions, and the usual justification for upholding the split property

16

We disagree with this justification and therefore with the importance of upholding the split property, but this is of course a subject for other work.

2.4
The interpretation of non-factor (sub)systems

While factors match our usual notion of a quantum system, it might not be clear to the reader how one should physically interpret the non-factorness of the algebra associated to a physical system. There are two, complementary, possible interpretations: one in terms of conservation rules, the other in terms of partial classicality.

2.4.1
Conservation rules, and the superposition of trajectories example

Let us start with conservation rules. The idea is that a system whose dynamics gets subjected (either in principle or in practice) to the constraint of preserving a certain quantity will then be best described by a non-factor C* algebra. Indeed, let us take a system
\Omega
, normally described with
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
, but of which we stipulate that its dynamics has to preserve the value of a certain observable
\mathcal{O}\in\textrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})
. This means that any unitary operator encoding this dynamics should commute with
\mathcal{O}
. Writing
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
’s sectorisation into
\mathcal{O}
’s eigenspaces as
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}:=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{k}
, it is easy to see that this commutation relation is equivalent to the requirement
U\in{{{\omega}}}
, with
{{{\omega}}}
the C* algebra of block-diagonal operators with respect to this sectorisation, defined as in (
2
).

The non-factorness of the algebra, in this interpretation, is ascribable to a certain degree of degeneracy induced by the preservation condition. One is still allowed to perform any quantum operation within a given eigenspace of
\mathcal{O}
– i.e. ‘the whole block is allowed’ –, but not to map between two eigenspaces – i.e. ‘cross-block terms are forbidden’.

How can non-factor algebras as
sub
systems of a certain factor supersystem be understood in this perspective? The
superposition of trajectories
scenario
[
53
]
gives a good example. Suppose our supersystem
\Omega
starts as the combination of a localised qudit
P
– which we call a particle – and a control qubit
C
, and that we engineer a dynamics in which the qubit’s state in the computational basis coherently controls which of two paths
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
the particle follows. Agents at
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
can act on the particle if it is there, but cannot destroy it or create a new one. It is natural to think of the
A_{n}
’s as having access to subsystems of
\Omega
. Going down this path leads to finding that these subsystems correspond to non-factor subalgebras of
{{{\omega}}}=\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})
.

Take
A_{1}
, for instance: if she gets the particle, she can act on its inner degrees of freedom – i.e. implement a unitary on the qudit –; if the particle goes the other way, she only sees a vacuum and can do nothing; and importantly, she can also measure whether the particle is there, and dephase between the two alternatives in case of a superposition. In the representation corresponding to the ‘particle/control’ partition,
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}:=\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P}
, her set of possible unitary operations is of the form

\textrm{Unit}_{A_{1}}=\left\{\outerproduct{1}{1}_{C}\otimes U_{P}+\alpha\outerproduct{2}{2}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}\,\,\middle|\,\,U\in\textrm{Unit}(\mathcal{H}_{P}),\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\,,

(26)

which is precisely the unitary group of a sub-C* algebra
\mathcal{A}_{1}\subset{{{\omega}}}
, while the possible actions of
A_{2}
correspond to the commutant algebra,
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
. Their intersection and common centre is given by the atomic projectors
\outerproduct{1}{1}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}
and
\outerproduct{2}{2}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}
.

Thus, thinking of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
as subsystems of
\Omega
naturally leads to linking them to non-factor sub-C* algebras of
{{{\omega}}}
. Non-factorness is ascribable to the particle-number preservation condition imposed on the agents’ operations. Redundancy – the fact that both share a non-trivial set of common operations, corresponding to the common centre of their algebras – is due to the fact that both can locally measure the ‘which path did the particle go’ observable and dephase with respect to it. As for FOLT, it denotes the fact that in this scenario, the phase between the two paths is not only inaccessible to either agents, but cannot even be seen in correlations between results of any local measurements; one can only access it by acting globally on the two trajectories.

Preservation of the number of particles strongly suggests that there is a sense in which this number becomes a classical variable, at least as far as local agents are concerned. This ties in with the other way of interpreting non-factorness of subsystems.

2.4.2
Partial classicality

There is a sense in which the non-factorness of a C* algebra can be seen as corresponding to partial classicality of a system, although one has to be careful about what is meant by classicality here. The idea is that, if one reasons in terms of the observables accessible to an agent acting on the system, only the block-diagonal ones are allowed, meaning that the system cannot be measured to be in a superposition of the blocks – although it can be measured to be in a superposition of states within a given block. Thus, there is partial classicality in the sense that one cannot measure a superposition of answers to the ‘which block is the system in?’ question. For instance, in our paradigmatic example of the superposition of trajectories, an individual agent cannot measure a superposition of answers to the ‘do I hold the particle?’ question.

However, one should be aware of the specific features of this notion of classicality. First, the non-factorness also means that one cannot map between the blocks at all, even in a non-coherent way. This is at odds with the standard notion of a classical variable, which can of course be mapped between its different classical states. Second, it should be emphasised that even though an agent in the possession of a non-factor system cannot
measure
relative phases between the blocks, she can
modify
them, which is not a classical operation. Third, as we saw in the example of the superposition of trajectories, FOLT means that even though the phase between the blocks is not accessible
locally
in each system in a bipartition – and not even in the correlations between local measurements –, it still is accessible through operations on the global system
\Omega
. This ties in directly with the previous point: the local dephasings cannot be measured locally, but are still meaningful as they affect the global system.

This can all best be seen in the extreme example of a bipartition into subsystems corresponding to
commutative
sub-C* algebras. This is the other extreme compared to the case of a factor subsystem: here, each algebra is a direct sum of trivial, one-dimensional factors, with the algebra’s elements being diagonal,

f=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\pi^{k}\,,

(27)

as already stated in Theorem
2.2
. Furthermore, the algebras are then equal:

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\,.

(28)

Although this sounds like an odd state of affairs, it in fact has a natural interpretation. The idea is that a specific orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}
has been chosen, and that each agent has access to a copy of the system’s state in that basis. Thus, both agents have access to the same set of actions, which amount only to measuring in that basis or to dephasing with respect to it. Because all these possible actions commute, this is not in tension with the claim that the subsystems are separate. Keeping in mind our cautionary remarks about what is meant by ‘classical’ here, one can think of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
as completely classical systems.

3
An example of a multipartition: the superposition of three trajectories

Although we hope that there was some added value in the self-contained nature of the previous section, it was merely rehashing conceptions and results which can be found elsewhere in the literature. Our point will now be to show how these ideas can be further pushed to provide a consistent and satisfactory theory of
multi
partitions, consisting in more than two parts.

As we will shortly see, the definition of bipartitions does not generalise straightforwardly to multipartitions; subtleties will arise and force us to make decisions about what we deem a good multipartition. To motivate these decisions and start building some intuition, we start with a concrete case of a situation one would naturally call a multipartition.

3.1
The scenario

This concrete case is simply given by the natural extension of our previous ‘superposition of trajectories’ to the case of three parties. It consists in considering a system
\Omega
initially partitioned into a qudit
P
and a qutrit
C
, and subjected to a dynamics in which
P
is sent in one (or a superposition) of three trajectories
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
, dependent on
C
’s state. Similarly to before, it is natural to think of agents at each of the
A_{n}
’s as having access to subsystems of
\Omega
.

What does, say, the C* algebra
\mathcal{A}_{1}
corresponding to
A_{1}
’s actions look like? As before,
A_{1}
can only act on the inner degrees of freedom on
P
if she holds it, measure whether she holds it, and dephase with respect to that alternative. In the representation corresponding to the ‘particle/control’ partition,
\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}=\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P}
, this corresponds to the non-factor C* algebra

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\left\{\outerproduct{1}{1}_{C}\otimes f_{P}+\alpha(\outerproduct{2}{2}_{C}+\outerproduct{3}{3}_{C})\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}\,\,\middle|\,\,f\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{P}),\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\,.

(29)

We can already note a feature that will prove typical of multipartitions.
A_{1}
can only distinguish, as well as dephase between, the ‘
C=1
’ and the ‘
C\in\{2,3\}
’ alternatives. This translates the fact that, in the case where the particle is not sent to her, she has no way to figure whether it has been sent to
A_{2}
or to
A_{3}
(or to a superposition of both). Thus the centre
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
of her algebra (which we can think of as displaying the ‘classical’ – in the sense of Section
2.4.2
– information she has access to) is given by the atomic projectors
\outerproduct{1}{1}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}
and
(\outerproduct{2}{2}_{C}+\outerproduct{3}{3}_{C})\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}
. Because the situation for
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
is symmetric, we can see that

\forall m\neq n,\,\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{m})\neq\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{n})\,,

(30)

which stands in contrast to the case of bipartitions, in which one has Proposition
2.1
.

3.2
Conjunctions of systems and FOLT

There is another question we should mention: that of ‘conjunctions of systems’. Our theory should be able to specify a notion of the system ‘
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
taken together’, denoted
A_{\{1,2\}}
. This would correspond to an agent that can act globally on trajectories 1 and 2, but not on trajectory 3. As usual, such an agent can measure, and dephase with respect to, whether she holds the particle, and to do whatever action that preserves its presence when she holds it. Because the agent is acting globally on both trajectories, the latter set of actions now includes moving the particle from path 1 to path 2, or measuring, as well as creating, superpositions between these two paths. This corresponds to the C*-algebra

\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}=\left\{\sum_{i,j\in\{1,2\}}\outerproduct{j}{i}_{C}\otimes f^{ji}_{P}+\alpha\outerproduct{3}{3}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall i,j,\,f^{ji}\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{P}),\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\,.

(31)

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}})
is given by the atomic projectors
(\outerproduct{1}{1}_{C}+\outerproduct{2}{2}_{C})\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}
and
\outerproduct{3}{3}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}
, which makes it equal to
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{3})
; in fact, one can check that
(\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}},\mathcal{A}_{3})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
according to our previous Definition
2.4
.

We emphasise that here again, ‘
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
is more than the sum of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
’, in the sense that

\mathcal{A}_{1}\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}=\left\{\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}}\outerproduct{i}{i}_{C}\otimes f^{i}_{P}+\alpha\outerproduct{3}{3}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{P}\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall i,\,f^{i}\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{P}),\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}\,.

(32)

This corresponds to the fact that
A_{\{1,2\}}
has access to the coherence between trajectories 1 and 2, something that is not available even in correlations between potential measurements of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
; and that, correspondingly,
A_{\{1,2\}}
can also map, as well as create superpositions, between these trajectories, something that no coordinated action of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
can do.

This feature should not make us any more spooked than we (potentially) already were: it is just another avatar of FOLT, which we showed in Section
2.3.3
to be a necessary consequence of admitting subsystems corresponding to non-factor algebras. In that sense, it bears no novelty compared to the case of bipartitions, with (
32
) an analogue of (
25
).

3.3
Representation

The final question is whether we can find an adapted representation for our tripartition, i.e. a way to see
{{{\omega}}}
as a space of operators on a Hilbert space made of three parts corresponding to the
A_{n}
’s, in analogy to what we obtained for bipartitions in Section
2.3.2
. As it turns out, we can: denoting
\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}^{0}}\cong\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}^{1}}\cong\mathcal{H}_{P}
, denoting the Boolean matrix

\forall k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\{0,1\},\quad\sigma_{\vec{k}}:=\begin{cases}1\textrm{ if exactly one of the }k_{n}\textrm{'s is equal to }1,\\
0\textrm{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(33)

and

\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}:=\bigoplus_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\textrm{ such that }\sigma_{\vec{k}}=1}\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k_{1}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k_{2}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{3}^{k_{3}}},

(34)

we can find a representation
{{{\omega}}}\cong\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega})
in which

\mathcal{A}_{1}\cong\left\{\bigoplus_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\textrm{ such that }\sigma_{\vec{k}}=1}f^{k_{1}}_{A_{1}^{k_{1}}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{2}^{k_{2}}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{3}^{k_{3}}}\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall k_{1},f^{k_{1}}\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}^{k_{1}})\right\}\,,

(35)

and the other
\mathcal{A}_{n}
’s have a symmetric form (we leave the proof out, as it will derive from the general representation theorem we shall prove later). This gives us an analogue of Theorem
2.3
.

We can also, like we did in Corollary
2.2
, turn this into a representation on an extended space that would not feature the awkward blend of direct sums and tensor products. Indeed, defining

\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{\textrm{ext}}:=\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{k_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}^{k_{1}}}\right)}_{=:\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}}\otimes\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{k_{2}\in\{0,1\}}\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}^{k_{2}}}\right)}_{=:\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}}\otimes\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{k_{3}\in\{0,1\}}\mathcal{H}_{A_{3}^{k_{3}}}\right)}_{=:\mathcal{H}_{A_{3}}}\,,

(36)

\tilde{\mathbb{\Pi}}:=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\textrm{ such that }\sigma_{\vec{k}}=1}\mathbb{\Pi}_{A_{1}}^{k_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{\Pi}_{A_{2}}^{k_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{\Pi}_{A_{3}}^{k_{3}}\in\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{\textrm{ext}})\,,

(37)

and the relation
\eta^{\rm tot}:\{0,1\}^{3}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
defined by

\left(\eta^{\rm tot}\right)_{\vec{k}}^{\vec{l}}:=\sigma_{\vec{k}}\,\sigma_{\vec{l}}\,,

(38)

we have a representation given by the C*-algebra isomorphism

\iota:\quad{{{\omega}}}\hskip 11.74988pt\to\hskip 11.74988pt\Lin_{\eta^{\rm tot}}(\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^{\textrm{ext}})\,,

(39)

in which

\iota\left(\mathcal{A}_{1}\right)=\left\{(f_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{3}})\,\tilde{\mathbb{\Pi}}\,\,\middle|\,\,f\in\Lin_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}})\right\}

(40)

and the other
\mathcal{A}_{n}
’s have a symmetric form.

Furthermore, in this representation, we also obtain a clean form for the algebras corresponding to joint systems, like
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
; taking the relation
\eta
on
K\times K
to be the partial trace of
\eta^{\rm tot}
, i.e.

\forall k_{1},k_{2},l_{1},l_{2}\in\{0,1\},\eta_{k_{1}k_{2}}^{l_{1}l_{2}}:=\left(\Tr_{3}\left(\eta^{\rm tot}\right)\right)_{k_{1}k_{2}}^{l_{1}l_{2}}=\sum_{k_{3}}\left(\eta^{\rm tot}\right)_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}^{l_{1}l_{2}k_{3}}\,,

(41)

which can be checked to represent a partial equivalence relation on
\{0,1\}^{2}
, we can pick
\iota
such that

\iota\left(\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}\right)=\left\{(f_{A_{1}A_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{3}})\,\tilde{\mathbb{\Pi}}\,\,\middle|\,\,f\in\Lin_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}})\right\}\,,

(42)

in which we can see
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
as acting on the
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
Hilbert spaces.

How should we understand the route
\eta_{k_{1}k_{2}}^{l_{1}l_{2}}
? One can compute it to be equal to
1
if and only if either all its coefficients are null, or exactly one of its inputs and exactly one of its outputs are equal to 1. In other words, it encodes two constraints on a linear map
f
: first, that neither in the inputs nor in the outputs of
f
can there be two particles (which would correspond to having two coefficients equal to 1); second, that the presence or absence of the particle should be preserved by
f
. We see that this corresponds exactly to the operational constraints on agents acting globally on trajectories 1 and 2 that we described earlier.

This is from a physical perspective; from a mathematical perspective, on the other hand, we defined
\eta
in (
41
) solely from the sectorial correlations matrix
\sigma
, by considering the partial trace of
\eta^{\rm tot}
, itself obtained out of
\sigma
. We shall see that this procedure works in every multipartition to find the route followed by a certain joint-system algebra.

]

4
A theory of multipartitions

In this section, we propose a theory of what it means in general to take a partition of a quantum system into more than two parts, with some of these possibly corresponding to non-factor algebras.

4.1
Motivating our choices

Suppose we have a quantum system
\Omega
, described by an algebra
{{{\omega}}}
, and that we want to partition it into subsystems
A_{n}
, with the labels
n
belonging to a finite set
X
. The first important point is that
specifying the algebras corresponding to the individual
A_{n}
’s is not sufficient
; indeed, we also need to specify what the conjunctions of systems are, i.e. what it means to act globally on a collection
S\subset X
of systems, like we did with
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
in our concrete example above. In the standard case of factorisations, this is superfluous, as one then always has
\mathcal{A}_{S}=\bigvee_{n\in S}\mathcal{A}_{n}
; however, the generic presence of FOLT (cf. Sections
2.3.3
and
3.2
) means we cannot rely on this. Thus, a multipartition should take its labels not in X, but in its powerset
\mathcal{P}(X)
, i.e. be a mapping

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}:\,\,\mathcal{P}(X)&\to\textrm{Sub}\left({{{\omega}}}\right)\\
S&\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{S}\,,\end{split}

(44)

where we write
\textrm{Sub}({{{\omega}}})
for the set of sub-C* algebras of
{{{\omega}}}
.

How can we make sure that such a mapping yields a good multipartition? The safest way would be to check that, for any two disjoint sets of labels
S,T\subseteq X
,
(\mathcal{A}_{S},\mathcal{A}_{T})
forms a bipartition of
\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T}
. However, so far we only defined bipartitions of a
factor
, and
\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T}
might in general not be one – for instance, in our concrete example of a tripartition in Section
3
,
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
is not. Thus, our first goal should be to get a good definition of bipartitions
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})
of
{{{\omega}}}
when
{{{\omega}}}
is not a factor; our definition of multipartitions will directly derive from it.

A first idea would be to simply reuse our definition for the factor case, i.e. to ask for
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
. However, this turns out to be too strong. Indeed, this requirement would directly entail that

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\supseteq\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\,,

(45)

which is, for instance,
not
satisfied in our paradigmatic example, as can be seen in (
30
).

How can we understand this discrepancy? The idea is that any commutant taken within
{{{\omega}}}
necessarily includes its centre
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
, as the latter commutes with all of
{{{\omega}}}
by definition. If, following Section
2.4.2
, we think of
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
as encoding the classical information available in
\Omega
, we see that our naive definition would entail that both
A_{n}
’s necessarily hold copies of the whole of this classical information. We see how this sounds unnecessarily conservative: surely, one should allow this classical information to be split in whatever way between the systems, including but not restricted to the possibility that both systems fully share it.

In order to avoid the inconveniences caused by the naive use of the commutant definition, we should thus only apply it within individual blocks of
{{{\omega}}}
, which are factors by definition; i.e., writing
\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}}))=\{\pi^{k}\}_{k\in K}
, we should ask for
\pi^{k}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}=\pi^{k}\mathcal{A}_{2}\,\forall k
. This condition alone, however, is not sufficient; for instance, it would entail that when
{{{\omega}}}
is a commutative algebra, it can be partitioned into two trivial subalgebras
\{\lambda\mathbb{1}|\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
. What we are missing is the condition that
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
itself is spanned by
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
and
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
– in other words, that the classical information stored in
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
includes the classical information in
{{{\omega}}}
.

4.2
Definition

Thus, we define bipartitions in general in the following way. We remind that, for two C* algebras
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{{{\omega}}}
and an orthogonal projector
\pi\in\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
,
\pi\mathcal{F}
is the C* algebra
\pi\mathcal{F}:=\{\pi f=f\pi|f\in\mathcal{F}\}
.

Definition 4.1

(Bipartitions)
.

Let
{{{\omega}}}
be a C*-algebra, and
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\subseteq{{{\omega}}}
sub-C* algebras of it. We say that
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})
forms a
bipartition
of
{{{\omega}}}
(denoted
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
) if the following two conditions are satisfied:

\forall k\in K,\,\,\pi^{k}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}=\pi^{k}\mathcal{A}_{2}\,;

(46a)

\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\vee\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\,;

(46b)

where the atomic projectors of
{{{\omega}}}
’s centre are denoted as
\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}}))=\{\pi^{k}\}_{k\in K}
.

Note that this matches Definition
2.4
when
{{{\omega}}}
is a factor. We can now directly leverage this to get a definition of multipartitions.

Definition 4.2

(Partitions)
.

Let
{{{\omega}}}
be a C* algebra. A
partition
of it, labelled by the finite set
X
, is a mapping

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}:\,\,\mathcal{P}(X)&\to\textrm{Sub}\left({{{\omega}}}\right)\\
S&\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{S}\,,\end{split}

(47)

satisfying the following conditions:

\mathcal{A}_{X}={{{\omega}}}\,;

(48)

\mathcal{A}_{\emptyset}=\{\lambda\mathbb{1}\,|\,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}\,;

(49)

\forall S,T\subseteq X\textrm{, disjoint, }(\mathcal{A}_{S},\mathcal{A}_{T})\vdash\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T}\,.

(50)

We then denote
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}\vdash{{{\omega}}}
.

4.3
Properties

Let us now investigate the properties of partitions under our definition (all proofs for this Section are given in Appendix
A.2
). First, we can ask how bipartitions relate to commutants, since we know that we don’t necessarily have
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
. The idea is that the leeway
\mathcal{A}_{2}
has in not being equal to
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
corresponds to how much of
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
it includes.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
, then

\mathcal{A}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}\,.

(51)

Note that this is non-trivial since in a bipartition of a non-factor
{{{\omega}}}
,
the
\mathcal{A}_{n}
’s are not necessarily closed
, in the sense that

\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\vee\mathcal{A}_{n}\,.

(52)

so that in general
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\prime\prime}
. This non-closure under the bicommutant can be seen as the structural root for a certain number of troublesome features, but it is inescapable; ruling it out would mean requiring
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}\,\,\forall n
, which we saw is too strong for our needs.

Next, we want to investigate how centres of the different algebras are related. This can be summed up in a useful slogan.

Slogan 2
.

In a partition of a factor
{{{\omega}}}
, the centre of an algebra
\mathcal{A}_{S}
corresponds to redundant elements, i.e. to elements of
\mathcal{A}_{S}
that are also in the complementary algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{S}}
.

For
{{{\omega}}}
non-factor, the centre of
\mathcal{A}_{S}
might, in addition, include bits of
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
.

Let us make this rigorous, starting with the case of bipartitions, where we have the following Proposition generalising Proposition
2.1
. To alleviate notations, we take the habit of writing
\mathcal{Z}_{S}:=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{S})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})\vdash{{{\omega}}}
, then

\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\left(\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}\right)=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\left(\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\vee\mathcal{Z}_{2}\right)\,.

(53)

Given Definition
4.2
, this directly entails that in a partition, writing
\bar{S}:=X\setminus S
, we have

\forall S\subseteq X,\quad\mathcal{Z}_{S}=\mathcal{A}_{S}\cap\left(\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\vee\mathcal{A}_{\bar{S}}\right)=\mathcal{A}_{S}\cap\left(\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\vee\mathcal{Z}_{\bar{S}}\right)\,.

(54)

In addition, we can also use this to see how the centres of joint systems are determined by those of individual ones together with
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
. This is a remarkable property, showing that the structure of centres is actually quite rigidly constrained by the definition of a partition.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}\vdash{{{\omega}}}
, then

\forall S\subseteq X,\quad\mathcal{Z}_{S}=\left(\bigvee_{n\in S}\mathcal{Z}_{n}\right)\cap\left(\bigvee_{m\not\in S}\mathcal{Z}_{m}\vee\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})\right)\,.

(55)

In other words,
the centre of
\mathcal{A}_{S}
is made up exactly of these elements that can be found both in the centres of the individual algebras within it, and in the combination of the individual algebras outside of it with the centre of
{{{\omega}}}
itself.
In particular, information that is present only within the
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
’s with
n\in S
but not elsewhere is
not
in
\mathcal{Z}_{S}
– i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{S}
includes elements that do not commute with the projectors encoding it.

It is important to understand what this ‘disappearance of centres’ means physically. The idea is that if there is some classical information shared only between systems with labels
x\in S
, and not present in the centre of the whole system
\Omega
(i.e. not considered to be classical overall), then any agent acting globally on the whole of
A_{S}
(or on a supersystem of it) does not see it as classical and can map between its sectors. One can see this in action in our example of
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
in Section
3.2
, in which an agent acting globally on trajectories 1 and 2 can map between these trajectories: in that case, the classical information that becomes quantum in the eyes of the superagent is that about whether the particle is in trajectory 1 or in trajectory 2.

5
A representation theorem for partitioned algebras

We defined partitions in an algebraic manner; we now investigate how they can be concretely represented as sets of operators over Hilbert spaces. In Sections
2.3.2
and
3.3
, we showed how bipartitions and our paradigmatic example of a tripartition admitted representations over composed Hilbert spaces, in which each algebra could be seen as the one acting on the corresponding part of the Hilbert space. Here, we discuss the generalisation of this result to all partitions.

As it turns out, some features of what we did in this earlier example can always be reproduced, but not all. More precisely, we can always find a representation in which each of the algebras for individual parts is well-localised, i.e. is acting over the corresponding Hilbert space. Furthermore, in such a representation, we can also compute the routes for the algebras corresponding to joint systems in a systematic way. However, it is not always possible to find a representation in which each of the algebras corresponding to joint systems is the one acting on the corresponding part of the Hilbert space.

For the remainder of this section, we fix a C* algebra
{{{\omega}}}
and a partition
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}
of it.

5.1
Representations of centres

In Section
3.3
, we saw that the representation of our example of a tripartition involved the use of non-trivial Boolean matrices, such as the Boolean matrix
\sigma
encoding sectorial correlations defined in (
33
), and the route
\eta
used in the representation of the joint algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}
, defined in (
41
). Here, we show how this can be generalised.

As we showed in Proposition
4.3
, for any set
S\subseteq X
, the centre
\mathcal{Z}_{S}
of the joint systems
\mathcal{A}_{S}
is determined by the individual centres
\mathcal{Z}_{n},n\in X
and the centre of the global algebra
\mathcal{Z}_{X}=\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
. There is an analogous results for the route used to describe
\mathcal{Z}_{S}
: as we will now show, this route is determined by the sole data of
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
and of redundancies between the
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
’s.

We start by indexing each of the
\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z}_{n})
’s,

\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z}_{n})=\left\{\pi_{n}^{k_{n}}\right\}_{k_{n}\in K_{n}}\,.

(56)

We also define

K:=\bigtimes_{n\in X}K_{n}\,,

(57a)

\tilde{K}:=\left\{\vec{k}\in K\,\,\middle|\,\,\prod_{n\in X}\pi_{n}^{k_{n}}\neq 0\right\}\,\subseteq K\,,

(57b)

and we write the Boolean indicator of
\tilde{K}
in
K
as
\sigma
, defined by

\forall(k_{n})_{n\in X}\in K,\quad\sigma_{\vec{k}}=\begin{cases}1\textrm{ if }\vec{k}\in\tilde{K}\\
0\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(58)

This encodes sectorial correlations – or in more structural terms, redundancies in the centres –, and generalises (
33
). We can then generalise (
41
) in the following way. From
\mathcal{Z}_{X}=\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
, we can define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\tilde{K}
through

\forall\vec{k},\vec{l}\in\tilde{K},\quad\vec{k}\sim\vec{l}\iff\exists\mu\in\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z}_{X})\textrm{ such that }\prod_{n}\pi_{n}^{k_{n}},\prod_{n}\pi_{l}^{k_{n}}\preccurlyeq\mu\,;

(59)

we extend it to a partial equivalence relation on
K
, which we call
\eta_{X}
. From it, for any
S\subseteq X
, we define a relation (which we will prove is itself a partial equivalence relation)
\eta_{S}
on
\bigtimes_{n\in S}K_{n}
:

\begin{split}\forall S\subseteq X,\quad&\forall(k_{n})_{n\in S},(k_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in S}\in\bigtimes_{n\in S}K_{n},\\
&\vec{k}\sim_{\eta_{S}}\vec{k^{\prime}}\quad\iff\quad\exists(l_{n})_{n\in X\setminus S},\,\,\vec{k}\times\vec{l}\sim_{\eta_{X}}\vec{k^{\prime}}\times\vec{l}\,.\end{split}

(60)

More concretely,
\eta_{S}
is simply the partial trace (in the theory of Boolean matrices) of
\eta_{X}
over its indices in
X\setminus S
.

For a given
S
, we choose a set of indices
K_{S}
for the set of
\eta_{S}
’s equivalence classes, writing the latter as
\{E_{k_{S}}\}_{k_{S}\in K_{S}}
. With these notations, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
S\subseteq X
,
\eta_{S}
is a partial equivalence relation. In addition, picking a set of indices
K_{S}
to label
\eta_{S}
’s equivalence classes and denoting the latter as
\{E_{k_{S}}\}_{k_{S}\in K_{S}}
, we have
\textrm{AtomProj}(\mathcal{Z}_{S})=\left\{\pi_{S}^{k_{S}}\right\}_{k_{S}\in K_{S}}
where

\forall k_{S}\in K_{S},\quad\pi_{S}^{k_{S}}:=\sum_{\vec{k}\in E_{k_{S}}}\left(\prod_{n\in S}\pi_{n}^{k_{n}}\right)\,.

(61)

The proof of that Theorem is given in Appendix
A.3
.

In other words, the
\mathcal{Z}_{S}
’s are determined solely from the specification of
\eta_{X}
, which itself is the combination of the data contained in
\sigma
(encoded in
\eta_{X}
’s partialness) and in
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
(encoded in
\eta_{X}
’s equivalence classes).
\eta_{S}
is inferred from
\eta_{X}
as its partial trace (as a Boolean matrix) on the
n\not\in S
coefficients.

5.2
The main theorem

With the
\eta_{S}
’s defined, we can state our general representation theorem.

Theorem 5.2

(Representations of partitions)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}\vdash{{{\omega}}}
. Following Theorem
2.1
we denote, for every
n\in X
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}\cong\bigoplus_{k_{n}\in K_{n}}\Lin\left(\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}}^{k_{n}}\right)
,
\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}}:=\bigoplus_{k_{n}\in K_{n}}\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}}^{k_{n}}
, with
\mathbb{\Pi}_{A_{n}}^{k_{n}}
as the orthogonal projector of
\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}}^{k_{n}}
and

6.1
The algebra of physical operators on a fermionic system

Let

\displaystyle a_{i}a_{j}+a_{j}a_{i}=0\,,

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{j}a^{\dagger}_{i}=0\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A_{\emptyset}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathbb{1}\,,

(71a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\{1\}}

\displaystyle=\textrm{Span}(\{\mathbb{1},a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{1},a_{1}a^{\dagger}_{1}\})\,,

(71b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2\}}

\displaystyle=\textrm{Span}(\{\mathbb{1},a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{1},a_{1}a^{\dagger}_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{2},a_{2}a^{\dagger}_{2},a_{1}a_{2},a^{\dagger}_{1}a^{\dagger}_{2},a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2},a_{1}a^{\dagger}_{2}\})\,,

(71c)

etc.

6.2
Representations of the algebra of physical operators

The partition of a fermionic system into local modes is not fully representable.

Proposition 6.4
.

For
N\geq 3
, the partition
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}\vdash{{{\omega}}}
is not fully representable.

We want to emphasise that this result is
not
about the (rather trivial) fact that, in order to ensure anticommutation between an
a_{i}
and an
a_{j}
, at least one of them has to be represented as acting on the Hilbert space of the other. Indeed, an
a_{i}
by itself is not a physical operator, and thus not an element of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
(or even of
{{{\omega}}}
). What is shown here is that
even when restricting to physical operators
, at least some of them have to be represented as acting on Hilbert spaces outside of the modes they are affecting.

This fact is in itself not new: it has recently appeared in the literature in different guises – see e.g. Refs.
[
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
]
. These references proved obviously related but technically disjoint statements; our own Proposition, which we proved using our own language for clarity, states the form of the result that matches our framework.

The failure of full representability, therefore, does not mean that our framework captures non-physical examples: on the contrary, such a failure is a common feature of quantum theory, arising in natural physical theories, such as that of fermionic systems. This should not be surprising since full representability concerns not the morphological and relational features of a partition -- or in other words, its algebraic structure -- but only our ability to frame it in terms of Hilbert spaces. A non-fully representable partition is perfectly well-behaved at the algebraic level. The conclusion is simply that the formalism of Hilbert spaces sometimes fails to capture this good behaviour, and should therefore be taken with a grain of salt.
18
18

18

That Hilbert spaces can betray us is a common theme in infinite dimension, where they are routinely traded for an algebraic picture, essentially due to convergence issues. The case at hand shows that a similar point can be made even in finite dimension, where the use of Hilbert spaces can lead to pseudo-nonlocality.

7
Conclusion

Our contributions can be grouped into four classes. First, we made a case for the inclusion of non-factor subsystems in a decompositional approach to quantum theory, and discussed their operational meaning. Second, we motivated and put forward a theory of partitions of a quantum system into any number of parts. Third, we investigated the structural properties of this theory, in particular the well-structured behaviour of centres and its physical meaning. Fourth, we demonstrated how, using the framework of routed quantum circuits, algebraic partitions can be represented in terms of Hilbert spaces; but also how, for some partitions, this representation necessarily involves a residual amount of pseudo-nonlocality in the actions on joint parts, with the display of a concrete example of such a partition.

A remarkable payoff of this work is how it shows that, when non-factorness is involved, partitions display a strikingly rich mathematical structure. It would be surprising if this rich mathematical structure did not have a counterpart in a rich physical structure. Thus, we can reasonably hope that our framework holds potential for discovering as well as elucidating important physical facts in more concrete cases, especially in the presence of symmetries, and in particular in the fields we mentioned in the introduction. The fact that our framework is a central component of a proof of causal decomposability, elucidating the structural features of certain quantum causal structures
[
34
]
, is a first demonstration of its practical use.

We only provided a theory of partitions into ‘eternal subsystems’, in the sense that there was no attempt here to consider ‘
A
at
t_{1}
’ as a different subsystem than ‘
A
at
t_{2}
’. Yet, there is reasonable conceptual ground to hold that these constitute different loci of intervention. Following this cue would lead to a theory of ‘time-thin subsystems’ – which should more aptly be called ‘events’ –, a prospect which we think holds both great potential for discoveries, and major technical challenges. In particular, this would be the proper way to frame the ‘spacetime repartitions’ that we hinted at in our brief introductory discussion of the Quantum Switch controversy, and to bridge with the framework of time-delocalised subsystems
[
13
,
20
,
21
]
. This might also connect with the argument made in Ref.
[
40
]
that partitions should be evolving in time.

Another interesting direction for further research could be to lift our self-imposed restraint from entering too much the realm of operational justifications for framing subsystems as sub-C* algebras. More generally, investigating how this paper’s ideas can or cannot extend to operational frameworks, in which subsystems are defined through subsets of quantum
transformations
and the commutation thereof
[
46
,
28
]
, would be important. An idea to bring in further operational considerations while keeping the structural power of algebraic approaches would be to investigate whether the requirement of commutation between subalgebras could be replaced with
commutation up to a global phase
, since the latter is operationally meaningless.

z\in\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
; this is equivalent to asking that

commutes with every

,
a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}
,
a_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}
,
a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}
for
i,j\in\{1,...,N\}
.

First let us see what it means for
z
to commute with
n_{i}
,

. By definition of the algebra
{{{\omega}}}
,
z
can be decomposed in a unique way as
z=z_{0}+a_{i}z_{1}+a^{\dagger}_{i}z_{2}+n_{i}z_{3}
where the
z_{j}
’s contain no appearance of
a_{i}
or
a^{\dagger}_{i}

\begin{split}0=[z,n_{i}]&=[z_{0},n_{i}]+[a_{i}z_{1},n_{i}]+[a^{\dagger}_{i}z_{2},n_{i}]+[n_{i}z_{3},n_{i}]\\
&=0+[a_{i},n_{i}]z_{1}+[a^{\dagger}_{i},n_{i}]z_{2}+0\\
&=a_{i}z_{1}-a^{\dagger}_{i}z_{2}\,,\end{split}

(101)

and thus
z_{1}=z_{2}=0
. This means that for any
i
, the only operator acting on the
i^{\text{th}}
mode appearing in

is
n_{i}
.

Next, let us see what it means for
z
to commute with
a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}
,
i,j\in\{1,...,N\}

{{{\omega}}}
and the previous commuation result,
z

z=z_{0}+n_{i}z_{1}+n_{j}z_{2}+n_{i}n_{j}z_{3}
where the
z_{k}
contain no appearance of
a_{i}
,
a^{\dagger}_{i}
,
a_{j}
,
a^{\dagger}_{j}
. We can then compute

\begin{split}0=[z,a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}]&=[z_{0},a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}]+[n_{i},a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}]z_{1}+[n_{j},a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}]z_{2}+[n_{i}n_{j},a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}]z_{3}\\

z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}=0
. The commutation of
z
with
a_{i}a_{j}

Finally let us see what it means for
z

a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}
,
i,j\in\{1,...,N\}

z

z=z_{0}+n_{i}z_{1}+n_{j}z_{2}+n_{i}n_{j}z_{3}

z_{k}

a_{i}
,
a^{\dagger}_{i}
,
a_{j}
,
a^{\dagger}_{j}

\begin{split}[z,a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}]&=[z_{0},a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}]+[n_{i},a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}]z_{1}+[n_{j},a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}]z_{2}+[n_{i}n_{j},a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}]z_{3}\\
&=0+a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}z_{1}-a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}z_{2}+0\\
&=a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}(z_{1}-z_{2})\end{split}

(103)

so that
z_{1}=z_{2}
. The commutation with
a_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}

Combining these relations, we get that

\forall i\neq j\in X
, it can be decomposed as

z=z_{0}^{ij}+n_{i}z_{1}^{ij}+n_{j}z_{1}^{ij}-2n_{i}n_{i}z_{1}^{ij}

a_{i}
,
a^{\dagger}_{i}
,
a_{j}
,
a^{\dagger}_{j}
.

z

\mathbb{1}
in the canonical decomposition of
z
is
0
. Now up to multiplication by a scalar we can distinguish two possibilities: the coefficient of
n_{1}
in the canonical decomposition of
z

1

0
.
Suppose that it is
1
. Let
j\neq 1
; we can write the decomposition

z=z_{0}^{1j}+n_{1}z_{1}^{1j}+n_{j}z_{1}^{1j}-2n_{1}n_{i}z_{1}^{1j}\,,

(105)

where, because the coefficient of
n_{1}
in the canonical decomposition of
z

1

\mathbb{1}
has coefficient
1
in the the canonical decomposition of
z_{1}^{1j}
. It follows that
n_{j}
has coefficient
1
and
n_{1}n_{j}
has coefficient
-2
in the canonical decomposition of
z
. By applying this reasoning for the different

we get that
\forall i<j\in\{1,...,N\},
, the coefficient of
n_{i}
in the canonical decomposition of

1

n_{i}n_{j}

-2
. Let now
1\neq j<k

z=z_{0}^{1j}+n_{1}z_{1}^{1j}+n_{j}z_{1}^{1j}-2n_{1}n_{i}z_{1}^{1j}\,.

(106)

Because of the previous step we know that
n_{k}
has coefficient
-2
in the canonical decomposition of
z_{1}^{ij}
and thus that
n_{1}n_{j}n_{k}
has coefficient
+4
in the canonical decomposition of
z
. We apply the same reasoning for every

and get that
+4
is the coefficient of
n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}
in the canonical decomposition of
z
for all such triplets.
Iterating this reasoning up to the N-uplet gives us that

z=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\,.

(107)

On the other hand if the coefficient of
n_{1}
is
0
in the canonical decomposition of
z

.

It follows that

\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})=\textrm{Span}(\{\mathbb{1},\pi\})

(108)

with

\pi=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\,.

(109)

Let us now prove that the atomic projectors of
{{{\omega}}}
are
\pi
and
\mathbb{1}-\pi
. Indeed, 1/
\pi
and
\mathbb{1}-\pi
are obviously non-null as we have made explicit the coefficient of
\pi
; 2/ they are orthogonal projectors as

\begin{split}\pi^{2}&=(\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i})(\sum_{\emptyset\neq Z\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Z}n_{i})\\
&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\emptyset\neq U\subseteq Y\\
Y\setminus U\subseteq V\subseteq Y\end{subarray}}(-2)^{|U|+|V|-2})\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\\
&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}((\sum_{1\leq i\leq|Y|}\binom{|Y|}{i}\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}\binom{i}{j}(-2)^{|Y|+j-2})-(-2)^{|Y|-2})\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\\
&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-2}((\sum_{1\leq i\leq|Y|}\binom{|Y|}{i}\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}\binom{i}{j}(-2)^{j})-1)\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\\
&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-2}((\sum_{1\leq i\leq|Y|}\binom{|Y|}{i})-1)\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\\
&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-2}((0-1)-1)\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\\
&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\\
&=\pi\end{split}

(110)

and thus

(\mathbb{1}-\pi)^{2}=\mathbb{1}-2\pi+\pi^{2}=\mathbb{1}-\pi

(111)

and
\pi^{\dagger}=\pi
as the
n_{i}
are hermitian and two by two commute; 3/ they are orthogonal to each other as

\pi(\mathbb{1}-\pi)=\pi-\pi^{2}=0=\pi^{2}-\pi=(\mathbb{1}-\pi)\pi\,;

(112)

and 4/ they form a basis of
\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})
as

\mathcal{Z}({{{\omega}}})=\textrm{Span}(\{\mathbb{1},\pi\notin\mathbb{C}\mathbb{1}\})=\textrm{Span}(\{\pi,\mathbb{1}-\pi\})

(113)

To give intuition, it is interesting to remark that,
\pi
is ‘the projector on the subspace where we have an odd number of fermions in the system’. Indeed, consider a state
\ket{m}=\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}
such that
m_{1}+...+m_{N}=k
. We first remark that for
Y\subseteq X

(\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i})\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}=\begin{cases}\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}&\mbox{ if }Y\subseteq\{j|m_{j}=1\}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise }\,.\end{cases}

(114)

It follows that

\begin{split}\pi\ket{m}&=(\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq X}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i})\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}\\
&=\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\binom{k}{j}(-2)^{j-1}\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{1\leq j\leq k}\binom{k}{j}(-2)^{j})\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}\\
&=\frac{1-(-1)^{k}}{2}\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}\\
&=\begin{cases}\ket{m_{1}...m_{N}}&\mbox{ if }k\mbox{ is odd}\\
0&\mbox{ if }k\mbox{ is even}\end{cases}\end{split}

(115)

Conversely,
\mathbb{1}-\pi
is ‘the projector on the subspace where we have an even number of fermions in the system’.

Because the difference with the special case of
{{{\omega}}}
is just the choice of the modes, for any
S\subseteq X
the atomic projectors of
\mathcal{A}_{S}
can be computed in the same way: they are

where

\pi_{S}=\sum_{\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq S}(-2)^{|Y|-1}\prod_{i\in Y}n_{i}\,.

(116)

Proof of Proposition

B.3
Proof of Proposition
6.3

We now prove that
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}\vdash{{{\omega}}}
.

Let
S
and
T
be two disjoint subsets of
X
;
\mathcal{M}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{S\sqcup T}
and
\mathcal{M}_{T}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{S\sqcup T}
, thus because
\mathcal{A}_{U}=\textrm{Span}(\mathcal{M}_{U})

\forall U\subseteq X
,
\mathcal{A}_{S}
and
\mathcal{A}_{T}
are sub-
\mathbb{C}^{*}
algebras of
\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T}
.

The condition
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{S})\vee\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{T})
, is equivalent to showing that
\pi_{S\sqcup T}\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{S})\vee\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{T})
as
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T})
is spanned by
\pi_{S\sqcup T}
and
\mathbb{1}
which we know is in
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{S})\vee\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A}_{T})
. This is a direct consequence of Proposition
6.2
.

Finally it remains to show that

\displaystyle\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{T}\,,

(118a)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\mathcal{A}_{T}\,.

(118b)

First we can remark that because of the anti-commutations relation and the fact that elements of
\mathcal{A}_{S}
and
\mathcal{A}_{T}
are generated by even numbers of creation and annihilation operators,
\mathcal{A}_{T}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}
and thus
\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{T}\subseteq\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}
.

Now let
z\in\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}
; by the same reasoning that we used to deduce some information on the elements of the centre in the proof of Proposition
6.2
, this is equivalent to the fact that
\forall i\neq j\in S
,
z
can be decomposed as

z=z_{0}^{ij}+n_{i}z_{1}^{ij}+n_{j}z_{1}^{ij}-2n_{i}n_{i}z_{1}^{ij}

(119)

where the
z^{ij}_{k}
contain no appearance of
a_{i}
,
a^{\dagger}_{i}
,
a_{j}
,
a^{\dagger}_{j}
. As we know that this decomposition exists only for
i,j\in S
, we do not use the canonical decomposition but instead the
S/T
decomposition which tells us that
\forall z\in\mathcal{A}_{S\sqcup T}
,
z
can be decomposed in a unique way as

z=\sum_{\vec{k}\in\{0,...,3\}^{|S|}}z_{\vec{k}}\,\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}(k_{i})

(120)

where
z_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{T}
and
\forall i\in X
,
x_{i}(0)=\mathbb{1}
,
x_{i}(1)=a^{\dagger}_{i}
,
x_{i}(2)=a_{i}
and
x_{i}(3)=n_{i}
. In this decomposition we denote
z_{\vec{k}}
for the coefficient of
x^{\vec{k}}=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}^{k_{i}}
.

We can now use the same reasoning as in Section
B.1
, with the only difference being that coefficients are not complex numbers but elements of
\mathcal{A}_{T}
. Going through all the steps of the computation gives us that

z=z_{0}+z_{T}\pi_{S}

(121)

where
z_{0},z_{T}\in\mathcal{A}_{T}
. Then

\begin{split}\pi_{S\sqcup T}z&=\pi_{S\sqcup T}(z_{0}+z_{T}\pi_{S})\\
&=\pi_{S\sqcup T}z_{0}+(\pi_{S}+\pi_{T}-2\pi_{S}\pi_{T})(z_{0}+z_{T}\pi_{S})\\
&=\pi_{S\sqcup T}z_{0}+(\pi_{S}z_{T}-\pi_{S}\pi_{T}z_{T})\,.\end{split}

(122)

However, since

\begin{split}\pi_{S\sqcup T}(z_{T}-\pi_{T}z_{t})&=(\pi_{S}+\pi_{T}-2\pi_{S}\pi_{T})(z_{0}+z_{T}\pi_{S})\\
&=\pi_{S}z_{T}+\pi_{T}z_{T}-2\pi_{S}\pi_{T}z_{T}-\pi_{S}\pi_{T}z_{T}-\pi_{T}z_{T}+2\pi_{S}\pi_{T}z_{T}\\
&=\pi_{S}z_{T}-\pi_{S}\pi_{T}z_{T}\,,\end{split}

(123)

we have that

\begin{split}\pi_{S\sqcup T}z&=\pi_{S\sqcup T}z_{0}+\pi_{S\sqcup T}(z_{T}-\pi_{T}z_{t})\\
&=\pi_{S\sqcup T}(z_{0}+z_{T}-\pi_{T}z_{t})\end{split}

(124)

where
z_{0}+z_{T}-\pi_{T}z_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{T}
. This proves that
\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{T}
and thus that
\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}=\pi_{S\sqcup T}\mathcal{A}_{T}
.

The proof of (
118b
) is very similar. We have that
(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\mathcal{A}_{T}\subseteq(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}
from the anti-commutation relations. And letting
z=z_{0}+z_{T}\pi_{S}\in\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}
,

\begin{split}(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})z&=(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})z_{0}+\pi_{S}z_{T}-\pi_{S\sqcup T}\pi_{S}z_{T}\\
&=(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})z_{0}+\pi_{S}\pi_{T}z_{T}\\
&=(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})z_{0}+(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\pi_{T}z_{T}\\
&=(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})(z_{0}+\pi_{T}z_{T})\,.\end{split}

(125)

It follows that
(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\mathcal{A}_{S}^{\prime}=(\mathbb{1}-\pi_{S\sqcup T})\mathcal{A}_{T}
, which concludes the proof.

B.4
Proof of Proposition
6.4

To show this result we adapt to our formalism a proof of an analogous result given in
[
59
]
.
Let
N\geq 3
be an integer and
(\mathcal{A}_{S})_{S\subseteq X}
be the partitioned algebra of physical operators on an
N
-modes fermionic system. For
i,j\in X
with
i\neq j
, we define

b_{ij}=a_{i}a_{j}+a_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}=(a_{i}+a^{\dagger}_{i})(a_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{j})\,.

(126)

We remark that for
i,j,k,l\in X
with
i\neq j
and
k\neq l
,

b_{ij}b_{kl}=(-1)^{\delta_{ik}+\delta_{il}+\delta_{jk}+\delta_{jl}}b_{kl}b_{ij}\,;

(127)

in other words,
b_{ij}
and
b_{kl}
anti-commute if they have exactly one index in common. We also remark that

\begin{split}b_{ij}b_{jk}b_{ki}&=(a_{i}+a^{\dagger}_{i})(a_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{j})(a_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{j})(a_{k}+a^{\dagger}_{k})(a_{k}+a^{\dagger}_{k})(a_{i}+a^{\dagger}_{i})\\
&=(a_{i}a^{\dagger}_{i}+a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i})(a_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j})(a_{k}a^{\dagger}_{k}+a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{k})\\
&=\mathbb{1}\,.\end{split}

(128)

Now, suppose the partition is fully representable; we can then fix a representation
\iota
of it such that

\forall S\subseteq X,\quad\iota(\mathcal{A}_{S})=\left\{\Big(f_{A_{n},\,n\in S}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A_{n},\,n\not\in S}\Big)\,\tilde{\mathbb{\Pi}}\,\,\,\middle|\,\,\,f\in\Lin_{\eta_{S}}\left(\bigotimes_{n\in S}\mathcal{H}_{A_{n}}\right)\right\}\,.

(129)

In particular, as
b_{ij}\in\mathcal{A}_{\{i,j\}}
we can define

B_{ij}=\iota(b_{ij})=(\tilde{B}_{ij}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{X\setminus\{i,j\}})\tilde{\mathbb{\Pi}}

(130)

where
\tilde{B}_{ij}\in\Lin_{\eta_{\{i,j\}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{j}})\subseteq\Lin(\mathcal{H}_{A_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{j}})
. By orthogonal tensor decomposition we can write these operators as

\tilde{B}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}^{ij}B^{(ij;i)}_{k}\otimes B^{(ij;j)}_{k}

(131)

where the
B^{(ij;i)}_{k}
’s are pairwise orthogonal operators acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A_{i}}
, and the
B^{(ij;j)}_{k}
’s are pairwise orthogonal operators acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A_{j}}
; which means that

\Tr((B^{(ij;i)}_k)^{\dagger}B^{(ij;i)}_{k'})=\Tr((B^{(ij;j
)}_k)^{\dagger}B^{(ij;j)}_{k'})=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\,.

(132)

Let
i,j,k
be three different indices; since
b_{ij}
and
b_{jk}
anti-commute in
\mathcal{A}_{\{i,j,k\}}
we have that

0=\{\tilde{B}_{ij},\tilde{B}_{jk}\}=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\alpha_{l}^{ij}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{jk}B^{(ij;i)}_{l}\otimes\{B^{(ij;j)}_{l},B^{(jk;j)}_{l^{\prime}}\}\otimes B^{(jk;k)}_{l^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{X\setminus\{i,j,k\}}\,.

(133)

Multiplying this expression by
(B^{(ij;i)}_{l^{\prime\prime}})^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{X\setminus\{j\}}
and
(B^{(jk;k)}_{l^{\prime\prime\prime}})^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{X\setminus\{j\}}
for the different indices and then tracing on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
\mathcal{H}_{j}
allows us to separate the different anti-commutators in the sum. Indeed, for all indices
l^{\prime\prime}
and
l^{\prime\prime\prime}
appearing in the corresponding sums we have that

\begin{split}&0=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\alpha_{l}^{ij}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{jk}B^{(ij;i)}_{l}\otimes\{B^{(ij;j)}_{l},B^{(jk;j)}_{l^{\prime}}\}\otimes B^{(jk;k)}_{l^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{X\setminus\{i,j,k\}}\\
\Rightarrow&0=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\alpha_{l}^{ij}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{jk}(B^{(ij;i)}_{l^{\prime\prime}})^{\dagger}B^{(ij;i)}_{l}\otimes\{B^{(ij;j)}_{l},B^{(jk;j)}_{l^{\prime}}\}\otimes B^{(jk;k)}_{l^{\prime}}(B^{(jk;k)}_{l^{\prime\prime\prime}})^{\dagger}\\
\Rightarrow&0=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\alpha_{l}^{ij}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{jk}\Tr((B^{(ij;i)}_{l''})^{\dagger} B^{(ij;i)}_l)\otimes\{B^{(ij;j)}_{l},B^{(jk;j)}_{l^{\prime}}\}\otimes\Tr(B^{(jk;k)}_{l'}(B^{(jk;k)}_{l'''})^{\dagger})\\
\Rightarrow&0=\{B^{(ij;j)}_{l^{\prime\prime}},B^{(jk;j)}_{l^{\prime\prime\prime}}\}\,.\end{split}

(134)

From this we can conclude that for any
i,j,k\in X
and any appropriate indices
l,l^{\prime}

\Tr(B^{(ij;j)}_{l} B^{(jk;j)}_{l'})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\{B^{(ij;j)}_{l''} , B^{(jk;j)}_{l'''} \})=0\,.

(135)

Finally, let
i,j,k
be three different elements of
X
. As
b_{ij}b_{jk}b_{ki}=\mathbb{1}
we have that

B_{ij}B_{jk}B_{ki}=\mathbb{1}_{X}\,.

(136)

It follows that

\tilde{B}_{ij}\tilde{B}_{jk}\tilde{B}_{ki}=\mathbb{1}_{\{i,j,k\}}

(137)

which can be developed as

\sum_{l,l^{\prime},l^{\prime\prime}}\alpha_{l}^{ij}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{jk}\alpha_{l^{\prime\prime}}^{ki}(B^{(ij;i)}_{l}B^{(ki;i)}_{l^{\prime\prime}})\otimes(B^{(ij;j)}_{l}B^{(jk;j)}_{l^{\prime}})\otimes(B^{(jk;k)}_{l^{\prime}}B^{(ki;k)}_{l^{\prime\prime}})=\mathbb{1}_{\{i,j,k\}}\,.

(138)

However this implies that

\begin{split}&\Tr( \tilde B_{ij} \tilde B_{jk} \tilde B_{ki})\\
&=\sum_{l,l^{\prime},l^{\prime\prime}}\alpha_{l}^{ij}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{jk}\alpha_{l^{\prime\prime}}^{ki}\Tr(B^{(ij;i)}_l B^{(ki;i)}_{l''})\Tr(B^{(ij;j)}_{l} B^{(jk;j)}_{l'})\Tr(B^{(jk;k)}_{l'} B^{(ki;k)}_{l''})\\
&=\Tr(\id_{\{i,j,k\}})\neq 0\end{split}

(139)

which is impossible: we saw that all the
\Tr(B^{(ij;i)}_l B^{(ki;i)}_{l''})
’s are null and thus
\Tr( \tilde B_{ij} \tilde B_{jk} \tilde B_{ki})=0
. We have reached a contradiction, which concludes the proof.
