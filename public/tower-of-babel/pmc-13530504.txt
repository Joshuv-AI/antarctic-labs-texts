Poster 87. Relationship Between Socioeconomic Status and Functional Outcomes in Osteochondral Allograft Transplantation
Authors: Yusuf Mufti, Jason E. Jesse, Jared Sachs, Badr Squali-Houssaini, Chloe Franzia, Krish S. Sardesai, Thomas Moran, Andrew Bi, Brian J. Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Focal chondral and osteochondral defects are a common cause of pain and functional limitation, with the potential to progress to early osteoarthritis if left untreated. Osteochondral allograft transplantation (OCA) is a well-established surgical intervention showing marked pain relief and high levels of patient satisfaction. Social deprivation index (SDI) and area deprivation index (ADI) are estimates of the extent of social disadvantage in an area, which has been correlated with inferior patient outcomes following various orthopedic procedures. The purpose of this study was to examine the impact of ADI on demographic and intraoperative variables, patient reported outcomes (PROs), reoperation rates, and failure rates in osteochondral allograft transplantation.Methods:A retrospective review of a prospectively maintained database was performed to assess outcomes after primary OCA in patients between 2000 and 2020 with available preoperative and 2-year PROs. Higher SDI and ADI scores represent greater deprivation. Linear regression and logistic regression were used to assess the relationship between SDI demographic and intraoperative variables. Patient-reported outcomes were collected preoperatively and at 2-years follow-up included the Lysholm score, the International Knee Documentation Committee Score (IKDC), and Knee Osteoarthritis and Outcome Scale (KOOS), and the Short-Form 12 Mental Component Score and Physical Component Score (SF-12 MCS and PCS). Linear and logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between SDI and PROs as well as the achievement of thresholds for minimal clinically important difference (MCID), patient acceptable symptomatic state (PASS), and substantial clinical benefit (SCB) at 2-year follow-up.Results:A total of 547 OCAs were performed during the study period from 2002-2021. 128 patients had incomplete intraoperative data. 12 patients listed invalid home addresses and excluded from analysis. 5 patients were excluded who underwent bilateral OCAs or had repeat procedures. Of the remaining 402 patients, 136 had incomplete pre-operative or 2-year follow-up PROs yielding a final study group of 266 for analysis. The total of 266 patients consisted of 124 women (46.6%) and 142 men (53.4%), with a mean age of 31.1 ± 9.8 years and an average BMI of 26.6 ± 4.2 kg/m². The mean time to follow-up was 5.7 ± 2.9 years. The majority of patients (83.8%) identified as White. Twenty-seven individuals (10.2%) were current or former smokers. Forty-two patients (15.8%) had worker's compensation status. The average SDI score was 33.9 ± 25.0, and the mean ADI score was 41.0 ± 23.0. The mean ZIP-code-level median family income (MFI) was $108,205 ± $42,528 (64.3 ± 27.3 percentile). Both ADI and SDI were significantly correlated with MFI (p < 0.001). Patients with worker's compensation status were associated with higher ADI (p < 0.001) and SDI (p < 0.001), while those with private insurance were associated with lower ADI (p = 0.04) and SDI (p = 0.04). No other demographic or intraoperative variables demonstrated significant associations with either ADI or SDI. Reoperation was not significantly associated with either ADI (p = 0.10) or SDI (p = 0.84). Higher area-level MFI was negatively correlated with preoperative KOOS Pain (p = 0.01), KOOS ADL (p = 0.03), and SF-12 MCS (p = 0.04) and PCS (p = 0.03). MFI was negatively correlated with two-year postoperative IKDC (p = 0.02), KOOS Sport (p = 0.03), and KOOS QOL (p < 0.01). We found no correlation between ADI and SDI scores and any 2-year PROs. Higher ADI was associated with lower odds of achieving PASS in KOOS Pain (p = 0.03). Higher SDI was associated with greater odds of achieving SCB in KOOS QOL (p = 0.03).Conclusions:This study found no significant correlation between deprivation level and 2-year reported outcomes, reoperation, or failure rate following OCA. There was, however, a negative correlation between MFI and several preoperative and postoperative PROs, while higher SDI was paradoxically associated with greater odds of achieving SCB in the KOOS QoL subscale. Taken together, these findings suggest that broad, area-level deprivation indices such as ADI and SDI may not be robust or consistent predictors of OCA outcomes. Instead, individual-level determinants including personal resources, health literacy, motivation, and social support systems likely exert greater influence on recovery trajectories and patient satisfaction. The paradoxical association between higher deprivation and achievement of SCB underscores the possibility that patients with worse baseline function or lower expectations may experience greater perceived gains postoperatively, a pattern also described in other orthopaedic populations. Importantly, these results highlight both the limitations of using aggregated socioeconomic measures to predict individual outcomes and the need to avoid overgeneralizing population-level deprivation data when counseling patients. From a clinical standpoint, our findings are encouraging, suggesting that patients from disadvantaged areas can achieve meaningful improvements after OCA when provided with appropriate surgical care and rehabilitation resources. This has implications for equitable access to cartilage restoration procedures, underscoring that socioeconomic disadvantage alone should not serve as a barrier to surgical candidacy. At the same time, the negative associations observed with MFI emphasize the importance of considering financial hardship and resource limitations at the patient level, which may more directly impact the recovery process.
