Trapped in the past? Disentangling fluid and crystallized intelligence of large language models using chess
Leonard S. Pleiss, Maximilian Schiffer, Robert K. von Weizsäcker

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable capabilities, yet it remains unclear to what extent these reflect sophisticated recall (crystallized intelligence) or reasoning ability (fluid intelligence). We introduce chess as a controlled testbed for disentangling these faculties. Leveraging the game’s structure and scalable engine evaluations, we construct a taxonomy of positions varying in training corpus proximity–ranging from common states solvable by memorization to novel ones requiring first-principles reasoning. We systematically evaluate multiple GPT generations under varying reasoning intensities. Our analysis reveals a clear gradient: performance consistently degrades as fluid intelligence demands increase. Notably, in out-of-distribution tasks, performance collapses to random levels. While newer models improve, progress slows significantly for tasks outside the training distribution. Furthermore, while reasoning-augmented inference improves performance, its marginal benefit per token decreases with distributional proximity. These results suggest current architectures remain limited in systematic generalization, highlighting the need for mechanisms beyond scale to achieve robust fluid intelligence.

1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)
have achieved remarkable progress in recent years, setting new state-of-the-art results across a broad range of benchmarks and applications
(
17
)
. This rapid advancement has led to widespread adoption in both research and industry
(
21
;
18
)
. Yet, the origins of these performance gains are still under debate. It remains unclear whether they stem from improvements in genuine problem-solving and reasoning capability–commonly attributed to
fluid intelligence

(
3
)
–or from enhanced memorization and retrieval across ever-expanding training corpora, commonly attributed to
crystallized intelligence

(
3
)
. This distinction is crucial: while both can yield impressive scores on specific benchmarks, only fluid intelligence enables an agent to generalize to novel problems and systematically reason beyond its prior experience
(
5
)
. Understanding whether
LLMs
primarily rely on crystallized or fluid intelligence therefore carries direct implications for the trajectory, safety, and interpretability of their reasoning and behavior. However, empirically disentangling these two forms of intelligence remains a major challenge for two reasons.

First, measuring intelligence itself is difficult, as no universally accepted, unitary definition exists. To circumvent this theoretical difficulty, we adopt the common, yet simplifying assumption that intelligence is process-invariant and can therefore be assessed through task performance
(
5
)
. We explicitly acknowledge that this operational definition narrows the scope of inquiry to measurable functional competence rather than cognitive equivalence and refer to Appendix
F
for a detailed discussion on our framework’s validity.

Second, independently assessing fluid and crystallized intelligence poses an additional challenge: It requires evaluating models on tasks that differ in their proximity to the training data distribution. Performance on
within-distribution (WD)
tasks, i.e., tasks that were contained in the training data, is indicative of crystallized intelligence, whereas
out-of-distribution (OOD)
performance is indicative of fluid intelligence. Yet, because modern
LLMs
are trained on massive and largely opaque datasets, quantifying this distributional proximity typically remains out of reach. As a result, existing studies have relied on isolated benchmarks that often conflate retrieval, pattern matching, and reasoning, yielding fragmented evidence, leading to an ongoing debate on the nature of intelligence in
LLMs
(see Section
2
).

We address this gap by proposing a controlled empirical framework that leverages chess as a structured environment for studying strategic reasoning in
LLMs
. As a domain combining clear rules, deep combinatorial structure, and computationally verifiable performance metrics via chess engines, chess provides a rigorous testbed for disentangling fluid and crystallized intelligence: All chess games originate from a single initial state, but the number of reachable configurations grows combinatorically–approximately
10^{120}
possible games exist
(
25
)
–ensuring that most games and positions are effectively novel after only a few moves.

Therefore, chess also engages both forms of intelligence: early-game play benefits from crystallized intelligence, i.e., recalling known openings, whereas succeeding in later game stages often demands fluid intelligence, i.e, finding novel solutions in previously unseen situations (for details, see Appendix
C
). This dual nature–extremely common positions in early stages of the game, yet complete novelty in later stages–enables systematic variation in the likelihood that a given position was encountered during training. Extensive open-source game databases permit frequency-based estimation of positional familiarity, providing partial control over data contamination
(
16
;
14
;
15
)
. Finally, state-of-the-art chess engines offer scalable move evaluations
(
20
;
27
;
22
)
. While not being absolute ground-truths, these engines serve as high-resolution baselines for computational correctness. We thus utilize them to quantify decision regret relative to a calculated and approximated optimum (see Appendix
F
).

In this paper, we conduct a systematic evaluation of
LLM
performance in the structured domain of chess, across tasks that require differing degrees of fluid and crystallized intelligence. Although our empirical analysis focuses on chess, its implications extend to broader settings of strategic reasoning in formal systems, as the game embodies general principles of structured problem-solving such as abstraction, planning, and adaptation within explicit constraints. Our intent is not to advocate for or against
LLM
capabilities, but to provide rigorous, domain-grounded evidence that can inform ongoing discourse on the intellectual nature of these systems and their developmental trajectory.

Our results reveal a consistent performance gradient: accuracy declines as the need for
OOD
generalization increases. In positions that are unlikely to have been observed during training, model performance collapses toward random play. Given the impossibility of strictly precluding data leakage, these results suggest that our estimates likely represent an upper bound on true fluid capability. While newer generations of models exhibit steady improvement, gains diminish as tasks leave the proximity of the training data distribution. Reasoning-augmented modes provide measurable benefits, but the marginal benefits per token decrease with decreasing training distribution proximity. Collectively, these findings highlight persistent limitations in fluid intelligence, suggesting that current
LLM
architectures may face inherent challenges in meaningfully generalizing beyond memorized knowledge.

2
Related work

With this investigation, we aim to contribute to an active debate on the extent to which
LLMs
exhibit emergent reasoning and generalization–phenomena closely related to the notion of fluid intelligence. The distinction between
crystallized
and
fluid intelligence
parallels long-standing research on
WD

versus
OOD

performance and on
generalization
in neural networks. In machine learning terms, crystallized intelligence corresponds to performance grounded in recall within the support of the training distribution, while fluid intelligence reflects compositional generalization under limited priors
(
5
)
. An increasing number of studies explore the degree to which current
LLMs
can generalize beyond their training data distribution. However, the evidence remains inconclusive.

Some results point to genuine generalization–or even emergent capabilities
(
30
;
8
;
28
;
1
;
26
;
2
;
10
;
9
)
.
For instance,
26
found that
LLM
-generated research ideas were rated as more novel than those of human experts, while
10
observed that
LLMs
succeed in spatial reasoning tasks with negligible data overlap, suggesting the presence of genuine generalization.
Conversely, other work highlights persistent limitations in reasoning or generalization
(
5
;
4
;
31
;
6
)
.
The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) ARC-AGI-2
(
4
)
remains largely unsolved, with current
LLMs
solving fewer than 3% of tasks. Moreover,
31
demonstrate that while
LLMs
excel in low-level cognitive tasks, they struggle in higher-order reasoning settings.
Such findings suggest that, despite impressive in-context learning and retrieval capabilities,
LLMs
may still lack the kind of flexible, compositional reasoning associated with strong fluid intelligence.

One central reason for this ongoing debate and diverging findings is that existing benchmarks for distributional generalization often lack explicit high-quality information on task contamination
(
14
)
, which makes it difficult to disentangle memorization from adaptive reasoning. Without precise knowledge of the training corpus, attributing performance to fluid intelligence remains inherently speculative. The lack of consensus within this debate largely reflects the resulting challenge in reliable measurement and clean experimental design: A rigorous comparison between fluid and crystallized intelligence in
LLMs
requires evaluation across tasks that differ systematically in their proximity to the model’s training data distribution.
Because the precise content of training corpora remains unknown, most existing studies rely on benchmarks that implicitly conflate recall and reasoning.
These approaches yield fragmented insights but fall short of providing a framework for distinguishing between retrieval-based competence and true
OOD
generalization.

We aim to contribute to the ongoing discussion by proposing a systematic analysis into fluid and crystalline intelligence of
LLMs
using the structured domain of chess. Chess, indicative for strategic reasoning tasks, has played a foundational role in the history of artificial intelligence
(
11
;
24
)
.

Recently, transformer-based architectures have been applied to chess, primarily aiming to improve domain-specific performance through finetuning and architectural specialization
(
29
;
23
;
12
)
.
While such models demonstrate that transformers can learn chess,
LLMs
generally exhibit underwhelming performance in chess
(
13
)
, and systematic analyses of their performance in this setting remain scarce.
To the best of our knowledge, no prior work employs chess as a controlled environment to disentangle
fluid
and
crystallized
intelligence within
LLMs
. Our study closes this research gap by establishing an empirical framework based on chess, enabling a systematic analysis of how fluid and crystallized intelligence contribute to the strategic reasoning capabilities of
LLMs
.

Beyond its historical relevance, chess offers a uniquely structured environment for studying the balance between fluid and crystallized intelligence. Its well-defined rules and extensive corpora of games enable an estimation of how likely a given position has been encountered during training, thereby supporting a principled analysis of how
LLMs
reason across familiar and novel situations. Unlike open-ended reasoning benchmarks, chess combines explicit symbolic structure, measurable exposure frequency, and objective computational verification. This combination allows for controlled variation in data familiarity and direct quantification of decision quality relative to a chess engine, yielding a transparent and interpretable testbed for disentangling fluid and crystallized intelligence in
LLMs
.

3
Methodology

To systematically evaluate
LLM
capabilities as a function of training data proximity, we employ chess as a controlled testbed combining combinatorial complexity with scalable engine evaluation. This setup allows us to disentangle fluid and crystallized intelligence by varying positional familiarity. Our approach (i) formalizes the link between intelligence and generalization; (ii) instantiates this framework in chess; (iii) generates
WD
,
near-distribution (ND)
, and
OOD
datasets; (iv) defines performance metrics; and (v) details the experimental protocol.

Conceptual framework.
We study the origin of intelligence within
LLMs
through the lens of two complementary aspect of intelligence:
crystallized
and
fluid intelligence
. To align this distinction with machine learning terminology, we formalize the concepts in terms of performance under different data distributions.

Definition 3.1

(Crystallized and Fluid Intelligence in
LLMs
)
.

Let
p_{\text{train}}(x)
denote the (unknown) training data distribution of an
LLM
and let
p_{\text{test}}(x)
be a test distribution over inputs
x\in\mathcal{X}
within a given domain.
We define

•

Crystallized intelligence
as the model’s ability to perform accurately under
WD

conditions, i.e., when
x\sim p_{\text{train}}(x)
. This ability reflects retrieval and recall of previously observed solutions.

•

Fluid intelligence
as the model’s ability to perform accurately under
OOD

conditions, i.e., when
x\sim p_{\text{test}}(x)
with
p_{\text{test}}\neq p_{\text{train}}
. This ability reflects systematic generalization and adaptive reasoning beyond previously memorized solutions to solve novel tasks within a familiar domain.

To operationalize this distinction, we quantify an
LLM
’s performance difference between
WD
and
OOD
conditions as a
generalization gap
,

\Delta_{\text{gen}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{test}}}[L(f(x))]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{train}}}[L(f(x))],

(1)

where
L(f(x))
is a loss or performance metric associated with the model’s output
f(x)
on input
x
. High
WD
accuracy reflects strong crystallized intelligence. Accordingly, a small
\Delta_{\text{gen}}
indicates that
OOD
performance remains close to
WD
levels, signifying stronger fluid generalization. Naturally, this estimate is only meaningful if
WD
performance exceeds random levels.

Quantifying this gap requires an isolated measure of within- and
OOD
performance in
LLMs
, which is notoriously difficult because the underlying data distribution is both vast and ill-defined.

Approximating training data likelihood.
We address this challenge by approximating the training data likelihood, i.e., the probability that a given task was encountered during training and thus potentially memorized. To this end, we exploit the combinatorial structure of chess, which naturally induces a steep decay in positional frequency as games progress: After the first move, the number of reachable board states is approximately
|\mathcal{X}_{1}|\approx 400
; after the second move, this number increases to about
|\mathcal{X}_{2}|\approx 2{\times}10^{5}
; and by move 40, the total number of distinct games exceeds
10^{120}
(
25
)
.

While the theoretical space of chess positions grows combinatorially with each move, the coverage of positions represented in game corpora–those plausibly encountered during
LLM
pretraining–drops off exponentially, creating a natural shift from high- to low-likelihood states throughout the early-to-midgame of any chess match. This divergence between the vast theoretical state space and the narrow trajectory of standard human play allows us to use statistical improbability as a proxy for the requirement of first-principles reasoning. Late in the game, when few pieces remain, coverage increases again due to precomputed endgame tablebases
(
32
)
. We further illustrate this relationship empirically in Appendix
C
using the Lichess Masters database, which contains more than
2.7
million games between chess masters since 1952 and serves as a proxy for the crystallized chess knowledge potentially available in training corpora.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{\text{WD}}
,
\mathcal{X}_{\text{ND}}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{\text{OOD}}
denote subsets of the overall state space
\mathcal{X}
corresponding to positions with high, medium, and low likelihood under
p_{\text{train}}
.
Formally, we consider

p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{WD}})\gg p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{ND}})\gg p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{OOD}}),

(2)

with
\mathcal{X}_{\text{WD}}
approximating
WD
conditions and
\mathcal{X}_{\text{OOD}}
representing an OOD regime, and
\mathcal{X}_{\text{ND}}
serving as an intermediary. Following this theoretical pattern allows us to define categories of positions that vary within their likelihood under
p_{\text{train}}
.

Position categories.
To evaluate
WD
,
ND
and
OOD
performance, we construct three disjoint subsets of the chess state space

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\text{WD}}\cup\mathcal{X}_{\text{ND}}\cup\mathcal{X}_{\text{OOD}},\quad\mathcal{X}_{i}\cap\mathcal{X}_{j}=\emptyset\text{ for }i\neq j,

(3)

each corresponding to a different estimated likelihood of occurrence within the
LLM
’s training distribution. We provide a detailed empirical basis for these condition-specific design principles in Appendix
C
. Example positions from each category are shown in Appendix
E
.

Within-distribution positions (
\mathcal{X}_{\text{WD}}
).

These are high-probability, early-game positions that occur frequently in human play and are likely present within the training data.
Formally, a position
x\in\mathcal{X}
is assigned to
\mathcal{X}_{\text{WD}}
if it occurs at least
n_{\min}=1{,}000
times in the Lichess Masters database
(
15
)
.
Such positions typically arise within the first moves of common openings and approximate
WD
conditions,

p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{WD}})\approx\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{\text{train}}(x).

(4)

Near-distribution positions (
\mathcal{X}_{\text{ND}}
).

ND
positions serve as an intermediate regime between
WD
and
OOD
states.
They represent early-to-midgame configurations that are
absent
from the Lichess masters database yet mostly retain structural characteristics (material balance, piece development, pawn structure) commonly encountered in
WD
states.
We generate these by performing
k{=}10
random legal moves from the starting position and discarding any position that appears in the database.
These positions satisfy

p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{ND}})<p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{WD}}),\quad\text{but }\operatorname{sim}(x_{\text{ND}},x_{\text{WD}})\text{ is high,}

(5)

where
\operatorname{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes structural similarity in board representation space.
They therefore test limited
OOD
generalization and associative recall, i.e., the recall of solutions from structurally similar positions.

Out-of-distribution positions (
\mathcal{X}_{\text{OOD}}
).

OOD
positions represent low-probability, mid-to-lategame states that have unlikely been observed during training.
We construct them by uniformly sampling legal configurations through random placement of ten pieces per side onto the board, subject to position validity constraints.
These positions share minimal structural similarity with
WD
positions,

p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{OOD}})\ll p_{\text{train}}(x_{\text{ND}}),\quad\operatorname{sim}(x_{\text{OOD}},x_{\text{WD}})\approx 0.

(6)

Dataset summary.

For each condition, we generate
N{=}500
distinct positions using the
python-chess
library
(
7
)
.
This yields a balanced dataset of
1{,}500
total evaluation states spanning a continuous spectrum from
WD
to
OOD
.

Move evaluation.
To quantify the quality of model-recommended moves, we employ the
Centipawn Loss (CPL)
, a standard measure of decision regret in the chess domain. Unlike other metrics like Win/Loss/Draw Probability, CPL provides meaningful signal on move quality, even in imbalanced positions. The
CPL
measures the positional value lost through a move. A centipawn corresponds to one hundredth of a pawn’s nominal value, providing a more granular measure of move quality than other established metrics like win probability. Formally, for a position evaluated by a chess engine before and after a candidate move, we define

\ell_{\text{CPL}}=\max\!\left(0,\,\text{eval}_{\text{b}}-\text{eval}_{\text{a}}\right),

(7)

where
\text{eval}_{\text{b}}
and
\text{eval}_{\text{a}}
denote the evaluations from the moving player’s perspective before and after executing the selected move.
Lower values of
\ell_{\text{CPL}}
indicate stronger play, while higher values reflect increasing positional deterioration. As a broad empirical guideline,

\ell_{\text{CPL}}<10
signifies moves effectively on par with the engines recommended move,

10\leq\ell_{\text{CPL}}<50
corresponds to minor inaccuracies,

50\leq\ell_{\text{CPL}}<100
to noticeable mistakes,
and
\ell_{\text{CPL}}\gg 100
to blunders that substantially reduce winning chances. We acknowledge that CPL is a heuristic metric whose practical implication varies by position type; however, averaged over a large corpus, it serves as a robust proxy for systematic alignment with computational constraints (see Appendix
F
).

In addition to positional quality, we also track the frequency of
illegal moves
, i.e., moves that violate the rules of chess. The proportion of illegal moves per model and condition serves as a direct indicator of syntactic reasoning capabilities—the ability to strictly adhere to formal constraints in novel contexts. According to the rules, playing an illegal move results in instant loss, hence it is scored equally to losing through checkmate,
\ell_{\text{CPL}}=1{,}000
.

We conducted all evaluations using Stockfish 17.1 at search depth 30
(
22
)
, which provides a robust trade-off between computational cost and evaluation reliability.

Models.
A central goal of this study is to study the evolution of
WD
and
OOD
performance across successive generations of
LLMs
.
To enable meaningful cross-generation comparison, we focus on OpenAI’s GPT family, which provides three key advantages:
(i) public API access to distinct model versions;
(ii) a consistent architectural lineage that facilitates attribution of performance changes to scale, training data, or reasoning augmentation rather than unrelated design choices; and (iii) broad real-world deployment, ensuring that the resulting insights are directly relevant to production-level systems. This focus is methodologically motivated: comparisons across distinct model families (e.g., GPT vs. Gemini or Claude) would introduce uncontrolled confounders regarding training data mixtures and architectural priors. By strictly analyzing a single lineage, we isolate the effects of model scale and reasoning augmentation.

Model selection.

We evaluate model configurations spanning three generations, summarized in Table
1
.
GPT-3.5 represents the third generation and marked the mainstream launch of ChatGPT; it brought huge public awareness and adoption of
LLMs
for conversational
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
. GPT-4o marks the fourth generation, introducing architectural improvements, enhanced multimodal capabilities and larger effective context windows.
GPT-5 constitutes the fifth generation and OpenAI’s most recent model, marking the current state of the art. GPT-5 is the first model to allow for the specification of different reasoning efforts. We evaluate GPT-5 using two different levels of reasoning effort, minimal and moderate. This dual evaluation allows us to assess not only architectural improvements, but to further assess the impact of chain-of-thought reasoning. Chain-of-thought reasoning in GPT-5 refers to the model’s ability to generate intermediate reasoning steps–a structured, step-by-step explanation linking premises to conclusions–before producing its final answer.
This process allows the model to handle complex, multi-stage problems (e.g., logical inference, math, or planning) more reliably by explicitly representing and refining its internal reasoning trajectory rather than jumping directly to an output.

Evaluation protocol.

All responses were collected between August and October 2025 via the OpenAI API.
Each model received identical prompts requesting a single move recommendation in standard chess notation (see Appendix
A
). We set
\text{temperature}=0
to enforce deterministic decoding, thereby isolating reasoning quality from sampling variance. Each query–response pair was logged with model metadata and evaluation metrics to ensure full reproducibility.

Baselines.

To contextualize
LLM
performance, we introduce two reference points.
First, we obtain a
random baseline
by uniformly sampling from the set of legal moves in each position.
This represents a lower bound and mirrors play under knowledge of chess rules but without any strategic or tactical capability.
Second, as an upper bound representative of domain-specialized neural network capability, we employ
Leela Chess Zero
(Lc0, version 0.32.0,
20
), an open-source state-of-the-art neural network chess engine trained via self-play reinforcement learning and derived from the AlphaZero framework
(
27
)
. It employs Monte Carlo Tree Search guided by neural network evaluations, combining pattern recognition with lookahead search. We generate moves with 2 seconds thinking time per position, balancing
playing strength and computational feasibility. Together, these baselines define the boundaries of a spectrum from rule-constrained random play to domain-optimized neural systems, enabling us to situate
LLM
performance across model generations and reasoning configurations within a well-calibrated performance landscape.

Prompting and parsing.
Each chess position
x\in\mathcal{X}
was encoded using Forsyth–Edwards Notation (FEN) and provided to each model via a standardized prompt requesting the strongest move in Standard Algebraic Notation (SAN) and Universal Chess Interface (UCI) Notation, along with a short justification, in a standardized JSON format. The prompt template was held constant across all model configurations to eliminate stylistic bias and isolate systematic differences in capability.
The template prompt is shown in Appendix
A
. All prompt–response pairs, along with metadata on model identity, token expenditure, and evaluation outcomes, were logged in structured JSON format to enable full reproducibility and downstream analysis.

The recommended moves in SAN notation were checked for syntactical validity. Across all models, syntactically invalid moves occured in
0.32\%
of positions (
0.26\%
for GPT-3.5,
0.02\%
for GPT-4o,
0.73\%
for GPT-5 without reasoning,
0.07\%
for GPT-5 with reasoning). Moves in UCI notation were originally collected as a potential fallback should any model struggle to provide syntactically valid solutions in SAN; however, given the extremely low rate of such errors, the UCI-formatted moves were ultimately not used in the analysis.

Results

We begin by comparing the average centipawn loss and the frequency of illegal moves across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-5, highlighting consistent but decelerating improvements. We then normalize performance metrics to control for condition difficulty and project likely future trends based on observed improvement rates. Lastly, we investigate how increased reasoning effort affects GPT-5’s chess performance, both in terms of accuracy and computational cost.

Discussion.
This study examined fluid and crystallized intelligence, their scaling behavior, and reasoning dynamics in large language models within the structured, rule-based domain of chess. Using a hierarchy of test positions differing in their proximity to
WD
examples, we provide a thorough investigation into the chess capabilities of
LLMs
, thereby disentangling fluid and crystallized intelligence. Our findings reveal a consistent structure in
LLM
performance across model generations and reasoning conditions.

First, we note significant differences in crystallized and fluid intelligence: Crystallized intelligence remains a dominant strength. When positions resemble those likely encountered during training, models exhibit decent play. However, when fluid intelligence is required–such as in positions unlikely encountered during training–performance collapses, failing to improve upon random move selection. This sharp dichotomy suggests that current models do not learn an accurate representation of the chess world, but rather a vast library of surface-level heuristics that become brittle once the structural patterns of standard play are removed. Second, scaling trends show diminishing returns: although each new model generation exhibits measurable improvements, the magnitude of these gains decreases sharply. This trend is particularly steep for fluid intelligence, implying that additional scale alone will unlikely bridge the gap between both aspects of intelligence. If generalization were merely a function of scale, we would expect OOD capabilities to emerge linearly; instead, the asymptotic behavior points to a fundamental architectural bottleneck in processing novel syntax. Third, reasoning provides substantial performance benefits, lowering both illegal move rates and centipawn loss across all conditions. Nonetheless, the marginal benefit of reasoning diminishes with decreased training data likelihood, suggesting that reasoning mostly amplifies
WD
performance but may not confer
OOD
generalization. This supports the hypothesis that chain-of-thought mechanisms in their current form function primarily as retrieval optimizers–helping the model locate the correct memory–rather than as engines of first-principles derivation.

Overall, our results point towards severe weaknesses with respect to
LLMs
’
OOD
performance within the game of chess. We further find that both scaling and reasoning face intrinsic limitations in extending model competence beyond the learned training data manifold. Given that chess serves as a high-fidelity proxy for other formal systems, these findings cast doubt on the ability of pure LLMs to robustly generalize in domains like mathematics or software synthesis without the support of dense training coverage.

5
Implications beyond chess

While chess constitutes a clean and fully observable environment, its analytical richness makes it a uniquely suitable testbed for studying the interaction between fluid and crystallized intelligence. The game’s fixed rules, combinatorial structure, and reliable evaluations using superhuman chess engines enable a systematic assessment of generalization without the confounding factors that typically accompany natural language tasks. Consequently, the patterns observed in this study–robust memorization, brittle generalization, and diminishing returns with model scale–likely reflect intrinsic properties of current
LLM
architectures rather than idiosyncrasies of the chess domain. Because chess operates as a formal system governed by rigid syntax rather than semantic ambiguity, these failures likely extend to other formal domains such as mathematics and software synthesis, where rule adherence is equally non-negotiable. The sharp performance gradient between
WD
and
OOD
positions parallels the broader divide between crystallized and fluid intelligence in large-scale language models, suggesting that chess provides a controlled and quantifiable instantiation of a more general phenomenon: the difficulty of inferring consistent structural rules without direct prior exposure.

The diminishing gains observed across successive model generations further indicate that scaling alone may not suffice to enable robust generalization. If this pattern extends beyond the present domain, it implies that increasing model capacity primarily enhances recall and pattern completion within data-rich regions of the training distribution, while leaving structurally novel or low-density regions largely unresolved. In this sense, scaling appears to deepen crystallized competence (interpolation within the known manifold) rather than to broaden fluid adaptability (extrapolation to novel state spaces).

A similar limitation emerges in the context of explicit reasoning. Although chain-of-thought prompting improves performance, its marginal benefits diminish as the required abstraction increases. This finding challenges the view that reasoning can compensate for limited generalization. While extended reasoning chains can correct local inconsistencies and surface-level errors, they rarely induce new conceptual representations. Under current architectures, reasoning thus serves as a mechanism for more effective exploitation of existing knowledge rather than a pathway toward genuine conceptual synthesis.

In summary, while
LLMs
continue to exhibit rapid gains in task-specific competence, their inability to generalize within a closed, rule-based system such as chess highlights a structural constraint in fluid intelligence. Overcoming this limitation will likely require advances beyond architectural scale or explicit reasoning–specifically, new forms of representation and inference capable of capturing and composing latent causal and relational structure. Until such capabilities are realized, the deployment of LLMs in safety-critical formal systems should be approached with caution, as high performance in familiar contexts may mask a fundamental brittleness in novel ones.

Appendix B
Performance projection

To quantify and extrapolate progress across successive generations of large language models,
we compute relative performance improvements on a fixed benchmark.
Let
s_{t}
denote the mean task error (e.g., average centipawn loss) for model generation
t
.
The relative improvement from generation
t
to
t+1
is defined as

P_{t}=\frac{s_{t}-s_{t+1}}{s_{t}}.

(8)

We define the
progress acceleration ratio
as

R_{t}=\frac{P_{t+1}}{P_{t}},

(9)

which captures how the rate of improvement evolves across model generations.

Assuming that this multiplicative trend continues, we can project future progress as

P_{t+k}=P_{t+k-1}\cdot R_{t+k-2},

(10)

and recursively estimate hypothetical future model performance via

s_{t+k+1}=s_{t+k}\cdot(1-P_{t+k}).

(11)

This formulation provides a simple empirical framework for estimating
scaling trends and forecasting potential performance gains in upcoming model generations.
If
R_{t}\approx 1
, progress is steady (approximately linear improvement);
if
R_{t}>1
, progress is accelerating; and if
R_{t}<1
, progress is decelerating,
indicating diminishing returns.

Appendix C
Coverage study

To empirically assess and challenge our theoretical assumptions about the relationship between game progress and database frequency, we simulated 50 chess games. Each game was generated by iteratively selecting one of the three highest-rated engine moves for both Black and White using Stockfish
[
22
]
. For every resulting position, we queried the Lichess masters database to determine whether it had previously occurred
[
15
]
. The Lichess masters database contains more than 2.7 million games played since 1952 of players with FIDE ratings of
2{,}200
or more. In addition, we tracked the number of pieces remaining on the board, since modern tablebases are known to include all possible positions with seven or fewer pieces
[
32
]
. We then computed database coverage rates as a function of move number.

Our results, displayed in Figure
5
, show that coverage is initially high, drops rapidly as the game progresses, and then rises again in later stages–beginning around move 49–as the number of pieces begins falling below eight in some games. We thus conclude that the training data coverage likelihood is high in very early and very late game stages, gradually decreases towards the mid-game and reaches its minimum in the late mid-game, around move thirty, when there are approximately 20 pieces on the board.

Derivation of condition design principles.

Beyond validating our theoretical assumptions, the coverage study also aimed to inform the derivation of design principles for constructing positions with varying levels of familiarity. Our findings were threefold:

1.

WD
positions.

High coverage rates can be found in early and certain late game positions. Yet, coverage in late-game positions solely relies on tablebases, where each position occurs once. As such, even if tablebases are part of the training corpora, late-game position were likely seen few times. Contrary, early-game positions occur thousands of times within the Lichess database, and likely even in other sources such as opening books. Thus, early-game positions exhibit the highest likelihood of frequent and consistent coverage. We therefore obtained
WD
samples by performing a structured breadth-first search (BFS) starting from the initial chess position. We retained only positions that appeared in at least
1{,}000
0
0

0

We selected a threshold of
1{,}000
based on an empirical sensitivity analysis. Lower thresholds (e.g.,
100

10{,}000

2.

ND

The empirical finding of novel games beyond move nine in our case study suggested that generating positions by playing ten random moves per side from the starting position yields positions that are not memorized explicitly but remain structurally related to early-game configurations. Importantly, in some modern main lines, moves are known more than thirty moves deep. To ensure that the

To minimize structural similarity to

Appendix F
Framework validity and scope

Our experimental framework rests on five foundational pillars: (I) the operational definition of intelligence, (II) the reference world of chess, (III) the creation of different position types, (IV) the approximation of training distribution proximity, and the (V) reliance on engine-based evaluation. Each introduces specific constraints and abstractions. We explicitly delineate the scope of our claims below to address potential validity concerns regarding the use of chess as a proxy for general reasoning.

F.1
The operational definition of intelligence

Despite decades of research in psychology and artificial intelligence, a global consensus on the definition of intelligence remains elusive. However, as noted by
[
5
]
, the contemporary
AI
community typically benchmarks intelligence by comparing the skill exhibited by agents against humans or verifiers at specific tasks.

Adopting this practical framework, we operationally define intelligence as
process-invariant task performance
. We distinguish between:

•

Crystallized intelligence:
Performance derived from prior knowledge, pattern matching, and retrieval from the training corpus.

•

Fluid intelligence:
Performance derived from first-principles reasoning and adaptive calculation.

We acknowledge that this is a functional simplification that serves to quantify model behavior but does not capture the full, multi-faceted scope of biological cognitive processes.

F.2
The reference world: abstracted engine chess

It is critical to note that the reference world for this study is not the sport of chess as played by humans, but rather a reduced, abstracted world defined by chess engines. Stockfish and similar engines (including the neural-network-based Lc0) are not intelligent in the human sense. They are highly optimized, heuristic search algorithms rooted in massive calculation
[
22
,
20
]
. They do not understand chess strategy as a narrative; they solve it as a logic puzzle. Therefore, when we measure
LLM
performance against an engine, we are measuring the model’s ability to align with a computational optimum, not its ability to exhibit human-like intuition or creativity.

While engines are not ground truth solvers of chess (which remains unsolved), they exceeded human capability decades ago. For the purpose of evaluating
LLM
errors–which often involve fundamental misunderstandings of board state or rules–the engine serves as a sufficiently high-resolution ruler. A high
CPL
objectively represents a failure to optimize within the game’s logical constraints, regardless of the human quality of the move. Consequently, while engine evaluations may not capture the full semantic nuance of human strategy, they provide a necessary and rigorous baseline for assessing the tactical soundness of the models’ reasoning. Thus, within the scope of this study, we accept engine evaluations as a robust heuristic for distinguishing between valid reasoning and hallucination, without claiming they represent a ground truth for general intelligence.

F.3
Nature of
OOD
positions

Our
OOD
positions are generated via random placement to maximize distance from the training manifold. This generation process introduces specific structural biases that warrant clarification: proficient human play relies on a synergy of
strategy
(often termed
positional play
in domain literature, focusing on long-term planning and static evaluation) and
tactics
(immediate calculation of concrete variations). Due to their randomized nature, our
OOD
positions often assess tactical ability rather than strategical prowess. They frequently lack standard pawn skeletons, or King safety norms typical of human games. Consequently, they often appear chaotic or absurd to a human expert.

While unnatural relative to human play, we argue that this tactical focus is an essential design choice for effectively isolating fluid intelligence. Strategic positions almost always structurally resemble known games (opening theory or endgame tablebases), making them highly susceptible to memorization (crystallized intelligence). By forcing the model to solve positions that break standard heuristic patterns, we test its ability to process the syntax and rules of the game to derive a solution from first principles. In a formal system like chess, when pattern matching is precluded, calculation becomes the primary proxy for fluid reasoning. Consequently, a performance collapse in these positions indicates a fundamental limitation in adaptive reasoning: the model fails not because the position is absurd, but because it lacks the capacity to generalize its knowledge of rules to novel contexts outside its training distribution.

F.4
Approximation of Training Data Proximity

The taxonomy employed (
WD
,
ND
,
OOD
) relies on an approximation of the unobserved training distribution. We utilized the Lichess Masters database to approximate the training distribution. We acknowledge that unlike curated professional databases (e.g., ChessBase), public internet databases often contain absurd or theoretically irrelevant positions. To mitigate this noise, we enforced a strict threshold of
n_{min}=1,000
occurrences for the
WD
set. While this specific cutoff is heuristic, it serves as a robust filter against idiosyncratic games, ensuring that WD positions are not merely present in the database, but are statistically established standard positions likely to be over-represented in the
LLM
’s massive training corpora.

We cannot formally guarantee that all positions categorized as
WD
were present in the
LLM
training corpus, nor strictly preclude the appearance of
OOD
positions. However, given the combinatorial explosion of the state space, the probability of a specific, randomly generated position with high piece count appearing in the training data is negligible. Even if the categorization is imperfect, this uncertainty biases our results conservatively: if the
OOD
set were to contain leaked positions, the model’s performance would be artificially inflated by memorization. The fact that we observe a performance collapse despite this potential advantage suggests that the reported results likely represent an upper bound on the model’s true fluid reasoning capability, which may be even more limited than observed.

F.5
Choice of evaluation metrics

We employ
CPL
–a measure of decision regret relative to an engine’s optimal move–rather than binary Win/Loss rates as our primary metric. Critically, we argue that outcome-based metrics are insufficient for measuring fluid intelligence in this context, as they fail to distinguish between a reasoning process that is logically sound but imperfect, and one that is effectively random. However, as engines do not exhibit perfect play, we explicitly acknowledge that
CPL
is not a flawless descriptor of overall playing strength, particularly in strategic contexts where sub-optimal moves may result in long-term positional disadvantages that may exceed the evaluation depth of contemporary engines. Our choice is nonetheless motivated by

the fact that alternative methods like win/draw/loss probability do not sufficiently differentiate move quality in positions that are objectively decided. This saturation masks qualitative differences in play. Conversely,
CPL
provides a continuous measure of gradual error magnitude. It distinguishes between a sub-optimal move that retains the current balance (e.g.,
CPL
15) and a blunder that forces a loss (e.g.,
CPL
300), even in imbalanced positions. This granularity is essential for quantifying the subtle gradients of reasoning capability across model generations that would otherwise remain invisible under binary evaluation criteria.
