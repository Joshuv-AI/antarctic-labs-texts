Neurobiological illness models of schizophrenia and stigma reduction: has that ship sailed?
Authors: Philipp Sterzer, Nicolai Rohner, Christian Huber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stigma substantially contributes to the burden of psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia. Previous attempts to reduce stigma based on neurobiological illness models were unsuccessful. In this Perspective article, we argue that these models may have failed to reduce stigma in schizophrenia because of their lack of explanatory power and their connotations of biological otherness and determinism. We suggest that recent developments in the realm of computational psychiatry may help to remedy this situation. In particular, we discuss the potential of predictive processing, a highly influential computational framework of brain function, to reduce the stigma associated with schizophrenia. We argue that predictive processing accounts may do so by bridging the explanatory gap between biology and psychotic experiences within a normative theory of brain function, thus offering a normalizing perspective on the neural underpinnings of schizophrenia. This may facilitate not only a better understanding of psychotic experiences but also counteract connotations of biological determinism and foster belief in change. Viewing schizophrenia through the lens of predictive processing may thus pave the way for the integration of neurobiologically grounded models into the communication with patients, their relatives, and the public, which have the potential to reduce stigma.

Schizophrenia is a highly stigmatizing diagnosis. People diagnosed with schizophrenia are stigmatized based on their perception as being unpredictable or even dangerous, which leads to social exclusion, decreased quality of life, and poor health outcomes1–4. Stigma remains a severe barrier to treatment and recovery5,6 and its fundamental importance for the patients’ life has led to the notion of stigma as a “second illness”7. Anti-stigma campaigns since the 1990s have drawn on biological (or “biogenetic”) models of schizophrenia to reduce stigma8. Such models focus on neurobiological abnormalities, e.g., in brain morphology or function, and their association with genetic factors9.

The focus on neurobiology was intended to reduce personal accountability for the illness, thus reducing self-stigmatization and public stigma. However, this goal was not reached10,11. Longitudinal data even show a drop in the public acceptance of schizophrenia12. While correlational studies overall show no significant relationship between neurobiological explanations of mental health problems and stigma, experimental induction of beliefs in neurobiological explanations indeed increases desire for social distance, perceived dangerousness, and pessimism regarding recovery13. Neurobiological explanations may foster a notion of “biological otherness”, resulting in pessimism regarding the possibility of change, and contributing to social distancing12,14. Moreover, families of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia may suffer from the stigmatizing notion of passing on “bad genes”15. Together, these findings suggest that neurobiological models of schizophrenia might promote rather than reduce stigma1.

But has the ship of destigmatization through neurobiological models really sailed? Should psychiatrists still inform about genetic and neurobiological factors in schizophrenia, or should such information be withheld? In our view, it is currently unclear whether neurobiological models are stigmatizing in principle or whether models may be improved in a way that may indeed help to reduce stigma. We suggest that recent progress in the field of computational psychiatry has paved the way for the development of new models that may be helpful in this regard. In particular, we propose that the influential framework of predictive processing (PP) may enable new neuroscientifically-based perspectives on schizophrenia that might be less stigmatizing or even have the potential to reduce stigma.

Explaining psychosis through the lens of predictive processing

PP is a theoretical framework about the functioning of the brain16,17. Its key tenet is that the brain, having no direct access to the world, uses an internal model of the world to make inferences about the hidden causes of the data registered by the sensory organs. This view challenges our fundamental intuitions regarding our perception of the world. Most people intuitively believe that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the world and their perception of it, implying that our views on the world can either be correct or false. PP, in contrast, proposes that our brain generates perception by making “best guesses” about the likely causes of the available data17. Our perception of the world has therefore been likened to a “fantasy that coincides with reality” but can never be correct or false in an absolute sense17.

According to PP, the brain performs inferences based on Bayesian principles: Probabilistic predictions (“priors”) derived from the brain’s internal model of the world are combined with sensory data (“likelihood”) to compute posterior probabilities (“posteriors”) regarding the causes of these data (Fig. 1). The posterior corresponds to the current best guess about the state of the world. PP conceives of the internal model as a hierarchically organized system, whereby priors regarding basic sensory features are implemented at lower levels (e.g., sensory cortices), while higher levels (e.g., prefrontal cortex) represent more abstract beliefs. Mismatches between priors and sensory data result in prediction errors that can update the internal model, thereby minimizing prediction errors on the long run. Whether and to what degree the internal model is updated depends on the respective weighting of prior and likelihood. In Bayesian schemes, this weighting is determined by the respective “precisions” of the prior and the likelihood, respectively. Precision here corresponds to the inverse of the variance of the probability density function used to model a prior or a likelihood (Fig. 1). An increased precision—and thus stronger weighting—of the likelihood relative to the prior will result in larger prediction errors, making model updates by unpredicted sensory data more likely, and vice versa. In addition to such perceptual inference, which aims at updating the internal world model, an agent may also minimize prediction errors by acting upon the world, a process known as “active inference“. For illustration, imagine you are about to cross a street in a city while a car is approaching and have to decide whether to go or wait. Let’s assume further that you have never lived in a city, have no experience with cars, and no prior knowledge regarding their usual speed range. According to PP, your prior regarding the car’s speed will be imprecise, and your inference will therefore rely strongly on the sensory data provided by your eyes (likelihood). Your internal model regarding the car’s speed will be readily updated if the sensory data deviate from whatever your internal model’s prediction was. Upon repeated exposure to cars, your prior regarding their speed will become increasingly precise. The more precise your prior grows, the more strongly it will be weighted against the sensory data. This will render your inferences less dependent on the quality of the sensory data and thus more reliable, even when the sensory data are impoverished, e.g., under poor sighting conditions.

An individual’s ability to successfully navigate an uncertain world will critically depend on the delicate balance in the weighting of prior and likelihood. Alterations in estimating prior or likelihood precisions may lead to aberrant learning and false inferences. If priors are too precise, perception may be distorted by overly rigid beliefs. If, conversely, the likelihood is too precise, normally predictable sensory events will lead to increased prediction errors. PP accounts explain psychotic phenomena such as delusions and hallucinations, and possibly also other symptoms of schizophrenia, by changes in prior and likelihood estimation18,19.

As an example, we here outline the leading PP account of delusions. Delusions are thought to result from a reduced precision of priors relative to the likelihood, leading to stronger weighting of sensory data and thus larger prediction errors18. To minimize prediction errors, beliefs implemented at high hierarchical levels of the internal model may be modified to encompass the aberrant salience of sensory events that results from exaggerated prediction error signaling at low levels. In psychological terms, unusual experiences resulting from exaggerated prediction error signaling call for unusual explanations, triggering the development of delusional beliefs. For example, if a normally inconspicuous event (e.g., three black cars passing by in a row) is perceived as overly salient, this may trigger a delusional explanation (e.g., a sign of impending doom, Fig. 1).

PP cannot only plausibly explain the emergence of delusions, but also their persistence20. Aberrant salience causes uncertainty, which is experienced as stressful. Delusional explanations may alleviate this stress by making experiences more predictable or by increasing sense of control, notwithstanding all drawbacks they entail. Their stress-reducing effect may stabilize delusions, making them resistant to counterevidence. In addition, the notion of active inference can also help to explain the persistence of psychotic symptoms. Active inference is based on an agent’s prior beliefs about the world, and their own place in it. If these beliefs are poorly controlled, then the agent may become trapped in an action-perception loop, whereby actions are based on inaccurate predictions, and the resulting sensory data are interpreted as further evidence for the faulty belief, rather than being used to correct the model21.

There are three key aspects of the PP account that may, in our view, have destigmatizing effects. First, the PP account may bridge the explanatory gap between scientific facts and subjective experience, thereby facilitating a better understanding of how seemingly incomprehensible psychotic experiences emerge. Second, PP may resolve the oversimplifying dichotomy between “normal” and “abnormal” brain function, thus countering the detrimental notion of biological otherness. And third, PP entails the possibility of change through learning and may thereby foster belief in change. In the following sections, we will examine in detail the potential of these four aspects to reduce stigma.

Bridging the gap between neurobiology and subjective experience

PP models the brain’s inference mechanisms at an algorithmic level. While this approach does not model the implementation of these mechanisms at the neuronal level, it has the potential to bridge the explanatory gap between neurobiology and experience. For instance, a hypofunction of glutamatergic N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDAR) has been proposed as a candidate neuronal mechanism of schizophrenia. Reduced NMDAR activity caused by autoantibodies22 or NMDAR antagonists23 leads to psychotic phenomena resembling schizophrenia. Moreover, patients diagnosed with schizophrenia express fewer NMDARs24. Such knowledge may be promising as a starting point for the development of new pharmacological interventions, but it is rather useless in explaining how psychotic experiences arise. What might help, however, is an understanding of the functional role of NMDAR in an algorithmic framework such as PP.

NMDARs play a critical role in controlling excitation-inhibition (E/I) balance in cortical neural circuits. Hypofunction of NMDAR on inhibitory interneurons leads to a disinhibition of pyramidal neurons and thus a shift towards excitation. This has important implications for PP, as E/I interactions are thought to implement the local comparison between internal predictions and incoming sensory data16. The excitatory shift caused by NMDAR-related disinhibition may enhance the weighting of sensory data, thus increasing likelihood precision19. Moreover, E/I imbalance may contribute to striatal dopaminergic hyperactivity9, contributing to increased precision-weighting of prediction errors and thus aberrant salience. The PP alterations resulting from NMDAR hypofunction may thus result in aberrant inferences that give rise to psychotic experiences as described above17,25.

This example shows how PP can provide a skeletal understanding of how a neurobiological alteration can lead to a perturbation of algorithmic computations that may explain changes in subjective experience. Importantly, more research is needed to underpin such explanations of psychosis19. In particular, computational modeling work should take into account the process theories under PP, which provide the principled links between the models and neurobiological processes26,27. An improved understanding of these links will be essential in rendering psychotic phenomena less mysterious, thus helping to reduce the stigma associated with their incomprehensibility.

Explaining psychosis within a mechanistic continuum

Within PP, a range of possibilities exist about how variations in the functioning of the model’s components might be adaptive under certain circumstances. For example, an increased weighting of sensory data may be adaptive under stress. In situations of potential threat it is useful to increase the sensitivity to potentially relevant new information, rather than relying on what is already known. Thus, the very PP alteration identified as a relevant factor in psychosis18 may be adaptive under certain physiological conditions, while boosting the propensity towards psychosis under other conditions (e.g., without threat and when deployed over extended periods of time). Even then, it may be considered reasonable to come up with best guesses about the world’s state that help to explain the aberrant salience, regardless of whether these guesses are considered reasonable by other people. A delusional belief may thus be understood as an attempt to make sense of an environment that is perceived as threatening or estranged and can be considered stabilizing for the individual28,29.

This provides a normalizing perspective, as the brain function of a person experiencing psychosis does not need to fundamentally differ from “normal” function. This perspective corroborates the so-called continuum view of psychosis at the level of brain mechanisms. Epidemiological data point to a continuum of psychosis-like experiences in the general population, whereby psychosis-prone healthy individuals share the same associated risk factors as clinical samples without fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for manifest psychotic disorder30–33. The emergence of a psychotic disorder in this model is dependent on the persistence of environmental factors interacting with predisposing genetic factors34. The continuum view of psychosis is well established in the psychiatric literature32,33 and has been shown to reduce stigma. It is associated with lower stereotyping, reduced fear, and less social rejection of people with psychosis35, as well as better access to treatment35–37.

Similarly, brain function in schizophrenia may not be categorically “abnormal” but lie on a continuum with respect to neural mechanisms that determine the psychosis proneness. The shift in neural inference towards a reduced weighting of priors relative to sensory data may be one (but likely not the only) mechanism distributed along such a continuum. Indeed, evidence points to such a shift in inference mechanisms in psychosis-prone healthy individuals20. Manifest psychotic disorders may represent the extreme end of such a continuum, where the shift in inference mechanisms is particularly pronounced and present over prolonged periods of time. It may thus be a shift in otherwise fully functional neural inference mechanisms that makes perception, thoughts and beliefs develop in a way that is diagnosed as psychotic, rather than a categorical difference in an individual’s neurobiology.

What contributes to the notion of categorical otherness, is that the experiences reported by psychotic individuals often appear categorically distinct from what healthy people experience. It is therefore worth noting that PP requires selection of the internal model that provides the best explanation of the sensory data currently available. Thus, categorical shifts in internal modeling may occur when a simple but inaccurate set of beliefs explains the available sensory evidence better than a more complex but accurate set of beliefs. Internal model selection may thus give rise to experiences that appear categorically distinct, despite continuity in the underlying mechanisms. In addition, becoming trapped in action-perception loops based on active inference, as outlined above, may explain how even a slight shift in inference mechanisms may result in mental states that are perceived as categorically distinct by others.

The predictive processing framework incorporates change

It is a key premise of PP that the brain’s internal model of the world is continuously updated. Understanding psychosis as a consequence of changes in inference and belief-updating may thus facilitate a more optimistic attitude towards the possibility of change, compared to the notion of a genetically determined brain dysfunction. Critically, even if the idea of “correcting” such a putative brain dysfunction through medication might enhance treatment adherence, such a model, if used in isolation, may produce a feeling of irrevocable dependence on biological intervention. While the PP account does not preclude the possibility of effective biological treatment, it opens up a whole array of potential ways to change the workings of the PP machinery. Even if changes in neural inference have led to unusual experiences and the formation of dysfunctional beliefs, PP leaves room for change through continued updating of the brain’s internal model. Moreover, model updating can also be achieved through active inference, i.e. by acting to change the sensory input so that it matches the internal model’s predictions38.

This is not to suggest that delusions—or any form of high-level abstract beliefs—are changed easily. Belief updating across the cortical hierarchy is inherently difficult, as it involves integrating evidence over extended timescales to detect higher-order regularities, while accommodating many degrees of freedom in how evidence is interpreted. Still, emphasizing the dynamic nature of PP may counteract the unhelpful intuition of psychosis as a deterministic consequence of unchangeable biological underpinnings.

Updates of the brain’s internal model can be instigated through a variety of approaches, including biologically-based interventions that target neural mechanisms of precision estimation or cognitive and action-based techniques that modify prediction-error minimization (e.g., reappraisal of aberrantly salient events or behavioral experiments, also known as reality testing). Viewing psychosis through the PP lens may enhance the plausibility of such interventions, thus fostering empowerment to induce meaningful changes through own actions and therapy. If patients and families understand that the brain can relearn and adjust, they may feel more hope and less stigma about chronicity. This may improve self-efficacy, which has been linked to increased functionality in schizophrenia39 and is an important factor in psychotherapy, where the belief in self-induced change is critical for therapeutic success40.

Conclusions

Based on the arguments outlined above, we suggest that PP has the potential to reduce the stigma associated with schizophrenia (for an overview, see Table 1). Stigmatizing aspects of neurobiological illness models and possible solutions offered by a predictive-processing perspective.

Psychotic individuals are commonly characterized as having lost contact with reality, as opposed to mentally healthy people who seemingly have no difficulty recognizing what is real. PP suggests otherwise: Our brains do not have direct access to reality and can therefore only make best guesses about the world. Thus, people’s views on the world cannot be fundamentally categorized as being either correct or false, or as veridical or delusional. In fact, we are all out of direct contact with reality. Viewing psychotic experiences as an attempt to make sense of otherwise unexplainable experiences may help to better understand even bizarre beliefs and to empathize with those who hold them. Importantly, such a perspective may also shift the focus of therapy away from symptom reduction, in the sense of improving “contact with reality”, towards forms of functional recovery that will be most beneficial to the individual’s lasting well-being.

A few caveats should be noted. First, there is no empirical evidence yet to directly support the claims made here. We are not aware of any psychotherapeutic model that explicitly incorporates PP. It may seem intuitively plausible that it will be helpful to frame delusions and hallucinations as the brain’s rational inferences under heightened uncertainty, rather than signs of permanent insanity. Still, it is not expected that a PP-based therapeutic model will be a cure-all solution that will be experienced as destigmatizing by everybody36. Thus, therapeutic models incorporating this perspective will need to be developed and their therapeutic efficacy as well as their potential to reduce stigma will have to be evaluated. Studies should also examine the crucial question which elements of neurobiological illness models confer or counter stigma, whether PP will be successful in this regard, and which aspects of PP accounts may work for whom. The development of PP-based models may draw on experiences of existing programs that aim at a normalizing perspective on psychosis, such as the Hearing Voices movement41–44. Second, the neuroscientific and mathematical foundations of PP are complex and its assumptions may be counterintuitive to many people. Yet, the basic concept of PP can be expressed in simple and intuitive terms: “Your experience of the world depends on a balance between what you already know—and how certain you are about it—and what you are learning from your current sensory input. If your estimation of either how much you know or how much you are learning is inaccurate, this balance is disrupted, and false beliefs may emerge.” Third, neurobiological models must not be promoted at the expense of psychosocial accounts. Rather, they should be part of a comprehensive, unifying approach that integrates biological, psychological, and social levels of explanation. Fourth, normalizing perspectives come with the risk of trivializing mental disorders. Therefore, care must be taken when explaining psychosis within a normative model of brain function to still acknowledge the suffering and impairment often associated with schizophrenia. Fifth, our proposal does not address the fact that the term “schizophrenia” itself carries a strong stigma. However, we concur with suggestions to rename the disorder, such as “positive and negative symptom disorder”45 as they are in keeping with the spirit of our own proposal. Finally, it should be kept in mind that PP is still a theoretical framework that awaits further empirical investigation. Despite accumulating evidence supporting PP’s key tenets, its precise computational mechanisms and their neural implementation remain a matter of controversy46. We would therefore like to emphasize that we do not consider PP the only neuroscientific theory that may have the potential to reduce stigma. Other theories might do the job just as well or even better. We consider the views expressed in this article an exercise, for which we chose PP as an influential and sufficiently popular framework, to lay out some of the features of neuroscientifically-based models that might be useful in reducing stigma.

If the above considerations hold true, PP might open the door for new ways of integrating neurobiological models into communication with patients, relatives, and the public without increasing—and hopefully even decreasing—stigma associated with schizophrenia. The ship of stigma reduction through neurobiological illness models may not have sailed after all.
