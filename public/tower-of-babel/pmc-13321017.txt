Plasma Proteomic Profiling Was Used to Discover a Biochemical Recurrence Prediction Model for Prostate Cancer
Authors: Ning Xu, Linhui Zhang, Zhenmei Yao, Liqing Li, Maoping Cai, Yunzhi Wang, Xihao Wang, Subei Tan, Kai Li, Jiacheng Lyu, Hongkai Wang, Zhaoyu Qin, Jinwen Feng, Bo Dai, Yao Zhu, Hang Xiang, Xiaojian Qin, Guowen Lin, Yanzi Gu, Fei Ding, Jianyuan Zhao, Yuanyuan Qu, Chen Ding, Dingwei Ye, Wenjun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies in men. There is limited data available regarding potential minimally-invasive biomarkers for predicting PCa outcomes and disease monitoring. Here, we investigate the proteomic profile of plasma in 222 patients with PCa and 159 healthy controls. Integrative analyses of the proteome profile and clinical features identified protein networks related to International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grades and prostate-specific antigen (PSA). Proteome-based classification revealed three subtypes, PCa-I, PCa-II, and PCa-III, reflecting distinct clinical prognosis and molecular signatures. We develop a 17-protein panel and established a biochemical recurrence prediction model that effectively predicts biochemical recurrence for patients with PCa, which is better than ISUP grades and pathological stages. Finally, we validate the protein panel by parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) assay in an independent cohort. Collectively, this study portrays the plasma proteomic landscape of PCa cohort and provides a comprehensive resource for further biological and predictive research in PCa.Keywords: plasma proteome profiling, mass spectrometry, prostate cancer, biochemical recurrence

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common cancer among men, with nearly 1.5 million men diagnosed with the disease worldwide each year (1). It is usually diagnosed based on digital rectal examination (2), prostate-specific antigen (PSA) serum levels, and multicore scheme of prostate biopsy. Furthermore, PCa is a complex disease with varying clinical behavior, ranging from indolent tumors to aggressive tumors associated with lethal progression. In a more recent study, we disclosed the heterogeneity of prostate cancer through single-cell multi-omics integrated analysis (3). However, there is a lack of suitable noninvasive, comprehensive biomarkers for accurate diagnosis, classification, and the prediction or monitoring of disease recurrence.

The main treatment method for prostate cancer patients is radical prostatectomy combined with adjuvant/neoadjuvant therapy (4). However, among patients who have undergone radical prostatectomy, approximately 40% will experience an increase in PSA, which is defined as biochemical recurrence (BCR) (5). Once BCR is diagnosed, the risk of developing incurable distant metastasis and overall mortality significantly increases (6, 7). The commonly used clinical prognostic tools, such as the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grades, pretreatment serum PSA concentration, and tumor stage, do not accurately predict disease recurrence for individual patients. The current risk stratification schemes, such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and the European Association of Urology Risk Group system, can result in both overtreatment and undertreatment of individual patients (8). Furthermore, the clinical relevance of these risk stratification schemes may be limited due to a decrease in the incidence of advanced disease and mortality (9). Therefore, identifying molecular biomarkers that can serve as minimally invasive clinical tools to enhance the management and prognosis of patients with PCa remains a crucial clinical necessity.

Proteomic profiling, particularly plasma-based proteomic, has emerged as a powerful tool for uncovering molecular signatures associated with cancer. Plasma is an easily accessible biofluid, and its protein content reflects changes in the body’s physiological state, including the presence of cancer (10). The application of high-throughput proteomic technologies enables the identification and quantification of thousands of proteins simultaneously, providing a comprehensive view of the molecular alterations occurring in cancer patients (11, 12). We have applied the plasma proteomic technologies to identify the progression prediction model for predicting tumor progression in the urinary system (13), the diagnostic biomarkers to facilitate the diagnosis of gastrointestinal cancer (14), and the dynamic biomarkers for effective disease management (15).

In this study, we analyzed 381 plasma samples, which included 222 prostate cancer (PCa) samples and 159 healthy control samples, using mass spectrometry-based data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics. We mapped the proteomic characteristics associated with different tumor stages, ISUP groups, and pretreatment serum PSA concentrations, and identified three PCa subtypes with distinct clinical and molecular differences. Furthermore, we identified a 17-protein panel that efficiently predicts BCR, which outperforms the strategies currently used in clinical practice. We then validated the protein panel in an independent PCa cohort by PRM assay. Collectively, this study presented the plasma proteomic landscape of the PCa cohort and provided a valuable comprehensive resource for further biological, prognostic prediction, and drug discovery efforts.

Experimental Procedures

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

Participant Recruitment and Clinical Data Collection

In this study, plasma samples from all participants in two independent cohorts (Cohort one and 2) were recruited from the Department of Urology, Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC). Cohort one was the discovery cohort, consisting of 159 healthy controls and 222 PCa patients. Cohort two was the verification cohort consisting of 116 PCa patients. In accordance with the terminology standards established by the FDA Biomarker Qualification Program Guidance (2018) and the BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) Resource (2022), we use the term “verification” to denote the analytical performance confirmation and independent cohort testing, distinct from the broader biomarker qualification process (16, 17). The healthy controls matched for age, gender, and BMI were peripheral blood samples for physical examination from healthy persons. We excluded control samples in case of any medication or any cancer. Patients with prostate cancer who had not received any anti-cancer treatments before surgery were randomly selected during their initial visit. Clinical information of patients, including gender, age, ISUP grade, TNM staging, Gleason score, and routine blood test results, is listed in Supplemental Table S1. All the samples were stored at −80 °C until sample processing. The study was compliant with the ethical standards of Helsinki Declaration II and was approved by the institutional review board of FUSCC (050,432-4-1911D). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before any study-specific investigation was conducted. Each sample was assigned a new research ID, and the patient’s name or medical record number used during hospitalization was de-identified.

Blood Collection

In this study, we meticulously collected blood samples from both prostate cancer patients and healthy individuals using EDTA-containing tubes, which represents the gold standard for plasma proteomics. This approach has been widely adopted and validated in numerous high-impact studies (18, 19). Blood was collected in EDTA tubes, immediately placed on ice, and centrifuged at 4 °C, 2000×g for 15 min within 2 h of collection. Plasma was aliquoted (100 μl) and stored at −80 °C. Hemolysis was visually inspected, and samples with >10 mg/L hemoglobin were excluded. Following centrifugation, the plasma was carefully harvested and stored under optimal conditions to preserve its biochemical properties. This plasma was then used for subsequent analyses, which were critical for our research objectives. The same method of blood collection was published in a previous article (20, 21, 22). We ensured that all steps of the blood collection and processing were carried out with the utmost precision to maintain the reliability and validity of our study results.

Plasma Protein Extraction and Trypsin Digestion

Plasma was separated by centrifugation (16,000×g, 5 min) to remove insoluble solids, and the plasma was stored at −80 °C within 30 min after centrifugation until proteomic analysis. The protein concentration of each sample was measured and recorded using the BCA method on a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a wavelength of 562 nm. Two microliters of plasma samples were mixed with 98 μl of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) buffer and inactivated at 95 °C for 3 min in a heating oscillator at 1000×g. After cooling the samples to room temperature, trypsin was used to digest the samples at a mass ratio of enzyme to protein of 1:25 for 17 h in a 37 °C incubator. Subsequently, 5 μl of aqueous ammonia was added to each sample tube, vortexed to terminate the digestion reaction, and the supernatant was dried using a 60 °C vacuum drier (SpeedVac, Eppendorf). The peptides were then dissolved in 100 μl of 0.1% formic acid, vortexed for 3 min, and then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube for sample purification and desalting. Before desalting, two pieces of 3M C18 were inserted into the bottom of the T-400 column head to form the column for sample purification, and the following steps were used to activate it: First, rinse with 90 μl of 100% acetonitrile (ACN) twice, then rinse with 90 μl of 50% and 80% ACN once each, and finally rinse with 90 μl of 50% ACN once. After balancing the column with 90 μl of 0.1% FA twice, the supernatant from the peptide sample tube was loaded onto the column and centrifuged at 1000×g for 2 min, and this step was repeated once. The column was then balanced with 90 μl of 0.1% FA twice. The column was placed in a new EP tube, and 90 μl of elution buffer (0.1% FA in 50% ACN) was added to the column and centrifuged at 2000×g for 2 min for elution, and this step was repeated once. The eluate was collected and dried in a 60 °C vacuum drier for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

In this study, to avoid systematic bias caused by instrument drift or batch effects, the LC–MS/MS injection order of all samples was determined by “lottery-style” randomization: each sample was first given a sequential number, and a random, non-repeating sequence was generated with a random-number generator (e.g., R’s sample () function or www.random.org); the samples were then reordered according to this sequence. The plasma samples were measured using LC-MS instrumentation consisting of an EASY- nLC 1200 ultra-high-pressure system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled via a nano-electrospray ion source to a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (all Thermo Fisher Scientific). Eluted peptides were separated at 60 °C on a 150 μm I.D. × 8 cm column (C18, 1.9 μm, 120 Å, Dr Maisch GmbH). Mobile phases A and B were 99.9/0.1% water/FA (v/v) and 80/20/0.1% ACN/water/FA (v/v/v). The MS analysis was performed in a data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. The DIA method consisted of an MS1 scan from 300 to 1400 m/z at 60,000 resolution and the automatic gain control (AGC) target 3e6 or a maximum of 20 ms. Then, 31 isolated DIA segments (Supplemental Table S1) were acquired at 15,000 resolutions with an AGC target 1e6 or 22 ms for maximal injection time. The total cycle time was 0.02 m. The setting ‘‘inject ions for all available parallelizable time’’ was enabled. Higher energy collision dissociation (HCD) fragmentation was set to normalized collision energy of 27%. The spectra were recorded in profile mode. The default charge state for the MS2 was set to 3.

MS Database Searching

The 327-Hybrid Spectral Library

The Tissue Sample Preparation, Tissue Protein Extraction, and Trypsin Digestion

To generate 327 protein spectral libraries, FFPE specimens were prepared and provided by Zhongshan Hospital. A 3 μm slide from each FFPE block was used for H&E staining. For proteomic sample preparation, 10 μm sections were deparaffinized with xylene and washed with gradient ethanol. The specimens were selected according to H&E staining status and scraped. As a result, 327 tissue samples, such as urothelial carcinoma and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, were collected. The Research Ethics Committees of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, approved this study (B2019–200R), and written informed consent was obtained from all the involved patients. All materials were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Each sample was assigned a new research ID, and the patient pathology reports were de-identified.

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235). For each sample, 5 to 10 sections were mounted onto glass slides and subjected to macrodissection under a light microscope to enrich for tumor regions and exclude necrotic areas, extracellular matrix, and normal tissue contaminants, as annotated by a board-certified pathologist. The microdissected tissue fragments were deparaffinized by two sequential washes with xylene (5 min each at room temperature) to remove paraffin wax, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (2 × 3 min in 100% ethanol, 2 × 3 min in 95% ethanol, and a final wash in 70% ethanol). After complete air-drying for 15 min at room temperature, the tissue scrapings were carefully transferred to sterile 1.5 ml low-protein-binding microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf LoBind) using a scalpel blade. The tissue pellets were resuspended in 100 μl of freshly prepared TCEP buffer containing 2% sodium deoxycholate (DOC) as a detergent for efficient protein solubilization, 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide (CAA) for cysteine alkylation, 10 mM tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP·HCl) for disulfide bond reduction, 100 mM Tris·HCl as a buffering agent, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) as a serine protease inhibitor, all dissolved in MS-grade water and adjusted to pH 8.8. The samples were incubated in a thermomixer (Eppendorf ThermoMixer C) at 99 °C with vigorous agitation at 1400 rpm for 30 min to ensure complete protein denaturation and cell disruption, then gradually cooled to room temperature over 10 min to prevent protein re-precipitation. Proteins were digested with trypsin (enzyme-to-protein ratio 1:50) for 16 h at 37 °C.

Following tryptic digestion, peptide samples were acidified with 26 μl of 10% formic acid (FA) per tube to terminate enzymatic activity and protonate peptide residues. Each tube was vigorously vortexed at maximum speed for 3 min to ensure thorough mixing and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at room temperature to pellet precipitated detergents and insoluble debris. The clarified supernatants were carefully transferred to fresh microcentrifuge tubes, mixed with 350 μl of buffer containing 0.1% FA in 50% acetonitrile (ACN) to optimize peptide solubility, vortexed for an additional 3 min, and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min. The resulting supernatants were transferred to new 1.5 ml polypropylene tubes and vacuum-dried using a CentriVap concentrator (Labconco) at 60 °C for approximately 2 to 3 h until complete evaporation of the organic solvent.

Dried peptide pellets were reconstituted in 100 μl of 0.1% aqueous FA, vortexed for 3 min to facilitate complete dissolution and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min to remove any residual particulate matter. The cleared supernatants were then subjected to desalting using C8-packed solid-phase extraction columns (3M Empore Octyl SPE cartridges, 7 mm diameter/3 ml capacity). Prior to sample loading, columns were sequentially activated by gravity flow with 2 × 100 μl of 100% ACN, followed by 1 × 100 μl of 50% ACN, and 2 × 100 μl of deionized water to remove manufacturing residues and hydrate the stationary phase. Columns were then equilibrated with 2 × 100 μl of 0.1% FA to establish the appropriate binding conditions.

Each sample was loaded onto its respective column twice to maximize peptide binding efficiency, followed by washing with 2 × 100 μl of 0.1% FA to remove residual salts, detergents, and hydrophilic contaminants. Bound peptides were eluted with 2 × 100 μl of elution buffer (0.1% FA in 50% ACN) using gentle centrifugation at 1000×g for 2 min to control flow rate. Eluates from both elution steps were pooled in clean microcentrifuge tubes, vacuum-dried at 60 °C as described above and stored at −80 °C in a low-temperature freezer until subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis. All samples were processed in batches with intermittent quality control standards to monitor procedural consistency.

The LC-MS/MS Experimental Settings

To generate the protein spectral libraries, the peptides of 327 distinct tissue samples were analyzed on Orbitrap Fusion Lumos and QExactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Easy nLC-1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were re-dissolved in 12 μl loading buffer (5% methanol and 0.2% FA). Then, a 9 μl sample was loaded on a 30 cm long, homemade C18 nano-capillary analytical column (75 mm i.d.) packed with C18 resin (particle size: 3 μm, pore size: 100 Å; Dikma Technologies) for proteome analysis. The peptides were separated on an analytical column with a 150 min gradient (buffer A: 0.1% FA in water; buffer B: 0.1% FA in 80% ACN) at 600 nl/min (0 min, 4% B; 0 − 10 min, 4–15% B; 10 − 125 min, 15 − 30% B; 125 − 140 min, 30 − 50% B; 140 − 141 min, 50 − 100% B; 141 − 150 min, 100% B). MS was operated under a data-dependent acquisition mode. For full-scan MS1 spectra, ions with m/z values ranging from 300 to 1400 were acquired by Orbitrap mass analyzer at a high resolution of 120,000. The automatic gain control (AGC) target value was set as 3E + 06. The maximal ion injection time was 80 ms. MS2 spectrum acquisition was performed in top-speed mode. Precursor ions were selected and fragmented with higher energy collision dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 27%. Fragment ions were analyzed using an ion trap mass analyzer with an AGC target value of 5E + 04 and a maximal ion injection time of 20 ms. Peptides that triggered MS/MS scans were dynamically excluded from further MS/MS scans for 12 s.

The Library Construction Parameters

As for the background library construction, the raw data-dependent analysis MS files underwent conversion to the mzML file format utilizing MSConvert software (23). For the construction of consolidated spectral libraries, we engaged the FragPipe computational platform (version 12.1), equipped with MSFragger (version 2.2) (24), Philosopher (version 2.0.0) (25), and Python (version 3.6.7). The converted DDA mzML files, throughout the spectral library construction, were cohesively processed. Peptide identification was executed from MS/MS spectra using FragPipe integrated with the MSFragger search engine. This was matched against the UniProt human protein database (reviewed sequences only), which also encompassed reversed protein sequences appended as decoys for ensuing false discovery rate (FDR) calculations. Technical specifications included both precursor and initial fragment mass tolerances set at 20 ppm, enabling spectrum deisotoping, mass calibration, and parameter optimization. We established enzyme specificity to ‘trypsin’ and permitted up to two missed trypsin cleavages. Configurations for peptide length ranged between seven and 25, while peptide mass was set from 500 to 5000 Da. Only precursor ion score charges of +2, +3, and +4 were considered. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was fixed as a modification, while variable modifications entailed N-acetylation and methionine oxidation. A maximum of three variable modifications per peptide was allowed.

Peptide identification was performed using FragPipe (v19.1) with MSFragger (v3.7) against the UniProt human protein database (decoy sequences appended for FDR calculation). Precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm, enabling spectrum deisotoping, mass calibration, and parameter optimization. Enzyme specificity was set to strict trypsin, allowing missed cleavage. Peptide constraints: length 7 to 25 amino acids; mass range 500–5000 Da; precursor charges +2, +3, and +4. Fixed modification: cysteine carbamidomethylation. Variable modifications: N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation (maximum three per peptide).

Subsequent to the search, MSFragger search results (in pepXML format) were processed using the Philosopher toolkit. PeptideProphet (run with the high–mass accuracy binning and semi-parametric mixture modeling options) was employed to compute the posterior probability of correct identification for each peptide-to-spectrum match (PSM). The emergent output files from PeptideProphet were processed together using ProteinProphet to perform protein inference (assemble peptides into proteins) and synthesize a unified protXML file containing high-confidence protein groups. The combined ProteinProphet file was further processed using the Philosopher Filter command, which characterized each identified peptide as a unique peptide to a particular protein (or protein group containing indistinguishable proteins) or assigned it as a razor peptide to a single protein (protein group) that had the most peptide evidence. Both unique and razor peptides were used for subsequent analysis. The data were filtered to 1% protein-level FDR using the picked FDR strategy. The peptide-, PSM-, and ion-level reports were then generated and filtered using the 2D FDR approach (i.e., 1% protein FDR plus 1% PSM/ion/peptide-level FDR for each corresponding PSM.tsv, ion.tsv, and peptide.tsv file). The spectral library contained 215,529 precursors, representing 15,612 proteins.

Although the background library could significantly improve the proteome identification depth, meanwhile, we realized building these libraries from complex sample-derived spectra could introduce unwanted noise, potentially increasing the risk of false discoveries and introducing variability into the DIA proteome searches as described in the published study (26). Therefore, a robust algorithm is imperative for discerning and eliminating extraneous noise generated during the library search process, ensuring trustworthy spectrum identification and accurate protein quantification. To address this challenge in library-based DIA proteomics, we introduced the STAVER (https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-3111384/v1) (27), a standardized benchmark dataset-based algorithm that efficiently reduces non-biological variation in large-scale DIA-MS data. Considering the variability and spectrum quality of the peptides, the algorithm efficiently utilizes the prior information from the identified peptides in the standard benchmark dataset. Employing a peptide-centric scoring and ranking approach, STAVER mitigates nonbiological noise potential during library searches, enhancing spectrum identifications and protein quantification accuracy. After applying the STAVER algorithm, the resulting spectral library contained 185,012 precursors, 173,662 peptides, and 15,056 proteins. Furthermore, we analyzed DIA data against this refined spectral library.

Peptide Identification and Protein Quantification

DIA data were analyzed using DIA-NN (v1.7.0) against our previously generated 327-hybrid spectral library. This comprehensive library, derived from 327 samples spanning multiple disease types and experimental conditions, provided extensive peptide coverage and improved identification depth. The DIA-NN search employed the following optimized parameters: precursor FDR controlled at 1% using a target-decoy approach; MS1 mass accuracy set to 10 ppm and MS2 fragment mass accuracy to 20 ppm to accommodate minor instrumental drift; scan window of 30 for optimal peak group extraction; implicit protein grouping by genes to consolidate isoforms; robust LC (high accuracy) quantification strategy for precise peptide intensity measurements; maximum of two missed cleavages; fixed modification: carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues; and variable modifications: N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation. To enhance quantification accuracy, DIA-NN’s built-in MaxLFQ (Maximal Label-free Quantification) algorithm was applied, which employs delayed normalization and maximal peptide ratio extraction to minimize ratio compression and improve precision (28). The ‘iq’ R package (v1.0) was subsequently used for rigorous protein-level summarization and statistical processing, including variance stabilization and automated handling of technical replicates (29).

For each protein group, we computed iBAQ (intensity-based absolute quantification) by summing the normalized intensities of all tryptic peptides unique to that group (proteotypic peptides, razor peptides included). Although label-free DIA studies often report “Top-N” or “MaxLFQ” intensities, we chose iBAQ because: iBAQ exploits the full peptide inventory of DIA, thereby increasing quantitative depth for low-abundance proteins. iBAQ adds up the normalized intensities of all proteotypic peptides and razor peptides, which is equivalent to conducting a “full vote” on all quantifiable evidence of proteins. For regulatory proteins, kinases, transcription factors, or secreted factors with low copy numbers that can only be reliably tracked by 3 to 5 peptides, this cumulative effect of “small amounts adding up” significantly improves the signal-to-noise ratio, preventing these proteins from being filtered out due to “insufficient signal” in downstream differential analysis. In recent years, more and more public databases (such as ProteomeXchange, Human Protein Atlas) have begun to provide DIA-iBAQ absolute quantification layers. Using the same algorithm ensures that our data is consistent with these resources in scale, facilitating subsequent cross-experimental comparisons, machine learning model training, or the construction of tissue-specific absolute expression profiles.

Label-free protein quantifications were calculated using a label-free, intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) approach. We calculated the peak area values as parts of corresponding proteins. The fraction of total (FOT) was used to represent the normalized abundance of a particular protein across samples. FOT was defined as a protein’s iBAQ divided by the total iBAQ of all identified proteins within a sample. The FOT values were multiplied by 1 for the ease of presentation and missing values were imputed with 1e-5. The same strategy was used in published studies (30).

Quality Control of the Mass Spectrometry Data

For quality control of performance of mass spectrometry, the mixture of all plasma samples from PCA patients were measured every 20 samples, which was adopted in proteomic studies (31). The quality control (QC) plasma samples were analyzed using the same method and conditions as our plasma cohort. Finally, we obtained 19 repeat runs of the PCa samples pool during the MS detection.

A Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated for all QC runs. The average Pearson’s correlation coefficient of QC plasma samples in this study was 0.97 (range, 0.96–1.00) (Supplemental Fig. S1B; Supplemental Table S1), demonstrating consistent stability of the mass spectrometry platform.

Targeted PRM Analysis

To evaluate the accuracy of the classifiers, we designed parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) strategy to quantify the proteins in the biochemical recurrence prediction model in plasma samples from the new independent cohort composed of 116 patients with PCa. As defined in the MCP guidelines, Tier three workflows are designed for validating discovery-based results and performing relative quantification in PRM analysis. The procedures for plasma collection, plasma protein extraction, and trypsin digestion in the verification cohort followed the same protocols detailed in the preceding sections.

Using the library search results, a set of target peptides that are unique to the proteins in the biochemical recurrence prediction model was selected. For each candidate protein, we used the following filters: (1) fully tryptic, (2) unique in the human UniProt proteome (including isoforms), (3) no missed cleavage, (4) no methionine or known SNP, (5) length 8 to 16 aa, (6) no N-terminal Gln→pyro-Glu or Cys. The three highest-intensity precursor ions predicted by the DIA spectral library were pre-screened in pooled plasma; the two best-responding transitions per precursor (total four transitions per peptide) were retained if the ion ratio CV <20% across six technical replicates. Besides, housekeeping proteins, such as VCP, RPLP0, and PSMB4, were also included for reference.

The verification cohort was powered to detect an effect size (Cohen’s d) ≥0.6 for the mean difference of any targeted protein between BCR-positive and -negative PCa, assuming α = 0.05, power = 0.80 and a two-sided t test. A minimum n = 58 per group was calculated using G∗Power 3.1; we enrolled 116 PCa patients to allow for 20% dropout. An equal amount of plasma from each sample (the verification cohort with 116 patients with PCa) was digested as described in the part of profiling preparation. Sample processing was performed by a technician blinded to the clinical outcome. After tryptic digestion, peptides were randomized across plates using a blocked randomization list generated in R (“blockrand” package). The acquisition sequence was further randomized within each batch, with pQC and blanks inserted as above. The same strategy was used in published article (32).

The acquisition of samples was randomized to avoid bias. The peptides were measured using LC-MS instrumentation consisting of an EASY- nLC 1200 ultra-high-pressure system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled via a nano-electrospray ion source to a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (all Thermo Fisher Scientific). Eluted peptides were separated at 60 °C on 150 μm I.D. × 8 cm column (C18, 1.9 μm, 120 Å, Dr Maisch GmbH). Mobile phases A and B were 99.9/0.1% water/FA (v/v) and 80/20/0.1% ACN/water/FA (v/v/v). Peptides were separated using a linear gradient elution at a flow rate of 600 nl/min. The gradient conditions were: 0 to 6 min, 7 to 18% B; 6 to 8.2 min, 18 to 35% B; 8.2 to 9.2 min, 35 to 95% B; 9.2 to 17.3 min, 95% B; 17.3 to 18.3 min, 95–3% B; 18.3 to 20 min, 3% B. For peptide ionization, 1800 V was applied and a 250 °C capillary temperature was used. All samples were analyzed using a PRM method based on a scheduled inclusion list containing the 21 peptides. The full MS mode was measured at resolution 60,000 with AGC target value of 3E6 and maximum IT of 20 ms, with scanning range of 300 to 1400 m/z. Target precursors were then isolated through an m/z window of 1.2 Th, followed by fragmentation at 27% normalized collision energy. The product ions were scanned with a resolution of 15,000, AGC target value of 1E6 or a maximum injection time of 25 ms.

Raw data was searched by Skyline-daily (4.2.1.19004, University of Washington, USA). The proteins were quantified with the fragment total area reported by Skyline-daily. We selected peptides and tested their stability of signal and shape of peaks in the pool sample for final quantification and referred to the ranking offered by Skyline. Additionally, we updated the information about the targeted peptides and the result table from the PRM analysis in the Supplemental Table S6.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Missing Value Imputation

For the proteomic data, FOTs multiplied by 1E5 were used for quantification, and finally, log2 transformed, if necessary. This method has also been applied in other published proteomic studies (13, 14, 33).

Differential Protein Analysis

Proteins detected in >30% of samples across all groups were selected for differential expression analysis. This filtering threshold was chosen to balance statistical robustness with preservation of inter-patient heterogeneity in PCa. Differential expression between groups (e.g., PCa versus normal) was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Proteins were defined as significantly upregulated or downregulated if they met the following criteria: false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and fold change >1.5 or <0.67.

Batch Effect

To ensure balanced distribution of biological and technical variation in our single-batch design, we employed randomization based on key clinical covariates. We employed a single analytical batch (7-days continuous LC-MS/MS, n = 222) with a strategically arranged sample order that evenly distributed variables throughout the run, inherently minimizing inter-batch technical variation. Although batch effects are attenuated in single-batch (single-run) mass spectrometry experiments compared to multi-batch designs, technical variation remains impossible to eliminate entirely. Constrained by systematic factors such as chromatographic column stability, ionization efficiency fluctuations, instrument sensitivity drift, and ambient temperature variations, time-dependent trends in retention time shifts, intensity drift, and missingness patterns may still emerge during continuous injection sequences. This is widely recognized as an inherent and unavoidable technical noise in high-throughput LC-MS/MS proteomics studies (34, 35).

To address this challenge, the standard paradigm within the field emphasizes mitigating batch effects through rigorous experimental design (Design of Experiments, DoE) rather than relying solely on post-hoc data correction. Specifically, community consensus recommends employing a balanced run order to ensure that samples from different biological subgroups are evenly interspersed throughout the analytical sequence, preventing specific phenotypes from clustering in particular time windows. These principles have been validated and applied in recent large-scale cohort studies (35, 36).

In this study, we strictly adhered to these best practices. Addressing the heterogeneity of prostate cancer, we implemented a randomization algorithm based on four core clinicopathological covariates—patient age, ISUP grade, T stage, and Gleason score—to generate the acquisition sequence. Through systematic arrangement, we ensured that samples representing different malignancy grades and clinical stages were alternately distributed throughout the LC-MS/MS analytical queue rather than clustering in specific segments. As illustrated in Supplemental Figure S5, A–D, this design effectively achieved decoupling of biological variation from technical covariates: age distribution, ISUP grades, T stages, and Gleason scores all exhibited homogeneous scatter along the MS detection queue, with no systematic clustering of specific subtypes at particular time points.

Although our single-batch experimental design incorporated balanced run order and randomization based on key clinical covariates to orthogonalize biological and technical variation at the source, explicit assessment of residual batch effects through unsupervised multivariate analysis remains essential for rigorous data quality control. Accordingly, we performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to systematically evaluate whether any technical variation persisted despite our design safeguards.

Prior to PCA, we examined the distribution of samples in the MS detection queue (Supplemental Fig. S5, E–H). As shown in these scatter plots (PC1 versus PC2 from initial quality control PCA), samples are colored by ISUP grade (Supplemental Fig. S5E), T stage (Supplemental Fig. S5F), Gleason score (Supplemental Fig. S5G), and age (Supplemental Fig. S5H). The scattered distribution across the detection queue (x-axis) confirms successful randomization with no systematic clustering of specific clinical subtypes at particular time points. PCA plots colored by clinical covariates (ISUP grade, T stage, Gleason score, and age) demonstrate substantial overlap across different clinical subtypes, with 95% confidence ellipses showing extensive intersection (Supplemental Fig. S5, I–L). Together, the scattered distribution of clinical subtypes throughout the detection queue, combined with the lack of batch-specific clustering in PCA, demonstrates that potential technical variation was effectively controlled through our single-batch analytical strategy with systematically arranged sample injection order.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Differentially expressed proteins were subjected to Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis in DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) (37) and ConsensusPathwayDB (http://cpdb.molgen.mpg.de/) with a FDR <0.05. We used gene sets of molecular pathways from the KEGG (38)/Reactome (39)/GO (40) databases to compute pathways.

WGCNA Analysis

Weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) (41) was used to identify groups of co-regulated genes in an unsupervised manner. A sample network was constructed to identify outlying samples with a standardized connectivity score of less than −2.5. A signed gene co-expression network was constructed with a soft threshold power of 10. Groups of co-regulated genes (modules) correlated with each other with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.9, or better, were merged. Pathway enrichment analysis was used for the functional annotation of the identified modules. The eigengenes of each module were used to measure the association between modules and clinical information.

Pathway Scores and Correlation Analysis

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) (42) was utilized to obtain pathway scores for each sample based on proteomic data using the R package GSVA (43). Correlations between the plasma proteins and clinical indicators were determined using Spearman’s correlation. The spearman’s correlation was calculated in the R package Hmisc (v4.5–0).

Fuzzy C-Means Clustering Analysis

Proteins were grouped into different clusters using Mfuzz package (version 2.54.0) in R with fuzzy c-means algorithm (44). In the fuzzy c-means algorithm analysis, the membership value of the gene under each cluster was calculated, which indicates the degree of ownership of a gene or protein in a cluster. The average normalized protein quantities by Z score in each ISUP grade were used for fuzzy c-means clustering with the R (version 4.0.2) package Mfuzz. The number of clusters was set to twelve and the fuzzifier coefficient, M, was set to 1.25.

Construction and Verification of a Biochemical Recurrence Prediction Model of Patients with PCa

In this study, we selected the more inclusive 30% threshold to maximize statistical power and biological coverage while maintaining stringent quality control. We applied lasso regression analysis to screen featured proteins. Lasso regression was performed 100 times using the glmnet package (version 4.1.6) in R software, with the parameters set to family = “cox” and type.measure = “deviance”. 10-fold cross-validation was used, and other parameters were set to default. The predictive value of this model was verified using ROC analysis (pROC R package version 1.16.2 and Caret R package version 6.0–86). The predictive model was performed in the verification cohort.

Consensus Clustering Analysis

The protein expression matrix of the plasma samples from PCa patients was used to identify the proteomic subtypes using the consensus clustering method implemented in the R package ConsensusClusterPlus v.3.8 (45). Prior to clustering analysis, proteins that were expressed in more than 30% of patient samples were selected. The cluster analysis was performed with the following setting: maxK = 10, reps = 1000, pItem = 0.8, pFeature = 1, clusterAlg = “hc”, distance = “spearman” for the clustering runs. A preferred cluster result was selected by considering the profiles of the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) and delta area under the CDF curve for clustering solutions between 2 and 10 clusters.

Risk Score

The hazard ratios of each protein were calculated based on the biochemical recurrence-free survival (BFS). The hazard ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each protein were based on Cox proportional hazards models by the R package “survival”. The results were plotted using a forest map with the R package “ggplot2”. The risk score for each PCa patient was calculated using the formula: 17-protein panel risk score = Σβi∗Xi (Xi represents the screened protein expression, and βi represents the corresponding coefficient).

Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves (log-rank test) were used to determine the biochemical recurrence-free survival (RFS) of patients with high PSA versus low PSA. The coefficient value, which is equal to ln (46), was calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. p-values less than 0.05, were considered significantly different and selected for Cox regression multivariate analysis. Prior to the log-rank test of a given protein, survminer (version 0.2.4, R package) with maxstat (maximally selected rank statistics; http://r-addict.com/2016/11/21/Optimal-Cutpoint-maxstat.html) was used to determine the optimal cut-off point for the selected samples according to a previous study (47). RFS curves were then calculated based on the optimal cut-off point.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical data were analyzed using standard statistical methods. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare protein expression levels between prostate cancer (PCa) and normal plasma samples, between one ISUP grade and all other grades, and between one subtype and all other subtypes. Correlations were assessed using Spearman or Pearson correlation analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance defined as p-value <0.05. For multiple comparisons, p-value were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) probabilities were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves with Log-rank tests. Variables associated with biochemical recurrence-free survival were first identified through univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, and significant factors were subsequently entered into a multivariate Cox regression model using forward likelihood ratio (LR) selection. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.2). The significance levels were denoted as ∗p-value <0.05, ∗∗ p-value <0.01, ∗∗∗ p-value <0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ p-value <0.0001. All statistical analyses were independently reviewed and verified by two statisticians.

Results

The Characteristics of PCa Cohort for Plasma Proteome Profiling

Plasma samples from 338 PCa patients (discovery [n = 222], verification [n = 116]) and 159 healthy controls were analyzed using quantitative proteomic analysis with DIA strategy (Fig. 1A; Methods). All the plasma samples from PCa patients were collected at the time of diagnosis and all the studied patients were treatment-naiïve. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found for all cohorts in Methods. The overall workflow of this study was exhibited in Figure 1A. The basic information about our study cohort, including age, ISUP grade, Gleason score, TNM stage, and pretreatment serum PSA concentrations, was summarized in Figure 1B and Supplemental Table S1. Furthermore, to ensure unbiased model evaluation, we enrolled an additional 116 PCa patients as a completely independent verification cohort, distinct from the discovery cohort (Supplemental Table S1). Supplemental Figure S1A presents the demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of this verification cohort, confirming its comparability to the discovery population and clinical relevance for external verification.

The plasma samples were performed an unbiased and rapid “body fluid proteome profiling” using a data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based mass spectrometry strategy, which has been applied in our previous studies (13, 14, 15, 48). To monitor the stability of the MS platform, the mixture of all plasma samples from PCa patients (quality control samples) were measured every 20 samples, which was adopted in proteomic studies (31). The quality control samples (QC) samples were analyzed using the same method and conditions used for our cohort plasma samples. The average Pearson’s correlation coefficient, calculated for all quality control runs of QC samples, was 0.97 (Supplemental Fig. S1B, Supplemental Table S1). The median coefficient of variance (CV) of proteins was 20%, and 71% proteins had CV below 30% (Supplemental Fig. S1C), which demonstrates the consistent stability of the MS platform.

A total of 8686 protein groups were identified in all plasma samples (Supplemental Fig. S1D), with an average of 1779 and 1795 protein groups per PCa and normal plasma (healthy controls) samples, respectively (Fig. 1, C and E, Supplemental Table S1). The proteome of the samples exhibited a unimodal distribution, exhibiting high dynamic range in protein abundance when quantified by FOT (Fig. 1D). The sample proteome exhibited a unimodal distribution and passed the proteomic quality control procedure (Supplemental Fig. S1E), indicating the stability of our mass spectrometry platform. Annotating the identified proteins in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database revealed several functional groups, including 2326 enzymes, 2211 metabolic proteins, 991 cancer-related proteins, 873 transporters, 406 FDA approved drug targets, 233 transcription factors, and 133 immunoglobulin proteins (Fig. 1D).

Plasma Proteome Profiles Differed Between PCa and Normal Samples

To address these analytical challenges while maintaining robust statistical power, the plasma proteome dataset in this study was stringently filtered for data completeness, requiring a minimum of 30% valid values across all samples for each protein group. This thresholding strategy reduced the initial protein group identifications from over 8686 to a high-confidence subset of over 2,233, representing a 74% reduction in feature space. The 30% cutoff was selected as an optimal compromise between preserving proteomic breadth and ensuring sufficient data density for reliable statistical inference. To identify PCa-related plasma proteins, we compared the plasma proteome profiles of patients with PCa to that of the healthy controls (Normal). Differential proteins analysis between PCa and Normal resulted 569 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), of which 241 proteins were upregulated and 328 proteins were downregulated (Fig. 2A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, FDR <0.05, PCa/Normal ratio >1.5 or <0.67; Supplemental Table S2). Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins showed that PCa-enriched proteins were involved in rap1 signaling pathway (RAP1A, MAPK1, PRKCA, etc.), translational initiation (EIF3B, EIF3L, COPS5, etc.), and fatty acid degradation, whereas proteins enriched in normal mainly participated in glycolytic process (PKM, PFKL, PFKM, etc.), actin filament organization (MYH10, MYH3, MYH4, etc.), and glucagon signaling pathway (Fig. 2, B–C). The sample-specific gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA, Methods) further supported these enrichment results (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Table S2).

We reasoned that ideal signatures should be consistently differentially abundant in both tumor tissue and plasma proteomes. Therefore, we included data from a recently published proteomic landscape study involving 125 PCa patients (49). By performing comparative analysis, a total of 281 and 2159 DEPs were observed at the plasma and tissue samples, respectively (Fig. 2E). Among these, 43 proteins (27 downregulated and 16 upregulated) were found to overlap between the plasma and tissue samples (Fig. 2E). Proteins that were upregulated in both plasma and tissue samples were enriched in β-alanine metabolism and fatty acid degradation, while proteins that were downregulated in both plasma and tissue samples were involved in the glycolysis process and actin filament organization (Fig. 2F). We also found a divergence between the proteome of PCa tissue and plasma. The results showed that 95 proteins were significantly upregulated exclusively in the plasma proteome (Fig. 2F), and they were mainly enriched in pathways related to sterol transport and Fc gamma receptor-mediated phagocytosis. Conversely, proteins that were downregulated exclusively in the tissue proteome were mainly involved in base excision repair and protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum pathways.

To further filter out clinically available signature proteins related to PCa, we assessed the immunohistochemical expression of these 16 proteins that were upregulated in both tissue and plasma proteomes, in the Human Protein Atlas (Fig. 2, G–H, Supplemental Table S2). We found that nine out of these 16 exhibited overrepresented tumor staining in prostate cancer samples, including CSTF3, ASAH1, and ARSA (Fig. 2, I–J). These proteins have been reported to be related to the progression of PCa (50, 51, 52) and were also associated with prognosis in our study (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Taken together, PCa is reflected in the plasma proteome profiles of patients through changes in abundance of proteins originated from the diseased organ as well as biological processes that are directly related to the disease phenotype.

Plasma Proteomic Alteration in the PCa Progression

Patients with PCa were graded using the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade groups, which range from ISUP grade group G1 (least aggressive) to G5 (most aggressive) (53). To uncover protein patterns associated with the PCa aggression in our data, we analyzed differentially expressed proteins within tumor samples across different ISUP groups. The differentially expressed proteins were categorized into 12 distinct clusters spanning five ISUP grades by utilizing mFuzz analysis (Fig. 3A; Methods). We found that the expression of proteins in clusters 1 (n = 53) and 2 (n = 39) consistently decreased as the ISUP grades increased, while the expression of proteins in clusters 3 (n = 39) and 4 (n = 66) consistently increased with rising ISUP grades (Fig. 3, B–C, Supplemental Table S3). The proteins in cluster one were enriched in immunoglobulin production and immune response (IGKV3-7, IGKV1-16, IGLV1-36, etc.), and the expression of these proteins gradually decreased as the ISUP grade increased (Fig. 3D, Supplemental Table S3). Nevertheless, the expression of the dysregulated proteins in cluster two was rapidly downregulated with the increase in ISUP grade, and were associated with glucagon signaling (PYGB, PYGM, PYGL, etc.) and phagosome pathways (TUBB2A, TUBB2B, TUBB1, etc.) (Fig. 3E). The proteins in cluster three that were upregulated in ISUP grades 2 to 5 were mainly involved in B cell receptor signaling and DNA damage response pathways (DDB1, CCNK, PARP1, etc.) (Fig. 3F). Notably, the expression of proteins in cluster four peaked during the later stages (G5) of the disease and was associated with angiogenesis (MMP2, ANPEP, ECM1, etc.) and complement and coagulation cascades (F9, A2M, C4BPB, etc.) pathways (Fig. 3G). Therefore, evaluating these proteins could provide many novel possibilities as potential candidate biomarkers for early-stage prostate cancer and enhance our understanding of the clinical features associated with PCa progression.

Weighted Gene Correlation Network Analysis Reveals Co-regulation of Plasma Proteins With PSA

We used the plasma proteins and clinical features to perform weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) (Methods), which was an unsupervised manner to identify groups of co-regulated proteins and the association with clinical variables. Co-expression analysis yielded eight consensus modules (Fig. 4A). Among these modules, MEturquoise and MEgreen were significantly negatively correlated with the ISUP grade, Gleason score, pretreatment serum total PSA (tPSA) and free PSA (fPSA). In contrast, MEbrown, MEblue, and MEgreen exhibited a significant positive correlation with these clinical features (Fig. 4A). Pathway enrichment analysis of the protein module members revealed the various biological processes of different modules. In detail, the proteins in the MEturquoise and MEgreen modules were enriched in pathways related to the proteasome, fructose 6-phosphate metabolic process, immunoglobulin production, and immune responses (Fig. 4B). The proteins in the MEbrown, MEblue, and MEgreen modules were mainly involved in pyruvate metabolism, fibrinolysis, and complement activation pathways (Fig. 4B).

B, enrichment pathway of different modules. C, volcano plot showing the correlation between pretreatment serum total PSA (tPSA) and protein abundance in PCa plasma samples. D, pathways enriched for plasma proteins correlated with tPSA. E, heatmap showing the proteins that were significantly correlated with tPSA.

The early recognition of PCa and the prediction of tumor development following treatment mainly relied on changes of PSA level (54). High levels of pretreatment serum total PSA (tPSA) were associated with poor BCR-free survival (log rank test, p-value <0.05, Supplemental Fig. S2B). To evaluate if the PSA was associated with other plasma proteins, we employed a correlation analysis of the plasma proteome. The results showed that 93 proteins exhibited a significantly positive correlation with pretreatment serum PSA concentration, while 92 proteins showed a significantly negative correlation (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Table S4). We observed the KLK3 (PSA) quantified by plasma proteome profiling were the proteins with the strongest correlation with pretreatment serum PSA concentration, which demonstrated the reliability of the plasma proteomic data (Fig. 4C). Besides, we found that the alcohol dehydrogenase family (ADH1B, ADH1C, and ADH1A), which is involved in pyruvate metabolism, exhibited the strongest positive correlation with PSA. High expression levels of these proteins were associated with poor BCR-free survival (log rank test, p-value <0.05, Supplemental Fig. S2C). Genetic polymorphisms of alcohol dehydrogenases have been reported involving in the development and progression of many cancers, such as gastric cancer (55), esophageal cancer (56), and pancreatic cancer (57). Further pathway enrichment analysis showed that the proteins significantly positively correlated with PSA were mainly associated with the acute-phase response, fibrinolysis, and pyruvate metabolism pathways (ADH1B, MDH2, ADH1A, etc.), whereas the proteins significantly negatively correlated with PSA were enriched in immunoglobulin production and immune response pathways (IGKV3-7, IPO7, CHID1, etc.) (Fig. 4, D–E, Supplemental Table S5). Therefore, the PSA correlated with proteins annotated for pyruvate metabolism and immune response in the correlation map, highlighting the importance of metabolic reprogramming and immune microenvironment during the process of tumor transformation.

Plasma Proteomic Subtypes of PCa and Their Association with Distinct Clinical Outcomes

To understand the plasma proteomic patterns of PCa, we performed consensus clustering analysis on 222 plasma samples from PCa patients and identified three distinct subtypes: PCa-I, PCa-II, and PCa-III (Figs. 5A and S3A; Methods). Patients in PCa -I had the best BCR-free survival, whereas patients in PCa -III had the worst BCR-free survival (Fig. 5B, Supplemental Table S5). Further analysis within early stage (ISUP grade 1–3) patients among the three clusters showed that proteomic subtype was significantly correlated with BCR-free survival (log rank test, p-value = 0.031), indicating the prognostic power of proteomic clustering (Fig. 5C).

Comparative analysis of proteomic profiles resulted in 392 (PCa-I), 201 (PCa-II), and 272 (PCa-III) differentially expressed proteins among the three proteomic subtypes (Supplemental Table S5). Further pathway enrichment analysis showed that subtypes PCa-I was characterized by the highest level of innate immune response, fructose 6-phosphate metabolic process, and glycolytic process pathways (Fig. 5D). Subtypes PCa-II was more related to adherens/focal junction (FYN, FLNB, WASF2, etc.) and glutathione metabolism pathways (FYN, FLNB, WASF2, GSTM4, GSTM2, etc.) (Fig. 5E). Subtypes PCa-III was characterized by pathways, such as those pertaining to citrate cycle and fatty acid metabolic, ECM-receptor interaction, and translation pathway (SDC4, LAMC1, RPL15, RPS15, DARS1, etc.) (Fig. 5, E–F). The secretion of citrate is a crucial function of the normal prostate, where a high rate of glycolysis meets energy demands and compensates for the inhibition of the citrate cycle associated with citrate production. Interestingly, we observed that the elevated glycolysis pathway was associated with increased BCR-free survival, while an elevated citrate cycle and fatty acid metabolic pathway were associated with decreased BCR-free survival (Fig. 5, G–H and S3B). These observations suggest that increased citrate cycle and lipogenesis could contribute to the energy requirements of tumors and promote malignant growth in prostate cancer.

Recurrence Prediction Model of Patients With PCa identified by Machine Learning

To define a biochemical recurrence prediction model for predicting the progression of patients with PCa, we evaluated the correlation between BCR and protein expression level. The results showed that 111 proteins exhibited a significantly positive correlation with BCR, while 149 proteins showed a significantly negative correlation (Fig. 6A; Supplemental Table S6). Proteins showing a significantly positive correlation with BCR were mainly associated with the valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, fructose and mannose metabolism, and proteasome pathways (PSMA5, PSMA8, HADHA, ALDH6A1, ALDOA, HK1, etc.) (Fig. 6, B–C). In contrast, the proteins that were significantly negatively correlated with BCR were enriched in pathways related to extracellular matrix disassembly, translational elongation, and negative regulation of the apoptotic process (Fig. 6, B–C).

To further screen the prognosis related proteins, we intersected the results from the correlation analysis and the Cox proportional hazards model analysis based on the BCR to reveal that 37 proteins overlapped (Fig. 6D). Then, we applied feature selection (lasso [least absolute shrinkage and selection operator]) with the 37 overlap proteins to build a linear regression model that included 17 featured proteins (Fig. 6, E–F; Methods; Supplemental Table S6). Subsequently, we established the 17-protein panel risk score by multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model including the 17 proteins screened by lasso regression (Fig. 6G, Methods). Based on the expression of these 17 proteins, we clustered patients with PCa into high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 6H). Patients in the high-risk group were significantly associated with poor BCR-free survival, while those in the low-risk group were associated with better BCR-free survival (log rank p-value <0.0001, Fig. 6H). The area under the curve values (AUCs) based on the discovery cohort for BCR prediction until 1, 3, and 5 years was 0.914, 0.829, and 0.843, respectively (Fig. 6I). Then, we integrated the 17 proteins as a biochemical recurrence prediction model to construct a nomogram (Supplemental Fig. S4A). Based on the calibrated plots, the predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities of the nomogram performed well in the discovery cohort, and the concordance index (C-index) of the nomogram was 0.82 (Supplemental Fig. S4B). Notably, the nomogram provides only the probability of biochemical recurrence at the time points of 1, 3, and 5 years after diagnosis, and does not predict the exact timing of recurrence. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) displayed net benefit of the nomogram showing that it had good clinical utility (Supplemental Fig. S4C).

We next compared the prognosis prediction performance of our biochemical recurrence prediction model with commonly used prediction methods based on clinical parameters, such as the ISUP grade, tPSA, tumor stage, and Gleason score. We found that this biochemical recurrence prediction model performed well in predicting BCR-free survival across different ISUP grades (Fig. 6, J and K). The AUCs of BCR-free prognosis prediction within 1-, 3-, and 5-years using the biochemical recurrence prediction model were significantly higher than using clinical parameters (Supplemental Fig. S4, A–G), indicating that the biochemical recurrence prediction model has a superior performance in prediction prognosis than the commonly employed methods based on clinical parameters. We further evaluated the accuracy of the biochemical recurrence prediction model in the independent cohort (n = 116) using PRM assays. We selected a set of target peptides that were unique to these proteins using the library search results (Methods; Supplemental Table S6). Based on the PRM quantification, the AUC was 0.823, 0.798, and 0.794 at 1-, 3-, and 5- years, respectively (Fig. 6L). Furthermore, we compared the performance of biochemical recurrence prediction models using our discovery cohort and the established models in Zhao et al. (58). For rigorous evaluation, we calculated time-dependent ROC curves at 3, and 5 years, and net reclassification improvement (NRI). The result showed that the biochemical recurrence prediction model consistently outperformed both comparator models at every timepoint. Specifically, for 3-years BCR-free survival, the biochemical recurrence prediction model signature achieved an AUC of 0.829, significantly higher than the RS model (AUC = 0.739) (Supplemental Fig. S4H). Similarly, at 5-year timepoints, our panel maintained superior discrimination (AUC = 0.843), whereas the RS model’s performance degraded over time (AUC = 0.729) (Supplemental Fig. S4I). These results establish the clinical superiority of our biochemical recurrence prediction model and support its potential integration into postoperative risk stratification protocols.

These results indicate that the biochemical recurrence prediction model could be a potential predictive model to predict the biochemical recurrence ribased on of a blood sample from a patient with PCa.

Discussion

Although the diagnosis of PCa has improved with routine serum PSA testing, as well as abnormal MRI findings and PET-CT scans, their ability to accurately predict PCa progression and risk stratification remains limited (59, 60, 61). The current investigation utilizing plasma proteomic profiling provides a promising avenue to identify minimally-invasive biomarkers that can enhance the prognostic stratification of patients, ultimately leading to more personalized treatment approaches (13, 14, 15). In this study, we investigated the plasma proteomic landscape of 222 PCa patients and 159 healthy controls, identifying proteins related to PCa development and progression. In addition, we established a biochemical recurrence prediction model that effectively predicts biochemical recurrence for patients with PCa and validated it by PRM assays in an independent cohort of 116 PCa patients.

In this study, a total of 8686 protein groups were identified in all plasma samples, with an average of 1779 and 1795 protein groups per PCa and normal plasma (healthy controls) samples, respectively. Plasma (or serum) will be the preferred specimen for minimally invasive sampling of normal individuals and patients for biomarkers discovered and validated to reflect physiological, pathological, and pharmacological phenotypes. As is well-recognized; however, plasma presents enormous challenges due to the extreme complexity reflecting potentially all proteins in the body and their isoforms, at least 10 orders of magnitude range in concentration, intra-individual and inter-individual variation, and non-standardized methods of sample processing (62). Additionally, MS-based plasma proteomics is extremely challenging for a number of reasons, most prominently the large dynamic range of protein abundances (62). For example, it is estimated to encompass over 10,000 proteins (63), with the Plasma Proteome Database, for instance, documenting 10,546 proteins detected in serum or plasma samples (46). Cunningham et al. identified a total of over 7000 human proteins in the plasma samples (64). Qu et al. identified a total of 9336 protein groups in all plasma samples, with an average of 1860 and 1789 protein groups per UTUC and normal plasma (healthy control) samples, respectively (13). Yin et al. quantified an average of 2220 (group 1) and 1926 (group 2) proteins per plasma sample, and a total of 8624 and 7049 proteins were identified in groups 1 and 2, respectively (48). This ∼20% coverage gap between aggregate and individual sample depth strikingly illustrates the “potential versus realized” proteome in routine clinical applications. Collectively, these findings illustrate that the plasma proteome is inherently a complex and diverse system, with over 10,000 proteins identified to date. Advances in mass spectrometry have facilitated increasingly comprehensive coverage, such that single-run identifications now routinely exceed 2000 proteins in well-optimized workflows. This context reflects both the intrinsic complexity of the plasma proteome and the capabilities of modern analytical platforms. This context reflects both the intrinsic complexity of the plasma proteome and the capabilities of modern analytical platforms, with cohort heterogeneity ultimately determining the practical limits of coverage.

Comprehensive analysis of plasma samples from patients with PCa and healthy controls identified the downregulation of glycolysis-related proteins and upregulation of fatty acid degradation and oxidative phosphorylation in PCa. These findings were consistent with previous studies on tissue, which indicated that primary tumor tissue favors enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and lipogenesis, while glycolysis is limited (65, 66). Our investigation also discovered several distinct pathways enriched in PCa plasma samples, including rap1 signaling pathway, regulation of cell migration, and translational initiation. In addition, we mapped the proteomic characteristics associated with different tumor stages, ISUP groups, pretreatment serum PSA concentrations and other clinical indicators. We observed that as the ISUP grade increases, the immune response signals and glycometabolism signals in the plasma of patients with PCa gradually diminish, while signals related to DNA damage response, acute phase response, and amino acid metabolism progressively intensify. These results might underscore the tumor’s adaptive mechanisms and the metabolic changes that accompany the progression of malignancy. Interestingly, we identified a cluster of the following three proteins, ADH1B, ADH1C, and ADH1A, which showed the highest positively correlation with the pretreatment serum total PSA, in addition to KLK3 (PSA) quantified by plasma proteome profiling. All of these proteins belong to the alcohol dehydrogenase family, and high expression levels of these proteins were associated with a poor prognosis in our study. It has been reported that alcohol dehydrogenases were associated with the development and progression of gastric cancers and esophageal cancer (55, 56). Circulating levels of ADH1B, ADH5, and ADH6 were significantly higher in PCa samples. Whether this reflects altered hepatic secretion, changes in plasma stability, or secondary systemic effects remains to be determined; these data do not establish a causal role for ADH proteins in prostate cancer progression.

Molecular subtyping is a crucial strategy in addressing the heterogeneity of prostate cancer (67). Previous classifications of prostate cancer were mostly based on tissue samples, integrating information from genomics, transcriptomics, and other parameters for classification (23, 68, 69). We utilized plasma proteomic data for molecular subtyping and identified three distinct proteomic subtypes (PCa-I, PCa-II, and PCa-III) associated with varying clinical prognoses and molecular signatures. Specifically, PCa-I tumors showed high level of immune response and glycometabolism, indicative of a more favorable prognosis. PCa-II tumors showed high level of focal adhesion and glutathione metabolism. PCa-III presented the worst prognosis and highest fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation and citrate cycle. It has been reported that high level TCA cycle and oxidative metabolism promotes multiple cancer progression, such as kidney cancer (70), breast cancer (71), and melanoma (72). We found that the elevated glycolysis was a favorable prognostic signature, elevated citrate cycle and fatty acid metabolic pathway signaling both were associated with decreased survival. Nguyen et al. reported that fatty acid metabolism was related to the risk of prostate cancer or PCa-specific mortality (73). Notably, the proteomic subtype was significantly correlated with clinical prognoses, even in patients with early-stage prostate cancer, indicating the prognostic power of proteomic clustering. In our study, the plasma proteomic subtypes were linked to the prognosis of PCa, which could inform clinicians the molecular features for PCa with low or high BCR risk, and could serve as a foundation for future studies aimed at minimizing over- and undertreatment (74).

Disease progression in PCa is a multifaceted and prolonged process, often characterized by substantial biological variability among patients. Accurate prediction of disease progression not only aids in treatment planning but also enhances patient management (75, 76). Zhong et al. developed an 18-protein panel for prognostic prediction using proteomic data from prostate cancer tissue samples (77). Sun et al. identified a 16-protein panel that predicts biochemical recurrence in patients with PCa through proteomic profiling of tissue specimens (76). However, these studies relied on tissue samples, which are invasive and do not reflect real-time changes in biomarker expression. In this study, we utilized plasma proteomic technologies in a noninvasive and comprehensive manner to develop a biochemical recurrence prediction model for predicting BCR. The biochemical recurrence prediction model demonstrated superior BCR prediction performance over other commonly employed prediction system based on the PSA level, ISUP grade, Gleason score, and tumor stage. In addition, the biochemical recurrence prediction model performed well in predicting BCR in both early-stage and late-stage patients with PCa. The verification of this panel through PRM assays further underscores its robustness and applicability across diverse patient cohorts, reaffirming its clinical relevance. Thus, taken together, proteomic profiling of human plasma affords an attractive landscape to facilitate personalized medicine, allowing for more tailored treatment plans based on individual risk profiles (48, 78).

Recent advances in plasma-based proteomics have enabled robust biomarker discovery for prostate cancer prognosis. Sun et al. recently established a 16-protein panel for predicting biochemical recurrence using proteomic profiling of tissue specimens (76). Their analytical workflow, employing DIA-MS coupled with machine learning-based feature selection, shares methodological similarities with our approach. While both studies identified compact protein signatures with strong prognostic power, our plasma-based 17-protein panel offers the advantage of minimal invasiveness and real-time monitoring capability, complementing tissue-based prognostic models. The convergence of findings from independent cohorts—demonstrating that proteomic signatures outperform traditional clinical parameters such as ISUP grade and Gleason score—underscores the clinical utility of integrating protein-level information into postoperative risk stratification frameworks. Future multi-center prospective studies incorporating diverse ethnic populations will be essential to validate these complementary proteomic classifiers and establish standardized protocols for clinical implementation.

The primary goal of this study was to identify biomolecules associated with the molecular landscape of prostate cancer at the time of diagnosis, not to evaluate post-surgical treatment response or long-term survival. We therefore deliberately enrolled treatment-naïve patients who had not received neoadjuvant or pre-operative therapy, thereby minimized therapeutic interference and maximized our ability to capture the native proteomic signature of the untreated tumor. The retrospective design focusing on treatment-naïve patients represents a validated and widely adopted framework for capturing the native proteomic landscape without therapeutic confounding (2, 79). For the discovery-phase objective of the current study, namely to identify diagnostic biomolecules rather than evaluate post-treatment outcomes, the retrospective, treatment-naïve cohort design is not only appropriate but essential, and its scientific validity is firmly established by precedent in the field. While we recognize that post-surgical outcomes are multifactorial and heavily influenced by pathological staging and adjuvant interventions, emerging evidence suggests that the molecular composition of the primary tumor at diagnosis can predispose to adverse pathological events (e.g., upgrading, upstaging, or early biochemical recurrence). By capturing the baseline proteomic landscape, our study provides a molecular baseline against which future progression events can be measured. The findings of this study provide an important molecular foundation for future development of proteomics-based diagnostic tools and prognostic risk stratification strategies.

In disease biomarker research, the origin of plasma proteins exhibits considerable heterogeneity. It must be acknowledged that protein components in the circulatory system are not secreted in an organ-specific manner; rather, their composition encompasses contributions from multiple tissues. For instance, in infectious disease diagnosis, clinical indicators often detect not the pathogen itself but acute-phase response proteins such as albumin. Although these molecules do not originate from the site of infection, they sensitively reflect the organism’s pathophysiological status. Similarly, while the tumor microenvironment represents the disease origin, differentially expressed proteins detected in plasma may arise from: direct secretion by tumor tissues; systemic responses to tumorigenesis (e.g., immune activation, metabolic reprogramming); or even unrelated bystander effects (14, 80). Therefore, the concept that biomarkers do not exclusively originate from the target organ constitutes a fundamental understanding in the field.

This “non-organ-specific” characteristic equally applies to pathway analysis. The plasma proteome reflects a collective representation of systemic protein homeostasis, and alterations in its composition cannot be directly causally mapped to specific signaling pathway activation in a single organ. Nevertheless, the indirect inference strategy has been traditionally employed in the proteomics field: analyzing plasma proteome differences between disease and healthy groups, followed by functional enrichment using tools such as DAVID, GSEA, and Ingenuity Pathway Analysis to infer potentially activated biological pathways. Although this approach is correlative, it is widely accepted as an effective method for understanding disease mechanisms. Examples include: In Huang et al.’s study of Alzheimer’s disease, plasma proteomic differential analysis revealed enrichment of acute-phase response signaling, complement/coagulation cascades, and LXR/RXR lipid metabolism pathways—findings that align with neuroinflammatory pathophysiology despite proteins being predominantly contributed by the liver and vasculature, reflecting systemic rather than brain-specific origin (81); In Sun et al.’s study of cardiovascular disease, genome-wide plasma proteome network analysis of 3301 individuals revealed activation of IL-6/JAK/STAT inflammatory and lipid transport pathways—findings that align with cardiovascular pathophysiology despite these systemic protein alterations originating primarily from hepatic and vascular sources rather than target organs (heart and coronary arteries), reflecting systemic rather than organ-specific origin (82); In colorectal cancer research, Li et al. utilized longitudinal plasma proteomics to uncover diverse biomarkers related to diagnosis and cetuximab therapy response (14). While these systemic protein changes were mainly derived from host-tumor interactions rather than tumor cells themselves, this approach successfully identified potential biomarkers that reflect tumor-immune microenvironment dynamics. Thus, although plasma proteome alterations are systemically derived, they represent functionally relevant readouts of disease-specific pathway activation, providing mechanistic insights that are both biologically plausible and therapeutically actionable.

Based on this, the present study follows this established methodological framework: pathway enrichment analysis was performed on plasma proteome differences between prostate cancer patients and healthy controls to infer disease-associated biological processes. However, we must prudently emphasize its inherent limitation: pathway changes observed in plasma may not represent functional activation but rather statistical aggregation from multiple tissue contributions.

In conclusion, our study captured the clinically relevant changes in the plasma proteome of prostate cancer patients that correlate with disease manifestations and clinical outcomes. The establishment of a biochemical recurrence prediction model based on plasma proteomic represents a promising strategy for enhancing prognostic accuracy and patient management in prostate cancer. Our study not only generated a high-quality data resource that may benefit basic research but also provided additional biological insights underlying clinical features of prostate cancer.

Key Limitation

All 381 plasma samples were collected at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center between 2018 and 2022. The retrospective nature of the study restricts our ability to control for certain confounding variables and may affect the reliability of causal inferences. Moreover, the exclusive inclusion of Chinese patients raises concerns about the applicability of the identified signature proteins to other ethnic populations, as genetic background, environmental exposures, and tumor biology may vary significantly across different racial and geographic groups. Therefore, well-designed, multicenter, prospective studies involving diverse ethnic cohorts are essential to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of the proposed protein signature in a broader clinical context. Furthermore, the biochemical recurrence prediction model developed in this study, which comprises a 17-protein classifier, represents a promising but preliminary tool for predicting the recurrence rate of PCa. However, its clinical utility and mechanistic basis remain to be fully established. Future confirmatory trials with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and standardized protocols are necessary to evaluate the reproducibility and reliability of this classifier. These studies should also aim to uncover the underlying biological mechanisms driving PCa recurrence, which could provide critical insights into disease pathogenesis and reveal novel therapeutic targets. Furthermore, as plasma is a systemic biofluid and not directly collected from tumor tissue, the observed protein alterations may reflect systemic responses, contributions from other organs (including metastatic lesions), or non-tumor-specific pathological processes, rather than being exclusively driven by the primary prostate tumor. This cell-free nature of plasma precludes definitive determination of whether changes originate from prostate tissue, metastatic sites, or other physiological responses, making any inference about tissue-specific biology or functional contributions to PCa progression speculative. Thus, any inference about tissue-specific biology or functional contribution to PCa progression would be speculative.
