Diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in intradiverticular bladder carcinoma: A case report
Authors: Anvitha Manoj, Shuchismita Chakraborty, Akshay Kantha, Manoj Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intradiverticular bladder tumors (IDBT), rare at ∼1% of bladder cancers, present diagnostic difficulties due to absent muscularis propria, leading to frequent biopsy failures and staging errors. A 50-year-old man with gross hematuria had multiple negative cystoscopic biopsies, CT-guided biopsy, and cytology presented to oncology out patient. CT/PET-CT suggested an FDG-avid mass near the vesicoureteric junction, but MRI precisely located it in a diverticulum. Despite absent tissue diagnosis, partial cystectomy, diverticulectomy, distal ureterectomy, and psoas-hitch ureteric reimplantation were performed. Histopathology confirmed pTa high-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma. The patient is disease-free post-BCG highlighting that MRI-supported suspicion should prevail over negative histology in IDBT.

Introduction

Intradiverticular bladder tumors (IDBT) represent approximately 1% of all bladder malignancies, though their true incidence may be as high as 23.4% in asymptomatic populations.1,2 These tumors arise within bladder diverticula, an outpouchings of mucosa, through weakened or absent muscular layers. The unique anatomy of the diverticulum, specifically the absence of muscularis propria, complicates staging, often causing tumors to skip the T2 stage and progress directly to T3.3 Furthermore, a hypertrophied layer of muscularis mucosae can confound both imaging and pathological diagnosis.4,5 We present a unique case that exemplifies these diagnostic hurdles and highlights the critical role of MRI in establishing a diagnosis when histology remains elusive.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old male presented with a one-year history of painless intermittent gross hematuria and a single episode of urinary retention. He had no significant comorbidities. Previous evaluations, including ultrasound, had suggested only cystitis and grade 1 prostatomegaly. Physical examination was largely unremarkable except for pallor.

The patient underwent an extensive diagnostic workup characterized by repeated failures to obtain a tissue diagnosis. Initial cystoscopy identified a 2.5 × 1.5 cm polypoidal growth obliterating the left ureteric orifice, but two subsequent cystoscopic biopsies were negative for malignancy. Similarly, a CT-guided biopsy of an extravesical component and urine cytology both yielded inconclusive or negative results.

Multimodal imaging provided the primary evidence for malignancy. CT intravenous urogram demonstrated a mildly enhancing lesion at the left vesicoureteric junction with an extravesical solid component (6.9 × 7.5 cm) (Fig. 1). PET-CT scan revealed an FDG-avid mass (SUV max 11.8) causing significant distal ureteric occlusion. Despite four failed tissue sampling attempts, the persistence of gross hematuria and highly suspicious radiological findings prompted the decision for surgical exploration. Intraoperatively, a bladder diverticulum was confirmed near the left vesicoureteric junction (Fig. 3). Gross examination revealed a 5.5 × 4 × 3.7 cm friable polypoidal mass within the diverticular cavity. Microscopic examination confirmed non-invasive, high-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma with negative surgical margins and four negative lymph nodes. The final pathological stage was pTa N0, categorized as high-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). The patient's recovery was uneventful, and he subsequently received adjuvant intravesical BCG therapy. He remains disease-free under close surveillance.

Discussion

IDBTs present unique challenges that distinguish them from conventional bladder cancers. They typically occur in older males with bladder outlet obstruction, where urinary stasis may lead to chronic irritation and malignant transformation 6, 7, 8.

The primary challenge in this case was the anatomical proximity of the tumor to the vesicoureteric junction, which created imaging overlap with ureteric carcinoma. The diverticulum's location at the ureteric hiatus is characteristic of a Hutch diverticulum, a congenital out-pouching that occurs due to a defect in the detrusor muscle near the ureteric entry point. This specific anatomical variant explains the early involvement of the distal ureter and the subsequent necessity for ureteric reimplantation. While CT is the standard for urothelial malignancies, its sensitivity in IDBT is limited by contrast flow into the diverticulum.1,9 MRI proved superior for soft tissue characterization and anatomical localization.10

The repeated failure of biopsies is a well-documented phenomenon in IDBT and is attributed to limited endoscopic access, acute angulation of the diverticular neck, and the risk of perforation due to the absent muscularis propria.1 This case justifies the principle that strong clinical and radiological evidence may supersede negative histology when repeated sampling is inconclusive.

Preoperative imaging overestimated the disease stage as muscle-invasive, a common occurrence in IDBT because imaging can interpret inflammatory changes or the hypertrophic muscularis mucosae as evidence of invasion. Because these tumors lack a muscularis propria, they progress directly from Ta/T1 to T3.1 Surgical management must therefore be comprehensive; our use of the psoas hitch technique allowed for complete tumor excision while preserving renal function through a single-stage reconstruction.

Conclusions

Intradiverticular bladder tumors require a high index of clinical suspicion and a multimodal imaging approach, with MRI playing a pivotal role. Repeated biopsy failures should not deter definitive surgical management when clinical and radiological signs of malignancy are present. As demonstrated in this case, a comprehensive surgical approach can yield excellent oncological outcomes and organ preservation even in the absence of preoperative histological confirmation.
