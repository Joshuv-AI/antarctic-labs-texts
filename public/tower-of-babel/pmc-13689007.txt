Barriers and facilitators to PrEP knowledge, acceptability, uptake, and adherence among criminal-justice involved cisgender women: a systematic review
Authors: Isaiah Omari, Dorice Vieira, Danielle Ompad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCisgender women involved in the U.S. criminal justice system (CJI) experience heightened HIV vulnerability due to intersecting structural risks such as substance use, unstable housing, intimate partner violence, and limited healthcare access. Despite the effectiveness of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), uptake remains low in this population. This systematic review synthesized evidence on barriers and facilitators influencing PrEP knowledge, acceptability, initiation, uptake, and adherence among CJI cisgender women and examined how racial and ethnic disparities were addressed within the literature.DesignSystematic review of empirical studies examining PrEP-related outcomes among cisgender women involved in the U.S. criminal justice system.MethodsPubMed, PsycINFO, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Google Scholar, PAIS, EBSCO, and Criminal Justice Abstracts were searched for studies published between January 2013 and July 2025. Screening and study selection followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Data extraction and methodological quality appraisal were conducted using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Eligible studies examined one or more PrEP-related outcomes, including knowledge, acceptability, initiation, uptake, adherence, or barriers and facilitators to engagement.ResultsOut of 1,728 records identified, 568 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,160 unique records for screening. Following title/abstract review, 86 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 12 studies met inclusion criteria (qualitative, n = 8; quantitative, n = 3; mixed-methods, n = 1). Findings demonstrated consistently low PrEP knowledge among CJI cisgender women, particularly among Black and Latina women, due to limited targeted education, misinformation, and gaps in provider communication. Acceptability of PrEP was generally high when information was delivered through trusted healthcare providers, peer networks, or community-based programs. However, structural barriers, including stigma, healthcare mistrust, fragmented correctional healthcare systems, unstable housing, insurance discontinuity, and limited post-release care coordination, substantially restricted PrEP uptake and adherence. Facilitators included peer support, transitional care programs, community outreach, culturally tailored education, and access to public insurance and integrated healthcare services. Few studies explicitly centered racial and ethnic inequities despite the disproportionate HIV burden experienced by Black women within criminal justice settings.ConclusionCJI cisgender women remain substantially underserved within the U.S. PrEP implementation landscape. Findings highlight the importance of multilevel, equity-informed interventions that address structural barriers, strengthen continuity of care across incarceration and reentry settings, and incorporate culturally responsive, peer-driven, and trauma-informed approaches. Greater attention to structural, racial and ethnic disparities in future research and intervention design is essential to advancing HIV prevention equity among CJI cisgender women.Trial registrationThis review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251086367).Keywords: Pre-exposure prophylaxis, HIV prevention, Cisgender women, Criminal justice involvement, PrEP uptake, Knowledge, Acceptability, Awareness, Incarceration, Barriers and facilitators

Introduction

Cisgender women involved in the criminal justice system (CJI) in the United States experience disproportionately elevated vulnerability to HIV infection due to intersecting structural, behavioral, and social inequities [1]. These vulnerabilities include high rates of substance use disorders, unstable housing, limited access to healthcare, exposure to intimate partner violence, trauma, and persistent socioeconomic instability [2, 3]. Involvement in the criminal justice system may further exacerbate these risks through fragmented healthcare delivery, inadequate HIV prevention services, interruptions in continuity of care, and limited linkage to community-based services following release from incarceration [4, 5]. Collectively, these factors contribute to substantial disparities in HIV risk and prevention access among CJI cisgender women. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), a highly effective biomedical HIV prevention strategy that can reduce HIV acquisition by more than 90% when taken consistently, has transformed HIV prevention efforts in the United States [6]. Despite its demonstrated efficacy, PrEP utilization among CJI cisgender women remains disproportionately low [7]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have identified cisgender women, particularly Black and Latina women, as priority populations for HIV prevention and PrEP outreach due to ongoing epidemiologic disparities [8]. However, much of the PrEP literature, implementation science research, and public health programming has historically focused on populations such as gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender women, and people who inject drugs (PWID) [9]. As a result, CJI cisgender women have remained underrepresented within both HIV prevention research and PrEP implementation initiatives [10, 11]. This gap is particularly concerning given the unique barriers that CJI cisgender women face across the PrEP prevention continuum, including knowledge, acceptability, initiation, uptake, and adherence. Existing evidence suggests that barriers to PrEP engagement among CJI cisgender women operate across multiple levels, including individual, interpersonal, institutional, and structural domains [12]. Commonly reported barriers include limited awareness and knowledge of PrEP, HIV-related stigma, incarceration-related stigma, mistrust of healthcare systems, unstable housing, competing survival priorities, substance use, mental health challenges, and fragmented continuity of care during incarceration and reentry transitions [2, 13]. These barriers are often compounded by structural racism, gendered inequities, and healthcare marginalization, particularly among Black and Latina women who are disproportionately represented within the criminal justice system and continue to experience inequitable access to HIV prevention services [14, 15]. At the same time, emerging evidence suggests that several facilitators may improve PrEP engagement among CJI cisgender women. Qualitative and implementation-focused studies have identified peer-based education, community outreach, culturally responsive interventions, transitional care coordination, integration of PrEP services within correctional healthcare and substance use treatment settings, and eHealth-based interventions as promising approaches to support PrEP uptake and continuity of care [16]. Existing research suggests that willingness to engage with PrEP is influenced not only by individual perceptions of HIV risk, but also by broader institutional and systemic factors embedded within correctional and public health systems [17]. Additional qualitative evidence has emphasized the importance of initiating PrEP during incarceration to strengthen linkage to care and improve continuity following release [18]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of multilevel interventions that address both structural and interpersonal determinants of HIV prevention engagement. Although literature examining PrEP among justice-involved populations has expanded in recent years, important gaps remain. Existing studies vary considerably in design, setting, and outcome measurement, and relatively few reviews have synthesized evidence specifically focused on cisgender women involved in the criminal justice system. Furthermore, limited attention has been given to how racial and ethnic disparities are conceptualized, reported, or addressed within the literature despite the disproportionate HIV burden experienced by Black women in correctional and community supervision settings [19]. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to identify, synthesize, and evaluate the existing evidence on barriers and facilitators to PrEP continuum; knowledge, awareness, acceptability, initiation, uptake, and adherence among cisgender women involved in the U.S. criminal justice system. Specifically, this review sought to address the following questions: (1) What individual, social, and structural barriers impede PrEP-related knowledge, acceptability, uptake, or adherence among CJI cisgender women? (2) What facilitators have been identified that enhance engagement across the PrEP continuum? (3) What interventions or implementation strategies have been proposed or evaluated to support PrEP utilization in this population? and (4) How do justice-system contexts and reentry, influence access to and utilization of PrEP? A secondary exploratory objective was to examine how racial and ethnic disparities, structural inequities, and equity-related considerations were represented within the literature and to consider their implications for equity-informed HIV prevention practice and policy. By centering the experiences of CJI cisgender women, this review seeks to inform the development of gender-responsive, culturally grounded, and justice-centered HIV prevention interventions. Given the complex social, structural, and justice-system factors shaping HIV prevention in this population, the review also explored how barriers and facilitators operate across multiple socio-ecological levels.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [20]. The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration No. CRD420251086367). Supplementary protocol materials and study documentation were additionally archived through the Open Science Framework (OSF) to support methodological transparency and reproducibility. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist and flow diagram are provided as supplementary materials. The full protocol can be accessed at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?ID=CRD420251086367.

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted across major electronic databases: PubMed, APA PsycINFO, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Google Scholar, PAIS, EBSCO, and Criminal Justice Abstract. The search aimed to identify empirical studies published between January 2013, marking the year the FDA first approved pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for HIV prevention and July 2025. The search was concluded in July 2025, which corresponds to the final date on which all databases were systematically queried. This cutoff ensures that the review includes the most recent peer‑reviewed evidence available at the time of analysis. Given the limited but growing body of research on PrEP awareness and uptake among cisgender incarcerated women, the July 2025 end date balances the need for currency with the feasibility of completing the review in a timely manner. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to three core concepts: (1) PrEP or HIV prevention (e.g., “pre-exposure prophylaxis,” “HIV prevention”), (2) cisgender women or female-identifying individuals (e.g., “cisgender women,” “female”), and (3) criminal justice involvement (e.g., “incarceration,” “probation,” “criminal justice,” “jail,” “prison”). Boolean operators (incarcerat* OR prison* OR penitentiar* OR jail* OR correction facilit*) AND (female OR women OR woman OR cisgender) AND (barrier* OR facilitator* OR outcome*) AND (PrEP [tw] OR pre-exposure prophylaxis [mesh] OR pre-exposure prophylaxis [tw] OR pre-exposure prophylaxi [tw] OR primary prevention [tw] OR primary prevention [mesh]) (Table 1) were used to refine and combine the terms within and across these conceptual categories to ensure sensitivity and specificity. Truncation and wildcard characters were employed where applicable to capture multiple term variations. Search results were downloaded into EndNote, and duplicate entries were removed prior to screening (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To ensure relevance and methodological rigor, studies were included in the review if they met all of the following criteria: they focused specifically on cisgender women involved in the U.S. criminal justice system including, but not limited to, individuals who were currently or formerly incarcerated, on probation, or on parole; they reported empirical data, whether quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods, on PrEP-related outcomes such as knowledge, acceptability, uptake, or adherence; and they were peer-reviewed articles. There were no language restrictions. Studies were excluded if they exclusively examined male or transgender populations, failed to disaggregate findings by gender or criminal justice involvement, or did not provide primary data (i.e., commentaries, editorials, abstract, dissertations, theses or literature reviews). This inclusion strategy was designed to center the experiences of cisgender women whose interactions with the criminal justice system may present unique vulnerabilities and barriers to PrEP engagement. In addition to studies focused exclusively on women, we included research involving mixed-gender samples when findings were disaggregated and applicable to cisgender women’s experiences.

Screening and data extraction

Titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were screened independently by two reviewers using Covidence, a software platform designed to streamline the systematic review process. All articles deemed potentially relevant were retrieved and subjected to full-text review. Disagreements at any stage of the review process were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Data extraction was conducted using a standardized and pilot-tested extraction form. The form captured study characteristics such as author(s), publication year, geographic location, sample size, study design, and population demographics. Key outcomes of interest included PrEP knowledge, acceptability, uptake, and adherence. Reported barriers and facilitators to PrEP utilization were also documented. When studies reported effect sizes, odds ratios (ORs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), these data were extracted to support quantitative comparisons.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using design-appropriate critical appraisal tools. Qualitative studies were appraised using a checklist based on the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) [21] framework, covering study aims, methodological appropriateness, design rigor, recruitment strategy, data adequacy, reflexivity, ethical considerations, analytic rigor, and clarity of findings (Table 2). Each domain was rated as “Yes,” “Somewhat,” or “No,” and studies were assigned an overall risk of bias rating (low, moderate, or high).

Risk of bias assessment

Quantitative observational studies were assessed using a structured risk-of-bias tool evaluating clarity of objectives, population definition, measurement validity, follow-up adequacy, and confounding (Table 2). Intervention studies were appraised using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Controlled Intervention Studies, focusing on non-randomization, blinding, outcome completeness, and selective reporting (Table 2) [27].

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT, 2018) [27, 28] was used as a guiding framework for mixed-methods and non-randomized study designs; however, study-level assessments were conducted primarily using CASP and NIH tools aligned to study design. Meyer et al. (2022) and Murphy et al. (2024), as non-randomized intervention/implementation studies, were appraised using the NIH tool and relevant non-randomized criteria rather than randomized controlled trial specific instruments. Quality ratings were used to contextualize findings and were not applied as exclusion criteria.

Data synthesis

Due to heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome measurements, a narrative synthesis approach was employed. This method allowed for thematic categorization and comparison of findings across diverse methodological traditions. Studies were grouped according to the four domains of the PrEP continuum: knowledge, acceptability, uptake, and adherence. Within each domain, common barriers and facilitators were identified and synthesized. Where possible, data were stratified by race/ethnicity and other sociodemographic factors to explore disparities and intersectional vulnerabilities. Quantitative findings such as odds ratios and confidence intervals were reported when available to underscore statistically significant associations. The narrative synthesis was supplemented by summary tables to enhance clarity and accessibility of findings across studies. This methodology provides a rigorous foundation for identifying gaps in the literature and informing interventions aimed at increasing PrEP engagement among CJI cisgender women in the US. Across the PrEP continuum, barriers were cumulative and interconnected, with deficits in awareness, structural instability, stigma, and healthcare fragmentation reinforcing one another across knowledge, acceptability, initiation, and adherence.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,728 records were identified through database searching. After removal of 568 duplicates, 1,160 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. Following screening, 1,074 records were excluded based on irrelevance to the review objectives, non-U.S. study populations, lack of PrEP-specific outcomes, absence of criminal justice involvement, or failure to disaggregate findings for cisgender women. Eighty-six full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 74 were excluded for reasons including non-empirical study design, ineligible populations, insufficient reporting of PrEP-related outcomes, or inability to isolate findings specific to cisgender women involved in the criminal justice system. Twelve studies met inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (Table 3).

Characteristics of included studies

The 12 studies included in this review utilized various methodological approaches: eight were qualitative [3, 5, 10, 17, 18, 22–24], three were quantitative [25, 26, 29], and one was a mixed-methods study [4]. The study settings encompassed correctional facilities, jails, prisons, community-based organizations, addiction treatment programs, transitional reentry programs, and digital health intervention platforms across multiple regions in the United States [3–5, 10, 17, 18, 22–26, 29]. Sample sizes ranged from 13 to 814 participants. One qualitative study involved focus groups with 20 cisgender women in the Southeastern United States, examining how social relationships influenced attitudes toward PrEP [3]. Another study extended this inquiry through interviews with 32 women and members of their social networks, exploring how interpersonal dynamics affected PrEP decision-making [23]. Additional qualitative research included interviews with 35 stakeholders and justice-involved women in Rhode Island, assessing mechanisms for connecting incarcerated women to community-based PrEP care after their release [18], as well as interviews with jail detainees and health professionals in Arkansas to identify barriers to accessing care [5]. In another study, formerly incarcerated individuals expressed a strong preference for receiving PrEP education and initiation while still incarcerated to ensure continuity of care after their release [22]. Furthermore, one study explored multilevel barriers and facilitators affecting PrEP acceptability among CJI cisgender women [17]. Quantitative and implementation‑focused studies examined PrEP awareness, willingness, uptake, and structural barriers to engagement. One cross‑sectional survey assessed PrEP‑related knowledge and willingness among 173 CJI cisgender women [25]. Another survey evaluated PrEP awareness and interest among 39 women enrolled in addiction treatment programs [10]. A separate study assessed HIV risk perception and PrEP eligibility among 125 justice‑involved women [29]. Implementation research also evaluated the feasibility of an eHealth and outreach‑based intervention designed to increase PrEP uptake among women at elevated HIV risk [4]. A statewide correctional PrEP program involving 814 participants further examined implementation feasibility and continuity of care within correctional systems [30]. Across studies, participants commonly experienced overlapping structural vulnerabilities, including unstable housing, substance use, poverty, trauma exposure, intimate partner violence, limited healthcare access, and disruptions in continuity of care following release from incarceration [17, 24]. Few studies reported findings stratified by race and ethnicity, particularly among Black and Latina women, and others discussed structural inequities more broadly without subgroup‑specific analyses [10, 17, 18, 24, 30] (Table 4).

What barriers impede PrEP knowledge, acceptability, uptake, or adherence among criminal justice-involved cisgender women?

Limited PrEP knowledge and awareness

Across studies, PrEP awareness among CJI cisgender women was consistently low. Multiple studies reported that many participants had never previously heard of PrEP prior to study enrollment or educational intervention exposure [5, 17, 24, 29]. Limited awareness was particularly evident among women transitioning from incarceration to community settings, where access to preventive health information was fragmented or absent [31]. In one study, only 16% of justice-involved women reported prior awareness of PrEP, with awareness levels significantly lower among Black women compared with White participants [17]. Similarly one study found that only 23% of women enrolled in addiction treatment programs had heard of PrEP despite elevated HIV risk profiles [10]. Further, another study reported that fewer than one in five Latina participants had prior knowledge of PrEP before study enrollment [4].

Even among women who had heard of PrEP, misconceptions regarding its purpose, eligibility, and side effects were common. One study found more than half of women in substance use treatment programs believed that PrEP was intended exclusively for men who have sex with men (MSM), while approximately one-third associated PrEP with HIV-positive status [32]. These findings reflected broader gaps in gender-specific HIV prevention messaging and persistent misinformation surrounding HIV prevention [33–36]. Qualitative accounts revealed that many women had either never received targeted education regarding PrEP or held misconceptions about its safety, effectiveness, and intended use [37]. Several studies identified inadequate correctional health education, fragmented prevention services, and limited culturally responsive outreach as major contributors to low awareness [5, 10, 17, 25, 29, 30].

Structural and institutional barriers

Structural barriers emerged as among the most consistently reported obstacles to PrEP engagement in the literature. Women described substantial challenges related to unstable housing, transportation limitations, inadequate insurance coverage, fragmented healthcare access, and lack of continuity of care following release from incarceration [3, 18, 24].

The transition from incarceration to community reentry represented a particularly vulnerable period during which many women experienced interruptions in healthcare access and medication continuity. Several studies noted that women frequently prioritized immediate survival needs such as housing, employment, food security, substance use recovery, and family reunification over preventive HIV care [17, 23, 24]. Institutional barriers within correctional systems also limited PrEP engagement. Studies described inconsistent screening procedures, limited HIV prevention education within correctional facilities, and insufficient linkage-to-care planning prior to release [38]. In some cases, women reported difficulty identifying healthcare providers knowledgeable about PrEP after reentry into the community [5, 10, 29]. An unanticipated finding of this review was the emergence of a socio-ecological pattern across the included studies. Although the review initially sought to identify barriers and facilitators to PrEP engagement among CJI cisgender women, the evidence consistently clustered across interconnected individual, interpersonal, and structural domains. To capture these relationships, we inductively developed the Socio-Ecological Framework of Criminal Justice Involvement (SEM-CJI) (Fig. 2). The SEM-CJI conceptualizes criminal justice involvement not as a static demographic characteristic but as a dynamic context that shapes HIV prevention barriers and facilitators. The framework organizes findings into three interacting domains: (1) Individual Gendered Realities, (2) Interpersonal and Reentry Networks, and (3) Structural and Systemic Infrastructure. Together, these domains illustrate how barriers and facilitators operate across multiple levels to influence PrEP engagement among CJI cisgender women.

Stigma, medical mistrust, and interpersonal barriers

HIV-related stigma, incarceration-related stigma, and mistrust of healthcare systems were pervasive barriers across qualitative studies. Participants expressed concerns regarding confidentiality breaches, judgment from healthcare providers, and negative social perceptions associated with both HIV risk and PrEP use [17, 25, 30]. Several studies described how stigma intersected with race, gender, substance use, and criminal justice involvement to further complicate healthcare engagement. Women frequently reported fears that PrEP use would result in assumptions regarding promiscuity, HIV infection, or drug use [4, 17, 25]. Interpersonal relationship dynamics also influenced PrEP decision-making. Intimate partners, peers, and family relationships could either facilitate or undermine HIV prevention behaviors depending on levels of trust, support, and communication [24, 39]. In some cases, women expressed concern that partners might interpret PrEP use as evidence of infidelity or high-risk sexual behavior [4, 25].

What facilitators enhance PrEP engagement among criminal justice-involved cisgender women?

Peer support and trusted relationships

Peer-based support and trusted interpersonal relationships emerged as important facilitators of PrEP engagement. Women were generally more receptive to PrEP when information was delivered through trusted peers, community organizations, social support networks, or providers with established relationships [24, 39]. Several qualitative studies emphasized the importance of relational trust in shaping willingness to consider PrEP [37, 40]. Supportive social networks helped normalize HIV prevention behaviors and reduced fears surrounding stigma and judgment [23].

Education and targeted outreach

Educational interventions were consistently identified as important facilitators of PrEP acceptability and uptake. Structured educational programming improved understanding of HIV risk and increased receptiveness toward PrEP use, particularly when information was culturally responsive and tailored to women’s lived experiences [5, 10]. Concerns regarding side effects, confidentiality, and medical mistrust were often reduced when women received detailed information from trusted healthcare providers or peer educators [24]. Studies also suggested that correctional settings may represent important opportunities for HIV prevention education because incarceration can provide relatively stable access to healthcare services and structured programming [22].

Transitional care and navigation services

Several studies identified linkage-to-care programs, navigation services, and transitional support systems as promising facilitators of PrEP initiation and continuity following release from incarceration. Interventions that incorporated case management, benefit navigation, transportation support, and coordinated referrals appeared to improve healthcare engagement [18, 24]. Systematically identifying eligible women during incarceration and linking them to community-based care improved early PrEP uptake [39]. Quantitative studies also reported positive associations between navigation services and PrEP uptake among Black and Latina women [10, 26]. However, these findings demonstrated extremely wide confidence intervals, indicating substantial statistical uncertainty likely related to sparse data and small sample sizes. Consequently, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously and not as precise measures of intervention effectiveness.

Correctional-based PrEP initiation

Multiple studies suggested that initiating PrEP during incarceration may improve continuity of care following release. Participants frequently described incarceration as a period during which healthcare access was more predictable than during community reentry [5, 22]. Participants preferred receiving PrEP education and initiation while incarcerated because it facilitated continuity following release [22]. Similar findings were observed across other studies in which women expressed greater willingness to initiate PrEP when accompanied by discharge planning, community referrals, and post-release follow-up support [39].

What interventions or strategies have been proposed or evaluated to support PrEP use?

Several intervention approaches were identified across the included studies. These included peer-led education programs, eHealth and digital outreach interventions, integration of PrEP into correctional healthcare systems, linkage-to-care and reentry navigation programs, and integration of PrEP services into addiction treatment settings [4, 10, 39]. Implementation-focused studies suggested that combining educational interventions with structural support services may improve engagement across multiple stages of the PrEP continuum. Programs incorporating coordinated discharge planning and transitional healthcare support demonstrated particular promise for improving linkage to community-based care following release [24, 39]. An eHealth and community outreach intervention designed to improve PrEP uptake demonstrated that digital engagement strategies may increase access to HIV prevention information among underserved women [4]. However, several studies also noted that limited access to technology, inconsistent internet availability, low digital literacy, and mistrust of digital health systems may reduce the effectiveness of eHealth-based interventions among some populations [4, 25]. Additional intervention strategies included integrating PrEP services into medication-assisted treatment programs, correctional healthcare systems, and reentry planning services. Integrating HIV prevention into addiction treatment settings may be particularly valuable given the elevated HIV vulnerability among women receiving substance use treatment [10]. Despite promising findings, intervention heterogeneity across studies limited direct comparison of outcomes. Variability in study design, implementation settings, outcome measures, and operational definitions of PrEP engagement reduced the ability to identify best practices conclusively.

How do justice-system contexts and reentry, influence access to and utilization of PrEP?

Justice-system involvement shaped PrEP engagement in complex and sometimes contradictory ways. Correctional settings created opportunities for healthcare contact and HIV prevention programming while simultaneously reinforcing structural barriers, stigma, and mistrust [5, 10]. Several studies described incarceration as a potential intervention point where HIV prevention education, healthcare access, and PrEP screening could be delivered more systematically than in many community settings [22, 39]. Participants frequently identified incarceration as a period during which they were more accessible for healthcare engagement and structured programming [22]. At the same time, correctional systems were often described as fragmented and inadequately equipped to support long-term HIV prevention efforts. Participants reported inconsistent access to healthcare providers, limited privacy, insufficient education, and inadequate discharge planning related to PrEP continuity [5, 10]. The transition from incarceration to community reentry emerged as a particularly critical period influencing PrEP utilization. Women commonly experienced disruptions in insurance coverage, medication access, transportation, and healthcare continuity immediately following release [41]. These disruptions frequently interrupted PrEP initiation or adherence efforts and contributed to loss of follow-up [23, 24]. Collectively, findings suggest that criminal justice settings represent both structural barriers and potential intervention opportunities within the HIV prevention continuum.

Equity and racial/ethnic considerations

A secondary objective of this review was to explore how racial and ethnic disparities and equity-related considerations were addressed within the literature. Although disparities were not the primary focus of most included studies, several investigations documented disproportionately low PrEP awareness among Black and Latina women despite elevated HIV vulnerability [10, 26]. Greater PrEP awareness was associated with higher odds of PrEP initiation among Black women, while having a recent sexual relationship with an HIV-positive partner was also associated with increased uptake [30]. Among Latina women, PrEP awareness was strongly associated with uptake [10]. Unstable housing was also associated with increased PrEP initiation among Latina participants, potentially reflecting increased engagement with supportive services or elevated perceived HIV vulnerability [10, 42]. However, reporting of race- and ethnicity-specific outcomes was sparse and inconsistent across studies. Few studies stratified findings by race or ethnicity, whereas others discussed disparities more generally or failed to disaggregate findings entirely. As a result, conclusions regarding racial and ethnic disparities in PrEP engagement among criminal justice-involved cisgender women should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory rather than definitive, consistent with the limited number of studies specifically examining equity-related outcomes. Nonetheless, the reviewed literature consistently highlighted the importance of culturally responsive, trauma-informed, and equity-centered HIV prevention strategies tailored to the experiences of criminal justice-involved women.

Quality assessment

The twelve included studies were evaluated for risk of bias using standardized assessment tools tailored to each specific study design, such as the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for quantitative designs, the CASP checklist for qualitative design and the MMAT tool for mixed method studies (Table 4). Most studies clearly articulated research objectives and employed designs appropriate to their aims. Qualitative studies generally demonstrated strong contextual depth and thematic coherence, particularly those using interviews or focus groups to examine perceptions of PrEP, stigma, and structural barriers [17, 23]. Quantitative studies appropriately used observational or cross-sectional designs to assess prevalence, awareness, and associations related to PrEP engagement; however, these designs limited causal inference. Sample representativeness varied considerably across studies. Qualitative investigations frequently relied on purposive sampling strategies appropriate for exploratory inquiry but limited in transferability. Quantitative studies varied more substantially in sample size and representativeness. A large implementation cohort derived through routine clinical screening procedures within a statewide correctional system strengthened implementation relevance but not necessarily epidemiologic representativeness [26]. Measurement approaches lacked consistency across studies. Definitions of key outcomes such as PrEP awareness, acceptability, initiation, and adherence varied substantially, and few studies reported use of validated measurement instruments. Some studies measured awareness using a single self-report item, whereas others employed multi-item knowledge scales without reporting psychometric properties. Similarly, operational definitions of PrEP initiation ranged from willingness to initiate PrEP to documented medication initiation through clinical records. These inconsistencies reduced comparability across studies and limited the ability to synthesize findings quantitatively. Qualitative studies generally employed thematic analysis or grounded theory approaches; however, only a subset reported procedures for ensuring coding reliability, reflexivity, or thematic saturation [3, 17]. Mixed-methods and implementation-focused studies demonstrated moderate methodological rigor but often lacked detailed descriptions of integration procedures and analytic transparency. Completeness of outcome reporting was also variable. Several studies disaggregated findings by race, ethnicity, criminal justice status, or HIV risk behaviors, enabling intersectional analysis, whereas others did not provide subgroup-specific analyses relevant to cisgender women. Longitudinal data regarding PrEP adherence and retention in care were limited across studies. Despite methodological heterogeneity, thematic consistency across studies strengthened confidence in the overall synthesis. Recurring findings included persistently low PrEP awareness, substantial structural vulnerability, barriers related to stigma and mistrust, and the importance of peer-supported, continuity-focused, and structurally informed interventions to improve PrEP engagement among criminal justice-involved cisgender women [17, 24, 39].

Discussion

Included studies varied in how they conceptualized and measured PrEP-related outcomes, findings were synthesized across overlapping domains of knowledge/awareness, acceptability/willingness, uptake/initiation, and adherence rather than according to a uniform PrEP care continuum. Overall, the evidence indicates that CJI cisgender women experience a disproportionate burden of HIV vulnerability while remaining significantly underserved across the PrEP prevention continuum. Engagement with PrEP is shaped by intersecting structural, institutional, interpersonal, and sociocultural determinants, including housing instability, fragmented healthcare systems, stigma, medical mistrust, and disruptions in care continuity during incarceration and reentry. Across studies, barriers were consistently multilevel, while evidence on interventions was limited by small sample sizes and methodological constraints, restricting confidence in effect estimates.

PrEP knowledge and awareness

A consistent finding across studies was the persistently low level of PrEP awareness among CJI cisgender women. Many participants reported no prior knowledge of PrEP before study participation or intervention exposure, despite elevated HIV risk profiles [4, 17]. This suggests a critical gap in prevention outreach rather than an individual-level deficit in health knowledge. Misconceptions about PrEP were common, particularly the belief that PrEP is intended primarily for MSM [43]. This reflects enduring gender bias in HIV prevention messaging and highlights the limited tailoring of prevention strategies to cisgender women, particularly those with criminal justice involvement. Collectively, the evidence indicates that PrEP awareness gaps are structurally produced and reflect historical underinvestment in gender-responsive HIV prevention within both correctional and community health systems [34].

Barriers and facilitators of PrEP acceptability and uptake

Structural and institutional barriers

Structural instability was one of the most consistently reported barriers to PrEP uptake [44]. Women described unstable housing, unemployment, transportation barriers, insurance disruption, and competing survival needs as key factors limiting engagement in preventive healthcare following incarceration [44, 45]. The reentry period was particularly critical, marked by abrupt discontinuities in healthcare access and medication coverage. Institutional barriers within correctional settings further constrained PrEP engagement. These included inconsistent HIV prevention education, limited provider familiarity with PrEP, weak screening and prescribing protocols, and insufficient discharge planning for continuity of care [38]. Although incarceration provides a structured opportunity for healthcare delivery, the absence of standardized HIV prevention infrastructure limited sustained engagement beyond release [46].

Stigma and interpersonal influences

Stigma was a pervasive barrier operating across multiple levels. Participants reported concerns about being labeled promiscuous, high-risk, or HIV-positive if they sought PrEP services. HIV-related stigma intersected with incarceration stigma, substance use stigma, and racialized stereotypes, producing compounded barriers to engagement [47]. Interpersonal relationship dynamics also shaped PrEP decision-making. Concerns that PrEP use could be interpreted by partners as infidelity or sexual risk-taking were frequently cited as deterrents. These findings reinforce that PrEP uptake is embedded within relational and sociocultural contexts and cannot be fully understood as an individual-level behavioral decision.

Adherence and continuity of PrEP Care

Evidence on PrEP adherence and sustained use was limited but consistently highlighted the importance of continuity of care across the incarceration-reentry transition. Structural instability following release, including loss of insurance, housing insecurity, and transportation barriers, frequently disrupted ongoing engagement with healthcare services [37].

Facilitators of sustained engagement

Peer support, trusted healthcare relationships, and community-based organizations were consistently identified as facilitators of engagement and continuity. Trust was particularly important in mitigating medical mistrust shaped by prior experiences of marginalization within healthcare and correctional systems [17, 24]. Educational interventions improved PrEP acceptability when culturally responsive and trauma-informed, particularly when they acknowledged lived experiences of incarceration, substance use, and structural vulnerability. However, education alone was insufficient without systems to ensure linkage to care [3]. Navigation and transitional care interventions were commonly associated with improved PrEP initiation and linkage outcomes. These included case management, appointment coordination, insurance assistance, and transportation support [24]. Although several studies reported positive associations between navigation services and PrEP uptake, effect estimates were highly imprecise, with extremely wide confidence intervals and unstable models, reflecting sparse data and small sample sizes [25]. Therefore, while findings suggest a potential positive association, the magnitude and reliability of these effects cannot be determined with confidence. Correctional-based PrEP initiation also emerged as a promising approach. Women described incarceration as a period of relatively stable healthcare access and expressed willingness to initiate PrEP if supported by structured discharge planning and post-release linkage systems. However, effectiveness depended on continuity mechanisms that were often inconsistently implemented [18].

Equity, race/ethnicity, and structural determinants

A key secondary objective of this review was to examine how racial and ethnic disparities were reported and addressed across studies. Reporting was inconsistent: some studies provided stratified analyses by race and ethnicity, while others did not report racial composition or disaggregate outcomes. Where reported, findings suggest that Black and Latina CJI women experience lower PrEP awareness and greater exposure to structural HIV risk determinants [24]. However, these findings must be interpreted cautiously due to small sample sizes, heterogeneity in measurement, and inconsistent reporting practices. Importantly, disparities in PrEP engagement are inseparable from broader structural inequities, including racism, poverty, gender inequality, healthcare exclusion, housing instability, and criminalization [48]. Despite this, relatively few studies explicitly applied intersectional, structural racism, or equity frameworks to guide analysis or interpretation. This represents a critical gap in the literature and limits understanding of the mechanisms driving inequities. Given these limitations, conclusions regarding racial and ethnic disparities remain exploratory rather than definitive. Future research should prioritize standardized reporting of race/ethnicity, consistent stratification of outcomes, and explicit application of equity-informed frameworks to better elucidate structural drivers of inequity in PrEP engagement.

Implications for practice and policy

Findings underscore the need for multilevel, structurally informed, and trauma-responsive interventions rather than individual-level approaches alone. Effective PrEP implementation for CJI cisgender women will likely require integration of HIV prevention with housing support, substance use treatment, mental health care, and reentry services [49]. Integrated care models that span correctional and community settings may reduce fragmentation and improve continuity across the PrEP cascade [4]. Peer-led and community-based interventions may further enhance trust and engagement, particularly among populations affected by medical mistrust and structural marginalization [25].

Digital and eHealth interventions may expand reach among geographically mobile populations; however, concerns regarding access, literacy, and trust indicate that digital strategies should complement rather than replace in-person and community-based services [4].

Influence of the criminal justice system on PrEP engagement

The criminal justice system plays a dual role in shaping PrEP engagement. Correctional settings may provide structured opportunities for HIV screening, education, and initiation of PrEP. However, these potential benefits are often undermined by limited autonomy, inconsistent prevention infrastructure, and weak continuity planning [4, 26].

The transition from incarceration to community reentry consistently emerged as the most critical period influencing PrEP uptake and adherence. Disruptions in insurance coverage, housing, transportation, and healthcare access frequently resulted in loss of continuity in preventive care [50]. These findings highlight the importance of coordinated transitional care models that bridge correctional and community-based health systems.

Theoretical contribution: the inductive mapping of the SEM-CJI

A profound and entirely unanticipated contribution of this systematic review was the organic emergence of the SEM-CJI framework. Our initial research questions sought to catalogue disparate PrEP barriers; however, the synthesized data resisted siloed categorization, demanding instead a multi-tiered socio-ecological translation.

Our findings illustrate that the persistently low levels of PrEP awareness among CJI cisgender women (e.g., the enduring gender bias that PrEP is exclusively for MSM [17, 24] cannot be blamed on individual-level cognitive deficits. Rather, our emergent framework demonstrates that individual awareness is constrained by the interpersonal/social layer, where compounded carceral, substance use, and racialized stigmas intersect with relational dynamics such as fears that PrEP implies partner infidelity [51]. Most critically, the framework highlights how these individual and social dynamics are bounded by the structural/justice system level (Fig. 2). The structural instability characterizing the reentry transition (housing insecurity, insurance disruptions) [52, 53] alongside weak correctional prescribing infrastructures and fractured discharge planning [17, 54] act as the ultimate gatekeepers to care. Discovering the SEM-CJI as an emergent property of this literature represents an important conceptual pivot. It proves that education interventions are functionally insufficient if implemented without systems that span all three tiers of the framework [4]. Interventions must shift away from trying to modify individual behavior and instead target the structural and social rings such as leveraging the relative health stability of carceral stays via trauma-informed, peer-supported navigational systems that bridge the gap to community reentry [24, 44, 55].

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review provides one of the few focused syntheses examining PrEP engagement specifically among CJI cisgender in the U.S, a population that remains substantially underrepresented within HIV prevention research despite experiencing disproportionate HIV vulnerability. By integrating qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods evidence, the review offers a comprehensive understanding of barriers and facilitators operating across the PrEP continuum, including awareness, acceptability, initiation, and adherence. An additional strength is the incorporation of equity-focused considerations, particularly the examination of how structural inequities, stigma, and racial disparities shape HIV prevention engagement among Black and Latina women with criminal justice involvement. The use of PRISMA-informed methods and systematic quality appraisal through the risk of bias assessment tools further strengthened the rigor and transparency of the review process. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the number of eligible studies was relatively small, reflecting a broader gap in research focused specifically on CJI cisgender women. Many studies included mixed-gender or broader justice-involved populations without consistently disaggregating findings by gender, race/ethnicity, or justice status, limiting the ability to isolate outcomes specific to cisgender women [10, 25]. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed across study designs, populations, settings, and outcome definitions, complicating direct comparison and synthesis across studies. Definitions of key outcomes such as PrEP awareness, acceptability, initiation, and adherence varied considerably, and few studies employed validated or standardized measures [23]. Third, most quantitative studies relied on cross-sectional or observational designs with relatively small sample sizes, limiting causal inference and reducing statistical precision. Several reported associations demonstrated wide confidence intervals, indicating substantial uncertainty and the potential for model instability. In addition, reliance on self-reported measures introduced the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly in studies examining stigmatized behaviors and healthcare experiences [10]. Fourth, race- and ethnicity-specific findings were inconsistently reported across studies, limiting the ability to comprehensively assess disparities and intersectional inequities among Black and Latina women [29]. Lastly, although the search strategy captured studies published through July 2025, newer evidence published after the search cutoff may not have been included. Geographic representation was also uneven, with many studies conducted in urban or resource-rich settings, potentially underrepresenting experiences of women in rural or under-resourced regions, particularly in the Southern United States.

Implications for future research and public health practice

The findings of this review underscore the need for multilevel, equity-centered approaches to HIV prevention among CJI cisgender women. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and implementation-focused studies capable of examining sustained PrEP engagement across incarceration, reentry, and long-term community reintegration [26]. Applying the CJI Framework as a conceptual model will optimize future research and public health initiatives. Achieving greater methodological consistency specifically through standardized outcome definitions, validated instruments, and data disaggregated by race, ethnicity, and carceral context is critical to improve study comparability and strengthen the overall evidence base [25]. Additional research is needed to evaluate culturally responsive interventions specifically designed for Black and Latina cisgender women with criminal justice involvement, who experience intersecting burdens related to structural racism, gender inequity, poverty, housing instability, and healthcare exclusion. Future studies should also incorporate implementation science frameworks to better understand how correctional health systems, community-based organizations, and public health agencies can effectively scale and sustain PrEP delivery within real-world settings. From a public health and policy perspective, findings support the integration of PrEP education, screening, and linkage-to-care services into correctional healthcare systems, reentry programs, addiction treatment settings, and community-based care networks. Interventions that combine HIV prevention with wraparound services addressing housing, transportation, mental health, substance use treatment, and insurance navigation may be particularly important during the transition from incarceration to community reentry [56]. Trauma-informed, peer-led, and community-engaged approaches also appear critical for addressing stigma, improving trust, and enhancing sustained engagement in care. Importantly, correctional settings should not be viewed solely as sites of incarceration, but also as potential intervention points for addressing longstanding gaps in HIV prevention access among underserved cisgender women.

Conclusion

Cisgender women involved in the U.S. criminal justice system remain substantially underserved across the PrEP prevention continuum despite experiencing elevated HIV vulnerability and disproportionate exposure to structural inequities associated with HIV risk. This review demonstrates that PrEP engagement among CJI cisgender women is shaped not only by individual awareness and willingness, but also by broader institutional, structural, and interpersonal forces including stigma, healthcare fragmentation, unstable housing, disrupted care transitions, and mistrust of healthcare and correctional systems. Although awareness of PrEP was consistently low across studies, findings suggest that women are often receptive to PrEP when provided culturally responsive education, peer support, and accessible linkage-to-care services. Several promising strategies emerged across the literature, including peer-led interventions, trauma-informed programming, correctional-based PrEP initiation, integrated reentry services, and navigation programs designed to improve continuity of care following release from incarceration. However, substantial gaps remain in the equitable delivery of HIV prevention services for this population. Addressing these gaps will require coordinated, community-informed, and justice-centered approaches that extend across correctional and community settings while simultaneously addressing the broader structural inequities shaping women’s health outcomes. Optimizing PrEP delivery for CJI cisgender women requires comprehensive, equity-driven strategies that prioritize continuity of care and robust social support over narrow biomedical interventions. Our findings underscore the critical need to center CJI cisgender women in HIV prevention research, policy, and practice. Ultimately, safeguarding the health of this population requires looking beyond clinical environments to aggressively investigate and mitigate the specific social determinants of health namely poverty, stigma, and unstable housing that drive disparate carceral and health outcomes.
