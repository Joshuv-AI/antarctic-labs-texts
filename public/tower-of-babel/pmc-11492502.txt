Biosorption and transformation of cadmium and lead by Staphylococcus epidermidis AS-1 isolated from industrial effluent
Authors: Abhijit Kumar, Gunjan Mukherjee, Vishal Ahuja, Saurabh Gupta, Maryam Abbasi Tarighat, Gholamreza Abdi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid utilization of natural resources and other anthropogenic activities intruded heavy metals into the food chain and raised alarming concern for all life forms. The available methods proved insufficient in handling waste and pollutants due to the high cost and generation of toxic residues. Bioremediation strategies have offered sustainable solutions for toxic pollutants. In the current study, cadmium and lead (Cd and Pb respectively) tolerant strains have been isolated from industrial effluent and characterized for tolerance towards target pollutants. The strain was identified by 16s rRNA gene and further used for metal removal from the industrial effluents.ResultsBacterial isolates were obtained from industrial discharge and evaluated for their tolerance towards Cd and Pb. AS-1 bacterial isolate exhibited maximum tolerance towards both the metals and hence was selected for further study. The isolate was identified as Staphylococcus epidermidis. ICP-MS and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis of biomass revealed that a significant proportion of cadmium (90.89%) and lead (94.87%) available in effluent were sequestered within bacterial biomass. Characteristic peaks at 2Ɵ (31.8637 and 45.6247 for cadmium) and (21.0397, 27.0127, 46.0537, 54.2707 and 75.6547 for lead) confirmed the crystalline nature of the sequestered metals. The selected strain was characterized on biochemical and molecular basis and was found to be Staphylococcus epidermidis. Based on 16 S rDNA sequence analysis, a phylogenetic dendrogram was created for the maximum likelihood of the bacterial strain. The sequence was deposited in the NCBI repository (accession number PP587422).ConclusionThe work has shown the possible way out of heavy metal pollution sustainably. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this is the first report on the sequestration and reduction of cadmium and lead by a nonpathogenic strain of Staphylococcus epidermidis AS-1 that may be useful for alleviating heavy metal contamination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03568-y.Keywords: Biotransformation, Reduction, Sequestration, Cadmium, Lead

Background

Currently, environmental protection agencies are mainly worried about the changes caused to the environment by a wide range of anthropogenic activities that deliberately release organic and inorganic contaminants [1–3]. Anthropogenic as well as natural processes including urbanization, mining, agricultural practices, volcanic eruptions, weathering of rocks, and forest and litter burning [4] lead to the accumulation of toxic pollutants, xenobiotic compounds, and heavy metals [5–7]. In general, heavy metals are defined as elements with an atomic weight of more than 20 or a density of more than 5 g.cc− 1 [8]. Arsenic (As), cobalt (Co), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb), etc., are some of the prevalent heavy metals released via industrial operations and accumulated at different trophic levels of the ecosystem [9, 10]. In recent reports, more than 10 million sites have also been identified where soil has been contaminated and over 50% have shown contamination at lethal levels [11, 12]. Cd is a superfluous and malleable element with an ambiguous function in animals and plants but has detrimental effects on both plant quality, productivity, and animal health [13, 14]. According to the reports, around 2.79 × 109 m2 of agricultural soil has been contaminated with Cd [9], which deteriorates the quality of plants and plant products and reduces the grain yield [15–17]. Biomagnification of Cd (through the food chain) in the human body hampers the overall functioning of the human body [18]. According to reports by the Food Chain Pollutants Group (EFSA), the concentration of toxic elements should be 21.4 µg.d− 1. Still, in some countries, the average concentration of Cd in the diet is 32.7 µg.d− 1 (China), which is significantly greater than the tolerable range [19]. This has raised a major concern among countries that require proper treatment and removal of heavy metal contamination from soils and effluents.

Lead (Pb) is a highly toxic element whose biological function has not yet been characterized. It is having a serious lethal, and toxic impact on the living system contributing to approximately 0.6% of the burden of illness and other malfunctions globally [20]. According to WHO, Pb poisoning increases the chances of high blood pressure, cardiac ailments, and kidney failure. It also resulted in premature birth, miscarriages, and inborn defects [21]. In children younger than six years, lead poisoning has a strong impact on their mental and physical growth. Typical effects of lead poisoning include kidney inflammation, seizures, brain damage, anemia, and gastrointestinal symptoms [22, 23]. Minimal lead levels are also associated with poorer IQ scores and behavioral problems [20]. Traditional methods including physical and chemical processes, such as excavation, soil washing and dressing, vitrification, and stabilization have been employed in the remediation of contaminated sites which are very expensive and also increase the risk of secondary pollution [24–26]. Currently, researchers are primarily focused on exploiting bioremediation approaches with microorganisms over conventional methods to overcome their shortcomings [27].

Microbe-assisted bioremediation methods have shown great potential in alleviating heavy metal contamination efficiently and economically [28–30]. Some of the microorganisms have also exhibited resistance to metal ions due to the presence of toxicity-countering mechanisms including pollutant sequestration, enzymatic degradation and transformation, precipitation, pollutant/complex efflux, and metallothionein synthesis [31–33]. Chen et al., have reported white rot fungi including Coriolus versicolor, Ganoderma lucidum, Irpex lacteus, Lentinus edodes, Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Pleurotus ostreatus, Schizophyllum commune, and Trametes versicolor for the removal of heavy metals like Cr, Cu, Pb, Ni, Cd, and Zn [34]. It has also been suggested that in white rot fungi, cytochrome P450 has played a crucial role in pollutant degradation [35]. In a similar line, Cao et al., [36] also isolated 17β-Estradiol degrading Sphingobacterium sp. from sludge (Taozi lake). In the case of inorganic pollutants or metals, only removal is possible via the adsorption of pollutants at contaminated sites (biosorption), accumulating the metals as complexes with proteins like metallothionein (bioaccumulation), and exclusion [37, 38].

These remediation approaches employed microbes (bacteria, microalgae, fungi, and yeast) in eliminating pollutants and restoring the ecological balance in a sustainable manner [39] however, it is itself challenging due to the complexity of the environment and the toxic effect of pollutants which needs high adaptability in microorganisms to sustain and detoxify the pollutants. Thus, it is necessary to explore the tolerance and strong adaptability of microbial species to a wide variety of contaminants. Thus, the present research focused mainly on the isolation of nonpathogenic bacterial strains that can sequester heavy metals from effluents and contaminated sites in non-bioavailable forms.

Methods

Chemicals

All growth media and chemicals used for experimental purposes were of high-purity grades. Growth media including Tryptone soya broth (M011), and Bushnell Hass broth (M350) were supplied by Himedia Pvt. Ltd. Cadmium chloride (GRM8016), perchloric acid (AS012) and sulphuric acid (AS016) were of Hi-AR grade. Lead chloride (7758-95-4) was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich.

Industrial effluent sample collection

Industrial effluent samples were collected from electroplating and paint industries from Himachal (Baddi) (30.939570 N, 76.812180 E), Haryana (30.130820 N, 77.275700 E), and Punjab (30.319890 N, 76.411130 E and 30.894710 N, 75.862870E) (Fig. 1).

Effluent sample collection sites

Samples were collected in sterile cans, brought immediately to the laboratory, and stored till further use. The samples were processed (as defined below) for characterization and isolation. The sample was analyzed for appearance, color, and smell. The pH and conductivity of effluent samples were determined with a pH meter (Mettler Toledo) and conductivity meter (AS ONE pHTestr30 Lacom Tester pH Meter).

Determination of cadmium and lead concentrations in effluent

The effluent samples were subjected to nitric–perchloric acid digestion, following standard protocols described earlier [40]. The samples were digested at temperatures ranging from 70 to 100 oC until yellow HNO3 fumes and white HClO4 fumes appeared. For the dissolution of the digested samples, distilled water was used, followed by a filtration process to remove impurities, and the final volume was made to 25 mL [41]. The processed samples were transferred to polythene bottles and subjected to metal analysis via ICP-MS.

Enrichment and isolation of tolerant bacterial isolates

The cadmium-tolerant and lead-tolerant isolates were isolated by the method described earlier [42]. For enrichment and isolation of potent bacterial isolates, the samples were inoculated into tryptone soya broth (TSB) and Bushnell Hass broth (BHB). BHB media was supplemented with 100 mL glucose (0.2% aqueous solution) and sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min in an autoclave. Then, cadmium and lead (as PbCl2 and CdCl2) solution were autoclaved separately and added to the media to attain the final concentration of 1 mg.L− 1. For the enrichment process, 10% inocula of metal-tolerant bacterial isolates were transferred from overnight culture to fresh media with increasing concentrations of metal salt concentrations. The metal-tolerant isolates were characterized for metal tolerance and sequestration by biochemical and molecular characteristics. Pure cultures were maintained and preserved in glycerol at 4 °C until further use.

Tolerance studies

The tolerance of ten isolates (AS-1, AS-2, AS-3, AS-4, AS-5, AS-6, AS-7, AS-8, AS-9, and AS-10) to different concentrations of CdCl2 and PbCl2 were further explored. Broth-containing bacterial isolates were further incubated under aerobic conditions at 37 °C. The turbidity inside the medium is an indicator of growth, which was further correlated with the tolerance to different heavy metals. The isolates were further processed to assess their tolerance to Cd and Pb ions in broth. For comparative evaluation of the isolate’s performance in the presence of heavy metals, controls were used (isolates grown in a metal-free growth medium).

Biochemical and molecular characterization

The AS-1 isolate was identified and characterized based on its morphological, biochemical, molecular characteristics, and phylogenetic relationship. The selected bacterial isolate AS-1 was grown in TSB (without salt supplementation) for 24 h and active inoculum was used for biochemical kits (KB004 HiStaph™ Identification Kit; Himedia Pvt. Ltd. India) and observations were recorded.

For sequencing, the genomic DNA of the bacterial strain was isolated followed by amplified with 27 F and 1492R forward and reverse primers to specifically amplify 16 S rRNA gene [42, 43]. The sequencing was done by Chromous Biotech, Bangalore. The obtained 16 S rRNA gene sequence aligned with the available sequences in databases using BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and CLUSTAL W (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw). Sequences with more than 98% identity and query coverage have been selected for phylogenetic analysis. The aligned sequences and phylogenetic analysis were performed using MEGA 11 for maximum likelihood.

Mass balance profiling studies

The AS-1 strain was specifically chosen due to its heightened tolerance to cadmium and lead. Growth profiling of AS-1 was conducted by following the process as defined by Kumar et al. [42]. Initially, actively growing AS-1 cultures (24 h old 10% inoculum) were inoculated into TSB enriched with varying concentrations of cadmium and lead and incubated at 37 °C. At the culmination of profiling (upto 96 h), samples were collected after definite intervals and centrifugation at 8000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. These fractions were subjected to acid digestion as suggested by the USEPA [44], with subsequent dilution. For digestion, biomass and supernatant samples containing sequestered cadmium were separately treated with a concentrated HNO3 + HClO4 (3:1) [40]. Digestion continued until the appearance of white HNO3 fumes, indicating complete digestion. After digestion, the samples were subjected to analysis for elemental detection via ICP-MS.

Analytical characterization

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

The predigested effluent samples were analyzed by ICP-MS at Choksi Lab Ltd. (Panchkula, Haryana India), to quantify the heavy metals. The equipment was operated under inert conditions where the flow of argon gas was maintained throughout the plasma, auxiliary, and nebulizer. The samples were subjected to atomization with the help of plasma formation at 6000 оC, which was further detected by mass spectrometry.

Surface morphology and elemental analysis

The availability of sophisticated techniques such as electron microscopy and XRD facilitates the understanding of cell structure at a deeper level, particularly concentrating on the localization of sequestered metals within the cell. With the help of these techniques, it becomes possible to determine whether the uptake of metals is influenced by cellular metabolism or simply surface interactions. Analysis of biomass sequestration was done with FESEM-EDX.

Crystallography

The AS-1 strain was characterized for sequestered cadmium and lead by using an X-ray diffractometer (Panalytical X.Pert Pro; SAIF), from Punjab University, Chandigarh, India. For this purpose, the strain was grown in CdCl2 and PbCl2 (1 mg.L− 1) supplemented TSB media separately at 37 °C for 48 h. For XRD analysis, bacteria biomass was crushed using a mortar and pestle and air dried. The powdered biomass was scanned with K-α2 (1.54443 Å), Cu K-α1 (1.54060 Å), and K-β1 (1.39225 Å) radiations from angle ranging (2Ɵ) between 20o-80o (step size of 0.01700 S− 1) with an energy source of 45 kV (40 mA) and diffraction pattern was recorded.

Statistical analysis

The results obtained from the tolerance assay were evaluated for fitness by ANOVA with replicates using MS-Excel loaded with a data analysis tool pack.

Results

Microorganisms have shown great potential in dealing with toxic and xenobiotic pollutants. The current work has also emphasized this fact by removing Cd and Pb from effluent by bacterial strain. The upcoming section summarizes the results of biosorption and bioaccumulation of heavy metals.

Industrial effluent collection and characterization

Industrial effluent is considered a complex discharge where organic and inorganic contaminants are available in significantly higher concentrations beyond threshold levels. The presence and concentration of a particular element in effluent depends upon the type of industry and stage of the process. Agricultural chemicals like pesticides and fertilizers are rich sources of Cd, As, and Zn, geological minerals, weathering of rocks, forest fires, and volcanic eruption are the sources of Pb, As, Mn, Cr, poultry, and industrial waste released As while fossil fuels and sewage sludge is the source of Cd, Pb, and Hg [45, 46]. In current works as well, the concentrations of Cd and Pb have shown a substantial rise in effluent collected from various regions of Haryana, Punjab, and Himachal Pradesh mainly due to ongoing anthropogenic activities and industrial discharge. Physicochemical analysis of the collected samples further revealed that Cd and Pb were highly oxidized (Table 1).

Physio-chemical characterization

The bioavailability of Cd and Pb in effluent and its uptake is influenced by alkaline pH (7.5). The absorption of heavy metals is also affected by the complexity of contaminants present in effluent [47, 48]. The significantly higher electrical conductivity (347 mS.m− 1) of the samples indicates that the presence of charged contaminants is a higher concentration, which also reflected the mobility of ions. The effluent samples have also shown the presence of a microbial population which suggested that such microorganisms might have some defense mechanism to protect cells against the toxic effect of such heavy metals. Therefore, effluent samples were processed to find some tolerant strains that can be used for bioremediation.

Isolation and characterization of heavy metal tolerant bacterial strain

To selectively target tolerant strains, an enrichment approach was used. Effluent samples have offered shiny, cream-colored bacterial isolates on growth media supplemented with Cd and Pb salts (CdCl2 and PbCl2). Total ten bacterial isolates ‘AS-1, AS-2, AS-3, AS-4, AS-5, AS-6, AS-7, AS-8, AS-9, and AS-10’ were obtained from the effluent samples collected (Table 2).

Different strains isolated from different samples

The isolates were further processed to assess their tolerance to Cd and Pb ions in broth. The isolates were inoculated in Cd and Pb-containing medium and growth was observed. For comparison, two types of controls were used wiz control 1: isolated grown in metal-free growth medium, and control 2: medium without inoculating microorganisms. For tolerance, more than 75% growth over control in all metal concentrations was considered for tolerant strains. As shown in Fig. 2, isolates have shown significant tolerance towards both Pb and Cd however all the isolates were not equally effective in countering the toxicity.

Cadmium and lead tolerance of different isolates

Among all the isolates, AS-1 has shown maximum tolerance of up to 85 mg.L− 1 and 77 mg.L− 1 for Cd and Pb respectively, while AS-4 exhibited minimum tolerance of up to 10 mg.L− 1 and 15 mg.L− 1 respectively. In comparison to other bacterial isolates, AS-1 has more than 2-fold higher tolerance (AS-3) for Cd and 1.9-fold higher (AS-6) for Pb, hence selected for characterization and heavy metals biosorption. The data was assessed with one-way ANOVA which suggested that the model was fit. For a suitable model, the p-value must be below 0.05, and the analysis has shown a p-value of 3.94E-05, which emphasized that the analysis model was fit for consideration (Supplementary Table 1). The isolate AS-1 was characterized for morphological and biochemical characteristics, which suggested that it was a gram-positive, cocci, non-motile, non-pigmented, and non-spore-forming strain (Table 3). In addition, AS-1 was negative for gelatin hydrolysis, and citrate while positive for catalase and H2S production. According to Bergey’s manual, the isolate was identified as Staphylococcus.

For confirmatory identification, AS-1 was identified by molecular characterization via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing followed by phylogenetic analysis. The sequence was aligned and compared with sequences available in the NCBI-Genbank database using ClustalW [49]. The phylogeny was studied using the maximum likelihood method using MEGA-11 [50]. Analysis of the 16 S rRNA sequence of AS-1 showed 99% identity and query coverage with that of Staphylococcus epidermidis (OP852914 and MZ170710) (Fig. 3).

Molecular characterization and phylogenetic relatedness of selected bacterial strain

Based on similarity and phylogenetic analysis, the isolate AS-1 was identified as Staphylococcus epidermis. The sequence was deposited in NCBI Genbank (Accession No: PP587422.1). The strain S. epidermis AS-1 was assessed for the biosorption of Cd and Pb from effluent samples.

Biosorption of heavy metals in bacterial biomass

The strain AS-1 was grown in a growth medium containing heavy metals and the growth profile was assessed for the removal of heavy metals from the medium. Both bacterial biomass and supernatant fraction were analyzed for Cd and Pb presence via ICP-MS analysis. Approximately 90% Cd and 94% Pb were detected in the biomass while only a slight fraction remained in supernatant fractions (Fig. 4).

Distribution of cadmium in biomass and supernatant

Among different mechanisms suggested for heavy metal removal i.e. biosorption and bioaccumulation dominated to uptake of Cd and Pb followed by accumulation within cell biomass. The uptake of Cd and Pb in AS-1 microbial biomass was also confirmed by crystallographic analysis (XRD analysis) and EDX which also confirmed the presence of metals. X-ray diffraction pattern of cell biomass (Fig. 5) has shown two major peaks (2θ: 31.8637, and 45.6247) for Cd and five major peaks (2θ: 21.0397, 27.0127, 46.0537, 54.2707 and 75.6547) for Pb. These observations are in corroboration with the previous reports on Cd and Pb with characteristic peaks.

XRD analysis representing characteristic peaks a: Cadmium; b: Lead

Untreated bacterial cells have shown an almost amorphous nature. After treatment with Cd and Pb, distinct peaks appeared in XRD at different positions (for Cd and Pb) due to the uptake of both metals respectively. The appearance of well-defined peaks in both samples has also suggested the crystalline nature of the sample. Heavy metals are known for their toxic effect on cells and to escape it, cells must have some escape mechanism. Microbial cells can uptake the metals followed by retention on the cell surface or localization within the cell organelle. It has been suggested that functional groups with negative charges available on the cell surface act as the first line of defense and are attached to the positively charged metal ions and pollutants followed by the localization of the cell surface [51]. Similar observations were also recorded in SEM and associated EDX analysis of bacterial biomass pellets. (Fig. 6). EDX analysis of bacterial biomass has shown the presence of Cd and Pb over the control (untreated samples). EDX analysis revealed the presence of Cd and Pb as exceptional elements in bacterial biomass. In EDX, Cd appeared below 4 KeV while peaks of Pb were scattered between 2 and 3 KeV and above 10 KeV. Both these metals are not present in normal conditions and hence absent in untreated samples. However, in treated samples, distinct peaks of Cd and Pb have appeared.

The uptake of metals by microbial cells is also confirmed with Scanning electron micrographs (Fig. 7a and d). The micrographs of a treated sample have shown deviation from normal morphology in bacterial cells. Treatment with Cd and Pb has resulted in disrupted cell wall integrity. In addition, cells have also shown the localization of both the metals on the cell surface specifically. Collaborative analysis with ICP-MS, XRD, and SEM-EDX analysis confirmed the uptake of Cd and Pb by bacterial cells.

Scanning electron micrographs of biomass, a-b: Control (cells grown in the absence of cadmium and lead); c: Cadmium; and d: Lead

As displayed in Fig. 7a and b, the cells can be seen without any deposition of heavy metals on the cell surface (Fig. 7a) as well as within cells (Fig. 7b). In comparison to control cells, Cd and Pb treated cells have shown the accumulation of heavy metals in earlier results which was also confirmed in SEM imaging as metals particles are clearly visible on the cell surface (Fig. 7c-d).

Discussion

Heavy metal pollution is one of the biggest challenges for industries as well as society as there is no possibility for degradation (as in the case of organic pollutants). There is only one possibility in the case of heavy metals, which is removal from the site and storage in different non-toxic forms. Physical and chemical methods like leaching, washing, separation, filtration, etc., have been used for metal pollutants removal but the efficiency, cost, and scalability are some of the obstacles. In comparison to other locations, heavy metal pollution is in high proximity to the industrial zones due to the discharge of highly polluted effluents. Microorganisms have shown exceptional potential for adaptability and survival under various conditions. Therefore, the current work was planned to explore the potential of native microflora for the removal of Cd and Pb from industrial effluent. The sites near the polluted area have higher chances for the availability of pollutant degrading and tolerant strains hence, samples were collected from industrial effluents and screened for tolerance and removal potential for Cd and Pb. Among different bacterial strains, Staphylococcus epidermidis AS-1 has shown maximum tolerance for Cd and Pb. Earlier literature has also credited microbial populations for their commendable potential. Yu et al., [52] have isolated Lampropedia aestuarii GYF-1 from the soil sample located in the vicinity of a steel factory. The isolate has shown tolerance towards different heavy metals including Cd2+, Cr6+, Cu2+, Ni2+, Pb2+, Zn2+, and Fe2+, and can uptake pollutants from sole composition as well as mixed composition in substantial amounts. Genomic analysis revealed the overexpression of lipopolysaccharides biosynthesis, and transport-related genes as major mechanisms for uptake and counter the toxicity. Parades-Aguilar et al., [53] have also explored the microflora of heavy metal contaminated sites and isolated Staphylococcus species and Microbacterium ginsengisoli from Sonoran River basin mining tails (SRB) and Sphingomonas sp., Burkholderia cenocepacia, and Staphylococcus warneri from Nacozari de García mine tailings (NdG). All these isolates have shown high tolerance for Fe(III) and Cu(II) (up to 300 mg.L− 1). The heavy metal tolerant isolates obtained in the current work are cream-colored as also reported by Nwagwu et al. [54]. The isolates were cream-colored with raised colonies. Tolerance of microbial population towards heavy metals and toxic pollutants is attributed to various mechanisms including complexation with cell discharge and polysaccharides, cell uptake and fixation on the cell surface, and localization within cell organelle. Biosorption is one of the main mechanisms for heavy metal uptake followed by interaction with negatively charged functional groups available on the cell surface. Staphylococcus epidermidis AS-1 has also shown the fixation of heavy metals on the cell surface. Ahmad et al., [55] assessed heavy metal tolerance and biosorption potential of bacterial strains Exiguobacterium aquaticum, Mammaliicoccus sciuri, Micrococcus luteus, and Jeotgalicoccus huakuii, from hydrothermal vent against As, Co, Cd, Cu, Cr, Ni and Pb. The maximum removal of heavy metals was 83% As (E. aquaticum), 95% Cd (M. sciuri), 94% Cu (M. luteus), and 89% Ni (J. huakuii). The removal of heavy metals is mainly contributed by EPS production 84% (E. aquaticum), 85% (M. sciuri), 98% (M. luteus), and 91% (J. huakuii) respectively. In a similar line Vishwakarma et al., [56] isolated Mn2+-resistant thermophilic bacterial strains from Surya Kund hot spring, Yamunotri, Uttarakhand. Both live cells and dead biomass were assessed for Mn2+ removal by AAS. Amon all bacterial isolates, Acinetobacter sp. LSN-10 (YII-1) has shown maximum Mn+ 2 removal of > 41% (live cells) and > 64% dead microbial biomass. Table 4 summarizes some of the efforts for bioremediation by microbial cells.

Microorganisms-based bioremediation is one of the most prominent and sustainable approaches for pollutant degradation and heavy metal removal from soil as well as polluted water. Conclusion

Environmental pollution is one of the major concerns due to industrialization and exponentially rising population. Natural as well as anthropogenic activities are responsible for the release of various heavy metals into the environment and impose toxic impacts on living systems. Currently, a major concern is the elevated levels of heavy metals in the environment, which threatens public health as well as marine and wildlife health through the accumulation of heavy metals in foodstuffs. Among those heavy metals, cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb) have the potential to hamper and disrupt the normal functioning of ecosystems. In the present study, nonpathogenic Staphylococcus epidermidis AS-1 was isolated from industrial effluent. The bacterial strain has shown tolerance towards Cd and Pb and accumulates Cd and Pb mainly on the cell surface. The potential of bacterial strain Staphylococcus epidermidis AS-1 might become an effective tool for Cd and Pb removal from effluent. However, it needs further trial and scale-up studies with real wastewater.
