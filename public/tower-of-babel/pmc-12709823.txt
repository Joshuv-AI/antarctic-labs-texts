The rate of complicated appendicitis during a nonpharmaceutical intervention in two territories with different COVID-19 incidence
Authors: G Bauci, M Arigoni, S Passoni, K Ukegjini, L Marti, T Steffen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, many worldwide governmental agencies implemented non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) to contain its spread. Many general surgeons reported higher rates of complicated appendicitis in this period. Reports in the literature need to be more consistent. This study aimed to compare the rates of complicated appendicitis in two Swiss territories with very different COVID-19 incidences during the first lockdown period.MethodsWe performed a retrospective two-center cohort analysis in two Swiss territories during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. We compared the rates of complicated acute appendicitis during the lockdown period from March to June 2020 to those during the pre-pandemic period in 2019. The rate of complicated appendicitis was the primary endpoint.ResultsA total of 393 patients were identified. More patients (215, 55%) were operated on in the pre-pandemic period, compared to 178 (45%) during the COVID-19 pandemic. The rate of complicated appendicitis during the lockdown period was significantly higher than in previous years (39% vs. 28%) (p = 0.021). We found a significant increase from 17% to 32% (p = 0.024) in the highly affected region of Ticino. In patients under 20 years old, the rate of complicated appendicitis increased from 12% to 47% (p = 0.016). The median time to admission in Ticino was significantly higher during the lockdown period compared to both pre-pandemic period and the area of St. Gallen (p < 0.001).ConclusionA higher rate of complicated appendicitis was found in the region most affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. NPIs in a pandemic situation, such as a lockdown period or restricted access to health services, leads to higher morbidity in patients with acute appendicitis.Keywords: Appendicitis, Lockdown, Swiss, COVID-19

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic began in December 2019 in Wuhan, China. The illness spread worldwide over the course of weeks [1–3]. Since the virus is primarily transmitted through person-to-person contact and because the infection of further patients can occur before the onset of symptoms, many countries implemented measures to restrict the movement of people as part of their response plan to the pandemic [4–6]. These measures are called non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs).

The Federal Council, which constitutes the executive branch of the federal government of the Swiss confederation, imposed a 35-day lockdown on the country from March 16, 2020, to April 26, 2020. This lockdown represented the most extensive quarantine in Switzerland’s history of public health. By law, strategies to postpone elective surgery were implemented, and surgical therapy was canceled (if possible) to preserve hospital capacity for COVID-19 patients and urgent non-COVID patients [7–9].

Acute appendicitis is the most frequent indication for abdominal emergency surgery worldwide [10]. Globally, the annual incidence is 96.5 to 100 cases per 100 000 adults [11, 12]. Acute appendicitis can be divided into uncomplicated appendicitis, i.e., phlegmonous, and complicated appendicitis, which includes patients suffering from perforation, gangrenous inflammation, abscess, and putrid or fecal peritonitis [13]. The above classification is most used in the literature and clinical practice based on intraoperative or postoperative histologic findings. Despite many published studies on whether appendicitis should be treated with antibiotics, appendectomy remains the treatment of choice in adults in Switzerland and worldwide [14–16].

Reports suggest that during the COVID-19 pandemic, a significant change in emergency room visits was noticeable. As the number of patients who visited emergency departments due to COVID-19 significantly increased, health systems noticed a difference in emergency department visits for acute care unrelated to COVID-19 [17–20]. In this regard, collecting, sharing, and analyzing emergency Departments’ volume data are necessary for improved coordination of care and distribution of resources during high utilization times. There is evidence in the literature that during the pandemic, the time between symptom onset and presentation to the emergency department was longer and morbidity in patients after emergency surgery for appendicitis was higher [21–25].

The incidence of COVID-19 infections differed significantly during the lockdown period in 2020 between the regions in Switzerland. The maximal 14-day cumulative incidence was 478.9 per 100’000 inhabitants in the canton of Ticino compared to 67.4 per 100 000 inhabitants in the canton of St. Gallen [26]. The impact of the incidence of COVID-19 infections on the rate of complicated appendicitis remains unclear.

We hypothesize that a higher rate of complicated appendicitis occurred in territories with a higher incidence of COVID-19 infections. We consider this relevant since complicated appendicitis is associated with a higher morbidity and mortality rate than uncomplicated cases [27, 28].

This study aimed to compare the rates of complicated appendicitis in two Swiss territories, with significantly different incidences of COVID-19 infection during the first lockdown period.

Methods

The time intervals of interest were pre-COVID-19 and the first wave of COVID-19. The first wave period was defined as the interval from March 1 to June 30, 2020, the last date for which the requisite data were available, which included the first national lockdown in Switzerland. The corresponding pre-COVID-19 period was defined as March 1 to June 30, 2019. The study considered overall outcomes for patients who had emergency surgery for acute appendicitis in each group.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Eastern Switzerland (EKOS 21/066 Project-ID 2021 − 00854).

Study population

All patients who presented with acute appendicitis in the pre-COVID-19 period and the first COVID-19 wave period at the emergency departments of the Cantonal Hospital of St. Gallen and the Cantonal Hospital Organization in Ticino (EOC, Ente Ospedale Cantonale) were enrolled in the study.

Patients were identified retrospectively through the hospital’s electronic records. They were divided into two groups according to the two time periods considered. Both regions (Canton Ticino and Canton St. Gallen) belong to the same national healthcare system with equal therapeutic guidelines, access to medical care, comparable demography, and the same reimbursement system.

The inclusion criterion was appendectomy in laparoscopic or open technique for suspected appendicitis. Exclusion criteria were elective surgery for so-called “à-froid” appendectomy, conservative treatment; patients under 18 years; missing informed consent; and appendectomy as part of a different surgical procedure.

As this was a retrospective study based on histologically confirmed cases, all patients with other final diagnoses (e.g. no appendicitis) were excluded during data extraction. Therefore, no negative appendectomy cases were included in the analysis; no sample size calculation was necessary.

All patients provided informed consent for retrospective anonymous analyses of their medical records.

Definition of outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the rate of complicated acute appendicitis. We compared this rate during the “lockdown” period to the pre-pandemic period. Patient-related factors associated with the occurrence and severity of appendicitis were defined as secondary endpoints. We analyzed whether two public hospitals with very similar resources, yet different regional COVID-19 incidences, have different outcomes regarding acute appendicitis.

Complicated appendicitis was diagnosed if either of the following criteria were met: Gangrene, abscess, appendicolith, purulent or fecal peritonitis, or perforation, according to Di Saverio et al. [29]. Since our dataset was retrospectively derived from pathology-confirmed appendicitis cases, patients with a final diagnosis other than appendicitis were inherently excluded. This explains the absence of negative appendectomies in our study cohort and reflects a methodologically inherent selection bias [30–33].

Data collection and statistical analysis

Patient data were collected from electronic medical records and exported to an Excel database. The data included patient demographic information (age at surgery, sex, height, weight, comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification (ASA)); clarification by imaging (sonography, CT and MRI); diagnosis (complicated vs. non-complicated appendicitis); surgical data (surgical technique, time of surgery); time to admission; and laboratory findings (white blood cell count, C-reactive protein); postoperative morbidity (duration of hospitalization, clinical outcome, Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI), complication grade according to the Clavien–Dindo (CD) classification for surgical complications [34, 35].

Descriptive statistics are presented for patient characteristics, the process of care, and outcome measures for both periods, overall, and stratified by appendicitis severity. Clavien-Dindo and are reported using percentages stratified by appendicitis severity. The statistical analyses aimed to estimate the association between appendicitis severity admission during the first wave of COVID-19 and each outcome after adjusting for the available case-mix measures. Multilevel regression models were developed that included time interval, age (years and years-squared), sex, ethnicity (white, non-white, and unknown), diagnostic subcategories, IMD (quintiles), CCI index as independent variables, and all-cause mortality (binary) and duration of hospital stay (continuous) as dependent variables.

Numerical variables were summarized using means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages. The differences between groups were evaluated using the T-test or Wilcoxon-test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for proportions. For all tests, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS software (version 24.0; SPSS Inc, Armonk, NY).

Results

Patients and clinical data

393 patients who underwent appendectomy fulfilled the criteria and were enrolled in the study. More patients (215; 55%; Group 2019) were operated on in the pre-pandemic period, as compared to 178 (45%) during the COVID-19 pandemic period (Group 2020). During the pandemic phase, 75 (42%) patients underwent surgery in Ticino, and 103 (58%) patients underwent surgery in St. Gallen. The numbers during the pre-pandemic phase in Ticino were 103 (48%), and in St. Gallen, 112 (52%).

The demographic information of all patients are summarized in Table 1. Patients were significantly older (38.6 vs. 43.2 years; p = 0.009) in the 2019 group compared to the 2020 group; they also had a significantly higher body weight (24.6 vs. 25.8 BMI; p = 0.018). Apart from that, the two groups did not differ in demographic and clinical characteristics. Imaging was performed in the 2019 Group in 198 patients (92%) where the pre-treatment imaging was: 127 (59%%) US, 71 (33%) CT and nothing 17 (8%); In 2020, 166 patients (93%) underwent imaging examination: 65% US, 27% CT, 1% MRI and Nothing 7%; with no relevant difference between the two groups. Primary outcome

During the lockdown period, the rate of complicated appendicitis was 39% (n = 69), compared to 28% (n = 60) during the previous year in the corresponding months (p = 0.020). In the whole cohort, complicated appendicitis was significantly more common in older patients (> 70 years) than in younger patients (58% vs. 23%; p = < 0.001). During the lockdown period, a significant increase in the rate of complicated appendicitis was seen in the cohort of patients younger than 20 years (12% vs. 47%; p = 0.016). Comparing the two geographical regions, the rates of complicated appendicitis increased from 38% to 44% (p = 0.350) in the canton that was less affected by the pandemic and from 17% to 32% (p = 0.024) in the highly affected region of Ticino. The difference of complicated appendicitis between the first and the second period was significantly (p = < 0.001) higher in the more COVID-19-affected area of Ticino. Table 2.

Secondary outcomes

In the study group, complicated appendicitis was significantly more common in patients with an ASA score ≥ 3 than in patients with a lower ASA score (69% vs. 30%; p < 0.001).

381 (97%) patients underwent laparoscopic surgical procedures; 12 (3%) patients underwent an open procedure due to severe infection, severe adhesions, or intraoperative complications requiring conversion.

We also found that the time to hospital admission in the more affected area (Ticino) was significantly longer during the pandemic compared to the previous year (1.32 vs. 0.85 days, mean difference ≈ 11 h; p < 0.001). Although the overall difference across all patients may appear small (approx. 4 h), subgroup analysis revealed that the delay was substantially more pronounced in Ticino, the region with the highest COVID-19 incidence. This delay is clinically relevant, as previous studies have linked even moderate delays in presentation with increased risk of complicated appendicitis. To improve the transparency of our findings, we have included range for this variable in Table 1, illustrating the increased variability and distribution shift during the pandemic. (Table 1).

During the pandemic, the perforated appendicitis rate increased from 23% to 29% (p = 0.180). The mean operation time was significantly longer in the pandemic period, with 67 min compared to 60 min in 2019 (p = 0.020). There was no difference in the length of hospital stay between 2019 and 2020 (mean 3.1 days vs. 3.3 days; p = 0.664) (Table 2). For a better analysis of the complications, the CCI was calculated. In the study, however, there was no classical distribution, and we could also not determine any statistical relevance. As an out-of-range complication, we had one death in group 2020 due to sepsis with multiple organ failures from acute cholecystitis and perforated appendicitis.

During the lockdown period, 67 patients (37.6%) were tested on hospital admission for SARS-CoV-2 due to related symptoms. In the whole cohort, only one patient tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 upon access to the hospital.

Discussion

We found that the rates of complicated appendicitis were significantly higher in the region more affected by the COVID-19 pandemic during the lockdown period. We considered NPIs, such as a lockdown period, have relevant side effects on the healthcare system and patient care. To our knowledge, this is the first 1:1 comparison of the rates of complicated appendicitis during a lockdown period between two areas with varying incidences of COVID-19 infection rates. These two regions do not otherwise differ concerning the healthcare system or demography. The only difference was the incidence of COVID-19. We also found that the time to hospital admission in the more affected area was significantly longer during the pandemic than before. We interpret this delay as a possible contributing factor to the increased rate of complicated appendicitis, although our study design cannot establish a causal relationship. In the less affected area of St. Gallen, there was no remarkable increase in delayed admission. As a result, fewer cases of complicated appendicitis occurred. An observed longer operation time may be a surrogate for more complex operative procedures done for complicated appendicitis.

Previous studies have shown contradictory results concerning the role of SARS-CoV-2 in the increase in complicated appendicitis [36–38]. For example, an Italian report found no increase in complicated appendicitis during the first wave of the pandemic, highlighting the discordant findings [31]. Delayed hospital admission was also one possible factor leading to higher rates of complicated appendicitis [39, 40]. Possible reasons for delayed patient admission may be a closure of healthcare facilities, an overload of general practitioners’ offices, a reduction in public transportation, or maintenance of only essential healthcare services during the lockdown period. In addition, the influence of adjacent Lombardy, as the Italian area most affected and tragically prostrated by the pandemic, may have accentuated the feeling in the Ticino area population to isolate themselves at home and avoid unnecessary contact. Several factors may have contributed to the observed increase in complicated appendicitis beyond delayed presentation alone. These may include changes in healthcare access during the lockdown period, patients’ reluctance to seek care due to fear of COVID-19 exposure, an increased threshold for referral by general practitioners, and resource constraints in hospitals. These influences likely interacted and should be considered when interpreting our findings. Our results support the theory that complicated appendicitis results from uncomplicated appendicitis and leads to perforation if not treated in time. Previous studies reported a correlation between delayed surgical treatment and the risk of septic complications, with consequently higher morbidity and mortality [39–42]. We are aware of recent discussions on whether perforated appendicitis is not a consequence of any untreated form of appendicitis [15, 16, 43–51]. In our Study we didn’t find a relevant difference between our Groups about performed Imaging. In the current S1 German guidelines, a CT scan is not mandatory for the diagnosis, because of this we didn’t performed it routinely.

Besides the retrospective design and the absence of randomization, this study has some limitations: The present study could not investigate whether a positive SARS-CoV-2 test influences the rate of complicated appendicitis in patients with acute appendicitis. During the first lockdown period, we did not perform COVID tests routinely as a screening for hospital admissions because the availability of tests was minimal.

Interestingly, the incidence of SARS-CoV-2-positive patients in our 2020 cohort was very low, with only one patient testing positive. With a testing rate of 37.6%, many positive patients may have been unrecognized.

The side effects of NPIs, such as a lockdown period in a pandemic, need to be further studied. We recommend that health institutions and authorities deal with this important topic to be prepared for future pandemic.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that non-pharmaceutical interventions during the pandemic, such as lockdowns or restricted access to healthcare, may have contributed to higher morbidity in patients with acute appendicitis. However, as this is a retrospective observational study, causality cannot be definitively established.
