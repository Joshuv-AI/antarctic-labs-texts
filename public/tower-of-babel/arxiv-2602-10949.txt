Optimal Initialization in Depth: Lyapunov Initialization and Limit Theorems for Deep Leaky ReLU Networks
Constantin Kogler, Tassilo Schwarz, Samuel Kittle

Abstract
The development of effective initialization methods requires an understanding of random neural networks. In this work, a rigorous probabilistic analysis of deep unbiased Leaky ReLU networks is provided. We prove a Law of Large Numbers and a Central Limit Theorem for the logarithm of the norm of network activations, establishing that, as the number of layers increases, their growth is governed by a parameter called the Lyapunov exponent. This parameter characterizes a sharp phase transition between vanishing and exploding activations, and we calculate the Lyapunov exponent explicitly for Gaussian or orthogonal weight matrices. Our results reveal that standard methods, such as He initialization or orthogonal initialization, do not guarantee activation stabilty for deep networks of low width. Based on these theoretical insights, we propose a novel initialization method, referred to as Lyapunov initialization, which sets the Lyapunov exponent to zero and thereby ensures that the neural network is as stable as possible, leading empirically to improved learning.



1
Introduction

Training a neural network consists of iteratively optimizing its weights until a local minimum
is reached. To ensure convergence, beyond choosing a suitable optimization algorithm, one must therefore also consider where the optimization should begin. Consequently, prior to training, an initial set of weights must be selected, which is referred to as the
initialization
of the neural network.

Setting all weights to the same constant value at initialization is undesirable because, in a fully connected MLP, this choice results in identical gradients for all parameters, which prevents the network from learning.
Thus, random initialization of the weights has already been suggested almost 30 years ago
(
13
)
and is now common in practice.

When only the biases of the neural network are initialized as
\equiv 0
, individual neurons still behave differently, allowing effective learning. For this reason, it is standard practice to set all the biases
\equiv 0
at initalization. The most common initialization methods
(
7
;
9
)
therefore only randomly initialize the weight matrices while keeping the biases
\equiv 0
.
In this paper, we establish novel theoretical results for random unbiased Leaky ReLU networks leading to better behaved initializations especially in low dimensions and when the depth of the neural network is larger than its width.

To introduce notation, suppose we have an unbiased neural network of fixed width
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
where a depth-
\ell
layer is defined iteratively for
\ell\geq 1
as

X_{\ell}=\phi(W_{\ell}X_{\ell-1})\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad X_{0}=x_{0},

(1)

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input,
W_{\ell}\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is a
d\times d
weight matrix and
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an activation
function applied component-wise. The question addressed is from which distribution the initial weight matrices
W_{\ell}
should be chosen.

This paper establishes unique and remarkable properties of Leaky ReLU networks that do not extend to other activation functions, making a strong case for their use in specific applications. We proceed with exposing our theoretical results and then outline the resulting novel initialization method.

Law of Large Numbers.

Throughout the rest of this paper, we consider a Leaky ReLU activation function defined for a fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
as

\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)

(2)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. A main insight of this article is that while it is difficult to study the distribution of
|X_{\ell}|
directly, we can instead study
\log|X_{\ell}|
, where
|\circ|
is the euclidean norm.

Indeed, we will show in Theorem
3.3
the following analogue of the Law of Large Numbers: If
\mu
is a distribution on the space of matrices satisfying suitable assumptions and the weight matrices
W_{\ell}
are sampled independently from
\mu
, then there exists a number
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}
such that almost surely

\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}|\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\lambda_{\mu,\phi}.

(3)

In the case where
\phi
is the identity,
X_{\ell}
is simply a product of independent random matrices applied to a vector. In the latter case, (
3
) is well-know and was first established under certain assumptions by
(
6
)
. The novelty in our work lies in the extension to random Leaky ReLU neural networks, which is, to the authors’ knowledge, the first non-linear Law of Large Numbers and is therefore also of mathematical significance. When
\phi
is the identity, the limit in (
3
) is called the Lyapunov exponent and therefore we call
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}
the
Lyapunov exponent
of the neural network.

The Law of Large Numbers (
3
) states that as a first approximation
|X_{\ell}|\!\approx\!e^{\ell\cdot\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}.
This implies that when
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\!\!>\!\!0
, then
|X_{\ell}|
explodes as
\ell
increases while
|X_{\ell}|
vanishes when
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\!\!<\!\!0
. Therefore, the Lyapunov exponent governs the exponential growth rate of the activations.

We will show that in low dimensions all the previous initialization methods have a negative Lyapunov exponent and therefore lead to vanishing activations and inefficient learning. For example, when
d=2
and
\alpha=0.1
, He inizialization (see Section
2
) has a Lyapunov exponent of
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\approx-0.81
. As the dimension increases however, He initialization leads to a zero Lyapunov exponent.

Central Limit Theorem.

The Law of Large Numbers gives a rough estimate on the size of the values of
\log|X_{\ell}|
. We also establish a version of the Central Limit Theorem (Theorem
3.3
): If
\mu
is again a matrix distribution satisfying suitable assumptions, the matrix weights are sampled independently from
\mu
and
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}
is the Lyapunov exponent, then there exists
\gamma_{\mu,\phi}>0
such that

\frac{\log|X_{\ell}|-\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{\mu,\phi}),

(4)

where the convergence holds in distribution.

The Central Limit Theorem (
4
) provides a finer description of
\log|X_{\ell}|
than the Law of Large Numbers, stating that
\log|X_{\ell}|
roughly behaves like a Gaussian with mean
\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}
and standard deviation
\sqrt{\ell\gamma_{\mu,\phi}}
.

Lyapunov Initialization.

Building on these theoretical insights, we propose a novel initialization strategy, referred to as
Lyapunov initialization
. By choosing the weight distribution such that the Lyapunov exponent is exactly zero, we ensure that activations are as stable as possible for deep networks. We provide explicit formulas for calculating the optimal scaling factors for both Gaussian and uniformly sampled orthogonal weight matrices.

According to the Central Limit Theorem, setting the Lyapunov exponent to zero prevents exponential explosion or vanishing in expectation, but it nonetheless establishes stochastic variation. In particular, the logarithm of the activation norm,
\log|X_{\ell}|
, behaves as a Gaussian variable with a standard deviation of
O(\sqrt{\ell})
, causing the activation magnitude
|X_{\ell}|
to fluctuate between
e^{-O(\sqrt{\ell})}
and
e^{O(\sqrt{\ell})}
.

To counter these depth-dependent fluctuations, we propose a refinement to our method termed
sampled Lyapunov initialization
: We suggest to generate
O(\sqrt{\ell})
many candidate initializations prior to the training phase. By doing so, we ensure with high probability that at least one candidate yields an output norm
|X_{\ell}|
close to one, allowing us to select the most stable configuration for training.

Experiments.

We provide experimental evidence that our new Lyapunov methods outperform previous standard initialization strategies for deep networks of low width and also that sampling further improves Lyapunov initialization. From our viewpoint, the reason why the previous methods are not optimal, is because their Lyapunov exponent is negative and far from zero. Therefore, not even small changes in their coefficients lead to a regime where the activations have roughly the right norm, preventing efficient learning.

Outline and Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

•

Limit Theorems for Deep Leaky ReLU Networks:
We prove a Law of Large Numbers and a Central Limit Theorem for the logarithm of the activation norms, establishing that as depth increases, the activation growth is governed by a parameter we call the Lyapunov exponent. (Section
3
)

•

Analytical Formulas:
We derive explicit, closed-form formulas for the Lyapunov exponent for both Gaussian and orthogonal weight matrices. (Section
4
)

•

Lyapunov Initialization:
We propose a novel strategy that explicitly sets the Lyapunov exponent to zero to maximize stability. We further introduce Sampled Lyapunov Initialization to mitigate depth-dependent stochastic fluctuations predicted by our Central Limit Theorem. (Section
4
)

•

Theoretical Analysis of Standard Initialization:
We demonstrate that widely used methods, such as He and standard Orthogonal initialization, yield negative Lyapunov exponents in low-width regimes, precisely quantifying the vanishing of activations in deep networks. On the other hand, in infinite width, He initialization leads to zero Lyapunov exponent. (Section
4
)

We discuss our experimental evidence in Section
5
and conclude the paper with Section
6
.

2
Related Work

Glorot and He initilization

In their seminal work, Glorot and Bengio
(
7
)
proposed choosing all weights i.i.d. from distributions with mean zero and a variance of
\frac{1}{d}
, such as
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d})
or
\text{Unif}[-\sqrt{\frac{3}{d}},\sqrt{\frac{3}{d}}]
. Known as
Glorot (or Xavier) initialization
, this method aims to keep the mean and variance of activations constant across layers. However, this approach relies on the simplifying assumption that the activation function is linear and therefore only works well in practice for activation functions that are linear near zero (i.e.,
\phi(x)\approx x
), which holds for functions like
\tanh
but fails for ReLU and Leaky ReLU.

To address this,
He initialization

(
9
)
was developed specifically for ReLU-based networks. For Leaky ReLU with a slope parameter
\alpha
, it samples weights with a variance of
\frac{2}{d(1+\alpha^{2})}
. While this choice successfully preserves the second moment of the activations, that is ensuring
\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell}|^{2}]\!=\!\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell^{\prime}}|^{2}]
for all
\ell,\!\ell^{\prime}\!\geq\!0
, it does not preserve the mean or variance of
|X_{\ell}|
. In this paper, we demonstrate that in low dimensions, He initialization results in a negative Lyapunov exponent that is far from zero, establishing quantitatively how quickly the activations vanish rather than stabilize. On the other hand, our results prove that He initialization has a zero Lyapunov exponent in high dimensions. We believe that the specific methods and results established in this paper are essential for proving this particular result.

Orthogonal Initialization

(
20
)
proposed initializing weights with random orthogonal matrices rather than Gaussian entries. Orthogonal matrices preserve the norm vectors. While effective for deep linear networks, applying them to non-linear networks requires a rescaling of the orthogonal matrices to account for the activation function’s contraction or expansion (see also
(
17
)
). In this paper, we establish the optimal scaling factor for orthogonal initializations of infinite-depth Leaky ReLU networks.

In a complementary direction, using mean field theory for the infinite width case,
(
18
)
established the optimal scaling rate for certain networks with orthogonal initialization.
(
25
)
further extended these mean field limit based ideas to CNNs, allowing the training of a 10,000-layer CNN without batch normalization.

Lyapunov exponents and random matrix products

From a theoretical perspective, our work is inspired by classical results on random matrix products
(
6
;
12
)
. Lyapunov exponents arising from such products play a central role in probability theory and dynamical systems. Our work differs from these classical results by analyzing compositions of random matrices and nonlinear activation functions. This difference fundamentally changes the dynamics, meaning that existing linear results do not directly apply. To our knowledge, our work provides the first concrete non-linear Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem.

Edge of Chaos

In the literature, the
edge of chaos
refers to a critical phase transition of neural network, where the parameters are calibrated to a regime that maximizes stability and learning performance. Early papers studying this phenomenon were
(
3
)
and
(
4
)
. More recently, edge of chaos has been analyzed in the context of infinite-width networks using mean field theory
(
19
;
21
)
. In contrast, the Lyapunov exponent studied in this paper quantifies the edge of chaos for the regime of infinite-depth neural networks.

Limit Theorems for Leaky ReLU networks

Probabilistic Setup and Notation

For our results to be valid, we require that the weight matrices
W_{\ell}
are independent and identically distributed. With this assumption, the stochastic process
(X_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
is a Markov chain
in the sense that the transition probabilities do not depend on the layer.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of real
d\times d
matrices. We work with the probability space

(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})=(M_{d}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{N}},\mathscr{B}(M_{d}(\mathbb{R}))^{\mathbb{N}},\mu^{\mathbb{N}}),

(5)

that is the space of
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
-sequences endowed with the the natural product
\sigma
-algebra and the product measure coming from
\mu
. So every
\omega\in\Omega
is a sequence of matrices
\omega=(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\ldots)
and for a given
\omega\in\Omega
we denote by
W_{n}(\omega)
the projection of
\omega
to the n-th coordinate. So (
1
) formally means that for
\omega\in\Omega
we have for a fixed
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

X_{\ell}(\omega)=\phi(W_{\ell}(\omega)X_{\ell-1}(\omega))\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{0}=x_{0}.

(6)

While our methods require
W_{\ell}
to be independent and identically distributed, the choice of the underlying distribution is flexible. In particular, our theorems hold provided
\mu
satisfies either of the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on the space of
d\times d
real matrices
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with independent entries. For each
1\leq i,j\leq d
, assume the
(i,j)
-coordinate distribution has a density
p_{ij}
satisfying:

(1)

Boundedness:
Each density is bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}|p_{ij}(u)|<\infty.

(2)

Finite Second Moment:
Each distribution has a finite second moment:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u^{2}p_{ij}(u)\,du<\infty.

(3)

Local Positivity:
The density is strictly positive in a neighborhood of the identity matrix. That is, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for each
i,j
it holds that

\inf_{|u|\leq\epsilon}p_{ij}(\delta_{ij}+u)>0,
where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta defined as
\delta_{ij}=1
if
i=j
and otherwise
\delta_{ij}=0
.

For example, when
p_{ij}
is
\mathcal{N}(m_{ij},\sigma_{ij})
with
m_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{ij}>0
or
\mathrm{Unif}([-a_{ij},b_{ij}])
for
a_{ij},b_{ij}>0
, then Assumption

3.1
is satisfied. However, when
p_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,b]
for some
b>0
, then Assumption

3.1
(3) does not hold and we will actually show that our main theorems fail in this case.

Second, we want to deal with scaled orthogonal matrices, that is for a parameter
\eta>0
, we denote

\eta\cdot\mathrm{O}(d)=\{\eta\cdot Q\,:\,Q\in\mathrm{O}(d)\},

(7)

where the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(d)
is defined as

\mathrm{O}(d)=\{Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})\,:\,Q^{T}Q=QQ^{T}=\mathrm{Id}_{d}\}.

(8)

Orthogonal groups have a natural volume probability measure called the Haar probability measure as defined and discussed in section
B
. We denote by
m_{\eta\cdot\mathrm{O}(d)}
this Haar probability measure on the scaled orthogonal group
\eta\cdot\mathrm{O}(d)
for any
\eta>0
. We also remark that an absolutely continuous measure with respect to
m_{\eta\cdot\mathrm{O}(d)}
(see Definition
B.3
) is by Theorem
B.4
one with a density with respect to the Haar measure.

Assumption 3.2
.

Let
\eta>0
and let
\mu
be a probability measure that is absolutely continuous to
m_{\eta\cdot\mathrm{O}(d)}
with density
p:\eta\cdot\mathrm{O}(d)\to\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
p
is locally positive, that is there exists
\epsilon>0
such that the density is strictly positive in a
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the identity:

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathrm{O}(d)\\
||Q-\mathrm{Id}||\leq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}p(Q)>0.

Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem

We are now ready to state our version of the law of large numbers.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Law of large numbers for
|X_{\ell}|
)
Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying either Assumption
3.1
or Assumption
3.2
. Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})
be as in (
5
),
(X_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
be as in (
6
) and let
\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)
be a Leaky ReLU activation function with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
.

Then there exists a real number
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}
depending on
\mu
and
\phi
such that for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}
and for
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
-almost all
\omega\in\Omega
it holds that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}(\omega)|=\lambda_{\mu,\phi}.

(9)

The convergence is moreover in
L^{1}
, uniformly for
x_{0}
of constant modulus, i.e.,

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}}\left[\bigg|\frac{1}{\ell}\log\frac{|X_{\ell}(\omega)|}{x_{0}}-\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\bigg|\right]=0.

We now formally define the
Lyapunov exponent
of the neural network (
1
) as the number
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}
from (
9
). Next, we state a version of the central limit theorem in this setting.

Theorem 3.4
.

(Central Limit Theorem for
|X_{\ell}|
)
Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying either Assumption
3.1
or Assumption
3.2
. Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})
be as in (
5
),
(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}
be as in (
6
) and let
\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)
be a Leaky ReLU activation function with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
.

Then there exists
\gamma_{\mu,\phi}>0
such that for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}
it holds that as
\ell\to\infty
,

\frac{\log|X_{\ell}|-\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{\mu,\phi}),

where the convergence holds in distribution and
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}
is from Theorem
3.3
.

3.3

\phi(x)=\max(\alpha_{1}x,\alpha_{2}x)

Counterexamples to Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers and especially the Central Limit Theorem as stated above are subtle results. Indeed, we observe that the conclusion of our Law of Large Numbers (Theorem
3.3
) is not valid in the stated form for ReLU activation functions or for the Leaky ReLU case when all the matrices in the support of
\mu
have positive entries. We state the following precise lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

(Counterexample to Law of Large Numbers for
|X_{\ell}|
)
Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})
be as in (
5
),
(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}
be as in (
6
). Then the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
is false under either of the following assumptions:

(1)

(ReLU activation function) When
\phi(x)=\max(x,0)
and the entries of
\mu
are independent and all distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for
\sigma>0
.

(2)

(Measure supported on matrices with positive entries) When
\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
and the entries of
\mu
are independent and all distributed as
\mathrm{Unif}[0,a]
for some
a>0
.

Formulas for Lyapunov Exponent

In dynamical systems, Lyapunov exponents are usually difficult to calculate. It is remarkable that for the most standard initializations and for Leaky ReLU activation functions, we can actually give effective formulas for the Lyapunov exponents. We first state our formulas in the Gaussian case.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mu_{\sigma}
be the probability measure on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with all coefficients being independent and distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for some
\sigma>0
. Let
\phi=\max(x,\alpha x)
be a Leaky ReLU activation function with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}

3.3

\lambda_{\mu_{\sigma},\phi}=\log(\sigma)+I(d,\alpha),

(11)

Theorem 4.3
.

(Asymptotic Expansion)
Let

Discussion

Our experiments show that (Sampled) Lyapunov initialization yields quicker, more accurate training for narrow and deep networks than conventional methods. While our theoretical results ensure that by setting the Lyapunov exponent to zero, the network behaves well before training, empirical evidence confirms this initial stability translates into long-term learning advantages.
We hypothesize that this long-term efficacy is a consequence of the Law of Large Numbers established in this work. When the Lyapunov exponent is zero, small weight updates during optimization result in stable changes in the network’s output. This consistency likely creates a smoother loss landscape, allowing optimizers like Adam to navigate the parameter space more effectively.
In contrast, traditional approaches such as He initialization yield a negative Lyapunov exponent in narrow, deep architectures, causing activations to vanish rapidly. In such regimes, the optimizer needs to overcome exponential decay and any small weight fluctuation keep the output near zero. This pressure may lead the algorithm into sharp local optima as it seeks to escape the vanishing activation and gradient zone, ultimately making further optimization significantly more difficult.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we established a rigorous probabilistic analysis of deep unbiased Leaky ReLU networks by proving a Law of Large Numbers and a Central Limit Theorem for the logarithm of network activations. Our results demonstrate that activation growth is governed by the Lyapunov exponent, a parameter that characterizes the sharp phase transition between vanishing and exploding activations. We revealed that standard initialization methods, such as He or orthogonal initialization, fail to guarantee stability in deep networks of low width because they result in negative Lyapunov exponents. To address this, we proposed Lyapunov initialization, which sets the Lyapunov exponent to zero to ensure maximum stability, alongside a sampled refinement to counter depth-dependent stochastic fluctuations resulting from the central limit theorem. Empirical experiments confirm that these novel methods outperform previous initialization strategies in low-dimensional regimes, resulting in more efficient learning in deep architectures.

The limit theorems established in this paper only hold for Leaky ReLU activations and fail for other activations such as ReLU or
\tanh
. These remarkable mathematical results could be a reason to prefer Leaky ReLU activation functions when working with deep networks.

Haar Measures

In this section, we briefly discuss for convenience of the reader Haar measures on topological groups such as Lie groups.

A
tolopogical group
is a group
G

(g,h)\mapsto gh
and
g\mapsto g^{-1}
are continuous. Recall that the Borel
\sigma
-algebra is the smallest
\sigma

Definition B.1
.

Let
G
be a topological group. A Borel measure
m_{G}
on
G
is called a (left)

if
m_{G}(U)>0
for every open set
U\subset G
and

m_{G}(g\cdot C)=m_{G}(C)

for every Borel set
C\subset G
and
g\in G
, where
g\cdot C=\{gc\,:\,c\in C\}
.

To ensure that Haar measures exist, we need to introduce a few further definitions: A topological space
X
is called

(1)

locally compact
if for every
x\in X
there is a compact set
C\subset X
with
x\in C
.

(2)

Hausdorff
if for every
x,y\in X
there exist open sets
U_{x}
and
U_{y}
satisfying that
x\in U_{x}
,
y\in U_{u}
and that
U_{x}\cap U_{y}=\emptyset
.

Theorem B.2
.

(
8
, Chapter XI)

Let
G
be a topological group that is a locally compact and Hausdorff topological space. Then there exists a left Haar measure. The Haar measure is unique up to scaling, that is if
m_{G}
and
m_{G}^{\prime}
are left Haar measures then there is
\lambda>0
such that
m_{G}=\lambda\cdot m_{G}^{\prime}
.

Moreover,
m_{G}(G)<\infty
if and only if
G
is compact.

In this paper, we work with the topological groups

\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})=\{W\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})\,:\,\det(M)\neq 0\}\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\mathrm{O}(d)=\{W\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})\,:\,WW^{T}=W^{T}W=\mathrm{Id}\}.

These are Lie groups and therefore they are locally compact and Hausdorff. Thus they both have Haar measures that we denote as
m_{\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})}
and
m_{\mathrm{O}(d)}
. Since
\mathrm{O}(d)
is compact, we normalize

to satisfy
m_{\mathrm{O}(d)}(\mathrm{O}(d))=1
so that

is a probability measure.

In the main part of the paper we have also discussed measures absolutely continuous to the Haar measure. These are defined as follows.

Definition B.3
.

(Absolutely Continuous with respect to the Haar measure)
Let
G
be a topological group with Haar measure
m_{G}
and let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
G
. Then we say that

is absolutely continuous to

if for all Borel sets

.

The fundamental property of measures absolutely continuous to the Haar measure is that they have a density with respect to the Haar measure. This is an instance of the Radon-Nikodym Theorem, which we state here for the readers convenience.

Theorem B.4
.

(
(
8
, See Chapter VI)

Let
G
be a topological group with Haar measure

and let

be a Borel measure on

is absolutely continuous to

if and only if there is a density

C.1
Idea of proofs

The proof of the main results is based on similar ideas as for random matrix products, yet with a new viewpoint. Indeed, we decompose
X_{\ell}
into a directional component on the unit sphere and a magnitude component. More precisely, for the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,|x|=1\}
, one studies the spherical distribution

S_{\ell}=\frac{X_{\ell}}{|X_{\ell}|}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

In the linear case, one works with projective space instead of the unit sphere and the observation that the theory can be developed with activation functions using the unit sphere is the novelty of our proof. The core of the proof or our main results then relies on showing that the Markov chain
S_{\ell}
is uniformly ergodic (see Theorem
C.16
) and mixes exponentially fast towards a unique stationary distribution.

One then studies the magnitude component

Z_{\ell}=\log\frac{|X_{\ell}|}{|X_{\ell-1}|}

and observes

\log|X_{\ell}|=Z_{\ell}+Z_{\ell-1}+\ldots+Z_{1}.

The
Z_{i}
are independent, yet, in contrast to the standard Law of Large Numbers, they are not identically distributed. However, the ergodicity of
S_{\ell}
, in other words the rapid forgetting of the initial input direction, allows to deduce our results from a certain abstract Law of Large Numbers (Theorem
C.6
) and Central Limit Theorem (Theorem
C.10
).

We now explain how exactly the ergodicity of
S_{\ell}
leads to the main results, which also reveals the reason why our strategy only works for Leaky ReLU activation functions. Indeed, we observe that for
\phi=\max(x,\alpha x)
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
,
W\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with
\det(W)\neq 0
and assuming
X_{\ell}=\phi(WX_{\ell-1})
the following fundamental equality holds:

Z_{\ell}=\log\frac{|X_{\ell}|}{|X_{\ell-1}|}=\log\frac{|\phi(WX_{\ell-1})|}{|X_{\ell-1}|}=\log|\phi(W\tfrac{X_{\ell-1}}{|X_{\ell-1}|})|=\log|\phi(WS_{\ell-1})|.

(22)

The latter equation holds for the Leaky ReLU activation function
as it is positively homogeneous, that is
\phi(\lambda x)=\lambda\phi(x)
for every
\lambda>0
and
x\in X
. Thus,
Z_{\ell}
only depends on
S_{\ell-1}
and therefore convergence results for
S_{\ell-1}
, using in our case uniform ergodicity, can be translated to limit theorems for
Z_{\ell}
.

In contrast, other standard activation functions such as
\tanh
or
sigmoid
do not satisfy this positive homogeneity, which prevents the proof strategy from working. Indeed, as shown in Section
E
the Central Limit Theorem fails for example for
\tanh

and so that

for all

. Without loss of generality, we replace

is continuous.

The

-step transition density

is given recursively by

and by the convolution

for

We aim to show that there is

and
\delta>0
such that for all

(40)

which directly implies the Doeblin condition and therefore Proposition

follows by Theorem

.

To establish (

that

for all

as then (

and since we have assumed without loss of generality that

is continuous. Note that for

If

.

By induction, we find that

for all

. Since

Proposition C.17

Lemma C.18

(Smallest Singular Value Bound for a Random Matrix)
.

Let
\mu

with independent entries. For each

Then for

Proof.

The smallest singular value

is given by
s_{\min}(W)=\inf_{|x|=1}|Wx|
. Thus, it suffices to fix

As
|W(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}})^{T}|^{2}=d^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\mathrm{row}_{i}(W)|^{2}
for
\mathrm{row}_{i}(W)
the
i

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|W(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}})^{T}|\leq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{d}W_{ij}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{d}\varepsilon\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq d\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{d}W_{ij}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{d}\varepsilon\right).

(42)

Write
S_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}W_{ij}
. Since the

are independent and all distributed according to
p
, it follows that the density

of

satisfies

where

denotes the convolution of functions. By Young’s convolution inequality,

. Since the

Finally, we integrate this density bound to deduce

Using the lemma, we can deduce Proposition

Proof.

(of Proposition
C.17
)
Denote

. By the properties of operator norms,

. This implies that
N(W)\geq 1
. Therefore,
\log N(W)\geq\log(1)=0
.

Since therefore

is a non-negative random variable,
\mathbb{E}[|Y|^{k}]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{k}]
, and we can compute the
k
-th moment using the tail probability formula, that is

\mathbb{E}[Y^{k}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(Y>t)\,dt

This integral is finite if the tail probability
\mathbb{P}(X>t)
decays sufficiently fast. We will show that it decays exponentially.

Note
\mathbb{P}(Y>t)=\mathbb{P}(N(g)>e^{t})
and so we can split the probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y>t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\max(||W||,||W^{-1}||)>e^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(||W||>e^{t})+\mathbb{P}(||W^{-1}||>e^{t})

We will bound the integral of these two terms terms separately.

First, we bound
\mathbb{P}(||W||>e^{t})
. We use the Frobenius norm
||W||_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}W_{ij}^{2}
. Since all norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are equivalent, bounding the Frobenius norm is sufficient.
Since the
W_{ij}
have a finite second moment,
\mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}<\infty
for some

and all

Then, by linearity of expectation:

\mathbb{E}[||W||_{F}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^{2}]\leq d^{2}\sigma^{2}

Using Markov’s inequality for some

and a constant

\mathbb{P}(||W||>T)\leq\mathbb{P}(||W||_{F}>C_{1}T)=\mathbb{P}(||W||_{F}^{2}>C_{1}^{2}T^{2})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[||W||_{F}^{2}]}{C_{1}^{2}T^{2}}=\frac{d^{2}\sigma^{2}}{C_{1}^{2}T^{2}}

C.10
Counterexamples to Law of Large Numbers

We end this section by establishing Lemma
3.5
stating that the Law of Large Numbers also fails under certain conditions, particularly for Leaky ReLU activation functions. We restate the lemma here for convenience of the reader.

Lemma C.21
.

(Restatement of Lemma
3.5
)
Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})
be as in (
5
),
(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}
be as in (
6
). Then the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
is false under the following assumptions:

(1)

(ReLU activation function) When
\phi(x)=\max(x,0)
and the entries of
\mu
are independent and all distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for
\sigma>0
.

(2)

(Measure supported on matrices with positive entries) When
\phi(x)=\max(\alpha_{1}x,\alpha_{2}x)
for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
with
|\alpha_{1}|\neq|\alpha_{2}|
and the entries of
\mu
are independent and all distributed as
\mathrm{Unif}[0,a]
for some
a>0
.

We briefly give an intuitive explanation why these results fail. In the ReLU case, with positive probability
X_{\ell}
is zero and so the Lyapunov exponent could only be
-\infty
, which contradicts the statement of Theorem
3.3
that
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}
is a finite real number. In the Leaky ReLU case and when
\mu
consists of matrices with positive entries, the positive cone
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
and negative cone
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{<0}
are preserved, leading to different Lyapunov exponents depending on the starting point. Moreover, as we show in the proof of Lemma
3.5
, even for certain fixed starting points, the Lyapunov exponent does not converge almost surely to the same number.

Proof.

For (1), we simply note that for every
\ell
, with a positive probability
X_{\ell}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and so
\log|X_{\ell}|=-\infty
with positive probability. So the Lyapunov exponent is not
\in\mathbb{R}
, yet it holds that almost surely
\log|X_{\ell}|=-\infty
for sufficiently large
\ell
.

For (2), we assume without loss of generality that
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>0
as the other cases are analogous. We first note that if we write
Y_{l}=W_{l}\dots W_{1}x_{0}
for the standard linear matrix product, then as analogously to Proposition
C.15
there is a unique
\mu
-stationary on
\mathbb{PR}^{d}
, it follows by Proposition
C.8
that there is
\lambda_{\mathrm{mat}}\in\mathbb{R}
such that almost surely

\frac{1}{\ell}\log|Y_{\ell}|\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\lambda_{\mathrm{mat}}.

Moreover, as the matrices in
\mu
have almost surely positive entries, they map
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
to itself as well as
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{<0}
to itself. So if
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
, then
X_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
for all
\ell\geq 1
and thus

X_{l}=\alpha_{1}^{l}(W_{l}W_{l-1}\dots W_{1})x_{0}=\alpha_{1}^{l}Y_{\ell}

and hence almost surely

\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{l}|\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\log|\alpha_{1}|+\lambda_{\mathrm{mat}}.

(47)

On the other hand if
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{<0}
, then it analogously follows that almost surely

\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{l}|\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\log|\alpha_{2}|+\lambda_{\mathrm{mat}}.

(48)

Since
|\alpha_{1}|\neq|\alpha_{2}|
, the limits (
47
) and (
48
) are distinct. This contradicts the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
, which requires the limit
\lambda_{\mu,\phi}
to be independent of the starting point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\neq 0}
.

Moreover, the Law of Large Numbers fails more strongly for points not in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{<0}
. Indeed, if for example
x_{0}=(-1,1,1,\ldots)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then with positive probability
X_{1}
is in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
and also with positive probability it is in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{<0}
. Therefore, it follows that the events

A_{i}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\,:\,\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}(\omega)|=\log|\alpha_{i}|+\lambda_{\mathrm{mat}}\right\}

have positive probability, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(A_{\lambda_{i}})>0
for
i=1,2
. Thus, for such an
x_{0}
the sequence
\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}(\omega)|
does not converge to the same limit almost surely.
∎

Lemma D.6
.

For

We note

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{exp}(t\phi^{2}\left(Z\right))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(t\alpha_{1}^{2}Z^{2})\mathbf{1}_{Z<0}]+\mathbb{E}[\exp(t\alpha_{2}^{2}Z^{2})\mathbf{1}_{Z\geq 0}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(t\alpha_{1}^{2}Z^{2})]\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{Z<0}]+\mathbb{E}[\exp(t\alpha_{2}^{2}Z^{2})]\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{Z\geq 0}]

where in the second line we have used that the distribution of

Lemma D.7
.

(Frullani’s identity)
For

, it holds that

\log(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-xt}}{t}dt.

Proof.

For convenience write

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(x)

. To determine the constant

, we observe that

, which implies

and so

, concluding the proof.

Appendix G
Calculations for He initalization

In this section, we explain for completness why for He initialization, it holds that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell-1}^{2}|]
, yet neither the mean nor the variance of
|X_{\ell}|
are preserved. This gives another explanation to Theorem
4.3
for the reason why He initialization leads to a zero Lyapunov exponent as
d\to\infty
.

Note that if
Z
is a symmetric one dimensional random variable (that is
Z
and
-Z
have the same distribution), then we have for
\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
that

\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z)^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}|Z\geq 0]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\alpha^{2}Z^{2}|Z\leq 0]=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}].

Denote as in the rest of the paper by
X_{\ell}
a neural network as in (
6
). Assume further that the matrix coefficients
W_{\ell}^{ij}
are all independent and identically distributed like a fixed symmetric distribution of mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
for some
\sigma>0
. We fix for simplicity
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}
with
X_{0}=x_{0}
and write

s_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell}^{j}|^{2}].

Then it follows that
\sum_{j}W_{\ell}^{ij}X^{j}_{\ell-1}
is symmetric because the
W_{\ell}^{ij}
are symmetric and therefore we conclude by using lineary of expecations and independence of the
W_{ij}
that

s_{\ell}=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}(W_{\ell}^{ij}X^{j}_{\ell-1})^{2}\right]=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2}d\sigma^{2}s_{\ell-1}.

Thus we conclude that

s_{\ell}=\left(\sigma^{2}\frac{d(1+\alpha^{2})}{2}\right)^{\ell}.

For He initialisation, we aim to have
s_{\ell}=1
for all
\ell
, which is equivalent to
\sigma^{2}\frac{d(1+\alpha^{2})}{2}=1
or
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d(1+\alpha^{2})}},
leading to (
13
) from He initialization as discussed previously.

The same argument does not carry over for
|X_{\ell}|
and it can be shown experimentally that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell}|]
is not preserved.
