Shrinking Lung Syndrome in Adolescent Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: Diagnostic Utility of Diaphragmatic Ultrasound
Authors: Ayesha Farid, Abdul Basit, Ali Raza Rana, Sharaz Ahmed, Majeed Haq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTShrinking lung syndrome (SLS) is a rare pulmonary complication of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), characterized by dyspnoea, reduced lung volumes, and elevated diaphragms without significant parenchymal lung disease. We report a 17‐year‐old female with SLE presenting with progressive dyspnoea, dry cough, and pleuritic chest pain. Pulmonary function tests showed a restrictive defect (forced vital capacity 39% predicted) with preserved diffusing capacity. High‐resolution computed tomography demonstrated reduced lung volumes with bilateral diaphragmatic elevation and basal atelectasis, without interstitial lung disease. Diaphragmatic ultrasound revealed reduced excursion and decreased thickening fraction, indicating diaphragmatic dysfunction. Alternative cardiopulmonary and neuromuscular causes were excluded, establishing the diagnosis of SLS. The patient was treated with corticosteroids and theophylline, resulting in symptomatic improvement. This case highlights the importance of recognizing SLS and demonstrates the diagnostic value of diaphragmatic ultrasound as a non‐invasive tool facilitating early diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Shrinking lung syndrome (SLS) is a rare and underrecognized pulmonary manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), affecting approximately 0.5%–1% of patients. It is characterized by progressive dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, reduced lung volumes, and elevated hemidiaphragms in the absence of significant parenchymal lung disease [1]. Owing to its nonspecific presentation, SLS remains diagnostically challenging and often requires exclusion of more common cardiopulmonary conditions.

The pathophysiology is likely multifactorial, with proposed mechanisms including diaphragmatic myopathy, phrenic nerve dysfunction, pleural inflammation, and respiratory muscle weakness. Among these, diaphragmatic dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a central feature, supported by imaging studies demonstrating impaired contractility. However, the relationship between systemic disease activity and localized neuromuscular involvement remains unclear [2, 3].

Clinically, SLS typically presents with a restrictive ventilatory defect on pulmonary function testing, often with preserved diffusing capacity, and subtle radiological findings such as elevated diaphragms and reduced lung volumes. As a result, it is largely a diagnosis of exclusion [3].

Diaphragmatic ultrasound has emerged as a useful non‐invasive modality for assessing diaphragmatic function, although its application in SLS remains limited [4]. Here, we report a case of SLS in an adolescent with SLE, highlighting the diagnostic value of diaphragmatic ultrasound and its clinical implications.

Case Report

A 17‐year‐old female presented in the Pulmonology outpatient department with progressive dyspnoea, persistent cough, and intermittent pleuritic chest pain. The patient reported progressively worsening shortness of breath over several weeks accompanied by a persistent non‐productive cough and intermittent pleuritic chest pain. There was no history of fever, haemoptysis, orthopnoea, or prior pulmonary disease. She had a known diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus (ANA and positive Anti‐dsDNA titres) as shown in Table 1 and was maintained on hydroxychloroquine therapy. There was no relevant family history or personal history of chronic pulmonary disease.

Discussion

Shrinking lung syndrome represents a rare but clinically significant pulmonary complication of systemic lupus erythematosus, characterized by disproportionate dyspnoea in the setting of minimal radiographic abnormalities [1]. The present case illustrates the diagnostic challenges associated with this entity and underscores the importance of integrating functional imaging modalities into clinical evaluation.

The pathogenesis of SLS remains incompletely elucidated and is likely multifactorial. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including diaphragmatic myopathy, phrenic nerve dysfunction, chronic pleural inflammation, and respiratory muscle weakness. Among these, diaphragmatic dysfunction has emerged as a central feature [2]. In our patient, diaphragmatic ultrasound demonstrated reduced excursion and diminished thickening fraction, supporting impaired diaphragmatic contractility as a primary contributor to the restrictive ventilatory defect.

The role of systemic disease activity in the development of SLS remains debated. Some studies suggest an association with active lupus, characterized by hypocomplementaemia and elevated anti‐double‐stranded DNA titres, while others report occurrence in clinically quiescent disease. In this case, the presence of Anti‐dsDNA suggests that systemic inflammation may have contributed to diaphragmatic involvement. Anti‐dsDNA positivity alone does not establish active disease or a direct mechanistic link to diaphragmatic involvement; in this patient, the absence of hypocomplementaemia and other markers of active disease suggests SLS may occur independent of overt systemic flare, consistent with prior reports of SLS in clinically quiescent SLE [3]. This highlights the need for further studies to clarify whether SLS represents a manifestation of systemic disease activity or a localized autoimmune process targeting respiratory musculature.

From a diagnostic perspective, SLS is fundamentally a diagnosis of exclusion. The absence of interstitial lung disease on high‐resolution computed tomography, normal echocardiographic findings excluding pulmonary hypertension, and unremarkable neuromuscular studies were essential in narrowing the differential diagnosis in this patient. The preservation of diffusing capacity further supported the diagnosis by differentiating SLS from parenchymal lung disease [4].

Diaphragmatic ultrasound played a pivotal role in this case by providing direct functional assessment of diaphragmatic performance. Unlike static imaging modalities, ultrasound enables dynamic evaluation of diaphragmatic excursion and thickening fraction, allowing differentiation between diaphragmatic weakness and paralysis. Its non‐invasive nature, bedside applicability, and absence of radiation exposure make it an attractive adjunct in the evaluation of unexplained dyspnoea, particularly in resource‐limited settings. Despite these advantages, its use in SLS remains underutilized, representing a potential area for broader clinical adoption [5, 6].

Therapeutically, corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of treatment, reflecting the presumed inflammatory or autoimmune basis of the condition. Adjunctive therapies, including immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab, have been employed in refractory cases. The use of theophylline in this patient warrants particular attention. Theophylline is known to enhance diaphragmatic contractility and improve respiratory muscle endurance, which may contribute to symptomatic improvement in SLS, although evidence remains limited [7, 8]. In our case, the combination of corticosteroids and theophylline resulted in clinical improvement, supporting its potential role as adjunctive therapy.

The novelty of this case lies in several aspects. First, the occurrence of SLS in an adolescent highlights the need for heightened awareness in younger populations, where the condition may be overlooked. Second, the use of diaphragmatic ultrasound provided objective evidence of diaphragmatic dysfunction, reinforcing its diagnostic utility. Third, the favourable response to combined corticosteroid and theophylline therapy adds to the limited body of evidence supporting this approach.

Nevertheless, this report has limitations inherent to single‐case observations, including the inability to establish causality and the lack of long‐term follow‐up data. Further studies are needed to better define the pathogenesis, optimal diagnostic strategies, and therapeutic approaches for shrinking lung syndrome.

In conclusion, shrinking lung syndrome is a rare but important pulmonary complication of systemic lupus erythematosus that should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained dyspnoea and restrictive lung physiology despite normal parenchymal imaging. This case highlights the diagnostic value of diaphragmatic ultrasound as a non‐invasive tool for identifying diaphragmatic dysfunction and facilitating early diagnosis. Prompt recognition and timely initiation of therapy can lead to meaningful clinical improvement.
