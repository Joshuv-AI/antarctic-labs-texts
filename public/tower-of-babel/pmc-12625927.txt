Lived realities of symptom burden among haemodialysis patients: a phenomenological study from rural Pakistan
Authors: Tazeem Akhtar, Abel J Pienaar, Kainat Asmat, Siham Sikander
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasing at an alarming rate worldwide, leading to a growing number of patients developing kidney failure and requiring haemodialysis (HD). HD prolongs life and improves health, but it imposes substantial physical, nutritional, psychological and financial demands on patients. Patients on HD often endure a high symptom burden, which significantly impairs their lives.ObjectiveThe study aimed to explore and describe the lived experiences of patients residing in rural areas of Pakistan receiving HD.DesignA transcendental phenomenology.SettingDialysis unit of a tertiary care hospital in Mirpur, AJK, Pakistan.ParticipantsTwenty village-based patients with CKD receiving HD were recruited through a purposive sampling technique.Data collection and analysisThe data were collected through unstructured, in-depth, face-to-face interviews using an interview guide, as well as observation and document analysis. Thematic analysis used a Colaizzi seven-step method to derive key themes from the data.ResultsFive major themes emerged after the data analysis, reflecting the complex symptom burden experienced by patients: (1) Physical manifestations and loss of independence due to persistent pain and fatigue, leading to increased dependency in daily activities. (2) Emotional and cognitive impact characterised by emotional distress, including anxiety, helplessness and cognitive disturbances such as poor concentration and mental fatigue. (3) Challenges with dietary management and lifestyle adaptation due to difficulties in adhering to dietary and fluid restrictions, further complicated by limited resources, lack of individualised guidance and cultural dietary practices. (4) Financial and logistical hardships, including out-of-pocket expenses, loss of income due to reduced work capacity and geographic barriers, significantly affecting treatment adherence and continuity of care. (5) Post-dialysis experiences and side effects. Patients expressed diverse experiences: some mentioned transient relief after HD, while others described ongoing side effects.ConclusionPatients with CKD on HD may face multiple symptoms that impact physical, emotional, cognitive, dietary, financial and social domains, which potentially contribute to reduced quality of life. Persistent symptoms and limited support highlight the need for holistic, patient-centred care strategies that address their physical, emotional, psychosocial and financial challenges, particularly in rural and underserved populations.

Introduction

The global burden of kidney failure is rising. It contributes significantly to the overall disease prevalence, with chronic kidney disease (CKD) affecting 9.1% of the population—around 700 million people worldwide.1 CKD is associated with a substantially increased risk of premature death, with decreased kidney function contributing to 4.6% of fatalities each year.2 Haemodialysis (HD) is the most widely used form of renal replacement therapy (RRT) globally. It is the preferred treatment for patients with end-stage CKD, helping to prolong survival.3 Globally, over two million patients with CKD are receiving HD therapy.1 CKD also imposes a significant economic burden, particularly as it progresses to end-stage renal disease, with estimated annual costs ranging from US $20 000 to $100 000 per patient.4 5 HD is increasingly utilised in many countries to manage kidney failure, accounting for 2–3% of national healthcare expenditures.6

CKD is an increasing public health concern in Pakistan.7 However, reliable data remain limited, as no large-scale surveys have been conducted to determine its incidence and prevalence in the general population.8 The absence of a national CKD registry further complicates accurate assessment of disease prevalence, HD needs and associated mortality.9 10 Available evidence suggests that the prevalence of CKD in Pakistan is 21.2%, higher than the neighbouring countries such as Bangladesh (17.3%) and India (10.2%).11 According to Rehman and Khan,9 Pakistan ranks eighth globally in CKD prevalence, with approximately 16 000 new cases diagnosed annually. This rising burden is particularly concerning, given the high prevalence of diabetes mellitus (26.7%)12 and hypertension (33%), both of which are major contributors to CKD.13 14

Many patients with CKD require HD, which imposes substantial physical, emotional, psychosocial and financial burdens on both patients and their families. These challenges are particularly severe for individuals living in rural areas, where access to HD facilities is limited and healthcare resources are scarce.15 In Pakistan, there is a lack of qualitative research exploring patients’ lived experiences with CKD, partly because nephrology is not yet a well-established specialty. Moreover, nurses often lack specialised training to provide comprehensive, patient-centred care, resulting in knowledge and skill gaps that may contribute to an increased symptom burden and adverse outcomes.

Patients with CKD experience a range of symptoms that vary according to disease stage and the type of RRT they receive.4 Over time, HD can contribute to a decline in functional capacity, which is often accompanied by multiple physical and psychological symptoms, including weakness, pain, sleep disturbances, depression and anxiety.16 These symptoms not only adversely affect patients’ quality of life but may also worsen prognosis and increase the cardiovascular risk and mortality.17 18

Patients on HD often perceive dietary and fluid restrictions as additional challenges to manage. Patients report systemic, social and personal barriers, such as conflicting dietary advice, cultural incongruities, changes in appetite, social isolation and difficulty maintaining self-discipline, that complicate adherence and undermine their sense of control.19 It is estimated that 31–44% of the HD population experiences poor food palatability, often due to taste changes associated with HD.20 These changes can lead to food aversion, reduced dietary quality and ultimately malnutrition.21

Financial hardship is particularly severe in less socioeconomically developed countries,2 where HD can significantly limit patients’ functional capacity, hinder their ability to work and exacerbate economic strain. Patients in these settings often bear the cost of daily living and medical care, including medications, HD sessions and laboratory tests out of pocket,22 which can lead to debt accumulation or depletion of financial reserves.23 Moreover, financial stress may interact with symptom burden and other health outcomes, creating a cyclical effect that further compromises well-being.11 The symptom burden is notably higher in patients with advanced CKD (Stages 4–5) compared with those in earlier stages (Stages 1–3), who may experience few or no symptoms.1724

Besides financial issues, patients in Pakistan often encounter logistical barriers that greatly impact their daily routines, such as limited access to HD facilities and transportation problems, including long waiting times and difficulty securing seats on buses or vans. These challenges increase stress levels, especially for those living in rural or remote areas, and can hinder adherence to HD schedules, aligning with findings reported by Hallab and Wish.25

Given the growing use of remote and virtual care models, systematic symptom collection has become an essential component of optimal care for patients with CKD on HD.26 27 However, there remains a substantial lack of evidence on the burden of CKD-specific symptoms, with most existing literature drawn from high-income countries and limited qualitative insight from low-resource settings, particularly rural areas of Pakistan. To date, no studies have specifically explored the lived experiences of CKD patients on HD in the Pakistani context. It is important to recognise that each patient’s treatment needs are unique, influenced by the underlying cause of CKD, disease stage, medications, comorbidities and personal, psychological and sociocultural circumstances.28 Despite these complexities, the perspectives and lived realities of rural Pakistani patients remain underexplored, creating a critical gap in the development of culturally and contextually appropriate care strategies.

To address this gap, a transcendental phenomenological approach was deemed most suitable for exploring the lived experiences and contextual realities of village-based patients on HD. This approach enabled the identification of unique challenges and coping mechanisms shaped by the rural environment, informing culturally sensitive and contextually relevant care strategies for this underserved population. Accordingly, this study was designed to capture patients’ lived experiences, ensuring that their voices and perspectives, arising naturally from their embodied experiences, were respected and attentively documented. The findings will be intended to provide healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, with valuable insights to address the challenges faced by these patients and improve renal care outcomes.

Methodology

Research design and participants

The study employed a qualitative research approach, as it is most suited to answering subjective questions,29 providing in-depth explorations, and attempting to capture the essence of the explored phenomena.30 Phenomenology was selected as the methodological framework to comprehend the lived experiences of patients with CKD on HD. This approach allowed the researchers to gain a profound understanding of participants’ subjective lived experiences without imposing preconceived notions on the phenomenon.31 The exploratory nature of the study further justified the use of phenomenology, which aligns well with the nursing philosophy by facilitating an understanding of individuals, their relationships and their interactions with the environment.32 33 Phenomenology is increasingly recognised in nursing research for its ability to generate insights into the lived, contextual realities of patients, supporting culturally and socioeconomically sensitive care.3437

A transcendental phenomenology design was used to explore and describe the lived experiences of village-based patients with CKD undergoing HD maintenance and attending a dialysis centre of a tertiary care hospital in Mirpur, Azad Jammu Kashmir and Pakistan. The participants were recruited through purposive sampling to facilitate a diverse group, a sample of 20 village-based patients with CKD receiving HD met the eligibility criteria. The following were the inclusion criteria for participants: adults aged ≥18 years with a diagnosis of CKD who were receiving HD for ≥3 months and could read and write Urdu (the national language) or local languages (Punjabi and Pothwari). The exclusion criteria were1 children, pregnant women and patients who planned to move to another area or dialysis centre within 3–6 months. A detailed account of the study’s methodology has been published separately as a study protocol.38

Data collection and data analysis

An approval from the Institutional Review Board and Research Ethics Committee of Shifa Tameer-e-Millat University, Islamabad, Pakistan (EC & IRB #0307-23) was obtained. Patients with CKD on HD therapy were recruited from the adjacent villages located within 100 – 150 kilometres (approximately 1.5–2 hours’ drive) from the HD facility. This facility, which provides HD services, is the only public hospital unit in the Mirpur district. It operates with support from a non-governmental organisation and serves a population of 4 045 367 according to the 2017 census.

After obtaining the ethical approval and written permission from the facility, potential participants who met the inclusion criteria were approached and provided with a detailed information sheet. The primary researcher (TA) introduced herself, stated the purpose of the study and described the study procedures, confidentiality measures, potential risks and anticipated benefits. Participants were given sufficient time to review the information and ask questions, ensuring a thorough understanding of the study. Any queries raised by the participants were addressed and clarified before proceeding to the next step. Written informed consent was then obtained from those who agreed to participate. The participants were assured that their participation in this study was entirely voluntary and that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Data were collected using the following methods: interviews, observations and document analysis. Multiple sources for data collection methods allowed for triangulation, thereby strengthening the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings.39 Unstructured, in-depth face-to-face interviews were conducted by TA, using an interview guide (online supplemental file 1) to obtain rich and detailed accounts of patients’ lived experiences with HD. The interview guide was initially developed in English, translated into Urdu, and subsequently back-translated into English to ensure linguistic accuracy and consistency. Two pilot interviews were conducted to refine and maximise the validity of the interview guide. These participants were excluded from the final interviews for the data analysis.

Before conducting the interviews, the TA spent extended periods each day in the dialysis unit to become familiar with the setting and to build rapport with the potential participants. This prolonged engagement facilitated trust-building and helped the participants feel more comfortable and confident about taking part in the study. During this period, TA explained the study purpose, responded to participants’ queries and emphasised the voluntary nature of participation. Confidentiality and anonymity were assured to encourage honest and open communication, thereby creating an environment conducive to sharing personal experiences.

Interviews were conducted at the bedside during HD sessions, respecting participants’ preferences and comfort. Each interview started with a broad, open-ended question designed to prompt discussion and encourage participants to share their experiences in their own words. All questions were asked in Urdu or in local languages (Punjabi and Pothwari), depending on the participants’ understanding and language preference. TA’s proficiency in these three languages facilitated effective communication and helped the participants express their experiences comfortably. TA employed active listening techniques, maintained a supportive presence and used probes when necessary to seek clarification, elaborated key points and gathered detailed accounts. Interviews typically lasted between 30 and 60 min. To further enrich the data, all participants were invited to participate in the follow-up interviews using the same semi-structured interview guide. Four participants agreed, while others declined due to personal reasons or treatment-related constraints. On review, the follow-up interviews yielded overlapping and repetitive information; therefore, only two were included in the final analysis. Each follow-up interview lasted for approximately 20–30 min.

Throughout the data collection process, TA maintained field notes during and immediately after each interview. These notes systematically documented participants’ non-verbal cues, including appearance, body language, gestures and facial expressions, thereby adding contextual depth to the verbal accounts. Within 24 hours of each interview, TA recorded personal reflections and interpretations of the interview process in a reflective journal. These reflexive entries facilitated continuous self-evaluation of the researcher’s role and improved methodological transparency. The integration of field notes and reflective journaling strengthened the depth of analysis and contributed to the overall credibility and trustworthiness of the study.

Document analysis was conducted to collect supplementary information about the demographics of patients, their disease history, comorbidities and their financial situation. In this setting, some patients receive financial assistance through charitable sources such as zakat, an Islamic obligatory form of almsgiving that provides support to individuals in need. The document analysis provided contextual background that complemented the interview and observational data, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of participants’ circumstances.

Interviews were translated into English within 24–36 hours of completion, and the corresponding transcripts were cross-checked for accuracy against the original recordings. Pseudonyms were assigned to protect participants’ identities, with patients labelled as P-1, P-2 and so forth. The data organisation and analysis were conducted in collaboration with an independent co-coder and the research team, following Colaizzi’s seven-step method of thematic analysis.40 The electronic data were stored in encrypted files on a password-protected laptop. In contrast, the hard copies and written materials were securely stored in a locked cabinet within a locked room.

During the analysis phase, all audio recordings and related documents were read multiple times to ensure thorough familiarity with the data. The statements relevant to the research objective were identified and organised under some appropriate themes. Additionally, observations of participants’ demeanour and surroundings provided firsthand contextual insights, which were then combined with interviews and document analysis, facilitating a holistic understanding of the phenomenon.41

Rigour of the study

Lincoln and Guba’s42 evaluative criteria for qualitative studies, which include credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability and authenticity, were used to establish the study’s trustworthiness. Several strategies were employed to ensure the credibility and accuracy of participants’ accounts. These included peer debriefing to enhance analytical rigour and member checking, wherein participants were invited to review and verify the accuracy of their transcribed interviews. A prolonged engagement was also achieved by spending extended time with participants to build rapport and foster trust.

Dependability and transferability were strengthened through a systematic process of detailed description and auditing, which ensured transparency and consistency throughout data collection and analysis.43 44 This rigorous approach allowed for a rich and nuanced presentation of patients’ lived experiences. To enhance confirmability, reflective journaling was maintained throughout the research process to document significant observations, personal insights and critical reflections.45 46 Triangulation was employed through continuous consultation and expert discussions with the research team, helping to maintain objectivity and minimise potential bias, thereby enhancing the overall credibility and trustworthiness of the study.43 45

The study adopted authenticity as a guiding principle, ensuring that the voices of participants were represented with depth, accuracy and fairness, thereby contributing to a comprehensive understanding of their lived realities.47 To minimise researcher bias and maintain focus on participants’ subjective accounts, the bracketing technique was employed, allowing for deep contemplation and preservation of the purity of the phenomenon.48

Moreover, the study was guided by the principles outlined by Stewart et al49 to enhance rigour and crystallisation. A detailed description of the data collection, coding and analysis process, including disassembling and reassembling of data, facilitated a deeper understanding of the phenomenon. The use of the constant comparative method and the development of a transparent chain of evidence further enhanced the reliability and credibility of the study.

Results

All 20 patients who provided informed consent completed the study, with no withdrawals. Participants’ ages ranged from 27 to 67 years. Seventeen patients were receiving HD via an arteriovenous fistula, while three were using catheters. The sample included 11 women (55%) and 9 men (45%). All participants had comorbidities: 19 had hypertension, 10 had type II diabetes, 1 had type I diabetes, 1 had cardiomegaly and 1 had a history of bilateral renal stones. The duration of HD ranged from 4 months to 11 years. Most patients (18; 90%) underwent HD twice a week, while two patients received it three times a week.

Following the analysis, five core themes were identified, reflecting the multifaceted impact of HD on patients’ lives. These themes captured the physical, emotional and dietary aspects of their experiences, as well as the financial and logistical issues associated with the ongoing treatment.

Physical manifestations leading to loss of independence

The data from patients’ accounts vividly described their experiences with physical signs and symptoms of their condition, including persistent coughing, dyspnoea, pain, fatigue, weakness, body swelling, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite and sleep disturbance, leading to a gradual decline in strength and increasing physical dependency. Patients’ narratives are provided below to illustrate their experiences and underscore the debilitating effects of illness on their daily lives:

I became fragile, and at one point in the centre, my legs gave up. I went in a wheelchair. I could not walk; I was so weak, and my condition was such that every person used to feel it. It affects the body, which makes everything in the body non-functioning, and there is no strength (P-1).

I used to have a headache all the time and could not fall asleep. Vomiting started, and my body lost strength. The skin colour also started turning black, and blood deficiency (P-2).

Among physical symptoms, pain emerged as the most prominent and commonly reported complaint. However, no relief was noted even after taking the pain medicine. Below are some quotes that illustrate the significant influence of suffering they had on their lives:

Pain in my body hurts me a lot. I have such severe body aches that my kids keep massaging my legs and body all the time, but I do not feel any difference. I have body aches 24 hours a day before and after dialysis (P-12).

I had pain in my bones, and still, I have severe pain in my bones. These are my bones. I feel like the flesh has been separated from them, and I have pain in them (P-3).

No difference is felt after taking medicine; the body seems injured internally. It feels like wherever a body part touches, it causes pain, which is always present in the whole body (P-6).

Patients also suffered from excruciating joint pain, which rendered them immobile and made them more physically dependent on others to perform daily tasks. Their narratives persuasively convey the depth of intensity and severity of suffering:

I cannot do any work because my weakness is too severe. I feel tired and cannot talk, sit, or stand up (P-13).

I had so much pain in my joints that I could not even sit up. I was completely disabled; I could not stand or move on my own. My mother, wife and brother used to help me – they helped me sit and took me to the washroom. I was unable to go to the bathroom or sit up by myself. My wife also used to bathe me (P-18).

Along with physical pain, decreased functional capacity also affected participants’ ability to perform religious activities. Persistent pain further interfered with their sleep, causing difficulty in falling or staying asleep. As narrated below:

I cannot sleep at night or during the day. I do not sleep at all. If I do fall asleep for a short while, I suddenly get scared and wake up. Then, I could not sleep for the rest of the night. I just want to sleep to calm down; otherwise, I feel like I will go crazy. I am very restless, which leaves me feeling fatigued. Now, my head also feels very heavy. I even tried to sleep here but could not (P-14).

Another patient stated: Earlier, I used to pray and go to the mosque, but now, I can only pray while sitting on a chair and am unable to perform any other acts of worship (P-5).

Emotional and cognitive impact of dialysis

The patients experienced a wide range of emotional issues, including fear, anxiety, uncertainty about the course of treatment, resistance and denial of dialysis, feelings of impending death and grief. The following patients’ quotes portrayed the true essence of their experiences:

I had this feeling that I might die while on the machine (during hemodialysis), just as my brother did (P-2).

Similarly, two other patients shared their experiences when they were first diagnosed and learnt about the need for dialysis. They reflected on their fears, particularly influenced by the death of a close family member during dialysis. Their narrative:

I was not getting a fistula due to fear. I was afraid of dialysis because I did not want to undergo it. I had a cousin who died due to dialysis, so I had the same fear (P-9).

I was agitated and cried a lot, saying, ‘What will happen next? I did not know what would happen during the dialysis (P-20).

Patients experienced not only fear and anxiety but severe emotional distress, which presented as anger, resistance and denial of receiving haemodialysis, as has been expressed in their extracts below:

Before this, I was not an angry person, but since starting dialysis, I have become more irritable. I get angry over minor things, like when she (my wife) gives me food and there is a minor deficiency, like the curry having less spices or salt, I throw it away in anger (P-4).

This is what I keep thinking all the time, and that is why my health is not good. Every month, I get a breathing problem, and I have to go to the hospital. If the doctor had told me that I would have to undergo dialysis for the rest of my life, I would have accepted death instead; I would never have undergone dialysis (P-12).

In addition to their emotional challenges, patients also expressed concerns about cognitive changes they experienced as a consequence of HD. They recounted how their memory worsened over time, making their already problematic condition even more challenging to manage. These accounts highlighted the diverse effects of HD:

I felt that I had become weak and incapacitated, which means I am unable to do anything. Mentally, I used to be very smart. I served for 35 years in Saudi Arabia, where I was an expert in computer work, now I am unable to use my computer. I was an architect and a designer, but now, I cannot do anything (P-1).

Another patient stated, “My memory is weak now. When I see someone, I remember knowing their name, but I keep thinking about it and still cannot recall it. Earlier, I used to remember everyone’s name immediately, but now there is much confusion (P-5).

Challenges with dietary management and changes in eating patterns and lifestyle

Patients also reported challenges adhering to dietary restrictions, changes in food intake and taste and difficulties following medication guidelines. Additionally, they struggled with maintaining water intake within the recommended amount. The following quotes from their narratives depict their struggles and efforts to cope with these challenges.

I have to take many medicines, and after taking them, I do not feel hungry. I can only eat half a slice of bread or two small biscuits. My family makes separate meals for me, but I am unable to eat. The rest is up to Allah – He is the Healer (P-19).

Drinking water is sometimes difficult; otherwise, I follow a strict water routine. I am not lying when I say this, but sometimes I drink a little extra. When I walk outside and feel hot, I drink water at that time – just two sips. However, my BP suddenly rises. I have to control my water intake because drinking more water is causing my health to deteriorate so much (P-20).

The patients shared various methods to control their fluid intake.

I am allowed to drink one litre of water in 3 days, so when I feel thirsty, I put candy or an ice cube in my mouth to quench my thirst (P-7).

The patients discussed their attempt to follow the recommended diet, understanding which foods they could and could not eat. Below is the patient’s excerpt:

I only eat apples and guava and not bananas. They (doctors) forbade me from eating spinach, potatoes, and beef, and I have followed their instructions. My food intake has decreased, and I can no longer eat much; my weight has also gone down. I used to have three meals a day, but now I can only eat once because I have no appetite and do not feel well. I was pretty okay when I was eating well, but now my health is getting worse daily because I have no appetite (P-8).

Patients reflected on their experiential understanding and learning while living with CKD, which led to an awareness of the effects of particular nutrients on their bodies, as one patient described:

Just understand that when my potassium is high, I feel weak; it is like when you apply the brakes to a car, you cannot move it. When potassium levels become elevated, the only solution is dialysis. I have noticed that sitting for long periods also increases my potassium levels. I have also learned that if I eat potatoes or red beans, my potassium increases (P-16).

Patients’ reflections candidly described their struggles in following the dietary guidelines and the intricate relationship between their physical requirements and the strict nutritional recommendations within their available means.

Financial and logistical hardships affecting treatment continuity

Patients described the financial constraints and logistical issues they faced while managing CKD and undergoing HD, including challenges in accessing the dialysis facility and problems with the local transportation system, such as long waiting hours and difficulty getting seats in a local bus or van, especially those living in remote areas of the country. Their accounts, listed below, vividly depict the daily challenges and struggles they face while seeking treatment.

I come here by local transport, and it takes time. Sometimes I have to wait for an hour for the vehicle, and sometimes there is no space in it, so I have to wait for another bus. After getting the machine (hemodialysis), I feel very weak, but what can I do? It is a compulsion, obviously, and I have no other option. I have to take local transport. Whatever my husband earns, it becomes difficult to manage our living expenses because there is no other earner and no help. My husband works on a daily wage, so I travel to and from the hospital alone. That is, I do not ask him to pick me up, drop me off, or stay with me, because if he does, how will he work? (P-6).

The medicines and tests are done from outside. Each test costs around 4000 rupees, so it is difficult for me to manage all this. I was the sole earner at home, but for the last 14 months, I have been bedridden, and now it has become very hard to manage my household expenses (P-12).

Another patient explained how the cost of dialysis had depleted their resources and affected the whole family.

After I sold my plot, I had some money, which I spent on my treatment. In total, I spent around 30–35 lakhs of rupees (3 to 3.5 million) during my treatment in Islamabad. Later, I also sold my shop when I decided to go for dialysis. We used to pay for the medicine ourselves. My daughter is brilliant. She managed to continue her studies for two or three semesters before dropping out, as you know how expensive university education is, and I could no longer afford it. The financial situation of my house is not good, and my friends only help me when I ask them (P-18).

Another patient voiced profound concern regarding the financial impact for receiving a kidney transplant. Although his son and wife were willing to donate a kidney, the cost of the procedure was far beyond their means.

My son and wife said they would donate a kidney to me, but the operation (surgery) is too expensive. If I undergo the transplant, how would I manage its cost? The procedure costs about 30 to 35 lacs, and we do not even have 30 to 35 thousand. If it was around one and a half or two lacs, we could have somehow arranged it, but there are so many expenses that I cannot even imagine (P-15).

Many patients could not continue their jobs, which resulted in unemployment and negatively impacted their capacity to pay for their basic needs, as illustrated in the narrative below:

It was becoming challenging for me to manage both my job and illness at the same time because of the side effects of these medicines. Day by day, I became weaker and had much pain. My job as a salesman involved marketing, which required me to travel frequently to different cities (P-4).

Patients undergoing HD face significant financial and logistical problems, and those who live in rural areas or villages frequently face substantial challenges. Besides, they lose their employment, negatively influencing their ability to pay for their treatment and personal necessities like household expenses and medications.

Post-dialysis symptom experiences

The patients talked about mixed experiences related to the effects of dialysis. For many patients, HD provided significant relief from severe symptoms. Conversely, some patients experienced side effects.

I used to have nausea all the time; I did not feel like eating anything and often vomited. However, since I started dialysis, I have been feeling better. After dialysis, I can eat and drink properly without feeling nauseous. When I miss a session, my water intake increases, and I start feeling short of breath and coughing. When the cough starts again, I can neither lie down nor sit up (P-17).

When I get the dialysis done, I feel very relaxed. Dialysis gives me much relief. However, I can no longer follow the same routine I used to at home. I feel very weak while doing household chores. Now, I cannot work as before because I feel so tired, and it is because of the dialysis, my Hb level becomes very low after each session (P-11).

When dialysis is done, I feel extremely weak because the water is drained from my body. My condition (becomes such that I) feel as if someone is ’squeezing clothes’. There is severe pain in my whole body, and with this pain there is severe fatigue (P-6).

Alongside the physical effects of HD, patients also experienced cognitive and emotional challenges due to the long-term nature of CKD while undergoing treatment. The excerpts below offer insights into the impact of HD on patients’ mental health:

Since my kidneys are being washed, my mind does not work properly. I feel weak, cannot remember anything, and cannot sleep all night due to pain. This has been happening for quite some time (P-9).

It was an enjoyable and colourful life, but after starting dialysis, my life became completely disturbed. It no longer means anything, there is no colour, no activity, you just feel finished (P-7).

Discussion

Patients with CKD on HD often face a high burden of symptoms that negatively affect their lives and contribute to adverse outcomes. In this study, participants described their physical symptoms as difficult and distressing, including persistent pain, fatigue, weakness, anorexia, nausea, vomiting and sleep disturbances, which impaired their daily functioning and increased dependence on others. These findings are consistent with earlier reports documenting the heavy symptom burden among patients on HD.1 50 51 Pain, particularly related to musculoskeletal and bones, emerged as the most frequently reported symptom and was often described as excruciating and poorly controlled by analgesics, a pattern echoed in prior research.52 In addition to pain, profound weakness and physical exhaustion limited participants’ ability to perform even basic activities of daily living, findings supported by dos Santos et al53 Chronic sleep disturbance further compounded their suffering, leaving them persistently fatigued and diminishing their functioning, a finding which is reported by several other studies.4 54 55

A critical factor that may influence symptom burden is the adequacy of dialysis. International guidelines recommend thrice-weekly HD with a minimum single-pool Kt/V of 1.2 per session to achieve adequate solute clearance and fluid balance.56 However, in low-resource settings such as Pakistan, patients often undergo HD twice a week due to high patient load, limited dialysis capacity and financial constraints.57 Previous studies have reported that reduced frequency can lower delivered Kt/V, which is associated with fatigue, fluid overload, pruritus and reduced appetite.56 While this study did not directly assess HD adequacy or frequency, these contextual factors may help explain the severe symptom burden described by the participants.

A notable context-specific finding was the disruption of religious practices due to physical limitations. Since all participants were Muslim, religious rituals such as prayer and Quran recitation were integral to their identity and sense of well-being. Their inability to perform these acts not only deepened their distress but also highlighted the intersection of physical illness with spiritual and cultural dimensions of health in Pakistan.

Beyond the physical limitations, patients in this study described profound emotional challenges, including persistent fears, uncertainty regarding HD outcomes and an overwhelming sense of impending death. These experiences disrupted their social relationships and compounded the psychological burden of living with CKD. Such findings are consistent with a meta-synthesis of lived experiences of patients undergoing HD, which emphasised the emotional toll of HD and its pervasive impact on quality of life.58 Importantly, similar themes have been echoed in Pakistan, such as Shouket et al59 explored the HD experiences of patients and reported that feelings of helplessness, hopelessness and existential anxiety were deeply intertwined with dependence on the ‘machine’ for survival. The present study reinforces these findings, highlighting that patients in rural Pakistan suffer comparable emotional struggles, underscoring the need for culturally sensitive support interventions to help patients manage the emotional burden of HD.

Participants in this study also reported cognitive challenges, such as memory loss and difficulty concentrating, which illustrated their ongoing psychological apprehensions. These findings align with evidence that demonstrates that depression and emotional distress exacerbate cognitive impairment among patients on HD.60

In this study, patients on HD believed that controlling and managing their food intake could help lessen their symptoms and improve their health and well-being. However, many individuals cannot adhere to diet and fluid restrictions due to personal preferences for food, as well as social and economic constraints. The participants cited the cost of purchasing nutritious foods, such as meat and fruits, as a significant barrier to adhering to dietary recommendations, as well as cost-related challenges, including job loss and resource depletion. These findings are consistent with those reported in a previous review.61

Patients found it particularly difficult to manage their thirst and adhere to strict fluid restrictions, often resorting to coping strategies such as sucking on ice cubes or candies. This finding is consistent with Stevenson et al19 who described similar strategies among HD patients in other contexts. However, in Pakistan, where extreme summer heat is common, these challenges are further intensified, potentially placing patients at an even higher risk of non-adherence.62

In addition, patients reported that their food preferences and eating habits were altered due to symptoms such as nausea, vomiting and decreased appetite. Some even developed cravings for spicier foods, yet were unable to enjoy their traditional meals due to dietary restrictions on spices and salt. Schrauben et al63 observed comparable changes in appetite and preferences; however, in the Pakistani context, these restrictions are particularly burdensome, given the cultural significance of flavourful meals.

Another important finding was that patients had limited food choices when eating outside the home, which often conflicted with dietary recommendations. Despite this, many participants expressed a strong preference for communal eating practices, a deeply ingrained cultural and social norm in Pakistan. To accommodate these restrictions, families frequently adjusted their meal preparation to align with the patient’s dietary needs. This adaptation mirrors findings reported by Anees et al64 in Pakistani settings, highlighting the crucial role of family support in dietary adherence. Tailoring dietary advice to accommodate local food preferences, economic constraints and communal eating traditions may improve adherence and reduce the dietary burden for HD patients and their families.

The study’s findings revealed that HD affected patients’ lives in numerous ways, with significant repercussions on their daily functioning and overall well-being. Financial constraints emerged as one of the most substantial challenges, directly influencing the continuity of treatment. Patients reported difficulties affording essential expenses, such as medications, regular blood tests often conducted at private laboratories and household expenditures. These challenges were especially severe for families in which the patient was the primary breadwinner, further compounding financial stress. These findings are consistent with those reported in another Pakistani study,52 which similarly highlighted that the high cost of HD sessions, medications and transport posed substantial barriers to adherence. Due to HD scheduling, most patients in this study had lost their jobs and were having difficulty finding employment, given their limited physical abilities, and continued to face challenges in maintaining employment. This mirrors findings from previous studies,6567 highlighting that unemployed patients faced a more significant financial burden than those who remained employed.

Additionally, patients in this study face logistical challenges, including limited access to the HD facility through the local transportation system and lengthy waiting times for seats. This significantly affects their daily lives. All these problems further hampered their lives, especially for those living in villages or remote areas of the country. These findings are consistent with studies from Pakistan and similar contexts.68 69 Taken together, the findings suggest that financial burden is not only a medical concern but also a pressing socioeconomic issue for HD patients and their families in Pakistan. This highlights the urgent need for policy-level interventions such as subsidised medications, affordable laboratory services and social support schemes to reduce the financial strain associated with HD care.

Patients in this study experienced side effects of HD, including fatigue, irregular sleep patterns and chronic discomfort, as the therapy frequently limited their functional capacity to carry out daily tasks on their own, leading to loss of independence following HD. Chowdhury and Sharma70 also reported comparable side effects associated with HD therapy.

Overall, the accounts of HD patients in this study revealed a range of complex and interrelated difficulties that affected many aspects of their everyday lives. Findings of the study highlight the severity and multifaceted nature of symptom burden experienced by patients on HD. The findings underscore the need to establish multidisciplinary care teams and incorporate early symptom assessment and management strategies into clinical guidelines and routine care protocols. Given that physical symptoms tend to intensify over time, future studies involving larger, multicentre cohorts and mixed-method approaches are recommended to validate and expand on these results, as well as to evaluate targeted interventions using larger, more diverse samples. Additionally, the development of supportive health policies must include reforms to reimbursement systems that reduce financial barriers and improve access to care services.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Most patients in the study setting received HD twice a week rather than the standard thrice-weekly regimen, primarily due to high patient load and limited dialysis capacity. This contextual factor may have influenced patients’ experience and reported symptom burden; however, the study did not examine the relationship between HD frequency and symptoms. Future research should incorporate standardised measures of HD adequacy and treatment frequency to better understand their influence on symptom burden and overall patient outcomes. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may have limited the diversity of experiences captured, potentially narrowing the scope of the results. Finally, the study was conducted in a single rural setting, which may constrain the transferability of the findings to other geographical, institutional or cultural contexts.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the complexity and severity of the symptom burden faced by patients with CKD on HD. This burden appears to impact functional capacity, mental health and social well-being, potentially leading to a lower quality of life. By revealing these challenges, the study provides valuable insights into an underexplored area of the literature and enhances the current understanding of the lived experiences of HD.

Patient and public involvement

Haemodialysis patients were active participants in data collection, with the exclusion of the general public. Interview questions were meticulously designed based on participants’ experiences and preferences to ensure a deep understanding of their lived experiences. The research was firmly grounded in participants’ lived experiences, with a focus on benefiting the broader community. Participants were also involved in member checking, which involves confirming what had been shared by the participant. Additionally, participants may be able to disseminate study findings within their community.
