Different radiomics models in predicting the malignant potential of small intestinal stromal tumors
Authors: Yuxin Xie, Chongfeng Duan, Xuzhe Zhou, Xiaoming Zhou, Qiulin Shao, Xin Wang, Shuai Zhang, Fang Liu, Zhenbo Sun, Ruirui Zhao, Gang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo explore the feasibility of different radiomics models for predicting the malignant potential of small intestinal stromal tumors (SISTs), and to select the best radiomics model.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 140 patients with SISTs was conducted. Radiomics features were extracted from CT-enhanced images. Support vector machine (SVM), Decision tree (DT), Conditional inference trees (CIT), Random Forest (RF), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), Back-propagation neural network (BPNet), and Bayes were used to construct different radiomics models. The clinical data and CT performance were selected using univariate analysis and to construct clinical model. Nomogram model was developed by combining clinical data and radiomics features. Model performances were assessed by using the area under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). The models’ clinical values were assessed by decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsA total of 1132 radiomics features were extracted. Among radiomics models, SVM was better than DT, CIT, RF, KNN, BPNet, Bayes because it had the highest AUC with a significant difference (P<0.05). The AUC of the clinical model was 0.781. The AUC of the radiomics model was 0.910. The AUC of nomogram model was 0.938. Clinical models had the lowest AUC. Nomogram AUC were slightly higher than radiomics model, but the difference was not significant (P=0.48). The DCA of the nomogram model and radiomics model showed optimal clinical efficacy.ConclusionsThe model constructed with SVM method was the best model for predicting the malignant potential of SISTs. Radiomics model and nomogram model showed high predictive value in predicting the malignant potential of SISTs.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common primary mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract, originating from pacemaker Cajal cells and their analogous cells[1], [2]. Gastrointestinal stromal tumors are soft tissue tumors with malignant potential. According to the modified criteria of the National Institutes of Health risk stratification proposed by Joensuu[3], gastrointestinal stromal tumors can be classified into four risk levels: very low risk, low risk, intermediate risk, and high risk.

The small intestine is the second most common site of GISTs. Small intestinal stromal tumors (SISTs) account for approximately 30 %-35 % of GISTs[4]. SISTs have no specific clinical symptoms in the early stages due to the anatomical structure of the small intestine. Compared to gastric stromal tumors, clinically diagnosed SISTs are larger, more aggressive, and have a higher malignant potential [5], [6], [7]. Gastric stromal tumors>5 cm in size and with mitotic count >5 mitoses/50HPF are considered to be at high risk for metastasis. All Small intestinal stromal tumors with mitotic count >5 mitoses/50HPFs independent of size are regarded as high risk[8]. The overall frequency of malignancy in SISTs is higher than that of gastric stromal tumors[9]. Therefore, it is crucial to diagnose SISTs in the early stage. There are many screening methods for SISTs, such as: capsule endoscopy, electronic small bowel microscopy and CT small bowel imaging, however these examination methods have limitations in detecting SISTs.

Radiomics is a technique that can extract and select quantitative features in medical images, which is useful in aiding prediction the biological behavior of tumors [10]. Radiomics is widely used in clinical practice[11], [12], [13], [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that radiomics can quantitatively and objectively assess the malignant potential of gastrointestinal stromal tumors[15], [16], [17], [18].Radiomics models based on different machine learning methods have different prediction performances[19], [20], [21]. Given the critical role that accuracy plays in guiding high-quality clinical decision-making, it is imperative to explore the differences between various modeling approaches and evaluate their performance in predicting GIST malignancy.

To our knowledge, there is no study exploring the application of radiomics models based on different machine learning methods in predicting the malignant potential of SISTs. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare the predictive performance of several commonly used modeling methods based on CT small bowel imaging and select the optimal radiomic model for predicting the malignant potential of SISTs. Additionally, the study includes the development of a clinical model, radiomic model, and radiomic nomogram to predict the malignant potential of SISTs.

Materials and methods

Patient

This is a retrospective study, approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital, which waived the requirement of informed consent. A total of 140 patients with pathologically confirmed SISTs between January 2013 to January 2021 were selected from our hospital's HIS system database. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: 1) patients who underwent surgical treatment and had a postoperative pathological diagnosis of small intestinal stromal tumor; 2) patients who underwent CT small bowel imaging within one week before surgery; and 3) patients with good quality CT-enhanced images that could be used for hanging up the region of interest. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Patients receiving preoperative drug therapy, such as imatinib; 2) patients with recurrence of small intestinal stromal tumor. The patient selection flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Flowchart for selecting the study population.Fig. 1

Flowchart for selecting the study population.

Computed tomography image acquisition

All patients in this study underwent enhanced scanning. Philips 256-layer spiral CT (Philips, Best, The Netherlands) and dual-source CT (Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany) scanners were used for scanning. Scanning parameters: tube voltage 120 KV, tube current 300 mA, a 512 × 512 matrix, and layer thickness 5 mm. The scan area ranged from the top of the diaphragm to the superior border of the pubic symphysis. Iodine contrast agent (350 mg/ml, 1.5 ml/kg) was injected through elbow vein at a flow rate of 4 ml/s, with the scan time of 35 s for the arterial phase and 70 s for the venous phase.

Construction of the clinical model

The images were analyzed for consistency by two radiologists with 15 years of experience in abdominal imaging. Two radiologists independently evaluated the CT imaging findings of tumor size, shape, margins, and enhancement pattern. Tumor size was determined as the maximum diameter seen on axial CT images. The irregular shape was defined by a lobulated or mass-like appearance, while round or round-like tumors were considered regular. Tumor margins were categorized as well-defined or poorly-defined with surrounding normal tissue, and enhancement pattern was characterized as homogeneous or heterogeneous. Clinical information, including patient name, age, and gender, was obtained from the electronic medical records. Univariate analysis was performed on clinical data and CT performances of two groups of patients. The clinical model was constructed using significant variables from univariate analysis.

Tumor segmentation and Radiomics feature extraction

IBEX software (http://bit.ly/IBEX) was used to delineate region of interest (ROI) and extract radiomics features. The ROI was delineated as close as possible to the inner edge of the tumor. The flowchart is shown in Fig. 2. The radiologists who drew the ROI were blinded to the risk stratification of the patients with SISTs. IBEX software extracted 7 categories texture parameter, including First Order, Shape, Gray-Level Dependency Matrix, Gray-Level Cooccurrence Matrix, Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix, Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix, and Neighborhood Gray-Tone Difference Matrix. Each category contains a different texture parameter. To evaluate the consistency of the texture parameters, the intraclass correlation coefﬁcient (ICC) was used. A total of 40 cases of SISTs (20 low-risk and 20 high-risk groups) were randomly selected. The ICC was calculated for each texture parameter to test the reproducibility of features. The ICC was calculated based on a single rater, absolute agreement, two-way random-effects model[22]. ICC values were calculated for each texture parameter, and texture parameters with high consistency (ICC>0.8) were selected for future analysis.Fig. 2Flowchart of this study.Fig. 2

Flowchart of this study.

Radiomics feature selection and modeling

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to reduce the dimensionality of the texture parameters and retain the most valuable radiomics feature. Seven different modeling methods, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree (DT), Conditional Inference Trees (CIT), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Back-Propagation Neural Network (BPNet), and Bayes, were employed to construct seven radiomics models using the feature parameters obtained after dimensionality reduction. The accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, area under the curve (AUC), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of these models were compared to select the best radiomics model for preoperative predictive of risk stratification of SISTs.

Development of a nomogram model and assessment of the different models

LASSO logistic regression model with ten-fold cross-validation were used for feature selection (Fig. 3). Nomogram was constructed by combining significant indicators of the clinical model and Rad-score significant variables. The calibration curves were used to evaluate the calibration of models, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess model fit. The area under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and Delong test were applied to assess the diagnostic performance of prediction model in predicting the risk stratification of SISTs. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed for each model to assess the clinical efficacy of these three models in tumor classification through the net benefit rate for different threshold probabilities.

Statistical Analysis

Python software (version 3.5.6; http://www.python.org) was used for statistical analysis. Independent samples t-test was applied to continuous variables. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. P<0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Radiomics feature extraction, selection and radiomics signature construction

A total of 1132 radiomic features were extracted from each ROI, and among these features, 903 (79.77 %) features had high stability (ICC>0.8). These 903 features were input into the LASSO regression model to select the eleven most valuable radiomic features. These selected features were used to construct radiomics models.

The sensitivity, accuracy, and specificity of the seven radiomics models (SVM, DT, CIT, RF, KNN, BPNet, and Bayes) for predicting risk stratification of SISTs are shown in the Table 2. The SVM model had the highest AUC of 0.826 (95 %CI 0.670–0.982), with sensitivity, accuracy, specificity, PPV and NPV of 0.94, 0.8, 0.64, 0.75 and 0.9, respectively. The BPNet model has the highest specificity (0.71) and has the same accuracy as the SVM model, but it has lower AUC values (0.817) and sensitivity (0.88) than the SVM model. RF, KNN and Bayes models have the same sensitivity as the SVM model, but their AUC values (RF, KNN, Bayes: 0.797, 0.723, 0.817), accuracy (0.73, 0.77, 0.73), and specificity (0.50, 0.57, 0.50) are lower than the SVM model. The CIT model has the lowest AUC value (0.681), accuracy (0.63), specificity (0.36) and it has lower sensitivity (0.88) than SVM, RF, KNN and Bayes. The ROC curve showing the performance of the models is shown in Fig. 4. We compared the significant differences between the ROC curves of the models using the Delong test, as shown in Table 3. Among those with statistical differences were SVM and DT (Z = - 3.70920, P = 0.00020), SVM and CT (Z = −3.15020, P = 0.00160), SVM and RF (Z = −3.89060, P = 0.00010), SVM and KNN (Z = −3.19900, P = 0.00130), and SVM and BPnet (Z =-3.96900, P=0.00007), SVM and Bayes (Z=-3.85700, P=0.00010), CIT and BPnet (Z=-1.98620, P=0.04700). No significant difference among DT, CIT, RF or KNN (P>0.05). The results showed that, in addition to the CIT model, the SVM, DT, RF, KNN, BPnet and Bayes models all demonstrated good predictive performance in non-invasively predicting the malignant potential of SISTs before surgery, with SVM model being the best predictive model. Compared to DT, CIT, RF, KNN, BPnet and Bayes, SVM exhibited good stability and efficiency. The diagnostic performance of models varies between modelling methods, and selecting the predictive model with the highest diagnostic performance can help to improve the accuracy of clinical judgement of the malignant potential of SISTs. Discussion

SISTs have the malignant tendency. High and intermediate risk patients require imatinib treatment to reduce the risk of recurrence, while low-risk patients do not require medication[5], [24]. Therefore, accurate preoperative prediction of risk stratification for SISTs is crucial for individualized treatment and prognosis assessment. This study unveiled that radiomics models based on enhanced CT can predict the malignant potential of SISTs, and SVM is superior to other classifiers. In addition, we combined clinical and radiomics features to develop a nomogram to predict the malignant potential of SISTs with satisfactory outcomes.

Radiomics is a method to construct radiomics models for the diagnosis of diseases by computerized extraction of radiomic textures from lesions. With the rapid development of radiomics technology, it has been widely used in studies on predicting the malignant potential of GISTs. Chen et al.[15] constructed a three-level prediction model for the risk of GISTs and obtained a model AUC of 0.84, which showed high predictive ability. The prediction model built by Zhang et al.[25] also has good prediction performance on the training cohort (AUC: 0.869) and the external validation cohort (AUC: 0.809). These studies suggest that radiomics has good predictive efficacy in grading the malignant potential of GISTs. The present study also confirmed that radiomics has good efficacy in predicting the malignant potential grading of SISTs. However, most previous studies constructed prediction models based on a single modeling method. Although these prediction models have high diagnostic performance, better models are necessary. The diagnostic efficacy of models constructed by different modeling methods is variable. Zheng et al.[26] constructed three radiomics models using logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF) to predict the benignity and malignancy of parotid tumors with AUCs of 0.798, 0.840, and 0.779, respectively, the results showed that SVM has higher predictive efficacy. Bing Mao's[27] study constructed five radiomics models (k-nearest neighbor, logistic regression, multilayer perceptron, random forest, and support vector machine) for preoperative classification of primary and metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma. Results showed that logistic regression had the highest AUC (0.816) and accuracy (0.843). These studies showed that the predictive performance of radiomics models constructed by different machine learning methods. In our study, we used seven machine learning methods (SVM, DT, CIT, RF, KNN, BPNet, and Bayes) to generate models for predicting the malignant potential of SISTs. All models can be used to predict the malignant potential of SISTs, but choosing the best predictive model is necessary as it helps to improve the accuracy of predicting the disease in the clinic. We used the DeLong test to compare the AUC values among models. SVM was better than DT, CIT, RF, KNN, BPNet, Bayes as it had the highest AUC value with a significant difference. In addition, SVM had the highest accuracy and sensitivity. SVM model was more effective than DT, CIT, RF, KNN, BPNet, and Bayes models. SVM is characterized by wide applicability and robustness. It has good stability and efficiency. Relevant studies have shown that SVM can demonstrate similar performance to large samples in the case of small samples[28], [29].BPNet was better than CIT because BPNet had a higher AUC value with a significant difference. There was no significant difference among DT, RF, KNN, Bayes, and CIT. These findings confirmed that the SVM model was superior to other models and was the best model for predicting the malignant potential of SISTs.

A previous study found that some clinical imaging performances were associated with risk stratification of GISTs[30]. This was consistent with our findings. Our study found that larger tumor size, irregular shape, and inhomogeneous enhancement were risk factors for SISTs of high malignant potential. Therefore, we constructed a clinical model using these three factors, which had an AUC of 0.781 (95 % CI: 0.5692–0.9933). Previous studies had shown that combining clinical risk factors and radiomic features could improve the predictive efficacy of the model[23]. To our knowledge, ‌there were no nomogram studies addressing only the risk stratification of SISTs‌. We combined clinical risk factors with rad-scores to construct a nomogram model for predicting the malignant potential. Our nomogram model had an AUC of 0.938 (95 % CI: 0.8349–1.0000), which was higher than the clinical and radiomic models. AUC values were significantly higher in the nomogram and radiomics models than in the clinical model. nomogram AUC were slightly higher than radiomics model, but the difference was not significant. DCA curves demonstrated that the nomogram and radiomics model had a greater net benefit compared to the clinical models. The nomogram demonstrated the best predictive efficacy. Radiomics model also showed good predictive efficacy. Although radiomics model had good predictive capability, clinical data were also important. Only nomogram constructed by combining radiomics features and clinical data can accurately predict SISTs.

The study has some limitations that need to be acknowledged. Firstly, this study was a retrospective study and selection bias could not be avoided, therefore prospective studies are required to confirm the findings. Second, this study was a single-center study and lacked external validation, and we plan to conduct a multicenter study with a larger cohort to validate the results in the future. Thirdly, the CT images used in this study were obtained from different CT devices, and variations in the scanning devices could impact the extracted radiomic parameters. However, we reduce the impact of these confounding factors by preprocessing the images. Fourthly, the sample size of this study was small. However, with 1132 radiomic features extracted, a larger sample size would have yielded better results. In the future, we plan to include a larger sample size in the study to eliminate this limitation.

This study demonstrated that the radiomics model had good predictive performance and was capable of predicting the malignant potential of SISTs preoperatively and non-invasively. However, this was only a preliminary study. In the future, large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are needed to verify the validity and generalizability of radiomics. In addition, a standardized process for radiomics analysis needs to be established in future studies to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results. Through prospective studies and the establishment of standardized processes, radiomics is expected to become an important tool in routine clinical practice in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, we utilized seven different machine learning methods to construct radiomic models for predicting the malignant potential of SISTs. We then compared the diagnostic performance of these models and found that SVM performed best. In this study, we developed clinical models, radiomics models and nomogram for predicting the malignant potential of SISTs, and the radiomics models and nomogram demonstrated high predictive performance. This study demonstrates that radiomics can non-invasively predict the malignant potential of SISTs preoperatively, which can help clinicians to develop individualized treatment plans for their patients.
