PrivacyReasoner: Can LLM Emulate a Human-like Privacy Mind?
Yiwen Tu, Xuan Liu, Lianhui Qin, Haojian Jin

Abstract
This paper introduces PrivacyReasoner \mathrm{PrivacyReasoner} , an AI-agent design for simulating how individual users form privacy concerns in response to real-world news. Moving beyond population-level sentiment analysis, PrivacyReasoner \mathrm{PrivacyReasoner} integrates privacy and cognitive theories to model user-specific privacy reasoning grounded in personal comment histories and contextual cues. The agent reconstructs each user’s “privacy mind,” dynamically activates context-relevant privacy memory through a cognitively motivated contextual filter, and generates synthetic comments reflecting how that user would likely respond to new privacy scenarios. A complementary LLM-as-a-Judge evaluator, calibrated against an established privacy concern taxonomy, quantifies the faithfulness of the generated reasoning. Experiments on real-world Hacker News discussions show that PrivacyReasoner \mathrm{PrivacyReasoner} outperforms baseline agents in privacy concern prediction and captures transferable reasoning patterns across domains including AI, e-commerce, and healthcare.

Introduction

Public reactions to privacy-related news are often rapid, polarized, and difficult to predict, yet they can have an outsized impact on policy, product adoption, and institutional trust. A notable example is Apple’s CSAM (Child Sexual Abuse Material) scanning feature, which aimed to detect abusive content on users’ devices before uploading to iCloud
16
. Although technically constrained and privacy-aware by design, the feature triggered a widespread backlash. Users feared the normalization of on-device surveillance and potential misuse by authoritarian governments. Apple ultimately suspended the rollout. This outcome wasn’t a failure of engineering—it was a failure to anticipate how user communities would interpret and respond to the privacy-related design decisions. Similar dynamics have arisen in numerous backlashes against commercial products and in broader debates over government surveillance.

One of the most widely adopted theories in modeling privacy is Nissenbaum’s contextual integrity
17
, which frames privacy as the appropriateness of information flows within specific contexts. For example, people were willing to share their location data to help cities plan bus routes or obtain traffic information, but were reluctant to share the same data for ads or for maps showing their travel patterns
3
.
Previous research often adopts this perspective to study the normative expectations of privacy within such contexts. For example, researchers systematically vary contextual factors such as data type, recipient, and purpose to examine how these variables influence people’s judgments of whether an information flow is appropriate
1
.
However, research from this perspective has largely focused on population-level patterns, offering a limited understanding of the individualized cognitive processes through which people reason about and articulate their own privacy concerns.

This paper introduces
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
, an AI-agent design for modeling how individual users form privacy concerns in response to real-world news.
With the rapid advancement of LLM-based agents, their capacity for complex reasoning and behavioral simulation has grown significantly

Can an LLM-based agent emulate a “privacy mind” analogous to that of a human — that is, can it faithfully mimic human privacy concerns in context?

Our key innovation is to operationalize Contextual Integrity

at the level of individual reasoning rather than population averages.
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}

(
24

. When exposed to a new privacy-related event, the system dynamically activates the most relevant privacy orientations to generate a synthetic comment that simulates how that user would respond. This approach moves beyond aggregate attitude modeling by computationally instantiating context-dependent privacy reasoning for individual users, enabling fine-grained, interpretable predictions of their expressed concerns.

We test our approach with a curated real-world dataset of privacy-related discussions from
Hacker News

(
26

, a technical forum where users debate issues such as surveillance, data governance, and algorithmic decision-making. To quantitatively evaluate reasoning fidelity, we implement a Privacy Concern Judge, an LLM-as-a-judge model calibrated against an established privacy concern taxonomy
(
27
)
.

Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that our
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
achieves significant performance improvement over baseline models and demonstrates strong generalizability across both domains and individuals. Beyond empirical gains, personalized privacy reasoning opens up a broad range of practical applications. In design prototyping, development teams can simulate diverse privacy personas—such as privacy maximalists, convenience-seekers, and security pragmatists—to anticipate likely objections and friction points before deployment. In policy auditing and regulatory compliance, organizations can stress-test proposed policies against a library of simulated personas to surface predictable concerns (e.g., “lack of alternative choice” for mandatory telemetry) and design targeted mitigations.

We make the following contributions:

1.

We introduce a new research setting—
individual-level privacy reasoning
—and systematically design agent architectures grounded in established privacy theories such as Contextual Integrity. This bridges social and cognitive theories of privacy with large-scale, empirical evaluation.

2.

We propose a cognitively informed agent architecture that reconstructs user-specific “privacy minds” and dynamically activates context-relevant privacy orientations, achieving substantial gains over baseline LLM agents.

3
Methodology

Existing work on privacy reasoning with LLM-based agents has predominantly focused on
information access control
, whether sensitive data can be accessed, inferred, or exposed during training or deployment. While important, this perspective captures only a narrow aspect of privacy. Human reasoning extends beyond access control to include deeper
beliefs
,
values
, and
trade-offs
that shape how individuals interpret and respond to privacy risks. For example, users may distrust a recommendation system not because it leaks data, but because it undermines their
autonomy
or manipulates their behavior, leading to concerns that leakage-centric approaches fail to capture. Accordingly, rather than modeling LLMs solely as access-control decision makers, we frame privacy reasoning as a
generative prediction problem
: given a concrete privacy context (e.g., a forum post), how would an agent respond in a way that faithfully reflects an individual’s privacy preferences and concerns?

3.1
Privacy Reasoner

To tackle the problem, we formulate individualized privacy reasoning as a
two-stage design
that decouples the estimation of stable, person-specific privacy orientations from their context-dependent activation at inference time. Given an individual’s historical privacy-related text corpus, the agent first distills a structured representation of that individual’s latent privacy dispositions. At deployment, the agent conditions on a concrete privacy context (e.g., a post’s title, body, and metadata) and generates a response that reflects how the target individual would plausibly reason and behave in that situation.

Privacy Memory Distillation

In order to extract privacy-related orientations, we ground the offline representation in the
Antecedents–Privacy Concerns–Outcomes (APCO)
framework
(
24
)
, which models privacy reasoning as a causal process in which privacy concerns arise from a set of relatively stable antecedent factors, including

1.

prior privacy experiences

2.

privacy awareness

3.

personality traits

4.

demographic characteristics

5.

cultural background

, and subsequently influence observable behavioral outcomes. We treat APCO antecedents as latent linguistic proxies rather than ground-truth psychological attributes. Following this formulation, we prompt LLM to extract and summarize natural-language descriptors of atomic privacy-related orientations from the user’s historical data along each of the five APCO antecedent dimensions. For each dimension, the model produces a concise natural-language descriptor that captures recurrent patterns and stable tendencies evidenced across the user’s prior statements, rather than transient opinions tied to a single context. These descriptors form a persistent
privacy memory
that encodes the individual’s latent privacy predispositions in an interpretable, text-based representation.
An example distilled atomic privacy orientation is shown below:

[Privacy Experiences] The user is critical of restricted internet offerings being framed as acceptable substitutes for unrestricted access.

Contextual Filtering

Following
Working Memory Theory

(
2
)
, human reasoning is constrained by limited working memory and can engage with only a small subset at once. Therefore, we draw on the theory of
Contextual Integrity

(
17
)
, which conceptualizes privacy as adherence to context-specific norms governing information flows. Although contextual integrity offers a principled normative account, its parameters are not directly observable and must be operationalized at inference time. Given a target context, the online stage dynamically selects a subset of privacy memory that are most salient for the current situation. In practice, we implement this selection by querying LLM jointly with the set of distilled privacy memory and the current context, and outputs a filtered set of contextually relevant privacy memory.

LLM-as-a-PrivacyConcernJudge

Unlike information leakage, privacy concerns have a much wider spectrum, are more subjective and is challenging to evaluate. To maintain methodological rigor, we turn to well-established taxonomy of privacy concerns from

. To evaluate the faithfulness of the comments generated by our privacy reasoning agent, we construct a LLM-as-a-judge
PrivacyConcernJudge
using gpt-4o
(
18
)
.
PrivacyConcernJudge
is responsible for faithfully detect which privacy concern(s) are expressed and model the problem as a multi-label classification. We do not evaluate surface-level linguistic similarity, as our objective is concern-level faithfulness rather than stylistic mimicry.

Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate the performance of
PrivacyConcernJudge
and all competing methods using three standard multi-label classification metrics:

1.

Accuracy
, which measures the label-wise average correctness;

2.

Recall
, which captures how comprehensively the method identifies relevant concerns;

3.

Macro-F1 score
, which balances precision and recall across all concern categories and serves as our primary evaluation metric.

Faithfulness of
PrivacyConcernJudge
.

Privacy concern classification is inherently subtle and often ambiguous, and even human annotators rarely achieve complete consensus. To improve the reliability and interpretability of our classifier
PrivacyConcernJudge
, we construct a set of few-shot examples sampled from a labeled dataset
(
27
)
to help the model better grasp the meaning of each concern category. Specifically, we select comments describing privacy concerns from real human interviewees and pair each concern label with a salient, representative comment that explicitly expresses it. This grounding enables
PrivacyConcernJudge
to build a clearer semantic mapping between concerns and their linguistic realizations.

Why Comment Generation.

A natural question arises: why should
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
generate full comments rather than directly predicting privacy concerns? We argue this is essential for two reasons.

1.

First, natural language comments convey richer semantic, contextual, and stylistic cues than discrete labels, enabling analysis of not only
what
concerns are expressed but also
how
they are articulated, thereby revealing deeper patterns of user reasoning.

2.

Second, since golden labels are derived from real comments via the LLM-as-a-Judge classifier, generating synthetic comments and subjecting them to the same process ensures both real and synthetic data pass through an identical interpretive filter, reducing label mismatch and enabling fairer, more robust concern-level evaluation.

4.3
Baselines

In this section, we introduce all the baselines that we use in comparison with our
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
.

Privacy Persona

This agent architecture is grounded in
Privacy Persona Theory
, which posits that individuals can be categorized into distinct
privacy personas
based on their attitudes, knowledge, and behaviors. These personas capture stable, coarse-grained patterns in how different groups perceive and respond to privacy risks. Concretely, Privacy Persona agent embeds
attitudinal priors
into generation by conditioning synthetic comment production on an inferred
privacy persona
. We consider the taxonomy proposed by
7
, which distinguishes five personas:
Fundamentalists
,
Lazy Experts
,
Self-Educated Technicians
,
Amateurs
, and the
Marginally Concerned
. For a given user, we infer a persona from their historical comments using an LLM-based persona classifier. The agent then conditions its reasoning and comment generation on both the post context and the inferred persona. A sample persona-conditioned prompt is as follows:

You are a
Self-Educated Technician
when it comes to privacy. You are highly knowledgeable about technology, skeptical of corporate data practices, and prefer to retain fine-grained control over your information. Given the following post context and user history, write a comment that reflects your reasoning, concerns, and tone.

Retrieval Augmented Generation

To compare with current state-of-the-art approaches, we implement a Retrieval Augmented Generation(RAG)-based baseline. For each user, during offline stage, we index their historical privacy-related comments using sentence-level embeddings. We use RoBERTa-base sentence embeddings
(
14
)
with cosine similarity. At inference time, given a target context, the agent retrieves the top-
k
historical comments with the highest semantic similarity to the context. These retrieved comments, first summarized by an LLM agent, are then concatenated with the current context and provided to the LLM as additional conditioning information for response generation.

Naive Baseline

We also include a
Naive Baseline
that does not incorporate user-specific reasoning. It receives only a simple instruction prompt, for example:

Given the following discussion post, write a comment that reflects your privacy concerns.

Ablation Study

\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}

APCO vs. Unstructured Distillation.

We study whether imposing an explicit APCO-based structure during privacy memory distillation is necessary for effective individualized privacy reasoning. Specifically, we replace the privacy memory distillation with an unstructured distillation baseline, which prompts the LLM to extract a set of concise privacy-related statements directly from the user’s historical comments, without assigning them to predefined categories or modeling their semantic roles. Table
3
reports the performance of both variants under identical training and evaluation settings. We observe that the APCO-structured variant consistently outperforms the unstructured baseline across metrics. This suggests that organizing atomic privacy orientations along theoretically grounded antecedent dimensions provides a more stable and discriminative representation, enabling more reliable contextual activation and downstream reasoning. In contrast, unstructured atomic distillation tends to conflate heterogeneous factors, making relevance estimation noisier and less robust under domain shift.

Contextual Filter vs Summary.

First, we demonstrate the importance of contextual filter by comparing to a summary-based variant of
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
. This variant shares the same privacy memory distillation, but differs in the contextual filtering stage: instead of preserving structured, fine-grained privacy opinions, it conditions the LLM on antecedent factors only and relies on the LLM to summarize them into a single, condensed representation of the user’s privacy memory. We explore whether the way an agent manages contextual information affects its ability to accurately simulate user privacy concerns. Table
3
reports concern prediction performance under both strategies. Across all metrics, the context-filtering mechanism substantially outperforms the summary baseline. These results suggest that privacy reasoning benefits from cognitively plausible context management: filtering allows the agent to faithfully retrieve and apply orientations relevant to the current scenario, improving its ability to reproduce human-like concerns.

We study the impact of the size of privacy memory on the performance of

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
, an agentic design for simulating how individuals form and express privacy concerns in online discourse. By integrating principles from APCO, Contextual Integrity and Working Memory Theory,
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
moves beyond leakage-centric views to model privacy reasoning as a cognitively grounded, context-sensitive, and user-specific process. An LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation and a comprehensive concern taxonomy show that
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
captures fine-grained reasoning patterns and accurately predicts concern expression across scenarios. Empirical results demonstrate that
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
emulates bounded rationality, generalizes across domains, and remains effective even with limited user history, indicating it captures transferable cognitive structures. A case study on the Apple CSAM controversy further illustrates its value in revealing public sentiment and likely objections. This work lays the groundwork for a new paradigm in privacy-aware agent simulation. Future directions include richer cognitive modules, multi-agent simulations of societal dynamics, and human-in-the-loop evaluation, advancing the development of more interpretable, user-aligned, and trustworthy privacy-aware technologies.

8
Limitations

While
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
demonstrates promising results in modeling individualized privacy reasoning, several limitations warrant discussion. First, our evaluation relies on publicly available Hacker News discussions, which, despite their richness, represent a specific demographic of technically oriented users and may not generalize to broader populations with different privacy attitudes and discourse styles. Second, the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation design, while carefully calibrated and aligned with human annotation, is itself subject to potential biases and misclassifications inherent to large language models. Moreover, our study primarily focuses on textual reasoning and does not capture multimodal signals (e.g., behavioral or interactional data) that may shape privacy attitudes in real-world settings. Finally, while the design shows cross-domain transferability, further research is needed to assess its robustness in more dynamic contexts, evolving privacy norms, and under adversarial or strategic user behaviors. Third, this work only focuses on one-level response, future work could explore scenarios like privacy debates or conversations to yield richer information.

Potential Risk

While PrivacyReasoner advances the modeling of individualized privacy reasoning, its deployment also raises important risks. First, though we used a publicly available dataset where all users are anonymous, constructing user-specific privacy minds from historical comments on other corpora may introduce the possibility of privacy violations or misuse, especially if sensitive data is collected, stored, or analyzed without sufficient safeguards. Second, the reliance on large language models as both generator and evaluator may amplify underlying model biases or produce false inferences about user orientations, potentially leading to skewed simulations and inaccurate representations of individuals. Careful governance, transparency, and ethical safeguards are therefore essential to ensure that PrivacyReasoner’s capabilities are used responsibly.

Privacy Concern Judge Specification

To evaluate the faithfulness of generated comments with respect to privacy reasoning, we implement a
PrivacyConcernJudge
, an LLM-as-a-judge classifier. The judge models concern detection as a 14-way multi-label classification task. It is prompted in a few-shot fashion with representative examples of each concern category to align its classification behavior with human annotations. It’s worth noting that our evaluation focuses on relative, comparative faithfulness across agent variants rather than absolute ground-truth correctness.

Privacy Concern Judgment.

To assess the reliability of the LLM-based PrivacyConcernJudge, we conduct a small-scale human validation study. We randomly sample 100 of comments from the evaluation set and ask two human annotators to independently label expressed privacy concerns using the same 14-category taxonomy. We then compare the judge’s predictions against the human annotations. The PrivacyConcernJudge achieves substantial agreement with human labels, with a macro-averaged Cohen’s
\kappa
being 0.812 between the judge and human annotations. These results suggest that while the judge is not error-free, it provides a reasonably faithful and stable approximation of human privacy concern judgments and is suitable for comparative evaluation across agent variants.

Prompt Templates

We include the core prompt templates used in
PrivacyReasoner
for key tasks, enabling reproducibility and providing transparency into how the agent components interact with the underlying language model. Detailed specifications, such as user-specific privacy memories, post contexts, and few-shot examples for the privacy judge, are omitted.

Privacy Distillation.

To build a user’s privacy memory, we extract natural-language descriptors of atomic privacy orientations from their historical comments.

You are an expert in privacy analyzing an individual’s historical privacy-related texts. Following the APCO (Antecedents Privacy Concerns Outcomes) framework, extract atomic statements about the individual’s privacy antecedents along five dimensions: [...]. For each dimension, extract distinct atomic statements that capture specific tendencies evidenced in their past expressions.

Contextual Filtering.

At inference time, we activate the most contextually relevant orientation descriptors from the privacy memory to simulate bounded working memory.

Given the following user privacy memory and the post context, select the orientations based on relevance to the post context that are likely to be activated in this situation.

Synthetic Comment Generation.

Finally, the agent generates a synthetic comment simulating how the user would respond to a new privacy-related event.

You are [user id] when it comes to privacy. Given the following discussion post and the selected orientations from your privacy memory, write a comment that reflects the user’s likely privacy concerns, reasoning, and tone. The comment should be natural, context-aware, and faithful to the user’s historical reasoning style. Favor details to generality.

Privacy Concern Judgment.

The
PrivacyConcernJudge
is prompted with few-shot examples (see Table
6
) to classify expressed concerns.

Given the following comment, identify which of the 14 privacy concerns it expresses. Return a JSON object with concern names as keys and 1/0 values indicating presence or absence. Base your decision strictly on explicit evidence in the text.

Analysis.

Across all three scenarios,
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
consistently captures finer-grained reasoning patterns that are present in real user discourse but absent from baseline outputs. Baseline outputs tend to remain generic and avoid articulating specific structural concerns. For instance, in the IoT labeling case,
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
highlights downstream risks such as
inter-company data sharing
and explicitly calls for
user participation in shaping regulatory standards
, closely mirroring human concerns about autonomy and accountability. In the Triplebyte example,
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
situates the apology within the broader context of
job-search confidentiality
, emphasizing the specific stakes of privacy violation rather than generic appeals to “learning from mistakes” as the baseline does. Finally, in the Apple CSAM case,
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
identifies the
slippery slope toward expanded surveillance
and calls for
algorithmic transparency
and
false-positive safeguards
, while the baseline remains surface-level and avoids articulating the deeper structural risks raised by users. These examples illustrate how
\mathrm{PrivacyReasoner}
’s cognitively grounded reasoning enables it to surface implicit concerns and contextual nuances that baseline approaches systematically overlook.
