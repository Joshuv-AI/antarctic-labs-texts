Prophylactic Medium-Class Topical Steroids are Associated with a Lower Incidence of Capecitabine-Induced Hand-Foot Syndrome in Patients with Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Yohei Iimura, Yuka Ahiko, Satoko Monma, Naoki Sakuyama, Susumu Aikou, Dai Shida, Seiichiro Kuroda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere is limited clinical research evaluating the role of topical corticosteroids in preventing hand-foot syndrome (HFS) caused by capecitabine. Although the precise cause of HFS remains unclear, inflammatory mechanisms are thought to contribute to its development. This study investigated whether prophylactic use of a medium-class topical corticosteroid was associated with a lower incidence of capecitabine-related HFS in patients with colorectal cancer.MethodsWe retrospectively assessed 78 patients prescribed medium-class topical steroids as a preventive against HFS associated with capecitabine and 32 topical steroid-free patients undergoing capecitabine-based chemotherapy. We evaluated the incidence of each grade of HFS during chemotherapy.ResultsBetween January 2022 and March 2025, 110 patients were enrolled in the study. Grade ≥ 2 HFS occurred in 23.1% of patients in the medium-class topical steroid group and 50.0% of patients in the topical steroid-free group (OR, 0.30; 95% CI 0.13–0.72; P = 0.012, Fisher’s exact test). The incidence of other capecitabine-related adverse events did not differ significantly between the groups.ConclusionOur findings suggest that prophylactic use of medium-class topical steroids was associated with a lower incidence of capecitabine-induced hand-foot syndrome. Prospective randomized studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: Capecitabine-induced hand-foot syndrome, Medium-class topical steroids, Colorectal cancer, Adjuvant chemotherapy

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Capecitabine-induced hand-foot syndrome (HFS) is a common adverse event that can impair quality of life, reduce treatment adherence, and compromise the delivery of adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with colorectal cancer.Although several preventive strategies have been investigated, no widely accepted, evidence-based prophylactic approach has been established.We investigated whether prophylactic use of medium-class topical corticosteroids was associated with a lower incidence of capecitabine-induced HFS.What was learned from the study?In this retrospective cohort study, prophylactic medium-class topical steroid use was associated with a significantly lower incidence of grade ≥ 2 HFS (23.1% versus 50.0%; OR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.13–0.72; P = 0.012) and remained significantly associated with a lower hazard of grade ≥ 2 HFS after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR, 0.407; 95% CI, 0.206–0.803; P = 0.0096).These findings support further prospective randomized studies to determine whether prophylactic medium-class topical steroids can become a practical strategy for preventing capecitabine-induced HFS.

Introduction

Hand-foot syndrome (HFS) is a common adverse effect associated with capecitabine that can greatly diminish a patient’s quality of life (QOL) [1]. While clinical trials on colorectal cancer chemotherapy have reported that 30–80% of patients experience some degree of capecitabine-related HFS [2–4], real-world data showed a prevalence of > 90% [5]. In its more severe form, HFS can cause symptoms such as swelling, blisters, skin peeling, and even ulcers. These complications often result in treatment delays, dose reductions, or even stopping of capecitabine altogether. For adjuvant chemotherapy in colorectal cancer two main treatment options are typically recommended [6]: the CapeOX regimen, which combines oral capecitabine with intravenous oxaliplatin, and the FOLFOX regimen, which includes intravenous 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin. Although the CapeOX regimen offers the advantage of a shorter infusion time over the FOLFOX regimen, its clinical use is limited owing to concerns regarding HFS, which can negatively affect patient adherence to treatment [7]. Given that maintaining the dose intensity of capecitabine is vital for achieving optimal outcomes in colon cancer adjuvant chemotherapy [8] effective management of HFS is critical for preserving patient QOL and ensuring the continued therapeutic efficacy of capecitabine chemotherapy [9].

Preventive strategies for capecitabine-induced HFS typically involve skin moisturization and avoiding localized pressure [10–12]. Some studies have also shown that exfoliating agents [13, 14], celecoxib [15, 16], and pyridoxine [17] have also demonstrated the potential for alleviating HFS. Despite the use of strategies such as dose reduction or temporary treatment breaks [18], there is still no widely accepted, evidence-based method for preventing HFS. Although a large phase III trial showed that celecoxib could help prevent HFS, it has not been adopted in clinical practice due to concerns over the potential side effects of long-term systemic non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use.

The development of HFS is believed to result from several mechanisms, including suppressed proliferation of basal skin cells, drug secretion through eccrine sweat glands, effects of drug metabolites [9], and inflammatory responses involving interleukins (IL)− 1a, IL-1b, IL-6, and reactive oxygen species [19]. Corticosteroids are known to counter inflammation by inhibiting the release of these chemical mediators. In one prospective study [20], patients treated with oral dexamethasone (8 mg/day with gradual tapering) for pegylated liposomal doxorubicin-induced palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia were able to continue treatment without dose modifications. In contrast, those not receiving dexamethasone often required dose reductions or delays. Additional case reports have also suggested corticosteroids may help manage HFS associated with cytarabine or vinorelbine [21, 22]. Similarly, inflammatory skin rashes caused by epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors are believed to result from inflammatory reactions [23–25], which can be prevented by topical corticosteroids [26, 27]. These findings imply that corticosteroids, when applied topically, could potentially reduce HFS-related treatment interruptions and help maintain chemotherapy dose intensity, thereby improving patients’ QOL during adjuvant therapy. However, there is still a lack of studies specifically evaluating the preventive or therapeutic role of topical corticosteroids in managing capecitabine-induced HFS.

Therefore, we carried out a single-center, retrospective cohort study to evaluate whether medium-class topical steroids could help prevent HFS caused by capecitabine in patients with colorectal cancer. We compared outcomes between those who used topical steroids as a preventative measure and those who did not. Our hypothesis was that applying medium-class topical steroids was associated with a lower incidence and severity of capecitabine-induced HFS in this patient group.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective, single-center observational study involved patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer who received capecitabine treatment between January 2022 and March 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with colorectal cancer scheduled to receive adjuvant CapeOX (capecitabine plus oxaliplatin), (2) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1, and (3) age ≥ 18 years. Patients with stage IV colorectal cancer who had undergone complete (R0) resection of metastatic lesions and subsequently received adjuvant CapeOX chemotherapy were also eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they had (1) other skin conditions, chemotherapy-induced skin lesions on their palms or soles, or dermatitis that could interfere with assessing the effectiveness of study treatments; (2) ongoing need for steroids or NSAIDs; (3) planned treatment with other anti-cancer drugs, such as multi kinase inhibitors, that might affect study results; (4) pregnancy, breast feeding, or inability to use contraception during the study period; or (5) sensory nerve damage in the palms or soles. Patients in the medium-class topical steroid group and the topical steroid-free group were treated during overlapping calendar periods.

Patients in the topical steroid group were instructed to apply a medium-class topical corticosteroid (hydrocortisone butyrate 0.1% or clobetasone butyrate 0.05%) prophylactically to both the palms and soles beginning on the first day of capecitabine treatment. The corticosteroid was applied twice daily throughout the course of capecitabine therapy unless treatment was discontinued. Regardless of prophylactic topical steroid use, all patients received standard preventive skin care, including routine use of moisturizers and standardized verbal skin-care counseling from pharmacists. The decision to prescribe prophylactic medium-class topical steroids was made at the discretion of the treating physician as part of routine clinical practice. The potency classification was based on the topical corticosteroid classification recommended by the Japanese Dermatological Association (JDA), in which hydrocortisone butyrate 0.1% and clobetasone butyrate 0.05% are categorized as medium-class topical corticosteroids [28]. These agents correspond approximately to class 5 (medium potency) in the US topical corticosteroid classification.

We gathered clinical information retrospectively from medical records, including age, sex, cancer stage, and ECOG status. The study compared the incidence of HFS between patients using medium-class topical steroids and those who did not. The primary outcome was the rate of grade ≥ 2 HFS, based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Secondary outcomes included the overall incidence of HFS at all grades. Statistical Analysis

We assessed the incidence and severity of capecitabine induced HFS. The primary endpoint was the incidence of grade ≥ 2 HFS during capecitabine treatment. The incidence of grade ≥ 2 HFS was compared between the two groups using Fisher’s exact test, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because this was a retrospective observational study, no a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to evaluate the association between prophylactic medium-class topical steroid use and the time to first occurrence of grade ≥ 2 HFS. The model was fitted using the full study cohort (n = 110) and included topical steroid use, age, sex, and pathological stage. Time zero was defined as the first day of capecitabine treatment. Patients who developed grade ≥ 2 HFS were followed until the first occurrence of the event, whereas those who did not develop grade ≥ 2 HFS were censored at the completion or discontinuation of adjuvant CapeOX therapy, corresponding to the last available skin assessment during capecitabine treatment. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no evidence of violation was identified. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), 95% CIs, and P values were calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR ver.1.36 software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) [29].

Results

Patient Demographics

A total of 110 patients were included in the efficacy analysis between January 2022 and March 2025. Of them, 78 patients received topical medium-class steroids (hydrocortisone butyrate, 0.1%; clobetasone butyrate, 0.05%). No biased trends in baseline characteristics were observed for these criteria between the two groups. Pathological stage III cancer was present in 90.9% of the patients. All enrolled patients had an ECOG performance status of 0. The median number of CapeOX cycles was 8 (IQR, 8–8) in both groups. The median duration of capecitabine exposure was also identical between groups (112 days [IQR, 112–112] in both groups) (Table 1).

Primary Endpoint

Grade ≥ 2 HFS occurred in 18 of 78 patients (23.1%) in the medium-class topical steroid group and in 16 of 32 patients (50.0%) in the topical steroid-free group. The incidence of grade ≥ 2 HFS was significantly lower in the medium-class topical steroid group (OR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.13–0.72; P = 0.012, Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 2).

Incidence of HFS by two groups. Grade ≥1, 39.7% versus 81.3% [P = 0.001; odds ratio (OR), 0.15; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.06–0.41]; grade ≥ 2, 23.1% versus 50.0% (P = 0.012; OR, 0.30; 95% CI 0.13–0.72); and grade ≥3, 3.8% versus 15.6% (P = 0.045; OR, 0.22; 95% CI 0.05–0.97) (Fisher’s exact test)

Secondary Endpoints

Fisher’s exact test demonstrated that the incidence of HFS was consistently lower in the medium-class topical steroid group across all severity grades. The incidence of grade ≥1 HFS was 39.7% versus 81.3% (P = 0.001; OR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.06–0.41), grade ≥ 2 HFS was 23.1% versus 50.0% (P = 0.012; OR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.13–0.72), and grade ≥3 HFS was 3.8% versus 15.6% (P = 0.045; OR, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.05–0.97) (Fig. 2).

There were no significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of capecitabine-related adverse events other than HFS (Table 2). Additionally, no adverse events associated with the use of medium-class topical steroids were observed. In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model including all 110 patients and adjusted for age, sex, and pathological stage, prophylactic medium-class topical steroid use remained significantly associated with a lower hazard of grade ≥ 2 HFS (adjusted HR, 0.407; 95% CI, 0.206–0.803; P = 0.0096). Age, sex, and pathological stage were not significantly associated with the occurrence of grade ≥ 2 HFS (Table 3).

Discussion

This study explored whether medium-class topical steroids could help prevent grade ≥ 2 HFS caused by capecitabine in patients with colorectal cancer undergoing adjuvant CapeOX therapy. The findings indicate that these topical steroids were associated with a lower incidence of HFS while being well tolerated by patients.

Currently, there are no universally accepted strategies to prevent capecitabine-induced HFS. Although moisturizing and minimizing local pressure are commonly recommended in clinical practice because of their low risk of side effects, their effectiveness has not been confirmed through clinical trials. Various prophylactic treatments, including exfoliating agents [13, 14, 30], celecoxib [15, 16], and pyridoxine [17], have been investigated for preventing capecitabine-induced HFS. While exfoliating agents are sometimes used to reduce skin reactions caused by other anticancer drugs, such as sunitinib, sorafenib, and regorafenib, they can be irritating and may actually worsen symptoms of HFS.

Two studies examining celecoxib as a preventive treatment reported a reduction of at least ≥ 20% in the occurrence of grade 2 or worse HFS [15, 16]. However, since NSAIDs, such as celecoxib, carry a risk of adverse cardiovascular events [31, 32], their prolonged systemic use is generally discouraged. In this study, 23.1% patients in the topical medium-class steroid group had grade ≥ 2 HFS within the chemotherapy period (approximately 6 months), which is comparable with the results of the celecoxib prophylactic efficacy trial [15, 16]. This indicates that topical medium-class steroids may be safer than celecoxib and could serve as an alternative prophylactic therapy. The prophylactic effect of hydrocortisone butyrate is likely attributable to suppression of inflammatory pathways, similar to the cyclooxygenase inhibition achieved by celecoxib. Although the prophylactic effects of pyridoxine in HFS have been previously reported [33, 34], its overall efficacy remains questionable. Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have shown no significant benefit [17, 35–38], making its use controversial. This highlights the need for preventive strategies that are both effective and carry a low risk of adverse effects in managing capecitabine-induced HFS.

Topical corticosteroids offer several advantages over systemic (oral) corticosteroids, particularly in patients receiving chemotherapy. Oral steroids, when used continuously, can increase the risk of infections and are generally not recommended during chemotherapy. In contrast, topical corticosteroids act locally, minimizing systemic absorption thereby reducing the risk of infection. They also avoid interactions with cytochrome P450 3A4, simplifying their use alongside other medications. Additionally, topical corticosteroids can be administered simultaneously with moisturizers to ensure adherence. However, high-potency topical steroids can lead to local side effects such as skin thinning or fungal infections. To balance efficacy and safety, this study used medium-class topical corticosteroids, which carry a lower risk of adverse events and may represent a practical prophylactic option for HFS.

Although this study was not a randomized prospective study, the incidence of grade ≥ 2 HFS in our cohort appeared to be lower than that reported in the control and topical steroid groups of previous clinical trials [15, 16, 38]. Given that treatment-related toxicities with medium-class topical steroids have not been reported, these drugs may be a new treatment option for capecitabine-induced HFS.

A recent randomized trial from India demonstrated that prophylactic 1% topical diclofenac gel reduced the incidence of capecitabine-induced HFS [40]. However, the incidence of grade ≥1 HFS in the control group of that study (18.1%) was substantially lower than that reported in previous randomized trials [15, 16, 39], which may limit the generalizability of its findings to other clinical settings. In contrast, the incidence of HFS observed in the control group of the present study was comparable to those reported in previous clinical trials. Taken together, these findings suggest that prophylactic medium-class topical steroids may represent another promising topical strategy for preventing capecitabine-induced HFS. Ongoing prospective randomized studies, including the J-DIRECT trial [41], will further clarify the comparative effectiveness of topical anti-inflammatory approaches. Given the underlying inflammatory mechanisms of HFS, along with evidence from a previous prospective study [20], and two case reports [21, 22], we believe that medium-class topical corticosteroids are a promising option for preventing inflammation driven HFS. Additionally, this study demonstrated the tolerability of CapeOX therapy, which is consistent with previous Japanese studies [42]. Collectively, these findings suggest that prophylactic medium-class topical steroids may reduce the incidence of capecitabine-induced HFS without increasing other adverse events. Moreover, treatment-related adverse events were not observed.

Importantly, the association between prophylactic medium-class topical steroid use and a lower hazard of grade ≥ 2 HFS remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and pathological stage. Although residual confounding cannot be completely excluded because of the retrospective study design, this finding supports the robustness of the observed association. These findings reduce, although do not eliminate, concerns regarding confounding by indication inherent in retrospective observational studies.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective observational study in which allocation to prophylactic medium-class topical steroid use was determined by the treating physician rather than by randomization. Therefore, residual confounding and confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Second, because the study population consisted of patients undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy, the observation period was limited to 6 months, making it difficult to evaluate long-term efficacy and safety of medium-class topical corticosteroids. Third, no a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the retrospective study design. Therefore, the present findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in adequately powered prospective randomized studies. Fourth, treatment delivery outcomes, including dose reductions, treatment delays, treatment discontinuation, and relative dose intensity, were not systematically evaluated in the present study. Future prospective studies should investigate whether the reduction in HFS achieved with prophylactic medium-class topical steroids translates into improved treatment delivery and clinical outcomes. Lastly, the study was not a randomized controlled trial (patients were not randomly selected), had a limited sample size, and lacked a blinded assessment. However, on the basis of these results of this study, we plan to conduct a randomized controlled trial to further evaluate the preventive effects of both topical medium-class corticosteroids and NSAIDs on capecitabine-induced HFS. Thus, in the future, we can provide new, low-risk, and low-cost treatment options on the basis of the results of our clinical studies. Future prospective studies should evaluate whether reducing HFS translates into improved treatment adherence, relative dose intensity, and clinical outcomes.

Taken together, our findings provide preliminary evidence supporting prophylactic medium-class topical steroids as a potential strategy for reducing capecitabine-induced hand-foot syndrome. Confirmation in prospective randomized studies is warranted.

Conclusions

This study investigated whether prophylactic medium-class topical steroids could reduce the incidence of capecitabine-induced hand-foot syndrome in patients with colorectal cancer receiving adjuvant CapeOX therapy. Prophylactic use of medium-class topical steroids was associated with a lower incidence of hand-foot syndrome and was well tolerated. These findings support further prospective randomized studies to confirm the efficacy and safety of this preventive strategy.
