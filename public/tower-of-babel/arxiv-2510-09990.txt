An FPCA-Enhanced Ensemble Learning Framework for Photometric Identification of Type Ia Supernovae
Moonzarin Reza, Lifan Wang, Lei Hu

Abstract
Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) are essential tools for addressing key cosmic questions, including the Hubble tension and the nature of dark energy. Modern surveys are predominantly photometry-based, making the construction of a clean photometric SNe Ia sample crucial. In this study, we investigate whether functional principal component analysis (FPCA) scores derived from photometric light curves, combined with ensemble learning, can reliably distinguish SNe Ia from other transients using the PLAsTiCC dataset. FPCA provides a data-driven, flexible characterization of light curves without relying on rigid theoretical model assumptions. Light curves are fitted by minimizing residuals with penalty terms from clean samples, making the method robust to outliers or poorly sampled bands. The first two FPCA scores and peak magnitudes across the five LSST bands are used as classification features. We implement two complementary binary classifiers: an ensemble boosting model (CatBoost) and a statistical probabilistic method based on Euclidean distances. CatBoost slightly outperforms the statistical method, achieving 98.5% accuracy and 97.8% precision. Performance remains robust ( > 90 % >90\% ) under typical photometric redshift uncertainties ( σ = 0.1 \sigma=0.1 ). On the spectroscopic DES Y5 sample, both methods reach approximately 90% accuracy and 95% precision, demonstrating strong out-of-domain generalization compared to state-of-the-art methods with limited cross-survey applicability. Applied to DECam DDF and DESIRT transients, the predictions strongly agree, and their intersection provides a high-confidence SNe Ia sample for cosmological analyses. Overall, this FPCA-based framework offers a powerful, flexible tool for classifying transients in upcoming large-scale surveys such as LSST and Roman.



I
Introduction

The discovery of the accelerated expansion of the Universe in the late 1990s by the High-Z Supernova Search Team
[
54
]
and the Supernova Cosmology Project
[
46
]
, based on samples of both low- and high-redshift Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia), established a positive cosmological constant
(\Omega_{\Lambda})
and a negative deceleration parameter (
q_{0}
). This result revolutionized modern cosmology, revealing that approximately 70% of the mass–energy budget of a flat Universe today is associated with dark energy. Since then, SNe Ia have become a cornerstone of cosmological studies: with careful calibration of their peak luminosity, a pure sample of SNe Ia provides robust constraints on cosmological parameters.

Large-scale optical surveys such as the Dark Energy Survey
[
16
]
and Pan-STARRS
[
11
]
have already discovered thousands of transients, while upcoming surveys—including the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST;
Sweeney 59
) and the Roman Wide Field Imaging Survey
[
55
]
—are expected to detect on the order of
10^{5}
supernovae. However, the logistical demands of spectroscopic follow-up cannot scale accordingly, as such observations require high sensitivity and significant resources. The transient nature and faintness of distant SNe further exacerbate this challenge. Consequently, there is growing emphasis on developing reliable methods to classify and analyze supernovae using photometric data alone.

In parallel, machine learning has become an indispensable tool across astronomy, with successful applications spanning exoplanets
[
33
,
64
]
, galaxies
[
52
,
50
,
37
]
, stellar populations
[
63
,
38
]
, cosmological simulations
[
51
,
53
]
, and supernovae
[
14
,
15
,
31
]
.

For SNe Ia, the long-standing approach of template fitting
[
25
,
20
]
remains widely used in cosmological analyses, but machine learning techniques promise improved classification performance when paired with appropriate models and feature extraction strategies.
Several machine learning studies have already demonstrated strong performance on supernova classification tasks. For example,
Santos et al. [58]
benchmarked a variety of feature-extraction and classification methods using the Supernova Photometric Classification Challenge dataset and found that a tree-based pipeline combined with SALT2 features
[
24
]
outperformed alternatives. Recurrent neural networks trained on SNANA-simulated light curves achieved accuracies exceeding 96% for binary classification
[
44
]
. Ensemble-learning approaches combining simulated and observed data from the Supernova Legacy Survey (SNLS) reached an “Area Under the receiver operating characteristics Curve (AUC)”, a single metric which gives a comprehensive view of model performance across different probability cutoffs, of 0.98
[
43
]
. Gaussian process regression has also been widely employed for modeling and interpolating light curves. For example,
Qu et al. [48]
used Gaussian regression to generate flux maps in wavelength–time space, which were subsequently classified by a convolutional neural network, achieving accuracies of 99.7% (in-distribution) and 80.6% (out-of-distribution). This framework was later adapted for early supernova classification
[
49
]
, achieving 75% accuracy at the trigger date and 89% accuracy at 50 days post-trigger. Similarly,
Boone [6]
used Gaussian process regression to augment training data, extracting features for a LightGBM classifier that achieved an AUC of 0.957. Other approaches include recurrent networks for binary classification
[
12
]
, parametric feature extraction combined with gradient-boosted trees for multi-class classification
[
17
]
, and CNN-based methods trained on heat maps
[
9
]
.

Generative models have also been explored. For example,
Boone [7]
proposed ParSNIP, in which light curves are encoded through one-dimensional convolutional layers into latent variables, concatenated with meta-parameters (reference time, color, and amplitude) and passed to a classifier. However, the complexity of this architecture introduces risks of overfitting and strong dependence on survey-specific characteristics. Although data augmentation strategies were applied to mitigate these effects, the model has not been validated on independent datasets, leaving its generalization power uncertain.

In this work, we propose a machine learning–based framework for classifying astronomical transients in upcoming large-scale surveys, such as those from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s LSST
[
61
]
. We employ functional principal component analysis (FPCA) to represent multi-band light curves with arbitrary sampling rates using two principal components, providing a compact and survey-independent feature representation. The FPCA scores derived for the
grizy
filters serve as inputs to an ensemble-based gradient-boosting model, which performs binary classification of transients in the PLAsTiCC dataset. We further test the model on observed DECam light curves to evaluate its domain adaptability.

II
Dataset

The Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) will be carried out with the 8.4-m telescope at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, designed with a wide field of view to address key questions about the Universe, including the nature of dark matter and dark energy, the formation and structure of the Milky Way, the inventory of the Solar System, and the study of astrophysical transients
[
23
]
.

To prepare for the challenge of classifying LSST transients, the Photometric LSST Astronomical Time Series Classification Challenge (PLAsTiCC) was developed. The dataset consists of simulated light curves, i.e. fluxes measured as a function of time in six bandpass filters spanning the ultraviolet, optical, and infrared
[
1
]
. The PLAsTiCC training set contains about 8,000 transients from 14 categories, each with spectroscopic redshifts, reflecting what LSST is expected to observe in its first three years (average spectroscopic redshift
\sim
0.25). The PLAsTiCC test set, by contrast, includes 3.5 million more distant and fainter objects (average photometric redshift
\sim
0.51), of which only a small fraction have spectroscopic observations. In addition to the categories represented in the training set, the test set includes five additional hypothesized classes
[
34
]
. Approximately 99% of light curves are drawn from the Wide-Fast-Deep (WFD) survey, which covers
\sim
20,000 deg
2
and observes each field twice per week in at least one filter, while the remaining 1% are from the Deep-Drilling Fields (DDF), covering only
\sim
50 deg
2
with higher cadence to reach fainter objects.

In this work, we primarily use the PLAsTiCC test set for both training and evaluation, unless stated otherwise. Our choice is motivated by the novelty of our method—our main goal is to test whether FPCA-based scores can robustly separate Type Ia supernovae from other transients. For comparison, we also trained the model on the PLAsTiCC training set and evaluated on the PLAsTiCC test set, following the original challenge setup. We refer to this latter case as the out-of-distribution test (For a description of the dataset, see
Hložek et al. [30]
).

From the dataset, we extract all transients detected within 10 days of maximum light with at least five data points in at least one filter. The resulting dataset contains 11,158 light curves, summarized in Table
1
. Of these, 6,048 (54.2%) are Type Ia supernovae, grouped as “Type Ia,” and 5,110 (45.8%) belong to other categories, grouped as “Non–Type Ia.” Unlike previous studies (e.g.,
Qu et al. 48
), which restricted analyses to well-sampled light curves from the DDF survey or to specific transient types, we apply no such cuts. Our sample spans the full redshift range (
0<z<3
) and includes objects from both WFD and DDF surveys, covering the entire set of transient types available.

III
Methods

In this section, we describe the functional principal component analysis (FPCA) model adopted to fit supernova light curves. We also outline the fitting procedure, including the use of the MPFIT module for optimizing model parameters, and provide the detailed steps of our light-curve fitting pipeline.

III.1
FPCA Model

Functional principal component analysis (FPCA) is a purely data-driven model for Type Ia supernova (SN Ia) light curves, designed to project sparsely sampled observations onto a set of smooth, complete light curves
[
28
]
. The basis functions, or principal components, are mathematical constructs chosen to maximize the variance captured in the data. Unlike physics-based models such as SALT2
[
60
]
or SNooPy
[
10
]
, FPCA relies minimally on fixed assumptions, enabling it to capture more subtle and diverse features of the data.
The principal components used here are derived from a combined sample of well-observed SNe Ia from the Lick Observatory Supernova Search
[
39
]
, the Carnegie Supernova Project
[
26
]
, and the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics sample
[
29
]
. The training sample was restricted to supernovae with at least one observation within five days both before and after maximum light in all filters.
In FPCA, each light curve is expressed as a weighted sum of principal components that characterize its shape. For example, the zeroth-order component represents the mean light-curve shape, the first component encodes light-curve width, and the second component accounts for variations in post-peak decline rate
[
28
]
.

i

t_{ij}

i

\hat{t}_{i}
is the corresponding peak time, and

III.2
MPFIT Module

We fit the light curves using the MPFIT module, which solves non-linear least-squares fitting problems
[
41
]
using the Levenberg–Marquardt technique. The model parameters are determined by fitting a user-defined function to the observed data. In this work, we estimate the best-fit parameters through chi-square minimization. The module also provides flexibility by allowing both fixed values and bounded constraints on the parameters. It returns the optimized parameter values along with their covariance matrix.

III.3
Light-Curve Fitting

We fit the FPCA model, represented by
\Theta_{i}
, to multi-band photometric data of both SN Ia and non–SN Ia transients using MPFIT. Although the FPCA model was originally developed for Type Ia supernova light curves, we extend its application to the full range of transients in our dataset. By using a two-component FPCA model, we retain enough flexibility to capture diverse temporal behaviors across light curves, while avoiding the rigidity that would result from adding more components. IV
Machine Learning Model

In this section, we describe the machine learning model, Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), used in this study, followed by the performance metrics employed to evaluate it. We choose CatBoost because it can naturally handle missing values, which are common in light curves lacking observations in certain bands.

IV.1
Categorical Boosting

Categorical Boosting
[
32
]
is an ensemble learning method whose base learners are decision trees. Each tree splits the data at nodes based on features to minimize impurity, with leaf nodes assigning class labels. Trees are built sequentially, with each tree correcting the errors of previous trees by assigning higher weights to misclassified samples. Predictions from multiple trees are combined using weighted probabilities for classification, minimizing cross-entropy, which has been suggested as an optimal metric for challenges like PLAsTiCC, which needs to baalnce diverse science goals
[
40
]
. The model also provides a confidence probability based on the fraction of trees agreeing on the predicted class. Unlike bagging
[
8
,
62
]
, where trees are constructed independently and predictions rely on majority voting, CatBoost’s sequential approach improves performance on challenging datasets. CatBoost classifiers have been successfully applied in astronomy
[
35
]
, medicine
[
18
]
, cybersecurity
[
3
]
, and digital marketing
[
36
]
.

IV.2
Input Features

For each transient, we construct input features from the FPCA light-curve fits across all five LSST bands (g, r, i, z, y). We use the first two FPCA coefficients in each band, which capture the dominant temporal variations, along with the peak absolute magnitude derived from the fitted peak apparent magnitude assuming a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology. These features provide a compact representation of light-curve shape and intrinsic brightness, forming the basis for classification. We also tested including FPCA fit uncertainties, but found no significant improvement in classification performance; hence, these terms are not included in the final model.

Performance Metrics

We evaluate classifier performance using several standard metrics:

The derived metrics are calculated as:

Accuracy

\displaystyle=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FP+TN+FN}

Precision

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

F1 Score

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

ROC and AUC:
The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve plots the True Positive Rate (
TPR=TP/(TP+FN)
) against the False Positive Rate (

The Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) quantifies overall classifier performance; a value of 1 indicates a perfect classifier, 0.5 corresponds to random guessing, and values below 0.5 suggest potential systematic issues. Higher AUC indicates better model performance, especially in imbalanced datasets where overall accuracy may be misleading.

V.3
Feature Importance

V.3.1
Impurity based

In this section, we explore the default feature importance functionality of the CatBoost model in scikit-learn, which is calculated by minimizing cross-entropy
[
5
]
. The basic idea is that features appearing near the root nodes of decision trees are deemed more important than those appearing deeper in the tree. This hierarchy is chosen to minimize impurity at each split for a given feature. For example, if features A and B reduce impurity at the root node by 60% and 50%, respectively, feature A is preferentially selected and considered more important. For ensemble methods like CatBoost, feature importance is first calculated for individual trees in this manner and then averaged across all trees to obtain the overall feature importance.

Figure
7
(left panel) shows the resulting feature importance, with the x-axis representing the importance as a percentage. As expected, absolute magnitudes dominate four out of the top five features, reflecting the well-known precision of Type Ia peak luminosities. The remaining top feature is the first PC score in the
r

Permutation importance provides a model-independent measure of feature importance that can be applied to any model

Overall, these results indicate that very different methods consistently identify the same features as the most important, and the prominence of peak absolute magnitudes reinforces the model’s implicit recognition of Type Ia supernovae as standard candles.

V.6
Threshold probability

One of the key outputs of ensemble-based learning methods is the confidence probability, defined as the fraction of constituents (here, decision trees) that agree on the model’s final prediction. By setting a threshold probability (default 0.5), a sample is classified as Type Ia if its confidence exceeds the threshold, and as Non-Type Ia otherwise. This is particularly important for cosmological applications, where obtaining a pure sample of Type Ia supernovae is critical. Figure
10
shows the accuracy and precision for different threshold probabilities. Increasing the threshold monotonically increases precision (blue curve), as stricter criteria yield a purer sample. The overall accuracy (red curve) increases when the threshold rises from 0.3 to 0.5, but decreases beyond 0.5 due to more Type Ia supernovae being misclassified as Non-Type Ia, illustrating the trade-off between purity and completeness. Specifically, raising the threshold from 0.5 to 0.9 increases precision from 97.8% to 98.7%, while decreasing accuracy from 98.5% to 96.4%.

V.8
Introducing redshift noise

Large-scale surveys such as LSST and Roman will not be able to obtain accurate spectroscopic redshifts for all detected transients. Measured photometric redshifts are subject to uncertainties from photometric errors, limited wavelength coverage, calibration issues, and other factors. To assess the impact of these uncertainties on our classifier, we perturb the photometric redshifts of the test set as
z^{\prime}\rightarrow\max(0,z+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma))
, where
z
is the quoted photometric redshift, while keeping the training redshifts noise-free. The modified redshift is then used in light-curve fitting. We test
\sigma
values of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3, comparing the model’s performance to the base classifier (no noise). Each experiment is repeated three times per
\sigma
, with results summarized in Table

The overall accuracy of the base classifier is 98.5%, while it drops to 92.4%, 82.5%, and 72.6% for Gaussian noise levels of
\sigma=0.1,0.2,
and
0.3
, respectively, indicating an approximate 10% decrease in accuracy for every 0.1 increase in noise. Precision remains above 90% for all noise levels, demonstrating that the contamination of the predicted sample is relatively insensitive to redshift errors. Recall exceeds 90% for
\sigma=0.1

\sigma
values, indicating that higher redshift uncertainties make it increasingly difficult to correctly classify Non-Type Ia supernovae. The tolerance level thus defines the acceptable noise in the data, which in turn constrains the observational depth, since high-redshift measurements generally carry larger uncertainties. Importantly, at
\sigma=0.1
, which is typical for LSST photometric redshifts, all performance metrics remain above 90%, indicating that the classifier is robust and reliable at this noise level.

In this section, we train a CatBoost classifier using the original PLAsTiCC training set (spectroscopic sample) and evaluate it on the original PLAsTiCC test set, which has been used throughout this study. V.12
Statistical Probabilistic Method

In this section, we employ a statistical probability–based method to classify the transients. Such an approach is useful because, although ensemble methods (e.g., CatBoost) are generally more interpretable than black-box neural networks, they still introduce a degree of complexity and opacity due to the combined decision paths of multiple models, which can obscure intuitive understanding. The method used here is conceptually similar to the Nearest Neighbors algorithm, except that all training samples are included in determining the class of a given test sample. This method also serves as an independent validation check when classifying transients from observed data in real surveys.

This analysis is performed across three binary classification schemes: SNe Ia vs. Non-Ia, SNe II vs. Non-II, and SNe Ibc vs. Non-Ibc. Since the number of other transient types in the dataset is fewer than 500 (out of more than 11,000), we do not attempt separate classifications for those classes.

The log-likelihood
\ln L_{j}({x}_{\text{test}})
of an observed feature vector
{x}_{\text{test}}
(with uncertainty
\sigma_{x_{\text{test}}}
) given the
j
-th training sample
{x}_{\text{train},j}
(with uncertainty
\sigma_{x_{\text{train}},j}
) is calculated as:

\ln L_{j}({x}_{\text{test}})=-0.5\Bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{15}\frac{(x_{\text{train},j,k}-x_{\text{test},k})^{2}}{\sigma_{x_{\text{train}},j,k}^{2}+\sigma_{x_{\text{test}},k}^{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{15}\ln(\sigma_{x_{\text{train}},j,k}^{2}+\sigma_{x_{\text{test}},k}^{2})\Bigg)

This likelihood is computed for each sample
j
in the respective training set.

The total (unnormalized) scores for the SN Ia and Non-SN Ia classes, denoted
S_{\text{SN Ia}}
and
S_{\text{Non-SN Ia}}
, are obtained by summing the exponentials of log-likelihoods for samples belonging to each class that satisfy a numerical stability threshold (
\ln L_{j}
>-700):

S_{\text{SN Ia}}=\sum_{j\in\text{SNIa}}\exp\left(\ln L_{j}({x}_{\text{test}})\right)

S_{\text{Non-SN Ia}}=\sum_{j\in\text{Non-SN Ia}}\exp\left(\ln L_{j}({x}_{\text{test}})\right)

Finally, the probability of an object being classified as a Type Ia supernova,
p(\mathrm{SN~Ia})
, is determined by normalizing these scores and incorporating a weighting factor
w
. In particular, the normalized scores are computed by dividing the total score by the number of training samples in each class:

p(\text{SN Ia})=\frac{\tilde{S}_{\text{SN Ia}}}{\tilde{S}_{\text{SN Ia}}+w\cdot\tilde{S}_{\text{Non-SN Ia}}}

The weighting parameter
w
is tuned by minimizing the total loss function

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\sum_{\text{SNIa}}(1-p)+\sum_{\text{Non-SNIa}}p,

where
p=p(\text{SN Ia})
is defined above.

In a similar manner, these equations can be defined for SNe II vs. Non-SNe II and SNe Ibc vs. Non-SNe Ibc.

The validation loss curve is plotted in Figure
11
. The optimal value of
w
, determined by tuning on the validation set, is shown as the title of each subplot. Using this value of
w
and a threshold probability of 0.5, the overall accuracy of the model is evaluated on the test set to enable a meaningful comparison with the machine learning algorithm.

Figure 11:
Validation loss for the function
\Sigma_{\text{Type}}(1-p)+\Sigma_{\text{Non-Type}(p)}
, where Type refers to Ia (left), II (middle), and Ibc (right). The weighing parameter, w, is tuned such that the validation loss in minimized. The optimum value of w is used to report the accuracy on the test set.

VI
Applications to DECam data

VI.1
The Dark Energy Survey Supernova Program

The spectroscopic sample of light curves from the first five years of the Dark Energy Survey Supernova Program
[
57
]
provides a valuable benchmark for evaluating the domain adaptability of our method, i.e., its ability to maintain performance when applied to data with properties differing from the training set. Domain adaptability is crucial because surveys often have varying or partially unknown observational characteristics, and the classifier must generalize robustly across varying observational conditions.

Our selection criteria include observations with reasonable signal-to-noise ratios (magnitude error <0.5) and at least five observations in at least one filter, resulting in a sample of 456 spectroscopically confirmed transients and variables. The DES data is available in four filters (g, r, i, z); we retrain the model using PLAsTiCC simulations, discarding the y-band parameters. We then apply our model to these 456 spectroscopically confirmed transients to evaluate its classification performance and assess its domain adaptability.

VI.1.1
Ensemble-based boosting

The model trained with the reduced feature vector set, using the previously tuned hyperparameter values from the PLAsTiCC validation dataset, achieves an accuracy of 89.7% and a precision of 95.9%. Out of 338 spectroscopically confirmed SNe Ia in the DES dataset, 306 were correctly identified, and out of 118 Non-SNe Ia, 105 were correctly classified. No systematic trend is observed in the misclassifications. Since the accuracy difference between the training dataset (PLAsTiCC) and the DES data is less than 10%, we can conclude that the model’s performance is reasonably satisfactory on DES data.

The redshift distribution of the PLAsTiCC dataset differs significantly from that of the DES spectroscopic data, with the latter dominated by low-redshift objects. We investigated the effect of matching the redshift distribution between training and test samples on the model’s performance. This was done by dividing the test samples into redshift bins, determining the fraction of samples in each bin, and selecting the same fraction of samples from the training dataset within the same redshift ranges. However, this reduces the size of the training data (unless artificial samples are generated), because the fraction of samples within specific redshift ranges can vary widely between datasets. Using this approach, we selected 2390 out of the original 11158 training samples (
\approx 20\%
). The resulting training set contains only 28.3% SNe Ia, whereas the DES dataset has 74.1% SNe Ia. The overall accuracy and precision obtained using this matched redshift distribution are 90.1% and 96.5%, respectively.

Since the performance metrics of both methods are statistically indistinguishable, we conclude that an ensemble-based classifier trained using FPCA scores is largely insensitive to the redshift distribution of the training data. This is a major advantage of the method, as the model does not need to be retrained on new simulations before being applied to observed data from a particular survey.

VI.1.2
Statistical Probabilistic Method

We apply the method outlined in Section 5.13 to classify the DES samples, using the matched redshift distribution for selecting the training sample as described in the previous section, since matching the redshift distribution provides slightly better performance than using an unmatched distribution.
The weighting parameter (the only hyperparameter for this method) is retained from the PLAsTiCC validation set, and probabilities are calculated based on the separation in parameter space for the
g
,
r
,
i
,
z
band features. Out of 338 spectroscopically confirmed SNe Ia in the dataset, 308 were correctly identified, and out of 118 Non-SNe Ia, 101 were correctly classified. The overall accuracy and precision obtained using this method are 89.7% and 94.8%, respectively. The performance of both methods is nearly identical, with CatBoost slightly outperforming the statistical method by roughly 1%.

Although the two methods are based on different underlying principles, their comparable performance on this dataset suggests that both are robust. Consequently, objects consistently classified as Type Ia supernovae by both methods are likely to form a more reliable subsample with reduced contamination, suitable for high-precision cosmological analyses.

The results for all cases of DES transients are tabulated in Table
11
. This application demonstrates the high domain adaptability of FPCA summary statsitics, in contrast to previous state-of-the-art approaches such as ParSNIP, which require retraining when applied to a new dataset.

VII
conclusions and discussions

In this paper, we have demonstrated the feasibility of photometric identification of Type Ia SNe using functional principal component analysis (FPCA) light-curve modeling combined with ensemble learning (CatBoost). This approach is novel in leveraging FPCA scores—previously shown to correlate with physical properties of SNe Ia—to distinguish them from other transients. The FPCA basis functions are derived in a data-driven manner, providing a flexible and empirical modeling framework capable of capturing higher-order modes of variability in the data, in contrast to methods based on rigid theoretical assumptions such as SALT2. Compared to state-of-the-art methods like Gaussian process regression or convolutional neural networks, our approach is computationally simpler and does not require GPU-intensive resources.

Our analysis incorporates all transients detected within 10 days past maximum light with at least five observations in one filter, regardless of type or redshift. Light curves are fitted by minimizing the residuals with respect to the observations, including penalty terms derived from clean samples, to mitigate the effect of outliers. Interband peak-time offsets are incorporated to model bands with limited observations, provided at least one band is well-sampled. Feature importance analysis shows that the peak absolute magnitude and first PC scores in the
r
,
i
, and
z
bands are the most discriminative for SNe Ia. Overall, the model achieves accuracy, precision, and AUC of 98.5%, 97.8%, and 0.997, respectively, comparable to state-of-the-art-methods. Performance is slightly reduced for high-redshift transients (
z\sim 1
) or light curves with narrow time coverage (
\sim
20 days). The performance remains robust for typical photometric uncertainties expected in surveys such as LSST (
\sigma
=0.1). The FPCA scores also enable robust classification of other transient types, such as SNe II and SNe Ibc.

We developed a complementary statistical probabilistic model based on an exponential metric in parameter space based on the same FPCA scores. This method provides comparable results (95.7%) to the ensemble approach (98.5%), serving as an independent validation.

Application to DECam-based surveys—including DES YR5, DECam DDF, and DESIRT—demonstrates the method’s high domain adaptability. Using the DES YR5 spectroscopic sample as a benchmark, we achieve
\sim
90% accuracy despite significant differences in redshift distributions compared to the training set. Unlike other state-of-the-art methods (e.g., ParSNIP, Avocado) that require retraining on new datasets, our approach performs reliably without retraining and is insensitive to the redshift distribution of the training sample. Predictions from both methods show strong agreement for DECam DDF and DESIRT transients, and the intersection of the two independent methods provides a high-confidence SNe Ia sample suitable for cosmological applications.

Overall, our data-driven light curve modeling combined with automated machine learning demonstrates robust performance across multiple surveys and transient types. This makes it a promising tool for analyzing millions of light curves from upcoming large-scale surveys such as LSST and Roman. The FPCA scores can also serve as informative features in future cosmological analyses, enabling deeper insights into the fundamental nature of the Universe.

Different ML Models

In this section, we compare the performance of different machine learning models for distinguishing SNe Ia from other types of transients. Figure
14
shows the accuracy and precision for four alternative models alongside CatBoost (used in this study), with accuracy and precision indicated by blue circles and red squares, respectively. Among these five models, only CatBoost can handle missing values directly; for the other four models, missing values were imputed using a nearest neighbors algorithm before being input to the classifiers.

Briefly, the models are as follows: Bagging is similar to boosting but connects constituent decision trees in parallel rather than sequentially; k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) classifies samples based on the closest neighbors in parameter space; Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward neural network with neurons whose weights are adjusted to minimize the loss; and Support Vector Classification (SVC) finds an optimal hyperplane separating the classes. As shown in the figure, both accuracy and precision exceed 95% for all methods except SVC, with CatBoost outperforming the others.

Appendix C
Data Visualization

In this section, we explore two methods of data compression suitable for visualization. For very high-dimensional feature spaces, the compressed data vectors can also be used for model training to reduce computational complexity. In our study, since model training and testing require less than a minute, data compression does not provide a computational advantage. Nevertheless, we train the model using two compressed feature vectors and compare the performance with the base classifier.

C.1
Principal Component Analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
[
27
,
56
]
is a linear dimensionality reduction technique commonly used in statistics and machine learning to compress data while preserving variance. The principal components (PCs) are constructed to be orthogonal, with the first PC capturing the largest variance, followed sequentially by higher-order components. We construct a 2-component PCA from our original 15-feature vector and show the distribution in the left panel of Figure
16
.. For a quantitative evaluation, we train and test the model using these two features, with results shown in the second column of Table
14
. For comparison, the performance using all 15 features is shown in the first column. All metrics exceed 90%, except precision (89.3%). The accuracy and precision decrease by about 6% and 9% respectively compared to the full feature set.

C.2
UMAP

UMAP
[
42
]
(Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) is a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique. It constructs a graph of high-dimensional data points based on their nearest neighbors, and then calculates the connectivity of each point to others, forming a “fuzzy topological structure” of the dataset. While PCA captures global variance, UMAP preserves local structures, which may lead to overfitting. The 2-component UMAP embedding is shown in the right panel of Figure
16
, and the corresponding performance metrics are presented in the fourth column of Table
14
. The performance is comparable to the default method using all 15 features and notably better than the PCA-based results.
