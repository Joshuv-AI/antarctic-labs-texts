First Aid Interventions for Intentional Caustic Chemical Assaults in Adults and Children: A Scoping Review
Authors: Jen Heng Pek, Nathan P Charlton, Phuong Thao Nguyen, Therese Djärv, Amy Kule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is limited evidence guiding optimal first aid management for caustic attacks, and this topic has not been systematically evaluated. We aimed to review the available literature to identify first aid treatments that demonstrate benefit. We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health Literature, American Psychological Association PsycInfo, and Cochrane Library from inception through September 13, 2025. This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological standards of the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Task Force Scoping Reviews, and reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist. We identified six studies that met the inclusion criteria: two prospective cohort studies, three case reports, and one case series. An alkali was used in two studies, an acid in three studies, and an unknown caustic in one study. Three studies had victims with skin and eye involvement, one had a victim with only skin involvement, and two had victims with only eye involvement. The decontaminants included water, water and soap, physiological solution or normal saline, and Diphoterine®. The various outcomes reported for post-decontamination skin exposure included scarring, need for surgical intervention and grafting, re-epithelialization time, vision, and evisceration for eye exposure. Available evidence, despite being limited and of low certainty, consistently supports immediate removal of contaminated clothing and copious irrigation with water as a biologically plausible and clinically accepted first aid intervention.

Introduction and background

The incidence of acid or alkali attacks, also referred to as caustic agent attacks or chemical assaults, is rising globally, with an estimated 10,000 cases annually worldwide [1]. The highest burden is borne by low- and middle-income countries, though incidence has increased in high-income regions as well [1]. Fatality is rare, but as the intention is to maim [2-4], survivors frequently experience lifelong disability, including scarring, visual impairment, repeated reconstructive surgeries, and are often accompanied by significant socioeconomic morbidity [4]. Caustic attacks can also have severe psychological sequelae, including anxiety, depression, and social isolation [5]. Family members, too, can be indirectly affected by the injury [5].

Epidemiologic data underscore the public health importance of these injuries. A review of data from India indicates an increasing burden of attacks from 2010 to 2019 [6], and data from Pakistan show approximately 200 cases of acid attacks annually [7]. In the United Kingdom, a 2012 study found that despite a decline in industrial chemical injuries over a 25-year period, there was a 42% increase in domestic chemical burns [8]. During an attack, caustic substances are often thrown into the victim’s face, causing permanent scarring and blindness. Commonly utilized agents are easily accessible through household and industrial cleaning products, including sodium hydroxide (NaOH), nitric acid (HNO3), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and, in some regions, hydrofluoric acid (HF) [9-11]. Significant gaps remain in the evidence guiding optimal first aid management for these victims despite the introduction or proposal of regulatory measures by several countries [5].

The severity of injury following a caustic attack is determined by multiple factors, including the chemical’s concentration, volume, mechanism of tissue penetration, and duration of exposure [12]. Unlike thermal burns, caustic injuries may continue to progress until the agent is removed or neutralized, making early intervention critical. First aid measures, particularly rapid decontamination, represent an opportunity to meaningfully reduce tissue damage before advanced medical care is available [13,14]. A systematic review in 2022 identified several observational studies supporting early cool water lavage in improving clinical outcomes in chemical burns but concluded that current evidence was limited [13]. Therefore, it is intuitive that water lavage would play a major role in the treatment of caustic attacks. However, direct evidence guiding optimal first aid management of caustic attack remains sparse and largely extrapolated indirectly from industrial accidents and animal models, or from small observational studies.

Given the increasing global burden of caustic assaults and the central role of first aid in providing early care, the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) First Aid Task Force prioritized this topic for formal evaluation. This scoping review conducted by the First Aid Task Force of ILCOR, which provided the basis for the development of the Consensus on Science Treatment Recommendations (CoSTR), aims to systematically map the existing evidence on first aid interventions for caustic agent attacks, identify key outcomes and limitations in the literature, and highlight critical knowledge gaps to inform future research and guideline development.

Review

Methodology

ILCOR uses a continuous evidence process to evaluate evidence and develop treatment recommendations for relevant first aid topics, culminating in the publication of a CoSTR. This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological standards of ILCOR Task Force Scoping Reviews [15,16]. Reporting was done in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [17], as provided in the Appendices. There is no published protocol or funding for this scoping review.

Search Strategy

Search strings were developed by a medical librarian for the following databases: MEDLINE, Embase, Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health Literature, American Psychological Association PsycInfo, and Cochrane Library. A search was performed from the inception date of the databases until September 13, 2025 (Appendices).

Study Selection

Records from the database searches were downloaded and imported into Covidence software for the removal of duplicates and screening [19]. Four authors (NPC, JHP, PN, AK) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Reasons for exclusion were documented, and any disagreements were discussed until a consensus was reached.

Data Collection

Four authors (NPC, JHP, PN, AK) participated in data extraction, with one author extracting the following data from each study: design, population, intervention(s), comparator and outcome(s), while another author verified the data for accuracy.

Data Synthesis

The results and findings of the included studies were presented as a descriptive narration.

Results

Among the six studies, there were two prospective cohort studies [21,24], one case series [22], and three case reports [20,23,25]. Regarding the location of the six studies, half (n = 3) occurred in Asia [21,23,25], whereas two were reported from North America [20,24] and one from Europe [22]. Considering the PROGRESS-Plus framework to describe equity factors [26,27], one case series [22] and one case report [20] involved males primarily, whereas in one case series [21] and two case reports [23,25] females were more commonly targeted. Age was frequently reported, with the majority affecting a younger population within the first four decades of life. Study characteristics and findings of included studies.

A total of 208 patients were identified across the six studies. In two studies, the caustic agent was identified as an alkali [22,24], an acid in three studies [20,21,25], and unknown in one study [23]. Three studies had victims with skin and eye involvement [20,21,25], one had a victim with only skin involvement [23], and two had victims with only eye involvement [22,24]. The agents used for decontamination included water [21,23-25], water and soap [20], physiological solution or normal saline [21,22,24], and Diphoterine® [24]. Pre-hospital decontamination was performed as first aid in three studies [21,24,25], and in the emergency department or hospital in three studies [20,22,23].

Outcomes

Reported outcomes included scarring [21,23], need for surgical [20,21,23,25] and grafting [21,23,25] for skin exposure and eye evisceration [22], time to re-epithelialization [24], and vision [20,22] for eye exposure.

Das et al. provided observational evidence in a prospective cohort that first aid water irrigation reduces burn depth and subsequent scarring in victims of acid assault [21]. In their study, 93 of the 126 patients with acid burns who received immediate water irrigation had significantly lower rates of hypertrophic scarring (11.4% vs. 48.5%) with a relative risk of 0.24 (95% confidence interval = 0.13-0.47). Patients presenting with superficial or superficial partial-thickness injuries were noted to receive water irrigation, requiring fewer surgical interventions. A case series by Kessel et al. (2015) described severe ammonium hydroxide ocular injuries and initial irrigation with saline among 13 patients [22]. They did not evaluate the efficacy of saline relative to other treatments, and outcomes appeared primarily driven by initial injury severity, with many eyes progressing to phthisis or evisceration. Irrigation was performed either as a first aid measure or initial hospital treatment in several case reports and case series [20,23,25]; however, we were unable to draw any conclusions from these articles due to the nature of these reports [18].

One prospective cohort study evaluated Diphoterine®, a commercially available amphoteric irrigation solution for the skin and eyes, compared to physiologic saline for ocular alkali burns [24]. Time to corneal re-epithelialization was substantially shorter with Diphoterine® irrigation among grade 1-2 injuries. Data on severe (grade 3-4) burns were insufficient for meaningful conclusions. [1] Similar to the skin studies, delays to irrigation correlated with injury severity, which reinforces the importance of early decontamination.

Discussion

This scoping review identified a limited body of evidence addressing first aid interventions for caustic attacks. Across six included studies, comprising prospective cohorts, case series, and case reports, there was consistent but low-certainty evidence supporting immediate and copious irrigation as an early intervention for both skin and ocular exposures. No randomized controlled trials were identified, reflecting the challenges inherent in studying intentional chemical injuries.

For chemical burns, the goal of treatment is to limit exposure and long-term impairment [3,11]. Current recommendations for first aid following accidental chemical exposure consistently emphasize that the first step should be immediate removal of contaminated clothing, followed by copious irrigation with water [28,29]. Water acts through several mechanisms: physically removing the chemical from the skin or eye, diluting the offending agent, and restoring tissue hydration and pH [12,30]. Although treatments could be intuitively extrapolated from industrial accidental chemical injury, the pattern of exposure and injury may not necessarily be the same [8,13,21]. For instance, there is a preponderance of caustic attacks directed at the face compared with other areas of the body, and due to the deliberate nature of the attack, there is a potential for larger volumes and stronger types of caustic agents which differ from industrial and accidental injury [21]. Important knowledge gaps persist regarding how lay providers can best deliver first aid for caustic attacks, including optimal irrigation parameters and the role of specialized solutions. As no prior review has systematically mapped the direct evidence addressing first aid interventions for intentional caustic attacks in adults and children, this scoping review adds important information to the current medical literature that begins to address these gaps.

The strongest direct human evidence for the treatment of caustic chemical attack comes from observational cohort data. In victims of acid assault, prompt water irrigation was associated with a markedly lower risk of hypertrophic scarring (relative risk = 0.2440; 95% confidence interval = 0.1265 -0.4706) and reduced need for surgical intervention [21]. These findings are clinically significant, given that scarring and reconstructive surgery represent major drivers of long-term disability and healthcare utilization among survivors. Early self-initiated or hospital-initiated irrigation was documented in multiple case reports and case series; however, while supporting the concept of early irrigation, conclusions could not be drawn from this data due to a lack of controls [20,22,23,25]. While conclusions cannot be drawn due to the observational nature of this data, one animal study suggested that the protective effect of irrigation is most pronounced when initiated early, reinforcing the concept that contact time is a critical determinant of tissue damage [31].

Evidence supporting the use of specialized decontamination agents, including amphoteric solutions, remains limited. A single prospective cohort study reported more rapid corneal re-epithelialization with Diphoterine® compared with saline in patients with mild-to-moderate alkali eye injuries, but data were insufficient to draw conclusions for severe burns or acid exposures [24]. Further limiting utility, these products are not widely available in many regions, and recommendations that depend on specialized agents may widen existing disparities in first aid care. In contrast, clean water is more broadly accessible, inexpensive, and familiar to lay responders, making it the most practical first aid option in most regions of the world.

Limitations

This review also highlights several important gaps in the literature. Few studies specifically evaluate first aid delivered in the prehospital or lay rescuer setting, despite the concept that immediate intervention is the most impactful intervention. No studies directly compared different irrigation volumes or durations, evaluated the role of clothing removal, and only one study evaluated an alternative decontamination approach in situations where water is not readily available. In addition, reported outcomes varied, further limiting meaningful comparison across studies.

Because randomization of immediate intervention techniques is often difficult and potentially unethical, research in this area will often rely on observational designs, registries, and animal models. Public health interventions, such as community education campaigns, may provide valuable opportunities to assess the effectiveness of first aid indirectly by examining changes in injury severity, surgical requirements, and long-term outcomes over time.

Conclusions

Caustic attacks are an increasing global problem with the potential for profound physical and psychosocial consequences. Current low-certainty evidence and mechanistic rationale support immediate removal of contaminated clothing and copious water irrigation as the most practical first aid intervention following caustic assault exposure. These interventions are associated with reduced tissue injury and improved clinical outcomes. Future research should focus on optimizing delivery of irrigation, including duration and volume, improving public education, and evaluating alternative strategies to ensure timely decontamination across diverse resource settings.
