Green synthesis of immobilized hybrid biocomposite from natural waste for dye sequestration: a kinetic and isothermal approach
Authors: Zubia Sajid, Rabia Shaheen, Marrij Afraz, Shahid Mehmood, Koyachew Bitew Abebe, Mohamed Abu Shuheil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the development of a low-cost and sustainable immobilized hybrid biocomposite for the removal of Drimarine Black CL-B (DB CL-B) dye from aqueous solutions. The biocomposite was synthesized using waste-derived granite powder (G) and Syzygium cumini leaf biomass (S), immobilized within a sodium alginate beads matrix (NAB) to obtain GS@NAB biocomposite. The biocomposite was characterized using SEM and FTIR techniques, confirming the surface morphology favorable for adsorption and successful incorporation of functional groups. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the removal efficiency under optimum conditions. The parameters such as adsorbent dosage, contact time, pH, and temperature were optimized to conduct the adsorption experiments. The results showed that the adsorption process followed a pseudo-second-order kinetic model, indicating chemisorption as the dominant mechanism. Equilibrium studies revealed that the adsorption data were best described by the Langmuir isotherm, suggesting monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface. Among htree parameter isotherms, Vieth-Sladek offers best fit (R2 = 0.999). While Langmuir describes the dominant adsorption behavior, Vieth–Sladek provides supplementary evidence that the GS@NAB surface contains structurally diverse domains arising from the combined mineral, biomass, and polymeric components. Overall, the developed GS@NAB biocomposite demonstrated effective dye removal performance up to six cycles and offers a cost-effective and environmentally friendly alternative for wastewater treatment applications.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The discharge of synthetic pigments from varnishes and paints, textile and tannery, cosmetics and ink, food and pharmaceutical, oil and gas, and other sectors is major issue in developing and developed countries [1]. According to an estimate about 70,000 tons of dyes are manufactured and transported to meet the global demands of these pigments [2]. Furthermore, it is reported that 10–15% colored effluents are released into the aquatic ecosystem during production process and disturb the marine life as they inhibit the sunlight and interfere in the process of photosynthesis [3].

The geological time scale suggests that rapid development of industries and human activities are primary cause of water pollution. It has now become a substantial ecological threat and raised public and global concerns due to critical environmental challenges. Dyes contain inorganic substances such as metals, organic compounds like alcohol, diesel, petroleum ether, phenolic substances and salts of several types that affect dissolved contents, suspended particles and water color. Due to their inertness, stability, heat resistance, non-biodegradability, severe carcinogenic, mutagenic and toxic effects these chemicals are hazardous for human health and aquatic life [4].

The development of effective, efficient and ecofriendly materials is required to sequester these pigments from aquatic biota. In recent years, biomaterials such as agro-waste are being used for treating dye polluted water due to their porosity, reusability, surface area and adsorptive efficacy [5]. Effective use of adsorption technique using biocomposite materials is viable due to its modest design, better ability of separation and regeneration [6]. The procedures are green, inexpensive, easy to functionalize, simple equipment, lower quantities of sludge production and minimization of added chemicals makes them environmentally friendly [7].

The novelty of this study lies in the integration of low-cost natural waste materials with a polymeric matrix to fabricate a stable, reusable, and efficient biocomposite adsorbent, along with a detailed kinetic and isotherm-based mechanistic evaluation. This approach not only contributes to waste valorization but also offers a sustainable solution for wastewater treatment. In present study natural waste such as granite powder, sodium alginate and fallen Syzygium cumini leaves were used to synthesize biocomposite beads using immobilization technique [8]. The research work aimed to report, the adsorption ability of GS@NAB for removal of DB CL-B dye in aqueous media. The effect of crucial factors including contact time and temperature were evaluated to gain profound understanding of dye adsorption mechanism. To calculate the rate of dye adsorption kinetic models are fitted to adsorption data [9]. Various non-linear isotherms with varying parameters are also applied to equilibrium data [10].

Despite significant progress in adsorption-based technologies, there remains a clear research gap in developing multifunctional biocomposites that simultaneously offer high adsorption efficiency, structural stability, and mechanistic clarity [6]. Additionally, limited studies have explored the combined effect of natural mineral–biopolymer–plant systems for dye removal using advanced non-linear modeling approaches. The outcomes of this research have potential future applications in large-scale wastewater treatment systems, particularly in textile and dyeing industries [11]. The developed biocomposite can be further optimized for continuous flow systems, regeneration cycles, and real environmental samples, thereby contributing to sustainable water purification technologies and circular economy strategies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and were utilized without any further purification. Sodium alginate (≥ 99% purity) and calcium chloride (CaCl₂, ≥ 99% purity) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98% purity) and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37% w/w, analytical grade) were obtained from Merck. Drimarine Black CL-B was sourced from local textile factory. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure water produced by a Milli-Q purification system (resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C) supplied by Millipore.

Granite powder was collected locally and thoroughly washed, dried, and sieved prior to use. Fallen leaves of Syzygium cumini were collected from natural sources, washed repeatedly with distilled water to remove dust and impurities, dried under ambient conditions, and ground into fine powder for subsequent use.

Instruments

The adsorption experiment was performed on PA250/25H orbital shaker and IST-3075 shaking incubator. The UV-VIS measurements were conducted on 721D spectrophotometer. A digital pH meter (Mettler Toledo, model SevenCompact) was used for pH measurements. The Schottky field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) Nova nanoSEM 450 was used for SEM imaging at 10 kV. Infrared analyses were carried out on Agilent Cary 630 Fourier-transform infra-red (FTIR) instrument.

Preparation of granite

Granite dust, waste of marble factory was crushed, grinded and further sieved to obtain desired particle size. Sample was dried at 80 °C in oven to remove moisture content. In order to increase the reactivity of materials, calcination was performed in muffle furnace (range 30–3000 °C) at 750 °C for 4 h. Sufficient water was added in calcined granite sample to make paste. Again, calcined paste granite sample was placed in muffle furnace for 4 h at 750 °C for calcination [12].

Preparation of Syzygium cumini leaf powder

The leaves were sourced as a post-distillation biowaste. The leaves were washed and dried to remove any moisture from them. They are further ground in a pestle and mortar and converted to powder form. After sieving, the material was stored in air tight containers [13].

Preparation of GS@NAB biosorbent

For immobilization, 2 g sodium alginate was added in distilled water 100 mL by volume. The mixture was stirred constantly with slight heating and then cooled to obtain a gel like solution. To the sodium alginate mixture 1 g granite and 1 g Syzygium cumini leaf poweder were added with continuous stirring to obtain a homogenous mixture. Sample was dropped into 0.1 M Calcium Chloride (CaCl2.2H2O) solution to obtain uniform beads i.e., granite and Syzygium cumini immobilized at sodium alginate beads (GS@NAB). These immobilized beads were washed twice and stored at 6 °C [14].

Adsorption experiments

Dye solutions of DB CL-B were prepared at pH 5 with a molar concentration of 25 mg/L. 20 mL of dye solutions were used in each experiment. pH values were adjusted using NaOH and HCl. Adsorption tests were performed by orbital shaking the dye solutions at the speed of 120 rpm for 60 min in orbital shaker [15]. The zeta (ζ) potential of -19.7 mV indicates anionic nature of adsorbent.

The reusability of the GS@NAB biosorbent was evaluated through successive adsorption–desorption cycles using 0.05 M NaOH and 0.05 M HCl as desorbing agents. Initially, adsorption was carried out under optimized conditions, after which the dye-loaded biosorbent (0.25 g) was separated by centrifugation and gently washed with distilled water to remove loosely bound dye molecules. For the desorption step, the spent adsorbent was transferred into 20 mL of the respective eluent (HCl and NaOH) and agitated under constant shaking conditions (≈ 150 rpm) for a fixed contact time of 60 min at room temperature. The suspension was then centrifuged, and the supernatant containing the desorbed dye was analyzed using UV–VIS spectroscopy. The regenerated adsorbent was thoroughly washed with distilled water until neutral pH was achieved and reused in subsequent adsorption cycles. This adsorption–desorption procedure was repeated for six consecutive cycles to assess the long-term regeneration performance of the material.

Results and discussions

Characterization of GS@NAB adsorbent

Scanning electron microscope is appreciated to study the morphological uniqueness of GS@NAB. It is vital to study features, porous structures and surface properties of biocomposite beads and also gives information about particle shape and size [34]. The SEM micrograph shows that GS@NAB surface was highly uneven, fibrous and porous which predicts interpenetrating alginate network contributed in dye biosorption. Figure 1b represents the SEM image of GS@NAB after dye adsorption. Literature studies indicate that the capacity of adsorbent to hold dye molecules increases when the porosity increases [35]. It is confirmed that cross-linked polymerization of biocomposite due to Syzygium cumini leaves powder improves surface morphology due to presence of cellulose polymer [36]. Therefore, this rough surface of biosorbent is useful for the dye sorption in this study [37].

IR spectrum enables to identify different functional groups present in GS@NAB, as functional groups play crucial role in dye sorption. Figure 1c shows FTIR spectrum of GS@NAB before and after DB CL-B adsorption. The spectra indicate an intense peak at 3250 cm− 1 representing −OH bond stretching due to Si−OH and Al−OH in granite [16]. The stretching vibrations of C-H (−CH3, −CH2, −CH) are represented by a band around 2950 cm− 1. These vibrations can be attributed to presence of sodium alginate. The peaks at 1500 cm− 1, 1210 cm− 1, and 900 cm− 1 are recognized as C−OH, C−C and C−O. The bands at 1750 cm− 1 can be assigned to the stretching vibrations of C = O group [38]. The indication of peak at 690 cm− 1 is an indication of quartz as predicted [39]. Due to strong interactions between DB CL-B and GS@NAB, the FTIR spectra before and after adsorption is slightly different in the intensity of few adsorption peaks [40].

The spectrophotometric analysis is very useful, simple, sensitive, accurate, rapid, and low waste producing technique. The maximum wavelength of Drimarine Black CL-B was performed and the λmax value was calculated to be 600 nm, as shown in Fig. 1d [41].

Optimization of parameters

The adsorption process was systematically optimized by evaluating the influence of key operational parameters, including adsorbent dosage, contact time, and solution pH, along with the point of zero charge (pHₚzc) of the adsorbent [42]. An optimal adsorbent dosage of 0.25 g was identified, beyond which no significant increase in removal efficiency was observed, as indicated in Fig. 2a. This behavior is commonly attributed to the aggregation of adsorbent particles at higher dosages, which reduces the effective surface area and limits the availability of active adsorption sites [43]. The equilibrium contact time was determined to be 60 min (Fig. 2b), indicating relatively rapid adsorption kinetics and efficient interaction between the dye molecules and the active sites of GS@NAB. The initial rapid uptake followed by a plateau suggests that adsorption proceeds through fast surface binding followed by slower diffusion into internal pores [44].

Solution pH was found to play a critical role in the adsorption process [45], with maximum dye removal achieved at pH 4 (Fig. 2c). This can be explained by considering the point of zero charge (pHₚzc) of the adsorbent at which net surface charge on the adsorbent is zero, which was determined to be 5 (Fig. 2d). At solution pH values lower than pHₚzc, the adsorbent surface becomes positively charged, thereby enhancing electrostatic attraction toward anionic dye species such as DB CL-B. Conversely, at pH values above pHₚzc, the surface acquires a negative charge, leading to electrostatic repulsion and reduced adsorption efficiency. These findings clearly demonstrate that electrostatic interactions play a dominant role under acidic conditions, while other mechanisms such as hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions contribute synergistically to the overall adsorption process [46].

Effect of temperature

Temperature variability is known to affect chemical potential and particularly the solubility and act as a controlling factor in adsorption process. Temperature can increase and decrease the adsorption process, therefore, the effect of temperature was considered at five different temperatures i.e., 30 °C, 35 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C and 55 °C, and adsorption was performed for DB CL-B 47. The results in Fig. 3a clearly depicted that amount of adsorption (mg/g) gradually decreases with the increment of temperature. It can be attributed to the weakening of adsorptive forces and deactivation of sorption sites on immobilized GS@NAB at high temperatures. Increase in the temperature can cause more expansion of pores that can cause leaching of adsorbate present on the surface of porous GS@NAB. Similar results are evaluated in other studies as well [48].

Kinetic studies

The experimental results for pseudo-first order (PFO), pseudo-second order (PFO), Elovich kinetic model (EKM) and intra-particle diffusion model (IPDM) are listed in Table 3 and fitted in Fig. 3b. The PFO describes adsorption as a surface phenomenon without involving mass or charge transfer. In accounts of correlation coefficient R2, PSO is more precise to describe adsorption [12].

The PSO, on basis of values of correlation coefficient R2 larger than other models and reduced chi-square χ2 better fits the adsorption data. The PSO is based on Langmuir’s equation and establishes the hypothesis that chemical reaction is rate determining step. It involves electron sharing and electron transfer between DB CL-B and GS@NAB [12].

Additionally, in EKM value of R2 is higher than PFO and equal to PSO indicating the chemisorption phenomenon [49]. The EKM is commonly used to describe adsorption processes occurring on highly heterogeneous surfaces, particularly where chemisorption is the rate-controlling step. It assumes that the adsorption rate decreases exponentially with increasing surface coverage due to an increase in activation energy and the progressive occupation of active sites. The value of α indicative of adsorption rate is greater than β corresponding to desorption entails that adsorption is favorable [50].

The IPDM was studied to determine rate limiting step that governs the uptake of DB CL-B onto the surface of GS@NAB. It involves several steps. The first step involves the diffusion of dye molecules into a film layer covering the GS@NAB surface [47]. The second step is known as boundary layer diffusion. DB CL-B dye molecules move across the stagnant parts of film layer towards the pores (micropores and mesopores) of GS@NAB biocomposite. The next step is intraparticle diffusion of dye molecules. DB CL-B molecules move from pores to the active sites. In final step, the molecules of dye adhere to the porous surface of GS@NAB [12, 51].

The experimental results are consistent with applied kinetic models indicating that adsorption is controlled by surface diffusion as well as chemical reactions. Figure 4. predicts the formation of several paths for adsorption of DB CL-B i.e., external diffusion, intra-particle diffusion and chemisorption [52].

Isotherm studies

One-parameter adsorption isotherm

Henry’s equation was used to determine equilibrium results generated from the experimental data of DB CL-B adsorbed onto GS@NAB (Fig. 4a), representing linear adsorption at low concentrations. The lower correlation coefficient R2 (0.7667) value signifies that this isotherm equation is ineffective to evaluate equilibrium results. Other than that, the adsorption data for this model was computed at higher concentrations of GS@NAB, therefore, Henry’s equation fails to fit the equilibrium data [47].

Two-parameter adsorption isotherm

The interaction between DB CL-B molecules and GS@NAB was studied using various two-parameter models. The value of correlation coefficient R2 and reduced chi-square χ2 was considered when selecting the best curve for two- parameter isotherm. The result entails that, Langmuir isotherm is most suited to the equilibrium data of GS@NAB among two parameter isotherms, as indicated in Fig. 4b.

The highest value of correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.994) and low value of reduced chi-square (χ2 = 1.507) for Langmuir model, signifies monolayer adsorption of DB CL-B on the surface of GS@NAB [34]. Furthermore, GS@NAB has the ability to uptake up to 58.287 mg/g, if we compare the values of QmL [53]. The dimensionless constant (RL) which determines the feasibility of Langmuir isotherm, reveals that results (0.1781 − 0.0415) were between 0 and 1, concluding favorable adsorption of DB CL-B onto GS@NAB. The reduction of RL with increased Co (mg/g) concludes that adsorption process for decolorization of aqueous solution is more favored at higher dye concentrations [54].

The equilibrium data was further evaluated by Freundlich adsorption isotherm (Fig. 4b) to assess how well GS@NAB removed DB CL-B dye. The value of Freundlich model exponent (1/nF=0.493) was less than 1.0, suggesting that adsorbent has high adsorption intensity at room temperature. The values of parameters and corresponding R2 as well as reduced χ2 is listed in Table 4. It showed that Freundlich isotherm has slightly low value of R2 compared to Langmuir model [55].

Temkin model (Fig. 4b) illustrates that heat exchange occurs during adsorption of DB CL-B onto GS@NAB. It provides a curve corresponding to correlation coefficient (R2= 0.989) and reduced chi-square (χ2 = 2.632). The value (BT = 12.410 J/mol) illustrates that binding energies associated with DB CL-B adsorption onto GS@NAB surface are uniformly distributed. The given model predicts that adsorption energy of each dye molecule in biocomposite monolayer could reduce linearly overtime due to process of adsorption.

The comparative analysis of Jovanovic isotherm with Langmuir isotherm (Fig. 4b), provides another estimation for localized monolayer adsorption. This isotherm fits the adsorption data moderately when compared to Langmuir isotherm. The value of adsorption capacity (mg/g) is also high in case of Langmuir isotherm [56].

Three- parameter adsorption isotherm

The affinities of various three- parameter isotherms were studied and their comparative analysis is given in the Fig. 4c, d, and all the model constants are listed in Table 4. These include the Redlich–Peterson isotherm, which combines features of Langmuir and Freundlich models and is applicable over a wide concentration range; Toth isotherm, developed to correct Langmuir limitations at high concentrations for heterogeneous systems; Koble–Corrigan isotherm, a hybrid model integrating Langmuir and Freundlich assumptions; Khan isotherm, which accounts for surface heterogeneity and variable adsorption energies; Hill isotherm, describing cooperative adsorption behavior; Fritz–Schlunder III isotherm, a flexible empirical model suitable for complex adsorption systems; Vieth–Sladek isotherm, often applied to heterogeneous adsorption with partitioning characteristics; and the Radke–Prausnitz isotherm, which effectively describes adsorption over both low and high concentration ranges. The inclusion of these models allows a comprehensive evaluation of the adsorption mechanism, surface heterogeneity, and interaction behavior of DB CL-B dye on the GS@NAB biosorbent. The revised text now clearly reflects the theoretical basis and applicability of each model, thereby improving the scientific rigor and interpretability of the adsorption analysis. Additionally, all three- parameter isotherms reduces either to Langmuir or Freundlich isotherm and emphasize the homogeneity and heterogeneity of adsorption sites.

In case of Redlich-Peterson and Toth isotherm (Fig. 4c)the values of model exponents (βRP = 0.777, nT= 0.710) were close to 1, signifying that both models are indictive of Langmuir isotherm, and entails homogenous type of adsorption [57].

In case of Koble-Corrigan (R2= 0.997), Khan (R2= 0.998) there is an insignificant discrepancy between values of R2, as indicated in Fig. 4c. Moreover, model exponents nKC and nK being close to unity indicates homogeneity of adsorption. For Khan isotherm, QmK (mg/g) also indicates better adsorption.

Additionally, Hill (R2= 0.997) and Fritz-Schlunder III (R2= 0.998) adsorption isotherms (Fig. 4d), there is slight difference between values of R2. Hill adsorption isotherm estimates the binding ability of various adsorbates onto homogenous surface. The Fritz-Schlunder III and Hill model exponent (nFS= 0.778, nH = 0.796) was found to be less than unity, describing that binding interaction between DB CL-B and GS@NAB is homogenous.

Radke-Prausnitz (Fig. 4d) value of model exponents close to 0.5 indicates both homogenous and heterogenous absorptivity but owing to low Qm value this model is not indicative of determining adsorption phenomenon [57].

Among the analyzed three-parameter isotherms Vieth-Sladek (Fig. 4d) showed best curve with better values of correlation coefficient R2 (0.999) and reduced χ2 (0.4580). Additionally, Vieth-Sladek isotherm is applied to solutes which are sorbed specifically: linear component applied to physical sorption in amorphous regions, and non-linear component corresponds to adherence of DB CL-B on the external surface of porous GS@NAB. The value of βVS (0.8466) was found to be close to 1, predicting that equilibrium data corelate with Langmuir isotherm [58]. Among the two-parameter isotherm models, the Langmuir model showed the best fit to the experimental data, indicating that the adsorption of DB CL-B onto GS@NAB mainly occurs as monolayer coverage on energetically similar active sites. This suggests that, at the macroscopic level, the adsorption behavior can be satisfactorily described by a relatively homogeneous surface. However, the applicability of the Vieth–Sladek model and the obtained βVS value of 0.8466 indicate that the adsorption system is not perfectly ideal and may involve slight surface heterogeneity and partial partitioning of dye molecules within the biocomposite network. Therefore, while Langmuir describes the dominant adsorption behavior, Vieth–Sladek provides supplementary evidence that the GS@NAB surface contains structurally diverse domains arising from the combined mineral, biomass, and polymeric components.

Adsorption mechanism

The adsorption mechanism of DB CL-B onto GS@NAB was interpreted by integrating the FTIR findings with the surface morphology obtained from SEM analysis. SEM images reveal that the adsorbent possesses a heterogeneous and porous structure with irregular surface features, providing a large surface area and numerous active sites for adsorption. After dye uptake, partial pore filling and surface coverage are evident, supporting the formation of an adsorbed layer and indicating the feasibility of multilayer adsorption, consistent with the Freundlich isotherm model.

The adsorption process is governed by multiple interaction mechanisms. Electrostatic attraction plays a significant role due to interactions between oppositely charged sites of the adsorbent and the anionic DB CL-B dye, particularly under favorable pH conditions. Hydrogen bonding occurs between hydroxyl and carbonyl groups of the adsorbent and electronegative atoms (–N, –O, –F) present in the dye molecules. Coordination interactions may also arise from lone pair electrons of oxygen-containing groups on the adsorbent interacting with electron-deficient centers of the dye. Furthermore, hydrophobic interactions between the organic framework of sodium alginate and the nonpolar regions of the dye contribute to adsorption [59].

The concept of π–π interactions has been carefully revised. Such interactions occur between aromatic π-electron systems rather than simple double bonds. In this system, π–π stacking can occur only if aromatic or conjugated domains are present within the adsorbent structure, which may arise from carbonaceous or graphitic components formed during composite preparation. These domains can interact with the aromatic rings of DB CL-B, facilitating additional stabilization of the adsorbed dye molecules (Fig. 5). Therefore, π–π interactions, if present, are attributed to interactions between aromatic structures of the dye and conjugated regions of the adsorbent rather than isolated double bonds [60].

Overall, the adsorption of DB CL-B onto GS@NAB is a multi-mechanistic process involving electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, coordination interactions, hydrophobic effects, and π–π stacking, as supported by both FTIR and SEM analyses. This comprehensive interaction network explains the high adsorption efficiency and heterogeneous adsorption behavior of the composite [61].

Reusability of adsorbent

The reusability results in Fig. 6, demonstrated that the GS@NAB biosorbent retained high adsorption performance over multiple cycles, particularly when regenerated using NaOH. The removal efficiencies of DB CL-B after regeneration with NaOH were found to be 87.85 ± 1.06, 88.97 ± 0.97, 86.35 ± 1.29, 83.21 ± 2.01, 82.89 ± 1.95, and 78.09 ± 1.09 for six successive cycles. In contrast, regeneration using HCl resulted in comparatively lower removal efficiencies of 80.98 ± 1.23, 79.65 ± 1.65, 78.54 ± 1.43, 72.65 ± 0.98, 67.65 ± 2.01, and 65.45 ± 1.89, respectively. The superior performance of NaOH can be attributed to its ability to enhance electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged dye molecules and the adsorbent surface, thereby facilitating more effective desorption, whereas acidic conditions are less efficient in disrupting these interactions. The gradual decrease in removal efficiency with increasing cycle number can be associated with partial blockage of active sites, incomplete desorption of dye molecules, and minor structural or surface alterations of the biosorbent during repeated regeneration.

Conclusion

The rapidly proliferating demand for commodities has triggered the release of various dyes from numerous industries into the aquatic ecosystems. Among these circumstances biowaste material is becoming a promising kind, alternative to replace conventional adsorbents in dye removal. GS@NAB was successfully synthesized by immobilization technique. This study demonstrates the successful development of a sustainable and cost-effective immobilized hybrid biocomposite (GS@NAB) derived from natural waste materials for dye removal applications. The integration of mineral, biopolymer, and plant-based components, along with the immobilization strategy, provides enhanced structural stability, ease of handling, and reusability compared to conventional adsorbents.

The overall findings indicate that the developed material exhibits reliable adsorption performance governed by surface interactions and adsorption mechanisms consistent with established kinetic and isotherm models. Beyond its adsorption efficiency, the key significance of this work lies in the utilization of waste-derived resources to produce an environmentally friendly adsorbent, contributing to waste valorization and sustainable water treatment strategies.

Despite these advantages, certain limitations remain, particularly regarding performance under complex real wastewater conditions and long-term operational stability. Addressing these aspects through scale-up studies, continuous flow systems, and extended regeneration cycles will be essential for practical implementation.

Overall, the GS@NAB biocomposite represents a promising and sustainable alternative for dye-contaminated wastewater treatment, offering a balance between performance, cost-effectiveness, and environmental compatibility.
