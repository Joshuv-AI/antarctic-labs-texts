Pair-density-wave superconductivity and Anderson’s theorem in bilayer nickelates
Hanbit Oh, Ya-Hui Zhang

Abstract
The recent experimental observations of high temperature superconductivity in bilayer nickelate have attracted lots of attentions. Previous studies have assumed a mirror symmetry ℳ \mathcal{M} between the two layers and focused on uniform and clean superconducting states. Here, we show that breaking this mirror symmetry via an applied displacement field can stabilize a pair-density-wave (PDW) superconductor, which is similar to the Fulde–Ferrell–Larkin–Ovchinnikov (FFLO) state, but at zero magnetic field. Based on a mean-field analysis of a model of d x 2 − y 2 d_{x^{2}-y^{2}} orbital with an effective inter-layer attraction, we demonstrate that the PDW phase is robust over a wide range of displacement field, interlayer hopping strengths, and electron fillings. Finally, we analyze disorder effects on interlayer superconductivity within the first Born approximation. Based on symmetry considerations, we show that pairing is weaken by disorders which break the mirror symmetry, even with unbroken time reversal symmetry. Our results establish bilayer nickelate as a tunable platform for realizing finite-momentum pairing and for exploring generalized disorder effects.

Bilayer structures introduce internal degrees of freedom that, when symmetry constraints are relaxed, can host unconventional states such as the pair-density-wave (PDW) superconductor
Agterberg et al. 2020

A typical realization of a PDW is the Fulde–Ferrell–Larkin–Ovchinnikov (FFLO) state, which relies on Zeeman splitting and explicitly breaks time-reversal symmetry

;

Identifying alternative mechanisms for time-reversal-symmetric PDW states remains a significant challenge, and the search for candidate materials is ongoing.
In this work, we propose that bilayer nickelates provide a promising platform to realize PDW states by breaking mirror symmetry.
Specifically, under the strong interlayer pairing scenario, the resulting superconducting state can acquire a finite center-of-mass momentum upon the application of an external electric field.
The momentum of the PDW is determined by the Fermi-surface mismatch between the two layers.
Using a mean-field analysis of an effective single-orbital model, we demonstrate the existence of a robust PDW state over a wide range of the phase space.

In addition, we investigate the interplay between interlayer pairing and disorder in bilayer nickelates.

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{0}+D\sum_{k}\left(n_{t,k}-n_{b,k}\right),

Mean-field theory.—

We now turn to interaction effects. We consider a phenomenological attractive interaction between the two layers, analogous to the effective interaction in conventional Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) theory. Specifically, we assume an interlayer interaction of the form,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J_{\perp}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\sum_{i}\left[\bm{S}_{t,i}\cdot\bm{S}_{b,i}-\frac{1}{4}n_{t,i}n_{b,i}\right].

(5)

While our aim is to demonstrate the qualitative possibility of a PDW state, a more quantitative description would require a strong-coupling approach based on detailed microscopic models, which we leave for future work. We emphasize that
J_{\perp}^{\mathrm{eff}}
here is not simply the
J_{\perp}
of the
d_{z^{2}}
orbital; rather, it is treated as an effective attraction. A more rigorous analysis would need to incorporate the local moments from the
d_{z^{2}}
orbital and interlayer repulsion
Oh et al. 2024
. Since we expect our qualitative results to depend primarily on the pairing symmetry, we employ this simplified model for illustrative purposes.

We perform a standard mean-field analysis using an ansatz for the interlayer pairing order parameter,

\displaystyle\langle c_{t,i\uparrow}c_{b,i\downarrow}\rangle=\Delta_{\bm{Q}}\exp(i\bm{R}_{i}\cdot\bm{Q}),

(6)

where
\bm{Q}
denotes the center-of-mass momentum of the Cooper pair. The resulting mean-field Hamiltonian is given by,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{MF}}=H_{\mathrm{eff}}-\frac{J_{\perp}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\Delta_{\bm{Q}}\,c_{t,\bm{k}^{+}}^{\dagger}(i\sigma_{y})\,c_{b,-\bm{k}^{-}}^{\dagger}+\text{H.c.},

(7)

where
\bm{k}^{\pm}=\bm{k}\pm\bm{Q}/2

Generalized Anderson theorem.—

We have shown that a mirror-symmetry breaking term, such as the displacement field
D
, significantly alters the phase diagram. We now turn our attention to disorder that breaks mirror reflection symmetry only locally. For conventional
s
-wave superconductors, Anderson’s theorem states that the pairing is stable against disorder as long as time-reversal symmetry
\mathcal{T}
remains unbroken. For our interlayer-paired superconductor, however, we demonstrate that disorder breaking mirror symmetry can suppress pairing even when time-reversal symmetry is preserved.

We consider a general spin-independent disorder potential defined in the two-layer basis,

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{dis}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0,1,2,3}V_{i}\,\tau_{i}\otimes\sigma_{0},

(8)

where
\tau_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
are Pauli matrices acting in layer and spin space, respectively. The symmetry properties of the disorder channels are determined by the antiunitary time-reversal operator
\mathcal{T}=i\sigma_{y}K
, where
K
denotes complex conjugation, and the unitary mirror-reflection operator
\mathcal{M}=\tau_{x}
. Accordingly, the four disorder channels are classified as, (i) mirror-symmetric and time-reversal-symmetric (
V_{0}
), (ii) mirror-symmetric and interlayer time-reversal-symmetric (
V_{1}
), (iii) mirror-antisymmetric and time-reversal-antisymmetric (
V_{2}
), and (iv) mirror-antisymmetric and time-reversal-symmetric (
V_{3}
).

Another important question is how this generalized Anderson theorem is modified in the presence of finite interlayer hopping
t_{\perp}
. To address this, we extend our analysis of

to the

Our derivation reveals that the expression for

Consequently, the generalized Anderson theorem established here remains applicable in realistic experimental settings where disorder is typically time-reversal symmetric. Notably, for mirror-symmetry-breaking disorder (

T_{c}
in bilayer nickelates is expected to be sensitive to disorder, and higher
T_{c}
values can be achieved in cleaner samples.

Conclusion.—

In this work, we have shown that breaking mirror symmetry via a displacement field in bilayer nickelates provides a natural tuning knob for realizing a pair-density-wave (PDW) state.
Within a mean-field framework, we demonstrated that the PDW phase is robust over a wide region of the parameter space.
Furthermore, through an analysis of disorder effects using the superconducting fitness formalism, we generalized Anderson’s theorem to interlayer spin-singlet pairing, showing that mirror-breaking disorder suppresses superconductivity.
Our results establish bilayer nickelates as a tunable platform for engineering finite-momentum superconductivity and exploring symmetry-protected robustness against disorder.

Appendix A
I. Two orbital model

We start from a two-orbital tight-binding model on a bilayer square lattice, described by the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t_{x}\sum_{l,\sigma}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(d^{\dagger}_{1,i,l,\sigma}d_{1,j,l,\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\right)

(S1)

\displaystyle-t_{z}\sum_{l,\sigma}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(d^{\dagger}_{2,i,l,\sigma}d_{2,j,l,\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle-t_{xz}\sum_{l,\sigma}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left((-1)^{s_{ij}}d^{\dagger}_{1,i,l,\sigma}d_{2,j,l,\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle-t^{\perp}_{z}\sum_{i,\sigma}\left(d^{\dagger}_{2,i,t,\sigma}d_{2,i,b,\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\right)+\Delta\sum_{i}(n_{i,1}-n_{i,2}).

Here,
l=t,b
labels the layer index, and
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
denotes the spin. We denote
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
as the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
and
d_{z^{2}}
orbitals, respectively. Interlayer hopping is included only for the
d_{2}
orbital, reflecting its strong out-of-plane character, while direct interlayer hybridization of the
d_{1}
orbital is negligible. The hopping parameters are estimated from density functional theory as
t_{x}=0.485
,
t_{z}=0.110
,
t_{xz}=0.239
, and
t^{\perp}_{z}=0.635

Luo et al. 2023
. The parameter
\Delta
represents the crystal-field splitting between the two orbitals.
The factor
s_{ij}=1
(
-1
) for bonds along the
x
(
y
) direction. The corresponding bond-dependent sign factor
(-1)^{s_{ij}}
encodes the symmetry of the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
–
d_{z^{2}}
hybridization on the square lattice.
On average, the electron filling is
n=2-x
per site (summed over spin), with
x\approx 0.5
relevant to experiments, corresponding to orbital fillings
n_{1}\approx 0.5
and
n_{2}\approx 1
.
The resulting band structure hosts
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
Fermi pockets, as depicted in Fig.
2
(d). Previous studies have shown that the
\alpha
and
\beta
pockets exhibit mixed orbital character, while the
\gamma
pocket is predominantly from the
d_{2}
orbital.

Appendix B
II. Details on Mean-field theory

We consider the effective one orbital model, introduced in Eq.
1
,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\phi_{\bm{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi(\bm{k})+D&\gamma(\bm{k})\\
\gamma_{(}\bm{k})&\xi(\bm{k})-D\end{array}\right)\phi_{\bm{k},\sigma}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\phi_{\bm{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{k})\phi_{\bm{k},\sigma}

with
\phi_{\bm{k},\sigma}^{T}=(c_{t,k,\sigma},c_{b,k,\sigma})
and

\displaystyle\xi({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})-\mu,

(S5)

\displaystyle\gamma(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t_{\perp}(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})^{2},

(S6)

and
D
is displacement field strength. Note that we implicitly set
\mu
to fix the electron density
n=1-x
.

We next incorporate the phenomenological attractive interaction as similar setup of BCS theory. Specifically interlayer density interaction
J_{\perp}>0
is considered,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J_{\perp}}{2}\sum_{i}\left[S_{t,i}\cdot S_{b,i}-\frac{1}{4}n_{t,i}n_{b,i}\right].

(S7)

We then employ a standard mean-field analysis with an ansatz for the interlayer pairing order parameter,

\displaystyle\langle c_{t,i\uparrow}c_{b,i\downarrow}\rangle=-\langle c_{t,i\downarrow}c_{b,i\uparrow}\rangle=\Delta_{\bm{Q}}\exp(i\bm{R}_{i}\cdot\bm{Q}),

(S8)

where
\bm{Q}
denotes the center-of-mass momentum of the Cooper pair.

The mean-field Hamiltonian in Nambu basis
\Phi_{\bm{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\phi_{\bm{k}^{+},\uparrow}\phi_{-\bm{k}^{-},\downarrow}^{*}\end{array}\right)
is

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{MF}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}\in B.Z.}\Phi_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}(\bm{k})\Phi_{\bm{k}}+E_{0}

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{k}^{+})&\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pair}}\\
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pair}}^{\dagger}&-\mathcal{H}_{N}^{T}(-\bm{k}^{-})\end{array}\right),\quad\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pair}}=-J_{\perp}\Delta\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

and constant energy term

\displaystyle E_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}\in B.Z.}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{H}_{N}(-\bm{k}^{-})\right)+NJ_{\perp}|\Delta|^{2}.

(S14)

Here,
\bm{k}^{\pm}=\bm{k}\pm\bm{Q}/2
is used.

For each
\bm{Q}
, the pairing amplitude
\Delta_{\bm{Q}}
is determined self-consistently from
H_{\mathrm{MF}}
and Eq.
S8
.
To further identify the phase among all
\bm{Q}
, we should compute the mean-field free-energy,

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{MF}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-T\log\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-{H_{\mathrm{MF}}/T}}\right)\right)+E_{0},

(S15)

and compare
F
for different
\bm{Q}
. The above mean-field analysis allows us to determine the energetically favored state as a function of system parameters.

Appendix C
III. Details on computing
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}

C.1
A. Setup

We again consider the two-band normal-state Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H_{N}=\xi(\bm{k})\tau_{0}+t_{\perp}(\bm{k})\tau_{x},

(S16)

where
\tau_{i}
(
i=0,1,2,3
) are Pauli matrices defined in the layer basis.
The corresponding band dispersions are given by

\xi_{\pm}(\bm{k})=\xi(\bm{k})\pm t_{\perp}(\bm{k})
,
which become degenerate in the absence of interlayer hopping,
t_{\perp}=0
.

Including interlayer pairing, the Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{BdG}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{N}(\bm{k}^{+})&\Delta\\
\Delta^{\dagger}&-H_{N}^{T}(-\bm{k}^{-})\end{array}\right),

with
\Delta=\Delta_{0}\,\tau_{1}
.

We consider a general spin-independent disorder potential in the two-layer basis,

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{dis}}=\sum_{i=0,1,2,3}V_{i}\,\tau_{i}.

(S20)

Here we restrict our analysis to the
s
-wave scattering channel, which is momentum independent.

It is convenient to transform from the layer basis to the band basis via a unitary transformation.
Under this transformation, the Pauli matrices in the layer basis are mapped to those in the band basis as

\displaystyle\left\{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\right\}\rightarrow\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{0},\tilde{\tau}_{3},\tilde{\tau}_{2},-\tilde{\tau}_{1}\right\}.

(S21)

C.2
B. 1st order Born approximation

We then employ the conventional first-order Born approximation (1BA), treating
V_{\mathrm{dis}}
as a perturbation
Abrikosov and Gor’Kov 1961
;
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
.
The corresponding self-energy is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma(i\omega_{n})=n_{\mathrm{imp}}\int_{\bm{k}}\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{dis}}\,G_{\mathrm{BdG}}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})\,\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{dis}},

(S22)

where
G_{\mathrm{BdG}}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})=(i\omega_{n}-H_{\mathrm{BdG}})^{-1}
is BdG Green’s function and
\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{dis}}
denotes the disorder potential written in the Nambu basis.
i\omega_{n}
is the fermionic Matsubara frequency. A diagrammatic representation is shown in Fig.
S1
(a).

We then decompose the self-energy in Eq.
S22
into contributions that renormalize the frequency and the pairing amplitude, denoted by
\Sigma_{\omega}
and
\Sigma_{\Delta}
, respectively.
These are given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\omega}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})=\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}\int_{\bm{k}}\frac{n_{\mathrm{imp}}V^{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{V}}\,G(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})\,{\widehat{V}}\Big],

(S23)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma_{\Delta}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})}{\Delta_{0}}=-\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}\int_{\bm{k}}\frac{n_{\mathrm{imp}}V^{2}}{2\Delta_{0}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{V}}\,F(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})\,{\widehat{V}}^{*}\,{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger}\Big],

(S24)

where
G
and
F
denote the diagonal and off-diagonal blocks of the BdG Green’s function
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{BdG}}
, respectively.
The limit
\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0
is taken since we will focus on the critical temperature, assuming that the superconducting transition is a continuous second-order phase transition. The hat notation in disorder potential and pairing denotes the normalized matrix, i.e.
{\widehat{\Delta}}=\tau_{1}
.

Figure S2:
Diagrammatic representation of (a) the first-order Born approximation and (b) the particle–particle susceptibility.

C.3
C.
t_{\perp}=0
case

We begin with the simple case
t_{\perp}=0
, where the two bands are degenerate with dispersion
\xi_{\bm{k}}
.
In the limit
\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0
, the normal and anomalous Green’s functions simplify to

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}G(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\bm{k}}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}},\quad\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}\frac{F(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})}{\Delta_{0}}=-\frac{{\widehat{\Delta}}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}}.

(S25)

Substituting these expressions into Eqs.
S23
and
S24
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\omega}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\omega_{n}}{|\omega_{n}|}\pi N(0)n_{\mathrm{imp}}V^{2},

(S26)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\Delta}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{0}}{|\omega_{n}|}\pi N(0)\frac{n_{\mathrm{imp}}V^{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{V}}\,{\widehat{\Delta}}\,{\widehat{V}}^{*}\,{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger}\Big],

(S27)

where we used
\int_{\bm{k}}=N(0)\int d\xi
, with
N(0)
the density of states at the Fermi energy.

The trace part in Eq.
S27
can be simplified using

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{V}}\,{\widehat{\Delta}}\,{\widehat{V}}^{*}\,{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger}\Big]=2-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{F}}_{c}^{\dagger}{\widehat{F}}_{c}\Big],

(S28)

where the superconducting fitness function is defined as

\displaystyle F_{c}=V\Delta-\Delta V^{*}.

(S29)

Substituting Eq.
S28
into Eq.
S27
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\Delta}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})=\frac{\Delta_{0}}{|\omega_{n}|}\pi N(0)n_{\mathrm{imp}}V^{2}\left[1-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}\Big({\widehat{F}}_{c}^{\dagger}{\widehat{F}}_{c}\Big)\right].

(S30)

The renormalized Matsubara frequency and pairing amplitude are therefore given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}+\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{n}}{|\tilde{\omega}_{n}|}\Gamma,

(S31)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}+\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{0}}{|\tilde{\omega}_{n}|}\left[1-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\tilde{F}_{c}^{\dagger}\tilde{F}_{c}\Big)\right]\Gamma,

(S32)

where scattering frequency,
\Gamma\equiv\pi N(0)n_{\mathrm{imp}}V^{2}=\tau^{-1}
is used.

As we show in the next subsection, the ratio between
\tilde{\omega}_{n}/\tilde{\Delta}_{0}\equiv u_{n}
is central quantity in determining the disorder-induced suppression of the critical temperature, as obtained from the linearized gap equation.
From Eqs.
S31
-
S32
, one can find

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{n}}{\Delta_{0}}=u_{n}\left[1-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}}{\Delta_{0}}\frac{\Gamma}{|u_{n}|}\right]

(S33)

with defining depairing coefficient

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\tilde{F}_{c}^{\dagger}\tilde{F}_{c}\Big).

(S34)

If the condition
\tilde{\omega}_{n}/\tilde{\Delta}_{0}=\omega_{n}/\Delta_{0}
is satisfied, meaning that the frequency and pairing renormalizations are identical (equivalently,
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}=0
), the superconducting state is robust against the corresponding disorder channel.

Finally, for the disorder channels
{\widehat{V}}=\tau_{i}
defined in Eq.
S20
and interlayer pairing

{\widehat{\Delta}}=\tau_{1}
, we find

\displaystyle F_{c}=0,\quad\text{for all channels except }\tau_{3}.

(S35)

consequently,
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}=0
for all channels except
\tau_{3}
, while
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}=2
for
\tau_{3}
.
This result directly leads to Table
1
.
The
\tau_{3}
disorder channel breaks the composite
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{T}
symmetry, while all other channels preserve it.

C.3.1
Renormalized gap equation and
T_{c}

We now evaluate the critical temperature in the presence (absence) of disorder, denoted by
T_{c}
(
T_{c,0}
).
In the presence of impurity scattering, the gap equation is modified as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2g}=\frac{T_{c,0}}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}}\;\rightarrow\;\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2g}=\frac{T_{c}}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\omega_{n}}\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{0}}{\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}},

(S36)

where
g>0
denotes the attractive pairing interaction. The gap equation is achieved by the second order Ginzburg Landau diagram as illustrated in Fig.
S2
(b).

Replacing the momentum summation by an integral over energies near the Fermi surface and using Eqs.
S31
-
S32
, the gap equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2g}\simeq\pi N(0)T_{c,0}\!\!\sum_{|\omega_{n}|<\Lambda_{UV}}\!\!\frac{1}{|\omega_{n}|}\;\rightarrow\;\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2g}\simeq\pi N(0)T_{c}\!\!\sum_{|\omega_{n}|<\Lambda_{UV}}\!\!\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{0}}{|\tilde{\omega}_{n}|},

(S37)

where
\Lambda_{UV}
is an ultraviolet energy cutoff imposed on the Matsubara frequencies.

Substituting the renormalized parameters from Eq.
S33
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2g}\simeq\pi N(0)T_{c}\sum_{|\omega_{n}|<\Lambda_{UV}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\Gamma\Bigg]

(S38)

To perform the Matsubara summation in Eq.
S38
, we make use of the digamma function
\Psi(z)
and its derivative
\Psi^{(1)}(z)=d\Psi(z)/dz
defined as

\displaystyle\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\xi-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[\frac{1}{n+z}-\frac{1}{n+1}\Bigg],\quad\Psi^{(1)}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+z)^{2}},

where
\xi
denotes the Euler constant.
For large
|z|
, the asymptotic forms

\Psi(z)\simeq\log z
and
\Psi^{(1)}(z)\simeq 1/z
can be used.

Applying these identities yields

\displaystyle 2\pi T_{c}\sum_{\omega_{n}=0}^{\Lambda_{UV}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigg[\Psi\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-\Psi\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Lambda_{UV}}{2\pi T_{c}}\right)\Bigg]

(S39)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-\Bigg[\Psi\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-\log\!\left(\frac{\Lambda_{UV}}{2\pi T_{c}}\right)\Bigg],

and

\displaystyle 2\pi T_{c}\sum_{\omega_{n}=0}^{\Lambda_{UV}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi T_{c}}\Bigg[\Psi^{(1)}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-\Psi^{(1)}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Lambda_{UV}}{2\pi T_{c}}\right)\Bigg]

(S40)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi T_{c}}\Bigg[\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}-\frac{2\pi T_{c}}{\Lambda_{UV}}\Bigg],

valid in the limit
T_{c}\ll\Lambda_{UV}
.

Finally, we obtain the disorder-suppressed critical temperature,

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T_{c,0}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}}{T_{c,0}}\Gamma,

(S41)

where the parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}
quantifies the suppression of the critical temperature due to disorder.

Figure S3:

Transition temperature
T_{c}
as a function of the scattering rate
\Gamma
, obtained from Eq.
S51
.
The
\tau_{3}
disorder channel, which breaks mirror symmetry, suppresses superconductivity (i.e.
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}=2
) irrespective of whether
t_{\perp}=0
or finite.

C.4
D.
t_{\perp}\neq 0
case

For the case where
t_{\perp}\neq 0
, the band splitting modifies the dispersion to
\xi_{\pm}(\bm{k})=\xi(\bm{k})\pm|t_{\perp}|
, which may modify the expression for the depairing coefficient
\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}
. Following the framework introduced in Sec. III A, we evaluate the normal and anomalous Green’s functions,
G
and
F
, in the limit
\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0
to calculate the self-energies.

In the band basis, the normal Green’s function is expressed as,

\displaystyle G_{N}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{+}(\bm{k}))^{-1}+(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{-}(\bm{k}))^{-1}\right]\tilde{\tau}_{0}

(S42)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{+}(\bm{k}))^{-1}-(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{-}(\bm{k}))^{-1}\right]\tilde{\tau}_{3},

where the tilde notation denotes Pauli matrices defined in the band basis. The anomalous Green’s function in the same limit is given by,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}\frac{F(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})}{\Delta_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{N}{\widehat{\Delta}}G_{N}^{*}=G_{N}G_{N}^{*}{\widehat{\Delta}},

(S43)

where we have used the fact that
[G_{N},{\widehat{\Delta}}]=0
, as both operators are diagonal in the band basis (i.e., expressed in
\tilde{\tau}_{0}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{3}
).

Substituting these expressions into the self-energy equations, we obtain the results for
t_{\perp}\neq 0
. For the frequency self-energy, we find,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{V}}G(\bm{k},i\omega_{n}){\widehat{V}}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\left(G_{N}{\widehat{V}}+[{\widehat{V}},G_{N}]\right){\widehat{V}}\Big]=\mathrm{Tr}\Big[G_{N}{\widehat{V}}{\widehat{V}}\Big]

(S44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{i\omega_{n}+\xi_{+}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{+}^{2}}+\frac{i\omega_{n}+\xi_{-}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{-}^{2}}\right),

(S45)

and for the pairing self-energy,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta_{0}\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta_{0}}\mathrm{Tr}\Big[{\widehat{V}}F(\bm{k},i\omega_{n}){\widehat{V}}^{*}{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\big([{\widehat{V}},G_{N}G_{N}^{*}]+G_{N}G_{N}^{*}{\widehat{V}}\big){\widehat{\Delta}}{\widehat{V}}^{*}{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger}\Big]

(S46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big[G_{N}G_{N}^{*}{\widehat{V}}{\widehat{\Delta}}{\widehat{V}}^{*}{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{+}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{-}^{2}}\right)\times\Big[1-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}({\widehat{F}}_{c}^{\dagger}{\widehat{F}}_{c})\Big].

(S47)

In Eqs. (
S44
) and (
S46
), the terms involving commutators vanish upon taking the trace
\mathrm{Tr}([{\widehat{V}},G_{N}]{\widehat{V}})=\mathrm{Tr}([{\widehat{V}},G_{N}G_{N}^{*}]{\widehat{\Delta}}{\widehat{V}}^{*}{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\dagger})=0
, because
{\widehat{V}}
is one of Pauli matrix
\tilde{\tau}_{i}
, and
G_{N}
and
G_{N}G_{N}^{*}
are expressed only by
\{\tilde{\tau}_{0},\tilde{\tau}_{3}\}
.

Performing the momentum summation, the effective density of states (DOS) is modified to
\bar{N}(0)=\frac{1}{2}(N_{+}(0)+N_{-}(0))
. Crucially, however, the ratio
u_{n}=\tilde{\omega}_{n}/\tilde{\Delta}_{n}
remains identical to the
t_{\perp}=0
case (See Eq.
S33
),

\displaystyle u_{n}\equiv\frac{\omega_{n}}{\Delta_{0}}=u_{n}\left[1-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}}{\Delta_{0}}\frac{\Gamma}{|u_{n}|}\right],

(S48)

with the same depairing coefficient,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{F}_{c}^{\dagger}\tilde{F}_{c}).

(S49)

C.4.1
Renormalized gap equation and
T_{c}

We now evaluate the critical temperature in the presence of disorder,
T_{c}
. The linearized gap equation is given by,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g}=\frac{T_{c}}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\omega_{n}}\left[\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{0}}{\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}+\xi_{+,\bm{k}}^{2}}+\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{0}}{\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}+\xi_{-,\bm{k}}^{2}}\right].

(S50)

After performing the summation over momentum and Matsubara frequencies, we observe that since the ratio
u_{n}=\tilde{\omega}_{n}/\tilde{\Delta}_{0}
is derived to be unchanged, the functional form of the
T_{c}
suppression remains the same with the
t_{\perp}=0
case,

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T_{c,0}}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{dis}}}{T_{c,0}}\Gamma.

(S51)

While the same ratio between
T_{c}/T_{c,0}
holds, the clean-limit critical temperature
T_{c,0}
itself is rescaled by the change in the DOS,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{c,0}(t_{\perp})}{T_{c,0}(t_{\perp}=0)}=\frac{2N(0)}{N_{+}(0)+N_{-}(0)},

(S52)

where
N_{\pm}(0)
denotes the DOS of the
\xi_{\pm}
band at the Fermi level.
