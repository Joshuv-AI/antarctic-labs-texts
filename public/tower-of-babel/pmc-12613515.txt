Prediction models for encephalitis and in-hospital death associated with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome
Authors: Xiaoxin Wu, Juntao Tan, Yuxi Zhao, Jianhua Niu, Xinting Cen, Mohammadjavad Feiz, Songjia Tang, Zhengyu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging infectious disease associated with encephalitis and high in-hospital death rates. This study aimed to develop and validate two predictive models for the early identification of encephalitis and prevention of in-hospital death in SFTS patients.MethodsThis retrospective study included SFTS patients admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2017 and July 2024. To construct the prediction models, we utilized least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) coupled with logistic regression, subsequently presenting the results through a comprehensive nomogram. The performance of the models was rigorously evaluated across multiple dimensions: discrimination ability was measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), calibration accuracy was assessed through calibration curves and Brier scores, and clinical applicability was determined using decision curve analysis (DCA) and a clinical impact curve (CIC).ResultsThe analysis of data from 196 SFTS patients revealed significant clinical outcomes, with an in-hospital death rate of 16.84% (33 patients) and an encephalitis incidence rate of 45.41% (89 patients). The in-hospital death prediction model and the encephalitis prediction model demonstrated robust performance, achieving AUROCs of 0.935 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.884–0.986) and 0.895 (95% CI: 0.839–0.952), respectively. Additionally, the Brier scores for these models were 0.066 and 0.120, respectively, indicating a high level of accuracy in their predictions. The DCA and CIC further revealed that both models achieved a relatively greater net benefit, suggesting their potential clinical applicability. Notably, age and ferritin levels were identified as the most significant variables coselected by both models, highlighting their importance in predicting outcomes.ConclusionThe predictive models developed in this study demonstrated robust performance in predicting both in-hospital death and encephalitis incidence among SFTS patients. Notably, age and ferritin levels emerged as significant independent risk factors, showing strong associations with both clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12018-7.Keywords: Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, In-hospital death, Encephalitis, Prediction model, Nomogram

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging infectious disease caused by the SFTS virus, which is also known as Dabie bandavirus [1–3]. SFTS was first reported in China in 2009 [1]. However, this disease has become a significant public health risk, with increasing incidence in other parts of the world, such as Japan, South Korea, and Vietnam [3–6]. Patients with SFTS typically present with fever, thrombocytopenia and leukopenia. Severe patients are more likely to exhibit respiratory infection symptoms and neurological symptoms [1, 7, 8]. Critically ill patients may experience multiorgan failure and even death. Thousands of SFTS patients are diagnosed worldwide every year, and the mortality rate of SFTS is still approximately 5%-30%, depending on the treatment centre [7, 9, 10]. Early diagnosis and treatment of SFTS may help reduce mortality. Owing to its heavy burden, high mortality rate, and lack of vaccines and effective therapies, this disease has become an important health issue [7].

The complications of SFTS include pneumonia, invasive pulmonary fungal infections, and SFTS-associated encephalitis (SFTSAE) [2, 10, 11]. Although it is a severe complication of SFTS, SFTSAE has not yet been fully studied. The mortality rate of SFTSAE has been reported to be over 40% [1, 12]. At present, some studies have shown that neurological symptoms are common among SFTS patients in the clinic, indicating that SFTSAE is highly important for clinical diagnosis and differential diagnosis. Previous reports have confirmed that the high mortality rate of SFTS is closely associated with neurological complications [13–15]. Moreover, the increasing incidence of SFTSAE merits the attention of clinicians [15, 16]. Although there is currently no recognized effective treatment for SFTSAE, early intervention and treatment can improve the prognosis of SFTS [17].

An increasing number of indicators have been found to be related to the incidence of SFTSAE and can be early disease markers [13–17]. To help clinicians quickly and easily predict the occurrence of encephalitis and in-hospital death associated with SFTS, developing predictive models based on basic demographic data, clinical symptoms, underlying diseases, laboratory parameters and common complications is highly important. Nomograms can integrate multiple predictive indicators, are a widely used prediction model, and have been used in the study of a variety of diseases, such as cancer and type 2 diabetes [18–20]. In this study, we aimed to develop and validate two predictive models for the early identification of encephalitis and in-hospital death in SFTS patients to help clinicians identify SFTSAE high-risk patients; achieve early prevention, detection, and intervention; and thus improve the outcomes of patients.

Methods

Data source and study participants

SFTS patients admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2017 and July 2024 were enrolled in this retrospective study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 0297). Given the retrospective design of the research, informed consent from participants was waived. This study was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments.

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) patients presenting with acute fever (body temperature ≥ 38 °C) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100 × 109/L); (2) patients with a history of exposure to endemic areas, contact with SFTS patients, or tick bites within 2 weeks prior to symptom onset; and (3) laboratory confirmation of SFTS virus infection through RT‒PCR, seroconversion of SFTS-specific IgM antibodies, or virus isolation. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) patients with other confirmed causes of fever and thrombocytopenia (e.g., bacterial sepsis, dengue fever, or other viral haemorrhagic fevers); (2) patients with incomplete clinical or laboratory data; and (3) patients who received immunosuppressive therapy or have underlying immunodeficiency disorders that may affect laboratory results. In total, 196 SFTS patients were included in this study.

The criteria for the clinical diagnosis of SFTS-related encephalitis were meeting the SFTS criteria and having encephalitis simultaneously. Encephalitis was diagnosed according to the definition put forth by the International Encephalitis Consortium [21], and the criterion for encephalitis is medical attention with altered mental status (defined as a decreased or altered level of consciousness, lethargy or personality change) lasting ≥ 24 h with no alternative cause identified.

A total of 196 eligible patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 137, 70%) and an internal validation set (n = 59, 30%) using computer-generated random numbers. The training set was used for variable selection and model development, whereas the internal validation set was used to assess model performance. Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were analysed using the t test, whereas nonnormally distributed data are reported as the median (interquartile range) and were assessed with the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical data are expressed as n (%) and were analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

The modelling variables were selected through a three-step process. First, univariate logistic regression identified variables significantly associated with outcomes (P < 0.05). Second, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to eliminate multicollinearity and reduce overfitting. The optimal lambda value (λ) was determined via the one-standard-error (λ.1se) rule, selecting the most parsimonious model within one standard error of the minimum cross-validation error. Variables with nonzero coefficients at λ.1se were retained. Finally, these variables were subjected to multivariate logistic regression. Multicollinearity among the final predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with a threshold of a VIF < 5 indicating no significant collinearity. Predictors with P < 0.05 in the final model were incorporated into the nomogram. This approach balanced model simplicity with predictive accuracy.

For model evaluation, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was calculated for both the training set and the internal validation set. Additionally, the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were assessed. A calibration plot was generated to visualize the agreement between the predicted risk values and actual observed outcomes, and the Brier score was computed. Definitions and formulas for all the performance metrics are provided in Supplementary Table 1. To evaluate the clinical utility of the model, decision curve analysis (DCA) and a clinical impact curve (CIC) were performed to quantify the net benefit to patients and consider the implications of incorrect and missed judgements.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.3, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). A significance level of P < 0.05 was applied for all the statistical tests.

Results

Demographics and clinical symptoms

In the training set, 20 (14.60%) patients died in the hospital. The percentages of patients that received ICU treatment (30.00% vs. 7.69%, p = 0.003), had convulsions (25.00% vs. 7.69%, p = 0.018), received a blood transfusion (65.00% vs. 31.62%, p = 0.009), received intravenous immunoglobulin (80.00% vs. 44.44%, p = 0.007), and presented with encephalitis (95.00% vs. 34.19%, p < 0.001) in the death group were greater than those in the survival group. The mean age of the patients in the death group was greater than that of those in the survival group (73.30 years vs. 63.32 years) (Table 1). In addition, compared with the nonencephalitis group, the encephalitis group had a greater proportion of patients that received ICU treatment, and these patients were more likely to have tremors, lethargy, dysphoria, and convulsions. Additionally, these patients were more frequently diagnosed with hypertension, cerebral infarction, and IPFI (Supplementary Table 2).

Selection of predictors for in-hospital death and encephalitis

Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate logistic regression (see Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Tables 2–3) were included in the LASSO regression for further selection. To identify predictors of in-hospital death, 23 variables were initially included in the LASSO regression analysis, three of which were identified as significant predictors: age, encephalitis, and ferritin (Fig. 2A, B). Subsequent multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed these variables as independent risk factors for in-hospital death. Specifically, older age (odds ratio [OR]: 1.174, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.075–1.317, p = 0.002), encephalitis (OR: 14.680, 95% CI: 2.541-279.522, p = 0.013), and ferritin levels (OR: 1.030, 95% CI: 1.013–1.053, p = 0.002) were significantly associated with increased mortality risk. The final logistic regression equation was logistic (Pin-hospital death) = -15.7219 + 0.1604*age + 2.6865*encephalitis + 0.0298*ferritin.

Five predictors of encephalitis risk were identified through LASSO regression: age, tremors, dysphoria, hypertension, and ferritin levels (Fig. 2C, D). The multivariate logistic regression model further validated these factors as independent predictors of encephalitis risk. Age (OR: 1.075, 95% CI: 1.017–1.145, p = 0.015), the presence of tremors (OR: 34.236, 95% CI: 7.113-278.279, p < 0.001), dysphoria (OR: 7.837, 95% CI: 2.124–38.496, p < 0.004), hypertension (OR: 3.529, 95% CI: 1.289–10.291, p < 0.016), and elevated ferritin levels (OR: 1.038, 95% CI: 1.013–1.053, p < 0.002) were significantly associated with an increased risk of encephalitis. The final logistic regression equation was logistic(Pencephalitis) = -7.1232 + 0.0727*age + 3.5333*tremor + 2.0588*dysphoria + 1.2609*hypertension + 0.0375*ferritin.

For full transparency, Supplementary Table 4 provides detailed coefficient trajectories for all candidate variables in both λ.min and λ.1se models, along with their retention status in the final models. This table includes variable names, coefficients at each λ threshold, and whether they were selected for inclusion in the subsequent multivariate logistic regression. In addition, multicollinearity diagnostics using the VIF values confirmed the robustness of the final models. In the in-hospital death model, the VIF values were 1.378 (age), 1.013 (ferritin), and 1.393 (encephalitis). In the encephalitis model, the VIF values were 1.164 (age), 1.146 (tremor), 1.010 (dysphoria), 1.168 (hypertension), and 1.090 (ferritin). All the VIF values were below the threshold of 5, indicating that there was no significant multicollinearity among the predictors in either model.

Development and validation of the in-hospital death model

A nomogram based on the three-predictor model was developed to predict in-hospital death (Fig. 3). The model demonstrated robust predictive performance, achieving an AUROC of 0.935 (95% CI: 0.884–0.986) in the training set and 0.896 (95% CI: 0.818–0.974) in the internal validation set (Fig. 4A). The calibration curves revealed excellent concordance between the predicted and observed outcomes, with Brier scores of 0.066 for the training set (Fig. 4B) and 0.125 for the internal validation set (Fig. 4C). These results collectively indicate that the nomogram possesses strong discriminative power and calibration accuracy for the prediction of in-hospital death. The detailed performance metrics are summarized in Table 3.

Furthermore, DCA suggested that the model has favourable clinical utility across a range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4D). The net benefit curve in the training set decreases at threshold probabilities > 80%, a phenomenon that is consistent with the DCA methodology. At extremely high thresholds, clinicians would treat only patients with near-certain predicted risk. In this scenario, the model identifies very few true positives (due to stringent thresholds), whereas the harm of false positives (unnecessary interventions) becomes proportionally significant, reducing the net benefit. The CICs further confirmed that the model delivers superior net benefits within the clinically relevant probability range for both the training set (Fig. 4E) and the internal validation set (Fig. 4F).

Development and validation of the encephalitis model

Additionally, a second nomogram was constructed using the five-predictor model to predict the risk of encephalitis (Fig. 5). This model also exhibited exceptional predictive accuracy, with an AUROC of 0.895 (95% CI: 0.839–0.952) in the training set and 0.838 (95% CI: 0.735–0.941) in the internal validation set (Fig. 6A). Calibration analysis also revealed a high degree of alignment between the predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, supported by Brier scores of 0.120 for the training set (Fig. 6B) and 0.176 for the internal validation set (Fig. 6C). These findings underscore the strong discriminative ability and precise calibration of the nomogram for encephalitis prediction. The comprehensive performance metrics are detailed in Table 3. Moreover, DCA also indicated that the model offers significant clinical utility across a wide range of probability thresholds (Fig. 6D). Further validation by the CICs also indicated that the model consistently delivers greater net benefits within the clinically meaningful probability range for both the training set (Fig. 6E) and the internal validation set (Fig. 6F).

Post hoc analysis of the relationship between encephalitis and disease severity in survivors

To address whether encephalitis is associated with disease severity among survivors, we performed a post hoc analysis comparing clinical and laboratory parameters between encephalitis (n = 57) and nonencephalitis (n = 106) patients in the survival group. Encephalitis survivors presented significantly higher peak ferritin levels (median: 14.44 vs. 4.98 mg/L, p < 0.001), D-dimer levels (median: 3.96 vs. 1.81 mg/L, p < 0.001), and increased ICU admission rates (21.05% vs. 0%, p < 0.001). CNS symptoms (e.g., tremors) were also more common in encephalitis survivors (38.60% vs. 2.83%, p < 0.001). The full results are detailed in Supplementary Table 5.

Discussion

Over the past 15 years, clinicians and researchers have made significant efforts to elucidate the characteristics and mechanisms of SFTS, as well as to improve its diagnosis and treatment. Despite these advances, SFTS remains associated with high mortality rates, and no specific antiviral drugs or vaccines have been approved for clinical use [22, 23]. The disease is highly sporadic and geographically widespread, and patients often initially present symptoms at various health care facilities. Many physicians lack comprehensive familiarity with SFTS, particularly when complications such as encephalitis arise, which are linked to a significantly elevated case fatality rate (CFR). Moreover, the incidence of SFTS-associated encephalitis (SFTSAE) has shown an increasing trend. Early detection, timely intervention, and appropriate treatment are critical for reducing mortality rates. Recent studies have identified risk factors for SFTSAE, although these vary across patient populations. For example, Youdong Xu et al. identified bleeding symptoms, elevated D-dimer levels, and hyperamylasemia as independent risk factors for central neurological complications in SFTS patients [13]. Similarly, Min Wang et al. reported that pulmonary rales, atrial fibrillation, high serum SFTS viral RNA loads, and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels independently predict SFTSAE [16]. Furthermore, Xiao Fei et al. reported that SFTS patients with neurological complications exhibit increased mortality rates, with decreased platelet counts and elevated creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) levels serving as independent prognostic indicators [7, 15]. Collectively, these findings have deepened the understanding of SFTSAE and support earlier clinical diagnosis and intervention.

Although risk factors are helpful to clinicians, a quick and easy-to-use tool to predict encephalitis risk and in-hospital mortality on the basis of basic medical information is urgently needed. Only a few studies have reported prediction models. Yijiang Liu et al. constructed an SFTSAE nomogram on the basis of age, C-reactive protein levels, D-dimer levels, and viral load [14]. Hexiang Zheng et al. established a reservoir computing with boosted topology (RC–BT) algorithm to predict the encephalitis risk and mortality of patients with SFTS [17]. In our study, the incidence rate of encephalitis was 45.41%. The encephalitis rate in our study population was higher than that in other populations. Five predictors for encephalitis (age, tremors, dysphoria, hypertension, and ferritin levels) were identified through LASSO regression. All of these factors were significantly associated with an increased risk of developing encephalitis. Age, encephalitis, and ferritin levels were identified as significant predictors of in-hospital death. These factors were all significantly associated with increased mortality risk. The encephalitis prediction model and in-hospital death prediction model demonstrated robust performance, suggesting their potential clinical utility.

SFTSAE is typically diagnosed based on clinical grounds. The symptoms vary from mild to severe. Some patients present with dysphoria, tremors, confusion, and lethargy. Some patients have constant convulsions and seizures and can even fall into a coma. Tremors and dysphoria are early symptoms of encephalitis and were indeed verified as predictors in our model. In our study, age was identified as an independent risk factor for encephalitis. This finding is consistent with other studies [14]. Elderly individuals are more susceptible to infection and have high viral loads. This phenomenon has also been confirmed in animal studies [24]. High viral loads can lead to cytokine storms, which can damage the blood‒brain barrier. The SFTS virus was detected in cerebrospinal fluid from elderly patients [25–27]. We should pay more attention to elderly patients in clinical practice, watch for symptoms of encephalitis, and take intervention measures as early as possible. Ferritin levels have also been verified as an independent risk factor for encephalitis. Ferritin is a tumour marker as well as an inflammatory marker and is one of the diagnostic criteria for haemophagocytic syndrome [28, 29]. Hyperferritinemia is often associated with a cytokine storm. The cytokine storm is considered one of the important mechanisms underlying SFTSAE [30]. We should pay more attention to the ferritin level when dealing with SFTS patients. According to our model, hypertension is an independent risk factor for encephalitis. Long-term high blood pressure can lead to atherosclerosis, which leads to impairment of the function of endothelial cells in brain vessels. Damage to cerebral vascular endothelial cells may weaken the function of the blood‒brain barrier, making it easier for viruses to enter brain tissue. When SFTS patients are being treated clinically, we should pay attention to whether the patient has a history of hypertension.

In addition to age and ferritin levels, encephalitis has been identified as another independent factor for predicting SFTS outcomes [31–33]. Encephalitis can significantly increase the case fatality rate of patients, which can reach 40%. In our study, all the patients who died had encephalitis. In the nonencephalitis group, only one patient died. The mortality rate of SFTSAE is 36%. Although the mortality rate of SFTSAE is significantly greater than that of SFTS, the long-term prognosis of surviving patients is good, with no obvious sequelae after active treatment. Early detection and treatment of encephalitis can increase overall survival. Intensive care, intravenous immunoglobulin plus steroids, plasma exchange plus convalescent plasma, and Janus kinase 1/2 inhibitors are reported to be somewhat effective in treating SFTSAE [34–36]. To quickly diagnose SFTSAE and reduce the mortality rate of SFTS, it is important for doctors to use prediction models. Our prediction models are very convenient for clinicians and can be used not only in cities but also in rural areas with limited medical resources.

Our study also has several limitations. First, we built a predictive model using data from a limited number of patients enrolled at a single centre. The single-centre design (n = 196) risks selection bias, particularly with a 45% encephalitis incidence rate, which is significantly higher than that reported in the literature (18.1–27.5%) [1, 11, 14]. External validation is critical. Our model has been internally validated but not externally validated. Second, the exclusion of patients from this study due to incomplete clinical or laboratory data may have led to potential selection bias. Third, to make the model more widely used, many indicators, such as serum cytokine levels, viral loads and other complications, such as gastrointestinal bleeding, pulmonary haemorrhage, sepsis, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, cerebral haemorrhage and multiple organ failure, were discarded. Fourth, SFTSAE patients were diagnosed on the basis of their clinical manifestations. A strict diagnosis of SFTSAE should be made not only on the basis of clinical manifestations but also on the basis of electroencephalograms, craniocerebral nuclear magnetic resonance imaging and cerebrospinal fluid tests in the future.

Conclusion

In this study, we revealed that age, tremors, dysphoria, hypertension, and ferritin levels are independent predictors of encephalitis. Moreover, we found that age, encephalitis, and ferritin levels are independent risk factors for in-hospital death. Notably, age and ferritin levels were strongly associated with both clinical outcomes. This finding warrants more attention from clinicians. On the basis of basic patient information, we developed predictive models that demonstrated robust performance in predicting both in-hospital death and encephalitis among SFTS patients. Our predictive models can be widely used not only in large medical centres but also in small hospitals, assisting clinicians in early detection and treating high-risk patients, thus improving their prognosis.
