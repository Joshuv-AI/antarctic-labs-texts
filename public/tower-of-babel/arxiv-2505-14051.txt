Information bounds for inference in stochastic evolution equations observed under noise
Gregor Pasemann, Markus Reiß

Abstract
We consider statistics for stochastic evolution equations in Hilbert space with emphasis on stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). We observe a solution process under additional measurement errors and want to estimate a real or functional parameter in the drift. Main targets of estimation are the diffusivity, transport or source coefficient in a parabolic SPDE. By bounding the Hellinger distance between observation laws under different parameters we derive lower bounds on the estimation error, which reveal the underlying information structure. The estimation rates depend on the measurement noise level, the observation time, the covariance of the dynamic noise, the dimension and the order, at which the parametrised coefficient appears in the differential operator. A general estimation procedure attains these rates in many parametric cases and proves their minimax optimality. For nonparametric estimation problems, where the parameter is an unknown function, the lower bounds exhibit an even more complex information structure. The proofs are to a large extent based on functional calculus, perturbation theory and monotonicity of the semigroup generators.



Keywords:

Hellinger distance, cylindrical Gaussian measure, stochastic partial differential equation, stochastic evolution equation, weak solution, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, minimax rate, Cauchy problem.

Introduction

Many dynamical systems in nature and society are subject to randomness and stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) yield prototypical examples of such stochastic dynamical models. Let us consider linear parabolic SPDEs of the form

\dot{X}(t,y)=D_{\vartheta}X(t,y)+\dot{W}(t,y),\quad t\geqslant 0,\,y\in\Lambda,

(1.1)

with space-time Gaussian white noise
\dot{W}
or equivalently in Itô form

dX(t,y)=D_{\vartheta}X(t,y)\,dt+dW(t,y),\quad t\geqslant 0,\,y\in\Lambda,

(1.2)

with a cylindrical Brownian motion
W(t)
on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
, subject to some initial condition
X(0,y)
,
y\in\Lambda
.
Here,
\Lambda\subseteq\R^{d}
is a spatial domain and
D_{\vartheta}
denotes a general second-order differential operator

D_{\vartheta}f(y):=\nabla{\scriptstyle\bullet}\big(c^{(2)}_{\vartheta}(y)\nabla f+c^{(1)}_{\vartheta}(y)f\big)(y)+c^{(0)}_{\vartheta}(y)f(y),\quad y\in\Lambda,

involving some boundary condition and (matrix-/vector-/scalar-valued) coefficients
c^{(2)}_{\vartheta},c^{(1)}_{\vartheta},c^{(0)}_{\vartheta}
, parametrised by a Euclidean or functional parameter
\vartheta\in\Theta
. First fundamental results for estimation in a general SPDE framework have been obtained by
Huebner & Rozovskii, (1995)
and
Ibragimov & Khas’minskii, (2001)
. Afterwards, estimators for
\vartheta
in several specific settings and from observing
X
globally, locally or at discrete points in time and space have been developed, see
Cialenco, (2018)
for an excellent survey of the state of the art up to this time. Recently, in the general setting (
1.2
) a parametric estimation theory has been developed by
Altmeyer et al., (2024)
under multiple local observations of
X
.

In many cases, however, measurements of
X
involve an additional error, see e.g
Pasemann et al., (2020)
for measurements of cell motility. Under measurement noise different observation schemes often lead to the same estimation theory because the measurement noise typically dominates the discretisation errors.
Therefore we consider the noisy observations
dY
of
X
given by

dY(t,y)=BX(t,y)\,dt+\varepsilon dV(t,y),\quad t\in[0,T],\,y\in\Lambda,

(1.3)

where

is a cylindrical Brownian motion on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
, independent of
W
,
B
is an observation operator and
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
is the noise level. This formulation is standard for stochastic filtering problems in dynamical systems, see e.g.
Bain & Crisan, (2009)
. From an alternative point of view, we may consider a regression setting with discrete data

Y_{i,j}=(BX)(t_{i},y_{j})+\varepsilon_{i,j}

at some time points
t_{i}
and spatial locations
y_{j}
with i.i.d. error variables
\varepsilon_{i,j}\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, independent of the driving noise

1.3

\varepsilon^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}T\lvert\Lambda\rvert}{n}
, compare
Reiß, (2008)
, Section 3 in
Bibinger et al., (2014)
and the references therein. This interpretation, however, requires that point evaluations of

are well defined.

Given the observations (
1.3
), we aim at establishing minimax optimal estimation rates for
\vartheta
. It turns out that these rates differ significantly whenever this parameter only appears in the
diffusivity
(or

) coefficient
c^{(2)}_{\vartheta}
, the
transport
(or
advection
) coefficient
c^{(1)}_{\vartheta}
or in the
source
(or

) coefficient

. Moreover, when these coefficient functions

are not parametrised by a real parameter, but assumed to belong just to a functional class of Hölder regularity

, then the rates become nonparametric and exhibit structural differences, depending on the asymptotics taken.

Establishing minimax optimal estimation rates requires to construct estimators attaining these rates as well as to derive general information-theoretic lower bounds proving that no estimator can converge faster, see
Tsybakov, (2009)
for a comprehensive introduction. Lower bounds provide a genuine insight into the intrinsic difficulty in drawing inference on the parameter and reveal the underlying information structure. We thus first concentrate on a general lower bound result in the framework of stochastic evolution equations (see e.g.
Da Prato & Zabczyk, (2014)
;
Lototsky & Rozovskii, (2018)
), generalizing the SPDE (
1.2

dX_{t}=A_{\vartheta}X_{t}dt+dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

(1.4)

on a real Hilbert space
H
where
A_{\vartheta}
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup on
H
. Then the observations are given by

dY_{t}=BX_{t}dt+\varepsilon dV_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

(1.5)

with an independent
H
-valued cylindrical Brownian motion

. By linearity, the observations

follow a cylindrical Gaussian law on

and our main information-theoretic result in Theorem

gives a tight bound for the Hellinger distance between the observation laws for two different parameters
\vartheta_{0},\vartheta_{1}\in\Theta

A_{\vartheta_{0}},A_{\vartheta_{1}}
. The fundamental functional-analytic difficulty consist in reducing the intrinsic Hilbert-Schmidt norm for the covariance operators of the time-continuous processes on

to a norm of the generators on

without assuming commutativity of the generators. A particular consequence is the absolute continuity of the laws, which for direct observations has been studied by

In Section
4

The concrete construction and analysis of the estimators is rather involved, but the main idea is to reduce the observational noise first by averaging with an operator-valued kernel and then to apply a continuous-regression estimator as in the case of direct observations of
X
.

2
Hellinger bounds for cylindrical Gaussian measures

To establish minimax lower bounds, we use the classical approach based on bounding the Hellinger distance between two parameters. For observations from the statistical model
({\cal X},{\mathscr{F}},(\PP_{\vartheta})_{\vartheta\in\Theta})
where the (non-empty) parameter set
\Theta
is equipped with a semi-metric
d
, we combine the reduction scheme in
Tsybakov, (2009, Section 2.2)
with
Tsybakov, (2009, Thm. 2.2(ii))
to obtain

2.1 Theorem
.

Let
\delta>0
. Assume there are
\vartheta_{0},\vartheta_{1}\in\Theta
with semi-distance
d(\vartheta_{0},\vartheta_{1})\geqslant\delta
such that their respective laws have Hellinger distance
H(\PP_{\vartheta_{0}},\PP_{\vartheta_{1}})\leqslant 1
. Then

\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}}\sup_{\vartheta\in\Theta}\PP_{\vartheta}\big(\delta^{-1}d(\hat{\vartheta},\vartheta)\geqslant 1/2\big)\geqslant\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{4}

(2.1)

holds where the infimum is taken over all estimators (measurable
\Theta
-valued functions) in the statistical model.

In a nutshell, the idea for establishing tight minimax lower bounds is to find two parameters (real values or functions) under which the observation laws satisfy a non-trivial Hellinger bound and which at the same time have a large (Euclidean or functional) distance. We shall apply the lower bound for sequences of models, which become more informative in
n\in\N
, and try to find the largest
\delta=\delta_{n}
in (
2.1
). Then no estimator sequence
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
can satisfy
\delta_{n}^{-1}d(\hat{\vartheta}_{n},\vartheta)\xrightarrow{\PP_{\vartheta}}0
uniformly over
\vartheta
. In other words, estimators
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
are minimax rate-optimal when they satisfy
d(\hat{\vartheta}_{n},\vartheta)={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}(v_{n})
for a rate
v_{n}\thicksim\delta_{n}
, uniformly over
\vartheta
(Appendix
A
recalls uniform stochastic convergence).

Next, we study the Hellinger distance between cylindrical Gaussian measures with different covariance operators.

2.2 Lemma
.

The squared Hellinger distance between Gaussian laws
{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
and
{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})
satisfies for
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}>0

H^{2}({\cal N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}),{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}))\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\Big(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{0}}-\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{1}}\Big)^{2}.

Proof.

By invariance of the Hellinger distance under bi-measurable bijections, see
Reiß, (2011, Appendix A.1)
, we have
H^{2}({\cal N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}),{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}))=H^{2}({\cal N}(0,1),{\cal N}(0,(\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{0})^{2}))
, so it suffices to show

\displaystyle H^{2}({\cal N}(0,1),{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}))\leqslant\tfrac{1}{4}(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^{2}

(2.2)

for
\sigma>0
.
If
(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^{2}>8
, this bound holds trivially as the squared Hellinger distance is always bounded by two.
Now assume
(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^{2}\leqslant 8
.
Following
Reiß, (2011)
the squared Hellinger distance for scalar Gaussian laws is given by

H^{2}({\cal N}(0,1),{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}))=2-2\sqrt{2\sigma/(\sigma^{2}+1)}.

We use
(\sigma^{2}+1)(1+\sigma^{-1})^{2}\geqslant 8
(the mininum is attained at
\sigma=1
) and bound, differently from that reference,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\sigma/(\sigma^{2}+1)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-(\sigma-1)^{2}/(\sigma^{2}+1)}\geqslant\sqrt{1-\tfrac{1}{8}(\sigma-1)^{2}(1+\sigma^{-1})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{1}{8}(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^{2}}\geqslant 1-\tfrac{1}{8}(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^{2},

given that
0\leqslant(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^{2}\leqslant 8
. This implies (
2.2
).
∎

This real-valued bound gives rise to a corresponding Hilbert space bound. For details on cylindrical Gaussian measures
{\cal N}_{cyl}(\mu,Q)
we refer to
Bogachev, (1998)
. Here, the main use is that
X\sim{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q)
means that
(\langle X,v\rangle)_{v\in H}
is a centred Gaussian process indexed by
v
with
\Cov(\langle X,v_{1}\rangle,\langle X,v_{2}\rangle)=\langle Qv_{1},v_{2}\rangle
where the covariance operator
Q:H\to H
is bounded (not necessarily trace class) and positive semi-definite, notation
Q\succcurlyeq 0
.

2.3 Proposition
.

Consider cylindrical Gaussian laws
{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf{}_{0}}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1})
in some separable real Hilbert space
H
with covariance operators
Q_{\bf 0},Q_{\bf 1}
. If
Q_{\bf 0},Q_{\bf 1}
are one-to-one and
\im(Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})=\im(Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})
,
then

H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1}))\leqslant\tfrac{1}{4}\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}-(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}\rVert_{HS}^{2},

provided the right-hand side is finite. In that case the laws are equivalent.

2.4 Remark
.

(a)

If
{\cal N}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}(0,Q_{\bf 1})
are equivalent proper laws on
H
(i.e.,
Q_{\bf 0},Q_{\bf 1}
are trace class),
the Feldman-Hájek Theorem
(
Da Prato & Zabczyk,, 2014
, Thm. 2.25)
shows that
\im(Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})=\im(Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})
and that
Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}(Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})^{\ast}-\Id
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on
H
(note that here
\im(Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})
is dense in
H
by the injectivity of
Q_{\bf 0}
). Since
(Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})^{\ast}
has the bounded inverse
(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}
, we deduce that also the product
Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}-(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and the Hellinger bound is finite.

(b)

We may write

Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}-(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}=Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}(Q_{\bf 1}-Q_{\bf 0})Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}

if we consider the Gelfand triple
H^{1}\hookrightarrow H\hookrightarrow H^{-1}
with
H^{1}=\im(Q_{i}^{1/2})
,
H^{-1}=(H^{1})^{\ast}
and interpret
Q_{\bf 1}-Q_{\bf 0}:H^{-1}\to H^{1}
. In contrast to the bound (cf.
Reiß, (2011)
)

H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1}))\leqslant 2\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}(Q_{\bf 1}-Q_{\bf 0})Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}\rVert_{HS}^{2},

which is asymmetric in
Q_{\bf 0}
and
Q_{\bf 1}
, the bound of Proposition
2.3
will allow us to obtain feasible expressions also for non-commuting covariance operators.

Proof.

Following the proof of the Feldman-Hájek Theorem (step 2 for Thm. 2.25 in
Da Prato & Zabczyk, (2014)
) we let

(e_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
be the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of

R:=Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}(Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})^{\ast}

with corresponding positive eigenvalues
(\tau_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
, noting that
R
is an injective positive operator and that

R-\Id=\big(Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}-(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}\big)(Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})^{\ast}

is by assumption a Hilbert-Schmidt operator, which has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

We can generate the laws via an i.i.d. sequence
\zeta_{k}\sim N(0,1)
:

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\zeta_{k}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k}\sim{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),\quad\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\zeta_{k}\tau_{k}^{1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k}\sim{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1}).

In fact, we must check for all
g\in H

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\zeta_{k}\langle Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k},g\rangle\sim{\cal N}(0,\langle Q_{\bf 0}g,g\rangle),\quad\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\zeta_{k}\langle\tau_{k}^{1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k},g\rangle\sim{\cal N}(0,\langle Q_{\bf 1}g,g\rangle),

compare
Bogachev, (1998, Thm. 2.2.4)
. The first statement follows from
\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\langle Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k},g\rangle^{2}=\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}g\rVert^{2}
, the second statement from

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\langle Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k},g\rangle\langle\tau_{k}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k},g\rangle=\langle Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}g,RQ_{\bf 0}^{1/2}g\rangle=\langle Q_{\bf 1}g,g\rangle.

Then by the subadditivity of the squared Hellinger distance under independence and Lemma
2.2

\displaystyle H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1}))

\displaystyle=H^{2}\Big(\bigotimes_{k\geqslant 1}{\cal N}(0,\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k}\rVert^{2}),\bigotimes_{k\geqslant 1}{\cal N}(0,\lVert\tau_{k}^{1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k}\rVert^{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 1}H^{2}\Big({\cal N}(0,\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k}\rVert^{2}),{\cal N}(0,\tau_{k}\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}e_{k}\rVert^{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\geqslant 1}(\tau_{k}^{1/2}-\tau_{k}^{-1/2})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\trace\big(R-2\Id+R^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\trace\Big(\big((Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2})^{\ast}-Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}\big)\big(Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}-(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\lVert Q_{\bf 0}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 1}^{1/2}-(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}\rVert_{HS}^{2},

where we used
R^{-1}=(Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2})^{\ast}Q_{\bf 1}^{-1/2}Q_{\bf 0}^{1/2}
and commutativity under the trace.

The equivalence follows verbatim as for the Feldman-Hájek Theorem.
∎

3

For a comprehensive treatment of stochastic evolution equations we refer to

and

For the observational noise level we assume

and usually remains fixed or tends to infinity.

A_{\bf 0}:=A_{\vartheta_{0}}
,
A_{\bf 1}:=A_{\vartheta_{1}}
and apply this rule to all further indexing. For simplicity the initial condition
X_{0}
is taken to be zero, noting that all minimax lower bounds derived later trivially extend when the maximum is taken over arbitrary
X_{0}\in H
.

The possibly unbounded linear operators
A_{\vartheta}:\dom(A_{\vartheta})\subseteq H\to H
are normal with the same domain
\dom(A_{\vartheta})
for all
\vartheta
and generate a strongly continuous semigroup
(e^{A_{\vartheta}u})_{u\geqslant 0}
by functional calculus. Note that then
(e^{A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}u})_{u\geqslant 0}
is its adjoint semigroup.
As a generator of a semigroup,
A_{\vartheta}
is necessarily quasi-dissipative, meaning that
A_{\vartheta}
has a spectrum whose real part is bounded from above, see e.g.
Bühler & Salamon, (2018, Lemma 7.2.6)
. Then

X_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-s)}dW_{s},\quad t\in[0,T],

(3.1)

defines for each
t
a cylindrical Gaussian measure on
H
via
\langle X_{t},z\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle e^{A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}(t-s)}z,dW_{s}\rangle
,
z\in H
, which solves for
z\in\dom(A_{\vartheta}^{\ast})
the weak formulation
(
Da Prato & Zabczyk,, 2014
, Thm. 5.4)

d\langle z,X_{t}\rangle=\langle A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}z,X_{t}\rangle dt+\langle z,dW_{t}\rangle,\quad t\in[0,T],\text{ with }\langle z,X_{0}\rangle=0.

Let us stress that we do not assume commutativity of the operators
(A_{\vartheta})_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
.

3.1 Example
.

Writing
\tilde{X}_{t}=BX_{t}
and assuming
B
to commute with all
A_{\vartheta}
, we observe

dY_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}dt+\varepsilon dV_{t}\text{ with }d\tilde{X}_{t}=A_{\vartheta}\tilde{X}_{t}dt+BdW_{t},\;\tilde{X}_{0}=0.

This way we can also treat cases of evolution equations with more general noise as a driver.
An instructive example is given by
B=\sigma\Id
for some known
\sigma>0
. Then we observe

dY_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}dt+\varepsilon dV_{t}\text{ with }d\tilde{X}_{t}=A_{\vartheta}\tilde{X}_{t}dt+\sigma dW_{t},\;\tilde{X}_{0}=0.

On the other hand, multiplying the observation process (
1.5
) by
\sigma^{-1}
and replacing
Y
by
\sigma^{-1}Y
, we observe equivalently

dY_{t}=X_{t}dt+\varepsilon\sigma^{-1}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=A_{\vartheta}X_{t}dt+dW_{t},\;X_{0}=0,

Consequently, introducing a dynamic noise level
\sigma>0
is statistically equivalent to replacing the observation noise level
\varepsilon>0
by the ratio
\varepsilon/\sigma
. This means that higher dynamic noise levels will lead to smaller statistical errors, compare the discussion in
Pasemann & Reiß, (2024)
.

3.2 Lemma
.

The observations
(\int_{0}^{T}\langle g(t),dY_{t}\rangle,\,g\in L^{2}(H))
with
dY
from (
1.5
) generate a cylindrical Gaussian measure
{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\vartheta})
on
L^{2}(H)
with

Q_{\vartheta}=\varepsilon^{2}\Id+BC_{\vartheta}B^{\ast}\text{, where }C_{\vartheta}=S_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}

and for
f\in L^{2}(H)

S_{\vartheta}f(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-s)}f(s)\,ds,\quad S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}f(t)=\int_{t}^{T}e^{A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}(s-t)}f(s)\,ds,\quad t\in[0,T].

3.3 Remark
.

Since
BC_{\vartheta}B^{\ast}
is positive semi-definite,
Q_{\vartheta}
is strictly positive for
\varepsilon>0
and an isomorphism on
L^{2}(H)
. For proper Gaussian measures and
\varepsilon=0
the result from Lemma
3.2
and consequences for absolutely continuous laws can be found in
Peszat, (1992)
.

Proof.

The variation-of-constants formula (
3.1
) and the stochastic Fubini Theorem
(
Da Prato & Zabczyk,, 2014
, Thm. 4.18)
yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle g(t),X(t)\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\langle g(t),e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-s)}dW_{s}\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\int_{s}^{T}e^{A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}(t-s)}g(t)\,dt,dW_{s}\Bigr\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}g(s),dW_{s}\rangle.

We deduce by Itô isometry

\E\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\langle g(t),X(t)\rangle\,dt\Big)^{2}\Big]=\lVert S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}g\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2}=\langle S_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}g,g\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}.

By independence of
dV
and
dW
we have

\displaystyle\E\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\langle g(t),dY_{t}\rangle\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\E\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\langle g(t),BX(t)\rangle\,dt\Big)^{2}\Big]+\varepsilon^{2}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle S_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}B^{\ast}g,B^{\ast}g\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}+\varepsilon^{2}\langle g,g\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}

\displaystyle=\langle Q_{\vartheta}g,g\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}.

By linearity and polarisation,
(\int_{0}^{T}\langle g(t),dY_{t}\rangle,\,g\in L^{2}(H))\sim{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\vartheta})
follows.
∎

In order to be able to apply functional calculus for normal operators, we complexify
H
and all operators in the usual way (see Section 5.1 in
Bühler & Salamon, (2018)
for more formal details) by introducing the complex Hilbert space
H^{\C}:=H+iH
with
\lVert v+iw\rVert_{H^{\C}}^{2}:=\lVert v\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert w\rVert_{H}^{2}
and the extension
A_{\vartheta}^{\C}:\dom(A_{\vartheta})+i\dom(A_{\vartheta})\subseteq H^{\C}\to H^{\C}
via
A_{\vartheta}^{\C}(v+iw):=A_{\vartheta}v+iA_{\vartheta}w
for
v,w\in\dom(A_{\vartheta})
. Then by normality of
A_{\vartheta}
(
(A_{\vartheta}^{\C})^{\ast}
denotes the complex adjoint)

(A_{\vartheta}^{\C})^{\ast}A_{\vartheta}^{\C}(v+iw)=A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}A_{\vartheta}v-iA_{\vartheta}^{\ast}A_{\vartheta}w=A_{\vartheta}^{\C}(A_{\vartheta}^{\C})^{\ast}(v+iw)

for all
v,w\in\dom(A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}A_{\vartheta})=\dom(A_{\vartheta}A_{\vartheta}^{\ast})
and
A_{\vartheta}^{\C}
is normal on
H^{\C}
. With the canonical isometric injection
\iota:H\to H^{\C}
,
\iota(v):=v+i{\scriptstyle\bullet}0
we have
A_{\vartheta}^{\C}\iota(v)=A_{\vartheta}v
,
v\in H
, and
A_{\vartheta}^{\C}
extends
A_{\vartheta}
. The extension of a bounded operator
T
on
H
satisfies
\lVert T^{\C}\rVert=\lVert T\rVert
and
\lVert T^{\C}\rVert_{HS}^{2}=2\lVert T\rVert_{HS}^{2}
in case of a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. In order to ease notation, the superscript
\C
will be dropped from now on and functional calculus for operators will be implicitly complexified. Let us emphasise that the observation model and in particular the Gaussian noise is always defined over the real numbers.

By
Bühler & Salamon, (2018, Theorem 6.3.11)
the normal operators
A_{\vartheta}
on the complex Hilbert space
H
satisfy
\dom(A_{\vartheta})=\dom(A_{\vartheta}^{\ast})
and can be decomposed by two self-adjoint operators
R_{\vartheta}:\dom(R_{\vartheta})\to H
,
J_{\vartheta}:\dom(J_{\vartheta})\to H
with
\dom(A_{\vartheta})=\dom(R_{\vartheta})\cap\dom(J_{\vartheta})
such that

A_{\vartheta}v=R_{\vartheta}v+iJ_{\vartheta}v,\quad A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}v=R_{\vartheta}v-iJ_{\vartheta}v,\quad\lVert A_{\vartheta}v\rVert^{2}=\lVert R_{\vartheta}v\rVert^{2}+\lVert J_{\vartheta}v\rVert^{2}

for all
v\in\dom(A_{\vartheta})
.
Note that any two operators from
A_{\vartheta},A^{*}_{\vartheta},R_{\vartheta},J_{\vartheta}
commute.
Since
A_{\vartheta}
is normal, we can use its spectral measure to define
f(A_{\vartheta})
for measurable
f:\C\to\C

(
Birman & Solomjak,, 2012
;
Schmüdgen,, 2012
)
, in particular
R_{\vartheta}=\operatorname{Re}(A_{\vartheta})
and
J_{\vartheta}=\operatorname{Im}(A_{\vartheta})
hold on
\dom(A_{\vartheta})
. Frequently, we shall make use of the bound
\lVert f(A_{\vartheta})\rVert\leqslant\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}
for bounded
f
. Moreover, we shall employ the Bochner integral over Banach space-valued functions (e.g. with values in a Hilbert space or a space of bounded linear operators) and use standard properties, as exposed e.g. in
Engel & Nagel, (2000, Appendix C)
. We lift linear operators
L
on
H
naturally to
L^{2}(H)
by setting pointwise
(Lf)(t):=L(f(t))
for
f\in L^{2}(H)
.

3.4 Lemma
.

In the setting of Lemma
3.2
,
we have the explicit representation

C_{\vartheta}f(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{iJ_{\vartheta}t}\Big(\int_{\lvert t-s\rvert}^{t+s}e^{R_{\vartheta}v}dv\Big)e^{-iJ_{\vartheta}s}f(s)\,ds.

Proof.

The asserted identity follows from
Fubini’s theorem for Bochner integrals with continuous integrands:

\displaystyle C_{\vartheta}f(t)=S_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}f(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-u)}\Big(\int_{u}^{T}e^{A^{*}_{\vartheta}(s-u)}f(s)\,ds\Big)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}e^{A_{\vartheta}t-2R_{\vartheta}u+A_{\vartheta}^{\ast}s}du\,f(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}e^{iJ_{\vartheta}t}\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}e^{R_{\vartheta}t-2R_{\vartheta}u+R_{\vartheta}s}du\,e^{-iJ_{\vartheta}s}f(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{iJ_{\vartheta}t}\Big(\int_{\lvert t-s\rvert}^{t+s}e^{R_{\vartheta}v}dv\Big)e^{-iJ_{\vartheta}s}f(s)\,ds.

∎

We proceed to bound the operator norm of
R_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}
on
L^{2}(H)
. This is analogous to the convolution operator
f\mapsto\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)e^{\lambda({\scriptstyle\bullet}-s)}f(s)ds
on
L^{2}([0,T];\C)
with the classical norm bound
\lVert\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)e^{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda){\scriptstyle\bullet}}\rVert_{L^{1}([0,T])}
. The proof involves, however, more involved functional calculus and tensorisation.

3.5 Proposition
.

With
(x)_{+}=x\vee 0
we have for the operator norm in

\lVert R_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}\rVert^{2}=\lVert S^{*}_{\vartheta}R_{\vartheta}\rVert^{2}\leqslant\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4}e^{2\lVert(R_{\vartheta})_{+}\rVert T}=:\alpha_{\vartheta}^{2}.

(3.2)

Proof.

See Section
B.1
.
∎

We have a tighter bound in the contractive setting where

and

3.6 Corollary
.

If
R_{\vartheta}\preccurlyeq 0
holds, then we can bound

\lVert\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T^{-1}}S_{\vartheta}\rVert=\lVert S^{*}_{\vartheta}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T^{-1}}\rVert\leqslant 1.

(3.3)

Proof.

For
R_{\vartheta}\preccurlyeq 0
we have
(R_{\vartheta})_{+}=0
and
\alpha_{\vartheta}=1
in Proposition
3.5
. By functional calculus,
\Pi_{\vartheta,T}:={\bf 1}(R_{\vartheta}\in[-T^{-1},0])
and
\Id-\Pi_{\vartheta,T}
are orthogonal projections in
L^{2}(H)
(
R_{\vartheta}
being lifted from
H
to
L^{2}(H)
). Since
S_{\vartheta}
commutes with
R_{\vartheta}
, it also commutes with
\Pi_{\vartheta,T}
so that by Proposition
3.5
for
f\in L^{2}(H)

\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\vartheta,T})\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert S_{\vartheta}f\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}=\lVert-R_{\vartheta}S_{\vartheta}(\Id-\Pi_{\vartheta,T})f\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}\leqslant\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\vartheta,T})f\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}

follows. On the other hand, we have by direct calculation

\displaystyle\lVert\Pi_{\vartheta,T}S_{\vartheta}f\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}{\Bigl\lVert\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-s)}\Pi_{\vartheta,T}f(s)\,ds\Bigr\rVert}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\lVert e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-s)}\rVert\lVert\Pi_{\vartheta,T}f(s)\rVert\,ds\Big)^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant T^{2}\lVert\Pi_{\vartheta,T}f\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2},

where

was used due to
R_{\vartheta}\preccurlyeq 0
. Combining the two bounds, we obtain the claim, writing
(\Id-\Pi_{\vartheta,T})\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert+T^{-1}\Pi_{\vartheta,T}=\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T^{-1}}
.
∎

In view of Proposition
2.3
we can bound the Hellinger distance between the observations in (

\vartheta_{0}

by bounding the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of

and

We establish simple properties of the operator

3.7 Proposition
.

Suppose
\dom(A_{\vartheta})=\dom(R_{\vartheta})
for some
\vartheta\in\Theta

(a)

S_{\vartheta},S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}
map

into

,
S_{\vartheta}
maps
{\cal H}^{1}(H)
into
{\cal H}_{0}^{1}(H)
and
S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}
maps
{\cal H}^{1}(H)
into
{\cal H}_{T}^{1}(H)
.

(b)

Let
\partial_{t}
denote the time derivative. Then we have the identities
(\partial_{t}-A_{\vartheta})S_{\vartheta}=(-\partial_{t}-A_{\vartheta}^{\ast})S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}=\Id
on
{\cal H}^{1}(H)
and
S_{\vartheta}(\partial_{t}-A_{\vartheta})=S_{\vartheta}^{\ast}(-\partial_{t}-A_{\vartheta}^{\ast})=\Id
on
{\cal H}_{0}^{1}(H)\cap L^{2}([0,T];\dom(A_{\vartheta}))

(c)

We have on
L^{2}(H)

S_{\bf 1}-S_{\bf 0}=S_{\bf 0}(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 1}=S_{\bf 1}(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 0}.

Proof.

See Section
B.1
.
∎

The perturbation property in (c) will be essential for us. The assumption

is required to have all evaluations well defined. Since always

holds, it means

is a sectorial operator.

The next step is to find an appropriate upper bound for

\bar{B}_{\vartheta}^{2}\succcurlyeq B^{\ast}B\text{ or equivalently }\lVert\bar{B}_{\vartheta}^{-1}B^{\ast}\rVert\leqslant 1.

(3.4)

Assuming

3.8 Lemma

For an invertible operator

Proposition
3.5

(a) below with
f(2TR_{\vartheta})^{2}\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{2}T^{-1}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T}^{-1}
, which follows from
f(-x)^{2}=(e^{-x}-1+x)/x^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\wedge\lvert x\rvert^{-1}
for
x>0
.
∎

4
Parametric minimax rates

We consider a slightly simpler model for
X
and
dY
than (
1.4
), (
1.5
), in particular assuming a real parameter
\vartheta
and commutativity of all operators. We construct an estimator whose error rate attains under mild restrictions the lower bound. This establishes the minimax rate in a quite general parametric setting.

For each
n\in\N
let the observations
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
be given by

dY_{t}=B_{n}X_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=A_{\vartheta}X_{t}\,dt+dW_{t}

(4.1)

and
X_{0}=0
,
T_{n}\geqslant 1
,
\varepsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
,
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
for
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}>\underline{\vartheta}_{n}\geqslant 0
. The parametrisation of the operator
A_{\vartheta}
is given by

A_{\vartheta}=A_{\vartheta,n}=M_{n}+\vartheta\Lambda

with known possibly unbounded linear operators
M_{n},\Lambda
.

4.1 Assumption
.

(a)

M_{n}
is selfadjoint and
\Lambda
is normal with
M_{n}\precsim 0
,
\operatorname{Re}(\Lambda)\precsim 0
. Moreover,
\dom(A_{\vartheta})=\dom(R_{\vartheta})
holds for
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
.

(b)

B_{n}
is a bounded and invertible selfadjoint operator.

(c)

All three operators
M_{n}
,
\Lambda
and
B_{n}
commute and allow for a common functional calculus.

(d)

A_{\vartheta}
has a compact resolvent for
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
.

We abbreviate
\bar{A}_{n}=A_{\bar{\vartheta}_{n}}
,
\bar{R}_{n}=R_{\bar{\vartheta}_{n}}
,
\bar{J}_{n}=J_{\bar{\vartheta}_{n}}
. By
M_{n}\precsim 0
,
\operatorname{Re}(\Lambda)\precsim 0
and
J_{\vartheta}=\vartheta\operatorname{Im}(\Lambda)
, we have the ordering
\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert\preccurlyeq\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert
,
\lvert A_{\vartheta}\rvert\preccurlyeq\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert
under Assumption
4.1
(a). For later applications note that under Assumption
4.1
(c) observing (
4.1
) is equivalent to observing

dY_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }d\tilde{X}_{t}=A_{\vartheta}\tilde{X}_{t}\,dt+B_{n}dW_{t}

(4.2)

with
\tilde{X}_{0}=0
as in Example
3.1
.
We obtain immediately a general lower bound for this setting.

4.2 Theorem
.

Grant Assumption
4.1
(a-c). Let

v_{n}=T_{n}^{-1/2}\Big(\trace\big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-1}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\big)\Big)^{-1/2},

and assume
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}-\underline{\vartheta}_{n}\geqslant v_{n}>0
. Then the lower bound

\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}_{n}}\sup_{\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]}\PP_{\vartheta}\big(v_{n}^{-1}\lvert\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta\rvert\geqslant 2^{-7/2}\big)\geqslant\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{4},

holds where the infimum is taken over all estimators based on observing (
4.1
). Hence, estimators
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
that fulfill
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}(v_{n})
uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
are minimax rate-optimal.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.9
for
R_{\bf 0},R_{\bf 1}\preccurlyeq 0
we have for
\vartheta_{\bf 0}=\bar{\vartheta}_{n}-2^{-5/2}v_{n}
and
\vartheta_{\bf 1}=\bar{\vartheta}_{n}
in our setting, noting
\vartheta_{\bf 0},\vartheta_{\bf 1}\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
,
\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert\preccurlyeq\lvert R_{\bf 1}\rvert
and the commutativity,

\displaystyle H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1}))

\displaystyle\leqslant T_{n}2^{-5}v_{n}^{2}\lVert(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{2}B_{n}^{-2}+\Id)^{-1}\Lambda\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1/2}\rVert_{HS}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant T_{n}v_{n}^{2}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}2^{4}\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-1}\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\Big).

We have
\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert^{-1}\preccurlyeq\frac{\bar{\vartheta}_{n}}{\bar{\vartheta}_{n}-2^{-5/2}v_{n}}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert^{-1}\preccurlyeq 2\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert^{-1}
so that our choice of
v_{n}
yields

H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,Q_{\bf 1}))\leqslant 1
.
Therefore the result follows from Theorem
2.1
with
\delta=2^{-5/2}v_{n}
.
∎

Estimation of the parameter
\vartheta
in (
4.1
) is non-trivial. In the finite-dimensional case the laws of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes are equivalent for different
\vartheta
and estimation of
\vartheta
could be based on standard filter theory. Even then, however, the maximum-likelihood estimator (MLE) is not explicit and a one-step MLE based on a preliminary estimator is preferred
(
Kutoyants & Zhou,, 2021
)
. In the infinite-dimensional setting, e.g. for the stochastic heat equation with
A_{\vartheta}=\vartheta\Delta
, the laws of
(X_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
are often singular for different parameters
\vartheta
, see also the interesting study by
Hildebrandt & Trabs, (2021)
for one-dimensional SPDEs under different observation schemes. In that case the observations
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
only become equivalent through the action of the observation noise
dV_{t}
and the likelihood becomes intractable besides an abstract Gaussian approach.

Our estimation procedure is based on a preaveraging method, where the observational noise is first reduced by local averaging and then a regression-type estimator is applied to the averaged data. A similar approach is employed in nonparametric drift estimation for diffusions under noise by
Schmisser, (2011)
, yet there the measurement noise level persists in the final rate, which we avoid, when specialised to the parametric Ornstein-Uhlenbeck case.

If we observed
X
directly, a natural estimation approach for
\vartheta
would be to regress
dX_{t}-M_{n}X_{t}dt=\vartheta\Lambda X_{t}dt+dW_{t}
on
\Lambda X_{t}dt
over
t\in[0,T]
. Given the noisy observations
dY
of
X
, we smooth out the observational noise
dV
by regressing the average
\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}K^{(n)}(t,s)-M_{n}K^{(n)}(t,s))\,dY_{s}
on
\Lambda\,dY_{t}
with an operator-valued kernel
K^{(n)}(t,s)
, similarly to estimation in instrumental variable regression. We require
K^{(n)}(t,s)=0
for
s>t
so that the stochastic integral is taken over
s\in[0,t]
to keep the martingale structure. Integration by parts with
(\partial_{t}-M_{n})^{\ast}=-(\partial_{t}+M_{n})
and vanishing boundary terms leads to our estimator.

4.3 Definition
.

For the operator-valued kernel
K^{(n)}
given by

\displaystyle K^{(n)}(t,s)=K_{n}\psi_{t-s,s}^{(n)}(\bar{A}_{n}),\quad t,s\in[0,T],\text{ with }

\displaystyle\psi_{v,s}^{(n)}(a)=v_{+}\wedge\big(\lvert a\rvert_{T_{n}-s}^{-1}-v\big)_{+},\;K_{n}=B_{n}^{2}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}+B_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-3}\big)^{-1}\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1},

consider the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}:=(Z_{n}/N_{n}){\bf 1}(N_{n}\not=0)
with

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle(\partial_{t}\Lambda K^{(n)}(t,s)+M_{n}\Lambda K^{(n)}(t,s))dY_{s},dY_{t}\rangle,

(4.3)

\displaystyle N_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}K^{(n)}(t,s)dY_{s},dY_{t}\rangle

(4.4)

based on observing
dY_{t}
according to (
4.1
).

A detailed analysis yields the main parametric upper bound.

4.4 Theorem
.

Grant Assumption
4.1
(a-d). Assume that

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta):=T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{3}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id\big)^{-1}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\Big)

is finite for all
n
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=\infty
and

\displaystyle T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{4}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{3}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-2}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}B_{n}^{-4}+\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-3}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=o({\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)^{2})

(4.5)

uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
.
Then
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
is well defined and

\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}\big({\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)^{-1/2}\big)

holds uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
.

Proof.

See Section
B.2
below.∎

4.5 Remark
.

If
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=\infty
holds already for finite
n
, then
\vartheta
can be usually identified non-asymptotically. To see this, denote by
\Pi_{j}
the orthogonal projection from
H
onto the eigenspace
V_{j}
for the first
j
eigenvalues of
A_{\vartheta}
(ordered arbitrarily, taking account of multiplicities). Remark that
A_{\vartheta}
has pure point spectrum under Assumption
4.1
(d). Then for fixed
n
we can consider the projected finite-dimensional observations
\Pi_{j}dY_{t}
of
dY_{t}
from (
4.1
) on
V_{j}
, involving
\Pi_{j}dX_{t}=A_{\vartheta,j}X_{t}dt+dW_{t,j}
with finite-rank operator
A_{\vartheta,j}=\Pi_{j}A_{\vartheta}
and
V_{j}
-valued Brownian motion
W_{t,j}=\Pi_{j}W_{t}
. The corresponding estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n,j}
converges for
j\to\infty
to
\vartheta
in probability (or identify
\vartheta
) if

\displaystyle T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda_{j}\rvert^{4}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n,j}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}\lvert\bar{A}_{n,j}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{3}B_{n,j}^{-4}+\Id)^{-2}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{A}_{n,j}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}B_{n,j}^{-4}+\lvert R_{\vartheta,j}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-3}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=o({\cal I}_{n,j}(\vartheta)^{2})

holds with
F_{j}:=\Pi_{j}F|_{V_{j}}
denoting the projection of an operator
F
onto
V_{j}
. The convergence follows from Theorem
4.4
with index
j
instead of
n

because
\dim(V_{j})<\infty
and
\bigcup_{j\geqslant 1}V_{j}=H
imply
{\cal I}_{n,j}(\vartheta)<\infty
and
{\cal I}_{n,j}(\vartheta)\uparrow{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=\infty
as
j\to\infty
.

We can find simple sufficient conditions for rate optimality.

4.6 Corollary
.

Grant the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
. If
\lvert\bar{J}_{n}\rvert\preccurlyeq C\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert
holds for a constant
C>0
, independent of
n
, then we have
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}({\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)^{-1/2})
uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
with

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\thicksim T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-1}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\Big)

(4.6)

and
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
is minimax rate-optimal if
\underline{\vartheta}_{n}+{\cal I}_{n}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n})^{-1/2}\leqslant\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\lesssim\underline{\vartheta}_{n}
.

Moreover, in this case a sufficient condition for (
4.5
) is

T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{4}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-1}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-3}\Big)=o({\cal I}(\vartheta)^{2})

(4.7)

uniformly for
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
, which is satisfied for

\lVert\Lambda\lvert R_{\underline{\vartheta}_{n}}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\rVert\lesssim 1
.

Proof.

From
\lvert\bar{J}_{n}\rvert\preccurlyeq C\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert
we infer
\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert\preccurlyeq\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert\preccurlyeq(C+1)\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert
and hence in Theorem
4.4

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\thicksim T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id\big)^{-1}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\Big).

The minimax optimality follows directly from the lower bound in Theorem
4.2
because
\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert
has the same order for all
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]
by
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\lesssim\underline{\vartheta}_{n}
.

Using that
\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert
is of the same order as
\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert
and
\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert\preccurlyeq\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert
, the left-hand side of Condition (
4.5
) is at most of order

T_{n}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{4}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}^{-1}}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-1}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-3}\Big)\leqslant{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\lVert\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-2}\rVert.

For
\lVert\Lambda\lvert R_{\underline{\vartheta}_{n}}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\rVert\lesssim 1
this bound has the order
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=o({\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)^{2})
due to
\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\preccurlyeq\lvert\bar{R}_{\underline{\vartheta}_{n}}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}
and
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\to\infty
. This yields the two sufficient conditions for (
4.5
).
∎

If the spectrum of the real part
R_{\vartheta}
is asymptotically approaching zero, then the upper bound may not match the lower bound. The reason is that the kernel
K^{(n)}(t,s)
used for
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
involves an indicator
{\bf 1}(\lvert\bar{A}_{n}\rvert\leqslant(t-s)^{-1})
, on which event
\operatorname{Re}(e^{A_{\vartheta}(t-s)})\succcurlyeq e^{-1}\cos(1)\Id
is ensured and thus
\E[N_{n}]\gtrsim{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)
in Proposition
B.3
below. For unknown
J_{\vartheta}
and significantly smaller
R_{\vartheta}
it remains an intriguing open question how to attain the lower bound. Yet, in our cases of interest it concerns only transport estimation under a very small diffusivity asymptotics, see Remark
5.6
below.

5
Fundamental parametric examples

5.1
Scalar Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes

Consider the scalar case
H=\R
of (
4.2
) where we observe
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
for each
n\in\N
given by

dY_{t}=X_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=-\vartheta X_{t}dt+\sigma_{n}dW_{t},\,X_{0}=0.

(5.1)

That is, we have
M_{n}=0
,
\Lambda=-1
and
B_{n}=\sigma_{n}>0
. The parametric theory yields directly upper and lower bounds.

5.1 Proposition
.

Assume
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}-\underline{\vartheta}_{n}\geqslant v_{n}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n})
with

v_{n}(\vartheta):=(\vartheta^{1/2}T_{n}^{-1/2}\vee T_{n}^{-1})(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\sigma_{n}^{-2}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{2}\vee 1)\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Then for a constant
c>0
we have the lower bound

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}_{n}}\sup_{\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}]}\PP_{\vartheta}\big(v_{n}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n})^{-1}\lvert\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta\rvert\geqslant c\big)>0,

where for each
n
the infimum is taken over all estimators
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
based on (
5.1
).

If
T_{n}\underline{\vartheta}_{n}(1+\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\sigma_{n}^{-4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4})^{-1}\to\infty
, then the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
from Definition
4.3
satisfies

\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}(v_{n}(\vartheta))\text{ uniformly over }\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta}_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}].

In particular, for
\vartheta_{n}\sigma_{n}^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}\lesssim 1
and
T_{n}\vartheta_{n}\to\infty
the rate
v_{n}(\vartheta_{n})=\vartheta_{n}^{1/2}T_{n}^{-1/2}

is minimax optimal, even locally over
[\vartheta_{n}-v_{n},\vartheta_{n}]
.

Proof.

The lower bound follows directly from Theorem
4.2
for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck case. For the upper bound we obtain in Theorem
4.4
due to
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\geqslant\underline{\vartheta}_{n}\gtrsim T_{n}^{-1}

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\thicksim T_{n}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4}\sigma_{n}^{-4}+1\big)^{-1}(\vartheta^{-1}\wedge T_{n})\thicksim v_{n}(\vartheta)^{-2}.

It remains to check condition (
4.5
) which reads here

\displaystyle T_{n}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4}\sigma_{n}^{-4}+1\big)^{-1}\big(\vartheta^{-3}\wedge T_{n}^{3}\big)

\displaystyle=o\Big(T_{n}^{2}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4}\sigma_{n}^{-4}+1\big)^{-2}(\vartheta^{-2}\wedge T_{n}^{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\iff\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4}\sigma_{n}^{-4}+1

\displaystyle=o(T_{n}\vartheta\vee 1).

This holds uniformly over
\vartheta
by
T_{n}\underline{\vartheta}_{n}(1+\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\sigma_{n}^{-4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4})^{-1}\to\infty
and yields the upper bound. Applying the lower bound to
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}=\vartheta_{n}
and
\underline{\vartheta}_{n}=\vartheta_{n}-v_{n}(\vartheta_{n})
yields minimax optimality due to
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}-v_{n}(\vartheta_{n})\thicksim\vartheta_{n}\geqslant 0
.
∎

Let us discuss this minimax rate in different cases and first assume
\varepsilon_{n}=0
(no noise). Then the rate
T_{n}^{-1}\vee\vartheta_{n}^{1/2}T_{n}^{-1/2}
combines the asymptotic Fisher information bound
\sqrt{2\vartheta/T_{n}}
for fixed
\vartheta>0
(positive recurrent case) as
T_{n}\to\infty
with the rate
T_{n}^{-1}
for
\vartheta=0
(null-recurrent case)
(
Kutoyants,, 2004
, Example 2.14, Prop. 3.46)
. For the upper bound we need the condition
T_{n}\vartheta_{n}\to\infty
to stabilise the denominator in
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
around its expectation. A more specific analysis, allowing for a diffuse distributional limit, would avoid this condition in a classical way.

In the noisy case
\varepsilon_{n}>0
we see that for the asymptotics
T_{n}\to\infty
and
\varepsilon_{n},\sigma_{n},\bar{\vartheta}_{n}
fixed the rate does not suffer from noisy observations even with constant noise intensity, which for fixed
\vartheta
is also well known
(
Kutoyants,, 2004
, Example 3.3)
. In view of the spectral approach to SPDE statistics
(
Huebner & Rozovskii,, 1995
)
, where the operator
A_{\vartheta}
is unbounded and each Fourier mode forms an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with drift given by the eigenvalues of
A_{\vartheta}
, we note that the noise level intervenes for the asymptotics
\vartheta_{n}\sigma_{n}^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}\to\infty
.

5.2
Stochastic evolution equations without noise

For
\varepsilon=0
,
B=\Id
and
R_{\bf 0},R_{\bf 1}\preccurlyeq 0
Theorem
3.9
shows that
H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 1}))
is bounded by

\displaystyle\tfrac{T}{4}\big(\lVert(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})\lvert R_{\bf 1}\rvert_{T^{-1}}^{-1}\rVert_{HS}^{2}+\lVert(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})\lvert R_{\bf 0}\rvert_{T^{-1}}^{-1}\rVert_{HS}^{2}\big).

Let us consider the case of selfadjoint, negative
A_{\bf 0}
and
A_{\bf 1}
that can be jointly diagonalized with common eigenvectors
v_{k}
and negative eigenvalues
\lambda_{k,0}
and
\lambda_{k,1}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 1}))

\displaystyle\leqslant T\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{k,0}-\lambda_{k,1})^{2}}{\lvert\lambda_{k,0}\rvert\wedge\lvert\lambda_{k,1}\rvert}

follows.
A well known example is given by the fractional Laplacian
A_{\bf 0}=-(-\Delta)^{m/2}
and
A_{\bf 1}=A_{\bf 0}-\delta(-\Delta)^{m_{1}/2}
for the Laplace operator
\Delta
on a smooth bounded domain
\Lambda\subseteq\R^{d}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions and
m\geqslant m_{1}\geqslant 0
,
\delta>0
. Then
\lvert\lambda_{k,0}\rvert\wedge\lvert\lambda_{k,1}\rvert=\lvert\lambda_{k,0}\rvert\thicksim k^{m/d}
and
\lvert\lambda_{k,1}-\lambda_{k,0}\rvert\thicksim\delta k^{m_{1}/d}
hold by the Weyl asymptotics of Lemma
C.5
below. Using that
A_{\bf 0},A_{\bf 1}
have the same eigenfunctions, we obtain

\displaystyle H^{2}({\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 1}))\lesssim T\delta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{(2m_{1}-m)/d}.

(5.2)

Consequently, the equivalence statement of Proposition
2.3
yields

5.2 Proposition
.

For
A_{\bf 0}=-(-\Delta)^{m/2}
and
A_{\bf 1}=A_{\bf 0}-\delta(-\Delta)^{m_{1}/2}
with

\displaystyle m_{1}<(m-d)/2

(5.3)

and
\delta>0
the laws
{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 0})
and
{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 1})
are equivalent.

For
A_{\bf 0}=\Delta
(
m=2
), (
5.3
) holds if
d=1
and
m_{1}<1/2
. This is exactly the
Huebner & Rozovskii, (1995, Cor. 2.3)
condition for equivalence of the laws
{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 0}),{\cal N}_{cyl}(0,C_{\bf 1})
. In the asymptotics
T\to\infty
we can then estimate
\vartheta
in
A_{\vartheta}=A_{\bf 0}-\vartheta(-\Delta)^{m_{1}/2}
at best with rate
T^{-1/2}
, setting
\delta\thicksim T^{-1/2}
in the lower bound of Theorem
2.1
. This asymptotic result slightly extends a result in
Huebner & Rozovskii, (1995)
for fixed
T
.

In the sequel we are mainly interested in the noisy case
\varepsilon>0
where equivalence of observation laws holds in far greater generality. Still, the Hellinger bound will become infinite for differential operators in large dimensions.

5.3
Parameter in the fractional Laplacian

We study a generalisation of the classical stochastic heat equation
dX_{t}=\vartheta\Delta X_{t}dt+dW_{t}
where the diffusivity
\vartheta>0
is the target of estimation.
For each
n\in\N
let the observations
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
be given by

dY_{t}=X_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=-\vartheta(-\Delta)^{\rho}X_{t}dt+(\Id+(-\Delta)^{\rho})^{-\beta}dW_{t},

(5.4)

with
X_{0}=0
,
T_{n}\geqslant 1
,
\varepsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
,
\vartheta,\rho>0
and
\beta\geqslant 0
.
The Laplacian
\Delta
on a
d
-dimensional domain
D
is supposed to have eigenvalues
-\lambda_{k}\thicksim k^{2/d}
according to the Weyl asymptotics. By Lemma
C.5
this holds for a Laplacian with Dirichlet, Neumann or periodic boundary conditions, or on a compact
d
-dimensional manifold without boundary. We are in the general parametric setting of (
4.2
) with
M_{n}=0
,
\Lambda=-(-\Delta)^{\rho}
,
B_{n}=(\Id+(-\Delta)^{\rho})^{-\beta}
and
J_{\vartheta}=0
. We study the dependence of the estimation rate on the dimension
d
, the fractional index
\rho
, the dynamic noise correlation index
\beta
, the observational noise level
\varepsilon_{n}
and the observation time
T_{n}
.

We apply Corollary
4.6
with

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=T_{n}\trace\Big((-\Delta)^{2\rho}(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}^{4}(-\Delta)^{4\rho}(\Id+(-\Delta)^{\rho})^{4\beta}+\Id)^{-1}(\vartheta^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-\rho}\wedge T_{n})\Big)

For a fixed range of parameters
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]
with
\bar{\vartheta}>\underline{\vartheta}>0
we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\trace\Big(((-\Delta)^{\rho}\wedge T_{n}(-\Delta)^{2\rho})(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}(-\Delta)^{4\rho(1+\beta)}+\Id)^{-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\Big(\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}^{\rho+\beta\rho}\leqslant\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}}\lambda_{k}^{\rho}+\varepsilon_{n}^{-4}\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}^{\rho+\beta\rho}>\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}}\lambda_{k}^{-3\rho-4\beta\rho}\Big)\thicksim T_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{-(2\rho+d)/(2\rho(1+\beta))},

whenever
d<(6+8\beta)\rho
. From Corollary
4.6
, noting
\lVert\Lambda\lvert R_{\underline{\vartheta}}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\rVert\leqslant\underline{\vartheta}^{-1}\thicksim 1
, we thus deduce the following minimax rate.

5.3 Proposition
.

Assume
d<(6+8\beta)\rho
and the asymptotics

v_{n}:=T_{n}^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{(2\rho+d)/(4\rho(1+\beta))}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Then uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]
the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
from Definition
4.3
satisfies

\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}(v_{n})

and the rate
v_{n}
is minimax optimal.

The rate for
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
is becoming faster with the dimension
d
and slower with the fractional index
\rho
and the dynamic correlation index
\beta
. The fact that the rate slows down for larger
\beta
, i.e. with more correlation in the dynamic noise, reflects classical behaviour, in contrast to the inverse scaling of the dynamic noise from Example
3.1
.
In the fundamental case of the classical white-noise stochastic heat equation with
\rho=1
and
B_{n}=\Id
(
\beta=0
) the minimax rate becomes

v_{n}=T_{n}^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{(2+d)/4}\text{ for }d\leqslant 5.

(5.5)

Note that
\vartheta
is not identifiable for fixed
T_{n},\varepsilon_{n}>0
in dimension
d\leqslant 5
because the observation laws are equivalent. This is in stark contrast to the noiseless case
\varepsilon_{n}=0
where
\vartheta
is identifiable for fixed
T_{n}

(
Huebner & Rozovskii,, 1995
)
. In case
d\geqslant 6
(or generally
d\geqslant(6+8\beta)\rho
) one can check by Remark
4.5
that
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=\infty
holds and
\vartheta
is identifiable already non-asymptotically, which means that the observation laws are singular for different
\vartheta
.

5.4
Parameter in the transport term

We consider the estimation of the first order coefficient
\vartheta
in a second order differential operator
A_{\vartheta}
. This is exemplified by
A_{\vartheta}=\nu_{n}\Delta+\vartheta\partial_{\xi}

with the directional derivative
\partial_{\xi}=\xi{\scriptstyle\bullet}\nabla=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}
for
\xi\in\R^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. For the Laplacian
\Delta
the operator
A_{\vartheta}=\nu_{n}\Delta+\vartheta\partial_{\xi}
on the
d
-dimensional torus
[0,1]^{d}
with periodic boundary conditions is normal with eigenfunctions
e_{\ell}(x)=e^{2\pi i\langle\ell,x\rangle}
,
\ell\in\Z^{d}
, and corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell}=-(2\pi)^{2}\nu_{n}\lvert\ell\rvert^{2}+2\pi i\langle\xi,\ell\rangle
. Thus, for each
n\in\N
let the observations
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
be given by

dY_{t}=X_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=(\nu_{n}\Delta X_{t}+\vartheta\partial_{\xi}X_{t})\,dt+(\Id-\Delta)^{-\beta}dW_{t}

(5.6)

and
X_{0}=0
,
T_{n}\geqslant 1
,
\varepsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
,
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]
,
\nu_{n}\in(0,1]
,
\beta\geqslant 0
. This has the general form (
4.2
) with
R_{\vartheta}=M_{n}=\nu_{n}\Delta
,
\Lambda=\partial_{\xi}
,
B_{n}=(\Id-\Delta)^{-\beta}
. Theorem
4.2
yields the minimax lower bound rate

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=T_{n}^{-1/2}\trace\Big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\bar{R}_{n}^{4}B_{n}^{-4}+\Id)^{-1}\lvert\bar{R}_{n}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\Big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}^{-1/2}\Big(\sum_{\ell\in\Z^{d}}\Big(\langle\xi,\ell\rangle^{2}(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\nu_{n}^{4}\lvert\ell\rvert^{8}(1+\lvert\ell\rvert^{2})^{4\beta}+1)^{-1}(\nu_{n}^{-1}\lvert\ell\rvert^{-2}\wedge T_{n})\Big)\Big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}^{-1/2}\Big(\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\Big((\varepsilon_{n}^{-4}\nu_{n}^{-4}k^{-(8+8\beta)/d}\wedge 1)(\nu_{n}^{-1}\wedge T_{n}k^{2/d})\Big)\Big)^{-1/2}.

The sum over
k\geqslant(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n})^{-d/(2+2\beta)}
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n})^{-d/(2+2\beta)}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-4}\nu_{n}^{-4}k^{-(8+8\beta)/d}\nu_{n}^{-1}\thicksim\nu_{n}^{-(d+2+2\beta)/(2+2\beta)}\varepsilon_{n}^{-d/(2+2\beta)}

for
d<8+8\beta
.
The sum over
1\leqslant k<(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n})^{-d/(2+2\beta)}
is

\sum_{1\leqslant k<(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n})^{-d/(2+2\beta)}}(\nu_{n}^{-1}\wedge T_{n}k^{2/d})\lesssim\nu_{n}^{-(d+2+2\beta)/(2+2\beta)}\varepsilon_{n}^{-d/(2+2\beta)}

and thus bounded by the sum over larger
k
. Theorem
4.2
therefore yields a simple lower bound result.

5.4 Proposition
.

Let
d<8+8\beta
and

v_{n}=T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{(d+2+2\beta)/(4+4\beta)}\varepsilon_{n}^{d/(4+4\beta)}.

Then there is a constant
c>0
such that

\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}_{n}}\sup_{\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]}\PP_{\vartheta}\big(v_{n}^{-1}\lvert\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta\rvert\geqslant c\big)\geqslant\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{4}

holds for all
n\in\N
, where the infimum is taken over all estimators based on observing (
5.6
).

The lower bound suggests that
\vartheta
can be consistently estimated under any of the three asymptotics
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
,
T_{n}\to\infty
,
\nu_{n}\to 0
. The small diffusivity asymptotics
\nu_{n}\to 0
quantifies how the SPDE (
5.6
) approaches the usually singular first order SPDE
dX_{t}=\vartheta\partial_{\xi}X_{t}dt+(\Id-\Delta)^{-\beta}dW_{t}
, see also
Gaudlitz & Reiß, (2023)
.

The upper bound is more involved because
J_{\vartheta}=\vartheta\partial_{\xi}
is not dominated by
R_{\vartheta}=\nu_{n}\Delta
when
\nu_{n}\to 0
. In Theorem
4.4
we have

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\thicksim T_{n}\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\Big(k^{2/d}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}(\nu_{n}k^{2/d}\vee T_{n}^{-1})(\nu_{n}k^{2/d}+k^{1/d})^{3}k^{8\beta/d}+1\big)^{-1}(\nu_{n}^{-1}k^{-2/d}\wedge T_{n})\Big)

Consider indices
k_{n}^{\ast}
satisfying

k^{\ast}_{n}\thicksim(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n})^{-d/(2+2\beta)}\wedge(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{4})^{-d/(5+8\beta)}.

Let us assume
\nu_{n}(k_{n}^{\ast})^{2/d}\gtrsim T_{n}^{-1}
, that is
\nu_{n}^{3+8\beta}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{8}T_{n}^{-5-8\beta}
. Then we can lower bound

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\gtrsim T_{n}\nu_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k_{n}^{\ast}\leqslant k\leqslant 2k_{n}^{\ast}}\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\big(\nu_{n}^{4}k^{(8+8\beta)/d}\vee\nu_{n}k^{(5+8\beta)/d}\big)+1\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\nu_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{\ast}\thicksim T_{n}\nu_{n}^{-1}\big((\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n})^{-d/(2+2\beta)}\wedge(\nu_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{4})^{-d/(5+8\beta)}\big),

where we used that the summands are of order
1
for
k\thicksim k_{n}^{\ast}
.

It remains to check Condition (
4.5
) in Theorem
4.4
. A simple bound of its left-hand side is
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\lVert\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{2}\lvert R_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-2}\rVert\lesssim T_{n}{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)
, which for
\varepsilon_{n}\nu_{n}\to 0
is
o({\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)^{2})
as required. Otherwise, we have
\nu_{n},\varepsilon_{n}\thicksim 1
and
T_{n}\to\infty
. In that case Condition (
4.5
) follows from
T_{n}=o(T_{n}^{2})
. Disentangling the cases thus yields:

5.5 Proposition
.

Let
d<8+8\beta
and
\nu_{n}^{3+8\beta}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{8}T_{n}^{-5-8\beta}
. Under the asymptotics
v_{n}\to 0
for

v_{n}:=\begin{cases}T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{(2+d+2\beta)/(4+4\beta)}\varepsilon_{n}^{d/(4+4\beta)},&\text{ if }\nu_{n}\geqslant\varepsilon_{n}^{1/(1+2\beta)},\\
T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{(5+d+8\beta)/(10+16\beta)}\varepsilon_{n}^{2d/(5+8\beta)},&\text{ if }\nu_{n}<\varepsilon_{n}^{1/(1+2\beta)},\end{cases}

(5.7)

the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
from Definition
4.3
satisfies uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]

\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}(v_{n}).

The rate
v_{n}
is minimax optimal for
\nu_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{1/(1+2\beta)}
.

5.6 Remark
.

The results hold generally for real-valued
\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}
because
R_{\vartheta}
is here independent of
\vartheta
, compare the proofs of Theorem
4.2
and
4.4
.

For
\nu_{n}=o(\varepsilon_{n}^{1/(1+2\beta)})
the upper bound does not match the lower bound because the real part
R_{\vartheta}=\nu_{n}\Delta
is too close to zero compared to the imaginary part
J_{\vartheta}=\vartheta\partial_{\xi}
. If even
\nu_{n}^{3+8\beta}=o(\varepsilon_{n}^{8}T_{n}^{-5-8\beta})
holds, then the upper bound rate stays the same as for
\nu_{n}^{3+8\beta}=\varepsilon_{n}^{8}T_{n}^{-5-8\beta}
.

In the fundamental case
B_{n}=\Id
(
\beta=0
) and
\nu_{n}>0
fixed, the minimax rate is

v_{n}=T_{n}^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{d/4}\text{ for }d\leqslant 7

(5.8)

for all
T_{n},\varepsilon_{n}
.
Compared with the rate (
5.5
) for a parameter in front of the Laplacian, the rate is by a factor
\varepsilon_{n}^{-1/2}
slower.

Again, for
d\geqslant 8+8\beta
we have
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=\infty
and the parameter
\vartheta
is non-asymptotically identifiable, checking Remark
4.5
.

5.5
Parameter in the source term

Finally, we consider estimation of the coefficient in the zero order term of a second order differential operator
A_{\vartheta}
. We specify
A_{\vartheta}=\nu\Delta-\vartheta\Id
which satisfies
A_{\vartheta}=R_{\vartheta}\preccurlyeq 0
for
\vartheta\geqslant 0
,
\nu>0
.
For each
n\in\N
let the observations
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
be given by

dY_{t}=X_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=(\nu_{n}\Delta X_{t}-\vartheta X_{t})\,dt+(\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta)^{-\beta}dW_{t}

(5.9)

and
X_{0}=0
,
T_{n}\geqslant 1
,
\varepsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
,
\nu_{n}\in(0,1]
,
\beta\geqslant 0
. The Laplacian
\Delta
on a
d
-dimensional domain
\Lambda
is supposed to have eigenvalues
-\lambda_{k}\thicksim k^{2/d}
according to the Weyl asymptotics. By Lemma
C.5
this holds for a Laplacian with Dirichlet, Neumann or periodic boundary conditions, or on a compact
d
-dimensional manifold without boundary. We apply Corollary
4.6

with
M_{n}=\nu_{n}\Delta
,
\Lambda=\Id
,
B_{n}=(\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta)^{-\beta}
and a fixed parameter range
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]
,
\bar{\vartheta}>\underline{\vartheta}>0
, so that

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=T_{n}\trace\Big(\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}(\vartheta\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta)^{4}(\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta)^{4\beta}+\Id\big)^{-1}\lvert\vartheta\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\trace\Big(\big(\varepsilon_{n}^{4}(\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta)^{4(1+\beta)}+\Id\big)^{-1}(\Id-\nu_{n}\Delta)^{-1}\Big).

Given the Weyl asymptotics
-\lambda_{k}\thicksim k^{2/d}
, we calculate

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\Big(\sum_{k:\nu_{n}\lvert\lambda_{k}\rvert\leqslant 1}1+\sum_{k:1<\nu_{n}\lvert\lambda_{k}\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon_{n}^{-1/(1+\beta)}}\lvert\nu_{n}\lambda_{k}\rvert^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{n}^{-4}\nu_{n}^{-5-4\beta}\sum_{k:\nu_{n}\lvert\lambda_{k}\rvert>\varepsilon_{n}^{-1/(1+\beta)}}\lvert\lambda_{k}\rvert^{-5-4\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\Big(\nu_{n}^{-d/2}+\nu_{n}^{-1}\sum_{\nu_{n}^{-d/2}<k\leqslant\nu_{n}^{-d/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{-d/(2+2\beta)}}k^{-2/d}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{n}^{-4}\nu_{n}^{-5-4\beta}\sum_{k>\nu_{n}^{-d/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{-d/(2+2\beta)}}k^{-(10+8\beta)/d}\Big)

\displaystyle\thicksim T_{n}\nu_{n}^{-d/2}\big(1+\varepsilon_{n}^{-(d-2)/(2+2\beta)}\big),

where we assumed
d<10+8\beta
for summability and
d\not=2
for bounding the second sum by the first and third sum. In the case
d=2
the second sum dominates and

{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)\thicksim T_{n}\nu_{n}^{-1}\log(e\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})

holds. Thus Corollary
4.6
, noting
\lVert\Lambda\lvert R_{\underline{\vartheta}}\rvert_{T_{n}}^{-1}\rVert\lesssim 1
, gives the following result.

5.7 Proposition
.

For
d<10+8\beta
and under the asymptotics
v_{n}\to 0
for

v_{n}:=\begin{cases}T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{1/4},&\text{ if }d=1,\\
T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{1/2}(\log(e\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}))^{-1/2},&\text{ if }d=2,\\
T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{d/4}\varepsilon_{n}^{(d-2)/(4+4\beta)},&\text{ if }d\geqslant 3,\end{cases}

(5.10)

the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
from Definition
4.3
satisfies uniformly over
\vartheta\in[\underline{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}]

\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta={\cal O}_{\PP_{\vartheta}}(v_{n})

and the rate
v_{n}
is minimax optimal.

5.8 Remark
.

If the noise covariance operator is
B_{n}=(\Id-\Delta)^{-\beta}
, that is not depending on
\nu_{n}
, then similar calculations for
\varepsilon_{n}\lesssim\nu_{n}^{\beta}
yield the same rate for
d\in\{1,2\}
, but
v_{n}=T_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{n}^{(d+2\beta)/(4+4\beta)}\varepsilon_{n}^{(d-2)/(4+4\beta)}
for
3\leqslant d<10+8\beta
.
This is slower in
\nu_{n}
than before which is reasonable in view of Example
3.1
because the noise covariance operator is smaller in the high frequencies.

This means that in dimension
d=1
the minimax rate is independent of
\beta
and
\varepsilon_{n}
. It matches the upper bound derived by
Gaudlitz & Reiß, (2023)
in the vanishing diffusivity regime
\nu_{n}\to 0
without noise. In the fundamental case
\beta=0
and
\nu_{n}=1
the rate is

v_{n}=\begin{cases}T_{n}^{-1/2},&\text{ if }d=1,\\
(T_{n}\log(e\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}))^{-1/2},&\text{ if }d=2,\\
T_{n}^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{(d-2)/4},&\text{ if }3\leqslant d\leqslant 9.\end{cases}

(5.11)

The fact that vanishing observation noise
\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
leads to consistent estimation of the reaction parameter in
d\geqslant 2
agrees with the results from
Huebner & Rozovskii, (1995)
, where in the noiseless setting
\varepsilon_{n}=0
the reaction parameter is identified in these dimensions. Generally, for
d\geqslant 10+8\beta
we have
{\cal I}_{n}(\vartheta)=\infty
and
\vartheta
is non-asymptotically identifiable by Remark
4.5
.

6
Fundamental nonparametric examples

6.1
Space-dependent diffusivity

Passing to nonparametric problems, we now consider a space-dependent coefficient in the leading order of the second-order differential operator, more specifically the weighted Laplacian
\Delta_{\vartheta}=\nabla{\scriptstyle\bullet}(\vartheta(x)\nabla)
on
H=L^{2}(\R^{d})
with space-dependent diffusivity
\vartheta:\R^{d}\to\R^{+}
in a nonparametric regularity class.
For each
n\in\N
we consider observations
(dY_{t},t\in[0,T_{n}])
given by

dY_{t}=(\Id-\Delta)^{-\beta}X_{t}dt+\varepsilon_{n}dV_{t}\text{ with }dX_{t}=\Delta_{\vartheta}X_{t}\,dt+dW_{t},

(6.1)

where

X_{0}=0
,
T_{n}\geqslant 1
,
\beta\in[0,1/2]
,
\varepsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
, and

\vartheta({\scriptstyle\bullet})
belonging to the class

\displaystyle\Theta_{dif}(\alpha,R):=\Big\{\vartheta\in C^{4\vee\alpha}(\R^{d})\,\Big|\,\inf_{x\in\R^{d}}\vartheta(x)\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2};\;\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{C^{\alpha}}\leqslant R\Big\},\;\alpha>0,\,R>1.

(6.2)

6.1 Remark
.

For the domain of the weighted Laplacians we need
\dom(\Delta_{\vartheta})={\cal H}^{2}(\R^{d})
.
Taking into account that by Theorem
6.4
below we
only consider
d<6+8\beta\leqslant 10
,
an easy sufficient condition is
\vartheta\in C^{4}(\R^{d})
by
Triebel, (2010, Section 2.8.2)
.
Notice that for different
\vartheta
, the operators
\Delta_{\vartheta}
do not commute.
The bound
\inf_{x\in\R^{d}}\vartheta(x)\geqslant 1/2
ensures that
(u,v)\mapsto\langle u,(-\Delta_{\vartheta})v\rangle=\langle\nabla u,\vartheta\nabla v\rangle
defines a positive definite bilinear form on
{\cal H}^{1}(\R^{d})
.

In order to apply Theorem
3.9
, we consider
A_{\bf 0}=\Delta
and the alternative
A_{\bf 1}=\Delta_{\vartheta}
with
\vartheta(x)=1+L(x/h)
for some test function
L:\R^{d}\to\R
and a bandwidth
h\in(0,1)
, to be chosen later.
We have
B=(\Id-\Delta)^{-\beta}
and set
\bar{B}_{\bf 0}=B
.
B
does not commute with
A_{\bf 1}
, but
-\Delta_{\vartheta}\preccurlyeq\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}(-\Delta)
implies

(\Id-\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}^{-1}\Delta_{\vartheta})^{-2\beta}\succcurlyeq(\Id-\Delta)^{-2\beta}=B^{\ast}B\text{ for }\beta\in[0,1/2],

using operator monotonicity of
t\mapsto-t^{-r}
on
\R^{+}
for
r\in(0,1]

(
Bhatia,, 2013
)
. For
\bar{B}_{\bf 1}:=(\Id-\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}^{-1}\Delta_{\vartheta})^{-\beta}
this yields (
3.4
).
By
Engel & Nagel, (2000, Thm. VI.5.22)

\dom(A_{\bf 1})=\dom(A_{\bf 0})={\cal H}^{2}(\R^{d})
holds, as soon as
L\in C^{1}(\R^{d})
.
The minimax lower bound later can still be established over Hölder classes with
\alpha<1
by using smooth
L
whose bounds on the first derivatives are finite, but grow with the asymptotics.

To deal with the non-commutativity of
A_{\bf 0}
and
A_{\bf 1}
, we establish for certain functions
f:\R^{+}\to\R
that
f(-\Delta_{\vartheta})\preccurlyeq C_{f,\vartheta}f(-\Delta)
with a well quantified constant
C_{f,\vartheta}
.
Let us remark that most monotone functions
g
are not operator monotone so that operators
T_{1},T_{2}
with
T_{1}\preccurlyeq T_{2}
do not necessarily satisfy
g(T_{1})\preccurlyeq g(T_{2})
. In particular,
g:\R^{+}\to\R
,
g(\lambda)=-(\varepsilon^{2}\lambda^{2+2\beta}+1)^{-1}
is not an operator monotone function because
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{2+2\beta}
is not operator monotone, see
Bhatia, (2013, Chapter V)
for this and more results on operator monotonicity for matrices which directly extend to linear operators.

Based on perturbation ideas from
Engel & Nagel, (2000, Prop. VI.5.24)
, however, we are able to establish
(\varepsilon^{2}(-\Delta_{\vartheta})^{2+2\beta}+\Id)^{-1}\preccurlyeq C_{d,\beta}(\varepsilon^{2}(-\Delta)^{2+2\beta}+\Id)^{-1}
for some precise constant
C_{d,\beta}
, provided a suitable smoothness norm of
\vartheta
is bounded by
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

6.2 Proposition
.

Consider the operators
\Delta_{\vartheta}
and
\Delta
on
{\cal H}^{2}(\R^{d})\subseteq L^{2}(\R^{d})
with

,

,

and

.

C_{d,\beta}^{(i)}
,

, only depending on

and

,
such that:

(a)

if
C_{d,\beta}^{(1)}h^{-2}(\lVert\nabla L\rVert_{\infty}^{4}+\lVert\nabla^{2}L\rVert_{\infty}^{4/3})\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1/(1+\beta)}
, then

\big(\varepsilon^{2}(-\Delta_{\vartheta})^{2+2\beta}+\Id\big)^{-1}\preccurlyeq C_{d,\beta}^{(2)}\big(\varepsilon^{2}(-\Delta)^{2+2\beta}+\Id\big)^{-1};

(6.3)

(b)

if

C_{d,\beta}^{(3)}h^{-2}(\lVert\nabla L\rVert_{\infty}^{4}+\lVert\nabla L\rVert_{\infty}^{10/3}+\lVert\nabla^{2}L\rVert_{\infty}^{4/3}+\lVert\nabla\Delta L\rVert_{\infty}^{4/5})\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1/(1+\beta)}
,
then

\lvert\Delta_{\vartheta}\rvert_{T^{-1}}^{-1}\big(\varepsilon^{2}(-\Delta_{\vartheta})^{2+2\beta}+\Id\big)^{-1}\preccurlyeq C_{d,\beta}^{(4)}\lvert\Delta\rvert_{T^{-1}}^{-1}\big(\varepsilon^{2}(-\Delta)^{2+2\beta}+\Id\big)^{-1}.

(6.4)

Proof.

See Section
B.3
.

6.3 Remark
.

The condition
L\in C^{7}(\R^{d})
ensures
\dom(\Delta_{\vartheta}^{4})={\cal H}^{8}(\R^{d})
.
Later we shall consider kernels
L
of the form
L=h_{n}^{\alpha}K
with
h_{n}\downarrow 0
and
K
fixed so that the norms in terms of
L
matter for the asymptotics.

In the case
\beta=0
Proposition
6.2
(a) holds already under the condition
C_{d}^{(1)}h^{-2}\lVert\nabla L\rVert_{\infty}^{4}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}
and Proposition
6.2
(b) under
C_{d}^{(3)}h^{-2}(\lVert\nabla L\rVert_{\infty}^{4}+\lVert\nabla^{2}L\rVert_{\infty}^{4/3})\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}

For each

6.5 Remark
.

(a)

The same lower bound holds for

at any
x_{0}\in\R^{d}
, the concrete choice

just simplifies notation. Working on the unbounded domain

allows us to use Fourier transforms which facilitates the analysis considerably. It is intuitive, but not rigorously established that for our pointwise estimation risk the asymptotic results transfer to smooth bounded domains.

(b)

We can understand the rate in case

in terms of the parametric rate
T_{n}^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{n}^{(d+2)/(4+4\beta)}
from Proposition

For fixed

the rates simplify to

v_{n}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{n}^{(d+2)\alpha/(4\alpha+2d)}&\text{, if }\alpha\geqslant 1,\\
\varepsilon_{n}^{5\alpha/(4\alpha+6)}&\text{, if }\alpha\in(1/2,1].\end{cases}

(6.6)

In view of Remark
6.3
the second case holds even for

. With considerable efforts
Pasemann & Reiß, (2024)
have obtained a corresponding upper bound for the case
\alpha\geqslant 1
.

(c)

In dimension

stems from the bound (

of negative order for relatively rough
\vartheta

a standard preaveraging estimator does no longer work
(

)
, which might give an upper bound perspective on this effect.

(d)

The additional condition (

Proof.

For simplicity we drop the index

at

.
We transfer the problem into the spectral domain by the Fourier transform

Consider

Note that using

\displaystyle(\varepsilon^{2}R_{\bf 0}^{2}\bar{B}_{\bf 0}^{-2}+\Id)^{-1}

\displaystyle(\varepsilon^{2}R_{\bf 1}^{2}\bar{B}_{\bf 1}^{-2}+\Id)^{-1}

6.9 Proposition

and the first statement follows from the assumption
\dom(A_{\vartheta})=\dom(R_{\vartheta})
.

For the second property we use standard differentiability properties of Bochner integrals. For

we obtain by substitution and the chain rule

\partial_{t}S_{\vartheta}f(t)=\partial_{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f(t-u)\,du\Big)=e^{A_{\vartheta}t}f(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f^{\prime}(t-u)\,du

(B.1)

and the right-hand side is in

.
This shows
S_{\vartheta}f\in{\cal H}_{0}^{1}(H)
in view of
S_{\vartheta}f(0)=0
by definition.

Proof of (b).
For
f\in{\cal H}^{1}(H)
we have by (a) that
S_{\vartheta}f\in{\cal H}_{0}^{1}(H)\cap L^{2}([0,T];\dom(A_{\vartheta}))
. Thus, by formula (
B.1
) and partial integration

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-A_{\vartheta})S_{\vartheta}f(t)

\displaystyle=e^{A_{\vartheta}t}f(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f^{\prime}(t-u)\,du-\int_{0}^{t}A_{\vartheta}e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f(t-u)\,du

\displaystyle=e^{A_{\vartheta}t}f(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f^{\prime}(t-u)\,du-\Big(e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f(t-u)\Big|_{u=0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A_{\vartheta}u}f^{\prime}(t-u)\,du\Big)

\displaystyle=f(t).

This gives
(\partial_{t}-A_{\vartheta})S_{\vartheta}=\Id
on
{\cal H}^{1}(H)
. Clearly,
A_{\vartheta}
and
S_{\vartheta}
commute on
L^{2}([0,T];\dom(A_{\vartheta}))
. Moreover, for
f\in{\cal H}_{0}^{1}(H)
we find by formula (
B.1
) with
f(0)=0

\partial_{t}S_{\vartheta}f(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{Au}f^{\prime}(t-u)\,du=S_{\vartheta}\partial_{t}f(t).

(B.2)

This finishes the proof of (b).

Proof of (c).
For
f\in{\cal H}^{1}(H)
we have
S_{\bf 0}f,S_{\bf 1}f\in{\cal H}_{0}^{1}(H)\cap L^{2}([0,T];\dom(A_{\vartheta}))
by (a). Part (b) yields

\displaystyle S_{\bf 0}(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 1}f

\displaystyle=S_{\bf 0}(\partial_{t}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 1}f-S_{\bf 0}(\partial_{t}-A_{\bf 1})S_{\bf 1}f=S_{\bf 1}f-S_{\bf 0}f,

\displaystyle S_{\bf 1}(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 0}f

\displaystyle=S_{\bf 1}(\partial_{t}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 0}f-S_{\bf 1}(\partial_{t}-A_{\bf 1})S_{\bf 0}f=S_{\bf 1}f-S_{\bf 0}f.

Hence,
S_{\bf 1}-S_{\bf 0}=S_{\bf 0}(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 1}=S_{\bf 1}(A_{\bf 1}-A_{\bf 0})S_{\bf 0}
holds on
{\cal H}^{1}(H)
. Since all three terms present bounded linear operators on
L^{2}(H)
and
{\cal H}^{1}(H)
is dense in
L^{2}(H)

(
Amann,, 2019
, Eq. (1.2.3))
, the assertion follows by a continuity argument.
∎

C.1 Lemma
.

Let

(C.1)

holds for any

. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\lVert(\nabla\vartheta)u\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(2dh)^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}\lVert u\rVert^{1/2}\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{1/2},\quad u\in{\cal H}^{1}(\R^{d}),

(C.2)

\displaystyle\lVert\langle\nabla\vartheta,\nabla u\rangle\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(2d^{2}h)^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{1/2}\lVert\Delta u\rVert^{1/2},\quad u\in{\cal H}^{2}(\R^{d}),

(C.3)

\displaystyle\lVert(\nabla^{2}\vartheta)\nabla u\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(2d^{3}h)^{1/2}\lVert\nabla^{2}\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{1/2}\lVert\Delta u\rVert^{1/2},\quad u\in{\cal H}^{2}(\R^{d}),

(C.4)

\displaystyle\lVert(\nabla^{2}u)\nabla\vartheta\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(2d^{3}h)^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\Delta u\rVert^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\Delta u\rVert^{1/2},\quad u\in{\cal H}^{3}(\R^{d}),

(C.5)

\displaystyle\lVert\langle\nabla^{2}\vartheta,\nabla^{2}u\rangle_{HS}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(2d^{4}h)^{1/2}\lVert\nabla^{2}\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\Delta u\rVert^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\Delta u\rVert^{1/2},\quad u\in{\cal H}^{3}(\R^{d}).

(C.6)

Proof.

To prove (
C.1
), use integration by parts and obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\vartheta u\rVert^{2}=\int_{\R^{d}}\vartheta^{2}(x)u^{2}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle={\Bigl\lvert-\int_{\R^{d}}\Big(\int_{-h/2}^{x_{i}\wedge(h/2)}\vartheta^{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{d})\,dy\Big)2u(x)\partial_{i}u(x)\,dx\Bigr\rvert}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2h\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\int_{\R^{d}}\lvert u(x)\rvert\lvert\partial_{i}u(x)\rvert\,dx\leqslant 2h\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert u\rVert\lVert\partial_{i}u\rVert.

All other estimates follow by replacing

and
\vartheta
in (

) by partial derivatives of
u
and
\vartheta
, where we take into account
\lVert\nabla^{2}u\rVert=\lVert\Delta u\rVert
(and thus
\lVert\nabla^{3}u\rVert=\lVert\nabla\Delta u\rVert

For example, we have

\displaystyle\lVert(\nabla^{2}\vartheta)\nabla u\rVert^{2}

.

C.6 Lemma
.

and treat each part separately.
First,
\int_{0}^{1}z^{a}(t-z)^{c}dz\lesssim(t-1)^{c}\lesssim t^{c}
.
Next,

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{t-1}z^{b}(t-z)^{c}\,dz

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{1+b+c}\Big(\int_{1/t}^{1/2}u^{b}\,du+\int_{1/2}^{1-1/t}(1-u)^{c}\,du\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{1+b+c}(1\vee\log(t)\vee t^{-b-1}+t^{-c-1})\lesssim t^{b\vee c},

then
\int_{t-1}^{t+1}z^{b}dz\lesssim t^{b}
and finally

\displaystyle\int_{t+1}^{\infty}z^{b}(z-t)^{c}\,dz

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{1+b+c}\Big(\int_{1+1/t}^{2}(u-1)^{c}\,du+\int_{2}^{\infty}u^{b}(u-1)^{c}\,du\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{1+b+c}(t^{-c-1}+1)\sim t^{b}.

∎

C.7 Lemma
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
p_{i},q_{i}\in\R
for
i=1,2,3
. Then

\displaystyle T_{n}^{p_{1}\alpha+q_{1}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-(p_{2}\alpha+q_{2})}\nu_{n}^{-(p_{3}\alpha+q_{3})}\gtrsim 1

(C.8)

holds for positive sequences
(T_{n})
,
(\varepsilon_{n})
,
(\nu_{n})
if
\lvert p_{1}\log(T_{n})+p_{2}\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})+p_{3}\log(\nu_{n}^{-1})\rvert\rightarrow\infty
and

\displaystyle\alpha>\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-q_{1}\log(T_{n})-q_{2}\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})-q_{3}\log(\nu_{n}^{-1})}{p_{1}\log(T_{n})+p_{2}\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})+p_{3}\log(\nu_{n}^{-1})}.

Proof.

By taking logarithms, (
C.8
) is equivalent to

(p_{1}\alpha+q_{1})\log(T_{n})+(p_{2}\alpha+q_{2})\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})+(p_{3}\alpha+q_{3})\log(\nu_{n}^{-1})\geqslant\log(c),

where
c>0
is the proportionality constant. Solve for
\alpha
.
∎
