The Affective Bridge: Unifying Feature Representations for Speech Deepfake Detection
Yupei Li, Chenyang Lyu, Longyue Wang, Weihua Luo, Kaifu Zhang, Björn W. Schuller

Abstract
Speech deepfake detection has been widely explored using low-level acoustic descriptors. However, each study tends to select different feature sets, making it difficult to establish a unified representation for the task. Moreover, such features are not intuitive for humans to perceive, as the distinction between bona fide and synthesized speech becomes increasingly subtle with the advancement of deepfake generation techniques. Emotion, on the other hand, remains a unique human attribute that current deepfake generator struggles to fully replicate, reflecting the gap toward true artificial general intelligence. Interestingly, many existing acoustic and semantic features have implicit correlations with emotion. For instance, speech features recognized by automatic speech recognition systems often varies naturally with emotional expression. Based on this insight, we propose a novel training framework that leverages emotion as a bridge between conventional deepfake features and emotion-oriented representations. Experiments on the widely used FakeOrReal and In-the-Wild datasets demonstrate consistent and substantial improvements in accuracy, up to approximately 6% and 2% increases, respectively, and in equal error rate (EER), showing reductions of up to about 4% and 1%, respectively, while achieving comparable results on ASVspoof2019. This approach provides a unified training strategy for all features and interpretable feature direction for deepfake detection while improving model performance through emotion-informed learning.

Beyond these low-level representations, high-level task-oriented features have proven effective as well. These are often obtained from models trained for specific downstream applications, such as automatic speech recognition (ASR)

Despite these advances, the landscape of audio deepfake detection remains fragmented: there is no consensus on an optimal feature set. Moreover, humans often struggle to perceptually distinguish between bona fide and synthetic audio, as the generative models are largely developed and their attention is typically guided by global parts rather than fine-grained acoustic details that machine models may exploit

However, human could recognise emotion inconsistency from deepfake audio more easily compared to other features, as emotion remains a challenging aspect for machines to imitate authentically, highlighting a persistent gap between current artificial intelligence (AI) systems and general intelligence

. Therefore, emotion can serve as a connecting bridge between human perception and machine representation, potentially guiding the development of more interpretable and unified deepfake detection frameworks.

Emotion can also be represented through various sets of features, as demonstrated by studies utilising such features for emotion recognition. These include low-level descriptors of physical characteristics

and higher-level, application-based feature representations such as those derived from ASR systems

To address the lack of an optimal and interpretable feature representation, we propose a unified novel pre-training stage prior to the final deepfake detection classifier. This stage transfers knowledge from diverse feature domains into an emotion-based representation while mitigating catastrophic forgetting
[
1
]
of previously learned tasks. Although prior studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of aligning emotion features with other modalities
[
28
,
15

, they primarily inject affective cues into the pipeline rather than leveraging emotion as a bridging representation between existing features. Conventional fusion strategies, such as early or late fusion

2

, not only require careful feature selection but also often produce representations that are difficult to interpret or reuse for original tasks (e. g., ASR) or emotion recognition. In contrast, our approach preserves the physical meaning of the features, enabling a given representation to progressively learn emotion-related characteristics and transfer them effectively to deepfake detection. Experimental results show consistent improvements on the FakeOrReal (FoR)

and InTheWild (ITW)

In summary, our main contributions are twofold. First, we propose a unified novel training framework that transfers and aligns heterogeneous feature sets into an emotion-centered representation, yielding a robust and interpretable feature space for deepfake detection. In this framework, both the original and emotion-related features are preserved and effectively fused, preventing catastrophic forgetting and ensuring that the original representations remain functional. Furthermore, we demonstrate that employing affective bridge: emotion as a bridging representation consistently enhances deepfake detection performance without additional training on the deepfake detection task, thereby ensuring scalability and reducing the risk of overfitting on deepfake detection. This also suggests that emotion-related features remain a distinctive cue that current deepfake audio models fail to accurately reproduce.

II
Methodology: The Affective Bridge: EmoBridge

To address the aforementioned challenge of selecting appropriate features for deepfake detection, we align affective cues through a continual learning framework that jointly optimises existing features with an emotion-related task. This continual adaptation enables the feature representation to incorporate new affective information without forgetting previously learned knowledge, which is shown reliable from the continuous learning principle
[
10
,
22
]
. The overall framework is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Fig. 1
:
Emotion as a bridge framework. The pre-trained model for a specific task provides an original feature representation, where essential information is stored in the encoder. We further train the encoder, together with a fully connected layer, to perform an emotion recognition task, thereby fusing emotion-related features within the encoder (EmoBridge step). The outputs from each encoder layer, which now incorporate both original and emotion-based features, are then used as inputs to a classifier—such as a support vector machine (SVM)—for the final deepfake detection task.

Given the vast variety of feature representations, we consider the last hidden states of the feature encoder (for attention-based architectures) or the final layer outputs (for other deep learning architectures) as the optimal feature representations for each modality. We categorise commonly used features into three groups: (1) acoustic features, which can be extracted using openSMILE; (2) deep learning (DL) raw features, derived directly from deep neural models; and (3) application-based features, such as semantic features from ASR pre-trained models or perceptual features from SV pre-trained models.

Specifically, the EmoBridge configuration generally achieves lower EER and higher accuracy, indicating a trend particularly pronounced for systems using all features, where emotion-guided fusion effectively enhances the separability between genuine and fake speech. The results indicate that the observed degradation mainly occurs for real audio under our strategy, likely due to the imbalanced training distribution affecting the emotion-fused features. From the DL-raw results, performance on the real subset remains higher, and overall accuracy improves, suggesting that emotion-enhanced features still contribute positively. Notably, audio generated by methods A17, A18, and A19 shows more consistent improvements than other source types, as these fakes are based on real human speech, whereas other methods rely on text or TTS synthesis. This suggests that human speech provides richer prosodic and emotional cues, resulting in more realistic fakes and more detectable emotional inconsistencies, while text- or TTS-based synthesis lacks natural emotional variation, yielding smaller gains from emotion-based modeling. Overall, our strategy remains effective, and as deepfake generation advances toward more expressive synthesis, fusing emotion-related information is expected to become very important for future detection systems.

IV
Conclusion

We proposed the Emobridge strategy, a unified approach, to incorporate emotion information into pre-trained features. This approach not only preserves the original feature representations while adding affective cues, but also improves deepfake detection performance on multiple benchmarks. We consider emotion to be a more interpretable feature, perceivable by humans, which can serve as a bridge to obtain a unified feature representation. In future work, a unified representation for deepfake detection could be achieved by exploring methods to effectively combine representations from different pre-trained models through emotion.
