The Influence of Loneliness on Pain Outcomes for Adolescents: A Cross-Sectional Survey
Authors: Paula A Forgeron, Jennifer Stinson, Kathryn Birnie, G Allen Finley, Abbie Jordan, Pamela Qualter, Ligyana Candido, Michelle Lamont, Cassidy Bradley, Delane Linkiewich, Trinity Lowthian, Samuel McNally, Natasha Trehan, Bruce Dick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLoneliness, the perception that one’s social relationships do not meet the desire for social connection, is a risk factor for poor mental and physical health. Adolescents with chronic pain experience higher rates of peer loneliness which persists over time. Previous studies used a single loneliness measure, limiting our understanding of the nature of their loneliness. This study describes the types of peer loneliness (intimate, relational, and collective) experienced by these adolescents and the impact that peer loneliness has on pain-related outcomes.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was completed by 128 Canadian adolescents aged 12–18 years who experienced pain for at least 3 months. Validated measures captured demographics, pain-related characteristics, types of peer-related loneliness, measures of social well-being, and mental and physical health outcomes.ResultsFriedman’s tests of z-scores indicate that participants equally experienced dyadic, relational, and collective peer loneliness. MANCOVA revealed that those who identify as Black were lonelier after controlling for socioeconomic status. Multiple regression showed that loneliness was a robust predicter of worse scores on social well-being and mental health outcomes with males and females equally impacted by loneliness. Despite moderate correlations between loneliness and pain interference and pain intensity, loneliness did not predict school absences, suggesting that loneliness’ influence on physical pain outcomes may be temporally earlier (e.g. contribute to pain chronification).ConclusionsPeer loneliness among adolescents with chronic pain negatively impacts their social well-being and mental health outcomes. Interventions addressing loneliness to target all three types of peer loneliness may be key to improving pain-related outcomes.KEYWORDS: Adolescent chronic pain, social functioning, loneliness, pain-related outcomes

Loneliness has been described as a form of distress and dysphoria (e.g., negative emotional state, feelings of unhappiness and emptiness) as one’s relationships do not fulfill their desire for social connection.1 Therefore loneliness is subjective in nature, distinguishing it from objective social isolation.2 Social isolation in childhood is the objective lack of social connections or contact with peers (similar aged individuals) and is described as a state of aloneness.3 Although social isolation may be associated with peer rejection, not all rejected children are lonely, and, in fact, children who are rejected by peers at school but are not lonely (e.g., have peer connections) are no more at risk for negative sequela than nonlonely, nonrejected children, suggesting that peer loneliness is more concerning than peer rejection or observable aloneness.3 Moreover, enduring peer loneliness (not parent loneliness or social isolation) predisposes adolescents to depression and is linked to poor physical and mental health outcomes even into young adulthood as well as lower social well-being (e.g., self-esteem).4–7 Not only does peer loneliness in adolescents have negative associations with health, it may also have negative associations with broader life achievements as adolescents who are lonely at school reported fewer intentions of attending university after controlling for socioeconomic status (SES), literacy, racial background, and sex.8 Thus, it is concerning that adolescents with chronic pain (ACP) score higher on peer loneliness measures compared to those without pain, and these higher levels of loneliness persist over time but we know little about the relationship between loneliness and pain-related outcomes as previous research has viewed loneliness as occurring along side chronic pain or as an outcome from social challenges related to pain.9–11

Cacioppo and colleagues1 conceptualize loneliness as having an evolutionary role in human survival with the adverse feeling (loneliness) promoting affiliation with others to form a safe social group or withdrawal if social relationships are perceived as harmful. Within their Evolutionary Theory of Loneliness, Cacioppo et al.12 define three types of loneliness that individuals may experience. Intimate loneliness is the perceived lack of attachment to significant others (e.g., best friend) whom one can count on for social support and mutual help, acknowledging one’s value as a person. Relational loneliness is the perceived absence of quality friendships or family resulting in a lack of connections with a sympathetic group, which during adolescents may be their larger circle of friends. Collective loneliness is the absence of feeling connected to a larger group, which for adolescents may be school-related loneliness given the amount of time adolescents spend at school. ACP are at risk for all three types of peer loneliness as they experience best friend breakups more often than peers, often feel misunderstood by their friends to the point of self-isolating and are absent from school more than peers which can make ACP feel like an outsider at school.9–11,13–15 Studies involving ACP that captured peer loneliness used one measure of peer loneliness creating a knowledge gap concerning the types of peer loneliness ACP experience, making it difficult to target interventions.9,11

Although sex differences exist in the prevalence of adolescent chronic pain, with females experiencing pain more often, the existence of sex or gender differences in the experience of adolescent peer loneliness is inconclusive.16 Across the studies on peer loneliness that captured sex or gender, the variable was captured and reported as a binary variable (male/female; boy/girl; men/women) without a clarification if one had the ability to self-identify and the studies reported their findings based on binary outcomes. Therefore, it is unclear if identifying with being a boy or if being assigned the sex of male at birth differ in their association with peer loneliness. Some studies found no sex or gender differences in peer loneliness, nor between loneliness and physical health or mental health, while another study found small differences with males experiencing more intimate loneliness and females experiencing more relational loneliness.5,17–19 Sex differences were also found amongst loneliness trajectories with lonelier female adolescents having lower self-esteem in young adulthood.7 Thus, understanding if sex or gender differences in loneliness amongst ACP exist, is warranted.

This study sought to describe the types of peer loneliness experienced by ACP, the influence that these types of loneliness have on the pain-related outcomes of social well-being as well as mental and physical health outcomes and any differences based on socio-demographic characteristics.

Methods

A two-phased sequential mixed methods multisite study was undertaken. Phase 1 (reported here) consisted of a cross-sectional survey study. Phase 2 (ongoing) is an interpretative phenomenological analysis study to understand the loneliness experiences of ACP. The objectives for the current study were to: 1) describe the types of peer loneliness experienced by ACP, and 2) explore the influence of different types of peer loneliness on pain-related physical, mental and social outcomes. Additionally, this study sought to explore the influence of socio-demographics on expressions of peer loneliness in ACPs.

Participants

This study was approved by research ethics boards at the three sites across Canada, including the University of Ottawa REB file number H-07-22-8129, The Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto REB file number: 1000079873, and the Stollery Children’s Hospital REB number Pro00128531. Participants with chronic pain were recruited in two stages from November 2022 to June 2023. The first stage used a social media campaign (e.g., Instagram, Facebook, X [formerly known as Twitter], Solutions for Kids in Pain [SKIP network, a Canadian knowledge mobilization network]) which was supplemented by stage two halfway through recruitment. Stage two consisted of participant recruitment from three Canadian pediatric hospital chronic pain clinics from three different provinces (Nova Scotia, Ontario, Alberta) by providing the clinic attendees with a handout introducing the study by a member of their circle of care.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ACP between 12–18 years of age, (2) able to read and write in English (due to limited language of validated measures), (3) experience frequent or constant pain for 3 months or more, (4) be enrolled in school. The researchers’ contact information, the voluntary nature of participation, along with the link to the information and consent form were embedded into the social media postings and clinic handout invitations.

Procedures and measures

This 130-item self-report survey study captured data via REDCap, which was accessible by smartphone, tablet, or computer and hosted at one of the study sites in Canada. The first page listed the inclusion criteria that participants had to answer to gain access to the survey, following this, participants were provided with the information and consent letter and were asked for consent before gaining access to the survey. If participants did not meet inclusion criteria and/or if they did not consent to participate, they were transferred to a thank you page and could not access the survey.

Due to an influx of over 1,139 completed surveys within 3 hours on the first day of opening the study, data security measures were implemented. Surveys that did not meet these measures were removed before analysis. These data security measures (e.g., use of Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart [CAPTCHA]) and vetting measures to detect imposter data were based on the literature, best practices by other researchers, and additional verification questions added by the research team and approved by the research ethics boards.20,21 The vetting processes consisted of surveys submitted during a data flood (e.g., 10 surveys completed within 10 minutes of each other) and invalid postal codes (did not conform to Canadian postal code standards or were not associated with a residential address). The participant verification questions consisted of three repeated questions at the beginning and end of the survey (age, ethnicity, postal code).

Demographic data

All participants completed a set of study specific demographic questions to capture, age, sex assigned at birth, gender (e.g., boy/man, girl/woman, transgender girl/women, transgender boy/man; non-binary, two-spirit, other, prefer not to answer) racial identity, postal code, school absences over the previous month, co-morbid diagnosis by healthcare clinician (if any), and family structure (e.g., live with both parents, live with one parent). Perceived SES was captured using a 1-item question with five options based on the work of Svedberg and colleagues, who compared multiple measures to capture SES amongst adolescent participants.22 Their findings suggest that perceived SES may be understood better by asking adolescents “How well off do you think your family is?” with selections on an ordinal scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very well off). This measure can be further reduced to three categories: low (‘not at all’ and ‘not particularly’), middle (‘fairly’), and high (‘rather’ and ‘very’).

Pain characteristics

Pain characteristics included pain onset, frequency, location, intensity, and duration. Average pain intensity (0 ‘no pain’ to 10 ‘worse possible pain’) over the past week was captured using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS-11).23 The Pediatric Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pain Interference Short Form was used to capture pain-related disability and consists of an 8-item global measure for pain interferences across three domains (physical, social, psychological).24 Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert type scale with higher scores illustrating greater pain-related inference. Scores are converted to T-scores with 50 being the population mean. Internal consistency across three time points ranged from α =.68 to.83 and illustrated changes over time in relation to treatment with a pediatric pain sample.25 Internal reliability in this study was α = .94.

Measures of pain-related outcomes

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale was used to capture global self-esteem; it consists of a 10-item scale with scores ranging from 0 to 30, where higher scores indicate higher self-esteem.26 This measure has been used with this population in the past with an internal reliability of α = .94.11 The internal reliability in this study was α = .91. General anxiety was measured with the global 8-item, 5-point Pediatric PROMIS Anxiety Short Form.27 Scores are converted to T-scores with 50 being the population mean so that higher scores indicate greater levels of anxiety. Internal consistency in this study was α = .97. Similarly, the 8-item, 5-point Pediatric PROMIS Depressive Symptom Short Form captured depressive symptoms of participants over the past 7 days with higher scores indicating higher levels of depressed mood.27 The short-form questions do not include physical symptoms, which could be elevated due to chronic pain versus depressed mood and therefore provide a clearer indication of depressed mood in this population with internal consistency in this study of α = 0.98. However, once the data was collected and variables were inspected an error within the Ped PROMIS depression measure short form was found. One question was asked twice, and one question was missed. The repeated question asked participants if they felt lonely in the past 7 days. Given the goal of determining the role of loneliness in predicting physical and mental health outcomes the Ped PROMIS depression measure was modified with these two repeat questions removed, creating a more conservative score (in terms of overlap with loneliness) using 6 questions. The raw scores from this modified 6 question version were uploaded into the HealthMeasures™ website for analysis which returns T-scores ensuring that the population mean was 50 and higher scores indicated greater depressed mood.28 No other data errors were found.

Peer loneliness

Three different measures were used to capture the three types of peer loneliness. The UCLA 4-item loneliness scale is a global scale of nonspecific type of loneliness and was used to compare ACP ratings with nonclinical populations due to measurement invariance found with the UCLA-4 scale.29,30 The internal consistency in this study was α = 0.95.

The 16-item Peer Network and Dyadic Loneliness Scale (rated on a 4-point scale) consists of two subscales; one measures intimate peer loneliness (with a reported internal consistency α = .84) and the other measures relational peer loneliness (with a reported internal consistency α = 88).31 Higher scores on these subscales indicate higher levels of the associated type of loneliness. The internal consistency in this study was α = 0.96 for intimate peer loneliness and α = 0.94 for relational peer loneliness.

To capture loneliness at school, the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) – Sense of Belonging at School Scale was used to measure collective loneliness.8 This scale consists of 6 items each rated on a 4-point scale designed to capture an adolescent’s sense of belonging at their school. Three questions are reversed scored and then all scores are tallied and divided by the number of questions so that scores range from 1 to 4 with higher scores indicating more school loneliness. Twenge and colleagues32 report that a score of 2.22 or higher indicates a high degree of school loneliness. Internal consistency for this study was α = 0.94.

Analysis plan

Descriptive statistics were used to report participant demographic characteristics as well as self-reported loneliness, pain characteristics, and outcome measures. To answer research objective 1, which form of loneliness do ACP experience the most, mean z-scores were calculated (as intimate, relational, and collective loneliness measures use different scales) and a Friedman’s test was conducted. MANCOVA was run to determine if there was a demographic group (e.g., race, SES, sex, gender) who experienced a type of loneliness more than others. To answer research objective 2, a series of hierarchical multiple regression (HRM) were conducted based on previous research on loneliness and chronic pain in adolescents and study correlational analysis (Pearson’s r). To ensure adequate sample size for the HRM analysis with up to 10 predictors, a power analysis with α = .05, β = .20 and medium effect size was conducted using G* Power 3 suggesting a sample size of 118. Additionally, based on recommendations of Tabchnick and Fidell33 for regression using 104 + m (number of predictors = 10), a sample size of 124 would be needed.

Results

One hundred and twenty-eight participants were successfully recruited, met vetting and verification procedures, and completed the online survey (see Figure 1).

Self-identified gender resulted in very similar split as sex assigned at birth with 2 (1.6%) participants preferring not to answer, resulting in 59 girls (46.1%) and 67 boys (52.3%). Descriptive and correlations results

Participants had a mean pain intensity rating of 3.3 out of 10 (SD = 1.5) with almost all reporting pain in the past 7 days (N = 126; 98.4%) and just under half experienced pain at least one day per week (N = 58; 45.3%). Pain locations varied, with headache being the most common (33.6%) followed by abdomen (18.8%), pelvis (16.4%), lower lib (12.5%), upper limb (3.1%), back (6.3%) and neck (5.5%), only 3.9% indicated that they experienced pain in multiple sites. In terms of pain-related interference, the mean score on the PROMIS Pain Interference was 53.7 (SD 6.8), with an average of 3.9 (SD 1.6) days missed at school due to pain in the past month. Pain intensity and pain interference were significantly correlated, r =.750; p <.001). Almost all (N = 118; 92.2%) were receiving or had received pain care at a chronic pain clinic, and 17 participants (13.3%) had a family member with chronic pain (sibling and/or parent).

Scores on the loneliness measures are reported in Table 2. In addition to mean scores, frequency analysis was conducted on school loneliness, finding that 26.5% of the sample (n = 34) scored above the high degree of school loneliness cutoff of 2.2. Bivariate correlations between loneliness scales were all statistically significant and highly correlated with each other. See Table 3 for bivariate correlations. T-tests with 95% confidence intervals revealed that there were no statistically significant differences between sex and loneliness scores across all the measures, meaning that there were no differences in loneliness between males or females. There were also no significant correlations between sex and loneliness measures. However, race, as well as perceived SES of one’s family, were significantly correlated across all types of loneliness (see Table 3) with those who identified as Black reflecting significant and more worrisome correlations with pain (pain interference r =.325, p value =.001); loneliness (e.g., collective r =.435, p value =.001) and SES (r = −.512, p value =.001).

Friedman’s test and MANCOVA

Z-scores were calculated for the three measures representing the three types of loneliness (dyadic, relational, collective), as well as for the global measure (UCLA loneliness scale). Friedman’s test was conducted to identify which type of loneliness participants experience the most, finding no significant differences in the z-scores among the four loneliness measurements (X2 (3) = 1.24; p = .742). MANCOVA was conducted (see Table 4) to determine if loneliness type was associated with the demographic characteristics. Results indicated that participants who identified as Black were significantly lonelier compared to the other three racial groups (White, Indigenous, and Other) after controlling for perceived SES of one’s family (low, medium, high income) and that their mean score for school loneliness was above the 2.22 cutoff associated with a high degree of school loneliness.

Hierarchical multiple regression results

As participants experienced all three types of peer loneliness equally (no-statistical difference between measures) and the correlation between the three types of peer loneliness (intimate, relational, collective) and the global measure were high, the UCLA scores were used as the measure of loneliness across all HMR analyses as the UCLA had slightly larger bivariate correlations with all outcomes of interest. The bivariate correlation between pain intensity and pain interference (r = .075; p value = .01) prevented both from being included into the models due to issues with multicollinearity, so pain interference was used in HRM models as it had stronger correlations with loneliness compared to pain intensity. Demographic factors were entered in step 1, pain-related interference in step 2, loneliness in step 3 and the interaction of sex times loneliness was entered in step 4 (see Table 5). Loneliness contributed significantly to predicting self-esteem, anxiety, and depressed mood. However, unlike the anxiety and depressed mood models, pain-related interference was no longer significant in the presence of loneliness for predicting self-esteem. Demographic variables were not significant in predicting these three outcomes. Examining the role of loneliness in predicting the physical outcome, loneliness did not predict school absenteeism. To ensure that the UCLA was the correct measure, an HMR was run with the school belonging/loneliness measure scores instead of the UCLA scores, but the main outcomes were unchanged; thus, for consistency the HMR using the UCLA scores is reported here. Lower SES and greater pain-related interference predicted school absenteeism (see Table 5). Across all four regressions, there were no significant sex and loneliness interactions in predicting the outcomes.

Discussion

This study is the first to use validated measures capturing three types of peer loneliness (intimate, relational, collective) to examine the effect of peer loneliness on pain-related outcomes of ACP, and to determine any demographic differences in how they experience loneliness. ACP experienced all three types of peer loneliness equally. This is understandable as ACP are lonelier than peers even when they have a reciprocal best friend, suggesting that ACP continue to feel different from peers within their intimate relationships as some close friends have been found to not understand pain.11,13,14 Additionally, peers and classmates have been reported to question the validity of their pain, contributing to ACP experiencing relational and/or collective peer loneliness despite having an intimate platonic friendship.14,34

There were no age or sex differences in the types of loneliness experienced and no interactions between sex and loneliness on outcomes, demonstrating that the effects of loneliness on pain-related outcomes are equally felt by male and female ACP. Nevertheless, gender differences were not explored as those who were assigned male and female at birth were essentially the same as their self-identified gender despite the attempt to capture differing gender identities, thus future research to explore how loneliness affects ACP of diverse genders is warranted. However, there were other demographic differences.

Lower SES correlated with higher peer loneliness scores keeping with research invoving wider populations of adolescents.35 Like other adolescents, ACP from lower SES may not have the same opportunities for social connection with peers in contexts that require additional costs (e.g., organized sports, music). However, lonely ACP from lower SES may be more disadvantaged as they miss school more than peers contributing to decreases in school-related peer social opportunities.9,11,15 Strategies that help ACP attend school regularly and engage in social activities at school, especially for those who are of lower SES, may help to decrease feelings of peer loneliness.

Unexpectantly, adolescents who identified as Black were significantly lonelier compared to other racial identifications even after controlling for SES across all three types of peer loneliness. It is unclear what drove these findings as although research has identified that adolescents who are of an ethnic or racial minority experience more school loneliness, our results only found this difference for those who identify as Black and not those who identified as members of other racial minorities.36 Nevertheless, in terms of collective loneliness, it is concerning that their score of 2.52 was above what is considered a high degree of school loneliness (cutoff score of 2.22), indicating a potential for these ACP to have worse academically linked long-term outcomes.32 Moreover, ACP who identified as Black also experienced more intimate and relational loneliness suggesting that they have more loneliness within their best friendships and circle of friends than other racial minorities as well as ACP who identified as White.

Recent evidence from the United States suggests that Black adults are more socially isolated (e.g., time spent in social connections) than others, citing poorer social contexts of neighborhoods, negative interactions with law enforcement, and work conditions such as irregular work hours.37 Although this same study found that adolescents in the United States are more socially isolated than other age groups, an interaction between adolescent age group and those who identify as Black was not reported, nor did the authors report on loneliness.37 Furthermore, despite adults who identify as Black being lonelier, the relationship between loneliness and identifying as Black was not significant during adolescence apart from loneliness for racial minorities in the school setting.36,38,39 Perhaps an intersectionality framework is needed to understand the structural and power relationships that contribute to these findings beyond those of school loneliness as some strategies to decrease peer loneliness amongst ACP who identify as Black may be unique.40

The amount of variance that loneliness explained above and beyond demographics and pain interference in predicting social well-being and mental health outcomes for ACP cannot be ignored and may be key factor in ACP health. Although this was a cross-sectional study, previous research and theoretical understanding suggests that loneliness is a risk factor for poorer social well-being and health.1,4–7,12 Loneliness influenced depressed mood more than pain-related interference, explaining 35.8% of the variance, regardless of sex. Loneliness was the only significant predictor of self-esteem, explaining 40.9% of the variance. Adolescent loneliness is linked to lower self-esteem in young adult females but our findings suggest that loneliness is already negatively impacting the self-esteem of ACP, including male ACP.7 Loneliness contributed slightly more than pain interference in predicting anxiety (28.5% of the variance) suggesting that both social and physical pain-related factors may heighten anxious feelings. Chronic pain during adolescence increases the prevalence of depression in adulthood by 11.4% and adulthood anxiety by 8.7%, our findings suggest that loneliness may also contribute to these conditions.41 Research to determine if interventions targeting loneliness improve these conditions for those with chronic pain during adolescence as well as later in life is warranted.

This study focused on school absenteeism, a socially related physical outcome. Loneliness did not significantly predict school absenteeism. Although the correlation between collective loneliness and school absenteeism was weak (r = .225; p < .05) the correlation between loneliness and pain-related interference was moderate (r = .484; p < .001) suggesting an association with some measures of worse physical health. Lonely adolescents report more physical health conditions than nonlonely peers and perceive their health as worse.5,6 Perhaps the impact of loneliness on physical health is associated with pain onset or a factor in the chronification of pain as a recent study reported that ACP self-identified loneliness as a trigger for pain.42 However, loneliness has not been considered in pediatric chronic pain theories. For example, loneliness could be a mechanism in the resilience theory of pediatric chronic pain contributing to pain chronification.43 Other physical outcomes (e.g., sleep interference) and longitudinal studies (e.g., presence of loneliness before pain onset) are needed to investigate the temporal relationship of loneliness and physical characteristics of pain.

In addition to the immediate impact of loneliness on ACP health, loneliness has far-reaching effects on social well-being. Sense of school belonging was used to assess collective loneliness given the time adolescents spend at school and the challenges ACP often experience at school. ACP miss more school than peers, experience a decrease in grades, frequently experience peer victimization, and many teachers hold inaccurate beliefs about chronic pain, all of which may negatively contribute to school loneliness.9,11,15,44–46 One in five students reported feeling like an outsider at school and one in six reported feeling lonely at school, suggesting that physical conditions (e.g., chronic pain) alone do not account for higher levels of school loneliness.8 In our study 26.5% of the participants (n = 34) scored above the threshold for a high degree of school loneliness. This is concerning as individuals with a higher school loneliness report not foreseeing attending university and experience lower academic (e.g., reading) performance.8 Moreover, young adults with chronic pain do not attend university with the same frequency as nonpain peers.47,48 Understanding how, or if, collective loneliness adds to chronic pain as a barrier for postsecondary education is essential.

Despite the significant findings of this study, it has limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the data foregoes the ability to establish causal relationships. Loneliness may be an outcome or co-occur with outcomes. However, this study focused on loneliness as a potential driver of outcomes considering previous research and the Evolutionary Loneliness Theory. Another limitation is that comparators for loneliness levels are difficult as diverse measures have been used across adolescent loneliness studies. The 4-item UCLA measure provides some insights into our sample as invariant studies identify a mean loneliness of 9.22 (SD 2.61) for those 18 to 25 years of age.30 A population mean is not yet established for adolescents, but adolescence is usually a time of greater loneliness, suggesting our sample had a lower mean UCLA score (7.31) but a wider SD (3.96). Nevertheless, 25% of the sample scored above 11/16 and 16.5% scored above 13/16, indicating a proportion of ACP may have problematic levels of loneliness.30

Although there was an error in capturing the data requiring a modification to the pediatric PROMIS depression measure, this was an opportunity to create a more conservative depression score by removing the direct loneliness question. Additionally, although most of the adolescents either had, or were, receiving pain care at a pediatric pain clinic, recruitment was open to adolescents in the community who were experiencing chronic pain and thus, specifics about treatment were not captured (e.g., types of psychological treatment, physical treatment, group sessions). Therefore, it is unclear if existing pain management treatments improve loneliness. However, the correlation between loneliness and pain interference was moderate (e.g., intimate loneliness r = .484, p value =.01; global loneliness r = .578, p value = .01;) and these two variables had independent influences on outcomes, suggesting that despite their positive correlation, an ACP could have less pain interference and still be lonely and vice versa, indicating a need for specific strategies to target their loneliness and warrants further research.

Another limitation is that this study was conducted as the COVID-19 pandemic was resolving. Although physical restrictions, including school attendance, were lifted, it is possible that levels of loneliness were compounded by increases in loneliness associated with the pandemic among this age group which may or may not have resolved. Nevertheless, research prepandemic indicated that ACP experience higher rates of loneliness compared to peers and that ACP loneliness is persistent, suggesting that increases in loneliness among ACP require attention.11,49 Finally, despite employing robust methods to detect fraudulent participants, it is possible that a few were included. However, the midway analysis on major outcomes (e.g., Friedman’s test on loneliness measures, MANCOVA) prestarting direct recruitment from pain clinics revealed no changes (e.g., differences in the type of loneliness ACP experience or adolescents who identify as Black remain lonelier). Strengths were the use of validated measures to capture three forms of peer loneliness highlighting that pain is associated with all types of peer loneliness requiring strategies that improve all three types of loneliness. This study recruited more males (n = 67) than females (n = 60) allowing for subgroup analysis, finding males and females are equally at risk for the effects of loneliness on pain-related outcomes.

Clinically, loneliness amongst ACP requires assessment and attention. Both a general measure of loneliness (e.g., UCLA 4-item) along with the 6-item Sense of School Belonging Scale (as those who score 2.22 or higher have a greater risk of negative sequela of school loneliness) may effectively assess loneliness in ACP. In future research, specific types of loneliness (dyadic, relational, collective) measures are required if the focus is on loneliness (e.g., longitudinal studies of loneliness on pain, interventions effectiveness on a specific type of loneliness). However, if researchers are capturing loneliness as one variable among many, then a global measure (i.e., UCLA 4-item) will provide a robust assessment.

In addition to measuring loneliness, strategies to decrease loneliness are needed. A scoping review of psychosocial interventions to improve social outcomes amongst children and adolescents with chronic physical conditions found that social skill training may improve peer loneliness.50 Additionally, a meta analysis of interventions to decrease loneliness in young people under 25 years of age (with or without a health condition) found that social and emotional training interventions are effective at decreasing loneliness but that these should be targeted to the type of loneliness (transient versus persistent) and reasons behind one’s loneliness.51 Although these findings provide guidance on interventions to decrease loneliness, specific interventions need to be designed and evaluated that target the challenges faced by ACP.

In conclusion, loneliness, like pain, is a universal human experience and similar to chronic pain, persistent loneliness results in negative effects on health outcomes. Although there are multiple studies highlighting the relationships between psychological conditions and chronic pain, this study is one of the first to focus on a social condition – loneliness – and its meaningful and noteworthy impacts on pain-related outcomes for ACP. Interventions that target loneliness is a new avenue in chronic pain treatment for adolescents but needs to be evaluated. Although there is much to understand about loneliness in the context of chronic pain, helping adolescents find ways to decrease loneliness may prove powerful in improving their lives.
