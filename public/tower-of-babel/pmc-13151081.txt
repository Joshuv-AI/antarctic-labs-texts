A scoping review of guides and advice for scholarly citing in health research
Authors: Lasse Østengaard, Tine Tjørnhøj-Thomsen, Asger S. Paludan-Müller, Mia Elkjær, Tove Faber Frandsen, Asbjørn Hróbjartsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo identify, summarise and analyse documents with guides and advice for scholarly citing in health research.MethodsIn a scoping review, we included documents with guides and advice for scholarly citing in health research. We searched in Embase (Ovid), Google Scholar, 20 textbooks, 30 journal websites, and conducted supplementary searches. We categorised original documents as guides if their primary focus was on scholarly citing and if they contained a list of advice. We summarised the guides and pieces of advice, analysed the pieces of advice, and listed illustrative quotes.ResultsWe screened 9.746 documents for eligibility and included 118. The documents were published in 1934–2025, and the guides and advice were primarily identified in journal articles (63 out of 118 documents, 53%) and on websites (42 out of 118 documents, 36%). We identified 12 guides and 75 unique pieces of advice (e.g., “read the papers you cite”). The 12 guides were developed with a simple pragmatic approach, i.e., no systematic literature review of previous studies, and only one guide included a survey as a supporting study. The various pieces of advice were related to seven categories that researchers could reflect on while citing: 1) quality, 2) context, 3) when to cite, 4) integrity, 5) originality, 6) transparency, and 7) availability.ConclusionWe identified 12 guides and 75 unique pieces of advice for scholarly citing in health research. Our review may serve as a resource for authors of health research publications and as part of the development process for a comprehensive guideline for adequate citation practice in health research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41073-026-00195-1.

Background

Authors of a health research publication will almost always cite previous empirical or theoretical papers to provide context. In the ideal scenario, authors of a new publication will carefully select which papers to cite based on the perceived quality, to provide an overview of the most relevant previous research. When previous papers have been cited adequately, it will be clearer to the reader of the new publication whether its research question is relevant, and whether its chosen methodology is appropriate. Adequate citation practice will also enable the reader to make a balanced interpretation of the importance of the new research results, in light of the previous evidence.

However, citations are not always carefully selected, and sometimes, it can be questioned whether the cited papers were adequately read due to the inaccurate representation of information [1, 2]. A recent news paper in Nature has even described a black market for citations where researchers can inflate the number of citations they receive by buying citations for their own research papers [3]. Other common problems are excessive self-citation [4], selective citation of papers that support a specific point of view or “spin” [5–7], or predominant citation of studies with positive findings [8].

Authors of a research publication can be influenced by several factors when deciding which paper to cite, e.g., the perceived quality, and accessibility of the paper [9]. An interview study highlighted that the interviewees wanted to cite adequately, but were predominantly driven by the goal of getting their papers published [10]. The interviewees also recalled that they initially had received little advice on how to work with citations [10].

These problems with citations call for more attention to adequate citation practices, including accessible guidance. A few easily identified editorials provide short lists of advice on scholarly citing with little background, rationale, or evidence for the provided advice [11–13]. However, we suspected that more advice existed in a scattered and fragmented format.

We considered it relevant to identify and summarise existing guides and advice for scholarly citing, in part as a comprehensive resource for authors of health research papers, and in part as part of the preparation for developing a structured, consensus-based guideline for adequate citation practice in health research. Therefore, we decided to conduct a scoping review of documents with guides and advice for scholarly citing in health research.

Aim

Our aim was to identify, summarise and analyse documents containing guides and advice for scholarly citing in health research publications.

Method

This scoping review was conducted following the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [14], and reported according to the PRISMA-ScR reporting guideline [15]. The protocol for this review was registered at the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/q8gh2). We used Covidence [16] for the screening procedure, and Nvivo 14 [17] to analyse the relevant documents.

Terminology

We use the term “documents” for relevant publications (e.g., published on websites, journal publications, and book chapters).

We use the term “guides” for original documents (i.e., not reviews) if their primary focus was on citing and if they contained a list of advice for citing.

We use the term “advice” as suggestions authors could consider when citing previous research (for example, authors are encouraged to search for new literature). We have also included so-called “rules” and “principles” under the term “advice” since they are often context-specific (for example, some journals can have a rule that authors are not allowed to cite pre-print publications, and other journals do not).

Eligibility criteria

We included documents with advice (e.g., what should be cited, and when self-citation is acceptable) for scholarly citing in health research regardless of the publication year and language. We also included advice where scholarly citing was not the main focus; for example, a journal guide for authors where a single item relates to citing papers. The documents were included if the advice was aimed at health research or applicable to all research fields (incl. health research). Also, we included documents aimed at specific publication types (for example, a reporting guideline for systematic reviews) if they included advice for scholarly citing in health research publications.

We excluded documents that only addressed the format of citations (for example, referencing styles as American Psychological Association (APA), Chicago, and Vancouver). We also excluded documents that only addressed why researchers should cite (for example, to avoid plagiarism).

Search strategy

We searched for relevant documents in Embase (Ovid) published from database inception to August 8, 2025. We searched the full-text database Google Scholar, retrieving the first 200 references from six searches, and the websites for the Equator Network and the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE).

Also, a representative sample of 20 textbooks on writing in health research and clinical epidemiology was reviewed. We used Google to identify our sample of 20 textbooks about scientific writing in health research and clinical epidemiology. We searched for the following phrases respectively; ‘scientific writing health research’ and ‘clinical epidemiology’.

We then selected the category “books” in Google. The first 10 relevant textbooks about the two topics were selected for review. We excluded books with a specific aim unrelated to our focus, such as ‘Use of medical databases in clinical epidemiology’ [18].

Also, the websites of 30 journals were reviewed. The 30 journal websites were chosen based on the 2019 Journal Citation Reports from Clarivate Analytics where we selected the 30 journals with the highest journal impact factor within health research.

Furthermore, reference lists of all the included documents were read and assessed for eligibility. The final step in our search strategy was looking through our personal files for relevant documents. A detailed description of the search is displayed in Supplementary Material 1.

Screening procedure

One author (LØ) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified documents for obvious exclusions. In an additional round, one author (LØ) screened the full-text version of the remaining documents for eligibility. Another author (either ASPM or ME) confirmed the relevance of the included documents.

Data extraction

Two authors (LØ and ASPM) pre-tested the data charting form on five papers and five journal websites. One author (LØ) extracted data into a standardised data charting form, and a second author (ME) verified that no relevant information was missed in the included documents (except for data regarding the maximal number of references in different publication types extracted from journal websites, i.e., this data was not verified by a second author). Disagreements were resolved by discussion.

Analysis

Our analysis was inspired by Levac and colleagues [19]. The included documents were uploaded to NVivo 14, where the relevant text was highlighted. The guides were summarised and displayed in the text and a table. We used an inductive approach for our analysis (i.e., there were no predefined elements) to identify different characteristics of advice for scholarly citing in health research [20]. The relevant text was divided into suitable categories that researchers could reflect on while citing. Next, we summarised the advice in each category. If there were differences in the advice (for example, if some journals recommended the use of preprints and other journals did not), we made sure that our summary included all the recommendations in some form.

Results

We identified 9.746 documents for screening (Fig. 1). After the screening procedure, we included 118 documents for analysis (Table 1). The advice and guides were primarily identified in journal articles (63 out of 118 documents, 53%) and websites (42 out of 118 documents, 36%). The documents were published between 1934 to 2025. Guides for citing previous research

We identified 12 guidance documents whose primary focus was on citing and which contained a list of advice for scholarly citing [11–13, 22–30]. The characteristics of the guides are reported in Table 2. The majority (nine out of twelve) of the guides were written by one or two authors. None of the authors described conducting a systematic literature search (or other forms of evidence synthesis) before developing their guides, and none described a consensus-based approach (for example, by having multiple surveys/voting rounds or meetings) for developing their guides. One guide, by Campion et al. [22], developed their advice based on the results from a survey, two guides reported that the advice was developed based on the author's experiences, and nine guides did not report how the advice was developed. Campion 1997 [22]

The guide included 17 pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the quality, context, originality, transparency and availability of references. For example, the guide provided advice on what they consider to be preferred and less preferred documents, the number of references (i.e., avoid using an excessive number) and when self-referencing is considered appropriate. The guide also recommended that references and reference lists are double-checked.

Agarwal 2023 [23]

The guide included a total of 14 pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the quality, originality, transparency and availability of references. For example, the guide recommended that authors should not automatically exclude papers of interest if they were written in a language other than English and that authors try to obtain relevant papers that are unavailable (e.g., by requesting them through their library).

Brooks-Brunn 1998 [24]

The guide included a flow diagram with 20 questions in relation to when to cite. For example, the guide advises researchers to use a reference if a statement is not common knowledge and when the statement is a theory or model suggested by another researcher.

Harzing 2002 [25]

The guide included 12 pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, as well as the quality, originality, and transparency of references. For example, the guide suggested that authors use reliable sources, check the original papers (i.e., do not copy someone else’s references), avoid citing out-of-date references, and that authors actively search for counter-evidence (for example, papers that disagrees with the authors point-of-view).

Brender 2009 [11]

The guide included 12 pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the originality and transparency of references. For example, the guide recommended that authors make it clear whether a referenced statement or argument is a conclusion of a study, an observation or just an argument in a discussion, and that authors should not omit references just because they address the same topic.

Penders 2018 [12]

The guide included ten pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the quality and context of references. For example, the guide advised that authors should read papers before citing them and highlighted that citation cultures differ across boundaries (so researchers should accept that). The guide also included 10 sub-rules for prioritising references.

Friedman 2023 [26]

The guide included 12 pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the originality and transparency of references. For example, the guide recommended that authors never use second-hand references from other papers (i.e., always check the original source) and that authors review cited passages in the original sources to ensure exact quotes and accurate paraphrasing.

Cals 2013 [27]

The guide included a checklist with six pieces of advice about the references' originality, quality and availability. For example, the guide provided advice on what to consider when choosing a reference among several possibilities, and that the authors meticulously check the final reference list for errors.

Murphy 2011 [13]

The guide included ten pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, as well as the quality and originality of the references. For example, the guide recommended that authors read and comprehend the literature before citing it and that authors cite literature that agrees and disagrees with their point of view.

West 2017 [28]

The guide included seven pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the quality, originality and availability of references. For example, the guide advised that authors resist the propensity to prefer citations from themself and colleagues and avoid citing inaccessible sources wherever possible.

Bavdekar 2016 [29]

The guide included 12 pieces of advice about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the quality, context, originality, transparency and availability of references. For example, the guide advised authors to place citations after the relevant fact when multiple facts are reported in a sentence.

Jenicek 2014 [30]

The guide listed 20 pieces of ‘advice’ about the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and the quality of references. The guide used irony when presenting the advice i.e., authors are recommended to do the opposite of the ‘advice’ in the guide. For example, one ‘advice’ in the guide stated, “Do not quote contradictory findings from other studies. It only complicates your discussion and conclusions section(s) of your article.” [30], which means that contradictory findings should be cited.

Advice for citing previous research

Among the 715 sentences or paragraphs (identified in the 118 documents) with advice for scholarly citing in health research, we identified seven categories of advice for researchers: 1) quality, 2) context, 3) when to cite, 4) integrity, 5) originality, 6) transparency, and 7) availability. The seven categories are listed in Table 3 with illustrative quotes. We summarised the relevant information about advice for scholarly citing and condensed the information into 75 unique pieces of advice (Table 4) [22, 28, 31–40]. Quality

Forty-seven documents included advice on the quality. The quality of the cited article was mentioned in relation to both the methodological quality and where the article was published. It was recommended that the authors cite studies with sound methodology and those with the highest level of evidence. Peer-reviewed articles were often mentioned as reliable sources of knowledge in contrast to articles published in predatory journals, and non-peer-reviewed articles.

Context

The context is important because the advice for citing previous research can differ depending on the context. Twenty-four documents described that authors may use different strategies when citing papers in different contexts. These strategies could, for example, be affected by the article type, the research field, and the topic of interest in the article. The choice of journal can also affect the strategies, as we noted that 28 of the 30 journals with the highest journal impact factor within health research included limitations regarding the maximal number of references for research papers (Supplementary Material 2).

When to cite

Twelve documents included advice on when to cite. It was recommended to add references when authors used other researchers’ ideas or results and to add references when something was not common knowledge. One document described that the act of citing is subjective and culturally based, so there cannot be any absolutes about when to cite [41].

Integrity

More than half of the documents (n = 70) included advice concerning the researcher's integrity when citing papers, and almost half (35 out of 75, 47%) of the unique pieces of advice were related to this category. For example, authors are advised to cite themselves and others only when justified (e.g., avoiding excessive self-citation) and to read and comprehend the articles they cite. Two recent documents suggested that authors include a statement about the integrity of the citation practice in their manuscripts [42, 43].

Originality

The importance of citing original sources (i.e., sources that present new ideas and data in contrast to secondary sources that interpret original sources) was highlighted in thirty-eight documents. This is, among other things, to ensure that the information is accurate (i.e., avoid misinformation from secondary sources), and to give credit to the authors who first described the topic or discovery.

Transparency

Twenty-eight documents included advice on the transparency of references. It was recommended that authors make it clear if a reference does not address the same context and if they cite a pre-print. Using an excessive number of references to a single point should be avoided because it can be unclear to the reader which of the references is truly important.

Availability

Twenty-two documents included the advice that the cited papers should preferably be accessible to others (e.g., open-access publications), and citing papers that are difficult to obtain (e.g., a doctoral dissertation only available in a printed version at a specific library) were not recommended. This is, among other things, to ensure that others can verify the interpretation of the results.

Discussion

We identified 12 guides for citing in health research, none of which were developed based on a structured, consensus-based approach. We found 75 pieces of advice for scholarly citing in health research, which we divided into seven categories: 1) quality, 2) context, 3) when to cite, 4) integrity, 5) originality, 6) transparency, and 7) availability.

The main strength of our scoping review is its originality and methodological approach. No previous study has identified guides and advice for scholarly citing in health research with a systematic approach. We registered our scoping review on the Open Science Framework and followed the prespecified methods, e.g., a comprehensive search strategy.

Limitations

We acknowledge that we might have missed eligible documents, for example, if they did not use terms for citation and references in their title and abstract, making them difficult to search for. Also, for pragmatic reasons, we used one author for title and abstract screening instead of two. However, we believe that we have not missed any unique information that would have changed our main findings and conclusion, as we experienced a saturation of data when we updated the search in Embase (i.e., we did not identify new categories and only identified one new piece of advice).

Other similar studies

We identified, and included, two narrative reviews that summarised different advice for citing previous research [44, 45]. Both reviews did not describe how their searches were conducted, nor did they mention any preplanned method, and they only cited seven and 16 out of the 118 documents we included.

We identified one guide from 1997 [22] where the advice was developed based on a survey, but we did not locate other guides for adequate citation practice that were reported to be developed based on any supporting studies (for example, a scoping review, interview study or a Delphi study). The absence of other guides based on a supporting study represents a notable gap in the existing literature.

We did not identify other guides and advice for scholarly citing primarily aimed at fields outside health research in our screening, but we did not actively search for this as this was not a part of our aim.

Mechanisms and perspectives

The context is important when authors cite papers because the context may influence the advice; for example, newspapers will often be considered to be a less preferred source of information, but if the purpose of the reference is to show public awareness of a topic, a newspaper article may be an appropriate source [22]. Citation practices were also described as local practices [12]. Bart Penders provided an extreme example of what counts as proper citation practice in one research area may be considered unacceptable in another [12].

Some advice differed from journal to journal. For example, some journals allowed authors to cite preprints [46, 47], while others did not [48, 49]. We also identified guidance and advice that did not vary. For example, there was a general agreement that authors should cite original sources whenever possible.

Given the current technological development within the use of artificial intelligence (AI), we believe that future advice and guidance for citing previous research will include more information about AI and how it can be used in a trustworthy manner. This is especially important since it has been highlighted that AI can generate fake references [50, 51], potentially harming the trustworthiness of research. Current developments [52, 53] already help authors to efficiently identify relevant and problematic research papers (e.g., when a software tool flags retracted or potentially problematic papers).

Implications

Citations play an essential part in scholarly communication, and we believe there is a need for a well-developed guideline for adequate citation practice in health research, adhering to a strong tradition within health research to develop guidelines, for example, reporting guidelines [54] and methodological guidance [55].

Our scoping review may serve as a resource for authors of research papers within health research, but is also part of the evidence foundation for the development of a guideline for citing papers aimed at health researchers. More specifically, the results from our scoping review will help inform a qualitative study, where we will interview researchers within health research and editors from different medical journals about their reflections and views on adequate citation practice. Furthermore, we plan to conduct a Delphi survey with interest-holders later to develop a consensus-based guideline for adequate citation practice.

Conclusion

We identified 12 guides and 75 unique pieces of advice for scholarly citing in health research. Among the advice for scholarly citing, we identified seven categories that researchers could reflect on while citing: 1) quality, 2) context, 3) when to cite, 4) integrity, 5) originality, 6) transparency, and 7) availability. The 12 guides were developed with a simple pragmatic approach, i.e., no systematic literature review of previous studies, and only one guide included a survey as a supporting study. The results from our review may serve as a resource for authors of health research publications and as part of the development process for a comprehensive guideline for adequate citation practice in health research.
