Integrating clinical and image-based parameters for prediction of early post-prostatectomy incontinence recovery: simplified nomogram approach
Authors: I-Hung Shao, Sy-Yuan Chen, Hung-Yi Chen, Ting-Wen Sheng, Ying-Hsu Chang, Chung-Yi Liu, Liang-Kang Huang, Hung-Chen Kan, Po-Hung Lin, Kai-Jie Yu, Cheng-Keng Chuang, See-Tong Pang, Chun-Te Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to develop a novel model that combines both clinical and image-based parameters to predict early recovery of urinary incontinence after robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) more easily and precisely.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively enrolled data from patients who underwent RARP performed by a single surgeon. Clinical parameters were collected through medical chart review. All patients received cystography one week after RARP to evaluate the anastomosis healing condition. All cystography images were analyzed by a single radiologist who was blinded to the clinical status of the patients. Multivariate analysis was performed to select significant predictors for early post-prostatectomy incontinence (PPI) recovery, defined as being pad-free within four weeks after surgery.ResultsA total of 293 patients were enrolled in this study. Among them, 26.7% experienced immediate dryness after surgery, while 47.6% achieved being pad-free within one month. The overall continence rate was over 90% six months after surgery. In univariate analysis, factors associated with early PPI recovery were BMI, T stage, NVB preservation, surgical margin status, downward bladder neck, and bladder neck angle on cystography. BMI, NVB preservation, and downward bladder neck remained significant in multivariate analysis (p-values = 0.041, 0.027, and 0.023, respectively). A nomogram model was established based on these three predictors.ConclusionThis is the first model to combine preoperative clinical factors, peri-surgical factors, and postoperative image-based factors to predict PPI recovery after RARP. This model can assist clinicians in taking optimal actions for PPI and also reduce patient anxiety.Keywords: Robotic assisted radical prostatectomy, RARP, Prostate cancer, Urinary incontinence, Post-prostatectomy incontinence, Nomogram

Introduction

Prostate cancer is ranked as the 2nd most common male malignancy worldwide, with over 200,000 new cases diagnosed annually, leading to approximately 30,000 deaths in the U.S each year, showing an increasing trend [1]. The prognosis of prostate cancer varies across different disease stages. In the localized regional stage, the 5-year survival rate exceeds 95%. Radical prostatectomy is a crucial treatment modality in this stage and can be performed using open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted methods. With advancements in minimally invasive surgery, robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has emerged as the primary treatment method in recent years, offering improved oncologic and functional outcomes [2, 3].

Despite advancements in robotic-assisted surgery, Post-Prostatectomy Incontinence (PPI) remains a significant and bothersome surgical sequela. According to a meta-analysis by Ficarra et al., the average rates of urinary incontinence at 12 months after RARP range from 4 to 31%, with a mean value of 16%. Incontinence is defined as the need to use pads, even if they are only for safety [4]. Furthermore, the impact of urinary incontinence on affected patients is substantial, leading to stigmatization and a significant reduction in the quality of life [5]. Additionally, the economic burden of urinary incontinence is currently estimated to be between $19 and $32 billion in the USA [6].

The mechanism of PPI is multifactorial, involving both patient-related factors and consequences related to surgery. Certain biological factors and preoperatively known parameters, such as patient’s age, history of transurethral resection of the prostate, etc., may contribute to PPI [7, 8]. In addition to clinical factors, several studies have employed various image-based tools to predict PPI, achieving acceptable outcomes [9]. Among the images used for predicting PPI, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound have been the most commonly utilized tools, validated by multiple studies [9]. We previously reported that a specific pattern of postoperative cystography could predict PPI [10]. The predictive value of these specific features in cystography for PPI has been well established by numerous other studies [11–19].

In most previous studies, parameters were identified and studied separately concerning their correlation with PPI recovery. Only a few studies have established prediction models for PPI. In this study, our aim was to develop a novel predictive model that combines image-based parameters and clinical parameters, enabling a more precise and straightforward prediction of PPI recovery.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We retrospectively collected data from patients who underwent RARP performed by a single surgeon between May 2015 and January 2019. All the cases in our study had been discussed and images were reviewed in our multi-disciplinary team. Final decision on treatment is made after discussion with the patients after shared decision making. The study received approval from the institutional review board of our hospital (Number: 201504523B0C501) and was conducted in adherence to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). Informed consent requirements were waived by the board due to the retrospective design of the study.

Clinical information, including age, BMI (body mass index), initial prostate-specific antigen (iPSA), and prostate volume, was obtained through a review of medical charts. Intra-operative factors, such as operative time and estimated blood loss, were also recorded. During surgery, neurovascular bundle (NVB) preservation was decided and performed based on the tumor location and invasion, as assessed through pre-operative imaging and intraoperative judgment by the surgeon. Patients with pre-existing urinary incontinence (including urge incontinence, stress incontinence, or mixed incontinence) before RARP were excluded from this study.

Following RARP, we routinely educated the patients to perform post-operative pelvic floor exercise rehabilitation, and urinary catheters were routinely removed one week after surgery if no anastomosis leakage was detected on cystography. However, in cases where minor anastomosis leakage was observed, the urinary catheter would be retained for an additional week. For patients with severe anastomosis leakage, the decision to remove the urinary catheter was based on the clinical judgment of the physician.

The continence status was assessed promptly after catheter removal and at 4-, 12-, and 24-weeks post-surgery, followed by 12-week intervals, all conducted by the same physician. Evaluation was based on data recorded in a 3-day voiding diary and patient reports during outpatient department visits. If the patient reported that he is confident with no urinary leakage in daily life and no pad is needed, we will record it as pad-free.

Notably, no patients were lost to follow-up during the entire evaluation period.

Cystography features

All patients were followed up for a minimum of 12 months after RARP. Postoperative cystography was conducted within one week of the RARP, preceding the removal of the urethral catheter. The analysis of urinary bladder features on cystography encompassed bladder height and width, downward bladder neck (BN) length, bladder neck angle, and the presence of anastomosis leakage. These bladder features on cystography are visually represented in Fig. 1. All cystography images underwent analysis by a single radiologist who was blinded to the clinical status of the patients.

Statistical analyses

To analyze predictors for patients with rapid recovery from PPI, defined as being pad-free within 4 weeks, and late recovery from PPI, defined as being pad-free after 4 weeks, we initially conducted univariate analysis. Subsequently, multivariate analysis was carried out within those parameters that revealed a p-value < 0.05. Univariate analysis involved independent t-tests and chi-square tests, while multivariate analysis utilized binary logistic regression. A nomogram prediction model for early recovery from PPI was constructed based on the significant predictors identified in the multivariate analysis. In the aforementioned statistical analysis, p-values less than 0.05 were considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.

Among these patients, the mean iPSA was 17.0 ng/mL. Patients classified as high risk according to the D’Amico risk classification constituted the majority (78.6%), and 66.1% of patients were at pathological T2 stage. At least one-sided NVB preservation was performed in 70% of patients (22.9% unilateral and 46.8% bilateral preservation), while bladder neck sparing was performed in 45.1% of patients.

Approximately 26.7% of patients achieved immediate continence after RARP following the removal of the Foley catheter, and nearly half (47.6%) of the patients recovered from PPI within one month after surgery. More than 90% of patients achieved pad-free status six months after surgery.

Patients were then divided into an early recovery from PPI group and a late recovery group, defined as being pad-free within or after 4 weeks, respectively. In univariate analysis, patients with early recovery from PPI had a significantly lower BMI (24.7 vs. 25.6 kg/m2, respectively, p value = 0.012) and lower T stage (52.6% vs. 36.5% pT2, respectively, p value = 0.008) compared to those with late recovery. During the surgeries, the early recovery group also had a higher NVB preservation rate and margin-negative rate (p value = 0.001 and 0.047 respectively). In post-operative cystography, the early recovery group had a significantly shorter downward bladder neck distance, and a more obtuse bladder neck angle compared to those with late recovery (0.95 vs. 1.35 cm and 118.1 vs. 106.7 degrees, respectively, both p value < 0.0001).

In the subsequent multivariate analysis, only BMI, NVB preservation during RARP, and downward bladder neck remained significantly different in the comparison of the early and late recovery groups (p values = 0.041, 0.027, and 0.023, respectively). The nomogram incorporated BMI, NVB preservation, and downward BN. Discussion

Rather than focusing on the correlation of individual parameters with PPI, we established a comprehensive model to more precisely predict early recovery from PPI. This innovative prediction model is the first to combine pre-operative clinical factors, peri-surgical factors, and post-operative image-based factors. Core parameters, namely BMI, NVB preservation, and downward BN on post-operative cystography, were selected after multivariate analysis.

BMI serves as an index for evaluating obesity and has long been considered a risk factor for female stress incontinence. Numerous studies have explored the relationship between BMI and PPI. A meta-analysis in 2018 demonstrated a significant association between obesity and urinary incontinence at 12 and 24 months post-RARP [20]. However, more recent studies have contested this, suggesting that while obesity is correlated with a longer time to recover from PPI after RARP, it is not associated with long-term PPI [21–24] In our study, BMI was significantly lower in patients with early recovery from PPI (≤ 1 month) compared to the late recovery group. However, there was no significant difference in BMI between patients with and without PPI at 3 and 6 months after RARP, supporting the association between BMI and early recovery but not long-term PPI.

NVB preservation, a clinical factor identified in our study, was associated with early recovery from PPI. While NVB preservation during RARP is primarily intended to preserve erectile function, previous studies have also suggested its potential benefit in the recovery of urinary continence [25]. Male continence relies on two main sphincter units: the proximal sphincter unit, composed of involuntary non-striated muscle fibers in the bladder neck, prostate, and prostatic urethra, and the voluntary external or striated sphincter, formed by fibers of the deep transverse muscle of the perineum surrounding the membranous urethra. Additional structures, such as the internal obturator muscle and the levator ani muscle, contribute to supporting the external sphincter [26]. After RARP, the prostate gland is removed, and continence depends on the integrity of the remaining structures. The preservation of the NVB during surgery helps maintain the integrity of pelvic structures, including the peri-prostatic suspensory ligament, facilitating the recovery of PPI. Michl et al. also suggested that the observed beneficial effect on continence is attributed to meticulous apical dissection during the nerve-sparing technique [27].

In addition to clinical factors, we incorporated image-based parameters into our prediction model. Various imaging tools, including MRI, ultrasound, and cystography, have been utilized to predict PPI [E, 26350365]. MRI, a standard pre-operative evaluation for prostate cancer staging, has been explored as a predictive image for PPI. Studies have confirmed a correlation between increasing membranous urethral length (MUL) and the preservation of urinary continence after prostatectomy, supporting MUL as a predictor for recovery of urinary continence [28–43]. However, the disadvantage of using pre-operative MRI images as predictors for PPI lies in the delicate measurement of parameters, potentially requiring specialists. Additionally, pre-operative images may not fully represent the anatomical condition post-radical surgery, and post-operative MRI incurs additional medical costs and burdens. To overcome the bias, Ragusa et al. had reported a study using intracorporeal measurement to investigate the impact of surgical urethral length preservation on early incontinence recovery [44]. This method can overcome the disagreement between pre- and post- operative urethral length; however, it is not easy to be precisely and uniformly applied.

Ultrasound is another commonly used imaging tool to predict PPI. Studies have shown that the continent group had a smaller urethral angle, and the incontinent group exhibited significantly reduced anterior displacement during contraction compared to continent men [45, 46]. However, the application of ultrasound to predict PPI has limitations. Ultrasound relies heavily on the operator’s ability and experience, leading to varying accuracy between different operators. Moreover, all ultrasound studies to date have been in the postoperative setting, limiting comparisons with patients’ baseline conditions.

Instead of relying on MRI and ultrasound, we chose cystography as our preferred imaging tool for prediction. Cystography is a cost-effective and convenient examination with minimal adverse effects, suitable for patients with suspected anastomosis leakage after radical prostatectomy, simultaneously revealing the postoperative condition of the bladder neck. In a previous report, we identified a specific cystography pattern that combines downward bladder neck, bladder neck angle, and anastomosis leakage to predict PPI [10]. Subsequent studies have validated the predictive value of these three specific cystography features for PPI: a more downward BN level [11, 15, 17, 19, 47, 48], a sharp BN angle [13], and anastomosis leakage [49] are associated with a delayed and lower probability of recovery from PPI. Compared to preoperative imaging, postoperative imaging is theoretically more accurate in predicting postoperative bladder and sphincter function.

In more recent studies, models have been employed to predict PPI. Tutolo et al. established a model based on exclusive clinical factors, including risk for biochemical recurrence, adjuvant radiotherapy, quality of life questionnaire, urinary incontinence questionnaire, and age, to predict PPI [50]. On the other hand, Zhang developed a preoperative model with only image-based parameters collected by MRI images in the form of a nomogram to predict the risk of PPI after laparoscopic radical prostatectomy at 3 months [51]. Kitamura et al. established a unit-weighted regression model using a scoring system to predict PPI recovery after RARP. Although this model combined both clinical parameters and image-based parameters, it still lacked postoperative image factors that could more accurately represent the status after surgery.

Our prediction model is the first to combine preoperative clinical factors, peri-surgical factors, and postoperative image-based factors to predict PPI. There is a clinical unmet need to precisely predict patients at higher risk of developing PPI with late recovery. Early prediction can assist clinicians in selecting patients for optimal upfront management strategies for PPI or early salvage intervention. The modified treatment strategy can minimize the impact of PPI on the quality of life and decrease patient anxiety.

Despite combining parameters from preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative stages, there are some limitations in this study. First, the population consists of patients undergoing RARP by a single surgeon, and the results of the study need further validation in patients undergoing surgery by other surgeons. Second, NVB preservation is a procedure with diverse technical methods, and the degree of preservation may vary among different surgeons or even among patients treated by the same surgeon. Quantitative evaluation methods for NVB preservation may be needed for better definition. In addition, multiple surgical techniques were invented to improve early continence after RAPP, and future work to validate the prediction model in different surgical techniques is important for more widely clinical application [52, 53]. Finally, to complete the prediction model, further and more detailed studies with great number of patients is required and more reliable method to exam the cystography is needed to overcome the bias which is represented by a single radiologist.

Conclusions

This pioneering model combines pre-operative clinical factors, peri-surgical factors, and post-operative image-based factors to predict PPI recovery after RARP. Utilizing BMI, NVB preservation, and downward BN in cystography, we can accurately predict the probability of early recovery for patients. This model serves as a valuable tool for clinicians, enabling them to take optimal actions for managing PPI and ultimately reducing patient anxiety.
