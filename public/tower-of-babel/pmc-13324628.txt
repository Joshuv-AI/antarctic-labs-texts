Impact of 3D model quality on user experience in mobile augmented reality applications
Authors: Rafał Kaźmierczak, Paweł Dąbrowski, Robert Skowroński, Romuald Obuchovski, Jūratė Sužiedelytė-Visockienė, Paweł Burdziakowski, Jakub Szulwic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper explores the impact of 3D model quality on user perception in augmented reality (AR), using the visualization of Gediminas’ Tower in Vilnius as a case study. The research evaluates the relationship between polygon count, texture resolution, and user experience, focusing on visual realism, performance, and user satisfaction. Six variants of a 3D model were created, differing in mesh complexity and texture quality, and integrated into a mobile AR application. It should be noted that only a single diffuse texture map (albedo) was used, without normal maps or ambient occlusion. Technical performance (FPS, CPU load) and subjective feedback from 74 participants were analyzed using statistical methods such as ANOVA (Analysis of Variance), regression, and cluster analysis. The results show that polygon count significantly affects performance and user experience, while texture resolution has a marginal impact. Users preferred models optimized for mobile performance, suggesting the need for scalable model quality options in AR applications. The study provides actionable recommendations for balancing visual fidelity and system efficiency in heritage-focused AR design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50091-3.Keywords: Augmented reality, Gediminas’ tower, 3D model optimization, User experience, Polygon count, Cultural heritage visualization, Mobile AR application, Texture resolution, Performance analysisSubject terms: Sustainability, Environmental social sciences, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Modern visualization technologies, including augmented reality (AR), are gaining increasing importance in the field of cultural heritage presentation. Their growing popularity stems from the ability to integrate virtual elements with the real environment, enriching user experiences while opening new perspectives in education, tourism, and cultural heritage preservation. The Gediminas Tower, one of the most important historical symbols of Vilnius, serves as an excellent subject for research on AR applications. As an icon of Lithuania’s national heritage, the tower attracts not only tourists but also researchers seeking new ways to promote and preserve it.

The introduction of AR technology for presenting historical objects helps overcome the limitations of traditional visualization methods, such as physical models or static 3D representations. However, the quality of models used in AR is crucial for user perception. The polygon count and texture detail determine both visual realism and the technological performance of the application. Selecting the optimal model quality is therefore a significant challenge, especially given the hardware limitations of mobile devices, which are most used for AR applications. Despite the growing interest in AR technologies, few studies analyze the impact of model quality on visual perception and overall user experience, indicating a research gap that needs to be addressed.

While previous studies in mobile AR have addressed storytelling and visitor engagement in cultural heritage contexts1, and others have evaluated overall user experience (UX) or technical performance2, there remains a gap in systematic experimental comparison of 3D geometry (polygon count) and texture resolution trade-offs on mobile AR performance and perception. Recent surveys highlight pervasive design patterns and technical challenges in mobile AR heritage applications3 yet few works integrate both perceptual realism and rendering efficiency in controlled empirical studies. Additionally, novel techniques such as efficient NeRF rendering adapted for mobile platforms4 suggest emerging directions for model representation, yet their UX impact remains unexplored. More recently, research in architectural and design AR has begun to investigate appearance manipulation and geometry–material interactions using AI-based pipelines5, while advances such as ATM-NeRF demonstrate how geometric regularization can accelerate mobile rendering without compromising fidelity6. These studies confirm that balancing geometric complexity, material representation, and computational constraints remains an open challenge in mobile AR. Our study contributes novelty by experimentally linking geometric and textural fidelity to both user-perceived realism and system performance in a real-world heritage AR scenario, using a rigorous mixed-methods approach combining ANOVA, clustering, and regression analysis. To our knowledge, few studies have brought these dimensions together in a mobile AR context for cultural heritage.

The aim of this study is to examine the influence of 3D model quality on user perception in mobile AR applications designed for cultural heritage. Specifically, the research focuses on analyzing differences in visual perception, immersion, and overall user satisfaction depending on the polygon count and texture resolution of the 3D models. To address this, we prepared six versions of the tower model, varying in visual quality across two dimensions: polygon count (low, medium, high) and texture resolution (low, high). This setup allows for evaluating the impact of both model geometry and texture quality on user perception and AR application performance. These models will be integrated into an AR application and presented to users to collect data on their reception and experiences.

The scope of the research includes the design, implementation, and testing of six Gediminas Tower model variants, followed by user studies evaluating the perceptual and performance-related responses to each variant. The analysis of collected data will help identify user preferences regarding visualization quality and determine the most acceptable trade-offs between quality and performance in the context of presenting cultural heritage models in augmented reality.

Literature review

AR involves overlaying digital content onto the real world, often through devices like smartphones or AR glasses. While AR can include various types of digital content, 3D models are commonly used because they provide a realistic and interactive experience.

AR is a technology that integrates virtual elements into the user’s real environment in real time, creating an interactive visual experience. The definition of AR, proposed by7, includes three key features: the combination of reality and virtual elements, real-time interactivity, and the ability to accurately map 3D space. Since then, AR has evolved, finding applications in fields such as education, industry, marketing, and cultural heritage preservation8. The development of mobile devices and the increasing availability of AR-supporting technologies, such as cameras, accelerometers, and graphics processors have significantly contributed to their popularity. However, hardware limitations and the risk of cognitive overload remain critical challenges requiring further research.

AR is increasingly used as a tool to enrich physical reality with additional information, enhancing engagement and appeal. Studies highlight the personalization of user experiences through interaction with virtual models9,10. Conversely, concerns have been raised about sensory overload and distraction if designers fail to balance content quantity with usability. In cultural heritage, AR contributes to both the presentation and preservation of historical objects by enabling digital visualizations, thereby reducing the need for physical intervention11. AR’s potential in design and education is also growing12. demonstrate how integrating digital models into real-world environments accelerates design processes, boosting creativity and precision13. extends this to web-based AR systems, which support education and marketing by providing easier access to interactive content through web technologies.

In many AR projects, 3D models play a pivotal role in ensuring the visual realism of virtual objects14. Their creation typically involves geometric modeling, texturing, and performance optimization. A major challenge is adapting these models to the computational and memory constraints of mobile devices, which often requires reducing polygon counts or texture resolution8. Excessive simplification may compromise visual detail, while poor optimization can degrade application performance.

In industry, 3D models enable prototype visualization; in education, they simplify complex structures; and in tourism, they facilitate interactive exploration of inaccessible sites15. For cultural heritage, they also serve as a documentary role, creating digital replicas of artifacts. However, overreliance on virtual presentations risks diminishing direct engagement with heritage sites and diluting the authenticity of physical experiences.

In education, AR significantly enhances cognitive processes by visualizing complex concepts interactively16. emphasizes AR’s transformative potential, particularly when paired with 3D engines like Unity3D, which revolutionize learning through increased immersion and accessibility17,18. highlight AR’s role in vocational training and interactive education, where it fosters practical skills and positive student attitudes.

In gaming and entertainment19, note AR’s impact on multiplayer experiences, where virtual-real integration deepens engagement20. explores AR’s role in digital media and art, showcasing its ability to create immersive visualizations and interactions that enhance user satisfaction.

The quality of 3D models is a key factor in AR user experience. Polygon count and texture detail determine visual realism but also impact performance21. High polygon counts enhance immersion through precise detail but may reduce fluidity on low-power devices8. High-resolution textures improve realism but strain memory and processing7,9. Studies suggest users prefer medium-to-high detail, provided it doesn’t cause lag21. Narrative context and educational value may also justify slight reductions in realism.

AR excels in cultural heritage presentation and tourism, enabling interactive exploration of historical sites22,23. Projects like Rome Reborn offer virtual tours of ancient Rome, while Poland’s Torun in AR showcases medieval landmarks. For Gediminas Tower, AR could serve both tourism and education24. Critics caution, however, that excessive visual appeal may overshadow historical significance without substantive content25.

Quality assessment combines objective metrics (polygon count, texture resolution, file size) and subjective user feedback14. A hybrid approach ensures both technical performance and perceptual satisfaction.

AR demands a trade-off between visual fidelity and performance. Excessive polygons or high-res textures can hinder fluidity, especially on mobile devices26, while over-optimization may reduce appeal. Iterative optimization, gradual refinement and testing is recommended to achieve equilibrium27. Critics warn that overemphasis on visuals may yield superficial projects lacking cognitive or historical depth.

3D model quality in AR is multidimensional, requiring a balance between realism, hardware constraints, and user preferences28. For cultural heritage, this is particularly critical, as poorly optimized models may distort perceptions of historical value. Successful projects must merge technical excellence with educational narrative, ensuring both visual impact and meaningful engagement to attract tourists and historians alike.

Methodology

This study adopted a hybrid approach combining expert analysis of technical parameters of 3D models with end-user perception testing in an augmented reality (AR) environment. The aim of this approach was to comprehensively assess the impact of polygon count and texture quality on visual perception and AR application performance while maintaining a systematic experiment structure (Fig. 1.).

Diagram of the research process: Evaluation of 3D models in an AR environment.

In the first stage, an experimental plan was developed based on modifications of 3D models. To ensure the modifications had actual cognitive value and enabled reliable conclusions, two key factors were selected: polygon count and texture resolution. The experiment included three levels of polygon count (low, medium, high) and two levels of texture resolution (low, high), resulting in six model variants total. This setup enabled clear differentiation of the effects of polygon count and texture resolution across six model variants. In the statistical analysis, ratings were examined using descriptive, correlational, and comparative procedures, including group comparisons by polygon-count level and pairwise comparisons between texture-resolution variants. It was crucial to ensure differences between variants were noticeable to users while avoiding overloading participants with excessive comparisons.

The 3D models were prepared considering technical criteria such as mesh quality, polygon count optimization (using retopology techniques), file size, and texture quality. Technical analysis of the models was conducted using tools like Blender (Version number − 4.5.2 Long-term support, https://www.blender.org/download/releases/4-5/) and Unity Profiler (Unity – Version number – 2022.3.47f1, https://unity.com/download), enabling precise determination of technical parameters including file size, loading time, and frames per second (FPS) on mobile devices.

In the next stage, performance testing was conducted using the prepared models in an augmented reality (AR) application environment running on mobile devices. The goal of this stage was quantitative assessment of model complexity’s impact on system performance parameters such as central processing unit (CPU) load and frames per second (FPS). Tests were performed under controlled conditions ensuring measurement repeatability and test environment stability.

The models were divided into three groups according to geometric complexity, where each group – as shown in Table 1 – contains two variants differing in texture resolution.

The second stage of the study involved an experiment with end-users aimed at assessing the subjective perception of 3D models and their impact on immersive experience in augmented reality (AR) environment. The experiment involved 74 participants representing young adults with a mean age of 21 years (SD = 1.24), in the age range of 19 to 24 years. The majority of respondents were women (62%, n = 46), while men accounted for 38% of the study group (n = 28). Participants differed in their level of experience with AR technology, assessed on a five-point scale.

After a brief tutorial on using the prototype application, participants used mobile devices equipped with an augmented reality application that allowed free exploration of six variants of 3D models.

The six model variants were presented through separate interface buttons arranged in a fixed order. Participants were free to explore all variants, but they were not informed about the technical differences between the models or about the specific parameters being manipulated. This reduced the likelihood of expectation-driven evaluation; however, because the order was not randomized or counterbalanced, a potential order effect cannot be fully excluded and should be considered a limitation of the study.

For the main comparative analyses reported in the Results section, five core evaluation criteria were used: realism, detail visibility, texture quality, reaction smoothness, and loading speed. The remaining questionnaire items were treated as supplementary measures supporting the broader interpretation of user impressions.

The questionnaire was designed specifically for the present experiment and focused on task-relevant perceptual and performance-related attributes of the tested AR model variants. Its primary purpose was to support comparative evaluation of specific model features rather than to measure general usability as a standardized psychometric construct. For this reason, no benchmark-based validation against instruments such as SUS or UEQ was applied at this stage.

The questionnaire was completed anonymously, without the presence of the researcher, which eliminated the possibility of social influence and allowed participants to freely express their opinions. Simultaneously, unobtrusive observation of users during interaction with the application was conducted. Analyzed aspects included difficulties in operating the models, moments of frustration resulting from long loading times, and tendency to frequently switch between models.

Additionally, participant observation was conducted during the experiment, focusing on moments of potential frustration (e.g., prolonged model loading times), frequency of variant changes, and manner of interaction with the application. For a selected group of respondents, in-depth interviews were also conducted to collect qualitative data regarding preferences, expectations, and overall user impressions of the application’s performance.

The collected data, both technical (loading time, frames per second, file size) and subjective (perceptual ratings)—were analyzed using a sequence of statistical procedures corresponding to the structure of the Results section. First, descriptive statistics and visual summaries were used to compare the six model variants across the five core evaluation criteria. Next, exploratory cluster analysis (k-means) was applied to identify differences in rating patterns among participants. Spearman rank correlation analysis was then used to examine relationships between perceptual ratings and selected technical model parameters. To test differences across polygon-count levels, a parametric one-way ANOVA was applied separately for each evaluation criterion. Prior to analysis, the homogeneity of variances assumption was examined using Levene’s test. Because the ratings were collected on an ordinal five-point Likert scale, the robustness of the group differences was additionally checked using a non-parametric comparison, which confirmed the same general pattern of significance. For pairwise comparisons between models differing only in texture resolution (2 K vs. 8 K), the Mann–Whitney U test was used, as a non-parametric approach was considered more appropriate than a two-sample t-test for ordinal questionnaire data. Finally, linear regression was used to estimate the direction and strength of the relationship between selected technical parameters and user ratings.

This methodology, combining expert analysis, perceptual experiment, and advanced statistical methods, allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of the relationships between technical characteristics of 3D models and their reception by users in AR applications. Questionnaires conducted during workshops were subjected to detailed analysis of individual questions. Survey results were supplemented with notes from direct observations of participants’ behaviors and reactions. The study used methods that were conducted in accordance with appropriate guidelines and ethical regulations for scientific research. All experimental protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Warmia and Mazury in Olsztyn, according to decision no. 2/2023. Participation in the study was completely voluntary.

Participants were recruited from the University of Warmia and Mazury in Olsztyn and the Gdansk University of Technology. Eligibility required legal adulthood and voluntary participation; individuals with health-related contraindications (e.g., epilepsy) were excluded. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after they had been fully informed about the study procedures and their rights prior to participation.

The experiment was conducted on Samsung Galaxy S22 Ultra smartphones with 8 GB RAM and a Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Gen 1 processor to ensure consistent performance. All participants used devices provided by the researchers to maintain identical technical conditions. Although the use of a single smartphone model ensured identical technical conditions for all participants, it also limits the generalizability of the findings to devices with similar hardware characteristics. Future studies should therefore examine the same model variants across a broader range of smartphones representing different performance classes.

The sample consisted mainly of young adult university students recruited through convenience sampling. This limits the generalizability of the results to broader age groups but aligns with common practice in AR evaluation, where student cohorts serve as accessible, digitally proficient groups for initial controlled studies. A relatively homogeneous sample also reduces variability linked to unfamiliarity with mobile and AR interfaces, allowing clearer detection of experimental effects. While appropriate for a first-stage study, future research will aim to replicate these findings with more diverse and older populations to evaluate their broader applicability. The study followed ethical guidelines and complied with the highest standards of scientific responsibility.

Research object characteristics - Gediminas tower

Gediminas Tower, a remnant of the medieval Upper Castle in Vilnius, is one of Lithuania’s most important national symbols and a significant element of the city’s historical landscape. Situated on Castle Hill, it dominates Vilnius’ skyline and is frequently visited by both tourists and researchers studying the region’s history and cultural heritage. Due to its architecture, form, and historical significance, it is a suitable object for analysis in augmented reality (AR) visualization, enabling its digital reconstruction and exploration.

The tower was built at the turn of the 14th and 15th centuries as part of defensive fortifications, and its current appearance results from numerous reconstructions, including a 19th-century restoration. The structure features brick walls on a stone foundation, a cylindrical shape with rectangular window openings, and crenellations. This architecture presents a challenge for 3D modeling, as it requires accurate representation of both the object’s geometry and material textures, such as brick and stone.

For this study, Gediminas Tower was selected as the AR visualization object for several reasons. First, its distinctive architectural form allows for analyzing the impact of polygon count on the quality of detail representation, such as wall textures or decorative elements. Second, the materials used in the tower’s construction, particularly brick, require precise texture representation, enabling research into how texture resolution affects users’ visual perception. Third, its historical and cultural significance makes it a popular subject for digital viewing among a broad audience, allowing for realistic testing of user perception in AR applications.

Measurements of Gediminas Tower in Vilnius were performed using two LiDAR sensors: a terrestrial Leica P30 scanner and an aerial CHC AlphaAir 450 LiDAR scanner mounted on a DJI Matrice 300 RTK UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle - drone). The drone’s trajectory was determined based on correction data from a reference station of the Lithuanian active geodetic network LitPOS, located 5 km away. The TLS point cloud was registered using ICP and Plane Patch Filter (PPF) methods. The TLS point cloud, trimmed to the vicinity of Gediminas Tower, consists of 26 million points with an average spatial resolution of 16.8 mm. The ALS LiDAR point cloud has an average resolution of 400 pts/m2.

As a result of measurements and point cloud processing, a detailed 3D model of Gediminas Tower was developed, which served as the main research object in this experiment. This model was created with high geometric precision, reflected in its final mesh structure and the quality of the applied textures. The file was saved in glb. format, optimized for use in web applications and augmented reality environments. The total model size is about 550 MB, which results directly from the large number of details represented based on LiDAR data.

The model’s mesh structure was fully triangulated and contains over 44,656,558 polygons. Such high geometric complexity allows for faithful representation of irregular wall surfaces, window openings and characteristic architectural details of the tower. This makes the model suitable for analyzing the impact of polygon count on AR application performance and on users’ visual perception.

The model was equipped with a diffuse (albedo) texture in 8 K resolution (8192 × 8192 px), which enables realistic representation of material color and texture - primarily brick and stone. The use of only one texture map, without additional normal maps or ambient occlusion, simplified the material structure, which on one hand limits rendering load, and on the other opens possibilities for further experiments with extended shading and texturing.

The model prepared in this way constitutes an excellent starting point for research in computer graphics, cultural heritage digitization and 3D asset optimization. Its level of detail and compliance with measurement data make it valuable research material, both in the context of architectural visualization and performance testing in AR environments.

Characteristics of the test application

The test application was developed to conduct an experiment evaluating the impact of polygon count and texture quality on visual perception of 3D models in augmented reality (AR). Its main objective was to provide users with the ability to interact with six model variants corresponding to different levels of visual quality. The system allowed controlled switching between models, enabling the recording of data regarding user preferences and interaction time with individual model versions.

The application was designed in the Unity environment and based on ARCore libraries (Google ARCore XR Plugin Version number – 5.1.5, https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.xr.arcore@5.1/manual/index.html), which enabled the use of advanced spatial tracking features and precise placement of 3D models in augmented reality. Implementation in Unity allowed for easy adaptation of the application to various mobile devices and integration of interaction mechanisms such as rotation, zooming, and model switching.

The application did not include an integrated survey, and subjective data regarding model evaluation was collected separately using a questionnaire provided after the test session. Statistics related to user interactions were automatically saved on a web platform, enabling subsequent analysis and processing in a statistical environment. Each user had the freedom to freely explore the 3D models, with the time spent viewing each variant being recorded.

Models were displayed based on scanned surfaces, meaning the application recognized the user’s real environment and placed 3D objects in space in a way adapted to physical conditions. This made interaction with models more natural and realistic, reflecting how augmented reality is used in practical applications.

Shadow optimization was not implemented in the test application, meaning models did not include dynamic lighting effects and real-time shading. The absence of this functionality simplified rendering processes and allowed focus on the study’s key aspects, which were model geometry and texture quality.

The user interface was designed to be intuitive and transparent, enabling easy switching between models using six dedicated buttons. Each button corresponded to a different model variant, and users had complete freedom in selecting and exploring individual versions. Additionally, the application recorded the number of switches between models, allowing analysis of user preferences regarding model quality levels.

Research results

The conducted research included technical analysis of 3D models and user perception studies in AR environment. This chapter presents results regarding the impact of polygon count and texture quality on visual perception of models and their performance in AR applications. The analysis covered both quantitative data related to user interaction with the application, as well as subjective evaluations collected through questionnaires and in-depth interviews.

Technical analysis of model impact on application performance

The objective of the technical analysis was to determine the influence of 3D model complexity on augmented reality (AR) application performance, particularly regarding frame rate stability (FPS) and computational load on mobile device hardware. The methodology involved real-time monitoring of system parameters while sequentially introducing different model variants into the scene. Each measurement was repeated multiple times to ensure result reproducibility, with the recorded data including frames per second (FPS), central processing unit (CPU) utilization, and graphics processing unit (GPU) utilization.

Analysis of the “Frame Rate for Models” chart (Fig. 3) conclusively demonstrated that the primary factor determining AR application performance is the model’s triangle count. High-complexity models - particularly Model 1 and Model 2 containing 5,041,162 triangles each - caused significant FPS drops, indicating substantial GPU load and increased computational demands. Medium-complexity models (2,520,580 triangles - Model 3 and Model 4) also exhibited noticeable frame rate reductions, though remaining within acceptable limits for most users. Conversely, optimized models (250,025 triangles - Model 5 and Model 6) maintained stable high FPS regardless of texture resolution, making them the most performance-efficient test variants (Table 2).

The model initialization phase, which involves the initial loading and rendering of assets, proved especially significant. During this stage, the steepest FPS drops were recorded, linked to geometry transfer into GPU memory and mesh processing overhead. Although frame rates stabilized afterwards, high-polygon models continued to operate at lower FPS than optimized ones. When multiple complex models appeared simultaneously, their cumulative load led to severe performance degradation and potential user discomfort.

Regarding texture resolution impact on performance, tests demonstrated this factor’s secondary importance. Differences between 8 K and 2 K textures showed negligible FPS impact for both complex and optimized models. For high-polygon models, geometry - rather than texture size - constituted the primary system load source. For low-complexity models, differences were equally marginal, confirming that with properly optimized geometry, texture management doesn’t pose significant limitations (Table 2).

Overall, the tests identified a critical performance threshold: models exceeding 5 million triangles consistently produced perceptible FPS reductions, leading to lag and slowdown. This underscores the need to prioritize geometry optimization in mobile AR design. At the same time, the analysis revealed that optimized models (~ 250 K triangles) not only ensured smooth rendering but also offered substantial advantages in file size. Highly detailed models (e.g., Models 1 and 2, > 200 MB) are too demanding for mobile devices, requiring local embedding or caching strategies that inflate installation size and startup times. Optimized variants (Models 5 and 6), by contrast, maintained satisfactory visual quality with much smaller file sizes—around 23 MB for 8 K textures and 11 MB for 2 K—making them suitable for dynamic, on-demand loading from cloud or CDN resources without noticeable UX impact.

From an architectural perspective, these results highlight the importance of balancing geometric complexity, file size, and distribution methods. Offering both 8 K and 2 K texture variants enables adaptive quality scaling depending on device capabilities and network conditions. Consequently, while highly detailed models may be appropriate for offline use cases such as museum or demo installations, optimized models are far better suited to mobile AR applications and streaming-based delivery systems.

User study

The experiment participants used mobile devices with an installed AR application that implemented six variants of 3D models. The users were not provided with any information regarding the differences between the models to ensure their evaluation remained independent of technical knowledge. After completing the exploration phase, the participants proceeded to fill out a detailed questionnaire aimed at assessing the subjective perception of each presented model and collecting general user preferences regarding the application’s functionality.

The respondents evaluated each of the six models separately, answering questions about the level of visual realism, detail visibility, texture quality, smoothness of operation, and model loading speed. Each question was rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5) (Appendix 1).

Additionally, users indicated which model they liked the most, which was the easiest to use, and which operated the most smoothly, selecting one answer in each category. These questions allowed for the identification of subjective preferences toward specific model variants.

In the final part of the questionnaire, two supplementary questions were asked. The first explored user preferences for a feature enabling the selection of model quality in the application (e.g., high-quality mode or power-saving mode). The second question asked whether the differences between the presented models were sufficiently clear and noticeable to the users. Both questions were also rated on a five-point Likert scale.

The questionnaire also included an open-ended field where respondents could freely share their impressions, difficulties, suggestions, or comments regarding the models presented or the application’s overall functionality.

After collecting the questionnaires, data analysis began, initially focusing on the characteristics of the study participants. The experiment involved 74 respondents, constituting a group of young adults aged 19 to 24 (mean age: 21, SD = 1.24). The dominant group was female (62%, n = 46), while males accounted for 38% of the sample (n = 28). A preliminary assessment of experience with augmented reality (AR) technology, measured on a five-point Likert scale, showed an average level of 3.38, suggesting moderate familiarity with such solutions in the studied population.

The distribution of ratings assigned to the individual models and the variation in user perception were illustrated using two complementary visualizations: a heatmap (Fig. 4), presenting median ratings and standard deviations across the evaluated criteria, and a bar chart (Fig. 5), showing the multidimensional rating profiles of the six model variants. These visualizations provide a descriptive overview of the rating structure and serve as a reference point for the subsequent cluster analysis and user preference analyses.

A descriptive overview of user evaluations for the six AR model variants across five core criteria (realism, detail visibility, texture quality, reaction smoothness, and loading speed) is presented in Fig. 4. To better reflect the ordinal nature of the questionnaire data, the figure presents median ratings as the main visualized measure, while standard deviations are additionally reported in each cell as an indicator of response dispersion. This format allows simultaneous comparison of central tendency and variability across model–criterion combinations in a compact and interpretable form.

The figure shows that the tested variants were not evaluated uniformly across criteria. The low-polygon models received the most favorable ratings for performance-related aspects, particularly reaction smoothness and loading speed, whereas selected higher-complexity variants performed relatively better in visual criteria such as realism, detail visibility, and texture quality. Overall, the observed pattern suggests that participants responded most positively to model variants that offered a balance between visual quality and efficient mobile performance. To complement the heatmap-based overview, Fig. 5 presents the same evaluation dimensions in a bar-chart format, allowing a clearer comparison of the relative strengths and weaknesses of each model across the five criteria.

While Figs. 4 and 5 summarize the aggregate evaluation pattern across all respondents, they do not capture potential heterogeneity in user preferences. To examine whether participants formed distinct groups characterized by different rating profiles, an exploratory k-means cluster analysis was performed. The results of this analysis are presented in Fig. 6.

Clusters were identified using k-means clustering, and the three-cluster solution was retained based on the silhouette coefficient. A detailed comparison of the rating profiles across specific model variants is visualized in Fig. 6, which displays the cluster-wise mean ratings for each evaluation criterion. This visualization preserves the structure of the clustering input data and allows the correlation of rating profiles not only in terms of overall evaluation level, but also in relation to specific model variants. The three-cluster solution was retained based on the silhouette coefficient, indicating that this configuration provided the most appropriate balance between within-cluster cohesion and between-cluster separation.

The figure shows that participants did not evaluate the tested AR models uniformly. One cluster assigned the highest ratings to the visually richer variants, particularly Models 1–4, while simultaneously giving lower scores to these models in performance-related criteria, especially reaction smoothness and loading speed. A second cluster evaluated the lower-polygon variants, especially Models 5 and 6, more favorably, with the clearest advantage observed for performance-related aspects. The third cluster remained more critical across most criteria and model variants, assigning generally lower ratings regardless of the technical profile of the stimulus.

Collectively, these profiles indicate that participants differed in the way they balanced visual fidelity against technical responsiveness during AR model evaluation. The observed differences were not limited to isolated items but formed coherent multi-criterion patterns across the model variants. At the same time, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The cluster analysis was applied here as an exploratory segmentation method intended to identify recurring rating patterns, rather than as a confirmatory procedure for defining stable perceptual or cognitive user types. Therefore, the results presented in Fig. 6 should be treated primarily as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

From a practical perspective, the cluster profiles suggest that a single model configuration may not be equally optimal for all users. While some participants responded more positively to visually advanced variants despite reduced smoothness, others showed a clearer preference for lighter models that provided faster loading and more fluent interaction. This result supports the conclusion that mobile AR applications may benefit from adaptive or user-selectable quality settings, particularly when deployed on devices with heterogeneous performance characteristics.

To further evaluate the relationship between the technical characteristics of 3D models and their reception by users in an augmented reality (AR) environment, a correlation analysis and statistical significance tests were conducted, supplemented by regression modeling. The goal of these analyses was to determine which parameters—polygon count or texture resolution—most significantly influence perceived quality, smoothness, and overall user preferences in the application.

The results of the general Spearman correlation analysis are presented in Fig. 7. The strongest positive association was observed between reaction smoothness and loading speed (ρ = 0.94; p = 0.0048), indicating that model variants perceived as operating more smoothly were also rated as loading faster. At the same time, polygon count showed a very strong negative association with both reaction smoothness (ρ = − 0.96; p = 0.0028) and loading speed (ρ = − 0.96; p = 0.0028), indicating that increasing geometric complexity was associated with less favorable performance-related evaluations. In contrast, texture-related parameters showed weaker and less consistent relationships with user ratings. Overall, these results suggest that, within the tested range, geometric complexity had a stronger influence on perceived AR performance than texture-related properties.

For some models, preference was more strongly related to perceived realism, whereas for others it was more closely associated with performance-related impressions, especially reaction smoothness. This pattern indicates that the relative importance of visual and technical attributes depended on the specific technical profile of the presented model.

To further examine whether the ratings differed systematically across broader levels of geometric complexity, the six model variants were grouped into three polygon-count categories: low, medium, and high. The results of these group comparisons are presented in Fig. 9 and were evaluated statistically using one-way ANOVA.

Boxplots of user ratings across low-, medium-, and high-polygon-count groups, with one-way ANOVA results shown for each evaluation criterion.

To determine whether user ratings varied significantly across the three polygon-count groups (low, medium, and high), a parametric one-way ANOVA was applied to each evaluation criterion, with the results visually summarized via boxplots in Fig. 9.

Following the verification of variance homogeneity using Levene’s test, the analysis revealed significant differences for realism, reaction smoothness, and loading speed. In contrast, detail visibility and texture quality showed no statistically significant differences. These findings suggest that polygon count heavily influences performance-related metrics while having a more selective impact on visual perception. Ultimately, reducing geometric complexity improves operational performance without causing a proportional drop in perceived visual quality.

In the cluster analysis, the number of clusters was determined using the silhouette coefficient. In the regression models, potential multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), none of which exceeded the threshold value of 5.

To further assess the impact of texture resolution, a Mann–Whitney U test was conducted for models differing only in texture resolution (2 K vs. 8 K). No significant differences were observed either for texture quality ratings (U = 25868.0; p = 0.2589) or for realism ratings (U = 24820.0; p = 0.7555). These results suggest that reducing texture resolution within the studied range did not significantly affect perceived model quality.

To quantify the strength and direction of the technical parameters’ influence on model ratings, linear regression analysis was performed. The model for realism ratings showed a moderate fit (R² = 0.652), with polygon count approaching statistical significance (p = 0.099), while texture resolution had no significant effect (p = 0.879). Stronger results were obtained for reaction smoothness, for which the regression model showed a very high fit (R² = 0.971), and polygon count had a significant negative effect on ratings (p = 0.002). As in the previous analysis, texture resolution remained statistically insignificant (p = 0.578).

Taken together, the results of all analyses consistently indicate that polygon count is a critical factor affecting both technical performance and user perception in the design of 3D models for mobile AR applications. By contrast, texture resolution, within the analyzed range, did not significantly influence model evaluation, suggesting that this parameter may be optimized without a substantial loss of perceived visual quality. A limitation of the present study is that all participants evaluated each of the six model variants, resulting in a repeated-measures data structure. Accordingly, the comparative analyses reported here should be interpreted as simplified analytical comparisons intended to highlight general differences between polygon-count categories and texture-resolution conditions. Future studies should therefore verify these effects using statistical models specifically designed for repeated-measures data.

Another limitation of the present study is that the questionnaire was author-designed for the specific purpose of comparing AR model variants and was not based on a previously validated psychometric scale. Although this allowed precise targeting of visually and technically relevant model attributes, it limits direct comparability with standardized usability benchmarks. Future research should therefore complement this instrument with formal psychometric validation and/or established tools such as SUS or UEQ.

Conclusions and future work

The findings of this study provide comprehensive answers to the four research questions initially posed. Regarding RQ1, it was found that increasing geometric complexity (polygon count) significantly improves visual ratings (realism, detail visibility) but causes a sharp decline in performance-related ratings (smoothness and loading speed). Texture resolution, within the tested range, had only a marginal impact on these ratings. Addressing RQ2, user preferences were found to be most strongly associated with reaction smoothness and polygon count; technical efficiency outweighed pure visual fidelity for most participants. In response to RQ3, the statistical analysis (ANOVA) confirmed significant differences in user evaluations between polygon-count categories for realism, smoothness, and loading speed, while Mann-Whitney U tests showed no significant differences between texture resolution variants (2 K vs. 8 K). Finally, regarding RQ4, cluster analysis successfully identified three distinct user preference patterns: a “visually-focused” group, a “performance-focused” group, and a “critical” group, suggesting that a one-size-fits-all model configuration may not be optimal for all users.

The evaluation of 3D model quality for augmented reality (AR) applications requires a holistic approach that combines technical parameter analysis with user perception studies. The conducted research demonstrated that the key factors influencing model reception and application performance are primarily polygon (triangle) count and file size, while texture resolution had limited impact on both visual quality and technical system performance.

The technical analysis clearly shows that the highest system load is generated by models with extremely high geometric complexity (5 million triangles), which cause significant drops in frames per second (FPS) and substantially increase loading times (averaging 3.5 s). Despite their high detail level, these models noticeably reduce application smoothness and exceed safe operational resource limits for mobile devices. Analysis of the “Frame Rate for Models” graph revealed that the most critical system load moments occur during model initialization—specifically their first rendering and geometry loading into GPU memory. These effects are particularly pronounced when displaying multiple models simultaneously, leading to cumulative performance degradation.

Simultaneously, tests showed that differences in texture resolution (2 K vs. 8 K) do not significantly affect either perceived model quality or their impact on FPS. Models with low triangle counts (250 K) and 8 K textures maintain stable FPS even under scene load, and their file size (~ 23 MB) enables internet-based download strategies (e.g., streaming) without embedding models in the application. This approach supports modular system architecture and flexible content updates without requiring app republishing.

Survey results and user preference analysis confirmed that models with optimized geometry and high-resolution textures—particularly Model 5—were rated most positively. Users evaluated it as realistic, smoothly operating, and fast-loading, clearly indicating its balance between visual quality and technical performance. Conversely, Model 6—featuring identical geometry but 2 K textures—achieved the best loading speed results while maintaining satisfactory perceived quality.

Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between model smoothness and loading speed (ρ = 0.94), while polygon count showed strong negative correlations with these variables (ρ = − 0.96). Linear regression analysis confirmed that triangle count is the most critical factor affecting system performance (R² = 0.971 for operation smoothness), which aligns with ANOVA test results. The lack of statistically significant differences between 8 K and 2 K texture models indicates substantial optimization potential without visual quality loss.

Additionally, clustering results identify distinct user profiles, from those prioritizing maximum visual quality to others favoring operation smoothness. Such segmentation is particularly valuable for AR application design, as it justifies implementing user-selectable quality settings based on device parameters and system performance expectations.

For AR applications in mobile environments, 3D models should be designed to enable dynamic quality scaling, both in geometry and textures. The recommended approach involves using optimized meshes with ~ 250 K triangles combined with 8 K–2 K textures, depending on application architecture. This not only maintains the balance between aesthetics and performance but also supports real-time resource management, which is crucial for scalable and interactive augmented reality systems.

A limitation of the presented experiment is the use of only a single diffuse texture map (albedo), without the inclusion of more sophisticated shading models (e.g., normal maps or ambient occlusion). This simplification allowed methodological clarity but leaves open avenues for further exploration of advanced rendering techniques.

Our findings align with well-established knowledge that polygon count has a stronger effect on performance and user perception than texture resolution. The novelty of this work lies in empirically confirming this principle within a mobile AR heritage visualization context, providing practical insights into balancing fidelity and efficiency.

Future studies should extend the present framework by incorporating more advanced shading and lighting models to assess how illumination-related realism interacts with geometry, texture resolution, and perceived model quality in mobile AR environments.
