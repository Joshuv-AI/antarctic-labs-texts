Socio-ecological factors of dengue preventive practices among Myanmar migrants in Samut Sakhon Province, Thailand
Authors: Tin Soe Oo, Chun-Yu Lin, Yu-Te Tsai, Orapin Laosee, Piyapong Jamaimool, Tarinee Buadit, Cheerawit Rattanapan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is a mosquito-borne viral infection with recurring outbreaks across tropical and subtropical climatic regions worldwide. Dengue was one of the major public health threats and a leading cause of hospitalization in Thailand. High population mobility contributes to the spread of dengue in endemic areas. Our study sought to investigate the level of dengue preventive practices and its associated socio-ecological factors among Myanmar migrants in Samut Sakhon Province, Thailand.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out in Mueang Samut Sakhon District, Samut Sakhon Province, Thailand. A total of 415 participants from three migrant clusters within the district were recruited by using voluntary response sampling. Participants were approached during their leisure time with the help of community leaders. Data collection took place between June and July 2024 through interview-administered questionnaires, facilitated on mobile devices using KoboToolbox.ResultsGood levels of dengue preventive practices were reported by more than half of the respondents (56.6%). Multiple logistic regression revealed that participants residing in Thailand for ≥ 3 years (AOR: 1.647, 95% CI: 1.003–2.706), those with high knowledge about dengue (AOR: 1.741, 95% CI: 1.119–2.708), those reporting high social support for dengue prevention (AOR: 1.978, 95% CI: 1.255–3.118) and those exposed to five or more sources of stagnant water (AOR: 2.751, 95% CI: 1.476–5.128) were significantly more likely to practice effective dengue prevention.ConclusionWhile over half of Myanmar migrants in Samut Sakhon demonstrate good dengue preventive practices, notable gaps remain in knowledge, social support, and community engagement. Tailored health education targeting newly arrived migrants, strengthened social support networks, enhanced risk communication, and multi-stakeholder collaboration are recommended to sustain and improve dengue control efforts in migrant communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12017-8.Keywords: Dengue preventive practice, Socio-ecological factor, Myanmar migrant, Samut Sakhon, Thailand

Introduction

Dengue is an emerging and re-emerging mosquito-borne viral disease caused by dengue viruses (DENV). DENV is spread among humans by the bites of Aedes mosquitoes, primarily Aedes aegypti [1–3]. Almost half of the world’s population is now at risk of dengue disease, with 390 million infections expected each year [1, 2]. Asia accounts for approximately 70% of the global dengue burden, with South-East Asia (SEA) being one of the most effected WHO regions. Dengue cases in SEA region arose by 46% between 2015 and 2019, and five countries – including Thailand – were ranked among the top 30 dengue-endemic nations worldwide [4]. Dengue is one of the major public health threats and a leading cause of hospitalization in Thailand [3]. The first case of dengue fever in Thailand was documented in 1949, and several major dengue outbreaks occurred across the nation with the largest outbreak in 1958 [5]. In 2021, dengue was the fifth highest mortality among diseases under surveillance in Thailand, with 10,671 cases and six deaths reported [6]. By 2023, dengue cases in Thailand had surged dramatically, increasing by over 300% compared to 2022. Furthermore, the case fatality rate increased from 0.07% to 0.11% [7].

Thailand has become a hub for intraregional migration and a major destination country for migrants within Southeast Asia [8]. According to Thailand’s Ministry of Labor, 2.3 million migrants were documented as of October 2023, of which 1.7 million (74%) were from Myanmar [9]. High population movement across countries increases the risk of spreading dengue epidemics by creating favorable social, entomological, and environmental conditions for the growth of Aedes mosquitoes [10]. Additionally, the combination of high migration and poverty often results in substandard living conditions such as poorly constructed homes with drainage problems, insufficient water supply, and inadequate waste management systems [11]. Samut Sakhon Province, often known as “Little Myanmar”, is home to Thailand’s largest fishing and seafood processing industry. Latest estimates showed that the province had around 400,000 Myanmar migrant workers including an estimated number of undocumented migrants [12, 13]. An earlier survey conducted by the International Organization for Migration (IOM) showed that a substantial proportion of migrant households in Samut Sakhon were located in areas where garbage accumulation and standing water were common [14]. These conditions favor the formation of breeding places for Aedes mosquitoes. Meanwhile, dengue morbidity in Samut Sakhon escalated between 2020 and 2023, exceeding the national average by roughly 58% in 2023 with a reported morbidity rate of 286.34 per 100,000 population [14].

Whilst vaccines are typically the most effective measure for disease control, the Currently available dengue vaccine demonstrates moderate efficacy and offers uneven protection against all four serotypes. Furthermore, no effective antiviral drugs yet exist to cure dengue infection, leaving systematic management as the primary treatment option [15, 16]. In the absence of effective vaccines or specific treatment options, the only effective way to mitigate dengue transmission is through enhancing public awareness and mosquito control [17]. Human practice is vital in controlling dengue vector and viral transmission, as Aedes aegypti mosquitoes rely on human activities to create breeding environments [16]. The WHO recommends vector control as a key dengue prevention strategy, with a focus on minimizing individual exposure and eliminating potential breeding sites (i.e. source reduction). Understanding public knowledge and perceptions related to dengue infection is critical to adopting adequate preventive measures and designing effective disease control strategies [18]. Besides, community involvement plays an important role in achieving a sustainable dengue prevention program [19]. Prior studies investigating dengue preventive practices among Myanmar migrants in Thailand were limited to individual determinants such as knowledge and attitude-related factors. Our study, therefore, aimed to investigate socio-ecological factors influencing dengue preventive practices among Myanmar migrants in Samut Sakon Province, Thailand. Studying this target group can illuminate the experiences of the majority of migrants in Thailand and provide actionable insights for dengue control strategies that may potentially benefits other migrant populations in the country. Using the socio-ecological model (SEM), this study examined three key levels that influence dengue preventive practices: intrapersonal factors, including sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge, and attitudes regarding dengue infection; interpersonal factors, such as social support for dengue prevention; and community-level factors, including community capacity for dengue management and the presence of potential breeding grounds.

Methods

A cross-sectional survey of 415 Myanmar migrants was conducted in Samut Sakhon Province, Thailand, between June and July 2024. Located 30 km from Bangkok, the province hosts a large population of Myanmar migrants and is well known for its fishing and seafood processing industries. Among three districts in the province, we selected Mueang Samut Sakhon District due to its largest population. The minimum sample size was 415, determined by using Cochran formula for an unknown population. The calculation was based on a 95% confidence interval (CI), a 5% margin of error, an expected prevalence of 43%, and a 10% non-response rate. The respondents were recruited from three migrant clusters within the selected district – Talad Kung, Thai Union, and Talay Thai (Fig. 1). The study respondents were approached during their leisure time with the help of community leaders. We invited eligible respondents to take part in the survey, either at their workplaces or through door-to-door visits. Data were collected through structured interviews using an electronic questionnaire, administered via KoboToolbox on mobile devices. Participants were recruited voluntarily, and all interviews were conducted in Burmese. Two research assistants, fluent in Burmese and experienced in community-based assessments, assisted the researcher during data collection. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants at the start of the interview sessions. Myanmar migrants who were at least eighteen years old and had resided in Thailand for a minimum of one year at the time of interview were eligible, as they were assumed to have prior experience with the dengue control strategies implemented by Thai government.

Study Sites in Samut Sakhon Province

Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was pretested with 30 participants from Bang Pla migrant cluster in Nakhon Pathom Province, Thailand, who lived in a setting similar to that of the study respondents. Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the internal consistency of questionnaire domains measured on a Likert scale. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for attitude, practice, social support and community capacity scales were 0.67, 0.79, 0.78, and 0.87, respectively, indicating reasonable internal consistency [20]. The pretest data were excluded in the final analysis.

Research instrument

A structured questionnaire was designed using KoboToolbox (https://www.kobotoolbox.org/), with items adapted from pre-validated instruments used in similar studies [11, 17, 21–25]. The questionnaire was initially developed in English language and then translated into Burmese. To ensure the clarity and comprehensibility of the survey items, face validity was assessed by two public health physicians. The questionnaire comprised eight sections evaluating migration status (2 items), sociodemographic characteristics (6 items), previous dengue exposure (2 items), knowledge (12 items), attitudes (8 items), practices (15 items), social support (5 items), community capacity (9 items), and potential sources of stagnant water (6 items). In the knowledge section, each correct response was scored 1, while the incorrect or uncertain response was scored 0. The overall knowledge score was the sum of all 12 knowledge questions, and respondents were classified as having either ‘low’ or ‘high’ level of knowledge based on the median cutoff. The attitude section included eight items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree). The scores were summed, and respondents were categorized as having either ‘negative’ or ‘positive’ attitude toward dengue by the median cutoff. For the practice section, the responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (4 = always, 3 = often, 2 = sometimes, 1 = rarely, 0 = not at all), and respondents were grouped into exhibiting ‘poor’ or ‘good’ practice levels, using the median cutoff. Social support was assessed using an adapted 5-item Social Provisions Scale (SPS-5), validated by Heather M. Orpana [26] and previously applied in a similar study [27]. The responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree), and the total social support score for each respondent was the sum of scores across all 5 questions. It was then converted into a percentage of the highest possible score, and using a 75% cutoff [26], respondents were classified as having ‘low’ or ‘high’ level of social support. We adapted the Dengue Community Capacity Assessment Tool (DCCAT) developed by Charuai Suwanbamrung et al. to evaluate the ability of communities for dengue management [28]. The responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (5 = very high, 4 = high, 3 = moderate, 2 = low, 1 = very low), and the overall score for each respondent was computed by adding up their scores for all 8 questions and then converting the resulting score into a percentage of the maximum possible score. Community capacity was determined as either ‘inadequate’ or ‘adequate’, using a 50% cutoff point. Adapted from a previous study [23], we examined the presence of potential sources of stagnant water in respondents’ neighborhoods, and it was then classified into three groups: fewer than two, two-four, and more than four sources of stagnant water.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software version 25. A targeted sample of 415 were incorporated into analysis. Descriptives statistics such as – frequency, percentage, median, quartile deviation (Q.D), minimum, and maximum – were used to describe respondents’ characteristics and their levels of knowledge, attitude, practice, social support and community capacity related to dengue prevention. In addition to descriptive analyses, Chi-square analyses were conducted to determine significant differences in percentages. To identify the associated factors of good dengue preventive practice, a multiple logistic regression was conducted. Univariable analyses were first conducted to predict the relationship between each independent variable and the dengue preventive practice. Following that, a multivariable analysis was conducted by incorporating variables with a p-value of < 0.25 in univariable analyses. Two models were utilized in the analysis: the first model employed the enter method for variable selection while the final model used the backward elimination method using the Wald test. We considered a p-value of less than 0.05 statistically significant in all statistical tests.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Mahidol University – Central Institutional Review Board (MUCIRB), Office of the President, Mahidol University (MU-CIRB 2024/205.1405). Respondents were presented with informed consent and a brief explanation of the study before the interviews were conducted.

Results

Respondents’ characteristics

As planned, a total of 415 participants completed the survey, excluding those who were approached for the interviews but were unable to participate, primarily due to time constraints. Among those interviewed, no dropouts were reported, and all responses were complete, as predefined validation criteria were integrated into the data collection form to prevent missing information. Out of total participants (Table 1), 51.6% were males and 48.4% were females, with a majority aged between 18 and 34 years (58.1%) and completed secondary school education (35.2%). Over two-thirds of respondents (71.6%) had resided in Thailand for at least three years, and most respondents migrated from southern Myanmar, including Tanintharyi (34.5%), Mon (27.2%), and Kayin (13.3%), all of which shares a border with Thailand. The majority were employed, with substantial proportions being employed as factory workers (43.6%) and in seafood processing (28.3%). More than half of the respondents employed (54.5%) had a minimum monthly income of 11,000 THB, and approximately 90% of respondents employed worked more than 5 days a week. Previous dengue infections were reported by 37 respondents (8.9%), of whom 25 respondents (67.6%) had been hospitalized due to den- gue infection. About 72.8% of respondents stated that they observed between two and four stagnant water sources in their neighborhoods.

Practices on dengue prevention

Over half of respondents (n = 235; 56.6%) exhibited a good level of dengue preventive practices (Table 2). Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of respondents according to types of dengue preventive measures. The use of electric fans to avoid personal contact with mosquitoes was identified as the most frequently practiced approach among respondents, with nearly all participants adopting this method. Secondly, a majority of respondents (62.7%) consistently practiced proper disposal of household garbage to reduce mosquito breeding. However, only a small proportion (2.2%) of respondents reported the consistent use of bed nets during daytime sleep to protect themselves from mosquito bites. Likewise, adding larvicide (Abate) into water storage tanks to prevent mosquito breeding was very rare among respondents, with only 3.4% consistently practicing it.

Knowledge and attitudes toward dengue

A high level of knowledge was demonstrated by the majority of respondents (54.7%) (Table 2). Although 60.7% of respondents acknowledged that dengue is caused by a virus, their understanding of the vector’s characteristics and dengue symptoms was found to be limited (Table A1 in Appendix). Specifically, only 38.6% of participants correctly answered that dengue viruses breed in clean and stagnant water and merely 36.1% understood that Aedes mosquitoes predominantly bite during the daytime. In terms of dengue symptoms, nearly all participants (94.7%) identified fever as a dengue symptom, followed by headache (84.6%). However, the lowest percentage (40.2%) correctly identified eye pain as a symptom of dengue. Regarding preventive measures, most participants perceived that dengue can be prevented by adding larvicide into water storage tanks (89.6%) and proper disposal of items that retain water (95.7%).

Respondents’ attitudes on dengue risk and prevention strategies were found to be satisfactory, with the majority (53.5%) indicating positive attitudes toward dengue (Table 2). Most notably, nearly half of respondents (49.4%) perceived themselves to be at risk of contracting dengue (Table A2 in Appendix).

Social support for dengue prevention

Only 37.8% of respondents had a high level of social support for dengue prevention, while the majority (62.2%) indicated a low level of social support (Table 2). Across all five social provisions assessed by the SPS, fewer than half of respondents indicated receiving individual forms of social support for dengue prevention, while a substantial proportion (27.5%) revealed a lack of instrumental support from family or friends necessary for implementing dengue preventive measures (Table A3 in Appendix).

Community capacity for dengue management

An adequate level of community capacity was reported by the majority of respondents (70.6%) (Table 2). Fewer than half of respondents rated that their communities’ capacities for dengue management were high across all domains of the DCCAT, except for the domain assessing the community sense of dengue as a local health problem (Table A4 in Appendix). Notably, over half of respondents (51.6%) opined low community capacity in terms of dengue information communication. Similarly, nearly half (47.5%) reported low community capacity regarding the networking between leader groups and community members. Additionally, 43.6% of respondents also indicated low capacity in community network partnership with relevant counterparts for dengue management.

Socio-ecological factors of dengue preventive practices

Five variables, including sex, source of stagnant water, knowledge, attitudes and social support, were found to have significant association with good dengue preventive practice after the univariable analysis (Table 3). Following that, a multivariable analysis was conducted, including all variables with a p-value of less than 0.25. The results derived from the multivariable analysis are presented in Table 4. Individuals who had resided in Thailand for three or more years had 1.6 times higher odds of practicing dengue prevention compared to those who had resided in the country for fewer than three years (AOR: 1.647, 95% CI: 1.003–2.706). Additionally, respondents with a high level of dengue knowledge were 1.7 times more likely to practice dengue preventive measures compared to those with low knowledge (AOR: 1.741, 95% CI: 1.119–2.708). Moreover, respondents with high social support had nearly twice the odds of adopting good dengue preventive practices compared to those with low social support (AOR: 1.978, 95% CI: 1.255–3.118). Most notably, participants who reported five or more sources of stagnant water in their households and surroundings were 2.7 times more likely to have a good level of dengue preventive practices compared to those who reported fewer sources of stagnant water (AOR: 2.751, 95% CI: 1.476–5.128).

Discussion

In this study, 56.5% of participants showed a good level of dengue preventive practice, a finding consistent with a Malaysian study among migrant workers and Thai population in the Central part of Thailand, which reported a comparable percentage [11, 29]. In contrast, a higher percentage (73%) was observed in a study involving market traders in Malaysia [21], while a lower percentage (32%) was reported in an Indonesian study [22]. From these differences, it is inferred that the likelihood of practicing dengue prevention activities varies depending on socio-ecological contexts among different population groups. Moreover, we found that the use of electric fans was the most practiced preventive measure among participants, likely due to Thailand’s hot climate and the widespread availability of electric fans in migrant households. Similarly, another study conducted in Slums of Delhi, India, also reported the widespread use of fans as a method to prevent mosquito bites [23]. However, a local study revealed that most participants (51.9%) perceived sleeping under a mosquito net as an effective measure to prevent mosquito bites [29]. The WHO advises effective vector control strategies, such as larvicide and insecticide-treated nets [30]. Surprisingly, our study found the low usage of these measures, with only 14 participants (3.37%) consistently using larvicides (Abate) and just four (2.16%) regularly using bed-nets during daytime sleep. These results underscore that Myanmar migrants in Samut Sakhon may face barriers to accessing vector control materials and lack adequate informational support, which may then hinder their engagement in effective dengue preventive strategies. To tackle these barriers, we recommend these activities for local health authorities, such as distributing larvicides and organizing demonstration sessions to offer clear guidance on larvicide application.

The study results indicate that the majority of respondents (54.7%) had a high level of knowledge, and it is consistent with a Malaysian study where half of respondents showed a similar knowledge level [31]. However, significantly higher proportions of migrant workers in Malaysia (78.7%) and Thai residents in Central Thailand (72.2%) demonstrated a high level of knowledge [11, 29]. About 60.7% of participants correctly identified that dengue is caused by a virus, but only 38.6% of participants understood Aedes mosquitoes breed in clean and standing water. It aligns with another study done in Myanmar, where roughly 46% of participants provide a similar response [32]. Additionally, only 36.1% of respondents correctly answered that Aedes mosquitoes primarily bite during the daytime, contrasting with another study done in Thailand and Lao PDR, where more than 80% understood this vector habit [17]. While fever was a well-known dengue symptom among study participants, only 40% recognized eye pain as a dengue symptom. The comparable results were reported in another study, where 44.0% of Thai and 43.6% Laotian respondents exhibited similar knowledge [17]. It is evident that participants still lack adequate knowledge about vector habits and dengue symptoms. Hence, dengue educational campaigns should stress the specific knowledge about vector characteristics, habits, and dengue symptoms to address the knowledge gap among Myanmar migrant groups in Samut Sakhon.

Our study found that slightly over one-third of participants (37.8%) had a high level of social support for dengue prevention, conflicting another study using a similar measurement scale, where over half reported high social support [27]. Additionally, 27% of participants disagreed that they received instrumental support for dengue prevention from their close social circle (i.e. friends, family and co-workers). Contrarily, a Malaysian study found around 90% reported high familial support [21]. Given that social support is among key aspects of dengue prevention and control, notable gaps in receiving instrumental support may hinder respondents from implementing effective dengue preventive strategies.

Community capacity building is integral to a healthy community outcome, and to the process of enhancing a community’s ability to identify, analyze and act on individual concerns related to dengue. For example, understanding a community’s ability to communicate dengue-related information effectively is crucial in improving individual behaviors in disease prevention [28]. We utilized modified DCCAT to evaluate local dengue management capacity within Myanmar migrant communities, and our study found that nearly three-quarters of participants (70.6%) perceived adequate community capacity for dengue management. It contrasts with a study conducted in Sri Lanka using a similar tool, where 75.4% reported inadequate community capacity [25]. However, we found that 52% of participants perceived low capacity in dengue information communication. It differs from a Sri Lankan study, where nearly two-thirds provided the similar response [25]. Additionally, we discovered that 47% of participants opined low community capacity in terms of networking between leader groups and community members, while a similar opinion was provided by 60% in a previous study [25]. Furthermore, our study indicated that 44% of participants rated low capacity in community network partnership with relevant counterparts for dengue management. It is apparent that significant capacity gaps remain among Myanmar migrant groups in Samut Sakhon, particularly in terms of dengue information communication and networking, which are crucial to sustain dengue prevention efforts. To ensure sustainability, our study highlights the need for strengthening multi-stakeholder partnerships among local health authority, health organizations, community leaders and members to effectively disseminate dengue-related information within migrant groups.

The final model logistic model revealed that individuals who resided in Thailand for at least three years had 1.6 times higher odds of practicing dengue preventive measures compared to those with a shorter duration of stay. A similar result was obtained in an Indonesian study, which indicated longer-term residents in Malang City, a dengue endemic area in East Java Province, exhibited better dengue prevention behaviors [24]. According to these findings, longer residence in dengue endemic areas is associated with better preventive practices, likely due to increased awareness and understanding of local health issues. In order to improve dengue preventive efforts among Myanmar migrant communities in Samut Sakhon, it is essential to implement targeted health educational campaigns, particularly for newer or shorter-term migrants.

A scoping review stated that effective dengue prevention practices rely on awareness campaigns and information delivery on dengue fever [33]. This study found that high dengue knowledge was associated with better preventive practices. Individuals with high dengue knowledge were 1.7 times more likely to demonstrate good preventive practices compared to those with low knowledge. This finding aligns with past studies conducted in Bangladesh [34] and Malaysia [35], where high levels of preventive practices were found among participants with higher knowledge of dengue. The results underscore the critical role of dengue knowledge in improving dengue preventive efforts. Therefore, the current study recommends the local health authority design tailored health educational campaigns to address the knowledge gap among Myanmar migrants in Samut Sakhon. Given that volunteers play a critical role in health service provision to migrants, who often encounter communication barriers [36], their capacities should also be strengthened. The public health officials from the local health authority, in partnership with local health organizations, should evaluate the functioning status of existing migrant health volunteers in Samut Sakhon and organize capacity building workshops, enabling them to deliver migrant-friendly health education on dengue.

This study identified that high social support was significantly associated with better dengue preventive practices. Higher social support from friends, family or co-workers was associated with increased engagement in dengue preventive practices among participants. Previous studies performed in Thailand [29], Malaysia [21] and Indonesia [27] agreed with the results of this study, which also found a positive relationship between social support and preventive practices. This finding highlights the critical role of social support in enhancing disease prevention initiatives. Given that dengue is a significant public health concern, promoting a culture of shared responsibility among Myanmar migrant groups in Samut Sakhon is essential. This can be achieved through community workshops and peer education campaigns, implemented collaboratively by local health agencies, community leaders, migrant health volunteers and employers.

Environmental management plays an integral role in effective dengue control [31]. In the present study, participants who exposed to five or more sources of stagnant water were found to have better dengue preventive practices. Individuals who encounter multiple sources of stagnant water may develop a heightened awareness of their vulnerability to dengue, and this perception of risk could therefore motivate them to engage more effectively in preventive practices. However, this finding could reflect reverse causation too. For instance, people who are more vigilant about dengue prevention may notice more stagnant water sources in their surroundings. Although causality remains uncertain, this study highlights the integration of targeted risk communication in dengue prevention program. For example, local health authorities should carry out regular surveillance for mosquito larvae and clearly report the proportion of water sources found to contain larvae to the migrant communities, which contributes to improved risk awareness and promotes shared responsibility in prevention efforts.

While the current study highlighted the measures to enhance individual’s or community’s engagement in dengue preventive practices, it is important to note that disease transmissibility and severity are also influenced by viral factors. For example, the co-circulation of multiple serotypes in Thailand has been associated with an increased risk of severe dengue manifestations [37]. Therefore, comprehensive dengue prevention and control strategies should also take into account viral factors that contribute to disease burden.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the need for improving knowledge and risk communication on dengue, strengthening social support networks, and promoting multi-stakeholder collaboration, through migrant-inclusive dengue control programs. These findings provide critical insights to inform health authorities in reinforcing dengue prevention strategies for migrant populations in Thailand.

Limitations

The findings may not apply to the entire target population due to potential selection bias from voluntary response sampling, which relies respondents’ availability and willingness. Self-reported data on previous dengue exposure may lead to potential recall bias and not accurately represent the actual incidence. The cross-sectional design without control group from local resident, the state and living period of migrant participants should be concerned. Data on stagnant water sources relied on respondents’ perceptions rather than observations, which may not accurately reflect the actual situation. The cross-sectional design also limits the ability to infer casual relationship between socio-ecological factors and dengue preventive practices directly.
