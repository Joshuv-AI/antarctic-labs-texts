Engineered tRNA reduces vision loss in a mouse model of Leber congenital amaurosis
Authors: Pawan K. Shahi, Enes Akyuz, Lionel Gissot, Ahmad Al Saneh, Sanjai K. Pillala, Divya Sinha, Giovanni M. Hanstad, Meha Kabra, Maria A. Fernandez Zepeda, David M. Gamm, Samuel M. Young, Christopher A. Ahern, Bikash R. Pattnaik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Premature termination codons (PTCs) are a major class of pathogenic variants that underlie rare inherited disorders, including forms of childhood blindness. Therapeutic suppression of these “nonsense mutations” offers a gene- and position-agnostic strategy to restore protein function. Our previous work established that the W53X PTC in the KCNJ13 gene causes Leber congenital amaurosis type 16 (LCA16) by disrupting the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir7.1, leading to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction. Here, we present a proof-of-concept approach using anticodon-engineered transfer RNA (ACE-tRNA) to promote targeted translational readthrough. We engineered a suppressor tRNA encoding tryptophan (ACE-tRNATrp.UAG) to selectively recognize the UAG stop codon at the W53X site, enabling incorporation of the correct amino acid and restoration of full-length Kir7.1 protein. Delivery of ACE-tRNA via helper-dependent adenovirus (HDAd) resulted in robust rescue of channel function in heterologous systems expressing mutant KCNJ13 and in patient-derived human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-RPE cells. Functional recovery was confirmed by electrophysiological assays demonstrating restored inwardly rectifying currents and membrane potential. Importantly, subretinal delivery of HDAd-ACE-tRNATrp.UAG in a W53X mouse model led to partial restoration of RPE physiology, as measured by electroretinography, without evidence of retinal toxicity. Together, these findings establish ACE-tRNA-mediated suppression as a viable therapeutic strategy for nonsense mutations in multimeric ion channels and provide a translational framework for precision treatment of inherited retinal diseases.

Introduction

Nonsense mutations introduce premature termination codons (PTCs) into messenger RNA (mRNA), leading to truncated proteins that often lack normal structure and function. These variants represent a major class of disease-causing mutations across diverse human genetic conditions.1 Nonsense mutations account for approximately 15% of all known inherited disorders, including cystic fibrosis, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and certain forms of congenital blindness.2 Under such conditions, the presence of a PTC disrupts normal translation, preventing the synthesis of full-length proteins required for cellular function. The resulting loss-of-function phenotypes frequently lead to progressive and severe clinical manifestations, particularly in tissues with limited regenerative capacity, such as muscle and retina. Given the broad contribution of nonsense mutations to human disease context, there is a critical need to develop therapeutic strategies capable of restoring functional protein expression from PTC-containing transcripts.

A range of therapeutic approaches has been explored over the past several decades to overcome the effects of nonsense mutations, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Some of the recent developments are the pharmacological read-through agents, including aminoglycoside antibiotics, and gene-editing techniques.3 Both read-through drugs and aminoglycoside antibiotics, such as gentamicin, can induce partial read-through of PTCs by allowing near-cognate tRNA incorporation.4 While this strategy can partially restore protein synthesis, it often introduces incorrect amino acids at the mutation site, raising concerns about protein stability and function, particularly in proteins that are sensitive to missense substitutions.5 Genome editing technology, such as CRISPR/Cas9, has enabled precise correction of pathogenic variants at the DNA level. However, these approaches are inherently mutation-specific, require efficient delivery and transient expression of editing machinery, and demand extensive evaluations of potential off-target effects. These technical and biological challenges are especially pronounced in post-mitotic tissues, such as the retina, where long-term safety and precision are paramount.6–8 Furthermore, many nonsense mutations occur as ultra-rare “n of 1”, patient-specific variants, underscoring the need for therapeutic platforms that are not only effective but also adaptable across mutations and genes.

Anticodon-engineered transfer RNAs (ACE-tRNAs) represent a promising alternative strategy that directly engages the endogenous translational machinery to suppress PTCs. By modifying the anticodon sequence, ACE-tRNAs can be designed to recognize specific stop codons and insert the correct amino acid during translation, thereby restoring full-length protein synthesis without altering the underlying DNA sequence.9–11 This approach addresses key limitations associated with small-molecule read-through agents by ensuring accurate amino acid insertion and reducing the risk of generating dysfunctional proteins.12 ACE-tRNAs have demonstrated their capability to rescue expression of PTC-containing transcripts in vitro across a range of disease-relevant genes, including CFTR,13,14 CDKL5,15 and the cardiac potassium channel HERG.16 Although in vivo efforts using small molecules such as ataluren or aminoglycosides have shown limited success in achieving clinically meaningful mRNA or protein-level read-through, these approaches have not consistently yielded sufficient functional protein to produce meaningful physiological benefit.3,17–19 Similarly, early attempts at viral delivery of suppressor tRNAs have faced challenges achieving robust, sustained rescue in vivo, highlighting the critical need for optimized delivery systems and target-specific strategies. Notably, AAV-mediated tRNA delivery for Hurler syndrome results in poor rescue in vivo20 although such viral approaches could be helpful for tRNA delivery.21

We previously identified that Leber Congenital Amaurosis type 16 (LCA16), a form of early-onset retinal dystrophy, is caused by a nonsense mutation, c.158G>A (p.W53X), in the KCNJ13 gene (NM_002242.4).20 KCNJ13 encodes inwardly rectifying potassium channel 7.1 (Kir7.1), which is essential for maintaining retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell function and retinal ionic homeostasis.22 The nonsense mutation results in a loss-of-function Kir7.1 channel, leading to progressive photoreceptor degeneration and vision loss. Our primary goal in this study was to develop a novel therapy for LCA16 that can selectively target RPE cells within the retina.23 Here, we present a proof-of-concept study of ACE-tRNA, which was specifically engineered to recognize the tryptophan nonsense codon (ACE-tRNATrp.UAG) to restore Kir7.1 W53X mutant channel function in cellular and animal models of LCA16. In induced pluripotent stem cell-derived RPE (hiPSC-RPE) from an LCA16 patient (LCA16 hiPSC-RPE), we determined that helper-dependent adenovirus (HDAd)-delivered ACE-tRNATrp.UAG (tRNATrp.UAG) led to a read-through of the W53X mutation, resulting in a full-length functional Kir7.1 protein localized to the cell membrane. Subretinal delivery of HDAd-ACE-tRNATrp.UAG (HD tRNATrp.UAG) in a Kcnj13-mutant mouse model leads to measurable restoration of RPE cell function and visual responses, as assessed by improved electroretinogram (ERG) responses. Together, these findings provide compelling proof-of-concept for ACE-tRNA–mediated nonsense suppression as a precision therapeutic strategy for LCA16 and potentially other genetic diseases caused by PTCs.

Results

Suppression of UAG premature termination codons by ACE-tRNATrp.UAG

To assess UAG nonsense codon read-through by codon-edited tryptophan tRNA, we tested if anticodon-modified tRNA rescued the expression of a super-folder GFP harboring an in-frame TAG mutation at N150 (sfGFPN150X). This site has been shown to tolerate a wide range of side chain sizes and thus can help predict TAG suppression and stop codon read-through.16,24 The human genome has multiple iso-decoder tryptophan tRNA (tRNATrp) genes (GtRNAdb database: https://gtrnadb.ucsc.edu). Each of these Trp tRNAs carries the same CCA anticodon sequence that decodes the UGG codon.25 To identify our lead tryptophan tRNA that is used throughout our study, we systematically altered the anticodon loops of each of the Trp tRNAs so that they have the potential to suppress an in-frame premature TAG stop codon. To quantitatively examine TAG read-through efficacy, we then used sfGFPN150X, a site we have previously validated for assessing read-through efficacy and that has undetectable read-through in basal conditions. We screened six human CCA iso-decoder genes (tRNA-Trp-CCA 2-1 through 5-1) engineered to carry CUA anticodons, allowing them to read-through the in-frame UAG codon and insert tryptophan. The sfGFPN150X plasmid was co-transfected with each anticodon-edited Trp tRNA in HEK293T cells, and read-through efficacy was measured by the restored GFP fluorescence and flow cytometry analysis. (Fig. 1a). These data show that Trp-CCA 2-1 displayed statistically higher activity for rescued GFP when compared to the other Trp-tRNA suppressors, p < 0.001. Co-expression of engineered tRNA-Trp-CUA based on tRNA-Trp-CCA 2-1 (hereafter termed tRNATrp.UAG) led to 42.15 ± 0.09% of GFP-positive cells with an integrated Mean Fluorescence intensity (iMFI) of 84 ± 0.5 a.u. 24 h post-transfection (Fig. 1b, h). The tRNA-Trp-CCA-4-1 is second among the engineered Trp tRNA isodecoders, with GFP-expressing cells showing 41.43 ± 0.25% efficiency and an iMFI of 74.7 ± 0.7 a.u. (Fig. 1d, h). tRNA-Trp-CCA-3-1 and -tRNA-Trp-CCA-5-1 displayed the capability to read-though the TAG nonsense mutation, but with low efficacy, with GFP positive cells readouts being 33.63 ± 0.15% (iMFI = 45.1 ± 0.5), and 29.7 ± 0.15% (iMFI = 34 ± 0.2 a.u.), respectively (Fig. 1c, e, h and Fig. S1). Although all tRNA-Trp-CCAs had AUC in the tRNA anticodon loop structure, the nucleotide sequence shows few mismatches within the tRNA structural loop domains that may be important for ribosomal binding and could influence read-through efficiency (Fig. 1b–e). In control experiments, cells with the pUC19 plasmid and the sfGFPN150X plasmid showed no GFP expression (Fig. 1f–h). tRNATrp.UAG leads to readthrough of exogenous Kir7.1W53X and proper channel localization

To test if tRNATrp.UAG can mediate read-through of an mRNA encoding ectopic KCNJ13 with the 158 G > A mutation, we first tested if the efficacy in read-through depends on the number of tRNA copies expressed in the cell. To validate this, the functional rescue was tested in the cells transfected with either 1X or 4X tRNATrp.UAG along with GFP-Kir7.1W53X. The rescue observed is dose-dependent, and 4X could almost completely restore the WT level of potassium current; it also hyperpolarized the cells, as expected for functional channel expression. We concluded that 4X tRNATrp.UAG copy number was optimal for our study (Fig. S2), and this result aligns with previously published data.14 We then transfected HEK293T cells with plasmids encoding the heterologous N-terminally GFP-tagged Kir7.1 W53X (Kir7.1W53X) and 4X tRNATrp.UAG at a 1:3 ratio to find readthrough (Fig. 2a). Notably, we observed fluorescence, which was initially distributed throughout the cytoplasm and now trafficked to the plasma membrane in cells expressing tRNATrp.UAG (Fig. 2b). This membrane trafficking provides evidence of biological read-through of the mutant Kir7.1 by tRNATrp.UAG. This transition from cytoplasmic distribution of GFP-Kir7.1W53X alone to plasma membrane targeting of GFP was also quantified using Pearson’s coefficient and verified directly by measuring restored inwardly rectifying potassium currents. To verify the expression of a full-length protein, we also probed the Kir7.1 C-terminus by immunocytochemistry using an anti-Kir7.1 antibody (C-12). We used an anti-Na-K-ATPase antibody as a membrane marker. While cells expressing wild-type (WT) GFP-Kir7.1 showed membrane localization of the full-length protein (Fig. 2b, left panels), Kir7.1W53X cells showed cytoplasmic localization of the tagged protein (Fig. 2b, middle panel). In contrast, Kir7.1W53X cells co-expressing tRNATrp.UAG produced a full-length membrane-localized protein (Fig. 2b, right panel). Pearson correlation coefficient analysis (Fig. 2d) confirmed membrane localization of Kir7.1W53X in tRNATrp.UAG-expressing cells compared to that in Kir7.1W53X cells (P = 1.05E-15; one-way ANOVA test). Flow cytometry analysis meant to assess the C-terminal of the Kir7.1 protein detected a full-length protein in 89.7% of cells for WT-transfected cells, compared to only 0.9% of cells transfected with Kir7.1W53X. In both the controls, dual positivity for Kir7.1 and tRNA was null. We observed a significant increase in dual-fluorescence expression when tRNATrp.UAG was co-transfected with Kir7.1W53X, about a substantial increase in the dual-fluorescence expression was visible with 94.1% dual fluorescent positive cells compared to none for the untreated Kir7.1W53X cell (Fig. 2c and Fig. S3). Quantification analysis confirmed that the cells expressing Kir7.1 protein increased from almost 0% to 94.4 ± 0.8% after the treatment (Fig. 2e). Further quantification of a full-length protein expression was probed by Western Blot using a GFP antibody that detects the N-terminus of the protein to visualize a smaller size for the truncated protein vs. the full-length Kir7.1 protein. In Fig. 2f, the results show that, compared to the cytoplasmic truncated monomer of ~35 kDa in untreated samples, the full-length membrane tetrameric protein of around 250 kDa is observed in both wild-type protein-expressing cells and mutant cells treated with therapeutic tRNATrp.UAG is comparable to the protein size observed in the human RPE lysate (Fig. S4). Quantification of the protein also revealed that co-transfection with tRNATrp.UAG significantly increased the expression of the full-length Kir7.1 protein (Fig. 2g) while decreasing the expression of the truncated protein (Fig. 2h), thereby supporting the suppression of the nonsense mutation by the ACE-tRNA treatment. Restoration of Kir7.1 channel function

To test whether the full-length Kir7.1 protein was functional, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with Kir7.1W53X and tRNATrp.UAG, as described above, and measured whole-cell currents 48 h after transfection. A representative Kir7.1 channel current response to 50 mV voltage steps ranging from +50 to −150 mV over 600 ms from a holding potential of 0 mV is shown in Fig. 2i. We observed a distinct inwardly rectifying potassium current, a hallmark of Kir7.1 channel function, in cells expressing WT Kir7.1 and in cells expressing Kir7.1W53X and tRNATrp.UAG, but not in cells expressing Kir7.1W53X alone (Fig. 2i, left panels). As shown in Fig. 2j, WT Kir7.1 (black squares) and Kir7.1W53X cells co-expressing tRNATrp.UAG (purple triangle) showed an inwardly rectifying current-voltage (I-V) curve with a large inward current for hyperpolarized potentials compared to a slight outward current for depolarized potentials. This was significantly improved compared with mutant channel-expressing cells (−3.3 ± 1.1 pA/pF, P = 0.004; one-way ANOVA) (Fig. 2k).

Similarly, in the presence of extracellular Rb+, an established activator of the Kir7.1 channel, the inward currents in cells expressing WT Kir7.1 and in cells expressing Kir7.1W53X together with tRNATrp.UAG selectively increased, but not in cells expressing Kir7.1W53X alone (Fig. 2i, right panel). The increased Rb+ current density indicates a functional Kir7.1 channel, as observed for the WT channels (−177 ± 23.1 pA/pF) (Fig. 2l, black squares). Untreated (Fig. 2l, orange circle) vs. −93.1 ± 24.9 pA/pF in cells co-expressing tRNATrp.UAG (Fig. 2l, purple triangles). As shown in Fig. 2m, the Kir7.1W53X and tRNATrp.UAG co-expressing cells exhibited an Rb+ fold change, measured as IRb/IK at −150 mV, of 11.7 ± 2.7, which was comparable to that of the WT channel-expressing cells (9.0 ± 2.6 and more than ten fold higher than that of the mutant channel-expressing cells (0.9 ± 0.3) (P = 0.013; one-way ANOVA test). Similarly, the measured resting potential in Fig. 2n showed that cells co-expressing Kir7.1W53X and tRNATrp.UAG showed hyperpolarization (−48.7 ± 2.3 mV). This was comparable to cells expressing the WT (−61.6 ± 2.2 mV) but significantly hyperpolarized compared to mutant channel-expressing cells (−12 ± 5.1 mV) (Fig. 2n, P = 5.15E-07; one-way ANOVA test). The rescue of the Kir7.1 current profile, Rb+-induced increase in inward current, and membrane potential confirmed that tRNATrp.UAG leads to the expression of a fully functional Kir7.1 channel, a critical requirement for a beneficial therapeutic outcome.

tRNATrp.UAG-mediated restoration of Kir7.1 function in patient-derived hiPSC-RPE cells

For tRNATrp.UAG to be an effective therapeutic agent, it must function efficiently within the physiological target cells. Kir7.1 channels are expressed in the apical processes of the polarized RPE cells. We therefore used LCA16 patient-derived hiPSC-RPE cells (carrying the KCNJ13 W53X mutation). For therapeutic tRNA delivery, we chose helper-dependent adenovirus (HDAd) as a proof-of-principle vector. HDAd is a dsDNA virus that can be easily produced and can carry either 1X or 4X tRNA copy number within the payload. Furthermore, HDAds have demonstrated excellent success in preclinical trials, with long-term expression, and are currently being actively used in Phase I clinical trials.26–31 We transduced mature monolayer cultures with HDAd carrying 1X (Fig. S6) or 4X tRNATrp.UAG (HD tRNATrp.UAG) (Fig. 3a). In addition to detecting the presence of the HDAd viral genome in transduced cells by real-time PCR (Fig. S7), we analyzed expression of endogenous Kir7.1 and protein localization two weeks post-transduction (Fig. 3b and Fig. S8). Immunocytochemistry with the C-terminal Kir7.1 antibody revealed that, unlike LCA16 hiPSC-RPE cells (Fig. 3b, middle panel), WT hiPSC-RPE (Fig. 3b, left panel), and tRNATrp.UAG-treated cells (Fig. 3b, right panel) exhibited Kir7.1 protein on the membrane, as the Pearson’s coefficient showed colocalization between Kir7.1 and Na-K-ATPase (Fig. 3c). As an additional control, LCA16 hiPSC-RPE cells were transduced with HDAd carrying tRNAArg.UGA that suppresses UGA but not UAG stop codon. Immunocytochemistry imaging showed that the Kir7.1 protein was undetectable in these cells (Fig. S8b). Membrane localization of Kir7.1 is of paramount importance, and the read-through proteins observed were exclusively localized to the plasma membrane with no subcellular distribution, indicating the proper folding and trafficking of the newly synthesized protein. We further validated the protein expression in hiPSC-RPE cells using Western blot analysis. For the Kir7.1 protein, WT hiPSC-RPE cells showed a full-length product. Since the antibody targets only the C-terminus peptide, untreated LCA16 hiPSC-RPE cells with the nonsense mutation showed no detectable protein on SDS-PAGE. Cells transduced with HD tRNATrp.UAG expressed Kir7.1 at the same molecular weight as that of the WT band, confirming the production of a full-length protein (Fig. 3d). Quantification used Na-K-ATPase as a loading control, confirming the absence of full-length Kir7.1 in the LCA16 cells. WT cells had a normalized ratio of 1 ± 0.12, while untreated LCA16 cells showed minimal expression, with a ratio of 0.01 ± 0.01, corresponding to approximately 1.7% of WT. Cells treated with HDAd-Trp-tRNA restored full-length protein expression, with a ratio of 0.4 ± 0.09, equivalent to 40% of the WT levels (Fig. 3e). Discussion

To allow the correct translation of a UGG codon mutated to a UAG premature termination codon, we engineered Trp tRNA with a CUA anticodon, which is then endogenously charged with tryptophan by tryptophanyl-tRNA synthetase. tRNATrp.UAG allowed the read-through of the UAG premature stop codon by incorporating tryptophan during translation, as shown in our GFP reporter expression studies. The number and type of tRNA genes in tryptophan vary across species. In humans, there are at least seven anticodon CCA genes (1-1 to 5-1). CCA 2-1 tRNATrp.UAG showed optimal read-through, possibly due to genomic variations that stabilize ribosomal translation. Post-transcriptional modifications of engineered tRNA also ensure their stability and accuracy, reinforcing their critical function in maintaining cellular proteostasis.14,33 As multiple copies of the Trp tRNA gene may be needed to meet the translational demand for tryptophan during protein synthesis, we titrated to 4X tRNATrp.UAG, which enabled normal translation in both LCA16 hiPSC-RPE and mice.

tRNATrp.UGA has previously been shown to mediate read-through of UGA stop codons in cultured cells.16,34 We found that tRNATrp.UAG could achieve therapeutic rescue of clinical phenotypes in vitro and in vivo more efficiently than previously reported. We observed that the rescue efficacy was higher in iPSC-RPE than in HEK293T cells. One possible explanation is that the endogenous PTC-bearing mRNA is expressed at lower levels than the exogenous reporter or Kir7.1 expression plasmid. This suggests that the rescue of endogenous mRNA carrying a PTC is more predictive than that of model cell lines (e.g., HEK293T). Evidence has shown that ACE-tRNA-Arg incorporated arginine, the wild-type amino acid, into the growing polypeptide chain through the UGA stop codon and anticodon pairing.15 We reasoned that the physicochemical properties of the amino acids might impact the efficiency and fidelity of suppression, as tryptophan’s bulky aromatic side chain and arginine’s positively charged side chain interact differently with the ribosome and growing peptide chain.35,36 These differences underscore the importance of tailoring the Trp tRNA design to a specific nonsense codon and its surrounding sequence context for optimal read-through. Our study lacks direct in vivo protein quantification and comprehensive off-target safety profiling due to the absence of an LCA16 animal model and a high-affinity Kir7.1 antibody. Future work will focus on quantitative proteomic and ribosome profiling to assess the global readthrough events and identify the off-target effects. Extended safety and immunogenicity studies in non-human primates will also be performed to evaluate long-term expression and immune responses. These explorations will establish the specificity, safety profile, and clinical readiness of ACE-tRNA therapy for retinal diseases.

The therapeutic outcome of tRNATrp.UAG requires stable, long-term expression while minimizing toxicity and immune responses. The helper-dependent adenovirus (HDAd) transduction system provides a robust platform for long-term ACE-tRNA expression in post-mitotic hiPSC-RPE and animal models. For ocular gene delivery, HDAds can package large genes for gene augmentation therapy with limited immunogenicity.37 Previous studies have demonstrated the superior safety profiles of HDAds, which showed reduced inflammatory responses while still enabling sustained transgene expression38 due to episomal persistence without genomic integration. This minimizes insertional mutagenesis risks compared to lipid-based systems and conventional viral vectors,39,40 particularly for systemic delivery.41–43 Compared to first-generation adenoviral vectors, HDAds have significantly reduced acute toxicity and inflammatory responses and enhanced safety while providing therapeutic efficacy.44 In our study, subretinal injection of HDAd viruses provided a precise and effective method for delivering tRNATrp.UAG to retinal cells, making it a potential candidate for treating nonsense mutations that affect other organs. Another key strength of our approach is that the HDAd vector with the TrpUAG payload offers a gene-agnostic Trp-UAG variant therapy for other diseases by targeting nonsense mutations that affect a Trp codon, thereby accelerating the clinical course.

The tRNA therapy to address the nonsense mutation-related LCA16 eye disease in this study holds promise for broader application to all PTC-associated diseases, as PTC-associated diseases account for ~10–20% of all inherited diseases with clinical phenotype.45 Cystic fibrosis (CF) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) are two notable diseases studied for nonsense mutations, covering 10% for CF and 13% for DMD, respectively. In addition, hemophilia, choroideremia, Usher syndrome, and Stargardt are other diseases caused by nonsense mutations.46 The tRNA therapy has advantages over small-molecule read-through of nonsense mutations and overcomes the difficulties associated with CRISPR editing due to its specificity. This alone has the potential to treat a vast array of diseases, affecting over 3 million people in the United States.47

The absence of effective treatments for rare or ultra-rare conditions, such as LCA16, underscores the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches that target the genetic basis of these diseases. We suppressed the PTC caused by the W53X mutation in KCNJ13 through engineered tRNATrp.UAG treatment provides compelling proof-of-concept for using ACE-tRNAs to treat LCA16. Nevertheless, several challenges exist for clinical applications. The choice vector for delivering ACE-tRNA is of utmost importance. Despite its smaller size, ACE-tRNA packaging in AAVs is more challenging, resulting in very low-titer preparations that can impede clinical success. To achieve therapeutic efficacy, in addition to specificity, abundant transgene expression is very important, and HDAd successfully achieved this by including the necessary regulatory elements for tRNA expression. Moreover, the lack of viral sequences makes it a good alternative to AAVs, offering greater cloning capacity. However, we acknowledge that the safety profile of HDAd remains less characterized for the retina than that of AAV.27,48 Our study of HDAd delivery to mouse eyes subretinally showed no change in the ERG response until the 8-week time point. Comprehensive evaluation should still be performed in non-human primates to assess inflammation, toxicity, and long-term expression as preclinical safety studies. Long-term efficacy and safety assessments are necessary to determine whether repeated ACE-tRNA treatments are required to maintain therapeutic levels. Furthermore, it is currently unclear whether suppressor tRNAs disrupt cellular metabolism, compete with natural tRNA pools, or require strict regulation of their levels for therapeutic applications.49 Fortunately, high levels of UAG suppressor tRNA expression in transgenic mice showed no noticeable effects on tissue morphology during genetic code expansion experiments.9 Investigating alternative delivery methods, such as nanoparticles or newer viral vectors with enhanced retinal targeting, may further improve efficacy. Notably, with advancements in AAV capsid engineering and the emergence of dual AAV systems, tRNA delivery using these systems holds promise for the future and will be effective in suppressing sense mutations in vivo.50

In summary, this study demonstrates that tRNATrp.UAG-mediated read-through of the W53X nonsense mutation in KCNJ13 restored Kir7.1 protein levels and improved cell function in a human iPSC-RPE model of LCA16. Significantly, it also enhanced retinal function in a mouse model of LCA16. Our findings support ACE-tRNA as a promising therapy for suppressing nonsense mutations, offering specificity, adaptability, and reduced off-target effects compared to traditional gene therapies. These results pave the way for ACE-tRNA applications across a broader range of genetic diseases, potentially transforming the treatment landscape for gene disorders by enabling safe, effective, and tailored interventions for patients with nonsense mutations.

Materials and methods

ACE-tRNA

The structure and sequence of the ACE-tRNA-containing plasmid used for flow cytometry have been described previously.14 4x ACE-tRNATrp.UAG insert was synthesized by IDT and cloned via the Gibson reaction into the G0619 AAV plasmid to create a 4 × ACE-tRNATrp.UAG plasmid. The 1x ACE-tRNATrp.UAG insert was amplified from the G0619-4xACE-tRNATrp.UAG using LG213/LG214 primers and cloned downstream of the pCMV:mOrange2-TGA-BgHpA sequence at the SphI restriction site via Gibson reaction. LG196/LG219 primers were used to amplify the 4x ACE-tRNATrp.UAG The insert was cloned into the G1499_pHDAd plasmid via an in-fusion reaction at the AscI restriction site according to the University of Iowa Viral Vector Core (VVC) pHDAd cloning procedure. Primers used for cloning are listed in Table S2. The HDAd virus production was assessed using VVC.

Flow cytometry

Cultured HEK293T cells (10 cm dishes) at 80% confluency were transiently transfected with 2 µg of sfGFPN150X containing plasmid and 3 µg of ACE-tRNA-containing plasmid. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the cells were washed with 1 × Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) and resuspended in a 2% DPBS + fetal bovine serum (FBS) solution before sfGFP quantification by flow cytometry using a Becton Dickinson LSR II instrument and analyzed using LSRFortessa Analyzers. For the experiment involving the read-through of pCMV-Kir7.1W53X, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with pCMV-eGFP-Kir7.1 W53X and 4 × ACE-tRNATrp.UAG. The cells were collected after 48 h and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature. Following two washes with 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were permeabilized and incubated with anti-Kir7.1 antibody (1:100; sc-398810, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) for 1 h. After washing, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000; A21236, Invitrogen) for an additional hour. Flow cytometry was performed using an Attune Nxt (Thermo Fisher), and the cells were gated for GFP+ cells. Successful read-through was defined as the dual expression of tRNA and Kir7.1 within the cell population, as assessed by GFP-positive gating.

Cell culture and transfection

Human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s minimal essential medium (DMEM, GIBCO, 12800-017), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco™ Fetal Bovine Serum, Qualified, Cat. 26140095), 2 mmol/L GlutaMAX, and penicillin-streptomycin 100 U/mL. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For transfection, HEK293T cells were seeded into a 35 mm dish and transfected using PolyJetTM (Signa Gen) with pCMV-sfGFPWT, pCMV-sfGFPN150X, pCMV-eGFP-Kir7.1WT, and pCMV-eGFPKir7.1W53X when 70% confluence was reached. 4x ACE-tRNATrp.UAG plasmid was co-transfected with either pCMV-sfGFPN150X or pCMV-eGFPKir7.1W53X to allow read-through of the nonsense mutation.

Differentiation and maintenance of iPSC-RPE

The WT hiPSC-RPE and LCA16 hiPSC lines used in this study were generated using hiPSC lines cultured and differentiated into RPE according to a previously described protocol.51–53 Briefly, hiPSCs were maintained on Matrigel-coated plates in mTeSR Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies). For differentiation, cells were harvested using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies) to form embryoid bodies (EBs) and maintained as a suspension culture in mTeSR Plus, which was gradually transitioned to neural induction medium (NIM; DMEM: F12; 1% N2 supplement, 1% MEM nonessential amino acids, 1% L-glutamine, and 2 mg/mL heparin) by day 4. On day 7, EBs were plated onto laminin-coated culture plates to further differentiate into adherent cultures. On day 16, the 3D neural rosette-like structures were removed, and the medium for the remaining adherent cells was changed to retinal differentiation medium (RDM; DMEM/F12 [3.5:1.5], 2% B27 supplement (without retinoic acid), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic) for further differentiation. After ~75 days of differentiation, pigmented RPE cells were purified using magnetically activated cell sorting (MACS) as described by ref. 54 and plated on a laminin-coated surface of interest for the intended studies. Matrigel was purchased from WiCell, and all other tissue culture reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Immunocytochemistry

HEK293T cells were transfected with pCMV-eGFP-Kir7.1WT, pCMV-eGFPKir7.1W53X, or eGFPKir7.1W53X along with 4x ACE-tRNATrp.UAG plasmids were seeded onto coverslips. After 48 h of plating, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by three minutes of PBS washing. WT hiPSC-RPE, LCA16 hiPSC-RPE, and LCA16 hiPSC-RPE transduced with HDAd virus carrying ACE-tRNATrp.UAG (HD tRNATrp.UAG)on the transwells was handled similarly. The cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 5 minutes. Cells were blocked at room temperature for 2 h in a solution containing 2% BSA and 0.25% Triton X-100. Mass spectrometry

Proteins were isolated from WT iPSC-RPE, LCA16 iPSC-RPE, and LCA-iPSC-RPE cells transduced with HD tRNATrp.UAG. Protein concentration was measured using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Three micrograms of each sample were loaded onto a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel and then silver-stained to assess sample quality. The resulting digested peptides were resuspended in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid solution. The quality of the peptides was analyzed using 1 μL of each peptide for MALDI, and the samples were subjected to mass spectrometry if the peptides appeared suitable. For proteomics, 0.5 μg of the sample was loaded onto an Evotip (EVOSEP, Denmark) and analyzed using Trapped Ion Mobility Spectrometry Time-of-Flight (TimsTOF). The samples were run in duplicate using data-dependent acquisition (DDA)-PASEF and data-independent acquisition (DIA)-PASEF with a long-gradient method, at 30 samples/day.

Western blot

Protein samples were prepared by lysing the HEK293T cells transfected with pCMV-eGFP-Kir7.1, pCMV-eGFPKir7.1W53X, or pCMV-eGFPKir7.1W53X with 4x ACE-tRNATrp.UAG plasmids in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) supplemented with 1X protease inhibitor (Thermo Scientific). Untransfected cells and eGFP-transfected cells were used as controls. Proteins from LCA16 hiPSC-RPE cells transduced with HD tRNATrp.UAG were also isolated similarly, using the WT and untreated LCA16 hiPSC-RPE as controls. The concentration of the protein obtained is measured using the BCA assay, and 30 µg of protein is loaded into the well and run on a 10% polyacrylamide gel. The protein was then transferred to a PVDF membrane using the standard wet transfer protocol, followed by blocking it for 2 h in the 3% BSA solution. The membrane was incubated in primary antibody against eGFP (1:500; cat#2955, Cell Signaling Technology), Kir7.1 (1:500; sc-398810, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA), and Na-K-ATPase Recombinant Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody (1:10,000; ST0533, Invitrogen) overnight at 4 °C. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (anti-mouse: 31430, Invitrogen; anti-rabbit: 31460, Invitrogen) of choice was used to incubate the membrane for 1 h at room temperature. Patch-clamp electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on transfected HEK293T cells to determine the functional effects of the tRNATrp.UAG treatment. We also recorded freshly dissociated iPSC-RPE cells to evaluate W53X mutation readthrough after HD tRNATrp.UAG transduction. The extracellular HEPES-buffered Ringer’s solution (HR) included (in mM) 135 NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 1.8 CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 5 mM KCl. The solution pH was adjusted to 7.4 using NaOH, and an osmolality of 300 mOsm was confirmed. The pipette solution contained 30 mM KCl, 83 mM K-gluconate, 5.5 mM EGTA-KOH, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 4 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, and 4 mM adenosine triphosphate (ATP). The solution pH was adjusted to 7.2 using KOH, and the osmolarity was 280 mOsm. For experiments using 135 mM Rb+, equimolar Na+ in HR was substituted, the pH was adjusted to 7.4 using RbOH, and the solution osmolarity was confirmed to be 300 mOsm. Voltage-clamp experiments employed a ramp protocol from −150 to 50 mV, starting from a holding potential of 0 mV. Channel currents were also elicited using a voltage-step protocol from +50 to −150 in 50 mV increments from a holding potential of 0 mV for 300 ms. The pipette resistance ranged from 2.8 to 5.5 MΩ during sealing under GΩ conditions. We used an Axopatch 200 B, a Digidata 1550, and Clampex 11 for data acquisition, and analyzed the data using Clampfit 11.2 (Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

Animals

Kcnj13+/W53X mice were bred and maintained at the BRMC, the animal facility of the University of Wisconsin–Madison, in a controlled environment with a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, a humidity range of 55–60%, and a 12-h light:12-h dark cycle. These animals were used to test whether tRNATrp.UAG therapy restores channel function following the disruption of the wild-type allele. All animals were handled in accordance with the protocol approved by the UW-Madison Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). For ethical purposes, we minimize the number of animals used whenever possible.

Anesthesia and subretinal injection

After weighing, the animals (n = 4) were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) cocktails. A drop of 1% tropicamide was applied to the eyes of the mice (n = 5) for pupil dilation, and proparacaine hydrochloride was used for topical anesthesia. Mice were placed on a heating pad to maintain their body temperature. A transcorneal subretinal injection was administered to these mice to deliver the viruses. We used a 34-gauge blunt-end needle attached to a 10 µl Hamilton syringe for injections. First, a puncture was made using a 30 G disposable needle in the scleral region through which the 34 G needle was inserted to reach the subretinal space. Viral particles were delivered to the subretinal space using an UltraMicroPump3 (WPI, Sarasota, FL, USA) at 20 nl/s. The bleb was visualized to confirm the injection’s success. Antibiotic ointment was applied to the eyes after the injection to prevent injury-related infection.

W53X mice and WT allele disruption

Kcnj13W53X/+ mice were created using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome engineering, as previously described,32 because Kcnj13W53X/W53X mice could not survive beyond postnatal day 1. For this purpose, an in vivo evaluation of the tRNATrp.UAG readthrough was carried out after disrupting the wild-type allele.

For wild-type allele disruption in Kcnj13W53X/+ heterozygous mice, custom adeno-associated virus serotype 5 (AAV5) vectors were prepared from Genecopoeia (Rockville, MD) that carried CRISPR-Cas9 components, including spCas9 protein and guide RNA (sgRNA) “GAATCCTAATGGACATGCGCTGG” (titer-1.5E + 13 vg/ml). The sgRNA sequences were selected based on previous validation.32 AAV5 was chosen as the delivery vehicle for this translational study due to its specificity for RPE cells. It has advantages over lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), as they exhibit broader distribution when delivered subretinally. Another reason to use AAV5 is that the current study focuses on translational applications rather than on basic gene-editing research. AAV5 (3E + 10 GC/eye) was injected, and AAV-mediated delivery enabled efficient disruption for subsequent functional analysis after generation of Kcnj13W53X/ΔR.

Kir7.1 protein is not expressed in the inner retina and is exclusively present in the apical processes of the RPE cells. Hence, the disruption of an allele in retinal cells will not affect the function of the retina. We have previously conducted the efficacy and specificity of the guide using the mouse fibroblast cells.32

All HDAd viruses used in this study were produced by UI Viral Vector Core (University of Iowa). HDAd viruses used in this study carried either CMV-sfGFP, CMV-sfGFPN150X, or 5’Leader-tRNATrp.UAG (4X_Trp-tRNA). Kcnj13W53X/ΔR mice were injected with HDAd viruses to evaluate the ability of tRNATrp.UAG to detect tRNATrp.UAG read-through. RPE flat-mount imaging

On day 14, wild-type mice injected with HDAd viruses carrying GFP reporters with TAG nonsense mutations and 4X_Trp-tRNA were sacrificed and flat-mounted to evaluate GFP expression after successful readthrough translation by ACE-tRNA. Briefly, the injected eyes were harvested, and an incision was made at the ora serrata using a 30-gauge needle. The eyes were incised along the corneal edge, and the lenses were removed. The retina was removed by careful dissection, and the remaining eyecup was incised radially to flatten it. The flattened RPE layer was mounted on a coverslip and imaged with NIS Elements on a Nikon-C2 confocal microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc.).

Electroretinography

The mice were dark-adapted overnight a day before the ERG procedure. ERG was performed as previously described. A drop of 1% tropicamide was applied to the eyes of the mice for pupil dilation. As mentioned previously, mice were anesthetized by injecting a cocktail of ketamine and xylazine at concentrations of 80 and 10 mg/kg, respectively. The entire procedure was performed under a very dim red light, and the mice’s body temperature was maintained at 37 °C by placing them on a heating pad. The topical application of 2.5% hemicellulose ophthalmic solution (Goniovisc, HUB Pharmaceuticals LLC, CA) maintained eye moisture and enhanced electrical conductivity. A contact electrode was placed on the eyes, and ERG was performed using the Espion E3 system with a Ganzfeld color dome (Diagnosis LLC, Lowell, MA, USA) to ensure uniform illumination. A seven-step protocol with sequential increments in flash intensity (0.03 to 30 cd.s/m²) for 300 ms, with 2 s intervals between flashes, was used for scotopic ERG to measure the a-wave and b-wave. The c-wave was acquired by flashing the eyes with light intensities of 2.5 and 25 cd/s/m² and was recorded for 4 s. The acquired waveforms were analyzed using the Espion software V6 (Diagnosis LLC, MA) and Origin 2020 software (OriginLab Corp., MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Origin (version 9.0) with a two-tailed Student’s T-test. ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc tests were used for multiple comparisons. Data were expressed as the mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05.
