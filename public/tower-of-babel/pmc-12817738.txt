Prognosis of patients with a history of organ transplantation during active COVID-19 infection
Authors: Piątek-Dalewska Olga, Kuziemski Krzysztof, Petra M. Grešner, Zielińska Alicja, Stefaniak Tomasz, Gołębiewska Martyna, Palus Damian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSolid organ transplant recipients were among the most vulnerable groups during the COVID-19 pandemic. Chronic immunosuppression and multiple comorbidities greatly increase the risk of severe illness and death. Despite global advances in vaccination and treatment, practical tools for early risk assessment in this group are still limited. This study aims to address this gap by identifying simple, widely available laboratory markers that can be used upon hospital admission to predict short-term outcomes in COVID-19 patients with a history of organ transplantation.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed a cohort of solid organ transplant recipients hospitalized with confirmed COVID-19 infection at the University Clinical Center in Gdańsk, Poland, between 2020 and 2022. Patients were admitted to either a general COVID-19 ward or an intensive care unit based on disease severity. Data were collected from electronic medical records and included demographic characteristics, chest imaging, and routine laboratory tests: lymphocyte count, serum creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin. Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the association of these variables with in-hospital mortality.ResultsOur analysis identified that elevated levels of serum creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin were significantly associated with increased risk of in-hospital mortality. Conversely, a higher lymphocyte count at admission was associated with better survival. These markers are widely available, inexpensive, and can be assessed during the initial diagnostic workup.ConclusionsThis study offers a practical, evidence-based approach to early risk stratification in transplant recipients with COVID-19. The identified laboratory markers are not only simple and accessible but also clinically meaningful, providing frontline clinicians with actionable information during the early stages of care. In light of the continued presence of COVID-19 and the likelihood of future viral outbreaks, these findings have broad implications for the management of immunosuppressed patients. The study provides valuable insights into a currently underserved area of transplant medicine and has strong potential to inform clinical practice across diverse healthcare settings.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12269-4.

Introduction

Viral infections, especially of the respiratory system, have been a threat to the health of the population for many years. In recent years, the World has seen the emergence of new and rapidly spreading viruses, e.g., MERS (Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus) and hMPV (human metapneumovirus). Recently, infections caused by SARS-CoV-2 (Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2) have joined this group, and the virus now represents a persistent component among circulating pathogens in the human population. The first SARS-CoV-2 infection was detected in November 2019 in Wuhan, the capital of Hubei Province, China, with a population of 12 million. In March 2020, the coronavirus pandemic spread to Europe, North America, and other continents. After over 3 years, on the 5th of May 2023, the WHO (World Health Organization) lifted the global pandemic status [1]. COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) has changed previous views on the spread of infectious diseases. It significantly impacted health and survival in the population, contributing to excess deaths. Despite the end of the pandemic, COVID-19 remains a significant clinical and social problem. The initial stages of the pandemic were characterized by multiple early and late organ complications, respiratory failure, and substantial mortality. In subsequent stages of the pandemic, effective new drugs and widely available preventive vaccines were introduced, gradually reducing complications and mortality. Despite this, new cases are still being reported. The disease manifests with a broad, variable range of symptoms and varying severity. However, the symptoms of COVID-19 infection are varied: from an asymptomatic course with minor symptoms of a respiratory infection to severe lung failure requiring intensive care. In extreme cases, it can still lead to multi-organ failure and death [2]. The course and prognosis are influenced by advanced age, male gender [3], comorbidities, particularly the co-occurrence of obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, circulatory and lung diseases, and cancer [4, 5]. Organ transplant recipients create a group that is particularly susceptible to full-blown disease and poorer prognosis. These patients are subject to long-term immunosuppression, in whom comorbidities are much more frequently noted [6–8]. Long-term maintenance immunosuppression with a calcineurin inhibitor weakens the immune system and prolongs viral clearance. It may also reduce the severity of the virus-induced systemic inflammatory response. Reducing the inflammatory reaction may limit the occurrence of ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome), i.e. the most severe complication in this disease [9]. The global burden of COVID-19 cases prompts research to understand how risk factors and comorbidities may influence COVID-19 disease severity, ICU (Intensive Care Unit) admissions, and mortality. Organ transplant recipients experience an increased risk of respiratory and multi-organ failure during hospitalization due to generalized inflammation not only in the lungs but also in other organs [6–8]. For this reason, it is essential to identify markers that affect early prognosis in this patient group. The aim of the study was to identify simple and useful laboratory andbiochemical blood markers, as well as radiological features, which could predict the risk of death and short-term prognosis in organ transplant recipients hospitalized with COVID-19.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of selected laboratory, biochemical, and radiological parameters influencing early prognosis in organ transplant recipients hospitalized in the acute phase of COVID-19 infection. For this purpose, data from patients hospitalized at the University Clinical Center in Gdańsk, Poland (UCC) were used. Laboratory test results were analyzed according to the UCC Gdańsk laboratory’s standard. The UCC is the largest academic hospital in northern Poland, with the highest reference level, hospitalizing patients requiring multi-specialist care. The hospital is one of the leading transplant centers for heart, kidney, lung, and liver organs. During the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 to 2022, two hospital wards were designated for hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Patients who did not require intubation were hospitalised in the stable COVID-19 ward (COVID-S), which, depending on demand and the pandemic stage, had 50 to 75 beds. Patients in unstable, life-threatening conditions requiring intubation and intensive care were admitted to the COVID-Intensive Care Unit (COVID-ICU). All imaging studies included in our analysis were evaluated by experienced radiologists at our institution. They were assessed using the standardized CO-RADS (Covid-19 Reporting and Data System) scoring system, which provides a structured, reproducible approach to interpreting radiological findings related to COVID-19.

Group demographics

The study involved organ transplant recipients aged 18 or older who had confirmed COVID-19 infection diagnosed by a PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test, exhibited clinical symptoms of acute lower respiratory tract infection, and had significant comorbidities. The primary criteria for hospitalization in the COVID-S ward included:

1) oxygen peripheral saturation (SpO2) lower than 95% requiring oxygen therapy,

2) oxygen peripheral saturation (SpO2) equal to or higher than 95% with symptoms such as shortness of breath, severe cough, syncope, fatigue, diarrhea,

3) complications of COVID-19 infection requiring hospitalization with SpO2 equal or higher than 95%,

4) maintaining continuity of medical care in patients who had tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 during hospitalization in another ward [7].

For patients with confirmed COVID-19 requiring intubation, at least two of the following criteria, including criterion number 3, had to be met:

1. Exacerbation of respiratory failure regardless of non-invasive ventilation methods as a result of:

(a) oxygen peripheral saturation and partial oxygen pressure remaining persistently low.

(b) intolerance to non-invasive ventilation.

(c) insufficient non-invasive ventilation with worsening symptoms of acute respiratory failure.

2. Surgery, injury, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation requiring intubation.

3. The patient required intensive care and could benefit from hospitalization in the ICU as determined by an anesthesiologist according to national guidance [10].

Early-phase post-myocardial infarction patients, patients after ischemic stroke and gastrointestinal hemorrhage, as well as patients requiring dialysis or administration of pressor amines were not admitted to COVID- ICU unless they required intubation and mechanical ventilation. Instead, they were treated in COVID- S ward. Advanced metastatic cancer and DNR (do-not-resuscitate) disqualified patients from transfer to the ICU.

Patients were ready for discharge from COVID-ICU:

1. After discontinuation of mechanical ventilation or.

2. If further medical care could be continued in the COVID-S or pulmonology ward [11].

Study design

Upon admission to the COVID-S ward, blood tests (complete blood count, hemoglobin concentration, biochemical analysis, C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, creatinine, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), procalcitonin (PCT), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), liver enzymes: aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were performed in all patients. Chest radiographs were performed according to indications (High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT), angio-CT, or chest X-ray). Radiographs already conducted in other wards were accepted. Radiographs were performed only when the results could change the course of action or when COVID-19 complications were suspected. The severity of symptoms was assessed using the CRB-65 scale (confusion, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and 65 years of age) [12, 13]. The CRB-65 scale was used to assess pneumonia severity. Depending on the clinical situation, individual tests were repeated or supplemented with additional ones when necessary. The severity of changes in the lung parenchyma was assessed according to the generally accepted CO-RADS scale [14]. In subsequent analyses, laboratory and biochemical blood parameters measured at the beginning, in the middle and the end of hospitalization were considered individually for each patient, depending on the time of hospitalization. These time points correspond to the first, middle, and last available laboratory tests performed during hospitalization, and were defined separately for each patient based on the total duration of their inpatient care. This approach enabled a more individualized and dynamic assessment of parameter changes over the course of the disease. In the analyzed radiological images, ground-glass opacities, paving stones, consolidations in the lung parenchyma, inflammatory changes, fluid in the pleural cavities, nodules, and pulmonary embolism were observed. All radiological examinations were reviewed by board-certified thoracic radiologists, ensuring standardized, expert-level interpretation. Length of hospitalization, number of comorbidities, and disease stage were assessed. Although prior immunosuppressive therapy was considered (Table A – Supplementary Material) due to its potential impact on immune response and clinical outcomes, it was not included in the analysis because of insufficient data on treatment duration, dosage, and treatment modifications. Available COVID-19 treatment, complications during hospitalization, and death were taken into account. Treatment was consistent with current knowledge and the guidelines of the Polish Society of Infectious Diseases [15]. Therapeutic strategies were continuously adapted in response to emerging recommendations and updates introduced during the early phases of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Complications related to COVID-19 considered in the study included:

clinical complications:

death, dialysis in the COVID ward, prolonged hospitalization > 10 days, need for respiratory therapy;

radiological changes in the lung parenchyma:

fluid in CT, ground-glass opacities, paving stones, consolidation in the CT, embolism in angio-CT, other changes;

laboratory changes:

lymphopenia (< 1000 ul-1), anemia (blood hemoglobin concentration < 10 g/dl), high CRP concentration in blood plasma (> 100 mg/l), high creatinine concentration (> 1.3 mg/dl).

This study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Gdansk, Poland (NKBBN/327/2022). The informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were presented as either mean +/- standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the normality of data distribution, which was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk W test. Nominal and/or ordinal data were presented as both absolute and relative counts (percentages). Simple correlations between two quantitative variables were sought through Spearman’s rank correlation method, with the strength of correlation being expressed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (rSP). In the case of correlation analysis between two ordinal variables, Kendall’s tau correlation was used instead. Simple between-group differences were tested for statistical significance using the Mann-Whitney U test. Associations between the occurrence of COVID-19 complications and the length of the patient’s hospitalization were analyzed utilizing Fisher’s exact test. Plausible demise risk predictors were identified using simple univariate Cox proportional hazard regression models, unadjusted for comorbidities, disease severity or treatment. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data analysis was carried out using R [16].

Identification of prognostic factors of COVID-19-related death

The Cox proportional hazard regression model showed that the risk of death in COVID-19 patients increases significantly with increasing plasma creatinine concentrations, regardless of the time at which this increase was observed (beginning of hospitalization: HR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.1–1.7, p = 0.003; mid-hospitalization: HR = 1.5, 95% CI: 1.1–1.8, p = 0.004; end of hospitalization: HR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.5–3.7, p = 0.0001).

Additionally, the risk of death was shown to increase significantly with the increase in other biochemical parameters, such as AST (HR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00-1.04; p = 0.016) and PCT (HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.07–1.48; p = 0.006) measured at the beginning of hospitalization and with the increase in CRP (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.01; p = 0.04) and PCT (HR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.0-1.7; p = 0.052) measured during hospitalization. The risk of death also increases significantly with increasing CRB-65 (HR = 2.6, 95% CI: 1.3–5.2; p = 0.007).

Contrary to this the risk of death decreased significantly with increasing GFR.mdrd (glomerular filtration rate - Modification Of Diet In Renal Disease; HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.82–0.96; p = 0.005) and GFR CKD (glomerular filtration rate Chronic Kidney Disease; HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.96; p = 0.005) in plasma at the beginning of hospitalization, as well as with increasing lymphocyte count in the middle (HR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.05-1.00; p = 0.051) and at the end of hospitalization (HR = 0.3, 95% CI: 0.1–0.9; p = 0.0323). The impact of anemia and lymphopenia on COVID-19-related deaths among kidney transplant patients with active COVID-19 infection

The risk of death in kidney transplant patients with active COVID-19 infection significantly decreased as lymphocyte count increased at the end of hospitalization (HR = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.0-0.80, p < 0.05), indicating that lymphocyte recovery may serve as a marker of improved immune function and a better prognosis. No such association was observed for hemoglobin levels at any time point. Due to the much smaller sample size of other organ transplant recipients, reliable subgroup comparisons were not possible; therefore, our analysis was limited to kidney transplant patients. Discussion

This retrospective single-center study analyzed early in-hospital mortality in patients with COVID-19 following solid organ transplantation (SOT), with a particular focus on kidney transplant recipients. In Poland, kidney transplantation is the most common solid organ transplant procedure, which is reflected in our study population. National data indicate that in 2020, 717 kidney transplants were performed from deceased donors and 31 from living donors; in 2021, 709 were from deceased donors and 44 from living donors [17]. As a result, kidney transplant recipients comprised the majority of our cohort.

In the acute stage of COVID-19 infection, the respiratory system is mainly involved, but other organs, including transplanted organs, are also directly affected. The virus enters target cells by endocytosis via the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor on the cell membrane. ACE2 is present in the lungs, kidneys, heart, and bowels [18]. This has a fundamental prognostic significance for patients after organ transplantation with concomitant acute COVID-19 infection. In COVID-19, the expression of the ACE2 receptor is significantly reduced. The result is an increased concentration of angiotensin II (A2). A2 has a pro-inflammatory effect, increases neutrophil infiltration in organs and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Consequently, the permeability of blood vessels increases, damaging end organs such as kidneys, liver, heart, and lungs [18–20].

Patients after SOT are at an increased risk of severe infections due to long-term immunosuppressive therapy, which diminishes the immune response and predisposes them to opportunistic infections. In the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection, this immunocompromised state may increase the risk of systemic complications, including multiorgan failure. There is also evidence suggesting that transplanted organs may be directly targeted by the virus, leading to endothelial inflammation within the microcirculation, mediated by a cytokine storm, which can culminate in organ dysfunction and death [21]. Immunosuppression defines the studied population and likely influences both infection risk and outcomes. However, data on specific regimens were not available and thus not analyzed, which may limit the interpretation of our results. Interestingly, the study by Kates et al. [22] did not demonstrate a clear association between the intensity of initial immunosuppression and mortality. Factors such as time since transplantation, type of transplanted organ, recent increase in immunosuppression, or the number and type of maintenance medications were not associated with worse outcomes. However, caution should be exercised in interpreting these results, and further research in this area is warranted.

While our study provides valuable insights into the clinical course of COVID-19 in transplant recipients, it is important to emphasize that the sample size was limited. This small cohort represents a specific, high-risk patient population and, although clinically relevant, our findings should be interpreted with caution. The retrospective, single-center further limits the generalizability of the results.

Therefore, there is a clear need for further validation of these findings in larger, prospective, multi-center cohorts. Such studies are essential to confirm the observed associations, identify modifiable risk factors, and ultimately inform targeted interventions to improve outcomes in transplant recipients with COVID-19. The results obtained indicate that elevated plasma creatinine levels may represent a significant prognostic factor in patients following organ transplantation. Regardless of the timing during hospitalization when the increase was observed, higher creatinine levels were consistently associated with an increased risk of mortality. This suggests that renal dysfunction—whether occurring early or later during the hospital stay may have important prognostic implications and may reflect the overall severity of the clinical condition. An interesting and somewhat paradoxical finding was the inverse relationship observed between elevated creatinine level and longer hospitalization, defined as a stay exceeding 10 days. While creatinine is typically considered a marker of impaired kidney function and deteriorating clinical status, in our cohort, elevated levels may have indicated earlier recognition of critical illness. This, in turn, could have led to more rapid clinical decision-making and intensified management, including earlier transfer to the intensive care unit. As a result, these patients, despite their serious condition, may have received more prompt treatment, potentially leading to a shorter overall hospital stay. While this interpretation requires further investigation, it may carry significant clinical implications, highlighting the value of early identification of high-risk patients.

The rapid increase in creatinine level in people with COVID-19 infection is likely due to the virus spreading through the bloodstream to the kidneys, leading to renal cell necrosis [23]. In patients with high creatinine level, a more severe course of the underlying COVID-19 disease was observed. Our analysis showed that dialysis therapy conducted before admission to COVID-19 wards and dialysis in COVID-S did not affect the occurrence of COVID-19 complications.

Our findings also suggest that in addition to creatinine, other biochemical and laboratory markers may serve as predictors of poor outcomes. At the beginning of hospitalization, elevated levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), procalcitonin (PCT), and higher CRB-65 scores were associated with worse prognosis. These parameters may reflect ongoing inflammatory processes, organ injury, or burden on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems—findings consistent with previous studies in transplant recipients and patients with severe infections.

In the middle phase of hospitalization, persistently elevated CRP and PCT levels—both classic markers of inflammation and infection—were also associated with reduced survival.

These continued elevations may indicate an inadequate response to treatment or the development of infectious complications. CRP is widely recognized to correlate with disease severity [24–26], while increases in PCT are often linked to bacterial infections, which can represent late complications of COVID-19. The synthesis of PCT is triggered by bacterial toxins and proinflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-6, a key mediator of the cytokine storm [24]. In complicated viral COVID-19 cases, rising PCT levels suggest bacterial superinfection and necessitate treatment intensification, including appropriate antibiotic therapy. Such secondary bacterial infections are difficult to diagnose and frequently lead to serious complications, such as pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Published studies have demonstrated that these infections significantly increase mortality in critically ill COVID-19 patients [24]. Accordingly, CRP and PCT remain among the most effective and sensitive biomarkers for predicting COVID-19 progression [26, 27].

A characteristic feature at the onset of COVID-19 infection is lymphopenia, or low lymphocyte count, which tends to improve as the patient’s overall condition stabilizes and response to treatment becomes favourable. Notably, in our study, increased lymphocyte count at the end of hospitalization was associated with a better prognosis, possibly reflecting recovery of immune function and more effective disease management. This association appears particularly relevant in transplant recipients, whose immune response is deliberately modulated by immunosuppressive therapy. Indeed, an increase in lymphocyte concentration during hospitalization may serve as an important prognostic indicator of infection course and early mortality risk in this vulnerable population.

Another key biomarker of the risk of death in the study group was increased AST concentration. The mechanism of liver dysfunction in COVID-19 is by secondary damage to hepatocytes in a systemic inflammatory response and the use of hepatotoxic drugs, including antiviral drugs, used to treat COVID-19. Due to highly variable treatment regimens, changing clinical guidelines, and modifications made during hospitalization—especially early in the pandemic—we did not analyze the type or duration of antiviral therapy, which may have influenced liver function and outcomes. Similar hepatocyte damage may be induced by hypoxemia, a characteristic feature of full-blown COVID-19 [28–30].

In transplant patients, several additional diseases often coexist. The type and severity of diseases may affect early and long-term prognosis [31]. It has been shown that the coexistence of chronic kidney disease may be an independent risk factor for death [32–35]. Our study also analyzed comorbidity. Interestingly, we did not confirm that the number of these diseases significantly correlated with the degree of lung parenchyma involvement on radiological examinations, even though the marginally increased (p = 0.055) degree of lung parenchyma involvement among patients with more than 7 (median) comorbidities may warrant further investigation.

The type of radiological examination and the need for contrast often depend on renal function. Chest CT with contrast medium was performed when there was a significant suspicion of pulmonary embolism. In the transplant recipients, due to coexisting renal failure, chest CT without contrast was performed if indicated. In some patients, due to their serious health condition and high creatinine concentration, radiological examinations were not performed. Additionally, a 6-point radiological CORADS scale was introduced to diagnose COVID-19, assessing inflammatory changes in the lung parenchyma. The assessment of patients with a positive PCR result was problematic. A positive PCR result classified patients as CORADS 6, i.e., the most advanced stage of the disease, even without changes in the lung parenchyma. This approach significantly influenced patient assessment and therapeutic options [36, 37].

Along with the stages of the pandemic, variable treatment was used in accordance with current guidelines. In Poland, the Polish Society of Epidemiologists and Infectious Disease Physicians’ guidelines were widely used. These guidelines comprehensively assessed diagnostic and therapeutic options depending on the disease stage and the pandemic period [14]. Advanced treatment reflected the patient’s serious condition or the presence of complications requiring treatment. In the study group, secondary bacterial superinfections, including those caused by opportunistic bacteria, were common, necessitating treatment escalation and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. If profound anemia was detected, treatment with blood products was initiated. In people with increasing respiratory failure, HFNO (High-Flow Nasal Oxygen) and NIV (non-invasive mechanical ventilation) were used, and patients with critical respiratory and multi-organ failure were referred to the ICU-COVID department. In the analyzed study, the form of treatment was not interpreted as an independent factor increasing the risk of death from COVID-19, due to frequent changes in treatment guidelines over time, especially in the early phase of the pandemic.

An important limitation of the study is the lack of data on the SARS-CoV-2 vaccination status and prior COVID-19 infection history in the study population. In Poland, the vaccination campaign began only in December 2020, meaning many patients hospitalized during earlier pandemic waves had no access to vaccination. Both vaccination and prior infection are known to significantly influence disease severity, immune response, and the prognostic value of inflammatory markers. This is supported by the study of Carmen Infante-Domínguez et al., which demonstrated that unvaccinated solid organ transplant recipients had significantly worse clinical outcomes and higher all-cause mortality compared to vaccinated patients [38]. Therefore, future studies should incorporate these variables to allow a more comprehensive analysis of prognostic factors in this vulnerable group.

Limitations of the study

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. This was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results to broader populations. The relatively small sample size reduces the study’s statistical power, potentially limiting the ability to detect significant correlations or differences. Furthermore, key clinical variables, such as time since organ transplantation and duration of immunosuppressive therapy, were not included in the analysis, despite their likely impact on patient outcomes. The type and intensity of immunosuppressive regimens used were also not assessed, although they could have had a significant impact on disease progression and prognosis. Another significant limitation is the lack of data on patients’ SARS-CoV-2 vaccination status and prior COVID-19 infection history. Both factors are known to significantly influence disease severity, immune response, and the prognostic significance of inflammatory markers. Furthermore, patients were admitted to the COVID-S ward from different internal medicine and surgical departments and were housed in isolated multi-bed rooms, which may have contributed to a higher risk of complications and influenced the clinical course of the disease.

Additionally, due to highly variable treatment regimens, evolving clinical guidelines, and frequent modifications made during hospitalization—particularly in the early phase of the pandemic—the type and duration of antiviral therapy were not analyzed. This may have influenced liver function parameters and clinical outcomes, representing another limitation of the study.

Conclusion

The analysis of hospitalization among organ transplant recipients with active COVID-19 provides preliminary insights that may be useful in the diagnosis of viral infections and in estimating prognosis in immunosuppressed patients. Rapid diagnosis and intensive, multidisciplinary care remain essential in this high-risk group. Although individual prognosis is difficult to predict, our findings suggest that elevated creatinine, AST, CRP, and PCT levels may be associated with increased mortality risk, whereas higher lymphocyte counts may indicate a more favorable outcome. However, the final study cohort included only 38 transplant recipients, limiting the analysis’s statistical power. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory. Further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is needed to confirm the prognostic value of these potential biomarkers and to strengthen the clinical applicability of our findings.
