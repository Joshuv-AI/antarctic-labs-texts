Lamotrigine-induced Stevens-Johnson syndrome in the setting of mitochondrial encephalomyopathy: A case report
Authors: Ye Tian, Teng Chen, Jingwen Liu, Juan Wang, Lihong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 67-year-old male with Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy with Lactic Acidosis and Stroke-Like Episodes (MELAS) who developed life-threatening Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) more than one month after initiating lamotrigine (LTG) for epilepsy management. Despite prompt LTG discontinuation and aggressive immunomodulatory therapy, the patient succumbed to severe infection and multiple organ failure. LTG-induced SJS is associated with multiple risk factors. The immune-activating microenvironment resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction may potentiate the risk of LTG-induced SJS. Lamotrigine should be used cautiously for epilepsy in high-risk patients, particularly those with mitochondrial encephalomyopathy (ME), and comprehensive evaluation is required to minimize severe hypersensitivity reactions.

Introduction

Mitochondrial diseases encompass a heterogeneous group of disorders caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear DNA (nDNA). These genetic defects impair mitochondrial structure and function, leading to disrupted ATP synthesis and subsequent cellular energy deficiency, which often involves in multiple organ systems. When the central nervous system and skeletal muscle are primarily affected, it is classified as mitochondrial encephalomyopathy (ME).1,2 Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy with Lactic Acidosis and Stroke-Like Episodes (MELAS) is the most prevalent ME subtype, characterized by recurrent stroke-like episodes (SLEs), epileptic seizures, cognitive impairment, psychiatric symptoms, hyperlactatemia, and muscle weakness or fatigue. 3

Lamotrigine (LTG) is a widely used broad-spectrum anti-epileptic drug (AED) and is considered a first-line agent for epilepsy management in ME patients. 4 However, LTG carries a risk of inducing rare but life-threatening drug-related cutaneous adverse reactions, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), 5 a severe hypersensitivity-mediated cutaneous disorder characterized by polymorphous erythematous eruptions, extensive mucosal involvement, and potential systemic complications involving internal organs.6,7

Emerging evidence indicates mitochondrial dysfunction in ME patients triggers marked immune activation.8,9 This aberrant immune microenvironment likely heightens hypersensitivity risk to medications such as LTG. Herein, we report a rare case of MELAS—presenting initially with recurrent seizures—in which SJS developed post-LTG administration. The aim is to alert clinicians to conduct comprehensive pre-treatment evaluation (including immune status assessment) before LTG initiation in ME patients, minimizing rare but severe adverse reactions and guiding rational clinical decisions.

Case report

A 67-year-old retired male was admitted to our Department of Neurology with chief complaint of paroxysmal convulsions for 2 months, worsening in the past 14 hours. He had two unprovoked generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS) 2 and 1 month prior to admission, was hospitalized twice locally, and achieved stable symptom control with sodium valproate post-discharge. Fourteen hours before admission, recurrent GTCS progressed to status epilepticus, and emergency head computed tomography (CT) showed patchy hypodense lesions in the right frontoparietal lobe.

His medical history included 11 years of paroxysmal involuntary hand twitching (untreated), hypertension (>20 years), right carotid artery stenting (7 years prior), hypothyroidism (7 years), sinus bradycardia (17 years), and right eye blindness due to trauma (>20 years)—all well-controlled with regular medication. He had a 30-year smoking history, with no family history of similar neurological or genetic disorders.

On admission, combination antiepileptic therapy consisting of sodium valproate 0.5g once daily, levetiracetam 0.5g twice daily, and phenobarbital sodium injection 0.1g twice daily was initiated. While GTCS were controlled, intermittent limb myoclonus, hallucinations, impaired higher cortical function, and generalized muscle weakness developed. Follow-up brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed extension of lesions to the right frontoparietal-occipitotemporal lobe with left cerebellar crossed diaschisis; magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) revealed a prominent lactate peak in the right frontal lobe lesion. ME was suspected, so sodium valproate and phenobarbital sodium were discontinued, levetiracetam was escalated to 1g twice daily, and LTG was added at an initial dose of 25mg once daily, with gradual titration to 50 mg twice daily by the fourth week for antiepileptic treatment, leading to partial symptom improvement. Mitochondrial genetic testing (Beijing MyGenostics) identified a pathogenic mtDNA mutation (m.8993T>C), confirming the diagnosis of MELAS. Post-discharge, he maintained therapy with levetiracetam (1g, twice daily) and LTG (50mg, twice daily).​

Twenty days post-discharge, oral ulcers developed, rapidly progressing to mucosal bleeding, erosion, and inability to eat. Generalized dark red eruptions, petechiae, and ecchymoses (predominantly on palms and soles, some coalescing; Figure 1) and fever (max 39°C) followed. Follow-up head CT showed increased extent of patchy hypodense lesions in the right cerebral hemisphere.

Laboratory examinations: Complete blood count: leukocyte count 1.91×109/L (decreased); hypersensitive C-reactive protein 102.2 mg/L (elevated); procalcitonin 2.74 ng/ml (elevated); erythrocyte sedimentation rate 29 mm/h (elevated). Cytokines: interleukin-6 12.48 pg/ml (elevated). Autoantibodies: ANA (IIF) 1:100 (+). Immunoglobulins: IgG 8.68 g/L; IgA 0.7 g/L (decreased); IgM 0.67 g/L. Thyroid function: thyroid-stimulating hormone 6.27 μIU/ml (elevated). Complement levels, rheumatologic panel, anticardiolipin antibodies, Treponema pallidum antibody, anti-HCV antibody, anti-HIV antibody, and hepatitis B serology were unremarkable.

Following administration of antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), the patient developed acute fever and typical skin eruptions, with epidermal detachment involving less than 10% of the total body surface area and severe mucosal involvement of the eyes, oral cavity, and genitalia. A diagnosis of Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) was established after multidisciplinary consultation (dermatology, stomatology). An adverse drug reaction induced by AEDs, especially LTG, was highly suspected, with an ALDEN score of 5, which was the highest among all suspected agents. Unfortunately, further histopathological examination was not performed for confirmation. LTG was discontinued, and multimodal therapy was initiated: compound chlorhexidine + Kangfuxin liquid (gargling/topical compression) for oral lesions; dexamethasone sodium phosphate + methylprednisolone sodium succinate for immunomodulation; topical desonide cream + mupirocin ointment for skin lesions.​

Following glucocorticoid therapy, the patient’s oral ulcers, skin rash, petechiae, and ecchymoses showed slight improvement. However, intermittent high fever persisted, and inflammatory markers remained elevated. Discussion

In this case, LTG administration was followed by oral mucosal ulceration, fever, and generalized cutaneous lesions (eruptions, petechiae, ecchymoses), leading to the diagnosis of SJS. As a triazine-derived aromatic AED, LTG carries a higher SJS risk than non-aromatic AEDs. SJS is a type IV hypersensitivity reaction triggered by medications, infections, or non-infectious conditions, with genetic predisposition playing a key role in susceptibility. 10 Mechanistically, LTG can directly bind to T cell receptors (TCRs), initiating T cell activation and subsequent immune-mediated tissue damage. 11 LTG is primarily metabolized by hepatic glucuronosyltransferases (UDPGTs) into two non-toxic metabolites: LTG-N-2-glucuronide and LTG-N-5-glucuronide. When UDPGT activity is inhibited (e.g., by concurrent medication use), LTG metabolism shifts to an alternative pathway involving cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes, resulting in the formation of toxic arene oxide metabolites. These reactive intermediates can be further detoxified by epoxide hydrolase or glutathione transferase; however, deficiency or dysfunction of these enzymes leads to arene oxide accumulation. This accumulation can directly induce cell death or act as hapten precursors, binding to T cells and triggering excessive immune responses.12,13 In addition, genetic factors, most notably HLA alleles, confer a higher risk of SJS among Chinese and Southeast Asian individuals. A meta-analysis revealed that HLA-B*1502 is a risk allele for LTG-induced SJS in Chinese populations. 14

Notably, sodium valproate not only inhibits UDPGT activity but also depletes glutathione transferase levels, further increasing the accumulation of toxic LTG metabolites and thereby elevating the risk of SJS. 12 In this patient, valproic acid was administered continuously prior to the consideration of ME. Although LTG was initiated after the discontinuation of valproic acid, the serum concentration of valproic acid was not monitored. Therefore, the possibility that the residual effect of valproic acid exacerbated the risk of LTG-induced SJS cannot be excluded.

In addition, epidemiological data show that adults—especially those aged more than 65 years—have a significantly higher risk of drug-related SJS than other age groups, likely due to impaired drug metabolism, immune dysfunction, and comorbidity burden. Advanced age is also a well-recognized predictor of poor SJS prognosis; concurrent risk factors (e.g., underlying diseases, infections) further elevate adverse outcome risk, attributed to more frequent and severe complications, poor treatment tolerance, and increased long-term sequelae. 10

Furthermore, a unique feature of this case is that the patient’s confirmed mitochondrial dysfunction may have created an immune-activating microenvironment predisposing to severe drug hypersensitivity reactions, thereby potentially amplifying the risk of LTG-induced SJS. Oxidative stress (OS) from excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) is a core pathogenic mechanism in ME-related epilepsy. 15 Mitochondria are the primary intracellular source of ROS; under physiological conditions, ROS play essential roles in cell signaling and immune defense. 16 However, mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts the balance between ROS production and scavenging, leading to excessive ROS accumulation. 17 In ME, elevated ROS levels disrupt intracellular calcium (Ca2+) homeostasis, resulting in increased cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations. This Ca2+ overload induces excessive neuronal excitability, ultimately precipitating epileptic seizures. 18 In the present case, mitochondrial dysfunction likely contributed to ROS overproduction. Excessive ROS can activate inflammasomes (e.g., the NLRP3 inflammasome), promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18). These cytokines, in conjunction with ROS, trigger local and systemic inflammatory responses, leading to tissue damage and widespread immune activation.19,20 This pro-inflammatory state may enhance the susceptibility of ME patients to LTG-induced, hypersensitivity-mediated SJS.

Mitochondrial dysfunction may also lead to aberrant release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm or extracellular space. Released mtDNA can be recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) of the innate immune system—most notably Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9). TLR9 activation initiates downstream immune signaling cascades, leading to further pro-inflammatory cytokine release and activation of innate immune cells (e.g., dendritic cells, macrophages). These activated innate immune cells function as antigen-presenting cells, recognizing danger signals (e.g., pathogen-associated molecular patterns [PAMPs], metabolism-associated danger signals [MADS]) and presenting antigens to T cells.8,21 This amplified innate immune response may potentiate the hypersensitivity reactions underlying SJS. Additionally, mitochondrial dysfunction may impair the function of regulatory T cells (Treg cells). Treg cells play a crucial role in suppressing excessive immune responses and maintaining immune tolerance; impaired Treg cell function can lead to uncontrolled immune activation, 9 which may exacerbate drug-induced cutaneous adverse reactions.

Follow-up head CT on readmission showed expanded hypodense lesions in the right cerebral hemisphere, indicating active and progressive MELAS. This persistent pro-inflammatory state likely further increased the patient’s vulnerability to SJS. However, this case has certain limitations. Due to financial constraints and rapid disease progression, comprehensive immunological assessment was not performed, allowing only inference of potential correlations and mechanisms. Thus, the interplay of multiple factors should be considered in interpreting this case. Further clinical and basic research is needed to clarify mechanisms of drug hypersensitivity in mitochondrial dysfunction and optimize therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, LTG-induced SJS is associated with multiple risk factors. The immune-activating microenvironment resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction may potentiate the risk of LTG-induced SJS. Lamotrigine should be used cautiously for epilepsy in high-risk patients, particularly those with ME, and comprehensive evaluation is required to minimize severe hypersensitivity reactions.
