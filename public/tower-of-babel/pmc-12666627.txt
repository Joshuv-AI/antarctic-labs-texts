A case of cardiac involvement in Fabry disease caused by the p.D313Y variant of the GLA-gene
Authors: Alexandra L Schnieder, Stephan Zellerhoff, Christoph Bremer, Murat A Özgün, Vincenzo Nuzzi, Giulia Bruno, Annagrazia Cecere, Ugur Canpolat, Deepti Ranganathan, Anna Cantone, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFabry disease (FD) is a rare X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the GLA gene, leading to progressive sphingolipid accumulation in various tissues, including the heart. Cardiac manifestations often include left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), myocardial fibrosis, and conduction abnormalities. The p.D313Y variant of the GLA gene is considered a milder mutation and may present with milder or without classical FD symptoms.Case summaryWe report a 54-year-old male presenting with persistent fatigue and dry cough following a mild viral infection. Initial work-up showed premature atrial and ventricular contractions, stress-induced ischaemia on myocardial perfusion imaging, and mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (EF). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) revealed focal late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in the basal inferolateral myocardium, with normal native T1 (nT1) values. Follow-up imaging demonstrated progressive myocardial changes, including further EF reduction and decreasing nT1 values. These findings raised suspicion for FD. Genetic testing confirmed the p.D313Y variant in the GLA gene, and enzymatic analysis showed mildly reduced α-galactosidase A activity.DiscussionThis case highlights myocardial involvement in p.D313Y-associated Fabry disease (vFD) in the absence of LVH or classical symptoms. CMR, particularly native T1 mapping, proved useful in identifying early myocardial changes. The findings suggest that even patients with vFD and no LVH may develop significant cardiac pathology. This case emphasizes the importance of considering FD in patients with unexplained cardiac symptoms and unremarkable coronary findings. Further research is needed to assess the potential benefits of early intervention with enzyme replacement or chaperone therapy in vFD.Keywords: case report, Cardiac magnetic resonance, Cardiomyopathy, Fabry disease

Introduction

Fabry disease (FD) is a rare inherited X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the a-galactosidase A (GLA) gene. These mutations lead to a deficiency of its encoding lysosomal enzyme1 and progressive accumulation and deposition of GLA substrates, especially the sphingolipid globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) in plasma, urine and cellular lysosomes throughout the body. This results in a multisystemic pathology with life-threatening manifestations, including renal failure, cardiac and cerebrovascular disease.2 Deposition in various cells of the heart causes left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), myocardial fibrosis and conduction disturbances, which can progress to severe FD cardiomyopathy.3

More than 900 known GLA mutations have been identified causing a variety of clinical manifestations.2 Besides the classical phenotype of FD (classical Fabry disease; cFD), milder late-onset phenotypes and genetic variants (variant Fabry disease; vFD) of unknown significance have been described previously.4 The p.D313Y variant is a GLA mutation, caused by substitution of tyrosine for aspartic acid at codon 313 of the GLA protein. Patients with the p.D313Y mutation are more often oligosymptomatic compared to those with cFD and reported to present with predominantly neurological symptoms.5 Enzyme replacement (ERT) or chaperone therapy may reduce left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and improve myocardial function in cFD,6 whereas a treatment in vFD remains controversially discussed.

Case presentation

An otherwise healthy 54-year-old man experienced persistent weakness and dry cough after a mild viral infection (Table 1). His cardiologist performed a 24-hour electrocardiogram (ECG), which showed premature atrial and ventricular contractions while echocardiogram was normal at this point (December 2021) with an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. The patient then underwent myocardial perfusion scintigraphy for the evaluation of possible coronary heart disease as the cause for the arrhythmias, which revealed exercise-induced ischaemia of the apical, inferoapical and inferior left ventricular wall. In addition to microvascular changes in the right coronary artery outflow path, as a possible morphologic correlate to the findings in the scintigraphy, no signs of coronary heart disease were observed in coronary angiography. The patient did not experience angina pectoris symptoms at any time. A follow up Echocardiogram in February 2022 documented only mildly impaired left ventricular (LV) EF (Simpson’s biplane 46%), no wall motion abnormalities and besides a grade I mitral valve insufficiency no significant valve abnormalities (Figure 2).

Investigations

The patient was referred to our department for cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) to further evaluate the LV-dysfunction. CMR was performed at 1.5 T, and the protocol included Cine (videos provided as Supplementary material), Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), motion corrected native T1 mapping (nT1) and T2/T2* mapping (nT2).

CMR (February 2022; Figure 3) showed mild LV dilatation (end diastolic left ventricular diameter 5,9 cm; Figure 4), a slightly reduced EF (46%), focal LGE in the basal inferolateral myocardium (at that point appearing almost transmural and indistinguishable between subendocardial of intramyocardial) and normal nT1 values (918 ms septal and 940 ms lateral—in domo reference values: 960 +/− 45 ms). There was no left ventricular hypertrophy or oedema and nT2/T2* values were normal.

These findings were initially interpreted to be most likely related to a focal myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), with a differential diagnosis of subacute post-inflammatory changes.

Follow up CMR was performed to possibly further discriminate between the suspected differential diagnoses in April 2022, with now reduced nT1 values in the lateral wall (880 ms; Figure 3) while all other findings remained unchanged (Figure 4).

In December 2023, after documented ST-segment changes and episodes of narrow complex tachycardia, the patient was readmitted to our hospital. CMR performed at that time revealed a slight decline in the previously observed, now unmasked basal inferolateral mid-wall LGE, along with a progressive reduction in the myocardial nT1 value midventricular septal (866 ms, with focal spots down to 696 ms; Figure 3) and further reduction in EF (39%; Figure 4).

These findings, together with the observed exercise induced ischaemia on myocardial perfusion scintigraphy and microvascular changes seen on coronary angiography, which have been previously reported in FD previously led to the suspicion of an underlying FD.7

Genetic testing confirmed the presence of the p.D313Y variant of the GLA-gene. Further serum analysis revealed mildly reduced GLA-activity (2.1; reference > 2.4) and normal serum lyso-Gb3 enzyme activity (0.9; ref. < 1.4). The patient was referred to a specialized centre for FD to evaluate the option for ERT or chaperone therapy.

MRI follow-up

In a follow-up (April 2024; Figure 3) the myocardial nT1 e.g. in the midventricular septum showed similar values of 849–877 ms.

Medical treatment and clinical follow up

The initial medical therapy consisted of ACE inhibitor and beta blocker and was limited by the occurrence of hypotension and a mild chronotropic incompetence (maximum heart rate during exertion 140/min, prior to beta blocker therapy 170/min) as well as nocturnal bradycardia. The maximum tolerated doses were therefore 2.5 mg ramipril and 2.5 mg bisoprolol per day.

Due to the chronotropic incompetence manifest during cycling, the patient discontinued the beta blocker medication. Shortly afterwards, the premature ventricular contraction burden rose again up to 15% and the LV EF declined to 39%. After re-initiation of the beta blocker therapy by exchanging ramipril for bisoprolol the premature ventricular contraction burden again fell and the EF nearly normalized (EF 50% in 04/25). The addition of a SGLT2 inhibitor was declined by the patient. Currently, the patient is asymptomatic regarding his extrasystoles while on a medication of 2.5 mg bisoprolol per day.

Since the p.D313Y variant of the GLA-gene is still considered a genetic variant of unknown significance and serum lyso-Gb3 enzyme activity was normal the patient did not receive ERT or chaperone therapy after being referred to a specialized centre for FD.

Discussion

The CMR findings reported above are highly suggestive of myocardial involvement in the context of the p.D313Y vFD, even in the absence of LVH. These findings demonstrate that cardiac involvement in vFD patients warrants further investigation. The p.D313Y variant is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance (with growing evidence of its pathogenecitiy,5,8) and systematical investigation on cardiac involvement, especially in the early stages of the disease, is scarce.

The p.D313Y variant as well as other GLA mutations considered variants of unknown significance appear to often represent a GLA-pseudodeficiency with modest residual enzyme activity and minimal disease in many carriers, not leading to the cFD phenotype. Most evidence supports a benign or mild effect, but some individuals may exhibit organ-related symptoms, especially neurological, suggesting a possible mild late-onset FD phenotype in select cases. Some studies measuring the FD biomarker lyso-Gb3 even found levels within the normal range, supporting the idea that D313Y is not clinically relevant for FD phenotype in those cases.9

Since our patient did not show any other symptoms of FD (e.g. renal dysfunction, neurological symptoms, angiokeratomas, corneal opacities or hypohidrosis), it is possible that in this case, cardiac involvement may be the only ‘early’ organ manifestation of vFD, which has not been reported yet. As under-recognition of cardiac manifestations with only mild or nonspecific symptoms may occur in clinical practice, monitoring for cardiac involvement is important to identify patients with latent or early-FD, as those patients may qualify for ERT or chaperone therapy.

The relevance of cardiac nT1 mapping in identifying early stages of cardiac involvement in FD has been demonstrated previously,10 however, the role of nT1 mapping in assessing the effects of ERT remains a controversial topic of debate.11 While nT1 has been reported to be a helpful biomarker to track ERT effects in early stages of FD (increase and/or partial normalization of nT1 values after one year of ERT,12) pseudonormalisation of nT1 values occurs in FD patients with progression of myocardial fibrosis and detracts from its value in monitoring ERT effects in later stages of the disease.13
