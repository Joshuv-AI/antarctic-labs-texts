Ultrasound-assisted hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent extraction and purification of ursolic acid and oleanolic acid from Cornus officinalis: Optimization and mechanistic insights
Authors: Tengteng Yang, Chao Tan, Ruoliang Wang, Yixuan Shi, Junhai Liu, Yinku Liang, Chenyang Ren, Yongfei Wang, Yuan Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Cornus officinalis, also referred to as red jujube bark and medicinal jujube, is widely distributed across China and is an important plant resource in the Qinba Mountains of Shaanxi Province [1]. Its fruit is rich in various bioactive substances, including cycloartenolides, triterpenic acids, flavonoids, polysaccharides, organic acid esters, and other bioactive components, giving it a substantial therapeutic value [2], [3]. Ursolic acid (UA) and oleanolic acid (OA), pentacyclic triterpenic acids, are isomeric compounds abundant in C. officinalis. They are typically present as free acids or as aglycones of triterpenoid saponins, and are frequently isolated together during natural product extraction [4]. UA and OA have demonstrated several pharmacological properties, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and cardioprotective effects, as well as efficacy in treating rheumatoid arthritis, and are widely used in the food, pharmaceutical, and agricultural sectors [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. However, the extraction of UA and OA from natural sources requires large volumes of volatile organic solvents due to their poor aqueous solubility. This results in their low extraction yields and the process is inconsistent with the principles of green chemistry. Therefore, developing a green, efficient, and eco-friendly extraction method is urgently required to isolate UA and OA.

Traditional extraction methods, such as the Soxhlet extraction and hot reflux extraction, provide benefits including solvent recovery and ease of use. However, these methods are time-consuming and have poor solvent economy, which limits their applicability to only thermally stable compounds, thus restricting their application in green and sustainable development [13], [14], [15]. Due to the high volatility, toxicity, flammability, and environmental unfriendliness of traditional organic solvents, green solvents, such as ionic liquids (ILs) and deep eutectic solvents (DESs), have garnered attention. Despite the fact that ionic liquids are regarded as environmentally friendly, they have complex and costly synthesis, poor biodegradability, and relatively high toxicity. Due to their adjustable physicochemical properties, low toxicity, and low volatility, DESs have been extensively studied and applied in separation [16], [17], biocatalysis [18], [19], and electrochemistry [20], [21], and were initially used in 2003 by Abbott et al. They are typically prepared by the heating and mixing of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) with hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) until the formation of a homogeneous liquid, accompanied by flexible adjustment of their composition ratios to achieve highly selective extraction of target compounds.

Extraction from natural sources can be made more efficient by optimizing extraction solvents to increase extraction rates and incorporating physical methods, such as microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) and ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE). UAE application generates microscopic bubbles in liquids through ultrasonic cavitation, leading to violent implosion, inducing extreme temperatures, pressures, and intense shock waves, significantly enhancing mass transfer [22], [23]. It also disrupts plant cell walls and matrices, increasing contact between the target compounds and the solvents, resulting in increased and faster extraction rates [24]. Compared to traditional methods, UAE significantly reduces extraction time, decreases solvent consumption, and increases yield.

Extraction and purification are critical steps in the process of extracting natural products. Crude extracts are complex mixtures and typically require further purification [25]. Common purification methods include liquid–liquid extraction, column chromatography, and macroporous adsorption resin-based purification. Among these, macroporous adsorption resins are widely used in the field of separating natural products due to their ease of regeneration, simple operation, and suitability for large-scale applications [26]. The polarity, pore size, and specific surface area of the resin greatly affect the purification efficiency of certain target compounds. However, despite the relative advancement of macroporous resin purification techniques, their application in DES extraction systems has not yet been investigated in detail. DES achieves high extraction yields and forms stable bonds with the target compounds, hindering their subsequent adsorption onto macroporous resins and affecting adsorption kinetics and selectivity. In this study, DES extract was pretreated and combined with purification using macroporous adsorption resin to achieve higher adsorption efficiency and provide a methodological reference for subsequent separation and purification via DES-based extraction systems.

The combination of UAE and DES (UAE-DES) synergistically integrates the physical cell disruption effects of ultrasound with the adjustable dissolution properties of DES. UAE-DES has previously demonstrated a significantly greater extraction efficiency than DES extraction without ultrasonic assistance or traditional organic solvent extraction. Chaudhary et al. compared the extraction of bioactive compounds from Spirulina platensis and orange peel using UAE-NADES with conventional Soxhlet extraction and NADES extraction alone. UAE-NADES yielded over twice the total phenolic content compared to non-ultrasonic NADES and more than three times that of 70% ethanol Soxhlet extraction [27]. Saelee et al. used UAE-DES to extract pectin from the pericarp of Lycium barbarum, yielding 12.65%, which was 3.2 times greater than that of non-ultrasonic DES extraction (3.92%) and more than twice as high as traditional acid-heating extraction (5–6%) [28]. Xu et al. reviewed 75 studies and found that UAE-DES typically produces 2–5 times greater yields than conventional organic solvent extraction [29]. Despite the aforementioned progress, few reports have addressed the use of ultrasound-assisted hydrophobic DES to extract UA and OA from C. officinalis. Furthermore, most studies have not investigated the toxicity of DES, limiting its future applications.

Therefore, this study developed an effective method to extract UA and OA from C. officinalis. The extraction process parameters were systematically optimized using single-factor experiments combined with response surface methodology (RSM). Further, the extraction process was subjected to kinetic analysis and modeling. The extraction mechanism in the DES system was analyzed using quantum chemical calculations. The powders before and after extraction were characterized to investigate the synergistic effects of UAE-DES using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Finally, the adsorption and desorption conditions were optimized to obtain high-purity UA and OA using macroporous resin.

This study developed an effective UAE-DES extraction process to isolate UA and OA from C. officinalis, providing a practical solution for the high-value, comprehensive utilization of C. officinalis resources. This study is innovative in two aspects: (a) using hydrophobic DES with good biocompatibility to replace traditional organic solvents, leading to an improvement in extraction efficiency; and (b) the application of SEM and quantum chemical calculations to elucidate the extraction mechanism.

Experiments

Chemicals and materials

Detailed information is provided in Supporting Material Text S1.

Screening and biocompatibility of DES

Preparation and screening of DES

Eleven different DESs were prepared (see Supplementary Material Text S2). Dried C. officinalis powder (0.5 g, 40-mesh) was placed in a 15 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of varying DESs (15 mL) to individual tubes, which were then placed in an ultrasonic cleaner at 50 °C for 30 min (ultrasonic power: 600 W). The total UA and OA levels were then detected using HPLC.

DES biocompatibility

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were selected as a biocompatibility evaluation model, and the biocompatibility of the DES system was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Six concentration gradients of DES were centrifuged. The medium alone was used as the blank group, while the medium with cells was the control group, and the medium, cells, and varying DES concentration were the experimental groups. The absorbance (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a Microplate Reader. The biocompatibility was determined by calculating the cell survival rate using the following formula (1).(1)CellViability=As-AcAb-Ac×100%

where As, Ab, and Ac represent the OD values of the experimental, blank, and control groups, respectively.

UPLC method

The content of the target compound was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with an Agilent ZORBAX XDB C18 column (4.5 × 250 mm, 5 μm). An isocratic elution program was employed, with a mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile and ammonia solution in a 75:25 (v/v) ratio; the pH of the ammonia solution was adjusted to 7.8 using phosphoric acid. Target compounds OA and UA had retention times of approximately 12 and 13 min, respectively, with a detection wavelength of 210 nm, a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min, a column temperature of 30 °C, and an injection volume of 20 μL. Standard solutions with different concentrations of UA and OA were prepared to plot the calibration curves, with peak areas on the Y-axis and concentration on the X-axis. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate to minimize errors.

Single-factor test

Using the total extraction yield (Y) of OA and UA, the optimal DES was used to optimize the following four factors via single-factor analysis: XA (1:15, 1:20, 1:25, 1:30, and 1:35 mg/mL), XB (20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min), XC (240, 300, 360, 420, 480 W), and XD (20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C). The tables for each factor are presented in Table S1 of the Supplementary Material.

RSM

For further optimization, the aforementioned factors, XA, XB, XC, and XD, were selected using the total extraction yield (Y) as the response variable. A four-factor, three-level experiment, with a total of 29 sub-sections, was performed using Box-Behnken design (BBD) in Design Expert software.

SEM

The surface morphology of C. officinalis powder before and after extraction was observed using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi Regulus 8100, Japan). Untreated and UAE-DES extracted samples were vacuum oven-dried at 60 °C for 12 h until constant weight. The appropriate amount of dried powder was evenly distributed onto a conductive adhesive-coated sample stub, and any loose powder around the edges was carefully removed using a rubber suction bulb. To enhance electrical conductivity, the samples were sputter-coated at an accelerating voltage of 5.00 kV with a gold film using an ion sputter coater (E-1010, Hitachi, Japan) at a current of 15 mA for 60 s.

Validation of DES formation

FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy were used to confirm the successful synthesis of DES. The 1H NMR spectrum was recorded in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the chemical shift standard.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Forcite module in Materials Studio 2020 (MS2020), and the amorphous models were constructed with the Amorphous Cell (AC) module. The system was composed of a 2:3 mass (or molar) ratio of oleanolic acid and ursolic acid, with five composition cases evaluated. The solvents were methanol, ethanol, and a methanol/ethanol binary mixture (1:1; volume or molar ratio as defined in the model). The total number of molecules in each simulation box was set to 1500 using the COMPASS III force field. Before MD, the initial configurations were geometry-optimized to reach energy minimization. The systems were then simulated for 400 ps under the NPT ensemble for data collection and analysis after being equilibrated under the NVT ensemble for 100 ps. Temperature control was implemented using the Nosé thermostat, and all other parameters were kept at the default settings in MS2020.

The binding energy of a composite system was calculated using the Dmol3 module in Materials Studio (MS 2020). First, a structural model of the composite was constructed, followed by structural optimization using Dmol3. The exchange–correlation functional selected was GGA-PBE, the basis set was DNP, and DFT-D dispersion correction was applied. The convergence criteria for the self-consistent field, as well as the energy, force, and displacement convergence criteria for the geometric optimization, were set to their default values. Finally, the energy was calculated, and the binding energy was determined using the following formula:(2)Ebind=Ecomplex-EA-EB

Ecomplex is the total energy of the A–B complex after optimization; EA and EB are the energies of the individual organic molecules A and B, respectively.

Purification process

Selecting the optimal resin

After the adsorption, the concentrations of UA and OA were determined, and the macroporous resin was washed with distilled water and desorbed with methanol. UA and OA adsorption isotherms on AB-8 macroporous resin

Five extraction solutions were prepared at different concentration gradients. AB-8 macroporous resin (2 g in each flask) was accurately weighed into three conical flasks, followed by the addition of varying concentrations of extraction solutions to each flask. The three sets of solutions were placed in water-bath shakers maintained at 15 °C, 25 °C, and 35 °C, respectively, and at 120 rpm for 12 h to enable adsorption. Once the adsorption equilibrium was achieved, the total UA and OA concentration in the supernatant was determined. Optimization of static adsorption conditions

To determine the appropriate conditions for static purification, single-factor experiments were conducted separately for the adsorption and desorption processes. Adsorption experiments were carried out at different pH levels (2.5–6.5), while desorption experiments used different desorption solvents (methanol, ethanol, and dichloromethane), with an elution volume of 3 BV. Both adsorption and desorption were performed at 25 °C.

Dynamic adsorption and desorption

The pretreated AB-8 macroporous resin was loaded onto the chromatographic column using a wet method. When the pH of the extraction solution was adjusted to 5.5, the samples were loaded onto the column, and then the effect of flow rate on resin adsorption and desorption was investigated. Methanol solution was utilized as the eluent, and it showed the highest desorption rate, with an elution volume of 3 BV. Loading and elution were performed at flow rates of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 mL/min. The concentrations of the eluate and eluent were determined after being collected in test tubes.

Statistical analysis and data processing

All experiments were conducted with three independent replicates (n = 3). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using Design Expert 13 and SAS 8.0.1 software, and graphs were plotted using Origin software.

Results and analysis

Screening of solvents and evaluation of biocompatibility

Selection of the optimal solvent

Before the screening experiment, preliminary experiments were carried out to demonstrate that the relative effectiveness of the various DESs was unaffected by changing the extraction conditions. Therefore, fixed conditions were used for the screening to ensure experimental simplicity, result reliability, and data comparability.

The efficiencies of hydrophilic DES, hydrophobic DES, and the conventional solvent 70% ethanol were compared using total yield after extraction as the assessment criterion to extract UA and OA from C. officinalis. Based on hydrophilic DESs, Choline chloride: Acetic acid (1:1) showed the highest extraction yield of 1.26 ± 0.16 mg/g (Fig. 1a), which is evidently less than the value obtained when 70% ethanol was employed as the solvent (2.59 ± 0.25 mg/g). This discrepancy may be attributed to the strong polarity of the hydrophilic DES, coupled with its high viscosity, limiting the solubility and mass transfer efficiency of the target compound during extraction. Among the hydrophobic DESs, the DL-menthol: Isopropyl alcohol (IPA) (1:1) system showed remarkably high extraction efficiency for both OA and UA, with an extraction rate of around 2.43 times higher than that obtained with 70% ethanol. According to the concept that like dissolves like, this higher extraction rate is attributed to the synergistic interaction between DL-menthol and IPA, generating a strongly hydrophobic network through hydrogen bonding [31], demonstrating improved solubility for water-insoluble low-polarity compounds like UA and OA.Fig. 1(a) Extraction yields of UA and OA by different solvents. (b) Biocompatibility results for DES.

(a) Extraction yields of UA and OA by different solvents. (b) Biocompatibility results for DES.

These results suggest that extraction performance depends on the polarity match between the DES and the target compound. Despite the extensive use of hydrophilic DESs to extract natural products, their extraction efficiency was lower compared to traditional solvent ethanol for hydrophobic target compounds. Therefore, the hydrophobic DESs, with their low polarity and low viscosity, are more effective. DL-menthol: IPA (1:1) was selected as the optimal solvent.

Biocompatibility of DES

The potential cytotoxicity of varying DES concentrations was examined in HUVECs to measure cell proliferation using CCK-8 assays (Fig. 1b). Under the established concentration gradient, all groups showed no significant difference in cell survival rates over 97% after 24 h of treatment, compared to the negative control group (p < 0.05). These findings demonstrated that the DL-menthol: IPA (1:1) DES did not demonstrate cytotoxicity against HUVEC cells under the tested conditions, supporting its preliminary biocompatibility for subsequent in vitro bioactivity assays.

Analysis of the results of a single-factor test

The extraction yield showed a gradual increase with increasing solid-to-liquid ratio (Fig. 2a), reaching a peak at 1:20 g/mL. The extraction yield was gradually decreased with further increases in the solid–liquid ratio, which critically affects the extraction yield by modulating mass transfer efficiency. The extraction system rapidly reached saturation at a low ratio, resulting in a decreased mass transfer gradient and a small concentration gradient between the solids and liquids. The target component dissolution was accompanied by the dissolution of other impurities at a high ratio [32]. Therefore, a solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 g/mL was selected as the response surface factor for further optimization and analysis.Fig. 2Effects of (a) Solid-liquid ratio, (b) Ultrasonic time, (c) Ultrasonic power, and (d) Ultrasonic temperature on UA and OA extraction rates.

Effects of (a) Solid-liquid ratio, (b) Ultrasonic time, (c) Ultrasonic power, and (d) Ultrasonic temperature on UA and OA extraction rates.

The extraction rate was gradually decreased as the duration of ultrasonic treatment increased from 20 to 40 min (Fig. 2b), reaching a peak at 40 min followed by a slow decline. Compared to traditional extraction methods, ultrasound-assisted extraction uses cavitation to produce bubbles, which then break down the cell walls in the plant material during the initial extraction phase. This, in turn, enhances mass transfer and induces concentration gradients, thus accelerating the diffusion rates of target compounds. However, prolonged ultrasonic exposure may result in the leaching of other materials, which might degrade the target compound [33], [34]. Therefore, 40 min was selected as the optimal duration of ultrasound treatment in further experiments.

The extraction yield increased with increasing ultrasonic power from 240 to 300 W but reduced significantly above 300 W (Fig. 2c). At low power, plant cell walls were insufficiently disrupted, which prevented the full release of target compounds; however, excessively high power causes localized overheating and degradation of the target compounds, resulting in reduced extraction yields [35], [36]. Therefore, 300 W was selected as the optimal power setting for further experiments.

The extraction yields initially increased and then decreased with increasing ultrasonic temperature, achieving a peak value at 40 °C (Fig. 2d). Temperature largely regulates extraction efficiency by influencing the intensity of the cavitation effect and the rate of molecular diffusion. The viscosity of DES decreased with a slightly rising temperature, allowing solvent penetration into the powdered plant material and increasing the solubility of target compounds. However, severe cavitation could compromise both the integrity of heat-sensitive compounds and the solvent volume, leading to a significant depletion in the liquid [37]. Hence, 40 °C was chosen for all subsequent experiments.

The optimal extraction process predicted by the model was: XA = 1:20.71 g/mL, XB = 40.34 min, XC = 299.22 W, XD = 28.54 °C, with an optimal extraction rate of 9.36 mg/g. For practical application, the parameters were modified to the following values: XA = 1:21 g/mL, XB = 40 min, XC = 300 W, and XD = 29 °C. Under the above conditions, five consecutive extraction replicates were used for validation. The average extraction yield was 9.38 ± 0.17 mg/g. The optimal combination (29 °C, 300 W, 40 min, 1:21 g/mL) essentially represents the equilibrium point between extraction kinetics and thermodynamics. Specifically, 29 °C balances solvent permeability and ultrasonic cavitation intensity; 300 W provides an appropriate ultrasonic cavitation effect capable of generating shock waves that effectively disrupt cell walls; and 40 min is the kinetic time required for the solute to diffuse from the cells into the solvent and reach mass transfer equilibrium. The experimental results showed good agreement with the model predictions, with a deviation of only 0.21%, and can therefore be applied to direct the extraction of UA and OA in practical processes.

Yang et al. extracted OA and UA from wintergreen, with yields of 2.3 and 2.6 mg/g, respectively, using ultrasound-assisted supercritical CO2 extraction [39]. Li et al. achieved a 1.544% yield of UA extracted from apple peel using hydrophobic DES [40]. Jäger et al. extracted UA from C. officinalis leaves, with a yield of 1.5 mg/g using an accelerated solvent extraction method [41]. This study demonstrates significantly higher extraction yields compared to those reported in the aforementioned studies, highlighting the benefits of this technology for the extraction process.

Comparison of extraction methods

To evaluate whether the extraction yield of UA and OA from C. officinalis can be significantly increased by the method employed in this study, it was compared with DES extraction, ethanol extraction, UAE-EtOH, and MAE-DES. Compared to the DES extraction and the ethanol extraction methods, ultrasonic assistance significantly increased the extraction yield, with increases of 2.75-fold and 2.31-fold, respectively (Fig. 4). The MAE-DES process achieved an extraction yield of 6.45 ± 0.22 mg/g, while the UAE-DES process produced a yield 45.4% higher compared to the MAE-DES process. Although microwave-assisted extraction improved extraction efficiency by accelerating mass transfer, ultrasound-assisted extraction is more effective at disrupting cell walls, facilitating the elution of target compounds. Furthermore, UAE-DES is a simple and effective extraction method since ultrasonic equipment is less costly and safer.Fig. 4Comparison of extraction yields for five extraction methods.

SEM analysis

To investigate the mechanism of ultrasonic assistance on the extraction process, the surface microstructure of C. officinalis powder was examined using SEM before and after DES extraction (Fig. 5). The untreated sample showed a smooth and structurally intact surface. After UAE-DES treatment, the surface became rough, with numerous cracks, pores, and collapsed cell walls. As the magnification increased, the extent of damage became more evident, which can be attributed to ultrasound-induced cavitation in the structure, forming numerous microscopic bubbles in the extraction solution, which collapse to produce transient high pressures and shock waves. This process disrupts the cell walls, allowing DES to enter the interior of the cell and release bioactive substances from it. Furthermore, the mechanical effects resulting from rupture of the bubbles, combined with the bubble oscillation-induced localized turbulence surrounding the plant material, can expand heterogeneous reaction interfaces and enhance contact between the solvent and the raw materials. The energy generated by ultrasonic waves is partially converted into heat, raising the temperature of the entire system and reducing the viscosity of the DES, enhancing mass transfer within the solvent. This aligns with the quantitative extraction yield results, with an ultrasonication yield of 9.38 mg/g as opposed to just 3.41 mg/g without it. Ultrasonication increased the extraction yield by a factor of 2.75, confirming the significant enhancing effect of ultrasonic-assisted extraction on extraction efficiency. The SEM images confirmed the synergistic action between DES and ultrasonic shear forces, leading to the effective disruption of the C. officinalis cell walls and providing microstructural evidence of the mechanism by which this method increases extraction efficiency.Fig. 5Scanning electron micrographs of C. officinalis powder under different conditions: (a, c, e, g) untreated; (b, d, f, h) treated with UAE-DES.

Validation of DES formation

The infrared spectra of DL-menthol, IPA, and the resulting DES are displayed in Fig. 6a. DES formation was evidenced by significant shifts in characteristic functional group regions. The stretching vibration peak of the –OH group in DL-menthol was observed at 3327.1 cm−1, while the corresponding peak in the IPA spectrum was observed at 3329.1 cm−1. In the DES spectrum, the absorption peak was broad and blue shifted in the corresponding region, eventually resulting in a wide peak at 3346.4 cm−1, confirming the formation of new hydrogen bonds in the DES system [42], [43]. The cyclohexane ring skeletal vibration peak of DL-menthol and the C-O stretching vibration peak were shifted in IPA, demonstrating the presence of hydrogen bonding in the DES, resulting from the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds (O-H•••O) between the –OH group of IPA and the −O- group of DL-menthol.Fig. 6Infrared (a) and 1H NMR (b) spectra of DL-menthol, IPA, and DES.

Infrared (a) and 1H NMR (b) spectra of DL-menthol, IPA, and DES.

Fig. 6b illustrates the 1H NMR spectra of DL-menthol, IPA, and DES. The chemical shifts of the hydrogen atoms in DES confirmed the formation of hydrogen bonds, with the most significant shift being observed in the signal for the active hydrogen (–OH). The chemical shift of the hydroxyl hydrogen in IPA and DL-menthol was observed at 4.35 and 4.26 ppm, respectively, which are downfield shifted to approximately 4.40 and 4.49 ppm, in DES. This de-shielding effect suggests a decrease in the electron density surrounding the hydroxyl hydrogen atom [43], [44], resulting from the –OH group in IPA acting as a hydrogen bond donor, establishing a strong intermolecular hydrogen bond (O-H•••O) with the −O- group in DL-menthol, strongly deshielded, increasing the corresponding resonance frequency. The chemical shifts of methylene and methine hydrogens associated with the hydrogen-bond network in the DES spectrum showed small but noticeable changes, indicating the formation of new hydrogen bonds in DES [45].

Molecular dynamics

Dynamic spatial distribution analysis

From a microscopic perspective, the spatial distributions of molecules in ethanol, methanol, and DES at the simulated initial state (100 ps) and equilibrium state (400 ps) (Fig. 7) were compared by investigating the differences in the dissolution behavior of UA and OA in varying solvent systems. During the initial simulation phase, none of the three systems showed any discernible solute molecule aggregation. After reaching equilibrium, significant variations between the solutes were noted, with pronounced aggregation in the ethanol, relatively milder aggregation in the methanol, and nearly uniform dispersion without aggregation in the DES systems, which can be attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between UA and OA and DES. The uniform spatial distribution simultaneously increased the effective contact area between solute and solvent, establishing a favorable environment for solute dissolution. The observed differences in methanol and ethanol aggregation could result from their inability to fully dissolve the hydrophobic pentacyclic triterpene skeletons of UA and OA, inducing self-association of solute molecules, hindering efficient extraction. DES showed excellent diffusion properties, provided a chemically compatible dissolution environment for target solutes, effectively suppressed solute aggregation, and significantly increased the solute–solvent contact area. This multidimensional synergy serves as the structural foundation for effective extraction [46].Fig. 7The distribution of C. officinalis powder in different solvent systems at 100 ps and 400 ps was analyzed by molecular dynamics simulations (a-f).

Radial distribution function (RDF)

The RDF, denoted as g(r), describes the possibility of locating a neighboring atom at a distance “r” from a reference atom within an infinitely thin spherical shell [47]. The characteristic peaks typically observed in the 1.5‒3.5 Å range generally indicate hydrogen bonding interactions, while those in the 3.5‒5.0 Å range correspond to Van der Waals forces [48], [49]. As shown in Fig. 8a, the hydroxyl group of IPA in DES showed peaks at 2.1 Å and 2.2 Å with the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of UA and OA, respectively. Within the 3.5‒5.0 Å range, both UA and OA exhibited broad secondary peaks, confirming the contribution of Van der Waals forces. Compared to the sharp peak characteristic of hydrogen bonds, Van der Waals forces were represented by a broader, less intense peak, indicating their secondary nature. DES primarily generated hydrogen bonding interactions with UA and OA, enhanced by Van der Waals forces. The synergistic effect of these multiple forces constituted the microscopic mechanism that DES uses to produce highly effective extraction.Fig. 8Radial distribution function values for C. officinalis powder in DES (a), Mean square displacement values for C. officinalis powder in different solvent systems (b).

Radial distribution function values for C. officinalis powder in DES (a), Mean square displacement values for C. officinalis powder in different solvent systems (b).

Mean square displacement (MSD)

MSD provides a quantitative evaluation of molecular diffusion by statistical analysis of particle displacements over time. Higher MSD values indicate greater molecular mobility, which in turn facilitates mass transfer of solutes from the matrix to the bulk solvent, potentially enhancing the extraction efficiency. To evaluate the impact of UA and OA on extraction efficiency and the diffusion capabilities of DES, the MSD values for both compounds in the methanol, ethanol, and DES systems were calculated. As shown in Fig. 8b, the MSD values of UA and OA differed significantly across the three solvent systems, with the highest values of 38.3 Å2 and 22.1 Å2 for UA and OA in the methanol system, respectively. The corresponding values in the DES system were 9.38 Å2 and 4.12 Å2, respectively. The ethanol system yielded the lowest MSD values, at 8.23 Å2 and 4.11 Å2, respectively. The highest MSD value was not associated with DES, which may be attributed to the intermolecular interactions of UA and OA with the DES components. Based on the results of the RDF analysis, DES formed strong hydrogen bond-dominated interactions with target compounds. These interactions are involved in binding to the target compounds and reducing diffusion, resulting in lower MSD values compared to methanol. These results are consistent with studies showing that, whereas strong interactions increase solubility, they typically decrease diffusion rates [50], [51].

Average number of hydrogen bonds

The formation of hydrogen bonds is a key indicator for evaluating molecular interactions within a system. In the 100–400 ps time range, methanol, ethanol, and DES formed hydrogen bonds with UA and OA, respectively. As shown in Fig. 9, the average number of hydrogen bonds between DES and UA and OA was consistently higher than that between methanol and ethanol, indicating that DES has a stronger effect and is more conducive to extraction.Fig. 9Average number of hydrogen bonds for the three solvents.

The binding energy results further elucidate the molecular interactions between the solute and the solvent (Fig. 10). Together, these studies indicate that: the synergistic mechanism was not only the superposition of ultrasonic cell wall disruption and solvent dissolution. Instead, it was a multi-scale coupled enhancing process: ultrasonic cavitation physically disrupts the cell wall, reducing mass transfer resistance; DES forms specific molecular interactions with UA and OA via hydrogen bonding, significantly enhancing the dissolution selectivity and binding capacity for hydrophobic triterpenic acids; molecular dynamics simulations further confirm that ultrasound promotes DES penetration, while DES enhances the solubilization of the target compounds. The synergy between these two processes resulted in a concurrent enhancement in dissolution capacity and mass transfer efficiency, jointly elucidating the highly efficient extraction mechanism of the UAE-DES system at the microstructural and molecular interaction levels.

Purification of UA and OA

Selection of the optimal macroporous resins

The polarity, pore size, and specific surface area of macroporous resins are key factors determining their adsorption and desorption performance. When selecting macroporous resins for purification, it is important to consider the polarity and molecular weight of the target compound [52], [53], [54]. In terms of polarity, resins are typically classified into four categories: strongly polar, polar, weakly polar, and nonpolar. According to the principle of “like dissolves like”, the closer the polarity of the resin is to that of the target substance, the higher the adsorption capacity [55]. Therefore, the adsorption rate was adopted as the evaluation criterion, and six different polarity resins were selected for screening [38], [56]. As shown in Fig. 11, the highest adsorption rates of weakly polar resin for UA and OA was 90.1%, indicating a reduction in the adsorption capacity with increasing polarity of the resin. Therefore, the AB-8 resin was selected for further analysis.Fig. 11The effects of macroporous resin type on the total adsorption rate of UA and OA.

The effects of macroporous resin type on the total adsorption rate of UA and OA.

AB-8 resin adsorption kinetics

The adsorption efficiency of adsorbates on adsorbents was elucidated to determine the adsorption kinetics. It is essential to investigate how time affects the adsorption process [57]. The experimental data were fitted to clarify the dynamic influence of adsorption time on adsorption rate, using pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intra-particle diffusion models, with the fitting results shown in Fig. 12b, 12c, and 12d. Based on the adsorption kinetics curve (Fig. 12a), the AB-8 macroporous resin demonstrated the fastest adsorption rate between 0 and 100 min, which can be attributed to the presence of a large number of adsorption sites on the resin surface. As the adsorption time increased, the rate gradually slowed down and reached equilibrium at 300 min [58]. According to the pseudo-first-order kinetic model, adsorption rates are mostly controlled by the initial concentration of the solute, whereas the pseudo-second-order kinetic model also accounts for the influence of active sites on the adsorbent surface. Since AB-8 resin is a polystyrene-type weakly polar resin with hydrophobic benzene rings and a small number of polar ester groups distributed on its surface, UA and OA may bind to the resin through hydrophobic interactions. Therefore, the pseudo-second-order model is theoretically more appropriate. Adsorption isotherms of AB-8 resin

The adsorption data were fitted to elucidate the adsorption mechanism and interactions of AB-8 resin with UA and OA, using the Langmuir and the Freundlich models, with the results presented in Fig. 13. The Langmuir model was based on monolayer adsorption, where adsorbate molecules are adsorbed onto homogenous adsorption sites without interacting with one another. Adsorption reached saturation when all active sites were occupied. The Freundlich model applies to heterogeneous surfaces and posits that the adsorbent surface comprises active sites with an uneven energy distribution, resulting in multilayered adsorption. As shown in Table 3, the Freundlich model yielded considerably higher R2 values of 0.988, 0.991, and 0.993 at 15, 25, and 35 °C, respectively, compared to the Langmuir model, indicating that the adsorption process is better described by the Freundlich model and confirming the presence of heterogeneous multilayered adsorption. The Freundlich constant 1/n falls within the range of 0.255–0.358 (0 < 1/n < 1), presenting further evidence that this adsorption process was relatively simple. The KF value was highest at 25 °C, indicating significant adsorption capacity. Optimization of static adsorption conditions

To further enhance the purification efficiency of macroporous adsorption resins for target compounds, two key factors, the pH of the sample solution and the type of desorption solvent, were analyzed based on preliminary experiments. The pH of the solution significantly influenced the interaction forces between the target molecule and the resin by altering the molecule’s ionization state. As shown in Fig. 14a, the adsorption capacity of AB-8 resin for UA and OA increased continuously as the pH rose from 2.5 to 5.5, reaching a maximum at pH 5.5. Subsequently, adsorption decreased as the pH increased, probably because the UA and OA extracts have a pH of about 5.5, which deprotonates when the pH rises and forms ions, reducing the adsorption capacity. Therefore, the optimal pH for adsorption was determined to be 5.5. The effects of the desorption solvents on elution efficiency were then examined. As shown in Fig. 14b, methanol showed significantly higher desorption efficiency than both ethanol and dichloromethane. Therefore, methanol was used as the desorption solvent in subsequent steps.Fig. 14Effects of sample solution pH (a) and elution solvent (b) on adsorption capacity.

Effects of sample solution pH (a) and elution solvent (b) on adsorption capacity.

Optimization of dynamic adsorption conditions

To further optimize the dynamic adsorption process, the impact of flow rate on UA and OA adsorption by AB-8 resin was investigated. As the flow rate increased, the adsorption capacity first increased and then declined, reaching a peak at 2 mL/min (Fig. 15a), demonstrating that a 2 mL/min flow rate improved mass transfer efficiency in the mobile phase and improved diffusion of target analytes toward the resin surface. Excessively high flow rates significantly reduced the extract residence time in the column, allowing UA and OA to elute before achieving sufficient contact with the active sites of the resin and leading to reduced adsorption capacity. Therefore, this condition was used in subsequent experiments.Fig. 15Effects of sampling flow velocity (a) and elution flow velocity (b) on desorption yield from the AB-8 resin.

Effects of sampling flow velocity (a) and elution flow velocity (b) on desorption yield from the AB-8 resin.

To investigate the effect of methanol flow rate on elution efficiency during the desorption phase, the dynamic desorption performance was evaluated in the range of 1–5 mL/min. The desorption rate significantly decreased with increasing flow rate, reaching a peak at 1 mL/min (Fig. 15b). The extended contact time between methanol and the resin at lower flow rates would facilitate the complete desorption of target compounds from adsorption sites and their diffusion into the eluent. Excessively high flow rates, while shortening the desorption time, may weaken the internal diffusion process, leading to insufficient desorption. Considering both desorption efficiency and operating time, 2 mL/min was selected as the optimal flow rate for the methanol desorption phase.

Verification of optimal purification conditions

An optimized purification protocol was performed using AB-8 resin, methanol as the elution solvent, a pH of 5.5, a sample loading flow rate of 2 mL/min, and an elution flow rate of 2 mL/min, followed by validation using three sets of parallel experiments. The average recovery rates for UA and OA were 79.2 ± 2.86%, demonstrating the excellent efficiency and reproducibility of this purification method.

Reusability of DES

To investigate the reusability of DES, after extracting UA and OA under optimal conditions, the mixture was vacuum-dried to recover the DES phase. To confirm its reusability, the recovered DES was extracted again. As shown in Fig. 16, following at least five extractions of C. officinalis powder using the recycled DES, the extraction yields of UA and OA reached over 80% of the maximum extraction yield, indicating no significant loss, demonstrating the effectively maintained performance of DES during continuous extraction and recycling processes, with at least five reuses. As a highly efficient and sustainable solvent, it holds broad application prospects.Fig. 16The yield obtained after five consecutive extractions using the recovered DES.

Conclusion

This study established an eco-friendly and effective UAE-DES extraction process to extract UA and OA from C. officinalis powder. DL-menthol: IPA (1:1) was identified as the optimum extraction solvent. The DES system showed excellent biocompatibility using HUVEC cells, allowing for direct application without separation. The overall extraction yield, optimized using single-factor experiments combined with response surface methodology, was 9.38 ± 0.17 mg/g, which significantly outperformed that of traditional solvents. SEM analysis showed that UAE-DES significantly damaged cell wall structures in C. officinalis powder, increasing the release of target components. FTIR and 1H NMR analyses confirmed the formation of hydrogen-bonding networks in the DES. MD simulations revealed that UA and OA form multiple types of interactions with the DES, primarily hydrogen bonds supplemented by van der Waals forces, providing a microscale mechanistic explanation for the observed effective extraction. During the purification stage, AB-8 macroporous resin demonstrated outstanding adsorption performance, achieving an adsorption rate of 90.1%, corresponding to the Freundlich isotherm and pseudo-second-order kinetic models, signifying a heterogeneous multilayer adsorption process. A relatively higher extraction of 45.4% was achieved compared to the MAE-DES process, with no significant decrease in yield after six repeated extractions. Furthermore, due to the cost-effectiveness, low toxicity, and biodegradability, DL-menthol and IPA are suitable for industrial extraction. This study provides a reference framework for ultrasonic-assisted extraction methods of UA and OA from plant sources.
