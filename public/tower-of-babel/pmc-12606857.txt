Comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of USP35 and validation of its role in gastric cancer
Authors: Lirong Yan, Yuzhe Zhang, Shubao Wang, Jiahui Qin, Moye Chen, Dan Zou, Lulu Zhang, Lina Wu, Ye Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we systematically revealed the expression characteristics, clinical relevance, and potential molecular mechanisms of the ubiquitin-specific processing protease 35 (USP35) in 33 cancers for the first time by integrating pan-cancer data from TCGA, GTEx, and other databases. Bioinformatics analysis showed that USP35 was significantly highly expressed in nine cancers, including gastric cancer (GC) and thyroid cancer, and its expression level was closely related to the tumor stage, metastasis, and a poor prognosis. Diagnostic value analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) value of USP35 exceeded 0.7 for four cancers, including rectal adenocarcinoma, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic marker. Mechanistic studies revealed that USP35 may affect tumor progression by regulating myogenesis, the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and other pathways, and is significantly associated with apoptosis, cell cycles, and other activities. Experimental validation partially confirmed that USP35 is strongly associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts in GC. Knockdown of USP35 inhibited GC cell migration/invasion, down-regulated vimentin, and blocked energy metabolism reprogramming through the inhibition of glycolysis. In combination with USP35 knockdown, 2-DG further exacerbated the metabolic inhibitory effects, and the reversal of the EMT was even more significant. USP35 enhanced the tumorigenic capacity and the EMT of GC cells in vivo. This study demonstrates that USP35 may promote GC progression through metabolic reprogramming, exhibiting potential as a diagnostic and prognostic tumor marker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00850-6.

Introduction

Recently, studies have indicated that ubiquitin-specific processing protease 35 (USP35) is involved in the regulation of the cell cycle by inhibiting the degradation of Aurora B via the proteasome pathway. This leads to de-ubiquitination of Aurora B, thereby regulating mitosis [1]. Currently, there are few studies on the role of USP35 in tumors. It has been reported that USP35 can play an important role in the development of breast, ovarian, liver, lung, and rectal cancers, skin melanoma, and other tumors [2–6]. There is only one study on gastric cancer (GC), which confirmed that USP35 promotes gastric cancer metastasis by increasing the stability of Snail1 [7]. However, the mechanism of USP35 in GC has not yet been fully elucidated. Considering the high incidence of GC and its complex biological characteristics, it is particularly important to investigate the relationship between USP35 and GC. By studying this, we can provide new ideas and strategies for the early diagnosis and treatment of GC.

Currently, research in the field is focused on the investigation of energy metabolism changes [8, 9]. Warburg proposed that tumor cells have special properties, finding that “even in an aerobic environment, tumor cells will not preferentially choose to supply energy for their own growth through the oxidative phosphorylation pathway with higher energy production efficiency, but will preferentially carry out glycolysis”. This characteristic was later named the “Warburg effect” [10, 11], with tumor cells exhibiting this effect to satisfy their need for unlimited proliferation [12]. Existing studies have shown that metabolic reprogramming can promote the progression of various tumors, such as hepatocellular carcinoma and breast cancer [13, 14], while recent studies have shown that USP35 can promote the proliferation and glycolysis of breast cancer cells by mediating the level of PFK-1 ubiquitination [2]. However, it is still not known whether USP35 affects the progression of gastric cancer by regulating energy metabolism reprogramming.

Pan-cancer analysis is a comprehensive and systematic approach to cancer research, the aim of which is a comprehensive and in-depth exploration of the commonalities and differences between different cancer types by integrating multiple types of data [15–17]. Although an increasing number of studies have shown that USP35 can influence cancer development, to date, no study has performed a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of USP35. Therefore, in this study, multi-dimensional analysis is conducted using TCGA data to explore the expression level of the USP35 gene and its association with cancer-related pathways, diagnosis, prognosis, the tumor immune microenvironment, immune cells, and drug sensitivity. The aim of this is to provide new clues for further investigation of USP35’s functional role in various tumors; to reveal the potential value of USP35 as a novel diagnostic or prognostic cancer biomarker and therapeutic target; and to explore whether USP35 promotes the progression of gastric cancer through metabolic reprogramming.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

Immunohistochemical kits were purchased from Fuzhou Maixin Biological Technology (KIT-9710, Fujian, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Vivacell (04-001-1ACS, Australia), while vimentin (10366-1-AP), α-SMA (14395-1-AP), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig) antibodies were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). E-cadherin (3195 S) and FAP (#66562) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), while USP35 antibodies were purchased from LifeSpan BioSciences (LS-C178984, USA). 2-Deoxy-d -glucose (HY-13966) was obtained from MedChemExpress.

Data collection and processing

We obtained USP35 expression and related clinical expression data through the Xena platform at the University of California, San Diego, using the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx, https://gtexportal.org/home/) databases to obtain pan-cancer data. The mutation frequencies of USP35 in the TCGA cohort were calculated using the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Pan-cancer analysis of TCGA samples was performed using the online bioinformatics tool SangerBox 3.0 (http://sangerbox.com/) [18]. Various immune infiltration algorithms in the TIMER 2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org) were used to characterize correlation between USP35 expression and the tumor immune microenvironment.

Expression and variant analysis

To explore potential differences in USP35 expression between tumor and normal tissues, we initially conducted a comparative analysis of USP35 mRNA levels using the Wilcoxon test. Subsequently, we applied the same statistical test to paired samples for a more nuanced comparison. For a visual representation of USP35 expression across various human organs, we utilized the “gganatogram” R package. We also employed the “pROC” R package to compute the area under the curve (AUC) value, highlighting the diagnostic significance of USP35 in pan-cancer contexts. Additionally, cBioPortal (accessible at http://www.cbioportal.org) served as a valuable resource for delving into the mutation frequency, types, copy number alteration (CNA) data, and genetically altered characteristics of USP35.

Survival and clinical outcome analysis

Survival data were sourced from the TCGA database, and we leveraged the “survival” and “survminer” R packages to evaluate the prognostic significance of USP35 expression. We examined various survival metrics, including overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-free interval (DFI). By integrating Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis with univariate Cox regression analysis, we assessed whether USP35 acted as a protective or risk factor. Ultimately, we constructed a high-confidence survival landscape for USP35. To visually present the findings from the Cox analysis, we utilized the “forestplot” R package.

USP35 and immunotherapy

We used the BEST database to explore the relationship between USP35 and patient prognosis in an external dataset and to investigate the correlation between USP35 expression and immunotherapy response in cancer patients [19].

Exploring the tumor microenvironment

We conducted a pan-cancer analysis of stem cellularity employing the Spearman method and utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to evaluate immune scoring and tumor purity, thereby assessing the infiltration of immune cells in cancer tissues and the purity of the tumors. We examined the correlation between USP35 expression and immune-related genes, including MHC, chemokine receptor, immunostimulatory, and immunosuppressive genes. Subsequently, we estimated the immune signature of TCGA tumors using a sophisticated seven-algorithm approach. (CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT_ABS EPIC, MCPCOUNTER, QUANTISEQ, TIMER, XCELL) [20].

The tumor-associated fibroblast immune infiltration content and USP35 gene expression were z-score normalized; a z-score less than or equal to 0 was defined as low gene expression and a z-score greater than 0 was defined as high gene expression. Based on this, the samples were divided into four subgroups: tumor-associated fibroblasts with high infiltration and USP35 high expression, tumor-associated fibroblasts with low infiltration and USP35 high expression, tumor-associated fibroblasts with low infiltration and USP35 low expression, and tumor-associated fibroblasts with high infiltration and USP35 low expression. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed using the survfit function in the survival package, and Log-rank tests were performed to assess two-by-two comparisons between the four subgroups as well as the overall significance.

Analysis of pathways and functional mechanisms

To gain deeper insights into USP35-associated pathways, we stratified tumor samples into high and low USP35 expression groups, comprising the top 30% and bottom 30% of samples, respectively. We then conducted a Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to investigate differences in the activation or repression of 50 signature gene sets and 83 metabolic gene sets between these groups across various malignancies. For the 14 functional state gene sets, we utilized the “GSVA” R package and employed the z-score algorithm to assign values. Subsequently, we calculated the statistical correlation between the z-scores and USP35 expression for each gene set using Pearson correlation analysis. Additionally, we identified the genes that were differentially expressed between the high and low USP35 groups.

Identification of chemicals that interact with USP35

We identified the top 150 up- and down-regulated genes as USP35-related biomarkers. To facilitate this, we downloaded the CMAP_gene_signatures RData file, which contains 1,288 compound-associated properties. Each step of our analysis was based on methods described in the previous literature [21, 22].

Immunohistochemistry assay

In this study, 20 gastric cancer tissues from the First Hospital of China Medical University were analyzed. Informed consent was provided by all patients and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of China Medical University ([2024]433).

Fixed tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned into 5 μm slices, and stained via immunohistochemistry. Protein expression was detected using the streptavidin–peroxidase method. For antigen processing, dehydrated sections were treated with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase, then blocked with goat serum (room temperature, 30 min). Tissues were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by sequential incubation with secondary antibodies (10 min) and biotin-labeled horseradish peroxidase (10 min). Antibody binding was visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrachloride kit (Maxin, China), and the staining results were observed under an inverted phase-contrast microscope. Immunoreactivity was semi-quantified independently by two researchers: 5 representative regions were randomly selected from 400× microscopic fields. Scores were calculated as the product of two metrics: (1) Positive cell proportion: <9%, 0; 10–25%, 1; 26–50%, 2; 51–75%, 3; 76–100%, 4. (2) Staining intensity: No staining, 0; light yellow, 1; yellow, 2; brown, 3. The final immunoreactivity score was the product of the above two scores.

Cell culture

AGS and MKN-45 cells were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China) and came with an STR identification certificate. Cells were cultured in RPMI1640 (Vivacell, USA) containing 10% FBS at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Lentiviral transfection

USP35 knockdown lentivirus was designed and provided by Shanghai Sangong Biotechnology Co. GC cells in the logarithmic growth phase were prepared and inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105/well, and the lentivirus was used to infect the cells according to the viral titer of MOI = 10 after 24 h. Then, the medium was replaced completely after 16 h. Complete medium containing puromycin at a concentration of 2 µg/ml was prepared and added to cell culture flasks. Treatment was continued until the cells no longer appeared to die. Immediately after this, Western blotting was used to detect the infection efficiency of the cells, and the results were used to screen out the mixed clonal stable cell lines.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from the treated cells and quantified. Equal amounts of total proteins were separated by electrophoresis using the Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell System (Bio-Rad laboratories Inc. Gels) and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The PVDF membrane was placed in Protein-Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (PS108P, Epizyme, China) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Next, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and the following day, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membranes were developed using BeyoECL Plus (P0018S, Beyotime, China) for visualization.

Metabolic assay

We used an ATP Assay Kit (S0027, Beyotime, China), a Glucose Assay Kit (A154-1-1, China), and a Lactic Acid Assay Kit (A019-1-1, China) to measure the ATP, glucose uptake, and lactic acid production levels, respectively. All the procedures were performed according to the instruction manuals of the reagents.

Transwell assay

Cells in there logarithmic growth phase were inoculated into the upper chamber of a Transwell (Corning, New York, USA). AGS cells were inoculated at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well, after which the cells were incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the membranes were fixed with methanol for 20 min, followed by staining with crystal violet. The stained cells were photographed and recorded using a light microscope, and the number of cells was counted and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Animal experiments

Ten BALB/c nude mice aged 4–6 weeks were obtained from Huafukang Experimental Animal Technology Co., LTD. (Beijing, China). They were raised in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment. AGS cells were selected for subcutaneous tumor formation. After 1 week of domestication, the 10 mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (5 mice per group): a control group and a USP35 knock down group. No blinding method was employed in this animal experiment. Each mouse was subcutaneously injected with approximately 5 × 106 cells suspended in 200 µl PBS. Two weeks later, the nude mice were sacrificed and subcutaneous tumors were removed, weighed, photographed, and stained by immunohistochemistry and hematoxylin–eosin staining. When conducting comparisons between the two groups, the Student’s two-tailed t-test was applied if the data conformed to a normal distribution; in cases where the data exhibited a skewed distribution, a non-parametric test was utilized. A high-precision electronic balance was used to directly weigh the tumor. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the protocols approved by the Welfare Ethics Committee of China Medical University (CMU20250991).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA). All experiments were conducted independently at least three times, and the results are presented as mean ± SD. When comparing between the two groups, if the data followed a normal distribution, the Student’s two-tailed t-test was used; if the data were skewed, the non-parametric test was employed. Bioinformatics analysis and visualization were carried out using R (version 4.3.2). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression and mutation of USP35 in pan-cancer

We analyzed the data of unpaired and paired samples in the TCGA database, and the results showed that the mRNA expression of USP35 was elevated in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), and thyroid carcinoma (THCA) (Fig. 1A, B). Further, we analyzed the expression of USP35 in different organs of the human body, and the results showed that USP35 was expressed higher in normal gastrointestinal tissues than in other organs. USP35 expression was higher in brain tumor tissues than in other tissues (Fig. 1C). We thoroughly analyzed the distribution of USP35 mutations in pan-cancer and the classification of SNV types, with the results showing that the nonsense mutation was the main mutation (Fig. 1D). Finally, further analysis revealed that USP35 had the highest mutation frequency in Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC) compared to all other cancers (Fig. 1E).

Correlation between USP35 and clinical features in pan-cancer

We analyzed the relationship between USP35 expression and clinical features, including tumor stage, T stage, N stage, M stage, grading, and age. As shown in Fig. 2A, there was a correlation between USP35 expression levels and different tumor stages in ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), LUSC, Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM), Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA) and COAD. In prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), there was a positive correlation between the expression level of USP35 and the T stage, and in BLCA, there was a negative correlation between the expression level of USP35 and the T stage (Fig. 2B). In PRAD and COAD, there was some positive correlation between the expression level of USP35 and the N stage (Fig. 2C). In COAD and COADREAD, the expression of USP35 in M1 stage patients was significantly higher than that in M0 stage patients (Fig. 2D). In sarcoma (SARC) and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), female patients had higher USP35 expression levels than males (Fig. 2E), while in KIRC and the Pan-kidney cohort (KIPAN), USP35 expression was slightly higher in stage G1 patients. Finally, we analyzed the association between USP35 and age, in glioma (GBMLGG), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) and Brain Lower Grade Glioma (LGG), showing that USP35 expression and age were significantly negatively correlated (Fig. 2G).

Diagnostic value of USP35 in pan-cancer

To further explore the clinical value of USP35, we systematically evaluated the potential of USP35 as a diagnostic marker for a variety of cancers in the TCGA dataset and the combined TCGA-GTEx dataset (Fig. 3A). In THCA, READ, LUAD, and COAD, the AUC of the working characterization curve of subjects with USP35 was more than 0.7, indicating that USP35 has some diagnostic value for the above cancers (Fig. 3A). Figure 3B demonstrates the specific ROC curves of USP35 in THCA, READ, COAD, and LUAD. The findings shown that USP35 can be used as an effective diagnostic marker for some cancers, reflecting its diagnostic potential in the clinic.

Association between USP35 expression and prognosis in pan-cancer

Next, we analyzed the prognostic assessment value of USP35 in multiple cancers in the TCGA database to examine its correlation with OS, DSS, PFI, and DFI. For survival data, we used both the univariate Cox proportional risk model (Cox) and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Log-rank). The results showed that USP35 was a prognostic protective factor in BLCA, BRCA, LGG, and PAAD, while it was a prognostic risk factor in KIRC, MESO, PRAD, SKCM, STAD, UCEC, and uveal melanoma (UVM) (Fig. 4A). Figure 4B shows the results of one-way regression for pan-cancer OS, while Fig. 4C demonstrates the relationship between the expression level of USP35 and patient OS in UCEC, SKCM, KIRC, and UVM. These results suggest that USP35 has potential as a pan-cancer prognostic marker.

Relationship between USP35 and tumor-associated pathways in pan-cancer

To understand the possible roles and specific functions of USP35 in cancer, we analyzed the activity of the USP35 pathway by integrating characterized gene expression. For 14 functional state gene sets, including angiogenesis, apoptosis, cell cycle, differentiation, DNA damage, DNA repair, EMT, hypoxia, inflammation, invasion, metastasis, proliferation, quiescence, and stemness, the activity of these pathways was determined by integrating the characterized gene expression (combined z-score was obtained). Further Pearson correlation analyses between USP35 and each gene set score were conducted (Fig. 6). In addition, we surveyed datasets from cancer patients for GSEA. The results indicate that USP35 might be involved in a large number of tumor-related pathways and metabolic processes, including myogenesis and the EMT (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Relationship between USP35 and functional proteins and cellular pathways

Utilizing the Cancer Proteome Atlas (TCPA) database, we uncovered the Spearman correlation between USP35 expression and the functional protein levels available in the TCPA database. Our findings reveal that USP35 exhibits significant correlations with multiple functional proteins in cancer. Specifically, in uveal melanoma (UVM), USP35 was notably positively correlated with SYK, P27, and BAX, while it was significantly negatively correlated with SMAC, CDK1, PDCD4, and CMYC (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Figure Supplementary Fig. 2B presents a list of the functional proteins most strongly associated with USP35 in UVM.

Next, we calculated pathway activity scores for ten cancer-related pathways previously reported in the literature: TSC/mTOR, RTK, RAS-MAPK, PI3K-AKT, hormone estrogen receptor (ER), hormone androgen receptor (AR), EMT, DNA damage response, cell cycle, and apoptosis pathways. Patients were stratified into high and low USP35 expression groups based on the median USP35 value, and the differences in pathway activity scores between these groups were determined. Red indicates higher pathway activity in the high USP35 expression group, whereas green signifies a lower pathway activity. Our results demonstrate that USP35 was markedly associated with the apoptosis, cell cycle, and EMT pathways across seven different tumor types (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

Association of USP35 with the level of immune cell infiltration and immune-related genes in pan-cancer

The heatmap shows the landscape of USP35 immune infiltration in pan-cancer (Fig. 7A). USP35 was significantly positively associated with immune genes in UVM, PRAD, KIRC, and COAD, while in adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), BLCA, BRCA, cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESE), and MESO, USP35 showed a significant negative correlation with immune genes. We further explored the correlation between USP35 mRNA expression and immune infiltration and stromal cell abundance. Seven different algorithms were used for the analysis, and the results were validated against each other to ensure accuracy. The results showed that USP35 was positively correlated with the abundance of almost all immune cells in UVM and DLBC (Fig. 7B).

Relationship between USP35 and immunotherapy and targeted drug development in pan-cancer

Immunotherapy has emerged as a prominent treatment option for cancer, yet challenges persist. In light of this, we explored the correlation between USP35 and cancer immunity. Our analysis of three immunotherapy cohorts revealed that patients with low USP35 expression exhibited a higher response to immunotherapy (Fig. 8A). To assess the predictive potential of USP35 for immunotherapy response, we conducted ROC curve analysis (Fig. 8B). By also examining the impact of different treatment groups on patient OS, we found that patients with high USP35 expression had shorter OS (Fig. 8C).

Following an exhaustive review of the relevant literature, we did not identify any reports of small-molecule inhibitors specifically targeting USP35, which piqued our interest. To obtain similarity scores for 1,288 compounds, we compared gene-related features with those in the CMap dataset using the optimal feature matching method, XSum. Compounds with lower scores were considered more likely to inhibit USP35 expression. Our results indicate that imatinib and MK-886 had a significant effect on USP35 across multiple tumor types (Fig. 8D). Figure 9E presents the specific potential target drugs for STAD, PRAD, PAAD, and ACC.

USP35 is strongly associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts in GC

To further understand the potential role of USP35 in GC, we calculated the infiltration of immune cells in GC using multiple methods. The assessed immune cell content differed between high/low USP35 expression groups, where cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) were more abundant in the high USP35 expression group (Fig. 9A). This is consistent with the results of the pan-cancer analysis: CAFs were positively correlated with USP35 in STAD (Fig. 7B). Subsequently, to observe the synergistic effect of CAF infiltration and USP35 expression on survival, GC patients were divided into four subgroups based on median values. Performing separate calculations over the four survival periods, the results show that patients with high CAF infiltration and high USP35 expression had the worst prognosis (Fig. 9B). We further found a significant positive correlation between USP35 and the CAF markers αSMA and FAP in GC tissues (Fig. 9C).

USP35 promotes GC cell migration by regulating energy metabolism reprogramming

In the previous analyses, USP35 showed potential value as a prognostic marker for GC, a common malignant tumor in China, in several datasets. We thus chose it as a model for subsequent mechanistic studies. We constructed GC cells with stable knockdown of USP35 to verify the knockdown efficiency (Fig. 10A). Transwell assays showed that the migration and invasion ability of GC cells decreased after knockdown of USP35 (Fig. 10B). Western blot detection of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers showed that after knockdown of USP35, E-cadherin expression was elevated and vimentin was decreased (Fig. 10C). The above experiments confirmed that USP35 could promote GC cells to undergo the EMT and migration.

To further understand the potential effects of USP35 on GC, we collected USP35-related genes from the TCGA-STAD database. Enrichment analysis showed that these genes were closely related to ATP production (Fig. 10D). Metabolic reprogramming is characteristic of GC, and glycolysis levels have been shown to significantly influence the malignant biological behavior in these cells. Therefore, we then conducted a metabolic kit assay, with the results showing that knockdown of USP35 decreased ATP levels, glucose uptake, and lactate production in GC cells, suggesting that knockdown of USP35 could inhibit glycolysis levels in these cells (Fig. 10E-G).

To clarify whether USP35 affects GC cell migration by regulating energy metabolism reprogramming, we used 2-Deoxy-D-Glucose (2-DG) to inhibit glycolysis in GC cells. The results show that ATP levels, glucose uptake, and lactate production levels were more significantly reduced in cells in the combined 2-DG and USP35 knockdown group compared to the USP35 knockdown group (Fig. 10H-J). We also found that in the combined 2-DG and USP35 knockdown group, the migration and invasion abilities of GC cells were more significantly inhibited compared to those in the USP35 knockdown group. In addition, E-cadherin expression was elevated and vimentin decreased more significantly in the combined 2-DG and USP35 knockdown group compared with the USP35 knockdown group, indicating that the EMT was more significantly inhibited in the combined 2-DG and USP35 knockdown group (Fig. 10K-L).

USP35 promotes GC development in vivo

In this study, the effect of USP35 on tumor formation in nude mice was observed by constructing a subcutaneous tumor formation model with stable knockdown of USP35 in AGS cells. Three weeks later, the mice were sacrificed and the tumor volume and weight in the USP35 knockout group were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig. 11A-B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed that E-cadherin was up-regulated and vimentin was down-regulated after USP35 knockout, which was consistent with the results of in vitro experiments (Fig. 11C). This also indicates that USP35 significantly promoted the tumorigenic ability of GC cells.

Discussion

USP35 is a deubiquitinating enzyme involved in the regulation of intracellular protein degradation and stability [23]. It has been found that USP35 plays an important role in the cell cycle, apoptosis, and DNA repair [24]. Its aberrant expression is closely associated with the development of various tumors, potentially affecting tumor progression by regulating tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis [25–28]. In this study, through a comprehensive analysis of data retrieved from multiple pan-cancer databases, we conducted an in-depth investigation into USP35 across 33 tumor types. Specifically, we explored its expression profiles, prognostic significance, diagnostic potential, and involvement in relevant signaling pathways, as well as its correlations with immune genes and immune cells. Additionally, we further predicted potential drugs that target USP35 and validated the expression and role of USP35 in GC in vivo and in vitro. These findings not only enrich the understanding of USP35’s function in cancer, but also provide new perspectives and potential targets for future studies.

Our results showed that there were significant differences in the expression levels of USP35 in a variety of malignant tumor tissues. In particular, USP35 showed a high level of expression in GC tissues but a low expression level in glioblastoma and endometrial carcinoma, which suggests that the expression pattern of USP35 may be tumor type-specific. It has been demonstrated that patients with high USP35 expression in ovarian cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, renal clear cell carcinoma, and cutaneous melanoma have a poor prognosis. This study further validated the expression characteristics of USP35 in different tumor types by integrating data from the TCGA and GTEx databases, enhancing the reliability of the results. Using univariate Cox regression analysis and Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis, we found that USP35 expression was closely associated with the prognosis of multiple tumors. The results from more than two hundred pan-cancer datasets indicated that USP35 was regarded as a GC factor for DSS, DFI, and PFI in STAD. These findings reveal the potential value of USP35 in prognostic assessments for cancer and provide a theoretical basis for subsequent clinical applications. We also evaluated the diagnostic ability of USP35 in different cancer types. The results showed that the diagnostic value of USP35 was particularly prominent in thyroid and rectal adenocarcinomas, with AUC values of more than 0.8. This finding suggests that USP35 has great potential to become a diagnostic marker. However, these research conclusions are based solely on the transcriptome TCGA/GTEx datasets. In the future, we need to further verify the findings using multiple clinical samples from tumor patients to draw reliable conclusions.

The occurrence of malignant tumors is closely related to the abnormal activation of multiple signal transduction pathways [29, 30]. Classical oncogenic pathways have been found to be abnormally activated in a variety of cancers, promoting cell proliferation and survival [31, 32] and also potentially leading to the resistance of tumor cells to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [33, 34]. Therefore, targeted therapy against these signaling pathways is becoming an important strategy for cancer treatment. By inhibiting the activity of these pathways, tumor growth and metastasis can be effectively suppressed and the therapeutic effects can be improved [35–37]. Through GSEA analysis, we found that USP35 may activate myogenesis, the apical junction, and the EMT in STAD. These findings provide new perspectives for us to understand the complex role of USP35 in gastric cancer biology. In addition, through a Pearson correlation analysis, we revealed changes in the correlation between USP35 and a variety of immune genes in different tumor types, especially in tumors such as STAD, BRCA, MESO, SKCM, and ACC, in which USP35 and immune-related genes exhibited negative correlations. This suggests that in these tumor settings, USP35 expression may suppress the activity of immune responses. In two tumors, UVM and PRAD, a significant positive correlation between USP35 and most immune-related genes was observed. Previous studies have shown that while USP35 plays a role in tumor immune escape, it also interacts with other signaling pathways in the tumor microenvironment. For example, USP35 can affect the survival and immune escape ability of tumor cells under hypoxic conditions through the HIF-1α signaling pathway [38]. HIF-1α plays a key role in the tumor microenvironment by suppressing immune cell function and promoting tumor cell growth [39–41]. In addition, USP35 may further promote immune escape in tumors by affecting cytokine secretion and altering immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. These results suggest that the role of USP35 in the immune microenvironment may be tumor type-specific and that inhibition of USP35 activity may restore the function of immune cells and enhance the immune response to tumors [42, 43].

This study is the first to uncover a close association between USP35 and the response to cancer immunotherapy. Across three independent cohorts, we demonstrated that patients with low USP35 expression displayed marked sensitivity to immunotherapy—an observation whose predictive value was validated via ROC curve analysis. Conversely, patients with high USP35 expression exhibited significantly shorter OSs. Collectively, these findings highlight that USP35 is tightly linked to cancer immunotherapy outcomes, suggesting that inhibiting USP35 expression may enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients.

Although no small-molecule inhibitors targeting USP35 have been identified, our study identified potential inhibitors that may modulate the USP35 signaling pathway through CMap dataset analysis. Imatinib, in particular, significantly affected USP35 expression in a variety of tumors. This finding provides ideas for the future discovery and development of anticancer drugs by targeting the USP35 signaling pathway. However, it is important to note that these predicted results require further experimental validation to determine feasibility and effectiveness in practical applications.

It was found that in GC, high expression of USP35 was significantly associated with high levels of CAFs. CAFs are an important part of the tumor microenvironment, and they influence tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis by secreting growth factors, cytokines, and matrix-remodeling molecules [44, 45]. This finding reveals that USP35 may influence GC progression by regulating CAF infiltration. It was further found that patients with high CAF infiltration and high USP35 expression had the worst prognosis. This suggests that a combination of USP35 expression and CAF infiltration may be a more accurate predictor of the prognosis of GC patients, thus providing a basis for the development of personalized treatment plans [46, 47]. Given the association between USP35 and CAFs and its impact on prognosis, therapeutic strategies targeting USP35 or CAFs may have potential clinical value. For example, inhibitors against USP35 or targeted therapies against CAFs may be able to inhibit GC progression and improve patient prognosis [48, 49]. Our study establishes a critical foundation for future in-depth investigation of the mechanism underlying USP35-mediated regulation of CAFs.

As described in the well-known Warburg effect, tumor cells preferentially choose the glycolytic pathway even in an aerobic environment [50]. This seemingly anomalous metabolic approach is in fact a key feature of tumor cells [51], where this metabolic reprogramming phenomenon is bound to occur in order to meet the demand for unlimited cell proliferation. A high level of cell glycolysis plays a role in driving the growth and metastasis of GC cells, which in turn accelerates the process of GC development. In this study, we found that knockdown of USP35 expression could cause a decrease in ATP and lactate production and glucose uptake in GC cells, as well as a decrease in the ability of GC cells to migrate, invade, and undergo the EMT. After adding 2-DG to stimulate GC cells with knockdown of USP35, the decrease in migration, invasion, and the EMT was even more significant, suggesting that USP35 promotes GC cell migration and invasion by regulating the reprogramming of energy metabolism. In vivo, knockdown of USP35 inhibits the tumorigenic capacity and EMT in GC. Previous studies found that USP35 expression was significantly upregulated in breast cancer tissues and was closely related to tumor proliferation and apoptosis [4, 28]. In LIHC, high USP35 expression was associated with a poor patient prognosis, and inhibition of USP35 expression significantly reduced the proliferation, migration, and invasive ability of LIHC cells [52]. This finding suggests that USP35 may serve as a therapeutic target for LIHC. From this, we hypothesize that USP35 expression promotes GC progression by altering the reprogramming of energy metabolism, but the mechanisms involved remain to be explored in detail.

Our study is mainly based on bioinformatics analysis, and further experimental validation is needed to determine the specific mechanism of USP35 in cancer. In the future, we will continue to explore this in depth to provide new strategies and methods for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

This study exhibits innovation in multiple aspects. First, we systematically analyzed the expression patterns, prognostic value, diagnostic capacity, and potential biological functions of USP35 across various tumors—laying an important foundation for comprehensively understanding its role in cancer. Pan-cancer analysis further revealed that USP35 is significantly overexpressed in GC, and this overexpression correlates with the EMT process in GC cells. Second, we predicted USP35-mediated drug sensitivity, offering insights for the future exploration and validation of drug effects. Finally, we preliminarily validated the expression level of USP35 and its effect on GC progression both in vivo and in vitro, providing key findings for future studies on the pathogenic mechanism of USP35.
