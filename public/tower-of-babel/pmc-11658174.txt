Regulation of intestinal tissue‑resident memory T cells: a potential target for inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Xin Xia, Zhanjun Huang, Chengcheng Xu, Hailong Fu, Shengjun Wang, Jie Tian, Ke Rui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells are populations which settle down in non-lymphoid tissues instead of returning to secondary lymph organs after the antigen presentation. These cells can provide rapid on-site immune protection as well as long-term tissue damage. It is reported that TRM cells from small intestine and colon exhibited distinctive patterns of cytokine and granzyme expression along with substantial transcriptional and functional heterogeneity. In this review, we focus on the reason why they lodge in intestinal tract, their developmental plasticity of going back to to circulation, as well as their regulators associated with retention, maintenance, exhaustion and metabolism. We also elaborate their role in the inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and discuss the potential therapeutic strategies targeting TRM cells.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a complex set of diseases including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). Multiple factors, including genetics, immune response, environment and gut microbiome, can contribute to the pathogenesis of IBD. However, the pathogenesis of this condition remains poorly understood. This complexity drives patient heterogeneity including the disease location, progression, mechanisms and clinical phenotype [1, 2]. CD is a chronic intestinal inflammation that can affect the entire gastrointestinal tract from the mouth to the anus, causing the abdominal pain, weight loss and altered bowel pattern. On the contrary, UC affects mainly the colon and the rectum, leading to bloody diarrhea.

Memory T cells can be classified into three sub-populations, effector memory T (TEM) cells, central memory T (TCM) cells and tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells. Different from TCM cells and TEM cells, TRM cells have been described as a population with the memory function and long-term residence in non-lymphoid tissues, such as skin, lung, gut, brain, liver, kidney and joint [3–8]. Although TCM cells or TEM cells are able to recirculate between the blood and lymphoid organs or peripheral tissues, respectively, TRM cells permanently reside in the tissue. On reactivation, they rapidly produce pro-inflammatory mediators and exert immune responses. Though the location and function of TRM cells are similar to those of tissue resident innate lymphoid cells, TRM cells have diversified antigen-specific T cell receptors (TCR) [9–11].

TRM cells have heterogeneity. In mice, the definition of TRM cell can use parabiosis and fate-mapping experiments. However, these methods are not suitable for human studies. The description of human TRM cells is mainly based on their phenotype of long-term tissue residence. Previously, the TRM cells were identified as CD69+ CCR7− cells. C-type lectin CD69 can counteract sphingosine 1 phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1)-mediated tissue egress, ensuring residency in peripheral tissues [12, 13]. While CCR7 expressed on T cells helps them to enter the peripheral lymph nodes. Substantial studies find that there are many other markers on TRM cells, including CD69, CD103, CXCR3, CXCR6, CD49a, CCR8, PD-1 and CD101 [4, 14–16]. However, this paradigm does not necessarily apply to all TRM cells in different tissues. So this review focuses on the TRM cells in intestinal tract to elaborate their pathogenic and protective role in IBD.

Properties of TRM cells in gut

Phenotype

Though CD69 working as the negative regulation of S1PR1 is usually described as a marker of tissue retention. In the gut, CD69 is widely expressed throughout the T cells with no cluster specificity, and 96% of CD103+ cells express CD69 [17]. CD8+ cells expressing CD103 have a greater proportion than CD4+ CD103+ cells [18]. In general, mucosal TRM cells express CD103, CD69, CD49, CD101 and CXCR6. Moreover, they can express IL-2, TNF-α, TGF-β, IFN-γ and IL-17. IL-2 can promote the proliferation of T cells, contributing to IBD susceptibility [19, 20]. IFN-γ can regulate intestinal epithelial homeostasis [21], and disrupt vascular barrier [22]. IFN-γ and TNF-α promote the apoptosis of intestinal epithelium [23]. IL-17 acting as a pro-inflammatory cytokine can induce immune inflammation in the intestine, and it has been a pharmacological approach for IBD [24, 25]. Recently, a unique subset of TRM cell is identified as CD4+ CD103+ CD161+ CCR5+ [17]. This subset is specific to CD patients and shows cytotoxic activity. They are the predominant producers of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ and IL-2, highlighting the importance of this specific subset in the pathogenesis of CD.

Gut homing

Integrin β7 is a glycoprotein which associates with integrin α4 (CD49d) to form the α4β7 integrin LPAM-1, expressed on intraepithelial lymphocytes. It also associates with αE (CD103) to form the αEβ7 integrin HML-1, expressed on T cells adjacent to mucosal epithelium and intraepithelial lymphocytes. Integrin β7 is only present in the two heterodimeric integrins, α4β7 and αEβ7. Generally, the high-level gut homing phenotype α4β7 integrin is induced on naive T cells after the presentation of cognate antigen in gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALT), smaller lymph aggregates and mesenteric lymph nodes. After entrance to the lamina propria, with the presence of TGF-β, α4β7 may be brought to expression on memory T cells, especially the main CD8+ intraepithelial lymphocytes as well as lamina propria lymphocytes, facilitating the retention of these T cells close to the epithelium [30–32].

CCR9 and α4β7 are known as representative gut-homing markers of antigen-experienced T cells primed in the GALT. T cell activation through CCR9 by CCL25 is exclusive for homing to the small intestine, while binding of α4β7 integrin to MAdCAM-1 occurs predominantly in intestine and GALT [33]. Notably, intestinal TRM cells express lower levels of CCR9, compared with naive and circulating memory T cells, but a greater proportion of colon lamina propria TRM cells express CCR9 compared with TRM cells from the other intestinal compartments [26]. In addition, there are some other markers involved in the gut homing of lymphocytes, such as CXCR4 and its ligand CXCL12, CCR6 and its ligand CCL20. There is increased expression of CCL20 in human IBD [34]. It is suggested that CXCR4 mediates adhesion under steady-state and inflammatory conditions, while CCR6 seems to be more relevant in inflammation [35]. When comparing TRM cells derived from healthy individuals and UC patients. They found that intestinal resident memory T cell phenotypes had high expression of CCR6 by single-cell sequencing analyses [28]. Induction of gut-homing markers is part of this priming process in the GALT except for central memory T cells, which maintains the expression of L-selectin and CCR7 to patrol secondary lymphoid organs ready for the same antigen. Due to the increased expression of endothelial addressins like mucosal vascular addressin MAdCAM-1, recruitment of the primed T cells to the gut is upregulated under the circumstance of inflammation [33].

Retention

The majority of the immune cells seeding in the intestinal mucosa are CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. These cells migrate from circulation and acquire TRM phenotypes through CD103 expression, resulted from constitutive expression of TGF-β and IL-10 by intestinal epithelium and dendritic cells [36–38]. It is reported that TGF-β can induce CD103 expression on T cells both in vivo and in vitro [39, 40]. TGF-β can directly induce CD103 expression through Smad3 or indirectly promote CD103 expression by counteracting T-bet, Eomes, and TCF1- mediated suppression of CD103 expression, thus supporting TRM cell maintenance, especially in epithelial tissues [41]. The expression of CD103 is at quite a high level on T cells in the mucosa of the gut. CD103 which is belonging to αE subunit can bind to E-cadherin on intestinal epithelial cells, mediating adhesion and retention of T cells within the mucosa. It is reported that T cells activated by intestinal epithelial cells can gain suppressive function [42]. Hence intestinal epithelial cells can interact and active T cells to exert regulatory function.

Developmental plasticity

It was previously thought that TRM cells were terminally differentiated with limited proliferative capacity after reactivation and low developmental plasticity. There is the greatest Ki-67 expression in the CD69− population, with the less Ki-67 expression in CD69+ CD103− CD8+ T cells and the least Ki-67 expression in CD69+ CD103+ CD8+ T cells, inferring that the persistence of TRM cells is due to longevity, rather than to in situ proliferation and there is diversity among CD103+ and CD103− TRM cells in features of terminal differentiation [18].

It is demonstrated that TRM cells are able to proliferate after rechallenge in situ [43, 44]. Descendants of intravenously transferred exogenous intraepithelial TRM cells from small intestine prefer to repopulate in the small intestine rather than other tissues such as salivary gland or reproductive tract. Furthermore, these transferred TRM cells are inclined to acquire phenotype of their new environment while retaining some traits of their original resident tissue [26]. Besides, reactivated TRM cells can leave resident tissues and return to the circulation, supplying circulating memory T cells [45, 46]. It is found that there are circulating CD8+ TRM cells in peripheral blood of IBD patients and they have the ability to respond to intestinal bacteria, partly explaining the reason why inflammation can be observed in tissue outside the gastrointestinal tract in some cases of IBD [28, 47]. In the circulation, a CD8+ T cell expressing Id3 means greater memory potential. It is reported that intraepithelial Id3+ CD8+ TRM cells in small intestine have enhanced recall proliferative capacity and multipotency, appearing to have higher developmental potential than their Id3− counterparts [48].

Regulation of TRM cell

Residency-associated regulators

TGF-β is ubiquitously expressed in the intestine and critical for the retention and maintenance of TRM cells. The majority of residency-associated regulators and maintenance-associated regulators are involved in TGF-β signaling. Residency-associated regulators in TRM cells mainly include BLIMP-1 (coded by Prdm1), Hobit (coded by Zfp683), RUNX3, T-bet (coded byTbx21), BCL-6, Eomes and FOXO1.

Hobit and BLIMP-1 can cooperatively regulate TRM cell development in the gut through regulating Klf2, S1pr1, and Tcf7, acting as central regulators of universal program that instructs tissue retention in diverse tissue-resident lymphocyte populations [49]. They can regulate the ability of CD4+ TRM cells to recruit myeloid cells, through production of chemokines such as CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2, and CXCL3 [50]. There are decreased secretions of myeloid-cell-derived cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-12 and IL-23, as well as pro-inflammatory T cell cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-13 and IL-17A, which are mainly Th2 and Th17 cytokines, in Hobit-deficient and Hobit–Blimp-deficient mice [50]. Similar to CD4+ TRM cells, intestinal CD8+ TRM cells expressed Hobit and BLIMP-1 [50, 51], Hobit and BLIMP-1 may have synergistic roles in CD8+ as in CD4+ TRM cells. However, in CD4+ TRM cells, Hobit seems to have a dominant role over BLIMP-1.

RUNX3 plays a key role in establishing CD8+ TRM cells and supports cytotoxic activity of mature CD8+ T cells [49, 52]. In CD8+ TRM cells, RUNX3 can also downregulate the expression of S1PR1 and CCR7, which are required for tissue egress, and repress the transcription factors KLF2, which regulates survival and trafficking of circulating memory T cells [53]. In addition, RUNX3 controls CD4+ T cell localization within the intestinal epithelium [54]. However, there is a report showing that Th2 CD4+ TRM cells do not preferentially express Hobit, BLIMP-1 or RUNX3 in their RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data sets [55]. Deletion of RUNX3 results in the reduction of TRM cell in multiple non-lymphoid organs, such as kidney and salivary gland. Moreover, its deletion contributes to the greatest decrease of TRM cells in the small intestinal epithelium and affects CD103+ TRM cells more compared to CD103− TRM cells [56, 57].

BCL-6 and BLIMP-1 are usually known as antagonistic transcriptional regulators of the effector and memory differentiation of T cells. It is reported that inhibition of BCL-6 can promote the accumulation of TRM cell in the lung [58]. While BCL-6 is not required for lung Th2 TRM differentiation [58]. It is likely that the maintenance of BCL-6 in CD4+ T cells needs the interactions with B cells, implying that B cells have potential effects in the development of TRM differentiation.

It is reported that the T-box transcription factor Eomes can impede the tissue residency of CD8+ T cells in tumor. It can bound directly to the transcription regulatory regions of exhaustion and tissue residency genes [59]. However, Eomes which represses TRM cell formation in some tissues, can support the maintenance of established CD8+ TRM cells after their formation in the small intestine, but not in the colon [26]. CD103 absence allows TRM cells to home to areas of infection, as they do not stick to E-cadherin expressing cells. These CD103− TRM cells are not dependent on TGF-β to maintenance [60]. While both CD103+ and CD103− TRM cells from the small intestine require Eomes for their continued maintenance.

S1PR5 which is another receptor for S1P can regulate TRM cell residency and differentiation. The downregulation of S1PR5 is accompanied by the downregulation of T-bet [61]. T-bet can suppress CD103 (integrin-αE) expression by competing for binding to the integrin-αE promoter with Smad3 which is the downstream of TGF-β signaling [62, 63]. In addition, T-bet acts as an upstream regulator of ZEB2, responsible for the differentiation and formation of memory CD8+ T cells [64]. It is shown that S1PR5 can limit T cell entry and promote T cell egress from peripheral tissues through T-bet–ZEB2. It is reported that T-bet and ZEB2 are required for S1PR5 induction. Local TGF-β is necessary to promote coordinated T-bet, ZEB2, and S1PR5 downregulation [65]. It is suggested that TGF-β signaling coordinates suppression of the T-bet–ZEB2–S1PR5 emigration axis and induces the expression of CD103, thus promoting TRM cell retention, especially in epithelial tissues [56, 65, 66].

FOXO1 is regulated by the phosphorylation of Akt (Ser473) by mTORC2, which leads to inactivation by transport to the cytoplasm. FOXO1 controls the expression of CCR7 and S1PR1, regulating CD8+ T cell trafficking and memory formation [67, 68]. mTOR can direct the accumulation of TRM cells in mucosal tissues. Inhibition of mTOR and its subsequent activation of FOXO1 may then limit the ability of effector CD8+ T cells to egress from lymph nodes and reside in mucosal tissues [69].

Maintenance-associated regulators

Maintenance-associated regulators in TRM cells mainly include Notch, NR4A1, ID2 and ID3. Notch and NR4A1 are involved in the development and maintenance of TRM cells. Notch can regulate the expression of CD103 and contribute to the elevated transcript levels encoding for pro-inflammatory cytokines in TRM cells [70]. NR4A1 is specifically involved in the intraepithelial TRM cells and lamina propria TRM cells in the small intestine [71].

ID2 and ID3 are both critical regulators of TRM cell maintenance. Deficiency of both ID2 and ID3 results in a greater decrease in small intestine intraepithelial CD8+ TRM cells than deletion of ID2 alone. ID3 is able to mark multipotent or memory-like CD8+ T cells in the small intestine. As ID3+ CD8+ T cells can form a large pool of memory cells to supply both TCM and TRM cell populations [48]. ID3− Treg cells are highly enriched in non-lymphoid organs but absent from blood and lymph, suggesting that tissue-resident Treg cells develop tissue residency with Id3 downregulation [72].

Exhaustion-associated regulators

In the chronic infections and tumors, T cells are exposed to antigens and inflammation for a long time, gradually entering a state of dysfunction, known as T cell exhaustion. Its main manifestations include loss of killing function, upregulation of multiple inhibitory receptors and decreased proliferation and differentiation ability. Exhaustion-associated regulators in TRM cells mainly include BACH2, TCF1 (Tcf7) and BATF.

BACH2 can suppress the molecular program driving terminal exhaustion through transcriptional repression and epigenetic silencing. The regulation of BACH2 by cytokines and T-cell activation signals in CD4+ T cells predominantly works on B cells, which increased counts of CD19+ B cells and plasmablasts and promoted secretion of IgG.

Progenitor tissue-resident T cells with the low expression of TCF1 have the capacity to generate new effector lymphocytes and exert effective immune pressure [73]. These cells arise from transitional re-circulating T cells, which have high expression of TCF1. After repeated exposure to antigens, progenitor TRM cells may lead to exhausted TRM cells, dependent on the quality of T cell priming and persistence of cognate antigen. It is recently reported that BCL11b is upstream of TCF1 and BLIMP-1, promoting the tissue residency program of CD8 + TRM cells and restricting the effector program [74]. It is also reported that TCF1 can directly bind to the Itgae (CD103) and partly inhibit the TGF-β-induced CD103 expression, suggesting that tissue residency and homeostatic proliferation of memory T cell are reciprocally regulated by TCF1 [75].

BATF is associated with the development of precursor Treg in non-lymphoid tissues through the initiation of tissue program [76]. It is necessary for adipose Treg differentiation, cooperatively with IRF4, through directly regulating ST2 and PPAR-γ expression [77].

Apoptosis-associated regulators

FasL acts as an apoptosis-associated regulators in TRM cells. It is reported that CD8+ TRM cells are abundant in liver fibrotic areas and induce activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to apoptosis via CCR5-dependent chemoattraction and direct cytotoxic effect on HSCs. In this process, FasL, perforin and granzyme secreted by CD8+ TRM cells play substantial roles. Notably, the apoptosis-inducing effect is not evident in hepatocytes. It is suggested that the cytotoxicity of CD8+ TRM cells are regulated by the fibrosis phase and the targeted cells [78].

Metabolism-associated regulators

TRM cells in small intestine can upregulate expression of metabolic proteins as well as utilize glucose, fatty acids (FAs), oxygen, ATP and amino acids to maintain their long-term survival and function [79]. Metabolism-associated regulators in TRM cells mainly include BHLHE40, Hic1, P2RX7, FABP5 and AhR.

BHLHE40 and its associated genes are higher in CD8+ TRM cells compared to their circulating counterparts. It is essential for tissue residency, fitness and polyfunctionality, through enhancing the expression of TRM effector molecules required for anti-pathogen and anti-cancer process, as well as promoting long-term TRM cell survival. In addition, BHLHE40 can induce the expression of multiple mitochondrial genes that support oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and mitochondrial fitness, which promote downstream acetylation of effector and residency-promoting genes in CD8+ T cells [80, 81].

Hic1 plays a critical role in the regulation of T cell function in the intestine under homeostasis and inflammatory conditions [82]. Knockdown of Hic1 can reduce the number of TRM cells in the small intestine, and overexpression of Hic1 can enhance the differentiation of TRM cells and the protection from infection [83]. It can regulate the expression of P2RX7, which is a eATP sensor. P2RX7 is required for the establishment, maintenance and functionality of small intestinal TRM cells. Mechanistically, P2RX7 promotes mitochondrial homeostasis and metabolic function in differentiating memory CD8+ T cells, possibly via mTOR inhibition to activate AMPK [56, 83, 84]. It is also reported that P2RX7 regulates the metabolic function of TRM cells via promoting type II TGF-β receptor (Tgfbr2) expression through calcineurin signaling to make CD8+ T cell more sensitive to TGF-β [85]. Besides, P2RX7 can negatively regulate ZEB2 expression through TGF-β sensing in effector CD8+ T cells to affect the early TCM/TRM fate [86, 87].

There is a dramatic increase in lipid uptake of TRM cells expressing high levels of CD103 compared with the cells expressing medium or low levels of CD103 in the stomach. Apart from utilizing glucose, TRM cells also depend on fatty acid oxidation for cell survival. Deprivation of FAs leads to TRM cell apoptosis. It means that TRM cells may undergo a metabolic reprogram and switch to mitochondria fatty acid oxidation to meet their energy requirements upon relocation [88]. The FABP family have many different isoforms which are expressed in a tissue-specific manner. When TRM cells relocate to a new organ, they will modify their FABP isoform corresponding to the new location. For example, TRM cells in the skin are mainly dependent on FABP4 and FABP5 as part of their regulation program [56, 89].

AhR, an environmental sensor, is greatly expressed in the process of CD8+ TRM differentiation and granzyme B production. Ahr can promote the resident memory core gene program such as Itgae, Prdm1, and Hic1 [90]. It is important for the development and function of CD8+ TRM cells in the gut. Under the condition of infection, AhR acts as a cell-intrinsic promoter of long-lived, functional TRM cells in the gut [90]. Immune homeostasis in gut

It is well established that intestinal mucosal immune system is a major participator in IBD. Intestinal mucosa is mainly composed of intestinal epithelial cells, lamina propria lymphocytes, intestinal intraepithelial lymphocytes, Peyer's patches and other tissues [91]. The Peyer's patch is a group of lymphatic follicles within the intestinal mucosa, composed of B cells and T cells. They can recognize many intestinal pathogens antigens in the gastrointestinal tract, presented by antigens-presenting cells. During this process, the activated immune cells can return to the lamina propria of the intestinal mucosa through a cyclic homing process, becoming plasma cells and memory T cells, participating in the local immune response of the intestine [92].

Under homeostatic conditions, different cell populations such as microfold (M) cells, dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages transport acquired antigens from the lumen into intestinal tissue. Antigen presenting cells (APCs) carrying those antigens migrate to Peyer´s patches where they present the antigens to naïve T cells. Activation of T cells by costimulatory signal upregulates the expression of IL-2, leading to the T cell proliferation. Some of the cells differentiate into effector cells, others into memory cells. Effector cells are mainly divided into CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells and regulatory T (Treg) cells. CD8+ T cells secrete IFN-γ, perforins and granzymes. CD4+ T cells promote the recruitment of myeloid cells, resulting in broad immune activation in the gut. Tissue damage releases danger signals to restore tissue homeostasis. On the one hand, inflamed gut mucosa represents damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMPs) such as the S100A calgranulins, high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1), IL-1a and IL-33, which can be recognized by the innate immune system and result in a protective inflammatory response [93]. On the other hand, the DC-T cell interaction induces Treg cells to exert suppressive function. Simply put, Treg can be divided into three types. Type 1 is characterized by the production of IFN-γ and the transcription factor T-bet. Type 2 is characterized by IL-4, IL-13 and GATA3. Type 3 is characterized by IL-17 and RORγt. Type 1 Treg cells can be specifically recruited to local inflammatory sites via the chemokine receptor CXCR3. Type 1 Treg cells can inhibit Th1 cell responses, which are dependent on the activation of STAT1 and occur directly in response to IFN-γ produced by effector T cells [94]. In addition, type 1 Treg cells can express high amounts of the integrin β8, which enables them to activate latent TGF-β. The interplay between effector T cells and type 1 Treg cell through integrin β8 endows the bio-availability of TGF-β, thereby promoting the generation of TRM cells [95, 96]. This pathway is not essential in Treg-cell-mediated maintenance of immune homeostasis. However, Treg cells lacking expression of integrin β8 are unable to suppress pathogenic effector T cell responses during active inflammation, suggesting its important role in IBD [97].

Partial memory T cells return to secondary lymph through blood vessels. The others remain in the lamina propria or epithelium. After the same antigen is presented, CD4+ TRM cells will be activated and proliferate to exert direct cytotoxic responses. CD8+ TRM cells will be activated and proliferate to secrete IFN-γ, perforin and granzyme. It is reported that some CD103+ CD8+ TRM cells can inhibit CD4+ TRM cell proliferation through TGF-β and suppress inflammation [98]. Circulating memory T cells will go back to the lamina propria and epithelium, turning into effector cells. TRM cells in inflammatory bowel diseases

The expression of the residency marker CD69 is higher in IBD than the health control and is positively correlated with the disease activity [99]. Moreover, both CD and UC patients with high CD69 expression have a less favorable clinical course than the patients with low CD69 expression. CD69+ CD103+ cells in the LPMCs of patients with IBD are also increased. The majority express either CD4 or CD8. CD4+ CD69+ T cell are increased in CD and UC, whereas CD8+ CD69+ T cells are increased in CD [50, 100]. The intestinal CD4+ CD69+ cells show high expression of CD44 and CD11a, but low expression of S1PR1. Increased CD69+ CD103+ TRM cells in active IBD have higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-13, IL-17A, TNF and IL-12A/B [17]. KLRG1 is increased in CD8+ T cells in CD, exerting cytotoxic and proliferative potential. KLRG1+ CD8+ T cells are increased in inflamed tissue of CD patients compared to non-inflamed counterparts and controls [29]. It is reported that there are decreased CD103+ CD8+ TRM cells in inflamed tissues [29, 100–102]. Additionally, partial CD8+ TRM cells are able to express regulatory markers [98, 101, 103, 104]. It is suggested that CD4+ TRM cells play a dominant pro-inflammatory role in the pathology of IBD and CD8+ TRM cells may support the homeostasis of the non-inflamed gut.

Besides, CD4+ CD103+ TRM cells are related to flaring episodes. Median flare-free survival in patients with high CD4+ TRM cell frequency is significantly shorter than in patients with low CD4+ TRM cell frequency, suggesting that CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ TRM cells are driving disease flares in IBD. CD4+ CD103+ CD161+ CCR5+ TRM cells are more abundant in the gut of CD than in UC and control [17]. This population can be reprogrammed into effector and innate-like phenotype, directly damaging adjacent epithelial cells [105]. In CD, gut-resident CD4+ T cells are the most pathogenic and exert cytotoxic responses upon inflammatory stimuli. In UC, the shift toward the Tfh differentiation program is possibly associated with the upregulated pathological IgG+ plasma cells [28].

There is another report showing that the majority of mucosal infiltrating T cells in active IBD are CD4+ CD103− T cells at diagnosis and follow-up. Though CD103+ CD4+ and CD103+ CD8+ T subsets consist of a minority of T cell infiltration in the inflamed gut. There are decreased percentages of both CD103+ CD4+ and CD103+ CD8+ T cells in colon and ileum in active IBD. These cells increase to values comparable with health controls in remission [100]. This result may seem contradictory to the previous one, but it is actually consistent. CD103 and CD69 are used to broadly define TRM subsets, but CD69+ CD103+ and CD69+ CD103− TRM subsets in the intestine display some distinct functional capabilities, including localization to areas of infection and altered secondary responses. It is reported that CD103+ TRM numbers remain stable, and that these cells do not exit the intestine, however, CD103− TRM subsets preferentially expand in the population during secondary infection in the intestinal tissue [106, 107].

In the mice, CD103+ CD8+ T cells can inhibit proliferation of CD4+ T cells and attenuate transferred ileitis through TGF-β [98]. Local depletion of CD4+ TRM cells protects mice from subsequent TNBS-induced colitis, chronic DSS colitis and T cell transfer colitis, with reduced expression of TRM cell markers and reduced expression of pro-inflammatory mediators, confirming a key role of CD4+ TRM cells in driving intestinal inflammation. Deficiency of Hobit and Blimp-1 in TRM cells postpones the disease course. There are increased levels of pro-inflammatory T cell cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-13 and IL-17A as well as the decreased levels of myeloid-cell-derived cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-12 and IL-23 in Hobit- and Blimp-1-deficient settings. TRM cells deficient for Hobit or Hobit and Blimp-1 are functionally impaired, protecting mice deficient for Hobit and Blimp-1 from experimental colitis in the acute DSS colitis model [50]. Early depletion of Hobit+ cells almost completely prevents CD4+ T cell and myeloid cell infiltration and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production and the development of colitis, suggesting that TRM cells are involved in very early steps of colitis development.

Over 85% of intraepithelial lymphocytes and approximately 40% of lamina propria lymphocytes in the small intestine are αEβ7+. αEβ7 integrin expression on human colonic CD4+ T cells is associated with increased production of pro-inflammatory Th1, Th17 and Th17/Th1 cytokines, with reduced expression of regulatory T cell-associated markers [105]. The binding of the α4β7 integrin to MAdCAM-1 on endothelial cells contributes to the increased recruitment of primed effector T cells to the intestinal mucosa in IBD.

These data support an essential disease-driving role of TRM cells in IBD, arguing for a central position in the initiation of inflammatory cascades in the gut not only in the context of pathogen defense but also in the case of dysregulated immune cell activation in response to intestinal antigens. Pro-inflammatory signaling triggered by TRM cells leads to epithelial destruction predisposing for perpetuation of intestinal inflammation, suggesting that TRM cells represent a potential target for IBD therapy.

Therapy targeting TRM cells

IBD treatment response remains suboptimal, with clinical remission far from universal and loss of response increasing over time [108]. There are growing biological treatment options for IBD, including anti-TNF agents, anti-IL-12 and IL-23, Janus kinase inhibition, anti-α4β7 integrin, anti-α4 integrin, anti-β7 integrin, and S1PR inhibition [109, 110]. Here, we focus on the drugs targeting the adhesion of TRM cell to decrease their accumulation. The anti-α4β7 antibody vedolizumab is proven effective and safe in severe UC, interfering with intestinal T cell trafficking [111]. It is shown that vedolizumab leads to receptor internalization to prevent inducing antibody-dependent cytotoxicity. It can inhibit α4β7:MAdCAM-1 interaction and impede binding of α4β7 to fibronectin. In vivo data for human cells show that vedolizumab inhibits gut homing of T cells from UC patients [112, 113]. Natalizumab is an immune modulator treating multiple sclerosis (MS) and CD. It is a monoclonal antibody directed against the α4 integrin subunit, impeding cells bearing this integrin from interacting with MAdCAM-1 [105]. A large fraction of gut-associated T lymphocytes express the αE integrin. Blockade of α4 integrin leads to a significant reduction of colitis severity compared to control animals, showing its efficacy [114, 115]. S1P receptor modulator ozanimod is effective in both DSS colitis and T cell transfer colitis by disruption of S1PR1. There are significantly more patients who achieve clinical remission in ozanimod treated group compared to placebo treated group [116, 117].

There are still some modulators targeting TRM cells in the trial. Anti-β7 antibody Etrolizumab has recently shown efficacy during a phase III clinical trial in moderate to severe UC. This monoclonal antibody is selective for the integrin β7 subunit and targets both α4β7 and αEβ7 integrin-expressing cells. Anti-β7 treatment contributes to reduced colonic accumulation of αE+ cells, CD8+ and Th9 cells in vivo [118, 119]. Anti-MAdCAM-1 IgG2 antibody PF-00547659, targeting T cell trafficking dependent on integrin, needs evaluation in phase III trials in UC, but not in CD [120, 121].

Conclusion and perspective

TRM cells can fight on the front-line causing local inflammation, and serve as reserves of circulating memory cells leading to systemic diseases. Like central memory T cells, TRM cells have diverse subsets, acting as helper, cytotoxic and suppressor cells respectively [122–124]. As the development of CD103+ TRM cells dependent on TGF-β signaling is evident, the current studies of TRM cells in IBD predominantly focus on the CD103+ cells, which can not totally reflect the real properties of tissue-resident memory cells. Considering the role of CD103− TRM cell in the infection, it is necessary to figure out the other mechanisms how these cells are kept in the non-lymphoid tissues. Due to the essential disease-driving role of TRM cells in IBD, it can be speculated that pathogenic tissue-resident cells may be the key point of therapy. In addition, the current description of human TRM cells is mainly based on their phenotype of long-term tissue residence, which brings about the the contradictory outcomes when defining TRM cell with different phenotypes. It is urgent to reveal new markers and functional properties for human tissue-resident immune cells. For example, cells can be characterized by surface molecules, intracellular markers as well as specific cytokine or granzyme to recognize diverse TRM subsets.

Nowadays, the most therapies targeting TRM cells revolve round the process of retention by receptor internalization, particularly α4β7 and αEβ7, which mainly rely on the amount of CD103+ cells and lack tissue specificity. Thus these drugs are used when the disease is severe and other regular therapies do not work at all. Given that TRM cells are tissue-specific and have developmental plasticity, apart from genetically engineered immune suppressive T cells, we may also modify these cells and use them as navigation or vehicle, making therapy more specific.
