GFPT2 promotes macrophage dysfunction in atherosclerosis via ACADL glycosylation-dependent suppression of ACADL and Arg1
Authors: Kun Ke, Leye Yan, Rong Chen, Chuanrong Zhang, Weizhu Yang, Zefu Zhang, Junqing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by lipid-laden macrophage foam cell formation and defective efferocytosis. This study aims to investigate the impact of GFPT2 on macrophage function in AS and its underlying mechanisms.MethodsWe established an AS model using high-fat diet-fed ApoE−/− mice and ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 and bone marrow-derived macrophages. H&E staining was conducted to examine histopathological changes in the abdominal aorta. Foam cell formation was evaluated by Oil-red-O staining and Dil-ox-LDL uptake, while efferocytosis was measured using apoptotic thymocyte clearance assays. Protein glycosylation was analyzed via PNGase F digestion and site-directed mutagenesis. GFPT2 knockdown and overexpression were performed to assess its functional role.ResultsGFPT2 was upregulated in abdominal aortic tissues and peritoneal macrophages from AS mice, as well as ox-LDL-treated macrophages. In vitro, GFPT2 knockdown reduced foam cell formation, enhanced macrophage efferocytosis, suppressed pro-inflammatory responses, and attenuated mitochondrial ROS production. Notably, GFPT2 downregulated ACADL and Arg1 expression by promoting ACADL N-glycosylation. Moreover, GFPT2 disrupted macrophage function in AS models through regulating Arg1. ACADL knockdown suppressed GFPT2 knockdown-improved macrophage function in AS models. Furthermore, GFPT2 knockdown attenuated the ox-LDL-induced activation of NF-κB and STAT2 and suppression of PPARγ expression. In vivo, GFPT2 knockdown improved serum lipid profile, ameliorated plaque burden, enhanced macrophage efferocytosis, and reduced systemic inflammation in AS mice.ConclusionOur findings revealed a novel GFPT2-ACADL-Arg1 regulatory axis in AS, where GFPT2 suppressed Arg1 expression via promoting ACADL glycosylation, thereby promoting foam cell formation, impairing efferocytosis, and exacerbating inflammation. Moreover, GFPT2 regulated key transcriptional regulators (NF‑κB, STAT2, PPARγ). These results identified GFPT2 as a pivotal regulator of macrophage homeostasis in AS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06203-3.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS), recognized as the predominant contributor to global cardiovascular mortality, manifests pathologically as a chronic inflammatory condition initiated by lipid accumulation within arterial walls [1]. Contemporary research has elucidated the central role of macrophages in AS pathogenesis, wherein pro-inflammatory macrophages foster complex and unstable plaque formation by perpetuating an inflammatory microenvironment, while their anti-inflammatory counterparts facilitate tissue repair and plaque stabilization [2, 3]. The transformation of macrophages into foam cells through ox-LDL uptake not only exacerbates lipid deposition but also amplifies ROS production and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, collectively driving plaque progression [4–6]. Consequently, novel therapeutic strategies targeting macrophage functional modulation to attenuate foam cell accumulation represent a critical avenue for AS intervention [7, 8].

The maintenance of macrophage homeostasis within AS plaques is heavily dependent on efficient efferocytosis [9]. However, as the disease progresses, macrophage efferocytosis capacity declines, leading to the accumulation of apoptotic foam cells in plaque regions and subsequent secondary necrosis, which releases pro-inflammatory mediators to exacerbate plaque instability [10]. Enhanced efferocytosis has been demonstrated to mitigate foam cell accumulation, reduce pro-inflammatory mediator release, and attenuate AS progression [11, 12]. Arginase 1 (Arg1), a characteristic marker of M2 macrophages, orchestrates immune modulation by regulating intracellular arginine metabolism, thereby establishing a microenvironment conducive to inflammation resolution and tissue repair [13]. Notably, Arg1 potentiates macrophage efferocytosis through enhanced apoptotic cell recognition and uptake, thereby exerting protective effects against AS progression [14, 15]. However, the upstream regulatory networks governing Arg1 expression and its impact on macrophage efferocytosis in AS remain incompletely understood.

Glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase 2 (GFPT2), a pivotal modulator of metabolic reprogramming, exerts broad influence on cellular processes through protein glycosylation [16]. Of particular significance, GFPT2 overexpression induces glutamine competition, impairing mitochondrial dynamics and phagocytic function in macrophages [17]. In pancreatic cancer models, GFPT2-mediated YBX1 glycosylation regulates IL-18 secretion and macrophage M2 polarization, thereby reshaping the immunomodulatory landscape [18]. Our analysis of GSE43292 dataset reveals, for the first time, significant GFPT2 upregulation in AS plaques. However, the impact of GFPT2 on macrophage function and its underlying mechanisms in AS remains to be elucidated.

Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase long chain (ACADL), the principal enzyme governing fatty acid β-oxidation, plays an indispensable role in mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide generation and lipid homeostasis, with its deficiency leading to hepatic lipid deposition [19–21]. Recent investigations demonstrate that ACADL inhibition induces fatty acid accumulation in macrophages, modulating NF-κB signaling and iNOS expression to exert anti-inflammatory effects [22]. Transcriptomic analyses have revealed correlations between ACADL and plaque instability as well as immune cell infiltration in AS [23, 24]. Our bioinformatic analyses further identify ACADL as a putative downstream effector of GFPT2, exhibiting marked downregulation in AS plaques, which prompted us to investigate GFPT2-mediated regulation of macrophage function through ACADL.

In this study, we employ in vivo and in vitro AS models to demonstrate that GFPT2 promotes foam cell formation and impairs macrophage efferocytosis. Mechanistically, GFPT2 downregulates Arg1 expression by facilitating ACADL glycosylation, thereby disrupting macrophage functional homeostasis and accelerating AS progression. Additionally, GFPT2 was observed to modulate the activation state of NF-κB and STAT2 and the expression of PPARγ. Our findings provide the first elucidation of the GFPT2-ACADL-Arg1 regulatory axis in AS pathogenesis and reveal the association of GFPT2 with altered key transcriptional regulators, offering both mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic targets for macrophage-centric AS interventions.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

To analyze the expression patterns and relationship between GFPT2 and ACADL in AS, we downloaded the GSE43292 dataset from the GEO database. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package with screening criteria set at |logFC| > 0.32 and p < 0.05. The results were visualized through volcano plots and violin plots generated by ggplot2 package. The functional interaction network between GFPT2 and ACADL was predicted using the GeneMANIA online tool (https://genemania.org/search/homo-sapiens/GFPT2/ACADL//////).

Animals

Male C57BL/6 and ApoE-/- mice (6–8 weeks) were obtained from Sipeifu Biotechnology (Beijing, China) and acclimatized for 1 week in a controlled specific pathogen-free environment (21–26 °C, 40–70% humidity). All protocols were authorized by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital.

Animal models and experiment procedures

Male C57BL/6J mice were maintained on standard chow for 12 weeks as the control group (Con), while ApoE−/− mice were fed an atherogenic diet (#XT108C, Xietong Pharmaceutical, Jiangsu, China) for the same duration to induce AS models. ApoE−/− mice were further divided into AS (untreated), AS + sh-NC (pLKO.1-based control lentivirus expressing a non-targeting shRNA), and AS + sh-GFPT2 (pLKO.1-based GFPT2-knockdown lentivirus) groups, receiving lentivirus tail vein injections prior to AS induction while maintained on atherogenic diet. After the 12-week feeding period, blood was collected from mice in each group following a 12-hour fast. Serum was separated and the levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using a Hitachi 7180 automatic biochemical analyzer. All groups were sacrificed for abdominal aortic tissue and primary peritoneal macrophage collection.

Oil red O staining for lipid accumulation assessment

To evaluate cellular lipid accumulation, RAW264.7 macrophages from different experimental groups were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, and stained using the Oil Red O kit (#C0157S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were pretreated with washing solution for 20 s, incubated with filtered Oil Red O working solution for 15 min at room temperature, and counterstained with hematoxylin. After PBS washes, lipid droplets were visualized under bright-field microscopy, with red-stained lipid deposits indicating positive staining.

For lipid staining in abdominal aortic tissues, Sect. (8 μm thickness) were stained with freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution (#G1015, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) for 10 min in the dark. Differentiation was performed in 60% isopropanol followed by distilled water washes. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (#G1004, Servicebio) for 5 min. After mounting with glycerol gelatin (#80109218, Sinopharm, Shanghai, China), sections were examined under microscopy.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Sections were deparaffinized with xylene substitute and rehydrated through graded ethanol. After distilled water rinsing, sections were stained with hematoxylin (#G1004, Servicebio) for 5 min. Counterstaining was performed with eosin (#G1001, Servicebio) for 2 min, followed by dehydration and clearing. Slides were mounted with neutral balsam (#WG10004160, Servicebio). The pathological status of abdominal aortic tissues was examined under a Leica DM500 microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

The macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (obtained from Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank, Shanghai, China) was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Primary peritoneal macrophages isolated from experimental mice were cultured under identical conditions. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from tibiae and femora of 8-12-week-old mice. Bone marrow cells were treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, resuspended in differentiation medium (DMEM with L-glutamine, 1 g/L D-glucose and 3.5 g/L L-glucose) supplemented with 20% FBS and 10 ng/µL macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and cultured for 4–7 days to differentiate into BMDMs.

For experiments, RAW264.7 macrophages or BMDMs were seeded in appropriate culture plates until reaching 70–80% confluence. To establish an in vitro AS model, cells were treated with 50 µg/mL oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL, #IO1300, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 24 h. Pharmacological interventions of macrophages included 50 µM Numidargistat (CB-1158, #HY-101979, MCE, New Jersey, USA), 2 µg/mL Tunicamycin (TM, #HY-A0098, MCE), 9 µg/mL 4-Phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA, #HY-A0281, MCE), or 10 µM N-linked glycosylation inhibitor 1 (NGI-1, #HY-117383, MCE) co-treatment with ox-LDL for 24 h. For genetic manipulations, cells were transfected with 4 µg of specific plasmids (sh-GFPT2, OE-GFPT2, sh-ACADL, OE-NC, or sh-NC) using 10 µL Lipofectamine 2000 in serum-free DMEM for 6 h, followed by medium replacement and 48-hour culture.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For IF staining in abdominal aortic tissues, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and permeabilization, followed by blocking for 30 min. Then, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GFPT2 (#15189-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), ACADL (#17526-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech), or Arg1 (#16001-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech). For double IF co-localization analysis of GFPT2/CD68, ACADL/CD68, or GFPT2/F4/80, a sequential staining protocol was performed using primary antibody pairs: GFPT2 (1:200) with CD68 (#41077T, 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), ACADL (1:200) with CD68 (1:200), or GFPT2 (1:200) with F4/80 (#sc-377009, 1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies including FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG, (#A0568, 1:500; Beyotime) and Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (#A0516, 1:500; Beyotime) for 2 h at room temperature protected from light. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min.

For IF staining of cellular ACADL, Arg1, or phosphorylated NF-κB subunit p65 (p-p65), RAW264.7 macrophages or BMDMs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against ACADL (#17526-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech), Arg1 (#DF6657, 1:200; Affinity Biosciences, Liyang, Jiangsu, China), or p-p65 (#HY-P80839, 1:200; MCE) overnight at 4 °C. Then, cells were incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody: Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#A0516, 1:100; Beyotime) was used for ACADL and Arg1 detection, whereas FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#A0562, 1:200; Beyotime) was used for p-p65 detection. For double IF staining of GFPT2 and ACADL in BMDMs, a sequential protocol was used. Cells were first incubated with anti-GFPT2 antibody (#DF15367, 1:200; Affinity Biosciences), followed by Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:100). After thorough washing, cells were then incubated with anti- ACADL antibody (1:200), followed by FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200). After nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, fluorescent images were captured using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope. Specificity of the IF staining was confirmed by using corresponding isotype controls, which showed negligible background signal (see Fig. S10).

Dil-ox-LDL uptake analysis

To evaluate macrophage lipid uptake capacity, RAW264.7 macrophages from experimental groups were seeded in 24-well plates until 70–80% confluency. Cells were incubated with 20 µg/mL Dil-labeled ox-LDL (#YB-0010, Yiybiotech, Shanghai, China) in complete medium for 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the medium containing Dil-ox-LDL was aspirated and the cells were washed several times with probe-free medium. Fluorescent images were captured using a Keyence BZ-X800 microscope.

Efferocytosis assessment

For phagocytosis assays, RAW264.7 or primary peritoneal macrophages were serum-starved for 4 h prior to labeling with 5 µM CMTPX red fluorescent dye (#40717ES50, Yeasen, Shanghai, China; 20 min). Apoptotic thymocytes were labeled with 5 µM CFDA SE green dye (#40714ES76, Yeasen; 15 min) and added to macrophages for 4 h co-culture. After PBS washes to remove non-engulfed cells, samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and imaged under fluorescence microscopy.

Flow cytometry analysis of macrophage surface receptors

RAW264.7 or primary peritoneal macrophages were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum. Fixed cells were incubated with anti-cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) (#RM8091, 1:200; Biodragon, Beijing, China) and anti-Mer Tyrosine Kinase (MerTK) (#RM2280, 1:200; Biodragon) antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with FITC- or Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies (#A0562/A0516, Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry with appropriate gating and compensation controls. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each sample was presented as normalized MFI.

Measurement of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Mitochondrial ROS production in RAW264.7 macrophages was assessed using the MitoSOX™ Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (#B8808, ApexBio, Houston, TX, USA) followed by flow cytometric analysis. Briefly, cells from different treatment groups were digested, collected, and washed with PBS. The cell pellets were then resuspended in MitoSOX staining solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed immediately using a flow cytometer.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) or interleukin 8 (IL-8) in RAW264.7 macrophage culture supernatants and mouse serum were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Briefly, samples and standards were added to pre-coated 96-well plates from Mouse MCP-1 ELISA Kit (#E-EL-M3001, Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China), Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (#E-EL-M3063, Elabscience) or Mouse IL-8 ELISA Kit (#EM1592, FineTest, Wuhan, Hubei, China), followed by sequential incubations with detection antibodies and HRP-conjugated streptavidin. After TMB substrate development, the reaction was stopped with stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Quantitative real-time fluorescence PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from abdominal aortic tissues, RAW264.7 macrophages or primary peritoneal macrophages using TRIzol reagent (#G3013, Servicebio) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After RNA concentration and purity, cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (#RR037Q, Takara, Tokyo, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (#RR820Q, Takara) on a Real-Time PCR System (#SLAN-96 S, Hongshi Medical Technology, Shanghai, China). After each reaction was repeated three times, the data were analyzed using 2−∆∆CT method. The primers used in qRT-PCR were listed in Table 1.

Western blotting (WB)

Protein extracts from abdominal aortic tissues, RAW264.7 macrophages or primary peritoneal macrophages were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GFPT2 (#ab190966, 1:2000; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), Arg1 (#16001-1-AP, 1:5000; Proteintech), ACADL (#17442-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), glucose-regulated protein 78 (Grp78) (#A23453, 1:5000; Abclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China), C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) (#15204-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), X-box binding protein 1 splicing (XBP1 splicing) (#24868-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) (#10835-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), lysosome-associated membrane Glycoprotein 1 (LAMP1) (#21997-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (#18986-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), signal transducer and activator of transcription 2 (STAT2) (#A14995, 1:5000; Abclonal), p-STAT2 (#AP0284, 1:5000; Abclonal), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) (#16643-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), P65 (#HY-P80765, 1:1000; MCE), p-P65 (#HY-P80839, 1:1000; MCE), or β-tubulin (#10094-1-AP, 1:20000; Proteintech) as loading control. Following TBST washes, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (#AB0101, 1:5000; Abways, Beijing, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a gel imaging analysis system (SINSAGE, Beijing, China).

PNGase F deglycosylation assay

To verify N-glycosylation of ACADL, RAW264.7 cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer, followed by protein quantification with BCA assay. According to the instructions of PNGase F kit (#P2318L, Beyotime), denatured proteins were treated with PNGase F (500 U/µL) at 37 °C for 2 h. After terminating the reaction, samples were analyzed by WB as described above.

Statistical analysis

In in vivo studies, six mice per group served as biological replicates for histology, whereas three were used for molecular analysis. For in vitro assays, three independent biological replicates were conducted. Each biological replicate assay was performed with three technical replicates. Data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA) and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Two-tailed Student’s t test were used for comparing two variables. One-way ANOVA test was used for multiple variable comparison. P < 0.05 was considered as a significant difference.

Results

GFPT2 was upregulated in abdominal aortic tissues and peritoneal macrophages of AS mice

To induce AS, ApoE−/− mice were maintained on atherogenic diet for 12 weeks. Compared to control C57BL/6 mice on normal chow, AS mice exhibited accelerated weight gain (Fig. 1A). Histopathological assessment using H&E and Oil Red O staining showed increased lipid deposition, thickened vascular walls, and enhanced plaque and foam cell formation in abdominal aortas of AS mice (Fig. 1B–C). IF assay revealed elevated GFPT2 expression in abdominal aortic tissues of AS mice (Fig. 1D). qRT-PCR and WB analyses further confirmed significant increases in GFPT2 mRNA and protein levels in both abdominal aortic tissues and primary peritoneal macrophages from AS mice (Fig. 1E–F). In vitro, treatment of RAW264.7 cells with 50 µg/mL ox-LDL for 24 h also markedly increased GFPT2 expression compared to controls (Fig. 1G–H). Bioinformatic analysis of GSE43292 dataset validated GFPT2 upregulation in human AS plaques (Fig. S1A). Thus, GFPT2 expression was significantly elevated in macrophages from both in vivo and in vitro AS models.

GFPT2 promoted foam cell formation, impaired macrophage efferocytosis, and enhanced pro-inflammatory responses in AS

To investigate the role of GFPT2 in macrophage function, we knocked down GFPT2 in RAW264.7 cells and verified efficiency by qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 2A–B). Oil-red-O staining and confocal microscopy demonstrated that GFPT2 knockdown significantly attenuated ox-LDL-induced foam cell formation, evidenced by reduced lipid accumulation (Fig. 2C–D) and decreased Dil-ox-LDL uptake in RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 2E). Notably, GFPT2 knockdown partially reversed the ox-LDL-induced reduction in the phagocytosis of apoptotic thymocytes by RAW264.7 macrophages in an efferocytosis assay, suggesting enhanced efferocytosis (Fig. 2F–G). Flow cytometry and qRT-PCR confirmed that GFPT2 knockdown suppressed ox-LDL-induced upregulation of scavenger receptor CD36 and downregulation of efferocytosis mediator MerTK (Figs. 2H–J, S2A). Furthermore, GFPT2 knockdown alleviated ox-LDL-induced inflammation, including reduced MCP-1 and IL-8 levels measured by ELISA and decreased mRNA levels of MCP-1, IL-8, and TNF-α (Fig. 2K–O). Collectively, these results demonstrated that GFPT2 knockdown in RAW264.7 macrophages significantly reduced lipid accumulation and Dil-ox-LDL uptake, enhanced the phagocytosis of apoptotic thymocytes, and suppressed the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This indicated that GFPT2 played a critical role in regulating key aspects of macrophage dysfunction in AS, including foam cell formation, macrophage efferocytosis, and pro-inflammatory responses in AS.

Given the close relationship between ROS and the inflammatory phenotype, we investigated whether GFPT2 regulated mitochondrial ROS production in RAW264.7 macrophages. Assessment using the MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator revealed that ox-LDL treatment markedly induced mitochondrial ROS generation in RAW264.7 macrophages. Notably, GFPT2 overexpression significantly enhanced this effect, whereas GFPT2 knockdown further attenuated mitochondrial ROS production (Figs. 2P-Q, S2B). Furthermore, the changes in the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (MCP-1, TNF-α, and IL-8) as determined by ELISA in response to GFPT2 manipulation paralleled the changes in mitochondrial ROS level (Fig. 2R-T). These findings suggested that GFPT2 exacerbated the pro-inflammatory response in ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 macrophages, at least in part, by amplifying mitochondrial oxidative stress.

GFPT2 downregulated ACADL and Arg1 expression by promoting ACADL glycosylation

Bioinformatic analysis of the GSE43292 dataset confirmed ACADL downregulation in human AS plaques (Fig. S1A), while GeneMANIA predicted potential GFPT2-ACADL interaction (Fig. S1B). Compared to controls, AS mice showed significantly reduced ACADL expression in abdominal aortic tissues and primary peritoneal macrophages as assessed by qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 3A–B). In vitro, IF staining demonstrated the co-localization of GFPT2 and ACADL in mouse BMDMs, which was enhanced under ox-LDL stimulation (Fig. 3C). Moreover, ox-LDL treatment decreased ACADL mRNA and protein levels in RAW264.7 macrophages (Figs. 3D, S3A). Deglycosylation assays using PNGase F confirmed N-glycosylation of ACADL in ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 3E). Bioinformatic prediction via PhosphoSitePlus further supported the presence of potential N-glycosylation sites on ACADL (Fig. S3B). The mutation of glycosylation site N273 in ACADL abolished ACADL glycosylation (Fig. 3F). qRT-PCR and WB further demonstrated that GFPT2 knockdown reduced ACADL glycosylation while increasing its expression in ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 cells (Figs. 3G, S3C). To clarify the role of GFPT2 in regulating ACADL during AS, ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 cells or mouse BMDMs were transfected with OE-GFPT2, followed by treatment with or without glycosylation inhibitor TM. GFPT2 overexpression decreased ACADL and M2 polarization marker Arg1 expression while increasing ACADL glycosylation, which was blocked by TM (Fig. 3H-L). Thus, GFPT2 suppressed Arg1 expression by promoting the glycosylation of ACADL.

To distinguish between the effects of N-glycosylation inhibition and secondary endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress induced by TM on ACADL/Arg1 downregulation, two complementary strategies were employed in both RAW264.7 cells and BMDMs: co-treatment with the ER stress reliever 4-PBA and use of the specific N-glycosylation inhibitor NGI-1 (which does not induce unfolded protein response (UPR)). TM increased the expression of ER stress markers (Grp78, CHOP, XBP1 splicing, ATF4) and inhibited the N-glycosylation of LAMP1 and EGFR, whereas NGI-1 inhibited glycosylation without triggering ER stress (Fig. S4A-C, G-I). Notably, 4-PBA treatment effectively mitigated TM-induced ER stress but failed to reverse the TM-mediated upregulation of LAMP1, EGFR, ACADL and Arg1 (Fig. S4A-E, G-J). Crucially, NGI-1 recapitulated the effects of TM, leading to a significant upregulation of ACADL and Arg1 (Fig. S4D-E, J-K) and a concomitant reduction in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines MCP-1, TNF-α, and IL-8 (Fig. S4F, L). These data collectively demonstrated that the regulation of the ACADL/Arg1 axis and inflammation was a consequence of N-glycosylation inhibition, independent of ER stress/UPR activation.

GFPT2 disrupted macrophage function in AS by suppressing ACADL and Arg1

qRT-PCR and WB confirmed significant Arg1 downregulation in abdominal aortic tissues and primary peritoneal macrophages from AS mice (Fig. 4A–B). Stimulation with 50 μg/mL ox-LDL for 24 h also reduced Arg1 mRNA and protein levels in RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4C–D). GFPT2 knockdown rescued Arg1 expression in ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4E–F). Oil-red-O staining and confocal microscopy revealed that treatment with the Arg1 inhibitor CB-1158 reversed GFPT2 knockdown-induced reductions in lipid accumulation and Dil-ox-LDL uptake in ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4G–I). Moreover, CB-1158 partially offset GFPT2 knockdown-enhanced efferocytosis in ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4J–K). In ox-LDL-treated RAW264.7 macrophages, CB-1158 also inhibited GFPT2 knockdown-mediated CD36 reduction, MerTK elevation as assessed by flow cytometry and qRT-PCR (Figs. 4L–P, S5), and decreased MCP-1 and IL-8 secretion as measured by ELISA (Fig. 4Q–R). These findings demonstrated that GFPT2 promoted foam cell formation, suppressed efferocytosis, and enhanced inflammation through reducing Arg1.

GFPT2 knockdown inhibited the activation of NF-κB and STAT2 and restored PPARγ expression in ox-LDL-treated macrophages

To explore additional mechanisms contributing to the GFPT2-mediated phenotypic changes, its effect on key signaling pathways was investigated. In RAW264.7 macrophages and BMDMs, ox-LDL stimulation significantly increased p65, p-p65, STAT2, and p-STAT2 levels, leading to elevated p-p65/p65 and p-STAT2/STAT2 ratios, while it concurrently suppressed the expression of the nuclear receptor PPARγ. Notably, GFPT2 knockdown in these cells markedly attenuated the ox-LDL-induced activation of p65 and STAT2 and suppression of PPARγ (Fig. S8A-F). These findings indicated that alongside the glycosylation-dependent regulation of ACADL, GFPT2 simultaneously activated pro-inflammatory pathways (NF-κB, STAT2) and suppressed a key anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulator (PPARγ).

GFPT2 knockdown ameliorated AS progression in vivo

GFPT2 knockdown in AS mice was verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. S9A). GFPT2 knockdown slowed the rate of body weight gain in AS mice (Fig. 6A). Serum lipid analysis revealed that GFPT2 knockdown counteracted the atherogenic diet-induced dyslipidemia, reducing TC, TG, and LDL-C serum levels while increasing HDL-C serum level (Fig. 6B). Moreover, GFPT2 knockdown reduced the elevated serum levels of liver enzymes ALT and AST observed in AS mice (Fig. S9B), indicating an improvement in metabolic homeostasis. WB and IF staining confirmed that GFPT2 knockdown caused a decrease in GFPT2 and an increase in ACADL and Arg1 in abdominal aortic tissues of AS mice (Fig. 6C–D). IF double staining demonstrated co-localization of CD68 with GFPT2 or ACADL in abdominal aortic sections. GFPT2 knockdown reduced GFPT2 fluorescence intensity and increased ACADL fluorescence intensity within CD68+ macrophage areas in abdominal aortic plaques of AS mice (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, co-staining with the macrophage marker F4/80 confirmed that both the expression and the co-localization of GFPT2 with F4/80 were significantly upregulated in macrophages of AS mouse abdominal aortic tissue, which was attenuated by GFPT2 knockdown (Fig. S9C). Quantification of the macrophage marker F4/80 confirmed increased macrophage content in AS plaques, which was significantly suppressed by GFPT2 knockdown (Fig. S9D). Histological analysis using H&E staining showed that GFPT2 knockdown inhibited vascular wall thickening and plaque formation (Fig. 6F). Quantitative morphometric analysis further revealed a significant reduction in the overall plaque burden and the necrotic core area within abdominal aortic plaques of GFPT2-knockdown AS mice (Fig. 6G). Oil-red-O staining demonstrated that GFPT2 knockdown reduced lipid deposition and foam cell accumulation in abdominal aortic tissue of AS mice (Fig. 6H–I). Additionally, GFPT2 knockdown enhanced efferocytosis of primary peritoneal macrophages from AS mice, as assessed by a fluorescence microscopy-based phagocytosis assay using apoptotic thymocytes (Fig. 6J–K). Flow cytometry and qRT-PCR revealed decreased CD36 and increased MerTK expression in the surface of primary peritoneal macrophages from AS mice following GFPT2 knockdown (Figs. 6L–P, S9E). ELISA showed GFPT2 knockdown led to reduced serum MCP-1 and IL-8 levels in AS mice (Fig. 6Q–R). Thus, GFPT2 knockdown suppressed foam cell and plaque formation, enhanced efferocytosis, and reduced inflammation, ultimately mitigating AS progression.

Discussion

While lipid-lowering drugs remain the primary therapy for reducing AS risk in high-risk cardiovascular patients, they fail to effectively suppress chronic inflammation, a key driver of disease progression [25, 26]. Macrophage functional homeostasis significantly influences the pro-inflammatory response in AS [27]. Through experimental models and bioinformatic analysis, we provided the first evidence of GFPT2 and ACADL expression patterns and their interaction in AS. Mechanistically, GFPT2 inhibited Arg1 expression through N-glycosylation modification of ACADL, disrupting macrophage function. These findings not only offered novel insights into disease progression but also identified potential therapeutic targets for modulating AS plaque development.

The hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP) is a glucose metabolic pathway that generates UDP-GlcNAc, which is subsequently utilized for protein glycosylation modifications [16, 28]. GFPT2 serves as a key enzyme in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway [29]. Our AS models showed significant GFPT2 upregulation, consistent with emerging evidence highlighting HBP’s crucial role in cardiovascular diseases [30, 31]. Jin et al. demonstrate that HBP promote lipid accumulation in RAW264.7 macrophages, increasing AS risk [32]. Furthermore, GFPT2 has been shown to influence macrophage polarization [18]. Our study extended these findings by revealing that GFPT2 disrupts macrophage homeostasis through promoting foam cell formation, impairing efferocytosis, and enhancing pro-inflammatory responses, ultimately accelerating plaque development. These results established GFPT2 as a critical regulator of both plaque formation and inflammatory progression in AS.

Previous studies have confirmed the role of ACADL in fatty acid β-oxidation [19], while our work provided the first evidence that its expression was regulated by GFPT2-mediated glycosylation modification under AS conditions. These findings align with reported protein glycosylation regulatory functions of GFPT2 [18, 32]. Moreover, we identified N273 as the specific glycosylation site on ACADL in AS macrophages. Bioinformatic prediction (PhosphoSitePlus) further supported the existence of potential N-glycosylation sites on ACADL, including N273. Although ACADL is a mitochondrial enzyme and canonical N-linked glycosylation occurs in the ER, our inhibitor studies using the specific N-glycosylation inhibitor NGI-1, which blocked glycosylation without activating the UPR, demonstrated that the regulation of ACADL and its downstream effects were indeed attributable to glycosylation inhibition, not secondary ER stress. Zhang et al. have reported that stylissatin A exhibits anti-inflammatory activity in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages by inhibiting ACADL [22]. ACADL deficiency has been shown to limit alveolar inflammation by reducing CXCL2 production in type 2 alveolar epithelial cells [33]. In our study, ACADL knockdown counteracted the restorative effects of GFPT2 knockdown on macrophage function and inflammation reduction. The first identification of this glycosylation-dependent GFPT2-ACADL regulatory relationship, along with their impact on macrophage function, revealed a pathogenic cascade linking metabolic dysfunction to impaired inflammation resolution in AS plaque progression. In addition to post-translational regulation, we also observed that GFPT2 knockdown increased ACADL mRNA level, suggesting potential transcriptional regulation. While the present study primarily establishes glycosylation-mediated control of ACADL protein, this mRNA change hints at additional regulatory layers. We speculate this may involve a feedback mechanism where stabilized ACADL protein enhances its own expression, though the exact pathway requires future investigation.

The M2 macrophage marker Arg1 promotes inflammation resolution and tissue repair [13]. Studies demonstrate defective efferocytosis and impaired AS plaque regression in Arg1-deficient mice [15]. Thiazolidinedione drugs that stimulate Arg1 production enhance macrophage efferocytosis [34]. However, upstream regulators controlling Arg1 expression in lesional macrophages remain unclear. Recent studies suggest GFPT2 influences macrophage polarization through glycosylation modification [18]. Our findings further revealed that GFPT2 knockdown promoted Arg1 expression by inhibiting ACADL glycosylation, thereby enhancing macrophage efferocytosis, reducing plaque formation, and attenuating inflammation. These results suggested that metabolic reprogramming might drive functional polarization of macrophages in AS.

This study elucidated a novel mechanistic pathway through which GFPT2 promoted foam cell formation. The GFPT2-ACADL-Arg1 axis appears to function as a hub that coordinately regulates both lipid influx and processing in macrophages. On one hand, GFPT2-mediated glycosylation of ACADL led to the downregulation of Arg1, which resulted in a significant upregulation of the scavenger receptor CD36. This increase in CD36 likely potentiates the uncontrolled uptake of modified lipoproteins, providing a primary source for lipid accumulation [35, 36]. On the other hand, the glycosylation and consequent functional impairment of ACADL itself would be expected to directly compromise mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, a key process for the catabolism of internalized lipids [19, 21]. Furthermore, the suppression of Arg1 concurrently caused the downregulation of the efferocytosis receptor MerTK. This defect not only impairs the clearance of apoptotic cells [14, 37] but may also indirectly exacerbate lipid retention, as efferocytosis by macrophages is known to contribute to the maintenance of lipid homeostasis within plaques [38]. Therefore, the GFPT2-ACADL-Arg1 pathway likely promoted foam cell formation through a dual mechanism: by augmenting lipid influx via CD36 while simultaneously impairing lipid clearance via suppressed fatty acid oxidation and efferocytosis. This synergistic action provides an explanation for the robust reduction in foam cells observed upon GFPT2 knockdown.

Our study further revealed that GFPT2’s impact extended beyond the glycosylation-dependent ACADL-Arg1 axis. We found that GFPT2 was required for the activation of NF-κB and STAT2 in ox-LDL-stimulated macrophages, while it suppressed the expression of the nuclear receptor PPARγ. This regulatory pattern is highly relevant. NF-κB is a well-established suppressor of Arg1 expression [39, 40], and PPARγ is a key transcriptional activator of CD36 and a driver of anti-inflammatory responses [41, 42]. Furthermore, the activation of STAT2, a transcription factor involved in pro-inflammatory signaling [43, 44], aligns with the observed inflammatory shift. Thus, GFPT2 likely orchestrates macrophage dysfunction through a convergent, multi-pronged mechanism. It directly impairs lipid metabolism via ACADL glycosylation and simultaneously reprograms the transcriptional network by activating pro-inflammatory pathways (NF-κB, STAT2) and suppressing the anti-inflammatory regulator PPARγ. The coordinated downregulation of Arg1 and upregulation of CD36 emerges as a central node where these distinct pathways intersect, explaining the potent phenotypic effects of GFPT2 modulation. Future work should aim to determine whether these transcriptional effects are direct consequences of HBP flux, potentially through glycosylation of specific transcription factors or co-regulators, or are secondary to broader metabolic reprogramming.

Current AS therapies primarily focus on lipid-lowering or broad anti-inflammatory strategies [5]. The newly identified GFPT2-ACADL-Arg1 axis in this work represents a previously unrecognized pathway integrating macrophage metabolism and pro-inflammatory responses, offering special significance for understanding plaque progression and developing novel therapies. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while we confirmed the importance of GFPT2 in Apoe−/− mouse models, validation in human macrophages and clinical specimens, as well as in alternative AS models such as Ldlr−/− mice, is needed to confirm its broader relevance and dissect its role independent of the profound metabolic disturbances associated with ApoE deficiency. Second, our in vivo study utilized a systemic knockdown approach. While we observed GFPT2 reduction within plaque macrophages and associated therapeutic benefits, future macrophage-specific conditional knockout models are needed to definitively establish that the beneficial effects are specifically due to GFPT2 loss in macrophages, rather than in other cell types. Third, our study primarily elucidated a glycosylation-dependent mechanism. The concurrent regulation of key transcription factors (NF-κB, STAT2, PPARγ) by GFPT2 reported here suggests a broader and multifaceted regulatory network. How this transcriptional reprogramming interacts with the ACADL-Arg1 axis to coordinately drive macrophage dysfunction remains an important question for future investigation. Fourth, as this study focused on macrophage function, future investigations should explore the effects of GFPT2 on other immune cells, such as neutrophils, B cells, and T cells, to fully understand its impact on AS [45]. Finally, macrophage lipid load, receptor activation, and metabolic status may vary across different AS stages [46], making validation in staged patient cohorts valuable.

Conclusion

This study established GFPT2 as a critical upstream regulator that drove macrophage dysfunction in AS through a glycosylation-dependent suppression of the ACADL-Arg1 axis. We demonstrated that GFPT2 upregulation reduced Arg1 expression by promoting the N-glycosylation of ACADL at the N273 site, ultimately leading to macrophage dysfunction in AS. This was mechanistically linked to our key observations: increased expression of the scavenger receptor CD36, which enhanced modified lipid uptake, and decreased expression of the efferocytosis receptor MerTK, which crippled the clearance of apoptotic cells. Collectively, this GFPT2-initiated pathway synergistically promoted foam cell formation, efferocytosis deficiency, lipid accumulation, and a pro-inflammatory plaque microenvironment. Furthermore, GFPT2 was found to modulate key transcriptional regulators (NF-κB, STAT2 and PPARγ). Our findings reveal GFPT2 as a pivotal regulator that connects cellular metabolism to immune function in AS, identifying GFPT2 as a potential therapeutic target for simultaneously modulating lipid metabolism and pro-inflammatory responses in developed plaques.
