Healthcare professionals’ perceptions and needs for a Home-based Artificial Intelligence Pain Management System for cancer pain: A qualitative study
Authors: Mengmeng Chen, Xin Chen, Jiajun Yuan, Meiling Yang, Jiachun Lu, Wing Chi Lam, Cheuk Yan Ng, Thomas K.S. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore healthcare professionals’ (HCPs’) perceptions of and needs for a Home-based Artificial Intelligence Pain Management System (HAIPMS) for cancer pain in Hong Kong, China.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 14 HCPs from Jockey Club Home for Hospice (JCHH) in Hong Kong, China between March and June 2025. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsTheir professional experience ranged from 3 to 33 years. Thematic analysis generated three themes and ten subthemes. The themes were: 1) valued but under-supported home-based cancer pain management, including family-dependent and intermittent professional support; comfort, affordability, and system relief in home-based care; and subjective assessment, medication non-adherence, and delayed support; 2) cautious optimism toward AI integration, including expectations for visible, measurable, and continuous pain monitoring; concern of risks resulted from AI implementation; and heterogeneous attitudes shaped by professional and generational divides; and 3) practical requirements for HAIPMS implementation, including essential functional modules; user-centered design and adaptability; safety and ethical assurance requirements; and training and sustained support.ConclusionsHCPs viewed HAIPMS as a potentially useful supportive tool for home-based cancer pain management, provided that it preserves professional autonomy, fits local care contexts, and incorporates ethical safeguards, user-friendly design, and sustained support. These findings offer practical guidance for developing AI-assisted pain management systems in home-based cancer pain.

Introduction

The global incidence of cancer is rising rapidly, placing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1]. In Hong Kong, China population aging has further intensified the burden of cancer care [2,3]. Pain is one of the most common and feared symptoms among cancer patients, particularly those with advanced or metastatic cancer [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Cancer pain is often influenced by tumor progression, treatment-related effects, and psychological distress, significantly impairing patients’ comfort, mobility, emotional state, social interactions, and quality of life [4]. Current management paradigms, however, rely primarily on subjective patient reports and intermittent clinical assessments. This approach lacks objective and continuous monitoring, creating a critical gap in care continuity. The problem is particularly acute for home-based patients, who face limited access to timely medical guidance, leading to ineffective and non-personalized pain management [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. This pressing reality underscores the urgent need for innovative, technology-driven strategies.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has shown potential in cancer management by enabling objective, continuous, and multimodal assessment. Studies have explored AI-based pain recognition using facial expressions, video data, natural language processing, electronic medical records, and physiological signals such as heart rate variability (HRV) [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, evidence remains limited, and many studies lack external validation and real-world clinical testing [16]. To address this gap, our research team has initiated the development of a Home-based Artificial Intelligence Pain Management System (HAIPMS), a clinically oriented platform tailored for home-based cancer pain management. The preliminary framework includes four related modules: multimodal data acquisition, AI-powered pain analysis, real-time pain alerts, and remote collaboration between patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals (HCPs). The system is intended to support more objective, continuous, and personalized pain management in home settings.

Successful implementation of such a system depends not only on technical performance but also on whether it fits clinical workflows. HCPs’ perceptions and needs are therefore critical for informing system design and future adoption [17,18]. However, limited research has examined HCPs’ perspectives on AI-assisted home-based cancer pain management in Hong Kong, China's specific sociocultural and healthcare context. This qualitative study aimed to explore Hong Kong, China HCPs’ perceptions of and needs for the proposed HAIPMS, providing evidence to guide its subsequent design, development, and clinical translation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilized a qualitative approach to explore HCPs’ perceptions and needs regarding the HAIPMS through face-to-face, semi-structured interviews. The study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [19]. Interviews were conducted with HCPs in a palliative facility in Hong Kong, China between March and June 2025.

Participants and recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to recruit HCPs with experience in oncology or palliative care from Jockey Club Home for Hospice (JCHH) in Hong Kong, China. Eligible participants were licensed HCPs who had at least two years of experience in cancer pain management. To capture diverse perspectives, participants were recruited from community-based palliative care, inpatient palliative care, and oncology specialty settings. Recruitment continued until data saturation was reached. Data saturation was tentatively identified after 12 interviews, as no new themes or critical insights emerged. Two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation. Finally, 14 HCPs, including three doctors and eleven nurses, were included.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the university’s ethics committee (Ref. 202411017). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the interviews. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time. To protect confidentiality, no personally identifiable information was collected. Each participant was assigned an anonymous code. Audio recordings, transcripts, and field notes were stored on an encrypted computer accessible only to the research team. Quotations were de-identified before reporting.

Data collection

The interview guide was developed based on a literature review and research team discussions. It consisted of two parts: participant characteristics and open-ended questions related to home-based cancer pain management, perceptions of AI-assisted pain management, expectations for HAIPMS functions, and design and implementation considerations. Example questions included: “How do you currently manage cancer pain among home-based patients, and what difficulties do you encounter?”, “What are your views on using AI to support cancer pain management?”, “Would you be willing to use AI to support cancer pain management?”, “What functions would you expect HAIPMS to include?”, “What concerns should be considered before implementing such a system?” and “What suggestions do you have regarding the integration of AI into home-based cancer pain management?” To validate the interview guide, a pilot study was conducted on three frontline HCPs who met the study’s inclusion criteria but were not included in the final sample. Minor revisions were made to improve clarity and question flow.

The interviewers were two master’s students majoring in Nursing. Before data collection, they received systematic training in qualitative research, covering qualitative research protocols, interview skills, and ethical norms. To avoid potential bias, the interviewers had no prior professional or personal relationships with the participants, and their identity was clearly communicated to participants during the informed consent process.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in participants’ offices. Before the interview, participants were given a detailed explanation of the study and required to sign an informed consent form. During the interviews, participants were allowed to express their opinions freely so that the interviewers could capture as much information as possible to gain an in-depth understanding of their perceptions and needs regarding the HAIPMS. Each interview lasted between 25 and 55 min and was conducted in either Putonghua or Cantonese. All interviews were audio-recorded, and field notes were taken.

Data analysis

Participants’ demographic characteristics were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The interview data were studied using an inductive thematic analysis, following the six-step approach outlined by Braun and Clarke [20]. To enhance analytical rigor, two researchers were involved throughout the process, working both independently and collaboratively. First, all interviews were transcribed verbatim within 24 h, and both researchers repeatedly reviewed the transcripts to achieve data familiarization and record preliminary observations. Next, they independently performed line-by-line coding to generate initial codes. This phase was data-driven, aiming to keep the codes close to participants’ language and the semantic content of the data. Upon finishing independent coding, they met to compare their code lists, resolve discrepancies through discussion and by referring to the raw data, and finalized a unified coding framework. Using the initial coding framework, both researchers independently sorted and combined the codes into potential themes. They subsequently reviewed and refined these themes through a two-level check against coded extracts and the full dataset; redundant or inconsistent themes were merged, split, or discarded. After collaborative team review and discussion, a final set of coherent themes was established. Both researchers worked together to define the essence of each theme, determining its scope, core aspects, and significance to the research question, which informed the final thematic labels and formal definitions. Once themes were finalized and labeled, the scholarly report was drafted. Anonymized participant quotes are used to illustrate the findings, with all identifiable information removed to protect privacy.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was enhanced using the criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [21]. For credibility, researchers conducted empathetic interviews using probing and paraphrasing. Member checking was performed by having participants verify transcript accuracy and interpretive authenticity, with their feedback integrated into the analysis. For transferability, purposive sampling was employed to recruit a diverse group of HCPs, varying in role, experience, and practice setting. A thick description of participant characteristics and the use of verbatim quotes are provided to support readers' assessment of applicability. For dependability, a comprehensive audit trail was maintained to document all research decisions, the evolution of the coding framework, and the theme development process. Furthermore, two researchers conducted dual independent analyses and held regular consensus meetings to standardize procedures, minimize procedural drift, and ensure consistency and replicability. For confirmability, dual independent coding and consensus-based discussions minimized researcher bias. Finally, a third researcher, who had not been involved in data collection, initial coding, or preliminary theme development, conducted a full audit of the entire analytical process, verifying that all interpretations and conclusions were logically grounded in the raw data, thereby enhancing the neutrality of the findings.

Thematic analysis generated three themes and ten subthemes. The themes were: 1) valued but under-supported home-based cancer pain management; 2) cautious optimism toward AI integration; and 3) practical requirements for HAIPMS implementation.

Theme 1: Valued but under-supported home-based cancer pain management

Home-based cancer pain management in Hong Kong, China was characterized by a pragmatic hybrid model but remained constrained by systemic challenges. These challenges, navigated daily by HCPs, establish the essential rationale for an HAIPMS as a potential technological solution.

Family-dependent and intermittent professional support

HCPs described the current model as a fusion of “professional medical guidance” and “family-led self-management.” Professional guidance primarily involves scheduled home visits and intermittent remote consultations. In contrast, the family-led component places the onus of daily care on patients and their caregivers.“Patients at home primarily self-administer medications. Some also receive assessment through doctor or nurse home visits, as home-based patients often struggle with pain management.” (HCP7)

“Patients at home primarily self-administer medications. Some also receive assessment through doctor or nurse home visits, as home-based patients often struggle with pain management.” (HCP7)

It is noteworthy that some institutions are exploring an expansion of professional support, like home infusion pumps and complementary therapies. However, these advancements remain accessible only to a limited number of patients, highlighting issues of equity and scalability.“We now provide long-term infusion pumps for continuous home analgesia as well as complementary therapies such as traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture, and music therapy. While these services remain accessible to only a limited number of patients.” (HCP1)

“We now provide long-term infusion pumps for continuous home analgesia as well as complementary therapies such as traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture, and music therapy. While these services remain accessible to only a limited number of patients.” (HCP1)

Comfort, affordability, and system relief in home-based care

HCPs valued home-based care because it offered psychological comfort, reduced financial burden, and helped conserve healthcare resources. Remaining at home allowed patients to stay close to family members and avoid the unfamiliar hospital environment.“They probably prefer having family around them, which makes them feel more at ease. They might find the hospital environment unfamiliar, which is why many patients with cancer choose to stay at home.” (HCP4)

“They probably prefer having family around them, which makes them feel more at ease. They might find the hospital environment unfamiliar, which is why many patients with cancer choose to stay at home.” (HCP4)

HCPs also viewed home-based care as a practical option in Hong Kong, China where private medical care can be costly, and public hospital services are under pressure. For stable patients, home-based pain management could reduce unnecessary hospitalization and free limited hospital resources.“Most private medical institutions in Hong Kong, China are very expensive, and many patients cannot afford them. Public hospitals have a long queue because of high demand. Staying at home is a choice.” (HCP2)

“Most private medical institutions in Hong Kong, China are very expensive, and many patients cannot afford them. Public hospitals have a long queue because of high demand. Staying at home is a choice.” (HCP2)

Subjective assessment, medication non-adherence, and delayed support

Despite recognizing its value, HCPs reported several unresolved problems in home-based cancer pain management. First, pain assessment was largely subjective and dependent on patient self-reporting, caregiver observation, and nurses’ clinical judgment during home visits. This was particularly difficult for patients with impaired consciousness or communication barriers.“During our home visits, we may assess pain through their facial expressions. Family members also tell us the patient is in severe pain. However, if the patient cannot communicate, the assessment is unreliable.” (HCP4)

“During our home visits, we may assess pain through their facial expressions. Family members also tell us the patient is in severe pain. However, if the patient cannot communicate, the assessment is unreliable.” (HCP4)

Second, HCPs reported poor medication adherence related to misconceptions about analgesics, fear of addiction, and lack of continuous supervision. Routine home visits usually provided only brief medication guidance and could not ensure sustained adherence.“They often feel that taking medication for pain slightly worse than usual is hardly justified, and they fear addiction, leading them to delay taking the medicine. Patients living alone even have difficulty taking their medication.” (HCP6)

“They often feel that taking medication for pain slightly worse than usual is hardly justified, and they fear addiction, leading them to delay taking the medicine. Patients living alone even have difficulty taking their medication.” (HCP6)

Third, HCPs emphasized the lack of timely remote support. Home visits were scheduled rather than continuous, making it difficult to identify breakthrough pain promptly, especially at night or among patients living alone.“For home visits, we usually go twice a week and cannot visit daily. The biggest issue is our inability to see the patients promptly, which prevents us from knowing their condition promptly.” (HCP9)

“For home visits, we usually go twice a week and cannot visit daily. The biggest issue is our inability to see the patients promptly, which prevents us from knowing their condition promptly.” (HCP9)

Furthermore, the unique high-density living environment and limited portable medical equipment further hinder the implementation of standardized nursing interventions during home visits.“During home visits, much medical equipment is unavailable. At most, we can perform auscultation, measure blood pressure and blood sugar. Often, we rely heavily on our own experience and need to communicate intensively with the patient and family to gather more information.” (HCP7)

“During home visits, much medical equipment is unavailable. At most, we can perform auscultation, measure blood pressure and blood sugar. Often, we rely heavily on our own experience and need to communicate intensively with the patient and family to gather more information.” (HCP7)

These gaps highlighted the need for supportive tools capable of improving assessment, adherence monitoring, and timely response.

Theme 2: Cautious optimism toward AI integration

HCPs expressed cautious optimism toward AI integration. They recognized that AI could address important gaps in home-based cancer pain management by making pain more objective, visible, and continuously monitored. However, their acceptance was conditional and shaped by concerns about privacy, usability, accountability, costs, and differences in professional roles and digital familiarity.

Expectations for visible, measurable, and continuous pain monitoring

HCPs expected AI to generate quantitative, objective pain reports through multimodal data analysis, thereby “visualizing” pain and moving beyond subjective reports. This was seen as particularly valuable for non-communicative patients.“The AI system could help cancer patients. Sometimes when they are in pain, their families may not fully understand their suffering. AI could objectively inform us about the patient's pain.” (HCP5)

“The AI system could help cancer patients. Sometimes when they are in pain, their families may not fully understand their suffering. AI could objectively inform us about the patient's pain.” (HCP5)

They also recognized AI’s potential to drive personalized pain management by identifying individual pain patterns and thresholds, thereby supporting precise, personalized interventions.“Each patient has a different pain threshold. If AI can help record the pain characteristics of each patient, doctors and nurses could then develop personalized management plans based on these records.” (HCP11)

“Each patient has a different pain threshold. If AI can help record the pain characteristics of each patient, doctors and nurses could then develop personalized management plans based on these records.” (HCP11)

Furthermore, a strong expectation was that AI systems would enable continuous remote monitoring, reducing the frequency of labor-intensive in-person home visits, alleviating the workload of HCPs, and lessening the caregiving burden on families.“The system could monitor patients 24/7 continuously, while healthcare workers can only check at random time points. Having continuous data would be much better. With AI assisting at home, it would be more convenient.” (HCP9)

“The system could monitor patients 24/7 continuously, while healthcare workers can only check at random time points. Having continuous data would be much better. With AI assisting at home, it would be more convenient.” (HCP9)

Concern of risks resulting from AI implementation

Conversely, HCPs voiced several profound concerns. The most prominent revolved around data privacy and security, given the sensitivity of health information and the intimate home setting.“But how can AI ensure the privacy of patients and their families? And how can AI store the data from patients safely?”(HCP5)“If facial expressions of patients are to be collected, I think many patients would not agree … How can information security be guaranteed?” (HCP6)

“But how can AI ensure the privacy of patients and their families? And how can AI store the data from patients safely?”(HCP5)

“If facial expressions of patients are to be collected, I think many patients would not agree … How can information security be guaranteed?” (HCP6)

Another major concern was technological accessibility and usability, considering the advanced age and potential digital illiteracy of many cancer patients.“Many cancer patients are older adults and are not familiar with using smartphones. Are there any simpler methods available?” (HCP4)

“Many cancer patients are older adults and are not familiar with using smartphones. Are there any simpler methods available?” (HCP4)

HCPs were also deeply concerned about the boundaries of clinical decision-making. There was a unanimous consensus that AI must serve as an assistive tool, with ultimate accountability and judgment residing firmly with the HCPs.“I believe AI should serve as an assistive tool and should never replace doctors in making diagnoses, but the final judgment should predominantly rest with the physician.” (HCP7)

“I believe AI should serve as an assistive tool and should never replace doctors in making diagnoses, but the final judgment should predominantly rest with the physician.” (HCP7)

HCPs identified the underestimation of ongoing, non-technical costs — such as training, support, and capacity building — as a critical risk to the long-term viability of AI initiatives.“If we decide to implement this AI system, we must account for all associated costs. This includes not only the cost of the equipment but also the expense of assembling and training a dedicated team.” (HCP4)

“If we decide to implement this AI system, we must account for all associated costs. This includes not only the cost of the equipment but also the expense of assembling and training a dedicated team.” (HCP4)

Heterogeneous attitudes shaped by professional and generational divides

Professional and generational backgrounds shaped HCPs’ perception of AI’s value and implementation risks. Generally, younger HCPs demonstrated greater familiarity and a stronger willingness to embrace AI technology, while their senior colleagues focused more critically on the feasibility and practicalities of clinical integration.“I have seen some studies utilizing AI for assisted clinical diagnosis, and some hospitals are adopting AI for smart management. If AI were to assist in pain assessment and management, it would likely need to monitor objective indicators such as heart rate, sweating, and facial expressions.” (HCP1, younger nurse)“For us, what really matters is that any new AI is helpful at the bedside and doesn’t make our jobs harder. It needs to be simple and fit right into our existing routine.” (HCP3, senior nurse)

“I have seen some studies utilizing AI for assisted clinical diagnosis, and some hospitals are adopting AI for smart management. If AI were to assist in pain assessment and management, it would likely need to monitor objective indicators such as heart rate, sweating, and facial expressions.” (HCP1, younger nurse)

“For us, what really matters is that any new AI is helpful at the bedside and doesn’t make our jobs harder. It needs to be simple and fit right into our existing routine.” (HCP3, senior nurse)

Physicians focused more on the boundaries of clinical decision-making and professional autonomy, amplifying concerns about AI overstepping clinical judgment, while nurses emphasized A’s potential to reduce workload and enhance remote care efficiency, aligning more closely with AI’s perceived practical value. These role-based and generational differences further contextualize the overall cautious optimism toward AI.“AI should primarily serve as an assisted tool and decision-making should ultimately remain with doctors.” (HCP7, doctor)“If such technology were available, it would make home-based management much more convenient, allowing us to monitor the situation in real time.” (HCP8, nurse)

“AI should primarily serve as an assisted tool and decision-making should ultimately remain with doctors.” (HCP7, doctor)

“If such technology were available, it would make home-based management much more convenient, allowing us to monitor the situation in real time.” (HCP8, nurse)

Theme 3: Practical requirements for HAIPMS implementation

HCPs emphasized that the system should support clinical work rather than replace professional care. Its design should include useful functions, simple and adaptable interfaces, strong ethical safeguards, and sustained training and support.

Essential functional modules

HCPs expected HAIPMS to include dynamic pain assessment and trajectory mapping. They suggested integrating physiological indicators, behavioral cues, and patient-reported data to generate continuous pain records and identify changes over time.“It is essential to maintain a history of pain records, such as whether the pain occurs at night or during the day. With such records, doctors and nurses can track the patient's pain patterns.” (HCP6)

“It is essential to maintain a history of pain records, such as whether the pain occurs at night or during the day. With such records, doctors and nurses can track the patient's pain patterns.” (HCP6)

HCPs suggested that the system should record medication intake, provide reminders, and help evaluate the relationship between analgesic use and pain changes.“Patients often forget whether they have taken their medication or not. Therefore, medication reminders should be implemented.” (HCP4)

“Patients often forget whether they have taken their medication or not. Therefore, medication reminders should be implemented.” (HCP4)

For crises, HCPs expected the system to generate alerts and support remote response. Alerts should be visible to HCPs and linked with appropriate follow-up mechanisms.“However, when the patient’s pain scores are high, the system needs to issue an alarm, preferably one that is visible to healthcare professionals.” (HCP3)

“However, when the patient’s pain scores are high, the system needs to issue an alarm, preferably one that is visible to healthcare professionals.” (HCP3)

User-centered design and adaptability

HCPs emphasized that HAIPMS should be easy to use and minimally disruptive in the home environment. Hardware should be small, portable, and non-invasive, such as wearable devices or discreet sensors.“The system’s devices should be small, such as Bluetooth-enabled stickers or wristbands, so that they can blend seamlessly into the home environment.” (HCP1)

“The system’s devices should be small, such as Bluetooth-enabled stickers or wristbands, so that they can blend seamlessly into the home environment.” (HCP1)

The interface should be simple and age-friendly to accommodate older patients and caregivers with limited digital literacy. The incorporation of voice interaction was frequently suggested as a critical feature to overcome the digital literacy barrier faced by elderly patients.“It is crucial to support both Cantonese and English, as many elderly users cannot speak Putonghua, while some caregivers do not speak Chinese.” (HCP6)

“It is crucial to support both Cantonese and English, as many elderly users cannot speak Putonghua, while some caregivers do not speak Chinese.” (HCP6)

Safety and ethical assurance requirements

Underpinning all functional and design considerations was a strong emphasis on ethics and data security. HCPs insisted that the system must adopt a robust informed consent mechanism, requiring explicit user authorization before activation and data collection.“I believe that for this system to be used, consent from both patients and their families must be obtained. A confirmation of agreement should be required upon login before the system can be activated.” (HCP2)

“I believe that for this system to be used, consent from both patients and their families must be obtained. A confirmation of agreement should be required upon login before the system can be activated.” (HCP2)

They also required strict data protection measures, including secure devices, restricted access, and clear rules regarding who could view patient information.“Secure devices should be employed to protect the privacy of patients and their families. Access should be restricted — only healthcare professionals should be permitted to view the information.” (HCP4)

“Secure devices should be employed to protect the privacy of patients and their families. Access should be restricted — only healthcare professionals should be permitted to view the information.” (HCP4)

Training and sustained support

HCPs emphasized that HAIPMS implementation would require structured training and sustained support. HCPs would need training in system operation, data interpretation, alert response, and integration of system-generated information into clinical decision-making. Patients and caregivers would also need practical guidance.“We’re not familiar with the AI system yet, so we need to be taught how to use it first. For example, how to read the pain data and what to do when there's an alert—these all need to be taught.” (HCP6)

“We’re not familiar with the AI system yet, so we need to be taught how to use it first. For example, how to read the pain data and what to do when there's an alert—these all need to be taught.” (HCP6)

Beyond initial training, HCPs highlighted the need for technical support, a dedicated clinical response team, and long-term funding for maintenance and upgrades.“Usually, an AI system isn’t a one-time thing. It requires ongoing maintenance, updates, and upgrades, and that needs substantial funding to keep it running.” (HCP2)

“Usually, an AI system isn’t a one-time thing. It requires ongoing maintenance, updates, and upgrades, and that needs substantial funding to keep it running.” (HCP2)

Discussion

This study explored Hong Kong, China HCPs’ perceptions of and needs for a proposed HAIPMS for home-based cancer pain management. The findings show that HCPs considered home-based care valuable for patient comfort, family presence, affordability, and reduced pressure on hospital services. However, they also identified persistent unmet needs, including subjective pain assessment, medication non-adherence, delayed professional support, and limited home-based resources. These findings are consistent with previous evidence that nurses face practical and technological barriers in home-based palliative care [22], and that educational interventions alone may be insufficient to sustain effective cancer pain management behaviors [23]. This study adds a contextualized understanding by showing how the high-density living environment and limited home-based equipment further constrain objective assessment and timely response, reinforcing the need for technology-supported care models [24].

Regarding HCPs’ familiarity with medical AI, this study finds that Hong Kong, China HCPs have relatively limited knowledge of AI in healthcare, which aligns with previous studies [17,25]. This may be related to the limited integration of AI tools into routine local clinical practice, a barrier also reported in settings where medical AI adoption remains at an early stage [26,27]. Despite limited technical literacy, participants recognized AI’s potential to address unmet needs in home-based cancer pain management, particularly by improving the accuracy, continuity, and efficiency of pain assessment for end-of-life and nonverbal patients. This suggests that HCPs’ acceptance of AI may be shaped not only by technical familiarity but also by their awareness of existing workflow limitations and clinical pain points [28,29]. These expectations support the core design of HAIPMS, including dynamic pain assessment, trajectory mapping, real-time remote monitoring, and emergency alert functions.

The cautious optimism observed in this study is consistent with Joo’s review [30], which identified both expectations for efficient patient care and ethical concerns among nurses regarding AI. This study further shows that such attitudes vary by professional role and generational cohort, rather than being uniform across HCPs. Participants expected AI to enhance care efficiency and personalization, while also expressing concerns about data privacy, digital literacy, operational complexity, and the potential erosion of clinical autonomy [[31], [32], [33]]. These concerns are consistent with recent nursing literature emphasizing that AI integration must address ethical safeguards, patient consent, data privacy, AI competency, and the preservation of humanistic nursing care [34]. These findings align with the Technology Acceptance Model 2 (TAM2) [35]: recognition of AI’s clinical value reflects perceived usefulness, while concerns about usability and digital competence relate to perceived ease of use. In addition, subjective norms, including professional values and institutional expectations, may explain why HCPs emphasized that AI should support rather than replace clinical decision-making [36,37]. Therefore, HAIPMS should incorporate robust privacy safeguards, including data encryption, access control, and informed consent, while being positioned as a decision-support tool that preserves HCPs’ professional autonomy.

The “experience” construct in TAM2 may help explain generational differences in HCPs’ attitudes toward AI. Younger HCPs, who generally have more digital experience, appeared more receptive to AI and its ease of use. In contrast, senior HCPs tended to rely more on clinical experience and professional norms when evaluating AI [17,38]. This is consistent with TAM2’s view that experience moderates the relationship between perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness and aligns with findings by Gumus and Alan [39]. These generational differences suggest that HAIPMS should incorporate customizable interfaces and flexible functions that accommodate varying levels of digital literacy.

Professional roles also shaped HCPs’ perceptions. Doctors, who are ultimately responsible for clinical decision-making, emphasized the need to preserve professional autonomy, while nurses, who are more directly involved in home visit coordination, prioritized workflow efficiency and practical usefulness [40]. Senior HCPs valued seamless integration into existing workflows, whereas younger HCPs were more open to exploring new functions [17,38,40,41]. These findings indicate that a one-size-fits-all implementation strategy may be insufficient. Instead, HAIPMS should include role-specific workflows, customizable interfaces, and targeted training tailored to professional responsibilities and digital literacy levels.

The design requirements identified in this study extend previous work by Wong [42], which emphasized technical functionality as a key driver of AI acceptance. While real-time data analysis and personalized alerts remain important, our findings suggest that localized adaptability is equally critical in the home-based care context. High-density living environments and limited residential space require miniaturized and non-invasive devices [43], while the multilingual care context highlights the need for Cantonese voice interaction and age-friendly interfaces [44]. These findings support the concept of contextual fit in medical AI [45,46], suggesting that HAIPMS design should address hardware adaptability, human–computer interaction, and data governance. In particular, the system should use portable devices, provide multilingual and age-friendly interfaces, and comply with local privacy and ethical requirements.

In summary, this study contributes to the nursing and medical AI literature in several ways. First, it contextualizes the challenges of home-based cancer pain management within Hong Kong’s high-density and multilingual environment. Second, it shows that HCPs’ acceptance of AI is shaped not only by technical experience, but also by their awareness of clinical pain points and unmet care needs. Third, it extends the application of TAM2 to home-based cancer pain care by linking perceived usefulness, ease of use, subjective norms, and experience to specific system design requirements. Finally, this study provides an evidence-to-design pathway that translates qualitative findings into practical guidance for developing HAIPMS in nursing practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single palliative care facility in Hong Kong, China which may limit the transferability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Second, the sample was predominantly composed of nurses, with relatively few doctors, which may have limited the exploration of medical perspectives on AI-supported clinical decision-making and algorithmic boundaries. Third, this study focused only on HCPs and did not include patients or caregivers. Future studies should involve more diverse HCP groups, patients, and caregivers to develop a more comprehensive multi-stakeholder needs framework for HAIPMS implementation.

Conclusion

This study explored Hong Kong HCPs’ perceptions of and needs for a proposed HAIPMS for home-based cancer pain management. HCPs viewed home-based care as valuable but under-supported and expressed cautious optimism toward AI integration. Their acceptance of HAIPMS was shaped not only by perceived clinical usefulness, but also by concerns about usability, privacy, professional autonomy, and compatibility with local care workflows. In the Hong Kong, China context, limited home space, multilingual communication needs, and varying levels of digital literacy further shaped expectations for system design and implementation. The findings suggest that future HAIPMS development should prioritize clinically useful functions, user-friendly and multilingual design, ethical safeguards, role-specific training, and sustained technical and clinical support. Successful AI translation in home care requires alignment with professional values, contextual needs, and the humanistic core of care.
