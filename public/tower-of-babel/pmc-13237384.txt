Revealing institutional vulnerabilities in Nordic health systems through multidimensional scaling of COVID-19 mortality data
Authors: Giuseppe Orlando, Michele Bufalo, Varvara Nazarova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study introduces a novel application of Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS) to examine COVID-19 mortality trends across Nordic welfare states, addressing a significant gap in pandemic analysis. While existing literature has extensively compared Nordic COVID-19 responses through traditional epidemiological metrics, no previous study has employed MDS to visualize and analyze time-series mortality patterns in relation to institutional welfare structures. Drawing on excess mortality data from 2020 through 2024, this research reveals distinct national trajectories: Sweden experienced sharp increases in deaths and major outbreaks, Finland showed a steady decline, and Norway exhibited irregular patterns. The study’s methodological innovation lies in linking these MDS-derived patterns to varying degrees of market-oriented reforms across the Nordic welfare states, providing new insights into how institutional design shapes pandemic resilience. Excess mortality is measured using the P-score, which represents the percentage difference between observed and expected deaths based on pre-pandemic averages. Peak P-scores–3387.09% in Sweden (2020), 913.23% in Finland (2022), and 629.8% in Norway (2022) highlight the differing capacities of each system to respond to crises. This work fills a critical methodological gap by demonstrating how dimensionality reduction techniques can reveal institutional vulnerabilities in welfare systems during prolonged public health emergencies, offering new analytical tools for comparative welfare state research and pandemic preparedness planning.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has had substantial economic and health repercussions1–5. It has particularly affected vulnerable groups, including low-income households and countries that depend on food imports, disrupting food systems through trade barriers, supply chain challenges, and diminished market access.

Against this backdrop, Nordic countries (with their strong welfare institutions and varying degrees of market-oriented reforms) offer a valuable case study. However, existing comparative analyses of Nordic COVID-19 responses have predominantly relied on conventional epidemiological comparisons and policy analysis frameworks6,7. While these studies provide valuable insights into mortality outcomes and policy effectiveness, they lack sophisticated analytical tools to visualize complex temporal patterns and their relationship to underlying institutional structures. This represents a significant methodological gap in the literature, particularly given the multidimensional nature of pandemic impacts across different welfare regimes. Recent work has applied both mechanistic COVID-19 models and descriptive multidimensional scaling approaches8,9, with the present study adopting the latter for comparative visualization.

This paper addresses this gap by introducing Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS) as a novel analytical framework for examining COVID-19 mortality patterns in relation to welfare state characteristics. Unlike previous studies that examine Nordic pandemic responses through descriptive statistics or regression analysis, this research employs MDS to create low-dimensional visualizations of high-dimensional mortality data, enabling the identification of distinct national trajectories that may not be apparent through conventional analytical approaches. The methodological innovation lies in linking these visualized patterns to institutional differences in market-oriented reforms, providing new insights into how welfare state structures influence pandemic resilience.

According to Pizzato et al.10, Europe saw an estimated 1,642,586 excess deaths, representing an 8.0% rise compared to expected mortality rates, mainly between 2020 and 2022. Italy, Poland, and Germany reported the highest total numbers of excess deaths, whereas Bulgaria, Lithuania, and Slovakia experienced the most significant relative increases. Age-standardized death rates across Europe varied considerably during the pandemic, ranging from 1.8 per 10,000 population in Sweden to 24.7 in Bulgaria. These disparities reflect deeper structural inequalities: countries with higher poverty and income inequality generally experienced elevated excess death rates, while those with greater GDP per capita, higher health expenditure, and stronger vaccination coverage saw lower mortality. This context situates Sweden’s relatively low death rate not only as a policy outcome, but also as one shaped by broader welfare and economic factors.

Among the European Nordic countries, Sweden presents a particularly notable case–not only for its relatively low average mortality compared to the European mean, but also for its divergence from Nordic peers. Specifically, Conyon and Thomsen6 examine the progression of COVID-19 in Scandinavia during 2020 and evaluate whether Sweden’s unique public health approach led to different infection and mortality rates compared to its neighbours. They focus on two main questions: how COVID-19 spread in Scandinavia and the impact of Sweden’s less stringent public health measures. In the absence of a vaccine and community herd immunity, non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) were crucial. Denmark and Norway implemented strict lockdowns, including widespread business and school closures and significant travel restrictions. In contrast, Sweden adopted a more relaxed strategy, relying on community trust rather than enforced measures. This situation has provided a unique opportunity to analyze the effects of different lockdown strategies on health outcomes. New COVID-19 cases in Sweden surged sharply in March and November 2020, with a decline starting in early 2021. Sweden experienced higher daily COVID-19 deaths compared to Denmark and Norway, with a notable mortality spike during the first wave. By February 2021, Sweden’s COVID-19 mortality per capita remained high compared to its neighbours, and its reproduction rate of the virus stayed above one for most of the year. The Oxford University stringency index shows that the policy measures in Scandinavia had similar time-series trends, At the same time, mobility data indicated that Swedes maintained relatively higher interaction rates during the first wave. Testing rates were highest in Denmark, whereas Sweden had the highest positivity rate, surpassing the 5% threshold recommended by the WHO. In terms of vaccination, Denmark led in implementation as of early 2021, with Sweden and Norway following similar trends11. Based on the empirical evidence presented, Conyon, and Thomsen6 maintain that lockdowns result in fewer COVID-19 cases and deaths. However, their study does not evaluate other trade-offs associated with lockdowns, such as economic impacts or effects on political liberty. Additionally, it does not fully consider potential unobserved differences between countries, as highlighted in the studies by Pettersen and Lars12 and Yarmol-Matusiak et al.7. The latter study analyzed the early impact of COVID-19 from February to July 2020 across the Nordic countries. It found that Sweden experienced higher COVID-19 incidence rates across all age groups and a higher death rate related to COVID-19 compared to its Nordic peers. This higher death rate was only partially explained by demographic factors and was particularly pronounced in seniors’ care homes, contributing to elevated all-cause mortality. Yarmol-Matusiak et al.7 concluded that Sweden’s health system faced significant strain, revealing systemic issues in seniors’ care and challenges with contact tracing and testing. The initially looser government restrictions likely contributed to Sweden’s higher COVID-19 impact. In response, Sweden increased testing rates, imposed more restrictive measures, and expanded its intensive care unit capacity to better manage the epidemic.

Recent comparative research has examined why Nordic countries, despite shared welfare state traditions, responded differently to the COVID-19 pandemic. Laage-Thomsen and Frandsen13 used a structured case comparison and expert survey data from Denmark, Norway, and Sweden to explore whether national variation in pandemic responses reflected divergent expert preferences. Their findings suggest that policy differences were shaped more by politico-administrative organization than by expert dissensus. While valuable for understanding institutional dynamics, their approach is based on cross-sectional survey data and conventional regression techniques. Irfan et al.14 provide a quantitative analysis of the trade-off between public health policies and economic outcomes in the Nordic region. Using joinpoint regression on epidemiological and macroeconomic data from January 2020 to January 2021, they show that Sweden’s relaxed approach led to higher infection and mortality rates without clear economic gains. Though methodologically robust, their analysis treats mortality as a linear outcome and does not account for institutional variation. Saunes et al.15 analyze governance structures and pandemic responses across five Nordic countries using data from the COVID-19 Health System Response Monitor. Their study emphasizes variation in decentralization, emergency powers, and expert involvement, revealing differences in health system coordination. However, the focus remains on institutional arrangements rather than empirical outcome analysis. Hallberg et al.16 conduct a comparative study of epidemiological outcomes and policy responses in the five Nordic countries from 2020 to 2022. Using data on infections, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, and mortality, they link outcome trends to 13 policy measures via a stringency index. Their results highlight variation across waves, with Sweden showing the highest early burden and Denmark overtaking it later. While methodologically robust, the analysis relies on unidimensional time-series and policy indices.

In contrast to these prior comparative studies, this research introduces a novel methodological framework by applying Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS) to COVID-19 mortality data and health profiles across Finland, Norway, and Sweden. Rather than relying on static or unidimensional metrics, MDS enables spatial and temporal visualization of pandemic trajectories through dimensionality reduction of key indicators including total cases, new cases, total deaths, and deaths per million. This approach facilitates outcome-based comparisons that can uncover hidden patterns in mortality dynamics and relate them to structural features of welfare states, offering a complementary perspective on institutional resilience that has not been previously explored in the Nordic COVID-19 literature.

The analysis reveals distinct patterns: Sweden shows a sharp upward trend with a notable outlier indicating a surge in cases; Finland demonstrates a consistent downward trend in deaths per million; and Norway exhibits a more irregular pattern with two significant outliers in new cases per million. These results highlight the varying experiences and management of the COVID-19 pandemic in each country, offering insights into the effectiveness of public health interventions and the pandemic’s diverse trajectories across neighbouring nations. The analysis highlights how underlying health conditions and demographic factors shape population vulnerability to COVID-19, underscoring the need for context-specific health planning. It also compares policy effectiveness across Northern European countries, noting that proactive measures and sustained health system investments have been critical. Additionally, the study considers the implications of market-oriented reforms for the future resilience of Nordic welfare states. Excess mortality data from 2020 to 2024 reveals persistent public health challenges, with continued elevated death levels reflecting the pandemic’s lasting impact.

While numerous studies have assessed COVID-19 responses in the Nordic region6,7,10, few have systematically examined how structural welfare reforms intersect with pandemic mortality patterns using advanced visualization techniques. Most existing comparative analyses rely on descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, or traditional regression approaches that may obscure complex temporal relationships and multidimensional patterns in the data. This study addresses that gap by applying MDS to link temporal mortality data with institutional variation, providing both methodological innovation and substantive insights into welfare state resilience during health crises.

The study makes three distinct contributions to the literature. First, it demonstrates the analytical power of MDS for comparative pandemic research, showing how dimensionality reduction can reveal patterns invisible to conventional statistical approaches. Second, it provides new empirical evidence linking market-oriented welfare reforms to pandemic vulnerability through spatial analysis of mortality trajectories. Third, it offers a replicable methodological framework that can be applied to other health crises and comparative institutional analyses, expanding the analytical toolkit available to researchers studying welfare state resilience and public health outcomes.

The structure of this article is as follows. Section “Material and methods” describes the dataset and introduces the MDS methodology. Section “Results” presents national mortality patterns and pandemic responses, followed by MDS visualizations of cross-country trajectories. Section “Discussion” examines welfare state reforms and their potential influence on pandemic outcomes. Section “Conclusions” concludes with reflections on the implications for the resilience of Nordic welfare systems.

Material and methods

This section presents the data analyzed, provides introductory notes on MDS, and details the software and hardware utilized in the study.

Data

We selected Finland, Norway, and Sweden due to their shared institutional characteristics as Nordic welfare states, high-quality and comparable mortality data, and similar demographic profiles. Although geographic isolation was not modeled directly, these countries also share low population densities and limited cross-border connectivity relative to central Europe. These structural similarities support a meaningful comparative analysis of COVID-19 mortality trajectories and public health responses.

The primary data source for COVID-19 indicators is Our World in Data (OWID), which compiles official statistics from national and international sources into a harmonized cross-country format. OWID was selected for its standardized structure, broad indicator coverage, and temporal consistency–making it particularly suited for longitudinal, comparative analysis. While we recognize the availability of high-quality register-based data in the Nordic countries, OWID facilitates reproducible analysis across countries. A major advantage of using the OWID dataset is its high level of completeness for the Nordic countries during the period under study (March 2020–December 2023). Key indicators such as mortality rates, case counts, and policy indices are consistently available for Finland, Norway, and Sweden over most of the study period. No imputation or interpolation was performed. Years or periods with unavailable data were retained as missing and excluded from summaries where applicable (e.g., Sweden 2024 in Table 2).

The OWID project is managed by the Global Change Data Lab, a nonprofit organization affiliated with the University of Oxford. It was founded by social historian and development economist Max Roser and operates under the leadership of Hetan Shah11. The analysis centers on key indicators such as excess mortality, new COVID-19 cases, and the government stringency index. These variables were selected based on relevance, reliability, and cross-national availability. Other indicators were excluded to streamline the analysis and improve the interpretability of the MDS results. A full list of considered variables is provided in Appendix List of considered variables.

Multi-dimensional scaling

Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS) is a technique used for visualization that translates the distances or dissimilarities between objects into a lower-dimensional space to identify clusters. MDS is valuable across various domains, including marketing, ecology, molecular biology, and social networks, as it simplifies complex datasets and supports effective decision-making18,19.

MDS highlights distances or dissimilarities between objects, whether they are tangible or conceptual20. In MDS visualizations, objects that are more alike are placed closer together, while those that are less alike are spaced farther apart. Additionally, MDS serves as a dimensionality reduction technique, simplifying complex, high-dimensional data while retaining the relationships between data points21. Initially developed in psychometrics, MDS assigns numerical values to abstract concepts and maintains relative distances when reducing data from higher to lower dimensions, enabling visualization in two, three, or more dimensions22.

MDS is well suited for capturing dissimilarities in the temporal trajectories of mortality data, enabling a visual and non-parametric comparison of pandemic patterns across countries. Its effectiveness lies in revealing structural differences without relying on strong distributional assumptions.

A P-score of zero indicates mortality equal to the historical baseline, positive values indicate excess mortality, and negative values indicate fewer deaths than expected. A P-score of 100 corresponds to mortality that is twice the expected baseline level.

Sweden experienced its highest excess mortality in the initial pandemic year, with a P-score of 7.4% in 2020. In subsequent years, excess mortality declined markedly and remained relatively stable, fluctuating between approximately 1.3% and 4.2% from 2021 to 2023. This pattern indicates that the largest mortality impact in Sweden occurred early in the pandemic, followed by comparatively limited excess mortality in later years. Data for Sweden in 2024 are not available, preventing a complete assessment for the final year.

The results indicate moderate excess mortality across the three countries, with distinct temporal profiles rather than extreme or persistent deviations from historical norms. Finland shows the largest mid-period increase, Sweden displays an early peak followed by stabilization, and Norway generally exhibits lower excess mortality throughout the study period. These findings are consistent with broader European evidence indicating elevated mortality among adults aged 45 years and older during the 2023–2024 winter season, coinciding with the co-circulation of COVID-19, influenza, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)25.

COVID-19 impact across countries: a 2D MDS analysis of key metrics

This section analyzes the progression and severity of COVID-19 across Finland, Norway, and Sweden using three variants of Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS): classical (cMDS), nonmetric (nMDS), and robust (rMDS). MDS allows for visual comparison of pandemic trajectories based on key metrics such as smoothed new deaths and per-million indicators. The choice among these techniques depends on the dataset’s characteristics and analytical goals: cMDS is preferable for clean datasets that emphasize variance explanation; rMDS is more robust to outliers and data irregularities; and nMDS is best suited for preserving ordinal relationships when exact distances are less reliable. Together, these methods provide a comprehensive toolkit for exploring and interpreting multidimensional data.

In this study, we apply the said techniques to analyze COVID-19 mortality trends across Finland (FIN), Norway (NOR), and Sweden (SWE). The main analysis focuses on smoothed new death rates and per-million metrics. A complete list of the considered variables is provided in Appendix List of considered variables, Table 3. By reducing the dataset to two dimensions (Y1 and Y2), MDS enables a visual comparison of the countries’ relative positions with respect to key pandemic indicators. The resulting spatial configurations illustrate differences in the severity, timing, and progression of the pandemic across the three countries.

Figure 4 displays the results of Classic MDS, while Fig. 5 presents the results of Nonmetric MDS, and Fig. 6 illustrates the results of Robust MDS. The figures capture the primary variance across two dimensions. The x-axis represents the cumulative impact of the pandemic, encompassing factors such as total case numbers and deaths per capita. The y-axis captures secondary variance, potentially reflecting differences in the timing of outbreaks, healthcare capacity, or government responses.

The relative positioning of the countries in the MDS space highlights differences in multivariate COVID-19 mortality patterns over time. Finland and Norway appear closer to each other in the embedded space, indicating greater similarity in the selected indicators, whereas Sweden occupies a more distant position, reflecting a distinct multivariate trajectory. These differences are descriptive and summarize patterns present in the underlying data without implying causal relationships. These spatial differences align with each country’s early pandemic response: Sweden avoided lockdowns and mask mandates, while Finland and Norway implemented stricter containment measures. Such policy contrasts likely contributed to the distinct trajectories observed in the MDS plots.

The comparison between cMDS, rMDS, and nMDS highlights important differences in how each method captures the structure of the dataset, particularly in the context of COVID-19 data. While cMDS and rMDS often produce similar group separations, they differ in how they handle variance and outliers, each offering distinct and complementary insights into the data.

The cMDS analysis, based on Euclidean distances, shows a wide dynamic range in its eigenvalues, ranging from approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-69{,}344$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+79{,}619$$\end{document}. This broad spectrum indicates that cMDS captures extreme variations in the data, potentially amplifying the influence of outliers. The eigenvalue distribution in cMDS exhibits a distinct “elbow” pattern after the first few dimensions, suggesting that two to four dimensions account for most of the meaningful variance. This sharp decline highlights the dominance of a few dimensions in explaining the variance structure, making cMDS highly effective for visualizing clear group separations in datasets with minimal noise. However, its sensitivity to outliers can distort underlying relationships, as evidenced by sudden spikes in positive eigenvalues corresponding to extreme data points.

In contrast, rMDS, which also uses Euclidean distances but emphasizes the rank order of distances rather than their absolute magnitudes, presents a different pattern. The rMDS eigenvalues are skewed toward extreme negative values, extending to nearly \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-360{,}000$$\end{document}, indicating reduced sensitivity to outliers by penalizing dimensions with lower rank consistency. The absence of a pronounced “elbo” in the rMDS eigenvalue plot suggests a smoother transition between dimensions, with variance distributed more evenly across them. This reflects a more robust scaling approach, as no single dimension dominates the variance explanation. Additionally, rMDS shows more consistent eigenvalue magnitudes with fewer abrupt changes, emphasizing the robustness of inter-point relationships. The large negative tail in the rMDS results underscores its focus on preserving the ordinal structure of the data, making it better suited for datasets with non-linear relationships, missing values, or anomalies, such as those often found in COVID-19 reporting.

nMDS, on the other hand, focuses on preserving the rank order of dissimilarities rather than their exact values. This approach makes nMDS particularly robust to noise and outliers, as it prioritizes the relative distances between points rather than their absolute magnitudes. While nMDS may not capture the extreme variations as prominently as cMDS, it provides a more stable representation of the data’s underlying structure, especially in the presence of anomalies. The nMDS results often align closely with rMDS in terms of group separations, but with a stronger emphasis on ordinal relationships, making it ideal for datasets where the exact distances are less reliable.

cMDS is well-suited for clean datasets with strong separation; rMDS offers greater resilience to outliers; and nMDS is effective for preserving relative distances in noisy or nonlinear data. Applying all three methods enhances the reliability and interpretability of complex pandemic data.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the critical role of primary health care (PHC) systems in ensuring universal health coverage and delivering essential health interventions. In many low- and middle-income countries, PHC faces significant challenges, including insufficient funding, inequitable access, and high out-of-pocket costs, which perpetuate poor-quality care and a reliance on specialist services instead of accessible, community-based care. This situation further entrenches health disparities and limits the effectiveness of health systems in these regions26.

In Europe, an independent commission found that many health systems struggled during the pandemic due to chronic underfunding and a lack of investment in the health workforce, problems that were exacerbated by the 2008 financial crisis27. The World Health Organization (WHO) has drawn attention to the wide disparities in health outcomes across different social groups within European countries, attributing these inequalities to a combination of economic, social, environmental, and institutional factors28,29. For example, reports such as the Marmot Review and the European Commission’s Report on Health Inequalities demonstrate that while overall health metrics, like life expectancy and infant mortality, have improved, persistent disparities remain between social groups and regions30,31.

Health systems in Finland, Norway, and Sweden

The health systems in Finland, Norway, and Sweden have faced unique challenges and exhibited distinct strengths and weaknesses in responding to the COVID-19 pandemic. These differences are shaped by each country’s health infrastructure, policies, and historical evolution, influencing their effectiveness in managing the pandemic.

Espinosa-Gonzalez et al.32 identify several clusters of European health systems based on their approach to PHC. Cluster 2 includes countries like Greece, Ireland, Israel, Malta, Sweden, and Ukraine, characterized by centralized or partially centralized public financing and regulation of PHC, often with significant private sector involvement. In these countries, high private expenditure (over 30% of total health expenditure) or co-payment systems prevail, and gatekeeping mechanisms to access secondary care are often lacking. For instance, in some Swedish counties, patients face co-payments as high as €3332. Cluster 3, comprising Finland, Norway, Spain, and the United Kingdom, is marked by centralized public financing but devolved regulation of PHC. These countries also have lower levels of private sector involvement and implement gatekeeping systems that regulate access to secondary care, ensuring that patients first consult primary care providers before moving to specialized services32. In response to the liberalizing reforms of the 1980s, radical historians viewed these shifts as a revolutionary transformation that dismantled the social democratic welfare state33.

In 2016 the Finnish government proposed a health system reform allowing patients to choose between public, private, and third-sector providers, all licensed by regional authorities. Despite the Confederation of Finnish Industries supporting the change, concerns arise about its efficiency. Critics argue that instead of overhauling an already efficient system, efforts should focus on improving its weaknesses, as Finland provides excellent healthcare at a relatively low cost compared to other Nordic and European nations.

Sweden’s universal, tax-financed healthcare system, designed to provide comprehensive services with minimal out-of-pocket costs, was initially established post-war by the social democratic party to ensure egalitarian access based on medical needs rather than financial means. However, market-oriented reforms in the 1990s and 2000s, which increased the role of private, for-profit providers, have seemingly weakened these egalitarian principles, contributing to the rise of voluntary private health insurance34.

MDS patterns and institutional differences

The spatial divergence observed in the MDS plots–particularly Sweden’s distinct trajectory compared to Finland and Norway–can be understood in light of long-term institutional transformations that reflect deeper differences in public health governance and welfare state structure.

Recent scholarship shows that the Nordic welfare states have undergone varying degrees of marketization through quasi- and pseudo-market reforms, especially in eldercare, where legal and structural changes have fostered the expansion of for-profit provision35–38. Sweden’s more extensive adoption of market-oriented reforms, alongside its decentralized governance model, likely contributed to fragmented coordination and inconsistent service delivery during the pandemic. This is reflected in Sweden’s early and steep mortality peak in the MDS results. In contrast, Finland and Norway retained stronger central oversight and integrated public health infrastructures, which may have enabled more coherent responses.

These institutional differences align with Blomqvist34 and Diderichsen et al.39, who document the erosion of egalitarian principles in Sweden’s welfare state. Finland and Norway’s centralized regulatory structures may have preserved service coherence. As Kuhlmann et al.40 and Vabo et al.41 argue, hierarchical governance models facilitated faster mobilization and resource deployment during the crisis. The MDS configurations thus capture not only mortality divergence but also the effects of these institutional models.

Marketization and pandemic vulnerabilities

Recent research highlights that long-term care systems across the Nordic countries have undergone varying degrees of informalization and privatization, with Sweden showing particularly rapid growth in for-profit provision–challenging universalist principles and deepening inequalities37,42. These structural divergences, as visualized in the MDS plots, reflect underlying institutional reforms that have shifted the balance of care responsibilities and shaped national capacities to respond to the pandemic.

These interpretations are supported by qualitative assessments of Sweden’s long-term care (LTC) sector. Szebehely43 highlights chronic underfunding, staff shortages, reliance on hourly workers, and poor compliance with hygiene protocols in care home were structurally underprepared for the pandemic due to chronic underfunding, high proportions of untrained or hourly staff, poor hygiene compliance, and delays in PPE distribution and intervention. Although authorities emphasized protecting older adults, care home residents were initially overlooked. These systemic weaknesses reflect challenges tied to New Public Management-style reforms and align with the mortality patterns observed in the MDS plots.

Meagher and Szebehely44 trace these issues to Sweden’s broader transition from a universalistic, publicly managed LTC model to one shaped by municipal autonomy and for-profit provision. While disability services expanded, eldercare coverage declined, increasing reliance on informal family care. Municipalization–enabled by local taxation–produced uneven regional outcomes. Legislative changes, such as the Municipal Act (1991) and Disability Act (1993), introduced “layered” institutional shifts that fragmented service provision. These structural reforms help explain the vulnerabilities exposed by the pandemic and Sweden’s distinct MDS trajectory.

Nordic marketization and welfare sustainability

Broader socioeconomic shifts have also challenged the egalitarian ethos of Nordic welfare states. Kvist et al.45 report a marked rise in income inequality since the 1980s, driven by affluence among top income groups, labor market changes, and reduced redistribution. This erosion of equality exacerbates vulnerabilities during crises such as COVID-19, where unequal access to care, income security, and information amplify the effects of institutional fragmentation. These structural changes underscore the significance of our MDS findings: institutional design and social stratification intersect in shaping pandemic outcomes.

The move toward marketization in both quasi- and pseudo-market forms reflects a broader transformation in Nordic welfare regimes. Reforms such as Sweden’s “Friskolereformen” (1990s) and Norway’s “Helseforetaksreformen” (2001) exemplify this shift. Sweden’s reform path emphasized individual choice; Norway’s emphasized economic efficiency. Despite different motivations, both introduced a commodified logic into public service delivery, raising concerns about the long-term sustainability of the Nordic model46.

As Lethbridge47 notes, privatization has not delivered clear efficiency gains. Public provision shows better worker-client ratios, lower sickness and turnover rates. Austerity measures compound service exclusions, while the profit motive often leads to degraded labor conditions–undermining service quality. These findings echo broader concerns that extensive marketization can erode equity, cohesion, and resilience in public welfare systems.

Management, governance and communication

For Juranek and Zoutman48, a key factor in Sweden’s higher death rates is the effectiveness of social distancing measures. The study compares Denmark and Norway, which implemented strict measures, with Sweden, which took a more lenient approach. By using Sweden as a counterfactual, it estimates that if Denmark and Norway had adopted Sweden’s lenient measures, hospitalizations would have peaked 15-20 days later, with peak hospitalizations increasing by 133% in Denmark and 231% in Norway, and ICU patients rising by 107% in Denmark and 140% in Norway. This analysis highlights the significant impact of social distancing policies on pandemic outcomes.

Aguilar-Guerra et al.49 highlight that Denmark and Norway’s centralized ministerial governance system ensures agencies remain dependent on ministries for policy implementation, while Sweden’s decentralized system grants agencies independence from ministries. This difference led to three key findings: less government influence did not always yield better economic outcomes, reduced government interference was associated with higher morbidity and mortality rates, and certain public policies can have unexpected negative effects on the economy.

Bjorkdahl et al.50 maintain that Sweden distinguished itself from Denmark and Norway by delegating most pandemic communication to its Public Health Agency rather than political leaders, resulting in a more technical, expert-driven rhetoric. This approach reflects Sweden’s tradition of separating administrative and political roles, differing from practices in Denmark and Norway. Despite this, all Scandinavian countries effectively aligned their rhetoric with their specific situations and maintained public trust. However, the varying impacts of COVID-19 across these countries underscore that rhetorical success does not necessarily equate to overall pandemic response success.

Policy implications and actionable insights

The MDS analysis and mortality patterns observed across Finland, Norway, and Sweden provide several actionable insights for health policy makers seeking to strengthen pandemic preparedness and welfare state resilience. First, the spatial divergence of Sweden in the MDS plots, coupled with its early mortality peak, underscores the importance of maintaining strong central coordination mechanisms in public health governance. Policy makers should consider establishing robust inter-agency coordination protocols that can be rapidly activated during health emergencies, avoiding the fragmentation that may arise from excessive decentralization. This includes developing standardized data sharing systems, unified communication strategies, and clear chains of command that transcend administrative boundaries between national, regional, and municipal levels.

Second, the differential mortality outcomes highlight the vulnerability of market-oriented reforms in health and social care during systemic crises. The institutional analysis suggests that countries with more extensive privatization and market delegation–particularly in eldercare–may face greater challenges in coordinating emergency responses and ensuring equitable access to protective measures. Policy makers should therefore conduct comprehensive reviews of their care systems to identify potential coordination gaps, strengthen oversight mechanisms for private providers, and establish contingency protocols that ensure consistent standards across public and private sectors. This may involve developing regulatory frameworks that require private providers to maintain emergency preparedness plans, participate in coordinated response efforts, and adhere to unified quality standards during health crises.

Third, the persistent excess mortality documented through 2024 across all three countries indicates that pandemic impacts extend well beyond the acute phase, requiring sustained policy attention and resource allocation. Health systems should invest in long-term monitoring capabilities to track excess mortality patterns, develop targeted interventions for vulnerable populations, and build resilient primary care infrastructures that can maintain essential services during disruptions. This includes strengthening community-based care networks, enhancing digital health capabilities, and ensuring adequate staffing and resource reserves to handle both routine care and emergency surges. Additionally, policy makers should consider how welfare state structures can be adapted to better support population health resilience while preserving the egalitarian principles that have historically defined Nordic social models.

Conclusions

This study combined time-series analysis and multidimensional scaling (MDS) to examine COVID-19 mortality trajectories in Finland, Norway, and Sweden. Across multiple indicators, the three countries exhibited clearly distinct patterns in the timing, magnitude, and progression of mortality during the pandemic. Sweden experienced an earlier and sharper peak, Finland showed a more gradual trajectory, and Norway displayed intermittent surges, highlighting meaningful heterogeneity within an otherwise institutionally similar region.

The MDS analysis provided a compact, multivariate summary of these differences and demonstrated that a two-dimensional representation preserves the main structure of the data with acceptable fidelity. While MDS is inherently a descriptive technique, the observed configurations offer a coherent visual account of cross-country differences that is robust to alternative specifications and perturbations, as documented in the uncertainty analyses.

Within this three-country comparison, the relative positioning of Sweden, Finland, and Norway is consistent with well-documented differences in public health governance and pandemic response strategies during the early phases of COVID-19. In particular, Sweden’s distinct trajectory contrasts with the more similar patterns observed for Finland and Norway. These associations do not constitute causal evidence but provide empirically grounded context for interpreting how governance arrangements and coordination practices may be linked to observed outcomes.

More broadly, the findings contribute to ongoing debates on the resilience of Nordic welfare states and the implications of decentralization and market-oriented reforms for crisis management. Rather than demonstrating direct effects, the analysis illustrates how differences in institutional organization can manifest in divergent pandemic trajectories, even among countries with comparable socioeconomic profiles.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The analysis is restricted to three countries and relies on aggregated mortality data, which may mask regional and demographic heterogeneity. Differences in reporting practices and baseline health conditions may further affect comparability. In addition, the use of MDS supports exploratory pattern identification but does not enable formal statistical inference.

Future research should extend this framework to a larger set of countries and incorporate more explicit measures of institutional design and governance. Combining registry-level data with formal causal methods would allow a more systematic evaluation of how public health systems and welfare-state arrangements shape outcomes during systemic crises.
