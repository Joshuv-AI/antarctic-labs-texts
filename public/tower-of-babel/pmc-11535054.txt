A systematic study of interactions between sustainable development goals (SDGs) in Hainan Island
Authors: Linyan Bai, Xinjian Wang, Li Zhang, Jianzhong Feng, Jingjuan Liao, Bowei Chen, Penglong Wang, Xinyi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development issued by the United Nations is an important foundation for countries to achieve common economic, social and environmental development. Important progress has been made in the evaluation of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in Hainan Island; nevertheless, there is still a lack of understanding around the trade-offs and synergies between the SDGs. Studying the trade-offs and synergies between Hainan Island’s sustainable development goals is of great significance for the coordinated development of these goals and the promotion of the construction of free trade ports. Therefore, based on the United Nations Sustainable Development Assessment System and the existing SDG indicator system on Hainan Island, this paper identifies and quantifies the trade-offs and synergies within and between SDGs and targets on the county scale. Based on the different impacts of different spatial, dimensional and geographical directions, the results show the following: (1) Hainan Province made good progress on multiple SDGs between 2010 and 2021. (2) The most significant synergies between SDGs exist between SDG1 (No Poverty) and SDG10 (Reduce Inequalities), while the most significant trade-offs exist between SDG2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG4 (Quality Education). (3) Obvious spatial characteristics in trade-offs and synergies exist, with the highest level of synergy being in the Haikou and Sanya Economic Circles and their surrounding areas, and in the central region of Hainan Island which has a higher level of trade-offs. (4) The synergistic effect between the SDG targets and indicators in Hainan is much greater than the trade-off effect: the four aspects of people’s livelihood improvement, economic development, resource utilization and environmental quality all show synergistic effects in different regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77984-5.Keywords: Sustainable development goals (SDGs), Trade-offs and synergies, Hainan Island, Quantitative researchSubject terms: Environmental impact, Sustainability

Introduction

“Transforming Our World: The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development” provides a framework for the sustainable development efforts of countries around the world for the period 2015–2030. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) consist of 17 integrated, interrelated and indivisible goals, covering 169 specific indicators, which are further operationalized through a set of 232 indicators. The Agenda aims to monitor progress in the economic, social and environmental aspects and to coordinate their simultaneous development in order to address the multiple and complex challenges facing humanity1. The key to achieving harmonious development in the three dimensions of sustainability lies in understanding the interplay between the SDGs2,3. This interaction is, in fact, an implicit interdependency. We should, therefore, think of the SDGs as a system of interacting components, rather than simply a collection of goals and indicators4–6.

In recent years, countries have actively responded to the path of sustainable development and conducted more in-depth research into the interrelationship between the SDGs7. In general, this interdependence is categorized as a synergy effect (progress on one goal favors progress on another) or a trade-off effect (progress on one goal hinders progress on another)8,9. Studying the trade-offs and synergies between different SDGs is of great significance for coordinating the synergistic development of these goals and promoting the realization of the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development10.

Several studies have recently adopted qualitative methods to explain the interactions between the SDGs. For example, qualitative studies have conducted research on the interactions between indicators in SDG6 of the United Nations 2030 Agenda11. Similarly, Fuso et al12. maps the synergies and trade-offs between SDG7 and all other 16 SDGs from a global perspective. Weitz et al13. conducted a qualitative case study using Sweden as the subject of interest to illustrate prioritization for SDG implementation. Although such research has identified and qualitatively evaluated the interplay between SDGs, it lacks quantitative and overall analysis. A better understanding of SDG interactions enabled experts and scholars to undertake a series of quantitative studies. For example, Ronzon et al14. quantitatively analyzed the interaction of the SDGs relating to the bioeconomy at the EU level. In addition, Pradhan et al3. used time series data provided by the United Nations Statistics Division (UNSD) from 1983 to 2016 covering 122 indicators and 227 countries around the world; they quantitatively analyzed the synergies and trade-offs between all SDGs and different indicators within the targets from 1986 to 2016 on the global and national scales. On this basis, Warchold et al15. further analyzed the linear and nonlinear synergies and trade-offs between, and within, the SDGs at the global and national levels using newer and higher coverage data (171 indicators for 247 countries, from 1991 to 2019). There are strong synergies among the SDGs, particularly between the goals related to poverty reduction, health, education and gender equality. These synergies suggest that progress in one goal can have positive spillover effects on other goals16. At the same time, there are potential trade-offs among the SDGs, such as between economic growth and environmental sustainability, or between food production and biodiversity conservation17. These trade-offs highlight the need for integrated and coherent policy approaches to address the multiple dimensions of sustainable development. It is worth noting that the balance and synergy among the goals and targets can vary across different regions and time scales. Therefore, it is of great significance to conduct region-specific research on SDG trade-offs and collaborative quantitative research on local policies to promote sustainable development.

In recent years, Hainan Province has undertaken significant work to actively promote sustainable development, such as the construction of Hainan’s “ecological province”, the establishment of a “National Ecological Civilization Construction Demonstration Zone”, the construction of a “Beautiful New Hainan” and the construction of a “National Ecological Civilization Pilot Zone”, resulting in positive progress in social, economic and environmental aspects. However, Hainan Island’s environmental problems and uneven distribution of resources are worsening with its rapid economic development and continuous population growth. How to achieve sustainable economic and social development while protecting the ecological environment has become an important issue for Hainan18.

This paper aims to provide a scientific basis and policy suggestions for the construction of an ecological civilization and the sustainable development of Hainan Island. In order to further promote the sustainable development of the island, we conducted a quantitative study on the synergies and trade-offs between 46 indicators of 12 goals in 18 cities and counties. The specific aim was to achieve the first complete quantification of synergies and trade-offs within and between sustainable development goals at the city and county levels, and island-wide. Further, using geographically and temporally weighted regression models, we studied the interaction of four aspects—people’s livelihoods, economy, resources and environment.

Study area

Hainan Island, located at the southernmost tip of China, is the second largest island in China, with a total area of about 34,000 square kilometers. It is located between 18°10′~20°10′ N and 108°37′~111°03′ E. Hainan Island attracts a large number of tourists and investors with its unique geographical location19, abundant natural resources and pleasant climatic conditions. In recent years, with the strong support of the Chinese government, Hainan Island has gradually developed into an international tourist attraction and a pilot free-trade zone20, experiencing rapid economic development and continuous population growth. The Hainan Island climate is tropical monsoon, with an average annual temperature of 22–27 degrees Celsius, abundant precipitation, abundant sunshine, a unique ecological environment and rich biodiversity. In 2021, the forest coverage rate of Hainan Island reached 62.1%, and the annual average PM2.5 reached 13ug/m3 (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Data and preprocessing

This study utilized 46 sets of time series statistical data from the Hainan Statistical Yearbooks for 18 cities and counties in Hainan Province, from 2010 to 2021. The Sustainable Development Goals released by the United Nations in 2020 were used as a reference, and ultimately, 44 indicators (2 indicators each require 2 sets of evaluation data) for 12 Sustainable Development Goals were selected for the assessment of sustainable development in Hainan. Each indicator was evaluated using a time series dataset containing 12 periods (Appendix Table).

Data preparation included data cleansing, data integration and data transformation. For individual missing data points discovered during the inspection and data-cleaning process of the acquired raw data, we used the mean imputation method to assign values. We reversed several indicators, such as “pesticide use” and “traffic casualties”, to ensure that a one-unit increase in each indicator contributed to the achievement of the goal.

Methods

Quantitative evaluation of trade-offs and synergies

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient has become a widely used tool to explore synergistic relationships among sustainability indicators22,23. Synergy can exist when two elements have a positive association between them, and trade-off can exist when the two elements have a negative association between them. In this study, the unique indicator time-series pairs were analyzed in correlation using categorical data for each city and county in Hainan Island (Fig. 2).

Spearman hierarchy correlation coefficient determination of trade-off and synergy.

Nonparametric Spearman Rank Correlation Analysis evaluates monotonic relationships between all possible combinations of unique metric data pairs for each city and county. Geographically and temporally weighted regression models

When quantifying the interaction between SDG indicators, it is also necessary to consider the spatial distribution of indicators, according to Tobler’s “first law of geography”26: everything is spatially related, and the closer points are, the greater the spatial correlation. Therefore, unlike traditional cross-sectional data, the spatial correlation of spatial data results in spatial nonstationarity (spatial heterogeneity) of regression relationships. The geographically and temporally weighted regression model (GWTR) is a complex regression model that uses spatial data as well as time series data to predict the value of response variables. Results and analysis

Overall development trends for the SDGs

In order to study the process of sustainable development of Hainan Island and the interrelationships among various goals, we have conducted a comprehensive assessment using 12 sustainable development goals and 44 sustainable development indicators (Appendix Table) from four dimensions (i.e., people’s livelihood, economy, resources, and environment) (Fig. 3).

Construction diagram of the SDG evaluation system in Hainan Island.

We synthesized the overall 12 SDGs for Hainan Island and the development trends of the 44 indicators from 2010 to 2021 (Table 1). The results show that the overall trend of development for most of the 12 goals has been growing steadily since 2010, including SDG1, SDG6 (Clean Water And Sanitation) and SDG11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), although some individual indicators showed a downward trend. It is worth mentioning that for SDG2, grain production has been declining year on year; this is mainly due to the adjustment of the agricultural industry structure, as the area of grain cultivation has gradually given way to characteristic industries such as vegetables and fruits.

For SDG1, all indicators show an upward trend, but health spending showed a sharp downward trend in 2020–2021, having reached a peak in 2020; the peak may reflect the outbreak of the pandemic in early 2020 which caused local governments to increase spending on epidemic prevention and control, while explaining the downward trend in 2020–2021 for some indicators in SDG3 (Health And Well-Being). In addition, we can also intuitively observe the impact of the epidemic from the data trends. For example, the unemployment rate in Hainan Island’s Sustainable Development Goal 8 (decent work and economic growth) has been rising year on year since 2019 and the unemployment rate among Hainan Island residents has increased year on year. For SDG6 (Clean Water And Sanitation), we find that the urban water penetration rate has been at a very high level over the years, and there is no upward or downward trend. This is the same as the trend for the urban gas penetration rate and the number of primary school students, which indicates that Hainan Island has achieved the targets specified by the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals for these indicators to have completed their sustainable development processes.

Overall characteristics of trade-offs and synergies between SDGs goals and indicators

Our analysis shows that when taking into account the cities and counties in Hainan Province, the synergies within each SDG generally exceed the trade-off effects (Fig. 3), By collating the number of trade-offs and synergies within the SDGs, a total of 2,106 pairs were obtained. Synergies accounted for 31.4% of the total effect, while trade-offs accounted for 25.0%. However, the sum of all SDGs for each city and county in Hainan Province shows the highest proportion of nonclassification (Spearman coefficient between − 0.5 and 0.5), which may be due to the fact that the time series data we have chosen is short, the correlation between the goals is not significant enough, and the other reason is that we only count the correlation within the SDGs and do not address the correlation between the SDGs. Except where data are incomplete, and in addition to SDG10 and SDG12 (responsible consumption and production), there are synergies between most SDGs.

In all data pairs for SDG1, and according to our definition of synergies, 75.56% are synergistic effects, indicating that most of the indicators mutually promote development trends. For example, for poverty eradication, there is a synergistic effect between “per capita disposable income of urban and rural residents” and “expenditure on medical care, education and social security”; that is, the trend in increasing per capita disposable income of urban and rural residents is positively correlated with the trend in increasing expenditure on health, education and social security. In contrast, the trade-off effects of SDG8 and SDG9 are slightly higher than the synergistic effects; that is, progress in one goal hinders progress in another. Specifically, the unemployment rate for SDG8 has been increasing year on year, while the share of manufacturing employment in SDG9 has been decreasing year on year, indicating that the impact of the epidemic has led to industrial stagnation in recent years, making employment more difficult to achieve. Hainan Island should, therefore, focus on promoting sustainable economic growth, and full and productive employment, by building infrastructure, promoting inclusive and sustainable industrialization, and promoting innovation to reduce the recent rising unemployment rate.

The trade-offs and synergies between Hainan Island’s sustainability goals demonstrate that the overall sustainability synergies on the island are higher than the trade-off effects (Figs. 4 and 6). A notable example is SDG1, which has synergies with most SDGs. Statistics show that poverty eradication data have a significant impact on SDG3, SDG8, SDG10 and others. Among the top ten synergistic pairs for sustainable development in the island, SDG1 appeared five times, with SDG1 and SDG10 having the highest reciprocal synergy effect, amounting to 84.44%. This indicates that the increase in urban and rural residents’ income and education, medical and social security expenditure on the island has played a more obvious role in promoting other sustainable development goals.

Based on numerous statistical data analyses, and taking into account the trade-offs and the number of synergistic data pairs in each city and county on the island, it was found that the proportion of data pairs of synergistic effects is higher than the proportion of data pairs of trade-off effects, both within and between SDGs. After categorizing the trade-offs and synergy pairs for the top 10, it was found that the data for the top 10 synergistic pairs accounted for 40.12–84.44% of the total data, while the top 10 trade-off pairs accounted for 24.03–35.56% of the total data. There were more trade-offs between SDG10 and other SDGs. For example, SDG2 had a strong trade-off effect with five SDGs—SDG1, 3, 8, 9 and 10—with six pairs of the top 10 trade-offs (Fig. 5, right); this indicates that the increase in food production and the development of sustainable agriculture-related indicators in Hainan Island do not contribute to the promotion of other SDGs.

In addition to studying the trade-offs and synergies of the SDGs as a whole on Hainan Island, we also discussed the trade-offs and synergies of each city and county. Among them, we took Haikou City as an example (Fig. 6) and collected statistics for Haikou City, which include 46 time series data from SDG1.1.1b to SDG 14.7.2. We used SPSS to calculate the Spearman correlation coefficients between these data fragments. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient quantifies the relationship between the sustainable development indicators of Haikou City.

From the results, it is evident that the Spearman correlation coefficients tend to be close to 1 for SDG1, 11, and 14. Additionally, it is noticeable that these coefficients are clustered, indicating consistent results across different SDGs, regardless of the number of indicators they have for other goals.

In addition to analyzing the correlation coefficients of these targets in Haikou, we also examined the other 17 cities and counties. The results indicate that the majority of trade-offs and synergies between indicators align with their respective goals. Therefore, it is necessary to study their spatial location characteristics in order to gain a comprehensive understanding of the overall situation on Hainan Island.

Trade-offs and synergistic spatial characteristics between SDGs and indicators

City (county) sustainable development trade-offs and synergy models

The synergies of the 18 cities (counties) participating in the Hainan Island assessment were much higher than the trade-off effects. The number and proportion of synergy and trade-off pairs of all index pairs in each city (county) were counted, and the natural breakpoint method was used to divide the synergy and trade-off effects of each city (county) into five levels (Fig. 7).

Distribution of Hainan synergies: SDGs and trade-off shares; the higher the synergy (trade-off) level, the higher the synergy (trade-off) effect. Map from ArcGIS 10.8.

From the perspective of the spatial distribution of synergy levels, the main high-value areas are concentrated near the Haikou and Sanya Economic Circles, among which the highest level and the highest proportion of collaborative data belongs to Lingao County in the north of Hainan Province. The overall synergy data pair is higher than the trade-off data pair, at 170 pairs. The trade-off data pair is close to 100%, and its SDG1 and SDG2 are almost all synergistic effects for other sustainable development goals. This enhances the situation in Lingao County, meaning it has different degrees of sustainable development, thereby providing more prominent advantages. From the perspective of the level of trade-off effect, the main high-value areas are concentrated in the central regions of Baisha, Wuzhishan and Qiongzhong, indicating that Hainan must strengthen assistance in the central region to achieve sustainable development so that island as a whole can achieve the 2030 sustainable development goals.

In addition, Wuzhishan City is in a high-value area in terms of both synergy and trade-off effect levels because the hierarchical division of synergy and trade-off is divided by the proportion of synergy and trade-off to the total data pairs, which contain unclassified data. In contrast, Wuzhishan City has fewer unclassified data pairs, resulting in an increase in the proportion of synergy and trade-off pairs. However, in general, as a conservation area city in central Hainan Province, Wuzhishan has a serious internal trade-off effect, and the island’s sustainable development should focus particularly on the development of inland cities, including Wuzhishan.

We observed the distribution of synergistic effects and found that a significant portion of high-proportion areas are concentrated in coastal cities surrounding Haikou City and Sanya City. Additionally, in recent years, the “Three Poles, One Belt, and One Area” initiative (Fig. 1) has emerged as a distinctive feature of Hainan Island. Building upon this, we analyze the trade-offs and synergies of Hainan Island based on specific geographical divisions.

Sustainable development goals’ trade-offs and synergistic analysis of “Three poles, one Belt and One Zone”

Coordinated regional development is an important engine for promoting high-quality development for the Hainan Free Trade Port. This comprises “Three Poles, One Belt and One Area” (Fig. 1)28, namely the Haikou Economic Circle (composed of Haikou, Chengmai, Wenchang, Ding’an, Tunchang and other cities and counties); the Sanya Economic Circle (composed of Sanya, Lingshui, Ledong, Baoting and other cities and counties); the Danyang Economic Circle (composed of the Danzhou Yangpu integrated development); the Coastal City Belt (composed of Haikou, Wenchang, Qionghai, Wanning, Lingshui, Sanya, Ledong, Dongfang, Changjiang, Danzhou, Lingao, Chengmai and other coastal cities and counties) and the Central Conservation Area (composed of Wuzhishan, Baoting, Qiongzhong, Baisha and other central cities and counties).

For the trade-offs and synergies in the “Three Poles” area of Hainan Island (Haikou, Sanya and Danyang Economic Circles), the Haikou Economic Circle is the most prominent: its goal of synergy being greater than trade-off is achieved in 61 groups, compared with the 54 groups in the Sanya Economic Circle and the 55 groups in the Danyang Economic Circle (Fig. 8a, b and c). Analysis reveals that the “Three Poles” region of Hainan has a certain positive effect on the sustainable development pattern of the province, and it has an overall effect on the direction of development of its cities and counties.

Comprehensive analysis of multi-objective trade-off and synergy relationships for the SDGs in Haikou Economic Circle (a), Sanya Economic Circle (b), Danyang Economic Circle (c), Coastal City Belt (d) and Central Conservation Area (e).

In general, the synergistic effect of the Haikou Economic Circle is much higher than that of other economic circles, and the higher trade-off effect is mainly concentrated on SDG2 (Table 2). Our study of its indicators found that the yield of grain for SDG2 has been declining in recent years, while the areas of cultivated land and planted fruit and vegetables are increasing, indicating that, in recent years, the planting species in Hainan Island have changed from rice to fruit and vegetables with greater yields. In addition to the high trade-off effect between SDG2 and SDG4, we also need to pay attention to SDG 6 in Sanya, which shows that Sanya has not paid enough attention to drinking water safety, so Sanya should strengthen clean drinking water, provide water and sanitation for residents, and manage it sustainably. The main trade-off effect of the Danyang Economic Circle is concentrated in SDG 4, while SDG 2 has a slightly higher proportion of synergies for other coastal economic circles, indicating that the agricultural output value of cities and counties in Danyang Economic Circle is higher than that of Hainan and Sanya, but the education level needs to be strengthened, and the government’s education fiscal expenditure in the province is still at a low level. Expanding educational and teaching reforms and comprehensively improving the quality of compulsory education would be significant factors in accelerating the modernization of education in the Danyang Economic Circle and developing sustainable and high-quality education.

“Three poles, one Belt and One Zone” trade-off and synergy analysis.

The overall number of coordination groups in the “One Belt” (coastal urban belt) is as high as 55, which is higher than either that of the Sanya Economic Circle or the Danyang Economic Circle in the “Three Poles” region, indicating that Hainan Province’s achievement of sustainable development goals is mainly concentrated in coastal areas (Fig. 8d and e). However, the main trade-off in the coastal belt is concentrated in SDG7, and the trade-off with other SDGs is greater than the synergy of six pairs, indicating that coastal cities should strive to ensure affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all. “One Area” (Central Conservation Area) has higher trade-offs than synergies and lags behind the coastal urban belt, with SDG4 and SDG8 showing significant trade-offs with other SDGs. The central region should strengthen the government’s support policies in terms of education and employment; it should also ensure that the central conservation area promotes production and employment by improving the level of education.

In general, investment in education in Hainan Island is mainly concentrated near the Haikou Economic Circle. In contrast, the Sanya and Danyang Economic Circles, and inland cities and counties still have relatively serious education problems, indicating that a large part of the government expenditure on the development of SDG4 in Hainan Island is in the areas surrounding Haikou City and that educational resources are unevenly distributed. Other economic circles also have the shortcomings in SDG10, indicating that Hainan should focus on strengthening government investment in cities and counties outside the Haikou Economic Circle, and strengthen the support for education and employment support mechanisms.

The impact of Hainan’s multidimensional situation on the SDGs

The realization of the SDGs has resulted from improvements in the Hainan people’s livelihoods, economic development, resource utilization, environmental quality and other different dimensions; however, the synergistic and trade-off effects of SDG indicators are manifested differently.

Synergies and trade-offs between SDGs 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, and 11 relating to livelihood improvement and SDG targets were analyzed (Figs. 2 and 9a): the synergies between livelihood improvement and most of the SDGs outweighed the trade-offs. The synergistic effect between the improvement of people’s livelihoods and SDGs 6, 12 and 14 was relatively high, indicating that Hainan is not only solving people’s livelihood problems such as employment, education, medical care, social security, and housing, but also paying attention to improving ecological protection and environmental quality.

Proportion of synergies and trade-off effects for SDGs in four dimensions: improvement of people’s livelihoods (a), economic development (b), resource utilization (c) and environmental quality (d).

The synergy and trade-off analysis of the indicators contained in SDGs 8, 9, 10 and 11 relating to economic development and SDG targets found that Hainan’s economic development includes different degrees of synergy with other SDGs, indicating that economic development has a promoting effect on Hainan’s SDGs (Fig. 9b). The synergy effect of Hainan’s economic development on SDG1 is the most obvious, achieving 45.97%; this indicates that economic development has a positive impact on residents’ disposable income and government income, and economic development benefits people’s livelihoods.

Synergies and trade-offs between SDG 6 and 7 indicators relating to resource use and SDG targets were found previously (Fig. 9c). The synergistic effects of Hainan’s use of resources on other SDGs far exceeded the trade-off effects. The synergistic effects of SDG1, SDG8 and SDG10 accounted for 39.33%, 32.59% and 33.33%, respectively. This indicated that strengthening Hainan’s use of resources can also better promote the disposable income of the government and people, and further improve the employment environment and economic growth.

The synergy and trade-off analysis of the indicators contained in SDGs 6, 11, 12, 14 and 15 relating to environmental quality and each SDG target (Fig. 9d) found that, similar to the results of resource utilization, the improvement in environmental quality in Hainan had the greatest impact on SDGs 1, 8 and 10. This indicates that the improvement in environmental quality in Hainan is conducive to reductions in the economy, employment and the gap between the rich and the poor, and other targets. This may be due to the fact that environmental protection increased jobs related to tourism and ecological agriculture, thereby promoting employment and increasing people’s incomes as a result.

In general, whether from the perspective of improving people’s livelihoods, economic development, resource utilization or environmental quality, the high-value areas with synergistic effects on all SDG indicators are concentrated in SDG1, 8 and 10. This demonstrates that Hainan has achieved significant results in narrowing the gap between the rich and the poor, increasing jobs and promoting the economic development of the whole island. Furthermore, the virtuous circle existing between the SDG indicators promotes the development of Hainan. The trade-off effects are mainly concentrated within SDG2 and SDG4, which demonstrates that Hainan has certain problems in grain output and educational resources. In the future, Hainan should strengthen further the planting areas and fair distribution of educational resources.

In terms of Hainan’s sustainable development, there are obvious spatial differences in the promotion of people’s livelihoods, economic development, resource utilization and environmental quality. The use of geographically weighted regression analysis can well illustrate this phenomenon and provides corresponding targeted recommendations for different regions. Based on the spatial and temporal distribution map of the mutual effects of improvement in Hainan people’s livelihoods, economic development, resource utilization and environmental quality (Fig. 10), it is clear that all have different degrees of promotion to each other; for example, the improvement in people’s livelihoods has the most obvious impact on the coastal areas of western Hainan, especially near the Danyang Economic Circle. The areas with the highest impact on resource utilization are concentrated in the central and northern regions, which are relatively rich in natural resources and improve people’s livelihoods while increasing the utilization of resources in order to better promote economic development. The role of resource utilization in promoting economic development is mainly concentrated in the eastern coastal areas such as Wenchang, Qionghai and Wanning. These eastern coastal areas are relatively rich in water resources and the output of aquatic products is relatively high: this can better drive the economic development of the region. At the same time, economic development also has an effective role in promoting environmental quality in Hainan, which mainly affects the southern coastal areas such as Sanya. Furthermore, the development of tourism around Sanya City makes environmental protection an important development direction in this region.

Uncertainty analysis and discussion

The achievement of the SDGs depends to a large extent on the availability of synergies; any trade-offs will largely determine the difficulties encountered in the sustainable development process. The uncertainty of sustainable development trade-offs and synergistic assessments arises, predominantly, from the following two aspects:

Uncertainty from data. The accuracy, completeness and reliability of the data may lead to increased uncertainty about the assessment results. The main data source of this paper was the Hainan Statistical Yearbook, which underwent strict data quality verification and demonstrated high overall accuracy29,30. However, since the compilation of the yearbook is a systematic work, involving a wide range of aspects, it is inevitable that certain errors will occur in the process of data collection and aggregation31. When designing the evaluation method, this paper considered the data quality issue, and the impact of data quality on the evaluation results was minimized.

Uncertainty in the selection of sustainability assessment indicators. The choice of evaluation indicators may affect the accuracy and reliability of evaluation results, as different indicators may reflect different aspects and are weighted differently due to their importance to the SDGs32,33. Based on the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals system, this paper comprehensively considered Hainan’s own characteristics and the availability of data required for assessment. For some goals (e.g., SDG1 and SDG12), only one indicator each was included in the assessment. In future work, we will continue to enrich the evaluation indicators so as to perform relevant assessment work more comprehensively and accurately.

We realize that correlation does not imply causation; this means that the observed synergy between two SDG indicators may be independently correlated with another process driving these two indicators, resulting in a correlation34. However, within the context of this research, based on theory and previous studies, it could be believed that there are theoretical links and actual direct or indirect interactions among most of the selected elements. Given this foundation, the application of the Spearman rank correlation coefficient as a methodological tool to examine the interrelationships among these variables is, to a considerable extent, justified. In subsequent research, it would be advantageous to employ more sophisticated statistical methodologies, such as structural equation modeling or interaction analysis, to further investigate the synergistic effects among these elements.

In addition, considering that the impact of synergies can be both positive and negative, and that this impact is uncertain and may vary depending on the time series, the time series data selected in this paper for the 12 periods, although they quantify the relevance of the SDGs, do not take into account their changes in time, and this should be considered in subsequent studies.

Conclusion

This paper analyzes the statistical data from Hainan Island’s sustainable development goals and various indicators in 12 periods from 2010 to 2021. It also analyzes correlations between data at each city and county level. In addition, we divided Hainan Island into regions and studied different ways of presenting trade-offs and synergies between the different regions. The results show that: (1) The sustainable development goals of 18 cities/counties in Hainan Island reflect the trend of continuous optimization between 2010 and 2021, which is consistent with the research results of Xinyi Zhang35. (2) The trade-offs and synergy analysis between the internal indicators of SDG targets demonstrates that the highest proportion achieved for indicators with a synergistic effect within SDG1 was 75.56%, and the proportions achieved for indicators with a trade-off effect on SDG8 and SDG9 affected by the epidemic were also relatively high. (3) The evaluation results between the targets demonstrate that the index pairs showing a synergistic relationship between SDG1 and the other eleven goals account for the highest proportion, the synergistic effect between SDG1 and SDG10 being the strongest, and the trade-off effect between SDG2 and SDG10 being relatively strong. (4) Trade-offs and synergies between SDGs and indicators have obvious spatial characteristics, with the highest level of synergy being near the Haikou and Sanya Economic Circles, and the higher trade-off level being in the central region. (5) The impact assessment of Hainan’s multi-dimensional situation on the SDGs found that the improvement in people’s livelihoods (the impact on other indicators), especially near the Danyang Economic Circle, was the most obvious. The regions with the highest impact on resource use were concentrated in the central and northern regions. The improvement to people’s livelihoods has the most obvious impact in the western coastal areas of Hainan, especially near the Danyang Economic Circle. The regions with the highest impact on resource use are concentrated in the central and northern regions. The role of resource utilization in promoting economic development is mainly concentrated in eastern coastal areas such as Wenchang, Qionghai and Wanning. Furthermore, economic development also plays an effective role in promoting the environmental quality of Hainan, the main impact areas being Sanya and other southern coastal areas. In general, the synergistic effect between the SDG targets and indicators on Hainan Island is greater than the trade-off effect, and there are obvious spatial characteristics. The four aspects of improving people’s livelihoods, economic development, resource utilization and the environmental quality of the island demonstrated synergistic effects in different regions. Secondly, in order to contribute to the sustainable economic development of Hainan Island, the relevant government should formulate relevant policies, for the agricultural production value, should encourage the sowing area of relevant grain, etc., for education, the government should introduce relevant policies to solve the problem of uneven distribution of educational resources for residents on the island, and at the same time increase government expenditure on education, which will help improve the SDG2 and SDG4 of Hainan Island. The synergistic indicators identified in this study will help the Hainan government formulate more effective and integrated strategies, policies and actions, address multiple objectives at the same time, and maximize the overall process of sustainable development. The trade-offs found in this study can help local governments in Hainan prioritize interventions and allocate resources more effectively to achieve the 2030 Agenda in a comprehensive and inclusive manner.
